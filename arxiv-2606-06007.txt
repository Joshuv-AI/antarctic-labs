Diffusion Models for Adaptive Sequential Data Generation
Haoyang Cao, Minshuo Chen, Yinbin Han, Renyuan Xu

Abstract
Generating realistic synthetic sequential data is critical in real-world applications across operations research, finance, healthcare, energy systems, and scientific computing, where time-indexed observations are used for prediction, simulation, risk assessment, and data-driven decision-making. While diffusion models have achieved remarkable success in generating static data, their direct extensions to sequential settings often fail to capture temporal dependence and information structure. Designing diffusion models that can simulate sequential data in an adapted manner, and hence without anticipation of future information, therefore remains an open challenge. In this work, we propose a sequential forward–backward diffusion framework for adapted time series generation. Our approach progressively injects and removes noise along the sequence, conditioning on the previously generated history to ensure adaptiveness. A novel score-matching objective is introduced for efficient parallel training. We derive rigorous statistical guarantees under a generic framework, then establish score approximation, score estimation, and distribution estimation results with ReLU networks serving as a concrete instance. Empirically, we validate our method on synthetic data, including ARMA models and Gaussian processes, and demonstrate its effectiveness in constructing mean-variance optimal portfolios. Our code is available at https://github.com/yinbinhan/adapted_diffusion_model .

Introduction

Generating realistic synthetic sequential data is important for many real-world applications across operations research, financial markets, healthcare, energy systems, and scientific computing, where time-indexed observations are used for prediction, simulation, risk assessment, and data-driven decision-making. At the same time, this task remains challenging in complex sequential settings, where the data may exhibit temporal dependence, nonstationarity, nonlinear dynamics, and multiple sources of uncertainty. Beyond matching the high-dimensional joint law of the observed process, a sequential generator must also respect the information flow under which the data are observed: each generated value should depend only on the past and present information available at that stage, and not on future observations. This adapted (i.e., non-anticipative) structure is a basic modeling requirement in domains where the timing of information affects prediction or decision-making.

A direct extension to sequential data—jointly noising and denoising the full trajectory—can match the joint distribution, but does not enforce the underlying information flow. In particular, it does not guarantee that each generated value depends only on past information, and thus may violate the causal ordering inherent in sequential systems.

This gap motivates the central question of our paper:

How can we unlock diffusion models for sequential data generation, while preserving the adaptiveness and retaining theoretical guarantees?

We address this question by introducing a sequential forward–backward diffusion framework, Adaptive Diffusion for Sequential Data Generation
(
AD-Seq
)
, that aligns the diffusion dynamics with the temporal information flow. The key idea is to generate the sample path through conditional reverse diffusion steps, such that each generated coordinate depends only on the previously generated history. This construction preserves the adapted structure of sequential data while retaining the flexibility of diffusion-based generation.

Beyond full time series generation, this conditional perspective also suggests a broader use of
AD-Seq
as a generator of admissible scenarios conditional on available information. Given a realized history, the model can generate multiple future continuations that respect the same information flow, which can then be used in downstream tasks such as statistical inference, predictive control, and risk evaluation. Although we focus on time series in this paper, the same principle can be applied whenever generated data must respect certain causal structure, including panel data, network data, and causal inference.

Our work and contributions.

Our contributions are threefold.

(i) A new diffusion model framework for adapted sequential data generation.

We introduce a novel Adaptive Diffusion for Sequential Data Generation
(
AD-Seq
) framework
for generating time series that are adapted to an information flow. Unlike classical diffusion models that operate on the full time series as a single object, our construction generates each coordinate sequentially, conditioning only on the previously generated history. This yields a generative model that preserves the conditional distributions of the data while enforcing adaptiveness by design. At a conceptual level, this provides a principled way to incorporate filtration constraints into diffusion models. On the algorithmic side, we develop a novel score-matching objective that decouples the learning problem across time steps, enabling parallel training of the score functions and ensuring scalability to high-dimensional sequences. Beyond synthetic data generation, this framework opens the door to a broad range of downstream applications, including multi-step prediction, predictive decision-making, and statistical inference under information-flow constraints.

(ii) Statistical learning theory for the
AD-Seq
framework.

We develop a general statistical learning theory for our proposed
AD-Seq
framework
, including score approximation, score estimation, and distribution estimation guarantees. Our analysis is network architecture-agnostic and can incorporate generic approximation and covering number assumptions.
A key technical challenge is to mitigate the growing length of the history for long-horizon generation. We introduce a history truncation mechanism based on the temporal dependence decay in the sequence. This yields the following finite-sample total-variation bound for the conditional law generated by
AD-Seq
:

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}}).

Here,
n
is the sample size and
\beta
is the distribution smoothness parameter defined in Assumption
4.5

d_{\rm trunc}
, rather than the full horizon length, showing that long-horizon generation can remain statistically tractable when temporal dependence decays; see for example the exponential and polynomial decays in Propositions
4.16
–
4.17
. Moreover, despite the additional adaptiveness constraint, this rate matches the best-known rates for static diffusion models.

(iii) Empirical validation for temporal structure learning and decision-making.

We implement the proposed framework using transformer architectures with causal masking and evaluate it on both synthetic and real data. The empirical results indicate that the method captures meaningful aspects of the sequential dependence structure, rather than merely reproducing static marginals. On ARMA models, it accurately recovers the autocorrelation structure, including in small-sample regimes, and produces synthetic samples whose estimated autocorrelation functions are more accurate than those obtained directly from the empirical training data. On Gaussian processes, it reconstructs the covariance matrix with the correct decay pattern, showing that the model captures the dependence structure of the underlying process. We further evaluate performance in a downstream decision problem, namely mean–variance portfolio optimization on the S&P 500. In this experiment, policies trained using diffusion-generated samples attain the highest Sharpe ratios among several benchmark methods. These findings suggest that respecting temporal dependence and information flow in sequential data generation can lead to measurable improvements in both statistical fidelity and downstream decision quality.

Problem Setup

Our objective is to design a diffusion model that generates adapted time series whose law matches the underlying data distribution, with respect to a specified (and meaningful) filtration. For notational convenience, we present the development in the one-dimensional case: let
\mathbf{X}_{0}=(X_{0}^{h})_{h=1}^{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
denote a length-
H
time series in discrete-time and adapted to the filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{h})_{h=1}^{H}
, i.e.,
X_{0}^{h}\in\mathcal{F}_{h}
. The methodology extends straightforwardly to higher dimensions. In addition, denote
\mu_{0}^{[1:H]}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{H})
as the distribution of
\mathbf{X}_{0}
.

Preliminaries on diffusion models.

In the classical setting
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, diffusion models include a forward process that gradually adds noise to data and a time-reverse process that gradually removes noise to generate new data. Mathematically, the forward process is often chosen to be an
H
-dimensional Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process defined on
\mathbb{R}^{H}
:

{\rm d}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}g(t)\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{t}{\rm d}t+\sqrt{g(t)}{\rm d}{\bf W}_{t}^{\prime},\quad\mathbf{X}_{0}^{\prime}\sim\mu_{0}^{[1:H]},

(1)

where
\{{\bf W}^{\prime}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
is a
H
-dimensional Brownian motion and
g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a positive-valued function. The time-reversed SDE of (
1
) is given by

{\rm d}\overline{\bf X}^{\prime}_{t}=\Big[\frac{1}{2}g(T-t)\overline{\mathbf{X}}^{\prime}_{t}+g(T-t)\nabla_{x}\log p_{T-t}(\overline{\bf X}_{t}^{\prime})\Big]{\rm d}t+\sqrt{g(T-t)}{\rm d}\overline{\bf W}^{\prime}_{t},

(2)

where
\{\overline{\bf W}^{\prime}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T}
is another independent
H
-dimensional Brownian motion and
p_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{H}\to\mathbb{R}
represents the marginal density of
\mathbf{X}_{t}^{\prime}
at time
t
in the forward process (
1
).

Such a framework is appropriate for generating static data or fully predictable sequences (e.g., videos), where no filtration-based adaptiveness constraint is imposed. Even when the forward OU process is driven by independent Gaussian noise across coordinates, the corresponding backward dynamics generally fail to preserve the information flow of the data, since the score term
\nabla\log p_{T-t}(\cdot)
depends on the joint density and thus introduces cross-coordinate dependence. As a result, the value at a given time step may implicitly depend on future components of the sequence. Consequently, adaptiveness with respect to a prescribed filtration is not enforced, and the generated data may violate the intended information flow or temporal structure. This limitation motivates us to develop the
AD-Seq
framework
, which adds and removes noise progressively along the time index, conditioning only on previously generated history, and thereby preserves the prescribed information flow by construction.

Adaptive Sequential Diffusion Model.

For each coordinate/timestamp
h

(1\leq h\leq H)
, we define a pair of forward and time-reversed one-dimensional SDEs, driven by OU processes. In the construction below, we specialize to the constant noise schedule
g(\cdot)\equiv 1
.

For
h=1
, we consider the following one-dimensional forward process on
\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle{\rm d}X_{t}^{1}=-\tfrac{1}{2}X_{t}^{1}{\rm d}t+{\rm d}W_{t}^{1},\;\;X_{0}^{1}\sim\phi^{1}(0,\cdot),

(3)

where
\phi^{1}(0,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the probability density of
X_{0}^{1}
. To generate new samples, we consider another stochastic process on
\mathbb{R}

3

\displaystyle{\rm d}\bar{X}_{t}^{1}=\Big[\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{X}_{t}^{1}+\partial_{x}\log\phi^{1}(T-t,\bar{X}_{t}^{1})\Big]{\rm d}t+{\rm d}\bar{W}_{t}^{1},\;\;\bar{X}_{0}^{1}\sim\phi^{1}(T,\cdot),

(4)

where
\phi^{1}(t,\cdot):\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the probability density of
X_{t}^{1}

Score matching.

To estimate the score function, a natural choice is to minimize the weighted quadratic loss for all coordinates:

\displaystyle\min_{s^{h}\in\mathcal{S}^{h}}\;\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{[1:h)}_{T}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{[1:h)}_{T}}\big[\big|s^{h}(t,X_{t}^{h},\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{[1:h)}_{T})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\bar{\mathbf{X}}^{[1:h)}_{T}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\big|^{2}\big]{\rm d}t,

(11)

is a function class (often neural networks) for coordinate

. Here,

[

\displaystyle\min_{s^{h}\in\mathcal{S}^{h}}\;\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\big[\big|s^{h}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\big|^{2}\big]{\rm d}t.

Adaptiveness property.

We now establish the adaptiveness property of the time series generated by Algorithm
1
. This property is
not automatic
for vanilla diffusion models. Specifically, when a vanilla diffusion model is applied to the full time series jointly, the reverse-time score

generally depends on all time indices, so the update of an earlier coordinate may use information from later coordinates. Such dependence breaks the intended information structure of a sequential system. By contrast, Algorithm

generates coordinates sequentially and uses only the previously generated history when sampling the next coordinate.

Assumption 3.1
.

For each coordinate

Proposition 3.2

(Adaptiveness)

Suppose Assumption

Then the output

4
Distribution Estimation Theory

In this section, we present our main distribution estimation results for
AD-Seq
. To ease the presentation, we consider utilizing the continuous-time
backward processes (
4
) and (
6
) for distribution estimation. In practice, a proper discretization is applied
to generate samples, whose deviation from the continuous-time backward process can be controlled
by the step size of the discretization; see
Chen et al. 2023a
, which requires a Lipschitz score, and
Benton et al. 2024
, which removes this assumption via stochastic localization.

The key step is decomposing joint distribution learning to a series of conditional distribution learning given the sequential conditional nature of our framework. For learning one-step conditional distributions, we leverage recent advances in conditional diffusion models
[
86
,
70
,
94
]
. Recall the ground-truth joint distribution is
\mu_{0}^{[1:H]}
and we denote the learned joint distribution of
\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{T-t_{0}}^{[1:H]}
in Algorithm
1
by
\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{[1:H]}
. In addition, let
\mu^{h}_{0}[\mathbf{z}]
be the ground-truth distribution of
X^{h}_{0}|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}=\mathbf{z}
and
\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{z}]
be the learned distribution of
\tilde{X}_{T-t_{0}}^{h}|\tilde{\mathbf{X}}_{T-t_{0}}^{[1:h)}=\mathbf{z}
. The chain rule of TV distance implies that

\displaystyle{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{[1:H]},\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{[1:H]})\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}\sim\mu_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}\left(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}]\right)\right].

(16)

Inspired by (
16
), it suffices to bound the one-step conditional distribution for each
h\in[H]
in TV distance. The truncated nature of loss function
\ell^{[k:h]}(\cdot)
implies
\hat{\mu}^{h}_{t_{0}}[\mathbf{z}^{[1:h)}]=\hat{\mu}^{h}_{t_{0}}[\mathbf{z}^{[k:h)}]
for any
\mathbf{z}^{[1:h)}\in\mathbb{R}^{h-1}
. Moreover, triangle inequality implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}])\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}])\right]}_{\text{learning error}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}])\right]}_{\text{truncation error}}.

(17)

Here, we overload the notation
\mu^{h}_{0}[\mathbf{z}^{[k:h)}]
as the distribution of
X_{0}^{h}|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}=\mathbf{z}^{[k:h)}
for any
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{h-k}
.
We make the following assumption on the truncation error.

Assumption 4.1
.

Denote
d_{\rm trunc}=h-k+1
as the truncated sequence length. Fix
h\in[H]
and consider
k<h
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}])\right]\leq u(d_{\rm trunc}),

where
u(\cdot)
only depends on
d_{\rm trunc}
and is monotonically decreasing.

This assumption imposes a quantitative decay-of-dependence property in expectation: the expected total variation distance between the conditional law given the full history and that given only the most recent
d_{\rm trunc}
observations is bounded by
u(d_{\rm trunc})
. As
u
decreases in
d_{\rm trunc}
, the dependence on the remote past vanishes progressively. Such an assumption is justified in many financial applications
[
92
,
93
]
. In particular, our numerical experiments in Section
5.2
show that log returns of the S&P 500 index are close to Markovian
[
95
,
93
]
.
Thus, conditioning on a short history is nearly as informative as conditioning on the full past. The decay rate
u(\cdot)
plays an important role in our theoretical analysis. We will explicitly discuss how different decay regimes influence the choice of
k
in Section
4.4
.

It remains to bound the learning-error term in (
17
).
To proceed, we decompose it informally into three sources: the bias from early stopping, the mismatch in the initial distribution, and the error from score estimation. The first source comes from stopping the time-reverse SDE at time
T-t_{0}
rather than evolving it all the way to time
T
. The second arises because the sampling process is initialized from the standard normal distribution instead of the exact forward marginal at time
T
. The third is due to replacing the true conditional score along the sampling trajectory by its learned approximation
\hat{s}^{h}
. This decomposition is discussed in more detail in Section
4.3
.

We then reduce the score-estimation contribution to a statistical learning problem, consisting of an approximation component and a finite-sample statistical error component. Section
4.1
controls the former by showing that the neural network class contains a candidate whose discrepancy from the target conditional score is bounded at a prescribed rate. Section
4.2
controls the latter by proving that the empirical score-matching minimizer attains small population score-matching risk. Combining these two estimates yields a bound on the score-estimation contribution, and hence on the learning error and the overall distribution estimation error.

4.1
Score Approximation

We devote this subsection to conditional score approximation theory. For ease of exposition, we overload

Assumption 4.2

(Conditional sub-Gaussianity)

There are constants

\displaystyle\phi^{1}(0,x)\leq C_{1}^{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{1}\left|x\right|^{2}/2\right)

and a vector

\displaystyle\phi_{\mathbf{z}}^{h}(0,x)\leq C_{1}^{h}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{h}\left|x-(\mathbf{v}^{h})^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}/2\right)

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{h-k}

in Appendix

As a consequence of Assumption
4.2
, the following lemma shows that all joint distributions of interest are also sub-Gaussian.

Lemma 4.3
.

Under Assumption
4.2
, the probability density
p^{[1:h]}(\cdot)
of
\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h]}
is sub-Gaussian for each

C_{3},C_{4}>0

p^{[1:h]}(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{3}\exp\left(-C_{4}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}/2\right).

Similar to constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

are also

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction on

C_{3}=C_{1}
and
C_{4}=C_{2}
. Assume the inductive hypothesis holds for
p^{[1:h-1]}(\cdot)

Set
C_{4}^{\prime}=\min\left\{C_{4},C_{2}\right\}
. For every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{h}

\displaystyle p^{[1:h]}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=p^{[1:h-1]}(\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)})\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)}}(0,x^{h})

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}C_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{4}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)}\right\|^{2}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}}{2}\left|x^{h}-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}C_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{4}^{\prime}}{2}\left(\left\|\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)}\right\|^{2}+\left|x^{h}-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{[1:h)}\right|^{2}\right)\right).

(18)

Define
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\times h}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{h-1}&-\mathbf{v}\\
-\mathbf{v}^{\top}&1\end{pmatrix}.

We further bound (

) with

\displaystyle p^{[1:h]}(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{3}C_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{4}^{\prime}}{2}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\right).

Here, the matrix
\mathbf{M}
is positive definite as long as the Schur complement of the top-left block
\mathbf{S}\coloneqq\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{h-1}
is positive definite. To see this, the Woodbury identity implies that

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}/\mathbf{S}\coloneqq 1-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\mathbf{I}_{h-1}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{v}=1-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{I}_{h-1}-\frac{\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}}{1+\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}}\right)\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{1+\left\|\mathbf{v}\right\|^{2}}>0.

Combining (
18

\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\geq\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}
yields

\displaystyle p^{[1:h]}(\mathbf{x})\leq C_{3}C_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{4}^{\prime}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{M})}{2}\left\|\mathbf{x}\right\|^{2}\right),

which completes the proof.

\displaystyle\left|f^{\rm net}(x,\mathbf{z},t)-f_{3}(x,\mathbf{z},t)\right|\lesssim N^{-\beta},\;\forall x\in[-R_{1},R_{1}],\mathbf{z}\in[-R_{1},R_{1}]^{h-k},t\in[t_{0},T].

Since
f_{3}
is Lipschitz continuous over the compact domain, many universal approximation results
[
98
,
99
]
guarantee the existence of neural networks as required in Assumption

. In Section

, we will justify this assumption using ReLU networks as a concrete example.
We are ready to state the main result in this subsection.

Theorem 4.9

(Score approximation)
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.2
,
4.5
,
4.8
hold. Let
N>0
be a sufficiently large integer and we set early-stop time
t_{0}=N^{-C_{\sigma}}
and terminal time

for some positive constants
C_{\sigma}
and
C_{\alpha}
. There is a neural network
s^{\star}\in\mathcal{S}^{h}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{B}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-\beta}(\log N)^{2+\beta/2},\;\;\forall t\in[t_{0},T].

(26)

Theorem
4.9
establishes a score approximation result over the time interval
[t_{0},T]
. It will serve as a building block for the subsequent score estimation theory. Compared with prior work
[
86
]
, the main technical difference is the introduction of an additional truncation step. This is necessary because the conditional information

is not assumed to be bounded.

Proof.

Let
s^{\star}(t,x,\mathbf{z})=f^{\rm net}(x,\mathbf{z},t)
given in Assumption
4.8
. It follows from Lemma
4.7
that

\displaystyle\left|s^{\star}(x,\mathbf{z},t)-\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{B}{\sigma_{t}^{2}\phi_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)}N^{-\beta}\log^{\frac{\beta+2}{2}}N.

(27)

when
x,\mathbf{z},t
are bounded as in Assumption
4.8
.

Consider the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]}_{(E_{1})}+

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|>R_{5}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]}_{(E_{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|\leq R_{5}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]}_{(E_{3})},

where
R_{1}
and
R_{5}
will be chosen later.

We begin with the error term
(E_{1})
. Note that

\displaystyle(E_{1})\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})\right|^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}\right\}\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right].

As
\left|s^{\star}(x,\mathbf{z},t)\right|\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}
by Assumption

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}}p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}}\exp\left(-C_{5}\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|^{2}/2\right){\rm d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}(R_{1})^{-1}\exp\left(-C_{5}R_{1}^{2}/2\right),

(28)

where

is given in Corollary
4.4
.

Combining (
28
) and Lemma
A.5
, and choosing
R_{1}\geq\sqrt{\frac{4\beta}{\min(C_{5},C_{6})}\log N}
, we bound the term

with

\displaystyle(E_{1})\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}(R_{1})^{-1}\exp\left(-C_{5}R_{1}^{2}/2\right)+\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\exp(-C_{6}R_{1}^{2}/2)\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-2\beta}.

(29)

Next, we bound
(E_{2})
with

\displaystyle(E_{2})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|>R_{5}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|>R_{5}\right\}\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]

(30)

Lemma
A.6
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|>R_{5}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}\int_{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|>R_{5}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x){\rm d}xp^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}(R_{5})^{-1}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}R_{5}^{2}/2\right),

(31)

and moreover

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|>R_{5}\right\}\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}\int_{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|>R_{5}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|^{2}{\rm d}xp^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}R_{5}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}R_{5}^{2}/2\right).

(32)

With (
30
), (
31
) and (
32
), we choose
R_{5}\geq\sqrt{\frac{4\beta}{C_{2}^{\prime}}\log N}
to obtain an upper bound for
(E_{2})
:

\displaystyle(E_{2})

\displaystyle\lesssim{\sigma_{t}^{-4}}(R_{5})^{-1}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}R_{5}^{2}/2\right)+\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}R_{5}\exp\left(-C_{2}^{\prime}R_{5}^{2}/2\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\cdot\frac{N^{-2\beta}}{\sqrt{\log N}}+\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-2\beta}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-2\beta}.

(33)

We further bound
(E_{3})\leq(E_{4})+(E_{5})
with

\displaystyle(E_{4})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\bigg[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|\leq R_{5},\left|\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|<\epsilon_{\rm low}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\bigg]

\displaystyle(E_{5})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\bigg[1\left\{\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|\leq R_{5},\left|\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|\geq\epsilon_{\rm low}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\bigg]

We bound
(E_{4})
with

\displaystyle(E_{4})

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\ \left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|\leq R_{5},\\
\left|\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|<\epsilon_{\rm low}\end{subarray}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})\right|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mathbb{E}\left[1\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\left\|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1},\ \left|X_{t}^{h}-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}\right|\leq R_{5},\\
\left|\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|<\epsilon_{\rm low}\end{subarray}\right\}\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right],

where
\epsilon_{\rm low}>0
will be chosen later.
Lemma
A.7
implies the following upper bound for
(E_{4})
:

\displaystyle(E_{4})\lesssim\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}\left(\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}R_{5}\epsilon_{\rm low}+\frac{\epsilon_{\rm low}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}R_{5}^{3}\right)p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}\mathbf{z}\lesssim\frac{\epsilon_{\rm low}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}\cdot(\log N)^{3/2}.

(34)

By the approximation guarantee (

), for any fixed

\displaystyle\int_{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|\leq R_{5}}1\left\{\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\geq\epsilon_{\rm low}\right\}\left|s^{\star}(x,\mathbf{z},t)-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|^{2}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|\leq R_{5}}1\left\{\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\geq\epsilon_{\rm low}\right\}\frac{B^{2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)}N^{-2\beta}\log^{\beta+2}N{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{B^{2}\log^{\beta+2}N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}\epsilon_{\rm low}}N^{-2\beta}R_{5}.

Consequently, we obtain an upper bound for
(E_{5})

\displaystyle(E_{5})\lesssim\frac{B^{2}\log^{\beta+2}N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}\epsilon_{\rm low}}N^{-2\beta}R_{5}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}\mathbf{z}\leq\frac{B^{2}\log^{5/2+\beta}N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}\epsilon_{\rm low}}N^{-2\beta}.

(35)

Combining (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-2\beta}+\frac{\epsilon_{\rm low}(\log N)^{3/2}}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{B^{2}\log^{5/2+\beta}N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}\epsilon_{\rm low}}N^{-2\beta}.

(36)

Substitution

back into (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|s^{\star}(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]\lesssim\frac{B}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-\beta}(\log N)^{2+\beta/2},\;\;\forall t\in[t_{0},T].

(37)

4.2
Score Estimation

In this section, we present the score estimation result for one-step conditional diffusion models. Define the risk function as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{[k:h]}(s)=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{h}|\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|X_{0}^{h}}\left[\left|s(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)})-\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}(t,X_{t}^{h})\right|^{2}\right]{\rm d}t.

(38)

Our goal is to bound expected risk
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}^{[k:h]}(\hat{s})]
, where we recall that
\hat{s}^{h}
is a minimizer to the empirical score matching risk
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}^{[k:h]}(s)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell^{[k:h]}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{[k:h]};s)
with
\ell^{[k:h]}(\cdot;s)
defined in (
15
). In the subsequent analysis, we suppress the superscript
[k:h]
when the context is clear. Recall the dataset
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
.

One key step towards the score estimation theory is bounding the logarithmic covering number of a function class over a compact domain. To this end, it is convenient to recenter the empirical by defining

\displaystyle\ell_{0}(x,\mathbf{z};s)=\ell(x,\mathbf{z};s)-\ell(x,\mathbf{z};\bar{s}),\;\;\text{with}\;\;\bar{s}(t,x,\mathbf{z})=\partial_{x}\log\phi_{\mathbf{z}}^{h}(t,x).

(39)

Moreover, we define a truncated loss associated with
\ell(\cdot;s)
as

\displaystyle\ell^{\rm trunc}(x,\mathbf{z};s)=\ell(x,\mathbf{z};s)1\left\{\left|x-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|\leq R_{3},\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}\right\}.

(40)

The truncated centered loss
\ell_{0}^{\rm trunc}(\cdot;s)
is defined in a similar way.
Let
\mathcal{G}=\left\{\ell_{0}^{\rm trunc}(\cdot;s):s\in\mathcal{S}^{h}\right\}
be a loss function class induced by the neural network class
\mathcal{S}^{h}
. We make the following definition on the network complexity.

Definition 4.10

(Network Complexity)
.

We denote
Network-Complexity
by the logarithmic covering number
\log(\mathscr{N}(\delta))
.

We are ready to state the main result of this subsection.

Theorem 4.11

(Score estimation)
.

Suppose Assumptions
4.2
and
4.5
hold and let
\mathcal{S}^{h}
be a neural network class satisfying Assumption
4.8
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})]\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{t_{0}}N^{-\beta}+\frac{1}{t_{0}}N^{-\beta}(\log N)^{2+\beta/2}+\frac{\log N}{nt_{0}}\cdot\texttt{Network-Complexity}+\delta.

Theorem
4.11
bounds the score estimation error by four error sources: the first term is the truncation error from restricting the unbounded conditioning variable to a bounded domain; the second term is the score approximation error inherited from Theorem
4.9
; the third term is the statistical error over a prescribed network class; and the fourth term
\delta
is the covering resolution of
\mathcal{G}
. We emphasize that the analysis is
agnostic
to the choice of network class: any class satisfying Assumption
4.8
can be substituted into Theorem
4.11
. Section
4.4
instantiates the ReLU networks to obtain a concrete rate.

Proof.

We follow the proof strategy as in
[
74
, Appendix C.1]
and only mention the differences here. With
\ell_{0}(\cdot;s)
in (
39
) and the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we define centered empirical risk as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(s)\coloneqq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{0}(x_{i}^{h},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{[k:h)};s)

Note that
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(s)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}(s)
share the same minimizer
\hat{s}
. One key observation is that population centered risk is exactly the risk we would like to minimize, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{0}(s)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h]}}\left[\ell_{0}(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)},s)\right]=\mathcal{R}(s).

(41)

We define truncated versions
\mathcal{L}_{0}^{\rm trunc}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\rm trunc}
using
\ell_{0}^{\rm trunc}(\cdot;s)
accordingly.

Consider the following decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})]=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(s^{\star})]}_{\rm(I)}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})-\mathcal{R}(s^{\star})]}_{\rm(II)}.

Here,
s^{\star}
is the constructed score approximator given in Theorem
4.9
with approximation error:

\displaystyle{\rm(I)}\lesssim\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{B}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}N^{-\beta}(\log N)^{2+\beta/2}{\rm d}t\lesssim\frac{1}{t_{0}}N^{-\beta}(\log N)^{2+\beta/2},

(42)

where we apply Lemma D.1 in
[
86
]
to bound the integral
\frac{1}{T-t_{0}}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{-4}{\rm d}t=\mathcal{O}(1/t_{0})
. We proceed with the calculation as in
[
74
]
. By applying the fact that
\hat{s}
is the minimizer of
\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}
, we further bound
{\rm(II)}
with

\displaystyle{\rm(II)}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{L}_{0}(\hat{s})-\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{0}(\hat{s})]}_{\rm(II-A)}.

Bounding term (II-A).

This part is an extension from a bounded domain to an unbounded domain with truncation argument. The key difference compared to
[
74
]
is the truncation error and the covering number. Specifically, we first have

\displaystyle\text{(II-A)}\leq\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D},\bar{\mathcal{D}}}\left[\sup_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\ell_{0}(\bar{x}_{i}^{h},\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{[k:h)},s)-\ell_{0}(x_{i}^{h},\mathbf{x}_{i}^{[k:h)},s)]-a\mathcal{R}(s)\right]}_{(\spadesuit)}+a\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})],

(43)

where
a>0
is a parameter to control bias-variance trade-off commonly used in the literature.
Here,
\bar{\mathcal{D}}\coloneqq\left\{\bar{\mathbf{x}}_{i}^{[k:h]}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an independent copy of samples following the same distribution as
\mathcal{D}
. We use the covering number to bound
(\spadesuit)
. We construct a covering of
\mathcal{G}
in
L^{\infty}
-norm as follows. Let
\delta>0
be given. We select a collection of score networks

such that for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
there is an index

with

Recall that
B_{2}=\left[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-R_{3},\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+R_{3}\right]
.
Note that for each
s\in\mathcal{S}
(
s
can depend on
\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h]}
) we have

\displaystyle(T-t_{0})\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h]}}\left[\left|\ell(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)-\ell^{\rm trunc}(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|X_{0}^{h}=y}\left[\left|s(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{z})-\frac{\alpha_{t}y-X_{t}^{h}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|^{2}\right]\phi_{\mathbf{z}}^{h}(0,y)p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}y{\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|X_{0}^{h}=y}\left[\left|s(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{z})-\frac{\alpha_{t}y-X_{t}^{h}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|^{2}\right]\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}y{\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|X_{0}^{h}=y}\left[\left|s(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{z})\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\alpha_{t}y-X_{t}^{h}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|^{2}\right]\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}y{\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{t}^{h}|X_{0}^{h}=y}\left[\left|s(t,X_{t}^{h},\mathbf{z})\right|^{2}+\left|\frac{\alpha_{t}y-X_{t}^{h}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|^{2}\right]\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}y{\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t.

Since
\phi_{\mathbf{z}}^{h}(0,\cdot)
and

are both sub-Gaussian (Assumption
4.2
and Corolary

), we deduce that

\displaystyle(T-t_{0})\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h]}}\left[\left|\ell(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)-\ell^{\rm trunc}(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int_{\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}>R_{1}}\left(\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\exp(-C_{6}\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|^{2}/2){\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\int\int_{\left|y-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|>R_{3}}\left(\frac{\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\exp(-C_{2}\left|y-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}/2){\rm d}y{\rm d}\mathbf{z}{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq 2(1+\log N)\big(\exp(-C_{6}R_{1}^{2}/2)(R_{1})^{-1}+\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2)(R_{3})^{-1}\big)\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\rm d}t

We take
R_{1}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\beta}{C_{6}}\log N}
and
R_{3}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2\beta}{C_{2}}\log N}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h]}}\left[\left|\ell(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)-\ell^{\rm trunc}(X_{0}^{h},\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)};s)\right|\right]\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{\log N}}{t_{0}}N^{-\beta}.

(46)

4.3
Distribution Estimation

In this subsection, we apply the score estimation theory from the previous subsection to obtain one-step and joint distribution estimation theory. Recall
d_{\rm trunc}=h-k+1
.

Theorem 4.12

(Distribution estimation)
.

Let
h\in[H]
and
k\in[h-1]
. Assume that

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}[{\rm KL}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}]\|\mathcal{N}(0,1))]<\infty.

Under Assumptions
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.5
,
4.8
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}])]]\lesssim\sqrt{t_{0}}(\log(1/t_{0}))^{\frac{d_{\rm trunc}}{2}}+\exp(-T)+\sqrt{T\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})]}+u(d_{\rm trunc}).

Theorem
4.12
translates the score estimation result in Theorem
4.11
into a distribution estimation guarantee. The first term is the early-stopping error from terminating the sampling process at
T-t_{0}\in(0,T)
, and the second is the initialization error from starting the sampling process at the standard Gaussian rather than the true forward marginal at time
T
. The third term propagates the score estimation error through the sampling process, with
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[\mathcal{R}(\hat{s})]
controlled by Theorem
4.11
. The last term,
u(d_{\rm trunc})
, is the truncation error, controlled by Assumption
4.1
.

Proof.

The proof largely follows from
[
86
, Appendix D.3]
. We first check that condition (D.20) in
[
86
, Lemma D.4]
holds. Note that for any
s\in\mathcal{S}^{h}
and
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{h-k}
, Assumption
4.8
, Lemmas
A.2
and
A.4
imply that

\displaystyle\int\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\left|s(t,x,\mathbf{z})-\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|^{2}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}+\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\int\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}^{\prime}}{2}(x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z})^{2}\right)\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}+\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}^{\prime}}{2}u^{2}\right)\frac{u^{2}+\log N}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}{\rm d}u\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{4}}.

We also prove an analog to
[
86
, Lemma D.5]
to bound the early stopping error. Let
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{h-k}
be given. Recall in Lemma
A.3
we define
B_{2}=\left[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-R_{3},\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+R_{3}\right]
and furthermore we denote
B_{3}\coloneqq\left[\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-R_{6},\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+R_{6}\right]
. Then we first decompose the TV distance as

\displaystyle{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{z}],\mu_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{z}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|{\rm d}x.

(47)

For the second term in (
47
), Assumption
4.2
and Lemma
A.6
imply

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x){\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{3}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim(R_{3})^{-1}\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2)+(R_{6})^{-1}\exp(-C_{2}^{\prime}R_{6}^{2}/2)\lesssim\epsilon.

(48)

as long as we take
R_{3},R_{6}\asymp\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}
.
Take expectation of
\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}
to have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}}[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}],\mu_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}])]\lesssim\int\int_{(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}x{\rm d}\mathbf{z}+\epsilon.

We consider a region
B_{4}\coloneqq\left\{\mathbf{z}:\left\|\mathbf{z}\right\|_{\infty}\leq R_{1}\right\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{4}}\int_{(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}x{\rm d}\mathbf{z}\lesssim\epsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)},

(49)

as long as we choose
R_{1}\asymp\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}
.

To bound the first term in (
47
), we let
K_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\exp\big(-(2\sigma_{t}^{2})^{-1}\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}\big)
be an auxiliary kernel. Recall

\displaystyle\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y))\alpha_{t}K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y+(\alpha_{t}-1)\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{1}}(\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y))\alpha_{t}K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y+(\alpha_{t}-1)\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{1}}(\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y))\alpha_{t}K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y.

(50)

Since
\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)\leq C_{1}
for any

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{1}}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\right|\alpha_{t}K_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y\lesssim\int_{\left|u\right|>R_{2}}\exp(-u^{2}/2){\rm d}u\lesssim(R_{2})^{-1}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2).

We choose

to make the integral less than
\epsilon
. To handle the integral over

, we apply Assumption

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\right|\leq B\left|x-y\right|\leq\frac{B}{\alpha_{t}}\left|\alpha_{t}y-x\right|+\frac{B(1-\alpha_{t})}{\alpha_{t}}\left|x\right|\leq\frac{B\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}R_{2}+\frac{B(1-\alpha_{t})}{\alpha_{t}}\left|x\right|.

Thus, Eq. (

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}R_{2}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\left|x\right|+(\alpha_{t}-1)\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)+\epsilon.

Then, integration
x
over

gives us

\displaystyle\int_{(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}R_{1}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\int_{B_{2}\cup B_{3}}\left|x\right|{\rm d}x+\int_{B_{2}\cup B_{3}}(\alpha_{t}-1)\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)+\epsilon{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}R_{1}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\int_{B_{2}\cup B_{3}}\left|x\right|{\rm d}x+(\alpha_{t}-1)+\epsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}.

Consequently, the integration over

with respect to the density

\displaystyle\int_{B_{4}}\int_{(B_{2}\cup B_{3})}\left|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,x)-\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|p^{[k:h)}(\mathbf{z}){\rm d}x{\rm d}\mathbf{z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}R_{1}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\log(1/\epsilon)+(\alpha_{t}-1)+\epsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}\right)(\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)})^{h-k},

(52)

where we use the fact that

over

Combining (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}^{[k:h)}}[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}],\mu_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[k:h)}])]

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}+\frac{1-\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}\log(1/\epsilon)+(\alpha_{t}-1)+\epsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}\right)(\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)})^{h-k}+\epsilon\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}+\epsilon.

where we use the assumption that

.

As an intermediate consequence, we establish a joint distribution estimation result following from Corollary
4.14
and the chain rule (
16

Corollary 4.15
.

Suppose the assumptions in Corollary
4.14
hold for all
h\in[H]
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}[{\rm TV}(\mu_{0}^{[1:H]},\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{[1:H]})]=\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\Big[n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}}(\log n)^{\max\left\{d_{\rm trunc},3+\beta/2,19\right\}/2}+u(d_{\rm trunc})\Big]\right),

where

and

is the optimal index chosen for each

.

Selection of
d_{\rm trunc}
.

We now discuss how to choose the truncation length
d_{\rm trunc}
to balance the two competing terms in Corollary
4.14
: the statistical learning error, which grows with
d_{\rm trunc}
, and the truncation error
u(d_{\rm trunc})
, which decreases with
d_{\rm trunc}
. We state the resulting rates for two canonical decay regimes.

Proposition 4.16

(Exponential decay)
.

Suppose there are constants
C_{u},\kappa>0
such that

u(d)\leq C_{u}\left(e^{-\kappa d}-e^{-\kappa h}\right),\qquad 1\leq d\leq h.

If we take

\displaystyle d_{\rm trunc}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{h,\left\lceil\Delta_{n}\right\rceil\right\},\;\text{with}\;\Delta_{n}\coloneqq\frac{-\beta+\sqrt{\beta^{2}+(\beta/\kappa)\log n}}{2},

(54)

then under the assumptions of Corollary
4.14
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}\left(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}]\right)\right]\right]=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left((\log n)^{\max\left\{d_{\rm trunc},3+\beta/2,19\right\}/2}e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\right),&h>\Delta_{n}\\
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(h+\beta)}}(\log n)^{\max\left\{h,3+\beta/2,19\right\}/2}\right),&h\leq\Delta_{n}.\end{cases}

For a fixed horizon
h
, Proposition
4.16
provides a truncation rule when
u(\cdot)
follows an exponential decay. For timestamps such that
h>\Delta_{n}
, the rule selects a truncated history length of order
\Theta(\sqrt{\log n})
. Since the information from a small dataset is limited, truncating to the most recent timestamps reduces the input dimension and thus improving the statistical learning error. As
n
grows, the rule increases
d_{\rm trunc}
until
d_{\rm trunc}=h
, at which point the bound reduces to the statistical learning error with dimension
h
.

Proof.

By definition,
\Delta_{n}
satisfies the quadratic equation

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\log n}{4(\Delta_{n}+\beta)}=\kappa\Delta_{n}.

(55)

Taking exponentials on both sides gives us

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(\Delta_{n}+\beta)}}=e^{-\kappa\Delta_{n}}.

When
d_{\rm trunc}<h
, we have
d_{\rm trunc}=\left\lceil\Delta_{n}\right\rceil
and thus
\Delta_{n}\leq d_{\rm trunc}\leq\Delta_{n}+1
. Consequently, the following bounds hold

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\leq e^{-\kappa\Delta_{n}}=n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(\Delta_{n}+\beta)}}\leq n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}},

(56)

and (
55
) implies

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}}

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(\Delta_{n}+1+\beta)}}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\beta\log n}{4(\Delta_{n}+1+\beta)}\right\}=e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\exp\left\{\kappa d_{\rm trunc}-\kappa\Delta_{n}\frac{\Delta_{n}+\beta}{\Delta_{n}+1+\beta}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\exp\left\{\kappa(\Delta_{n}+1)-\kappa\Delta_{n}\frac{\Delta_{n}+\beta}{\Delta_{n}+1+\beta}\right\}=e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\exp\left\{\kappa\left(1+\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\Delta_{n}+1+\beta}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\kappa}e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}.

(57)

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}\geq e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}-e^{-\kappa h}=e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}(1-e^{-\kappa(h-d_{\rm trunc})})\geq(1-e^{-\kappa})e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}.

(58)

Therefore, we combine (
56
), (
57
) and (
58
) to conclude that

\displaystyle e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}-e^{-\kappa h}\leq n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}}\leq\frac{e^{2\kappa}}{1-e^{-\kappa}}(e^{-\kappa d_{\rm trunc}}-e^{-\kappa h}).

This completes the proof together with
d_{\rm trunc}=h
case.

∎

Invoke the Lambert W function
W:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
as the inverse of the mapping
x\mapsto xe^{x}
, i.e.,
W(z)e^{W(z)}=z
for all
z>0
. We have the following result for the polynomial decay case.

Proposition 4.17

(Polynomial decay)
.

Suppose there are constants
C_{u},\kappa>0
such that

u(d)\leq C_{u}\left(d^{-\kappa}-h^{-\kappa}\right),\qquad 1\leq d\leq h.

If we take

\displaystyle d_{\rm trunc}=\min\left\{h,\left\lceil\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\right\rceil\right\},\;\text{with}\;\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\coloneqq\frac{{\beta\log n}/{4\kappa}}{W({\beta\log n}/{4\kappa})},

(59)

then under the assumptions of Corollary
4.14
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}}\left[{\rm TV}\left(\mu_{0}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}],\hat{\mu}_{t_{0}}^{h}[\mathbf{X}_{0}^{[1:h)}]\right)\right]\right]=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}\left((\log n)^{\max\left\{d_{\rm trunc},3+\beta/2,19\right\}/2}\left(d_{\rm trunc}\right)^{-\kappa}\right),&h>\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\\
\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(h+\beta)}}(\log n)^{\max\left\{h,3+\beta/2,19\right\}/2}\right),&h\leq\Delta_{n}^{\prime}.\end{cases}

For polynomial decay, it holds that

W(\beta\log n/4\kappa)=\Theta(\log\log n)
in (
59
). Consequently, the truncation rule selects a history length of order

\Theta(\log n/\log\log n)
before it reaches the horizon
h
.
Compared with the exponential-decay case, polynomial decay requires a larger truncation window, since the impact from the remote history decays more slowly.

Proof.

Denote
A_{n}\coloneqq\beta\log n/4\kappa
. Then we have
\Delta_{n}^{\prime}=A_{n}/W(A_{n})
.
The definition of the function
W
gives

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}^{\prime}=\frac{W(A_{n})e^{W(A_{n})}}{W(A_{n})}=e^{W(A_{n})},\;\text{and}\;\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\log\Delta_{n}^{\prime}=e^{W(A_{n})}W(A_{n})=A_{n}.

(60)

Consider the case when
d_{\rm trunc}<h
. Then we have
d_{\rm trunc}=\lceil\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\rceil\leq\Delta_{n}^{\prime}+1
. It follows from (
60
) that

\displaystyle n^{-\frac{\beta}{4(d_{\rm trunc}+\beta)}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\kappa\frac{A_{n}}{d_{\rm trunc}+\beta}\right\}\leq\exp\left\{-\kappa\frac{A_{n}}{\Delta_{n}^{\prime}+1+\beta}\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\kappa\frac{\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\log\Delta_{n}^{\prime}}{\Delta_{n}^{\prime}+1+\beta}\right\}=(\Delta_{n}^{\prime})^{-\kappa}\exp\left\{\kappa\frac{(1+\beta)\log\Delta_{n}^{\prime}}{\Delta_{n}^{\prime}+1+\beta}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\kappa(1+\beta)}{e}\right\}(\Delta_{n}^{\prime})^{-\kappa},

(61)

where we use the fact that
\frac{\log x}{x+1+\beta}\leq\frac{\log x}{x}\leq 1/e
for all
x\geq 1
. Moreover, since
d_{\rm trunc}=\left\lceil\Delta_{n}^{\prime}\right\rceil\geq\Delta_{n}^{\prime}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\rm trunc}^{-\kappa}-h^{-\kappa}\leq d_{\rm trunc}^{-\kappa}\leq(\Delta_{n}^{\prime})^{-\kappa}.

(62)

This completes the proof together with
d_{\rm trunc}=h
case.

∎

5
Empirical Analysis

In this section, we numerically evaluate the performance of our
AD-Seq
framework
proposed in Section
2
through extensive experiments. We first test the model on synthetic data generated by ARMA models and Gaussian processes. Specifically, we evaluate its effectiveness numerically in terms of recovering both the ground-truth distribution and the underlying temporal structures. Next, we apply our
AD-Seq
framework
to real-world data and evaluate its performance in constructing mean-variance optimal portfolios. In implementation, we use a single transformer to parameterize the score functions across all timestamps. The causal mask ensures that the score at each timestamp depends only on the past history, while the objective (
14
) allows us to train the coordinatewise scores
in parallel
.

5.2
Mean-Variance Portfolio Optimization

In this subsection, we study a mean-variance portfolio optimization problem which was studied by
Gao et al. 2025
and
Wang and Zhou 2020
. Specifically, an agent invests
in the S&P 500 index and a risk-free asset with interest rate
r=2\%
for half a year with
H=128
trading days. The problem is to find a self-financing strategy
{\bm{\theta}}=\left\{\theta^{h}\right\}_{h=1}^{H}
where
\theta^{h}\in[0,1]
denotes the proportion of wealth invested in the risky asset at time
h
that minimizes the variance of terminal wealth
X^{H,\theta}
for a given target wealth level
z
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bm{\theta}}}\;\;{\rm Var}(X^{H,\theta})\;\;{\rm s.t.}\;\;\mathbb{E}[X^{H,\theta}]=z.

(63)

Following
[
7
]
, we focus on two approaches:

1.

Plug-in policy:
A classical method that estimates coefficients of a GBM model and plugs in the estimators into the expression of the optimal deterministic policy of (
63
).

2.

RL policy:
A model-free reinforcement learning (RL) approach proposed in
[
6
]
which requires a market data simulator.

Furthermore, we consider four data sources for obtaining each policy:

1.

Split:
We split daily returns of S&P 500 data from 1990-2009 into 40 half-year trajectories (called split paths). These paths are used to train our sequential diffusion models.

2.

Split + Synthetic:
In addition to 40 split paths, we augment 40 synthetic paths generated by the approach in
[
7
]
and develop investment policies.

3.

Split + Ours:
Compared to
Split + Synthetic
, we replace the synthetic paths with 40 trajectories generated by our sequential diffusion model.

4.

Bootstrap:
For RL policies, we also construct investment polices using 40 bootstrap paths as in
[
6
]
.

Main results.

We test all the above policies on S&P 500 daily return data from 2010 to 2019 with bootstrap. As shown in Table
2
, including diffusion-generated samples do not improve plug-in policies. This is aligned with the observations in
[
7
]
as the data paths do not necessarily satisfy the GBM assumption. On the other hand, similar to
Split + Synthetic
, our diffusion-generated samples also significantly improve the performance of RL policies. Notably, our approach achieves the highest Sharpe ratio and outperforms the method in
[
7
]
. This observation consolidates the belief that our diffusion models learn the underlying temporal structure of financial data in an adapted sequential manner. We also remark that the S&P 500 daily return has strongly Markovian behaviors with only short-range dependence on the past. This explains why our method performs comparably to
[
7
]
in this setting. However, for financial time series exhibiting longer historical dependence and more complex temporal structure, we expect our adapted sequential diffusion approach will demonstrate clearer advantages.

\displaystyle B_{2}\coloneqq\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\left|y-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|\leq R_{3}\right\}=\left[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}-R_{3},\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}+R_{3}\right].

It follows
\bar{B}=B_{1}\cap B_{2}
. We bound the integral with

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus\bar{B}}\left|\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}}\right)^{\nu}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{1}}\left|\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}}\right)^{\nu}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}_{(A_{1})}

\displaystyle\qquad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\left|\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}}\right)^{\nu}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}_{(A_{2})}.

(68)

Over each region, we bound the integral in two ways.
Note that for all
R_{2}>\sqrt{\nu}
, monotonicity implies

\displaystyle\left|\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}}\right)^{\nu}\right|\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)\leq R_{2}^{\nu}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2).

Thus, we bound the term
(A_{1})
with

\displaystyle(A_{1})\leq\frac{R_{2}^{\nu}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2)}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{1}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y){\rm d}y\lesssim\frac{R_{2}^{\nu}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2)}{\sigma_{t}}.

(69)

On the other hand, change of variable
w=\alpha_{t}y-x/\sigma_{t}
leads to

\displaystyle(A_{1})\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\int_{\left|w\right|>R_{2}}w^{\nu}\exp(-w^{2}/2){\rm d}w\lesssim\frac{R_{2}^{\nu-1}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2)}{\alpha_{t}}.

(70)

Combining (
69
) and (
70
), we deduce that

\displaystyle(A_{1})\lesssim R_{2}^{\nu}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2).

(71)

Similarly, we have the following two bounds for
(A_{2})
:

\displaystyle(A_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2)\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\left|\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}}\right)^{\nu}\right|\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2)\int_{\mathbb{R}}w^{\nu}\exp(-w^{2}/2){\rm d}w\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2),

(72)

and

\displaystyle(A_{2})\leq\frac{C_{1}}{\sigma_{t}(2\pi)^{1/2}}\left(\frac{v}{e}\right)^{v/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B_{2}}\exp(-C_{2}\left|y-\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}/2){\rm d}y\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2).

(73)

Combining (
72
) and (
73
) leads to

\displaystyle(A_{2})\lesssim\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2).

(74)

Consequently, adding up (
71
) and (
74
) gives us

\displaystyle(A_{1})+(A_{2})\lesssim(R_{2})^{\nu}\exp(-R_{2}^{2}/2)+\exp(-C_{2}R_{3}^{2}/2)\lesssim\epsilon,

as long as
R_{2},R_{3}\gtrsim\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}
.

∎

Lemma A.4
.

Under Assumption
4.2
, the following inequality holds:

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|+1\right).

Proof.

We write the score function as

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)=\frac{\int\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}{\int\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}.

(75)

By taking
\nu\in\left\{0,1\right\}
in Lemma
A.3
, for any
\epsilon>0
to be chosen later, there are sufficiently large constants
R_{2}
and
R_{3}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B}\left|\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y\leq\epsilon,

(76)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}\setminus B}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y\leq\epsilon.

(77)

where
B
is defined as in (
67
). Lemma
A.2
implies
\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\geq 2\epsilon
with
\epsilon\asymp\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|^{2}+1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)
. It follows from (
77
) that

\displaystyle\int_{B}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y\geq\epsilon.

(78)

Combining (
75
)–(
78
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}{\int_{B}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{B}\left|\frac{\alpha_{t}y-x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right|\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}{\int_{B}\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(0,y)\exp\left(-\frac{\left|x-\alpha_{t}y\right|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right){\rm d}y}+\frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon}\leq\frac{R_{2}}{\sigma_{t}}+1.

Let
R_{2}\asymp\sqrt{\log(1/\epsilon)}
. We conclude that

\displaystyle\left|\partial_{x}\log\phi^{h}_{\mathbf{z}}(t,x)\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\left(\left|x-\alpha_{t}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{z}\right|+1\right).

∎

Lemma A.5

Lemma A.6
.

,
