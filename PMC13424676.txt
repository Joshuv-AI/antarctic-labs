Chromosome-level genome assemblies of Brownlowia tersa and Brownlowia argentata (Brownlowioideae: Malvaceae)
Authors: Panthita Ruang-areerate, Duangjai Sangsrakru, Poompat Phadphon, Chutima Sonthirod, Chaiwat Naktang, Wasitthee Kongkachana, Nukoon Jomchai, Tamanai Pravinvongvuthi, Sumet Thongdon-um, Poonsri Wanthongchai, Sithichoke Tangphatsornruang, Wirulda Pootakham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Although Brownlowia tersa and Brownlowia argentata are classified as near-threatened and data-deficient mangrove species, respectively, their genomic resources remain limited. Here, we present chromosome-level genome assemblies for both species and a SNP data for B. tersa in Thailand. The assembled genomes of B. tersa and B. argentata are 392.91 Mb and 395.11 Mb in size, with scaffold N50 values of 25.70 Mb and 26.03 Mb, respectively. Both genomes showed approximately 98% complete BUSCOs and LAI scores of 10. Approximately 96% of each assembly was anchored to 14 pseudochromosomes. Repetitive elements comprised approximately 45% of both genomes, including 25% unclassified repeats. A total of 31,864 and 28,920 protein-coding genes were predicted in B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively, of which approximately 88% were functionally annotated and 90% were complete BUSCOs. Additionally, 1,196 and 1,222 ncRNAs were identified in B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively. SNP data from 37 B. tersa accessions were also generated. These genome assemblies and the SNP dataset provide valuable resources for studying mangrove evolution and conservation.

Background & Summary

Brownlowia is a genus in the subfamily Brownlowioideae within the family Malvaceae, comprising approximately 30 accepted species, of which two species are true mangroves, Brownlowia tersa and Brownlowia argentata1–3. The Brownlowia mangrove species adapted to the unique condition of the upper margin of mangrove ecosystems such as saline soil and periodic flooding. B. tersa (Dungun air, Durian of the sea or Nam nong) is a mangrove shrub or small tree reaching up to 2 m in height, characterized by slender branches, lanceolate leaves, and reddish-brown bark2. It has been used in traditional medicine for the treatment of boils, diarrhea, dysentery, and wounds4 due to its potential pharmacological properties5. B. tersa is predominantly distributed in tropical Asia including Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Malaysia, Myanmar, Philippines, Thailand and Singapore1,6,7. According to the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List, B. tersa is considered as near threatened due to habitat loss driven by coastal development and deforestation8. In addition, B. argentata, a lesser-known mangrove tree species (Dungun or Durian Laut), is a tree growing to 10 m, pulvinate petioles, cordate to ovate leaves with a glossy green upper surface and silvery-grey underside, and greyish bark2. It has distributed from the Malay Peninsula through Indonesia to New Guinea2. B. argentata is classified as data deficient based on the IUCN Red List categories9.

Recent advances in long-read sequencing (e.g., PacBio, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT), and single-tube long fragment read (stLFR)) and chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) have enabled the generation of highly contiguous and chromosome-scale genome assemblies, respectively10,11. Notably, stLFR is a cost-effective long-read sequencing approach that has been applied in plant genome assemblies12–14. In addition, numerous chromosome-level scale genome assemblies of mangrove species have recently been published using integrated long-read sequencing and Hi-C technologies15–28.

For B. tersa and B. argentata, only chloroplast genome sequences have been reported to date29. In this study, we generated genome assemblies for both species. For B. tersa, stLFR and Hi-C data were used to construct a chromosome-scale genome assembly spanning 392.91 Mb, with a scaffold N50 of 25.70 Mb. Approximately 95.64% of the assembled sequences were anchored to 14 pseudochromosomes. For B. argentata, stLFR data was used, and scaffolding was performed using the chromosome-level genome of B. tersa with RagTag. The assembled genome of B. argentata was 395.11 Mb in size, with a scaffold N50 of 26.03 Mb, and 95.59% of the sequences were anchored to 14 pseudochromosomes. A total of 177.85 Mb and 182.18 Mb of repetitive sequences and 31,864 and 28,920 protein-coding genes were identified in B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively, of which 87.57% and 88.69% were functionally annotated. In addition, single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data from 37 B. tersa accessions in Thailand were generated. These high-quality genome assemblies of B. tersa and B. argentata and the SNP dataset for B. tersa provide valuable resources for evolutionary and conservation studies.

Methods

Sample preparation

For whole genome and RNA sequencing, fresh leaves of B. tersa (Fig. 1a) and B. argentata (Fig. 1b) were collected from mangrove forests at Ao Luek Noi District in Krabi Province, Thailand (N 8.280251, E 98.737898) and Phunphin District in Surat Thani Province, Thailand (N 9.233155, E 99.238231), respectively29. For SNP analysis, a total of 37 B. tersa accessions were collected from Andaman coastal regions of Thailand in five provinces including Krabi (KBI), Phang Nga (PNA), Ranong (RNG), Satun (STN), and Trang (TRG) (Table S1). The sampling included five individuals from Krabi (KBI-Bt01 to KBI-Bt05), five accessions from Phang Nga (PNA-Bt01 to PNA-Bt05), ten accessions from Ranong (RNG-Bt01 to RNG-Bt10), ten accessions from Satun (STN-Bt01 to STN-Bt10), and seven accessions from Trang (TRG-Bt01 to TRG-Bt07). Genome library and sequencing

High-molecular-weight genomic DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method30. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Long-read sequencing was performed using stLFR technology (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Libraries of stLFR were prepared following the protocol of the MGIEasy stLFR Library Prep Kit (MGI) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 100 bp) was conducted on the DNBSEQ-G400 (MGI) in house according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 98.96 Gb and 92.08 Gb of stLFR reads were generated for B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively. The GC contents were approximately 38.26% and 38.10%, and the Q20 and Q30 values were 98.85% and 95.82%, for B. tersa and 98.56% and 94.95% for B. argentata, respectively.

For Hi-C sequencing, a Dovetail Omni-C library and Hi-C data were conducted by Dovetail genomics (Santa Cruz, California, CA, USA). Briefly, formaldehyde was used to fix the chromatin in the nucleus that was extracted. The fixed chromatin was degraded and ligated to a biotinylated bridge adapter. DNA was purified and removed biotin. The libraries were qualified and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq X platform with paired-end 150-bp reads (2 × 150 bp). A total of 11.50 Gb of Hi-C data for B. tersa were generated for scaffolding pseudochromosome assembly. The Q20 and Q30 values exceeded 95.49% and 88.90%, respectively.

Transcriptome library and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from leaves of the same individuals of B. tersa and B. argentata used for genome sequencing using the CTAB method. RNA was precipitated overnight with one-quarter volume of 8 M LiCl. RNA integrity was evaluated using the Fragment Analyzer machine (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the MGIEasy RNA Library Prep Kit v3.0 (MGI, Shenzhen, China) and were sequenced using DNBSEQ-G400 (MGI) in house following the manufacturer’s protocol. In total, 6.79 Gb and 10.69 Gb of RNA-seq data were generated for B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively.

RAD-seq library and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from young leaves of B. tersa 37 individuals using the CTAB method. The extracted DNA was further purified using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration was measured using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) with the dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). RAD-seq libraries were prepared from 1 ng of genomic DNA using the MGIEasy RAD Library Prep Kit (MGI, Shenzhen, China). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on DNBSEQ-G400 (MGI) in house following the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 73.16 Gb of sequencing data were generated from the 37 B. tersa accessions, with an average of 1.97 Gb per accession.

De novo genome and chromosome assembly

The stLFR reads of B. tersa and B. argentata were used for de novo genome assembly using stLFRdenovo v1.0.531 with default parameters.

To anchor and orient scaffolds into chromosomes, a chromosome-level assembly of B. tersa was generated using a combination of stLFR and Hi-C data, which was analyzed by Dovetail genomics (Santa Cruz, California, USA). Briefly, Omni-C reads were aligned to the draft genome of B. tersa using BWA v0.7.17-r118832 and the resulting alignments were used for chromosome-scale scaffolding by HiRise v2.0.533 with a minimum mapping quality threshold of 50. The final B. tersa assembly was 392.91 Mb in size, with scaffold N50 values of 25.70 Mb and the longest scaffold length of 40.33 Mb (Table 1). The Hi-C contact map revealed 14 pseudochromosomes (Fig. 2a), accounting for 95.64% of the assembled B. tersa genome. Repeat annotation

Predicted LTR and unclassified elements were further classified using TEsorter v1.5.137. Gene prediction and annotation

Protein-coding genes were identified by integrating three sources of evidence, de novo gene prediction, homology-based prediction, and transcript-based evidence. These predictions were combined using EVM v.r2012-06-2540, with weights 0.3, 0.5, and 1.0 assigned to the de novo gene, homology-based, and transcript-based evidence, respectively. For de novo gene prediction and homology-based prediction, protein-coding genes were predicted based on gene models and protein sequences from three Malvaceae species, Durio zibethinus41, Hibiscus syriacus42, and Theobroma cacao43, and two dicot species, Eucalyptus grandis44 and Populus trichocarpa45. De novo gene prediction was performed using Augustus v3.1.046, whereas homology-based prediction was conducted using the AAT tool v.r0305201147 with default parameters. For transcript-based evidence, RNA-seq reads were assembled using Trinity v2.1448 with default parameters, and the resulting transcripts were used to predict protein-coding genes using PASA v2.4.149. Predicted protein-coding genes with > 20% repeat content and missing start or stop codons were excluded. In total, 31,864 and 28,920 protein-coding genes were predicted in the B. tersa and B. argentata genomes, respectively (Table 3). The completeness of the predicted genes was assessed using BUSCO v5.8.3 with the embryophyta_odb12 dataset. Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs), including microRNAs (miRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs), were identified using the cmscan program, part of the Infernal tool v.1.1.450 with Rfam v14.2 database51. SNP identification

To identify single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), RAD-seq reads from 37 B. tersa accessions were aligned to the B. tersa reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.5.155. The resulting SAM alignment files were converted to sorted BAM files using SAMtools v1.1756. Variant calling was performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.4.0.057 with the HaplotypeCaller workflow, followed by joint genotyping using CombineGVCFs. Raw variants were filtered using VCFtools v0.1.1658 to retain high-quality SNPs based on the following criteria: missing data ≤ 20% (--max-missing 0.8), minor allele frequency ≥ 0.1 (--maf 0.1), minimum read depth ≥ 10 (--minDP 10) and maximum read depth ≤ 200 (--maxDP 200). After filtering, a total of 2,007 SNPs were retained across the 37 B. tersa accessions. To reduce redundancy caused by linkage disequilibrium (LD), SNP pruning was performed using PLINK v1.9.0-b.859 with a sliding window of 200 kb, a step size of 50 SNPs, and an LD threshold of r2 = 0.2 (--indep-pairwise 200 kb 50 0.2). The retained SNPs were extracted from the filtered VCF file using VCFtools (--positions pruned_positions.txt). Following LD pruning, 1,890 SNPs remained for population genetic analyses (Tables S2 and S3).

Technical Validation

Comparative genome analysis

Synteny analyses were conducted between B. tersa and B. argentata as well as between B. tersa and Diplodiscus trichospermus65, a closely related species of Brownlowia species, using WGDI v0.7566 with the “-icl” option. Protein sequences from each species pair were aligned using DIAMOND v 2.1.2467 with the parameters “-e 1e-5 -k 5”, and the resulting alignments were used as input for WGDI. Synonymous substitution rates (Ks) for syntenic gene pairs were calculated using the “-ks” option of WGDI. Orthologous syntenic blocks were filtered using the filter function in SOI v1.3.068 with a minimum orthology index cutoff of 0.6 (-c 0.6) and subsequently visualized using SOI. A strong collinearity was observed between B. tersa and B. argentata, with nearly continuous diagonal alignments across all chromosomes (Fig. 3a). A total of 38 syntenic blocks comprising 23,046 collinear gene pairs were identified the two species, representing 72.33% and 90.76% of the annotated genes in the B. tersa and B. argentata genomes, respectively (Table S4). These syntenic blocks varied considerably in size, ranging from 0.14 Mb (5 genes) to 40.31 Mb (2,907 genes), with an average length of 9.89 Mb (606 genes). The Ks distribution of collinear gene pairs between B. tersa and B. argentata showed a major peak at approximately Ks = 0.009 (Fig. 3b). In contrast, synteny between B. tersa and D. trichospermus characterized by more fragmented and discontinuous collinear blocks (Fig. 4a). A total of 94 syntenic blocks comprising 23,209 collinear gene pairs were identified between B. tersa and D. trichospermus, representing 72.84% and 54.04% of the annotated genes in the genomes, respectively (Table S5). These syntenic blocks ranged in size from 0.04 Mb (5 genes) to 24.46 Mb (1,318 genes), with an average length of 3.85 Mb (247 genes). The Ks distribution of collinear gene pairs between B. tersa and D. trichospermus showed a major peak at approximately Ks = 0.158 (Fig. 4b). To construct a phylogenetic tree, single-copy orthologous gene sequences among B. argentata, B. tersa, twelve plant species within the Malvaceae, and two outgroups (Arabidopsis thaliana73 and Oryza sativa74) were detected using Orthofinder v2.5.571. The best substitution model for each block was evaluated using ModelTest-NG86. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using RAxML-NG87 with 1,000 bootstraps. The divergence time was estimated using MCMCtree in PAML v4.10.788. The divergence time of monocots-dicots (130–150 million years ago (MYA))89, the crown nodes of Malvaceae (74–84 MYA)90 and Bombacoidae (56–62 MYA)91 were used as calibration points. A random birth and death model was used to obtain gene gain or loss in gene families across a specified phylogenetic tree based on P-values < 0.05. Inference of population structure

To assess the SNP dataset of B. tersa, population structure was inferred using STRUCTURE v2.3.493 with 10,000 iterations. The optimal number of clusters (K) were determined using StructureSelector94. Eigenvectors and eigenvalues were obtained from PLINK v1.9.0-b.859 to perform principal component analysis (PCA) using R v4.3.395. The first two principal components (PCs) were visualized using the ggplot2 package96 in R, with sample groups according to their geographic locations. All sampling locations were mapped using QGIS v3.24.297 (Fig. 7a). PCA showed individual relationships and clusters, following each collection site (Fig. 7b). The optimal number of clusters was K = 2, followed by K = 3 (Fig. 7c). Population structure analyses at K = 2 and K = 3 revealed distinct groups corresponding to geographic locations (Fig. 7d). Assessment of the genome assembly

The genome of B. tersa and B. argentata was evaluated using QUAST v5.0.298 with default parameters. The cumulative scaffold length plot showed both assemblies reached approximately 400 Mb (Fig. 8a). The completeness of the genome assembly was assessed using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) v5.8.399 with the embryophyta_odb12 database. BUSCO analysis showed 98% completeness in both genomes (Fig. 8b and Table 1). Long terminal retrotransposons (LTRs) were identified using LTR_FINDER v1.07100 with the following parameters “-D 15000 -d 1000 -L 7000 -l 100 -p -C” and LTRharvest101 in GenomeTools v1.6.5 with the following parameters “-minlenltr 100 -maxlenltr 7000 -mintsd 4 -maxtsd 6 -mindistltr 1000 -maxdistltr 15000 -similar 85 -vic 10”. Long terminal repeat assembly index (LAI) was calculated using LTR retriever v.2.8.2102. The LAI score for both genomes was approximately 10 (Table 1). In addition, genome size estimation for the two Brownlowia species was performed using jellyfish v2.2.10103 with stLFR sequencing data, and the results were visualized using GenomeScope v2.0104. In addition, BUSCO was used to evaluate the completeness of gene annotations, observing 90.4% and 89.7% of the expected land plant genes (embryophyta_odb12 database) in B. tersa and B. argentata, respectively (Table 3).

As the chromosome-level assembly of B. argentata was generated using reference-guided scaffolding with RagTag, the potential effects of reference-guided scaffolding should be taken into account in synteny and collinearity analyses. However, several genome quality assessments, including assembly continuity, BUSCO completeness, LAI score, read-mapping statistics, and comparison with the previous scaffolded assembly, support the reliability of the RagTag-scaffolded genome assembly. The quality of the two assemblies was assessed using QUAST with default parameters. BUSCO completeness and LAI scores were evaluated as described above. The stLFR reads were mapped using Bowtie2 v2.5.155 with the “--very-sensitive-local” option. Additionally, the comparison with the two assemblies of B. argentata was performed using the web-based D-GENIES v1.5.0105 with minimap2 as the aligner and visualized with a minimum identity threshold of 0.6 and the hide noise feature. Following RagTag scaffolding, the scaffold N50 increased from 10.66 Mb to 26.03 Mb, whereas BUSCO completeness decreased slightly from 99.5% to 98.7% (Table S6). The LAI score increased slightly from 10.07 to 10.57, and the read-mapping rate remained high at 95.9% (Supplementary Table 6). Furthermore, the D-GENIES dot plot revealed high synteny and structural concordance between the previous scaffolded and RagTag-scaffolded genome assemblies (Figure S1).
