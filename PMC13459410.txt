Biosynthesis of O‑Prenylated Chaetophenols Triggers Nonenzymatic Pathways toward Structural Diversity
Authors: Hiroto Matsui, Yohei Morishita, Yuto Homma, Kento Tsukada, Takashi Yamamoto, Shuntaro Morita, Yoshiteru Oshima, Taro Ozaki, Teigo Asai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chaetophenols (CPs) are structurally diverse aromatic polyketides that have been isolated from Chaetomium fungi. They exhibit two distinct prenylation patterns that have key roles in generating chemical diversity and controlling the dimerization modes. We investigated the mechanism of CP biogenesis through heterologous expression and in vitro assays, revealing that CP structural diversification involves both enzymatic and nonenzymatic steps. In particular, C-prenylated CPs are generated nonenzymatically from enzymatically synthesized O-prenylated CPs. We also discovered a nonenzymatic conversion pathway that forms a chaetoline skeleton. These findings highlight a unique strategy for the structural diversification of fungal aromatic polyketides through enzymatic and nonenzymatic chemistries.

Natural product structural diversity, which contributes to drug discovery and development, , is often generated by core enzymes, such as polyketide synthase (PKS) and terpene cyclase constructing core skeletons, and tailoring enzymes. These enzymatic machineries provide natural products with vast chemical space. On the other hand, nonenzymatic reactions, such as intramolecular cyclization, heteroatom replacement, and dimerization, also expand their structural diversity. Nonreducing PKS (NR-PKS)-derived polyketides represent a natural product family that tends to undergo such nonenzymatic reactions owing to high reactivity. For example, sorbicillinol condenses with amino acids or other natural products to give more than 170 sorbicillinoids. Understanding the biosynthesis of highly reactive monomers and the following nonenzymatic reaction will enable the development of asymmetric synthesis, exploration of new natural products, , biomimetic synthesis, and pseudo-natural product synthesis. −

Chaetophenols (CPs) are aromatic polyketides produced by Chaetomium sp. (Figure a–c). − Their core structures are suggested to be synthesized by NR-PKS, followed by oxidation and prenylation on the PKS product, which expand the structural diversity of CPs. CPs are divided into two groups based on the position of the prenyl group: C- and O-prenylated CPs. In addition, dimerization reactions also play a role in CP structural diversification. Previously, we revealed that three types (types A, B, and C) of nonenzymatic dimerization proceed under acidic conditions to afford different skeletal dimers (Figure c). , In type A and B dimerizations, a C-prenylated CP, CP-B (1), featuring a nucleophilic isochromene isomerizes to electrophilic ortho-quinone methide (o-QM), which is attacked by another isochromene form of 1. In type C dimerization, an O-prenylated isochromene monomer, CP-I (2), dimerizes with an electrophilic para-quinone methide (p-QM) monomer, CP-K. Notably, the three types of dimerization reactions are controlled by the prenylation pattern, with the O-prenyl group preventing CP-I (2) from isomerizing to o-QM. A recent study also reported the suppression of the isomerization to o-QM by O-alkylation. That is, the reactivities of isochromene and QM monomers result in CP structural diversity. Thus, the biosynthetic study of monomers 1 and 2 that have different reactivities could provide the foundation for the application of CPs to other studies. Here, we conducted a biosynthetic study of CP monomers through the heterologous expression and in vitro enzymatic reactions. As a result, we revealed the mechanism of CP monomer formation: O-prenylated CP is synthesized enzymatically, whereas C-prenylated CP arises from O-prenylated CP.

First, we hypothesized that C-prenylated monomers 1 and O-prenylated monomers 2 are derived from different biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs). According to the hypothesis, the plausible NR-PKS product for 1 was assumed to be 3, and that for 2 was sclerotinin B (4) (Figures d and S1). Thus, we searched the genome of C. indicum for NR-PKS genes responsible for the synthesis of 3 and 4. As a result, five NR-PKS genes were found, including the previously identified pksCH-1 and pksCH-2. Among these, PksCH-4 and PksCH-5 were excluded as candidates because they lack a methyltransferase domain (Figure S2), which would be essential for installing methyl groups in 3 and 4.

Next, we investigated the genomic regions flanking the candidate genes. Unexpectedly, only one BGC, including pksCH-2, encoded a prenyltransferase (PT) gene, suggesting that the PksCH-2 cluster is involved in the biosynthesis of 1 and 2. Although PksCH-2 was previously shown to synthesize 5, we had no evidence that 5 is a precursor of 1 or 2. Thus, we conducted 13C-labeling analysis. Feeding [1,2-13C2]-NaOAc to C. indicum or Aspergillus oryzae transformants harboring pksCH-2 afforded labeled 1, 2, and 5 (Figure S3). The skeletal labeling pattern of 2 was the same as that of 5. The labeling pattern of 1 was also identical to that of 5, except that the C-9 of 1 was a singlet. These results suggest that C- and O-prenylated CPs originate from 5 and that C-10 of 5 is removed for C-prenylated CP generation (Figure e).

We investigated CP biosynthesis using A. oryzae heterologous expression systems. − We renamed pksCH-2 as chphA and annotated the genes in the vicinity of chphA as chphB (P450), chphC (oxidase), chphD (dehydrogenase, DH), and chphE (PT) (Figure a). We introduced chph genes into A. oryzae and analyzed their metabolites by HPLC (Figure b). As a result, we detected CP-I (2) and CP-H (6) as the major products and CP-B (1) as a minor component in AO-chphABDE. The coexpression of chphC with chphABDE did not produce any new products (Figure S4). These findings suggest that the chph cluster is responsible for both the C- and O-prenylated CPs (1 and 2) and that ChphABDE is sufficient to produce them.

Next, we investigated the detailed pathways of 1 and 2. We constructed AO-chphAB, AO-chphAD, and AO-chphABD. AO-chphAB produced stachiol (7) (Figures b, f, and S5). The hemiacetal structure of 7 observed by NMR analysis suggests that ChphB oxidizes the methyl group at C-12 of 5 and that the resulting hydroxy group attacks the C-2 keto group. The hemiacetal structure is also found in CP-H (6) (Figure S5), which undergoes spontaneous dehydration to form isochromene (Figure f). These observations indicate that oxidation by ChphB is essential for the formation of isochromene. AO-chphAD produced sclerotinin B (4) (Figures b, f, and S5), indicating that ChphD oxidizes a formyl group of 5 to a carboxylic acid and that the lactone structure is formed by the attack of the carboxyl group on the carbonyl carbon. AO-chphABD produced 8 and isochromene 9 (Figures b, f, and S5). During the isolation step, 8 was spontaneously converted to 9, as observed for 6. Based on its UV and MS spectra, along with the structure of 6, 8 was estimated to be in the same equilibrium states as 6 (Figure S5). It was suggested that 8 is synthesized either via oxidation of 7 by ChphD or via hydroxylation of 4 by ChphB. Feeding 7 into AO-chphD successfully yielded 8 and 9, while 4 was not converted by AO-chphB (Figure S6), indicating that 9 was generated via the former pathway.

To investigate whether 5, 7, and 4 are prenylated by ChphE, we constructed AO-chphAE, AO-chphABE, and AO-chphADE. AO-chphAE and AO-chphABE produced no prenylated products (Figure b), which suggests that 5 and 7 are not substrates of ChphE. On the other hand, AO-chphADE produced O-prenylated 10 as a major product and C-prenylated 11 as a minor product (Figures b and f). However, feeding 10 or 11 to AO-chphB resulted in no conversion (Figure S6), suggesting that 10 and 11 are shunt products and that prenylation of 8 or 9 leads to 1 and 2.

To elucidate whether 8 or 9 is accepted by ChphE, we performed in vitro reactions. As 8 was not isolable owing to spontaneous dehydration, we employed sequential enzymatic reactions of ChphD and ChphE with 7 as the substrate. After 24 h, we detected 2 and 6 (Figure S7), which indicates that 8 is recognized by ChphE. Reaction with 9 also gave 2 and 6 (Figure S8). Considering the interconversion between isochromene and hemiacetal in the reaction system, we cannot conclude that the isochromene is accepted by ChphE. However, the ratio of 2 to 6 was higher when 9 was used as the substrate than in the sequential reaction (Figures S7 and S8), which suggests that 9 was also accepted by ChphE.

Reactions of ChphE with 8 or 9 produced C-prenylated monomers, 1 and 12, as minor products (Figures S7 and S8). The structure of 12 was proposed to be a water adduct of 1, based on its MS spectrum and its formation from 1 under acidic conditions (Figure S9). When 4 was incubated with ChphE, C-prenylated 11 was detected as a minor product and O-prenylated 10 was detected as a major product (Figure S10). These findings imply that ChphE transfers a prenyl group to a phenolic hydroxy group at the para position of the carboxyl group and that C-prenylated CPs arise from O-prenylated CPs. However, some fungal DMATS family PTs are multifunctional, such as TdiB catalyzing both C- and O-prenylation to a common substrate. In addition, rearrangement reaction should be considered to discuss PT activity. For example, LynF, a TruF family bacterial PT, catalyzes reverse prenylation to the phenolic hydroxy group, and the following spontaneous Claisen rearrangement affords a regular C-prenylated product. Given these properties of PTs, we conducted a time-course experiment to examine whether ChphE, a DMAT family PT, catalyzes direct C-prenylation or reverse O-prenylation at the ortho phenolic hydroxy group other than its main function. We incubated 4 and ChphE and analyzed the reaction mixture by LC–MS at 8 time points (Figures c and S11). ChphE efficiently prenylated 4 to produce 10 at the first point. After 1 h, while 4 was fully consumed, 11 was not detected until after 3 h. Based on these results, we conclude that ChphE catalyzes prenylation only onto the phenolic hydroxy group at the para position of the carboxyl group. This is supported by the docking analysis of ChphE and the in vitro experiments with analogs of 4 (Figures S12 and S13).

In the time course experiment, the amount of 11 was increased over time. Thus, we tested whether ChphE can convert O- to C-prenylated CPs. When 10 and 2 were incubated with ChphE, 11 and 1 were detected, respectively; however, these conversions were also observed without an enzyme (Figure S14). This clarified that C-prenylated CPs were generated nonenzymatically from the corresponding O-prenylated CPs. In the case of LynF, the C-prenylated product is spontaneously generated from an O-prenylated intermediate in water. The effect of water accelerating the pericyclic reaction is well-known. , Indeed, the conversion from O- to C-prenylated CP did not occur in organic solvent (Figure S15). Thus, we propose that C-prenylated CPs are generated from O-prenylated CPs via Claisen and Cope rearrangements followed by decarboxylation (Figure g).

To examine favorable conditions for the rearrangements of O-prenylated CPs to C-prenylated CPs, we tested the influence of the solvent acidity (Figure S16). In the range from mildly acidic to mildly basic conditions, C-prenylated CPs were generated at the same level (Figures d and S16). On the other hand, the strongly basic condition inhibited the conversion. In the strongly acidic condition, removal of the prenyl group competed with the rearrangements. During this analysis, we detected a new compound, 13 (Figures e and S16), that has a dihydropyran ring derived from the prenyl group (Figure f). Compound 13 likely forms via isomerization of 2 to p-QM, followed by an ene-like reaction between the exo-methylene moiety and the prenyl group to afford a tertiary cation intermediate that is quenched by water (Figure h).

The dihydropyran ring was also found in chaetolines, which were isolated from Chaetomium sp. and proposed to be biosynthesized from 5 by Ancheeva et al. Based on their proposed pathway, we tested that the lactol moiety of 13 could be converted to isoquinolinone via isocoumarin by using 4 as an analog of 13. Under acidic conditions, 4 underwent dehydration, forming anhydrosclerotinin B (14). , This dehydration was not observed under basic conditions, while the methyl ester of 6 (15) was converted to 14 under basic conditions. This reaction likely proceeded by enolate attack to the methyl ester moiety similar to the biosynthesis of 14. An isoquinolinone 16 was generated when 14 was heated in ethanolamine, which suggests that 13 can be a precursor for chaetolines.

We propose the formation pathways of O- and C-prenylated CP monomers (Figure f). ChphA synthesizes 5, which is oxidized by ChphB and ChphD to form 8. ChphE then catalyzes the O-prenylation of 8 to afford 6. Subsequently, 6 was spontaneously converted to 2, which underwent nonenzymatic Claisen and Cope rearrangements and decarboxylation to yield 1. Although the C-prenylated products could arise from prenylation of decarboxylated intermediates in nature (Figure S17), such products were not detected from our transformants, and the docking analysis implies that the carboxyl group is required for substrate recognition by ChphE (Figure S12). These findings support the idea that the decarboxylation must not precede prenylation by ChphE.

In this study, we investigated the formation mechanism of CPs, focusing on the O- and C-prenylation of monomeric CP, which are key tailoring steps that govern the modes of dimerization. We demonstrate that O-prenylation is catalyzed by ChphE, whereas C-prenylation occurs via a nonenzymatic process. This study sheds light on the potential of CPs to undergo further structural expansion through nature or chemistry.
