This study evaluated the inﬂuence of two storage media on wear, sorption, surface roughness, ﬂexural strength,
and hardness of milled and 3D-printed provisional restorations after 72 h aging in distilled water and acidic
solution at 37 °C. Two 3D-printed materials (Arma·, NextDent· C&B MFH) and one milled PMMA block
(Audental·) were evaluated for mechanical properties (wear resistance, Vickers microhardness) and physical
performance, including sorption measured as mass (%) change. Surface characteristics were analysed using
non-contact optical proﬁlometry and scanning electron microscopy. The 3D printed restorations (ARMA &
C&B) exhibited lower hardness and ﬂexural strength, along with the high surface roughness and surface area
loss, with ARMA reaching 0.86 « 0.11 ¯m after water aging and 1.02 « 0.15 ¯m after acidic aging. In contrast,
milled materials showed lowest mass changes and superior overall stability proving its suitability for provisional
restorations.
Keywords: 3D-printed, Low-pH degradation, Surface roughness, Flexural strength, Wear resistance

1. Introduction
Provisional (temporary) crowns are normally used after
tooth preparation to protect the tooth structure and restore
function and esthetics, while maintaining periodontal
health during the interim period before deﬁnitive prosthetic rehabilitation.1) Their clinical performance depends
largely on their mechanical and surface properties, as
these restorations must withstand masticatory forces,
thermal variations, and continuous exposure to the oral
environment.2)
Digital fabrication techniques, including computeraided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/
CAM) milling and three-dimensional (3D) printing, have
recently gained popularity for the fabrication of provisional restorations due to improvements in eﬃciency,
precision, and reproducibility.3) Milled polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) materials generally demonstrate higher
polymer conversion and a denser microstructure, contributing to improved mechanical durability, whereas 3Dprinted materials may contain lower cross-link density or

However, provisional restorations are continually exposed to variations in intraoral pH, particularly in patients

(GERD), conditions known to accelerate material deterioration. Low-pH environments promote water sorption
and hydrolytic breakdown of polymer matrices, resulting
in increased surface roughness, decreased hardness, and
reduced ﬂexural strength.7–9) Increases in surface roughness exceeding 0.2 ¯m can enhance bacterial retention and
plaque accumulation, thereby aﬀecting periodontal health
and the longevity of restorations.10)
The wear resistance of provisional materials is critical,
as wear can lead to occlusal instability, material loss, and
clinical failure. Chewing simulation using a steatiteceramic antagonist is a recognized in-vitro method for
mimicking occlusal contact forces.11) Flexural strength
testing provides insight into a material’s ability to withstand bending and functional loads, particularly in posterior regions. Surface hardness reﬂects the resistance of
the surface to indentation and softening, particularly after
exposure to acidic environments.7) Additionally, surface

Therefore, assessing the surface properties and mechanical stability of various provisional materials under

restorative option. Understanding how CAD/CAM-milled
and 3D-printed provisional materials respond to erosive
environments can help predict their clinical performance
and support evidence-based material selection.
This study aimed to compare the eﬀects of water and
acidic aging on the surface roughness, surface hardness,
wear behavior, sorption, and ﬂexural strength of CAD/
CAM-milled and 3D-printed temporary crown materials.
Thus, the null hypotheses of this study were: (i) there
would be no diﬀerences in surface roughness, surface
hardness, wear, and ﬂexural strength among the temporary
crown materials after acidic and water aging; (ii) there
would be no changes in sorption after storage in water or
acidic media; and (iii) there would be no correlation
between surface area loss (mm2) and percentage weight
loss following water or acidic storage.

2.3

Immersion process and sorption

Two storage media were used in this study: distilled
water and an acidic solution. Distilled water served as the
control, with a measured pH of 7.05 « 0.03. The acidic
medium was prepared using citric acid monohydrate
(Avochem Ltd., Manchester, UK). A 0.26 mol/L citric
acid solution was prepared in deionized water, resulting in
a pH of approximately 2.3. The pH of the solution was
monitored using a calibrated pH meter and adjusted as
needed using NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China). to
ensure a stable acidic environment throughout the experiment. The specimens were immersed in the citric acid
solution for 72 h. This immersion period was selected as an
accelerated aging model to evaluate the resistance of the
material to acidic degradation. Although continuous exposure for 72 h does not directly mimic clinical conditions, it
represents the cumulative eﬀect of repeated exposure to
acidic challenges encountered in daily life. Citric acid is
one of the most common dietary acids and is widely used
in erosion studies because of its strong demineralizing and
calcium-chelating properties. Therefore, the selected immersion period provides a practical and clinically relevant
approach for assessing the long-term stability and durability of restorative materials under acidic conditions.13)
Each specimen was suspended in 50 mL of the assigned
solution using dental ﬂoss to prevent contact with the container walls. The containers were placed in an incubator
(Thermo Fisher Scientiﬁc, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C
for 72 h. The solutions were refreshed daily to maintain
consistent chemical conditions.

2.4

Wear

For each material investigated, 15 specimens were
prepared and allocated for wear analysis. The specimens
were randomly divided into three subgroups (n = 5) according to the storage condition prior to wear testing:
baseline (control), water storage, and acidic storage.

The wear resistance of the CAD/CAM-milled and
3D-printed materials was evaluated using a computercontrolled chewing simulator (Proto-tech, USA). A steatite ceramic antagonist with a diameter of 6 mm (SD
Mechatronik GmbH, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany)
was selected as the opposing material because its hardness
and wear characteristics closely resemble those of natural
enamel. Prior to testing, each antagonist was ultrasonically
cleaned, dried, and weighed using an analytical balance
(Precisa EP 225 SM-DR, Precisa Gravimetrics AG,
Zurich, Switzerland) with a precision of 0.0001 g to determine its initial mass (W1). Each specimen was securely
ﬁxed in the lower sample holder of the chewing simulator,
while the steatite antagonist was mounted onto the upper
stylus assembly. Care was taken to ensure proper alignment and uniform contact between the antagonist and
specimen surface throughout the testing procedure.
The wear simulation was conducted under a vertical
load of 49 N, corresponding to the average masticatory
force generated in the posterior region. A total of 120,000
chewing cycles were applied at a frequency of 1.6 Hz,
which has been reported to represent approximately six
months of clinical mastication. During the course of
chewing simulation, all specimens of the three subgroups
(n = 5) were immersed in distilled water to minimize
frictional heat generation and reproduce oral ﬂuid conditions. After testing, the antagonists were removed, rinsed
with running water, dried, and re-weighed to determine
post-test mass. The percentage mass loss of the antagonist
was calculated using the formula:

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on a pilot study
using G*Power software (Version 3.1.3, Heinrich Heine
University, Germany). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 25.0; IBM SPSS
Statistics, Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was
used to assess normality, and Levene’s test was applied to
verify homogeneity of variance.
A multiple one-way ANOVA was performed independently in which all combinations of material type (Milled
PMMA, ARMA, C&B MFH) and storage condition (baseline, water storage, acidic storage) were treated as a single
categorical factor with nine levels. Tukey’s post hoc test
was applied for all pairwise comparisons at a signiﬁcance
level of ¡ = 0.05.
For comparisons between two storage conditions (water
vs. acidic) for surface area loss and percentage weight
change, independent-samples t-tests were conducted.
Additionally, linear regression analysis was performed
to assess the correlation between surface area loss (mm2)
and percentage weight loss after water and acidic storage.

4. Discussion
Dental restorative materials must survive in dynamic,
chemically complex oral conditions, where environmental
aging can signiﬁcantly inﬂuence their mechanical characteristics and long-term durability. Among these factors,
repeated exposure to acidic conditions has been shown to
compromise surface integrity, increase degradation, and
ultimately reduce clinical life. To assess the performance
of dental composites, surface roughness is one of the critical parameters to evaluate the quality and aesthetics. There
are multiple factors that inﬂuence the surface roughness

This study evaluated the eﬀect of water and acidic aging
on the surface roughness of diﬀerent dental composite
restorations fabricated by CAD-CAM and 3D printed techniques. It can be inferred from the results that, at baseline,
the 3D-printed specimens demonstrated the highest surface
roughness, whereas CAD-CAM–milled group demonstrated the lowest surface roughness as given in Table 2 and
Fig. 1. Similarly, the post-aging results demonstrated that
ARMA specimens fabricated via the 3D printing method
exhibited the greatest change in surface roughness (0.86
¯m in water and 1.02 ¯m in acid solution) and surface loss,
followed by C & B (Table 2). On the other hand, milled
composites demonstrated minimal changes in surface
roughness and showed smoother surfaces both before and
after aging (Fig. 2).
The signiﬁcant increase in surface roughness and deterioration of the 3D-printed restorations may result from the
leaching of residual monomers due to hydrolytic and acidinduced degradation.15) Water sorption causes plasticization of the polymer matrix, release of residual unreacted
monomers, chemical and physical modiﬁcations that ultimately leads to materials degradation and compromised
integrity.16,17) Similar ﬁndings have been reported by earlier studies with milled composite restorations exhibiting
signiﬁcantly lower surface roughness and superior mechanical properties compared with their 3D-printed counterparts, both before and after artiﬁcial aging procedures,
indicating enhanced resistance to environmental and degradative challenges.18) In 3D printing, layer-by-layer deposition causes surface defects and sometimes poor interlayer
bonding. This may lead to the formation of visible layering
lines and decreased reproducibility. These ﬁndings indicate that the processing technique exert signiﬁcant inﬂuence on surface smoothness than material composition
alone.3) These results are consistent with our previous
study, which also reported signiﬁcantly higher surface
roughness in 3D-printed interim crowns.19)
Clinically, lower surface roughness is important because
increased surface roughness promotes greater tissue irritation and bacterial adhesion, thereby elevating the risk of
plaque accumulation and the growth of oral diseases.20)
In this study, the notable increase in surface roughness
(after acidic aging) of ARMA and C & B specimens
(ARMA: Ra increased from 0.52 to 1.02 ¯m; C & B: 0.11
to 0.22 ¯m) may be attributed to hydrolytic degradation of
the polymer matrix and deterioration at the ﬁller–matrix
interface. Acidic environments promote the cleavage of
ester linkages within methacrylate-based resin networks,
accelerating matrix softening and structural weakening.
This degradation process facilitates ﬁller debonding,
microcrack formation, and the development of surface
voids, collectively contributing to the observed increase in
surface roughness.14,21) This structural compromise leads
to material loss and rougher surface textures, as observed
in Table 3 and the SEM micrographs in Fig. 2. Previous
studies have reported that acidic beverages and low pH
environments can signiﬁcantly roughen the surfaces of
resin-based composites, leading to increased plaque accu740

mulation, bacterial adhesion, and clinical failure.22)
Likewise, the greater surface area loss observed in
ARMA following both water immersion (3.35 mm2) and
acidic exposure (3.69 mm2) further highlights its reduced
resistance to mechanical and chemical degradation. Water
sorption followed by hydrolytic expansion can induce
internal stresses that weaken the composite structure, while
acids can facilitate the leaching of ﬁller particles and the
degradation of the resin matrix. These combined mechanisms likely explain the higher material loss in ARMA
compared to the other materials tested.
Diﬀerence in fabrication methods is not the only factor
that aﬀect the surface properties of dental restorations. The
signiﬁcantly lower surface roughness and reduced wear
demonstrated by milled composites reﬂect enhanced structural stability and improved long-term durability. In addition, materials composition also inﬂuence surface material
composition also inﬂuence surface characteristics and
overall smoothness substantially.19,23) Methacrylate-based
materials have been reported to exhibit smoother initial
surface ﬁnishes compared with bisacrylate materials,
largely owing to diﬀerences in their composition. The
incorporation of smaller ﬁller particles improves polishability, facilitating the development of a more homogeneous and reﬁned surface texture.24) Accordingly, methacrylate-based materials may represent a more reliable
choice for interim restorations, as their ﬁller-free composition reduces surface irregularities that are not inherently
related to ﬁller particle dimensions. Furthermore, these
materials facilitate the formation of more uniform, smooth,
and defect-free surfaces upon polishing.25)
Higher ﬂexural strength enhances a material’s resistance
to breaking, which is crucial for the clinical eﬀectiveness
of interim restorations. In the current study, the milled
specimens exhibited signiﬁcantly higher ﬂexural strength
compared with the 3D-printed groups (ARMA and C&B).
The higher ﬂexural strength of the milled composites may
be attributed to optimal curing conditions and to the highly
cross-linked methacrylic acid ester-based polymer in milled
PMMA, which enhances its mechanical properties.26) Similar ﬁndings have been reported by studies showing superior ﬂexural strength for milled to that of 3D-printed
PMMA.27,28) On the other hand, the lower ﬂexural strength
in the 3D-printed specimen may be due to challenges in
building, postcuring, and the use of a thin layer thickness,
which can cause specimen shrinkage and modiﬁcations
during data conversion and STL ﬁle manipulation.26)
The lower ﬂexural strength of the 3D-printed specimens
may be attributed to inherent diﬀerences in material composition, polymer network architecture, ﬁller characteristics, and fabrication processes between 3D-printed and
CAD/CAM-milled materials. The 3D printing technique
based on stereolithography (SLA) fabricates restorations
by sequentially polymerizing a photoreactive resin in discrete layers. Owing to this layer-by-layer fabrication
approach, surface-related defects such as interlayer lines
and staircase artifacts are inherently formed and are often
diﬃcult to eliminate, despite the high dimensional accu-

racy oﬀered by the technology.29) Consequently, even after
polishing, 3D-printed materials may retain microscale
surface irregularities and micro-voids, resulting in higher
surface roughness as well as greater sorption than homogeneously polymerized, industrially milled blocks. In contrast, CAD/CAM milled blocks are manufactured through
high-pressure, high-temperature polymerization, resulting
in a smooth, densely packed, cured, and compact material
structure with uniform surface, particularly at the ﬁller–
matrix interface. This compact network limits water and
acid penetration, reduces chemical degradation, and maintains ﬂexural strength even after prolonged exposure to
aging challenges.30,31) The variation in surface roughness
and ﬂexural strength of the study groups rejected the ﬁrst
null hypothesis.
Surface hardness which shows resistance to surface
indentation, is another indicator of a material’s durability
and inﬂuenced by environmental aging. In this study, the
surface hardness of milled and 3D-printed specimens, aged
in water and acid solution, was evaluated. Likewise, ﬂexural strength, surface hardness showed a similar trend,
with 3D printing groups (ARMA and C&B) exhibiting the
lowest surface hardness, likely due to their high residual
monomer content and porosity.32) The absorption of water
into the polymer matrix causes hydrolytic degradation
and reduced intermolecular forces, thereby reducing surface hardness through a plasticizing eﬀect. The 3Dprinted interim materials are more prone to these eﬀects
due to their layer-by-layer construction, water penetration
through microvoids, and low degree of polymerization.31,33) The eﬀect of acidic aging is more severe, as it
causes superﬁcial erosion of the resin matrix due to a faster
hydrolytic reaction, leading to a greater reduction in surface hardness compared with distilled water.34,35) On the
other hand, the milled specimens demonstrated the highest
baseline surface hardness, which may be due to their high
density, as denser materials are more resistant to wear and
surface deterioration.26)
Since milled dental blocks have densely cured polymer
network with improved ﬁller distribution and minimal residual monomer content. This homogeneous structure limits
water diﬀusion and acid penetration, thereby preserving
surface microhardness to a greater extent after aging protocols.31,33) Although a small reduction in microhardness
was observed upon prolonged exposure to distilled water
or acidic solutions, their superior polymer conversion and
microstructural integrity confer greater resistance to hydrolytic and chemical degradation.
The negative linear slope and correlation between surface area loss and weight percent (Fig. 3) indicate that
greater surface area loss is associated with a greater reduction in weight percent. Thereby rejecting the third null
hypothesis. Moreover, ﬁller–matrix debonding and superﬁcial erosion may also contribute to progressive surface
deterioration. These ﬁndings suggest that interim restorative composites, which are susceptible to surface loss in
water and acidic solutions, may also lead to bulk structural
deterioration, ultimately compromising long-term mechan-

ical durability.

5. Conclusion
This study demonstrated the eﬀects of water and acidic
aging on the surface properties, ﬂexural strength, and
surface hardness of 3D-printed and milled provisional
restorations. Based on the results of this in vitro study, it
can be concluded that diﬀerent manufacturing methods
signiﬁcantly aﬀect the properties of restorative materials
exposed to similar oral conditions. Milled temporary
restorations outperform 3D-printed crowns by demonstrating higher ﬂexural strength, lower surface roughness, and
the highest surface hardness after exposure to water and
acidic conditions. The enhanced resistance of CAD/CAMmilled materials to hydrolytic and acidic degradation is
primarily associated with their densely packed, homogeneous, and highly crosslinked microstructure, which eﬀectively limits water and acid diﬀusion, reduces surface
defects, and maintains ﬂexural strength even after longterm aging. Conversely, 3D-printed materials are inherently more vulnerable to environmental degradation owing
to their layer-by-layer fabrication, which generates interlayer boundaries, staircase artefacts, and microstructural
voids. Combined with their relatively lower degree of conversion, these characteristics facilitate moisture absorption
and acid penetration, leading to increased surface degradation and a greater reduction in mechanical properties.
