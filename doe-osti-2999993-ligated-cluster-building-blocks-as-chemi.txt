B.1. Superatomic State Beyond the Conventional Magic Numbers: Ligated Metal-
Chalcogenide Superatoms:

 The conceptual formulation of superatoms has provided a unifying framework for the
classification of stable metallic clusters with well-defined valence opening the pathway to a three-
dimensional periodic chart. The superatomic concept was originally derived from the observed
periodicities in the stability, electronic characteristics, and reactivity of clusters of metallic elements.
These periodicities were reconciled within a model based on a confined nearly free electron gas
where the quantum states bunch into groups that can be labeled by the principal and angular
momentum quantum numbers. The electronic shells order as 1S, 1P, 1D, 2S, 1F, 2D ... and clusters
with 2, 8, 18, 20, 34, 40 ... valence electrons correspond to filled shells and are marked with large
HOMO−LUMO, enhanced energetic stability, higher ionization energy, and lower electron affinity.
The enhanced electronic stability at filled shells further prompts clusters with unfilled shells to
acquire stability by donating or accepting electrons to attain the filled status, thus exhibiting a well-
defined valence. The stable clusters with precise valence are regarded as superatoms, and the
attainment of such a closed electronic shell is termed the superatomic state. The above classification
inspired by a confined nearly free electron gas is suited to identify the superatomic state in simple
metallic and noble metallic species. However, not all clusters conform to this electronic description,
and a different paradigm is needed for different classes of clusters. We extended the superatomic
framework to ligated metal-chalcogenide clusters.
 One of the most common classes of clusters is the octahedral TM6E8L6 where TM = transition

metal; E = chalcogen; and L = ligand. In our work, we focused on this class of clusters that have
high symmetry, producing significant degeneracy in their electronic structures that confer high
electronic stability at certain electron counts. We carried out electronic structure calculations on
TM6S8(CO)6 clusters where the TM included the transition metals from the 4d and 5d series. As
stated previously, the key criteria for a cluster to be considered a superatom is that the cluster has a
well-defined valence. For the valence to be identified, we need to find electron counts that enhance
stability as determined by a filled electronic shell and a large HOMO−LUMO gap. Additional
markers for the attainment of a zero valent superatomic state are possessing a high ionization energy
and a low electron affinity. We used three markers to test the attainment of the superatomic state. (1)
The bunching of electronic states into shells can lead to large gaps between the shells. Consequently,
the HOMO-LUMO gap is a clear marker of the shell filling. (2) The next marker is the ionization
energy as electronically stable species are unlikely to give up an electron, so ionization energy
should exhibit a peak at a shell filling. For this reason, we examined the changes in the ionization
energy as a function of the filling of the electronic shells. (3) Filled shells are also marked by a
decrease in the ability to attract additional electrons. Therefore, a decrease in the electron affinity is
another electronic marker of the attainment of filled shell status.

B.2. Novel Class of Magnetic Superatoms Stabilized by Dual Subshell Filling: Joint Theory
Experimental Investigations.
 Hund’s rule in atomic physics and the Stoner model in solid-state physics describe the traditional
mechanisms by which spin magnetic moments arise in atoms and ferromagnetic metals. In both models,
valence electrons avoid pairing by occupying half-filled orbitals, which increases the exchange energy
and lowers the Coulomb repulsion.
 In a joint theory/experimental effort we proposed a new mechanism for the formation of magnetic
moments in which the spin magnetic moment originates in filling of distinct sub-shells.57 As discussed in
section B.I, for octahedral transition metal chalcogenide clusters the electronic states present a filled shell
status at electron counts of 96, 100, and 114 valence electrons. At these electron counts, the electronic
shells are completely filled and the clusters exhibit diamagnetic behavior. Our idea to stabilize a magnetic
moment (net spin moment) was to combine the orbital sub-spaces from two different closed shell clusters,
using the subshell spin up channel of one magic size and the spin down spin channel of the other magic
size to create a hybrid electronic structure. Figure 6 illustrates how such a situation may be realized by

selecting a cluster based on Fe. Consider the prototypical diamagnetic closed shell clusters Co 6S8(CO)6
and [Re6S8(CO)6]2+. Co6S8(CO)6 has an electron count of 114 valence electrons (6×9 electrons derived
from Co, 8×6 electrons from S, and 6×2 electrons in the Co–CO bonds), and a large HOMO–LUMO gap
of 1.71 eV. [Re6E8(CO)6]2+, on the other hand, has an electron count of 100 and a HOMO–LUMO gap of
1.88 eV. When compared to [Re6E8(CO)6]2+, the additional 14 electrons of Co6S8(CO)6 occupy A2g, T1g,
and T2u orbitals; these close-lying sets of high symmetry orbitals are the reason why certain electron counts
are favored.

Figure 6. The electronic structure of Co6S8(CO)6, [Fe6S8(CN)6]5-, and [Re6S8(CO)6]2+. Valence electrons
for the cluster core are assigned by counting all valence electrons from the transition metals and Sulfur,
and 2 valence electrons per CO or CN molecule.
 To design an electronically stable dual subshell magnetic cluster, consider a cluster [Fe6S8(CN)6]5– that
has an ideal electron count of 107 electrons (see Figure 6). In [Fe6S8(CN)6]5–, the majority-filled subshell
consists of orbitals corresponding to the 114 electron configuration (spin up channel), with 57 electrons
before a large energy gap. The minority subshell configuration (spin down channel) consists of orbitals
derived from the 100 electron configuration, with 50 electrons below its energy gap. The result is a cluster
with a sizeable HOMO-LUMO gap and a large spin magnetic moment resulting from 7 excess (un-paired)
electrons in the majority spin channel (shown in red in Figure 6).
 Can such a cluster be synthesized in actual experiments? The experimental group at Columbia
University synthesized the novel cluster [Fe6S8(CN)6]5– using electron-accepting cyanide ligands to
stabilize the high charge state. The reason for focusing on this novel cluster instead of another previously
reported [Fe6S8(PEt3)6]+ cluster with same electron count was that cyanides can be used as bridging ligands
capable of mediating strong magnetic exchange, for example in Prussian Blue-type materials. Consistent
with theoretical predictions, the experiment studies found that the cluster [Fe6S8(CN)6]5– is stable and has
an isolated magnetic spin state of S = 7∕2.

 We also worked with experimental groups to seek out unusually high stable magnetic clusters whose
stability is rooted in covalent metal-carbon bonds. We found an unusual Met-Car cluster Fe12C12– that is
energetically stable, as demonstrated by its preponderance in mass spectra, but exhibits a large spin
magnetic moment.58 In reaction with acetylene, the anionic Fen– clusters exhibit minor size dependence
of dehydrogenation but eventually generate a prominent metal–carbon cluster Fe12C12–. It is worth
mentioning that the doping of carbon generally quenches the magnetic moment of metal clusters. Here,
the addition of carbon not only stabilizes the Fen– clusters but also retains a very high-spin magnetic
moment of 36 µB. We demonstrate how the prominent product Fe12C12– is associated with a plumb–bob
structure of Jahn–Teller distortion. The spin accommodation rather than geometric/electronic shell closure
opens a new dimension to develop cluster chemistry theory and design cluster-genetic materials. Further,
we have attained soft-landing deposition of the Fe12C12– clusters and measured the magnetization
responses. This 1 nm-sized Fe12C12– cluster with a highly stable structure and strong ferromagnetism could
have great potential applications in semiconductors, quantum computing, and microchips as well as
spintronics and information storage devices.

B.3. Interfacial Magnetism in fused Superatomic dimers: Case of [Co6Se8(PEt3)5]2:

 While ligated metal chalcogenide clusters have emerged as a new class of superatoms, several of
the newly discovered superatoms including Co6Se8(PEt3)6 cluster are non-magnetic. We focused on the
possibility of making magnetic units based on such clusters. We have shown that by linking two
Co6Se8(PEt3)5 clusters, it is possible to form a composite dimer [Co6Se8(PEt3)5]2 that is magnetic. The
spin magnetic moment is primarily localized on the interfacial Co-sites, where the two clusters are fused
into a dimer. This means that we have stabilized a ligand protected magnetic cluster by combining two
highly stable superatomic clusters. Furthermore, the dumb-bell shaped dimer is marked by low
ionization energy of 4.17 eV, which can interact with the similar shaped C140 composed of two C70 units,
characterized by a high electron affinity to form a stable ionic solid. Additionally, the theoretical
calculations also support the experimental work done by Nuckolls and co-workers, who have stabilized
the anisotropic solids by using toluene molecules as space fillers. Theoretical studies confirm that the

[Co6Se8(PEt3)5]2 dimer loses electrons to C140 in the solid, leaving each metal-chalcogenide dimer as
cations. The neutral dimers have a spin moment of 2 µB per cluster, while the cationic dimers have spin
moments of 1 µB. The cluster-dimer is proposed to show an intriguing magnetic characteristic when the
electronic charge is added to the system. When an electron passes through the metal-chalcogenide
dimer, the transient neutrality of the cationic species will favor the higher magnetic state of the neutral
dimer, which might lead to a spin-polarized current. This situation could find spintronic-related
applications. In fact, X. Roy (J. Laskin?) have already embarked on experiments to detect the interfacial
magnetism in such clusters.

B.4. Controlling Quantum States in Phosphorene Nanoribbons Using Ligands and Transforming
Their Electronic Properties Using Superatomic Dopants:

 Phosphorene, a single layer of black phosphorus is the subject of a growing number of studies
due to its extraordinary, directional and layer-dependent properties including high carrier mobility (up to
1000 cm2/Vs) and a direct bandgap of 1.51 eV compared to 0.3eV in bulk. One of the salient features of
the electronic structure is the dispersion of energy bands along the zigzag and armchair directions. While
the bands display significant dispersion along armchair direction, they are nearly flat along the zigzag
direction. This asymmetry can provide a unique opportunity to control properties to engineer novel
electronic materials with desired electronic features. For electronic applications, two of the most
important properties are the band gap and the work function. While the former dictates the range of
radiation that can be absorbed, the placement of conduction band minima, and valence band maxima
govern the transport of holes and electrons as a semiconductor is interfaced with other semiconductors.
Finding ways to control the gap and the work function is therefore an important objective.
 We proposed a novel approach to controlling the placement of conduction and valence band
edges and the band gap. We first showed that the addition of that of terminal ligands NH2, OH, H, SCH3,
SH, Cl, and OCN ligands affects the work function by band bending of the induced dipoles of the
terminal groups. The dipole is often caused by the ligand itself rather than the charge transfer between
the ligand and phosphorene.59 We also showed that the band gap itself can be tuned by adding alkali
atoms on the surface that lower the band gap ultimately driving the system to a metal. In a later work,
we showed al alternate approach to controlling the band gap and the nature of carriers. We showed that
by depositing Co6S8(PH3)5-n(CO)n superatoms on the surface, one can control the band gap energy by
exchanging phosphine ligand by electron withdrawing CO ligands.60 We then showed that by depositing
Re6Se8(PH3)5-nCln clusters, phosphorene can be converted into a p- or n- type semiconductor as the
relative composition of the ligands is changed thus offering a novel approach to controlling the carrier
type as well as rthe band gap. We are collaborating with experimental groups to demonstrate some of
these new findings.

B.5. Fast ab initio design of high-entropy magnetic thin films using machine learning.

High-entropy alloys are a class of solids with a long range ordered structure where the atomic sites are
randomly occupied by the homogeneous, random mixing of five or more different elements. After the
mixing, the change in the Gibbs free energy ΔG = ΔH – TΔS is allowed to be negative even with a
significant increase in the mixing enthalpy ΔH > 0. This is enabled by the large configurational entropy
gain ΔS > 0. Such a mixing can thus spontaneously stabilize a crystal structure that hosts many chemical
bonds normally not favored in regular intermetallic alloys.

We have shown that the magnetic properties of high-entropy alloys can be captured via first principles
density functional calculations within the coherent potential approximation.61 Here, the atomic details of
the high entropy mixing are considered as an effective medium with translational symmetry of the
underlying lattice. We demonstrate this using the FCC phase of FeCoNiMnCu and the L10 phase of
(FeCoNiMnCu)Pt by comparing the density functional theory results with the experimental values.
Working within the first Brillouin zone and the primitive unit cell , we show that the DFT can capture
the smooth profile of magnetic properties including saturation magnetization, the Curie temperature, and
the magnetic anisotropy energy, using only a sparse set of sampling points in the vast computational
space. The smooth profiles given by the DFT indeed follow the experimental trend, thus demonstrating
the promising potential of using machine learning to explore the magnetic properties of the high entropy
alloys by establishing reasonably large datasets with high – throughput calculations based on DFT.
