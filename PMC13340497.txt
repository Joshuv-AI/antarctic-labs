Validity of the Translated Turkish Version of the Tool for Nutrition Risk Screening for Paediatric Cancer
Authors: Buket Meral, Melda Kangalgil, Erol Erduran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMalnutrition in paediatric cancer patients increases adverse clinical outcomes; however, methods for assessing malnutrition risk remain unclear. This study aimed to assess the validity of the Nutrition Risk Screening for Pediatric Cancer (NRS‐PC).MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted in the paediatric oncology unit of a tertiary care university hospital. The nutritional risk and malnutrition status were assessed by the Nutrition Screening Tool for Childhood Cancer (SCAN) and Pediatric Subjective Global Nutritional Assessment (SGNA), respectively. Receiver–operator characteristic (ROC) curve method was used to evaluate the validity of NRS‐PC. Sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values were calculated.ResultsThe median age of children was 8 years, and 60.2% were boys. The prevalence of malnutrition was 48.4%. NRS‐PC had excellent accuracy compared with SCAN (AUC: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.827–0.944; p < 0.001) and SGNA (AUC: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.847–0.951; p < 0.001). Using SGNA as a reference method, the NRS‐PC tool had a sensitivity of 77.4%–100% and specificity of 24.2%–86.4% according to different cut‐off points.ConclusionThe Turkish version of the NRS‐PC is a simple, valid and practical method for nutritional risk screening in the paediatric oncology population. Further feasibility and acceptability studies are needed to determine the most appropriate screening tools in children.Keywords: malnutrition, nurses, nursing, nutritional assessment, paediatric oncology, sensitivity, specificity

Introduction

Paediatric cancer remains a significant public health challenge and is the second leading cause of death among children and adolescents worldwide. Survival rates vary significantly by region, with rates as low as 30% in low‐ and middle‐income countries. The Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer aims to increase childhood cancer survival to at least 60% by 2030 (World Health Organization 2021). Achieving a twofold increase in the global cure rate requires ensuring treatment access and reducing dropout rates, but these efforts alone have been shown to be insufficient. To significantly improve childhood cancer outcomes, interventions focused on enhancing the quality of care are essential (Ward et al. 2019).

Malnutrition in paediatric cancer patients may be present at the time of diagnosis, develop during the treatment process, and even persist post‐treatment, with a prevalence ranging approximately from 6% to 68% (García‐Guzmán et al. 2025; Guida et al. 2023; Ouyang et al. 2021; Razzaq et al. 2025; Zimmermann et al. 2013). In paediatric cancer patients, malnutrition is associated with lower survival rates, higher risk of toxicity, reduced ability to tolerate treatment and increased susceptibility to infection (Dong et al. 2024; Loeffen et al. 2015; Orgel et al. 2014). Nevertheless, appropriate nutritional interventions have been shown to improve patient outcomes (Castillo‐Martinez et al. 2018). Therefore, the integration of nutritional support into multidisciplinary cancer care is essential to ensure more sustainable and effective outcomes in paediatric oncology (World Health Organization 2021).

Nutritional assessment and monitoring are essential components of care in paediatric cancer patients. The nutritional status of all patients should be assessed using a standardized method at the time of diagnosis and monitored regularly throughout treatment and during post‐treatment follow‐up (Fabozzi et al. 2022). Nutritional risk screening is the first step in the nutritional care process. It is best to identify the risk of malnutrition and implement nutritional interventions before deficiencies in anthropometric measurements become apparent. If children are identified as being at risk of malnutrition, their nutritional status should be assessed and diagnosed, followed by appropriate interventions, including dietary counselling, medical nutrition therapy and specialized care, as part of ongoing treatment (Murray et al. 2021). Pediatric Subjective Global Nutritional Assessment (SGNA) is a valid and reliable method for assessing the nutritional status of children at nutritional risk and for making a nutritional diagnosis. However, due to the need for it to be performed by a dietitian and its time‐consuming and comprehensive nature, it is not suitable for routine nutritional risk assessment by nurses (Secker and Jeejeebhoy 2007).

Authorities including the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition (ASPEN), Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN), and the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN), agree that routine nutritional screening and, if indicated, nutritional assessment should be performed at hospitalization and regular intervals (Correia 2018; Hulst et al. 2022). Integrating nutritional screening into care has been reported to reduce the risk of malnutrition as well as improve weight gain in the paediatric oncology patient population (Franke et al. 2022). However, validated nutritional risk screening tools are not used in many paediatric oncology inpatient and outpatient clinics, leaving a large gap between current and good practice (Selwood et al. 2010; Huysentruyt et al. 2019). This is further complicated by insufficient data on the use of screening tools developed for the general paediatric population in children with cancer. As a result of these issues, the need for nutritional risk screening tools specific to children with cancer was recognized. In this context, two nutritional screening tools for children with cancer have been developed. One of these, The Nutrition Screening Tool for Childhood Cancer (SCAN), developed in 2016, provides comprehensive screening by targeting cancer‐related malnutrition, considering cancer diagnosis, treatment parameters, nutritional symptoms, dietary intake and disease severity (Murphy et al. 2016). The second, Nutrition Risk Screening for Pediatric Cancer (NRS‐PC), was reported more recently and includes nutritional symptoms leading to impairment of nutritional status and changes in nutritional habits in children with cancer (Gallo et al. 2021). There is no consensus on a nutritional screening tool for assessing the risk of malnutrition in the paediatric cancer population. This requires further investigation of existing screening tools in children with a variety of features and increased efforts to identify the most appropriate screening tool (Gallo et al. 2021; Saraiva et al. 2023). Furthermore, assessing the validity of these tools in different cultural contexts will provide clinicians with the opportunity to use validated and reliable screening tools.

Methods

Study Aims

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the validation of the NRS‐PC in Turkish. The secondary aim was to determine the performance of the NRS‐PC in identifying malnourished children compared with SGNA and SCAN.

Study Design

This cross‐sectional study was consistent with the Strengthening of the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Participants

This study included children with cancer who were admitted to the paediatric oncology unit of a tertiary care university hospital, between April 2024 and January 2025. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling with the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 2 to 18 years; (2) undergoing treatment at a paediatric haematology clinic or outpatient clinic, or a survivor (post‐treatment of cancer) now receiving care in an outpatient setting; and (3) ability to understand study information. Patients diagnosed with hematologic–oncologic conditions who were hospitalized in the intensive care unit or other clinics, children with hemodynamic instability, children for whom anthropometric assessment was not feasible and children of parents who did not accept to participate in the study were excluded from the study.

Data Collection

NRS‐PC

The translation and adaptation process of the NRS‐PC was carried out in accordance with the guidelines (Sousa and Rojjanasrirat 2011). The forward translation was independently conducted by a dietitian and a paediatric nurse, both proficient in English and Turkish. Simultaneously, a bilingual and bicultural translator outside the healthcare field translated the text into Turkish. After obtaining these translated versions, a paediatric haematology–oncology professor compared these translations for word and meaning ambiguities and inconsistencies and then combined them into a single version. Subsequently, this Turkish version was back‐translated by another professional translator, whose native language is English and who was not familiar with the health terminology or the original screening tool. The back‐translated version was compared with the original version by a research group consisting of a dietitian, a nurse and a physician, who then approved the final version. The items of the nutritional risk screening tool were evaluated by a total of eight experts, including three dietitians, two physicians and three nurses. The experts were asked to evaluate each question for simplicity, clarity, and relevance and revisions were made to the screening tool based on their feedback. The final version was provided in Table S1.

Nutritional Risk Screening Tools

NRS‐PC and SCAN were administered simultaneously by the nurse, whose area of expertise was paediatrics. SCAN is the first screening tool developed specifically for children with cancer and assesses six parameters: cancer risk, type of treatment, gastrointestinal symptoms, changes in food intake, body weight loss and clinical signs of malnutrition. The total score, ranging from 0 to 10, indicates a high risk of malnutrition if it is ≥ 3 score (Murphy et al. 2016). The Turkish validation of the SCAN was conducted by our study team, and it shows high sensitivity and specificity in determining the nutritional risk in children with cancer (Kangalgil et al. 2025). The NRS‐PC consists of two stages. In the first stage, the child's use of oral nutritional supplements and nasogastric tube, and body mass index (BMI)‐for‐age percentiles, are assessed. BMI‐for‐age percentiles in this part were calculated based on the World Health Organization child growth standards (World Health Organization 2007). In the second stage, there are six items with ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ answers, which can impact the child's nutritional status. The total score that can be obtained from the NRS‐PC ranges from 0 to 6, and each point is calculated based on the number of ‘Yes’ answers to the questions in the second stage (Gallo et al. 2021).

Assessment of Nutritional Status

SGNA was used as a reference method for the assessment of nutritional status. The SGNA is a comprehensive, clinically based assessment tool that evaluates nutritional status through a structured evaluation of medical history and physical examination, including recent changes in weight, dietary intake, gastrointestinal symptoms, functional capacity and disease‐related metabolic stress, as well as a focused physical assessment of subcutaneous fat stores and muscle mass. After completion of the nutritional risk screening tools, a single dietitian who was blinded to the screening results performed the SGNA for each child to independently determine the presence and severity of malnutrition. The average time required to complete the SGNA was approximately 15–20 min per child. Based on the SGNA, children's nutritional status was categorized as well nourished, moderately malnourished or severely malnourished according to established criteria (Secker and Jeejeebhoy 2007). For the purposes of statistical analysis, the categories of moderate and severe malnutrition were combined and analysed under a single ‘malnutrition’ category.

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Clinical data (diagnosis, treatment protocol) and demographic data (age, sex) were collected from medical files. The oncological diagnoses were classified using the International Classification of Childhood Cancer (ICCC‐3) (Steliarova‐Foucher et al. 2005).

Anthropometric Measurement

Anthropometric measurements were performed by a nurse. The children's heights were measured using a stadiometer without shoes, with their feet together and their head aligned in the Frankfurt plane. Body weight was measured with an electronic scale while the children were wearing light clothing and no shoes. BMI (kg/m2) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m2).

Ethical Considerations

Before the study was conducted, permission was obtained by email from Gallo et al. (2021), who developed the NRS‐PC scale for the validation study. The protocol of this study was approved by the Karadeniz Technical University Health Sciences Scientific Research Ethics Committee. All parents provided written informed consent. Written consent was also obtained from children aged ≥ 12 years. All procedures are performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the hospital and with the Helsinki Declaration.

Data Analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test the distribution of variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. To determine the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) of the NRS‐PC, 2 × 2 tables were created to compare the results obtained from the no malnutrition and moderate–severe malnutrition categories of the SGNA with the results obtained from the not at risk of malnutrition and risk of malnutrition categories of the SCAN (Florkowski 2008). The exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) method was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV. A receiver–operator characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the relationship between SCAN, SGNA and NRS‐PC. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify diagnostic accuracy. For clinical interpretation, an AUC value of 0.5 indicates no discriminative ability, whereas values closer to 1.0 represent excellent diagnostic accuracy. No missing data was present for the variables included in these analyses. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant. The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS statistical software (version 24.0, IBM).

In instrument validation studies, a participant‐to‐item ratio of 10:1 to 30:1 is recommended to ensure adequate sample size (Boateng et al. 2018; Tsang et al. 2017). As the NRS‐PC is a six‐item tool, the target sample size ranged between 60 and 180 participants. Our study sample consists of 62 malnourished children and 66 well‐nourished children, classified using SGNA. This is consistent with the methodological standards established for ROC curve analysis and exceeds the minimum number of participants per group required to obtain reliable sensitivity and specificity estimates (Bujang and Adnan 2016; Hajian‐Tilaki 2014). Thus, these conditions meet methodological standards for evaluating the diagnostic accuracy and psychometric evaluation of screening tools.

Discussion

This is the first study to evaluate the validity of the NRS‐PC, developed specifically for children with cancer, against the SGNA, which is used as a reference method for assessing nutritional status. In this context, the Turkish version of the NRS‐PC is a rapid, practical and validated screening tool for use in paediatric oncology patients.

As in our study, previous studies have demonstrated a high prevalence of nutritional risk among paediatric oncology patients (Cañedo et al. 2022; Kangalgil et al. 2025; Ouyang et al. 2021; Saraiva et al. 2023). Considering that malnutrition is one of the most important determinants of clinical outcomes (Karalexi et al. 2022), screening and assessment processes are still poorly defined in this population. Routine nutritional screening at the time of hospital admission and at regular intervals allows for the timely identification of potentially malnourished children. This enables earlier intervention before nutritional status deteriorates further, potentially improving their clinical condition. Therefore, nutrition screening should be a critical component of the paediatric nutrition care process. However, due to the nature of paediatric cancer patients, the aetiology of malnutrition differs from other patient populations (Budka‐Chrzęszczyk et al. 2024; Fabozzi et al. 2022; Pedretti et al. 2023; Viani et al. 2020). A recent study reported an increase in the use of nutritional risk screening tools in clinics but emphasized that none of the tools used were specific to paediatric cancer patients, highlighting the need for further validation of oncology‐specific nutritional risk screening tools (Henry et al. 2022). To date, the NRS‐PC has only been developed and validated in its original setting, and evidence regarding its applicability across different clinical, cultural and healthcare system contexts is limited. To our knowledge, the present study represents the first cultural adaptation and validation of the NRS‐PC. The psychometric performance of the Turkish version was comparable to that reported in the original validation study, supporting its applicability in paediatric oncology. Future multicentre studies are needed to validate the NRS‐PC in other settings, enabling direct international comparisons and broader global use.

One of the main components of nutritional screening tools is anthropometric measurements (Rasmussen et al. 2010). There is concern that some patients with malnutrition may be overlooked because SCAN does not include anthropometric parameters (Cañedo et al. 2022). The first part of the NRS‐PC, a BMI percentile of less than 10 and less than 5, is questioned. However, as the first part is not included in the total score of the NRS‐PC, there is confusion about how to interpret these parameters. The second part of NRS‐PC consists of six questions asking about weight loss, changes in eating habits, effects on gastrointestinal symptoms, and finally changes in physical activity status. In the present study, the most common of these factors was a reduction in physical activity compared with before diagnosis. It is well known that a decrease in physical activity is common in children during and even after cancer treatment (Ha et al. 2022; Stössel et al. 2020). Reduced physical activity, fatigue and impaired nutritional status are interrelated and associated with many adverse outcomes (Bauer et al. 2011; Brinksma et al. 2015). Therefore, in the nutritional risk screening tool, in addition to nutritional symptoms, assessing physical activity status could be an important parameter.

To evaluate the effectiveness of nutritional screening tools in identifying malnutrition, a reliable method that accurately determines the nutritional status is essential. The SGNA is a nutritional status assessment tool that is based on clinical judgement and is often considered the reference method for assessing the validity of nutritional risk screening tools in children (Klanjsek et al. 2019). When the sensitivity of screening tests is high, patients with malnutrition can be accurately identified, leading to a lower rate of false‐negative results. Conversely, screening tests with high specificity result in a better identification of well‐nourished patients, thereby reducing the occurrence of false‐positive results (Charney 2008). In the original study, the NRS‐PC was validated using SCAN. When the cut‐off point was at one, sensitivity was 98% and specificity was 62%, whereas increasing the cut‐off point to two reduced sensitivity to 86% and increased specificity to 77% (Gallo et al. 2021). In the current study, when SGNA was used as the reference method and the cut‐off point for the NRS‐PC was set at one, the screening tool demonstrated a sensitivity of 100%, but a markedly low specificity (24.2%). The determination of cut‐off points in screening tests is based not only on statistical performance but also on strategic considerations such as the costs of unnecessary further diagnostic procedures and the efficient use of clinical resources (Trevethan 2017). In an ideal screening tool, choosing a low specificity leads to unnecessary further assessment, which is not in line with the purpose of nutritional risk screening (Lalkhen and Mccluskey 2008). In this context, increasing the cut‐off to two reduces sensitivity by approximately 3%, while improving specificity to 57%, thereby decreasing the false‐positive rate and enhancing the PPV. This approach optimizes the discriminative capacity and practical applicability of the screening tool, particularly in clinical settings where follow‐up procedures are costly or workload‐intensive (Trevethan 2017). Consequently, increasing specificity while allowing only a marginal reduction in sensitivity may reduce the burden on both patients and the healthcare system and improve overall diagnostic accuracy.

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

The main important strength of the study is the use of both a nutritional assessment method and another nutritional risk screening tool that is specific to the target population and unique in its field. This allows for a more comprehensive and comparative evaluation of the diagnostic accuracy of the scale. This study has certain limitations that need to be identified. Firstly, the single‐centre design and the use of convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias and limit the generalizability of this study. Although the study included patients with different paediatric oncology diagnoses and treatment phases, the sample may not fully represent the broader paediatric oncology population in Turkey in terms of clinical and sociodemographic characteristics. Therefore, these factors should be taken into consideration when interpreting the results. Another limitation is that patients could not be evaluated longitudinally to clarify the performance of the NRS‐PC at different diseases and treatment modalities. Furthermore, testing nutritional screening tools with body composition measurements supports their validation. As such, NRS‐PC was tested with body composition measurements in their original development and validation studies. However, in this adaptation study, they were only validated against SGNA and SCAN. Therefore, future studies should focus on testing the scale's predictive validity by examining its correlation with objective clinical outcomes such as infection rates, prognosis, length of hospital stay, healthcare utilization (Chrástecká et al. 2023). Furthermore, the assessment of the clinical acceptability and feasibility of this instrument among nursing professionals would be valuable to ensure its successful integration into routine practice.

Conclusions

The Turkish version of the NRS‐PC developed to assess the risk of malnutrition in children with cancer is a valid screening tool. Patients with an NRS‐PC score of two or more are at risk of malnutrition and should be referred for further assessment as well as appropriate nutritional intervention. Further investigations on feasibility and acceptability are required to determine the most appropriate screening tools for use in paediatric oncology settings.

Clinical Implications

The findings of this study highlight the importance of nutritional risk screening in paediatric oncology and provide significant implications for clinical practice. Malnutrition screening is widely recognized as a critical component of care for the paediatric oncology patient population (Han et al. 2021). Nurses are the healthcare professionals responsible for screening patients for malnutrition; therefore, it is important to have an easy‐to‐use and quick nutrition screening tool that can be applied by nurses, especially in clinics such as paediatric oncology where malnutrition is a serious problem (Meehan et al. 2019; Xu et al. 2017).

In this context, the NRS‐PC offers a significant advantage, particularly in outpatient oncology settings, which constitute a substantial proportion of cancer treatment programmes, as well as in rural and other low‐resource settings, where access to dietitians is frequently limited. With its concise structure of six items and a simple binary scoring system, the NRS‐PC enables nurses to effectively identify patients at nutritional risk without requiring specialized training, making it particularly suitable for settings with limited human and institutional resources. Evidence suggests that integrating a paediatric oncology‐specific nutrition screening tool into routine clinical practice enables nurses to facilitate earlier access to dietitians and timely nutritional interventions for patients (Han et al. 2021). For this reason, the use and integration of nutrition risk screening tools specifically developed for these patients is essential for an optimal nutrition care process.

Nevertheless, previous studies have indicated that nurses' knowledge levels regarding malnutrition and, in particular, nutritional screening remain inadequate (Bassola et al. 2020; Bauer et al. 2011; Dello Iacono et al. 2025). Furthermore, nurses' educational background, prior training and the presence of institutional support mechanisms have been identified as determinants of effective nutritional care delivery (Bauer et al. 2011; Dello Iacono et al. 2025). Therefore, although no specific training is required to implement NRS‐PC, the development of short and structured training programmes for nurses is recommended to support the effective integration of nutritional screening into clinical practice. Indeed, recent evidence suggests that implementing strategies such as targeted education, audit and feedback and the assessment of local barriers and facilitators could significantly improve the integration of nutritional interventions into cancer care (Fanning et al. 2026). Such training programmes may include the fundamental principles of nutritional screening, standardized interpretation of screening elements, referral pathways based on risk levels and interdisciplinary collaboration processes (Bauer et al. 2011; Dello Iacono et al. 2025; Fanning et al. 2026). For nurses working in outpatient and rural healthcare settings, educational approaches such as online training modules, practical implementation guides and decision‐support tools have the potential to further enhance the consistent and sustainable use of the NRS‐PC.
