Biochemical discordance of a urinary fluoroquinolone-resistant Escherichia coli ST1193 as a Shigella species: A case report
Authors: Brian Peine, Ma Rowena San Juan, Hosoon Choi, Munok Hwang, Chetan Jinadatha, Dhammika H Navarathna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEscherichia coli (E. coli) sequence type 1193 (ST1193) is an emerging pathogenic organism that is increasingly identified in urinary specimens and often demonstrates multi-drug resistance. Although ST1193 is a well-documented E. coli clone, its lactose-nonfermenting phenotype and other atypical biochemical features may complicate routine laboratory interpretation. We report a urinary isolate from a 38-year-old woman with asymptomatic bacteriuria and a history of recurrent urinary tract infections that was identified as E. coli by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry and confirmed as E. coli ST1193 by whole-genome sequencing but was classified as Shigella group with 91% confidence by an automated biochemical identification panel. The isolate demonstrated delayed growth as pinpoint pale-tan colonies on CHROMagar, non-lactose fermentation on MacConkey agar, lysine decarboxylase positivity, and orange-yellow colony formation on Hektoen Enteric agar. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing demonstrated resistance to ampicillin, some 1st and 2nd generation cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones, with susceptibility to nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, later-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin-tazobactam, aminoglycosides, and carbapenems. This case highlights a practical diagnostic pitfall. E. coli ST1193 may be accurately identified by MALDI-TOF MS and genomic methods yet still generate misleading biochemical-panel results suggestive of Shigella due to their similar genomic profile. Awareness of this phenotype is important when interpreting discordant E. coli-Shigella biochemical profiles from urine cultures.Keywords: Escherichia coli ST1193, Urinary tract infection, Asymptomatic bacteriuria, Shigella, Biochemical identification, VITEK 2, MALDI-TOF MS, Whole-genome sequencing, Fluoroquinolone resistance

Introduction

Escherichia coli (E. coli) sequence type 1193 (ST1193) is an emerging multidrug-resistant extraintestinal pathogen that is increasingly recognized among urinary isolates. This lineage has demonstrated concerning epidemiologic features including global dissemination, association with extraintestinal infection, and frequent multi-drug resistance [1]. In a multicenter study, ST1193 comprised approximately one quarter of fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli isolates in urine and has increased substantially over time [2]. In younger adult women with E. coli urinary tract infections across Canada, ST1193 also represented a notable uropathogenic lineage [3].

The laboratory recognition of ST1193 has practical implications. Reported ST1193 isolates generally fall within phylogroup B2/O75 and typically show fluoroquinolone resistance with absent lactose fermentation [1], [4]. The lactose-negative phenotype is an important diagnostic feature because non-lactose fermentation can lead laboratory technicians to consider enteric pathogens such as Shigella during routine work-up. Genomic studies have linked this phenotype to disruption of lacY, supporting that non-lactose fermentation is a stable feature of the ST1193 lineage rather than evidence against E. coli identification [5].

Routine MALDI-TOF MS and whole-genome sequencing can support species-level identification of E. coli ST1193. However, biochemical identification systems may be more vulnerable to misleading results when an E. coli isolate expresses atypical or Shigella-like biochemical traits. This issue is especially relevant because E. coli and Shigella are closely related and can show overlapping biochemical and proteomic profiles [6]. True urinary infection or asymptomatic bacteriuria caused by Shigella has been reported but remains uncommon [7]. We describe an E. coli ST1193 urinary isolate that was accurately identified by MALDI-TOF MS and confirmed by whole-genome sequencing but was misclassified as Shigella group with 91% confidence by an automated biochemical panel. The purpose of this report is to highlight a cautionary point for clinical microbiology practice: biochemical-panel identification should be interpreted in the broader context of colony morphology, MALDI-TOF MS results, antimicrobial phenotype, and genomic findings, when necessary for confirmation.

Case report

A 38-year-old woman with a reported history of recurrent uncomplicated urinary tract infections presented for follow-up evaluation of vaginal discomfort. She denied urinary symptoms at the time of presentation. Concurrent vaginal testing was positive for bacterial vaginosis and negative for Candida species and Trichomonas vaginalis. A urinalysis, performed due to the vaginal discomfort, demonstrated positive nitrites, trace leukocyte esterase, moderate blood, trace protein, numerous bacteria, 15 red blood cells per high-power field, and 4 white blood cells per high-power field.

Urine culture was inoculated onto CHROMagar (Bio-Rad. UriSelect™ 4) medium. No growth was observed after 24 h of incubation. After 48 h, pinpoint pale-tan colonies were present at greater than 100,000 CFU/mL. Gram stain performed demonstrated short to medium-length Gram-negative rods (Fig. 1).

Gram stain of the urinary isolate. Gram stain demonstrates short to medium-length Gram-negative rods, a morphology compatible with either Escherichia coli or Shigella spp.

MALDI-TOF MS (VITEK MS PRIME, bioMérieux, Inc).identified the organism as E. coli at the species level; however, the indole spot test was negative, and given the known overlap in identification profiles, Shigella could not be definitively excluded. An automated biochemical identification using the VITEK 2 Gram-negative card classified the isolate as belonging to the Shigella group with 91% confidence. The biochemical profile demonstrated lysine decarboxylase positivity, glucose fermentation, mannitol fermentation, and trehalose fermentation, with negative reactions for hydrogen sulfide production, citrate utilization, urease production, and ornithine decarboxylase activity. Additional culture media demonstrated non-lactose fermenting colonies on MacConkey agar and orange colonies on Hektoen Enteric agar without hydrogen sulfide production.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the VITEK 2 instrument, incorporating the VITEK−2 AST N812 and VITEK−2 AST-XN−33 custom panels for Gram-negative organisms, with interpretive categories assigned according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute criteria [8] and FDA break points [9] as our AST cards were validated for the latest breakpoints in accordance with CLIA 88 requirements. The isolate was found to be resistant to ampicillin, ampicillin-sulbactam, cefazolin, cefuroxime axetil, cefpodoxime, ciprofloxacin, delafloxacin, and levofloxacin. It had intermediate sensitivity to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid and cefotoxitin, and it was susceptible to piperacillin-tazobactam, cefuroxime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime-avibactam, ceftolozane-tazobactam, cefepime, aztreonam, ertapenem, imipenem, meropenem, meropenem-vaborbactam, amikacin, gentamicin, tobramycin, doxycycline, eravacycline, tetracycline, nitrofurantoin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (Table 1).

Because of the discordance between MALDI-TOF MS and biochemical-panel identification, whole-genome sequencing was performed. Genomic DNA was extracted from bacteria cultured 37 °C overnight using QIAmp DNA Micro kit (Qiagen) and library for sequencing was prepared with Rapid Barcoding kit (ONT). The sequencing was performed on the GRIDION platform (ONT) and the reads were assembled and processed with DragonFlye v1.2.1. Sequencing demonstrated that the isolate was nearly identical to E. coli ST1193. Average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were above conventional species-level thresholds (> 95%) with both E. coli ST1193 and several Shigella spp., reflecting the close genomic relationship within the Escherichia-Shigella complex. However, the final species classification by pubMLST, using both multilocus sequence typing (MLST) and 16S rRNA gene identification approaches, supported E. coli. Sequencing also identified chromosomal quinolone resistance-determining region mutations gyrA S83L, gyrA D87N, and parC S80I, consistent with the observed phenotypic fluoroquinolone resistance and with the known ST1193 resistance profile.

After susceptibility results were reported, the patient was treated with nitrofurantoin for her asymptomatic bacteriuria. After 14 days of follow-up, she had still not developed any urinary symptoms or complications. No repeat urine samples were received.

Discussion

This case is best classified as an E. coli ST1193 urinary isolate with a potentially misleading biochemical identification result. MALDI-TOF MS identified the organism as E. coli, and whole-genome sequencing supported final classification as E. coli ST1193. The central diagnostic pitfall resided in the conflicting results of the automated biochemical panel that classified the organism as Shigella group with 91% confidence.

Several aspects of the isolate are consistent with ST1193. ST1193 is a globally distributed extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli lineage associated with urinary tract infections, bloodstream infections, and multidrug resistance, including fluroquinolone resistance [1], [2]. Its prevalence among fluoroquinolone-resistant urinary E. coli isolates is substantial in multiple studies, and recovery of ST1193 from urine is not uncommon. The non-lactose-fermenting phenotype was the major reason Shigella was considered during the work-up. However, non-lactose fermentation is a recognized feature of ST1193 rather than an argument against E. coli. Wu et al. found that 96.1% of ST1193 isolates in their collection were non-lactose fermenters [4], due to disruption of lacY [5]. This is directly relevant to the present case because the non-lactose-fermenting phenotype contributed to the consideration of Shigella group.

The fluoroquinolone resistance profile also strongly supports ST1193. The isolate was resistant to ciprofloxacin, delafloxacin, and levofloxacin, and sequencing demonstrated gyrA S83L, gyrA D87N, and parC S80I. These same quinolone resistance-determining region mutations are characteristic of ST1193 and are regarded as the basis for its chromosomally mediated fluoroquinolone resistance [1], [4], [5]. The isolate also demonstrated resistance to ampicillin, ampicillin-sulbactam, and several earlier-generation cephalosporins, while retaining susceptibility to nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, later-generation cephalosporins, piperacillin-tazobactam, aminoglycosides and carbapenems. This pattern is compatible with the broader concept of ST1193 as a multidrug-resistant lineage, although individual susceptibility profiles vary depending on acquired resistance determinants [1], [10].

The Shigella group result from the biochemical panel is understandable but misleading. E. coli and Shigella are closely related organisms, and routine laboratory methods may struggle when isolates have atypical biochemical features [6]. In this case, the VITEK 2 biochemical profile favored Shigella group, likely driven by a combination of non-lactose fermentation and other reactions. However, several findings argued against true Shigella. First, MALDI-TOF MS identified the organism as E. coli. Second, whole-genome sequencing supported E. coli ST1193. Third, the isolate was lysine decarboxylase positive, which is more typical of E. coli than Shigella in most routine diagnostic contexts. Recent work combining MALDI-TOF MS with detection of lysine and ornithine decarboxylation products demonstrated that lysine decarboxylase-derived cadaverine was characteristic of E. coli, whereas tested Shigella species lacked the corresponding peak [6]. Fourth, the isolate produced orange colonies on Hektoen Enteric agar without hydrogen sulfide production, a finding suggestive of carbohydrate utilization rather than the classic green to blue-green colonies expected for Shigella.

Although Shigella urinary tract infection has been reported, it remains uncommon [7]. When a urine isolate is classified as Shigella by a biochemical system but is identified as E. coli by MALDI-TOF MS, laboratories should be cautious before reporting Shigella. The work-up should integrate colony morphology, key biochemical reactions such as lysine decarboxylase, results from selective/differential media, antimicrobial phenotype, and, when available and necessary, genomic data. In the present case, this integrated approach supported a final interpretation of E. coli ST1193 with a degree of Shigella-like biochemical behavior.

This case also reinforces a broader point about ST1193 surveillance. ST1193 is a well-recognized, globally disseminated extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli clone [1], [10], [11]. Genomic studies from multiple regions have shown that ST1193 can carry extensive antimicrobial resistance determinants and may circulate among both infected patients and colonized individuals [10]. Population-based studies further support its role as an important antimicrobial-resistant E. coli clone [11]. Awareness of its characteristic phenotype, particularly non-lactose fermentation, may prevent unnecessary confusion with Shigella during urine culture work-up.

Conclusion

We report a fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli ST1193 urinary isolate that was accurately identified by MALDI-TOF MS and confirmed by whole-genome sequencing but was misclassified as Shigella group by an automated biochemical identification panel. A key lesson is that ST1193 may display biochemical features, especially non-lactose fermentation, that can mislead biochemical identification systems. When biochemical-panel results suggest Shigella from urine but MALDI-TOF MS, selective media, and lysine decarboxylase testing support E. coli, laboratories should interpret the biochemical profile cautiously and consider ST1193 among the differential explanations.
