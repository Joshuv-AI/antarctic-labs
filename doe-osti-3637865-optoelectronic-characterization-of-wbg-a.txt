I. Introduction
Wide and Ultrawide bandgap (WBG, UWBG) semiconductors are at the forefront of new
electronic devices, offering an alternative to silicon. These devices are characterized by
their large bandgaps, higher breakdown fields, and greater thermal conductivity. These
properties allow these materials to operate at higher voltages and temperatures, allowing
for smaller, yet more efficient devices to be fabricated. Additionally, these devices allow for
greater switching speeds [1].

These devices can be used to manufacture higher performance photoconductive
semiconductor switches (PCSS). PCSS are often used in ultra-fast electronics because of
their high power, fast response, and low switch jitter [2]. They are composed of a
photoconductive material between electrodes, such that light increases the conductivity of
the material and allows current to flow. Typically, these devices should have minimal dark
current, but with exposure to short bursts of laser radiation, the conductivity, and therefore
the current should rapidly increase.

Our goal specifically is to develop an X-ray detector. In the absence of radiation, the device
will act like an insulator, allowing for minimal current to go through. However, even in the
presence of a small amount of radiation, the device will become conductive and generate
current orders of magnitude greater than the dark current. Thus, we would be able to detect
any X-rays (or, equivalently, other forms of radiation) by monitoring the output signal from
the device.

A. 532nm PL Spectroscopy
Photoluminescence (PL) Spectroscopy can provide unique fingerprints of a material,
including its structure and defects. A laser of known wavelength is illuminated onto the
material. The photon is absorbed by an electron, and the electron is excited into the
conduction band. When the energy is released, it emits a wavelength of light equal to the
energy difference between the final and initial electronic states. Defects in the material can
cause electrons to exist in energy states other than the conduction and valence bands
(defect states). Thus, electrons can also emit photons as they relax from the conduction
band to the valence band (bandgap), the conduction band to a defect state, a defect state
to the valance band, or a defect state to another defect state. By measuring the different
wavelengths of light emitted, PL allows us to see different energy separations between
conduction, defect, and valance bands.

To measure the PL Spectrum of the AlN photodetectors, a Horiba Standard Microscope
Spectroscopy System was used. A 532nm laser was used to illuminate the material, which
is below the bandgap of AlN (6.1eV, 203nm). A PL spectra from 530nm to 1000nm was
plotted using the accompanying software. Spectra for inside of the metal conductive ring
and the outside of the ring were collected, as the inside of the ring has an ITO layer while
the outside does not.

B. 532nm Raman Spectroscopy
Similar to PL Spectroscopy, Raman spectra can also uniquely identify a material, its stress
and quality. A laser of known wavelength is illuminated onto the material, but instead of

absorbing the photon, the light is scattered. The most common form of scattering is
Raleigh (elastic) scattering, where the photon creates a change in polarizability of the
molecule, but is then relaxed into its original state. This emits the same wavelength of light
as the incident light beam (532nm). In Raman (inelastic) scattering, after the polarizability
changes, the molecule ends up in a different vibrational state than before. If it results in a
higher vibrational mode than before, energy is absorbed and a longer wavelength of light is
emitted (Stokes), while if the molecule results in a lower vibrational mode, it released more
energy and a shorter wavelength is emitted (Anti-Stokes). The difference between the
original wavelength and emitted wavelength (measured in cm-1) corresponds to the energy
distance between vibrational states. By measuring this shift in wavelengths, we can identify
the different vibrational modes of the material. Moreover, observing slight shifts in peaks
compared to established literature can identify stresses in the material, and measuring the
FWHM can identify the quality of the material.

The Raman Spectra was also created using the Horiba Standard Microscope Spectroscopy
System with the 532nm laser. The Raman shift from 100cm-1 to 1000cm-1 was measured
and plotted using the accompanying software. Spectra for inside of the metal conductive
ring and the outside of the ring were collected.

C. Current-Voltage (I-V) Characteristics
When incident light excites an electron into the conduction band, electron-hole pairs are
created. If an external voltage difference is applied across the material, these free charge
carriers will produce a current. However, if there is no light, most electrons remain in the
valence band, so there are very few charge carriers, producing little to almost no current.
Thus, the intensity of light can affect the conductivity of the material.

To measure the photoconductivity of these materials, we performed I-V characteristics of
the AlN photodetectors. A probe station was used, and an external voltage double sweep
from 200 V to -200 V was applied across the photodetector. First, a dark current
measurement was taken. A 405 nm laser was then centered on the detector and another
current measurement was taken.

III. Results and Discussion
A. 532 nm PL Spectrum
For both the inside-ring and outside-ring spectra, three peaks of varying intensity was
identified. The largest peak lied around 700nm. Two smaller peaks can be seen around
625nm and 900nm. Because the incident laser wavelength is 532 nm, whose energy is less
than the bandgap for AlN, these PL peaks must represent various defect states present in
the material.

The 900nm peak (1.37 eV) may correspond with the 1.4 eV PL peak tentatively assigned by
Lamprecht et al [3], of an electron emission from an oxygen donor state to an isolated Al
vacancy. The 625nm and 700nm peak may match with an emission of an electron from an
Al vacancy and substitutional O defect complex to the valence band of 2.01 eV (617 nm)
and 1.83 eV (678 nm) modeled by Zhou et al [4]. Therefore, these observations suggest that
these AlN detectors may contain numerous oxygen defects as well as some Al vacancies.

Moreover, each device exhibited slightly different peak intensity ratios between the peaks.
For example, in the outside-ring spectra shown in Figure 4, the peak around 700nm is 40%
more intense than the 900nm peak for device D23, while for D11 the increase is only
around 10%. The differences across each device, even when they were fabricated on the
same wafer demonstrates the varying defect concentration throughout the material.

B. Raman Spectrum
Numerous peaks are identified in the captured Raman spectra. The most prominent peak
was centered around 657 cm-1, which is identified as the E2 (high) vibrational mode [5]. The
second highest spike lay around 890 cm-1, corresponding to the A1 (LO) mode [5], while the
third highest peak was centered around 250cm-1, aligning with the E2 (low) mode [6]. All
three of these peaks were seen for both the spectra inside and outside the conductive ring,
as seen in Figure 5.

 Fig. 5
 Raman spectra of the D23 device for both inside and outside the ring

By increasing the acquisition time, we were able to see smaller peaks in the spectra. For
the outside spectra, peaks at approximately 270 cm-1, 330 cm-1, 430 cm-1, 511 cm-1,
610 cm-1, 750 cm-1, and 955 cm-1 were detected. The peak at 511 cm-1 corresponds to an
acoustic overtone [5]. The 610cm-1 corresponds with the A1 (TO) modes [5], [6]. The other,
smaller peaks could not be mapped to a vibrational mode of AlN, thus they can be
attributed to different defects present in the material. More research and literature review
is necessary to map these peaks.

There are a few vibrational modes of AlN that we did not see in these spectra. The E1 (TO)
peak should be around 670 cm-1, while the E1 (LO) should have been seen around 912 cm-1
[5], [6]. One explanation why is that the orientation of the device prevented the E1 symmetry
modes from being activated.

While the inside and outside spectra have similar peaks and intensity ratios, one major
difference was the 610 cm-1 peak. Looking at device D21 (Figure 6a), the 610 cm-1 peak is
much more prominent for the outside peak and is approximately 1.35x more intense than
the second largest peak (750 cm-1), while for the inside spectra, the 610 cm-1 is only 1.22x
more intense. Moreover, in some of the devices, the 610 cm -1 peak was virtually nonexistent
for the inside peak, such as in D13 (Figure 6b). The increase in intensity of this A1 (TO) peak
outside of the conductive ring can be attributed to the lack of ITO substrate. This may be
because the layer of ITO is limiting the vertical vibration of the A1 (TO) mode, or because
the signal intensity is reduces as it goes through an extra layer of material.

Finally, we compare the spectra across different devices. Most peaks, as well as peak
widths seem consistent across devices, except for the A1 (TO) peak at 610 cm-1. The
intensity of this peak with respect to the other peaks varied across devices, such as how
D31 had a low intensity peak for both inside and outside compared to D23. According to
Wang et al [7], the A1 (TO) mode is forbidden for high (002) oriented AlN films, but with the
deterioration of this orientation and appearance of others, the A1 (TO) mode can be
enhanced. Thus, there is a possibility these fluctuations in the A1(TO) mode may be
attributed to slight changes in the surface and crystal orientation of the material.

The photoresponsivity for the AlN device was on average, 2.87 x 10 -6 A*cm / kV*W. To
determine how well these devices function as photodetectors, we must compare with

previous experimentation and literature. Chatterjee et al has found that similar PCSS
devices created from GaN:Mn have a photoresponsivity value of 3.88 x 10-4 A*cm / KV*W
with a 405 nm laser, which is over 2 orders of magnitude greater than our AlN devices [8].
Moreover, there was only 2 – 2.5 orders of magnitude increase in current from the dark
current measurements to the laser current measurements for the AlN. However, the
GaN:Mn devices are reported to have a difference of almost 6 orders of magnitude [8]. With
these comparisons, we can conclude that the photoconductivity of our AlN devices is
lower compared to previously reported GaN:Mn photoconductors. However, with a
significantly wider bandgap than GaN, and the demonstrated three orders of magnitude
increase in current under 405 nm illumination AlN can have potential applications in high
voltage switching applications.

IV. Next Steps
Further Raman and PL analysis may provide more in-depth analysis of these devices.
Because the 532nm laser exerts energy much lower than the bandgap of AlN, PL analysis
with a higher energy, 355nm laser may provide more information about the structure and
defects of the material. Moreover, further analysis of the Raman spectra, including peak
position, FWHM, and relative peak intensities may yield more accurate and conclusive
results.

Moreover, testing of more devices is planned, including two different GaN:Mn with varying
doping ratios as well as a device manufactured from gallates. Raman, PL and IV
characteristics of these devices will also be collected and can be compared with the AlN
device we have tested.
