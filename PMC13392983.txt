Motivational and Behavioural Physical Activity as Differential Correlates of Well‐Being Following Mass Trauma: A Three‐Group Study of Direct, Vicarious, and Non‐Exposed Individuals
Authors: Gil Zukerman, Adilson Marques, Hagit Bonny‐Noach, Riki Tesler, Avi Zigdon, Orit Shapiro, Ofer Frish, Liat Korn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRegular physical activity is associated with improved mental health, yet its role following mass trauma—and the differential contributions of motivational versus behavioural components—remains insufficiently understood. Drawing on stress‐buffering and self‐determination frameworks, this study examined whether value‐based motivation for fitness and weekly vigorous physical activity are differentially associated with well‐being as a function of trauma‐exposure severity. A cross‐sectional design was used with three groups (N = 306): survivors of the October 7, 2023 Nova music‐festival attack (direct exposure; n = 74), university students assessed in 2024 (vicarious exposure; n = 82), and students assessed in 2022 (pre‐event, non‐exposed; n = 150). Measures included value fitness commitment (VFC), weekly vigorous physical activity (WPA), subjective happiness, somatic complaints, and PTSD symptoms (directly exposed group only). Group differences were examined using ANOVA/MANOVA. Associations and moderation effects were tested with correlation analyses and Hayes' PROCESS models controlling for age and family status. Directly exposed participants reported lower subjective happiness and higher somatic complaints than comparison groups. WPA was associated with fewer somatic complaints in the full sample and with lower PTSD symptom severity among directly exposed participants. VFC was associated with greater subjective happiness and fewer somatic complaints among directly exposed participants. Moderation analyses showed that the inverse association between VFC and somatic complaints was significant only in the directly exposed group, whereas WPA–well‐being associations were not moderated by exposure level. Both motivational and behavioural physical‐activity indices were linked to better well‐being following trauma, but through partially distinct and context‐dependent pathways. Value‐based fitness commitment functioned as a protective resource specifically under conditions of severe trauma, consistent with stress‐buffering and psychological‐flexibility models, whereas actual vigorous activity showed a more general association with reduced somatic distress and PTSD symptoms. Low‐cost, scalable physical‐activity–related interventions that target both engagement and motivational processes may support post‐trauma recovery.

Introduction

Exposure to traumatic, life‐threatening events is often followed by elevated psychological distress characterized by intrusive recollections, physiological hyperarousal, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and persistent avoidance of trauma‐related cues (American Psychiatric Association [APA], 2013; Sartor et al. 2023). When such post‐traumatic stress (PTS) symptoms persist beyond 1 month, they may culminate in post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD; American Psychiatric Association 2013). Individuals may also exhibit clinically significant but subthreshold symptomatology (Cukor et al. 2010). Epidemiological studies suggest that lifetime PTSD prevalence in the general population ranges from approximately 3%–8% worldwide, although rates vary substantially across countries and populations exposed to trauma, whereas subthreshold PTSD may reach rates up to 15% (Atwoli et al. 2015; Brancu et al. 2016; Koenen et al. 2017).

First‐line treatments for PTSD are predominantly trauma‐focused cognitive–behavioural therapies, including Prolonged Exposure, Cognitive Processing Therapy, and EMDR, all of which show robust efficacy (Foa et al. 2007; Resick et al. 2017; Shapiro 2018). In parallel, converging evidence indicates that everyday health behaviours—such as regular physical activity, adequate sleep, and relaxation‐based stress regulation—are associated with lower post‐traumatic symptom severity and better overall well‐being (Gerber et al. 2014; Kredlow et al. 2015; Rosenbaum et al. 2015). These behaviours are thought to facilitate recovery through reductions in physiological hyperarousal, improvements in affect regulation, and enhancement of perceived self‐efficacy and resilience (Hobfoll et al. 2007; van der Kolk 2014). Because such strategies are low‐cost, accessible, and scalable, they can be implemented at the population level following large‐scale traumatic events and may complement trauma‐focused treatments by strengthening natural recovery processes and extending the reach of post‐trauma care (Wathelet et al. 2021).

Regular physical activity is consistently associated with improved psychological well‐being across populations (Singh et al. 2023). Benefits may stem from enhanced self‐efficacy, stress distraction (Rebar et al. 2015; Schuch and Stubbs 2019), and neurobiological effects, such as increased serotonin and norepinephrine availability (Schuch et al. 2016; Meyer et al. 2018). Randomised controlled trials show that structured exercise reduces PTSD symptoms, re‐experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal, and improves comorbid depression, sleep, and quality of life (Rosenbaum et al. 2015). Moreover, higher activity levels are linked with greater resilience and subjective well‐being among trauma‐exposed individuals, with moderate‐to‐large effect sizes (Mizzi et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024; Joseph 2025). In addition to actual behaviour, motivation and commitment to engage in physical activity may constitute an important psychological resource following trauma, as autonomous motivation and the internalization of exercise‐related values that are associated with greater behavioural persistence, psychological need satisfaction, and well‐being (Teixeira et al. 2012; Ng et al. 2012).

Evidence also suggests that the strength of the association between physical activity indices and wellbeing outcomes varies as a function of trauma exposure (Nilsson et al. 2021; Rosenbaum et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2024). Physical activity may function as a behavioural stress‐regulation strategy that buffers the physiological and psychological consequences of extreme stress exposure. Stress‐buffering models (Cohen and Wills 1985) propose that the benefits of adaptive behaviours are amplified under conditions of heightened stress, allostatic load, or vulnerability. Accordingly, individuals exposed to severe trauma—characterised by emotional dysregulation, heightened physiological arousal, and altered interoceptive processing (van der Kolk 2014)—may derive particularly strong regulatory and restorative benefits from physical activity compared to those with less severe trauma exposure (Nilsson et al. 2021; SantaBarbara et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024). This amplification may apply not only to behavioural engagement in physical activity but also to its motivational underpinnings, such as value‐based commitment to fitness (VFC), which can operate as a self‐regulatory resource that supports persistence and meaning‐based coping under distress, consistent with psychological flexibility and value‐driven action frameworks (S. C. Hayes et al. 2012; Teixeira et al. 2012). Physical activity and its motivational drivers may therefore be especially relevant in high‐exposure contexts because they simultaneously target affective, cognitive, and somatic processes disrupted following trauma. Nevertheless, despite these theoretical expectations, this moderation hypothesis—particularly with respect to motivational indices—has only rarely been examined directly in empirical studies.

The present study examined the association between physical activity and well‐being across three groups that differed in their level of exposure to the October 7, 2023 terrorist attack in Israel: directly exposed, vicariously exposed, and unexposed individuals. The directly exposed group consisted of survivors of the Nova music festival massacre, in which Hamas militants infiltrated southern Israel and carried out a large‐scale assault involving mass civilian casualties, hostage‐taking, and prolonged life‐threatening conditions. This context provided a naturalistic model for testing whether the benefits of physical activity vary as a function of trauma severity. We aimed to determine (a) whether physical activity is positively associated with well‐being following trauma and (b) whether this association is stronger under conditions of high exposure. We hypothesised that the association between physical activity and well‐being would be strongest among directly exposed participants (Nova survivors) compared with both the vicariously exposed and the non‐exposed groups, and that it would also be stronger among the vicariously exposed group than among those who were not exposed.

Methods

Participants

The total sample consisted of 306 participants across three groups. The first group comprised 74 Nova survivors who were present at music festivals held simultaneously in southern Israel on October 7, 2023, during the terrorist attack. Participants attended several events, including the Nova music festival (the largest), Psyduck, and Midburn. During this terrorist attack, Hamas militants carried out a large‐scale assault that resulted in widespread civilian casualties and abductions. Participants completed the structured questionnaire anonymously between January 2024 and January 2025 and were recruited through Izun–Arena, a crisis‐intervention centre in Israel where individuals come to receive treatment following traumatic incidents, operated by the Izun Association ‘Back to balance’. Nova survivors reported high PTSD symptom levels, with 70% scoring in the severe range (PDS ≥ 36; McCarthy 2008). The second group (University students in 2024, after October 7, 2023) consisted of 82 university students assessed in 2024 (post–October 7), who completed an anonymous Qualtrics questionnaire between February and July 2024 as part of academic requirements in the Faculty of Health Sciences. Although not directly exposed, they experienced vicarious trauma through extensive media coverage and by living in Israel during an ongoing national crisis and widespread societal stress, which likely intensified their indirect exposure. The third group (University students during 2022 before October 7, 2023) comprised 150 university students who were assessed in 2022 (pre–October 7) and completed an anonymous Qualtrics questionnaire between April and July 2022 as part of their academic coursework in the Faculty of Health Sciences. Measures

Reactions to Terror Exposure Indicators

Participants' reactions to exposure to traumatic terror events were assessed using a scale developed by Hamama‐Raz et al. (2008). The nine items comprise three subscales: (1) Subjective feeling of danger (three items; e.g., ‘I feel that my life is under threat’), (2) Perceived efficacy (two items assessing confidence in one's own or the authorities' ability to respond to a traumatic event), and (3) Avoidance behaviour (four items reflecting behavioural changes following exposure; e.g., ‘I avoid travelling to certain places as a result of the event’). Responses were rated on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Not at all’) to 5 (‘To a very large extent’). Previous research has shown that higher levels of objective or perceived exposure are associated with increased feelings of danger, greater avoidance, and reduced perceived efficacy, supporting the scale's validity as a sensitive measure of anxiety‐ and efficacy‐related responses in trauma‐exposed populations (Hamama‐Raz et al. 2008; Korn and Zukerman 2011; Zukerman et al. 2016; Zukerman et al. 2016).

Physical Activity Indices. Physical Activity Was Assessed Usingtwo Scales

Value Fitness Commitment (VFC)

This was assessed using two items designed to capture the personal importance attributed to physical fitness and the willingness to invest effort in maintaining it. Participants rated (a) how important it is for them to feel in good physical shape and (b) the extent to which they strive to remain physically fit even when it requires additional effort. Conceptually, this construct reflects a value‐based motivational orientation towards physical well‐being, consistent with Self‐Determination Theory's notion of identified and integrated regulation, in which health‐related behaviours are pursued because they are personally valued and internalized (Deci and Ryan 2000; Ryan and Deci 2017). In line with expectancy–value models emphasising subjective task value and effort investment (Eccles and Wigfield 2002), value fitness commitment represents the internalized importance of physical fitness and a readiness to engage in sustained, goal‐directed behaviour to maintain it, rather than actual activity levels. Items were rated on a 4‐point Likert scale from 1 (‘Not important at all’) to 4 (‘Very important’).

Weekly Time Spent in Vigorous Physical Activity (WPA)

This was assessed by asking participants to report the number of hours per week they engaged in vigorous exercise (e.g., activities causing breathlessness for at least 20 minutes), with six response options ranging from ‘Not at all’ to ‘More than 15 hours per week’.

Well‐Being Indices

Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS)

Subjective happiness was assessed using the Subjective Happiness Scale (SHS; Lyubomirsky and Lepper 1999), a four‐item measure of global, enduring happiness. The scale includes two 7‐point Likert items assessing overall happiness and happiness relative to others, and two items assessing similarity to happy or unhappy prototypes. The SHS demonstrates strong reliability and validity (Lyubomirsky et al. 2011). In this study, the sum of the first two items was used as the measure of subjective happiness.

Somatic Symptoms

Somatic symptoms were measured using the Health Behaviour in School‐aged Children (HBSC) symptom checklist (Ravens‐Sieberer et al. 2008), which has been validated for adult populations. Participants reported the average frequency of symptoms such as headaches, stomachaches, backaches, irritability, nervousness, sleep difficulties, and dizziness on a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘Rarely or never’) to 5 (‘About every day’). Since Nova survivors completed the questionnaires in January–February 2024—approximately two months after the October 2023 attack—we asked them to report their average somatic complaints during the previous two months in order to obtain data that accurately reflects somatic symptoms specifically since the traumatic event. Participants in the comparison groups reported average symptom frequency for the preceding 6 months.

Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS)

Posttraumatic stress symptoms were assessed using the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS; Foa 1995; Foa et al. 1997), a 49‐item self‐report measure based on DSM‐IV criteria. The 17 symptom items yield a total severity score across re‐experiencing, avoidance, and arousal clusters. The PDS shows excellent reliability and strong agreement with diagnostic interviews, including in trauma‐exposed samples. In the present study, the PDS was administered only to the directly exposed group. Several considerations support the use of the DSM‐IV–based PDS in the present study. First, empirical research indicates strong conceptual and psychometric continuity between DSM‐IV and DSM‐5 PTSD symptom structures, with high overlap in core domains and very strong correspondence between severity scores derived from the two systems (Friedman et al. 2013; Hoge et al. 2014; Moshier et al. 2019). The re‐experiencing, avoidance, and arousal clusters assessed by the PDS map closely onto DSM‐5 criteria, and studies comparing parallel instruments (e.g., PCL‐C and PCL‐5) show very high convergence and similar associations with external validators (Blevins et al. 2015; Wortmann et al. 2016). In addition, the DSM‐IV PDS was selected for design‐related reasons: a validated Hebrew version was available at the time of data collection and was administered across the broader project, ensuring methodological consistency and comparability between cohorts. This is particularly important in multi‐group observational designs, where maintaining measurement invariance across samples reduces method variance and supports unbiased estimation of group differences and moderation effects. Accordingly, the DSM‐IV PDS provides a reliable and valid dimensional index of post‐traumatic symptom severity for the present analyses.

Procedure

The study was approved by the University's Ethics Committee. Survivors of the terrorist attack at the Nova music festival were approached while receiving treatment at the Izun‐Nova intervention centre, in Israel. The centre specialises in supporting young people coping with crises, trauma, post‐trauma, emotional distress, or substance use from functional backgrounds. Its holistic model integrates mind‐body and emotional therapy, delivered through group, individual, and family sessions, alongside psychiatric and medical support. Participants were recruited on a rolling basis between February 2024 and June 2025, during the early stages of their engagement with the programme. Eligible participants were approached on‐site by a research assistant and invited to complete an anonymous, voluntary questionnaire via a secure online link. All participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the questionnaires.

The two comparison groups consisted of university students approached during their studies on campus. Questionnaires were administered online between April and July 2022 for the 2022 group and between February and July 2024 for the 2024 group (after October 7, 2023). A research assistant visited the classrooms, explained the study, and invited participants to join. Students who provided written consent were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could skip questions. They received a link to the Qualtrics‐based survey, which is accessible on mobile devices, personal computers, or tablets, with a printed version available upon request. All participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the questionnaires.

Data Analysis

Data analysis was conducted with SPSS 29 (IBM Corp., 2022). A repeated‐measures analysis of variance was conducted to assess group differences in Indices of reactions to Terror exposure indicators. Univariate analyses were conducted to evaluate group differences in Subjective Happiness (SHS) and Somatic complaints. Correlation analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between Physical Activity indices (VFC, WPA) and wellbeing (subjective happiness, somatic complaints and PTSD symptoms). The moderating effects of group affiliation on the association between Physical Activity (MPA, WPA) and subjective happiness and somatic complaints were tested using Hayes' PROCESS 5 computational procedures (A. F. Hayes 2022; A. F. Hayes and Matthes 2009). Hayes’ PROCESS macro allows for moderation analysis by estimating interaction effects between predictors and moderators. It provides conditional effects across moderator values, enabling rigorous testing of whether relationships between variables depend on a third factor. Age and family status were used as control variable in the moderation analyses. Consistent with the a priori hypothesis that the protective association would be stronger in direct trauma exposure (Nova survivors), a planned contrast comparing directly exposed participants with all others was conducted. This contrast was selected to test the theoretically derived prediction that the association between physical activity indices and well‐being outcomes would be strongest under conditions of direct trauma exposure.

Moderation Analysis

Value Fitnness Comitment (VFC)

A moderation analysis using Hayes' PROCESS macro (Model 1) examined whether trauma‐exposure group (Nova‐directly exposed, non‐exposed, vicariously exposed) moderated the association between value fitness commitment (VFC) and subjective happiness, controlling for family status and age. The overall model was significant, F (7, 200) = 5.54, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.16. Higher VFC was associated with greater subjective happiness, b = 0.49, SE = 0.23, p = 0.033; however, the interaction between VFC and exposure group was not significant, ΔR 2 = 0.008, F (2, 200) = 0.95, p = 0.390, indicating that this association did not differ across exposure levels. Family status was a significant covariate, b = 1.42, SE = 0.47, p = 0.003, whereas age was not.

A parallel three‐group moderation model predicting somatic complaints was also significant, F (7, 264) = 5.25, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.12. Higher VFC was associated with fewer somatic complaints, b = −1.31, SE = 0.50, p = 0.009. The overall interaction between VFC and exposure group was not statistically significant, ΔR 2 = 0.015, F (2, 264) = 2.22, p = 0.111; however, the coefficient comparing the directly exposed and non‐exposed groups was significant (b = 1.35, SE = 0.66, p = 0.043 (See Figure 3). Consistent with the a priori hypothesis that the protective association would be stronger in direct trauma exposure (Nova survivors), a planned contrast comparing directly exposed participants with all others was conducted. This two‐group moderation model was significant, F (3, 285) = 41.86, p < 0.001, R 2 = 0.31, and yielded a significant VFC × exposure interaction, ΔR 2 = 0.013, F (1, 285) = 5.38, p = 0.021. Simple slopes indicated that higher VFC was associated with fewer somatic complaints among directly exposed participants, b = −0.85, SE = 0.39, p = 0.031, whereas this association was not significant among non‐directly exposed participants, b = 0.23, SE = 0.25, p = 0.361. Although the observed moderation pattern was consistent with the hypothesised stress‐buffering framework, the additional variance explained by the interaction was relatively small (ΔR2 = 0.013). Therefore, the practical significance of this effect should be interpreted with caution pending replication in future research.

Hayes moderation analysis for the association VFC to Somatic Complaints.

Weekly Amount of Time Dedicated to Intense Physical Activity (WPA)

A moderation analysis was conducted using A. F. Hayes’ (2022) PROCESS macro (Model 1) to examine whether weekly physical activity (WPH) was associated with subjective happiness as a function of exposure group (Nova‐directly exposed, non‐exposed, vicariously exposed), controlling for family status and age. The overall model was significant, F (5, 201) = 2.32, p = 0.045, explaining 5.45% of the variance in subjective happiness (R 2 = 0.055). However, the interaction between weekly physical activity and exposure group was not significant, ΔR 2 = 0.001, F (1, 201) = 0.21, p = 0.644, indicating that the association between physical activity and subjective happiness did not differ across exposure groups. The main effects of WPA, b = 0.01, SE = 0.47, p = 0.986, and exposure group, b = −0.05, SE = 0.52, p = 0.919, were also not significant. Among the covariates, family status was positively associated with subjective happiness, b = 1.07, SE = 0.37, p = 0.004, whereas age was not a significant predictor, b = −0.02, SE = 0.03, p = 0.414.

A moderation analysis tested whether the association between WPA and somatic complaints differed by group affiliation, controlling for family status and age. The overall model was significant, F (7, 234) = 17.31, p < 0.001, explaining 34.1% of the variance. WPA had a significant negative main effect (b = −2.03, p = 0.010), indicating fewer somatic complaints with higher activity levels. Both the 2022 (b = −12.47, p < 0.001) and 2024 (b = −9.44, p < 0.001) groups reported fewer somatic complaints than Nova Survivors. The interaction between WPA and group affiliation was nonsignificant.

Discussion

The present findings indicate a significant association between physical activity–related constructs and well‐being, particularly following mass trauma. Value Fitness Commitment (VFC)—the personal importance attributed to being physically fit and the willingness to invest effort in maintaining it—was associated with greater subjective happiness and fewer somatic complaints among Nova survivors, whereas weekly vigorous physical activity (WPA) was related to fewer somatic complaints across the entire sample and to lower PTSD symptom severity within the directly exposed group. These results suggest that value‐based engagement in physical fitness is particularly relevant for psychological well‐being under severe trauma exposure, while actual participation in vigorous activity shows a broader association with somatic aspects of well‐being.

Several explanations may account for these findings. The inverse association between VFC and somatic complaints can be understood within the Self‐Determination Theory (SDT) sequence linking intrinsic goal content to well‐being through autonomous motivation and basic psychological need satisfaction. Attributing personal importance to being physically fit reflects an internalized health goal that fosters autonomous regulation and perceived bodily competence and agency (Deci and Ryan 2000; Ryan and Deci 2002). Longitudinal evidence shows that intrinsic goal content predicts increases in autonomous motivation and need satisfaction, which in turn relate to greater vitality, higher positive affect, and lower ill‐being (Gunnell et al. 2014; Mack et al., 2012; Sylvester et al. 2012). Because need fulfilment directly energises adaptive functioning and reduces distress (Ryan and Deci 2000, 2017), stronger VFC may be linked to fewer somatic complaints and greater subjective happiness through enhanced embodied competence and more regulated physiological and interoceptive processes, even without higher activity levels.

The negative association between weekly physical activity (WPA) and somatic complaints, found in this study, is consistent with extensive evidence linking regular activity to better physical and psychological well‐being. Population‐based and meta‐analytic studies show that more active individuals report fewer mental health symptoms, better self‐rated health, and lower overall symptom burden (Biddle and Asare 2011; Rebar et al. 2015; White et al. 2017), and prospective findings indicate reduced physical complaints over time (Hamer et al. 2009). These effects are commonly attributed to improved stress regulation and perceived physical functioning, suggesting that higher weekly physical activity is reflected in lower somatic distress.

From a theoretical perspective, the differential pattern between VFC and WPA highlights the distinction between motivational–value and behavioural components of physical activity. VFC reflects an internalized, self‐determined orientation towards health behaviour (Deci and Ryan 2000; Ryan and Deci 2017) that is closely tied to perceived control and adaptive coping, which may be particularly important for emotional well‐being when regulatory capacities are challenged by trauma (Robinson and Lachman 2018). In contrast, WPA represents the enacted behavioural component, which has been consistently associated with reductions in somatic and posttraumatic symptoms through psychophysiological and affect‐regulation mechanisms (Schuch and Stubbs 2019). Accordingly, the motivational and behavioural dimensions of physical activity may contribute to wellbeing through partially distinct pathways.

The findings also indicate that WPA was associated with less PTSD symptoms among Nova survivors. Intensive WPA may reduce PTSD symptoms through several complementary mechanisms. First, WPA repeatedly induces trauma‐relevant interoceptive cues (e.g., elevated heart rate, rapid breathing) in a safe and controllable context, thereby functioning as an exposure‐like process that promotes fear extinction, reduces catastrophic misinterpretations of bodily sensations, and weakens conditioned threat responses (Fetzner and Asmundson 2015; Powers et al. 2015; Rauch and Foa 2006). Second, regular activity contributes to autonomic recalibration by lowering baseline sympathetic activation and enhancing parasympathetic recovery, which may directly attenuate hyperarousal and improve stress reactivity—core features of PTSD (Hegberg et al. 2019; Kokkinos and Myers 2010; Yehuda et al. 2015). Third, WPA may counteract the maintenance cycle of PTSD by reducing behavioural and experiential avoidance, increasing approach behaviour and environmental reward, and restoring a sense of agency and functional engagement in daily life (Gros et al. 2016; S. C. Hayes et al. 2006).

The observed moderation effect in this study is consistent with our stress‐buffering hypothesis, which was based on the premise that the health‐protective role of WPA would be most evident under conditions of elevated stress and physiological burden. In line with stress‐buffering models (Cohen and Wills 1985; McEwen 1998), VFC was associated with fewer somatic complaints only among directly exposed Nova survivors, for whom trauma‐related hyperarousal, body vigilance, and symptom sensitivity likely increased the need for effective self‐regulatory resources (van der Kolk 2014). In this context, strong fitness values may motivate engagement in restorative and regulating behaviours, facilitate more adaptive interoceptive interpretations, and attenuate autonomic reactivity, whereas when exposure and distress are lower the buffering function of such values is less likely to be mobilised. Beyond this regulatory pathway, the findings also align with meaning‐based and psychological flexibility models suggesting that commitment to personally important values can support post‐traumatic adaptation by restoring agency, continuity of the self, and goal‐directed action despite distress (S. C. Hayes et al. 2006; Park 2010). Such values‐driven engagement may counteract helplessness and experiential avoidance while promoting behavioural activation, processes that are known to reduce both PTSD‐related distress and somatic symptom amplification. Accordingly, VFC appears to function as a context‐dependent motivational resource whose protective effects emerge primarily when trauma has substantially disrupted physiological regulation and self‐structure, as hypothesised in the introduction. Notably, although our hypothesis pertained to all physical activity measures, the moderation effect emerged only for VFC, reflecting the more motivational, value‐based aspect of physical activity.

The findings extend prior research in two important ways. First, while most studies have examined physical activity and well‐being in clinical or general community samples (e.g., Nilsson et al. 2021; SantaBarbara et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2024), few have done so in the context of a real‐world, large‐scale terror event. Research on trauma has primarily focused on combat veterans or post‐disaster recovery (Kitchiner et al. 2019; Rosenbaum et al. 2015), with limited evidence from civilian mass‐trauma contexts (North and Pfefferbaum 2013). Second, although prior studies suggest that individuals with higher trauma exposure may benefit more from physical activity (Schuch and Stubbs 2019; White et al. 2024; Nilsson et al. 2021; SantaBarbara et al. 2025), this study is the first to simultaneously compare directly exposed, vicariously exposed, and non‐exposed groups using consistent measures. This design enables a more nuanced examination of how trauma severity moderates the benefits of physical activity. With regard to physical activity as an augmentation strategy, recent research suggests that physical activity may serve as a valuable augmentation to evidence‐based psychotherapies for PTSD, particularly exposure‐based and cognitive‐behavioural. Studies indicate that adding aerobic exercise to exposure‐based treatment can enhance reductions in PTSD symptoms (Powers et al. 2015), while resistance training has been associated with improvements in PTSD‐related symptoms, including avoidance, hyperarousal, and sleep difficulties (Whitworth et al. 2019). Consistent with these findings, a recent systematic review concluded that exercise‐augmented psychotherapy shows promising effects on PTSD symptom reduction, although further high‐quality trials are needed to establish optimal intervention protocols (Niemeyer et al. 2025). The present study extends this emerging literature in two important ways. First, whereas previous intervention studies have primarily been conducted in clinical treatment settings, the current study examined the role of physical activity within a real‐world context of ongoing trauma exposure, including individuals directly and indirectly affected by the October 7th attacks. Second, our findings suggest that not only actual engagement in physical activity, but also motivational factors related to physical activity—specifically Value Fitness Commitment (VFC)—may be associated with psychological well‐being. These findings broaden current conceptualizations of physical activity as a protective factor in PTSD and highlight the potential importance of both behavioural and motivational pathways in promoting resilience following trauma.

Before considering the clinical implications of these findings, it is important to note that Nova survivors were recruited while participating in the Izun support programme, and some may have received additional psychological or psychiatric support. Because treatment participation was not systematically assessed or controlled, the present findings should be interpreted as demonstrating associations between physical activity indices and well‐being rather than evidence that physical activity alone was responsible for lower PTSD symptoms or improved psychological functioning. Clinically, the findings suggest that actual engagement in physical activity may alleviate somatic complaints and PTSD symptoms, whereas motivation and commitment to physical activity may promote broader well‐being, reflected in fewer somatic complaints and greater subjective happiness following trauma exposure. Accordingly, in addition to standard PTSD treatments such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), incorporating psychoeducation on the benefits of regular intensive physical activity may improve therapeutic outcomes. Meta‐analytic findings suggest that physical activity can augment the effects of psychotherapy, although additional research is required before its routine integration into treatment protocols can be recommended (Björkman and Ekblom 2022; Martínez‐Calderón et al. 2024; Whitworth et al. 2019). Given its low cost and high accessibility, physical activity may be particularly advantageous for individuals with high levels of trauma exposure. Furthermore, motivation to engage in physical activity may constitute a meaningful personal resource, underscoring the potential value of structured, guided, or peer‐led exercise programs in both clinical and community trauma‐focused settings.

The study has several strengths, including the comparison of three groups representing distinct trauma exposure levels, the use of validated measures (PDS, SHS, HBSC), and a naturalistic design following a large‐scale terror event, enhancing ecological validity. However, limitations include the cross‐sectional design, reliance on self‐report, demographic group differences, and a modest sample size. Additionally, the assessment of motivation for physical activity (VFC) and weekly level of actual physical activity (WPA) was based on only two and one items, respectively. The use of such brief measures may have increased measurement error and consequently attenuated the observed effect sizes, suggesting that the reported associations may represent conservative estimates of the underlying relationships. Nevertheless, significant associations were detected despite these psychometric limitations, providing preliminary support for the robustness of the links between physical activity indices and well‐being outcomes. An additional limitation is that participants in the direct trauma exposure group (Nova survivors) were recruited through the Izum support programme during their active participation in the programme. Although ongoing support processes may have influenced the observed associations between physical activity and well‐being, available data suggest considerable variability in the type of support received. Among the 46 survivors who provided this information, support groups were the most commonly reported activity, whereas only 37.0% reported individual treatment. Nevertheless, because support participation was not systematically assessed or controlled, its potential influence cannot be ruled out, and caution is warranted when generalising the findings to trauma‐exposed individuals not engaged in a support programs. Additionaly, detailed information regarding prior psychiatric difficulties and trauma history was not available across all study groups. Although limited information regarding previous mental health crises was collected among Nova survivors, comparable data were not obtained for the other groups and therefore could not be incorporated into the primary analyses. Notably, among Nova survivors, only one participant reported a previous psychiatric hospitalisation, suggesting that severe psychiatric morbidity was relatively uncommon within this subgroup. Future studies would benefit from systematically assessing psychiatric and trauma history across all participants to further evaluate their potential contribution to physical activity and well‐being outcomes. Finaly, an additional limitation concerns the assessment of somatic complaints, which referred to the preceding 2 months among Nova survivors but to the preceding 6 months among participants in the comparison groups. Although this difference in reporting windows may have influenced observed group differences in somatic complaints, it is noteworthy that the directly exposed group reported higher levels of somatic complaints despite the shorter recall period. Nevertheless, the potential impact of this methodological difference cannot be ruled out and should be considered when interpreting group comparisons. Future research would benefit from employing more comprehensive, validated multi‐item measures of both motivational and behavioural physical activity, as well as longitudinal designs, larger and more balanced samples, and objective activity measures to clarify causal pathways and optimise intervention parameters.
