Debiased Counterfactual Generation via Flow Matching from Observations
Hugh Dance, Johnny Xi, Peter Orbanz, Benjamin Bloem-Reddy

Abstract
Estimating counterfactual distributions under interventions is central to treatment risk assessment and counterfactual generation tasks. Existing approaches model the counterfactual distribution as a standalone generative target, without exploiting its relationship to the observational data. In this work, we show that under standard assumptions, observational and counterfactual outcome distributions are tightly linked: they have identical support and tail behavior, remain statistically close under weak confounding, and share any features of high-dimensional outcomes which are invariant to confounders. These properties motivate learning counterfactual distributions not from scratch, but via a deconfounding flow from the observational distribution. We formulate this problem via flow-matching and derive a semiparametrically efficient estimator based on a novel efficient influence function correction. We subsequently extend our estimator to target minimal-energy flows in high-dimensions, which we show can be especially simple targets between observational and counterfactual distributions. In experiments, deconfounding flows outperform existing debiased counterfactual distribution estimators, while also mitigating known failure modes of flow-based methods.

Introduction

Estimating how the distribution of an outcome
Y
would change under a
hypothetical intervention on a binary variable
A
is a central problem in causal
inference. In economic policy analysis, for example,
A
may
represent eligibility for a policy or program participation, and one may ask how the distribution of incomes
Y
would
change if the whole population were assigned
A=a

(
9
;
13
;
5
)
. In generative modeling, analogous counterfactual questions arise when sampling
high-dimensional outputs
Y
with a specified semantic attribute
A=a
, while
preventing generated samples from inheriting dataset-specific correlations with
nuisance attributes
X
that may harm controllability or representational
fairness
(
34
;
6
;
4
)
.

Such counterfactual distributions of outcomes are challenging to estimate, as one usually has access only to confounded or otherwise biased observational samples.
An emerging framework addresses this problem by using causal identification theory and semiparametric debiasing techniques to adapt generative models to train on observational data, while preserving asymptotic efficiency and consistency under general conditions
(
17
;
25
;
24
;
23
)
. Such approaches have proven effective, but so far treat the counterfactual distribution as an unstructured generative target, relying on generic model classes that do not adapt to the geometry of the observed data.

In this work, we take a different modeling strategy: rather than using the observational distribution purely as a source of training data for counterfactual distributions, we use
it
as the starting point for the model itself. Our approach is motivated by the fact that the target counterfactual
distribution,
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, and the observational conditional distribution,
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
, can both be represented as mixtures of the same conditional outcome distributions—differing only in
their covariate mixing distributions. As we later show, this induces identical
support and tail behavior under standard positivity assumptions, statistical closeness under weak confounding, and shared
feature-embeddings for any features which are invariant to variation
in confounders or nuisance variables
X
. Accordingly, rather than modeling
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
from
scratch, we propose to learn a
“deconfounding flow”

f_{a}
that transports
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
to
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, which we instantiate via flow
matching
(
21
)
.

\mathrm{HairColor}=\mathrm{Blonde}

While our idea is conceptually simple, it creates challenges for efficient estimation. Unlike previous counterfactual generative estimators, our learning objective depends on the data distribution through the model itself. This requires us to derive a new efficient influence function for debiased estimation, and to non-trivially extend the standard efficiency results to handle V-processes. Despite this, through careful estimator design, our method is similarly practical to implement as previous debiased methods.

As we show theoretically and empirically, learning a transport from

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
can mitigate known learning difficulties in flow-based
models that arise from mismatched support, tail behavior, or multimodality of
the base distribution. Our approach is also very effective for
high-dimensional images
Y
, where
X
may be a confounder or spurious
correlate of
A
that affects only structured aspects of the image, such as
color, texture, or lighting, while leaving semantic content intact.
In cases where one wishes to generate samples with a specified attribute

A=a
while removing any spurious association with
X
, a deconfounding
flow can reuse the observed sample structure and learn only the edits needed to rebalance the distribution of
X
, rather
than synthesizing
Y
from scratch.
Figure

1
illustrates this for an image-rebalancing task on CelebA, where
A=\mathrm{Sex}
and

X=\mathrm{HairColor}
.

"deconfounding flows"

3.

4.

Background and Related Work

Basic Assumptions and Notation.

Unless otherwise stated, all measurable spaces are standard Borel. For a random
variable
X
, let
\mathbb{P}_{X}
denote its law and
\text{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
its support. For a measurable map

, we write
f_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{X}
for

. We write
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x}

conditional law of

given

, and abbreviate

to

2.1
Counterfactual Distributions under Binary Interventions

Let
A\in\{0,1\}
be a binary variable of interest such that
\mathbb{P}_{A}(a)>0
for each
a\in\{0,1\}
,

X\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
observed covariates,

Y\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
an outcome, and
\mathbb{P}_{X,A,Y}
denote their joint observational distribution. In this work, we are interested in estimating the
counterfactual distribution of outcomes
Y
that would arise under a regime that fixes
A=a
while keeping the distribution of
X
unchanged.

In the classical causal setting,

A
is a treatment (e.g., medication),
X
are pre-treatment confounders, and one is interested
in the distribution of potential outcomes
Y(a)
that would arise if the whole
population were assigned
A=a
. Under the usual assumptions of (i) consistency:
Y=Y(A)
; (ii) conditional
exchangeability:
Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid X
; and (iii) positivity:

\exists\epsilon>0:\mathbb{P}(A=\cdot\mid X=x)\geq\epsilon,\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e., this distribution is identified by the formula
(
29
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(\cdot)=\textstyle\int\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(\cdot)\,d\mathbb{P}_{X}(x).

(1)

The same distribution also arises in
generative settings where
Y
is a high-dimensional output (e.g., an image),

A
is a semantic attribute to control, and
X
is a correlated nuisance
attribute. In such cases the causal relation between
X
and
A
may not always be clear, and/or
X
may not capture all confounders, but one may still want to ensure generated
samples under
A=a
do not inherit any empirical association with
X
,
either to reduce spurious dataset correlations or for representational fairness (e.g., when
X
is a sensitive attribute)
(
6
)
. For clarity, we define
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
by (
1
) and refer to it as the counterfactual
distribution throughout, while noting that in some generative settings it
is better interpreted as the distribution of outcomes under a counterfactual regime where
\mathbb{P}_{A,X}
is replaced by
\delta_{a}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{X}
, while preserving

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,A}
, rather than under a causal intervention on
A
.

2.2
Existing Methods for Counterfactual Distribution Estimation

A straightforward plug-in approach to learning the counterfactual distribution is
to estimate
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,A}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X}
from observational data
with flexible models, then obtain
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
via (
1
)

(
20
;
26
;
31
;
30
;
16
;
8
)
.
However, this approach can inherit bias from estimating
{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X,A}
, which may be substantial when
X
is
high-dimensional. Another classical strategy is inverse-propensity weighting
(IPW), which reweights
observed outcomes by the inverse of the propensity score
\pi_{a}(x):=\mathbb{P}(A=a|X=x)
. However, IPW is known to be sensitive to weak overlap
(
7
)
, which can lead to poor practical performance. It also does not yield a generative model for the counterfactual distribution.

More recently, a line of work has used techniques from semiparametric theory to directly target generative models for

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}

(
17
;
25
;
24
;
23
)
. The basic idea is to specify
a generative model class
\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{a}}:\theta_{a}\in\Theta\}
and define
the target parameter as the projection of
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
onto this class via a
discrepancy
\mathcal{D}
. This yields a first-order optimality condition of the form

m(\theta_{a,0}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbb{E}[g(Y;\theta_{a,0})\mid A=a,X]\right]=0,

where
g
is typically the gradient of the chosen loss. Based on semiparametric theory, this moment condition is estimated using its efficient influence
function (EIF). The resulting estimator for
m
uses both an outcome nuisance,

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A,X}
, and a propensity nuisance,
\hat{\pi}_{a}(x)
. However, unlike plug-in or IPW estimators, the estimator is consistent if either nuisance estimator converges (i.e., is “doubly robust”) and can achieve the asymptotic semiparametric efficiency bound
(
17
)
. As such, this framework provides the current state-of-the-art for efficient, generative
counterfactual distribution estimation. Further details on this framework and implementations are in
Section

A.1
.

3
Deconfounding Flows from Observational to Counterfactual Distributions

Rather than modeling the counterfactual distribution “from scratch”, the central idea in this work is to learn counterfactual distributions (targets) via a “deconfounding” flow
f_{a}
from the observed conditional distribution (source),

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}=(f_{a})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}.

The key observation underlying this approach is that both
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
are mixtures over the
same family of conditional outcome distributions, with their mixing weights differing only through the degree of dependence between
X
and
A
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(\cdot)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\int\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(\cdot)\,d\mathbb{P}_{X}(x),\qquad\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(\cdot)=\textstyle\int\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(\cdot)\,d\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(x)\;.

As we detail below, this common structure induces several shared properties and statistical similarities between source and target, with direct implications for the simplicity of deconfounding transports.

3.1
Shared Tails and Support Under Positivity

We start by establishing basic properties shared by the source and target distributions under the usual positivity assumption in causal inference, and that are beneficial for learning flows between them.

Theorem 3.1

(Shared Support and Tail Class)
.

Assume
\exists\epsilon>0
such that

\pi_{a}(x)\geq\epsilon
for all
a\in\{0,1\}
,

\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e. Then,
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y|a}
share the same support and tail-class: for each
a\in\{0,1\}
,

1.

\text{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)})=\text{supp}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a})

2.

For any measurable
s:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\mathbb{E}[s(Y(a))]<\infty\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\mathbb{E}[s(Y)\mid A=a]<\infty.

Top: illustrative examples of different conditional and counterfactual distributions

(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=1},\mathbb{P}_{Y(1)})
. Although covariate overlap is weak (top left),

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(1)}
share the same support and tails (as predicted by

), and are statistically much closer than the covariate mixing distributions (as predicted by (

Shared support and tails are important properties to share in the context of flow-based learning, as they are precisely the properties that popular flow-based methods using smooth diffeomorphisms from a fixed base distribution struggle or are unable to adapt.

In particular, it is known that no Lipschitz transport can generate heavier tails than its base distribution
(
15
)
, no continuous transport can split mass into disconnected support components, and no invertible transport can map between distributions supported on sets of different dimension. Since these properties need not be adapted when flowing between
\mathbb{P}_{Y|a}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, learning a deconfounding flow can therefore avoid mis-specification limitations of standard flow-based methods, including those used for counterfactual distributions
(
25
;
23
)
.
Figure
2
illustrates several toy examples where these
geometric constraints impede transporting from
\mathcal{N}(0,I)
, but not from
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
.

3.2
Statistical Similarity Under Mild/Smooth Confounding

The statistical distance between
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
can also be bounded by a notion of confounding strength. In particular, since
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
are mixtures of the same components, their Total Variation (TV) distance can be bounded using the
Dobrushin Inequality

(
10
)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}(

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\mathbb{P}_{Y|a})\leq\text{sup}_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{Y|x,a},\mathbb{P}_{Y|x^{\prime},a})\cdot\mathrm{TV}(\mathbb{P}_{X},\mathbb{P}_{X|a}).

(2)

Thus, the discrepancy between
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}

is small whenever either the association between
A
and
X
is weak, or
the conditional outcome law varies little with
X
within the stratum

A=a
. Since each TV term is at most one, the product bound is also no
larger than either component individually. This contraction is illustrated in

Figure

2
, where the outcome distributions are
substantially closer than their covariate mixing distributions. Under stronger regularity, we can also bound the size of the quadratic-cost optimal transport map
(
32
)
between these distributions.

Theorem 3.2

(Brenier Map Displacement)
.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
. Assume: (i)
\mathbb{P}_{Y|a}
is absolutely continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
; and (ii)

W_{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid x,a},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid x^{\prime},a}\right)\;\leq\;L_{a}\,\|x-x^{\prime}\|_{2},

for every
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then the (quadratic cost) Brenier map
f_{a}
transporting
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
to

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
exists and satisfies

\displaystyle\|f_{a}-\mathrm{id}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}\;\leq\;L_{a}\,W_{2}\left(\mathbb{P}_{X},\,\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}\right).

(3)

Thus, under
W_{2}
-Lipschitz outcome variation, covariate shift directly controls
the size of the optimal deconfounding transport. If

\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{X\mid A=a}
are close, or the conditional outcome law
varies slowly with
X
, then
f_{a}
is near-identity. At a high level, estimating simpler functions is statistically easier than estimating complicated ones. This motivates targeting
low-energy or optimal-transport deconfounding flows, as developed in

Section

4.3
.

3.3
Shared Confounder-Invariant Features

The shared mixture structure can also preserve important features of

Y
that are insensitive to confounders
X
. Let
\phi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
be a measurable feature map. We say that

\phi(Y)
is
confounder-invariant
in arm
a
if

\phi_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}=Q_{a}\qquad\mathbb{P}_{X}\text{-a.e. }x.

For example, in the image-based settings discussed above,

X
may affect nuisance visual attributes such as color, texture, lighting,
or background, while leaving much of the image geometry unchanged. In this
case,
\phi
encodes invariant structure—for
instance, object shape, pose, identity, edge placement, or class-relevant
semantic content. The following result formalizes that every
confounder-invariant feature has the same distribution under

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, and under transportability assumptions, there is a transport between them that preserves this feature pointwise.

Theorem 3.3

(Feature-preserving deconfounding)
.

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and let
\phi:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Z}
be measurable and confounder-invariant. Then

\phi_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}=\phi_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}=Q_{a}

Moreover, if there exist a jointly measurable family of maps
\{T_{z}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}\mid z\in\mathcal{Z}\}
such that

(T_{z})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a,\phi(Y)=z}=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)\mid\phi(Y(a))=z}
for
Q_{a}
-a.e.
z
, then there is a map
f_{a}:\mathcal{Y}\to\mathcal{Y}
such that

(f_{a})_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(f_{a}(Y))=\phi(Y)\quad\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}\text{-a.s.}

One implication of this result is that when confounders or nuisance attributes
X
affect only
certain aspects of an image-based outcome, a deconfounding flow can in principle correct the correlated attributes while preserving shared
image-level
structure or content. Thus the learning problem is closer to structured editing than generation from scratch. As we later show in experiments, deconfounding flows perform especially well in such settings, even with simple model architectures.

4
Debiased Flow-Matching for Deconfounding Flows

Having laid out the theoretical motivation for deconfounding flows
f_{a}
from
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}

to
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, we now derive an efficient estimator of such a transport using observational data
(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}{\sim}_{iid}\mathbb{P}
, via flow matching
(
21
)
. In what follows, we specify the model parameterization and
population objective, derive a debiased estimator for the gradient of
the objective, and prove statistical efficiency. The key distinction from previous debiased generative
estimators is that our target functional for estimation depends on the data through the model itself, via the flow base. This requires us to derive a new efficient influence function
correction for debiasing, and to establish new results that extend the technical devices used to prove statistical efficiency.

4.1
Velocity Parameterization and Flow-Matching Objective

Following standard flow-matching, we model the deconfounding flow
f_{a}
as the time-
1
solution of an ODE with velocity field
v_{\theta_{a}}:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{Y}
parameterized by
\theta_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\displaystyle dy_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{\theta_{a}}(y_{t},t)dt,\quad t\in[0,1],\qquad f_{a}^{\theta}(y):=y+\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}v_{\theta_{a}}(y_{t},t)\,dt,\qquad y_{0}=y.

The target velocity field is the minimizer
of the usual conditional flow-matching objective,

\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta_{a}):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\bigl\|v_{\theta_{a}}(\phi_{t}(Y_{0},Y_{1}),t)-\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(Y_{0},Y_{1})\bigr\|^{2}\Big]\,.

(4)

Here
Y_{0}\sim\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
,
Y_{1}\sim\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
,

t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
, and
\phi_{t}(y_{0},y_{1})=y_{0}(1-t)+y_{1}(t)
is a linear interpolation
path between
y_{0},y_{1}
with time derivative
\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(y_{0},y_{1})=y_{1}-y_{0}
. Minimizing (
4
) is known to target a velocity
v^{*}

whose induced flow transports
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
to
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, thus providing a natural, likelihood-free objective for deconfounding flows.

4.2
Debiased Estimation via Novel EIF Correction

We now derive a debiased estimator of the gradient of (
4
) for optimization, using the semiparametric estimation framework discussed in
Section

2
and
Section

A.1
. Under (
1
), the gradient of (
4
) can be expressed as the moment functional

\displaystyle m_{a}(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\!\left[g_{\theta_{a}}(Y)\mid A=a,X\right]\right],

(5)

where
g_{\theta_{a}}
is an integral of the flow-matching loss score under the source
\mathbb{P}_{Y|a}
,

g_{\theta_{a}}(y):=\textstyle\int r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y}),\qquad r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\!:=\!\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}\nabla_{\theta_{a}}\|v_{\theta_{a}}(\phi_{t}(y,\tilde{y}),t)-\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(y,\tilde{y})\|^{2}\,dt.

A naive plug-in estimator for (
5
) first estimates

\mu_{\theta_{a}}(x):=\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta_{a}}(Y)\mid A=a,X=x]

and averages
\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}(X_{i})
over the empirical covariate distribution.
To remove the resulting first-order bias in this estimator that arises from estimation of
\mu_{\theta_{a}}
, we instead use the usual
one-step correction via an estimator of the efficient influence function (EIF)
\varphi_{\theta_{a}}
of
m(\theta_{a},\cdot)
. The resulting estimator is

\hat{m}(\theta_{a})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}(a,X_{i})+\hat{\varphi}_{\theta_{a},a}(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})\Bigr)\;.

(6)

In previous debiased generative estimators,
g_{\theta}
is not a functional of the data distribution
\mathbb{P}
, and so the EIF
\varphi_{\theta_{a}}
takes the usual “AIPW” form given in
Section

A.1
. However, in our case
g_{\theta_{a}}
depends on
\mathbb{P}
through

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
. Thus, the EIF takes a different form and needs to be derived from scratch.

Theorem 4.1

(EIF for Deconfounded Flow-Matching)
.

Let
\mu_{\theta_{a}}(x)=\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta_{a}}(Y)\mid A=a,X=x]

and
\chi_{\theta_{a}}(y):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[r_{\theta_{a}}(Y,y)\mid A=a,X]]
. Then, under the overlap and
integrability conditions in Assumption
D.10
in
Appendix

, the EIF for
m_{a}(\theta_{a})
is

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta_{a}}(\tilde{x},\tilde{a},\tilde{y})\!=

\displaystyle\frac{\bm{1}_{\{\tilde{a}\!=\!a\}}}{\pi_{a}(\tilde{x})}\!\bigl(g_{\theta_{a}}\!(\tilde{y})-\mu_{\theta_{a}}\!(\tilde{x})\bigr)+\mu_{\theta_{a}}\!(\tilde{x})-m_{a}(\theta_{a})+\frac{\bm{1}_{\{\tilde{a}\!=\!a\}}}{\mathbb{P}_{A}(a)}\bigl(\chi_{\theta_{a}}\!(\tilde{y})\!-\!\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\theta_{a}}\!(Y)|A\!=\!a]\bigr)

The first two terms are the usual EIF correction when

is fixed; the final
\chi_{\theta_{a}}
-terms are new and
account for the dependence on the estimated base
distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
.

A Practical Estimator for Optimization

The standard approach to estimating (
6
) is to (i) estimate
\mu_{\theta_{a}}
and the components of
\varphi_{\theta_{a}}
on one half of the data, and (ii) substitute these estimators into (
6
) and sum over the other half of the data. Naively,
this would require repeatedly estimating several
\theta_{a}

\hat{\pi}_{a},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A,X},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{A},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}
which do not depend on
\theta_{a}
, and then estimating
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{A},\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}
via the empirical measures on
the

used to evaluate (
6

\hat{g}_{\theta_{a}}(y):=\textstyle\int r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}(d\tilde{y}),\qquad\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}(x):=\textstyle\int\hat{g}_{\theta_{a}}(y)\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a,X=x}(dy),

the resulting one-step estimator (
6
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\hat{m}(\theta_{a})\!:=\!\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{1}_{\{A_{i}=a\}}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\!\!\int\!\!r_{\theta_{a}}(Y_{i},\tilde{y})\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y})+\Bigl(1\!-\!\frac{\bm{1}_{\{A_{i}=a\}}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\Bigr)\!\!\!\int\!\!r_{\theta_{a}}(\hat{y},\tilde{y})\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}(d\hat{y})\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y})\Bigg]

The benefit of outsourcing estimation to components independent of
\theta_{a}
is that

\hat{m}(\theta_{a})
can be approximated for any
\theta_{a}
using Monte Carlo with minibatches of samples from
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a,X}
and

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
, and hence can be used for stochastic gradient descent
(see Algorithm
2
in
Appendix

C
). The benefit of using the evaluation fold for

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{A}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
is that the additional
\chi_{\theta_{a}}
-type nuisance terms appearing in
the full EIF cancel empirically, and so do not need estimating in practice.

Efficiency of Estimator

By using the same data for estimating
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{A}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
and evaluating the moment, one cannot use the usual sample-splitting
arguments for statistical efficiency of debiased estimators under fully non-parametric nuisance models
(
18
, e.g.,)
. However, because
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{A}
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
are empirical measures, we can rewrite the estimator as a
collection of V-processes. By carefully extending the usual proof techniques to handle such processes (see
Section

D.1
), in Theorem
D.14
we recover the usual
asymptotic expansion,

\displaystyle\hat{m}(\theta_{a})-m(\theta_{a})=O_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2})\;+O_{\mathbb{P}}\big(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}+\Delta_{1})+\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\Delta_{1}\big)\;,

where
\Delta_{1}:=\|W_{1}\!\big(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a,x}\big)\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}
, and the leading term is from the mean-zero empirical average of the EIF.
Hence, our estimator retains the usual double robustness and efficiency
properties: it is consistent if either
\hat{\pi}_{a}
or

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a,X}
is consistent while the other is bounded, and
attains the semiparametric efficiency bound whenever

\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\Delta_{1}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-1/2}).

Nuisance Model Estimation

In the spirit of deconfounding flows, we estimate
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}
via a flow from
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
, using standard flow-matching for conditional distributions. Although
{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
do not necessarily share the same structural similarities analyzed in
Section

3
, they do share the same tails and support whenever these properties of
{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}
are not affected by
x
, and are closer in distance than the supremum term of the Dobrushin bound in
Section

3.2
. The propensity model
\hat{\pi}_{a}
can be estimated with any probabilistic classifier. Further details on nuisance estimation are in
Appendix

B
.

4.3
Extension for Targeting OT Flows In Higher Dimensions

In one dimension (
\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
), the population objective
(
4
) targets the velocity
v^{*}

whose induced time-1 flow coincides with the quadratic cost OT map
f_{a}
studied in
Section

3.2
. Moreover, its kinetic energy
E(v^{*})
corresponds to the transport cost,

E(v^{*}):=\textstyle\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int\|v_{t}(y)\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{t}(dy)\,dt=\|f_{a}-\mathrm{id}\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}.

This energy is minimal among all velocities with flows that transport
P_{0}:=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
to
P_{1}:=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
.
Thus, a “near-identity” deconfounding OT flow (e.g., under structured or mild confounding) implies a “near-zero” velocity, and regularity properties of
f_{a}
are known to directly carry over to
v^{*}
. For example, if
f_{a}
is bi-Lipschitz, then
f_{t}
is bi-Lipschitz and
v_{t}
is Lipschitz
(
2
)
.

In higher dimensions, however, this equivalence only holds when replacing the
independent coupling
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
in
(
4
) by the OT
coupling
\gamma
between
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
.

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\gamma}(\theta_{a}):=\!\!\!\!\!\underset{(Y_{0},Y_{1})\sim\gamma}{\mathbb{E}}\!\bigl\|v_{\theta_{a}}(\phi(Y_{0},Y_{1}),t)-\partial_{t}\phi(Y_{0},Y_{1})\bigr\|^{2}

(7)

Therefore, we extend the estimator of
Section

4.2
to target such OT flows in higher dimensions, as an additional implementation option.
To do so, we write the coupling
\gamma
that defines (
7
) as
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}\otimes\gamma_{0\mid 1}
, with
\gamma_{0\mid 1}
the conditional distribution of
Y_{0}\mid Y_{1}
. For any fixed
\gamma_{0\mid 1}
, we derive a debiased estimator of
\nabla_{\theta_{a}}\mathcal{L}_{a}^{\gamma}(\theta_{a})
using the equivalent EIF, resulting in an analogous gradient estimator for optimization,

\displaystyle\hat{m}(\theta_{a}):=\frac{1}{n}\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\Bigg[\frac{\bm{1}_{\{A_{i}=a\}}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\!\!\int\!\!r_{\theta_{a}}\!(Y_{i},\tilde{y})\gamma_{0\mid 1}\!(d\tilde{y}|Y_{i})\!+\!\Bigl(\!1\!-\!\frac{\bm{1}_{\{A_{i}=a\}}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\Bigr)\!\!\!\int\!\!r_{\theta_{a}}\!(\hat{y},\tilde{y})\gamma_{0\mid 1}\!(d\tilde{y}|\hat{y})\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}(d\hat{y})\Bigg]

The independent-coupling estimator of
Section

4.2
is recovered by taking

\gamma_{0\mid 1}(\cdot\mid y)=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}
. Here we instead propose to estimate
\gamma_{0\mid 1}
via an entropic-OT coupling between

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a}
and a plug-in estimate of
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
, recomputed on
minibatches using Sinkhorn’s algorithm
(
33
)
. To reduce sensitivity to plug-in misspecification, one can optionally tilt samples from
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y(a)}
to match doubly-robust moment estimates. Details are in
Appendix

B
and Algorithm
3
.

Estimating the OT coupling introduces an
additional source of statistical error not corrected at first order by the fixed-
\gamma
EIF correction. Bias-correcting OT couplings is not well-understood and left to future work. However, in exchange, we get
a simpler geometric target: straighter, lower-energy paths and near-identity flows under mild confounding. In high-dimensional experiments, we find this estimator improves distributional accuracy, demonstrating that the trade-off is worthwhile.

6
Discussion of Impact, Limitations and Future Work

Societal Impact

Beyond the methodological benefits established in this paper, deconfounding
flows may also have positive societal impact by helping mitigate harmful
generative biases, for example by rebalancing generated samples with respect to
sensitive or socially salient attributes. As with all generative methods,
there is also potential for misuse, for example by producing misleading or
manipulated synthetic content, and so the usual safeguards, provenance tools, and deployment
constraints required for generative models remain necessary here.

Limitations and Future Work

Deconfounding flows inherit the usual limitations of observational causal
inference methods such as relying on no-unobserved confounding, positivity, and sufficiently accurate nuisance estimation. The benefits of deconfounding flows also depend on
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
being a useful geometric anchor for

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
; despite shared support, tails, and closeness under structured
confounding, challenging transports may remain under weak overlap and/or highly
variable outcome mechanisms. Future work could develop diagnostics for this
regime, and also characterize
the extent to which distributional similarities hold in other causal regimes such as under the front-door criterion.

By choosing
\mathcal{D}
such that

\arg\min_{\theta_{a}\in\Theta}\mathcal{D}\left(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{a}}\right)=\arg\min_{\theta_{a}\in\Theta}\mathbb{E}\left[\ell(Y(a);\theta_{a})\right]

for a suitable loss
\ell
, first-order optimality of
\theta_{a,0}
yields a
population estimating equation

m(\theta_{a,0}):=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta_{a,0}}(Y(a))\right]=0,\quad g_{\theta_{a}}(y):=\nabla_{\theta_{a}}\ell(y;\theta_{a}).

For example, when
\mathcal{D}
is the Kullback–Leibler divergence,

g(\theta_{a},y)=-\partial_{\theta_{a}}\log p_{\theta_{a}}(y)
,
where
p_{\theta_{a}}
denotes the density of
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{a}}
.

summarizes the model
classes, discrepancies, and induced moment functions used in the literature. We note that while the recent flow-matching and score matching estimators used in this framework are not gradients of divergences, they can still be characterized as first-order optimality conditions, giving rise to equivalent moment conditions.

From here, one uses semiparametric theory to derive efficient estimators for
\theta_{a,0}
. The basic idea is to write
m_{a}(\theta_{a})
as a functional
of the observational distribution
\mathbb{P}:=\mathbb{P}_{Y,A,X}
,

m(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[g_{\theta_{a}}(Y)\mid A=a,X\right]\right],

Assuming pathwise differentiability of
m
, one can take a first-order von Mises expansion
around estimator
\hat{\mathbb{P}}
,

m(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})=m(\theta_{a},\hat{\mathbb{P}})+\int\varphi_{a}(o)\,d(\mathbb{P}-\hat{\mathbb{P}})(o)+R_{2}(\hat{\mathbb{P}},\mathbb{P}).

Here
o:=(x,a,y)
,
\varphi_{\theta_{a},a}
is called the

efficient influence function
(EIF) of
m(\theta_{a},\cdot)
and
R_{2}
is a second-order remainder term. The EIF is the unique, mean-zero function with minimum asymptotic variance that characterizes the first-order sensitivity of
m(\theta_{a},\cdot)
to perturbations of
\mathbb{P}

18
. Operationally, it captures the leading (first-order) error incurred when approximating
m(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})
by
m(\theta_{a},\hat{\mathbb{P}})
. In the present setting, the EIF takes the explicit augmented inverse propensity weighted (AIPW) form

\varphi_{a}(X,A,Y)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi_{a}(X)}\bigl(g(\theta_{a},Y)-\mu_{\theta_{a}}(a,X)\bigr)+\mu_{\theta_{a}}(a,X)-m_{a}(\theta_{a}),

where
\pi_{a}(X)=\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid X)
and

\mu_{\theta_{a}}(a,X)=\mathbb{E}[g(\theta_{a},Y)\mid A=a,X]
are called ‘nuisance models’.

Using this expansion, one constructs a ‘debiased’ estimator for
m(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})
in two steps. (i) form the plug-in estimator

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mathrm{PI}}(\theta_{a}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}(a,X_{i}),

using an estimator
\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}
for

\mu_{\theta_{a}}
, and (ii) remove its first-order bias by adding an empirical approximation of the
EIF term, using the nuisance estimators
\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}
and
\hat{\pi}_{a}
. This yields the so-called “one-step’ or “doubly-robust” estimator

\displaystyle\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a}):=\hat{m}_{\mathrm{PI}}(\theta_{a})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{\varphi}_{\theta_{a},a}(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})

(9)

In practice, one finds the target parameter that solves
\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})=0
either in closed form (when available)
17
, or by minimizing an antiderivative
of
\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}

25
;
24
;
23
. For statistical efficiency reasons, one usually trains
\hat{\mu}
and
\hat{\pi}
on a separate fold of data than
(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, or uses “cross-fitting” estimators which combine multiple sample split estimators to make use of the full data for nuisance training and moment evaluation.

Fast Rates, Efficiency, and Double Robustness of the Debiased Estimator

The naive plug-in estimator

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{PI}}(\theta_{a})=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}(a,X_{i})

inherits its convergence rate directly from the nuisance estimator

\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}
, and is therefore typically limited by the accuracy with which

\mu_{\theta_{a}}
can be estimated.

In contrast, the estimation error of the debiased (or doubly-robust) estimator

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})
admits an expansion with a second-order remainder,
whose leading term depends on the
product
of the estimation errors of the
nuisance models
\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}
and
\hat{\pi}_{a}
. As a consequence, under standard
regularity conditions (e.g. Donsker classes) or under cross-fitting,

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})-m_{a}(\theta_{a})=\mathcal{O}_{p}\left(\|\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}-\mu_{\theta_{a}}\|\,\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|\right),

up to empirical process terms of order
n^{-1/2}
.

This structure yields two important properties.
First, if both nuisance estimators converge at rates faster than

\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/4})
, the remainder term is
o_{p}(n^{-1/2})
, and the resulting estimator
is known to achieve
\sqrt{n}
-consistency and semiparametric efficiency
18
.
Second, the estimator is
doubly robust
in the sense that consistency of

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})
only requires one of the nuisance models

\hat{\mu}_{\theta_{a}}
or
\hat{\pi}_{a}
to be consistently estimated.

B.1
Implementation Details

B.1.1
(DecFM) Deconfounding Flows

In this section, we provide the details on implementation of our method.

Plug-in estimation of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X,A}
via conditional flow matching.

As a nuisance component of our method, we estimate the conditional outcome
distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X,A}
using a plug-in conditional flow-matching model.
Specifically, we learn a conditional transport that maps the empirical
treatment-conditional distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
to

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}
, for
(x,a)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
.

Concretely, we parameterize a
single
time-dependent velocity field

v_{\eta}:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{Y},

which takes the treatment indicator
A
as part of the conditioning context, and
define the neural ODE

\frac{d}{dt}y_{t}=v_{\eta}(y_{t},t,x,a),\qquad y_{0}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}.

For fixed
(x,a)
, the corresponding time-
1
flow map induces a conditional
distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}
.

Training is performed using standard
conditional flow matching

21
. That is, given observational samples

\{(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, we optimize the plug-in conditional flow-matching
objective

\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{plug\text{-}in}}(\eta)\;=\;\hat{\mathbb{E}}\!\left[\bigl\|v_{\eta}(Y_{t},t,X,A)-u^{\star}\bigr\|^{2}\right],

where the empirical expectation is taken over minibatches constructed as
follows. For each observed datapoint
(X_{i},A_{i},Y_{i})
in a minibatch, we draw an
independent base sample by resampling an outcome from the same treatment arm as
Y_{i}

\tilde{Y}_{i}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=A_{i}}\,.

We then
sample an interpolation time
t\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,1)
and form the linear
interpolation

Y_{t,i}:=(1-t)\tilde{Y}_{i}+tY_{i},\qquad u_{i}^{\star}:=Y_{i}-\tilde{Y}_{i}.

The procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

After training, samples
\hat{Y}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}
are obtained by
drawing
\tilde{Y}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
and numerically integrating the ODE
from
t=0
to
1
under context
(x,a)
. This conditional model is used solely as a
plug-in nuisance estimator within our debiased and doubly robust estimator for the deconfounding flow.

Propensity Score Estimation

In all experiments except CelebA, propensity scores
\pi_{a}(x)=\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid X=x)
are estimated
using random forests. We fix the minimum number of samples per leaf node to one,
and select the maximum tree depth by cross–validation on the training data, from the grid
[1,3,5,10]
. In CelebA, as
X\in\{0,1\}
is binary, we just use the empirical estimator of the propensity score.

Sample Splitting for Nuisance Models

The semiparametric efficiency results in
Section

4.2
require that nuisance
models (e.g., propensity scores and conditional outcome models) be trained on
data that are independent of the samples used in the empirical averages
defining the estimating equations. This can be achieved by sample splitting,
where the dataset is partitioned into disjoint folds, with nuisance models
trained on one fold and evaluated on another.

In practice, this requirement can be relaxed using standard cross-fitting
techniques. In the present setting, cross-fitting amounts to pre-estimating a
collection of nuisance models

\{\hat{m}^{(k)}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, each trained on a different data fold, and evaluating
each sample using the model corresponding to the fold in which that sample was

not
used for training. During stochastic optimization with minibatches,
each data point is passed through its associated nuisance model according to
its fold assignment.

In our experiments, we follow this principle as follows. In the outcome geometry ablation we train nuisance models on a separate hold-out
sample of size
n
and fix them throughout training, ensuring independence
between nuisance estimation and the empirical estimating equations. In the causal benchmarks, we use two-fold cross-fitting. In the
image-based experiments, we do not apply sample splitting or cross-fitting in
order to reduce computational cost and maximize sample efficiency. As a result, the corresponding results
should be interpreted as empirical performance evaluations rather than
guarantees of semiparametric efficiency.

Parameter Sharing Across Treatment Arms

For notational clarity, the theoretical development in the main text treats the
deconfounding flow and its associated velocity field separately for each
treatment level
a\in\{0,1\}
. In practice, however, we implement a single
parameterized velocity field

v_{\theta}:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\times\{0,1\}\to\mathcal{Y},

which takes the treatment indicator
a\in\{0,1\}
as an additional input and is shared
across treatment arms. The arm-specific velocities are then recovered as

v_{\theta,a}(y,t):=v_{\theta}(y,t,a)
.

This parameter-sharing strategy reduces variance and computational cost by
allowing statistical strength to be shared across arms, and is natural in
settings where the geometric structure of the outcome space is largely common
across treatments. Importantly, this modeling choice does not restrict
expressiveness: since
a
is provided as an explicit input, the shared network
can represent distinct velocity fields for each treatment level. Our efficiency
results apply to each arm-specific velocity
v_{\theta,a}
, and parameter
sharing should therefore be viewed as a practical architectural choice rather
than an additional modeling assumption.

Details on Minibatch EOT Implementation

Recall in
Section

4.3
our debiased gradient estimator is written in
terms of a conditional entropic coupling

\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}(\cdot\mid y)
,
which defines, for each query point
y
, a conditional distribution over base
samples
\tilde{y}
:

\displaystyle\hat{m}(\theta_{a}):=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\!\sum_{i=1}^{n}\!\Bigg[\!\frac{\bm{1}\{A_{i}=a\}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\!\!\int\!r_{\theta_{a}}(Y_{i},\tilde{y})\,\gamma_{0\mid 1}(d\tilde{y}|Y_{i})+\Bigl(\!1-\frac{\bm{1}\{A_{i}=a\}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\!\Bigr)\!\!\iint\!r_{\theta_{a}}(\hat{y},\tilde{y})\,\gamma_{0\mid 1}(d\tilde{y}|\hat{y})\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,x}(d\hat{y})\!\Bigg]

Here we briefly show how we derived this estimator and describe how the conditional coupling is
approximated in practice using minibatch entropic optimal transport, at each iteration of gradient descent.

Derivation

Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and a (possibly entropic) coupling
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
between

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
(base) and
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
(target), and write its disintegration as

\gamma^{\varepsilon}(d\tilde{y},dy)=\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}(d\tilde{y}\mid y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(dy)
.
Using the per-pair score
r_{\theta_{a}}(\cdot,\cdot)
defined in
Section

4.2
, define

g^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a}}(y):=\int r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\,\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}(d\tilde{y}\mid y),\qquad m(\theta_{a}):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a}}(Y(a))\right].

Under the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
in (
1
),
m(\theta_{a})
admits the representation

m(\theta_{a})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mu_{\theta_{a}}(X)\right]
with

\mu_{\theta_{a}}(x):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[g^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a}}(Y)\mid A=a,X=x\right]
.
Treating
\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}
as fixed, the efficient influence function for

m(\theta_{a})
(as a functional of the observational law) is the standard AIPW form

\phi^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a},a}(X,A,Y)=\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi_{a}(X)}\Big(g^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a}}(Y)-\mu_{\theta_{a}}(X)\Big)+\mu_{\theta_{a}}(X)-m(\theta_{a}).

From here, replacing

\pi_{a}
and
\mu_{\theta_{a}}
with nuisance estimators and empirically averaging

\phi^{\gamma}_{\theta_{a},a}
recovers the gradient estimator above.

Practical Approximation
Fix a treatment arm
a
and a minibatch of size
B
. Let

\mathcal{S}:=\{\hat{Y}_{i,k}\}_{i=1,k=1}^{B,M}
denote plug-in samples drawn from

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X_{i},A=a}
, and let

\mathcal{T}:=\{\tilde{Y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
denote base samples drawn from

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
.

For each set of such minibatches drawn during an iteration of gradient descent, we estimate the entropic OT coupling between
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{T}
with quadratic
cost
c(y,y^{\prime})=\|y-y^{\prime}\|^{2}
and regularization parameter
\varepsilon>0
, by running
K
iterations of Sinkhorn’s algorithm. The
resulting coupling has the form

\hat{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}(\ell,j)\;\propto\;a_{\ell}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\hat{Y}_{\ell}-\tilde{Y}_{j}\|^{2}}{\varepsilon}+v_{j}\right),

where
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{B}
are the target dual potentials obtained by Sinkhorn
iterations, and
\{a_{\ell}\}
are source weights defined below. In practice, we
compute only the target dual potentials
v_{j}
, which suffice to define the
conditional distribution.

Given the dual potentials, the conditional coupling appearing in the loss is
approximated via the standard entropic out-of-sample extension

\hat{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}(\tilde{Y}_{j}\mid y)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left(v_{j}-\frac{\|y-\tilde{Y}_{j}\|^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right),

from which a single base draw
\tilde{Y}
is (re)-sampled for each query point
y
. Note that the out-of-sample extension is tractable because we only use the
extension in the
conditioning direction
. In particular, the dual
potentials are never required for new conditioning points
y
, and by
restricting the left-hand side of

\hat{\gamma}^{\varepsilon}_{0\mid 1}(\tilde{Y}_{j}\mid y)
to base samples

\{\tilde{Y}_{j}\}
that were used to construct the minibatch coupling, the
conditional distribution depends only on the target duals
\{v_{j}\}
, which
are already available from the Sinkhorn iterations.

As a result, the conditional coupling can be viewed as inducing a
data-dependent
re-ordering
of the base minibatch, where the effective
pairing between
\tilde{Y}_{j}
and a query
y
depends on the term of the loss
being evaluated. This procedure is applied both to observed outcomes
Y_{i}

and to plug-in samples
\hat{Y}_{i,k}
when evaluating the inner functional

h_{\theta}(y)=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{Y}\mid y}[\ell_{\theta}(y,\tilde{Y})].

Exponential tilting.
When using minibatch entropic optimal transport, the coupling
\gamma^{\varepsilon}

is computed from samples drawn from a plug–in estimate of the counterfactual
distribution
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y(a)}
, which in the above implementation is constructed via
a nuisance model for
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A,X}
. If this plug–in model is misspecified,
the induced empirical coupling may allocate mass to regions of
\mathcal{Y}
that
are incompatible with the moment condition defining
m(\theta_{a})
. In particular,
because the coupling itself now depends on
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y(a)}
, the usual
double–robustness property of the debiased estimator—consistency under correct
specification of either the propensity or outcome model—no longer holds in
general once the coupling is treated as data–dependent.

To reduce sensitivity to this dependence, one can optionally apply an exponential tilting (or
exponential reweighting) scheme to a fixed reservoir of plug–in samples

\{\hat{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}\sim\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y(a)}
prior to computing the entropic
coupling. Specifically, we define a tilted empirical measure

\hat{\mathbb{P}}^{\mathrm{tilt}}_{Y(a)}:=\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}\,\delta_{\hat{y}_{j}},\qquad w_{j}(\lambda):=\frac{\exp\{\lambda^{\top}s(\hat{y}_{j})\}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\exp\{\lambda^{\top}s(\hat{y}_{\ell})\}},

where
s(\cdot)
denotes a chosen collection of moment features. The tilt
parameter
\lambda
is selected once, prior to flow training, so that the
weighted empirical moments of the reservoir approximately match the
corresponding moments estimated via the doubly robust estimating equations,

\sum_{j=1}^{M}w_{j}(\lambda)\,s(\hat{y}_{j})\;\approx\;\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{\mathrm{DR}}\!\left[s(Y(a))\right].

The resulting tilted empirical distribution is then used as the target marginal
when computing the minibatch entropic coupling
\gamma^{\varepsilon}
. Algorithm
4
in
Appendix

C
summarizes the procedure. In an additional coupling ablation experiment we ran in
Section

A.2
, we choose
m=128
random moments
s(y)=\cos(Wy+b)
, where the rows of
W\in\mathbb{R}^{128\times\dim(Y)}

are drawn independently from a mean–zero Gaussian distribution and

b\in[0,2\pi]^{128}
is drawn uniformly at random). We do not apply exponential tilting on ColorMNIST/CelebA for computational reasons.

We distinguish between non–image and image–based experiments.

Non–image based Architectures.

For all non–image experiments, both the plug–in conditional flow and the

multilayer perceptron with one hidden layer of width
64
, using SiLU
activations. The time variable
t
is concatenated directly to the input, and no
positional encoding is used in low–dimensional settings.

Models are trained for
1000
epochs using minibatches of size
512
and the Adam
optimizer with learning rate
10^{-3}
and default hyperparameters. No learning
rate schedules or additional regularization are applied unless stated otherwise.
For experiments involving minibatch entropic optimal transport, we use
50

Sinkhorn iterations per optimization step and a fixed entropic regularization
parameter
\varepsilon=0.1
.

Image-based architectures.

For image-based experiments, velocity fields are parameterized using a U-Net
architecture with skip connections. The base channel width is denoted by
c
.

The plug–in conditional flow used to estimate
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid X,A}

uses a U-Net that conditions on covariates
X
, the treatment indicator
A
,
and time
t\in[0,1]
. The encoder consists of four convolutional blocks with
channel dimensions
(c,2c,4c,8c)
, where each block applies two
3\times 3

convolutions with SiLU activations, and downsampling is performed using

2\times 2
max pooling between blocks. The decoder mirrors the encoder using
transposed convolutions for upsampling. At each decoder stage, the upsampled
feature map is concatenated with the corresponding encoder skip connection and a
time embedding, resulting in channel dimensions
(12c,6c,3c)
at successive
resolutions. A final
1\times 1
convolution maps the decoder output to the
velocity field.

Conditioning on
A
is implemented via class-conditional normalization layers,
while conditioning on
X
is incorporated through feature-wise linear
modulation (FiLM) layers
(
27
)
applied to intermediate feature
maps. Time is encoded using sinusoidal embeddings followed by learned linear
projections, with separate embeddings of dimensions
(c,2c,4c)
injected at
the corresponding decoder resolutions. The resulting plug–in velocity field has
the form

v_{\eta}:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{Y}.

The deconfounding target flow uses the same U-Net backbone and time-conditioning
scheme, but omits conditioning on
X
. In our shared-arm implementation, the
target model conditions only on the treatment indicator
A
and time
t
,
resulting in a velocity field of the form

v_{\theta}:\mathcal{Y}\times[0,1]\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{Y}.

Across all image-based experiments, we use SiLU activations throughout. In ColorMNIST we vary
the base channel width
c\in\{8,16,32\}
as part of the experimental design, in CelebA we use fixed
c=64
.
Models are trained using the Adam optimizer with learning rate
10^{-4}
and
minibatches of size
128
for ColorMNIST and
64
for CelebA. For entropic optimal transport, we use
20
Sinkhorn
iterations per optimization step for ColorMNIST and 10 for CelebA, with the entropic regularization parameter

\varepsilon
set to
0.1
times the empirical standard deviation of the
minibatch cost matrix. We ran on ColorMNIST for 1000 epochs, and CelebA for 500 epochs. To avoid overfitting, during training we inject the base samples from
\mathbb{P}_{Y|A=a}
with
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
Gaussian noise with
\sigma=0.1
.

B.1.2
Baselines

KDE (AIPW density estimator)
19
.

We implement the augmented inverse–probability–weighted (AIPW) kernel density estimator as follows. In particular, let
K_{h}(u)=h^{-1}K(u/h)
denote the Gaussian kernel with bandwidth
h
. The doubly robust
(AIPW) estimator of the smoothed counterfactual density
p_{Y(a)}
at
y
is

\hat{p}^{\mathrm{AIPW}}_{Y(a)}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{1}\{A_{i}=a\}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\Bigl\{K_{h}(Y_{i}-y)-\hat{m}_{a}(y\mid X_{i})\Bigr\}+\hat{m}_{a}(y\mid X_{i})\Bigg],

where
\hat{\pi}_{a}(x)
is the estimated propensity score and

\hat{m}_{a}(y\mid x):=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{p}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}}\!\left[K_{h}(Y-y)\right]

is a plug–in estimate of the conditional smoothed density obtained from the
outcome regression model. We use the following implementation choices and hyperparameters: (i) The kernel bandwidth is selected using the median heuristic, separately within each treatment arm. (ii) Outcome regression and propensity score models are estimated using random forests, trained identically to DecFM, including cross–validation over maximum tree depth. (iii) Two–fold cross–fitting is used to ensure independence between nuisance estimation and evaluation. (iv) Wasserstein–1 distances are computed by numerical integration of the estimated density.

TSE (Truncated Series Estimator)
17
.

We implement the Truncated series estimator (TSE) for counterfactual density
estimation, following the doubly robust construction described in

17
. The counterfactual density is approximated by
expanding it in an orthonormal cosine basis on a bounded interval.

Let
[y_{\min},y_{\max}]
denote the maximum and minimum outcome in the observational set, and define the scaled
outcome

\tilde{Y}:=\frac{\min\{\max\{Y,y_{\min}\},y_{\max}\}-y_{\min}}{y_{\max}-y_{\min}}\in[0,1].

We use the cosine basis

\phi_{j}(\tilde{y})=\sqrt{2}\cos(\pi j\tilde{y}),\qquad j=1,\dots,d,

with
d=20
basis functions. The counterfactual density is represented as

p_{Y(a)}(y)\;\approx\;\sum_{j=1}^{d}\theta_{a,j}\,\phi_{j}(\tilde{y}),

where the coefficients
\theta_{a,j}=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{j}(\tilde{Y}(a))]
are estimated
using a doubly robust estimating equation.

Specifically, for each basis coefficient we use the AIPW estimator

\hat{\theta}_{a,j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\frac{\bm{1}\{A_{i}=a\}}{\hat{\pi}_{a}(X_{i})}\Bigl(\phi_{j}(\tilde{Y}_{i})-\hat{\mu}_{a,j}(X_{i})\Bigr)+\hat{\mu}_{a,j}(X_{i})\Bigg],

where
\hat{\pi}_{a}
is the estimated propensity score and

\hat{\mu}_{a,j}(x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi_{j}(\tilde{Y})\mid X=x,A=a]
is estimated via outcome
regression.

The estimated density
\hat{p}_{Y(a)}
is obtained by combining the estimated
coefficients
\{\hat{\theta}_{a,j}\}_{j=1}^{d}
. Wasserstein–1 distances are computed
by numerical integration of the resulting density.

In all experiments, propensity scores and outcome regressions are estimated using
random forests with the same hyperparameter grid as DecFM, and two–fold
cross–fitting is used to ensure independence between nuisance estimation and
evaluation.

Interventional Normalizing Flows (INFs)
25
.

We implement Interventional Normalizing Flows following

25
, which directly model the counterfactual
distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
via a normalizing flow trained using a
KL–divergence–based estimating objective.

For each treatment arm
a\in\{0,1\}
, the target interventional distribution is
parameterized by a neural autoregressive flow and trained by minimizing an
empirical KL divergence between the model density and the distribution implied by
the identifying moment condition in
25
. The target
flow architecture is fixed across experiments and consists of a fully connected
network with a single hidden layer of width
64
.

INFs additionally employ nuisance models for treatment assignment and outcome
modeling. These are implemented as two fully connected networks, where the
propensity network produces a
9
–dimensional embedding of the covariates that
is used as input to the outcome model. The architectures of these nuisance
networks are selected by cross–validation over hidden layer widths

\{16,32,64\}
and depths
\{1,2\}
.

In line with the original INFs methodology, we do not apply sample splitting or
cross–fitting. For evaluation, we draw
10{,}000
samples from each fitted
interventional model and compute Wasserstein–1 distances empirically using these
samples.

DR-MKSD
24
.

We implement the doubly robust kernel Stein discrepancy (DR-MKSD) estimator of

24
, which estimates the counterfactual distribution

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
via an energy-based model trained to satisfy a Stein moment
condition.

The target distribution is parameterized as an energy-based model (EBM) with
energy function given by a fully connected neural network with a single hidden
layer of width
64
and SiLU activations. Model parameters are estimated by
minimizing the empirical kernel Stein discrepancy. Optimization details are identical to DecFM.

The estimator relies on nuisance models for both the propensity score and the
conditional outcome. Propensity scores are estimated using random forests,
trained identically to those used for DecFM, including cross-validation over tree
depth. The outcome regression is estimated using kernel ridge regression in an
RKHS, with the regularization parameter selected by cross-validation over a grid
of
10
values logarithmically spaced between
10^{-3}
and
10^{-1}
.

We use two-fold cross-fitting to ensure independence between nuisance estimation
and evaluation. Wasserstein–1 distances are computed by drawing
n=10,000
samples from the
fitted energy-based model via the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm and estimating the distance empirically.

DoubleGen
23

We implement the flow-matching variant of DoubleGen using our own estimator and architectural/training details but with a Gaussian base. Concretely, DoubleGen uses the same velocity architecture, optimizer, training
epochs, nuisance models, and cross-fitting scheme as DecFM, but replaces the
empirical observational base
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid A=a}
by a standard Gaussian base.

B.2
Experimental Designs

B.2.1
Synthetic 1d Outcome Designs

In
Section

5.1
we consider three one–dimensional outcome designs with a continuous confounder

X\in[0,1]
, a binary treatment
A\in\{0,1\}
, and scalar outcome
Y\in\mathbb{R}
.
Across all designs, confounding is induced through the treatment assignment
mechanism

A\mid X=x\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\pi(x)),\qquad\pi(x)=\sigma\bigl(5(x-\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the logistic sigmoid.

Gaussian outcome.

In the Gaussian design, outcomes are conditionally normal:

Y\mid X=x,A=a\sim\mathcal{N}(10x+a,\;\sigma^{2}),

with
\sigma=1
unless stated otherwise. This setting serves as a baseline with
matched support and light tails, for which Gaussian base distributions are
well-aligned with the target geometry.

Inverse–Gamma outcome (heavy tails).

To study the effect of tail mismatch, we consider a heavy–tailed outcome model:

Y\mid X=x,A=a\sim\mathrm{InverseGamma}(\alpha(x),\beta(x,a)),\qquad\alpha(x)=10-8.5x,\;\;\beta(x,a)=a+1+10x.

Here, treatment affects scale but not tail index, while confounding through
X

induces substantial heteroskedasticity and heavy tails in both observational and
interventional distributions.

Uniform mixture (split support).

To isolate support mismatch, we consider a bimodal outcome distribution with
disconnected support:

Y\mid X=x,A=a=a+U,

where
U
is drawn from a symmetric mixture of uniforms

U\sim\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Unif}[-\beta(x),-\alpha(x)]+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Unif}[\alpha(x),\beta(x)],

with

\alpha(x)=1-\varepsilon(cx+1),\qquad\beta(x)=1+\varepsilon(cx+1).

In this design, the two support components are separated by a gap around zero,
whose width depends on
X
. We use the parameterization
c=100
,
\epsilon=0.001
.

B.2.2
Synthetic 2d Mixture of Normals

In
Section

A.2
we consider an additional two–dimensional synthetic design with continuous covariates

X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, binary treatment
A\in\{0,1\}
, and outcome
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The
design is constructed to induce geometric confounding through a linear
projection of
X
, while allowing control over multimodality and support
separation in the outcome space.

Covariates are drawn as

X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2}).

Treatment assignment follows a logistic propensity model

A\mid X=x\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\sigma(\gamma\,w^{\top}x)\right),

where
w=(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}
is a fixed confounding direction and
\gamma>0
controls the
strength of confounding. In all experiments, we vary
\gamma
to interpolate
between weak and strong confounding regimes.

Outcomes are generated as

Y=X+\varepsilon,

so that there is no average or distributional treatment effect. The observed
outcome is given by
Y=Y(A)
.

The noise term
\varepsilon
induces a bimodal mixture structure:

\varepsilon=\begin{cases}\eta,&\text{with probability }\tfrac{1}{2},\\
(\delta,\delta)+\eta,&\text{with probability }\tfrac{1}{2},\end{cases}\qquad\eta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{2}),

where
\delta>0
controls the separation between mixture components and
\sigma

controls within–component variance. In our experiments, we fix
\sigma=0.2

and vary
\delta
(referred to as the
mixture width
) to control the degree
of support separation.

B.2.3
Causal Benchmarks

In
Section

5.2
we evaluate our method on a collection of standard causal benchmark datasets,
combining both fully synthetic and semi–synthetic designs.

ACIC 2016 Challenge datasets.

We consider
10
datasets from the ACIC 2016 Causal Inference Challenge, each of
which provides observational data generated from a known structural causal
model with heterogeneous treatment effects and complex confounding. For each
dataset, we construct our own random train–test split of the available data,
using
70\%
of the samples for training and
30\%
for evaluation. All models
are trained exclusively on the training split, and performance is reported on
the held–out test split.

Semi–synthetic benchmarks (ACIC 2019, 401(k), and Twins).

For the ACIC 2019, 401(k), and Twins datasets, we adopt a semi–synthetic
construction. In each case, we begin from a real observational dataset with
covariates
X
and treatment assignment
A
, and then generate potential
outcomes using one of the outcome–generation mechanisms used in

Section

5.1
(In
Table

2
, SplitSupport = Mixture of Uniform, HeavyTail = InverseGamma). This allows us to evaluate distributional
counterfactual estimators under different outcome geometries while preserving
realistic covariate and treatment structures.

For these datasets, we randomly split the data into
70\%
training and
30\%

test sets. All nuisance models and deconfounding flows are trained on the
training split, and evaluation is performed on the held–out test split.

Strengthening confounding via feature selection.

To ensure challenging confounding regimes, we construct a low–dimensional
summary
\phi(X)
of the covariates by ranking features according to their
marginal Wasserstein–1 distance between treated and untreated groups.
Specifically, for each covariate
X_{j}
, we compute a confounding score

s_{j}={W1}\!\bigl(\mathbb{P}(X_{j}\mid A=1),\,\mathbb{P}(X_{j}\mid A=0)\bigr).

Covariates with larger
s_{j}
exhibit stronger distributional separation between
treatment groups and are therefore more strongly confounded with
A
. We retain the top
K=5
covariates with the largest scores and define

\phi(X)
as their normalized sum,

\phi(X):=\bm{1}^{\top}X/\sqrt{K}.

Outcomes are then generated using the mechanisms described in

Section

5.1
, with
\phi(X)
replacing the original one–dimensional
covariate. However,
all covariates are used when training the models
.

Twins dataset.

For the Twins dataset, we follow the treatment assignment construction of

22
. We first form an observational dataset by selecting one
twin at random from each same–sex twin pair and restricting attention to
low–birthweight pairs. Treatment assignment is then generated according to a
logistic model depending on gestational age,

A\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\sigma(w_{h}z)\right),

where
z
denotes a standardized gestational age covariate and
w_{h}
is a
randomly drawn slope parameter.

B.2.4
Image Generation: ColorMNIST

In
Section

5.3
We consider an image–based debiasing task constructed from the MNIST dataset,
following a foreground–color confounding design. The goal is to estimate
counterfactual image distributions in the presence of strong confounding between
treatment assignment and a visual attribute.

Data generation.

We restrict attention to two digit classes
(d_{0},d_{1})=(1,6)
and construct RGB
images by recoloring grayscale MNIST digits. Each image is generated from a
latent scalar confounder
X\in[0,1]
, drawn uniformly. Treatment assignment
follows a logistic propensity model

A\mid X=x\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\!\left(\sigma\bigl(w(x-\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)\right),

where
w>0
controls the strength of confounding.

Conditional on
(X,A)
, the observed image
Y
is generated by selecting a digit
from class
d_{A}
and recoloring its foreground using an RGB mapping that depends
on
X
. Foreground recoloring is implemented via a soft mask derived from the
grayscale digit intensity. Let
S\in[0,1]^{28\times 28}
denote the original MNIST
digit. We define a foreground mask

M=\sigma\bigl(k(S-\tau)\bigr),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the logistic sigmoid and
(\tau,k)
control the
foreground–background separation. Given a foreground RGB color

c_{\mathrm{fg}}(X)\in[0,1]^{3}
and a fixed background color
c_{\mathrm{bg}}
, the
recolored image is constructed pixelwise as

Y=M\odot(c_{\mathrm{fg}}(X)\,S)+(1-M)\odot c_{\mathrm{bg}}.

In our experiments, the foreground color varies smoothly with the confounder
according to

c_{\mathrm{fg}}(X)=(X,\alpha,1-X),

with fixed
\alpha\in[0,1]
and constant background color
c_{\mathrm{bg}}
.
This construction induces a nonlinear (approximately logistic) distribution over
the relative red–blue intensity ratio, creating a strong but smooth association
between color, treatment assignment, and digit class.

Evaluation setting.

Models are trained on
n=20{,}000
observational samples
(X,A,Y)
. To
evaluate counterfactual performance, we generate
5000
reference samples from
each interventional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
by fixing the digit class to

d_{a}
and resampling
X
independently from its marginal distribution. The
foreground colors are then assigned using the same
X
-dependent recoloring
rule as in the observational data, so that reference images preserve the latent
color variation induced by
X
while removing treatment-selection bias. We
compare generated and reference samples using sliced Wasserstein–2 distance,
averaged over
a\in\{0,1\}
and over three random seeds.

Lemma D.1

(Bounded RN Derivative)
.

Assume
\exists\epsilon>0
such that
\pi_{a}(x):=\mathbb{P}(A=a|X=x)\geq\epsilon\;\forall a\in\{0,1\},\;\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
Fix
a\in\{0,1\}
and write
r_{a}(x):=\mathbb{P}(A=a)/\pi_{a}(X)
. Then
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and, for
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
-a.e.
y
, the Radon-Nikodym derivative
g_{a}(y):=\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}}{d\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}}(y)
exists and satisfies

\displaystyle g_{a}(y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[r_{a}(X)\;|\;A=a,\,Y=y\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{1-\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;g_{a}(y)\;\leq\;\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for $\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}$-a.e.\ $y$.}

(11)

Proof.

Let
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{X})
be measurable. By the law of total probability,

\mathbb{P}(X\in B,A=a)=\int_{B}\pi_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx),\qquad\mathbb{P}(A=a)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\pi_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx).

If
\mathbb{P}_{X}(B)=0
, then
\mathbb{P}(X\in B,A=a)=0
hence
\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(B)=0
, so

\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}\ll\mathbb{P}_{X}
.

Conversely, suppose
\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(B)=0
. Then
\mathbb{P}(X\in B,A=a)=0
, i.e.

\int_{B}\pi_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)=0
. Since
\pi_{a}(x)\geq\varepsilon>0

\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.s., this implies

\mathbb{P}_{X}(B)=0
. Hence
\mathbb{P}_{X}\ll\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}
.
Therefore
\mathbb{P}_{X}\sim\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}
.

Moreover, for
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.e.
x
,

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}}{d\mathbb{P}_{X}}(x)=\frac{\pi_{a}(x)}{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{a}(x):=\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{X}}{d\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}}(x)=\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\pi_{a}(x)}.

By overlap,
r_{a}(x)
is essentially bounded:

\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{1-\varepsilon}\leq r_{a}(x)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{for $\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}$-a.e.\ $x$.}

Let
h:\mathbb{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded measurable. Using the representation from (
1
),

\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(\cdot)=\int\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(\cdot)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)
,
Tonelli/Fubini yields

\displaystyle\int h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(dy)\,r_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,r_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X,Y\mid A=a}(dx,dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}}r_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X\mid Y=y,A=a}(dx)\right)\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,\mathbb{E}[r_{a}(X)\mid A=a,Y=y]\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy).

Since
h
was arbitrary, the Radon–Nikodym theorem gives
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}\ll\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and

\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}}{d\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}}(y)=\mathbb{E}[r_{a}(X)\mid A=a,Y=y]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\mathbb{P}(A=a\mid X)}\ \Big|\ A=a,Y=y\right]

for
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
-a.e.
y
.

By our earlier result,
r_{a}(X)\in\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{1-\varepsilon},\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\varepsilon}\right]
a.s.
Conditioning preserves bounds, hence for
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
-a.e.
y
,

\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{1-\varepsilon}\leq\mathbb{E}[r_{a}(X)\mid A=a,Y=y]\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}{\varepsilon}.

Define
\tilde{r}_{a}(x):=\frac{d\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}}{d\mathbb{P}_{X}}(x)=\frac{\pi_{a}(x)}{\mathbb{P}(A=a)}
,
which is strictly positive
\mathbb{P}_{X}
-a.s. (indeed
\tilde{r}_{a}(x)\geq\varepsilon/\mathbb{P}(A=a)
).
Repeat the calculation in Step 2 but now start from

\int h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid X=x,A=a}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(dx)

and substitute
\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(dx)=\tilde{r}_{a}(x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)
. This yields

\int h(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)=\int_{\mathbb{Y}}h(y)\,\mathbb{E}[\tilde{r}_{a}(X)\mid Y(a)=y]\,\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(dy),

so
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\ll\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
.
(Here
\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid Y(a)=y]
is well-defined on standard Borel spaces.)
Therefore
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}\sim\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
.
∎

Lemma D.4

(EIF for a two-measure product functional)
.

Let
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A})
be a measurable space and let

k:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
be measurable.
Fix a pair
(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
and define,
for
(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})
in a neighbourhood of
(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})
,

T(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}):=\iint k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{1}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{2}(dy^{\prime}).

Assume that, at
\mathbb{P}_{1,0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{2,0}
, the linear functionals
\mathbb{P}\mapsto\int\phi(y)\,\mathbb{P}(dy)

are regular and pathwise differentiable for any measurable
\phi
satisfying

\int\phi^{2}\,d\mathbb{P}_{1,0}<\infty
or
\int\phi^{2}\,d\mathbb{P}_{2,0}<\infty
,
respectively.
Define the partial integrals

\phi_{1}(y):=\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{2,0}(dy^{\prime}),\qquad\phi_{2}(y^{\prime}):=\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{1,0}(dy).

Then
T
is regular and pathwise differentiable at

(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})
, with efficient influence function

\varphi_{T}=\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}+\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}},

where
\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}
is the EIF of the functional

\mathbb{P}_{1}\mapsto\int\phi_{1}\,d\mathbb{P}_{1}
at
\mathbb{P}_{1,0}
and

\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}}
is the EIF of the functional

\mathbb{P}_{2}\mapsto\int\phi_{2}\,d\mathbb{P}_{2}
at
\mathbb{P}_{2,0}
.

Proof.

Recall that the efficient influence function
\varphi_{T}
of a regular
functional
T
at a distribution
\mathbb{P}_{0}
is the (unique) mean-zero
function satisfying

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}T(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon})=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi_{T}(O)\,s(O)\right]

for every regular parametric submodel
\{\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\}
through

\mathbb{P}_{0}
with score
s(O)
. We establish the result by applying this
pathwise derivative characterization to the product functional

T(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})=\iint k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{1}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{2}(dy^{\prime}).

Fix a true pair
(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})
and let

\{(\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon},\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon})\}
be a regular
parametric submodel through
(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})
at

\varepsilon=0
, with score
s(O)
. Write

T_{\varepsilon}:=T(\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon},\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon})
.
Then

T_{\varepsilon}=\int\phi_{1,\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon}(dy),\qquad\phi_{1,\varepsilon}(y):=\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon}(dy^{\prime}).

Differentiating at
\varepsilon=0
and applying the chain rule yields

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}T_{\varepsilon}=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\int\phi_{1}(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon}(dy)+\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}\int\phi_{1,\varepsilon}(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{1,0}(dy),

where

\phi_{1}(y):=\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{2,0}(dy^{\prime}).

The first term corresponds to perturbing
\mathbb{P}_{1}
while holding

\mathbb{P}_{2}
fixed. By the assumed pathwise differentiability of linear
functionals at
\mathbb{P}_{1,0}
, it equals

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}(O)\,s(O)\right],

where
\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}
is the EIF of the functional

\mathbb{P}_{1}\mapsto\int\phi_{1}\,d\mathbb{P}_{1}
at
\mathbb{P}_{1,0}
.

The second term corresponds to perturbing
\mathbb{P}_{2}
while holding

\mathbb{P}_{1}
fixed. Writing

\phi_{2}(y^{\prime}):=\int k(y,y^{\prime})\,\mathbb{P}_{1,0}(dy),

and using pathwise differentiability at
\mathbb{P}_{2,0}
, this term equals

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}}(O)\,s(O)\right],

where
\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}}
is the EIF of the functional

\mathbb{P}_{2}\mapsto\int\phi_{2}\,d\mathbb{P}_{2}
at
\mathbb{P}_{2,0}
.

Summing the two contributions yields

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}T_{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{E}\!\left[(\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}+\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}})(O)\,s(O)\right],

which identifies
\varphi_{T}=\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}}+\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}}
as
the efficient influence function of
T
at

(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})
.
∎

Lemma D.5

(Empirical mean of independent random function (scalar/vector))
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. with distribution
P
, and let
S_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
another random variable such that
\mathcal{T}_{n}:=\sigma(S_{n})
is
independent from
\sigma(\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n})
.
Let
\psi_{n}:\mathcal{S}_{n}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a measurable function, and assume that

\mathbb{E}[\|\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]<\infty
almost surely.
Define
P_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
. Then

\left\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\right\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{\|\psi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

Proof.

Write

(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z_{i})-P\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\Big),

where
S_{n}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}_{n}
-measurable and
P\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}):=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Define
U_{i}:=\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z_{i})-P\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})
. Observe that
\mathbb{E}[{P}_{n}\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]=P\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})
, so that
\mathbb{E}[(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})]=0
.
Moreover,

\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\right\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right].

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|U_{1}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\|\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)-\mathbb{E}[\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]=\textrm{Var}\left(\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}\right),

and since
\textrm{Var}(V\mid\mathcal{T})\leq\mathbb{E}[\|V\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|V\|_{2}^{2}]
for any
square-integrable
V
,

\textrm{Var}\left(\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|\psi_{n}(S_{n},Z)\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\|\psi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}\;.

Now, fix
M>0
and set

t:=M\,\frac{\|\psi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}}{\sqrt{n}}
. By the conditional Markov (Chebyshev) inequality applied to the nonnegative random variable

\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\|_{2}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\|_{2}^{2}>t^{2}\ \middle|\ \mathcal{T}_{n}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}_{n}]}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}}.

Taking expectations over
\mathcal{T}
yields the unconditional bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\right\|_{2}>M\,\frac{\|\psi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{M^{2}},

which implies

\|(P_{n}-P)\psi_{n}(S_{n},{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})\|_{2}=O_{\mathbb{P}}\big(\|\psi_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}/\sqrt{n}\big)
.
∎

Lemma D.6

(Second-order V-statistic with an independent random kernel (vector-valued))
.

Let
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. from
\mathbb{P}
, and let
\mathcal{T}
be a
\sigma
-algebra
independent of
\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Let
k_{n}:\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a measurable kernel that is
\mathcal{T}
-measurable
(hence fixed conditional on
\mathcal{T}
), and assume

\mathbb{E}[\|k_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\|_{2}^{2}]<\infty
almost surely and
\mathbb{E}[\|k_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{1})\|_{2}^{2}]<\infty
almost surely.
Define
\mathbb{P}_{n}:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Z_{i}}
and the (vector-valued) V-statistic

V_{n}(k_{n}):=\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Then,

\left\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\right\|_{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\qquad\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}^{2}:=\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}].

Moreover, if
k_{n}
is
first-order degenerate
(conditional on
\mathcal{T}
), i.e.

\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{T}]=\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(Z^{\prime},z)\mid\mathcal{T}]=\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\qquad\text{for all }z,

then the improved rate holds:

\left\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\right\|_{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}}{n}\right).

Proof.

We work conditionally on
\mathcal{T}
throughout. Since
k_{n}
is
\mathcal{T}
-measurable,
all objects defined from
k_{n}
below are deterministic given
\mathcal{T}
, while

\{Z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
remain i.i.d. from
\mathbb{P}
conditional on
\mathcal{T}
.
For readability we keep the index
n
explicit.

Let
\theta_{n}:=\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and define the first-order projections

k_{n,1}(z):=\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})]-\theta_{n},\qquad k_{n,2}(z):=\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(Z^{\prime},z)]-\theta_{n},

and the degenerate remainder

\tilde{k}_{n}(z,z^{\prime}):=k_{n}(z,z^{\prime})-\theta_{n}-k_{n,1}(z)-k_{n,2}(z^{\prime}).

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{k}_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})]=0
for all
z
and

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,z^{\prime})]=0
for all
z^{\prime}
(as vector equalities), where all expectations
are under
\mathbb{P}
(and conditional on
\mathcal{T}
if desired).

Expanding
k_{n}=\theta_{n}+k_{n,1}+k_{n,2}+\tilde{k}_{n}
yields the decomposition

V_{n}(k_{n})-\theta_{n}=\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{n}k_{n,1}}_{A_{n}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{n}k_{n,2}}_{B_{n}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\otimes 2}\tilde{k}_{n}}_{C_{n}},

where
A_{n},B_{n},C_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

L_{2}
bounds for
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{T}
,
k_{n,1}
is deterministic and
Z_{i}
are i.i.d., hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(k_{n,1}(Z_{i})-\mathbb{P}k_{n,1}\big)\right\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|k_{n,1}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right],

where we used
\mathbb{P}k_{n,1}=0
and the standard identity

\mathbb{E}\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}U_{i}\|_{2}^{2}=n\,\mathbb{E}\|U_{1}\|_{2}^{2}
for i.i.d. mean-zero vectors.
Likewise,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|B_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\|k_{n,2}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right].

By Jensen and
\|u-v\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2(\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2})
, for each
z
,

\displaystyle\|k_{n,1}(z)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\|\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})]-\theta_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})]\|_{2}^{2}+2\|\theta_{n}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[\|k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}]+2\mathbb{E}[\|k_{n}(Z,Z^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}].

Taking expectation over
z\sim\mathbb{P}
(conditional on
\mathcal{T}
) yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\|k_{n,1}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq 4\,\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}],\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\|k_{n,2}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq 4\,\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}].

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|A_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{4}{n}\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}],\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\|B_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{4}{n}\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}].

L_{2}
bound for
C_{n}
.

Split
C_{n}
into off-diagonal and diagonal parts:

C_{n}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i\neq j}\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{i})=:C_{n,\mathrm{off}}+C_{n,\mathrm{diag}}.

For the diagonal term, by Jensen,

\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n,\mathrm{diag}}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{1}{n^{4}}\,n\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]=\frac{1}{n^{3}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right].

Using
\|u_{1}+\cdots+u_{m}\|_{2}^{2}\leq m\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\|u_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}
on

\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,Z)=k_{n}(Z,Z)-\theta_{n}-k_{n,1}(Z)-k_{n,2}(Z)
gives

\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 4\Big(\|k_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\theta_{n}\|_{2}^{2}+\|k_{n,1}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}+\|k_{n,2}(Z)\|_{2}^{2}\Big).

Taking conditional expectations and using

\|\theta_{n}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(Z,Z^{\prime})]\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}]
together with the
previous bounds on
k_{n,1},k_{n,2}
yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq c\Big(\mathbb{E}[\|k_{n}(Z,Z)\|_{2}^{2}]+\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}]\Big)

for a universal constant
c>0
.
By the diagonal integrability assumption, the right-hand side is finite almost surely so

\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n,\mathrm{diag}}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]=O(n^{-3}),

which is negligible at the
n^{-1}
scale.

For the off-diagonal term, degeneracy implies that for
i\neq j
,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\mid Z_{i},\mathcal{T}]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\mid Z_{j},\mathcal{T}]=0.

A second-moment expansion shows only index-pairings with
(p,q)=(i,j)
or
(p,q)=(j,i)
contribute, hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n,\mathrm{off}}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{2}{n^{4}}\,n(n-1)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{2}{n^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right].

Finally, since
\tilde{k}_{n}=k_{n}-\theta_{n}-k_{n,1}-k_{n,2}
, the same 4-sum inequality and the bounds above yield

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\tilde{k}_{n}(Z_{1},Z_{2})\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq c^{\prime}\,\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}]

for a universal constant
c^{\prime}>0
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n,\mathrm{off}}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{2c^{\prime}}{n^{2}}\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}],\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{n^{2}}\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}],

for a universal constant
\tilde{c}>0
.

Using
\|u+v+w\|_{2}^{2}\leq 3(\|u\|_{2}^{2}+\|v\|_{2}^{2}+\|w\|_{2}^{2})
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\theta_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{E}[\|A_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}]+3\mathbb{E}[\|B_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}]+3\mathbb{E}[\|C_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12}{n}\,\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}]+O(n^{-2})\;.

Fix
M>0
and set
t:=M\,\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}/\sqrt{n}
.
Applying conditional Markov to the nonnegative random variable

\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}>t\ \middle|\ \mathcal{T}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}>t^{2}\ \middle|\ \mathcal{T}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{T}]}{t^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{12}{M^{2}}.

Taking expectations over
\mathcal{T}
yields the unconditional bound

\mathbb{P}\left(\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}>M\,\frac{\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\leq\frac{12}{M^{2}},

which implies

\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Degenerate case.

Assume now that
k_{n}
is first-order degenerate conditional on
\mathcal{T}
, i.e.

\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(z,Z^{\prime})\mid\mathcal{T}]=\theta_{n}
and
\mathbb{E}[k_{n}(Z^{\prime},z)\mid\mathcal{T}]=\theta_{n}

for all
z
. Then
k_{n,1}\equiv 0
and
k_{n,2}\equiv 0
, so
A_{n}=B_{n}\equiv 0
and

V_{n}(k_{n})-\theta_{n}=C_{n}=\mathbb{P}_{n}^{\otimes 2}\tilde{k}_{n}.

The bounds above already give

\mathbb{E}\left[\|C_{n}\|_{2}^{2}\middle|\mathcal{T}\right]\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{n^{2}}\,\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}[\|k_{n}\|_{2}^{2}].

Fix
M>0
and set
t:=M\,\|k_{n}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2})}/n
.
Applying conditional Markov to
\|C_{n}\|_{2}^{2}
yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\|V_{n}(k_{n})-\mathbb{P}^{\otimes 2}k_{n}\|_{2}>t\ \middle|\ \mathcal{T}\right)\leq\frac{\tilde{c}}{M^{2}}.

Taking expectations over
\mathcal{T}

Now, note that for any measurable

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid A=a,X=x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(Y\in B\mid A=a,X=x)\,\mathbb{P}_{X\mid a}(dx).

By Lemma

, the equivalence of the mixing distributions implies

and

Using the definition of

Under the strong positivity assumption

\mathbb{P}_{X}

Fix

Then the moment functional

\varphi_{\theta_{a}}(O)=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\!\left[\psi_{\theta_{a}}(O,\cdot)\right]=\int\psi_{\theta_{a}}(O,y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy),

where
\psi_{\theta_{a}}=\psi_{1,\theta_{a}}+\psi_{2,\theta_{a}}

\psi_{1,\theta_{a}},\psi_{2,\theta_{a}}

are as in

D.9
. Equivalently,

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

Fix
a
and suppress the subscript
a
on
\theta
for readability. Now, recall that under the definition of
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
in (

)

m_{a}(\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}_{a}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}(Y)\mid A=a,X\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[g_{\theta}\big(Y(a)\big)\right],\qquad g_{\theta}(y):=\int r_{\theta}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y}).

To derive the EIF, it is helpful to write
m_{a}(\theta)
as a functional of both
the counterfactual marginal
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and the conditional observational law

\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
. Specifically, define the functional

T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}):=\iint r_{\theta}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{1}(dy)\,\mathbb{P}_{2}(d\tilde{y}),\qquad(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y}),

and note that for
P_{1}=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and
P_{2}=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
, we have
m_{a}(\theta)=T_{\theta}\big(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\big)
. Along any regular parametric submodel
\{\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\}
of the
observed-data law
\mathbb{P}
, define the induced measures

\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}),\qquad\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}),

and write
m_{a}(\theta;\varepsilon):=T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon},\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon})
.
By the functional chain rule and Lemma
D.4
,

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}m_{a}(\theta;\varepsilon)=\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{1,\varepsilon},\mathbb{P}_{2,0})+\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\right|_{\varepsilon=0}T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{1,0},\mathbb{P}_{2,\varepsilon}),

where
\mathbb{P}_{1,0}=\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
and
\mathbb{P}_{2,0}=\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
.

By a simple application of the chain-rule for EIFs (see Lemma
D.4
),
the EIF of
m_{a}(\theta)
is given by the sum of the EIF of

T(\cdot,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})
for fixed
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
and the EIF of

T(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\cdot)
for fixed
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
. Thus, the EIF of
m_{a}(\theta)
is given by the sum of the EIF of
T(\cdot,\mathbb{P}_{Y|a})
for fixed
\mathbb{P}_{Y|a}
and the EIF of
T(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\cdot)
for a fixed
\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}
. To recover these EIFs, we introduce the linear functionals

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)})

\displaystyle=\int g_{\theta}(y)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(dy)=\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(Y(a))]

\displaystyle\Lambda_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y|A=a})

\displaystyle=\int\chi_{\theta}(\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y})=\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\theta}(Y)\mid A=a].

where
\chi_{\theta}(\tilde{y}):=\int r_{\theta}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)}(dy)
. Note that, for any probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
,

T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})=\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{1}),\qquad T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\mathbb{P}_{2})=\Lambda_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{2}),

The first functional
\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)})=\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(Y(a))]
is a
counterfactual mean of the vector-valued outcome
g_{\theta}(Y)
.
Its efficient influence function is therefore
given by the standard AIPW expression for a counterfactual mean
(e.g.,
18
),

\varphi_{\Lambda_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)})}(O)=\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi_{a}(X)}\bigl(g_{\theta}(Y)-\mu_{\theta}(X)\bigr)+\mu_{\theta}(X)-\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(Y(a))].

The second functional
\Lambda_{2}(P_{Y\mid a})=\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\theta}(Y)\mid A=a]

is a stratum mean of the scalar outcome
\chi_{\theta}(Y)
. Its efficient
influence function is therefore
(e.g.,
18
)

\varphi_{\Lambda_{2}(P_{Y\mid a})}(O)=\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{p_{a}}\Bigl(\chi_{\theta}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\theta}(Y)\mid A=a]\Bigr),\qquad p_{a}:=\mathbb{P}(A=a).

As mentioned above, by the chain rule (Lemma
D.4
), the efficient influence function of

m_{a}(\theta)=T_{\theta}(\mathbb{P}_{Y(a)},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})
is the sum of these two
components. Using that

\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(Y(a))]=m_{a}(\theta)
and

\mathbb{E}[\mu_{\theta}(X)]=\mathbb{E}[g_{\theta}(Y(a))]
,
we obtain

\varphi_{\theta,a}(O)=\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{p_{a}}\Bigl(\chi_{\theta}(Y)-\mathbb{E}[\chi_{\theta}(Y)\mid A=a]\Bigr)+\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi_{a}(X)}\bigl(g_{\theta}(Y)-\mu_{\theta}(X)\bigr)+\mu_{\theta}(X)-\mathbb{E}[\mu_{\theta}(X)],

which is the expression stated in
Theorem

4.1
.

Asymptotic Analysis of Debiased (One-Step) Estimator in

Theorem D.13

(Asymptotic expansion and efficiency of the one-step estimator)
.

Under Assumption
D.10
, the following expansion holds:

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})-m(\theta_{a},\mathbb{P})=\underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\varphi_{\theta_{a}}(\cdot;\mathbb{P})\big]}_{=:T_{1}}+\underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\hat{\varphi}_{\theta_{a},1}-\varphi_{\theta_{a},1}(\cdot;\mathbb{P})\big]}_{=:T_{2}}+R_{n}(\theta_{a}),

(13)

where the von Mises remainder satisfies

R_{n}(\theta_{a})=O_{p}\left(\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\hat{h}_{a,\theta_{a}}-h_{a,\theta_{a}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}\right).

(14)

and the empirical process drift term
T_{2}
satisfies

T_{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|\hat{h}_{a,\theta_{a}}-h_{a,\theta_{a}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}+\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(15)

Proof.

Since all analysis is for a fixed
a,\theta_{a}
, in what follows we suppress the dependence of all quantities on these objects notationally.

To start, note that by the definition of the one-step estimator
\hat{m}
we can write

\displaystyle m(\mathbb{P})-\hat{m}

\displaystyle=m(\mathbb{P})-m(\hat{\mathbb{P}})-\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi})

\displaystyle=m(\mathbb{P})-m(\hat{\mathbb{P}})-\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{1})-\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{2})

(16)

Now, first note that
\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{2})=0
. To see this, recall that

\hat{\varphi}_{2}:o\mapsto\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\hat{\psi}_{2}(o,\cdot)]
, where

\hat{\psi}_{2}(o^{\prime},y)=\frac{\bm{1}\{a^{\prime}=a\}}{\hat{p}_{a}}\big(\hat{\chi}(y^{\prime})-\hat{\chi}(y)\big)
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{2})=\mathbb{P}_{n}\left[\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}\left(\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\hat{p}_{a}}\big(\hat{\chi}(Y)-\hat{\chi}(\cdot)\big)\right)\right].

By Fubini’s theorem and the definition of
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}
,

\mathbb{P}_{n}(\hat{\varphi}_{2})=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}\left[\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}\mathbb{P}_{n}\left[\bm{1}\{A=a\}\hat{\chi}(Y)\right]-\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}\{A=a\}]\hat{\chi}(y)\right].

But
\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}\{A=a\}]=\hat{p}_{a}
and

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\hat{\chi}]=\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}\{A=a\}\hat{\chi}(Y)].

Hence the two terms cancel exactly.

Now, adding and subtracting
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\varphi}_{1})
and
(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})
to (
16
) we get

\hat{m}-m(\mathbb{P})=\underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})}_{=:T_{1}}+\underbrace{(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\hat{\varphi}_{1}-\varphi_{1})}_{=:T_{2}}+R_{2}

where

R_{2}:=m(\hat{\mathbb{P}})+\mathbb{P}(\hat{\varphi}_{1})-m(\hat{\mathbb{P}})-(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{2})

We now analyze each term.

Empirical Process Term
\bm{T}_{2}

For
o^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},a^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, define

\Delta(o^{\prime},y):=\hat{\psi}_{1}(o^{\prime},y)-\psi_{1}(o^{\prime},y).

All statements below are conditional on the training fold. In particular,

\hat{h}
and
\hat{\pi}
(and hence
\hat{\psi}_{1}
and
\Delta
) are deterministic,
while all expectations are taken with respect to the true law
\mathbb{P}
.

By the square–integrability assumptions in
D.10
,
we may apply the sample–splitting empirical–process bound established in

Lemma

D.7
(Appendix
D.1
), which yields

(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\hat{\varphi}-\varphi)=O_{p}\left(\frac{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\Delta(O,y)\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)\right]\right)^{1/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(17)

It therefore suffices to bound the
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})
norm of
\Delta

in terms of the nuisance estimation errors.

For
O=(X,A,Y)
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
, we have the exact decomposition

\displaystyle\Delta(O,y)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\hat{\pi}(X)}\big(r(Y,y)-\hat{h}(X,y)\big)+\hat{h}(X,y)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad-\left[\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{\pi(X)}\big(r(Y,y)-h(X,y)\big)+h(X,y)\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\big(\hat{h}(X,y)-h(X,y)\big)}_{=:D_{h}(X,y)}+\underbrace{\bm{1}\{A=a\}\left(\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}-\frac{1}{\pi(X)}\right)\big(r(Y,y)-h(X,y)\big)}_{=:D_{\pi}(O,y)}

\displaystyle\qquad-\underbrace{\bm{1}\{A=a\}\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}\big(\hat{h}(X,y)-h(X,y)\big)}_{=:D_{\pi h}(O,y)}.

By Assumption
D.10
(i),
\hat{\pi}
is clipped so that

\hat{\pi}(X)\geq\epsilon/2
almost surely. Consequently,

\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon},\qquad\left\|\frac{1}{\hat{\pi}(X)}-\frac{1}{\pi(X)}\right\|\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}}\,\|\hat{\pi}(X)-\pi(X)\|\quad\text{a.s.}

Using
\|u+v+w\|^{2}\leq 3(\|u\|^{2}+\|v\|^{2}+\|w\|^{2})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|\Delta(O,y)\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)\right]\leq 3\sum_{\star\in\{h,\pi,\pi h\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|D_{\star}(O,y)\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)\right].

First, by definition we have

(19)

(20)

Finally, for the propensity term, note that

\|r(Y,y)-h(X,y)\|^{2}\leq 2\|r(Y,y)\|^{2}+2\|h(X,y)\|^{2},

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\int\|D_{\pi}(O,y)\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{8}{\epsilon^{4}}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\pi}(X)-\pi(X)\|^{2}\bm{1}\{A=a\}\int\big(\|r(Y,y)\|^{2}+\|h(X,y)\|^{2}\big)\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(dy)\right]

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}\,\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}^{2},

for a finite constant

depending only on

and the second moments
of

and

Assumption
D.10
(iv).

Substituting (

)

II(a) Product term

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a})[w\Delta_{h}]

II(a).1 Population term
\bm{C}_{1}

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\leq\frac{2}{c}\,\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}.

Combining the above bounds yields

\big|(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})[w\Delta_{h}]\big|\leq\frac{2}{c}\,\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\hat{h}-h\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}

C_{1}=O_{p}\left(\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\hat{h}-h\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}\right)

II(a).2 Stochastic base term

Substituting this definition of

\displaystyle C_{2}=(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}))[w\Delta_{h}]

\displaystyle=(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})F

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}\,(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{a}}\,(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big]+\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}-\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big)\mathbb{P}\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big],

where the second last equality used the identities

\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[F]=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}_{a}F]}{\hat{p}_{a}}
,
\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[F]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[\bm{1}_{a}F]}{p_{a}}.
and the last equality used
1/\hat{p}_{a}=1/p_{a}+(1/\hat{p}_{a}-1/p_{a})
.

To control the first term, note that, conditional on the training fold,
\bm{1}\{A=a\}F(Y)
is a fixed
square-integrable function.
Indeed, for each fixed
\tilde{y}
, Cauchy–Schwarz in
L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})
yields

\|F(\tilde{y})\|=\left\|\int w(x)\Delta_{h}(x,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{X}(dx)\right\|\leq\|w\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\Delta_{h}(\cdot,\tilde{y})\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}.

Squaring and integrating with respect to
\tilde{y}\sim\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
gives

\displaystyle\|F\|^{2}_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}=\int\|F(\tilde{y})\|^{2}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}(d\tilde{y})\leq\|w\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}^{2}\;\|\Delta_{h}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}^{2},

(25)

Since
\|F\|_{L_{2}}\geq\|1_{a}F\|_{L_{2}}
, we can apply
Lemma

D.5
, to bound
(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big]
asymptotically as

(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big]=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|\bm{1}\{A=a\}F(Y)\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|F\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

It remains to control the correction term involving
(1/\hat{p}_{a}-1/p_{a})
. Since
\mathbb{P}[\bm{1}_{a}F]=p_{a}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[F]
and
(1/\hat{p}_{a}-1/p_{a})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),
by Jensen’s equality we obtain

\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}-\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big)\mathbb{P}\big[\bm{1}_{a}F\big]\leq\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}-\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big)\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[F]\leq\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}-\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big)\|F\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}

By the Central Limit Theorem
\hat{p}_{a}-p_{a}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
and

1/\hat{p}_{a}=O_{p}(1)
, so
(1/\hat{p}_{a}-1/p_{a})=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
(the latter follows from
\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{y}=\frac{1}{xy}(x-y)
). As a consequence we obtain

\Big(\frac{1}{\hat{p}_{a}}-\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big)\mathbb{P}[\bm{1}_{a}F]=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|F\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Summing the
O_{p}
bounds for both terms and applying the inequalities in (
25
) and (
24
) gives

C_{2}=O_{p}\left(\frac{\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\hat{h}-h\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

Putting this together with
C_{1}
, we get

B_{1}=O_{p}\left(\|\hat{\pi}-\pi\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\;\|\hat{h}-h\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}\right)

II(b): Drift term
\bm{B}_{2}
.

Applying Fubini/Tonelli, we can write the first part of the drift term as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{h}-\mu_{h})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{X}\Big[\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}\big[h(X,\cdot)\big]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\big[h(X,\cdot)\big]\Big]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}\big[\mathbb{P}_{X}[h(\cdot,\cdot)]\big]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}\big[\mathbb{P}_{X}[h(\cdot,\cdot)]\big]

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[g]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[g].

(26)

Now, by definition
Definition

D.9
,

\varphi_{2}(O)=\frac{\bm{1}\{A=a\}}{p_{a}}\Big(\chi(Y)-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi]\Big),\qquad\mathbb{P}[\varphi_{2}]=0.

Hence

\displaystyle(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{n}(\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p_{a}}\Big(\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}\{A=a\}\chi(Y)]-\mathbb{P}_{n}[\bm{1}\{A=a\}]\;\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi]\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\hat{p}_{a}}{p_{a}}\,\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\chi]-\frac{\hat{p}_{a}}{p_{a}}\,\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi].

Usign these definitions we get

\displaystyle B_{2}=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{h}-\mu_{h})-(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{2})

\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{\hat{p}_{a}}{p_{a}}\Big)\big(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\chi]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi]\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\hat{p}_{a}-p_{a}}{p_{a}}\,\big(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\chi]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi]\big).

Now, by the CLT for the mean of
A
and conditional mean of
Y
, we have
\hat{p}_{a}-p_{a}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
and
\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid A=a}[\chi]-\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}[\chi]=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})

(since
\chi\in L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})
).
Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(\hat{\mu}_{h}-\mu_{h})-(\mathbb{P}_{n}-\mathbb{P})(\varphi_{2})=O_{p}(n^{-1}).

Putting this together with
B_{1}
yields the stated result for
R_{2}
and completes the proof.

∎

Corollary D.14

(Efficiency bound in the main text)
.

Suppose the conditions of
Theorem

D.13
hold. In addition, assume
that for each
\theta_{a}\in\Theta
the score kernel
r_{\theta_{a}}
is uniformly
Lipschitz in its first argument, i.e. there exists
L<\infty
such that for all

\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and all probability measures
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Y})
,

\Big|\int r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{1}(dy)-\int r_{\theta_{a}}(y,\tilde{y})\,\mathbb{P}_{2}(dy)\Big|\;\leq\;L\,W_{1}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}).

Then the one-step estimator satisfies

\hat{m}_{\mathrm{DR}}(\theta_{a})-m(\theta_{a})=O_{p}(n^{-\frac{1}{2}})+O_{p}\!\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\big(\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}+\Delta_{1}\big)+\|\hat{\pi}_{a}-\pi_{a}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}\,\Delta_{1}\right).

where
\Delta_{1}:=\Big\|W_{1}\!\big(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,X},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a,X}\big)\Big\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X})}.

Proof.

By the assumed Lipschitz property of
r_{\theta_{a}}
, for all
x
and

\tilde{y}
,

\big|\hat{h}_{a,\theta_{a}}(x,\tilde{y})-h_{a,\theta_{a}}(x,\tilde{y})\big|\leq L\,W_{1}\!\big(\hat{\mathbb{P}}_{Y\mid a,X=x},\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a,X=x}\big).

Squaring both sides and integrating with respect to

\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a}
yields

\|\hat{h}_{a,\theta_{a}}-h_{a,\theta_{a}}\|_{L_{2}(\mathbb{P}_{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}_{Y\mid a})}\leq L\Delta_{1}

The result then follows by substituting this bound into the expansion of

Theorem

D.13
.
∎
