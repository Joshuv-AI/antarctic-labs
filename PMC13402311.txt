Carbon nanotube fiber electrodes modified with surface-arrayed nanowires for enhanced mass transport in electrochemical nanofabrication
Authors: Lingchao Meng, Pengxiang Yi, Yabing Li, Zhicheng Ai, Yongchao Yang, Hao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbon nanotube fiber (CNF) is a promising wire electrode material for wire electrochemical micromachining due to its high strength, electrical conductivity, and corrosion resistance. However, the smooth and hydrophobic surface of pristine CNF promotes bubble adhesion and interfacial velocity slip, which severely restricts mass transport and degrades machining stability. In this study, a surface-arrayed nanowire-modified carbon nanotube fiber electrode is proposed to enhance mass transport during micromachining. Vertically aligned NiCo₂S₄ nanowires, with an average height of approximately 10 μm, were grown in situ on the fiber surface, resulting in a surface that is both microtextured and functionalized. The nanowire array effectively penetrates the gas cushion, suppresses boundary velocity slip, and strengthens fluid-solid coupling. Flow field simulations and theoretical analysis demonstrate intensified near-wall convection and improved mass transport induced by the microtextured surface. Wire electrochemical micromachining experiments on Ni-based metallic glass show that the modified electrode significantly improves machining performance. The maximum stable feed rate was increased to 0.8 μm/s, the power fluctuation frequency was reduced from 2.2/min to 0.2/min, and the surface roughness Ra was decreased by approximately 60% to 25.5 nm. In addition, high-precision batch fabrication of 25 stepped cantilever micro-components was successfully achieved using the multiwire and multilayer electrochemical machining with a relative standard deviation below 0.1%, demonstrating the engineering applicability of the proposed electrode. This work provides a practical approach for overcoming mass transport limitations in wire electrochemical micromachining through electrode surface microstructure engineering.

Introduction

The ongoing push toward miniaturization in aerospace and biomedical fields, such as interventional surgical robots, implantable neural interfaces, and bio-absorbable vascular stents, has driven the development of high-precision micromanufacturing technologies1,2. In these applications, surface integrity is critical, as microcracks or thermal alteration can compromise biocompatibility and fatigue life. Among micromachining approaches, wire electrochemical micromachining (WECMM) has attracted considerable attention. Fundamentally, WECMM removes metallic materials through anodic dissolution at the atomic and ionic levels, which theoretically endows it with nanofabrication capabilities. Furthermore, because the process is conducted at room temperature, it generates no residual machining stress and avoids any microstructural deterioration, thereby fully preserving the excellent intrinsic properties of hard-to-machine conductive materials such as superalloys, titanium alloys, and metallic glasses3. In WECMM, machining accuracy critically depends on maintaining a narrow inter-electrode gap (IEG), typically below 20 μm, in which efficient mass transport and controlled electrolyte flow are required to stabilize the electrochemical environment and suppress accumulation of electrolytic products4.

However, the confined geometry of the IEG inherently restricts mass transport. Hydrogen bubbles generated at the wire electrode surface tend to accumulate and form a transient gas cushion, distorting the current density distribution and inducing interfacial velocity slip5. Such a gas cushion weakens fluid-solid coupling and reduces the ability of the reciprocating wire to drive fresh electrolyte into the machining zone, leading to unstable dissolution, increased ohmic resistance, and frequent power fluctuation. In addition to mass-transport limitations, Ni-based metallic glass, which is increasingly used in orthopedic and orthodontic applications due to its high elastic limit and corrosion resistance, is prone to passivation in acidic electrolytes. The formation of a dense oxide layer hinders continuous dissolution6,7. While bipolar pulse currents have been shown to depassivate the electrode surface and improve machining stability, they cannot eliminate the severe gas cushion effect in the narrow IEG8,9. Therefore, combining a high-performance electrode structure with an optimized pulse waveform is necessary to address these limitations. Although axial reciprocating motion of the wire electrode can enhance flushing efficiency, the extent of improvement strongly depends on the surface drag characteristics of the electrode. Conventional metallic wires, such as tungsten, platinum, and molybdenum, possess inherently smooth surfaces that generate limited fluid-solid coupling, thereby constraining further enhancement of mass transport.

To overcome the limitations of inherently smooth surfaces, recent studies have explored surface modification strategies for conventional metallic wires. For instance, geometric macroscopic modifications, such as the utilization of rotating helical electrodes, have been developed to mechanically stir the electrolyte and facilitate the evacuation of hydrogen bubbles and electrolytic products10. At the microscale, nanosecond laser ablation has been employed to fabricate spike-like microstructures on tungsten wires, which successfully improves surface hydrophilicity and significantly enhances mass transport efficiency during axial reciprocating motion11. However, these conventional modification strategies face inherent limitations. Directly applying physical texturing to wire electrodes often compromises their tensile strength and structural integrity, making them vulnerable to breakage during reciprocating motion. Furthermore, simple physical texturing struggles to achieve the ideal superhydrophilic state required to thoroughly eliminate the gas cushion effect. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop a new type of wire electrode that can simultaneously support hierarchical surface microtextures and robust mechanical stability.

Carbon nanotube fiber (CNF) has recently emerged as an alternative wire electrode material due to its high electrical conductivity, corrosion resistance, and hierarchical bundle structure12–14. Advances in wet spinning and dry spinning techniques have enabled the fabrication of CNF with mechanical and electrical properties suitable for microelectrochemical applications15. However, pristine CNF typically exhibits a relatively smooth and hydrophobic surface16,17, which promotes bubble adhesion and exacerbates interfacial slip, an undesirable characteristic for WECMM where strong fluid-solid coupling is required. Although surface treatments such as acid oxidation or electrochemical activation can improve CNF hydrophilicity, they primarily modify surface chemistry and introduce limited surface microtopography, which is insufficient to disrupt the gas cushion responsible for interfacial slip18. Consequently, existing CNF modification strategies do not simultaneously enhance surface roughness and superhydrophilicity, which is essential for suppressing interfacial slip and strengthening mass transport in the narrow IEG.

To address this limitation, we developed a surface-arrayed nanowire-modified carbon nanotube fiber (SA-CNF) to fundamentally enhance mass transport during WECMM. A vertically aligned NiCo₂S₄ nanowire array was synthesized in situ on the CNF surface via a controllable hydrothermal method19,20. NiCo₂S₄ provided a conductive, stable, and highly wettable surface that improved fluid-solid coupling and mass transport. Simulations and experiments showed that SA-CNF enhanced flow dynamics, doubled the feed rate, and improved machining stability and surface finish compared with pristine CNF. These results demonstrated the potential of the proposed strategy for high-precision electrochemical nanofabrication in MEMS and biomedical manufacturing.

Mechanistic analysis

Influence of wall slip on mass transport in reciprocating WECMM

In WECMM, mass transport within the narrow IEG critically affects machining stability and surface quality. Natural convection alone cannot sufficiently refresh the diffusion layer, so axial reciprocating motion of the wire electrode is introduced to enhance local flow and ion transport. As shown in Fig. 1, this motion generates periodic shear flows and transient pressure differences, which continuously disturb and renew the diffusion layer, improving ion replenishment and product removal. This forced convection reduces concentration polarization and suppresses bubble accumulation, especially in high-viscosity or low-diffusivity electrolytes where stable conductivity and uniform anodic dissolution are required.Fig. 1Schematic of mass transport enhancement via wire electrode axial reciprocating motion.a Schematic of the reciprocating WECMM process. b Comparison of bubble and electrolytic product transport around CNF and SA-CNF

Research indicates that slip behavior at the solid-liquid interface depends on microscopic wall morphology21,22. Surface macrotextures modify roughness and local flow fields, forming stagnation zones that enhance near-wall adhesion and reduce the slip length b2. Surface protrusions also disrupt streamlines and generate micro-vortices, promoting momentum exchange and suppressing interfacial slip. Debnath demonstrated that periodic macrotextures on tungsten wires enhance electrolyte flushing and reduce product accumulation within the IEG23. Therefore, modifying electrode surface morphology is an effective way to improve mass transport. Based on this, the proposed SA-CNF electrode features a surface-arrayed nanowire structure, and a fluid dynamics simulation was established to compare velocity distributions around SA-CNF and CNF electrodes, clarifying its effect on flow and slip.

Simulations were first conducted to establish a baseline by examining the y-direction velocity distribution of CNF over a quarter motion cycle (t = 0–0.05 s). As shown in Fig. 4c, the flow exhibits typical Couette characteristics. At t = 0, due to fluid viscosity and no-slip conditions, the fluid near the electrode reached a peak velocity, with momentum transferred mainly via viscous effects. However, the smooth surface led to rapid radial velocity decay and a stable laminar structure, limiting disturbance far from the wall and reducing mass transport efficiency.

In contrast, the SA-CNF results in Fig. 4d show boundary layer thickening and fluid trapping. High-velocity regions extend toward the gap center, and fluid between nanowires moves synchronously with the electrode. As further confirmed in Fig. 4b, the SA-CNF velocity profile deviates from linear decay, showing a nonlinear convex distribution. Within 0–2 μm, velocity remains near peak, forming a stable locked zone, while the overall curve indicates slower kinetic energy decay. This behavior results from stronger form resistance induced by microstructures, enhancing momentum transfer and increasing flow rate. Consequently, SA-CNF improves fluid transport, facilitating the removal of bubbles and electrolytic products in the IEG.

Experimental

System and Instrumentation

As shown in Fig. 5, the experimental system consisted of a numerically controlled XYZ motion stage, a piezoelectric transducer, a programmable bipolar nanosecond pulse generator, an oscilloscope, a computer-controlled charge-coupled device camera, a multifunction electrolyte tank, and associated fixtures. The multifunction electrolyte tank comprised two separate sections to accommodate different experimental stages. The first section was used for conductivity testing and connection to the fixture. A metallic coating was electroplated at the junction between the CNF and the wire electrode fixture to ensure a stable electrical interface. The second section served as the main processing zone. The CNF was mounted on the wire electrode fixture. The Z-axis motion stage was employed to generate axial reciprocating motion of the fiber, thereby assisting mass transport in the IEG during both electrochemical surface modification and WECMM.Fig. 5Schematic diagram of the WECMM experimental system.The system consists of the motion stages, nanosecond pulse power supply, oscilloscope, CCD camera, multifunction electrolyte tank, and control units

Materials

In the WECMM experiments, a CNF and a Ni-based metallic glass sample (Ni72Cr19Si7B2) were used as the wire electrode and the anodic workpiece, respectively. Owing to its high corrosion resistance, the metallic glass exhibits typical passivation behavior in dilute acidic electrolytes. Previous studies have reported that metallic glass tends to generate a larger number of electrolytic products than crystalline alloys during WECMM24.

To ensure the stability and uniformity of the wire electrode during machining, the preparation process of the CNF is described as follows. First, continuous and self-supporting carbon nanotube sheets were drawn from a spinnable forest via a dry-spinning process, as shown in Fig. 6a. Through this continuous drawing and subsequent twisting process, as shown in Fig. 6b, uniform carbon fibers were prepared. Multiple straight CNF bundles were then twisted into a single yarn and subsequently passed through an ethanol bath to induce densification, resulting in a helical fiber with periodic surface textures. The twisting process maintained dimensional consistency, ensuring the structural uniformity of the fiber. After being rinsed repeatedly with deionized water and dried at 60 °C, the pristine CNF was obtained. The uniformity of this fabricated fiber was confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, as shown in Fig. 6c, which exhibits a continuous and uniform helical morphology. A high magnification view of the surface texture shown in Fig. 6d demonstrates that the constituent carbon nanotube bundles are densely packed and tightly aligned along the twisting axis. Furthermore, the energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum shown in Fig. 6e confirms its chemical purity, which is dominated by a carbon peak at 99.14 atomic percent with negligible trace amounts of silicon and iron. In practice, the effective working length is selected according to the thickness of the workpiece. For instance, an electrode length of about 100 μm is utilized for machining a 50 μm thick single-layer metallic glass. Depending on the thickness of different workpieces, the length of the electrodes can be prepared to around or above 500 μm.Fig. 6Fabrication and characterization of the CNF.a, b Schematic illustration of the CNT drawing and the helical twisting for CNF fabrication. c, d SEM images showing the overall morphology and surface texture of the CNF (including 3D/2D profiles). e EDX spectrum of the CNF surface

Surface modification process

The hydrothermal method is a widely used approach for nanomaterial synthesis, offering high crystallinity, good dispersion, high purity, controllable morphology, and low fabrication cost. In this work, a hydrothermal growth strategy was employed to construct surface-arrayed nanowires on CNF, thereby enhancing surface microtexture and improving electrode hydrophilicity. The resulting surface-arrayed nanostructure consisted of densely packed and vertically aligned NiCo₂S₄ nanowires with high uniformity. Compared with the pristine CNF surface, the SA-CNF exhibited a larger specific surface area and a well-developed porous network, as shown in Fig. 7. The nanowire synthesis process mainly involved two steps. First, Ni²⁺ and Co³⁺ ions in the solution reacted to form a NiCo₂O₄ precursor. Subsequently, a sulfidation reaction occurred to generate the NiCo₂S₄ nanostructure25. In the raw materials used in this study, graphene quantum dots are quasi-zero-dimensional nanomaterials with all three dimensions smaller than 100 nm. Their primary role is to introduce additional nanoscale textures onto the NiCo₂S₄ nanowires, thereby increasing the effective surface area and enhancing the electrical conductivity of the surface-arrayed structure. The experimental parameters in Table 2 were optimized through a series of comparative experiments. The solution composition and temperature dictated the initial nucleation and the feasibility of nanowire growth and were therefore fixed. The growth time governed the morphology of the nanowire arrays and was selected as the primary variable. Regarding the specific chemical kinetics, the nickel and cobalt salts were maintained at a 1–2 molar ratio to meet the stoichiometric requirement for the NiCo2S4 phase. The combination of solvent and urea ensured a controlled release of hydroxyl ions to maintain a supersaturation level that promotes uniform heterogeneous nucleation on the fiber surface while avoiding bulk precipitation. Graphene quantum dots were incorporated at a mass of 0.1 mg as a structure-directing agent to provide active sites and regulate nucleation kinetics. A hydrothermal temperature of 120 °C provided the thermodynamic driving force for growth while preventing thermal degradation of the CNF substrate. The sulfidation process utilizing 200 mg of Na2S at 150 °C for 3 h ensured anion exchange into crystalline NiCo2S4 without causing structural collapse of the nanowire skeleton.

Characterization techniques

The surface morphology and hierarchical nanostructures of pristine and SA-CNF were systematically characterized using field emission scanning electron microscopy equipped with EDS for elemental analysis. The wettability of the fiber electrodes was evaluated by measuring the meniscus height at the solid-liquid-vapor interface using the Wilhelmy method, serving as a direct indicator of surface hydrophilicity. Furthermore, the surface quality and average roughness of the machined metallic glass slit sidewalls were analyzed via atomic force microscopy (AFM) and high-resolution SEM. To guarantee data reproducibility, all reported values represented the average of at least three individual samples.

To evaluate the wettability of the wire electrodes, the Wilhelmy method was employed. As demonstrated in previous studies on WECMM26, this method is used to characterize curved microwires. In the Wilhelmy model, a meniscus spontaneously forms at the three-phase contact line driven by capillary or surface tension forces, and an increase in meniscus height indicates improved hydrophilicity of the wire. Enhanced performance of WECMM

In the experiments, the thickness of the electrolytic products layer increased progressively with machining time, accompanied by a corresponding rise in power fluctuation frequency. Figure 10a shows the time-dependent evolution of both the adhered electrolytic products layer thickness and the power fluctuation frequency on the wire electrode surface. These quantitative variations are consistent with the corresponding morphological observations shown in Fig. 10b. During the initial 30 min of machining, accumulation of electrolytic products remained minimal and the power fluctuation frequency stayed low, ranging from 0 to 2/min. After approximately 60 min, the electrolytic products layer with a thickness of about 1.1 μm was formed, accompanied by an increase in fluctuation frequency to 7/min. At 90 min, the thickness further increased to 2.3 μm with a corresponding frequency of 13/min. Subsequently, the fluctuation frequency increased to 20/min at 120 min. As machining proceeded to 150 min, the electrolytic products layer thickness increased to approximately 6.2 μm, completely obscuring the original surface morphology, while the fluctuation frequency sharply increased to 33/min. By 180 min, the electrolytic products layer reached a thickness of about 9.1 μm, and the fluctuation frequency peaked at 48/min. When electrolytic products adhere to the wire electrode, the adhered layer with a thickness of Δd can be simplified as a flat-contact model. The dynamic equilibrium of the adhered particle is governed by the following force balance:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${F}_{{\rm{g}}}+{F}_{{\rm{\tau }}}+{F}_{{\rm{b}}}+{F}_{{\rm{e}}} > {F}_{{\rm{v}}}+{F}_{{\rm{c}}}$$\end{document}Fg+Fτ+Fb+Fe>Fv+Fcwhere Fg, Fτ, Fb, and Fe represent gravity, fluid shear force, buoyancy, and electrophoretic force, which promote detachment. Conversely, Fv and Fc denote the wall viscous force and surface tension that hinder detachment. The fluid shear force Fτ is a function of the viscosity coefficient μ and reciprocating speed v. Theoretical calculations indicate that a critical speed vcrit of 33.6 mm/s is required for detachment. In these experiments, the axial speed vactual was approximately 1.0 mm/s, which is insufficient to overcome the adhesion forces Fv and Fc. This kinematic limitation indicates that vibration alone is insufficient to suppress electrolytic products adhesion on smooth electrodes, thereby necessitating functional surface modification of the electrode.

To evaluate whether SA-CNF overcomes this bottleneck, comparative experiments were conducted to examine mass transport differences. As shown in Fig. 11a, pristine CNF exhibited insufficient transport capacity: although most bubbles detached, residual bubbles accumulated and coalesced around the electrode, while little debris was expelled. After 60 min, the fiber surface was covered with black electrolytic products, forming a thick, irregular layer in Fig. 11b, indicating poor transport. Compared to the notable edge deterioration produced by the pristine CNF, the SA-CNF successfully preserved high geometric accuracy and sharp structural profiles. Further localized magnification reveals that the slit sidewalls machined by the pristine fiber were uneven, with conspicuous defects on the end face, as shown in Fig. 12c. In contrast, the SA-CNF yielded uniform sidewalls and a smooth, reflective end face as depicted in Fig. 12d. Figure 12e, f shows the significant improvement in surface integrity. To evaluate the engineering application potential of the proposed method, a high-efficiency batch machining experiment was performed. Figure 12h illustrates a simultaneous machining configuration with five wire electrodes and five stacked layers, enabling the fabrication of 25 micro-components in a single run and thus improving the processing efficiency by 25 times. This configuration places stringent demands on mass transport within the deep IEG. Compared with conventional CNF electrodes, the superhydrophilic nanowire surface of the SA-CNF electrode effectively suppresses wall slip, enhances fluid-solid interaction, and facilitates the replenishment of fresh electrolyte in deep channels. The dimensional consistency across the five layers was assessed, and the results in Fig. 12i show highly overlapping measurement curves. The relative standard deviation is below 0.1%, while the maximum dimensional deviation between the innermost and outermost layers is only 0.41 µm. No obvious electrode wear was observed after prolonged machining.

Conclusions

A surface-arrayed nanowire-modified CNF wire electrode was developed and systematically investigated for high-performance WECMM. The main conclusions are summarized as follows.NiCo₂S₄ nanowire arrays were synthesized in situ on the surface of CNF via a controllable hydrothermal method. Vertically aligned nanowires with a typical height of approximately 10 μm created a superhydrophilic interface and achieved geometric compatibility with the boundary layer thickness, enabling effective penetration into the gas cushion.Theoretical analysis combined with flow field simulations demonstrated that the hierarchical surface microtexture significantly suppresses boundary velocity slip by reducing slip length and disrupting the insulating gas cushion. Enhanced fluid-solid coupling allowed the reciprocating wire electrode to function as an efficient pump for mass transport and bubble detachment.WECMM experiments on Ni-based metallic glass confirmed superior machining stability and efficiency compared with pristine CNF. The feed rate increased to 0.8 μm/s, while power fluctuation frequency decreased from 2.2/min to 0.2/min. The surface roughness Ra was further reduced by approximately 60% to 25.5 nm, resulting in improved surface integrity.Engineering applicability was validated using a multiwire and multilayer batch machining configuration. Twenty-five stepped cantilever micro-components were simultaneously fabricated with high dimensional consistency, exhibiting a relative standard deviation below 0.1%. Uniform electrochemical dissolution was maintained even within high aspect ratio channels.
