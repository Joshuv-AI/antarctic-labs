Transcranial direct current stimulation enhances delayed retention after 5 days of lower‐limb motor skill learning
Authors: August Lomholt Kvistad, Lasse Jespersen, Jonas Rud Bjørndal, Lasse Christiansen, Anke Ninija Karabanov, Jesper Lundbye‐Jensen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) holds the potential to affect behaviour by modulating ongoing neural activity, and tDCS paired with hand motor practice can enhance motor learning. While augmenting the behavioural benefits of motor practice is relevant for neurorehabilitation following central and peripheral lesions to the motor system, as well as in sports, the effects of tDCS targeting the mesial motor cortex (M1‐Leg) during multi‐session lower‐limb motor skill practice remain unexplored. We tested whether 5 days of anodal tDCS over M1‐Leg during training of a sequential visuomotor tracking task improves within‐ and between‐session learning and 1‐week retention. Participants were randomized to skill practice with active tDCS, skill practice with sham stimulation, or volume‐matched non‐skilled ankle movements with sham stimulation. Changes in corticospinal excitability accompanying skill and non‐skill motor practice with real and sham tDCS, were assessed as motor‐evoked potential amplitudes recorded from the tibialis anterior muscle at rest. Compared with non‐skill practice, motor skill practice yielded robust sequence‐specific performance gains that transferred to the untrained leg and persisted for at least 1 week after practice ended. Concurrent tDCS did not increase learning within or between training sessions, but it did lead to improved one‐week retention compared with sham stimulation. Corticospinal excitability did not increase after practice and was unaffected by tDCS. These findings suggest that combining lower‐limb motor skill practice with tDCS over the M1‐Leg can enhance retention of skill learning without measurable changes in resting corticospinal excitability. This is relevant for motor practice scheduling and potentially in neurorehabilitation. Key points Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a weak electrical brain stimulation that may boost learning when paired with motor practice; however, its effects during lower‐limb skill training are not well known.We tested whether stimulation over the leg area of the motor cortex during 5 days of ankle skill training improves learning, consolidation and delayed retention.Ankle skill training produced clear sequence‐specific improvements that transferred to the untrained leg and were still present 1 week later.Stimulation did not increase short‐term learning, but it did improve 1‐week retention compared with sham stimulation.Corticospinal excitability did not change with motor training or stimulation, consistent with the possibility that the observed retention effect relies on mechanisms that do not evoke sustained changes in resting corticospinal excitability (e.g. state‐dependent or network‐level processes).

Introduction

Human motor skill learning encompasses our ability to acquire, refine and retain new motor skills, underpinning everything from daily activities to high‐level athletic performance and rehabilitation after injury. The process of skill learning depends on repeated practice of movements and movement sequences with high spatiotemporal acuity. Plastic changes in the central nervous system are necessary for skill learning, and repeated, goal‐directed practice is essential for inducing the synaptic and network‐level plasticity that underlies durable skill gains (Peters et al., 2017; Roth & Ding, 2024). Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) offers a scalable adjunct to motor practice; it is portable, low‐cost, and suitable for at‐home use. Recent evidence has shown that pairing motor practice with concurrent tDCS improves motor skill learning (Nielsen, Gad et al., 2025). However, most evidence comes from single‐session practice of upper‐limb movements, rendering both the longevity of tDCS effects, as well as the potential for boosting effects of lower extremity motor practice, unexplored. Given the prominent role of lower extremity rehabilitation in both progressive neurological disorders and following lesions to the motor system, it is pertinent to clarify whether the application of tDCS during motor practice carries long‐lasting benefits for lower‐extremity motor learning.

Direct current stimulation modulates neuronal activity by inducing weak electric fields (E‐fields) that shift firing probabilities, as demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo (Bindman et al., 1962, 1964; Lafon et al., 2017; Rahman et al., 2017; Reato et al., 2010). Although tDCS yields E‐fields in the targeted brain tissue that are substantially weaker than those needed to drive firing in vitro robustly (Liu et al., 2018; Rahman et al., 2013; Vöröslakos et al., 2018), these intensities are sufficient to bias ongoing neural activity rather than activate quiescent circuits (Bikson & Rahman, 2013). Consequently, tDCS can be considered a non‐invasive and safe (Antal et al., 2017) neuromodulatory tool that, when paired with task‐specific practice, can increase network engagement and amplify plasticity in already active neurons, while leaving unrelated pathways comparatively unaffected (Fritsch et al., 2010; Gellner et al., 2020; Kronberg et al., 2017, 2020; Madhavan & Shah, 2012; Reis & Fritsch, 2011; Wang et al., 2022). Therefore, functionally targeted tDCS applied concurrently with motor practice may offer a mechanistic route to behavioural or therapeutic gains by biasing plasticity within networks recruited during practice.

Despite the conceptual rationale, extending findings from the lateral hand representation to the mesial leg representation of the primary motor cortex (M1) is not straightforward. The lower‐limb representation (M1‐Leg) lies along the medial wall within or near the interhemispheric fissure, implying greater depth and more variable E‐field orientation relative to the local cortical geometry than in the hand ‘knob’ region (M1‐Hand). As a result, small deviations in electrode placement and individual anatomy can meaningfully alter field strength and current‐flow direction, complicating polarity‐based predictions and increasing inter‐individual variability in physiological responses (Foerster, Rezaee et al., 2018; Hamajima et al., 2023; Madhavan & Stinear, 2010). Nevertheless, empirical work indicates that tDCS can modulate corticospinal output of the leg motor representation under some protocols, supporting M1‐Leg as a plausible entry point for influencing dorsiflexion‐related control (Jeffery et al., 2007). Computational modelling further suggests that conventional and alternative montages can deliver measurable E‐fields to the leg motor area while simultaneously engaging adjacent motor regions, emphasizing that lower‐limb montages should be selected with explicit consideration of both target engagement and expected co‐stimulation (Foerster, Rezaee et al., 2018; Hamajima et al., 2023). Importantly, our framework does not assume that behavioural effects depend exclusively on focal, polarity‐specific modulation of a single cortical patch. Rather, consistent with ‘functional targeting’ accounts, weak E‐fields may bias task‐engaged circuits, thereby influencing learning‐related plasticity within the broader motor network recruited during practice (Bikson et al., 2013). In humans, tDCS has been shown to alter functional connectivity within cortico‐cortical and cortico‐striato‐thalamo‐cortical motor circuits, and such network‐level changes provide a plausible route by which stimulation delivered during practice could influence the stabilization of skill representations (Liebrand et al., 2020; Polanía et al., 2011, 2012). We further acknowledge that M1‐Leg and M1‐Hand are embedded in partly different motor‐network architectures: visually guided hand actions engage lateral frontoparietal circuits linking anterior intraparietal and premotor regions (Castiello & Begliomini, 2008; Davare et al., 2010), whereas ankle dorsiflexion and lower‐limb force control recruit a broader sensorimotor network including supplementary motor area (SMA), cingulate motor areas, basal ganglia and cerebellum (Dobkin et al., 2004; Francis et al., 2009; Yoon et al., 2014), with hand movements exhibiting stronger and more lateralized motor‐network coupling than foot movements (Volz et al., 2015). Consequently, our rationale does not assume anatomical equivalence between hand‐ and leg‐area stimulation; rather, tDCS effects during ankle skill practice are expected to be shaped by the specific lower‐limb network architecture, while remaining mechanistically plausible under functional targeting.

Mechanistically, we expected concurrent stimulation to influence both acquisition and retention via activity‐dependent plasticity. In experimental models, direct current stimulation can facilitate associative, Hebbian‐like plasticity when paired with patterned activity, and candidate pathways include NMDA receptor‐dependent mechanisms and neurotrophins signalling (e.g. BDNF/TrkB) that support long‐term synaptic stabilization (Fritsch et al., 2010; Liebetanz et al., 2002; Nitsche et al., 2003; Stagg & Nitsche, 2011). At the systems level, such plasticity could be expressed as measurable changes in corticospinal output from M1‐Leg, which we probed using resting motor‐evoked potential (MEP) amplitude as a widely used and tractable index of corticospinal excitability (CSE) in the trained effector representation. Simultaneously, we acknowledge that behavioural retention may also reflect consolidation‐related reweighting within distributed motor networks and state‐dependent changes that are not necessarily expressed as sustained shifts in resting corticospinal excitability.

In support of this, findings from predominantly upper limb motor practice lend credence to the notion that anodal tDCS applied to the M1 has been reported to augment practice‐induced plasticity, thereby enhancing motor skill learning (Buch et al., 2017; Nielsen, Gad et al., 2025; Reis et al., 2009). Evidence supporting lower‐limb applications is sparse. A few single‐session studies suggest that anodal tDCS concurrent with practice can enhance within‐session learning and 24 h retention (Foerster, Dutta et al., 2018; Shah et al., 2013; Sriraman et al., 2014). However, whether repeated pairing across days accumulates benefits for lower‐limb skill learning and delayed retention remains unknown.

Here, we address this gap by applying anodal M1‐tDCS during 5 days of lower‐limb motor skill sequence training and assessing within‐ and between‐session learning (online/offline learning) and 1 week retention of skill learning. As exploratory analyses, we additionally modelled block‐wise learning trajectories using a non‐linear growth model to derive an interpretable learning‐rate parameter as a compact summary of learning‐curve shape, and we examined whether baseline performance moderated tDCS effects. Specifically, we employ a parallel three‐arm design, including a group engaged in motor practice with concurrent active tDCS (SKILL‐STIM), one group undergoing motor practice with sham (SKILL‐SHAM), and a control group that practices without a fixed sequence and without demands for precision but with an equivalent number and amplitude of movements combined with sham tDCS (CON). To separate sequence‐specific gains from general improvements in motor acuity, we included a near‐transfer condition using a randomized target sequence. To test practice‐dependent corticospinal modulation over time, we additionally investigate CSE via MEPs before and after practice on Days 1 and 5 (Christiansen et al., 2018). We used Bayesian predictive modelling to test a priori graded hypotheses for both immediate learning and one‐week retention (SKILL‐SHAM > CON; SKILL‐STIM > SKILL‐SHAM). In parallel, we evaluated whether skilled training modulates CSE, operationalized as the pre‐to‐post change in resting MEP amplitudes obtained from the tibialis anterior muscle. Specifically, we tested the effects of motor skill practice on MEP amplitudes compared with non‐skill control (SKILL‐SHAM > CON) and whether concurrent anodal tDCS further potentiates changes (SKILL‐STIM > SKILL‐SHAM), consistent with an activity‐dependent (functional targeting) account in which stimulation may bias practice‐engaged corticospinal processes.

Methods

Ethical approval

The study was reviewed and approved by the local ethics committee (protocol H‐17019671), and all experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was not preregistered in a database before recruitment. All participants received written and oral information about the study and provided written and verbal informed consent prior to enrolment.

Participants

Sixty healthy and able‐bodied participants (28 females, aged 20–30 years) were enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were verified using a standardized health screening questionnaire confirming no history of neurological, psychiatric or musculoskeletal disorders and no current medication use. One participant withdrew during the first experimental session. The participants were randomized into three groups: (1) motor skill practice and concurrent tDCS (SKILL‐STIM), (2) motor skill practice and sham tDCS (SKILL‐SHAM) and (3) non‐skill motor practice and sham tDCS (CON). All participants performed motor practice using their non‐dominant leg, determined by preferred leg to kick a ball with which aligns with the Waterloo Footedness Questionnaire‐Revised (WFQ‐R) (Van Melick et al., 2017). Experimental design and procedure

A double‐blinded randomized between‐group design was employed. Both participants and experimenters were blinded to the type of stimulation (real vs. sham tDCS), but not to the type of motor practice (motor skill practice vs. non‐skilled movements). Participants were randomly allocated to three groups, stratified by age and sex, prior to the start of the intervention (SKILL‐STIM, n = 20; SKILL‐SHAM, n = 20; CON, n = 20). The testing and training spanned five consecutive days. The SKILL‐STIM group received real/active tDCS concurrently with motor skill practice, the SKILL‐SHAM group received sham tDCS concurrently with motor skill practice, and the CON group received sham tDCS and performed non‐skilled ankle dorsal flexion. The main experiment involved behavioural and electrophysiological testing procedures and practice of the visuomotor task for approximately 20 min/day for five consecutive days. All participants returned after 7 days for a delayed retention test. Before and after the practice sessions on Days 1 and 5, corticospinal excitability and maximal muscle compound action potential (Mmax) were measured. Motor practice continued on Days 2–5, with each session comprising six blocks of 12 movement sequences (i.e. trials). On Day 5, an EEG recording session was inserted prior to the final practice block (no stimulation), which added one extra block (seven blocks in total that day). To maintain the consistency of the behavioural/transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) outcomes across days and to keep the focus of the present report on behavioural and TMS endpoints, EEG data (and analyses tied to the EEG session) are not included here and are presented elsewhere (Kvistad et al., 2026). A randomized block (near transfer task) was added after the final sequence block. The transfer leg performance was assessed on Days 1 and 5. Follow‐up testing was conducted on Day 12 to assess skill retention. The experimental design is shown in Fig. 1B , and the procedures for behavioural and electrophysiological testing are described in detail below.

Sequential visuomotor dynamic ankle flexion task

The motor learning paradigm was adapted from the well‐established Sequential Visual Isometric Pinch Task (SVIPT) (Reis et al., 2009) but reconfigured to emphasize dynamic position control of the ankle rather than isometric force control. Therefore, we developed a sequential visuomotor dynamic ankle flexion task. Participants were seated with their non‐dominant foot secured in a custom‐built dynamic ankle force and position pedal (Fig. 1A ), which permitted free dorsiflexion and plantar flexion of the ankle joint. A goniometer tracked the ankle angle, beginning from a standardized resting position of 120°. Each participant's active range of motion was measured beforehand, and the task movement span was set to 80% of the maximum dorsiflexion excursion. Following presentation of a ‘GO’ cue, participants executed a smooth dorsiflexion through the sequence of five targets (Home–1–Home–2–Home–3–Home–4–Home–5) (Fig. 1A ). The ankle angle was mapped in real‐time to the vertical cursor position on a computer display. Upon reaching target 5, a 4 s rest period followed. One trial was defined as one complete Home‐to‐5 sequence; blocks consisted of 12 consecutive trials, followed by a rest interval. The objective was to traverse the entire sequence as rapidly and accurately as possible, and the completion time (CT) was recorded from the movement onset until the cursor reached target 5. Because the visuomotor task is a continuous, multi‐segment task, accuracy was quantified as the mean deviation rate per block, that is, the average fraction of gate‐crossing errors (either overshoots or undershoots when attempting to hit each target) across all trials, rather than simply the proportion of fully successful trials (Reis et al., 2009). Furthermore, the use of the tibialis anterior (TA) muscle enabled the investigation of CSE changes in a single lower‐limb muscle, which is readily accessible with TMS. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 1A . The visuomotor task source code is available at GitHub (https://github.com/MovementAndNeuroscience/TrackIt‐LiteV2).

Motor practice

A motor practice session consisted of six blocks of 12 trials with a 2 min break between each block. For SKILL‐STIM and SKILL‐SHAM, the visuomotor task was performed with full visual feedback from the CT and ER in every trial. Inter‐trial breaks were set to 4 s with a visual countdown. The CON group performed non‐skill ankle dorsiflexion exercises matched in number to the skilled practice. At trial onset, a baseline target appeared, and its disappearance (screen blackout for 1 s) cued a single dorsiflexion without speed–accuracy goals or performance feedback. To sustain attention and motivation, all participants received standardized verbal encouragement (‘do your best and strive for improvement in both speed and accuracy’) at the beginning of each rest period.

Validation of the speed–accuracy function for motor skill

We assessed performance in the visuomotor task by utilizing the two‐parameter speed–accuracy trade‐off (SAF) model proposed by Reis et al. (2009). We included a dedicated speed–accuracy validation set to confirm that the present apparatus and task modifications preserved the expected lawful relationship between CT and error rate (ER), thereby supporting construct validity and enabling a direct comparison with previous work using the same framework. Within this model, each trial produces an ER (the proportion of trials producing over‐ or undershoot) at a designated CT. The relationship is characterized as follows: (1) ER=11+a(ln(CT)b), where a (the skill parameter) reflects the participants’ ability to execute fast, accurate movements, and b characterizes the slope of the trade‐off. CT denotes the prescribed movement time, and ER is the observed error rate (fraction of missed targets per block). To permit log‐scale analyses, blocks with perfect accuracy (ER = 0) were assigned a nominal error rate of 0.01. To validate that the SAF model captured behaviour in our visuomotor task and apparatus, we conducted a dedicated speed–accuracy protocol in an independent sample of 19 healthy adults (mean age 27.0 ± 3.1 years; 11 females). The participants completed nine pacing conditions, each consisting of one block of 10 trials, with the target movement time (MT) fixed within each block. The prescribed MT values were 2.4, 1.9, 1.3, 1.1, 0.9, 0.8, 0.75, 0.6 and 0.5 s, presented in randomized order across blocks. Consequently, each block lasted approximately 10 × MT seconds (i.e. ∼24 s at MT = 2.4 s to ∼5 s at MT = 0.5 s), excluding brief inter‐trial transitions. MT was imposed by timing the appearance of successive targets at fixed intervals (one target every MT seconds). The participants were instructed to synchronize their movements to the target cadence (i.e. complete each movement within the prescribed MT) while still aiming to hit each target as accurately as possible. ER was computed per block as the proportion of trials that resulted in an over‐ or undershoot. Previous studies have used metronomic‐paced cueing; however, because of the nature of visuomotor paradigms, we decided to pace the participants with quantifiable visual cueing, relying purely on visuo‐motor interaction rather than including auditory sensory processing (Reis et al., 2009). Ten of these participants completed five days of practice, followed by an identical post‐test. We had fitted a and b to each individual's baseline and post‐test data using nonlinear least‐squares regression (Fig. 1C ). Examination of the resulting residuals showed no systematic deviation or trend, confirming an adequate model fit. Consistent with prior SVIPT findings, b remained stable (6.8% decrease), while a increased by 479% (Fig. 1D ) with practice, validating the model for lower‐limb position control. For our main analyses, we isolated a using algebraic inversion: (2) a=1−ERER(lnCT)b

We then applied a natural logarithmic transform (log a, henceforth skill measure) to stabilize the variance. To characterize learning, we summed within‐session skill gains over Days 1–5 (online learning) and overnight changes between sessions (offline learning). In all cases, b was fixed at 5.79, so that changes in skill reflect the modulation of the skill parameter a alone.

Stimulation technique

tDCS was applied online during motor practice and delivered through two rubber electrodes, 3 mm thick; an anode (25 cm2) was positioned over the TA hotspot identified via TMS, oriented with the plugin posteriorly to maximize current flow along the central sulcus, and the cathode (35 cm2) was placed over the contralateral supraorbital region, Fp2/Fp1 according to the 10–20 EEG system. The TA hotspot was identified on Day 1 using neuronavigation‐guided mini‐mapping (see TMS section). The corresponding scalp location was marked on the stimulation cap and used as a reference for anode placement across sessions. Cap placement was standardized across days using the same 10–20 landmarks and strap positioning, and the electrode was secured to minimize displacement during practice. The electrodes were covered with 2 mm Ten20 conductive paste (Weaver and Company, Aurora, CO, USA). The paste was evenly distributed across the electrode surface using a specialized 3D‐custom‐made device. The electrodes were fixed on the head using headbands or rubber straps. Stimulation was delivered at 2 mA for 20 min per session with a 30 s ramp‐up/down using a battery‐driven, constant‐current stimulator, Nurostym TES device (Brainbox, United Kingdom). Sham stimulation mimicked the initial ramp‐up/down but did not deliver current throughout the session. The impedance measured directly between the stimulator and electrodes was kept below 5 kΩ. Prior to electrode placement, the skin surface was treated with Nuprep (Weaver and Company, Aurora, CO, USA), a mild abrasive gel that lowers skin impedance. The participants and the investigator performing the tests were blinded to the type of tDCS using a double‐blind code for the tDCS stimulator. Note that the duration of the motor practice session varied according to the participants’ task performance (differences in CT). Thus, stimulation was sometimes terminated before session completion but never after task completion. Non‐overlap between session end and tDCS offset was 4.6 min in CON (fixed and paced by design), 4.27 ± 4.25 min in SKILL‐STIM and 4.80 ± 4.52 min in SKILL‐SHAM (mean ± SD), and this non‐overlap was comparable across groups. Participants completed a questionnaire after the last training session to determine whether they thought they had received real or sham tDCS (Table 1). Blinding efficacy was assessed using participants’ end‐of‐study guess of stimulation condition (real vs. sham; Table 1). The guess distributions did not differ across the groups (χ2 test, P = 0.66). Guess accuracy did not differ significantly from chance in any group (binomial tests; see Table 1). To estimate the E‐field distribution induced by the montage, computational modelling was conducted using SimNIBS 4.0 (https://simnibs.github.io/simnibs) (SimNibs, DRCMR and DTU, Copenhagen and Lyngby, Denmark), deploying the ‘Ernie’ head model (Saturnino et al., 2015, 2019) (Fig. 1E ). This simulation was performed on a template head to provide a representative estimate of the field distribution for the selected montage; the subject‐specific field strength and orientation are expected to vary with individual anatomy and electrode placement. Given medial‐wall folding, current‐flow direction relative to the local cortical column orientation is likely heterogeneous, with mixed radial and tangential components across nearby tissue. The magnitude of the electric field (normE) was evaluated within the grey matter (region index = 2). The peak E‐field strength, defined as the 99.9th percentile, was 0.697 V/m. The 95th and 99th percentiles were 0.328 V/m and 0.43 V/m, respectively. Focality was assessed as the volume of grey matter (in mm3) exposed to at least 50% or 75% of the peak E‐field intensity. This resulted in focality estimates of 27,000 mm3 and 3290 mm3, respectively. These values indicate that while the field intensity peaks were localized, the stimulation also engaged relatively broad cortical areas, which is consistent with conventional tDCS focality profiles. Finally, region‐of‐interest (ROI) analysis at MNI coordinates (−4, 72, 46), corresponding to the lower‐limb representation in the M1 (Francis et al., 2009), revealed a mean electric field magnitude of 0.23 V/m at the leg motor ROI in the template model, indicating non‐zero field exposure at the intended ROI while also reflecting the expected diffuse field distribution of conventional montages. The resulting ROI magnitude (0.23 V/m) lies within the range typically reported for conventional human tES and is generally considered subthreshold for directly eliciting spiking but potentially sufficient to bias ongoing activity when circuits are engaged (Bikson & Rahman, 2013; Liu et al., 2018; Vöröslakos et al., 2018). The E‐field simulation and Fig. 1E were originally reported in Kvistad et al. (2026) and are reproduced here to provide methodological context for the present study.

Electrophysiological recordings

Electromyography

Surface electromyography (EMG) was recorded from the TA and soleus (SOL) muscles of the non‐dominant leg using Ag‐AgCl electrodes (0.5 cm diameter, 1 mm between electrodes; Blue Sensor, Ambu Inc., USA) in a bipolar montage across the muscle belly and a ground electrode mounted on the patella. Signals were amplified (×200), band‐pass filtered (5 Hz–1 kHz), digitized at 2 kHz using a Cambridge Electronic Design system (CED 1401 Ltd., UK; Signal v6.05), and stored on a PC for offline analysis. Line noise was suppressed using a 50 Hz Hum Bug eliminator (Digitimer).

Peripheral nerve stimulation

To elicit maximal compound muscle action potentials, the maximal M‐wave or Mmax in the TA muscle, electrical peripheral nerve stimulation was applied to the common peroneal nerve below the caput fibulae as a 200 µs pulse delivered by a constant‐current stimulator (Digitimer DS27A, Digitimer, UK) through bipolar surface stimulation electrodes. Peripheral nerve stimulation was elicited at interstimulus intervals of 4 s, and the current was increased until the maximal peak‐to‐peak amplitude of the M‐response was elicited. Mmax was obtained immediately before generating TMS measurements, both before and after practice, to enable day‐to‐day comparisons (Day 1 and Day 5) and adjust for any practice‐induced fatigue.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

Single‐pulse TMS were delivered to the contralateral hemisphere M1 of the performing leg via a figure‐of‐eight coil (MagPro MC‐B70 Butterfly coil; MagVenture A/S, Denmark) connected to a MagPro X100 stimulator (MagVenture A/S, Denmark) for focused biphasic stimulation. To ensure that the orientation was optimal, the TA hotspot was identified through a mini‐mapping procedure and defined as the location of the coil that elicited the largest and most consistent motor‐evoked potential amplitude (MEPs) during suprathreshold stimulations while the participant was at rest. During the mapping procedure, the coil was initially placed tangentially on the scalp at a 90° angle to the mid‐sagittal plane, inducing medio‐lateral to lateral‐medio (ML‐LM) current across the leg representation of the motor cortex. Subsequently, we rotated the coil to different orientations, from ML to posterior–anterior, which induced an antero‐posterior followed by postero‐anterior (AP–PA). It is suggested that ML coil orientation for the lower limb induces a current perpendicular to the axonal direction of corticospinal neurons and the central sulcus, which is thought to be a relatively effective orientation for activating corticospinal output from the leg area by maximizing the induced electric field component normal to the cortical surface (Hand et al., 2020). A TMS Patient Cap (Magstim, UK) was used to ensure the same positioning of the coil throughout the experiments. For each participant, the optimal coil position and orientation (including handle direction) were determined during the Day 1 hotspot search by rotating the coil across candidate orientations and selecting the configuration that produced the largest and most consistent TA MEPs at rest. The hotspot and coil‐handle orientation were then marked on the neuronavigation cap and used as landmarks to reproduce coil placement across sessions (Day 1 and Day 5; pre‐ and post‐practice). Cap placement was aligned to the same anatomical landmarks at each visit, and coil position/orientation were monitored throughout stimulation to match the cap markings as closely as possible. Residual variability in coil tilt relative to the scalp cannot be fully eliminated and may contribute to within‐ and between‐session variability in MEP amplitudes. The resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the stimulus intensity required to elicit recognizable MEPs with an amplitude above 0.05 mV in a minimum of five out of 10 consecutive stimulations (Rothwell et al., 1999). The rMT was assessed at four time points: before and after practice on Day 1, and before and after practice on Day 5. For standardization across sessions, single‐pulse MEPs used to index CSE were elicited at a fixed stimulation intensity (120% of pre‐practice Day 1 rMT) at all the time points. This fixed‐intensity approach prioritizes the comparability of MEP amplitude across days and avoids day‐to‐day adjustment of stimulator output, which could confound interpretation of pre–post changes. rMT values on Day 1 and Day 5 (and pre/post within day) are reported to document potential threshold drift and practice‐related changes (see TMS results). Muscle relaxation was monitored using concurrent EMG recordings. Twenty TMS stimulations (120% of rMT) were delivered at each time point (before and after practice on Day 1 and Day 5) to measure MEP amplitudes. CSE was assessed using the MEP amplitude recorded at rest to ensure standardized measurement conditions across sessions. Resting MEPs provide an index of corticospinal output from stimulation of a cortical area at controlled time points and thus corticospinal excitability at rest. At rest, confounds introduced by variable contraction levels, task engagement, fatigue and background EMG are reduced, which can be particularly challenging in lower‐limb TMS. Conversely, corticospinal excitability assessed at rest may be less sensitive to state‐dependent or network‐distributed changes during task engagement. Therefore, CSE was included as a complementary physiological measure rather than an exhaustive readout of learning‐related plasticity. For a subset of participants (SKILL‐STIM = 1, SKILL‐SHAM = 1, CON = 4), rMT could not be reliably determined according to the rMT criterion (MEPs ≥ 0.05 mV in 5/10), despite high stimulator output (≥95% MSO). In these cases, we identified the hotspot based on consistent responses of <0.05 mV and/or occasional suprathreshold MEPs ≥ 0.05 mV, and we used the best available hotspot estimate for tDCS electrode placement. As CSE measures could not be quantified reliably, measures from these individuals were excluded from the rMT and MEP analyses. These participants completed the behavioural training protocol, and their data were included in the behavioural analyses.

Data analysis and statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.1.3), primarily via the brms package (Bürkner, 2017), which interfaces with Stan (Carpenter et al., 2017) to implement Bayesian models using Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling. Primary and exploratory outcome measures, skill measures, CT and ER were analysed. Skill‐learning dynamics were further decomposed into online learning (within‐session improvements, for example, last block – first block) and offline learning (between‐session changes, for example, first block of Day 2 ‐ last block of Day 1). MEP amplitude was averaged across TMS stimulations for each participant. All signals were visually inspected during and after the experiment to exclude M‐waves and MEPs exhibiting pre‐stimulus EMG activity within the 100 ms preceding stimulation. Post hoc, outliers exceeding ±2 standard deviations from the mean were excluded. MEP amplitudes were normalized to the corresponding Mmax to enable comparisons across sessions. Because a small subset of participants did not show measurable resting TA MEPs (precluding rMT/MEP quantification), physiological analyses necessarily excluded these individuals, and we performed a sensitivity analysis by repeating the primary behavioural model after excluding them to confirm that the behavioural conclusions were unchanged. Finally, as an exploratory analysis, we tested whether the baseline skill level moderated the effect of tDCS on the learning rate. Participants were divided into high and low baseline performers based on their initial baseline skill estimate (pre‐practice). We then evaluated whether the estimated learning rate differed between active tDCS and sham within each subgroup, treating this analysis as hypothesis‐generating, given the reduced power and post hoc subgrouping. Baseline performance at Day 1 and pre‐session performance at Day 5 was also used as the primary behavioural outcome in a companion analysis of directed cortical connectivity reported separately (Kvistad et al., 2026).

No a priori sample size calculation was performed prior to recruitment. The target sample (n = 60; n = 20 per group) was chosen based on the feasibility of a five‐day neuromodulation protocol with neurophysiological measurements and a one‐week follow‐up. The sample size aligns with prior multi‐session motor learning tDCS studies using similar group sizes (e.g. Reis et al., 2009). Recent meta‐analytic work on concurrent tDCS and motor practice reports effects in the small‐to‐moderate range for online learning (≈0.4 standard mean difference (SMD)) and small effects for offline learning (≈0.2 SMD), alongside substantial heterogeneity across protocols and tasks (Nielsen, Gad et al., 2025). Given these effect sizes and heterogeneity, we emphasize estimation of posterior uncertainty and practical equivalence (ROPE) rather than dichotomous ‘significant/non‐significant’ conclusions (see below).

Model specification

We used a Bayesian multilevel framework because four specific features of our dataset and design motivated this choice over conventional frequentist mixed‐effects modelling. First, our primary contrasts were pre‐specified and directional (SKILL‐STIM > SKILL‐SHAM; SKILL‐SHAM > CON). Bayesian posterior probabilities directly quantify the probability that the data are consistent with a directional hypothesis given the observed data, aligning naturally with confirmatory directional inference in a way that frequentist P values do not. Second, with small to medium expected effect sizes, as referred to in the previous section, and n = 20 per group, several contrasts were expected to fall near the boundary of detectable effects. A frequentist non‐significant result cannot distinguish between the absence of effect and insufficient power to detect it. The ROPE framework addresses this explicitly: posterior mass concentrated within the ROPE supports practical equivalence, whereas a wide highest density interval (HDI) spanning both negligible and meaningful values is reported as inconclusive, a distinction that is critical for interpreting null acquisition findings. Third, motor‐learning studies exhibit substantial inter‐individual variability (Anderson et al., 2021). Bayesian multilevel models address this through partial pooling: participants with noisier trajectories are regularized more strongly toward the group estimate, stabilizing population‐level inference without discarding data, and handling missing observations and imbalanced block structures more robustly than standard repeated‐measures approaches (Gelman & Hill, 2006; Nalborczyk et al., 2019). Fourth, our primary retention finding rests on a contrast at a single follow‐up time point that is inherently more variable than within‐training measures. The full posterior distribution and HDI for this contrast provide a more transparent characterization of uncertainty than a point estimate and confidence interval, and directly support the probabilistic framing of our primary conclusion (Kruschke, 2015, 2018).

All models were fit as hierarchical Bayesian multilevel models, offering the flexibility of which different levels can have both constant and varying effects (fixed or random effects in frequentist statistics) and repeated measures nested into participants (Gelman & Hill, 2006). The brms package uses formula syntax similar to that of the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015), allowing for the flexible specification of complex hierarchical models. Posterior distributions are estimated using Stan, which employs Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampling based on the HMC algorithm (Carpenter et al., 2017). For linear models, the outcome variable (e.g. skill measure) was regressed on group and time predictors, with a participant‐specific intercept to account for repeated measures. Weakly informative Student's t priors centred at zero were used for the intercept (α), slopes (β) and variance parameters, reflecting prior uncertainty while constraining the values within plausible ranges based on the outcome distribution. Models were fit using four chains with 20,000 iterations each (2000 warmup), with adapt_delta = 0.99 and max_treedepth = 20, to ensure robust convergence. Model diagnostics and convergence were evaluated using the Gelman‐Rubin statistic (Ȓ = 1.00 for all parameters), and effective sample sizes (Bulk_ESS and Tail_ESS) indicated stable posterior estimates. Posterior predictive checks and trace plots were performed and confirmed that the model predictions were aligned with the observed data. To model learning trajectories over time, we fitted non‐linear growth models. Several candidate functions were evaluated, including S‐shaped, polynomial and domain‐specific exponential growth models (Bürkner, 2018; Williams et al., 2019). Candidate models were compared using Pareto‐smoothed importance‐sampling leave‐one‐out cross‐validation (PSIS‐LOO), which evaluates out‐of‐sample predictive performance and thereby reduces the overfitting risk when selecting among alternative functional forms (Vehtari et al., 2017). The growth model described by Williams et al. (2019) provided the best predictive fit and was therefore used as the learning‐curve model for trajectory summaries (including the learning‐rate parameter).

Bayesian inference

Bayesian inference was conducted using the ‘hypothesis’ function from the brms package, which enables formal testing of directed and interval‐based hypotheses on posterior distributions. For behaviour, block‐level skill measures were analysed using hierarchical regression, with group as a predictor. To evaluate within‐group changes in CSE, we estimated pre‐to‐post differences in MEP amplitudes normalized to Mmax on Days 1 and 5 for each group. We pre‐specified directional contrasts based on theory: SKILL‐STIM > SKILL‐SHAM (additive tDCS effect) and SKILL‐SHAM > CON (benefit of skilled over non‐skilled practice), with a primary focus on post‐practice and follow‐up. These were evaluated as one‐sided Bayesian hypotheses for posterior contrasts, P(Δ>0). Baseline comparisons used two‐sided tests (and ROPE for practical equivalence). Crucially, we kept the directional framing fixed regardless of raw trends to preserve confirmatory inferences and avoid post hoc reversals. Because the study was not preregistered, these directional hypotheses were not preregistered; however, they were specified a priori based on theory and prior work. If posterior evidence had favoured the opposite direction, we would have reported this explicitly as evidence against the directional hypothesis rather than reversing the hypothesis. To interpret the practical relevance of the effects, we applied the region of practical equivalence (ROPE) decision framework (Kruschke, 2018). This approach offers a more nuanced interpretation of posterior evidence and avoids the limitations of rigid, threshold‐based rules (Etz et al., 2026). This is particularly valuable in studies involving subtle intervention effects or high between‐subject variability, where practical significance must be contextualized within broader scientific and behavioural frameworks. The ROPE region was based on the suggested standardization (Kruschke, 2018) and the determination often used, that is, 10% of the response scale. Posterior contrasts were calculated as the difference in posterior draws between groups or conditions and were transformed into standardized effect sizes (Cohen's d) (Cohen, 1988; Gelman et al., 2020; Kruschke, 2015; McElreath, 2020). A ROPE of ±0.1 was used for Cohen's d to represent effects that are practically negligible in this context, that is, clearly smaller than the typical magnitude of tDCS effects reported in meta‐analytic work on motor learning (≈0.2–0.4 SMD) (Nielsen, Gad et al., 2025) and consistent with suggested boundaries for negligible effects (Kruschke, 2018). Both the 95% HDIs and the percentage of the posterior distribution falling within the ROPE (ROPE%) were reported. ROPE% was treated as a graded summary of how compatible the posterior distribution was with practically negligible effects rather than as a binary decision rule. Posterior contrasts were interpreted using the joint information from the posterior probability of the directional hypothesis, the 95% HDI, and the proportion of posterior mass within the ROPE. Effects were interpreted as supported when posterior mass was concentrated in the predicted direction (illustrated with a ‘*’) and little posterior mass fell within the ROPE (ROPE% ≤ 5%). In contrast, practical equivalence was only considered supported when the posterior distribution was concentrated near zero and the 95% HDI fell entirely, or almost entirely, within the ROPE. When HDIs were wide and included both negligible and potentially meaningful values, the result was described as uncertain or inconclusive, regardless of whether the posterior mean was close to zero or whether some posterior mass fell within the ROPE. Thus, ROPE% was used to aid interpretation of practical relevance, but not as a stand‐alone criterion for concluding equivalence.

Posterior comparisons indicated that rMT changes were generally small across time points, with no evidence of systematic within‐session (pre–post) shifts or day‐to‐day changes across groups.

Discussion

In this study, we compared five days of lower‐limb motor skill practice with non‐skilled ankle movements and investigated whether pairing practice with active tDCS benefits motor learning and retention. Practice alone produced clear gains in the skill measure relative to non‐skilled movements and transferred to the untrained leg but not to an untrained sequence. Adding tDCS improved one‐week (delayed) retention without affecting online (within‐session) or offline (between‐session) performance changes across training days. Exploratory moderation analysis suggested that baseline performance influenced stimulation responsiveness. Participants with higher baseline performance showed steeper learning rates with tDCS, whereas lower‐performing participants did not benefit. There was no consistent pattern in how the amplitude of MEPs obtained at rest changed within or between days, and the changes were not associated with changes in motor performance. Taken together, these findings indicate that while multi‐session tDCS did not accelerate acquisition, it may have stabilized learning over time.

Multiple days of lower‐limb practice combined with tDCS improve delayed retention of skilled sequence learning

Lasting improvements in visuomotor skill performance following motor practice (i.e. retention) require structural changes in the neural circuitry involved in executing the skill. Retaining visuomotor skill improvements after practice reflects the persistence of learning‐related changes in the nervous system. In motor learning research, a follow‐up assessment after several days is commonly used as an index of delayed retention, although it does not in itself specify the underlying physiological mechanisms. We used a modified version of the SVIPT task to investigate the effect of sequential skill practice against non‐skilled movements and the potential additive effects of tDCS on sequential skill acquisition and one‐week retention. Multi‐day practice paired with tDCS improved one‐week retention of lower‐limb skill learning. This was expressed as reduced behavioural decay from post‐practice to follow‐up, despite no detectable stimulation effect on acquisition. This pattern suggests that stimulation may have preferentially influenced processes that stabilize or protect the learned skill representation after practice, rather than accelerating within‐training gains. Several studies have reported enhanced delayed retention with anodal tDCS (Pixa et al., 2017; Saucedo Marquez et al., 2013; Talimkhani et al., 2018), whereas others have found no effect (Ciechanski et al., 2017), and in related multi‐day paradigms, stimulation effects have sometimes emerged during training rather than as a selective retention advantage (Reis et al., 2009). Notably, the retention advantage observed in the present study emerged without reliable changes in resting MEPs, either acutely or over days. This dissociation suggests that tDCS can bias consolidation processes without necessarily shifting corticospinal excitability. One possibility is that weak, behaviour‐contingent E‐fields modulate late‐phase plasticity, including protein synthesis‐dependent LTP, which unfolds over hours to days (Brashers‐Krug et al., 1996; Kantak & Winstein, 2012; Luft & Buitrago, 2005; Rioult‐Pedotti et al., 2000; Robertson, 2005; Rosenkranz et al., 2007). Another possibility is that tDCS influenced sequence‐specific cortico‐striatal representations that are not well captured by single‐pulse resting TMS (Debas et al., 2014). These possibilities include (but are not limited to) late‐phase plasticity processes described in previous motor‐learning studies. Because we did not directly measure synaptic or circuit‐level mechanisms, any mechanistic interpretation remains speculative, and our behavioural data cannot distinguish enhanced consolidation from reduced forgetting or other sources of variability at follow‐up. Collectively, these findings indicate that pairing practice with tDCS can enhance the durability of lower‐limb motor‐skill learning over a one‐week interval. This may be relevant for future work on performance training and rehabilitation‐oriented motor learning, although clinical efficacy remains to be tested directly.

tDCS across multiple days did not enhance acquisition during or between practice days

Contrary to our hypothesis and previous findings (Reis et al., 2009), five consecutive days of anodal tDCS targeting M1‐Leg delivered during motor practice did not enhance online or offline learning compared with sham stimulation. Our findings diverge from seminal SVIPT studies on the upper limb, which reported enhanced learning rates with concurrent tDCS, particularly in the cumulative offline learning phase between practice sessions (Fritsch et al., 2010; Reis et al., 2009). However, the present study differs from previous studies in several important methodological aspects. We consider three categories of explanation for this null finding, ordered roughly by the directness of supporting evidence. (i) Methodological constraints related to stimulation delivery. One might hypothesize that lower‐limb practice could benefit from concurrent tDCS in a manner broadly analogous to upper limb paradigms, as skilled dorsiflexion relies on corticospinal and corticomotoneuronal pathways that support precise voluntary control (Brouwer & Ashby, 1992; Nielsen et al., 1993; Perez et al., 2006). However, upper‐ and lower‐limb neuromodulatory effects cannot be assumed to be equivalent. The mesial leg representation differs from the lateral hand area in cortical depth, folding within or near the interhemispheric fissure, and field orientation relative to cortical geometry (Madhavan et al., 2016). These factors can influence effective target engagement and increase inter‐individual variability in physiological responses. Consistent with this, relatively few studies have targeted M1‐Leg during ankle visuomotor training, and those that are available have reported no selective offline gains (Foerster, Dutta et al., 2018; Sriraman et al., 2014). Directing a sufficiently strong and well‐oriented E‐field to M1‐Leg is technically challenging given its mesial location within or near the interhemispheric fissure. Our exemplary E‐field simulation yielded a mean magnitude of 0.23 V/m at the M1‐Leg ROI, within the lower range of intensities considered capable of biasing ongoing neural activity (Bikson & Rahman, 2013; Vöröslakos et al., 2018), and the field was likely as strong or stronger in adjacent cortical areas, including medial area 6 (estimated 0.3 V/m), a region implicated in sequence learning (Hardwick et al., 2013; Sami et al., 2014). Because medial‐wall geometry produces heterogeneous current‐flow orientation, polarity‐specific effects at a single cortical patch cannot be assumed. Behavioural outcomes are therefore more likely to reflect modulation of a distributed motor network. Additionally, practice duration varied with individual CT, introducing variability in stimulation‐practice temporal overlap that may have increased noise and reduced sensitivity for detecting small state‐dependent acquisition effects, even though mean non‐overlap was comparable across groups. We note that Foerster, Dutta et al. (2018) reported consolidation benefits only in participants from whom a resting TA MEP could be evoked, consistent with the interpretation that effective corticospinal target engagement is a prerequisite for behaviourally meaningful effects at M1‐Leg. These methodological constraints represent the most directly supported explanation for the absence of acquisition effects and should be considered when designing future lower‐limb tDCS studies. (ii) Task‐specific factors. Our task was a dynamic, sequential ankle‐tracking task requiring fast and precise position control. It therefore differs from previous lower‐limb force‐control paradigms (Foerster, Dutta et al., 2018; Sriraman et al., 2014) from the upper‐limb finger‐sequence tasks that have most consistently shown tDCS‐related learning gains (Fan et al., 2017; Greeley et al., 2020; Kunaratnam et al., 2022; Nitsche et al., 2003; Reis et al., 2009). For an overview, see Nielsen, Gad et al. (2025), including work using a comparable sequential precision task (Reis et al., 2009). Position‐ and force‐control tasks differentially engage corticospinal circuits (Nielsen, Norup et al., 2025), which may modulate responsiveness to tDCS. However, Foerster, Dutta et al. (2018) also found null acquisition effects in a force‐control paradigm, suggesting that task control modality alone does not fully explain the pattern. The sequence structure and transfer demands of our task may also be relevant. Performance increments on an untrained sequence were comparable across groups, suggesting that tDCS did not selectively enhance sequence‐specific representations during acquisition, though the retention advantage emerged for the trained sequence specifically. (iii) Potential physiological differences between upper‐ and lower‐limb motor learning systems. We cannot exclude the possibility that genuine physiological differences between hand and leg motor learning contribute to the divergence from upper‐limb findings. M1‐Leg and M1‐Hand are embedded in partly different network architectures: visually guided hand actions engage lateral frontoparietal circuits, whereas ankle dorsiflexion and lower‐limb force control recruit a broader sensorimotor network including SMA, cingulate motor areas, basal ganglia and cerebellum (Dobkin et al., 2004; Francis et al., 2009; Yoon et al., 2014), with hand movements exhibiting stronger and more lateralized motor‐network coupling than foot movements (Volz et al., 2015). Whether these network differences translate into differential responsiveness to tDCS‐augmented plasticity during acquisition remains an open empirical question. We treat this as the most speculative of the three explanations, as it is not directly testable from the present data. Consistent with a network‐level view, analyses of this dataset examining directed cortical connectivity have been reported separately (Kvistad et al., 2026). Using spectral Dynamic Causal Modelling, the study showed that skill practice modified directed connectivity within a frontoparietal–premotor network. Accuracy gains covaried more strongly with prefrontal–premotor coupling than with premotor–parietal feedback. Concurrent tDCS further modulated prefrontal–premotor coupling without enhancing acquisition. This pattern supports the interpretation that tDCS reweights how cortical regions interact during skill acquisition rather than producing a uniform upregulation of corticospinal drive, and is consistent with the behavioural dissociation observed here between acquisition and one‐week retention. In the present study, we also assessed whether learning generalized beyond the trained sequence and limb. Performance increments in an untrained sequence were comparable across groups, whereas sequence‐specific improvement was transferred to the untrained leg, suggesting that practice primarily established a sequence representation accessible to both legs rather than enhancing general visuomotor acuity.

As an exploratory, hypothesis‐generating analysis, we probed whether baseline performance moderated the stimulation effect on the learning rate. Interestingly, high‐baseline performing participants benefited more from active tDCS than from sham tDCS, exhibiting steeper learning trajectories. In contrast, tDCS did not benefit learning rates for participants with the lowest baseline performance. This unexpected result challenges the assumption that low performers, who arguably have more room for improvement, are more responsive to neuromodulation (Sánchez‐Kuhn et al., 2018; Vergallito et al., 2022). Given the post hoc subgrouping and lower power in this analysis, this finding should not be considered confirmatory. However, our findings suggest that baseline performance level may interact with tDCS in complex and non‐linear ways, warranting further investigation into individual variability in stimulation responsiveness.

No reliable CSE modulation, consistent with sequence‐specific learning outside M1

Corticospinal excitability was assessed using single‐pulse MEP amplitudes obtained at rest from the TA muscle. This measurement provides a standardized and widely used measure of corticospinal output. However, it is an inherently limited proxy for the neurophysiological processes that support skill learning and retention. Resting MEPs are sensitive to multiple factors not related to neuroplasticity (e.g. arousal, attention, coil–cortex geometry) and, particularly for leg representations, can show substantial within‐ and between‐session variability. Moreover, practice‐ and stimulation‐related plasticity may instead be expressed during task engagement, within intracortical or spinal circuits, or as distributed network reweighting during consolidation. Such effects may not appear as sustained changes in resting MEP amplitude. Accordingly, the absence of robust resting MEP changes should be interpreted as no detectable change in corticospinal output at rest under our measurement conditions, rather than evidence that no relevant plasticity occurred. Across groups, no practice‐dependent modulation of resting MEPs was observed after practice on either Day 1 or Day 5. Because tDCS effects and practice‐related plasticity can be state‐dependent, resting MEP amplitude may not capture changes expressed preferentially during task engagement or consolidation‐related network reweighting. Task‐engaged measures (e.g. MEPs during controlled tonic contraction, stimulus‐response curves, paired‐pulse metrics or active thresholds) may offer greater sensitivity to these effects (Darling et al., 2006; Spampinato et al., 2023). In addition, leg‐area TMS is sensitive to small variations in coil position and tilt relative to the medial wall (Terao et al., 1994), and residual orientation variability across sessions may have contributed to the substantial within‐ and between‐session variability. Considering behaviour and improvements on the trained target sequence without advantage on the near‐transfer (random) block, these findings favour a sequence‐specific account of learning rather than a generalized increase in motor acuity, where demands for speed and accuracy have been demonstrated to increase CSE (Christiansen et al., 2018, 2020). Converging findings from animal studies indicate that once a sequence is well learned, its execution can be supported predominantly by subcortical circuits, the sensorimotor striatum, and thalamo‐striatal pathways, while M1 becomes less involved in expression (Dhawale et al., 2021; Kawai et al., 2015; Mizes et al., 2024; Wolff et al., 2022). Under such circumstances, M1‐based resting‐state MEPs cannot provide insights to the locus of plastic changes. This helps reconcile robust sequence‐bound behavioural gains in the absence of group‐level CSE effects.

This interpretation also places our findings within the context of previous results for effects of lower‐limb skill practice on CSE. A seminal study reported that a single session of position‐control practice of randomized foot flexion series increased CSE when targeting the M1 leg area and improved accuracy relative to non‐task‐specific movement (Perez et al., 2004), which may be a result of an increase in motor acuity. As emphasized by a recent lower‐limb review (Woodhead et al., 2024), leg‐area targeting faces additional variability from coil‐target geometry and depth, and resting single‐pulse MEPs may be less sensitive than input–output curves, paired‐pulse metrics or task‐engaged/active‐contraction probes as compared with effects in the M1 hand knob area due to neuroanatomical and morphometric reasons. More broadly, resting MEPs are influenced by arousal, attention and expectancy (Bestmann & Krakauer, 2015), which modulate corticomotor communication without necessarily indexing durable synaptic changes.

Although anodal tDCS applied to M1 is often associated with acute increases in CSE after a single session (Ambrus et al., 2016; Nitsche & Paulus, 2001), but also with varying results (Chew et al., 2015; Madhavan et al., 2016), our multi‐day protocol showed no reliable resting MEP modulation on Day 1 or Day 5. Recent work illustrates increased MEP amplitudes across multi‐session finger‐sequence learning, regardless of stimulation intensity (0, 4 and 6 mA) and without a clear link to performance (Hsu et al., 2025). Their design lacked transfer control; therefore, broader motor acuity versus sequence‐specific gains could not be disambiguated. At the mechanistic level, in vitro work using lower, human‐relevant electric fields still demonstrates synaptic plasticity (Sharma et al., 2022), supporting the plausibility that weak fields can bias active circuits even if resting MEPs remain unchanged. Taken together, the absence of meaningful resting‐MEP changes, despite sequence‐specific behavioural improvement, suggests that plasticity may have consolidated outside M1 or emerged in task‐dependent neural states not captured by resting TMS.

Conclusion

We demonstrate that motor skill practice, relative to non‐skilled movements, produced robust learning, interlimb transfer, and superior performance at follow‐up. Adding active tDCS during practice did not amplify online (within‐session) or offline (between‐session) gains during training but selectively strengthened the delayed retention of a complex discrete motor task requiring precise and rapid ankle movements under high visuomotor demands. Baseline performance level emerged as an important factor in determining the extent to which tDCS could augment skill acquisition, with higher‐performing individuals deriving the greatest benefit from concurrent stimulation and practice. Together, these findings indicate that concurrent tDCS may preferentially support skill retention rather than immediate performance, whereas practice itself drives the bulk of acquisition and transfer processes. These results support the potential relevance of tDCS as an adjunct to promote retention of lower‐limb skill learning, while emphasizing that a well‐designed motor practice protocol remains the primary driver of improvement. The findings may therefore inform future studies on timing and dosing in performance settings and rehabilitation‐oriented motor‐learning paradigms, although clinical efficacy remains to be established directly in patient populations.
