1.0 Executive Summary
The Forest County Potawatomi Community (“FCPC” or “the Tribe”), is a federally recognized
Indian Tribe located in Wisconsin. With the support of the United States Department of Energy, Office
of Indian Energy (DOE IE) grant funding, the Tribe implemented their New Community Center
Integrated EEMs and Solar Photovoltaic System in the Forest County Potawatomi Community project
(the “Project”). The Project was developed and executed with the goal of partially energizing the
Community Center while concurrently pursuing Tribal energy sovereignty through cost effective energy
efficiency and generation measure.

2.0 Background
FCPC has a membership exceeding 1,400 individuals and is located on reservation lands in Forest
County, Wisconsin, and tribal trust lands in Milwaukee County, Wisconsin. The Tribe owns and
operates significant governmental and business operations in both areas. Until relatively recently, FCPC
was one of the poorest tribes in the Midwest. Due to its successful gaming operations, it now has
improved its financial situation and is using its resources to benefit both its members and the public
through environmental and other community efforts, many of which are long delayed because of the
extended period of abject poverty.

The Potawatomi culture and values direct FCPC to take only from Mother Earth what is needed to
survive. This led FCPC to its mission of energy independence through renewable resources. Renewable
energy enables FCPC to invest in its future, rather than to take from it.

The Tribe has developed the long-term goal of becoming energy independent. To meet this goal, the
Tribe has taken several important steps. As an interim step, nearly 55 million kWh of annual renewable
energy certificates (“RECs”) have been purchased, which offset more than 100% of the combined
electricity use of the Tribe’s facilities.

The Tribe also recognizes improving energy efficiency is critical to reaching its long-term energy
independence goal. Numerous energy efficiency measures and quarterly energy audits have been
implemented to track energy usage are conducted. Since 2007, energy usage per gross square foot has
been reduced by 13.6%.

In 2010 and in partnership with DOE IE, the Tribe completed an upgrade of the lighting in the parking
facility at Potawatomi Bingo Casino in Milwaukee, WI. The lighting upgrade reduced the parking
facility’s annual electricity use by approximately 73%.

As part of its renovation of the historic Concordia College campus in central Milwaukee, the Tribe, in
partnership with DOE IE, invested over $2.2 million in energy efficiency improvements for Wunder Hall,
a 34,000 square foot former dormitory building which is listed on the National Register of Historic
Places.

In 2011, as an initial step in creating the Tribe’s own renewable energy, a solar PV energy system was
installed on the Administration building in Milwaukee. Local, American-made products were utilized,
showing commitment not only to energy efficiency, but the local economy as well. Further proving its
energy pledge, a biogas facility was developed in Milwaukee, which operates with the liquid waste
materials from local food and beverage industry operations that would’ve otherwise gone to landfills
and other disposal areas.

The Tribe worked with DOE through a grant awarded in 2013 to install nearly one megawatt of energy
on several of its government buildings in both Forest and Milwaukee Counties, generating between 15
and 97% of the energy needed at each location.

3.0 Project Objectives
The Project proposed three objectives to increase energy savings and reduce energy usage for the new
Community Center. The Community Center combines and facilitates the Tribe’s goals for sustainable
development and cultural perseverance. The Community Center also serves as the Community’s
emergency shelter.

Objectives:
 1. Install a comprehensive range of eight energy efficiency measures (EEMs).
 2. Deploy a roof-top 200 kW of roof-top solar photovoltaic (PV).
 3. Evaluate, monitor, and verify system functioning over a 12-month period.

The Project would benefit the Tribe by reducing the Community Center’s natural gas needs by 42.1%
and electricity needs by 16.5%, saving an average of $111,109 per year over 30 years and $72,379 in
year 1.

While planning for the project, the Tribe evaluated a host of potential EEMs choosing those that would
pay for themselves through the lifespan of the materials while also adding life to the building itself and
ensuring sustainable space for a challenging environment. Additionally, the technology needed to be
deployable within the building construction timeline. Thus, the timing of the grant was important
because it did not require retrofitting of existing plans and items could easily be coordinated with the
Tribe’s construction team. Finally, the Solar PV at the site was increased in conjunction with another
Department of Energy, Office of Indian Energy grant (DE-IE0000120, “Community-Scale Solar
Photovoltaic for Forest County Potawatomi Community”), allowing the Tribe to meet more of its
energy needs.

The Tribe did not fully manage the construction itself but worked with a tribally owned entity,
Greenfire Management, to oversee construction and the FCPC energy department verified inclusion of
the EEMs throughout. During construction of the shell of the building, EEMs 1-3 were installed,
allowing FCPC to partially meet Objective 1. Greenfire and FCPC’s energy team verified installation.
Spray foam wall insulation, improved windows and improved roofing were included at the early stages
of the project.

EEMs 4-8 were installed during interior construction, which included improved slab insulation, lighting
controls, a pool heat recovery unit to improve overall boiler efficiency, and HVAC and electrical
optimization. New construction and the use of skilled project management from both FCPC and
Greenfire improved the ease of EEM implementation.

The last step was design and installation of the solar PV to offset a portion of the energy use at the
building. The solar design began in 2020 but challenges with timing and coordination of other grants
were faced. The previously mentioned DE-IE0000120 project was adjusted to allow for an increase to
the size of the solar PV system at the Community Center. This grant allowed for the installation of 200
kw and the DE-IE0000120 DOE grant allowed the Tribe to add 283 additional kw for a total of 483 kw.

One of the final steps prior to move-in and grand opening was commissioning by an energy
subcontractor to ensure all building equipment and materials were properly installed.

6.0 Lessons Learned
This project was an interesting opportunity because it allowed the Tribe to incorporate EEMs and
green energy production at the planning stage, during building design. So often Tribes are faced with
retrofitting existing systems which is difficult both from a financial perspective and because integration
of new technologies is not always as easy with older systems. FCPC has developed several motions
over its energy journey requiring that new construction be built to at least LEED standards and that
design and construction incorporate energy efficiency and green energy. The Community Center was
the first time the Tribe was able to design from the ground up in a manner that reinforces its cultural,
sustainability and community expectations. The result is a building that provides not only meeting
space, learning areas and physical activity locations but one which truly highlights the Tribe’s goals and
expectations for itself. With this building as a model, the Tribe is certain its future goals of energy
efficiency can be met.

Although the Tribe would have liked to install more solar PV to offset even more of the daily use, the
space is not conducive to that. The Tribe’s members have strong environmental initiatives which
include preserving forested areas. The Tribe has a motion stating that trees should not be removed for
solar installations requiring smart design and placement. While the Tribe was able to build a structure
that could hold the solar panels and racking it will be required to plan future building designs with this
in mind. Other options are being considered, such as a Tribal Utility that could construct solar near
rural load centers. Wisconsin’s legal landscape currently limits community solar garden opportunities,
and the incumbent utility has not been supportive of off-site solar projects for larger users.

In the winter of 2025 the Tribe, and most of Northern Wisconsin, experienced a significant ice storm.
Wisconsin Public Service, the incumbent utility, was unable to provide energy for some time. The Tribe
relied on its back up generators but has considered whether the addition of battery energy storage
systems (BESS) may be a component of future work. BESS would allow the Tribe to use renewable
energy in times of natural disaster rather than relying on generators. Additionally, the generators did
not work as expected leaving some needs unmet.
