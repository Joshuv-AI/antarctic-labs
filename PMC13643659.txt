Genome Analysis of Corynebacterium ulcerans Isolates Chronologically Recovered From the Same Cat for Two Years: Circulation in a Cat Shelter
Authors: Ryoko Jikihara, Miyuki Kimura, Hyun Kim, Tsuyoshi Sekizuka, Masatomo Morita, Makoto Kuroda, Akihiko Yamamoto, Mitsutoshi Senoh, Masaaki Iwaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTToxigenic Corynebacterium ulcerans, a zoonotic pathogen, was isolated repeatedly from a cat in an in‐house group in Japan. Although the cat was cured once by antibiotic treatment, clonal isolates of the original pathogenic strain were recovered repeatedly during a 2‐year course. Clonal isolates of those isolates were also obtained from other asymptomatic cats in the same shelter, suggesting that environmental circulation of the pathogen had occurred. Care should be taken to monitor and cure the animals if any of their groupmates test positive for the pathogen, even if asymptomatic.

Introduction

Diphtheria toxin‐producing (toxigenic) Corynebacterium ulcerans, a zoonotic pathogen, elicits clinical manifestations indistinguishable from those induced by toxigenic Corynebacterium diphtheriae upon infection in humans [1, 2, 3]. The World Health Organization defines diphtheria as an infectious disease caused by toxigenic strains of C. diphtheriae, C. ulcerans, and Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis [2, 3, 4]. Unlike C. diphtheriae, which generally occurs only in humans and rarely in horses [5, 6, 7, 8], C. ulcerans can proliferate in a much wider range of mammals, including cats [9] and dogs [10].

In many developed countries, the number of C. ulcerans human cases is surpassing that of C. diphtheriae cases. In Japan as well, human cases of C. ulcerans are emerging. Thirty‐four cases, including two fatal cases, have been reported during the 2001–2019 period [11], whereas no diphtheria cases caused by C. diphtheriae have been notified since 1999 [12]. The predominant source of C. ulcerans infecting humans is companion animals, particularly cats in Japan [11].

Herein, we report a case of repeated C. ulcerans infections of a cat in a shelter. Based on the whole‐genome sequence analysis of the isolates, the possibility of pathogen circulation among the cats in the shelter is discussed.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Bacterial Isolation

Using TRANSWAB Nasal devices (Iwaki & Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), mouth and throat swabs and environmental specimens were collected from cats in a shelter in Okayama City, Japan. Subsequently, bacteria were isolated from the swab specimens by plating on Katsukawa agar medium [13]. After incubation at 36°C for 3 days, colonies that appeared on the plates were transferred to dextran sulfate salt medium (Nikken Bio, Kyoto, Japan), cultured at 36°C for 2 days, and then purified by plating on sheep blood agar plates. In each case, one isolate per specimen was analyzed.

Polymerase Chain Reaction Amplification of tox and rpoB Gene Sequences

Bacterial cells from the purified colonies were suspended in 100 μL of sterile water and boiled at 100°C for 10 min. The suspension was centrifuged and 1 μL of the supernatant was used as template for amplification of the diphtheria toxin subunit A (tox) [14] and rpoB genes [15] using universal primers. The amplified rpoB gene sequences were then used for species‐level identification via BLAST searches [16].

Toxigenicity Test

One loopful of fresh bacterial cells on sheep blood agar was seeded into 2 mL of Pope liquid medium [17, 18, 19], and cultivated overnight at 37°C with shaking. The culture supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter and subjected to Vero cell toxicity assays according to the methods described by Miyamura et al. [20].

Antibiotic Sensitivity Tests

Bacterial sensitivity to 19 antibiotics listed in Table 1 was tested using DryPlate “Eiken” kits (Eiken Chemical Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) or KB Disk “Eiken” kits (Eiken Chemical).

Genome Sequencing

Bacterial cells were mechanically lysed via bead beating in a Geno Grinder homogenizer (Cole‐Parmer, now Antylia Scientific, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) for 10 min at 1500 rpm. Total genomic DNA was then isolated from the lysed cells and purified using the ZR‐96 Zymoclean Gel DNA Recovery Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared using the QIAseq FX DNA Library Kit (QIAGEN, Tokyo, Japan). Paired‐end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 500 system with the NextSeq 500/550 Mid Output Kit v2.5 (300 cycles) for 150 bp reads and on the Illumina MiSeq system with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (600 cycles) for 300 bp reads.

Single Nucleotide Variant Phylogenetic Analysis

Adapter sequences in short reads were trimmed using Trim Galore (version 0.6.10) [21] with default parameters, following which low‐quality bases were trimmed using Sickle (version 1.33) [22] with the minimum length threshold ‘−l 40.’ Simulated short reads of contig sequences were generated using NGSngs (version 0.9.2) [23]. Repeat and prophage regions of the reference sequence (C. ulcerans strain 0102: accession number: AP012284.1) were analyzed using NUCmer from the MUMmer package (version 3.23) [24] and phispy (version 4.2.21) [25], respectively. The trimmed or simulated short reads were aligned to the reference genome using bwa‐mem (version 0.7.17) [26] with default parameters. Mapping coverage and statistics were calculated using BEDTools (version 2.31.1) [27] and SAMtools (version 1.20) [28], respectively. Duplicate reads were marked using the MarkDuplicates tool from GATK (version 4.5.0.0) [29], following which variant calling was performed with the HaplotypeCaller tool of GATK. Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (indels) were extracted using the SelectVariants tool from GATK and filtered with the VariantFiltration tool. To avoid excessive filtering in low‐coverage regions, the DP threshold was defined as the greater of mean depth divided by 10 or 5. To predict recombination regions, consensus sequences were constructed from the SNV data and reference sequences using BCFtools (version 1.21) and BEDTools, and the predictions were performed using Gubbins (version 3.4) [30]. All SNV datasets were merged using vcftools (version 0.1.16) [31], and SNVs located in non‐core, repeat, and predicted prophage regions were then filtered. The SNVs were concatenated to construct a pseudo‐sequence, which was used for core‐genome phylogenetic analysis using IQ‐TREE (version 2.4.0) [32] with the parameters ‘‐mMFP+ASC ‐b 100’. The phylogenetic tree was visualized using FigTree (version 1.4.4) [33] (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). The SNV network was analyzed using the median joining network method of PopART (https://popart.maths.otago.ac.nz/).

Results and Discussion

A 16‐year‐old male cat (designated as “A”) was found to harbor toxigenic C. ulcerans. He had been sheltered at the facility since the age of 2 months and kept indoors with other cats. In general, cats in the shelter reside either alone in their own cages or with their siblings (~2 or 3 cats/cage). Occasionally, the animals emerge from their cages to spend time with cats from other cages. Cat A was also positive for feline immunodeficiency virus (FIV) and negative for feline leukemia virus and pathogenic parasites. He regularly exhibited nasal and ocular discharge, cough, and sneezing symptoms. C. ulcerans was first identified in cat A in January 2020 (Figure 1), whereupon the isolated strain was designated as CUL_2020_095. Environmental sampling was carried out once after the isolation but no C. ulcerans was recovered from the environment. Azithromycin (10 mg/kg) was administered for 2 weeks to treat the infection, and further testing for C. ulcerans in February and March 2020 returned apparently negative results.

However, C. ulcerans was again isolated from cat A in August 2020 (designated as CUL_2020_096), and erythromycin (20 mg/kg) was administered for 2 weeks. In spite of the treatment, the same bacterial species was isolated from the cat in October 2020 (designated as CUL_2020_097).

Despite being re‐infected by the pathogen, cat A did not show signs of illness. No further treatment was administered until July 2021, when the next C. ulcerans isolate (designated as CUL_2021_098) was recovered. The cat was kept untreated; however, he fell ill in October 2021, whereupon CUL_2021_099 was isolated. Drug sensitivity testing of the five isolates (CUL_2020_095 through CUL_2021_099) revealed all of them to have the same susceptibility profile: sensitivity to oxacillin 2 μg/mL, amoxicillin 0.25 μg/mL, clindamycin 2 μg/mL, fosfomycin > 32 μg/mL, and sensitive to all other drugs, including azithromycin, erythromycin, and levofloxacin (Table 1). Cat A survived more than 2 years following the last infection episode and died in March 2024.

Ten months after the isolation of CUL_2021_099, mouth swab specimens collected from 40 cats (~3 months old) in the same shelter from August to September 2022 were screened for the pathogen. C. ulcerans was isolated from three cats: CUL_2022_127 from cat B on August 31, and CUL_2022_128 and CUL_2022_129 from cats C and D, respectively, on September 20. At this time, the cats were unlikely to have experienced contact with cat A. A 2‐week series of azithromycin treatment was initiated for cat B on September 19. A follow‐up swab test for cat B on October 7 proved to be positive, with CUL_2022_130 being isolated. On October 18, erythromycin treatment was initiated for all three cats. Follow‐up swab tests on November 2 showed C. ulcerans negativity for cats B and C. All three cats were confirmed to be negative for the pathogen on November 7, and cat D tested negative again on December 1 (Figure 1).

In total, five C. ulcerans isolates were obtained from cat A and four isolates from cats B, C, and D. The whole‐genome sequences of the nine isolates were determined (Table 2). Multilocus sequence typing (MLST) analysis revealed that all nine isolates belonged to sequence type 337, the predominant sequence type in Japan [34] and the identical type to the first and second toxigenic C. ulcerans isolates from humans in Japan [35]. Figure 2A shows the phylogeny of the nine isolates, as determined via the SNV analysis of core‐genome genes using C. ulcerans 0102 as the reference. The ratio of core region against the total genome was 94.32%. One hundred eighteen SNVs were detected, all of which were subjected to phylogenetic analysis. Sequencing depth, genome coverage and other miscellaneous information are shown in the Supplementary Table. The isolates differed by only one to three nucleotides, indicating that they were clonal and had a close epidemiological link (Figure 2B, Table 3). When viewed chronologically (Figures 1 and 2B), the isolates appear to have undergone an interesting transition. CUL_2020_095 was the first isolate in January 2020 and the cat was apparently cured by azithromycin treatment (Figure 1). Seven months later CUL_2020_096 was isolated, which had SNVs in three nucleotides. The cat was treated with erythromycin but the treatment was not successful, 2 months later resulting in isolation of CUL_2020_097. This could be interpreted as the bacterium residing in the animal during the 2‐month period with one nucleotide change. Until this point, the transition can be surmised to have followed a linear path (Figure 2B). However, CUL_2021_098 (isolated ~ 9 months after the previous isolate) had the identical SNV to that of CUL_2020_096, as if it had returned. Finally, CUL_2021_099 (the last isolate) had a four‐nucleotide distance from CUL_2021_098. The number of SNVs detected among these isolates was substantially lower than that observed across the entire collection of Japanese ST337 isolates, and the lineage associated with this outbreak was genetically distinct from lineages identified in other outbreak‐associated and sporadic isolates [34]. The observed transition with intermittent detection with apparent disappearance during the period could simply be due to a series of mutations that occurred during long‐term colonization in cat A. However, other interpretations are also possible. One is that the bacterium might have circulated within the shelter. The attempt to isolate the organism from the environment was unsuccessful, but sampling was done only once, possibly missing the existing organism. In addition, cat A might have been prone to re‐infection because he was FIV positive. Another possibility cannot be excluded. At each time point, we have isolated only one isolate. Even clonal, individual bacterial cells residing in a host can be diverse (within‐host diversity), with a small number of SNVs [36]. Isolating multiple clones at each time point, which was not done in the present study, could have increased the accuracy of analysis and more precisely depicted how the organism varied in the host.

Ten months after the last isolation from cat A, cats B, C, and D tested positive (Figure 1), with no evidence of contact with cat A. Whole‐genome sequence analysis revealed that the four later isolates were completely identical to one another, on the branch from the path CUL_2021_098 to CUL_2022_099 (Figure 2A,B, Table 3).

The prevalence of C. ulcerans isolates from a common source in cats in a shelter [37] and dogs in a custody [38] was suggested. In another report, a common source was suggested for an outbreak of C. ulcerans infection among water rats in a zoo [39].

The isolates obtained in the present study belonged to ST337, the most prevalent in Japan [34] and in the region of the present case. In Japan, except in an extremely rare case, toxigenic C. ulcerans isolates are lysogenized by a single bacteriophage carrying a single tox gene. Two types of bacteriophages are distributed in the region. The isolates in this case harbored one of them.

In Japan, C. ulcerans infection is now the major cause of diphtheria‐like disease, whereas diphtheria caused by C. diphtheriae has not been reported since 2000 [11]. C. ulcerans human infections occur mainly through contact with companion animals, in most cases, cats [11]. The findings of the present study suggest circulation of the pathogen among cats in a facility where a number of the animals were kept at the same time. Notably, cat A showed symptoms, whereas cats B, C, and D were asymptomatic. In most cat‐associated human cases, the animals had exhibited ocular or nasal discharge or developed skin lesions from which C. ulcerans was isolated [40, 41, 42]; however, asymptomatic carrier animals might also serve as an infection source. Therefore, care should be taken to monitor and cure the animals if any of their group mates tested positive for the pathogen.
