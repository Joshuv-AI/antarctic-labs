Abstract
The Cosmology Large Angular Scale Surveyor (CLASS) is measuring the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) polarization anisotropy on the largest angular scales ( > 1 deg >$1\text{\,}\deg$ ) to probe the epochs of inflation and reionization. To enhance the CMB mapping speed, we have built, tested, and commissioned in August 2025 a second 90 GHz 90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} receiver (CLASS-W2) with a detector focal plane composed of feedhorn-coupled Transition Edge Sensor (TES) bolometers fabricated at NIST-Boulder. The focal plane consists of four modules, each containing 37 feedhorns coupling orthogonal polarizations onto two TES bolometers, for a total of 296 optically sensitive detectors. Laboratory tests show highly uniform TES properties with an array average critical temperature of 184 ± ± 3 mK 184\pm\pm 3\text{\,}\mathrm{mK} , a thermal conductance of 460 ± ± 47 pW K − 1 460\pm\pm 47\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1} , and a normal resistance of 7.8 ± ± 0.3 m ​ Ω 7.8\pm\pm 0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{m\SIUnitSymbolOhm} . The detector array has an average band center frequency of 95.2 GHz 95.2\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} with 28.3 GHz 28.3\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} bandwidth, and achieves a detector yield of 94%. On-sky measurements indicate a mean detector optical load of 3.3 pW 3.3\text{\,}\mathrm{pW} , corresponding to an antenna temperature of ∼ 23 K \sim$23\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$ . The array’s average beam solid angle is 124 µ ​ sr 124\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro sr} , with a full width at half maximum of 0.592 deg 0.592\text{\,}\deg , and the end-to-end average optical efficiency is 0.37. We find that high-frequency “blue-leak” radiation couples directly to the TES bolometer islands; adding a metal-mesh low-pass filter with cutoff frequency of 157 GHz 157\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} in front of the focal plane suppresses the “blue-leak” power by 0.9 pW 0.9\text{\,}\mathrm{pW} . The four-module array achieves a noise-equivalent temperature of NET = 16 ​ µ ​ K \mathrm{NET}=16~$\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}$ . Adding this array has boosted the CLASS 90 GHz 90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} mapping speed by 41%.

2
CLASS-W2 Telescope

The CLASS telescopes observe from a high-altitude (
5200\text{\,}\mathrm{m}
) site in the Atacama Desert of Chile, covering over 75% of the sky by continuously scanning
720\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
in azimuth at constant elevation (
45\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
) and boresight angles. The boresight angle is changed every day by
15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
in between the range of
-4545\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
.
The sky polarization signal is modulated at
\sim$10\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$
with a front-end polarization modulator. CLASS has successfully deployed and operated both Variable-delay Polarization Modulators (VPM)
[
8
]
, and rotating Reflective Half Wave Plates (RHWP)
[
39
]
. The VPM modulates
U
and
V
Stokes parameters providing sensitivity to circular polarization, while the RHWP is optimized for linear polarization, modulating Stokes parameters
U
and
Q
at four times the rotation frequency (
4f
). The RHWP signal band at
4f
is separate from the RHWP synchronous emission/reflection modulated at
2f
, adding another layer of systematic control. Additionally, the RHWP modulates all polarization angles on the sky at each telescope boresight.

The first
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
telescope, CLASS-W1, was installed in 2018 and received a major detector upgrade in 2022. To improve sensitivity at
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
, the CLASS frequency band most sensitive to the CMB, we developed and deployed a second
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
telescope, CLASS-W2. First light was observed in August 2025 coupled to a VPM. In February 2026, a free-space metal-mesh low-pass filter with a cutoff at
157\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
[
1
]
was installed in front of the detector focal plane on the
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
stage to eliminate a low level of ‘blue leak’ optical power detected during the initial deployment. In May 2026, after making pioneering new measurements of the circular polarization of foregrounds
[
32
]
, and setting the best current upper limits on circular CMB polarization
[
29
]
, we turned our full attention to linear polarization, the VPM was replaced with a RHWP to improve sensitivity to linear polarization.

2.1
Telescope Optics

The CLASS-W2 telescope is similar in design to the CLASS-W1
[
12
,
28
]
telescope, but observes from the second CLASS mount. Deploying two
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
telescopes observing from two different mounts simultaneously allows or better characterization and mitigation of systematics.
Briefly, the telescope sits inside a protective cage with a large forebaffle extending from the cage opening to reduce sidelobe pickup. The first optical element is a VPM or RHWP that modulates the polarization of the sky signal. Both modulators are reflective, with no dielectric materials. Two reflective mirrors then couple the modulator output to the cryostat window. Inside the cryostat, two cryogenically cooled high-density polyethylene (HDPE) lenses focus the light onto the detector focal plane. Details of the optical design of the telescope can be found in Ref.
[
12
]
. Infrared filters reduce thermal loading on the
60\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
,
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
,
1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
stages
[
20
]
. CLASS uses multi-layer IR-absorptive foam filters behind the window, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) absorptive filters at
60\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
and
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, and a Nylon filter at
1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
. A Bluefors
3
3

3

https://bluefors.com/
dilution refrigerator cools the detector focal plane to below
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
during observations. Cylindrical magnetic shields at the
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
and
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
stages surround the focal plane, reducing the susceptibility of the TES bolometers and superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID) readout to Earth’s magnetic field and to its modulation from azimuth scanning.

2.2
Detector focal plane

The light reaching the focal plane couples into precision-machined copper feedhorns
[
45
]
.
Thirty-seven feedhorns are mounted on one side of a module’s SiAl composite baseplate
[
2
,
3
]
. Each feedhorn feeds a circular waveguide that delivers the polarization signals to the detector’s superconducting planar ortho-mode transducer (OMT) antenna probes. A full CLASS
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
focal plane comprises seven modules; the initial CLASS-W2 focal plane (Figure
1
) was deployed in 2025 and includes four modules. A second detector fabrication run is underway to complete the seven-module focal plane. The seven modules mount onto a copper-web that is well heatsunk to the
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
cryostat stage. A Ruthenium oxide (Rox) thermometer mounted on the copper-web tracks the detector bath temperature (
T_{\mathrm{b}}
), while a copper-can bolted to the edge of the web holds a free-space metal-mesh low-pass filter (LPF)
[
1
]
as shown in the right panel of Figure
1
. The LPF is clamped and heatsunk to a copper ring with a spiral spring gasket
[
21
]
. The LPF together with the copper can assembly were deployed in 2026 to form a light-tight enclosure that only allows radiation below
157\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
to reach the focal plane. Before the LPF installation, out-of-band power contributed significantly to detector loading, as described in Section
4.4
.

The NIST-fabricated detector wafers are integrated with a backshort-cap, backshort, waveguide interface plate (WIP), and choke. The backshort includes moats around the waveguide that are filled with dark epoxy to absorb stray light. A similar NIST detector integrated wafer design is presented in
[
42
]
. Figure
B.1
in the appendix shows a cross-sectional view of a CLASS detector wafer. The detector wafers mount on the SiAl baseplate, on the opposite side to the feedhorns. The detector wafer is aligned and secured using the same pin-and-spring mechanical constraints as the previous CLASS-W1 telscope modules that used NASA-fabricated detector wafers
[
9
,
27
]
. Over two hundred gold bonds are placed between the gold plated surfaces of the detector wafer and the gold plated SiAl baseplate. These gold bonds improve the thermal heat sinking of the detector wafer to the
50\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
stage.
The TES bolometers are connected with superconducting aluminum wire bonds to the shunt resistors and SQUID readout. The cryogenic circuit board and cables that carry the detector signal to the
300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
Multi-Channel Electronic (MCE) readout are identical to those deployed in the CLASS-W1 telescope
[
6
]

2.3
Microwave architecture and TES design

The OMT waveguide probes are impedance matched to the on-chip co-planar waveguide (CPW) lines, which transition to low-impedance microstrip (MS) lines built on SiN dielectric.
The orthogonal polarization signals carried by the MS lines are combined in a hybrid-tee
[
23
]
where unwanted sum modes are terminated. The MS continues to the on-chip band-defining filters before being terminated on a lossy gold meander adjacent to the AlMn TES on the SiN bolometer island. A PdAu thermal ballast sets the heat capacity of the TES bolometer, which determines the stability and time constant of the detector response. A more detailed description of the detector microwave design is presented in Ref.
[
22
]
(Figure 1), including a photo of the fabricated microwave detector components.

3
TES detector parameters

The TES bolometer parameters are measured in-lab with the detectors mounted inside a dark
1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
cold copper cavity, allowing us to characterize the detector under effectively zero optical loading (
P_{\gamma}=0
).
CLASS detectors are read by cryogenically cooled SQUID
[
13
]
that are multiplexed in the time domain with an MCE warm readout system
[
6
]
. Each CLASS
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
module is addressed by 4 columns with 22 multiplexed rows. A full seven module CLASS
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
focal plane contains 28 columns and a total of 616 readout channels.
Each TES bolometer is voltage biased by a small
250\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro\SIUnitSymbolOhm}
shunt resistor (
R_{\mathrm{sh}}
) fabricated by NIST. The bias current (
I_{\mathrm{b}}
) is applied by low-noise MCE bias lines. The SQUID input is connected in series with the TES to measure changes in the TES current, which are logged by the MCE readout cards. For a circuit diagram of the TES voltage biased circuit see Ref.
[
4
]
(Figure 1).

The absolute TES current (
I
) is determined from I–V curve measurements. These measurements consist of the recorded change in TES current as the TES voltage
V
(or equivalently the bias current
I_{\mathrm{b}}
) is linearly decreased from a high value down to zero; tracing out an I–V curve. The voltage sweep starts at a high voltage such that the TES is operating as a normal resistor with resistance
R_{\mathrm{n}}
. As we decrease the voltage the bias power dissipated on the TES bolometer island drops, and therefore the TES temperature also decreases until it reaches its superconducting critical temperature (
T_{\mathrm{c}}
). At
T_{\mathrm{c}}
the TES becomes sensitive to small temperature fluctuations and operates under negative electro-thermal feedback on its superconducting transition. As the voltage continues to drop eventually the TES “falls-off” the transition and becomes superconducting. See an example of an I–V curves collected at multiple bath temperatures in Figure
2
.

The
y
-intercept of a linear fit to the normal branch of the I–V curve, found at the high end of the voltage bias, provides the absolute current offset used to convert the measured change in TES current into an absolute current.
With knowledge of both TES
I
and
V
we can compute the TES resistance
R
and TES bias power
P
. In particular we define the saturation power
P_{\mathrm{sat}}
, as
P
measured at
R
equal to 70% of
R_{\mathrm{n}}
.
P_{\mathrm{sat}}
represents the maximum amount of power a bolometer can absorb while operating on the superconducting transition, or equivalently the amount of phonon power (
P_{\phi}
) that flows from the TES volometer island to the bath when the TES is operating on-transition and there is zero optical loading (
P_{\gamma}=0
) on the bolometer.

The saturation power extracted from I–V curves acquired at multiple bath temperatures (
T_{\mathrm{b}}
) are used in the following sections to determine the TES thermal conductivity parameters for each detector in the CLASS-W2 focal plane. From I–V curves we also determine the TES responsivity calibration (
S
) to convert measured TES current to power. The ratio of the responsivities calculated based on the slope of a single I–V curve versus multi-
T_{\mathrm{b}}
I–V curves yields an estimate of the TES loop gain
\mathscr{L}_{I}
and the logarithmic temperature sensitivity of the superconducting transition
\alpha=\frac{T}{R}\frac{dR}{dT}
. Finally, on-sky time constants measured from the phase of the polarization modulation synchronous signal are compared to the expected TES time constants derived from dark multi-
T_{\mathrm{b}}
I–V curve
\alpha
estimates.

3.1
TES thermal conductivity parameters

The phonon power,
P_{\phi}
, is modeled as the following function of bath temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
, critical superconducting temperature
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, thermal conductivity constant
\kappa
, and thermal conductivity power law index
n
:

P_{\phi}=\kappa(T_{\mathrm{c}}^{n}-T_{\mathrm{b}}^{n}).

(1)

Dark I–V curves acquired every
1\,\mathrm{mK}
step between bath temperatures of
70\,\mathrm{mK}
and
200\,\mathrm{mK}
compose a dataset of
P_{\phi}
vs.
T_{\mathrm{b}}
that is used to extract parameters
n
,
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, and
\kappa
. The focal plane temperature is recorded by a Rox thermometer and stabilized with a resistive heater whose current is continuously adjusted by a closed-loop servo system. We use indices
i
or
j
to denote an I–V curve acquired at a particular
T_{\mathrm{b}}
. At each
T_{\mathrm{b}}
we estimate the slope
\Delta P_{\phi}/\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}
by computing
\Delta P_{\phi}
and
\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}
from the I–V curves acquired at
T_{\mathrm{b}}\pm dT_{\mathrm{b}}
with
dT_{\mathrm{b}}=1\,\mathrm{mK}
. The
P_{\phi}
vs.
T_{\mathrm{b}}
slope is related to the thermal conductivity parameters through:

\left(\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{i}=-\kappa n(T_{\mathrm{b}})_{i}^{n-1}.

(2)

To isolate
n
from
\kappa
we compute the ratio of slopes at two different
T_{\mathrm{b}}
:

\frac{\left(\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{i}}{\left(\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{j}}=\frac{(T_{\mathrm{b}})_{i}^{n-1}}{(T_{\mathrm{b}})_{j}^{n-1}}.

(3)

We start our analysis by constraining
n
, which captures the curvature of the
P_{\phi}
vs.
T_{\mathrm{b}}
curve (see Figure
2
), and can be constrained for a single detector by the following logarithmic ratio of data points:

n=1+\log\frac{\left(\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{i}}{\left(\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{j}}/\log\frac{(T_{\mathrm{b}})_{i}}{(T_{\mathrm{b}})_{j}}.

(4)

A pair of I–V curves acquired at
T_{\mathrm{b}}<T_{\mathrm{c}}
provides a single estimate of
n
. With
\sim 100
I–V measurements below
T_{\mathrm{c}}
in our dataset we obtain
\sim 50
independent measurements of
n
, which we average to obtain a single value for each detector. This method relies on high quality I–V data acquired across a wide range of
T_{\mathrm{b}}
with small temperature spacing of
\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
. Reliable sub-mK
T_{\mathrm{b}}
thermometry and servo control is necessary to accurately measure
P_{\phi}
vs.
T_{\mathrm{b}}
.

For each detector we obtain an estimate of
n
. We average
n
across all detectors in the array to obtain a single array averaged
n
. This single value is used for all further per-detector fitting to extract
\kappa
and
T_{\mathrm{c}}
. Choosing a single array average
n
allows us to easily compare TES thermal parameters across detectors. Any intrinsic variation of
n
between detectors translates to additional variance in
\kappa
.

n
is computed only from module 3 and module 4 data since module 1 and module 2 I–V data quality is affected by discrete jumps in
I
that are under investigation.
We find an average
n=3.6
with a mean error of
0.01
. This result is consistent with the
100\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
SiN membrane ballistic phonon-transport measurements reported in
[
18

.
With
n
set to 3.6,
\kappa
can be derived from the derivative of the
P_{\phi}
with respect to
T_{\mathrm{b}}

\kappa_{i}=-\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{b}}^{n-1}}\frac{\Delta P_{\phi}}{\Delta T_{\mathrm{b}}}\right)_{i}.

(5)

A per-detector
\kappa
value is determined by averaging across all
T_{\mathrm{b}}
measurements (
\kappa=\overline{\kappa_{i}}
).
T_{\mathrm{c}}
is calculated per detector from:

(T_{\mathrm{c}})_{i}=\left(\frac{(P_{\phi})_{i}}{\kappa}+(T_{\mathrm{b}})^{n}_{i}\right)^{1/n}.

(6)

Again, the per-detector
T_{\mathrm{c}}
is calculated from the average across the multiple
T_{\mathrm{b}}
measurements (
T_{\mathrm{c}}
=
\overline{(T_{\mathrm{c}})_{i}}
).

3.2
Responsivity

The SQUID input, connected in series with the TES, measures changes in the TES current (
dI
) that are recorded by the MCE readout cards.
To combine data across multiple detectors and observing conditions, we determine the responsivity (
S
) calibration factors that convert a change
dI
to a change in power dissipated at the TES (
dP
). For CLASS, responsivity is determined from I–V data acquired before the observed time-ordered data (TOD). A per-detector optical efficiency factor (determined from planet observations) combined with bandpass measurements provides a final calibration from
dP
to sky optical power
dP_{\gamma}
or equivalently to CMB temperature
dT_{\mathrm{cmb}}
.

Three I–V responsivity estimates are considered for calibrating on-sky data, all proportional to the TES shunt resistor
R_{\mathrm{sh}}
. For CLASS we use shunt chips fabricated by NIST with
R_{\mathrm{sh}}=$250\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro\SIUnitSymbolOhm}$
.
We have no indication the shunt resistance changes across time and qualifying tests of the shunt chips show their resistances match the design value closely. Any small variation of shunt resistances across the array are expected to be constant across time, and therefore are absorbed into the per-detector optical efficiency calibration.

The simplest
I_{\mathrm{b}}
responsivity estimate is given by:

S_{I_{\mathrm{b}}}^{-1}=\left.\frac{dP}{dI}\right|_{I_{\mathrm{b}}}=-R_{\mathrm{sh}}I_{\mathrm{b}}.

(8)

I_{\mathrm{b}}
is always known since we set it to a value that places the detectors on the superconducting transition. Computing
I_{\mathrm{b}}
responsivity does not require I–V data, but note that in practice, we use I–V data to choose the optimal
I_{\mathrm{b}}
target. The systematic error of
I_{\mathrm{b}}
responsivity is typically in the tens of percent range, resulting in an over-estimate of the detector noise and an under-estimate of the optical efficiency. A second improved responsivity estimate (

I
at the operating bias:

(S^{*})^{-1}=\left.\frac{dP}{dI}\right|_{I_{\mathrm{b}}}=-R_{\mathrm{sh}}(I_{\mathrm{b}}-2I).

(9)

The absolute TES current
I

S^{*}

is constant throughout the transition.

A third more accurate responsivity estimate described in Ref.
[
4

relies on the slope
\Delta I_{\mathrm{b}}/{\Delta I}
of the I–V data:

S^{-1}=R_{\mathrm{sh}}I\frac{\Delta I_{\mathrm{b}}}{\Delta I}\left(1-\mathscr{L}_{I}^{-1}\right),

(10)

where the loop gain
\mathscr{L}_{I}
is defined as:

\mathscr{L}_{I}=\frac{P\alpha}{GT}=\frac{P}{GT}\left.\frac{T}{R}\frac{\partial R}{\partial T}\right|_{I}=\frac{I^{2}}{G}\left.\frac{\partial R}{\partial T}\right|_{I}.

(11)

Typically
\mathscr{L}_{I}
is large at the operating bias, and we approximate Equation
10

S^{-1}\approx R_{\mathrm{sh}}I\frac{\Delta I_{\mathrm{b}}}{\Delta I}.

(12)

[
4
]
. Responsivity results from I–V curves acquired under similar observing conditions across all observing seasons are binned and averaged, yielding an improved responsivity for each scan set.

3.4
Time constant

The response of the TES bolometer to a time varying optical signal is modeled with a single-pole transfer function with an optical time constant
\tau_{\gamma}
. The CLASS polarization modulators place the sky polarization near 10 Hz; therefore to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio we desire detectors with
\tau_{\gamma}\ll 1/(2\pi\times$10\text{\,}\mathrm{Hz}$)=$16\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
.
By fitting the hysteresis of the VPM synchronous signal
[
5
]
across the CLASS-W2 2025 dataset, we extract per-detector median time constants. The average detector time constants across modules are summarized in Table
2
. The median array time constant of
0.67\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}
achieves the desired detector speed goal. Figure
3
shows that the per-detector time constants are similar across the array.

The optical response of the TES bolometer is determined by the electro-thermal speed up of the bolometer’s thermal time constant
\tau=C/G
, where
C
is the heat capacity of the TES bolometer island. The CLASS design target of
C=$3\text{\,}\mathrm{pJ}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}$
, together with the array median thermal conductivity
G=$460\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}$
imply a thermal time constant of
\tau=$6.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
. Therefore, the TES electro-thermal feedback speeds up the thermal time constant
\tau
by a factor of
\sim
10.
Using the TES time constant model in
[
4
]
the array average speedup factor, equation
15
, is consistent with
\mathscr{L}_{I}\approx 24
, and
\beta\approx 1.5
, where
\beta
is the logarithmic current sensitivity of the superconducting transition
\beta=\frac{I}{R}\frac{dR}{dI}
.

\frac{\tau_{\gamma}}{\tau}=\frac{1+\beta+R_{\mathrm{sh}}/R}{1+\beta+R_{\mathrm{sh}}/R+\left(1-R_{\mathrm{sh}}/R\right)\mathscr{L}_{I}}.

(15)

4.2
Optical efficiency

The end-to-end detector optical efficiency (
\eta
), including the telescope optics, is obtained by comparing the measured point source signal power (
P_{\mathrm{s}}
) to that predicted for an ideal optical system. The observed amplitude is extracted from the TOD by fitting a model based on the detector beam profile, and calibrating the data to pico-watt power units with I–V responsivity. The received power depends on the optical efficiency, the solid angle of the source (
\Omega_{s}
), and its brightness temperature
T_{\mathrm{s}}
. The brightness temperatures of Jupiter and Venus —the point sources most commonly observed with CLASS—lie in the Rayleigh–Jeans regime; therefore:

P_{\mathrm{s}}=\eta\frac{\Omega_{s}}{\Omega}k_{\mathrm{B}}\Delta\nu T_{\mathrm{s}}.

(17)

The per-detector telescope optical efficiency was determined from 2025 Jupiter observations before the low-pass filter was installed. This analysis follows the same calibration procedure described in Ref.
[
12
]
.
The mean array telescope optical efficiency was 0.37, while module 1 had a higher average optical efficiency of 0.44 with six detectors above 0.5 (see Figure
5

\eta_{d}
) by dividing out the telescope optical efficiency
\eta_{t}=0.71

[
28
]
that includes beam spill, as well as reflection and absorption of the multiple telescope optical elements such as filters, lenses, and window. The array average detector-only optical efficiency is
0.52
, and the average for module 1 is
0.61
. The six detectors on the higher end of the optical efficiency distribution achieve a detector-only optical efficiency of
0.7
. The lower optical efficiency of detectors in module 3 and module 4 is under investigation. Nevertheless, all modules achieve photon noise limited performance.

\eta_{\mathrm{LPF}}=0.93
within the CLASS

An updated optical efficiency and beam model will be constructed in 2027 once Jupiter enters our observing sky area again. The Jupiter antenna temperature is small, well within the small-signal limit of our TES bolometers. Moreover, Jupiter brightness temperature is well characterized at

by measurement from WMAP

P_{\mathrm{sat}}=P_{\phi}-P_{\gamma}.

(18)

In the ideal case
P_{\gamma}
is entirely composed of radiation in the detector bandpass frequency range that is coupled through the OMT antennas. We find that a small fraction of the optical power appears to couple directly to the TES bolometer island across a wide frequency range illustrated by the orange curve in Figure
7
(left); often this excess power is referred to as a ‘blue-leak’, since a majority comes at frequencies above the detector bandpass. The waveguide connecting the feedhorn to the OMT has a cutoff frequency that efficiently attenuates optical power below the instrument’s bandpass.

This blue-leak power is measured by tracking the changes in saturation power of the dark TES detectors (DD). Ten DD are distributed across a module, and they share the same basic design as the optical detector (OD) TES but are not connected to any microstrips or antennas. Optical power coupling directly to the TES bolometer island is expected to be similar between OD and DD. Light that travels through the feedhorns can leak through the small gap between the detector wafer and the backshort (see Figure
B.1
), and a fraction of this optical power will dissipate directly on the TES bolometer island.
We assume a simple optical loading model for the OD with a component of power coupled through the OMT antenna
P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}
, and a blue-leak component that couples directly to the TES
P^{\mathrm{bl}}_{\gamma}
:

P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}=P_{\gamma}-P^{\mathrm{bl}}_{\gamma}.

(19)

The total optical loading
P_{\gamma}
is measured per-detector with every I–V curve. The amount of blue-leak power
P^{\mathrm{bl}}_{\gamma}
is estimated from the average optical loading across the ten dark detectors in a module
P^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\gamma}
. The average loading across all optical detectors in a module
P^{\mathrm{od}}_{\gamma}
minus
P^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\gamma}
provides a per-module estimate of the in-band sky signal power
P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}
coupled through the OMT antennas.

Due to the previously discussed I–V data noise jumps, module 1 and module 2 detector data acquired in dark-lab tests suffer from systematic errors. To avoid unphysical negative optical loading estimates for DD TESs, we apply offsets
\Delta P_{\mathrm{sat}}^{m1}=$1\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$
and
\Delta P_{\mathrm{sat}}^{m2}=$1.5\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$
to the module 1 and module 2 optical loading estimates, respectively. These offsets are determined from the average
P^{\mathrm{dd}}_{\gamma}

\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$

4.4.2
Optical loading results in 2026 with LPF

The 2026 LPF dataset includes data acquired between February 2026 and May 2026.
After the CLASS-W2 LPF filter was installed in February 2026, the optical loading on the dark detectors decreased from
\sim$1.1\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$
to
\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$

157\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}

0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}

Optical power below the LPF cutoff coupling directly to the TES; 3. Residual optical power transmitted through the LPF across a wide frequency range.

With the LPF installed, the optical loading on module 3 and module 4 OD TESs averaged to
2.9\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}

4.1\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}
after applying the
\Delta P_{\mathrm{sat}}^{m1,m2}

\sim$0.2\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$
of blue-leak power
P^{\mathrm{bl}}_{\gamma}

The detector’s antenna temperature,
T_{\mathrm{a}}
, is calculated by dividing the measured in-band optical power,
P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}

\eta

3

T_{\mathrm{a}}=\frac{P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}}{\eta}\frac{dT_{\mathrm{RJ}}}{dP_{\gamma}}=\frac{P^{\mathrm{*}}_{\gamma}}{\eta k_{\mathrm{B}}\Delta\nu}.

(20)

Module 4, located at the center of the focal plane, measures an antenna temperature of
\sim$20\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
(with the LPF installed). The other modules record values about
\sim
10% higher, due to two effects: 1. extra beam spill onto the warm optics for detectors closer to the focal plane edge; 2. module 1 and module 2 sit at the bottom of the array observing through lower elevation angles that sample a larger atmospheric column and thus a higher effective atmospheric antenna temperature. At the 45 degree elevation angle pointing of the center module, the
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
antenna temperature of the atmosphere together with the CMB account for
\sim$12\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
. The remaining
\sim$8\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
come from beam spill onto the cold and warm optics, as well as the emissivity of the optical elements.

When the cryostat window is covered with a metal plate the observed antenna temperature of the module 4 is
\sim$7\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
. Since module 4 beams strike the metal plane at normal incidence, the measured optical loading represents cold optical beam spill plus twice the in-band emissivity of the cryostat’s internal optical elements (including the window) since each pixel sees their direct emission and the reflection from the metal cover. The beam spill onto the cold stage is expected to contribute
\sim$1\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
; therefore, the emissivity of the IR filters, lenses, and window account for
\sim$6\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
of the loading with the metal plate and therefore
\sim$3\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
in the on-sky configuration.
Thus, in the on-sky configuration roughly
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
of the antenna temperature arises from emission by the cryostat optics and cold spill, while an additional
\sim$4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}$
comes from emissivity and beam spill in the warm optical elements—the primary and secondary mirrors, the VPM, and the black forebaffle at the telescope-cage aperture.

(21)

(23)

5
conclusions

We have deployed and characterized, through dark-lab and on-sky observations, the performance of the first four modules of the CLASS-W2 detector focal plane. This version of the CLASS-W2
90\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
telescope achieves an array sensitivity of
\mathrm{NET}=16~$\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro K}$
and improves the CLASS CMB mapping speed by
41\%
, with further improvement expected in 2027 when three additional modules will be installed.

On-sky observation with and without a free-space metal-mesh LPF installed in front of the focal plane shows that high frequency out-of-band ‘blue-leak’ optical power couples directly to the TES bolometer island affecting both dark (DD) and optical detectors (OD). By installing the LPF we reduced the ‘blue leak’ power by
0.9\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}
and the per-detector NEP
2
by
($13\text{\,}\mathrm{aW}$)^{2}
. After accounting for the LPF transmission, the array sensitivity improves by 4%.
Even with the LPF installed, we still measure excess noise comparable to the detector phonon noise. This excess may be explained by a combination of noise contributions including: readout noise, higher
F_{\mathrm{link}}
factor in the phonon noise model, additional detector noise sources not included in the model, and residual out-of-band power below the LPF cutoff coupling directly to the TES.
The CLASS-W2 average in-band detector optical loading of
3.3\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}
is consistent with the loading seen by the CLASS-W1 telescope when differences in optical efficiencies are taken into account. This loading translates to an average focal plane antenna temperature of
23\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
(
20\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
for the center module that is subject to less warm spill), with a
13\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
contribution from the atmosphere and the CMB, and a
10\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
contribution from the optical spill and emissivity of the cold and warm optical components.

The end-to-end optical efficiency including detector and telescope optics for module 3 and module 4 is about 0.33, while for module 1 and module 2 it is higher at 0.42. The module 3 and module 4 optical efficiency is about half the efficiency of the CLASS-W1 telescope upgraded detector modules. To maximize optical efficiency, the CLASS-W1 detectors employ monocrystalline silicon as the dielectric in their microwave components, reducing loss and enabling precise bandpass fabrication. On the other hand the detector yield of the CLASS-W1 telescope is 66%
[
28
]
compared to the 94% yield achieved by the CLASS-W2 detectors. Additionally, the
T_{\mathrm{c}}=\pm$3\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
uniformity of the CLASS-W2 AlMn TES is exceptionally good, compared to the larger
T_{\mathrm{c}}=\pm$8.5\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
for the CLASS-W1 MoAu TES.
Fine-tuning the AlMn recipe
[
14
,
15
]
to achieve the average targeted
T_{\mathrm{c}}=$160\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
is a principal goal of the ongoing detector fabrication of the remaining three CLASS-W2 modules.

The achieved array averaged detector bandpass has a center frequency of
95.2\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
,
2.6\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
higher than the simulated bandpass. The
28.3\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
bandwidth is
2.7\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
below the target. Increasing the detector bandwidth in future fabrication cycles is one path to improve detector sensitivity. Nevertheless, the realized bandpass falls well within the atmospheric band, allowing for high sensitivity mapping of the CMB.
Observations of Jupiter provide a measure of the total optical efficiency and detector beam profile. The array average beam solid angle
\Omega=$124\pm\pm 0.3\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro sr}$
, and Full-Width-Half-Maximum angle of
0.592\pm\pm 0.003\text{\,}\deg
match expectations. Moon observations reveal side-lobe pick up at
<0.1\%
level that is co-aligned with other feedhorns in the same module. The amplitudes of these optical cross-talk signals are suppressed after installing the LPF.

The responsivity of the detectors is estimated from on-sky I–V data. For module 3 and module 4 the I–V slope responsivity closely matches
S^{*}
when biased low on the transition, as expected in the high
\mathscr{L}_{I}
limit. For module 1 and module 2 the I–V slope responsivity is
\sim
20% larger than
(S^{*})^{-1}
. This unexpected difference is under investigation but was confirmed by multi-
T_{\mathrm{b}}
responsivity measurements.
The expected TES thermal time constant is
\tau=$6.5\text{\,}\mathrm{ms}$
, while the measured optical time constants, sped-up by electro-thermal feedback, are about ten times faster. This speed-up factor is consistent with the average array
\mathscr{L}_{I}=24
and an inferred
\beta=1.5
.

Multi-
T_{\mathrm{b}}
I–V data are combined across module 3 and module 4 detectors to determine the TES
\alpha
parameter as a function of TES resistance. The detectors are typically biased near the middle of the transition, at half their normal resistance; therefore we expect
\alpha\approx 100
during typical CMB observations.
From dark-lab multi-
T_{\mathrm{b}}
I–V data we also extract the average TES thermal parameters:
T_{\mathrm{c}}=$184\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
,
\kappa=$10.4\text{\,}\mathrm{nW}\text{\,}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}$
,
G=$460\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}\text{/}\mathrm{K}$
,
P_{\phi}=$23.3\text{\,}\mathrm{pW}$
,
n=3.6
, and
R_{\mathrm{n}}=$7.8\text{\,}\mathrm{m\SIUnitSymbolOhm}$
.
The average achieved
T_{\mathrm{c}}
is
24\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}
above the target
T_{\mathrm{c}}=$160\text{\,}\mathrm{mK}$
. This means the detectors have higher saturation power than initially targeted but also higher detector NEP. The high saturation power is convenient for observing bright sources like the Moon, but to optimize sensitivity we prefer to reduce
T_{\mathrm{c}}
and cut the detector saturation power by half.
