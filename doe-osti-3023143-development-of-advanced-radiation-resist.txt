SC0017810 Final Technical Report

  Development of Advanced, Radiation Resistant, Optical-based Detector Technology for Future
 HEP Experiments

1. Introduction and Project Objectives.
The objective and goals of this R&D effort have been focused on the development of ultra-compact,
precision electromagnetic (EM) calorimetry modules for future particle physics experiments, addressing
key Priority Research Directions (PRD) for calorimetry outlined in the DOE Basic Research Needs
(BRN) workshop for HEP Instrumentation [1]. The ECFA study for a future hadron collider [2], set
metrics for the performance for EM calorimetry in various regions of a collider experiment designed for
FCC-hh operation. These include: a timing resolution st ≤ 50 ps, and an energy resolution: σ! / E =
10%/√E ⊕ 0.3/E ⊕ 0.7%. The objectives of this project have been to meet and exceed these values, or
to indicate future directions to achieve the desired performance. The research team is actively
participating in the CPAD RDC9 [3, 4] and ECFA DRD-CALO [5] calorimetry efforts, ensuring that the
proposed research aligns with broader calorimetry development goals. The R&D effort is broadly
applicable, relevant to collider environments such as the FCC-ee [6, 7, 8], FCC-hh [2], muon collider
applications as well as forward and fixed target and beam dump experiments.

2. Experimental Approaches.
To carry out the R&D, a modular, sampling calorimetry structure, called a RADiCAL module, has been
created and studied in beam tests at Fermilab and at CERN. In this section we provide background on the
RADiCAL techniques implemented during this program [9] and analysis of results from recent beam tests
at CERN using electrons in the energy range (25 GeV < E < 150 GeV) [10].

2.1 Modular Structure.
Modules are dimensionally similar in size to a human index finger (see Figs. 1 and 2). For the experimental
measurements reported here the sampling structure has consisted of interleaved layers of 29 LYSO:Ce
crystal scintillation tiles of 1.5 mm thickness and 28 tungsten plates of 2.5 mm thickness, each of cross-
sectional area 14 x 14 mm2 and stacked to a physical length of 135 mm. This configuration provides a
sampling fraction of ~17%, a depth of 25 Xo and a Molière radius of 13.7 mm, yielding a structure that is
ultracompact both transversely and longitudinally.

The use of LYSO:Ce crystal scintillation tiles (λ ~ 425 nm) provides both high density and excellent timing
characteristics due to the high overall light yield (3 x 104 photons/MeV) and significant light yield in the
first nanosecond of response (740 photons/MeV). The light emitted from the LYSO:Ce tiles is then
wavelength-shifted to the 495 < l < 510 nm wavelength range utilizing the quartz capillaries, which are
filled with polymer or ceramic wavelength shifting filaments. Quartz (fused silica with high water content)
was selected as the capillary material due to its radiation hardness, a critical requirement for experiments
in high-radiation environments like the FCC-hh, muon colliders and beam dump experiments. The
development and testing of these radiation-hard materials, particularly LYSO:Ce plates, WLS materials
such as DSB1 and LuAG:Ce, and quartz capillaries, have been an ongoing priority in our work [9].

The distinction between modules for testing thus far has been based on the type of wavelength shifting
capillaries used and the number of SiPM used in the readout and their location. Initially the capillaries were
filled with an organic liquid wavelength shifter based upon an EJ-309 and containing the WLS dye DSB1
and then sealed. To handle the challenge of liquid contraction and expansion, a tiny spherical expansion
reservoir was blown in the end of each capillary into which the WLS liquid was filled. And this limited the
read out of the optical signals to the opposite end indicated in Fig. 1, where the ends of the capillaries could
be polished optically for connection to SiPM, either directly or via optical cables dependent upon SiPM
placement. A 4 x 4 test array of such modules was created for the CMS Endcap Calorimeter Downselect
competition by the group, prior to this program of support. Results from the subsequent analysis are
indicated in Section 2.2 below and focused on energy resolution.

 1

Subsequent to this initial testing, the liquid WLS methodology with its “single ended readout” was replaced
through the use of solid WLS materials, either organic plastic or inorganic ceramic filaments inserted into
the capillaries. This afforded significant benefits in several ways: first, the optical signals could be read
out from both ends of the capillaries; second, short length WLS filaments inserted into the capillaries could
be placed at specialized depths within the module, particularly advantageous for timing or position
measurement (Fig. 3); third, WLS filaments extending through the full length of the module are
advantageous for energy measurement (Fig. 4); fourth, there would be no risk of fluid leakage since all
materials are solids.

Additionally, and very importantly, it was noted from GEANT4 simulation that timing and position
resolution measurements could be studied effectively using a single module, rather than with a costly
modular array.[11] That this is indeed the case, Fig. 5 indicates the transverse shower profile of a 50 GeV
electron incident at the center of the 14 mm x 14 mm cross sectional area of a RADiCAL module, when
observed at the depth of EM shower maximum (“shower max”). The simulation indicates that, at that
depth, the transverse profile of the EM shower is localized within a region of very small size: dr ~ X0 = 4.5
mm. When a beam electron is incident near the module’s symmetry axis, the EM shower is effectively be
fully contained within the single module. And within this small region of shower containment, there are a
substantial number of charged (shower) particles depending upon beam energy, which clearly distinguish
EM signals from MIP signals due to charged hadrons or muons. And these particles provide a large and
spatially-localized optical pulse.

Fig. 6 presents the energy fraction deposited in successive LYSO:Ce tiles in a module for electrons over a
range of beam energies, based on GEANT4 simulation [10]. The EM shower max shifts logarithmically
with increasing beam energy, allowing WLS filaments placed in this general region to measure effectively
the shower timing over a broad range of energies. The positioning of the WLS filaments in this region is
crucial or both for position and timing resolution. Timing performance will be primarily determined by the
detected light yield within the first nanosecond of the optical pulses and the rise time of the corresponding
photosensor and high-gain amplifier signals.

At that depth, the width of the shower is given by the radiation length, rather than the Molière radius. As
Fig. 5 shows, the shower is fully contained at shower max. And, as shown in Fig. 6, the location of shower
max is logarithmically dependent upon energy, so WLS filaments of 15 mm length, and positioned within
the capillaries in that region, will effectively measure position, timing and local energy.

2.2 Measurement of Energy Resolution
Beam tests of a 4 x 4 modular array (Fig. 7) were conducted at CERN in the H4 beamline [9]. The active
volume of the array measured 56 mm x 56 mm x 114 mm, and there were 64 independent readout channels
for this structure, with 4 WLS capillaries per module. The capillaries for this study were filled with
EJ309/DSB1 based liquid wavelength shifter, and the modules were structurally of the type shown in Fig.
1 above. Each capillary was connected at its downstream end by a clear fiberoptic waveguide to its own
individual HPK SiPM photosensor having 15μm square pixels located outside the beam region (Fig.7).

 3

The results from these studies provided several key insights upon which further improvements in energy
resolution are expected to be possible. Specifically, the energy measurements recorded with electron beam
(Fig. 8) are well described by a fit of the form σ! / E = 15.74%/√E ⊕ 0.1/E ⊕ 1%, indicating that the
constant term is already reasonably well controlled. A GEANT4 simulation study [9] was performed to
assesses the various contributors to the energy resolution (see Fig. 9). These include sampling fraction,
photo statistics, noise, energy leakage, longitudinal and transverse non-uniformity. As Fig. 9 shows, the
dominant contributor at low energy is the sampling fraction. In future work, this will be readily addressed
by increasing the thickness of the LYSO:Ce scintillation tiles, and by detecting the WLS light from the
capillaries at both ends, using solid WLS filaments rather than liquid WLS materials.

2.3. Measurement of Shower Timing Resolution at EM Shower Max. For these measurements, the
RADiCAL module was instrumented with capillaries as indicated in Fig. 3. The capillaries were of 183mm
length, having an outer diameter of 1150 µm and an inner (core) diameter of 950 µm. Within each core
was positioned a solid DSB1 WLS filament of 900 µm diameter and 15 mm length, situated in the region
of shower max for showers in the energy range of 25 GeV < E < 150 GeV. The capillaries transmitted the
wave-shifted light (at l ~ 495 nm) to Hamamatsu HDR2 SiPM photosensors mounted on custom readout
card positioned at both the upstream and downstream ends of the module. The module was enclosed in a
3D printed light-tight enclosure. Initial beam tests were conducted at the Fermilab Test Beam Facility
(FTBF), but the principal data recording occurred at the CERN H2 Beamline where the full energy range
25 GeV < E < 150 GeV for electrons could be explored. The arrangement of instrumentation for the CERN
test (see Figs. 10 and 11) included in order along the beamline: a beam chamber provided by CERN to
measure the horizontal and vertical position of beam electrons incident on the test apparatus; followed by
(A1) trigger scintillators of dimension 1 cm x 1 cm; (B) a microchannel plate PMT used to provide a high-
resolution timing reference; (A2 ) scintillation counters of 2 cm x 2 cm; (C) the RADiCAL module under
test; and (D) and a Pb glass array at the downstream end of the detector chain, used as a tail catcher for
energy leakage if any from the RADiCAL module and as a monitor for beam muons or hadrons.

Timing for each of the eight SiPM channels (four at the upstream end of the module, four at the downstream
end) was determined by measuring the time-over-threshold on the rising edge of their differential high-gain
signals. A reference time stamp for synchronization was provided by the MCP PMT. The time resolution
of the module was determined using several methods, two of which were: (Method-1) the average of the
time stamps from all eight channels (four upstream and four downstream SiPMs) compared to the MCP
timing, and (Method-2) the difference between the average time stamps of the four downstream SiPM
channels and the average of the time stamps of the four upstream SiPM channels. Method-2 effectively
removed the contribution of MCP resolution, and thus was considered the “best estimator” of the timing
resolution.

2.4 Study of Shower Position Localization at EM Shower Max.
For shower position determination, the spatial localization of an EM shower is derived from the signal
amplitudes captured by the capillaries. These signals are obtained from low-gain amplification of the SiPM
signals at shower max. This allows the shower position to be localized within a module to within a few
millimeters, which is beneficial for event reconstruction in high-pileup environments, such as the endcap
and forward regions of collider detectors. Fig. 14 compares the incoming electron beam position,
determined from a beam chamber just upstream of the RADiCAL module, based on the pulse heights of
the low-gain signals from the four upstream and four downstream SiPMs independently. The vias (holes in
the tiles) of diameter 1.5 mm in the module plates are clearly visible, which allow for the penetration of the
WLS capillaries through the module, indicating that the module itself can localize the shower position
independently of the incoming beam position [10]. It is interesting to note that in each of the Fig.14 images,
the detected pulse height in the spatial region surrounding each capillary location, has a radius that matches
closely the simulation prediction (Fig. 8).

A module-specific coordinate system for shower location can be established by comparing the SiPM pulse
heights among the low-gain signals from all four capillaries [25]. For example, one coordinate (v) can be
formed by subtracting the pulse height in the lower right capillary from that in the upper left and dividing

 6

by the sum of both: v = (EUPL – ELOR)/ (EUPL + ELOR). An orthogonal coordinate (u) can be calculated
similarly from the upper right and lower left capillaries: u = (EUPR – ELOL)/ (EUPR + ELOL). This (u,v)
coordinate system provides a measure of the EM shower location within the RADiCAL module, which can
be correlated with the (x,y) coordinates provided by a beam chamber measurement, on an event-by-event
basis. Analyses employing this approach as well as other methods are currently ongoing.

It is also noteworthy that while the capillary vias are visible in Fig. 14 because, in those locations, the
material consists of quartz rods and DSB1 WLS filaments and no scintillator present, when LuAG:Ce is
used as the WLS material, scintillation signals can be detected even at the via locations since LuAG:Ce is
also a scintillator. Detailed analyses comparing these characteristics are in progress.

3. Comments on instrumentation development.

3.1 Scintillators and Wavelength Shifters:
The group, led by Caltech, has extensive background in crystal and ceramic scintillators and their
experimental applications. [13-21] The scintillation crystal LYSO:Ce has been utilized for its density and
fluorescence efficiency, with significant light within the first nanosecond. While the overall decay time
is relatively long (40ns), it has a single exponential decay, avoiding a long tail. Two well matched WLS
materials have been used: filaments containing the organic dye DSB1 [22], with fast ~3 ns decay, and
LuAG:Ce ceramic filaments, whose radiation resistance exceeds even that of LYSO:Ce. The Radiation
Induced Absorption Coefficients (RAIC) are plotted for both proton and neutron exposures (see Figs.15,
16) [9]. A new and potentially exciting ceramic material LuO:Yb has been identified and produced [23]
with ultrashort pulse rise and fall. Commercially available WLS materials (EJ-286) and specialized
wavelength shifters called flavonols have been identified and synthesized [24], which can provide a
promising spectral match to the LuO:Yb.

3.2 Photosensors.
SiPM R&D: Early in the RADiCAL project, the concept of thermoelectric cooling (TEC) and annealing
of SiPMs was first introduced [9], and the approach has since been embraced by CMS for HL-LHC
application, where the TEC methodology is now standard practice for the CMS BTL detector. The Notre

 7

Dame group, in collaboration with FBK, developed small pixel SiPMs (5 µm, 7.5 µm, 10 µm and 12 µm)
to create very fast signals with very fast risetime and reduced recharge tail, and to avoid the prospect of
signal saturation in shower energy measurements [9]. The devices showed very sharp signal rise and fall
with tails reduced with the smaller pixel devices (see Fig. 17). Larger pixel SiPMs, with improved PDE,
are now the preferred option for timing. For the RADiCAL tests reported here for timing and position
resolution, HPK HDR2 SiPMs with 15 µm pixels have been used [10].

GaInP Photosensor R&D: Also early in the RADiCAL project, the concept of large bandgap photosensors
was considered. The Virginia group, in collaboration with LightSpin, Inc, worked to develop GaInP
photosensors, whose large band gap offered the potential for significant improvement in radiation tolerance
over conventional Silicon-based (SiPM) devices and possible room temperature operation.[27] The device
development proceeded through 5 generational steps, and in the 5th generation, devices with 10mm and
25mm pixels were produced and with which individual photopeaks were observed similar to SiPM (see
Fig.18). While bench tests and irradiation studies with the devices have shown promise, the studies
indicated that significantly more work would be needed on surface preparation to avoid the presence of
surface currents. The project was ended without a fully operational device useful for detector application.

3.3 Front end electronics.
Signal amplification has been accomplished using custom front end electronics developed by Virginia
with differential high gain amplification of the SiPM signals for timing measurements (time over
threshold), and low gain amplification for energy measurements to provide time-walk corrections as well
as shower position localization within a module [10]. The waveforms have been digitized using CAEN
DT5742 digitizers with 5 GHz sampling to allow detailed analysis of signal shape for timing.

4. Summary and Conclusions
The next generation of accelerator and collider facilities proposed by the high-energy physics (HEP)
community is at least 15 years away. During this time, there is a valuable opportunity to develop and refine
new detector technologies that can offer powerful solutions for future HEP experiments.

Calorimetric detectors are a key area for innovation in timing, spatial, and energy resolution. The ultra-
compact RADiCAL modules serve as an ideal testbed for testing new optical materials, photosensors, and
electronics for electromagnetic calorimetry. Focusing on measurements at the electromagnetic (EM)
shower maximum—where the shower size is determined by the radiation length—can significantly improve
timing and spatial resolution, while increasing the sampling fraction improves energy resolution. Advances
in silicon photomultipliers (SiPMs) and readout electronics are critical to handling faster scintillator and
wavelength shifter signals.

Current studies have demonstrated the capabilities of the RADiCAL sampling calorimetry technique.
Although originally aimed at future collider applications, the research has broad potential across other
fields. The technologies being developed here could also benefit particle physics more broadly, nuclear
physics, materials science, medical physics, and homeland security, highlighting the versatility and
potential of this R&D program.
