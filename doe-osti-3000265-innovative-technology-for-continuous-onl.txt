 SECTION II: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This project aims to evaluate and develop the concept of optical basicity to solar molten salts,
design an online monitoring system for implementation in commercial plants, and train a diverse
workforce for the CSP industry. This is the final report.

Major Goals and Objectives
The overall objective of this project is to evaluate and develop the concept of optical basicity to
solar molten salts, design an online monitoring system for implementation in commercial plants,
and train a diverse workforce for the CSP industry.
The outcome of a successful project will help DOE’s effort to improve online monitoring, improve
lifetime and reliability of structural components, lower operational costs, and enhance safety in
CSP plants. The project will also help develop a diverse workforce.
The overall objective is to validate, test, and create an optical basicity scale for chloride salts.
However, the concepts will originally be tested and validated in nitrate salts for reduced
complexity of experiments and optimization of experimental conditions. The concepts will then be
evaluated for chloride salts. If concept does not appear to apply for chloride salts, then we will
attempt using carbonate salts.
The objectives for each period are as follows:
 • Identify a probe ion (from Bi 3+, Pb2+, Tl+, Cd2+) for use in solar nitrate and chloride salts
       (Na/K at 565oC);
 • Design an optical basicity scale based on the experimental data of optical absorbance;
 • Correlate the optical basicity scale with the experimentally measured corrosion potentials
 at different oxidizing conditions.
 • Develop a training module and train a diverse workforce on online corrosion monitoring
 using the optical basicity data.

The proposed research will be accomplished via the following specific tasks:
 Task 1. Literature review
 Task 2. Identification of probe cation for optical basicity scales
 Task 3. Development of optical basicity scale
 Task 4. Correlation of optical basicity and corrosion activity
 Task 5. Determine sensitivity of the probe to corrosion conditions at concentrations
 expected in real situations
 Task 6. Development of a procedure for an untrained operator to follow and conduct.
 Task 7. Training of a diverse set of students

Technical Abstract
As demand for sustainable energy sources increases in response to calls for lower carbon

emissions, research and development to improve relevant materials is imperative. Concentrated

solar power (CSP) and thermal energy storage (TES) are valuable technologies that, when

coupled, allows for intermittent solar power to be stored as thermal energy in molten salt.

Thermal storage allows for energy to be accessed when sunlight is not available. This improves

wide-scale grid stability, which is a common issue with other renewable energy sources.

Molten salt is commonly used in CSP/TES, as its high operating temperature, high energy density,

and thermal conductivity contribute to high operating efficiencies. However, molten salts are

highly corrosive in atmospheres containing water and oxygen that are present in the CSP/TES. The

salt can corrode the storage materials, typically nickel-based alloys, causing ruptures and

subsequent plant shutdowns. In aqueous systems, pH can be an predictor of corrosion , but the

non-aqueous, harsh environment of molten chlorides makes pH measurements irrelevant.

Alternative basicity metrics, such as optical basicity, are needed to predict corrosion. Optical

basicity allows for measurement of the Lewis basicity, or the extent of electron donation of

ligands in the molten salt, via the nephelauxetic effect of an added probe ion. In this work the

optical basicity of several alkali and alkaline earth chloride salts was measured by doping them

with d10s2 probe ions, Pb2+ and Bi3+, and quantifying the probe ion’s redshift in frequency relative

to that observed in the reference salt, LiCl-KCl eutectic. The probe ion absorbance frequency was

observed in-situ using UV-vis spectroscopy for salts melted in a custom furnace.

This work found Pb2+ and Bi3+ to be valid probes for molten chloride salts, with alkali chlorides

being the most basic, alkali/alkaline earth chlorides containing Ca 2+ and Sr2+ being slightly less

Page 5 of 99

basic, and AlCl3-NaCl being the least basic. Increasing temperatures also reflected a higher

basicity for LiCl-KCl and AlCl3-NaCl. While Bi3+ OB data was consistent with Pb2+ data, there was

significant volatilization of the probe that made detection impractical.

The addition of transition metals such as Fe (III) and Cr (II) introduced charge transfer spectra that

overshadowed the probe ion absorption peaks in the UV region, making OB determination

impossible. Finally, UV-vis absorption data was collected for molten Li-Na-KCO3 eutectic. This

eutectic was observed to rapidly corrode silica, so sapphire was used as an alternative material.

The carbonate eutectic was also observed to readily precipitate the Pb2+ probe ion as lead oxide,

so the OB could not be determined by UV-vis in this case.

In conclusion, while the concept of optical basicity was proven as hypothesized in the original

proposal, the technical challenges to monitor it online under real-world concentrations of species

were unsurmountable. This project explored several pathways, many of them innovative and

custom solutions requiring significant amount of time, to achieve the goals. However, it was

concluded that it is not possible to monitor optical basicity using present day systems. An overall

schematic of the workflow shown in next page provides an overview of the studies undertaken

and pathways explored.

Abstract
Over several decades, extensive research has been devoted to developing the optical basicity

concept to quantify Lewis basicity of oxidic media. Spectroscopy (ultraviolet (UV), infrared (IR),

and fluorescence) was originally used to experimentally determine optical basicity, but linear

refractive index and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) have been more recent techniques

for determining basicity of glasses. While glasses have been one media of study, slags, oxyanion

melts, and molten halide salts are also of interest. The concept has been only briefly explored in

molten salts, but optical basicity could be a valuable tool for assessing its corrosivity. This review

summarizes different techniques used to determine optical basicity, their pitfalls, and the

preliminary work outlined for molten salts.

Page 8 of 99

1. Introduction

Molten salts are becoming increasingly important in the sustainable energy sector, particularly

in storing thermal energy in concentrating solar power plants (CSP). They have desirable

properties as heat transfer media, such as high operating temperatures with high thermal

stability, high density, high heat capacity, and low vapor pressure. 1 However, in the high-

temperature conditions molten salts become corrosive to metal they contact. To prevent

degradation and promote longevity of equipment exposed to molten salt, methods to monitor

and quantify the basicity must be developed. Traditional methods of measuring acidity and

basicity of a system are defined by the ability of an acid to donate protons, as described by the

Bronsted-Lowry theory. These are suitable for aqueous systems, but optical basicity (OB), a

metric historically used to study glasses, slags, and other oxide systems, also has the potential

to monitor the harsh environment of molten salts. OB uses absorption spectrometry and other

optical indicators to determine the effects of changes in the molten salt composition on the

basicity. Despite its relevance, little research has been done to explore OB in molten salt

systems. This review intends to explore the history of OB, its challenges, and the work done so

far for different media through the lens of its application to molten salt chemistry.

2. Discussion

2.1 Definition of Optical Basicity

Optical basicity (OB) is empirically rooted in glass systems. Much of OB theory concerns oxidic

and oxyanion systems, but OB can also quantify the Lewis basicity of halide and sulfide systems.

Page 9 of 99

The negative charges, i.e., the oxide electron density, are associated with the Lewis acid-base

theory where the oxygen atoms donate their electrons to a positively charged ion. The transfer

reduces the charge of positively charged ions that are present in accordance with Pauling’s

theory of electronegativity.

2.2 Optical Basicity Factors

2.2.2 Electronegativity

OB is also related to the electronegativity difference between the ligand and its cation. In oxidic

systems, the degree of ionicity or covalency of the oxide ion and its cation will vary. Duffy’s

relationships with electronegativity stem from Jorgensen’s work that correlates the optical

electronegativity with h, and in turn, the electronegativity of the metal, which closely matches

Page 14 of 99

experimentally-determined Pauling oxide electronegativity values. 8, 12 Discrepancies typically

stem from covalent bonding and heat of formation contributions to electronegativity. 4 Some

authors have related polarizability to the electronegativity to predict OB, 13 and others contest

that average electron density should be used in place of electronegativity to calculate

theoretical OB, particularly in the context of oxide-halide systems.14, 15

Different ligands will have more ionic or more covalent bonds with the metal ion, and this will

dictate the basicity. Halide systems typically have more covalent interactions at lower

electronegativities, but this is not true for all systems. Halides are observed to have decreasing

basicity with increasing electronegativity due to the increased covalent interactions. 16 Two

ligands with the same electronegativity will not necessarily have similar basicity, and this is

because they may be coordinated differently with the probe ion. For example, sulfates and

chlorides have similar electronegativities but the former has greater ionicity and yields more

basic melts.6

and energy gap which are more convenient for solid oxides and glasses. 17 OB can be measured

using glass refractivity; this method is beneficial for instances where transition metals reduce

the transparency, the probe ion absorption spectra are no longer visible in the UV region, and

the d→d spectra are difficult to interpret. Also, as heterogeneous glass structures are

developed, probe-ion site selectivity becomes more pronounced, and average OB is hard to

obtain experimentally.18 Oxygen ionic refraction is dependent on the glass composition and the

type of oxygen present (bridging, nonbridging, and free ion) which also makes it a good

2.3 Lux-Flood Theory and Corrosion

As opposed to an aqueous system where the proton is of primary focus, the negatively charged

oxide or halide ion is used to determine the basicity. The basicity depends on the activity of the

oxide or halide ion and is directly related to the corrosivity by creating conditions for dissolution

and reaction with the metal oxide or bulk metal. The Lux-Flood theory is used to describe

oxyanion salts and is analogous to aqueous systems and uses pO 2- rather than pH to quantify

basicity. The oxide ion is the base, and the oxide ion acceptor is the acid. The dissolution of a

metal oxide film by salts in acidic and basic conditions according to the Lux-Flood theory is

illustrated in Figure 3.26 When there are fewer oxide ions, the salt acts as an acid and the oxide

layer on the metal surface dissociates into metal ions and oxide ions in the salt. Alternatively, a

basic salt has excess oxide ions that react with the metallic oxide layer. This forms oxyanions

that dissolve in the salt, removing metal ions from the surface. 26 Depending on both the

potential and basicity of the system, dissolution of the bulk metal can also occur by oxidation.

Halide salts will also corrode the bulk metal directly by halogenation, particularly when there

are water or oxygen impurities.26 Halide anions in a salt are typically less basic than oxide ions

because they are less polarizable and are not as likely to give up their charge in the presence of

metal cations.27 The nephelauxetic parameter h describes the orbital expansion power of the

Page 18 of 99

halide, and though it varies for fluoride and oxide ions, it provides a general trend for basicity of

halide salts.12 As h and the covalency of the halide-metal ion interaction increases, there is an

increased OB (F- < Cl- < Br- < I-).16, 28

Figure 3. Corrosion of metal in oxyanion molten salt (Reproduced with copyright permissions 26)

Page 19 of 99

The Lux-Flood theory defines basicity according to the oxide ion activity whereas OB expresses

Lewis basicity and the tendency to release oxide ions. For example, in a sulfate or carbonate

melt, the Lux-Flood reaction describes the transference of oxide ions from the sulfate or

carbonate to the metal ion solute, and the reaction between the metal ion solute and freed

oxide ion is considered. OB, however, considers the metal ion solute interaction with the oxide

ions that are within the sulfate or carbonate. 29 Because the Lux-Flood theory does not consider

the covalent or ionic association of the oxide ions, basicities derived from the Lux-Flood theory

do not always agree with OB. The OB theory was initially developed for oxide glasses and slags

that incorporate oxide ions into their structure. Because of limited dissociation and fewer free

oxide ions in the network system, the Lux-Flood theory doesn’t accurately describe the

basicity.9 Furthermore, the Lux-Flood theory only considers single ion activities, but the activity

of the oxide depends on the cation that which the oxide ion is associated. For example, the

oxide ions are associated with different cations in CaO and MgO, and these compounds have

different activities and basicities.9 Oxide ion activities are also difficult to measure on their own,

and have previously been estimated using the concentration of the alkali metal oxide. Duffy

suggests that this is not a valid assumption for non-dilute and multicomponent systems

because they are non-ideal.6 OB directly measures covalent bond formation between a probe

ion and oxide or halide which allows for comparison of basicity across multicomponent

systems.6,9

2.4 Glass and Oxide Systems

2.4.1 Probe Ion Doping

Oxide and oxyanion systems have been the prioritized area of research for optical basicity for

the purpose of studying slags for steel production (e.g., removing sulfur from molten metal),

glassmaking, and deep-ultraviolet materials.9, 30 Doping the oxidic system with a probe ion

allows for experimental determination of the optical basicity where different system

compositions yield a redshift in the UV probe-ion spectra. Preliminary research focused on

“doping” systems with probe ions and collecting data on various glass compositions. These data

were later used to construct relationships to other measurable parameters (refractivity, IR

spectra, electronegativity, basicity moderating parameter) to predict OB according to

composition.4 Binary and ternary alkali and alkali earth oxide glasses with phosphate, borate,

and silicate oxides contributed to early studies along with effects of different probe ions. The

most commonly studied probe ions are Pb2+, Tl+, and Bi3+ that have absorbance spectra visible

in the UV region resulting from a 6s→6p transition. It should be noted that when using a probe

ion to determine the basicity, the probe ion will seek out specific sites in the glass that may or

may not be representative of the average glass basicity. In other words, the OB is determined

locally and depends on where the probe ion is sited. Basicity moderating parameters and the

average, or “bulk” theoretical OB, can be used to predict the microscopic, or local, OB. The local

OB describes the basicity of an oxygen atom in an oxyanion unit, (assuming there are no other

The Pb2+ probe is popular for investigating OB and has been assessed in various glasses and

frequencies, with a large jump to even lower frequencies when the alkali oxide becomes 20% of

2.4.2 Probe Ion Site Selectivity

Glasses provide sites of different basicity, and the probe ion will seek out “low” and “high”

basicity sites. In other words, they occupy sites with different electron densities. 3 When the

probe ions occupy multiple sites, multiple absorption frequencies are present. Depending on

the glass composition, the probe ions typically prefer one site over the other. 3 The

Page 22 of 99

nephelauxetic effect that is used to determine the site basicity is induced by increased nuclear

shielding and symmetry-restricted covalency. The former describes the presence of electrons

from donor atoms and the latter describes the conversion of atomic orbitals to antibonding

molecular orbitals.3 These underlying effects alter the electron density shape and effective

charge of the probe ion, and depending on the transition, have differing degrees of influence. 3

Nuclear shielding and symmetry-restricted covalency are about equally influential for transition

metal cations with a d→d transition, but for the s→p transition (i.e., for Pb2+, Bi3+, Tl+) the

nephelauxetic effect is predominantly due to nuclear shielding. 3 If the extent of each effect is

accounted for, a range of effective charges of the probe for a given OB can be determined. For

instance, Tl+ spans a wider range of OB (i.e., larger difference in site frequencies) than Pb 2+ that

indicates a more variable effective charge. This also applies to the host cation where a

unipositive ion like Na+ has a wider basicity span than a dipositive ion like Ba 2+. While

experimental OB measures the polarizability locally, it does not account for the overall

structure and changes to electron density due to the metal ions present. 3, 34 Pb2+ and Tl+ fill the

higher basicity Na+ sites when Na+ is displaced, and increasing temperature increases site

basicity which makes it more favorable for Pb2+. When Tl+ is used as the probe ion, analyses are

typically limited to systems with less than 25% Na 2O to prevent formation of Tl3+.

IR and Raman spectroscopy have also been used to correlate local glass structure with probe

site selectivity to further understand local basicity and other properties. Raman spectroscopy

can provide insight into structure in glasses, including the coordination of cations and whether

oxygen atoms are bridging or nonbridging. Knowledge of the structure can be used to

understand how the probe ion is coordinating with the system to explain irregularities between

Page 23 of 99

predicted and experimental OB. For example, Velli et al.10 used Raman spectroscopy of

fluorophosphate glasses to identify various phosphate groups and how the presence of each

change with increasing phosphate-to-fluoride compositions. Glasses with greater fluoride

compositions were identified to have PO32- and PO43- units which provide greater negative

charge density compared to the PO2O2- metaphosphate units (containing a bridging and

nonbridging oxygens) in the phosphate-only glass. The Pb2+ is more likely to associate with the

PO32- and PO43- units than with the fluoride, and therefore the probe signals a local basicity that

is higher than the predicted average. IR has been used to verify site selection by measuring

alkali ion motion frequency to determine optical basicity.35 This method does not require an

invasive probe ion that can select for or create new basicity sites. The frequency associated

with the alkali metal increases in glasses with higher metal oxide content; this reflects a greater

oxide electron density due to greater covalency between itself and the metal ion. The increase

in electron density is synonymous with increased optical basicity.35 Alkali ion motion frequency

verifies the efficacy of Tl+ because it presents two absorption frequencies representative of the

two basicity sites in sodium borate glass. Likewise, the Pb2+ probe suggests an abrupt change in

basicity due to its change in site preference for a particular glass composition. 3 The dual

frequency and basicity distribution can be observed in binary glasses with ionic oxides,

including silicates, phosphates, and germanates. 34 This is because phosphate and silicate glass

structures increase in nonbridging oxygens and therefore OB with greater alkali oxide content. 18

2.4.3 Alternative Probes

Limited studies have been performed using transition metal ions as probes, but being that they

are common contaminants in molten salts, it may be valuable if their spectra can be interpreted

in terms of OB. It has been shown that the spectra of transition metals in aluminum

borophosphate glasses correspond with calculated OB, and doped with Cr3+, present spectra in

UV and visible regions.39 The ligand field strength determines the visible absorption, and the

charge transfer determines the UV absorption. The former increases with basicity in glasses

with few nonbridging oxygens (greater electron donation leads to greater interactions between

the ligand and ion), but this was not witnessed in soda-lime silicate glasses. 39 Duffy argues that

metalyl ions (MoO3+, VO2+) may also be good candidates as probe ions as they have shown

shifts d→d absorption spectra in aqueous systems, and their frequency also decreases with

theoretical OB in sodium borate glasses, as expected. Good agreement was found between

experimental and theoretical optical basicity for Co2+ and Cr6+ as probes in ternary oxide

glasses.29 VO2+ was observed to have d→d spectra in oxides that occur in the visible and near-IR

region.40 The vanadyl ion resembles the Pb2+ probe in terms of predicting OB in sodium

Page 26 of 99

phosphate glasses but experiences a less gradual change in frequency with OB, possibly due to

a change in coordination.40 VO2+ may not be suitable for low basicity media because it has

multiple spectral bands that redshift together and become indistinguishable. The ion may also

shift to a different oxidative state and similarly compromise the spectral bands of VO 2+ (i.e., V3+

in high-phosphate, V4+ in alkali borates, and V5+ in silicate glasses).40, 41

Another issue with the presence of transition metals is the uncertainty of their redox ratio in

the system. Basicity changes affect activity and standard free energies of species in the glass

that make the melt favor the upper oxidative state of the metal. 5 Thermodynamics of redox

reactions are difficult to study in oxide melts due to unclear distinction between what is solvent

and the species of study, unlike in aqueous systems. In theory, if the oxide electron density is

known, the redox ratio can be determined regardless of the composition because it could

provide information about changes in the oxide activity and standard Gibbs free energy, which

are not typically known.5 The electron density is proportional to the OB, so it is reasonable that

OB provides information about the redox ratio. Previously, color was used to assess the basicity

of glasses because they indicate different coordination numbers. For example, tetrahedral Co2+

is blue, and octahedral Co2+ is pink. However, the oxidation state of the metal ion does not

dictate its tendency to complex. The area under the d→d absorption band of the metal is

contributed to by different coordination numbers, and in a study by Paul, these areas were

correlated with the alkali oxide content to understand how the ratio of tetrahedral to

Page 28 of 99

octahedral Co2+ changed.5, 45 For three different compositions of glasses, this ratio was

compared and found to occur around the same OB for each sample, indicating that OB can be

correlated with the ratio of coordination numbers. 5 The logarithm equilibrium ratio can be

plotted against alkali oxide content and then against calculated optical basicity to yield a

straight line, allowing prediction of the equilibrium ratio for a given composition. 5 The accuracy

of these predictions, however, require further validation in more oxyanion systems. The higher

oxidative state is typically favored in more basic melts due to their stabilization from the oxygen

ligands, except for Cu+/Cu2+, and this effect is stronger than increasing oxide content. 9, 11,46 Pb2+

and Tl+ are known to be reliable descriptors of the glass OB, and this has been shown for Mn 2+

and octahedrally-coordinated Fe3+. For Mn2+ and Mn3+, this phenomenon is further verified by

photoluminescence using excitation and emission spectra. These spectra increase with

decreasing OB and verify that Mn2+ is more stable and more abundant in less basic glasses.

Because of the high oxide activity in a basic system, it is initially expected that the lower

oxidative state will generally be preferred (Le Chatelier’s principle), but this is not the case.

Metal ions rely on negatively charged environments to stabilize them and reduce their positive

charge. The higher oxidative state is preferred in more basic glasses because the oxygen ions

are more negatively charged. This stabilizes the higher oxidative state of the metal ion because

it needs more negative charge than the lower state to comply with electroneutrality

principles.47, 48 There are a number of explanations for why Cu + is favored in more basic melts,

and this is expected when the lower state has a much lower coordination number than the

higher state. This limits the electron donation to the metal ion and prevents charge reduction.

As the melt becomes less basic, the Cu+ becomes less stable. Alternatively, if the lower state

Page 29 of 99

tends to form an insoluble oxide at higher basicities, the redox equilibria could shift away from

the higher state.5 Another exception is for Fe3+ in silicate glasses that have low local OB. The

higher oxidative state is preferred in a low basicity site because the ion demands a much higher

electron donation from each oxygen atom. This is because its d-orbital expansion is more

dependent on symmetry restricted covalency than nuclear shielding, and the glass does not

restrict the tetrahedral to hexahedral coordination transition. 11 However, there is a threshold

for trivalent metal ions; if the OB is too low altogether, there will be insufficient charge to

support it. Divalent metals are typically found in glasses with local optical basicities close to the

average OB and near the OB of water (0.40).11 This was similarly observed by Sumita et. al,

where the redox ratio of Fe2+/Fe3+ increased to a maximum at an OB of 0.5 and subsequently

decreased at higher basicities.2

2.4.5 Opaque Applications

Another common issue with using s→p probe ion spectra is their inapplicability in opaque and

UV-opaque systems.2 Transition metals like Mn, Fe, Cu, Ni, and Cr are common contaminants in

glasses, salts, and slags that reduce UV transparency. Electron transfer from the glass to the

metal ion creates a charge transfer absorption band that dominates the UV region. 40 In slags

and other extractive metallurgical applications, transition metal oxides like Fe 2O3 and MnO also

complicates the relationship between sulfide capacity and OB. 49 Spectral d→d bands are not

exclusive to the UV region and are useful indicators of the stereochemistry of available sites. 50

The d→d spectra can inform about the metal ion’s electron density due to the effect of ligands

on the expansion of the atomic orbitals and the nephelauxetic effect. d5 transition metals like

Mn2+ and Fe3+ are simpler transition metal ions to study because only one frequency ( 6A1g

these transitions are electronically spin-forbidden and easily hidden by stronger bands, such as

those induced by charge transfer. Low concentrations of the complexed metal also diminish the

absorption bands. 11 However, if visible, the d→d spectral bands can be studied for frequency

shifts, associated with glass composition, and studied for OB. They are not as well understood

in terms of measuring OB, and their charge transfer absorption bands are studied even less. 11

d→d transitions are also sensitive to changes in coordination structure that also complicate

absorption spectra.11, 21, 41 The charge transfer absorption frequency was originally correlated

2.5 Molten Salt Systems

2.5.1 Fluoride Systems

Fluoride systems are not as well studied in terms of OB, but they are crucial to understand for

developing modern optical glasses and understanding molten salts. They have been proven as

feasible systems of probe-ion study due to the fluoride ion’s detectable interactions with

polarizing cations.27, 28 In past work, the h function (derived from the nephelauxetic parameter)

has been considered fixed for the halide (F- = 0.8, Cl- = 2.0, Br- = 2.3, I- = 2.7). However,

experimental data shows that h can vary substantially depending on the probe ion used (i.e., h=

1.91 and 1.38 for Tl+ and h=2.19 and 1.09 for Bi3+ in LiCl-KCl and F-LiNaK, respectively).28

Likewise, the trend in large electronegativity variability for fluoride systems and h parameter

variability for chlorides indicate that average constants do not accurately represent the

chemistry of halide systems. The fluoride variability in polarizability is nearly as great as that of

oxide ions, which suggests that fluoride may interact similarly with other ions.12 In glasses

Page 33 of 99

where one halide is substituted for another (i.e., chloride for fluoride), the change in

electronegativity may explain the redshift observed. 52 This effect has also been observed in

alkali halide molten salts with mixtures of F- and Cl- anions, where the position of 34trivalent

lanthanide absorption maxima blueshifts as the proportion of F - increases and the proportion of

Cl- decreases.53

Salts composed of complexed halide anions are also shown to alter measurement of h. In a

study conducted by Bruce and Duffy, fluoride ions were shown to covalently interact with

boron that reduced their electron-donor power, and the probe ions were able to detect this. 28

Compared to uncomplexed F-, there is a decrease in h for fluoro-complexes.28 This reflects a

change in basicity due to the fluorine bonding interaction in the complex. Compared to an

oxidic system where the oxide ion complexes with cations of higher charge, F - in molten salt

may complex with metals of lower charge and can potentially complex with Tl +, Pb2+, and Bi3+.

The redshift of the optical spectra is attributed to the addition of uncomplexed F - and loss of

complexed fluorides in the probe coordination sphere when the salt becomes more basic (i.e.,

with the addition of KF).28 Similar to alkali borate glasses, Tl+ tends to select for highly basic F-.28,

 Thermal decomposition can also release more F - and increase the detected h value

unwittingly.28

2.5.4 Chloride Systems

Experimentation with halide molten salts in terms of OB mirrors that of glasses, where a change

in the composition or modifying cation composition is used to induce a change in basicity that

has been previously indicated by changes in transition metal coordination number. Duffy

proposes calculation of OB using the same definition as for oxidic systems, but with eutectic

LiCl-KCl (59:41 mol%) as the reference (i.e., OB = 1) instead of CaO.6 By this definition, a salt of

NaCl-AlCl3 (33:67 mol%) has an OB of 0.87, and again indicates that chloride ion is less

polarizable than the oxide ion.6 It should be noted that one cannot compare OB of different

halide melts because by definition, OB is the proportion of h values for one monatomic anion.6

OB’s relationship with temperature, which seems to mildly increase with OB, should be further

researched in terms of ionicity and covalency between the alkali metal, halide, and probe ions. 6

It is also possible that the probe ion could experience a change in coordination that could affect

OB measurements and would be difficult to detect. 6

2.5.3 Sulfide Systems

For extractive metallurgy applications, sulfide and sulfide-oxide (common in slags) are also

considered on the same OB scale as fluorides and oxides, as indicated by similar anion

Page 36 of 99

electronegativity-OB and anion polarizability-OB relationships. Again, chlorides do not align

closely with these values and must be considered separately. As sulfide replaces oxides, which

occurs at the slag-iron interface, there is an increase in OB according to refractive index and

density data.56

2.5.5 Ionic Liquid Systems

Optical basicity has also been assessed in media that are not glasses and molten salts, such as

oxide ionic crystals and ionic liquids (IL). Ionic crystals do not have an amorphous structure, and

because so much of the work done for OB is in terms of amorphous oxides, much of the OB

calculated from redshifts rely on specific experimental correction factors, can ignore 3p 1 crystal

field splitting effects, and attribute bonding structure and coordination changes to the probe

ion instead of the system.13 The probes typically used in the oxidic systems have been assessed

in low-melting chloride solutions by Angell and Bennett. The chloride ion activity is expected to

change with composition, and experimentation verified the concept of OB in IL’s by association

with thermodynamic basicity (i.e., determining the equivalence point) which is not feasible in

glasses.57 Measurements using different probes need to be reported separately, and in these

systems the efficacy of the probe ion is dependent on the media. The probe must have a

basicity between that of the reacting species, similar to aqueous indicators. 57 Further studies of

ionic liquid systems have been dedicated to creating a Lewis basicity scale according to the

anions of the ionic liquid to determine the IL’s suitability for certain applications, like solvents.

The IL cation has little influence on the basicity, unlike oxidic amorphous systems. 58, 59

2.6 Alternative Measurement Techniques

2.6.1 XPS

Development of another Lewis basicity scale using x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) O1s

spectra is currently being studied.25, 60 XPS can differentiate between oxidation states and

therefore differentiate between bridging and nonbridging oxygens according to their binding

energy differences. In phosphate glasses, higher binding energies can be associated with

bridging oxygens and lower binding energies non-bridging oxygens.61 This can theoretically be

done for different sites and assess the local basicity, similar to the probe ion method. In oxide

glasses, spectra change shape and shift to lower energies with increasing basicities. 61, 62 Energy

gap values in the O1s spectra have also been correlated with experimental basicity probed with

Pb2+ for different systems but these systems could not be unified by one relationship. 62

2.6.2 Fluorescence Spectroscopy and Electron Paramagnetic Resonance

Fluorescence spectroscopy and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) are also valuable

techniques for studying probes like Mn2+ whose optical spectra is obscured by Mn3+. EPR can

determine how Mn2+ is bonded and the site symmetry at low concentrations whereas

fluorescence can differentiate between coordination structures and show evidence of

clustering.37 For example, Mn2+ is tetrahedrally coordinated in high-OB borosilicate glasses, but

when introduced to a lower basicity glass with higher Mn2+ concentrations, it becomes

octahedrally coordinated, forms clusters, and begins to share edges and corners with other

octahedra.37 Glasses with similar concentrations of Mn2+ tend to have larger emission

wavelengths at higher OBs, but the fluorescence intensity has an unclear relationship with OB

Page 38 of 99

due to complications from electron-phonon coupling, concentration quenching, and bonding

environment. Fluorescence lifetimes for borosilicate glasses with high OB are also longer

compared their less basic counterparts.37 The hyperfine splitting and g-shifts while studying EPR

are also dependent on glass basicity and probe ion site selection as they are affected by the

covalency of the Mn-oxide bonds.37 Fluorescence spectroscopy is also being studied as a means

of directly determining optical basicity. Emission and excitation spectra are shown to follow the

same trends in frequency with changes in composition and basicity in alkali oxide phosphate

glasses.35 However, using Pb2+ and Tl+ as probes, the excitation maximum redshifted at slightly

lower frequency, indicating that the same scale for calculating OB with absorption data cannot

be used.35 This could also be because the excitation spectra indicate higher basicity sites. Tl +

signaled more symmetrical emission spectra and more pronounced excitation band maxima

compared to Pb2+. If more research can be performed to establish a reliable link between OB

and fluorescence spectroscopy, OB can possibly be determined in less UV-transparent

systems.35

3. Summary and Outlook
Optical basicity provides a way to measure Lewis basicity by observation of the nephelauxetic

effect. OB is especially convenient for non-aqueous media, such as glasses, molten salts, and

slags. Much research has been invested in creating a system that accurately predicts OB, and

the review highlights differences between predicted and measured OB. OB and its relationship

with electronegativity and polarizability is discussed, and the differences between OB and the

Lux-Flood theories are also explored. Initial research established OB experimentally by doping

Page 39 of 99

oxide glasses with probe ions (Tl+, Pb2+, Bi3+), and complications such as probe ion selectivity,

transition metal impurities, and reduced UV transparency are examined. More recent

experimental methods like refractivity, XPS, fluorescence, EPR, IR and Raman spectroscopy

intend to address how structure and coordination relate to the basicity. The relationship

between OB, redox ratio, and standard reduction potentials is also briefly reviewed. Although

more limited, the current applications of optical basicity by probe ion doping are analyzed for

halide, oxide-halide, sulfide, and ionic liquid systems.

OB has developed into a mature analytical technique for many oxidic applications, but more

attention should be devoted to its application to molten salts and to creating a relevant OB

scale that considers water, oxygen, and transition metal impurities. This is motivated by the

difficulties encountered in monitoring basicity related phenomena, like dissolution, solubility,

and corrosion, in molten salts. Efforts should first focus on expanding the database of single

anion OB measurements for all halide salts. Subsequently, an OB scale for halide salts

containing water, hydroxide, and oxide impurities can be developed, considering the sensitivity

 Experimental Set-up
The setup to conduct optical basicity was commissioned and tested. Optical absorbance spectra

is collected using the in-situ UV-Vis-NIR capability for molten salts has been set up. This setup

allows 5 different samples to be studies in each cycle and is shown in Figure 1. Optical absorption

is measured using the fiber optic cables that are fed through beam ports. The results from this

system are provided below.

Figure 1: In situ UV-Vis spectroscopy setup for molten salts with cuvette samples (left), furnace cross-section (center), and inner
furnace structure (right)

Current sample preparation is as follows:

 • Create a blank salt (no probe-ion or impurities) by massing salt components. Combine

 and melt in alumina crucible, then cool and grind into powder to homogenize.

 • Create a concentrate powder (1-10 mM) of the probe (BiCl3, PbCl2) using another blank

 salt mixture. Combine and melt all components in alumina crucible, then cool and grind

 into powder to homogenize.

 • Mass concentrate powder into an empty quartz cuvette and add blank, homogenized

 powder to dilute sample to detectable concentration.

 i. Electrochemical additions of Fe and Ni to LiCl-KCl
Attempts to add Fe and Ni ions to LiCl-KCl were done electrochemically to add them in very low

quantities, comparable to that of a CSP, and possibly to avoid saturation in the UV region and

maintain detection of the probe transition. Using a mild steel and pure nickel working electrodes

and platinum counter electrode, cyclic voltammetry and galvanostatic holds (1 mA/cm 2 and 0.1

mA/cm2) were performed. The baseline of the Pb2+ transition became more unclear with time,

but the transition was still visible. The lack of clarity in the baseline made probe transition

frequency ambiguous. No Ni (II) features were observed in the vis-NIR region, which makes it

unclear as to whether Ni (II) successfully dissolved into the molten LiCl-KCl. Fe (II) does not

usually have any features in the vis-NIR.

ICP analysis was conducted and verified that Fe and Ni were successfully added

electrochemically. A blank (LiCl-KCl + 3% HNO3) and 3 standards (LiCl-KCl + 3% HNO3 + 0.5, 1,

and 10 ppm of ICP Fe and Ni standard) was prepared for each sample (Fe and Ni). A set for each

sample was needed to match the concentration of LiCl-KCl in their respective samples. The

blanks and the standards were prepared independently of another. The LiCl-KCl likely contained

some Ni and Fe impurities to begin with, so the blanks have a measurable quantity prior to

adding any via the multistandard. Because the blank and standards were prepared individually,

the concentration of Ni and Fe in the blank cannot necessarily be subtracted out from that in the

standard concentration. This explains the senselessness of the Fe intensity in the blank.

However, there is great agreement between the standards as they were prepared with the same

LiCl-KCl solution. Excluding the Fe blank and using the standards only for calibration, the

additional concentration of Fe in the sample is about 24 ppm. The additional concentration of Ni

Page 54 of 99

is about 6 ppm. The Fe and Ni net intensities of the samples are notably greater than those for

their respective blanks, suggesting that the electrochemical experiment successfully dissolved Fe

and Ni. Ideally, the ICP experiment would be repeated with blanks and standards batched from

the same LiCl-KCl solution to control for the existing Ni and Fe impurities. Fe concentration was

also higher than expected, so more concentrated standards between 20 and 30 ppm should also

be prepared.

 Preparation of acidic salts (AlCl3– NaCl and SnCl2 – NaCl) for UV-Vis spectroscopy

AlCl3 undergoes sublimation, making it difficult to retain AlCl 3-NaCl in the desired ratio of 67 to

33 mol% during heating. Several tactics to seal the silica cuvette and trap the AlCl 3 vapor were

attempted (foil and PTFE tape; PTFE lid with ceramic sealant; and PTFE stopper-top and screw-

intermediate (NaAlCl4) that remains in the liquid phase at the temperatures tested (200 and 250

°C). The AlCl3-NaCl is also sensitive to organic contamination which contributes a dark coloring

to the liquid salt. AlCl3-NaCl in silica cuvettes was heated between 3-9 °C/hour to limit

sublimation. The silica cuvettes were also heated in secondary containers (alumina crucibles)

sealed with aluminum foil to limit AlCl 3 deposition on glovebox surfaces.

 Design of spectroscopy furnace for higher temperature experiments (exceeding 600 °C)
 using silica cuvettes
The spectroscopy furnace discussed in the experimental set-up section is limited to 600 °C

because the optical fibers reside in the furnace and can be damaged. By moving the fibers

outside the furnace, the furnace can operate at higher temperatures (up to nearly 1000 °C). This

makes it possible to work with off-eutectic salts and carbonate salts at higher temperatures. A

muffle furnace was modified to allow for optical fiber and sample entries. A special cuvette

sample holder was designed using a tee-fitting, and this is aligned with the optical fibers. The

optical fibers are thermally decoupled, attached, and aligned using alumina rods and Swagelok

(compression) fittings. A custom 3D print was designed to expand the diameter of the optical

fiber to make alignment possible. An alternative cuvette holder made of graphite was also

designed to simplify setup.

 Alternative Design of spectroscopy furnace without silica cuvette for melting carbonate salts

The carbonate salts were observed to react with the silica cuvettes, so alternative salt holders

were needed. Sapphire was selected as an alternative, due to its higher UV transparency and

chemical resistance. However, sapphire is typically sold as a lens or sheet, not cuvettes, so

alternative sample holders needed to be designed to contain the molten carbonate. Two designs

were attempted; the first being a sapphire window with a copper gasket secured on top to

create a well for the molten salt; the second being a commercial aluminum lens tube with a

secured sapphire lens. The second design allowed for a greater volume of salt to be tested, but

the aluminum is undesirable due to its low operating temperatures. To accommodate the new

sample holder designs, the furnace was oriented vertically using a Swagelok alignment tool

similar to the used previously.

Technical Conclusions:
In conclusion, while the concept of optical basicity was proven as hypothesized in the original

proposal, the technical challenges to monitor it online under real-world concentrations of species

were unsurmountable. This project explored several pathways, many of them innovative and

custom solutions requiring significant amount of time, to achieve the goals. This project studied

nitrate, carbonate, and chloride salts. However, it was concluded that it is not possible to monitor

optical basicity using present day systems. An overall schematic of the workflow shown in next

page provides an overview of the studies undertaken and pathways explored. The approaches

are also indicated as whether they were successful or not and where the studies needed to pivot.

We hope this report serves as a starting point for future optical basicity studies.

veteran (Dr. Jeremy Moon) was trained and well involved in the project. We trained one woman

student, Laurel Sharpless, who obtained her Masters degree based on the work conducted in

this project. We also had one undergraduate Hispanic student get trained on molten salts and

conduct work.

graduate degree. Of these three students, one have already joined the accelerated Masters

program and another has applied for the Masters degree program at UNR.

Project Budget:
Project was completed at the proposed and authorized budget. The project did not have any

cost overruns.

Conclusions
Project confirmed the concept and hypothesis regarding optical basicity to be correct. The

studies indicated that it is not feasible to use optical basicity for measuring corrosivity of

molten salts in its current form of using optical spectroscopy. The project also led to training of

a veteran, two women, 2 Hispanics, and a total of six students. The project also resulted in two

publications, one thesis, and two conference presentations.
