A nonlinear windkessel model for cardiovascular dynamics: Variable compliance and pathological simulations
Authors: Marcus Varanis, Mateus Ferraz, Daniel Longo, José Manoel Balthazar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLumped-parameter cardiovascular models are computationally efficient and physiologically interpretable, but many still represent arterial compliance as linear and pressure-independent. Here, we introduce a nonlinear extension of a classical closed-loop Windkessel model by defining aortic compliance as an exponential function of aortic pressure. The heart, systemic circulation, and pulmonary circulation are represented as an RLC network with time-varying ventricular elastances, yielding a twelve-state formulation derived from Kirchhoff’s laws. Under nominal conditions, the nonlinear model recovers the linear baseline, with near-superimposable pressure and flow waveforms and physiologically plausible steady-state indices. Away from the nominal operating point, the pressure-dependent law selectively modulates proximal pulsatility, while mean arterial pressure, stroke volume, and cardiac output remain comparatively preserved. Targeted parameter changes produce qualitative waveform and index-level signatures consistent with aortic regurgitation and arterial stiffening. The proposed formulation provides a compact, physiologically grounded platform for sensitivity analysis, pathological emulation, and early-stage evaluation of cardiovascular modeling scenarios.Graphical abstract

Introduction

The cardiovascular system operates across multiple spatial and temporal scales. At the organ level, the heart drives blood through a branching vascular network; at smaller scales, inertial, viscous, and elastic effects shape the propagation and damping of pulsatile waves. Mathematical modeling provides a mechanistic framework for interpreting these interactions and for exploring scenarios that are impractical or unethical to study in vivo [1, 2].

Zero-dimensional, or lumped-parameter, formulations represent the heart and vasculature as interconnected compartments linked by resistive, capacitive, and inductive elements. These models are computationally inexpensive and provide access to global hemodynamic indices, including pressure-volume loops and cardiac output. They have therefore been used for sensitivity analysis, inverse parameter estimation, and early-stage evaluation of cardiovascular devices [3, 4]. One-dimensional and two-dimensional models enrich this description by resolving wave propagation and reflections along compliant tubes and networks [5–7]. Fully three-dimensional models capture local flow patterns and wall deformation but require detailed anatomical data and substantial computational resources [8]. The choice of model dimensionality, therefore, depends on the balance between physiological detail and computational feasibility required by each application.

Within the 0D family, the Windkessel concept has become a cornerstone. Originally proposed by Frank and later extended by Westerhof and co-workers, the Windkessel analog formalizes the correspondence between vascular hemodynamics and electrical RLC networks [9, 10]. Classical three-element and four-element Windkessel models assume constant arterial compliance and have been extended with inertial and resistive elements, time-varying ventricular elastance, and valve dynamics [11–13]. Closed-loop formulations couple the left and right heart chambers to the systemic and pulmonary vascular beds, enabling clinically interpretable waveforms and supporting simulations of valvular dysfunction and atrioventricular interaction [14, 15].

A persistent limitation of many lumped-parameter models is the representation of large-artery compliance as a linear, pressure-independent parameter. Experimental studies show that arterial walls exhibit nonlinear stress-strain behavior and that effective stiffness increases with pressure and age. Constant linear compliance can therefore underrepresent pressure-dependent changes in systolic loading and pulse pressure under hypertensive, aging, or disease-related conditions. Several authors have proposed nonlinear or viscoelastic extensions of the Windkessel model, including pressure-dependent compliance elements and fractional-order representations [16–22]. Recent studies have revisited these ideas within modern closed-loop frameworks and multiscale couplings [3, 4, 23, 24]. Capturing the essential nonlinearity of arterial compliance within a parsimonious and interpretable model remains a central challenge for reduced-order cardiovascular modeling.

We introduce a nonlinear extension of a classical closed-loop Windkessel model by expressing aortic compliance as an exponential function of aortic pressure. The circulatory system is formulated as a twelve-state network of resistors, inertances, and compliant elements, with time-varying elastance describing ventricular contractility. Embedding the pressure-dependent compliance in the aortic branch preserves the linear baseline near nominal pressures while allowing effective stiffness to vary dynamically with the instantaneous aortic state. The formulation remains computationally lightweight and provides a controlled way to study stiffness-mediated changes in proximal pulsatility and pressure-volume dynamics. In benchmark comparisons, the nonlinear law selectively modulates pulse pressure while mean arterial pressure, stroke volume, and cardiac output remain comparatively preserved. Targeted parameter changes further reproduce qualitative waveform signatures consistent with aortic stiffening and aortic regurgitation.

Section 2 presents the physics-based closed-loop formulation, including ventricular dynamics, valve mechanisms, constitutive laws, numerical integration, and the baseline parameter set. Section 3 compares the nonlinear formulation with the linear baseline under nominal, perturbed, and pathological scenarios. Section 4 discusses the physiological interpretation, modeling implications, and limitations of the results, and Section 5 summarizes the main findings and outlines future extensions toward subject-specific calibration.

Physics-based model for the cardiovascular system

We formulate the cardiovascular system as a closed-loop, Windkessel-type electrical analog. In this framework, the dominant mechanisms of macrocirculatory hemodynamics are represented by an RLC network: resistive elements account for viscous pressure losses, capacitive elements describe compliant volume storage in distensible compartments, and inductive elements represent blood inertance. Consistent with the classical Windkessel analogy [9], each main physiological component is assigned an electrical counterpart. Ventricular pumping is represented by time-varying elastance branches coupled to myocardial viscous resistances, yielding pressure-generating units that drive the circulation. Valves are modeled as ideal diodes in series with resistances, enforcing unidirectional flow while retaining transvalvular dissipation. The atria act as compliant, low-pressure reservoirs. The systemic and pulmonary vascular beds are each described by two-stage RLC networks, where R, L, and C represent viscous dissipation, blood inertance, and elastic storage in vessel walls, respectively. Within this modeling class, arterial compliance is a primary determinant of waveform morphology and global hemodynamic buffering, as supported by extensive characterizations of arterial pressure-volume behavior [25–28].

The central modeling step is the replacement of constant aortic compliance by a nonlinear pressure-dependent element. This formulation relaxes a standard assumption of classical lumped-parameter cardiovascular models by allowing compliance to vary with the instantaneous aortic pressure. Arterial stiffening is therefore introduced in a low-order model through pressure-dependent changes in effective compliance, which primarily modulate proximal pulsatility and flow buffering within the closed-loop system. In Fig. 2, this nonlinearity is localized in the aortic compliance branch, enabling direct comparison between the linear and nonlinear formulations under otherwise identical architecture and parameterization.

The complete model is written as a system of ordinary differential equations obtained from Kirchhoff’s laws applied to the circuit topology. RLC circuit analysis

The governing equations are obtained by combining the constitutive relations of the circuit elements with Kirchhoff’s laws. Pressures and flow rates are mapped to voltages and currents, respectively, so the standard RLC relations provide a compact route to the state-space formulation.

For a capacitive element,2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} q(t) = C\,v(t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and, since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$q(t)=\int i(t)\,dt$$\end{document}, differentiation gives3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} i(t) = C\,\frac{dv(t)}{dt}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}In hemodynamic terms, this branch represents compliant volume storage, relating pressure variations to flow exchange in distensible compartments.

For an inductive element,4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} v(t) = L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where L denotes blood inertance, namely the pressure contribution required to accelerate or decelerate the moving fluid mass.

The global closed-loop dynamics is obtained by applying Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL) at the circuit nodes and Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL) along the independent loops. Eqs. (1) through (4) define the local constitutive behavior of the network, whereas KCL and KVL impose flow conservation and pressure balance. Their combination yields the coupled ODE system governing the time evolution of pressures, flow rates, and chamber volumes.

Simulation pipeline used for the numerical experiments reported in this work. The setup stage defines the initial state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{x}_{0}$$\end{document} and parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{p}$$\end{document}, which feed the initial-value formulation in Eq. (11). The right-hand side includes the time-varying elastances, ideal-diode valve switching, and pressure-dependent aortic compliance described in Section 2.2. The gold-highlighted integration stage solves the ODE system with scipy.integrate.solve_ivp, using RK45 as the default solver and Radau IIA for stiffness-sensitive cross-validation. The post-processing stage computes the hemodynamic indices, detects the dicrotic notch, and evaluates the central-to-distal pulse-pressure ratio

An adaptive explicit Runge-Kutta method of order p approximates the solution between successive instants \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_n$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{n+1}=t_n+h_n$$\end{document} by combining intermediate evaluations of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{f}$$\end{document} within each step. The corresponding local truncation error scales as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(h_n^{p+1})$$\end{document} [31]. The error is estimated from an embedded lower-order approximation computed from the same function evaluations, and the step size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_n$$\end{document} is adjusted to keep the estimate below the prescribed tolerance. The integrator uses larger steps in smooth segments of the trajectory and refines automatically near valve transitions, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textbf{f}$$\end{document} changes abruptly.

Two complementary solvers are used. The default RK45 method is the explicit Runge-Kutta-Dormand-Prince scheme of order 4(5) [32, 33], implemented through scipy.integrate.solve_ivp [34]. This method is well suited to the smooth portions of the dynamics away from valve transitions. For the stiffness-dominated behavior observed at the largest values of b in the sensitivity sweep (Section 3.2), the simulations are cross-checked with the implicit, L-stable Radau IIA method of order 5 [35], which is appropriate for systems with separated timescales. The two solvers produce indistinguishable hemodynamic indices within the reported precision in all scenarios considered, supporting the interpretation that the results reflect the model dynamics rather than artifacts of the integration scheme. Tolerances are set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{rtol} = 10^{-6}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{atol} = 10^{-9}$$\end{document} throughout, which is conservative for the magnitudes of the state variables (Table 2).

The trajectory is sampled at 30 000 uniformly spaced points over a 30-second window, corresponding to approximately 37.5 cardiac cycles at the nominal period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_c = 0.8$$\end{document} s and about 800 samples per cycle. Steady-state averages of SBP, DBP, MAP, PP, SV, and CO are computed over the last ten cycles to reduce the influence of the initial transient. The dicrotic notch is detected automatically in each steady-state cycle by locating the first local minimum of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{ao}}(t)$$\end{document} between the systolic peak and the subsequent diastolic secondary peak. This detection uses the find_peaks routine of SciPy [34], with minimum-distance and prominence constraints set according to the cardiac timescale. The central-to-distal pulse-pressure ratio in Section 3.2 is computed cycle by cycle from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{ao}}(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_{\textrm{sys}}(t)$$\end{document} and reported as the mean over the steady-state window.

The complete pipeline is implemented in Python 3 using NumPy [36], SciPy [34], and Matplotlib [37], following established practices for scientific computing and dynamical-systems simulation in Python [38, 39]. The ODE system is integrated through scipy.integrate.solve_ivp [34].

Mechanistically, the notch is associated with aortic valve closure. When ventricular ejection terminates, the abrupt change in outflow produces a localized decrease in proximal aortic pressure, followed by partial recovery through elastic recoil and inertial redistribution within the lumped network. The reproduction of this feature in a single-node 0D representation is consistent with prior observations that valve switching combined with lumped inertance can reproduce both the notch and the secondary pressure rise without explicit 1D wave propagation [27]. The notch should therefore not be interpreted, by itself, as evidence of spatially resolved wave dynamics. Instead, its persistence under the nonlinear compliance law indicates that the proposed pressure-dependent element preserves a temporal waveform feature commonly used in clinical aortic-pressure assessment.

Discussion

The Windkessel paradigm provides a mature lumped-parameter framework for macrocirculatory hemodynamics, with a transparent mapping between electrical-analog elements and physiological quantities and a modular architecture in which local changes preserve global consistency. The present model occupies a deliberate position within this landscape: a closed-loop, twelve-state, zero-dimensional formulation embedding a single mechanistically interpretable nonlinearity, the pressure-dependent aortic compliance, within an otherwise classical structure. Spatially distributed phenomena such as pulse-wave reflection and segment-specific elasticity require one- or three-dimensional treatments [9, 44, 45] and lie outside the present scope by construction; in return, the formulation retains the interpretability and computational modesty that make reduced-order models attractive for sensitivity analysis, hypothesis testing, and device-oriented exploration.

A central contribution of the formulation is the pressure-dependent aortic compliance as an explicit nonlinear element within an otherwise classical closed-loop Windkessel, preserving backward compatibility with the linear baseline (Figs. 6 and 7, Table 3) and agreeing with established reference models [14, 16]. Within the exponential law, b acts as a compact surrogate for arterial stiffness, aligning with the literature on arterial stiffening, vascular aging, hypertension, and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [29, 46–50]. The compliant regime narrows pulse pressure; the stiffer regime widens it; in both, MAP, SV, and CO remain essentially unchanged, with proximal pulse pressure spanning approximately 36 to 49 mmHg across the sweep while SV and CO stay within narrow ranges (Table 3). The nonlinear capacitor therefore acts as a controlled gateway: quiet at nominal physiology, where it reproduces the linear baseline, and progressively expressive away from it, with broader phase portraits and reflection-like structures in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(x_1, x_2)$$\end{document} plane (Fig. 8a, b). In this setting, nonlinear effects appear only as the vascular response moves away from the nominal operating range, so the added compliance law gives those changes a direct mechanical interpretation.

Beyond global indices, the closed-loop coupling reproduces two waveform features routinely used in clinical assessment. The reconstructed pressure-volume loops (Fig. 9) exhibit the four canonical phases, with end-systolic and end-diastolic volumes, peak pressures, and stroke volumes within the ranges reported by established closed-loop lumped models [14, 16] and within physiological norms for the resting healthy adult. These loops are not imposed but emerge from the interaction of time-varying elastance, valve dynamics, and the upstream and downstream lumped compartments, providing a non-trivial validation of the cardiac module independent of pressure-waveform comparisons. The dicrotic notch (Fig. 10) is reproduced with a lag of approximately 0.07 s after the systolic peak, consistent with reported physiological values [41], together with the secondary diastolic rise. That a single-node zero-dimensional representation can generate this feature, traditionally invoked as a signature of distributed wave dynamics, accords with prior observations that valve switching and proximal pressure-flow inertance suffice for both even without explicit 1D propagation [27]. Both features survive the nonlinear compliance modification, confirming that the proposed extension does not degrade waveform signatures clinicians routinely inspect.

The closed-loop coupling was probed under four single-parameter challenges (Fig. 11): an afterload increase, a faster heart rate, an increase in left-ventricular contractility, and a reduction of venous return through elevated systemic-venous compliance. The first three reproduce the textbook lumped-parameter responses [14]: hypertensive shift under afterload; elevated mean pressure with reduced per-beat SV offset by higher beat frequency under tachycardia; widened LV pressure-volume loop with elevated SV and SBP under increased contractility. The preload-reduction challenge produced only modest changes in SBP, MAP, SV, and CO. In the closed-loop model used here, where explicit autonomic regulation is not included, the shift of blood volume toward the more compliant venous reservoir is largely accommodated by the periodic dynamics of the system. The combined action of the time-varying ventricular elastance and the systemic inertance limits the response, so the mean operating point does not undergo a sustained displacement. Reproducing the SV and MAP reductions observed clinically during severe orthostatic stress would require explicit volume removal from the systemic compartment or a baroreflex-mediated heart-rate response, neither of which is included here. The same proximal–distal pattern observed in the sensitivity analysis is also present in the challenge scenarios (Fig. 12). In all perturbations, the pressure shift is more pronounced at the proximal site, while the distal response remains attenuated after passing through the lumped systemic network. This behavior supports the interpretation that the nonlinear compliance mainly modulates proximal pulsatility, with part of this effect being filtered before it reaches the peripheral compartment. The pathological emulations of aortic regurgitation and arteriosclerosis (Fig. 14, Table 3) reinforce this picture from a complementary angle: both phenotypes are obtained through localized parameter changes without altering the closed-loop architecture; the arteriosclerosis case in particular highlights distortions inaccessible in a constant-compliance formulation.

Across the sensitivity sweep (Fig. 8a, b), the distal-to-proximal pulse-pressure ratio remains close to 0.63 for b ∈ [0.010, 0.040] mmHg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}, indicating attenuation of pulsatility from proximal to distal compartment. This contrasts with the central-to-peripheral pulse-pressure amplification reported in vivo [9, 41, 44, 45], which arises from wave reflection along a spatially distributed arterial tree and cannot be reproduced by any single-segment 0D representation. The model is therefore complementary to higher-dimensional formulations: it captures the aortic-wall constitutive nonlinearity as a proximal pulsatility regulator without claiming distributed-wave fidelity. A natural extension is a 0D-1D coupling in which the present nonlinear element replaces the proximal lumped capacitance of a 1D arterial tree, preserving the mechanistic interpretability of the constitutive law. The pulmonary bed was also examined because it operates over a much narrower pressure interval, approximately 15 to 28 mmHg. Along this orbit, the factor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e^{-b_{\textrm{pul}}\Delta P}$$\end{document} changes only slightly, so the induced pulse-pressure modulation remains much smaller than in the systemic circulation, roughly one order of magnitude lower. The systemic response is practically unaffected, with no detectable spillover in Fig. 13. Restricting the nonlinearity to the systemic circulation is therefore a deliberate choice justified by the operating-pressure window of each bed.

The numerical analysis adds a final methodological observation. Both an adaptive explicit Runge-Kutta solver and a stiffly stable implicit method converge to essentially identical steady-state indices across the regimes explored (Table 3); solver choice therefore does not alter the reported physiology, but it reveals the computational character of the regime. As b increases and effective compliance collapses, the model develops sharper transients and stronger time-scale separation, manifesting as numerical stiffness. Fixed-step explicit schemes may become fragile or inefficient in this regime, whereas stiffly stable implicit methods remain robust. This stiffness signature works as an operational marker that the system has entered a regime in which pressure-dependent compliance materially reshapes pulsatility while mean loading and pump output remain preserved, and motivates solver-aware pipelines for sensitivity analysis: pathological sweeps should be performed with implicit integrators to avoid conflating numerical artifacts with physiological effects.

Beyond these methodological notes, the model sits within a broader landscape spanning spatial dimensionality and the form of nonlinearity admitted into the arterial-wall constitutive relations. Its spatial complement is the family of one- and three-dimensional arterial-tree models in which pulse-wave propagation, reflection, and segment-specific elasticity can be resolved [9, 43–45], a complementarity articulated in recent reviews of multiscale and reduced-order strategies [51–53]. Fractional-order Windkessel models have also been proposed for aortic impedance and hypertensive hemodynamics [12, 13]; the present integer-order, mechanistically interpretable formulation is a complementary alternative. Two parallel lines generalize the constitutive elements of the Windkessel. The first, to which this work belongs, generalizes the compliance by allowing the elastic-storage term to depend on instantaneous luminal pressure, a line originating with Cappello et al. [21] and developed by Burattini and Natalucci [54]. The second generalizes the resistance by allowing peripheral resistance to depend on flow, pressure, or downstream metabolic demand, capturing autoregulation phenomena [55–57]. Both modify the diastolic decay constant τ = RC through mechanistically distinct substrates: peripheral vascular tone at the microcirculatory scale, versus aortic-wall constitutive nonlinearity at the macroscopic vessel-wall scale. A natural future direction is a joint Windkessel containing both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_n(P)$$\end{document} and R(P, Q), which would in principle allow their respective contributions to the diastolic decay to be disentangled.

The 0D lumped-parameter formulation, while well suited to the analysis of global hemodynamic indices, carries several structural limitations that identify priority directions for future extensions.

Windkessel-class models cannot reproduce the spatially distributed phenomena of pulse-wave propagation, including the in vivo pulse-pressure amplification between central and peripheral sites in healthy young adults. Amplification arises from wave reflection and from the distributed mechanical properties of the arterial tree, neither of which can be represented in a lumped capacitive-resistive-inertial network, which means this limitation is structural rather than numerical. In the sensitivity analysis discussed above, the distal-to-proximal pulse-pressure ratio remained close to 0.63 across b ∈ [0.010, 0.040] mmHg\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-1}$$\end{document}. A hybrid 0D-1D coupling, in which the present nonlinear element replaces the proximal lumped capacitance of a 1D arterial tree, is the natural way to address this directly while preserving the mechanistic interpretability of the constitutive law.

The model treats peripheral resistance as fixed, neglecting neurogenic, myogenic, and metabolic feedback. A joint Windkessel with both nonlinear compliance and dynamically regulated peripheral resistance is the natural follow-up. The disparate time scales of the two nonlinearities (milliseconds-scale constitutive response versus seconds-to-minutes autoregulatory adjustment) mean they act on distinct portions of the hemodynamic response and may, in principle, be separable in clinical data of appropriate temporal resolution. The muted preload-reduction response noted earlier reflects precisely this absence of autonomic feedback.

The model has been validated against canonical hemodynamic indices of the resting healthy adult and against reference closed-loop lumped models [14, 16], but has not been calibrated against individual-subject in vivo recordings. Future work directly calibrating A and b of Eq. (10) against catheter or non-invasive aortic-pressure recordings, possibly together with arterial-stiffness indices such as pulse-wave velocity or augmentation index, would extend the framework’s practical utility. Such subject-specific calibration is the natural bridge between the present mechanistic framework and the data-driven and machine-learning surrogates increasingly prevalent in cardiovascular modeling [58, 59].

The cardiac period \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_c$$\end{document} is held constant, neglecting heart-rate variability and baroreflex coupling; the ventricular elastance functions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_L(t)$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_R(t)$$\end{document} follow a fixed deterministic profile; and the coronary and cerebral circulations are not represented. These extensions lie outside the present scope. A deeper dynamical-systems characterization of the nonlinear regimes identified here, including bifurcation structure, attractor robustness, and stability descriptors, would be a complementary refinement.

Taken together, these analyses support a focused conclusion: introducing pressure-dependent aortic compliance as an explicit nonlinear element in a classical closed-loop Windkessel model increases physiological expressiveness without sacrificing interpretability or computational accessibility. Near the nominal operating point, the nonlinear formulation reproduces the linear baseline. Away from that point, it selectively modulates proximal pulsatility while largely preserving mean loading, stroke volume, and cardiac output. The same mechanism remains compatible with waveform-level markers, including the dicrotic notch and ventricular pressure-volume loops, and responds to physiological and pathological perturbations in patterns consistent with expected hemodynamic behavior. The main structural limitation is intrinsic to the 0D representation: peripheral pulse-pressure amplification cannot be resolved without spatial propagation. This limitation defines constructive extensions toward hybrid 0D-1D formulations, Windkessel architectures combining nonlinear compliance with nonlinear peripheral resistance, and subject-specific calibration against in vivo recordings.

Conclusions

This study introduced a closed-loop, twelve-state, zero-dimensional cardiovascular model in which pressure-dependent aortic compliance, defined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_n(P) = A\,e^{-bP}$$\end{document}, is embedded as a single explicit nonlinear element within a classical Windkessel architecture. The formulation preserves the interpretability and computational efficiency of lumped-parameter modeling while extending the framework to stiffness-mediated hemodynamics. Under nominal conditions, the nonlinear model recovers the linear baseline in waveform morphology, phase-space structure, and steady-state indices. Away from that operating point, the compliance law selectively modulates proximal pulsatility while MAP, stroke volume, and cardiac output remain comparatively preserved.

The numerical results support the physiological consistency of the framework at waveform, index, and scenario levels. Increasing aortic stiffness widens proximal pulse pressure and reshapes the pressure-flow limit cycle. The model also preserves ventricular pressure-volume loops, the dicrotic notch, and the diastolic secondary pressure rise. The physiological challenges produce responses in the expected direction, whereas targeted parameter changes generate qualitative signatures consistent with aortic regurgitation and arterial stiffening. At the same time, the central-to-distal analysis confirms a structural limitation of the 0D formulation: spatial pulse-wave propagation and peripheral pulse-pressure amplification require a distributed arterial model.

These findings motivate three extensions: coupling the nonlinear compliance element to a 1D arterial tree, combining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_n(P)$$\end{document} with regulated peripheral resistance R(P, Q), and calibrating A and b against subject-specific pressure recordings and arterial-stiffness indices. In this form, the proposed framework provides a compact, physiologically grounded basis for sensitivity analysis, pathological emulation, and future integration with data-driven cardiovascular modeling.
