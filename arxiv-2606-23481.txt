Continuity equations
in the Generalised Lagrangian Mean theory

Abstract




Abstract

Generalised Lagrangian Mean (GLM) theory aims to describe the joint evolution of the mean flow and its perturbations based on the exact system of governing equations.
This paper analyses the formulations of exact GLM continuity equations (GLM-CEs) and clarifies the conditions of their applicability.
We restrict ourselves only to general statements and do not consider the equations of motion.
The tools used are Lagrangian
\bm{X}
, Eulerian
\bm{x}
, averaged Eulerian
\bar{\bm{x}}
coordinates of fluid particles, and ensemble-based averaging over the parameter
\alpha
.
The targeted forms of CEs operate with functions
\bm{x}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)
and
\rho(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)
where
\rho
is density.
We present the actual velocity divergence
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}
via
\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}
, where
\bm{u}
and
\bar{\bm{u}}
are the actual and average fluid velocities.
Then we derive three versions of exact GLM-CEs and divide each into average and
\alpha
-dependent (tilde) parts.
The latter can be seen as compatibility equations that restrict the class of admissible fluid motions to be considered in the averaged equations.
The main result of the paper is that each original CE is explicitly presented as a self-consistent system of GLM-CEs consisting of average and tilde equations.
For comparison, we consider two versions of the McIntyre-Andrews Transformation (MAT) for CEs.
The original MAT introduces an auxiliary function that satisfies a PDE with special initial conditions.
It also requires introducing specific compatibility equations.
Hence, the original MAT is applicable to a special class of fluid flows.
The generalised MAT yields results similar to those described above, but uses a more complex derivation.
Finally, we consider examples of average flows with small perturbations, thus linking our exposition to the classical GLM theory.

Keywords:
General Lagrangian Mean theory, average coordinates, Eulerian description, continuity equations, compatibility equations, McIntyre-Andrews transformation.

1.
Introduction

The Generalised Lagrangian Mean (GLM) theory (which, for the purposes of this paper, coincides with the Hybrid Euler–Lagrange theory) was developed in [1 - 5, 8, 9, 12 - 14] and in several other papers.
These books and papers represent a major attempt in fluid dynamics to derive and use a system of
exact governing equations
that describe the joint evolution of the unsteady mean flow and its perturbations.
There are several achievements and many problems to study.
Among them is the completeness of GLM forms of continuity equations, abbreviated as GLM-CEs.
The original forms of continuity equations (CEs) are as follows

\displaystyle\textbf{(a)}\ \Div\bm{u}=0;\quad\textbf{(b)}\ \Div\bm{u}=0\ \text{and}\ D\rho=0;\quad\textbf{(c)}\ \Div\bm{u}+D\ln\rho=0,

(1.1)

where
\bm{u}=\bm{u}(\bm{x},t)
and
\rho=\rho(\bm{x},t)
are the velocity and density fields,
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
and
t
are the Cartesian coordinates and time, and
D
is the material derivative.
Eqn.(
1.1
)
(a)
is valid for an incompressible fluid with
\rho=\const
, (
1.1
)
(b)
for an incompressible fluid with
\rho\neq\const
, and (
1.1
)
(c)
for a compressible fluid, which contains
(a)
and
(b)
as special cases.
All Eqs.(
1.1
) are written in forms that separate the velocity divergence.
Any CE does not constitute an independent equation; it forms a system with the equation of motion.
Therefore, we deal only with exact formulae and the most general statements for CEs.
This paper clarifies the transformations of CEs (
1.1
) into GLM-CEs and analyses their formulations.
The distribution of material is as follows:

Section 2 introduces the basic notation.
There are three types of coordinates: Lagrangian
\bm{X}
, Eulerian
\bm{x}
, and average Eulerian
\bar{\bm{x}}
.
The keystone of the theory is to move the description of fluid motion from the original independent variables
\bm{x}
and
\bm{X}
to the average coordinate
\bar{\bm{x}}
.
We consider ensembles of flows with particular realisations labelled by a scalar parameter
\alpha
, and employ the ensemble average operation denoted as
\langle\cdot\rangle
.
The main functions in the GLM description are the following

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{x}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha),\quad\bm{u}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha),\quad\text{and}\quad\rho(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha),

(1.2)

where any function
F(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)
can be divided into its bar and tilde parts:

\displaystyle F=\overline{F}(\bar{\bm{x}},t)+\widetilde{F}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)\quad\text{where}\quad\langle F\rangle=\overline{F}\quad\text{and}\quad\langle\widetilde{F}\rangle=0.

(1.3)

In contrast to the GLM literature, we adopt a flexible use of
\bar{\bm{x}}
,
\bm{x}
, and
\bm{X}
, which can be interchanged frequently to simplify both calculations and interpretations, even within a single formula.

Section 3 contains the main results of the paper.
First, we express the actual velocity divergence
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}
in terms of
\overline{\nabla}\cdot\overline{\bm{u}}
and the Jacobian
H
of transformation from
\bm{x}
to
\bar{\bm{x}}
.
Second, we introduce three forms of exact GLM-CE corresponding to (
1.1
).
Third, we show that each of these equations possesses a special structure of type
\overline{\Phi}=\widetilde{\Psi}
with some functions
\overline{\Phi}
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
,
which entails equations
\overline{\Phi}=0
and
\widetilde{\Psi}=0
.
The former leads to the averaged GLM-CEs, while the latter is called the tilde (or compatibility) equation.
As a result, each accurately formulated exact GLM-CE represents a system of explicit average and tilde equations.
It is important that in the presence of small parameters (e.g. the amplitudes), the orders of the exact, averaged, and tilde equations are the same.
In a different type of GLM averaging, such as a spatial average for periodic waves, the tilde equations naturally appear as the oscillatory part of the governing equations.
One may notice that the tilde equations were omitted from the considerations of GLM-CEs in [1-3, 5].

Section 4 analyses the McIntyre-Andrews Transformation (MAT) of CEs (see [1-3, 5]).
We give it in two versions.
The first represents a generalised form of MAT.
It yields results similar to those in Section 3 but is more complex to derive.
In contrast, the original MAT is based on the introduction of a new auxiliary function
\sigma(\bar{\bm{x}},t)
(in [1] it is indicated as
\tilde{\rho}
) that satisfies a postulated PDE with special initial conditions connecting
\sigma
and
\rho
.
In addition, it is subject to a specific compatibility equation.
Hence, it applies to a special class of fluid motions.

Section 5 provides examples of an average flow with small Lagrangian perturbations, which connects the paper to the classical GLM theory.
The tilde equations for these cases are explicitly demonstrated in terms of Lagrangian displacements
\bm{\xi}
.

2.
The variables and their flexible usage

Consider a fluid motion described by two smooth and invertible vector functions,

\displaystyle\bm{x}=\bm{x}(\bm{X},t,\alpha),\qquad\bar{\bm{x}}=\bar{\bm{x}}(\bm{X},t),

(2.1)

where
\alpha

The first Eqn.(

\bm{x}
and Lagrangian
\bm{X}

These two one-to-one mappings
\bm{x}\Leftrightarrow\bm{X}

\bar{\bm{x}}\Leftrightarrow\bm{X}

\displaystyle\bm{x}\Leftrightarrow\bar{\bm{x}},\quad\text{or}\quad\ \bm{x}=\bm{x}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha),\ \bar{\bm{x}}=\bar{\bm{x}}(\bm{x},t,\alpha).

(2.2)

As a result, any function that describes the motion of a fluid can be expressed through any of the three independent variables
(\bm{X},t)

(\bm{x},t)
, or
(\bar{\bm{x}},t)

Two types related to (

(2.3)

and

We use three different expressions for the same material derivative,

\displaystyle{\partial}/{\partial t}|_{\bm{X}}={\partial}/{\partial t}|_{\bm{x}}+{\bm{u}}\cdot\nabla={\partial}/{\partial t}|_{\bar{\bm{x}}}+{\bar{\bm{u}}}\cdot\bar{\nabla}\ \ \text{or}\ \ D_{\bm{X}}=D=\bar{D}.

The variables

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{x}}\equiv\langle\bm{x}\rangle,\quad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\bm{u}}\equiv\langle\bm{u}\rangle,

(2.5)

We use only the ensemble averaging

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{x}}\equiv\langle\bm{x}\rangle\equiv\int{\bm{x}}(\bm{X},t,\alpha)f(\alpha)d\alpha=\int{\bm{x}}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)f(\alpha)d\alpha,\quad\int f(\alpha)d\alpha=1,

(2.6)

and similar expressions for
\bm{u}

The parameter
\alpha
is an ensemble variable, and
f(\alpha)\geq 0
is a normalised distribution function (say, in a wave package or in turbulence).
The two averages in (
2.6

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{x}}=\bar{\bm{x}}(\bm{X},t)\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{X}=\bm{X}(\bar{\bm{x}},t)

(2.7)

\alpha
(
2.1

(
2.1

\bm{x}(\bar{\bm{x}},t)
and
\bar{\bm{u}}(\bar{\bm{x}},t)

2.2

\bullet
The coordinates
\bm{x}

This means that any formula containing
\bm{x}
and

Similarly to the flexibility in using

\displaystyle f(\bm{x},t,\alpha)=f(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)=f(\bm{X},t,\alpha).

(2.8)

3.
Transformations of continuity equations

3.1. Transformation of velocity divergence.

Euler’s kinematic equalities for Jacobians (see formula (3.8) in [11]) are:

\displaystyle{(\rm i)}\quad\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=D\ln J,\qquad{(\rm ii)}\quad\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=\bar{D}\ln\bar{J}.

(3.1)

We rewrite (i) by adding and subtracting
\bar{D}\ln\bar{J}
, and then use (ii),

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=(D\ln J-\bar{D}\ln\bar{J})+\bar{D}\ln\bar{J}=\bar{D}\ln H^{c}+\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}},

(3.2)

which gives us the required link between two forms of divergence.
As a result, GLM-CEs follow after substituting
\nabla\cdot\bm{u}
from (
3.2
) into (
1.1
).
In particular, for (
1.1
)
(c)
, it gives

\displaystyle D\ln\rho=-\nabla\cdot\bm{u}=-\bar{D}\ln H^{c}-\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}},\qquad\text{or}

(3.3)

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla}\cdot\overline{\bm{u}}=Q;\qquad Q\equiv-\bar{D}\ln(\rho H)^{c},

(3.4)

where (
3.4
) represents the exact GLM-CE
(c)
required.

3.2. The List of GLM-CEs
is given below using the bar and tilde notation (
1.3
) and the shortcuts,

\displaystyle h\equiv\ln H,\qquad\lambda\equiv\ln\sigma,\qquad\sigma\equiv\rho H.

(3.5)

(a)
For
a homogeneous incompressible fluid
(
1.1
)
(a)

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=-\bar{D}\,h^{c},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=-\bar{D}\,\overline{h^{c}},

(3.7)

\displaystyle\bar{D}\,\widetilde{h^{c}}=0,

(3.8)

(b)

For an inhomogeneous incompressible fluid
(

(b)

\displaystyle\bar{D}\rho^{c}=0\ \ \text{and}\ \ \bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=-\bar{D}\,{h}^{c},

(3.9)

\displaystyle\bar{D}\overline{\rho^{c}}=0\ \text{and}\ \ \bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=-\bar{D}\,\overline{h^{c}},

(3.11)

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}\cdot\bar{\bm{u}}=-\bar{D}\,\lambda^{c},

(3.13)

\displaystyle\bar{D}\,\widetilde{\lambda^{c}}=0.

(3.14)

The meaning of different lines in
(b)
and
(c)
is the same as in
(a)
.

3.3. The role of tilde (compatibility) equations.

The first aim of GLM theory is to obtain the system of exact governing equations based on independent variables
(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)
.
The related GLM-CEs are given by (
3.6
),(
3.9
),(
3.12
), which contain bar (average) and tilde terms.
In an accurate formulation, each of these equations represents a system of two equations: an average (bar) equation (taken as one of (
3.7
),(
3.10
),(
3.13

Historically, the bar equations were derived by the original MAT procedure (see section 4.2 below), which was built to avoid the tilde equations.
In (

\displaystyle\bar{D}\,\widetilde{h^{c}}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)\equiv\bar{D}\,\widetilde{\ln H^{c}}=0\qquad\text{for the cases}\ \textbf{(a)}\ \text{and}\ \textbf{(b)},

(3.15)

\displaystyle\quad\bar{D}\,\widetilde{\lambda^{c}}(\bar{\bm{x}},t,\alpha)\equiv\bar{D}\,\widetilde{\ln(\rho H)^{c}}=0\qquad\text{for the case}\ \textbf{(c)},

(3.16)

where, generally

Due to the definitions (

(in Jacobian

2.10

A particular example of the above equations is the following.

\displaystyle H=\widetilde{H}(\alpha),\qquad(\rho H)=(\widetilde{\rho H})(\alpha)

(3.17)

where the arbitrary functions of
\alpha
assign different constants to each flow in the ensemble.
An even more specific example is the following

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}\equiv 0,\ \text{for}\ \textbf{(a)}\ \text{and}\ \textbf{(b)}\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\rho H}\equiv 0,\ \text{for}\ \textbf{(c)}.

(3.18)

Both (

In contrast, Eqs.(

The dynamic meaning of the constraints (

\displaystyle{\partial\,\widetilde{h}}/{\partial t}|_{\bm{X}}=0,\qquad\widetilde{h}=\widetilde{\chi}(\bm{X},\alpha),

This demonstrates the conservation of

4.
The McIntyre - Andrews Transformation (MAT)

4.1. The generalised version.

The set of required general equations is

5.
Examples of small perturbations.

\tilde{H}=0
where

\displaystyle H\equiv\det(\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{K})=\exp\!\left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{n+1}\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbb{K}^{n})/n\right),

(5.6)

or, as its polynomial version,

\displaystyle{H}=1+\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbb{K})+\frac{1}{2}[(\operatorname{Tr}\mathbb{K})^{2}-\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbb{K}^{2})]+O(\varepsilon^{3}).

(5.7)

For the method of successive approximations, the different order terms in
\tilde{Q}=0
and
\tilde{H}=0
vanish independently for each
n
.
More complex equations (involving the density field) for (
3.16
) follow automatically and are not given here.

Taking into account all given compatibility equations represents a topic for further research.
