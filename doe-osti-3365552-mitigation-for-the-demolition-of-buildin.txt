 INTRODUCTION
The purpose of this letter report is to document the mitigation of adverse effects of a proposed
undertaking that would demolish Building 25-3124 in Area 25 of the Nevada National Security Site
(NNSS) in compliance with the terms of the 2024 Programmatic Agreement among the U.S. Department
of Energy and the Nevada State Historic Preservation Officer and the Advisory Council on Historic
Preservation Concerning the Protection of Historic Properties on the Nevada National Security Site, Nye
County, Nevada (NNSS PA). Building 25-3124 (State Historic Preservation Office [SHPO] Resource #
B19009) is a contributing element to the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District
(SHPO Resource # D424), which has been determined eligible for listing in the National Register of
Historic Places (NRHP) under Criteria A, B, C, and D (Reno et al. 2023).
The proposed demolition constitutes an adverse effect. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) National
Nuclear Security Administration Nevada Field Office (NNSA/NFO) consulted with the SHPO on an
adverse effect finding for the undertaking and notified the SHPO of its intent to use the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA on December 11, 2024 (Julian to Reed). The SHPO concurred on December
30, 2024 (Reed to Julian). Therefore, NNSA/NFO has prepared mitigation pursuant to the standard
mitigation in the NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible
districts.
 MITIGATION CONTENTS
The standard mitigation for contributing elements to recorded, NRHP-eligible districts consists of an
updated Architectural Resource Assessment (ARA) form to document current conditions (Appendix
D.I.A), including high-quality digital images (Appendix D.I.A.1) and plans, drawings, or annotated
sketch plans (Appendix D.I.A.2), and a brief letter report describing mitigation contents and summarizing
the building’s historic significance (Appendix D.I.B).
This mitigation report documents the completion of mitigation for Building 25-3124 in accordance with
NNSS PA Appendix D. An ARA Update form was prepared to satisfy NNSS PA Appendix D.I.A (see
Appendix A). It provides additional information about the historic property, an individual NRHP
evaluation, and interior description, engineering drawings, and current and historical photographs. This
letter report fulfills NNSS PA Appendix D.I.B.
 DESCRIPTION OF BUILDING 25-3124
Building 25-3124, or the Equipment Test Laboratory (ETL), is adjacent to the main Test Cell A (TCA)
complex. The ETL is a one-story rectangular-plan building executed in tan concrete and concrete block.
A crenellated wall pattern lines the top of the front façade beneath a narrow eave with a plain fascia
(Figure 1). The building is composed of two distinct masses. The larger front (south) portion of the
building is made of concrete block and the rear is reinforced concrete. Though the rear looks like an
addition, they were built at the same time, according to available information. The front portion has a flat
roof, while the rear has a steeply pitched shed roof that slopes downward from the rear elevation to the
point where it meets the adjacent flat roof.
The front (south) façade is dominated by a central exterior sliding bay door. A steel frame for a traveling
crane protrudes over a concrete apron in front of the door. The crane has been removed. A flush personnel
door is immediately west of sliding door, and another is near the east corner. Floodlights are mounted
high on the wall.

The Control Room in 103 held a floor-to-ceiling trapezoid-shaped console with communication,
instrumentation, and electrical panels (H&N 1962e). The console has been removed, but the wiring in the
foundation may still be in place. Two small viewing windows allowed workers in the Control Room to
see into the Flow Bay.
The Clean Room, 104, was originally accessible only through the Cleaning Room, 105. A Clean Room is
a space that has been carefully cleared of dust and/or contaminants that could interfere with the operation
of equipment. Room 105 had tanks for the specialized cleaning of items that would be used in the Clean
Room. The small room labelled 104A in Figure 2 was added during the renovation. The grates on the
floor in the Cleaning Room cover a pit that sat below the acid, alkaline, and rinse water tanks. Room 105
is the second room that has the monorail system attached to the ceiling (H&N 1962f, 1962g; REECo
1992).
Room 106 was the Utility Room. The wall-sized motor control center is still in place along the south wall
of the room (Figure 5). According to engineering drawings, the motor control center controlled the power
to the interior and exterior lighting, water heater, air conditioner, evaporative cooler motors, and
receptacle strips (H&N 1962h).
Room 107 is a single-stall restroom, and Room 108 is an equipment room accessible from the exterior of
the building. The Static Test Bay, 109, has an interior sliding door with a bullet-resistant window. Static
testing involves applying pressure to an object at a steady rate to determine at what point a piece of
equipment is likely to fail. The specialized window allowed engineers to see what was happening in the
room but could also withstand projectiles that might result from an equipment failure. The blow-out
panels that previously surrounded the exterior doors of Rooms 108 and 109 have been replaced with
wood panels (H&N 1962a).

The interiors of some rooms were full of pipes. A flow diagram indicates there were lines for air, cold
water, hydrogen, hydraulic fluid, helium gas, nitrogen gas, and vent and vacuum systems (H&N 1961). In
addition to sourcing gas from the adjacent tank farm on the east side of the ETL, pipes also ran from both
large and small tanks on the west side of the building. The two large high-pressure gas tanks are still
present. The pipes attached to the interior walls were all removed, but some pipes are still visible running
along the ceiling, and numerous pipes are still in place outside the building (Figure 6). Photographs were
taken by Holmes and Narver soon after the building was completed and before the engineers brought in
their more specialized equipment. A selection of these is presented at the end of the ARA form in
Appendix A.
One of the more interesting features of this building is the numerous heavy-duty, blast-resistant sliding
doors. They were installed on both exterior and interior 8 in.-thick walls. Exterior doors have hoods
covering the tracks and rollers from which the doors hang. There is a stay roller or floor guide at the base
of each door to ensure stability. The sliding door between Rooms 102 and 105, the Cleaning Room,
includes a narrow pilot door (H&N 1962i, 1962j), and the sliding door in the Utility Room has a vent. In
total, there are three exterior and three interior sliding doors (Figure 7).

added. Exposed pipes were also removed from the interior of the building, and two large pipe fixtures

 SIGNIFICANCE SUMMARY
In 1956, the Atomic Energy Commission (now the U.S. Department of Energy) and the National
Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (later the National Aeronautics and Space Administration) selected
an area in the southwestern portion of today’s Nevada National Security Site (NNSS) to test reactors and
engines developed by Los Alamos National Laboratory and other private contractors under the Rover and
NERVA nuclear thermal rocket propulsion programs. The overall objective was to design an efficient and
reliable system to boost both piloted and autonomous rockets to the moon, Mars, and beyond.
Construction began in 1957 on the specialized test stands, maintenance, assembly, and disassembly
buildings, transportation system, and infrastructure that became the NRDS.
The ETL was constructed in 1962 and was primarily used by engineers from LASL. The facility
supported the nuclear rocket program until the program was cancelled in 1973. Work included water flow
testing, gas flow testing, static pressure testing, equipment maintenance and cleaning, and limited
analytical work (DOE/NOO 1998).
Individual Significance Evaluation
Building 25-3124 is recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP at the local level of the NNSS
under Criteria A and C. See the ARA form in Appendix A for the full NHRP evaluation.
Under Criterion A, the building has a direct association with the development of nuclear rocket
technology in the United States. The facility was used to test critical components of the rockets assembled
after its construction in 1962, but the blow-out panels and the blast-resistant doors indicate work carried
out in the building was potentially very dangerous. The activities that occurred here were directly related
to the successful testing of rocket components designed during the Rover and NERVA programs, with
some risk to the individuals involved.
Under Criterion C, the ETL embodies the distinctive characteristics of a building constructed to advance
the field of nuclear propulsion at that time in history. Character-defining features of the original design
included the painted design on the front elevation, the extensive pipe network throughout the building; the
blast-resistant sliding doors; the blow-out panels; additional conduits placed in trenches in the floor and
walls; the monorail tracks and equipment installed on the ceiling; the console in the Control Room; and
the motor control panel. Engineering drawings show that the trenches were built into the foundation to
accommodate the typical wiring found in any building, as well as empty conduit that would hold the
wiring for instruments to monitor the many types of equipment brought in by LASL (H&N 1962k). Blast-
resistant doors are heavy and held in place by heavy-duty hardware on 8-in. concrete brick walls at the
top and set in grooves in the floor on the bottom. The blow-out panels were installed to release pressure
from within a confined space in a controlled manner in the event of an accident. Some of these features
are still present. The distinctive characteristics, construction, and technology of the ETL resulted in a
unique and important space.
Integrity
The following relates to the integrity of the ETL as it pertains to the recommendation that it be considered
individually eligible for the NRHP. The building retains all seven aspects of integrity to varying degrees.
Integrity of location, feeling, and association are intact. The building is in its original location adjacent to
the TCA tank farm. Its proximity to the existing compressed gas tanks enabled easy access to the same
resources that were used during actual engine tests. Its location within the NRDS complex also made it
easier for LASL scientists to transport equipment from the testing laboratory to the test cells. This
reduced the need to move delicate equipment from Los Alamos to Jackass Flats and enabled in-person

collaboration with on-site scientists while preparing for the tests. The immediate and broader settings of
the TCA and the NRDS convey an association, setting, and feeling related to the creativity and
perseverance required for the new inventions and modifications needed to accomplish something that had
never been done before. The aspect of feeling is somewhat diminished because there is little activity in
Jackass Flats today, but not completely diminished because several of the NRDS structures like Engine
Test Stand 1 are still visible in the distance.
Integrity of materials, design, and workmanship have been somewhat affected by the removal of many
features in the lab, but not to the degree that the building no longer conveys its significance. Of the
character-defining features, the decorative design on the front of the building, the sliding doors, the hoist,
exterior pipes, and the motor control panel are still present. Despite the removal of the other features,
Building 25-3124 retains sufficient integrity and has the ability to convey its significance as an
individually eligible resource. Through its physical features and context, the building demonstrates its
role as a critically important component of the Rover and NERVA programs.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information, methods
for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

 For the purposes of the present survey, resources within the boundary of the Nuclear Rocket Development Station
 (NRDS) Historic District were evaluated to determine if they contribute to the significance of the district. The NRDS
 Historic District is recommended eligible for listing to the NRHP under all four criteria as the testing center for the
 Rover/NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) program from 1956 to 1973.
 In the initial recording of the Test Cell A facility (Beck et al. 2000), the report mentions the Engine Test Laboratory
 (ETL) is an element of the Test Cell A facility. However, the building was not recorded or evaluated.
 Building 25-3124(B19001) contributes to the significance of the NRDS Historic District as a laboratory that supported
 the NRDS Rover/NERVA mission. The Los Alamos J-7 (Mechanical Engineering) Group oversaw the ETL. It
 included equipment for testing mechanical systems and components using a water flow system, gas flow system,
 and a static test bay. Testing performed at the ETL included flow calibration of valves, meters, and other pipeline
 components; valve life tests under operating conditions; calibration of flow meters; dynamic response tests of valves
 and other components under flow conditions; evaluations of instrumentation systems; and fluid hammer studies.
 The building was also used by the Los Alamos J-5 (Test Cell Operations, NRDS) Group’s Systems Quality
 Assurance Program. This Group specialized in piping systems components and welding specifications required to
 withstand the unprecedented conditions created by the high-pressure, highly reactive, and cryogenic operations
 needed for the Rover testing program.
 The building retains all aspects of integrity to convey its significance. It is eligible for listing in the NRHP as a
 contributing element to the NRDS Historic District.
 Building 25-3124 is also a potential contributing element of the unevaluated Test Cell A subdistrict. This subdistrict
 was a component of the NRDS program as the test platform for nuclear reactors developed under the Rover
 program. The subdistrict possesses a significant concentration of elements, which convey a visual sense of the
 overall environment for testing nuclear reactors. This subdistrict was identified but not evaluated as part of the
 current survey.
 Due to the limited scope of this investigation, the ETL (25-3124, B19001) was not individually evaluated for eligibility
 to the NRHP under any of the Secretary of the Interior’s Significance Criteria (36 CFR 60.4).

 7. Narrative Architectural Description
Provide a detailed description of the resource, including all character defining features, potential construction methods,
potential alterations (both historic and non-historic), and any accessory resources.

 The ETL is adjacent to the main Test Cell A complex, but it functioned well beyond supporting this particular test cell.
 Test Cell A was already in operation before the ETL was constructed just west of the perimeter fence. The protective
 dike and ditch around Test Cell A were flattened for construction, and these protective structures were moved farther
 west to encompass the ETL. Posts from the original perimeter fence still stand in the rear yard of the ETL, and
 remnants of the original dike and ditch are just in front of its compound. The chain link, barbed wire perimeter fence
 was extended to go around the new facility. The building is surrounded by asphalt paving. The outer areas of the
 fenced yard are gravel. The yard was eventually extended farther to the west to accommodate large high-pressure
 gas tanks (AR1). At that time, a cluster of trailers and small temporary buildings occupied the southwest corner of
 the yard. None remain in place today.
 The last use of the building was in the early 1990s and is still proclaimed on a fading sign on the principal (south)
 façade “U.S. DEPARTMENT OF ENERGY OFFICE OF TECHNOLOGY DEVELOPMENT / TREATABILITY TEST
 FACILITY.” One of the purposes of this program was to pursue a plutonium soil separation pilot test project (Beck et
 al. 2000:6-7).
 The ETL is a severe one-story rectangular-plan Brutalist building executed in tan concrete and concrete block. A

 The front (south) façade is dominated by a central exterior sliding bay door. A steel frame for a traveling crane

 The north wall has a protruding enclosed eave. Just beneath it is a continuous row of louvered vents. There is a

 is not known what originally filled these huge openings.
 The west elevation of the front block has a personnel door behind the walls of the pressure testing enclosure. Near
 the front (right) corner is an exterior sliding door with louver vents. A lightly constructed porch with pipe columns and

 The east elevation of the front block has a steel roof ladder, a metal hood which protected some removed
 equipment, and a personnel door. A cryogenic pipeline exits from the rear block.
 A large green steel machine of unknown function is on the sidewalk at the northeast rear corner of the building. It
 could not be closely inspected. The machine is large enough that it has a built-in platform and access ladder. Other

 in the early 1990s.

 6. Narrative Eligibility Justification
Provide a detailed explanation of the resource’s eligibility for the National Register, including supporting historic information, methods
for evaluation under the four criteria, discussion of the seven aspects of integrity, and conclusions about eligibility.

 The Equipment Test Laboratory (ETL) (25-3124, B19001) was determined to be a contributing element to the
 Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS) Historic District (SHPO No. D424). The NNSA/NFO provided full
 documentation on its NRDS determinations (Reno et al. 2023) and requested concurrence via letter on November
 11, 2023 (Huck). On February 2, 2024, the SHPO replied via email stating that the office was unable to complete a
 timely review of the documentation (Reed). The NNSA/NFO finalized its determinations as a result and archived the
 documentation at the Nuclear Testing Archive and on OSTI.gov.
 In 1956, the area known as Jackass Flats at the NNSS was chosen to test reactors and engines developed by Los
 Alamos Scientific Laboratory (LASL, now known as Los Alamos National Laboratory) and other private contractors
 under the Rover and Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application (NERVA) propulsion programs. The area
 became known as the Nuclear Rocket Development Station (NRDS). The overall objective of the programs was to
 design an efficient and reliable system to send both piloted and autonomous rockets into space. Construction began
 in 1957 on the specialized test stands; maintenance, assembly, and disassembly buildings; transportation system;
 and infrastructure that became the NRDS (Dewar 2004).

 The NRDS Historic District is eligible for inclusion in the National Register at the national level of significance under
 all four criteria. Its significance is important not only in the history of the United States, but also due to its potential
 effects on space travel. As such, a primary theme identified for the NRDS was the development of nuclear rocket
 technology in the United States. The significance of the district was reported in the architectural survey of the NRDS
 (Reno et al. 2023).
 The ETL was constructed in 1962 and was primarily used by engineers from LASL. The facility supported the
 nuclear rocket program until the program was cancelled in 1973. Work included water flow testing, gas flow testing,
 static pressure testing, equipment maintenance and cleaning, and limited analytical work (DOE/NOO 1998). The
 following is an excerpt from a 1965 quarterly status report on the Rover program describing activities at the ETL:
 Pressure and leak tests were conducted in the static test bay on 110 valves of various types and sizes, seven
 pressure regulators, three pressure gages, two tubing assemblies, a small pressure vessel, an emergency purge
 spider, 10 flex hoses, and a small heat exchanger. Evaluation flow tests using H2O were completed for a 4-in.
 Annin pneumatic control valve with a fail-in-place operator, a 4-in. check valve, and a 2-in. Annin control valve
 with special modified linear plug. Flow tests using hydraulic oil were made on two hydraulic filter banks. A GH2
 flow test was performed on an emergency purge spider. Evaluation of a 1/4-in. Hoke ball valve for
 instrumentation lines was completed using GN2 (LASL 1965).
 The ETL was converted in 1992 into the Treatability Test Facility where studies were conducted to determine if
 uranium and plutonium could be separated from contaminated soils (DOE/NFO 1992).
 NRHP Eligibility
 The ETL is a contributing element to the eligible NRDS Historic District. It contributes to the significance of the
 district under Criterion A as one of the facilities in the NRDS area that supported research related to the nuclear
 rocket development theme from the time of its construction in 1962 to 1973. The building was used for testing
 individual equipment components and systems in preparation for final rocket testing at other locations within the
 NRDS.
 Building 25-3124 is also recommended as individually eligible for the NRHP at the local level of significance under
 Criteria A and C for its contribution to historical activities at the NNSS.
 Criterion A. To be significant under Criterion A, a property must be directly associated with events that have made a
 significant contribution to the broad patterns of our history. Regarding Criterion A, the building has a strong
 association with the development of nuclear rocket technology in the United States. The facility was used to test
 critical components of the rockets assembled after its construction in 1962, activities that occurred here were directly
 related to the successful testing of rocket components designed during the Rover and NERVA programs.
 Criterion B. To be significant under Criterion B, a property must be directly associated with the productive life of a
 significant person. No historically significant person is known to be associated with this building.
 Criterion C. Properties significant under Criterion C must embody the distinctive characteristics of a type, period, or
 method of construction; represent the work of a master; possess high artistic values; or represent a significant and
 distinguishable entity whose components may lack individual distinction. According to National Register Bulletin #15,
 under the first part of Criterion C, “A structure is eligible as a specimen of its type or period of construction if it is an
 important example (within its context) of building practices of a particular time in history” (NPS 1997:18). The second
 part of Criterion C recognizes the work of master architects, builders, engineers, designers, artists, and the like. As
 defined in National Register Bulletin #15, “A master is a figure of generally recognized greatness in a field, a known
 craftsman of consummate skill, or an anonymous craftsman whose work is distinguishable from others by its
 characteristic style and quality” (NPS 1997:20).
 Character-defining features of the original design included the painted design on the front elevation, the extensive
 pipe network throughout the building; the blast-resistant sliding doors; the blow-out panels; additional conduits
 placed in trenches in the floor and walls; the monorail tracks and equipment installed on the ceiling; the console in
 the Control Room; and the motor control panel. Engineering drawings show that trenches were built into the
 foundation to accommodate the typical wiring found in any building, as well as empty conduit that would hold the
 wiring for instruments to monitor the many types of equipment brought in by LASL (H&N 1962a). Blast-resistant
 doors are heavy and held in place by heavy-duty hardware on 8-in. concrete brick walls at the top and set in grooves
 in the floor on the bottom. The blow-out panels were installed to release pressure from within a confined space in a
 controlled manner in the event of an accident. Some of these features are still present. The distinctive

 characteristics, construction, and technology of the ETL resulted in a space that was unique in its ability to advance
 the field of nuclear propulsion at that time in history.
 Criterion D. To be significant under Criterion D, a property must have yielded or be likely to yield information
 important in prehistory or history that can only be answered by the actual physical material of the resources.
 Regarding Criterion D, the testing lab, as a physical resource, is unlikely to yield significant information important to

 Integrity
 The following relates to the integrity of the ETL as it pertains to the recommendation that it be considered individually
 eligible for the NRHP. The building retains all seven aspects of integrity to varying degrees. Integrity of location,
 feeling, and association is intact. The building is in its original location adjacent to the TCA tank farm. Its proximity to
 the existing compressed gas tanks enabled easy access to the same resources that were used during actual engine
 tests. Its location within the NRDS complex also made it easier for LASL scientists to transport equipment from the
 testing laboratory to the test cells. This reduced the need to move delicate equipment from Los Alamos to Jackass
 Flats and enabled in-person collaboration with on-site scientists while preparing for the tests rather than only
 traveling back and forth for periodic meetings. The immediate and broader settings of the TCA and the NRDS
 convey an association, setting, and feeling related to the creativity and perseverance required for the new inventions
 and modifications needed to accomplish something that had never been done before. The aspect of feeling is
 somewhat diminished because there is little activity in Jackass Flats today, but because several of the structures like
 Engine Test Stand 1 and the Engine Maintenance and Disassembly facility are still visible in the distance, a visitor
 may still feel in awe of the vast complex utilized to conduct such ground-breaking research.
 Integrity of materials, design, and workmanship have been somewhat affected by the removal of many features in
 the lab, but not to the degree that the building no longer conveys its significance. Of the character-defining features,
 the decorative design on the front of the building, the sliding doors, the hoist, exterior pipes, and the motor control
 panel are still present. Despite the removal of the other features, Building 25-3124 retains sufficient integrity and has
 the ability to convey its significance as an individually eligible resource. Through its physical features and context,
 the building demonstrates its role as a critically important component of the Rover and NERVA programs.

 7. Narrative Architectural Description
Provide a detailed description of the resource, including all character defining features, potential construction methods,
potential alterations (both historic and non-historic), and any accessory resources.

 The exterior of the ETL remains unchanged from the description provided in the original ARA form and is briefly
 summarized here with some additional information. It is a 3,850 sq. ft., one-story rectangular-plan concrete block and
 reinforced concrete building and painted in a light yellow. A light salmon-colored crenellation-shaped pattern is
 painted at the top of the front or southern elevation beneath a narrow eave with a plain fascia. This type of
 decoration is extremely rare at the NNSS, where most buildings are very utilitarian in appearance. The building is
 composed of two distinct masses. The larger front (south) portion of the building is made of concrete block and the
 rear is reinforced concrete. The front portion has a flat roof, and the rear has a steeply pitched shed roof that slopes
 downward from the rear elevation to the point where it meets the adjacent flat roof.
 The south elevation has a central exterior sliding bay door. A steel frame for a traveling crane protrudes over a
 concrete apron in front of the door. The crane has been removed. A single personnel door is immediately west of the
 sliding door, and another is near the east corner, the latter of which was installed during a later renovation. All single
 and double doors in the building are flush. Floodlights are mounted high on the front wall.
 The north elevation has a protruding enclosed eave. Just beneath it is a row of louvered vents. There is a large,
 centered, double exterior sliding bay door. Nearly the entire wall surface on each side of the sliding door originally
 held corrugated plastic blow-out panels measuring 9.5 ft tall (H&N 1962b). These were replaced with plywood panels
 sometime between 1973 and 1992. An aluminum sliding window was later added in the center of each of these
 retrofitted wall panels.
 The west elevation has two single personnel doors providing access to two separate rooms, plus a pressure testing
 enclosure to the south, and a shed-roofed awning supported by two thin posts to the north. A sliding door to the
 Utility Room is near the south end of this elevation. The east elevation of the building has a steel roof ladder, a metal
 hood which protected some removed equipment, and a single door.

 The building interior includes nine purpose-built rooms. The Flow Bay, Room 101, originally had a trench built into
 the floor, extending nearly the entire length of the room. Water and gas supply and return pipes were in and above
 the trench. The gas pipes continued outside and connected to the tank farm within the adjacent Test Cell A (TCA)
 complex. Some of the exterior gas pipes are still in place. A concrete pad on the exterior side of the east wall shows
 the markings of three 36-ft tall calibration tanks that were part of the water return system (H&N 1962c, 1962d,
 1962e). The doorway between Rooms 101 and 102 has both a double personnel door and a sliding door. The
 calibration tanks were removed, and the trench in Room 101 was filled with concrete sometime after 1972.
 The Maintenance and Disassembly (M.A.D.) area, Room 102, extends from the south side of the building to the
 interior door of Room 101. It is one of two rooms in the building with a ceiling monorail system. There is a 1-ton
 Chisholm-Moore “Meteor” wire rope electric hoist still attached to the rail in this room. The crane that was attached to
 the steel frame outside the south exterior door moved materials to the entry point of the monorail. Room 102 was
 divided into three sections during the renovation in the 1990s (Reynolds Electrical & Engineering [REECo] 1993).
 The Control Room in 103 held a floor-to-ceiling trapezoid-shaped console with communication, instrumentation, and
 electrical panels that were connected to wiring in conduits installed in a trench in the foundation (H&N 1962f). The
 console has been removed, but the wiring in the foundation may still be in place. Two small viewing windows allowed
 workers in the Control Room to see into the Flow Bay.
 The Clean Room, 104, was originally accessible only through the Cleaning Room, 105. A Clean Room is a space
 that has been carefully cleared of dust and/or contaminants that could interfere with the operation of equipment.
 Room 105 had tanks for the specialized cleaning of items that would be used in the Clean Room. The small room
 labelled 104A on the 1993 REECo engineering drawing and its exterior door were added during the renovation, as
 were the exterior door on the south side of Room 105 and the door between Rooms 103 and 104. The south wall of
 104A was ripped out during remediation activities in approximately 1998 (DOE/NOO 1998). The grates on the floor
 in the Cleaning Room cover a pit that sat below acid, alkaline, and rinse water tanks. Room 105 is the second room
 that has the monorail system attached to the ceiling (H&N 1962g, 1962h; REECo 1993).
 Room 106 was the Utility Room. The wall-sized motor control center is still in place along the south wall of the room.
 According to engineering drawings, it controlled the power to the interior and exterior lighting, water heater, air
 conditioner, evaporative cooler motors, and receptacle strips (H&N 1962i). The Utility Room also has an exterior
 sliding door on the west wall.
 Room 107 is a single-stall restroom, and Room 108 is an equipment room accessible from the exterior of the
 building. The Static Test Bay, 109, has an interior sliding door with a bullet-resistant window (H&N 1962b). The blow-
 out panels that previously surrounded the exterior doors of Rooms 108 and 109 have been replaced with wood
 panels.
 The interiors of some rooms were full of pipes while the NRDS was active. In addition to sourcing gas from the
 adjacent tank farm on the east side of the ETL, pipes also ran from both large and small tanks on the west side of
 the building. The two large high-pressure gas tanks are still present. The pipes attached to the interior walls were all
 removed, but some pipes are still visible running along the ceiling and numerous pipes are still in place outside the
 building.
 One of the more interesting features of this building is the numerous heavy-duty, blast-resistant sliding doors. They
 were installed on both exterior and interior 8 in.-thick walls. Exterior doors have hoods covering the tracks and rollers
 from which the doors hang. There is a stay roller or floor guide at the base of each door to ensure stability. The
 sliding door between Rooms 102 and 105, the Cleaning Room, includes a narrow pilot door (H&N 1962j, 1962k), and
 the sliding door in the Utility Room has a vent. In total, there are three exterior and three interior sliding doors.
