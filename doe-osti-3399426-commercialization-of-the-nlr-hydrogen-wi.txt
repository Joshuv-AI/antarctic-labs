 Executive Summary:

 Hydrogen wide area monitoring refers to the temporal and quantitative 3-dimenasional spatial
 profiling of hydrogen plumes following either intentional or unintentional hydrogen releases. A
 hydrogen wide area monitor (HyWAM) would have applications as a research tool, for example
 to provide empirical data on the behavior of hydrogen dispersions following a release, which in
 turn can be used to validate modelling studies. Support of modeling studies and commercial
 applications are interrelated, since modeling can serve to guide HyWAM deployment for
 enhanced safety within medium to large scale hydrogen operations, such as those envisioned by
 H2@Scale [1]. An economical, automated, and easily implemented HyWAM design did not
 exist prior to the TCF project. The NLR Sensor Laboratory (formerly, the NLR Sensor
 Laboratory had previously developed and demonstrated a prototype multi-point hydrogen
 detection system that can form the basis of a commercial HyWAM [2]. The initial NLR
 HyWAM was based on a distributed network of gas measurement points based on commercial
 hydrogen point sensors. The early HyWAM prototype analyzer was developed to address
 knowledge gaps identified by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 2 Hydrogen
 Technologies Code, Hydrogen Storage Safety Task Group. Critical identified safety gaps
 pertained to the behavior of cold hydrogen plumes associated with operational venting and other
 release scenarios of a LH2 system. The NLR HyWAM concept of a distributed network of
 measurement points has been shown to be amenable either to liquid hydrogen (LH2) [3] [4] [5]
 and gaseous hydrogen (GH2) systems [6], [[7], [8].

 Figure 1: Field deployment of the prototype NLR Hydrogen Analyzer during LH2 venting. This
 analyzer can form the basis for a commercial Hydrogen Wide Area Monitor

 The first HyWAM prototype was developed in response to a need to elucidate the behavior of
 released hydrogen to inform development of hydrogen safety codes and standards. Within the
 United States, NFPA 2 (National Fire Protection Code 2 “Hydrogen Technologies Code”)
 provides the fundamental safeguards for the use of hydrogen in both GH2 and LH2 forms . The
 NFPA 2 Hydrogen Storage Task Group was formed, in part, to explore approaches to minimize
 the risks and hazards associated with LH2 storage and utilization, and to facilitate its use in
 commercial applications. Although advances have been made in understanding the behavior of
 cold hydrogen plumes in the past few years, at the beginning TCF CRADA project, there were
 significant gaps in understanding LH2 dispersion behavior because empirical field data were
 essentially nonexistent. Even now, LH2 dispersion behavior remains neither fully understood
 nor fully characterized and is an ongoing subject of research. Modeling of LH2 releases

 2

 continues to be an active area of interest for the NFPA 2 Storage Task Group. Within the United
 States, this activity is being led by Sandia National Laboratory (SNL), which has developed
 models of LH2 release behavior under controlled conditions [4], [10]. At the request of the NFPA
 2 Task Group, the NLR Sensor Laboratory developed an early prototype hydrogen detection
 system for the multi-point continuous, quantitative monitoring of hydrogen; the system was
 explicitly developed to measure cold hydrogen plume dispersion during a routine venting of a
 LH2 storage tank at a commercial LH2 facility (see Figure 1) [10]. This proof-of-concept
 measurement system for cold hydrogen plume modeling was developed and field tested at a
 commercial LH2 facility. One outcome of the TCF CRADA project was the development and
 field deployment of a second-generation HyWAM NLR Hydrogen Analyzer for several LH2
 venting studies [4], [5]. Such deployments show that the HyWAM system can form the basis for
 a commercial Hydrogen Wide Area Monitor.

 Figure 2 illustrates the response of the first HyWAM prototype that was deployed for an LH2
 release [10],. Although significant results were obtained in the initial HyWAM design and
 deployment, several limitations were identified and addressed for the next generation HyWAM.
 Specifically, the early prototype instrument could not perform continuous and simultaneous
 monitoring of multiple measurement points; operationally, the first HyWAM prototype used a
 single sensor that sequentially analyzed gas samples collected from multiple measurement
 points. This design was unable to properly obtain temporal data simultaneously from all
 measurement points to model release behavior. These limitations were addressed in the next
 generation HyWAM instrument, as developed with support provided by the TCF project, with a
 primary initial application to further elucidate the behavior of cold hydrogen plumes. Several
 factors have been identified that can impact or control the dispersion process, including physical
 environment, structures, release process, and prevailing weather conditions. The quantitative
 impact of these factors is not yet fully understood, nor have all factors been fully identified.

 Figure 2: Transient hydrogen and oxygen levels as measured by the NLR Hydrogen Analyzer
 during LH2 venting

 The NLR HyWAM was first developed as a research monitoring tool to support internal NLR
 activity to profile hydrogen dispersions following releases under variable weather conditions and
 release scenarios. Multiple instrumented support structures were developed and deployed to
 establish a distributed 3-dimensional network of HyWAM measurement points that enable
 continuous monitoring during release operations. The HyWAM data on hydrogen profiles could
 empirically characterize plume dispersion to support modeling and research into hydrogen
 dispersion and to ascertain critical parameters controlling the dispersion. This activity will help
 identify design features for a commercial HyWAM and thus support product development.

 Current methods for wide area monitoring of gas releases are predominantly based upon IR or
 LIDAR methods, which work well for natural gas releases, but these methods are not amenable
 for hydrogen. The NLR HyWAM concept is based upon commercial or otherwise mature sensor
 technology (e.g., point sensors) that are sensitive to hydrogen that can be distributed around a
 hydrogen facility. The NLE HyWAM concept can be readily adapted for specialized research
 applications and commercialization strategies. A properly configured array of point sensors can
 provide, in the short-term, a low-cost and efficient hydrogen monitoring system, that is readily
 available to the hydrogen community.

 Summary of Research Results:

 Task 1: HyWAM Deployment Within NLR – Monitoring of Planned and Unplanned
 Releases

 A HyWAM will be designed with input from KWJ Engineering, Inc., built and internally
 deployed to profile hydrogen dispersion following scheduled hydrogen releases at NLR under
 variable weather conditions and release conditions. KWJ's role in this task will include
 identification of advanced hydrogen sensors (which may not yet be commercially available) that
 may meet critical HyWAM requirements (robustness, detection limits etc.) and guidance on their
 operation. KWJ will also provide manufacturable hardware designs for sensor control and signal
 management that could be deployable in real-world hydrogen facilities. These releases are part
 of current projects within the NLR hydrogen program. This activity will support design of the
 HyWAM, as well as to provide critically needed data on the dispersion of hydrogen following
 releases. In addition to hardware development and qualification, this activity supports hydrogen

 4

 safety research activity within NLR and other national laboratories by providing dispersion data
 and quantitative correlations between ambient conditions and release behavior.

 Task 1.1: HyWAM Hardware Development

 Instrumented support structures will be constructed by NLR with multiple units of different
 sensors types for internal HyWAM deployment around the NLR hydrogen system during
 scheduled release events. The support structures will be able to measure in real-time both the
 hydrogen dispersion and ambient conditions (e.g., wind speed and direction, temperature,
 humidity, and pressure). Sensors and hardware designs identified by KWJ in Task 1 will be
 incorporated into the NLR HyWAM for assessment.

 Summary of Work Completed and Changes:

 The hardware development phase of the TCF CRADA included a comprehensive focus on
 integrating advanced sensors, pumps, and plumbing systems to ensure reliable hydrogen gas
 monitoring. Digital and analog compatible sensors capable of detecting broad concentration
 ranges of hydrogen were selected and supplemented with thermocouples in each pump box to
 monitor hardware conditions. The initial HyWAM units were optimized and demonstrated to
 support modeling studies of large-scale hydrogen releases, where concentrations approaching
 100 vol% H2 could be encountered. A commercial thermo-conductivity sensor, with a range up
 to 100 vol% H2 (or 100 vol% He) was selected for the first HyWAM prototypes (Xensor, model
 XEN-5320, Delft, the Netherlands. The sensor developed by Xensor is currently produced and
 marketed by RS Product Solutions, Delfgauw, the Netherlands, https://rsproductsolutions.nl/ ).
 Subsequent work performed by NLR indicated that requirements for safety monitoring
 applications of hydrogen facilities will require a sensor that can respond to lower hydrogen
 concentrations ranging from low to medium ppmv H2 levels [11]. This was due to the fast
 dispersion and dilution of hydrogen in an outdoor environment, where hydrogen levels can be in
 the ppm range beyond 10 meters from the leak source. Required modifications for a commercial
 HyWAM that serves as commercial facility safety monitor include the use of alternative
 hydrogen sensors with a detection range from low ppmv to approximately between 1.0 and 10
 vol% H2. Commercial versions of the range of HyWAM thermal conductivity sensors became
 available with adjustable measurement range to meet the high ppmv detection requirements,
 although not quite to low ppmv; sensors with low ppmv detection limits remain a technical gap
 for hydrogen application. To achieve the lower measurement range, the thermal conductivity
 sensors currently in use for the NLR HyWAM were shipped back to the current manufacturer
 and updated to achieve lower detection limits and improve noise. The sensor selection process
 for HyWAM also explored adapting electrochemical sensors for improved detection limits,
 particularly in low-concentration environments, however, the present HyWAM configuration
 still utilizes thermal-conductivity sensors.

 Embellishments to the HyWAM monitor addressed improvements to support systems comprising
 the balance of plant elements, including pneumatic system elements, data collection and storage
 system, and packaging. To enable reliable flow control, centralized system options were
 considered, including the use of PWM-controlled brushless pumps which will be enhanced by
 PID flow control in the next system iteration. The pumps use robust hose attachment systems

 5

 designed to pass leak checks and withstand operational stress. In terms of plumbing, permanent
 solutions were implemented with flexible seal fittings, strain relief mechanisms, and straight
 tubing orientations to improve robustness and ease of maintenance. This has not yet been
 implemented but would be considered for commercial applications.

 HyWAM modules were constructed consisting of 8 hydrogen sensors with independent
 pneumatic systems (e.g., pumps and tubing), and electronic control elements for sensor operation
 and signal management. Two modules of 8 sensors and support systems could integrate into a
 control module, thereby producing a 16-measurement point HyWAM. For housing the HyWAM
 components (sensors, pumps, control systems), Pelican 1610 equipment cases were chosen due
 to their impact-resistant, crushproof, and watertight properties. The enclosures featured built-in
 screens to monitor real-time hydrogen concentration (as vol% H₂), ensuring the system's
 adaptability for field deployment and long-term operational stability. Unitronics controllers were
 used for data acquisition to handle environmental variability, which allowed for precise and
 accurate hydrogen profiling. Included in the hardware are I/O expansion units which enable the
 receiving and recording of 16 analog sensor channels per control box. Each sensor box also
 contains a USB hub connected to the 8 sensors which feeds their digital outputs to a USB
 feedthrough on the side of the case. This allows users to retrieve either analog or digital data
 from the sensors and increases modification flexibility.

 Task 1.2: HyWAM depolyment and Demonstration at NLR

 HyWAM consisting of multiple instrumented support structures developed in Task 1.1 will be
 deployed around the NLR hydrogen system for continuous monitoring during planned releases.
 The support structures will measure both the hydrogen dispersion and prevailing localized
 environmental conditions to investigate the impact of test parameters (e.g., release rate and
 direction) and ambient conditions (e.g., wind speed and direction) on the dispersion behavior.

 The deployment of HyWAM at NLR involved setting up an optimized grid of sensors to monitor
 both intended and unintended hydrogen or a hydrogen surrogate (e.g., helium) releases within
 and across the demonstration facility. One unique feature of the HyWAM thermal conductivity
 sensor is that it is sensitive to both hydrogen and helium, albeit with different calibration factors;
 this makes the HyWAM ideally suited for doing controlled releases using helium. Helium is
 often used as a hydrogen surrogate for dispersion modelling. Demonstrations included both
 indoor and outdoor applications. The system’s performance was tested across a range of
 medium-sized facilities, including hydrogen fueling stations, and NLR hydrogen laboratories to
 simulate real-world applications.

 Summary of Work Completed and Changes:

 Iterative testing during hardware development resulted in significant improvements to sensor
 calibration, signal noise, and overall hardware reliability, thus rendering a more robust, accurate
 and reliable HyWAM. Adjustments in plumbing and pump configurations further enhanced the
 system’s ability to function effectively under diverse conditions. The NLR HyWAM was
 configured with a hard-wire interface to the PLC system. To alleviate the complications with
 deploying a hard-wired instrument within hydrogen facility, a wireless, modular alternative
 deployment architecture was also developed and demonstrated. The wireless HyWAM was
 developed to increase arrangement flexibility and further enhance useability of the HyWAM
 system (see section 2.1).

 Task 2: End-User Interface

 The prototype HyWAM required a hardwire physical interface to a data acquisition system just to
 store data, with subsequent manual workup for analysis. This is adequate for a "research"
 HyWAM deployment with a relatively small number of sampling points and short duration but is
 inadequate for a commercial system that is to interface into a facility safety system. There are
 several aspects associated with the interface, including getting a (remote) signal to the facility
 control unit and physically interfacing the signal into the control system. This task will be
 performed in coordination with KWJ, Inc., a sensor and instrument manufacturer with experience
 in commercial chemical monitoring technology for industrial applications, and to a lesser extent
 with First Element to assure compatibility with hydrogen fueling facilities (see Task 3).

 Task 2.1: Wireless Communication with Sensors

 Most contemporary point sensors need a physical wire connection to provide power and a means
 for the end-user to measure and store the signal. This can be a concern for remote deployments,
 which may be required for some applications envisioned within H2@Scale. Power can be
 supplied by a (solar-rechargeable) battery, but presently this is not routinely done for
 hydrogen sensors and some ·configurational development is necessary. Wireless sensor
 communication may also be needed. Even with ready access to power wireless interrogation of
 the sensor would simplify HyWAM deployment by eliminating the physical implementation of
 cables. Wireless communication will certainly facilitate integration into remote facility control
 systems. Numerous wireless platforms exist but the incorporation into hydrogen sensor
 technology is not yet common. KWJ will design and build advanced H2 sensors and deliver to
 NLR for appropriate testing.

 A complementary hardware configuration was developed to utilize low-latency wireless
 technologies. The system architecture was built off of XBee DigiMesh radios, integrated into the
 system for seamless data communication. These radios were implemented along with arduino
 microcontrollers, enabling complex data handling and hardware flexibility. This configuration
 makes digital and analog inputs available and other protocols can be added with simple hardware
 adjustments. The integration of wireless communication significantly improved the system’s
 range and reduced dependency on cumbersome wired configurations and pumped line sampling

 7

 systems. The wireless HyWAM module was deployed simultaneously with the 16-channel
 HyWAM [4], with favorable performance.

 Task 2b: Data Management and Control System Interface

 Data Management includes not only storage of each sensor response for record keeping and
 modelling studies, but also to provide real-time indications of the c\mbient condition, including of
 course the hydrogen levels, especially as hazardous levels are approached. A HyWAM will
 output multidimensional data from many sensors, with information on the chemical and physical
 environment, which must be interfaced to the facility control system. KWJ and NLR will conduct
 research into updating the current HyWAM Data Management System to allow better real-time
 integration into facility control systems.

 A user-friendly data management and control interface was developed to simplify monitoring.
 The option to bypass PLC-based systems and pass digital signals directly to a PC was
 introduced, enabling real-time data handling and system analysis. The system included a
 customizable UX/UI with features like OLED displays, LED system indicators, system controls,
 and sensor feedback. The data transfer and logging system used binary encoding to ensure
 efficient data relay between sensors and the central platform, contributing to timely alerts and
 streamlined operational oversight.

 Summary of Work Completed and Changes:

 The control interface evolved to a more modular architecture that now supports digital, analog,
 or modular wireless sensor readings. The user experience was significantly enhanced by
 providing both local and remote monitoring options, including flexible user interface platforms
 for release event tracking. These updates offered simplified access to critical data and reduced
 the need for extensive operator intervention.

 Task 3: Field Deployment at Commercial Facility for Unintended Releases

 The improved signal controls and end-user interface from Task 2 will be integrated into a pre-
 commercial HyWAM system. Upon being identified other modifications from the internal NLR
 deployment may be incorporated into the HyWAM developed for a field deployment at a
 commercial hydrogen facility provided by First Element. The deployment will be for up to 60
 days. Instrument performance will be reviewed to ascertain critical design elements for a
 commercial system. In the deployment, First Element will cover electricity and other utility
 (e.g., internet) costs. KWJ will subcontract and collaborate with First Element to collect and
 report data, including reports on any H2 leaks, their relative sizes, locations, etc. for reference to
 HyWAM responses. KWJ will be responsible for the interface with First Element including
 meetings and reports on First Element activities.

 8

 The HyWAM system was deployed in multiple facilities to test its effectiveness in detecting
 unintended hydrogen releases under real-world conditions. The deployment focused on adapting
 the system to the facility's operational layout and specific environmental challenges. Flexible
 modular configurations were introduced to scale the number of sensors based on the facility's
 size, ranging from 8 to 40 sensors grouped as needed. This modular approach ensured the system
 could be tailored to meet the safety needs of both medium and large-scale hydrogen operations.
 Demonstrations included: i) deployment of the HyWAM within an indoor hydrogen facility to
 measure flow patterns to guide sensor placement [7], ii) deployment downstream from cold
 hydrogen releases to support behavior modelling of cold plumes under controlled wind speeds
 [5], and iii) enclosed LH2 releases with controlled air flow patterns [4].

 Field deployments included the calibration of sensors to monitor unintended releases in hard-to-
 access areas. To adapt the system for deployment in Class 1 Division 2 safety zones the unrated
 electronics were kept outside of the hazardous area while the pumped lines (e.g., sample
 collection tubes) were fed into the desired locations. For indoor locations, the tubes were bundled
 together and run up the the wall where they then dispersed to distributed points across the
 ceiling. For outdoor locations, tubes were mounted to existing structures when possible, and
 poles were added to mount additional sample points wherever they were needed.

 Summary of Work Completed and Changes:

 The deployment validated the system’s readiness for commercial use while identifying specific
 challenges, such as optimizing sensor placement to account for airflow dynamics in larger
 facilities. Changes were made to reposition equipment, improving its leak detection accuracy and
 operational consistency across varying environmental conditions. This optimized configuration
 demonstrated the system’s capability for effective field deployment and provided feedback for
 future iterations.

 There were several causes for the delay in the publication of the report. This project initiated
 during the COVID shutdown which impeded work and deployment studies, resulting in
 numerous project extensions. More important, several critical NLR personnel on the project,
 including the technical lead of the project, left NLR during the execution of the project. Finally,
 the main industrial partner is no longer in business.
