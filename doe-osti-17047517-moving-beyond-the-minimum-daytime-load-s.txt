Understanding the Minimum
Daytime Load Screen

 Origin of Load-Based Screens
Introduction
• Interconnection procedures can vary by state and utility.
• In many interconnection procedures, a series of technical screens are defined that allow for expedited interconnection. If
 those screens fail, detailed studies are required.
• The minimum load screen is a part of such an expedited interconnection and load capacity screening process.
• The definition for minimum load screen* can vary slightly across utilities—for example, at what point on the circuit (e.g., line
 section, feeder head) or what time of day the minimum load is computed.
History
• In the early 2000s, the impacts of distribution generation (mainly photovoltaics [PV] at the time) on the grid were still being
 studied and understood.
• To allow for expedited screening at low PV adoption levels, a simple, heuristic-based load screen was developed.
• Per Coddington et al. (2012), in 1999, the California Public Utilities Commission (CA PUC) initiated the process to set new
 rules for devices connecting to its electric power grid. And as a part of the CA PUC Rule 21 reform, the 15% penetration
 threshold was defined. This screen was then adopted by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission’s (FERC’s) Small
 Generator Interconnection Procedures (SGIP) in 2005, as well as by other utilities. Other early load-based screens
 focused on Peak Export and Aggregate Peak Export.
*Note, for solar generation interconnection studies, minimum daytime load is used, to study minimum period during possible periods of solar production.​

Screens Prior to the Minimum Daytime Load
 Screen
SCREEN 1. 15% Penetration Threshold
 This screen was used to identify cases in which the amount of distributed generation (DG) capacity on a line-section exceeded 15% of the line-section
 annual peak load. Today, this screen is referred to as the 15% Peak Load Screen.

Where did the value 15% come from?

 As a general practice, typical minimum load was approximated as 30% of peak annual load; 15% (half of 30%) was chosen as a conservative general
 screening level.

What was the 15% threshold intended to screen?

 The original purpose was to screen for islanding—that is, situations when there could be enough DG capable of sustaining an unintentional island (for
 concerns relating to safety), as well as a “catch-all” rule for other potential grid issues.

 Per Coddington et al. (2012), keeping total DG generation below the line-section’s minimum load would ensure that risks such as unintentional
 islanding, voltage deviations, and protection miscoordination issues remain negligible.

 This heuristic-based screen was highly restrictive. As DG deployment increased, the use of this screen led to the need for more time-consuming,
 detailed studies.

Below are other supplementary metrics that were formulated by Coddington et al. (2012):

SCREEN 2. Peak Export > 10% of line section peak load: For islanding concerns

SCREEN 3. Aggregate Peak Export > 15% of line section peak load: For voltage concerns

For screens (2) and (3), peak export at any given line-section signifies an instantaneous DER penetration exceeding 100%.

Minimum Load and Minimum Daytime Load
 Screen
SCREEN 4. Minimum Daytime Load:
Origin
• This screen was formulated by Michael Coddington et al. (2012) (although it is mentioned that some utilities had already
 begun using the MDL screen by then). The MDL screen is defined as Distributed Generation (DG) penetration level <
 minimum daytime load.
• If data on minimum load is available, and if the technology being evaluated was PV, minimum load during daytime was
 considered (i.e., minimum daytime load) since PV power generation is during the daytime only.
• It was also adopted by FERC SGIP (2013, FERC Order No. 792) and the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers
 standard (IEEE 1547.7-2013), introducing technology-specific time-filtering (absolute minimum load vs. daytime minimum
 load).
• Here, penetration level can mean DG capacity or DG export.
• Minimum load screen (or daytime load for PV) was formulated to more accurately capture the condition that might cause
 adverse impact on the grid.

Note: In subsequent slides, we use the term “MDL (minimum daytime load),” which is applicable if the technology considered is
PV. However, for another technology, “minimum load screen” would be applicable.

Minimum Load and Minimum Daytime Load
 Screen
Today
• The Minimum Load screen is still being used in fast-track or expedited screening processes in some
 interconnection procedures or reference documents. If this fails, detailed studies are required before
 approval.
• With accelerated adoption of DERs, the Minimum screen is starting to fail more often, leading to the need
 for detailed, time-consuming studies.
For example:
• As per the Interstate Renewable Energy Council’s (IREC’s) Model Interconnection Procedures (2023),
 the screen is that the aggregate generation capacity on the line-section must be below 100% of the
 minimum load for all upstream line-sections bounded by automatic sectionalizing devices.
• According to the Integrated Planning Guidance Document (2023), the joint utilities of New York (New
 York State Electric and Gas [NYSEG], Rochester Gas and Electric [RG&E], Orange and Rockland
 Utilities [ORU], ConEdison, Central Hudson) and Rhode Island report an anti-islanding screen, is defined
 as 67% of the light load at the feeder head.

Impacts Assessed by the Minimum Daytime
 Load Screen
• Voltage
 ◦ Voltage rise concerns: Can cause voltages to rise above the American National Standards Institute (ANSI)
 limits (ANSI C84.1)
 ◦ Impact on existing voltage regulation equipment (load tap chargers, capacitor banks, voltage regulators):
 Increase number of operations
 ◦ Voltage flicker.
• Overloading of thermal equipment (e.g., transformers, lines):
 ◦ If reverse power exceeds the equipment rating.
• Reverse power flow (at distribution transformer, at feeder, at substation)
• Unintentional islanding:
 ◦ Poses a safety risk to personnel and equipment.
• Protection:
 ◦ Could impact protection coordination settings
 ◦ Could possibly desensitize relays
 ◦ Overvoltage during fault.

Discussion on Anti-Islanding

 Discussion on Anti-Islanding

• NLR reviewed anti-islanding concepts because the MDL screen historically was used as a screen to
 prevent unintentional islands; understanding the low actual risk of sustained islanding with modern inverters
 enables a shift toward less-restrictive, data-driven interconnection metrics.
• In the distribution system, islanding is a condition in which some part of a distribution circuit remains
 energized entirely by local DERs while being disconnected from the main grid.
• Walling et al. (2025) provides a detailed discussion on the topic of anti-islanding. For an island to be
 sustained, the following conditions must be fulfilled:
 ◦ Opening of the circuit
 ◦ Real and reactive power demand of all local load and grid elements matched
 ◦ No disruption of the island (e.g., by operation of protection systems or DER islanding detection
 algorithms).
• According to Walling et al. (2025), the probability that these matched conditions will persist long enough to
 create an unsafe, sustained island is negligible, estimated to occur once within a 3,800- to 10,000-year time
 frame. Hence, it is incorrect to screen solely on real power.

                                                                                             National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   13

 Anti-Islanding Screening and Provisions

IEEE 1547.7-2018 highlights the conditions under which the DER should cease to operate.
• Upon the formation of an unintentional island, the DER shall detect the condition, cease
 energizing the Area Electric Power System (EPS), and trip within 2 seconds.
 ◦ It doesn’t require active anti-islanding schemes but has a strict threshold, which has
 to meet UL1741 certifications and usually requires active anti-islanding schemes.
• Momentary Cessation: DERs are required to stop the injection of power into the grid
 when the voltage or frequency goes above or below defined thresholds (trip points) for
 defined periods of time.
 ◦ For example, for category III inverters, a DER island will not persist longer than 0.16
 second if the frequency goes above 62 Hz or below 56.5 Hz. Additionally, an island will
 not persist longer than 0.083 second if the voltage goes above 110% or below 50% of
 nominal.

                                                                                    National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   14

 Anti-Islanding Screening and Provisions

Ellis and Ropp (2012) and Ropp et al. (2018) discuss Sandia National Laboratories screening
and risks for anti-islanding. According to Bower and Ropp (2002), inverters can have the
following types of islanding detection methods:
• Passive detection methods monitor voltage and frequency and/or their characteristics and
 prompt the inverter to stop operation when deviations from normal grid conditions are
 detected.
• Active detection techniques inject intentional disturbances into the circuit and observe the
 resulting response to verify the presence of the utility grid’s stable voltage, frequency, and
 impedance.

Anti-Islanding Screening and Provisions

Today, some utilities (e.g., Pacific Gas and Electric Company [PG&E]) have anti-islanding
screens that take into account the generation mix and different anti-islanding mechanisms.
For example:
• If there is a mix of rotating generators without direct transfer trip (DTT) and inverters from
 different manufacturers deploying different anti-islanding mechanisms, then there is a
 possibility that anti-islanding mechanisms could fail.
 ◦ Even with that, for an island to be sustained, voltage and frequency need to continue
 to operate within set limits. Combination of Group 1 (Sandia Frequency Shift) and 2A
 (Sandia Frequency Shift with saturation limits) were discovered to be effective even
 when used in combination.
• Traditionally, information on anti-islanding mechanisms has not been available from inverter
 manufacturers. There are ongoing industry-wide efforts to get manufacturers to report anti-
 islanding mechanisms and develop more relevant and reliable screens based on the
 percentage mix of rotating generators without DTT, inverters with different manufacturers
 groups 1, 2A, etc. type of anti-islanding mechanisms.

Beyond the Minimum Daytime
Load Screen

 Why We Could Move Beyond the MDL
 Screen Today
• The minimum daytime load is a screen based on levels of DER
 adoption/penetration and was intended to expedite connection of DER at a
 time when impacts of PV or other DERs were still being understood.
• Availability of grid data, as well as consumption data, was not as common as
 today and as levels of DERs increased, the MDL screen started failing.
• Today, with more data available and an increasing level of automation, each
 of the identified impact areas could be assessed with more accurate metrics.

                                                                  National Laboratory of the Rockies   |   18

Why We Could Move Beyond the MDL
 Screen Today
• The widespread deployment of AMI and supervisory control and data
  acquisition (SCADA) systems provides actual, granular data, eliminating the
  need to rely on conservative “worst-case” estimates.
• Modern, UL 1741-certified smart inverters manage many of the historic risks
  (like unintentional islanding and voltage fluctuations) that the MDL screen
  originally sought to prevent.
• Automated power flow simulations enable precise assessments of grid
  conditions rather than static, one-size-fits-all limits.
• Leveraging these modern tools, targeted, data-driven metrics could safely
  assess interconnection requests while reducing unnecessary study delays.

 Data- and Model-Based Metrics

• These assessment categories are meant to capture the various impact areas.
  By evaluating metrics in each assessment category, the utility could ensure the
  critical grid impacts of DER are captured.
• Metrics that lie in each of these assessment categories are based on detailed
  models and utility databases and could be adopted depending on data
  availability.
• These model-based metrics could be programmed into automated
  interconnection workflows, preventing the need for manual, time-consuming
  supplemental reviews.
• The data table on the previous slide (Abraham et al. 2026) outlines detailed
  metrics for each of the assessment categories.

 What Are the Solutions if Subsequent
 Studies Fail?
• Reduced DER size: Downsizing the proposed system capacity to fit within
  existing grid constraints
• Export limitations: Utilizing storage or controls to ensure power exported to the
  grid does not exceed a set threshold even if the generation system capacity is
  larger
• Smart Inverter functions: Utilizing smart inverter controls (e.g., Volt-var, volt-
  watt) to manage localized voltage rise
• Flexible interconnection: Utilizing time-varying exports
• System Upgrades: Traditional grid upgrades (e.g., upgrading conductors or
  transformers).

Summary

 Summary and Key Takeaways

• Evolution of Screening: The MDL screen was a necessary, conservative
  heuristic during early DER adoption, but it is now overly restrictive and triggers
  unnecessary detailed studies.
• Reassessing Islanding Risks: Modern, UL 1741-certified smart inverters
  drastically reduce the risk of sustained unintentional islands, minimizing the
  need for strict, real-power-only load screens.
• Pivoting to Model-Based Metrics: Advances in grid data and automation
  allow utilities to replace heuristic screens with precise, model-based
  assessments (e.g., thermal, voltage, and protection metrics).
• Transitioning beyond the MDL screen to these automated, granular metrics
  could safely expedite DER deployment, increase grid capacity, and reduce
  interconnection backlogs.
