Wandering Exponents and the Free Energy of the High-Dimensional Elastic Polymer
Gérard Ben Arous, Pax Kivimae

Abstract
. We study the behavior of the elastic polymer, a model of a directed polymer in a continuous Gaussian random environment that is independent in time and correlated in space, as the dimension of the environment is taken to infinity. We give an explicit asymptotic formula for the free energy, which is given in terms of the distribution of the inner product of two sampled configurations, which we also obtain an implicit formula for. From this, we provide an explicit characterization of both the low- and high-temperature phases of this model in terms of the spatial correlation function of the environment. We find asymptotics for the wandering exponent when the spatial correlation function is either an exponential or a power-law decay. Our results show that when the correlations are either suitably weak or short ranged, the model is asymptotically diffusive. On the other hand, for suitably strong long ranged correlations, the model is asymptotically superdiffusive. Moreover, we show that this transition coincides exactly with another transition where the model goes from being one-step replica symmetry breaking to full-step replica symmetry breaking. This rigorously confirms many of the findings of Mézard and Parisi [ 56 ] in the physics literature.



1.
Introduction

Understanding the behavior of a directed polymer in a random potential is a classical question in the theory of disordered systems, which has been actively studied by both mathematicians and physicists for decades. At its core, the system consists of a random walk (i.e., the polymer) subjected to an independent random potential function, which we call the environment. This environment encourages the walk to move toward the environment’s minima, a set that is constantly changing in time. However, this preference is often at odds with the behavior of the original random walk. This leads to a competition between the environment, which seeks to pin the model to rough configurations, and the base path-measure of the random walk, which is often much smoother. The outcome is a random process with a complex behavior that often mixes these two extremes.

One of the most intriguing aspects of these models is a transition in their behavior from the simple diffusive behavior of a simple random walk to a novel superdiffusive behavior as the strength of the environment is increased. This transition can be seen through a number of statistics of the model, as it simultaneously affects the quenched (typical) free energy of the model, the decay of the polymer’s spatial covariance, and the amount that distinct samples localize on the same path. For an introduction to the mathematical theory of random polymers, one may consult

The purpose of this work is to pursue these results rigorously. In particular, we will compute the value of the quenched free energy for this model in the high-dimensional continuum limit. We give this in terms of a Parisi-type variational problem, which allows us to determine both the overlap distribution of two samples from the model and the mean radius of the path as the unique solution to this variational problem. Moreover, we find a simple analytic description for the low-temperature phase of the model, which is characterized by a transition from replica symmetry to replica symmetry breaking in the overlap distribution (see Definition

We then move on to another well-studied aspect in the theory of directed polymers, namely the wandering exponent and the phenomenon of superdiffusivity. Roughly, it is believed that if the disorder is suitably strong, the polymer will drift farther to reach a more favorable environment, which will lead the path to move far more quickly than Brownian motion. In particular, if we denote the polymer by
\bm{u}(x)
and the Gibbs measure by
\langle*\rangle

\eta>0
such that correlations of
\bm{u}(x)
scale as

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(x)-\bm{u}(y)\|^{2}\rangle\approx|x-y|^{2\eta},

when the distance
|x-y|
is large.

This exponent

and

The exponent

is called the wandering exponent. Note that when
\bm{u}
is Brownian motion one has that
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, and we will say that a polymer with
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
is diffusive, while we will call a polymer with
\eta>\frac{1}{2}
is superdiffusive. In general, it is believed that when the model is diffusive, it will converge to Brownian motion after rescaling, as was shown by Comets and Yoshida
[
17
]
in a discrete model, while in the superdiffusive case, this will fail.

We study this exponent by first providing a general formula for the mean-squared displacement of the model in terms of the overlap distribution (Theorem
1.7
). Taking the massless limit, we then show that in the entire high temperature (i.e., weak disorder) regime, the model is asymptotically diffusive. We then compute the exact wandering exponent in the low temperature regime when the spatial decay is given by either exponential or power-law decay.

In the exponential case, we find that the model is always diffusive. However, in the case of the power-law, we find a transition occurs based on the exponent of the power. For large exponents (i.e. short-ranged models), the model is diffusive, whereas for small exponents (i.e. long-range models), the model is superdiffusive with a wandering exponent that is sensitive to the exact exponent of the power. Moreover, we study the behavior of the overlap distribution in these models, and show that this dichotomy in the power-law case exactly coincides with a transition in the behavior of the overlap distribution. When the model is diffusive, the model is one-replica symmetry breaking (1RSB), whereas in the superdiffusive case the model is full replica symmetry breaking (FRSB). We will define these terms in Definitions
1.8
and
1.23

While this picture was first found in the physics literature by Mézard and Parisi
[
56
]
, some aspects of this transition have been shown in the mathematical polymer community. For a related model, Lacoin
[
49

showed a lower bound for the wandering exponent in the case of long-ranged power-law correlations at any fixed

[

N\geq 3
, Lacoin
[
49
]
has shown that there is always a non-zero transition temperature. We confirm these results asymptotically, and moreover give an explicit formula for the asymptotic quenched free energy in these cases.

1.1.
Model

We now introduce the model we will consider. Choose a length parameter
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Our configuration space will be the space of functions
\bm{u}:\Lambda_{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where

(1.2)

\Lambda_{L}=\{0,L^{-1/2},2L^{-1/2},\cdots,(L-1)L^{-1/2}\}.

We will denote the set of such functions as
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L}}
. In what follows, we will consider functions on this set to have periodic boundary conditions so that
\bm{u}(L^{1/2})=\bm{u}(0)
. We note that
\Lambda_{L}
contains precisely
L
points, evenly spaced apart by a distance of
L^{-1/2}
. Thus, roughly as
L\to\infty
, the distance between any two points tends to zero, while the total length is
L^{1/2}
, which diverges to
\infty
.

Next, we introduce the base path-measure of our polymer on
(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L}}
. Roughly, this will be a discretization of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. For this, we fix a mass parameter
\mu>0
, an elastic strength parameter
t>0
, and an inverse temperature
\beta>0
. For our base path, we consider the periodic random walk on
\Lambda_{L}
given by

(1.3)

\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\mu,N,L}(d\bm{u})=\frac{1}{\tilde{Z}_{N,L}}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\left(\mu\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}-t(\Delta_{L}\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(x))\right)L^{-1/2}\right)d\bm{u},

where
\Delta_{L}f(x)=L(f(x+L^{-1/2})+f(x-L^{-1/2})-2f(x))
is the discrete Laplacian on
\Lambda_{L}
, and
\tilde{Z}_{N,L}
is a normalization constant given by

(1.4)

\tilde{Z}_{N,L}=(\left(2\pi/\beta\right)^{L}L^{L/2}\det(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})^{-1})^{N/2}.

The component functions
\bm{u}_{i}(x)
are an i.i.d. family of centered stationary Gaussian random walks on
\Lambda_{L}
. It is not difficult to show that this process
\bm{u}_{i}(x)
converges in law to stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process as one takes
L\to\infty
, and that the process
\bm{u}_{i}(x)-\bm{u}_{i}(0)
converges in law to a Brownian motion in the limit
\mu\to 0
(infact, these claims routinely follow from the more general Corollaries
4.3
and
1.13
below).

To define our polymer measure, we will fix a random smooth function
V_{N}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define the polymer Gibbs measure as

(1.5)

G_{\beta,N,L}(d\bm{u})=\frac{1}{Z_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)}\exp\left(-\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\beta V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))L^{-1/4}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\mu,N,L}(d\bm{u}),

where
(V_{N,x})_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}
are family of i.i.d copies of
V_{N}
, and
Z_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)
is the partition function of the polymer. We will use the standard notations of
\langle f(\bm{u})\rangle
and
\langle f(\bm{u},\bm{u}^{\prime})\rangle
for the expectations of a function
f
with respect to
G_{\beta,N}
and
G_{\beta,N}^{\otimes 2}
, respectively. We will also adopt this notation elsewhere in the document when the measure is clear.

We will consider the case where
V_{N}
is an isotropic centered Gaussian function. The law of
V_{N}
is then specified by its covariance, and for this, we will assume that there is some fixed function
B
such that

(1.6)

\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(u)V_{N}(u^{\prime})]=NB(\|u-u^{\prime}\|^{2}_{N}),

where
\|u\|_{N}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}^{2}
. It is a result of Schoenberg
[
68
]
that for a fixed function
B
, such a function
V_{N}
exists for each
N\geq 1
if and only if
B
admits a representation of the form

(1.7)

B(x)=c_{0}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(-\lambda^{2}x)\nu(d\lambda),

where
\nu
is a finite non-negative measure on
(0,\infty)
and
c_{0}\geq 0
. For convenience, we will assume that there is
\epsilon>0
such that
\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp(\lambda^{2}\epsilon)\nu(d\lambda)<\infty
and that
c_{0}=0
.

We note that this Gibbs measure can alternatively be written as

(1.8)

G_{\beta,N,L}(d\bm{u})=\frac{1}{\hat{Z}_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)}\exp\left(-\beta\cal{H}_{N,L}(\bm{u})\right)d\bm{u},

where
\cal{H}_{N,L}:(\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{L}})^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
is the Hamiltonian

(1.9)

\cal{H}_{N,L}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\left(\mu\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}-t(\Delta_{L}\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(x))\right)L^{-1/2}+\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))L^{-1/4},

and
\hat{Z}_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)
is a normalization factor. This presentation is more in line with the one used in the physics literature
[
56
,
54
,
29
,
32
]
.

In particular, heuristically our Hamiltonian can be viewed as a discretization of the continuum Hamiltonian

(1.10)

\cal{H}(\bm{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mu\|\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}+t\|\nabla\bm{u}(x)\|^{2}\right)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}V_{N}(x,\bm{u}(x))dx,

where
V_{N}(x,\bm{u}(x))
is a centered Gaussian function with covariance

(1.11)

\mathbb{E}[V_{N}(x,\bm{u})V_{N}(y,\bm{u}^{\prime})]=\delta_{D}(x-x^{\prime})B(\|\bm{u}-\bm{u}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{N}),

where
\delta_{D}
is the
Dirac

\delta
-function. In particular, we can view the first term of
\cal{H}_{N,L}(\bm{u})
as a Riemann sum for the first integral in (
1.10
) with spacing
\Delta x=L^{-1/2}
. The second term of
\cal{H}_{N,L}(\bm{u})
can similarly be thought of as a Riemann sum for the second integral in (
1.10
), with the scaling factor of
L^{-1/4}=L^{-1/2}L^{1/4}
coming from the fact that
V_{N,x}(\bm{u})
needs to be rescaled by
L^{-1/4}
, to account for the different normalization conventions between the Dirac and Kronecker
\delta
-functions.

Remark 1.1
.

A natural variant of this model is given by changing the base random walk to one still given by (
1.3
), except instead letting
\bm{u}
be periodic (i.e.
\bm{u}(0)=\bm{u}(L^{1/2})
), one takes the boundary conditions
\bm{u}(0)=0
, and simply omits the term
\nabla_{L}f((L-1)L^{-1/2})
from exponential in (
1.3
). We believe the results for this model would be similar, and often identical, to the results we obtain here. However, our methods do not generalize directly to this case. The issue is that in this paper we rely heavily on results for the finite-
L
model from our companion papers
[
8
,
7
]
. Many of the results in
[
8
]
would easily generalize to treat this variant. However, the results in
[
7
]
crucially rely on the underlying model being translation invariance. These results significantly simplify the formulas we obtain in
[

]
, and these simplifications make studying the limit as

1.2.
Results on the Gibbs measure and the Wandering Exponent

We will now give results for a number of statistics of the Gibbs measure in the high-dimensional limit. Our first results will concern the behavior of two statistics of the marginal distribution of the polymer at a fixed site
x\in\Lambda_{L}
. While these two limiting quantities are of interest in their own right, they will also appear naturally in our computation of the mean-squared displacement below. As the model is translation invariant, this marginal is independent of the choice of site
x\in\Lambda_{L}
. However, as the functions
\bm{u}:\Lambda_{L}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
often distinct domains, to state our results, it will be useful to define the function
[x]_{L}
which rounds
x
into
\Lambda_{L}
. Explicitly,

(1.12)

[x]_{L}=L^{-1/2}([L^{1/2}x]\mod L),

where
[x]
is the floor of
x
. If
x\in\Lambda_{L}
then
x=[x]_{L}
, but for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have
[x]_{L}\in\Lambda_{L}
.

With this notation, our first result on the Gibbs measure at any given site
x\in\Lambda
, the value of
\|\bm{u}(x)\|_{N}
concentrates on some fixed value.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists
q_{c}\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(1.13)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\langle\left|\|\bm{u}([x]_{L})\|^{2}_{N}-q_{c}\right|\right\rangle=0.

Thus in the limit, the model is essentially supported on a product of thin annuli of radius
\sqrt{Nq_{c}}
. Our next result studies the the distribution of the overlap,
(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}^{\prime}(x))_{N}
, when averaged over the choice of disordered
\{V_{N,x}\}
(i.e. the annealed overlap distribution). We show that this converges in the limit to a fixed probability measure. For this, let
P([0,q))
denote the set of probability measures on
[0,q]
whose support does not contain
q
.

Theorem 1.3
.

There exists
\zeta_{c}\in P([0,q_{c}))
, such that for any bounded continuous function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and any choice of
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

(1.14)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\langle f((\bm{u}([x]_{L}),\bm{u}^{\prime}([x]_{L}))_{N})\rangle=\int_{0}^{q_{c}}f\left(r\right)\zeta_{c}(dr).

The pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
is thus seen to be an important invariant of the Gibbs measure. Our next result shows that this pair can also be given implicitly as the solution to two fixed equations.

Theorem 1.4

(Characterization of The Pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
)
.

The pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
is the unique solution to the following equations:

(1.15)

\beta\int_{0}^{q}\zeta([0,u])du=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}},

(1.16)

\zeta\left(\big\{s\in[0,q]:f_{\beta,q}(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<q}f_{\beta,q}(s^{\prime})\big\}\right)=1,

where for
s\in[0,q)
, we define

(1.17)

F_{\beta,q}(s)=-2B^{\prime}(2(q-s))-\int_{0}^{s}\frac{2du}{\beta^{3}\delta(u)^{3}t},\;\;f_{\beta,q}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}F_{\beta,q}(u)du,

(1.18)

\delta(s)=\int^{q}_{s}\zeta([0,s])ds.

As this pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
will play an important role below, we will give it a name.

Definition 1.5

(Parisi Pair)
.

We will call the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
from Theorems
1.2
-
1.4
as the Parisi pair. We will call the measure
\zeta_{c}
the Parisi measure.

The Parisi measure
\zeta_{c}
in particular will turn out to be of key importance to understanding the model. Indeed, the overlap distribution is known to be a fundamental object in the theory of disordered systems
[
64
]
.

Our next result will give a formula for the limiting mean-squared displacement of the model. This formula is given in terms of a functional of the Parisi pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
, which we now introduce.

We first define the function

(1.19)

\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)=\frac{e^{-|x|\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{t}}}-1}{2\sqrt{t\mu}}.

This function can be identified in terms of the mean-squared displacement of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process. Namely, if the process
U_{t}
is given by the stationary solution to
dU_{t}=-\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{t}}U_{t}dt+dB_{t}
, where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion, then
\mathbb{E}[(U(x)-U(y))^{2}]=-\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)
. In particular, this function captures the mean-squared displacement of the base random walk in the limit
L\to\infty
. With this function defined, we now define our functional.

Definition 1.6
.

Let
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
be the Parisi pair as in Definition
1.5
. Let
q_{*}=\sup(\text{supp}(\zeta_{c}))<q_{c}
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

(1.20)

H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x)=-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}(q_{c}-q_{*})^{2}t}\right)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(u)t}\right)\frac{4\;du}{\beta^{3}\delta(u)^{3}t},

where here

(1.21)

\delta(s)=\int_{s}^{q}\zeta_{c}([0,u])du.

With this functional, we now give our results on the mean-squared displacement at a fixed non-zero mass.

Theorem 1.7

(Formula for the Mean-Squared Displacement)
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

(1.22)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}([x]_{L})-\bm{u}([y]_{L})\|^{2}_{N}\rangle=H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x-y).

This result allows us to compute the mean-squared displacement in any model where we suitably understand the Parisi pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
.

The simplest case is when
\zeta_{c}
is supported on a single point (i.e., when
\zeta_{c}
is a Dirac mass). To distinguish this case, we will recall the following terminology from the theory of disordered systems
[
55
]
.

Definition 1.8

(RS and RSB)
.

When
\zeta_{c}
is supported at one point, we call the model replica symmetric (RS). If a model is not RS, we call the model replica symmetry breaking (RSB).

The RS phase serves as the high-temperature phase of the model, while the RSB phase is the low-temperature phase. In particular, the Parisi measure is a functional order parameter that detects this phase transition. The importance of this distinction between RS and RSB comes first and foremost from the phenomenon of replica symmetry breaking, famously introduced by Parisi
[
62
,
63
]
to study the Sherrington-Kirkpatrick model, and further elaborated on in the works of Mézard, Parisi, Sourlas, Toulouse, and Virasoro
[
53
,
55
]
. In this picture,
\zeta_{c}
is conjectured to encode deep structural properties of the Gibbs measure. This is because in the RSB phase, the Gibbs measure is expected to decompose into an infinite number of states whose properties are determined by
\zeta_{c}
. One may consult
[
64
]
for a more in-depth review of these general physical predictions, as well as
[
56
]
for predictions specific to the model studied here.

Returning to our study of the mean-squared displacement, in Theorem
1.21
below, we show that one can explicitly find the value of the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
when the model is RS. This will allow us to obtain the following corollary of Theorem
1.7
.

Corollary 1.9

(Formula for the Mean-Squared Displacement: RS Case)
.

If the model is RS, then for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

(1.23)

H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x)=-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\mu\right)+4B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\mu\right).

In particular, in the case of
B=0
, this recovers the fact that the base random walk converges to a collection of i.i.d. Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes in the limit. The second term represents a perturbation to the mean-squared displacement coming from the quenched disorder.

We now move to discuss our applications of Theorem
1.7
to the study of the asymptotics of the wandering exponent. For this, we must first define what we precisely mean by the heuristic definition of the wandering exponent given in (
1.1
).

Note that for the wandering exponent to be non-zero, one must consider models with zero mass (i.e.
\mu=0
). This is because if we have that
\eta>0
, the value of
\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(x)\|_{N}^{2}\rangle
must diverge as
x\to\infty
, which Theorem
1.3
shows does not happen for fixed
\mu>0
. One may also compare this to the fact that the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process has a wandering exponent of
0
, whereas when you send its bias parameter to zero, it converges to a Brownian motion, which has a wandering exponent
\frac{1}{2}
.

Definition 1.10

(The Asymptotic Wandering Exponent in The High-Dimensional Limit)
.

For a fixed choice of
\beta,t>0
and disorder function
B
, the polymer has asymptotic wandering exponent
\eta
if

(1.24)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x)\asymp|x|^{2\eta},

where here and elsewhere the notation
f(x)\asymp g(x)
means that there are
C,c>0
such that for all
|x|\geq C
,
cg(x)\leq f(x)\leq Cg(x)
.

This order of limits taken here (i.e.,
N\to\infty
, then
L\to\infty
, then
\mu\to 0
) is the order originally introduced by Mézard and Parisi
[
56
]
. In particular, by taking the limit in
N
first, we simultaneously avoid having to show the existence of a wandering exponent for any finite
N
, as well as dealing with convergence to a continuum model. However, we do believe that the order in which these limits are taken does not affect the final result. Moreover, we note that the sense in which we define the wandering exponent is quite strong, in the sense that we do not allow any sub-polynomial terms in
|x|
. With this definition given, we can now define what we mean by the terms diffusive and superdiffusive.

Definition 1.11

(Diffusivity and Superdiffusivity)
.

Assume that the polymer has a wandering exponent of
\eta
. We will say the model is diffusive if
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
. We will say the model is superdiffusive if
\eta>\frac{1}{2}
.

Equipped with Theorem
1.7
, we see that to calculate the asymptotic wandering exponents, we only need to have careful control over the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
as
\mu\to 0
. As before, the case where our results are clearest is the RS case. We do not define the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
in the limit
\mu\to 0
, so instead we will take the following definition.

Definition 1.12

(Massless RS)
.

Consider the model at some fixed
(t,\beta)
. We will say that the massless model is RS at
(t,\beta)
if the massive model is RS at
(\mu,t,\beta)
for all sufficiently small
\mu>0
. Otherwise we will say that the massless model is RSB.

Our next results show that in the massless RS phase, the wandering exponent is always
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
(i.e., the model is diffusive). In fact, we do not even need to take the limit
x\to\infty
in this case, and we find that the limiting correlation function is identical to that of Brownian motion.

Corollary 1.13

(High Temperature Correlations)
.

Fix
(\beta,t)
, and assume that the massless model is
RS
. Then for any choice of
x\in\mathbb{R}
we have that

(1.25)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x)=\frac{|x|}{\beta}.

In particular, the wandering exponent is
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
and the model is diffusive.

In particular, we see in this case that the limiting mean-squared displacement in the massless RS case is independent of
B
, and coincides with that of the limiting base random walk (i.e., Brownian motion).

More complex phenomena occur in the RSB phase. For this, we will establish results for the cases of
B
which are most well studied
[
56
,
50
,
22
,
65
]
.

Definition 1.14
.

Choose some
g,a,\gamma>0
. The case of an exponential correlator is given by

(1.26)

B(u)=ge^{-au},

and the case of a power-law correlator by

(1.27)

B(u)=g(a+u)^{-\gamma}.

To show that the model is well-defined for these choices of correlators, one may use the result of Schoenberg
[
68
]
recalled above, which states that
V_{N}
exists for all
N\geq 1
if and only if it can be written in the form (
1.7
). This is obvious for
B(u)=ge^{-au}
, in the case of
B(u)=g(a+x)^{-\gamma}
this follows from the integral relation
(1+u)^{-\gamma}=\frac{2}{\Gamma(\gamma)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda^{2}u}e^{-\lambda^{2}}\lambda^{2\gamma-1}d\lambda
. By applying a scaling transformation
\bm{u}\to\lambda\bm{u}
, it suffices to consider the case of
a=1
. Moreover, we may also assume that
g=1
by shifting the structure constants as
(\mu,t,\beta)\mapsto(\mu/g,t/g,\beta g)
.

Our first result gives the wandering exponent when
B
is a power-law correlator. In this case, we find that the model experiences a transition in terms of the exponent
\gamma
. When this exponent is above one (i.e., the field is short-ranged), the model is always asymptotically diffusive. However, when the exponent is below this threshold (i.e., the field is long-ranged), the model becomes superdiffusive with a wandering exponent that is sensitive to the exact exponent of the power law.

Theorem 1.15

(Power-law Correlations)
.

Fix
(t,\beta)
and assume that
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
. If
\gamma\geq 1
, we have that
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, so that the model is diffusive. However, if
\gamma<1
, then
\eta=\frac{3}{2(\gamma+2)}>\frac{1}{2}
. In particular, the model is superdiffusive.

As mentioned above, this result complements the lower bound produced by Lacoin
[
49
]
for fixed
N
, which shows in a related model that
\eta\geq\frac{3}{2(\gamma+2)}
for the same choice of
B
if
N\geq 2
.

Our next result shows that if we consider the model with exponential correlations, then the model is always asymptotically diffusive.

Theorem 1.16

(Exponential Correlations)
.

Fix
(t,\beta)
and assume that
B(x)=e^{-x}
. Then
\eta=\frac{1}{2}
, so that the model is diffusive.

Now we note that comparing these results to Corollary
1.13
, we first see that in the short-range cases (Theorem
1.15
with
\gamma\geq 1
and Theorem
1.16
), the wandering exponent does not distinguish between the RS and RSB phases for these models, and so the model is always diffusive. On the other hand, in the long-range case (Theorem
1.15
with
\gamma<1
), the model is never diffusive at any temperature. This is consistent with Corollary
1.13
, as we will see that in the long-range case, the massless model is always RSB for any temperature, so that Corollary
1.13
never applies. In fact, there is a surprisingly simple condition that determines when this is the case.

Theorem 1.17
.

The massless model is RSB for all
\beta>0
if and only if

(1.28)

\limsup_{x\to\infty}B^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{3}=\infty.

Otherwise, it is RS for sufficiently small
\beta
and RSB for sufficiently large
\beta
.

It is easily verified that
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
satisfies (
1.28
) if and only if
\gamma<1
. Moreover, the choice
B(x)=e^{-x}
does not satisfy (
1.28
).

To show these results, we will need to understand the behavior of
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
, which we do via Theorem
1.4
. The equations in Theorem
1.4
arise as the critical points of a certain functional. In particular,
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
will appear as the unique critical point for this functional, which is how we will obtain Theorem
1.4
. This functional appears naturally in the study of the quenched free energy of the model. Infact, we will show that the limiting quenched free energy of this model is given by this functional evaluated on the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
. As such, we now focus on the analysis of the model’s quenched free energy.

1.3.
Results for the Quenched Free Energy

Our next series of results will involve the quenched free energy of this model. For this, we now define the quenched free energy as

(1.29)

F_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)=\log Z_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t),

where
Z_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)
is the partition function of the model given by

(1.30)

Z_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)=\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L}}}\exp\left(-\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\beta V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))L^{-1/4}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\mu,N,L}(d\bm{u}).

To give our computation of
F_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)
, we will need to introduce a Parisi-type functional.

Definition 1.18

(The Parisi Functional)
.

For
q\in(0,\infty)
and
\zeta\in P([0,q))
let
\delta
denote the function defined in (
1.18
). We define the functional

(1.31)

\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\frac{du}{\beta t\delta(u)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\beta t(q-q_{*})}-2\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{q}\zeta([0,u])B^{\prime}(2(q-u))du-\beta\mu q+\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}\right),

where here
q_{*}\in[0,q]
is any point such that
\zeta([0,q_{*}])=1
. It is easily verified that
\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)
is independent of the choice of
q_{*}
.

With this definition, we have the following asymptotic formula for the quenched free energy.

Theorem 1.19

(Computation of The Asymptotic Free Energy)
.

We have that

(1.32)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}L^{-1/2}N^{-1}F_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t)=\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\left(\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)\right),

both almost surely and in expectation.

Moreover, we show that the optimization problem on the right is achieved at a unique critical point, which is infact the Parisi pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
.

Theorem 1.20

(Critical Point Characterization of
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
)
.

We have that

(1.33)

\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\left(\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)\right)=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q_{c},\zeta_{c})

where
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
is the unique critical point of the functional
\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)
.

We will show that the critical points equations for the functional
\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)
can be simplified to (
1.16
)-(
1.17
). In particular, Theorem
1.4
is essentially a more explicit version Theorem
1.20
.

1.4.
Results on the Replica Symmetric Phase

Next we present some results on the RS phase. The first characterizes when the model is RS based on an explicit variational problem in one variable, and in addition gives us a formula for
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
when the model is RS.

Theorem 1.21

(Characterization of the Replica Symmetric Phase)
.

The model is RS if and only if

(1.34)

\sup_{0<s<\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}}g_{\beta,\mu}(s)=g_{\beta,\mu}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}\right),

where here

(1.35)

g_{\beta,\mu}(s)=\beta^{2}B\left(\frac{2s}{\beta}\right)-\frac{1}{st}-s\left(2\beta B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)+\mu\right).

When the model is RS, its parameters are given by

(1.36)

(q_{c},\zeta_{c})=\left(\delta_{q_{*}},q_{*}+\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\;\;\;\;q_{*}=-B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}.

and its limiting quenched free energy is given by

(1.37)

\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(B(0)-B\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\right).

We will next give some simpler results that allow us to more easily check whether the model is RS or RSB. They are based on the Larkin equation:

(1.38)

B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}=1.

While the origin of this equation is in physics literature, its present appearance may be explained in terms of the function
g_{\beta,\mu}
occurring in Theorem
1.21
. In particular, observe for (
1.34
) to hold, it is necessary that
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}
is a local maximum of
g_{\beta,\mu}
. One may check that
g_{\beta,\mu}^{\prime}(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}})=0
, and routine computation gives that

(1.39)

g_{\beta,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)=4B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)-\frac{2\sqrt{\mu^{3}t^{3}}}{t}.

In particular, by rearranging the equation
g_{\beta,\mu}^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)>0
, we see that the model must be RSB if

(1.40)

B^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\frac{2}{\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}>1.

The boundary line for this condition gives (
1.38
). Remarkably, this local condition is often enough to guarantee global maximization, which controls when the model is RS. The following result gives a sufficient condition for this to be true, and so a partial characterization of the RS phase.

Corollary 1.22
.

Fix the choice of
t
and
\beta
, and consider (
1.38
) as an equation in
\mu
.

(1)

If (
1.38
) has no solutions, then the model is RS for all choices of
\mu
.

(2)

If (
1.38
) has one or more solutions, we will call the largest solution
\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
. Then if
\mu\geq\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
the model is RS.

When it exists, we will call
\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
the Larkin mass. This quantity is not only important to the RS behavior of the model, but also to the topological complexity of the model. In particular, the zero-temperature Larkin mass (i.e.
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
) is also the mass for which the Hamiltonian experiences “topological trivialization”
[
31
]
. This was predicted in the works of Fyodorov, and Le Doussal
[
32
,
33
]
, and made rigorous in the case of finite
L
by the work of Bourgade, McKenna, and the first author
[
6
]
and Xu and Zeng
[
77
]
. Moreover, the Larkin mass is believed to be related to the critical parameter in the pinning-depinning transition (see
[
11
]
). However, no rigorous results in this direction appear to be known.

1.5.
Results on the Replica Symmetry Breaking Phase

Having treated the RS phases, we now give some results on the RSB phase. Inside the RSB phase, identification of the Parisi measure is an important, though often difficult, problem. While a explicit formula for the Pairi measure in general is not known even in the
L=1
case, we are able to obtain some significantly simplifications to critical point equations in Theorem
1.4
under certain conditions on
B
.

For this, we need to recall another definition, based on
\zeta_{c}
, which can be used to characterize how simple
\zeta_{c}
is, and generalizes the simpler RS/RSB dichotomy.

Definition 1.23

(
k
RSB vs FRSB)
.

When
\zeta_{c}
is supported at precisely
(k+1)
points, the model is called
k
-step replica symmetry breaking (
k
RSB). If
\zeta_{c}
is supported on an infinite number of points, the model is full-step replica symmetry breaking (FRSB).

In this definition, note that being RS is equivalent to being
0
RSB, while if
k\geq 1
, a
k
RSB measure is automatically RSB. The simplest RSB case is 1RSB, with the opposite end being the FRSB case. The difference between these cases has been well studied, and we will return to a discussion of this after giving the remainder of our results.

We will provide here both a sufficient condition on
B
for the Parisi measure to be 1RSB in the entire RSB phase (Theorem
1.24
) as well as a complementary condition on
B
for the Parisi measure to be FRSB in the entire RSB phase (Theorem
1.25
). These conditions suffice to deal with the cases introduced above in Definition
1.14
.

We begin by giving the sufficient condition for the model to be 1RSB. This generalizes the criterion found by Crisanti and Sommer
[
19
]
in the spherical pure
p
-spin glass model.

Theorem 1.24
.

Let us assume that
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/3}
is strictly convex or linear, and that the model is not RS. Then the model is 1RSB. Moreover, if we write the Parisi measure as
\zeta_{c}=m\delta_{q_{0}}+(1-m)\delta_{q_{*}}
, then
(q_{0},q_{*},q_{c},m)
is the unique solution to the following equations:
0<q_{0}<q_{*}<q_{c}
,

(1.41)

\beta(q_{c}-q_{*}+m(q_{*}-q_{0}))=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}},

(1.42)

F(q_{0})=F(q_{*})=\int_{q_{0}}^{q_{*}}F(u)du=0.

where here
F
is the function

(1.43)

F(x)=-2B^{\prime}(2(q_{c}-x))-2q_{0}\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}-\frac{1}{\beta mt}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}+\beta m(q_{0}-x)\right)^{-2}+\frac{\mu}{\beta m},

We now give a similar condition for the model to be FRSB. In this case, our result also gives a more explicit formula for
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
.

Theorem 1.25
.

Let us assume that
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/3}
is strictly concave and fix
\beta,t>0
. If (
1.38
) has a solution and
\mu<\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
then the model is FRSB. Otherwise the model is RS. Moreover, when the model is FRSB, the Parisi pair is given by

(1.44)

\zeta_{c}([0,s])=\begin{cases}\beta^{-1}\left(\frac{4}{t}\right)^{1/3}\frac{-B^{\prime\prime\prime}(2(q_{c}-s))}{3\left(B^{\prime\prime}(2(q_{c}-s))\right)^{4/3}};\quad s\in[q_{0},q_{*})\\
1;\quad s\in[q_{*},q_{c}]\\
0;\quad s\in[0,q_{0})\end{cases},

where
(q_{0},q_{*},q_{c})
is the unique triple with
0\leq q_{0}<q_{*}<q_{c}
which satisfies:

(1.45)

q_{c}-q_{*}=\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}},\;\;\;2B^{\prime\prime}(2(q_{c}-q_{0}))=\sqrt{\mu^{3}t},\;\;-B^{\prime}(2(q_{c}-q_{0}))=\frac{q_{0}}{\mu^{3}t}.

Remark 1.26
.

If one works instead with the one-site model (i.e.,
L=1
), then analogs of these results are also known. In this case, it is known that if
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/2}
is strictly concave, then the model is either RS or FRSB, and if
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/2}
is strictly convex, then the model is either RS or 1RSB.

For the spherical analog of this model (i.e., the spherical mixed spin glass), this follows from Proposition 1.1 of
[
72
]
in the FRSB case and Proposition 2.1 of
[
73
]
in the 1RSB case.
Moreover, in our case, this follows by relating our Euclidean case to the spherical case by fixing
q
. In particular, note that for fixed
q
,
\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)
coincides up to a constant with the Parisi-functional for the mixed spherical spin glass with mixing parameter
r\mapsto B(2q(1-r))
(after rescaling
\zeta
to lie on
[0,1)
instead of
[0,q)
). So taking
q=q_{c}
yields the result.

Finally, we note that Theorem
1.25
and Corollary
1.22
immediately give a complete description of the RS-RSB phase diagram in this case. In particular, the RS-RSB boundary line is given by taking the largest solution to the Larkin equation (
1.38
). However, in the cases covered by Theorem
1.24
, Corollary
1.22
only provides a partial characterization of the RS-RSB boundary and we are not aware of an explicit formula (see Figure
1
).

1.6.
Examples

With these general results stated, we can now apply them to our cases of interest, namely
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
and
B(x)=e^{-x}
. We record the fact that the function
x^{\alpha}
is strictly concave on
(0,\infty)
if and only if
\alpha<1
and strictly convex on
(0,\infty)
if and only if
\alpha>1
. In particular, if
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
then we have that
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/3}
is strictly concave if and only if
\gamma<1
, and is strictly convex if instead one has that
\gamma>1
. Lasting, note that if
\gamma=1
, then
(B^{\prime\prime}(x))^{-1/3}
is linear. In particular, Theorems
1.24
and
1.25
give the following result.

Theorem 1.27
.

Consider the model with
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
. If
\gamma<1
, then the model is always either FRSB or RS. If
\gamma\geq 1
, then the model is always either 1RSB or RS.

Remark 1.28
.

Both the transition in Theorem
1.27
and the one in Theorem
1.15

1.17
shows that the massless model with

in this case.

Corollary 1.30
.

Consider the model
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
for
\gamma<1
at some fixed
(\beta,t)
. The Larkin mass
\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
exists and is given by the unique

(1.46)

2\gamma(\gamma+1)\left(1+\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)^{-\gamma-2}=\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}.

For all

the model is RS, and for all

Moreover, in the FRSB case, the Parisi pair,

, in Theorem

immediately follows from the equations

(1.47)

q_{c}-q_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{0}}{\mu^{\frac{3}{2(2+\gamma)}}}-1\right),\;\;\;q_{0}=c_{1}\mu^{\frac{3(\gamma+1)}{2(2+\gamma)}+3},\;\;\;q_{c}-q_{*}=\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}},

with constants given by
c_{0}=(\frac{2\gamma(\gamma+1)}{\sqrt{t}})^{1/(\gamma+2)}
and
c_{1}=\frac{\gamma t}{c_{0}^{1+\gamma}}
. The measure

(1.48)

\zeta_{c}([0,s])=\frac{\gamma+2}{3\beta}\left(\frac{4}{t\gamma(\gamma+1)}\right)^{1/3}(1+2q_{c}-2s)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{3}},

and otherwise by
\zeta_{c}([0,s])=0
if

\zeta_{c}([0,s])=1
if

.

.

In the critical case where

) and (

.

We now complete this section with three open questions. The first involves the generalization of these results to a wider class of
B
.

Open Problem 1.32
.

Show that Theorems
1.15
and
1.27
hold under weaker assumption that
B(x)\asymp|x|^{-\gamma}
, and show that Theorems
1.16
and
1.29
hold under the weaker assumption that
B(x)\asymp e^{-\lambda x}
.

We believe this result is likely to hold, as in the massless model, the behavior of the model at long distances should only depend on the tail behavior of the correlation function. Our second problem is to relate the model in the massless continuum limit to one introduced by Rovira and Tindel
[
67
]
. In particular, we believe that the following should hold.

Open Problem 1.33
.

Show that after centering at the origin (i.e. taking
\bm{u}(x)-\bm{u}(0)
) our discrete model converges to the continuum polymer model of Rovira and Tindel
[
67
]
(with
d=N
,

and
\beta=\beta
) in the limit
\mu\to 0
and
L\to\infty
(taken in either order).

This result would be of great interest as it could allow one to transfer our results on the limiting wandering exponent to this model. In particular, if one could show that one may interchange the limit in
N
as well, this would allow one to transfer results for the wandering exponent to this model exactly.

Finally, we give the following broader question on the nature of the replica symmetry breaking phase.

Open Problem 1.34
.

Show that when the model is RSB, the Gibbs measure can be asymptotically decomposed into a hierarchy of states as described in Section 6 of
[
56
]
.

This would relate our definition of RSB to the more in-depth picture in the physics literature. We mention that for spin glasses (which is similar to the
L=1
case here), such a rigorous description has been obtained by Jagannath
[
44

1.7.
History and Related Works

The earliest mathematical work on directed polymers is due to Imbrie and Spencer
[
43
]
. In a discrete model with no spatial correlations, they showed that for
N\geq 3
, the polymer remains diffusive for all sufficiently high temperatures. Bolthausen
[
12
]
strengthened this result and showed that a central limit theorem holds under the same conditions. Comets and Yoshida
[
17
]
extended this to show that diffusivity is equivalent to a condition on the partition function. As a consequence of this, they established that for
N\geq 3
, diffusivity holds precisely until some critical temperature, and fails for all temperatures below this. Moreover, for
N\leq 2
, they show that this model is superdiffusive for all temperatures. This result is expected to hold generally for a spatially correlated environment as long as the correlations decay sufficiently quickly.

While these results establish superdiffusivity in the correct regime, they do not compute the exact wandering exponent for the model. In the future diffusive case, the wandering exponent has only been rigorously found in dimension one for a very specific choice of environment
[
70
]
, and otherwise only bounds for
\eta
are known (see
[
49
]
and the references therein). We note that there is a very general bound due to Méjane
[
52
]
, which shows that
\eta\leq 3/4
for essentially any choice of
\beta
,
N
, and
B
(see Proposition 1.9 of
[
49
]
). This matches the bound of
\eta\geq 3/2(2+\gamma)
given by Lacoin
[
49
]
for models with polynomial decay in the limit
\gamma\to 0
.

We also mention that there is another very well-studied exponent associated with polymer models - the fluctuation exponent. This is given roughly by the exponent in
L
at which the variance of the quenched free energy scales. In addition to results on the wandering exponent, Mézard and Parisi
[
56
]
also give conjectures on the value of the fluctuation exponent for the cases of
B
considered above, which we recall in the following open question.

Open Problem 1.35
.

Show that

(1.49)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(N^{-1}F_{N,L}(\beta,\mu,t))\asymp L^{\chi},

with
\chi=0
if either
B(x)=e^{-x}
or
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma\geq 1
, and with
\chi=\frac{3}{\gamma+2}-1
if
B(x)=(1+x)^{-\gamma}
with
\gamma<1
.

They obtain these results from a more general conjecture, which is that the fluctuation exponent is related to the wandering exponent via the relation
\chi=2\eta-1
. If one knew this relation in general, this open question would follow immediately from our results above. However, such a relation is far from being known in general, though see the work of Chatterjee
[
15
]
for some results in this direction.

We now turn to related works in the area of replica symmetry breaking. To begin, we note that the difference in behavior between the 1RSB and FRSB states has previously been studied from a number of angles. It can first be seen in terms of the structure of the Gibbs measure. This is because in the RSB phase, the Gibbs measure is expected to decompose into an infinite number of states. Moreover, these states are expected to be grouped by proximity into a hierarchy, whose structure resembles an infinite ultrametric tree. The structure of this tree is encoded in
\zeta_{c}
, and when the model is 1RSB, this tree is expected to have height one, while it is expected to be of infinite height when
\zeta_{c}
is FRSB
[
56
]
. Indeed, such a decomposition has since been confirmed rigorously in a wide class of one-site (i.e.,
L=1
) spin glass models. In particular, this was shown to hold for a class of generic Ising spin glass models by Talagrand
[
75
]
and in further generality by Jagannath
[
44
]
(based on the resolution of the Parisi ultrametricity conjecture by Panchenko
[
59
]
), as well as in the case of the pure spherical
p
-spin model by Subag
[
71
]
, and finally in a subset of the 1RSB regime in the mixed spherical spin model by Subag, Zeitouni, and the first author
[
9
]
.

This difference can also be understood in terms of algorithmic barriers. In
[
72
]
, Subag showed that for a certain class of FRSB mixed spherical spin glass models, there exists a polynomial-time algorithm that finds near-optimal configurations, whose energy is near the true ground-state energy. Conversely, in the context of both the mixed spherical and Ising spin glass models, it was shown by Huang and Sellke
[
42
,
41
]
that such a near optimal configurations can be found by a “stable” algorithm - a class which includes both gradient descent, Langevin dynamics, and approximate message passing ran for a finite amount of time - if and only if a certain generalization of the FRSB condition holds at zero temperature. These works build on earlier fundamental works of Montanari
[
57
]
, El Alaoui, Montanari and Sellke
[
23
]
, and Gamarnik and Jagannath
[
36
]
, all of whom worked on the same problem, though with a restricted subset of algorithms and models.

A final perspective on the meaning of FRSB comes from the concept of marginal stability. One notion of marginal stability is essentially that a Gibbs measure is marginally stable if the Hessian of the Hamiltonian has eigenvalues near zero when evaluated at a typical point of the Gibbs measure. This can be visualized as saying that the Gibbs measure concentrates on a region where the Hamiltonian is almost flat in some directions, as opposed to something like a collection of deep, well-separated valleys. One may consult
[
58
]
for a more thorough discussion of marginal stability in the context of spin glass models. In
[
69
]
, Sellke showed that in the spherical spin glass model, marginal stability of the low temperature Gibbs measure implies that the model is FRSB at zero temperature, and moreover, they find a stronger condition on the Parisi measure which is sufficient. As it has been conjectured that finding any non-marginal near-optimal configuration is computationally hard
[
4
]
, this result can be viewed as a reason for the above algorithmic results. Heuristically, this connection is due to the fact that non-marginal stability implies that the Gibbs measure should satisfy the Overlap gap property
[
36
,
37
]
, a well-known criterion for algorithmic intractability in random structures.

Our computation of the quenched free energy builds on the computation in the one-site case. Starting with the spherical model, an expression for the quenched free energy was given by Crisanti and Sommers
[
19
]
. A rigorous derivation of these formulas in the even case was obtained by Talagrand
[
73
,
74
]
, building upon an interpolation method used by Guerra to obtain the upper bound in the Ising case
[
40
]
. The general Ising case was later obtained by Panchenko
[
60
]
, with the general spherical case being obtained by Chen
[
16
]
.

Moreover, a number of computations for the quenched free energy of different multi-site models have recently been obtained. Many of these rely on the foundational work of Panchenko
[
61
]
, who computed the quenched free energy of the multi-species Ising spin glass model in the convex case. Following this, Bates and Sohn
[
3
,
2
]
computed the quenched free energy of the multi-species spherical spin glass model, again in the convex case. In the Euclidean case (which is what is actually considered in this paper) an asymptotic formula for the quenched free energy was essentially conjectured by Mézard and Parisi
[
56
,
54
]
, and was further studied in the one-site case by Engel
[
24
]
. The one-site computation was then made rigorous in the note of Klimovsky
[
46

, by employing the computation of the spherical case.

The elastic polymer has also been studied, both in mathematics and physics literature, through the lens of topological complexity. This began with the work of Fyodorov, Le Doussal, Rosso, and Texier
[
30
]
, as well as the works of Fyodorov and Le Doussal
[
32
,
33
]
on the more general case of the elastic manifold. Some of these computations were later made rigorous through the work of Bourgade, McKenna, and the first author
[
6

5

[

[

.

We finally mention that this paper is the third in a series of works. In our previous works
[
8
,
7
]
, we studied the discrete elastic manifold. This is roughly a massive Gaussian free field on a fixed discrete
d
-dimensional graph, accompanied by an i.i.d Gaussian potential function at each site. This recovers the model above in the case of
d=1
up to normalization. In
[

we consider the behavior of the model for a

where the number of points is allowed to diverge.

We have restricted this work to the case of

to further work. This is due in part to the fact that the

, the limiting value of the ground state energy is expected to be both divergent in the continuum limit and sensitive to the exact method of regularization

In addition, recent work on such a random surface model has been undertaken in the mathematics literature. In

Now as

\int_{x}^{y}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{zt}}=\frac{2\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{t}},

we obtain the following corollary from the uniform convergence of
R_{1;L,t}
in Proposition
2.3
.

Corollary 2.4
.

For fixed
x,y,t>0
, we have that

(2.27)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(xI-t\Delta_{L})-\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(yI-t\Delta_{L})\right)=\frac{2\sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{t}}.

Moreover, if one fixes
t>0
and chooses some
\epsilon>0
, all these limits converge uniformly for all choices of
x,y\in[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]
.

With the discussion of our results on the free energy complete, we will complete this section by introducing a couple of functions that will appear in our analysis of the Parisi function in the next section. The first is

(2.28)

R_{2;L,t}(\mu)=-\frac{d}{d\mu}R_{1;L,t}(\mu)=L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}\left((\mu I+t\Delta_{L})^{-2}\right).

Like
R_{1;L,t}
, the function
R_{2;L,t}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is also a decreasing smooth diffeomorphism. Differentiating (
2.23
) suggest the approximation
R_{2;L,t}(\mu)\approx\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}
. For the second function, we note that by differentiating the relation
R_{1;L,t}(K_{L,t}(\mu))=\mu
, we see that

(2.29)

-K_{L,t}^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{1}{R_{2;L,t}(K_{L,t}(\mu))}.

In particular, we see that
-K_{L,t}^{\prime}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is invertible, and so we can define its inverse function as
U_{L,t}
. By inverting the approximation
-K_{L,t}^{\prime}(\mu)\approx\frac{2}{\mu^{3}t}
, we predict that
U_{L,t}(\mu)\approx(\frac{2}{\mu t})^{1/3}
. This motivates the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

For any
\mu,t>0
,

(2.30)

\lim_{L\to\infty}R_{2;L,t}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}},\;\;\;\;\lim_{L\to\infty}U_{L,t}(\mu)=\left(\frac{2}{\mu t}\right)^{1/3}

Moreover, if one fixes
t>0
and chooses some
\epsilon>0
, all these limits converge uniformly for all
\mu\in[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]
.

3.
Results on The Free Energy

In this section, we will prove Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
,
1.4
,
1.19
, and
1.20
, which constitute our main general results on the free energy as well as our characterizations of the Parisi pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
. All of these theorems have finite-
L
analogues, so we are primarily concerned with understanding how
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
and
\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}
behave in the limit as
L\to\infty
.

To explain our method, we note that by applying the uniform approximations in Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
to the terms in
\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\zeta)
, one can easily see that

(3.1)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\zeta)=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta).

Thus to show Theorem
1.19
, we just need to make this convergence uniform in some sense. Moreover, by doing the same to the stationary point equations which characterize the pair
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
in Theorem
2.1
, we obtain the stationary point equations which characterize the pair
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
in Theorem
1.4
. Thus we should expect that
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})\to(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
in some sense.

Our proofs will essentially formalize these heuristics. We will do this in three steps. The first step is to show a result (Lemma
3.1
) which establishes that if
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
converges in an appropriate sense to some limit (possibly along some subsequence of
L
), then the free energy converges as well. The second step is to show that such subsequences exist, which will consist of showing that
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
live in a suitable compact subset of the parameter space. This will be done in Lemmas
3.2
and
3.5
. In the third step, we will show that given such a convergence,
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
must actually converge to
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
. This will involve studying the stationary point equations for
\zeta_{L}
, and exploiting properties of the concave functions
q\mapsto\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\zeta)
.

The first step is the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose there are sequences
q^{L}\in(0,\infty)
and
\zeta^{L}\in P([0,q^{L}))
, and some fixed
q\in(0,\infty)
and
\zeta\in P([0,q))
. Let us assume that as
L\to\infty
, that
q^{L}\to q
and that
\zeta^{L}\to\zeta
in the weak sense as probability measures on
[0,\infty)
. Finally, let us assume that there is some sequence of
q_{L,*}\in(0,q^{L})
such that
\zeta^{L}([0,q_{L,*}])=1
such

(3.2)

\inf_{L}\left(q^{L}-q_{L,*}\right)>0.

Then we have that

(3.3)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\cal{P}^{L}_{\beta}(q^{L},\zeta_{L})=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta).

Proof.

Our conditions imply that
\limsup_{L}q_{L,*}<q
, so we see that for some small
\epsilon>0
, we have that
q_{L,*}<\limsup_{L}q_{L,*}+\epsilon<q^{L}
for sufficiently large
L
. Working with such large
L
for the remainder of the proof, we may define
q_{*}=\limsup_{L}q_{L,*}
, which satisfies
\zeta^{L}([0,q_{*}])=1
and
q_{*}<q^{L}-\epsilon
.

Next, we denote by
\delta_{L}
the function associated to
(q^{L},\zeta^{L})
, reserving the notation
\delta
for the function associated to
(q,\zeta)
. As
\zeta^{L}([0,s])\to\zeta([0,s])
for a.e.
s
, we have that
\delta_{L}(s)\to\delta(s)
point-wise for
s\in[0,q_{*}]
. As each
\delta_{L}
and
\delta
are Lipschitz with constant less than or equal to
1
, we may conclude that
\delta_{L}\to\delta
uniformly on
[0,q_{*}]
. We also note that for all
s\in[0,q_{*}]

(3.4)

\epsilon\leq q^{L}-q_{*}=\int_{q_{*}}^{q^{L}}\zeta([0,s])ds\leq\delta(s)\leq\int_{0}^{q^{L}}\zeta([0,s])ds\leq q^{L}.

Thus for some
C,c>0
and
s\in[0,q_{*}]
we have that
c\leq\delta(s)\leq C
. In particular, employing Proposition
2.3
and the uniform convergence of
\delta_{L}
to
\delta
, we see that

(3.5)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\beta K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u))du=\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\frac{du}{\beta t\delta(u)^{2}}.

A simpler application of Proposition
2.3
shows that

(3.6)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(\beta(q^{L}-q_{*})K_{L,t}(\beta(q^{L}-q_{*}))-\beta\mu q^{L}\right)=\frac{1}{\beta(q-q_{*})t}-\beta\mu q,

and by the dominated convergence theorem, we have that

(3.7)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{0}^{q^{L}}\zeta^{L}([0,u])B^{\prime}(2(q^{L}-u))du=\int_{0}^{q}\zeta([0,u])B^{\prime}(2(q-u))du.

This just leaves the determinant terms, for which we use Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
to compute that

(3.8)

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\bigg(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(K_{L,t}(\beta(q-q_{*}))I-t\Delta_{L})\bigg)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\beta^{2}(q-q_{*})^{2}t^{2}}}=\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2}{\beta(q-q_{*})t}.

Combining all these limits, term by term, completes the proof.
∎

We now proceed to the second step. For this, we need to control
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
, the pair from Theorem
2.1
. The first bound we want is for
q_{L}
. We show that this family must live in a compact subset of

.

Lemma 3.2
.

There are
C,c>0
such that for all
L

(3.10)

c\leq q_{L}\leq C.

Proof.

Our goal will be to find a good upper bound and lower bound for

, which we will show forces
q_{L}
to satisfy the desired bounds.

For this, we first recall the evaluation of
\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}
on a Dirac

(3.11)

\displaystyle\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\delta_{q_{*}})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})+\beta q\left(K_{L,t}(\beta(q-q_{*}))-\mu\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\log\det(K_{L,t}(\beta(q-q_{*}))I-t\Delta_{L})+\beta^{2}\left(B(0)-B(2(q-q_{*}))\right)\bigg).

We trivially have that

(3.13)

\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\zeta)\leq\inf_{q_{*}\in(0,q)}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\delta_{q_{*}}).

Now we define the variable

, so that
(q-q_{*})=\beta^{-1}R_{1;L,t}(s)

Thus, we have obtained the upper bound

(3.16)

\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q,\zeta)\leq\inf_{s\in(K_{L,t}(\beta q),\infty)}\cal{Q}_{\beta,L,q}(s).

Employing Proposition
2.3
and Corollary
2.4
again we see that

(3.17)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\cal{Q}_{\beta,L,q}(s)=\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(s),

where here

\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(s):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta q\left(s-\mu\right)+\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2\sqrt{s}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2\beta B^{\prime}(0)}{\sqrt{st}}\right).

In fact, employing the uniformity of these lemmas, we see that

(3.19)

\limsup_{L\to\infty}\left(\inf_{s\in(K_{L,t}(\beta q),\infty)}\cal{Q}_{\beta,L,q}(s)\right)\leq\inf_{s\in(\beta^{-2}q^{-2}t^{-1},\infty)}\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(s),

Next, we will obtain a simpler lower bound. For this, we note that by Jensen’s inequality

(3.21)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L}}}

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\beta V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))L^{-1/4}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\mu,N,L}(d\bm{u})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{(\mathbb{R}^{N})^{\Lambda_{L}}}\exp\left(-\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{L}}\beta\mathbb{E}V_{N,x}(\bm{u}(x))L^{-1/4}\right)\mathbb{P}_{\beta,\mu,N,L}(d\bm{u})=1,

so that in particular

(3.23)

\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,L}(\beta,t,\mu)\geq 0.

Thus by Theorem

, we must have that

(3.24)

\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q_{L}))}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}(q_{L},\zeta)\geq 0.

Now we will use these bounds on

to obtain our bounds on

(3.25)

\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(\beta^{-2}q^{-2}t^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\frac{1}{\beta qt}-\beta q\mu+\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}-2\beta^{2}qB^{\prime}(0)\right),

and so we see that

(3.26)

\lim_{q\to 0}\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(\beta^{-2}q^{-2}t^{-1})=-\infty.

Combined with (
3.20

. Next, on the point

(3.27)

\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(\beta^{-2}q^{-1}t^{-1})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\beta t}-\beta q\mu+\frac{2\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{t}}-\frac{2q^{1/2}}{t\beta}-2\beta^{2}B^{\prime}(0)q^{-1/2}\right).

This lives in the domain of the infimum as long as

(3.28)

\lim_{q\to\infty}\cal{Q}_{\beta,q}(\beta^{-2}q^{-1}t^{-1})=-\infty.

Combining this with (
3.20
) and (
3.24

C>0
such that
q_{L}\leq C
.
∎

Next, we will bound the quantity
q_{L}-q_{L,*}
away from zero. This is not only a requirement to apply Lemma

, but also if one assumes that
q_{L}
converges to some

actually live in space of probability measures on

(which is compact) for small enough

and large enough

. For this, we will need the following basic inequality.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
A
be any positive semi-definite matrix, and let
K
be the functional inverse of the trace of its resolvent (i.e.
\mathrm{tr}(A+K(u)I)^{-1}=u
). Then for any
x,y>0
we have that

(3.29)

\frac{K(x)-K(x+y)}{y}\geq\frac{K(x+y)}{x}.

Proof.

Note that by Hilbert’s resolvent identity, we have for
z,w>0
that

(3.30)

\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A+zI)^{-1}-\mathrm{tr}(A+wI)^{-1}}{w-z}=\mathrm{tr}(A+zI)^{-1}(A+wI)^{-1}\leq z^{-1}\mathrm{tr}(A+wI)^{-1}.

Now if we take write
z=K(x+y)
and
w=K(x)
, we see that

(3.31)

\frac{y}{K(x)-K(x+y)}=\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A+zI)^{-1}-\mathrm{tr}(A+wI)^{-1}}{w-z}\leq\frac{\mathrm{tr}(A+wI)^{-1}}{z}=\frac{x}{K(x+y)},

which is the reciprocal of (
3.29
).
∎

Note that this lemma applies to the function
K_{L,t}(\mu)
. Moreover, it is easily checked that its result (
3.29
) applies to the continuum analogue,
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}
, as well. The next result will show that one of the critical point equations in Theorem
1.4
suffices to give a uniform lower bound on
q_{L}-q_{L,*}
. We will give this lemma in more generality, though, as it will be useful to treat arbitrary values of

(i.e., not just

Lemma 3.4
.

Let either
K(\mu)=K_{L,t}(\mu)
and
U(\mu)=U_{L,t}(\mu)
for some

K(\mu)=\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}

. Next, fix some

and
\zeta\in P([0,q))

\zeta

(3.32)

\zeta\left(\{s\in[0,q]:f(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<q}f(s^{\prime})\}\right)=1,

where here for

(3.33)

(3.34)

\beta(q-q_{*})\geq\min\left(U(4B^{\prime\prime}(0)),\frac{1}{4B^{\prime\prime}(0)}K\left(\int_{0}^{q}\beta\zeta([0,u])du\right),U\left(\frac{-4B^{\prime}(0)}{q}\right),\frac{\beta q}{2}\right).

We first show that (

\zeta\left(\{s\in[0,q]:F(s)=0\}\right)=1.

\{s\in[0,q]:f(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<q}f(s^{\prime})\}\subseteq\{s\in[0,q]:F(s)=0\}\cup\{0,q\},

so we only need to show that
\zeta(\{0,q\})=0

, we have that
\zeta(\{q\})=0

From here, the proof will be broken into three separate cases.

q_{*}

This assumption implies that there are points in

, we have that

When

, we get the following tighter result, as we have an additional stationary point equation to use.

Lemma 3.5
.

If

be the supremum of the support of

This completes the second step. For the final step, we first want to show that for fixed

as

. For this, we first recall from our companion paper some basic facts about the functional

.

Lemma 3.6

(Corollary II.4.5)
.

Fix
q>0
and
L\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the function
\cal{P}_{\beta}^{L}
is strictly convex on

which is the unique solution to the equation

(3.52)

\zeta\left(\{s\in[0,q]:f(s)=\sup_{0<s^{\prime}<q}f(s^{\prime})\}\right)=1,

where here for

,

Lemma 3.8

By Lemma

\epsilon_{L}=\frac{1}{\beta}\min\left(U_{L,t}(B^{\prime\prime}(0)),\frac{K_{L,t}(\beta q)}{4B^{\prime\prime}(0)},U_{L,t}\left(\frac{-4B^{\prime}(0)}{q}\right),\frac{\beta q}{2}\right).

\epsilon:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\epsilon_{L}=\frac{1}{\beta}\min\left(\left(\frac{2}{B^{\prime\prime}(0)t}\right)^{1/3},\frac{1}{\beta^{2}q^{2}tB^{\prime\prime}(0)},\left(\frac{2q}{-4B^{\prime}(0)t}\right)^{1/3},\frac{\beta q}{2}\right)>0.

Proof of Theorem
1.19
.

We define the functions

(3.66)

\mathscr{P}^{L}(q)=\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}^{L}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)=\cal{P}^{L}_{\beta}(q,\zeta_{q}^{L}),\;\;\;\mathscr{P}(q)=\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q))}\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta_{q}),

where
\zeta_{q}^{L}
and
\zeta_{q}
are as in Lemma
3.8
. By Lemma
3.8
, we have that
\zeta_{q}^{L}\to\zeta_{q}
in the weak sense. Moreover, it is clear from the proof of Lemma
3.8
(or alternatively Lemma
3.4

\epsilon:=\epsilon(q)>0
such that
\zeta_{q}([0,q-\epsilon])=\zeta_{q}^{L}([0,q-\epsilon])=1
for large
L
.
Thus, by employing Lemma
3.1
we see that
\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathscr{P}^{L}(q)=\mathscr{P}(q)

We note that this point-wise convergence implies that

(3.67)

\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\mathscr{P}(q)\leq\liminf_{L\to\infty}\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\mathscr{P}^{L}(q).

Moreover, by Lemma II.4.8, each
\mathscr{P}^{L}
is concave in
q\in(0,\infty)
. In particular,
\mathscr{P}(q)
must also be concave in
q
, and so we have that
\mathscr{P}^{L}\to\mathscr{P}

(3.69)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\mathscr{P}^{L}(q)=\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\mathscr{P}(q),

\mathrm{Var}(\cal{H}_{N,L}(\bm{u}))=L^{1/2}\beta^{2}B(0)

Lemma 3.9

The function

\beta\int_{0}^{q}\zeta_{q}([0,u])du=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}},

\zeta_{q}
is bounded away from

\zeta_{q}([0,m))=0

from the choice of

(3.72)

F(0)=-B^{\prime}(2q)>0,

. Moreover, if we let

\delta(s)=\delta(0)

(3.73)

0=F(m_{0})=-B^{\prime}(2(q-m_{0}))-\frac{m_{0}}{\beta^{3}\delta(0)^{3}t}.

(3.74)

m_{0}=-B^{\prime}(2(q-m_{0}))\beta^{3}\delta(0)^{3}t\geq-B^{\prime}(2q)\beta^{3}\delta(0)^{3}t.

Now using the proof of Lemma

\zeta([0,q-\epsilon_{q}])=1

m\geq m_{0}\geq-B^{\prime}(2q)\beta^{3}(q-\epsilon_{q})^{3}t.

\cal{P}^{L}_{\beta}(q,\zeta^{r})=\cal{P}^{L}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)+r\left(\beta K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(0))-\beta\mu\right),

\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta^{r})=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta)+r\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(0)^{2}t}-\mu\right).

\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(0)^{2}t}-\mu=0,

\mathscr{P}(q)=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta_{q})=\cal{P}_{\beta}(s,\zeta_{q}^{s-q})\geq\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,s))}\cal{P}_{\beta}(s,\zeta)=\mathscr{P}(s).

1.16

that there is

such that

(3.81)

\sup_{q\in(0,\infty)}\mathscr{P}(q)=\sup_{q\in[c,C]}\mathscr{P}(q).

\mathscr{P}(q)=\cal{P}_{\beta}(q,\zeta_{q})=\cal{P}_{\beta}(s,\zeta_{q}^{q-s})\geq\cal{P}_{\beta}(s,\zeta_{s})=\mathscr{P}(s).

However, as
s
is also a maximizer, these are all equalities. Moreover, as the infimizer in

is unique, this implies that for each

, we have that
\zeta_{s}=\zeta_{q}^{s-q}
.

We will show that this contradicts the minimization condition (
3.54
). Indeed, let us fix some
\zeta\in P([0,q))
and consider the functions

and
F_{q+\epsilon}
defined as Lemma

and

and

, while
\delta_{\epsilon}(u)=\delta(0)

(3.83)

(3.84)

\{s\in[0,q):F_{q}(s)=0\}\cap\{s\in[\epsilon,q+\epsilon):F_{q+\epsilon}(s+\epsilon)=0\}=\varnothing.

\zeta_{q}\left(\{s\in[0,q):F_{q}(s)=0\}\right)=1,

\zeta_{q}^{\epsilon}\left(\{s\in[\epsilon,q+\epsilon):F_{q+\epsilon}(s)=0\}\right)=1.

. Then we have that

(3.94)

\lim_{L\to\infty}q_{L}=q_{c},\;\;\;\;\lim_{L\to\infty}\zeta_{L}=\zeta_{c},

where the convergence in the second limit is in the weak sense when all measures are extended to probability measures on
[0,\infty)
.

4.
Results on The Wandering Exponent

We will now show our results involving the mean-squared displacement of the Gibbs measure (Theorem
1.7
), as well as our results on the asymptotic wandering exponents (Corollary
1.13
and Theorems
1.15
and
1.16
). To do this, we will first give a result which allows one to calculate certain statistics of the Gibbs measures which are quadratic in
\bm{u}
, namely Proposition
4.1
. This proposition is for finite
L
, and could actually easily be generalized to the more general setting of our previous works
[
8
,
7
]
, where the elastic polymer is replaced with a more general (discrete)
d
-dimensional elastic manifold. This result will be specialized to give the mean-squared displacement of the Gibbs measure for finite
L
, which is given in Corollary
4.2
.

To then obtain Theorem
1.7
, we study Corollary
4.2
in the limit
L\to\infty
. A difficulty in doing this is that the quantities present Corollary
4.2
are functions whose asymptotics are not covered by Proposition
2.3
. Instead, the asymptotics for these functions is given in Proposition
4.4
, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A
.

After we prove Theorem
1.7
, we will focus on the proof of Corollary
1.13
and Theorems
1.15
and
1.16
. All of these results involve studying Theorem
1.7
carefully as one takes
\mu\to 0
. The proof of Corollary
1.13
is quite easy, as Corollary
4.2
simplifies quite nicely in this case. The other theorems are less simple. They rely heavily on the characterizations in Corollary
1.30
and Theorem
1.24
to reduce the quantities in Theorem
1.7
to something whose limits are more explicit and manageable.

We now begin by giving our main result for quantities at fixed
L
. Recall that a matrix
A
is circulant if the value of
A_{x,y}
only depends on
x-y
. Circulant matrices serve as a discrete analogue of translation-invariant operators. This analogy is further strengthened by the classical fact that a matrix is circulant if and only if it may be diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform. Our next result allows us to calculate the expectation with respect to the Gibbs measure of the quadratic form associated with a symmetric circulant matrix on
\Lambda_{L}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
L\in\mathbb{N}
and some
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\Lambda_{L}\times\Lambda_{L}}
which is a symmetric circulant matrix. Define functions

(4.1)

R_{i,A}(u)=L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}((uI-t\Delta_{L})^{-i}A).

Let
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
be the Parisi pair from Theorem
2.1
, let
\delta_{L}
be the function associated to
(q_{L},\zeta_{L})
by (
1.18
), and finally let
q_{*}
be any point such that
\zeta_{L}([0,q_{L,*}])=1
. Then we have that

(4.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{E}\langle

\displaystyle A_{x,y}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))_{N}\rangle=

(4.3)

\displaystyle\beta^{-1}R_{1,A}(K_{L,t}(\beta(q_{L}-q_{L,*})))-\int_{0}^{q_{L,*}}R_{2,A}(K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u)))K^{\prime}_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u))du.

We defer the proof of Proposition
4.1
to the end of this section. To apply this proposition to the study of the mean-squared displacement, we need a function. For
x\in\Lambda_{L}
, let

(4.4)

G_{x,t,L}(\mu)=L^{1/2}[(\mu I-\Delta_{L})^{-1}]_{[x]_{L},0}-L^{1/2}[(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})^{-1}]_{0,0}.

This is the discrete Green’s function for the heat equation regularized on the diagonal. In particular is the discrete analogue of the continuum function
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)
defined in the introduction (see (
1.19
)).

Next we introduce a finite-
L
version of the functional
H_{\beta,t,\mu}
(see Definition
1.6
). Namely, with notation as in Proposition
4.1
, we define the function

(4.5)

H_{L,\beta,t,\mu}(x)=-\frac{2}{\beta}G_{x,t,L}(K_{L,t}(\beta(q_{L}-q_{L,*}))-2\int_{0}^{q_{L,*}}G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u)))K_{L,t}^{\prime}(\beta\delta_{L}(u))du.

With this function defined, we can now give our main application of Proposition
4.1
.

Corollary 4.2
.

For any
x,y\in\Lambda_{L}
, we have that

(4.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\langle\|\bm{u}(x)-\bm{u}(y)\|^{2}_{N}\rangle=H_{L,\beta,t,\mu}(x-y).

Proof.

Let us form the matrix

(4.7)

[E^{x,y}]_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}:=\sum_{w\in\Lambda_{L}}\left(2\delta_{x^{\prime},w}\delta_{y^{\prime},w}-\delta_{x^{\prime},x+w}\delta_{y^{\prime},y+w}-\delta_{x^{\prime},y+w}\delta_{y^{\prime},x+w}\right),

where
\delta
denotes the Kronecker
\delta
-function. This matrix is clearly symmetric and circulant. Using translation invariance of the model on
\bm{u}
repeatably, we see that

(4.8)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{E}\langle E^{x,y}_{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(\bm{u}(x^{\prime}),\bm{u}(y^{\prime}))_{N}\rangle

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{w\in\Lambda_{L}}\big(2\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(w),\bm{u}(w))_{N}\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(x+w),\bm{u}(y+w))_{N}\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(y+w),\bm{u}(x+w))_{N}\rangle\big)

(4.10)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(x))_{N}\rangle-2\mathbb{E}\langle(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))_{N}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle\|\bm{u}(x)-\bm{u}(y)\|^{2}_{N}\rangle

Similarly, we compute that

(4.11)

R_{1,E^{x,y}}(\mu)=2L^{1/2}[\mu I+t\Delta_{L}]^{-1}_{x,x}-2L^{1/2}[\mu I+t\Delta_{L}]^{-1}_{x,y}=-2G_{x-y,L,t}(\mu),

and as
R_{1,E^{x,y}}^{\prime}(\mu)=-R_{2,E^{x,y}}(\mu)
we see that
R_{2,E^{x,y}}(\mu)=2G_{x-y,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu)
. Thus, applying Proposition
4.1
in the case of
A=E^{x,y}
gives the desired result.
∎

When a model is RS, the expression in Corollary
4.2
has a significantly simpler description. The formulas for the RS phase in Theorem II.1.7 give that

(4.12)

\beta\delta_{L}(0)=\beta\delta_{L}(q_{L,*})=\beta(q_{L}-q_{L,*})=R_{1;L,t}(\mu).

Using these equations and the fact that
-K_{L,t}^{\prime}(R_{1;L,t}(\mu))R_{2;L,t}(\mu)=1
, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 4.3
.

If the model is RS, then for any
x\in\Lambda_{L}
,

(4.13)

H_{L,\beta,t,\mu}(x)=-\frac{2}{\beta}G_{x,t,L}(\mu)-4B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta}R_{1;L,t}(\mu)\right)G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu).

These results give a concrete formula for the mean-squared displacement when
L
is fixed, and so we now proceed to study what happens when in the continuum limit. For this, we first need the following result, which establishes that
G_{x,L,t}
converges to the function
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}
in the continuum limit.

Proposition 4.4
.

For any
\mu,t>0
and
x\geq 0
, we have that

(4.14)

\lim_{L\to\infty}G_{x,t,L}(\mu)=\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu),\;\;\;\;\;\lim_{L\to\infty}G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu)=\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}(\mu).

Moreover, if one fixes
x
and
t
and chooses some
\epsilon>0
, these limits converge uniformly for all choices of
\mu\in[\epsilon,\epsilon^{-1}]
.

The proof of this result will be deferred to Appendix
A
. Equipped with this result, we are ready to establish Theorem
1.7
, which is the continuum analogue of Corollary
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

In sight of Corollary
4.2
, to prove Theorem
1.7
, we need to show that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

(4.15)

\lim_{L\to\infty}H_{L,\beta,t,\mu}(x)=H_{\beta,t,\mu}(x).

To write this out more explicitly, let
(q_{L},\zeta_{L},q_{L,*},\delta_{L})
be as in Proposition
4.1
. Moreover, let
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
denote the Parisi pair for the continuum model (see Definition
1.5
), and denote by
\delta
the functions associated to
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
by (
1.18
). Finally, let
q_{*}<q_{c}
be any value such that
\zeta_{c}([0,q_{*}])=1
. We only need to show that

(4.16)

\begin{split}\lim_{L\to\infty}\left(\beta^{-1}G_{x,t,L}(K_{L,t}(\beta(q_{L}-q_{L,*})))+\int_{0}^{q_{L,*}}G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u)))K^{\prime}_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u))du\right)\\
=\beta^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\frac{1}{(\beta(q_{c}-q_{*}))^{2}t}\right)+\int_{0}^{q_{*}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{(\beta\delta(u))^{2}t}\right)\left(\frac{-2}{(\beta\delta(u))^{3}t}\right)du.\end{split}

For this, note that by Corollary
3.12
we have that
\lim_{L\to\infty}q_{L}=q_{c}
and
\lim_{L\to\infty}\zeta_{L}=\zeta_{c}
, where the latter convergence is in the weak sense as measures on
[0,\infty)
. In particular, we see that
\lim_{L\to\infty}\delta_{L}(u)=\delta_{L}(u)
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.5
and the asymptotics of
R_{1;L,t}
and
U_{L}
in Propositions
2.3
and
2.5
, we may choose
q_{*,L}
and
q_{*}
so that
\lim_{L\to\infty}q_{*,L}=q_{*}
and such that
\inf_{L}(q_{L}-q_{*,L})>0
. As this value is both bounded above and bounded away from zero, we may use Propositions
2.3
and
4.4
to conclude that

(4.17)

\lim_{L\to\infty}\beta^{-1}G_{x,t,L}(K_{L,t}(\beta(q_{L}-q_{L,*})))=\beta^{-1}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\frac{1}{(\beta(q_{c}-q_{*}))^{2}t}\right).

Similarly, we note that for
u\in[0,q_{L,*}]
,

(4.18)

q_{L}-q_{L,*}=\delta_{L}(q_{*})=\delta_{L}(u)\leq\delta_{L}(0)\leq q_{L},

so that
\delta_{L}(u)
is also bounded above and bounded away from zero. In particular, using Propositions
2.3
,
2.5
and
4.4
again, we see that for
u\in[0,q_{*}]

(4.19)

\lim_{L\to\infty}G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(K_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u)))K^{\prime}_{L,t}(\beta\delta_{L}(u))\\
=\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{(\beta\delta(u))^{2}t}\right)\left(\frac{-2}{(\beta\delta(u))^{3}t}\right).

So by applying the dominated convergence theorem, and recalling (
4.17
), we conclude (
4.16
).
∎

Now that the proof of Theorem
1.7
is complete, we proceed to prove our remaining results on asymptotic wandering exponents. These results involve understanding the quantities in Theorem
1.7
in the limit
\mu\to 0
. For this, we will need the following lemma concerning
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)
, whose proof we omit.

Lemma 4.5
.

Fix
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Then
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)
is a negative, increasing, strictly concave function of
\mu\in(0,\infty)
. This function and its derivative have

(4.20)

\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)=\lim_{\mu\to\infty}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}(\mu)=0,

(4.21)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)=-\frac{|x|}{2},\;\;\;\;\;\lim_{\mu\to 0}\left(\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}(\mu)-\frac{x^{2}}{8\sqrt{\mu t^{3}}}\right)=-\frac{|x|^{3}}{12t^{2}}.

In particular, for
t,x\in(0,\infty)
, the function
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)
is bounded on
[0,\infty)
, while
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}(\mu)=O(\mu^{-1/2})
as
\mu\to 0
.

We note that Corollary
1.9
immediately follows from Lemma
4.5
and Corollary
4.3
. We will now give the proof of Corollary
1.13
.

Proof of Corollary
1.13
.

As in Corollary
4.3
, we may use Theorem
1.21
to reduce the statement of Theorem
1.7
to

(4.22)

\lim_{L\to\infty}H_{L,\beta,t,\mu}(x)=-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t,L}(\mu)-4B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\mathbb{G}_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu).

By Lemma
4.5
,

(4.23)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\left(-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t,L}(\mu)\right)=\frac{|x|}{\beta},

so to complete the proof, it suffices to show that

(4.24)

\limsup_{\mu\to 0}4B^{\prime}\left(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}}\right)\mathbb{G}_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu)=0.

Using Lemma
4.5
again, we see that this follows if

(4.25)

\limsup_{\mu\to 0}B^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\right)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}=0\text{ or equivalently if }\limsup_{x\to\infty}B^{\prime}\left(x\right)x=0.

However, as we have assumed that the massless model is RS, by Theorem
1.17
, we must have that

(4.26)

\lim_{x\to\infty}B^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{3}<\infty.

Letting
\sup_{x\to\infty}B^{\prime\prime}(x)x^{3}=c
, we see that

(4.27)

B^{\prime}(x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}B^{\prime\prime}(y)dy\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{c}{y^{3}}dy=\frac{c}{2x^{2}},

so that
B^{\prime}(x)x^{2}
is bounded as well, which implies (
4.25
).
∎

Next, we will move onto the proof of Theorem
1.15
in the case of
\gamma<1
. As before, we must consider the formulas in Theorem
1.7
as
\mu\to 0
. To do this, we may rely on the explicit formulas for the Parisi measure given in Corollary
1.30
. This allows us to rewrite the expression in Theorem
1.7
explicitly for fixed
\mu
. This reduces the proof to a calculus problem, which we may solve explicitly.

Proof of Theorem
1.15
with
\gamma<1
.

First, we simplify the formula in Theorem
1.7
by using the formulas in Corollary
1.30
. In particular, we fix
(\beta,t)
and assume that
\mu<\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)
for the remainder of the proof. We will let
(q_{0},q_{*},q_{c},\zeta_{c})
be the quantities from Corollary
1.30
, and we let
\delta
be the function associated
to
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
by (
1.18
).

By squaring the third equation in (
1.47
),

(4.28)

-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}(q_{c}-q_{*})^{2}t}\right)=-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\mu_{Lar}(\beta,t)\right).

As this is independent of
\mu
, this effectively deals with the first term in Theorem
1.7
, and so we will turn our focus on the second term in Theorem
1.7
. This term is an integral, and we study it by splitting it into two integrals, one from
0
to
q_{0}
, and the other from
q_{0}
to
q_{*}
, which we study separately.

For
u\in(0,q_{0})
,
\beta\delta(u)=\beta\delta(0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}
where in the last equality we used (
1.15
). Using this and (
1.47
) we compute that

(4.29)

\int_{0}^{q_{0}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(u)^{2}t}\right)\frac{4}{\beta^{3}\delta(u)^{3}t}du=q_{0}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\mu\right)4\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}=\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\mu\right)c_{1}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3(\gamma+1)}{2(2+\gamma)}+3}(4\sqrt{t}).

In particular, by Lemma
4.5
, we see that

(4.30)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\int_{0}^{q_{0}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(u)^{2}t}\right)\frac{4}{\beta^{3}\delta(u)^{3}t}du=\lim_{\mu\to 0}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\mu\right)c_{1}\mu^{\frac{3}{2}+\frac{3(\gamma+1)}{2(2+\gamma)}+3}(4\sqrt{t})=0.

Next, we consider the second part of the integral. As a preliminary step, note the third equation of (
1.47
), we have that
\mu=\beta^{-2}(q_{c}-q_{*})^{-2}t^{-1}
solves (
1.46
), or more explicitly

(4.31)

2\gamma(\gamma+1)\left(1+2q_{c}-2q_{*}\right)^{-\gamma-2}=\frac{1}{\beta^{3}(q_{c}-q_{*})^{3}t}.

Now by using (
1.48
) we have for
u\in(q_{0},q_{*})
that

(4.32)

\displaystyle\beta\delta(u)=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma+2}{3}\left(\frac{4}{t\gamma(\gamma+1)}\right)^{1/3}\int_{u}^{q_{*}}(1+2q_{c}-2s)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{3}}ds+\beta(q_{c}-q_{*})

(4.33)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4}{t\gamma(\gamma+1)}\right)^{1/3}\frac{1}{2}\left((1+2q_{c}-2u)^{\frac{\gamma+2}{3}}-(1+2q_{c}-2q_{*})^{\frac{\gamma+2}{3}}\right)+\beta(q_{c}-q_{*})

(4.34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2t\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}(1+2q_{c}-2u)^{\frac{\gamma+2}{3}},

where in the final equality we have used (
4.31
) to cancel the second and third terms. From this we see that for
u\in(2(q_{c}-q_{0})+1,2(q_{c}-q_{*})+1)
,

(4.35)

\beta\delta(q_{c}+1/2-u/2)=\frac{u^{\frac{\gamma+2}{3}}}{(2t\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}.

Thus, by a change of variables, we may simplify the second integral as

(4.36)

\displaystyle\int_{q_{0}}^{q_{*}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\delta(u)^{2}t}\right)\frac{4}{\beta^{3}\delta(u)^{3}t}du

\displaystyle=\int_{2(q_{c}-q_{*})+1}^{2(q_{c}-q_{0})+1}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{(2t\gamma(\gamma+1))^{2/3}}{u^{2(\gamma+2)/3}t}\right)\frac{4(2t\gamma(\gamma+1))}{u^{\gamma+2}t}\frac{du}{2}

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{c_{0}\mu^{-\frac{3}{2(\gamma+2)}}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{2/3}}{u^{2(\gamma+2)/3}t^{1/3}}\right)\frac{4\gamma(\gamma+1)}{u^{\gamma+2}}du,

where in the second step we used (
1.47
). Note that the integrand is independent of
\mu
, which only appears in the integral bounds. By Lemma
4.5
, we see that for large
u
, the integrand is positive and bounded above by
Cu^{-2(\gamma+2)/3}\leq Cu^{-4/3}
for some
C:=C(x,t,\beta)>0
. In particular, this integral is convergent at
\infty
. From this we see that

(4.38)

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu\to 0}\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{c_{0}\mu^{-\frac{3}{2(\gamma+2)}}}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{2/3}}{u^{2(\gamma+2)/3}t^{1/3}}\right)\frac{4\gamma(\gamma+1)}{u^{\gamma+2}}du

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{2/3}}{u^{2(\gamma+2)/3}t^{1/3}}\right)\frac{4\gamma(\gamma+1)}{u^{\gamma+2}}du.

This completes our simplification of all the terms in Theorem
1.7
. Collecting terms, we conclude that

(4.40)

\begin{split}H_{\beta,\mu,t}(x)=&-\frac{2}{\beta}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}\left(\mu_{Lar}(\beta,t)\right)+\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{2/3}}{u^{2(\gamma+2)/3}t^{1/3}}\right)\frac{4\gamma(\gamma+1)}{u^{\gamma+2}}du.\end{split}

Now, finally, we take the limit
x\to\infty
. For this, we note that for fixed
t,y>0
, the function
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(y)
is exponentially decaying in
x
. Thus, we may ignore the first term on the right-hand side of (
4.40
). To deal with the second term, we must make its
x
-dependence clear. Using the definition of
\mathbb{G}_{x,t}
in (
1.19
), one may routinely check that for
x>0

(4.41)

\mathbb{G}_{x,t}^{\prime}(y)=\frac{-e^{-x\frac{\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{t}}}+1}{4\sqrt{y^{3}t}}-\frac{xe^{-x\frac{\sqrt{y}}{\sqrt{t}}}}{4\sqrt{y^{2}t^{2}}}=\frac{f\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\sqrt{y}\right)}{4\sqrt{ty^{3}}},

where
f(z)=1-e^{-z}-ze^{-z}
. So we may rewrite the integral term in (
4.40
) as

(4.42)

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{\infty}

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{t}}\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}{u^{(\gamma+2)/3}t^{1/6}}\right)\left(\frac{u^{(\gamma+2)}t^{1/2}}{4\sqrt{t}2\gamma(\gamma+1)}\right)\frac{4\gamma(\gamma+1)}{u^{\gamma+2}}du

(4.43)

\displaystyle=\int_{\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{x}{u^{(\gamma+2)/3}}\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}{t^{2/3}}\right)du

(4.44)

\displaystyle=x^{\frac{3}{\gamma+2}}\int_{x^{-(\gamma+2)/3}(\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1)}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}{u^{(\gamma+2)/3}t^{2/3}}\right)du.

As
y\to 0
we have that
f(y)=O(y^{2})
and in addition
\lim_{y\to\infty}f(y)=1
. This shows that the integrand in the final integral is convergent on
(0,\infty)
. In particular

(4.45)

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_{x^{-(\gamma+2)/3}\frac{2}{\beta\sqrt{\mu_{Lar}(\beta;t)t}}+1}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}{u^{(\gamma+2)/3}t^{2/3}}\right)du=\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(\frac{(2\gamma(\gamma+1))^{1/3}}{u^{(\gamma+2)/3}t^{2/3}}\right)du

(4.46)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2\gamma(\gamma+1)}{t^{2}}\right)^{1/(\gamma+2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(u^{-(\gamma+2)/3}\right)du.

To find the final integral, note that for
\alpha>\frac{1}{2}
if we make the substitution
v=u^{-\alpha}
and then integrate by parts we see that

(4.47)

\int_{0}^{\infty}f(u^{-\alpha})du=\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(v\right)\frac{v^{-1-1/\alpha}}{\alpha}dv=\int_{0}^{\infty}f^{\prime}\left(v\right)v^{-1/\alpha}dv=\int_{0}^{\infty}v^{1-1/\alpha}e^{-v}dv=\Gamma\left(2-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right).

Altogether, we conclude that

(4.48)

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{H_{\beta,\mu,t}(x)}{x^{\frac{3}{\gamma+2}}}=\left(\frac{2\gamma(\gamma+1)}{t^{2}}\right)^{1/(\gamma+2)}\Gamma\left(2-\frac{3}{\gamma+2}\right),

which suffices to complete the proof.
∎

To complete our results on the wandering exponents, what remains is the proof of Theorem

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}L^{-1/2}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}^{\kappa,q}(\epsilon)|_{\epsilon=0}=-\frac{\beta}{2L}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{L}}\mathbb{E}\langle A_{xy}(\bm{u}(x),\bm{u}(y))_{N}\rangle^{\kappa,q}.

Moreover, by Hölder’s inequality the function
L^{-1/2}N^{-1}\mathbb{E}\log Z_{N,\beta}^{\kappa,q}(\epsilon)
is also convex in
\epsilon
. As the point-wise limit of a family of convex functions is convex, Proposition
4.7
implies that the function

(4.96)

\mathscr{P}(\epsilon):=\inf_{\zeta\in P([0,q_{L}))}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta)

is convex in
\epsilon
. However, Proposition
4.7
shows that for fixed
\epsilon
, the function
\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta)
is strictly convex in
\zeta
, with a unique minimizer, which we denote
\zeta_{\epsilon}
. Now if denote the left and right derivatives in
\epsilon
as
\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{-}
and
\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{+}
, we see that convexity of
\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)
implies that

(4.97)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{+}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)=\liminf_{h\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathscr{P}(\epsilon+h)-\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)}{h}.

However for
h>0

(4.98)

\frac{\mathscr{P}(\epsilon+h)-\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)}{h}=\frac{\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon+h})-\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon})}{h}\leq\frac{\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon})-\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon})}{h},

so that

(4.99)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{+}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}\right)(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon}).

A similar argument shows that

(4.100)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{-}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)\geq\left(\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}\right)(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon}).

Combined, we see that

(4.101)

\left(\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}\right)(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon})\leq\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{-}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)\leq\frac{d}{d\epsilon}^{+}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)\leq\left(\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}\right)(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon}),

where in the second equality we have used the convexity of
\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)
. In particular, (
4.101
) is actually a chain of equalities, which implies that
\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)
is differentiable in
\epsilon
with derivative

(4.102)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)=\left(\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}\right)(q_{L},\zeta_{\epsilon}),

and so if
\epsilon=0
,

(4.103)

\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathscr{P}(\epsilon)\big|_{\epsilon=0}=\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\cal{P}_{\beta}^{\epsilon,L}(q_{L},\zeta_{c})\big|_{\epsilon=0}.

Now we use the fact that if
f_{n}\to f
is a pointwise limit of differentiable convex functions, such that
f
is also differentiable, then
\nabla f_{n}\to\nabla f
(see Theorem 25.7 of
[
66
]
). Thus, combining (
4.76
) with the computation of (
4.103
) above completes the proof.
∎

Next, given such a triple

Thus, to show that

Now note that any concave function

So as

However, if we note that

Lastly, we show that

\displaystyle\delta(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{s}^{q}\zeta([0,u])du=(q-q_{*})+\int_{s}^{q_{*}}\beta^{-1}U_{B}^{\prime}(q-u)du

\displaystyle=(q-q_{*})-\beta^{-1}U_{B}(q-q_{*})+\beta^{-1}U_{B}(q-u)=\beta^{-1}U_{B}(q-u),

where in the last equality we have again used (

\delta(u)=\delta(q_{0})=\beta^{-1}U_{B}(q-q_{0})=\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{\mu t}},

where in the last equality we have used (

To show the second identity, we will need to study the function

In particular, the third equation of (

F_{q}(q_{0})=-2B^{\prime}(2(q-q_{0}))-2q_{0}\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}=0.

Moreover, for

Finally, note that for

(5.18)

F_{q}^{\prime}(u)=4B^{\prime\prime}(2(q-u))-\frac{2}{(\beta(q-u))^{3}t}.

Proof of Theorem

.

As before, we will use the notation

and

to refer to the functions defined in Theorem

We first prove that if the model is not RS, then it must be 1RSB. This proof is a modification of the one given by Crisanti and Sommers

for the one-site spherical
p
-spin model. For this, assume that the model is not RS. Let
(q_{c},\zeta_{c})
denote the Parisi pair, and let
\delta
be the function associated to this pair. As
\zeta_{c}
is not a Dirac mass by the RSB assumption, we may choose two points
q_{0}<q_{*}
such that
q_{0},q_{*}\in\text{supp}(\zeta_{c})
. Note that if
q_{0}=0
then (
1.16
) implies that
F_{q_{c}}(0)\geq 0
, however
F_{q_{c}}0)=-2B^{\prime\prime}(2q)>0
, which is a contradiction. Thus, we must have that
q_{0}>0
. Now for (
1.16
) to hold,
f_{q_{c}}
must be maximized at both
q_{0}
and
q_{*}
, which implies that

(5.21)

F_{q_{c}}(q_{0})=F_{q_{c}}(q_{*})=\int_{q_{*}}^{q}F_{q_{c}}(ds)=0.

This implies that
F^{\prime}_{q_{c}}(s)=0
for at-least two points
s\in[q_{0},q_{*}]
.

Now if
\text{supp}(\zeta_{c})
contains more than two points. We may apply the argument again to conclude that
F^{\prime}_{q_{c}}(s)=0
has at least three solutions for

. However,

may be rewritten as the equation

(5.22)

-4B^{\prime\prime}(2(q_{c}-s))=-\frac{2}{(\beta\delta(s))^{3}t},

or equivalently, the equation

(5.23)

U_{B}(q_{c}-s)=\beta\delta(s),

Now, in the case where

is strictly convex, the left-hand side is also strictly convex, and we simply note that the right-hand side is concave. Hence, there can only be at most two solutions to this equation, which gives a contradiction. Thus

can contain at most two points, so the measure must be 1RSB.

In the case where

is linear, the left-hand side of (

. The right-hand side is concave, and for it to coincide with a linear function at three points, say

. Thus if we let

and

, we have that (

. As
\beta\delta^{\prime}(s)=-\zeta_{c}([0,s])
for a.e.

, we see that for
\epsilon>0

\beta\delta^{\prime}(s-\epsilon)>\beta\delta^{\prime}(s+\epsilon)
. However, for small enough
\epsilon>0

5.23
) as the line on the left-hand size has a fixed slope.

Next, fix some pair
(q,\zeta)
, such that
\text{supp}(\zeta)=\{q_{0},q_{*}\}
and

. Let
\delta
now denote the function associated to
(q,\zeta)
by (
1.18

. The above argument shows that

has at most two solutions. However, combined with (

. Let us denote these solutions as

. We have that

is either increasing or decreasing on each of the intervals
[0,r_{0}]

[r_{0},r_{1}]
and

. Now as

is decreasing on the interval

. However, (

must alternate between increasing and decreasing on these intervals, so

is also decreasing on the interval

, and increasing on the interval

is maximized at the points

, which shows that

satisfies (

is indeed the pair from Theorem

Appendix A
Discrete Approximation Results

In this appendix, we prove our results concerning the discrete limit of functions involving the Laplacian, following the heuristics given in Section
2
. More concretely, the primary purpose of this section is to prove Propositions
2.2
,
2.3
,
2.5
, and
4.4
.

All of the functions considered in these propositions can be defined in terms of the function
R_{1;L,t}(\mu)
by some combination of integration, differentiation, and inversion. Using general results, we will essentially be able to derive each limit from very precise asymptotics for
R_{1;L,t}(\mu)
(Lemma
A.2
). The key idea here is that we want to treat (
2.18
) as an integral approximation. However, due to the singular nature of the resolvent (especially at
\mu=0
), we will find it easier to instead represent
R_{1;L,t}(\mu)
in terms of a less singular function - the heat kernel - which will make this approximation easier. We note that when
A
is positive semi-definite and
\mu>0

(A.1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t\mu}\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{-tA}\right)dt=\mathrm{tr}(\mu I+A)^{-1}.

Traces of other powers may also be recovered from the identity

(A.2)

\mathrm{tr}(\mu I+A)^{-z}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(z)}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t\mu}\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{-tA}\right)dt.

So we only need to obtain precise estimates for
\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{-tA}\right)
to obtain results for the trace of the resolvent. We do this in the following lemma.

Lemma A.1
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
t>0
,

(A.3)

\left|L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{t\Delta_{L}}\right)-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\right|\leq C\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}+\frac{e^{-tL^{1/2}}}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{tL}}\right).

Proof.

Before proceeding to the proof, we note that

(A.4)

2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}.

The idea of the proof is to show that when one expands the quantity
L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{t\Delta_{L}}\right)
as a sum of eigenvalues, this sum approximates the integral on the left. Note by (
2.18
) that

(A.5)

L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}\left(e^{t\Delta_{L}}\right)=\frac{2}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}+\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\left(1+I_{L\text{ is even}}e^{-4tL}\right).

The second term is less than
2/L^{1/2}
, so we focus on bounding the first term. Our first step will be to show that we may neglect the tail of this sum. For this, note that for
x\in(0,1/2]
, one has that
\sin^{2}(\pi x)\geq x^{2}
. From this we see that

(A.6)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4t\left(\frac{k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}.

We recognize the term on the right as the right Riemann sum with spacing
\Delta x=L^{-1/2}
for the integral
\int_{\ell L^{-1/2}}^{[(L-1)/2]L^{-1/2}}e^{-4tx^{2}}dx
. As the function
e^{-4tx^{2}}
is decreasing on
[0,\infty)
, this sum is bounded above by the integral itself, so that

(A.7)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4t\left(\frac{k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}\leq\int_{\ell L^{-1/2}}^{[(L-1)/2]L^{-1/2}}e^{-4tx^{2}}dx.

To bound the term on the right, we recall an elementary bound, which follows by applying the Chernoff bound to a Gaussian random variable: for any
x\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
, we have that

(A.8)

\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha y^{2}}dy\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}e^{-\alpha x^{2}}.

From this we see that

(A.9)

\int_{\ell L^{-1/2}}^{[(L-1)/2]L^{-1/2}}e^{-4tx^{2}}dx\leq\int_{\ell L^{-1/2}}^{\infty}e^{-4tx^{2}}dx\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4t}}e^{-4t\ell^{2}L^{-1}}.

If we take
\ell=[L^{3/4}]
, it is easy to see that this term is bounded above by
\frac{C}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-tL^{1/2}}
. Noting the last two terms of (
A.5
) are bounded by
2/L^{1/2}
, we see that to complete the proof it suffices to show that

(A.10)

\left|\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{[L^{3/4}]}e^{-tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}\right|\leq CL^{-1/2}+\frac{C}{\sqrt{tL}}.

Let us denote
\ell=[L^{3/4}]
. By using the inequality
\sin^{2}(x)\leq x^{2}
we see that

(A.11)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\geq\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4t\left(\frac{\pi k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}.

We may again employ an integral comparison (this time using a left Riemann sum) to see that

(A.12)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4t\left(\frac{\pi k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}\geq\int_{L^{-1/2}}^{\ell L^{-1/2}}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx\geq\int_{0}^{\ell L^{-1/2}}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx-L^{-1/2}.

Using (
A.8
)

(A.13)

\int_{0}^{\ell L^{-1/2}}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-4\pi^{2}\ell^{2}L^{-1/2}},

and so by using (
A.4
) we see that

(A.14)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}\geq-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-\pi^{2}[L^{3/4}]^{2}L^{-1/2}}-L^{-1/2},

which is more than sufficient to show the lower bound needed for (
A.10
). For the upper bound, we use the global inequality
\sin^{2}(x)\geq x^{2}(1-x^{2})
to obtain that

(A.15)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-t\left(\frac{\pi k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}\left(1-L^{-2}\pi^{2}k^{2}\right)}

(A.16)

\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-t(1-L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2})\left(\frac{\pi k}{L^{1/2}}\right)^{2}}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(1-L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2})\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx.

Evaluating the integral on the right, and noting that
(1-x)^{-1/2}\leq 1+x
for
x\in(0,1/4)
, we see that if
2\pi\ell\leq L
, we have the final bound of

(A.17)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t(1-L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2})\pi^{2}x^{2}}dx=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t\pi}}+\frac{L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{2\sqrt{t\pi}}.

Thus, for sufficiently large
L
, this gives the upper bound of

(A.18)

\frac{2}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\leq\frac{L^{-2}\pi^{2}\ell^{2}}{\sqrt{t\pi}}.

Combined with (
A.14
) this shows (
A.10
).
∎

Combining this estimate with (

), we are able to obtain a bound for
R_{1;L,t}
.

Lemma A.2
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any
L\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mu,t>0

\left|R_{1;L,t}(\mu)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t\mu}}\right|\leq\frac{C}{L^{1/4}}\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/4}\mu}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{L^{1/4}\sqrt{\mu t}}\right),

Proof.

We observe that
R_{1;L,t}(\mu L^{-1/2})
may be rewritten as

(A.20)

L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})^{-1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu y}L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}(e^{yt\Delta_{L}})dy.

In particular, we have that

(A.21)

\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu y}L^{1/2}\mathrm{tr}(e^{yt\Delta_{L}})dy-\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-\mu y}}{\sqrt{\pi yt}}dy\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu y}\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}+\frac{e^{-ytL^{1/2}}}{\sqrt{yt}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{ytL}}\right)dy.

\frac{1}{\mu L^{1/2}}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{\mu+tL^{1/2}}}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{\sqrt{t\mu L}}.

This immediately gives the stated bound.

Our next goal is to prove Proposition

\frac{d}{d\mu}\log\det(\mu I-A)=L\mathrm{tr}((\mu I-A)^{-1}),

Lemma A.3
.

For every choice of

We start by studying the behavior around

(A.25)

\lim_{\mu\to 0}\left(\log\det(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})-\log\mu\right)=\log\mathrm{det}_{+}(-t\Delta_{L}),

\mathrm{det}_{+}(\Delta_{G})=|G|\times\#\{\Gamma\subseteq G:\Gamma\text{ is a spanning tree}\},

Now for any

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}

\displaystyle\bigg(L^{-1/2}\log\mathrm{det}(\epsilon I-t\Delta_{L})+\int_{\epsilon}^{\mu}R_{1;L,t}\left(\mu^{\prime}\right)d\mu^{\prime}\bigg)

\displaystyle=

where in the last equality we have used (

We observe that the first three terms in the rightmost expression may be rearranged as

If

(A.35)

First, we establish some generalities. In this lemma, we will say that a sequence of functions
f_{n}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
converges to
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
compactly if for each compact subset
K\subseteq(0,\infty)
, the restricted functions
f_{n}|_{K}
converge uniformly to
f|_{K}
. If we assume that each
f_{n}
is decreasing and that
f
is continuous, then Dini’s theorem implies that if
f_{n}
converges to
f
point-wise, then
f_{n}
also converges to
f
compactly. An identical result holds if each function is increasing.

Now assume that each
f_{n}
is also continuously differentiable and convex, and that
f
is also differentiable and convex. Then by Theorem 25.7 of
[
66
]
, if
f_{n}
converges to
f
pointwise, we have that
f_{n}^{\prime}
converges to
f^{\prime}
pointwise as well. Thus, by Dini’s theorem, we have that
f_{n}^{\prime}
converges to
f^{\prime}
compactly.

Now instead assume that each
f_{n}
is a decreasing homeomorphism
f_{n}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, and that
f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is a decreasing homeomorphism as well. Then by Proposition X.10 of
[
13
]
, if
f_{n}
converges point-wise to
f
, we also have that the inverse functions
f_{n}^{-1}
converges point-wise to
f^{-1}
, and so by Dini’s theorem
f_{n}^{-1}
converges compactly to
f^{-1}
.

As noted, Lemma
A.2
implies that
R_{1;L,t}(\mu)
converges to
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}
compactly (as

). We also note that
R_{1;L,t}^{\prime}(\mu)=-R_{2;L,t}(\mu)
and the inverse function of
R_{1;L,t}
is
K_{L;t}
, and similarly
\frac{d}{d\mu}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}=\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}
and the inverse function of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu t}}
(in
\mu
) is
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}
. We also note that each
R_{1;L,t}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is continuously differentiable, decreasing, convex, homeomorphism (and similarly for the function
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}
). In particular, this satisfies all of the above generalities, which shows that
K_{L,t}(\mu)
converges to
\frac{1}{\mu^{2}t}
compactly, and that

converges to
\frac{-1}{2\sqrt{\mu^{3}t}}
compactly as well. This suffices to show all the claims involving

and
R_{2;L,t}
.

Moreover, the functions

can also easily be seen to be continuously differentiable, decreasing, homeomorphisms. Moreover, by differentiating the identity

(A.44)

K_{L,t}^{\prime}(\mu)=\frac{-1}{R_{2;L,t}(K_{L,t}(\mu))},

and differentiating again

so that

is convex as well. As the same can be said for

, we see that the above generalities imply that

converges to

compactly. Finally, we observe from (
A.44
) that
-K_{L,t}^{\prime}:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is also a continuously differentiable, decreasing homeomorphism. These facts also hold for
\frac{2}{\mu^{3}t}
. Thus the inverse function of
-K_{L,t}^{\prime}
, namely
U_{L,t}
, converges to the inverse function of
\frac{2}{\mu^{3}t}
, namely
\left(\frac{2}{\mu t}\right)^{1/3}
, compactly. These convergences verify the remaining claims.
∎

Now that the proof of Propositions
2.3
is complete, all that is left is to prove Proposition
4.4
. The main ideas in this proof will roughly mimic those above, except that instead of working with the trace, we work with individual matrix elements. To do this, we first need a version of (
2.18
) for matrix elements. For this, note for each
0\leq k\leq L-1
, the vector
v_{k}=[e^{2\pi i\frac{kl}{L}}]_{l}
is an eigenvalue of
\Delta_{L}
with eigenvalue
2(\cos(2\pi k/L)-1)=-4L\sin^{2}\left(k\pi/L\right)
. These vectors are easily seen to be linearly independent, and thus these eigenvectors form a complete set. We also note that for each
k
, we have that
\|v_{k}\|=L^{1/2}
. Thus, by the spectral theorem, we see that for any matrix-valued function
f:M_{L}(\mathbb{R})\to M_{L}(\mathbb{R})
which satisfies
f(U^{-1}AU)=U^{-1}f(A)U
, we have that

(A.46)

[f(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})]_{x,y}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}f(\mu+4tL\sin^{2}(k\pi/L))\exp\left(\frac{2\pi ik(x-y)}{L}\right).

However,
f
is real-valued, so by taking the real part, we see that

(A.47)

[f(\mu I-\Delta_{L})]_{x,y}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}f(\mu+4tL\sin^{2}(k\pi/L))\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k(x-y)}{L}\right).

Now note that
\cos\left(2\pi(L-k)(x-y)/L\right)=\cos\left(2\pi k(x-y)\right)
. Then, in the same way that we obtained (
2.18
), we may obtain the formula

(A.48)

\begin{split}[f(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})]_{x,y}=&\frac{2}{L}\sum_{k=1}^{[(L-1)/2]}f(\mu+4tL\sin^{2}(k\pi/L))\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k(x-y)}{L}\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{L}\left(f(\mu)+(-1)^{x-y}I_{L\text{ is even}}f(\mu+4tL)\right).\end{split}

This expression gives us a way to represent
G_{x,t,L}(\mu)
(defined in (
4.4
)) as a sum. However, as before, it is much easier to estimate a different quantity, and then relate it to
G_{x,t,L}(\mu)
. For this, we have the following formula for
G_{x,t,L}(\mu)
in terms of entries of the matrix exponential:

(A.49)

G_{x,t,L}(\mu)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu t}L^{1/2}[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{x,0}dt.

This is similar in form to the representation we used for this matrix’s trace in (
A.2
).
As such, we begin by estimating the entries of this matrix exponential.

Lemma A.4
.

There is
C>0
such that for any choice of
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
x,y\in\Lambda_{L}
and
t\in(0,\infty)
, we have that

(A.50)

\left|L^{1/2}[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{x,y}-\frac{e^{-(x-y)^{2}/4t}}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\right|\leq C\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}+\frac{e^{-tL^{1/2}}}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{tL}}+\frac{\sqrt{t}+|x-y|}{L^{1/4}}\right).

Proof.

Before beginning the proof, we note that

(A.51)

2\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\cos(2\pi xy)dy=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}+2\pi ixy}dy=\frac{e^{-x^{2}/4t}}{2\sqrt{\pi t}}.

Thus, the idea of the proof is to show that the spectral expansion of
L^{1/2}[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{x,y}
approximates the integral on the left.

As
[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{x,y}=[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{|x-y|,0}
, we may assume that
y=0
without any loss in generality. Next, we use (
A.48
) to find that

(A.52)

L^{1/2}[e^{t\Delta_{L}}]_{x,0}=\frac{2}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\cos(2\pi kL^{-1/2}x)+\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\left(1+I_{L\text{ is even}}e^{-4tL}\right).

We begin by estimating the tail of this sum. As
|\cos(y)|\leq 1
, we may use the tail estimates from the proof of Lemma
A.1
. Explicitly, if we let
\ell=[L^{3/4}]
, (
A.7
) and (
A.9
) show that

(A.53)

\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}|\cos(2\pi kL^{-1/2}x)|\leq\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=\ell+1}^{[(L-1)/2]}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\leq\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4t}}e^{-4t\ell^{2}L^{-1}}.

Thus, using trivial bounds for the last two terms in (
A.5
), we see that to show the desired bound it suffices to show that

(A.54)

\left|\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\cos(2\pi kL^{-1/2}x)-\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}\right|\leq C\left(\frac{e^{-tL^{1/2}}}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{tL}}+\frac{\sqrt{t}+|x|}{L^{1/4}}\right).

At this point, the proof of Lemma
A.1
does not generalize directly due to the fact that
\cos(2\pi kL^{-1/2}x)
is not monotone in
x
and not necessarily positive. However, we can still use the main idea, which is to view our sum as a Riemann sum. In particular, let us define the function

(A.55)

f_{L}(y)=e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi y/L^{1/2})}\cos(2\pi xy).

Then we see that the sum on the left-hand side of (
A.54
) is the right Riemann sum with spacing
\Delta x=L^{-1/2}
for the integral
\int_{0}^{L^{-1/2}\ell}f_{L}(y)dy
. We recall that for a right Riemann sum (with
\Delta x=(b-a)/n
) we have the error bound

(A.56)

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(a+k\Delta x)-\int_{a}^{b}f(y)dy\right|\leq\frac{(b-a)^{2}}{2n}\sup_{y\in[a,b]}|f^{\prime}(y)|.

To bound
|f^{\prime}_{L}(y)|
, we first compute

(A.57)

\begin{split}f^{\prime}_{L}(y)=&e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi yL^{-1/2})}4tL\sin(\pi yL^{-1/2})\cos(\pi yL^{-1/2})2\pi L^{-1/2}\cos(2\pi xy)\\
&-e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi yL^{-1/2})}\sin(2\pi xy)2\pi x.\end{split}

Employing the bounds
0\leq|\cos(y)|,|\sin(y)|\leq 1
on all but the copies of
\sin(\pi yL^{-1/2})
in the first term, we see that

(A.58)

|f^{\prime}_{L}(y)|\leq e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi yL^{-1/2})}4tL|\sin(\pi yL^{-1/2})|2\pi L^{-1/2}+2\pi|x|.

For
\alpha>0
, the function
e^{-\alpha y^{2}}\alpha|y|
is maximized at
y=1/\sqrt{2\alpha}
, so that
e^{-\alpha y^{2}}\alpha|y|\leq e^{-1/2}\sqrt{\alpha/2}\leq\sqrt{\alpha}
. We may use this to bound on the right of (
A.57
) to see that

(A.59)

|f_{L}^{\prime}(y)|\leq(2\pi\sqrt{t}+2\pi|x|).

Thus by (
A.56
)

(A.60)

\begin{split}\bigg|\frac{1}{L^{1/2}}&\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi k/L)}\cos(2\pi kL^{-1/2}x)\\
&-\int_{0}^{L^{-1/2}\ell}e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi y/L^{1/2})}\cos(2\pi xy)dy\bigg|\leq\frac{\ell}{2L}(2\pi\sqrt{t}+2\pi|x|).\end{split}

The rightmost side is bounded above by
CL^{-1/4}(\sqrt{t}+|x|)
for large
L
. We now seek to replace the integrand with an
L
-independent quantity. For we recall that for
y\in[0,1/2]
, we have that
y^{2}(1-y^{2})\leq\sin^{2}(y)\leq y^{2}
. In particular, we have for any
y\in[0,L^{1/4}]
that

(A.61)

e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\leq e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi y/L^{1/2})}\leq e^{-4t\pi^{2}(y^{2}-y^{4}/L)}\leq e^{-4t(1-L^{-1/2})\pi^{2}y^{2}}.

Again using that
|\cos(y)|\leq 1
we have that

(A.62)

\begin{split}\bigg|\int_{0}^{L^{-1/2}\ell}&e^{-4tL\sin^{2}(\pi y/L^{1/2})}\cos(2\pi xy)dy-\int_{0}^{L^{-1/2}\ell}e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\cos(2\pi xy)dy\bigg|\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{L^{-1/2}\ell}\left(e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}-e^{-4t(1-L^{-1/2})\pi^{2}y^{2}}\right)dy\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(e^{-4t(1-L^{-1/2})\pi^{2}y^{2}}-e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\right)dy\\
&=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t(1-L^{-1/2})}}-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}\leq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi tL}},\end{split}

where in the last line we have used the fact that
(1-x)^{-1/2}\leq 1+x
for
x\in[0,1/4]
, which holds if
L
is large. Now to finally control this
L
-independent integral, we see from (
A.8
) that

(A.63)

\left|\int_{L^{-1/2}\ell}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\cos(2\pi xy)dy\right|\leq\int_{L^{-1/2}\ell}^{\infty}e^{-4t\pi^{2}y^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\pi t}}e^{-4t\pi^{2}L^{-1}\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}e^{-tL^{1/2}},

where the last inequality holds for large
L
. As the statement is clear for any fixed
L
, combined (
A.51
), (
A.60
), (
A.62
) and (
A.63
) complete the proof of (
A.54
), and thus yield the desired result.
∎

With this bound established, we may obtain estimates for
G_{x,t,L}(\mu)
by employing (
A.49
).

Lemma A.5
.

There is
C>0
, such that for all
L\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\Lambda_{L}
and
\mu,t\in(0,\infty)
we have that

(A.64)

\bigg|G_{x,t,L}(\mu)-\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu)\bigg|\leq\frac{C}{L^{1/4}}\left(\frac{1}{L^{1/4}\mu}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}+\frac{1}{L^{1/4}\sqrt{\mu t}}+\frac{\sqrt{t}}{2\mu^{3/2}}+\frac{x}{\mu}\right).

Proof.

We need the following integral formula (see 3.471.9 and 8.432.1 of
[
38
]
):

(A.65)

\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{-1/2}e^{-ax-bx^{-1}}dx=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}e^{-2\sqrt{ab}}\text{ for }a,b>0.

From this, we conclude that

(A.66)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu z}\frac{e^{-x^{2}/4tz}}{2\sqrt{\pi tz}}dz=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{t\mu}}e^{-x\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{t}}}=\mathbb{G}_{x,t}(\mu).

Equipped with this, the proof of (
A.19
) of Lemma
A.2
may be generalized immediately to show (
A.64
) with the only change being that the analogue of (
A.21
) picks up an additional error term of

(A.67)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\mu z}\left(\frac{\sqrt{tz}+|x-y|}{L^{1/4}}\right)dz=\frac{\sqrt{\pi t}}{2\mu^{3/2}L^{1/4}}+\frac{|x-y|}{\mu L^{1/4}}.

Adding this to the error term in (
A.19
) yields (
A.64
).
∎

We are now ready to give the proof of Proposition
4.4
.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

The claim involving
G_{x,t,L}
immediately follows from Lemma
A.5
. Now with these, note that

(A.68)

G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}(\mu)=-L^{1/2}[(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})^{-2}]_{x,0}<0,

so that
G_{x,t,L}
is decreasing. Moreover,

(A.69)

G_{x,t,L}^{\prime\prime}(\mu)=2L^{-1}[(\mu I-t\Delta_{L})^{-3}]_{x,0}>0,

so
G_{x,t,L}
is convex. Thus, the desired claim for
G_{x,t,L}^{\prime}
follows from the general facts established in the proof of Propositions
2.3
and
2.5
.
∎
