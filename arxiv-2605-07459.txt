On the Complexity of Discounted Robust MDPs with L p L_{p} Uncertainty Sets
Ali Asadi, Krishnendu Chatterjee, Alipasha Montaseri, Ali Shafiee

Abstract
A basic model in sequential decision making is the Markov decision process (MDP), which is extended to Robust MDPs (RMDPs) by allowing uncertainty in transition probabilities and optimizing against the worst-case transition probabilities from the uncertainty sets. The class of ( s , a ) (s,a) -rectangular RMDPs with L p L_{p} uncertainty sets provides a flexible and expressive model for such problems. We study this class of RMDPs with a discounted-sum cost criterion and a constant discount factor. The existence of an efficient algorithm for this class is a fundamental theoretical question in optimization and sequential decision making. Previous results only establish a strongly polynomial-time algorithm for L ∞ L_{\infty} uncertainty sets. In this work, our main results are as follows: (a) we show that for any compact uncertainty set, the policy iteration algorithm for RMDPs is strongly polynomial with oracle access to solutions of Robust Markov chains (RMCs); (b) we present strongly polynomial-time bounds on the policy iteration algorithm for RMCs with L 1 L_{1} and L ∞ L_{\infty} uncertainty sets; and (c) we establish hardness results for RMCs with L p L_{p} uncertainty sets for integer p p satisfying 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty . Finally, motivated by our theoretical bounds, we present experimental results showing how fast policy iteration converges for RMDPs with L 1 L_{1} and L ∞ L_{\infty} uncertainty sets.



1
Introduction

Robust Markov Decision Processes.

In a Markov decision process (MDP), an agent interacts with a stochastic environment of finite state space, where, given the current state and the choice of an action of the agent, the interaction with the environment updates the state
probabilistically
21
.
This is the basic model in sequential decision making, where the common assumption is that the transition function is known.
In many practical scenarios, the transition function is not known, but rather
constructed from data, which results in a level of uncertainty.
This leads to the study of
Robust Markov decision processes
(RMDPs)
(
20
;
18
)
.
In RMDPs, the assumption of MDPs is relaxed by considering that there is an
uncertainty set containing the original transition function, which is
chosen by an adversary.
We consider
(s,a)
-rectangular RMDPs where the uncertainty sets are separate for each state-action pair. This class has been the standard in the literature
18
;
20
.

Discounted-sum costs and values.

The classical objective in MDPs and RMDPs for the agent is to minimize the discounted-sum cost criterion, where each state is assigned a cost and given a discount factor; the discounted cost of a run (infinite trajectory) is the discounted sum of the costs of states appearing in the run.
The value of an RMDP is the minimum expected discounted cost that can be guaranteed by the agent against all possible choices of the transition function from the uncertainty sets by the adversary.

Exact value computation.

While approximation of the value is a relevant problem, the solution for
the exact value is important for two reasons. First, the exact value is a fundamental theoretical
question. Second, for discounted-cost MDPs and RMDPs, additive approximation guarantees are easy
to obtain via value iteration. However, multiplicative factor approximation that guarantees a high fraction of the value is difficult, and solving for the exact value gives a stronger guarantee.

L_{p}
uncertainty sets and motivation.

In this work, we focus on constant discount factors and primarily on
L_{p}

uncertainty sets, for
p\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
The main motivation to study
L_{p}
uncertainty sets is as follows: for learning transition probabilities given the experimental samples, estimates of the uncertainty sets can be obtained using
concentration bounds, which are then naturally approximated with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets
22
;
6
;
14
;
18
. Thus, the study of RMDPs with various
L_{p}
uncertainty sets is a fundamental algorithmic problem that we address in this work.

Previous computational results.

Linear programming solves MDPs with discounted cost in polynomial time
(
11
;
10
)
.
The existence of a strongly polynomial-time algorithm where the number of arithmetic operations is polynomial independent of the bit length is a fundamental theoretical question.
The seminal work of
24
presents the first strongly-polynomial time algorithm for constant discount factors for MDPs, and
15
extends it to
stochastic games. Recently, a strongly-polynomial time bound was established for RMDPs with
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets by
4
. While previous results exist for
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets, a comprehensive algorithmic study of RMDPs with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets is missing, which is the focus of this work.

Our contributions.

Our main contributions are as follows.
Robust Markov chains (RMCs) are a special class of RMDPs where the agent does not have the choice of actions.
First, we show that for any compact uncertainty sets, the policy iteration algorithm for RMDPs is strongly polynomial given an oracle access to solutions of RMCs (
Theorem

1
).
Second, for
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets, we show that RMCs can be solved in strongly polynomial time. While this result exists for
L_{\infty}

sets
4
, our analysis improves the previous result by a factor of the number of states, and for
L_{1}
sets, our result is novel (
Theorem

2
).
Third, we establish hardness results for RMCs with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets for
1<p<\infty
, and also present upper bounds for
L_{p}
uncertainty sets (
Theorem

3
).
Finally, motivated by these theoretical bounds, we complement our analysis with experimental results showing how fast policy iteration converges for RMDPs with
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets.

Technical contributions.

The main technical contributions are threefold. First, we present a
policy iteration algorithm for RMDPs that provides a reduction from RMDPs to RMCs. Second, we introduce a cumulative
transition-probability potential with respect to the optimal distribution chosen by the adversary. With this potential, we obtain a new strongly-polynomial bound for
L_{1}
sets and an improved bound for
L_{\infty}
sets. To the best of our knowledge, the previous potential function presented in
4
does not extend to
L_{1}
sets. Third, for other
L_{p}
uncertainty sets
with integer
1<p<\infty
, we prove hardness via a reduction from
p
-ROOT-SUM and give
an upper bound in the existential theory of the reals by encoding the adversary’s optimization problem via KKT conditions.

Related works.

The exact value computation for RMDPs with various
L_{p}
uncertainty sets is the focus of this work. Several related results have been established for the value approximation for more general models. For discounted-cost RMDPs, the value iteration algorithm is established concurrently in
20
;
18
. Moreover, policy iteration is also introduced in
18
for value approximation. Another variant of policy iteration was established in
19
along with convergence guarantees. It is noteworthy that none of the above results present polynomial or strongly-polynomial bounds for the exact value computation.

Preliminaries

Notation.

Let
S
be a set. We denote the set of all probability measures over
S
by
\Delta(S)
, and its power set by
2^{S}
.
We use
[n]
to represent the set of all natural numbers from 1 to
n
. The sets of natural numbers and non-negative integer numbers are denoted by
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{N}_{0}
, respectively. For vectors
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
, we write
\boldsymbol{v}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{u}
to indicate that every component of
\boldsymbol{v}
is greater than or equal to the corresponding component of
\boldsymbol{u}
. Moreover, we use
\text{size}(n)=\lceil\log_{2}(|n|+1)\rceil+1
, and
\operatorname{size}(\frac{p}{q})=\operatorname{size}(p)+\operatorname{size}(q)
as the bit-size of integer
n
and rational number
\frac{p}{q}
.

Robust Markov Decision Processes.

A Robust Markov Decision Process (RMDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{A},\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
, where the state space
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,|\mathcal{S}|\}
is a finite set of size
n
. The action space
\mathcal{A}
is a finite set of size
m
. The function
c\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
gives the cost of visiting each state. The function
\textit{Succ}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{S}}
maps a state and an action to possible next states. The function
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow 2^{\Delta(\mathcal{S})}
gives a set of possible transition probabilities, where
\mathcal{P}(s,a)\subseteq\Delta(\textit{Succ}(s,a))
. We assume that
\mathcal{P}(s,a)
is a compact non-empty set.

Remark 1
.

We assume
(s,a)
-rectangular uncertainty, which means that the adversary observes the chosen action before selecting a transition function from the uncertainty set. This assumption is standard in the literature
18
;
20
. Other classes of uncertainty are not the focus of our work.

Dynamics.

At first, the initial state is
s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
. At each time step
t\geq 0
, the agent first chooses an action
a_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
. Then, the adversary selects a transition probability distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(s_{t},a_{t})
. The subsequent state
s_{t+1}
is drawn probabilistically from the distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{t}
. A run is an infinite sequence of states and actions
\omega\coloneqq(s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},\ldots)
where for
t\geq 0
, there exists a distribution
\mathfrak{p}_{t}\in\mathcal{P}(s_{t},a_{t})
such that
\mathfrak{p}_{t}(s_{t+1})>0
. A history is a finite sequence of states and actions
h\coloneqq(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{t})
.

Policies.

A history-dependent agent policy is denoted as
\sigma\colon(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{*}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
. An agent policy is memoryless if it only depends on the current state, i.e.,
\sigma\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{A})
and is pure if it prescribes deterministic actions, i.e.,
\sigma\colon(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{*}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
. We say an agent policy is positional if it is pure and memoryless. A history-dependent adversary policy is denoted as
\tau\colon(\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A})^{+}\rightarrow\Delta(\Delta(\mathcal{S}))
such that for every finite sequence of states and actions
h=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots,s_{t},a_{t})
, we have
\tau(h)\in\Delta(\mathcal{P}(s_{t},a_{t}))
. The notions of memoryless, pure, and positional adversary policies are defined analogously.

Probability Measures.

For a history
h
, the cone of
h
is the set of runs where
h
is their prefix. Given an agent policy
\sigma
, an adversary policy
\tau
, and an initial state
s_{0}
, the
unique probability measure over Borel sets of runs is denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{s_{0}}^{\sigma,\tau}(.)
, which is defined by Carathéodory’s extension theorem by extending the natural definition over cones of runs
7
. The expectation with respect to this probability measure is denoted by
\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}}^{\sigma,\tau}(.)
.

Discounted Cost Criterion.

A criterion
is a measurable function that assigns a real number to every run. For a run
\omega=(s_{0},a_{0},\ldots)
, the discounted cost criterion is defined as
\textit{Disc}(\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{t\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\gamma^{t}c(s_{t})
. The objective of the agent is to minimize the expected discounted cost criterion, while the adversary wants to maximize it.

Remark 2
.

For the discounted cost criterion, positional policies for both agent and adversary are as powerful as history-dependent policies
18
;
20
. Therefore, we restrict our attention to positional policies in the rest of the paper.

Values.

The discounted value of a state
s
is defined as

\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)\coloneqq\inf_{\sigma}\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma,\tau}(\textit{Disc}(\omega))\,.

(1)

We drop the
\mathcal{R}
label when it is clear from the context.

Optimal Policies.

An agent policy
\sigma^{*}
is optimal if it guarantees the value for every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,

\sup_{\tau}\mathbb{E}_{s}^{\sigma^{*},\tau}(\textit{Disc}(\omega))=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}(s)\,.

The notion of optimal adversary policy is defined analogously.

Robust MCs.

A Robust Markov Chain (RMC) is a special case of an RMDP that has one possible action for the agent; hence, we can drop the action set. An RMC is
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},c,\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
. The value of RMC is denoted by
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}(s)
. We drop the
\mathcal{M}
label when it is clear from the context.

Markov Chains.

A Markov Chain (MC) is a special case of an RMC where for every state
s
,
\mathcal{P}(s)
is a singleton. A MC is
M=(\mathcal{S},c,P)
, where
P\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
are the fixed transition probabilities. The value of an MC is denoted by
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{M}(s)
. We drop the
M
label when it is clear from the context.

Problem Statement.

We consider the following computational problems.

Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMDP}
.
Given an RMDP
\mathcal{R}
with a constant discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
, compute the value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
, optimal agent policy
\sigma^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, and optimal adversary policy
\tau^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
.

Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
.
Given an RMC
\mathcal{M}
with a constant discount factor
\gamma\in[0,1)
, compute the value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
and optimal adversary policy
\tau^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
.

The decision version of the above problems is, given an initial state
s
and a threshold
\lambda
, decide whether
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s)\geq\lambda
.

Overview of Results

In this section, we give an overview of our results.
First, we recall some complexity classes on which our results rely and then state our main theoretical contributions.

3.1
Complexity Definitions

Strongly Polynomial Algorithms.

An algorithm is strongly polynomial if both the number of arithmetic operations it performs and the size of intermediate values are bounded by a polynomial in the input size, independent of the numerical values of the coefficients. Recall that an algorithm is said to run in polynomial time if its overall execution time is bounded by a polynomial in the input’s bit-length. Note that strongly-polynomial time implies polynomial time, but not vice versa. Thus, strongly-polynomial time gives stronger efficiency guarantees.

\mathbf{p}
-ROOT-SUM Complexity.

The
p
-ROOT-SUM problem asks whether, given a sequence of positive integers
a_{i}
(for
i\in[n]
) and an integer threshold
\alpha
, the following inequality holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\alpha

For
p=2
, this corresponds to the classic Square-Root-Sum problem, which is a major and long-standing open problem in computational geometry since 1976
13
. This problem is known to be in
\mathsf{PSPACE}
, but neither polynomial-time algorithms are known nor even NP upper bounds are established
1
. We define the
p
-ROOT-SUM complexity class to contain all computational problems that are polynomial-time reducible to the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem.

Existential theory of the reals

A sentence in the
existential theory of the reals
is of the form

\exists x_{1}\dots\exists x_{k}~\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})

where
\varphi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
is a
quantifier-free
formula in the language of ordered fields over the reals
\mathbb{R}
. More concretely, atomic sub-formulas are
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0
or
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})<0
, where
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
is a polynomial over
\mathbb{Q}
, and arbitrary formulas are built by Boolean connectives, e.g., conjunction, disjunction, and negation. We denote by
\exists\mathbb{R}
the complexity class of problems that can be reduced in polynomial time to the problem of deciding the validity of an ETR sentence. This class naturally contains
\mathsf{NP}
and is known to be contained within
\mathsf{PSPACE}

9
.

Significance.

We now discuss the significance of our results, which present a comprehensive picture of RMDPs and RMCs with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets and a constant discount factor. Our first result shows that RMDPs can be solved in strongly polynomial time given oracle access to solutions of RMCs. This result is established via an analysis of the policy iteration algorithm, which we show requires only a polynomial number of iterations and RMC solutions for any compact uncertainty set, and thus the problem reduces to RMCs.
The second result presents strongly polynomial time bounds for RMCs with
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets.
Although the strongly-polynomial-time bounds for the
L_{\infty}
case were previously established by

L_{1}
uncertainty sets is novel.
Finally, the first item of the third result shows computational hardness for

and
L_{\infty}

The last item of the third result presents upper bounds for RMCs with

uncertainty sets.
Together, these results complete the study of efficient algorithms for RMDPs and RMCs with a constant discount factor with

uncertainty sets.

4
Overview of
Theorem

1
: Strongly Polynomial PI for RMDPs via RMC Oracles

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the proof of
Theorem

1
. The theorem states that for any compact uncertainty set and a constant discount factor, the policy iteration algorithm for Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMDP}
(Algorithm
1
) terminates after
\mathcal{O}\left(nm\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log\gamma}\right)
iterations given oracle access to the solution of Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
. The full proof is presented in Appendix
A
. The key insight is that, after fixing an agent policy
\sigma
, we obtain an RMC, and consequently, the policy-evaluation step can be handled by an RMC oracle call. Therefore, our main task is to show that the number of iterations is polynomial. We organize the proof into four main steps.

Robust Bellman Optimality Operator.

We first define the robust Bellman optimality operator as

(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}\coloneqq c(s)+\gamma\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}.

Since
\mathcal{A}
is finite and each uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}(s,a)
is compact, the minimum and maximum are attained. The operator
\mathcal{T}
is a
\gamma
-contraction in the infinity norm and therefore has a unique fixed point
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, which is the optimal value vector. Moreover, for every fixed agent policy
\sigma
, the induced value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
satisfies
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
, and consequently
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
. Using this contraction property, we show that
RMDP-PI
generates a monotonically non-increasing sequence of value vectors, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
. Moreover, the value error decreases exponentially, i.e.,

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty},

which gives a convergence rate that is later used to bound the number of iterations.

Potential Function.

We next define a potential function that quantifies the cost of choosing an action
a
instead of an optimal action at state
s
, where both choices are evaluated against the optimal value vector. Formally,

f(s,a)\coloneqq\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,\sigma^{*}(s))}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}.

Intuitively,
f(s,a)
measures the continuation cost incurred by choosing
a
at state
s
instead of
\sigma^{*}(s)
.

For any agent policy
\sigma
, the potential gives two complementary bounds. First, if
\sigma(s)=a
, then the value error at state
s
satisfies

\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}\geq\gamma f(s,a).

Second, if
\hat{s}\in\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma(s))
, then the value error is bounded by

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s})).

These inequalities establish lower and upper bounds on the difference between an arbitrary policy’s value vector and the optimal value vector, and are later used to prove that Algorithm
1
terminates in a polynomial number of iterations.

Elimination of Actions with Maximum Potential.

We combine the lower and upper bounds above with the exponential convergence rate. Let
\sigma^{l}
be the policy at iteration
l
, and let
\hat{s}\in\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma^{l}(s))
. If a later policy
\sigma^{k}
selects the same action at
\hat{s}
after more than
L\coloneqq\left\lceil\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma)\right\rceil
iterations, then the lower bound on the potential function contradicts the exponential decay of the value error. Hence, the state-action pair
(\hat{s},\sigma^{l}(\hat{s}))
is permanently removed after
L
iterations.
There are at most
nm
state-action pairs. Since at least one state-action pair is eliminated every
L
iterations, the total number of policy-iteration steps is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(nmL)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(nm\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log\gamma}\right)
.

Reduction to One RMC Oracle Call.

Finally, we show that the policy-improvement step can be implemented using an RMC oracle. For every state-action pair
(s,a)
, the algorithm needs to compute

c(s)+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}.

Instead of solving these maximization problems separately, we construct a batch RMC
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{batch}}
. This RMC has one transient state
z_{s,a}
for every pair
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and one absorbing state
x_{s}
for every original state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. The absorbing state
x_{s}
is assigned value
\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}_{s}
, and the uncertainty set at
z_{s,a}
is exactly
\mathcal{P}(s,a)
. Therefore, we have
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})=c(s)+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}
. Thus, all action-improvement quantities can be computed with one RMC oracle call on an RMC with
\mathcal{O}(nm)
states. Although the constructed RMC has
\mathcal{O}(nm)
states, its description has bit-length polynomial in the input RMDP.

5
Overview of
Theorem

2
: Complexity of RMC with
L_{1},L_{\infty}
Uncertainty Sets

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the proof of
Theorem

2
. The theorem states that for
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets and a constant discount factor, the policy iteration algorithm for Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
(Algorithm
2
) terminates in a polynomial number of iterations. Since each iteration runs in strongly polynomial time, this yields a strongly polynomial algorithm under
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets with a constant discount factor. Combined with
Theorem

1
, this completes the proof that RMDPs with
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets and a constant discount factor are solvable in strongly polynomial time. The full proof is presented in Appendix
B
.

The challenge of analyzing policy iteration for RMCs, in contrast to standard MDPs, is that the adversary’s action space is the continuous uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}(s)
. Although the worst-case distribution lies on the boundary of the
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
ball, the boundary can have exponentially many vertices (e.g.
L_{1}
ball of dimension
n
has up to
2^{n}
vertices). Thus, a naive enumeration over boundary points fails. Our argument bypasses this by exploiting the algebraic structure of the distributions returned by the policy improvement step and combining it with a number-theoretic combinatorial bound. We organize the proof into four main steps.

Policy Improvement and Structural Property.

The policy improvement step of Algorithm
2
requires solving
\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
. For
L_{1}
, the homotopy method of
17
sorts the successor states by descending value and greedily shifts the available budget from the lowest-value tail to the highest-value head. For
L_{\infty}
, the algorithm by
5
performs an analogous transfer subject to the entry-wise constraint
|\mathfrak{p}_{s^{\prime}}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,s^{\prime}}|\leq\delta_{s}
. Both procedures run in linear time after sorting the values, and expose a clean structural property: every coordinate of the resulting distribution is either equal to the nominal
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,s^{\prime}}
, shifted from it by a fixed value (
\pm\delta_{s}
for
L_{\infty}
and
\pm\delta_{s}/2
for
L_{1}
), or saturated at
0
or
1
, with at most one “incomplete” coordinate. This property is used in the convergence analysis.

Potential Function and Sandwich Bounds.

Fix a state
s
and order its successors
s_{1},\ldots,s_{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}
in descending order of
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
. For an adversary policy
\tau
, define the cumulative transition
F^{\tau}(s,i)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s,s_{j}}
. A first observation is that
F^{*}(s,i)\geq F^{\tau}(s,i)
for every feasible
\tau
and every pair of state and index
(s,i)
. We then define the potential function
f_{\tau}(s,i)
which is guaranteed to be non-negative:

f_{\tau}(s,i)\;\coloneqq\;\bigl(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)\bigr)\bigl(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}}\bigr).

Using the Bellman equations and summation by parts, we obtain a
lower bound
on the per-state value gap,
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}_{s}\geq\gamma f_{\tau}(s,i)
. A complementary
upper bound
is shown to bound the global value error by the worst-case potential, namely

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}\;\leq\;\frac{\gamma n}{1-\gamma}\,\hat{f}_{\tau},\qquad\text{where }\hat{f}_{\tau}\coloneqq\max_{s\in\mathcal{S},\,i}f_{\tau}(s,i).

These inequalities sandwich the global value error by a single scalar quantity associated with the worst state-index pair.

Halving of the Cumulative Gap.

Combining the sandwich bounds with the standard exponential convergence
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{0}\right\|_{\infty}
yields the key halving lemma: if
(s,i)
maximizes the potential at iteration
t
, then within
L\coloneqq\lceil\log_{\gamma}\!\bigl(\tfrac{1-\gamma}{2n}\bigr)\rceil
subsequent iterations the gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
must shrink by at least a factor of two; otherwise, the lower bound at iteration
l
and the upper bound at iteration
t
would contradict the exponential decay rate of policy iteration.

Combinatorial Bound on the Number of Halvings.

The structural property of policy iteration (The first step) implies that for every state
s
and every policy
\tau
visited by the algorithm, the cumulative gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
is a signed sum of at most
n+\mathcal{O}(1)
numbers (the nominal probabilities
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,j}
together with
\delta_{s}
and
1
), with coefficients in
\{-1,0,+1\}
. A combinatorial lemma
4
shows that any such set of signed sums takes
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
distinct binary scales
\lfloor\log_{2}|y|\rfloor
. Since every halving drops the gap to a smaller binary scale, each of the at most
n^{2}
state-index pairs
(s,i)
can have at most
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
halvings. Combined with the
L
-iteration cost per halving from the above step, this bounds the total number of policy iterations by
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log n\cdot L)
, which gives the polynomial bound of
Theorem

2
. The same argument improves the previously known bound of
4
for the
L_{\infty}
case by a factor of
|\mathcal{S}|
, and gives the first polynomial bound in the
L_{1}
case.

Overview of
Theorem

3
: Complexity of RMC with
L_{p}
Uncertainty Sets

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the proof for
Theorem

3
. This theorem states that for any integer
p>1
, solving the decision version of the problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets is
p
-ROOT-SUM-hard, and the problem belongs to
\exists\mathbb{R}
complexity class.

We break down the proof into three steps: (1) Establishing the equivalence between the complexity classes
p
-ROOT-SUM and
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM, (2) Proving the problem’s hardness via a reduction from the
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem to the decision problem, and (3) Showing the problem belongs to the
\exists\mathbb{R}
by encoding it as an
\exists\mathbb{R}
formula. The full proof of
Theorem

3
is detailed in Appendix
C
.

1. Equivalence of ROOT-SUM Problems.

Initially, we simplify the underlying complexity classes. We prove that the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem is polynomial-time equivalent to the
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem.
We achieve this through a sequence of reductions. First, we show that the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem is reducible to the
kp
-ROOT-SUM problem for any constant integer
k>1
. Next, to handle fractional roots, we introduce a “Greedy
p
-th Power Decomposition” algorithm, which breaks down any positive integer
n
into a sum of
p
-th powers using
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
terms in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time.
Using this decomposition, we prove that the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem can be reduced to the
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM problem for any integer
q
where
\gcd(p,q)=1
. Combining these steps shows that the denominator in the fractional root does not change the complexity class, establishing the polynomial-time equivalence between the
p
-ROOT-SUM and
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problems.

2. Proving Hardness via Reduction

We prove that the decision version of the problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
is at least as hard as solving the
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem. We do this by constructing a three-layered RMC that forces the RMC’s Bellman equations to mimic the ROOT-SUM inequality.
In an RMC, the adversary chooses transition probabilities from an
L_{p}
uncertainty set to maximize the expected cost. Formally, this corresponds to finding the maximum inner product between the transition probability vector and the value vector of successor states. According to Hölder’s inequality
12
, maximizing an inner product under an
L_{p}
constraint evaluates the dual norm, which is the
L_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
norm. This dual norm computes the fractional powers for the
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem. By this property, we construct the three-layered RMC as follows:

•

Absorbing States:
At the base layer, we create absorbing states with fixed costs, which are equal to the decomposition of the input integers
a_{i}
by the “Greedy
p
-th Power Decomposition” algorithm.

•

Transient States:
The middle layer has transient states for each
a_{i}
, which are connected to corresponding absorbing states. Because the adversary picks worst-case transitions from an
L_{p}
ball, the value of each transient state is proportional to the
L_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
norm of the corresponding absorbing state values, which is proportional to
a_{i}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}
.

•

Initial State:
The top layer is a single initial state that transitions uniformly to all transient states. Its value is the average of the transient states’ values, which is
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}
.

By structuring the RMC this way, the adversary’s optimal choices compute the fractional roots for us. Finally, deciding whether the value of the initial state meets the threshold
\lambda
is equal to deciding if the sum meets the threshold
\alpha
. Thus, the decision problem is
p
-ROOT-SUM-hard.

3. ETR Upper Bound

Finally, we prove that the decision version of problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
is in
\exists\mathbb{R}
. We show this by modeling the main problem (computing exact values and optimal strategy) as a convex optimization problem. Because this optimization problem satisfies Slater’s condition, we characterize its optimal solution using the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
8
.
Because
p
is a constant integer, the derivative of these constraints produces integer powers. This ensures that the optimization problem can be expressed by polynomial equations and inequalities.
To complete the proof, we combine these polynomial KKT conditions with the Bellman equations to create a system of polynomial equations and inequalities. By definition, finding a valid solution to a system of polynomial equations and inequalities over the real numbers is in
\exists\mathbb{R}
.

Conclusion and Limitations

Our work presents a comprehensive picture of algorithmic studies of RMDPs and RMCs with a constant discount factor. There are some limitations of this work, which naturally give rise to future directions. First, this work focuses on
L_{p}
uncertainty sets, and generalization to other sets is an interesting direction. Second, for general
L_{p}
uncertainty sets, there is a gap between the lower and upper bound (Square-root-sum hardness and ETR upper bound), and a tighter complexity result is another interesting direction. Third, for
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets, our work gives strongly polynomial upper bounds for policy iteration. However, lower bounds have not yet been studied in the context of RMDPs.

Appendix A
Proof of
Theorem

1
: Strongly Polynomial PI for RMDPs via RMC Oracles

In this section, we show that if we have access to an oracle that solves RMCs, policy iteration is a strongly polynomial-time algorithm to find the optimal policy and value function for any uncertainty set. We prove this by showing that the algorithm converges in a polynomial number of iterations. Because each iteration runs in strongly polynomial time, the overall complexity bound immediately follows. We detail the policy iteration procedure in Algorithm
1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}=(\mathcal{S},c,\mathcal{A},\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})
denote the input RMDP. We use
\mathcal{R}^{\sigma}
to denote the RMC induced by fixing the strategy
\sigma
for the agent. We first define the Bellman operator and state its properties in Lemma
1
. These properties guarantee the exponential convergence rate of
RMDP-PI
, as shown in Lemma
3
. Next, we introduce a potential function. We use this function in Lemma
4
to establish lower and upper bounds on the value error of the policies. Finally, Theorem
7
applies these bounds to prove that
RMDP-PI
terminates in a polynomial number of iterations.

A.1
Robust Bellman Optimality Operator

We define the robust Bellman optimality operator
\mathcal{T}
for the RMDP
\mathcal{R}
as follows:

(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}.

Note that because the action space
\mathcal{A}
is finite, the infimum is always achieved. Thus, we can use
\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
and
\min_{a\in\mathcal{A}}
interchangeably.
Also, because the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}_{s,a}
is compact, and the inner product
\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
is a continuous function with respect to
\mathfrak{p}
, the supremum over this set is attainable. Consequently, we can similarly use
\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}
and
\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}
interchangeably.

Furthermore, for a fixed agent policy
\sigma
, its value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
is the unique fixed point of the policy evaluation operator. For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, it satisfies the following Bellman equation:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma(s)}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

Let
P^{\sigma}
denote the transition matrix induced by the agent’s policy
\sigma
and the adversary’s optimal response, where the adversary’s transition probabilities at each state
s
are given by
\argmax_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma(s)}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
. In matrix form, the value vector can be expressed as:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}=\mathbf{c}+\gamma P^{\sigma}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

(2)

The following lemma establishes that
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction mapping with a unique fixed point. This is a standard result in robust dynamic programming
[
18
]
.

Lemma 1
.

The following statements hold:

•

For all
\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\,\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
.

•

For every agent policy
\sigma
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
,
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
.

•

There exists a unique
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

Proof.

•

To prove the contraction property, fix a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Using the properties of the supremum and infimum, we can bound the difference as follows:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}-(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}-\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\left(\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle(\inf f-\inf g\leq\sup(f-g))

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\left(\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}\right)

\displaystyle(\sup f-\sup g\leq\sup(f-g))

\displaystyle=\gamma\sup_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle(\text{Linearity})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{Since }\mathfrak{p}\text{ is a distribution})

Applying symmetric reasoning by swapping
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
yields
(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}-(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
. Combining these gives
|(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}-(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}|\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
. Since this holds for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
, it establishes
\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
.

•

To prove the second statement, we evaluate the operator on the value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
for a given policy
\sigma
. For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we have:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma})_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma(s)}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle(\text{Restricting infimum to }\sigma(s))

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma})

This proves
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
.

•

Finally, because
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction mapping on the complete metric space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
equipped with the infinity norm, the Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees the existence of a unique vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

∎

Now, we show that
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
has a lower value in each state than any other value vector induced by an arbitrary agent policy.

Lemma 2
.

For any arbitrary agent policy
\sigma
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
, the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
.

Proof.

We first establish the monotonicity of the robust Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
. Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
be two value vectors such that
\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}
. For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\inf_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle(\text{Since }\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}\text{ and }\mathfrak{p}\geq\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}

Thus,
\mathcal{T}
is a monotonic operator:
\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}\implies\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}
.

From Lemma
1
, we know that for any agent policy
\sigma
, the operator evaluated at the policy’s value vector yields
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
.

By repeatedly applying the monotonic operator
\mathcal{T}
to both sides of this inequality, we generate a chain of non-increasing vectors:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\quad\text{for any integer }k\geq 1

Because
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction mapping (Lemma
1
), the sequence
\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}
converges to the unique optimal fixed point
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Taking the limit of both sides preserves the non-strict inequality:

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}

This completes the proof.
∎

We next show that
Algorithm

1
generates a non-increasing sequence of value functions that converges exponentially to the optimal value. This property guarantees monotonic policy improvement at each iteration.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\sigma^{t}
and
\sigma^{t+1}
denote consecutive policies generated by
RMDP-PI
(Algorithm
1
), and let
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
denote the value function of
\sigma^{t}
. For all
t\geq 0
, the following statements hold:

•

\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
.

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
.

Proof.

•

We assume
P^{t}
is the probability transition matrix according to
\sigma^{t}
. We first establish that
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
. We express the difference as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle(\text{By Equation \ref{eq:value_definition_rmdp}})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\mathbf{c}\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Multiplying by }\mathbb{I})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\mathbf{c}\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Expanding the product})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-(\mathbf{c}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t})\right]

\displaystyle(\text{Rearranging terms})

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right]

\displaystyle(\text{By Definition of $\mathcal{T}$})

The final inequality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{T}
and the non-negativity of the matrix
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}
, which we establish below. By Lemma
1
, we know that
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
. To evaluate
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}
, we can express it using the Neumann series expansion. Because
P^{t+1}
is a stochastic transition matrix, its spectral radius (maximum magnitude of eigenvalues) is
1
. Multiplying it by the discount factor
\gamma\in(0,1)
bounds the spectral radius of
\gamma P^{t+1}
to less than
1
. This shows the inverse exists and can be expressed as the convergent infinite series:

(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\gamma P^{t+1})^{k}

Furthermore, because
\gamma>0
and every entry in the probability matrix
P^{t+1}
is non-negative, every matrix in this infinite sum is also non-negative. Consequently,
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}
is non-negative, and multiplying it by the non-negative vector
\left[\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right]
ensures that the final product is non-negative. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
, which proves
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
.

•

Next, we show that
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
. We analyze the difference:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\mathcal{T}\text{ and }\sigma^{t+1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle(\text{Substituting for }\mathbf{c})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}

\displaystyle(\text{Expanding the first term})

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1})

\displaystyle(\text{Simplifying})

Since
\gamma>0
,
P^{t+1}\geq 0
, and
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}
(as proved in the previous step), we conclude that
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
.

Finally, we prove the second statement of the lemma using induction on
t
. The base case for
t=0
holds trivially because substituting
t=0
yields
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{0}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
, which simplifies to
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}
. For
t>0
, we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1})

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{Since }\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\text{ is the fixed point})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{By contraction in Lemma \ref{lemma:contractive_value_iteration_rmdp}})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{By induction})

∎

A.2
Potential Function

We next define the potential function. Intuitively, the potential of an action
a
in state
s
measures the additional cost incurred by deviating from the optimal policy
\sigma^{*}
and selecting
a
instead of
\sigma^{*}(s)
.

Definition 1

(
Potential function
)
.

Given a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and an action
a\in\mathcal{A}
, the potential function
f(s,a)
is defined as:

f(s,a):=\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}.

Next, we use the potential function to establish lower and upper bounds on the difference between an arbitrary policy’s value vector and the optimal value.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\sigma
be an arbitrary policy for the agent. The following statements hold:

•

(Lower bound)
If
\sigma(s)=a
for some state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}\geq\gamma f(s,a)
.

•

(Upper bound)
Let
\hat{s}=\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma(s))
. Then
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s}))
.

Proof.

•

(Lower bound)
We lower bound the value difference as follows:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}}\right)-\left(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{By Equation~\ref{eq:value_definition_rmdp}})

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle(\text{Simplifying})

\displaystyle\geq\gamma\left(\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,a}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}\right)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\text{By \lx@cref{creftype\lx@tilde refnum}{lemma:optimal_value_lower_bound_rmdp}})

\displaystyle=\gamma f(s,a)

\displaystyle(\text{By Definition})

•

(Upper bound)
Assume
\tau
is the adversary’s best response to
\sigma
, and
\tau^{*}
is the adversary’s best response to
\sigma^{*}
. Let
P^{\sigma,\tau}
and
P^{*}
denote the corresponding transition matrices under these fixed policies. We expand the value difference using Equation
2
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{c}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=\bigg((\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\bigg)\mathbf{c}.

Applying the matrix identity
A^{-1}-B^{-1}=A^{-1}(B-A)B^{-1}
yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\bigg((\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})\bigg)(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle=\gamma\,(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}(P^{\sigma,\tau}-P^{*})(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle(\text{Simplifying terms})

\displaystyle=\gamma\,(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}(P^{\sigma,\tau}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle(\text{Substituting }\boldsymbol{v}^{*})

Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, we know
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
. We bound the vector
(P^{\sigma,\tau}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
component-wise. For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle((P^{\sigma,\tau}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*})_{s}

\displaystyle=P^{\sigma,\tau}_{s}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-P^{*}_{s}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\argmax_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}}\right)^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }P^{\sigma,\tau},P^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}-\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,\sigma^{*}(s)}}{\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}}

\displaystyle(\text{Taking worst case in l.h.s})

\displaystyle=f(s,\sigma(s))

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }f)

Let
\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}
be the vector where
\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}_{s}=f(s,\sigma(s))
. Thus,
(P^{\sigma,\tau}-P^{*})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\preccurlyeq\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}
. Because the matrix
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}
is non-negative, multiplying it preserves the inequality, forming a non-negative bounding chain:

\displaystyle\mathbf{0}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\preccurlyeq\gamma\,(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}

Because all vectors are non-negative, taking the infinity norm preserves the inequality:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\gamma\,(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\,\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\,\left\|\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{Submultiplicativity of }\ell_{\infty})

We bound the first term using a Neumann series expansion by the same approach as
Lemma

3
. Because
P^{\sigma,\tau}
is a stochastic matrix,
\left\|(P^{\sigma,\tau})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}=1
. The series converges as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\gamma P^{\sigma,\tau})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{i}\left\|(P^{\sigma,\tau})^{i}\right\|_{\infty}=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}.

Finally, we substitute this bound into the main inequality:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\,\left\|\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\cdot\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma(s))

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\mathbf{f}^{\sigma}\text{ and }\ell_{\infty})

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s}))

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\hat{s})

This completes the proof.

∎

We combine the lower and upper bounds to relate the value errors of two policies. Specifically, we show that if both policies choose the same action at the state with the maximum potential, the value error decreases by at most a factor of
1-\gamma
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
be arbitrary policies such that
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}
. Let
\hat{s}=\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma(s))
, and assume
\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{s})=\sigma(\hat{s})
. Then:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq(1-\gamma)\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

We bound the value error as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{\prime}}_{\hat{s}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\hat{s}}

\displaystyle(\text{Definition of }\ell_{\infty}\text{ norm})

\displaystyle\geq\gamma f(\hat{s},\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{s}))

\displaystyle(\text{Lower bound from Lemma~\ref{lemma:lowerbound_potential_rmdp}})

\displaystyle=\gamma f(\hat{s},\sigma(\hat{s}))

\displaystyle(\text{Since }\sigma^{\prime}(\hat{s})=\sigma(\hat{s}))

\displaystyle\geq(1-\gamma)\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle(\text{Upper bound from Lemma~\ref{lemma:lowerbound_potential_rmdp}})

This completes the proof.
∎

A.3
Elimination of Actions with Maximum Potential

We use the convergence rate found in Lemma
3
to show that the action selected in the state with maximum potential cannot repeat in the algorithm for more than
L:=\lceil\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma)\rceil
consecutive iterations.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\sigma^{0},\ldots,\sigma^{T}
denote the sequence of agent policies generated by
RMDP-PI
. For any policy
\sigma^{l}
in this sequence, let
\hat{s}=\argmax_{s\in\mathcal{S}}f(s,\sigma^{l}(s))
. Then,
\sigma^{k}(\hat{s})\neq\sigma^{l}(\hat{s})
for all
k>l+L
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that there exists
k>l+L
such that
\sigma^{k}(\hat{s})=\sigma^{l}(\hat{s})
. Because the value vectors are non-increasing,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}
. Applying Lemma
5
yields:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\geq(1-\gamma)\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

However, the global convergence rate from Lemma
3
dictates:

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{k}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{k-l}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

If
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}=0
, then
\sigma^{l}
is optimal, and the algorithm terminates at iteration
l
. Because the sequence continues to
k>l
, we must have
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\sigma^{l}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>0
. Combining the bounds and dividing by this strictly positive term yields:

1-\gamma\leq\gamma^{k-l}.

Taking the logarithm with base
\gamma
(and reversing the inequality because
\gamma<1
) results in:

\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma)\geq k-l\implies L\geq k-l.

This contradicts the assumption that
k>l+L
.
∎

Finally, we combine the elimination steps to establish a polynomial upper bound on the total number of iterations.

Lemma 7
.

RMDP-PI
terminates with an optimal policy in
\mathcal{O}\left(n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log{(1-\gamma)}}{\log{\gamma}}\right)
iterations.

Proof.

There are exactly
n\cdot m
distinct state-action pairs. By Lemma
6
, if an action
\hat{a}
maximizes the potential function at state
\hat{s}
during iteration
l
, the policy will not select
\hat{a}
at
\hat{s}
for any iteration
k\geq l+L+1
. Consequently, the algorithm permanently eliminates at least one state-action pair from the set of potential maximizers every
L
iterations. Therefore, we bound the total number of iterations
T
as follows:

T\leq n\cdot m\cdot(L+1)=\mathcal{O}(n\cdot m\cdot\log_{\gamma}(1-\gamma))=\mathcal{O}(n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log(\gamma)}).

∎

A.4
Reduction to One RMC Oracle Call

We show that the maximization part of the policy improvement step can be computed for all states and actions simultaneously with one call to the RMC oracle with the same size as the input size (
\mathcal{O}(nm)
).

Lemma 8
.

The policy improvement step in Line 10 of
Algorithm

1
for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
can be computed with one call to an RMC oracle on an RMC with
\mathcal{O}(nm)
states.

Proof.

In Line 10 of
RMDP-PI
, the algorithm updates the policy by computing:

\sigma^{t}(s)\leftarrow\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\{c(s)+\gamma\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}\}

To evaluate this inner maximization for all state-action pairs using a single RMC oracle call, we construct a batch RMC
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{batch}}=(\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{batch}},c_{\mathrm{batch}},\textit{Succ}_{\mathrm{batch}},\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{batch}})
. We introduce a set of transient states to represent each state-action pair, and a set of absorbing states to represent the original states. Let
Z=\{z_{s,a}\mid s\in\mathcal{S},a\in\mathcal{A}\}
be the transient states, and
X=\{x_{s}\mid s\in\mathcal{S}\}
be the absorbing states. We formulate
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{batch}}
as follows:

•

\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{batch}}=Z\cup X
. The total number of states is
n\cdot m+n=\mathcal{O}(nm)
.

•

c_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})=c(s)
for all
z_{s,a}\in Z
, and for all
x_{s}\in X
,
c_{\mathrm{batch}}(x_{s})=(1-\gamma)\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}(s)
.

•

\textit{Succ}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})=\{x_{s^{\prime}}\mid s^{\prime}\in\textit{Succ}(s,a)\}
for all
z_{s,a}\in Z
, and for all
x_{s}\in X
,
\textit{Succ}_{\mathrm{batch}}(x_{s})=\{x_{s}\}
(making the original states absorbing).

•

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})=\mathcal{P}(s,a)
for all
z_{s,a}\in Z
, and for all
x_{s}\in X
,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{batch}}(x_{s})
is restricted to the deterministic transition to
x_{s}
.

Because every state
x_{s}\in X
deterministically transitions to itself with a cost of
(1-\gamma)\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}(s)
, its value in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{batch}}
is
\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}(s)
.

Consequently, the value of any transient state
z_{s,a}\in Z
satisfies the robust Bellman equation:

\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})=c_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{batch}}=c(s)+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s,a)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}

Therefore, we construct
\mathcal{M}_{\mathrm{batch}}
and query the RMC oracle once to find the value of all states simultaneously. Then, for each state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, we extract the optimal action by evaluating
\sigma^{t}(s)\leftarrow\argmin_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\boldsymbol{v}_{\mathrm{batch}}(z_{s,a})
. This shows that the policy improvement step for all states requires only a single RMC oracle call on an RMC of size
\mathcal{O}(nm)
.
∎

Because the policy improvement step in each iteration runs in strongly polynomial time, and there is a strongly polynomial upper bound on the number of iterations, we now provide the proof of the
Theorem

1
.

Proof of
Theorem

1
.

By
Lemma

7
, the
RMDP-PI
algorithm converges to the optimal policy in
\mathcal{O}\left(n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log\gamma}\right)
iterations.

We count the number of RMC oracle calls required in each iteration. During the policy evaluation step (Line 7 of Algorithm
1
), the algorithm solves the RMC for the current policy, which requires one call to the RMC oracle. Then, during the policy improvement step,
Lemma

8
shows that the algorithm can process all states simultaneously with one additional RMC oracle call, whose size is linear according to the input size (
\mathcal{O}(nm)
). This results in two RMC oracle queries per iteration.

Because the total number of iterations is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(n\cdot m\cdot\frac{\log(1-\gamma)}{\log\gamma}\right)
, and each iteration requires two RMC oracle solutions, the algorithm finds the optimal policy with a polynomial number of calls to the RMC oracle.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of
Theorem

2
: Complexity of RMCs with
L_{1},L_{\infty}
Uncertainty Sets

The previous section established that RMDPs with a constant discount factor can be solved in strongly polynomial time if we have oracle access to an RMC solver. This section focuses on solving Robust Markov Chains (RMCs) with
L_{1},L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets.

We demonstrate that RMCs under
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets can be solved in strongly polynomial time using the policy iteration procedure,
RMC-PI
, detailed in
Algorithm

2
. While the strongly polynomial solvability of the
L_{\infty}
case was previously established by Asadi et al.
[
4
]
, our analysis yields a tighter bound by a factor of
|\mathcal{S}|
.

Before diving into the main proof, we briefly recall some standard results for Markov models that apply regardless of the specific uncertainty set
[
21
,
18
]
.

Robust Bellman Optimality Operator.

Given an RMC
\mathcal{M}
and a value vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, the robust Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
(for Algorithm
2
) is defined as:

(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}.

(3)

Note that because
\mathcal{P}_{s}
is compact and the inner product
\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}
is a continuous function with respect to
\mathfrak{p}
, the supremum over this set is attainable. Consequently, we can use
\sup_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}
and
\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}
interchangeably.

It is a well-known result that policy iteration converges monotonically to the unique fixed point of
\mathcal{T}
at an exponential rate. We summarize the core contractive and monotonic properties of this operator in the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

The following statements hold:

•

For any
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}
.

•

For any adversary policy
\tau
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
,
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
.

•

There is a unique optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}=\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.

Proof.

•

Fix an arbitrary state
s\in\mathcal{S}
. Without loss of generality, assume
(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}\geq(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime\prime}
be the respective maximizers in
\mathcal{P}_{s}
for
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
. We can bound the difference as follows:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}-(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}

\displaystyle=\gamma({\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}-{\mathfrak{p}^{\prime\prime}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})

(Definition of
\mathcal{T}
)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma({\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}-{\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v})

(Optimality of
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime\prime}
for
\boldsymbol{v}
)

\displaystyle=\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})

(Factorization)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\infty}.

(
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}
is a probability distribution)

Since this holds for any state
s
, the contraction property follows.

•

For the second claim, observe that for any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau})_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

(Definition of
\mathcal{T}
)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma({\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}})^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

(
\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}
is feasible in
\mathcal{P}_{s}
)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}_{s}.

(Definition of
\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
)

Because this applies to all states simultaneously, we conclude
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
.

•

Finally, existence and uniqueness follow immediately from applying the Banach fixed-point theorem to our contraction mapping.

∎

Next, we show that the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
is an upper bound in each entry for the value vector induced by any arbitrary adversary policy.

Lemma 10
.

For any arbitrary adversary policy
\tau
with value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
, the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
satisfies
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
.

Proof.

We first establish the monotonicity of the robust Bellman operator
\mathcal{T}
for RMCs. Let
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
be two value vectors such that
\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}
. For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u})_{s}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma\max_{\mathfrak{p}\in\mathcal{P}(s)}\mathfrak{p}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle(\text{Since }\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}\text{ and }\mathfrak{p}\geq\mathbf{0})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v})_{s}

Thus,
\mathcal{T}
is a monotonic operator, meaning
\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}\implies\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{u}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}
.

By
Lemma

9
, we know that evaluating the operator at the value vector of any adversary policy
\tau
yields
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
. By repeatedly applying the monotonic operator
\mathcal{T}
to both sides of this inequality, we generate a chain of non-decreasing vectors:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}^{2}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\quad\text{for any integer }k\geq 1

Because
\mathcal{T}
is a contraction mapping, the sequence
\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
converges to the unique optimal fixed point
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. Taking the limit of both sides preserves the non-strict inequality:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{T}^{k}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle\preccurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

This establishes that
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
, completing the proof.
∎

By the properties established in Lemmas
9
,
10
, we now show that the sequence of value vectors produced by the
RMC-PI
algorithm is strictly non-decreasing and converges at an exponential rate.

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
\tau^{0},\tau^{1},\dots
represents the sequence of policies obtained via
RMC-PI
(Algorithm
2
), with
\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
denoting the corresponding value vector for policy
\tau^{t}
. For any iteration
t
, the following properties hold:

•

\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

•

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

Proof.

•

Let
P^{t}
denote the transition matrix corresponding to the policy chosen at iteration
t
. We analyze the difference between consecutive value vectors:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\mathbf{c}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

(Definition of policy value)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[\mathbf{c}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right]

(Factoring out the inverse matrix)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[\mathbf{c}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right]

(Expanding the inner terms)

\displaystyle\succcurlyeq(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}\left[\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right].

(Definition of
\mathcal{T}
)

The final inequality follows from the definition of
\mathcal{T}
and the non-negativity of the matrix
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}
, which we establish below.
By Lemma
9
, we know
\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
. We can express the inverse matrix using the Neumann series:

(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(\gamma P^{t+1})^{i}.

Because
\gamma
and all entries of
P^{t+1}
are non-negative, the resulting sum consists entirely of non-negative terms and converges. Consequently, the matrix
(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})^{-1}
is component-wise non-negative, which implies
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
.

•

We first establish the intermediate bound
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
. We can show this by expanding the difference:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-(\mathbf{c}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t})

(Definition of
\mathcal{T}
and policy update)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\left((\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\right)

(Substituting
\mathbf{c}=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{t+1})\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}
)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-(\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}+\gamma P^{t+1}\boldsymbol{v}^{t})

(Expanding the expression)

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}).

(Simplifying)

Given
\gamma\geq 0
,
P^{t+1}\geq 0
entrywise, and our previous result that
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\mathbf{0}
, we conclude
\boldsymbol{v}^{t+1}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t}
.

Now we prove the second statement of the lemma using induction on
t
. The base case
t=0
holds trivially. For
t>0
:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

(Because
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{t}\succcurlyeq\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}
)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\mathcal{T}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

(Fixed point property, Corollary
9
)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{t-1}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

(Contraction of
\mathcal{T}
, Lemma
9
)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{0}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

(By induction)

∎

B.1
Main Proof of
Theorem

2

In this section, we will show that policy iteration is a strongly polynomial-time algorithm for RMCs with
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
uncertainty set and a constant discount factor. This, combined with
Theorem

1
shows that RMDPs with
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets and a constant discount factor are strongly polynomial.

We prove this by showing that policy iteration converges to the optimal policy in a polynomial number of iterations. To organize our analysis, we first introduce fast linear-time algorithms for the policy improvement step and identify the structural properties of the distributions they produce. Then, we use these properties to bound the convergence rate of our method.

Before we analyze the algorithms, we define the value of a policy in an RMC. For any adversary policy
\tau
, let
P^{\tau}
be its transition matrix and
\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}
be its transition probabilities at state
s
. The value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}
satisfies the following Bellman equation for each state

\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}_{s}=\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

(4)

We can also write this in matrix form to calculate the value vector directly:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

(5)

B.1.2
Potential Function and Sandwich Bounds

We now analyze the convergence rate of our policy iteration method. This analysis differs from the standard RMDP analysis because the action space of the adversary is infinite. Although it is easy to show that the optimal worst-case probabilities must lie on the boundary of the
L_{1}
or
L_{\infty}
ball, there can still be exponentially many such extreme points.

First, we define the cumulative transition probability for an adversary policy
\tau
as follows.

Definition 2
.

Fix a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and let
s_{1},\dots,s_{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}
be an enumeration of the successor states such that
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{1}}\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{2}}\geq\dots\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}}
. Let
\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}
denote the transition probability vector at state
s
under the adversary policy
\tau
.
For any index
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|]
, we define the cumulative transition probability
F^{\tau}(s,i)
as:

F^{\tau}(s,i):=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s,s_{j}}.

For the optimal adversary policy
\tau^{*}
, we use the notation
F^{*}(s,i)=F^{\tau^{*}}(s,i)
and
\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}=\mathfrak{p}^{\tau^{*}}_{s}
.

We now have the following lemma.

Lemma 12
.

For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, any policy
\tau
with
\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}\in\mathcal{P}(s)
, and any index
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|]
, we have
F^{*}(s,i)\geq F^{\tau}(s,i)
.

Proof.

Algorithms
3
,
4
operate on a given value permutation by greedily transferring probability mass from the lowest-value states at the end of the sequence to the highest-value states at the start until the budget saturates. Because the optimal policy evaluates states using the descending permutation of the
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
values,
\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}
shifts the maximum allowable mass as early as possible in this specific order. As a result, its cumulative probability vector is lexicographically maximal over the entire uncertainty set, which guarantees
F^{*}(s,i)\geq F^{\tau}(s,i)
for any feasible
\tau
.
∎

Potential Function.

For a given adversary policy
\tau
, we define the potential function
f_{\tau}(s,i)
for a state
s\in\mathcal{S}
and index
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1]
as:

f_{\tau}(s,i):=(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}}).

(6)

First, we show that the potential function is non-negative for any adversary policy
\tau
.

Lemma 13
.

For any adversary policy
\tau
, state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and index
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1]
, the potential function is non-negative, i.e.,
f_{\tau}(s,i)\geq 0
.

Proof.

By Definition
2
, the successor states are enumerated in descending order of their optimal values, meaning
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{1}}\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{2}}\geq\dots\geq\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}}
. Consequently, for any valid index
i
, the difference between adjacent state values is non-negative:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}}

\displaystyle\geq 0

Furthermore, by Lemma
12
, the optimal policy’s cumulative transition probability is lexicographically maximal. Thus, for all indices
i
:

\displaystyle F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)

\displaystyle\geq 0

The potential function is defined as the product of these two terms:

\displaystyle f_{\tau}(s,i)

\displaystyle=(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})

Thus, it follows that
f_{\tau}(s,i)\geq 0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we first prove the following lemma, which lower bounds the value difference in terms of the potential function.

Lemma 14

(Lower Bound)
.

For every policy
\tau
, state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, and index
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1]
, we have
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}_{s}\geq\gamma f_{\tau}(s,i)
.

Proof.

We start by bounding the value difference using the Bellman equations:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}_{s}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})-(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau})

(Equation
4
)

\displaystyle\geq(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})-(\mathbf{c}_{s}+\gamma{\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*})

(Since
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\succcurlyeq\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

by
Lemma

10
)

\displaystyle=\gamma(\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}-\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s})^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}.

(Canceling
\mathbf{c}_{s}
and factoring)

To evaluate this dot product, we use summation by parts. Because both
\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}
and
\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s}
are valid probability distributions over the successor states, their total cumulative probabilities at the final index are both equal to
1
. Therefore, their cumulative difference at
n
is zero:
F^{*}(s,|\textit{Succ}(s)|)-F^{\tau}(s,|\textit{Succ}(s)|)=1-1=0
. Applying summation by parts yields:

\displaystyle\gamma(\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}-\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s})^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{j=1}^{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}(\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s,s_{j}}-\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s,s_{j}})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{j}}

(Expanding dot product)

\displaystyle=\gamma\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1}\left((F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+(F^{*}(s,|\textit{Succ}(s)|)-F^{\tau}(s,|\textit{Succ}(s)|))\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{|\textit{Succ}(s)|}}\Bigg]

(Summation by parts)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1}(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})

(Last term is
0
)

\displaystyle=\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1}f_{\tau}(s,i).

(Definition of
f_{\tau}(s,i)
)

Because each
f_{\tau}(s,i)
is non-negative by
Lemma

13
, the total sum is bounded below by any single term
\gamma f_{\tau}(s,i)
.
∎

Now we show the following lemma, which upper bounds the value difference based on the potential function.

Lemma 15

(Upper Bound)
.

Let
\hat{f}_{\tau}=\max_{s\in\mathcal{S},i}f_{\tau}(s,i)
. Then,
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{\gamma n}{1-\gamma}\hat{f}_{\tau}
.

Proof.

First, we relate the value difference to the transition difference:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

(Equation
5
)

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})^{-1}\left[(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})-(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})\right]

\displaystyle\quad\cdot(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{*})^{-1}\mathbf{c}

\displaystyle(A^{-1}-B^{-1}=B^{-1}(B-A)A^{-1})

\displaystyle=\gamma(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})^{-1}(P^{*}-P^{\tau})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}.

(Simplifying terms and Equation
5
)

We take the infinity norm of both sides and apply the Neumann series expansion:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left\|(\mathbb{I}-\gamma P^{\tau})^{-1}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|(P^{*}-P^{\tau})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

(Submultiplicativity of
L_{\infty}
norm)

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{k}\left\|(P^{\tau})^{k}\right\|_{\infty}\right)\left\|(P^{*}-P^{\tau})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

(Neumann series, Triangle inequality)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\left\|(P^{*}-P^{\tau})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}.

(Since
P^{\tau}
is stochastic,
\left\|(P^{\tau})^{t}\right\|_{\infty}=1
)

Let
s\in\mathcal{S}
be the state that maximizes the row sum in the remaining infinity norm. Applying summation by parts to this specific row yields:

\displaystyle\left\|(P^{*}-P^{\tau})\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=(\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s}-\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s})^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}

(Definition of the infinity norm)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1}f_{\tau}(s,i)

(Summation by parts as in Lemma
14
)

\displaystyle\leq|\textit{Succ}(s)|\hat{f}_{\tau}

(Since
f_{\tau}(s,i)\leq\hat{f}_{\tau}
)

\displaystyle\leq n\hat{f}_{\tau}

(Since
|\textit{Succ}(s)|\leq n
)

Substituting this bounded row sum back into our norm inequality completes the proof.
∎

B.1.3
Halving of the Cumulative Gap

We now chain the two previous lemmas to show the following Lemma.

Lemma 16
.

Consider two policies
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
. Let
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
i\in[|\textit{Succ}(s)|-1]
be the state and index that maximize the potential function

\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}.

(8)

Proof.

We first show how the lemma’s assumption translates to the potential function. By rearranging the assumed inequality and multiplying both sides by the non-negative value
2(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})
, we obtain:

\displaystyle 2(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau^{\prime}}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})

\displaystyle\geq(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i))(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s_{i+1}})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow 2f_{\tau^{\prime}}(s,i)

\displaystyle\geq f_{\tau}(s,i).

(Definition of
f
)

The main proof follows by chaining the upper bound for policy
\tau
(Lemma
15
) with the lower bound for policy
\tau^{\prime}
(Lemma
14
). We can structure this as a direct sequence of inequalities:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\gamma n}{1-\gamma}f_{\tau}(s,i)

(Upper bound for
\tau
, Lemma
15
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\gamma n}{1-\gamma}f_{\tau^{\prime}}(s,i)

(Based on Lemma’s assumption)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n}{1-\gamma}(\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{\prime}}_{s})

(Lower bound for
\tau^{\prime}
, Lemma
14
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2n}{1-\gamma}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{\prime}}\right\|_{\infty}.

(Definition of the
L_{\infty}
norm)

Rearranging the first and last terms of this chain directly yields the claimed bound.
∎

The following lemma establishes that the difference
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
must decrease by at least a factor of two for at least one state-index pair
(s,i)
within a bounded number of iterations,

, of Algorithm

.

Lemma 17
.

Let
\tau^{0},\tau^{1},\dots
be the sequence of policies generated by Algorithm
2
.
For a specific policy
\tau^{t}

(s,i)=\argmax_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},j\in[|\textit{Succ}(s^{\prime})|-1]}f_{\tau^{t}}(s^{\prime},j)
be the state and index that maximize the potential in
\tau^{t}
. Then, for any subsequent policy
\tau^{l}
with
l>t+L

F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau^{l}}(s,i)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau^{t}}(s,i)\right).

Proof.

Let
(s,i)
be the state and index pair defined in the lemma. Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists some

such that:

F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau^{l}}(s,i)>\frac{1}{2}\left(F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau^{t}}(s,i)\right).

This assumption allows us to invoke Lemma
16
, giving us a lower bound on the value error at step
l
. Simultaneously, the standard convergence property of policy iteration gives us an upper bound. We combine these bounds as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{l}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{l-t}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{t}}\right\|_{\infty}

(Upper bound via Lemma
11
)

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{l}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{*}-\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{t}}\right\|_{\infty}

(Lower bound via Lemma
16
and our assumption)

If
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{t}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}=0
, then
\tau^{t}
is optimal, and the algorithm terminates at iteration
t
. Because the sequence continues to
l>t
, we must have
\left\|\boldsymbol{v}^{\tau^{t}}-\boldsymbol{v}^{*}\right\|_{\infty}>0
. Combining these two inequalities and dividing by this strictly positive term yields:

\displaystyle\gamma^{l-t}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}

(Canceling the positive value error norm)

\displaystyle l-t

\displaystyle\leq\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}\right)

\displaystyle\text{(Taking $\log_{\gamma}$, flipping the inequality since $\gamma<1$)}.

This implies
l-t\leq L
, which directly contradicts our initial premise that
l>t+L
. Thus, the assumption must be false, meaning the gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
must have decreased by at least half.
∎

B.1.4
Combinatorial Bound on the Number of Halvings

The previous Lemma showed that after
L
steps of Algorithm
2
, the difference
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
decreases by at least a factor of
2
. Now we need to show that this decrease only happens at most a polynomial number of iterations. We can show that in fact this only happens
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
times. We thus have the following Lemma, which was shown in
[
4
]
, and we mention it for completeness.

Lemma 18
.

Let
c
be a constant positive integer and let
W
be a finite set of non-negative real numbers. We define the set of all bounded-coefficient subset sums of
W
as:

\mathcal{A}(W)=\left\{\left|\sum_{w\in W}d_{w}w\right|\;\middle|\;d_{w}\in\{-c,\cdots,-1,0,1,\cdots,c\}\right\}.

Additionally, for any set of positive reals
Y
, let the degree
\mathrm{Deg}(Y)
denote the number of unique Maximum Significant bits (MSBs) of the set
Y
where numbers are written in binary, formally given by:

\mathrm{Deg}(Y)=\left|\left\{\lfloor\log_{2}y\rfloor\;\middle|\;y\in Y\right\}\right|.

Then, for any such set
W
, the number of distinct MSBs in
\mathcal{A}(W)
is:

\mathrm{Deg}(\mathcal{A}(W)\setminus\{0\})=\mathcal{O}(|W|\log|W|).

Proof.

We show this using a proof by
[
4
]
; we present the full proof here for completeness. Throughout this proof, all logarithms are taken in base 2. The strategy proceeds in three steps: (i) we introduce a notion of bounded-coefficient linear independence and show that real numbers with sufficiently separated MSBs satisfy it; (ii) we use Siegel’s lemma to extract a small-coefficient linear relation among any
\Theta(|W|\log|W|)
elements of
\mathcal{A}(W)
; (iii) we combine these via a greedy selection on MSBs to derive a contradiction whenever
\mathrm{Deg}(\mathcal{A}(W)\setminus\{0\})
exceeds the claimed bound.

Step 1: MSB separation implies linear independence.

For a positive integer
c^{\prime}
, we call reals
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}

c^{\prime}
-linearly independent
if
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}\neq 0
for every non-zero
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
\|\underline{x}\|_{\infty}\leq c^{\prime}
.

Claim 1
.

There exists a positive integer
D=D(c^{\prime})
such that for any
N\geq 1
, any non-zero reals
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
satisfying

\lfloor\log_{2}|y_{i+1}|\rfloor\geq\lfloor\log_{2}|y_{i}|\rfloor+D\quad\text{for }1\leq i<N

are
c^{\prime}
-linearly independent.

Proof of Claim
1
.

Choose
D
large enough that
2^{D-1}>2c^{\prime}
(e.g.,
D=\lceil\log_{2}c^{\prime}\rceil+3
). The MSB-separation hypothesis implies
|y_{i+1}|/|y_{i}|>2^{D-1}>2c^{\prime}
for every
i
, so in particular
|y_{i+1}|>2|y_{i}|
. Iterating yields
|y_{N-1}|>\sum_{i=1}^{N-2}|y_{i}|
via a geometric sum, hence

|y_{N}|>2c^{\prime}|y_{N-1}|>c^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}|y_{i}|.

Suppose
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}=0
with
\|\underline{x}\|_{\infty}\leq c^{\prime}
. If
x_{N}\neq 0
, then
|x_{N}|\geq 1
and

|x_{N}y_{N}|\geq|y_{N}|>c^{\prime}\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}|y_{i}|\geq\left|\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}x_{i}y_{i}\right|,

contradicting
x_{N}y_{N}=-\sum_{i<N}x_{i}y_{i}
. Hence
x_{N}=0
, and induction on
N
gives
\underline{x}=\underline{0}
.
∎

Step 2: Siegel’s lemma.

We use the following standard application of Siegel’s lemma (see, e.g.,
[
16
]
). There exist absolute constants
n_{0}\geq 4
and
C_{0}>0
such that for every integer
n\geq n_{0}
, every real
c\geq 1
, and every integer matrix
A
with
n
columns,
N
rows satisfying
n\log_{2}n-1\leq N\leq n\log_{2}n
, and
\|A\|_{\infty}\leq c
, there exists a non-zero
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
in the left-kernel of
A
with

\|\underline{x}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}\cdot c.

This follows from the classical bound
\|\underline{x}\|_{\infty}\leq(\|A\|_{\infty}N)^{n/(N-n)}
. For
n\geq n_{0}
sufficiently large, we have
n/(N-n)\leq 1/(\log_{2}n-1-1/n)\leq 1
, which justifies the inequality
(cN)^{n/(N-n)}\leq c\cdot N^{n/(N-n)}
since
c\geq 1
. The remaining factor
N^{n/(N-n)}
converges to
2
as
n\rightarrow\infty
(an absolute constant independent of
c
), and is therefore uniformly bounded above by
C_{0}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. We may also enlarge
n_{0}
to ensure
n_{0}\log_{2}n_{0}-1\geq n_{0}
, so that the row count
N
strictly exceeds the column count
n
and the kernel is guaranteed to be non-trivial.

Step 3: Combining the steps.

Set
c^{\prime}:=\lceil C_{0}c\rceil
and let
D:=D(c^{\prime})
be the constant from Claim
1
; both depend only on
c
. Let
K:=\mathrm{Deg}(\mathcal{A}(W)\setminus\{0\})
.

Base case.
If
|W|<n_{0}
, then
|\mathcal{A}(W)|\leq(2c+1)^{|W|}<(2c+1)^{n_{0}}
, which is a constant depending only on
c
. In particular,
K
is bounded by this constant, and the lemma holds trivially. Henceforth assume
|W|\geq n_{0}
.

Inductive case.
We show
K\leq D\cdot|W|\log_{2}|W|
. Assume for contradiction that
K>D\cdot|W|\log_{2}|W|
. Sort the distinct MSBs of
\mathcal{A}(W)\setminus\{0\}
as
m_{1}<m_{2}<\cdots<m_{K}
. Picking every
D
-th MSB yields
\lceil K/D\rceil>|W|\log_{2}|W|
MSBs with consecutive gaps at least
D
. Truncate to
N:=\lfloor|W|\log_{2}|W|\rfloor
such MSBs, so
|W|\log_{2}|W|-1\leq N\leq|W|\log_{2}|W|
, and pick a representative
y_{j}\in\mathcal{A}(W)\setminus\{0\}
with
\lfloor\log_{2}y_{j}\rfloor
equal to the
j
-th selected MSB. By Claim
1
,
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
are
c^{\prime}
-linearly independent.

On the other hand, by definition of
\mathcal{A}(W)
, each
y_{j}
admits a representation

y_{j}=\left|\sum_{w\in W}d_{j,w}w\right|\quad\text{with }d_{j,w}\in\{-c,\dots,c\}.

Letting
\sigma_{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
be such that
y_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sum_{w}d_{j,w}w
, and setting
\tilde{d}_{j,w}:=\sigma_{j}d_{j,w}
, we still have
|\tilde{d}_{j,w}|\leq c
and
y_{j}=\sum_{w\in W}\tilde{d}_{j,w}w
. Let
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{N\times|W|}
be the matrix with entries
A_{j,w}=\tilde{d}_{j,w}
; then
\|A\|_{\infty}\leq c
. Since
|W|\geq n_{0}
and
|W|\log_{2}|W|-1\leq N\leq|W|\log_{2}|W|
, Step 2 yields a non-zero
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
with
\|\underline{x}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{0}c\leq c^{\prime}
such that
A^{\top}\underline{x}=0
, i.e.,
\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\tilde{d}_{j,w}=0
for every
w\in W
. Consequently,

\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}y_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\sum_{w\in W}\tilde{d}_{j,w}w=\sum_{w\in W}w\sum_{j=1}^{N}x_{j}\tilde{d}_{j,w}=0,

contradicting the
c^{\prime}
-linear independence of
y_{1},\dots,y_{N}
. Therefore
K\leq D\cdot|W|\log_{2}|W|=\mathcal{O}(|W|\log|W|)
.
∎

Finally, we will show that Algorithm
2
is strongly polynomial for
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets. We prove
Theorem

2
by showing it separately for
L_{1}
and
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets.

Proof of
Theorem

2
for
L_{1}
uncertainty sets.

For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, Property
1
describes the structure of the transition probabilities generated by Algorithm
3
. Probability mass is shifted to the highest-value state by fully taking mass from a set of states
Z_{\mathfrak{p}}
and partially taking mass from at most one state
I_{\mathfrak{p}}
. The mass taken from
I_{\mathfrak{p}}
is the leftover budget:
\frac{\delta_{s}}{2}-\sum_{z\in Z_{\mathfrak{p}}}\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,z}
.

When we calculate the cumulative probability
F^{\tau}(s,i)
over any prefix set
S_{i}
, the sum simplifies algebraically. If
I_{\mathfrak{p}}\in S_{i}
, the
-\tfrac{\delta_{s}}{2}
from the partial term cancels the receiver’s
+\tfrac{\delta_{s}}{2}
, while the remaining
\sum_{z\in Z_{\mathfrak{p}}}\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,z}
adds each depleted state’s nominal with coefficient
+1
(regardless of whether
z\in S_{i}
). This ensures that
F^{\tau}(s,i)
is always a linear combination of the nominal probabilities
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,j}
and the budget
\frac{\delta_{s}}{2}
, using only the coefficients
\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
.

Therefore, the difference
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
strictly belongs to the set of unitary signed subset sums
\mathcal{A}(W_{s})
, which is defined over the base set
W_{s}=\left\{\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,j}\mid j\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\cup\left\{\frac{\delta_{s}}{2},1\right\}
.

By Lemma
18
, the number of distinct MSBs for the non-zero elements in
\mathcal{A}(W_{s})
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(|W_{s}|\log|W_{s}|)
. Because the size of the base set is
|W_{s}|\leq n+2
, the total number of possible MSBs is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

From Lemma
17
, we know that every
L=\lceil\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}\right)\rceil
iterations, the maximum cumulative probability gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
must decrease by at least half. Since the optimal values are fixed, this halving directly corresponds to dropping to a lower MSB within the set
\mathcal{A}(W_{s})
.

There are at most
n^{2}
state-index pairs
(s,i)
, and for any specific pair the gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
can drop its MSB at most
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
times. Combining with the
L
iterations per drop yields
n^{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}(n\log n)\cdot L=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log n\cdot L)
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

2
for
L_{\infty}
uncertainty sets.

For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, Property
2
describes the structure of the transition probabilities generated by Algorithm
4
. Probability mass is moved between states under strict
L_{\infty}
constraints. As a result, the final probability
\mathfrak{p}_{s,s^{\prime}}
for any successor state is always formed by adding or subtracting the budget
\delta_{s}
from the nominal probabilities
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,s^{\prime}}
, or by capping at the limits
0
or
1
.

Therefore, the difference between the optimal probability and the current policy’s probability,
\mathfrak{p}^{*}_{s,s^{\prime}}-\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s,s^{\prime}}
, strictly belongs to the set of signed subset sums
\mathcal{A}(X_{s})
with coefficients in
\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
. This set is defined over the base set
X_{s}=\left\{\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,j}\mid j\in\mathcal{S}\right\}\cup\left\{1\right\}\cup\left\{\delta_{s},2\cdot\delta_{s},\cdots,n\cdot\delta_{s}\right\}
.

The multiples
2\delta_{s},\dots,n\delta_{s}
are included because the at-most-one incomplete coordinate, by conservation
\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathfrak{p}^{\tau}_{s,s^{\prime}}=1
, contributes a coefficient
|D_{\mathfrak{p}}|-|R_{\mathfrak{p}}|\in\{-n,\dots,n\}
on
\delta_{s}
. Hence the cumulative difference
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
may carry a coefficient up to
\pm 2n
on
\delta_{s}
, which we encode using a single base element
k\cdot\delta_{s}
with coefficient
\pm 1
. This keeps the coefficient bound in
\mathcal{A}(X_{s})
a constant, as Lemma
18
requires.

By Lemma
18
, the number of distinct MSBs for the non-zero elements in
\mathcal{A}(X_{s})
is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(|X_{s}|\log|X_{s}|)
. Because the size of the base set is
|X_{s}|\leq 2n+1
, the total number of possible MSBs is strictly bounded by
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
.

From our convergence analysis, we know that every
L=\lceil\log_{\gamma}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{2n}\right)\rceil
iterations, the maximum cumulative probability gap
F^{*}(s,i)-F^{\tau}(s,i)
must decrease by at least half. Since the optimal values remain fixed, this halving directly corresponds to dropping to a lower MSB within the set
\mathcal{A}(X_{s})
.

There are exactly
n^{2}
possible state-index pairs
(s,i)
that can dictate this maximum cumulative probability gap. For any specific pair, the associated probability discrepancy can drop its MSB at most
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
times. Because it takes at most
L
steps to force a drop, the total number of policy iteration steps before termination is bounded by
n^{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}(n\log n)\cdot L=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}\log n\cdot L)
. This represents a polynomial number of iterations, completing the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of
Theorem

3
: Complexity of RMC with
L_{p}
Uncertainty Sets

In this section, we analyze the complexity of the Problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets for any constant integer
p>1
.
Since the goal of this section is to show the complexity bounds for this problem, we consider the Turing model of computation, where all the numbers in the input are assumed to be rational.

First, we analyze the
p
-ROOT-SUM complexity class. We prove that this class is polynomially equivalent to the class of
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM where
\gcd(p,q)=1
.
Second, we construct a polynomial-time reduction from the
\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem to the decision version of the main problem, which shows that it is
p
-ROOT-SUM-hard.
Finally, we establish an upper bound on the complexity of the problem. We show that it belongs to the class
\exists\mathbb{R}
. We achieve this by modeling the main problem (computing the exact values and optimal strategy) as an
\exists\mathbb{R}
formula. The corresponding decision problem can then be easily solved by adding a threshold constraint to this formula.

C.1
Equivalence of ROOT-SUM Problems

Our main objective in this subsection is to prove that the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem and the
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM problem (where
\gcd(p,q)=1
) are polynomial-time equivalent.

First, we show that the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem is reducible to the
kp
-ROOT-SUM problem. Next, we introduce a polynomial-time greedy decomposition algorithm that expresses any integer as a sum of
p
-th powers using a logarithmically bounded number of terms. Then, we construct a formal reduction from the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem to the
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM problem. Finally,
Lemma

22
combines these steps to conclude the polynomial-time equivalence of these classes.

In the following lemma, we show that the
kp
-ROOT-SUM problem is harder than
p
-ROOT-SUM problem for any rational number
p
and any constant integer
k>1
:

Lemma 19
.

For any rational number
p
and any constant integer
k>1
, the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem is polynomial-time reducible to the
kp
-ROOT-SUM problem.

Proof.

Let an instance of the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem be given by a sequence of positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
and an integer threshold
\alpha
. We need to decide if the following holds:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt[p]{a_{i}}\geq\alpha

We construct an instance of the
kp
-ROOT-SUM problem. For each index
i
, we define a new value
a^{\prime}_{i}=a_{i}^{k}
. Because
a_{i}
and
k
are integers,
a^{\prime}_{i}
is also an integer.

By the properties of exponents, we have:

\sqrt[kp]{a^{\prime}_{i}}=(a^{\prime}_{i})^{\frac{1}{kp}}=(a_{i}^{k})^{\frac{1}{kp}}=a_{i}^{\frac{k}{kp}}=a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}=\sqrt[p]{a_{i}}\Rightarrow\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt[kp]{a^{\prime}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt[p]{a_{i}}

Therefore, the condition
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt[kp]{a^{\prime}_{i}}\geq\alpha
is true if and only if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt[p]{a_{i}}\geq\alpha
.

Because
k
is a constant, computing
a_{i}^{k}
for all
n
terms takes polynomial time and has a polynomial number of bits. Therefore, the reduction is polynomial.
∎

Now we need an efficient way to write integers as a sum of powers of
p
. The following lemma provides a polynomial-time algorithm to express any integer as a sum of
p
-th powers.

Lemma 20
.

Let
p
be a constant positive integer greater than zero.
Any integer
n
can be written as a sum
n=\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{p}
for some positive integers
a_{i}
,
where the number of terms
k=O(\log\log n)
. Furthermore, the sequence of integers
a_{i}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
time.

Proof.

We construct the sequence
a_{i}
using the greedy approach by
Algorithm

5
.

Algorithm 5

Greedy
p
-th Power Decomposition

1:

Input:
Integer
n>0
, Integer
p>1

2:

Output:
Sequence
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}^{p}=n

3:

n_{0}\leftarrow n

4:

j\leftarrow 0

5:

while

n_{j}>0

do

6:

a_{j+1}\leftarrow\lfloor n_{j}^{1/p}\rfloor

\triangleright
Find the largest integer root

7:

n_{j+1}\leftarrow n_{j}-a_{j+1}^{p}

\triangleright
Update the remainder

8:

j\leftarrow j+1

9:

k\leftarrow j

10:

return

(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k})

Let
n_{0}=n
. For each step
j\geq 0
, if
n_{j}>0
, the algorithm chooses the largest integer
a_{j+1}=\lfloor n_{j}^{1/p}\rfloor
such that
a_{j+1}^{p}\leq n_{j}
, and updates the remainder to
n_{j+1}=n_{j}-a_{j+1}^{p}
. We bound
n_{j+1}
as follows:

\displaystyle n_{j+1}

\displaystyle=n_{j}-a_{j+1}^{p}

(Definition of the
n_{j+1}
)

\displaystyle<(a_{j+1}+1)^{p}-a_{j+1}^{p}

(Since
(a_{j+1}+1)^{p}>n_{j}\geq a_{j+1}^{p}
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\binom{p}{\ell}a_{j+1}^{\ell}

(Binomial expansion)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{p-1}\binom{p}{\ell}\right)a_{j+1}^{p-1}

(Since
a_{j+1}\geq 1
, we have
a_{j+1}^{\ell}\leq a_{j+1}^{p-1}
)

\displaystyle<2^{p}a_{j+1}^{p-1}

(Sum of binomial coefficients)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{p}n_{j}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}

(Since
a_{j+1}\leq n_{j}^{\frac{1}{p}}
)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\log_{2}n_{j+1}

\displaystyle\leq p+\frac{p-1}{p}\log_{2}n_{j}.

(Properties of logarithms)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\log_{2}n_{j+1}-p^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p-1}{p}(\log_{2}n_{j}-p^{2}).

(Subtracting
p^{2}
from both sides)

This shows that
\log_{2}n_{j}-p^{2}
decreases geometrically by the constant factor
0<\frac{p-1}{p}<1
. Therefore, the number of steps required for
\log_{2}n_{j}
to drop below the constant
p^{2}+1
is
O(\log\log n_{j})
.

Once
\log_{2}n_{j}\leq p^{2}+1
, we have
n_{j}\leq 2^{p^{2}+1}
. Since
n_{j}
is an integer that decreases by at least
1
in each step, the algorithm will reach
0
in at most
2^{p^{2}+1}
steps. Since
p
is a constant, this takes
O(1)
steps. Because
n_{0}=n
, the total number of terms
k=O(\log\log n)+O(1)=O(\log\log n)
.

Finally, at each step
j
, computing the largest integer root
a_{j+1}=\lfloor n_{j}^{1/p}\rfloor
takes polynomial time with respect to the bit-size of
n_{j}
. Because there are
O(\log\log n)
steps, the entire sequence
a_{i}
is computed in polynomial time.
∎

Next, we show that we can change the root from
p
to
p/q
without changing the complexity of the problem. The following lemma provides a polynomial-time reduction for this step.

Lemma 21
.

For any constant positive integers
p
and
q
such that
\gcd(p,q)=1
, the
p
-ROOT-SUM problem is polynomial-time reducible to the
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM problem.

Proof.

Because
\gcd(p,q)=1
, modular arithmetic guarantees there exists a positive integer

such that

divides

. Because
k<q
, we can treat

as a constant.

Let an instance of the
p

and an integer threshold
\alpha
. We want to decide if:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\alpha

We multiply both sides of the inequality by

.
We rewrite the new term

as follows:

\displaystyle w\cdot a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=z_{i}\cdot a_{i}^{k}\cdot a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}

(Factoring out

from

\displaystyle=z_{i}\cdot a_{i}^{\frac{kp+1}{p}}

(Combining the exponents of

)

\displaystyle=z_{i}\left(a_{i}^{\frac{kp+1}{q}}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}

(Properties of exponents)

\displaystyle=z_{i}y_{i}^{\frac{q}{p}},

(Substituting integer
y_{i}:=a_{i}^{\frac{kp+1}{q}}
)

where
y_{i}
is an integer because
q
divides
kp+1
.

By
Lemma

20
, we can decompose each integer

into a sum of
q
-th powers of integers
c_{i,j}
. This gives
z_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}c_{i,j}^{q}
, where the number of terms
m_{i}
is in
O(\log\log z_{i})
. We define
b_{i,j}:=c_{i,j}^{p}y_{i}
and simplify the main inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w\cdot a_{i}^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq w\alpha

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}y_{i}^{\frac{q}{p}}\geq w\alpha

(Substituting the term equivalence)

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}c_{i,j}^{q}\right)y_{i}^{\frac{q}{p}}\geq w\alpha

(Applying Lemma
20
to decompose
z_{i}
)

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\left(c_{i,j}^{p}y_{i}\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}\geq w\alpha

(Distributing
y_{i}^{\frac{q}{p}}
inside the inner sum)

\displaystyle\iff\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}b_{i,j}^{\frac{q}{p}}\geq w\alpha.

(Substituting
b_{i,j}
)

This final inequality is an instance of the
\frac{p}{q}
-ROOT-SUM problem with the threshold
w\alpha
.

Because
k
is a constant, computing and storing
w,z_{i},y_{i}
takes polynomial time and memory in the bit-size of
a_{i}
. Furthermore, the number of new terms is
O(n\log\log z_{i})
. By
Lemma

20

c_{i,j}
is computed in polynomial time. Finally,
b_{i,j}
can be computed by the arithmetic operations. Therefore, this reduction is valid and runs in polynomial time.
∎

By combining the previous reductions, we can establish the equivalence result for this subsection. This shows that the denominator in the fractional root in the

and

-ROOT-SUM problem are polynomial-time equivalent. In other words, the denominator does not matter in the complexity.

Proof.

By Lemma

, the

-ROOT-SUM problem.
Similarly, because

the

C.2
Proving Hardness via Reduction

By
Lemma

, the

-ROOT-SUM problem is polynomial-time equivalent to the

-ROOT-SUM problem. We now formalize the reduction from the

-ROOT-SUM problem to the decision version of the problem
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets. Formally, we do the following reduction:

Given an integer constant

\frac{p}{p-1}
-ROOT-SUM problem asks to decide if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\geq\alpha

in an RMC
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},c,\textit{Succ},\mathcal{P})

uncertainty sets and a constant discount factor

Reduction:

First, we introduce the following scaled variables to construct the RMC:

•

\forall i\in[n]:b_{i}:=2^{p}a_{i}
.

•

\forall i\in[n]:x_{i}:=b_{i}/2=2^{p-1}a_{i}

K:=2^{p-1}\alpha

By Lemma

as the sum of
p

M^{*}
terms. This preserves the sum.
By

We construct the RMC

•

\mathcal{S}:=\{s_{0}\}\cup\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{\bot_{i,1}^{+},\dots,\bot_{i,M^{*}}^{+},\bot_{i,1}^{-},\dots,\bot_{i,M^{*}}^{-}\}

The states consist of an initial state

0&\text{if }s\in\{s_{0},s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}.\end{cases}

•

\textit{Succ}\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{S}}

\mathcal{P}\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow 2^{\Delta(\mathcal{S})}

The successor mapping and
L_{p}

–

Initial State:

\textit{Succ}(s_{0}):=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
. The nominal distribution
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{0}}
is uniform over the transient states (
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{0}}(s_{i})=\frac{1}{n}

\delta_{s_{0}}:=0

–

Transient States:

\textit{Succ}(s_{i}):=\{\bot_{i,1}^{+},\dots,\bot_{i,M^{*}}^{+},\bot_{i,1}^{-},\dots,\bot_{i,M^{*}}^{-}\}

s_{i}

1/2M^{*}

–

Absorbing States:

\textit{Succ}(\bot_{i,k}^{+}):=\{\bot_{i,k}^{+}\},\textit{Succ}(\bot_{i,k}^{-}):=\{\bot_{i,k}^{-}\}

\lambda:=\frac{\gamma^{2}\delta K}{n}
.

Now, we analyze the values of the states in RMC
\mathcal{M}
. We first calculate the values for the absorbing states.

Lemma 23
.

For the absorbing states
\bot_{i,k}^{+}
and
\bot_{i,k}^{-}
, their values are
u_{i,k}^{p-1}
and
-u_{i,k}^{p-1}
, respectively.

Proof.

We first prove the claim for the positive absorbing state
\bot_{i,k}^{+}
. By construction, the absorbing state deterministically transitions to itself because its uncertainty radius is zero. Thus, the Bellman equation simplifies to:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{+})

\displaystyle=c(\bot_{i,k}^{+})+\gamma\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{+})

(Bellman equation for absorbing states)

\displaystyle\implies\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{+})

\displaystyle=\frac{c(\bot_{i,k}^{+})}{1-\gamma}

(Rearranging for
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\gamma)u_{i,k}^{p-1}}{1-\gamma}

(Substituting the defined cost)

\displaystyle=u_{i,k}^{p-1}.

(Canceling the
1-\gamma
terms)

By symmetry, substituting the cost
c(\bot_{i,k}^{-})=-(1-\gamma)u_{i,k}^{p-1}
for the negative absorbing state into the same derivation yields
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{-})=-u_{i,k}^{p-1}
.
∎

Using the values of the absorbing states, we can determine the value of the transient states. The following lemma calculates this value.

Lemma 24
.

For the transient state
s_{i}
, let
\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}
be the vector of values of its absorbing states. Then,
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s_{i})=\gamma\delta\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
.

Proof.

The Bellman equation for
s_{i}
yields:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s_{i})

\displaystyle=c(s_{i})+\gamma\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{P}(s_{i})}\tau^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

(Definition of the Bellman equation)

\displaystyle=\gamma\max_{\tau\in\mathcal{P}(s_{i})}\tau^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

\displaystyle(c(s_{i})=0)

\displaystyle=\gamma\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathbb{R}^{2M^{*}}\\
\|d\|_{p}\leq\delta\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}d=0\\
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}+d\geq 0\end{subarray}}\ (\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}+d)^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

(
\mathcal{P}(s_{i})
is an
L_{p}
uncertainty set)

\displaystyle=\gamma\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\in\mathbb{R}^{2M^{*}}\\
\|d\|_{p}\leq\delta\\
\mathbf{1}^{\top}d=0\\
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}+d\geq 0\end{subarray}}\ d^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

(
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}=0
as
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}
is uniform and
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{+})+\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(\bot_{i,k}^{-})=0
)

Because
\frac{p-1}{p}+\frac{1}{p}=1
, Hölder’s inequality states that the inner product is bounded from above
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle d^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\leq\|d\|_{p}\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}\leq\delta\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}.

To prove this upper bound is achievable, we construct the optimal perturbation

:

d^{*}_{j}=\delta\frac{\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j})|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}

This vector satisfies all constraints:

•

(
\|d^{*}\|_{p}=\delta
): By the definition of the
p
norm we have:

\|d^{*}\|_{p}^{p}=\sum_{j}|d^{*}_{j}|^{p}=\frac{\delta^{p}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{p}{p-1}}}\sum_{j}|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}

By the definition of the
L_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
norm, the sum is equal to the denominator. Thus,
\|d^{*}\|_{p}^{p}=\delta^{p}
. Therefore,
\|d^{*}\|_{p}=\delta
,

•

(
\mathbf{1}^{\top}d^{*}=0
): Since
|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}=(u_{i,k}^{p-1})^{\frac{1}{p-1}}=u_{i,k}
, and
\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}
is symmetric, the components of
d^{*}
form canceling pairs
\pm u_{i,k}
.

•

\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i}}+d^{*}\geq 0
: Since
\|d^{*}\|_{p}=\delta
, and
\left\|d^{*}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\|d^{*}\|_{p}
, the maximum negative perturbation is at most
-\delta
. Because
\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s_{i},j}=\frac{1}{2M^{*}}=\delta
, the minimum possible probability is
0
.

Finally, we evaluate the inner product to confirm the maximum bound:

\displaystyle d^{*\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{2M^{*}}\left(\delta\frac{\operatorname{sign}(\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j})|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}\right)\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}

(Expanding the dot product)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}\sum_{j=1}^{2M^{*}}|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}_{j}|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}

(
\operatorname{sign}(x)\cdot x=|x|
and
|x|^{\frac{1}{p-1}}|x|=|x|^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{1}{p-1}}}\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}^{\frac{p}{p-1}}

(Definition of the
L_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
norm)

\displaystyle=\delta\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}.

(Simplifying exponents)

Substituting this back yields
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s_{i})=\gamma\delta\|\boldsymbol{v}^{(i)}\|_{\frac{p}{p-1}}
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 25

For any transient state

,

(Definition of the

\displaystyle=\left(2\sum_{k=1}^{M^{*}}\left(u_{i,k}^{p-1}\right)^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}

(Grouping symmetric pairs

and

\displaystyle=\left(2\sum_{k=1}^{M^{*}}u_{i,k}^{p}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{p}}

C.3
ETR Upper Bound

In this section, we establish that the decision version of the
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
problem with
L_{p}
uncertainty sets belongs to the complexity class
\exists\mathbb{R}
for any integer
p>1
. Furthermore, we present an approach to solve the main computational problem by calling an
\exists\mathbb{R}
solver.

The main
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
problem asks to find the value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{\mathcal{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
and a best adversary policy
\tau^{*}\colon\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\Delta(\mathcal{S})
. The decision version of this problem asks, for a given state
s
and a threshold
\lambda
, whether
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s)\geq\lambda
. We will focus on formalizing the main problem as a polynomial-size
\exists\mathbb{R}
formula, which can be solved via an
\exists\mathbb{R}
solver. Once we find the exact answer using this approach, we can simply solve the decision problem by adding the threshold constraint to the formula.

Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT).

Before analyzing the adversary’s policy, we recall the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) technique
[
8
]
. This technique provides an equation to find the optimal solution in convex problems. Suppose we want to minimize a function
f(x)
, subject to inequality rules
g_{i}(x)\leq 0
and equality rules
h_{j}(x)=0
.

To apply KKT, we introduce a penalty variable for each rule. We use
\mu_{i}
for each inequality rule and
\nu_{j}
for each equality rule. We combine all conditions into a master equation called the Lagrangian:

L(x,\mu,\nu)=f(x)+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}g_{i}(x)+\sum_{j}\nu_{j}h_{j}(x)

The KKT conditions state that the optimal solution
x^{*}
must satisfy four conditions:

1.

The solution
x^{*}
must satisfy all the original rules (
g_{i}(x^{*})\leq 0
and
h_{j}(x^{*})=0
).

2.

The penalty variables for the inequalities cannot be negative (
\mu_{i}\geq 0
).

3.

If
g_{i}(x^{*})<0
, its penalty is ignored (
\mu_{i}=0
). In other words,
\mu_{i}g_{i}(x^{*})=0
for every rule.

4.

The derivative of Lagrangian must be zero (
\nabla f(x^{*})+\sum_{i}\mu_{i}\nabla g_{i}(x^{*})+\sum_{j}\nu_{j}\nabla h_{j}(x^{*})=0
).

Slater’s Condition.

The KKT conditions are necessary and sufficient for global optimality if strong duality holds. A standard constraint to establish strong duality is
Slater’s condition

[
8
]
.
Slater’s condition requires the existence of a feasible point
x
in the interior of the problem domain. Formally,
x
must strictly satisfy all non-linear inequality constraints (
g_{i}(x)<0
), but only needs to weakly satisfy linear inequality constraints (
g_{i}(x)\leq 0
) and equality constraints (
h_{j}(x)=0
). Satisfying this condition guarantees that the KKT equations are sufficient to find the optimal solution.

To prove that our problem can be formalized via an
\exists\mathbb{R}
formula, we need to express all conditions using polynomials. We first show that the
L_{p}
uncertainty set constraints can be written as a system of polynomial inequalities.

Lemma 26
.

For any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the condition
\tau_{s}\in\mathcal{P}(s)
can be expressed as a system of polynomial constraints.

Proof.

By definition, the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}(s)
contains the transition probabilities such that
\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}|\tau_{s,t}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}|^{p}\leq\delta_{s}^{p}
.

Because the absolute value function is not a polynomial, we introduce auxiliary variables
y_{s,t}\in\mathbb{R}
to bound the absolute differences. For each
t\in\mathcal{S}
, we add the following pair of linear inequalities:

\displaystyle\tau_{s,t}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}-y_{s,t}

\displaystyle\leq 0,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}-\tau_{s,t}-y_{s,t}

\displaystyle\leq 0.

These inequalities force
y_{s,t}\geq|\tau_{s,t}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}|
.
Note that the non-negativity of the auxiliary variables is logically implied.
We replace the
L_{p}
constraint with the following inequality:

\displaystyle\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}y_{s,t}^{p}-\delta_{s}^{p}\leq 0.

Since
p>1
is a constant integer,
y_{s,t}^{p}
is a valid polynomial term. Thus, the condition is expressed by a system of polynomial inequalities.
∎

Next, we formulate the adversary’s optimization problem. By applying the KKT conditions, we can describe the optimal worst-case transition distribution using a polynomial system.

Lemma 27
.

For any fixed value vector
\boldsymbol{v}
, the optimal transition distribution
\tau_{s}
chosen by the adversary can be defined by a system of polynomial equations and inequalities using KKT conditions.

Proof.

The adversary wants to choose the worst-case transition probabilities
\tau_{s}
to maximize the expected cost
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
, which is equivalent to minimizing
-\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
.

Using Lemma
26
, we can write the feasibility condition
\tau_{s}\in\mathcal{P}(s)
using auxiliary variables
y_{s,t}
. Thus, the adversary’s goal can be written as the following optimization problem:

Minimize:

\displaystyle-\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}\tau_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}

Subject to:

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}\tau_{s,t}\right)-1=0

(Probabilities must sum to 1)

\displaystyle-\tau_{s,t}\leq 0

(Probabilities cannot be negative)

\displaystyle\tau_{s,t}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}-y_{s,t}\leq 0

(Upper bound for distance)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}-\tau_{s,t}-y_{s,t}\leq 0

(Lower bound for distance)

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}y_{s,t}^{p}\right)-\delta_{s}^{p}\leq 0

(Total
L_{p}
distance limit)

This is a convex optimization problem because the goal function is linear and all constraints are convex. To prove that the KKT technique finds the true optimum, we verify that the problem has Slater’s condition.
We evaluate this in two cases based on
\delta_{s}
:

Case 1 (
\delta_{s}>0
):
We must find a strictly feasible point where the inequality constraints do not touch the exact edge (
<0
). We choose the default probabilities (
\tau_{s,t}=\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}
) and set the auxiliary variables to zero (
y_{s,t}=0
).

•

All of the linear constraints are satisfied by the definition as

\tau=\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}
is a valid probability, and all lower bounds and upper bounds are zero.

•

The non-linear distance limit evaluates to

(\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}0^{p})-\delta_{s}^{p}=-\delta_{s}^{p}
.
Because
\delta_{s}>0
, this inequality strictly satisfies.

Because we found a valid choice safely inside the boundaries, Slater’s condition is met.

Case 2 (
\delta_{s}=0
)
: In this case, the uncertainty set
\mathcal{P}(s)
is a single point with the nominal distribution. Therefore, the optimal transition distribution is the nominal probabilities (
\tau_{s,t}=\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}
). Because there is no optimization here, we bypass the KKT formulation in this case. Instead, we express this deterministic optimal policy using linear equality constraints (
\tau_{s,t}-\hat{\mathfrak{p}}_{s,t}=0
). Since linear equations are valid polynomial equations, the optimal policy is defined by a polynomial system.

Because Slater’s condition holds, the optimum is characterized by the KKT conditions. By assigning penalty multipliers to the constraints, we construct the Lagrangian
L
. The fourth KKT condition requires the partial derivatives of
L
with respect to
\tau_{s,t}
and
y_{s,t}
to be zero.

Because the objective and all but the last constraint are linear, their partial derivatives evaluate to simple constants. The only non-linear term in the Lagrangian is the distance limit, which contains
\sum_{t\in\textit{Succ}(s)}y_{s,t}^{p}
. Its derivative with respect to
y_{s,t}
is
py_{s,t}^{p-1}
.

Since
p>1
is an integer, the exponent
(p-1)
is a non-negative integer, meaning no fractional powers are introduced. Therefore, the KKT system consists of polynomial equations and inequalities.
∎

Finally, we combine the Bellman equations and the KKT conditions into a single system. The following lemma proves that this combined polynomial system identifies the optimal solution.

Lemma 28
.

Let
\Sigma
be the system of polynomial equations and inequalities formed by combining the Bellman equations and the KKT conditions for all states:

1.

For every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the Bellman equation holds:
\boldsymbol{v}_{s}=c_{s}+\gamma\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t}
.

2.

For every state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, the KKT polynomial system derived in Lemma
27
holds for
\tau_{s}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
.

A tuple
(\boldsymbol{v},\tau,y,\mu,\nu)
is a valid solution to
\Sigma
if and only if
\boldsymbol{v}
is the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
and
\tau
is an optimal adversary policy
\tau^{*}
.

Proof.

First, we show that the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
and the optimal worst-case policy
\tau^{*}
produce a valid solution to
\Sigma
.
By
Lemma

9
, the robust Bellman operator has a unique fixed point. Thus, the true optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
exists.
For this
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, there exists an optimal adversary policy
\tau^{*}
that achieves the worst-case transition, satisfying
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau^{*}_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{t}=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}(s)}p^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
.
By substituting this into the first part of
\Sigma
, we get
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{s}=c_{s}+\gamma\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau^{*}_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}^{*}_{t}
, which satisfies the Bellman equations.

Moreover, because
\tau^{*}_{s}
maximizes the expected cost for
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
, it is the optimal solution to the inner minimization problem in Lemma
27
. Because this inner problem is convex and satisfies Slater’s condition, strong duality holds. This guarantees that the KKT conditions are necessary for optimality. Therefore, there exist real auxiliary variables
y
and KKT multipliers
\mu,\nu
that satisfy the KKT system for
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
and
\tau^{*}_{s}
. Thus, the tuple
(\boldsymbol{v}^{*},\tau^{*},y,\mu,\nu)
forms a valid solution to
\Sigma
.

Second, suppose we have a solution
(\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime},\tau^{\prime},y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime})
for
\Sigma
. Because
\tau^{\prime}_{s}
satisfies the KKT conditions for
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}_{s}
, and Slater’s condition holds,
\tau^{\prime}_{s}
is the true worst-case transition distribution for
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
.
This means the inner product reaches the maximum:
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{S}}\tau^{\prime}_{s,t}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}_{t}=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}(s)}p^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
.
Substituting this into the Bellman constraint of
\Sigma
gives:

\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}_{s}=c_{s}+\gamma\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}(s)}p^{\top}\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}

This means
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
is a fixed point of the robust Bellman operator. By
Lemma

9
, this operator has a unique fixed point. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{v}^{\prime}
must be the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
. Consequently,
\tau^{\prime}
is an optimal adversary policy.
∎

We now have a complete polynomial system
\Sigma
that finds the exact value vector and a best policy for the environment. Since the decision problem asks whether
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s)\geq\lambda
, we can answer it by adding this linear constraint to the system
\Sigma
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
-Item
2
.

By
Lemma

28
, the polynomial system
\Sigma
identifies the optimal value vector
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}
and the optimal policy
\tau^{*}
as its real solutions.

The system
\Sigma
is constructed entirely using real variables, polynomial equations, and polynomial inequalities, where all coefficients are rational. By definition, finding a solution to a system of polynomial equations and inequalities over the real numbers is a problem in the existential theory of the reals. Thus, the main
\mathsf{DiscRMC}
problem can be solved via an
\exists\mathbb{R}
solver.
Moreover, by adding the constraint
\boldsymbol{v}^{*}(s)\geq\lambda
to
\Sigma
, we can evaluate the threshold condition. Therefore, the decision problem belongs to the complexity class
\exists\mathbb{R}
.
∎

Appendix D
Additional Experimental Details

Benchmarks.

We evaluate our algorithm on five benchmarks. For each benchmark, we scale the state-space size up to
n=|\mathcal{S}|=256
. Reward is represented as negated cost.

•

Gridworld
(Adapted from
[
2
]
). We consider a
k\times k
grid navigation task containing
n=k^{2}
states. The agent starts at the top-left
(0,0)
and navigates toward an absorbing goal at the bottom-right
(k-1,k-1)
to receive a continuous +1 reward. Actions consist of the four cardinal directions. The environment dynamics are stochastic: intended movements succeed with a probability of 0.8, while slipping perpendicularly occurs with a 0.1 probability in either direction. Movements into boundaries result in self-loops. An absorbing trap is placed at
x=\lfloor(k-1)/2\rfloor,y=k-1-x
, which imposes a continuous -1 penalty. All other transitions carry a standard step penalty of -0.01.

•

Inventory Management.
(Adapted from
[
21
]
). The state space
s\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
denotes the current inventory level. Let
d_{\max}=\max(1,\lfloor(n-1)/2\rfloor)
represent the maximum expected demand. At each step, the agent selects an order quantity from
\mathcal{A}=\{0,\operatorname{round}(d_{\max}/2),d_{\max}\}
, where
\operatorname{round}(x)
rounds to the nearest integer, with half-integers rounded to the nearest even integer. Customer demand is modeled as a discrete triangular distribution supported on
\{0,\dots,d_{\max}\}
with a peak at
m=\lfloor d_{\max}/2\rfloor
and unnormalized weights
w(d)=m-|d-m|+1
. The reward function evaluates the expected profit under this distribution, assuming a unit revenue of 1, a unit holding cost of 0.1, and a unit ordering cost of 0.5.

•

Machine Replacement
(adapted by
[
23
]
). The state space
s\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
tracks the machine’s degradation level, starting pristine at
s=0
and fully broken at the absorbing state
s=n-1
. The action space consists of 3 options:
operate
,
repair
, and
replace
. The
operate
action causes the machine to degrade to
s+1
with a 1/3 probability or remain in
s
with a 2/3 probability. The
repair
action improves the condition to
s-1
with a 3/4 probability, failing and remaining in
s
with a 1/4 probability. The
replace
action deterministically resets the machine to
s=0
. Operating provides a state-dependent reward defined as
\frac{n-1-s}{n-1}
, whereas the
repair
and
replace
actions impose fixed penalties of -0.25 and -0.5.

•

GARNET
(adapted from
[
3
]
). Generalized Artificial Random Networks serve as unstructured random MDP baselines. We consider a state space of size
n
, where each state has 4 actions and a fixed transition branching factor of
b=3
. The successor set
\textit{Succ}(s,a)\subseteq\mathcal{S}
of size
b
is drawn uniformly at random for every state-action pair. We assign normalized integer weights
w(s,a,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{U}\{1,1000\}
to all
s^{\prime}\in\textit{Succ}(s,a)
. The nominal transition probabilities are calculated as
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)=\frac{w(s,a,s^{\prime})}{\sum_{s^{\prime\prime}}w(s,a,s^{\prime\prime})}
. Immediate rewards are given by uniformly sampled integers
r(s,a)\sim\mathcal{U}\{0,10\}
.

•

Long Chain
. A construction introduced in this paper to show a
\Theta(n)
lower bound on policy iteration under a fixed discount factor
\gamma
. With chain length
k
(so
n=2k+1
), the state space consists of
k

path
states
s_{0},\dots,s_{k-1}
,
k
absorbing
leaf
states
l_{0},\dots,l_{k-1}
, and an absorbing sink
s_{\mathrm{end}}
. From each path state
s_{i}
, action
path
deterministically advances to
s_{i+1}
(or to
s_{\mathrm{end}}
when
i=k-1
), while action
leaf
jumps to
l_{i}
. Per-step rewards are
0
on path states,
1
on leaves, and
\gamma^{-(k+1)}
on the sink; the inflated sink reward is calibrated so that
path
is optimal at every state by a unit margin. Initialized with
leaf
everywhere, policy iteration flips one path state per outer step, requiring
k=\Theta(n)
iterations to converge. Although the sink reward grows in
k
, its bit-size remains
\mathcal{O}(k\log(1/\gamma))
, which is polynomial in the input size for any constant
\gamma
.
