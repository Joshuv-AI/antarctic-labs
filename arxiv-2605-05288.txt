Charge Scrambling in Strong-to-Weak Spontaneous Symmetry Breaking
Jong Yeon Lee

Abstract
Strong-to-weak spontaneous symmetry breaking (SWSSB) is diagnosed by nonlinear correlators, but its direct static implication for conserved charge fluctuations is not automatic. We show that, for continuous symmetries, long-range Rényi-1 correlator, together with a sufficiently rapid approach to its nonzero asymptotic value, forces subsystem charge indefiniteness: the block-charge variance has an extensive lower bound; equivalently, the truncated symmetry expectation has extensive curvature. This gives a precise static fluctuation footprint of charge scrambling. We construct examples to show that the implication is conditional and non-reversible: dephased superfluids retain Rényi-1 SWSSB with subextensive charge variance when the Rényi-1 tail is too slow, while sparse fixed-charge projectors have extensive charge variance but no local charge-transfer Rényi-1 order or long-range conditional mutual information. Finally, we introduce a twist overlap correlator, which serves as an analogue of charge variance applicable to both discrete and continuous symmetries. This naturally decomposes local block-charge fluctuations into strong- and weak-symmetry channels. We found that the weak-symmetry channel isolates coherent charge fluctuations and is directly related to the Wigner–Yanase skew information. Taken together, these results give a unified understanding for distinguishing nonlinear SWSSB order, local charge indefiniteness, and coherent charge fluctuations.

This dynamical connection raises a sharper question. The hydrodynamic mode is a statement about linearized charge dynamics, whereas SWSSB is diagnosed by nonlinear correlators of the mixed state. Thus one should not expect SWSSB alone to imply a hydrodynamic response without identifying the additional static fluctuation content carried by the steady state. At the microscopic level, the long-lived modes found in strongly symmetric Lindbladians are closely tied to the low-momentum static charge structure factor of the steady state
31

7

16

The static structure factor is an equal-time property of the state alone, while the susceptibility is a causal response function and therefore depends on additional dynamical or Hamiltonian data. In thermal equilibrium they are related by fluctuation-dissipation relations, but no such relation is guaranteed for generic mixed states or nonequilibrium steady states.
It is therefore important to better understand the purely static part that plays the key role in connecting SWSSB and hydrodynamics.

In this work we isolate a static fluctuation component of charge scrambling. For a continuous symmetry, the charge in a region

We prove that a long-range Rényi-1 correlator, together with sufficiently rapid convergence to its asymptotic value, enforces such subsystem charge indefiniteness. The converse, however, is not true in general. We construct examples with extensive charge fluctuations but no SWSSB, as well as examples in which SWSSB does not enforce an extensive charge variance because the approach to the long-distance limit is slow.
These results clarify the precise conditions under which SWSSB implies a nonzero static structure factor.

We then extend the discussion to discrete symmetries by introducing a Rényi-1

It also connects directly to the Wigner-Yanase skew information, which isolates the coherent part of block-charge fluctuations

Example 6

(Dephased superfluid state)

If we fully dephase the aforementioned fixed-charge superfluid state in the occupation basis, we obtain a strongly symmetric state with the same diagonal elements as the original superfluid state. As this state is diagonal, the off-diagonal correlator

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}

III
Twist overlap

In the previous section, we identified conditions under which the subsystem charge variance is extensive, providing a natural diagnostic of charge scrambling. This diagnostic, however, has two issues. First, it does not distinguish whether the fluctuation has purely classical or quantum coherent origin. Second, it is intrinsically tied to continuous symmetries. The underlying idea nevertheless extends to discrete symmetries once one replaces the subsystem charge by the corresponding truncated symmetry action. Indeed, the subsystem charge variance may be viewed as probing the response to the truncated symmetry transformation in Eq. (
4
). For a group element
g\in G
, let

\displaystyle U_{g,A}:=\prod_{i\in A}U_{g,i}

(33)

denote the symmetry action restricted to a subregion
A
. This operator is often referred to as a disorder operator
17
;
10
. We now introduce its Rényi-1 analogue, which is visualized in Fig.
2
.

Definition 8

(Twist overlap)
.

Given a state
\rho
, we define a
twist overlap

\chi_{g,A}^{s/w}
on subregion
A
as

\displaystyle\chi^{w}_{g,A}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\sqrt{\rho}\|U^{\vphantom{*}}_{g,A}\otimes U_{g,A}^{*}\|\sqrt{\rho}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=\mathrm{tr}\big(\sqrt{\rho}U_{g,A}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sqrt{\rho}U_{g,A}^{\dagger}\big),

\displaystyle\chi^{s}_{g,A}

\displaystyle:=\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle\sqrt{\rho}\|U^{\vphantom{*}}_{g,A}\otimes\mathrm{Id}\|\sqrt{\rho}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=\mathrm{tr}\big(\rho U_{g,A}\big).

(34)

Both correspond to the disorder operators in the purified state:
\chi^{s}
against strong symmetry, and
\chi^{w}
against weak symmetry.
However, there is an important subtlety.
Following the discussion in Example.
7
, the volume-law decay of the strong twist overlap
\chi^{s}_{g,A}
is not sufficient to diagnose SWSSB. This is contrasted with one-dimensional ground states of local Hamiltonian, where volume-law decay of the disorder operator indicates ordering
25
. On the other hand, the weak twist overlap
\chi^{w}_{g,A}
is a genuinely nonlinear diagnostic. In particular, it captures the local symmetry charge indefiniteness originated from coherent part of the fluctuation.

Example 9

(Subtlety of the disorder operator)
.

Consider the prototypical model studied in Ref.
22
, namely a two-dimensional spin system under strongly
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-symmetric decoherence.
Let
U_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}X_{i}
.
Under the Kramers-Wannier duality,
\chi^{s}_{A}
maps to the loop expectation value along the boundary of
A
in the decohered toric code model
8
.
However, upon increasing the decoherence strength, which maps to the onsite
Z
error, the loop expectation value always decays exponentially with the perimeter of
A
, even in the SWSSB phase. Thus, there is no universal order-vs-disorder dichotomy
25
. On the other hand,
\chi^{w}
remains insensitive to the decoherence.

The weak-symmetry twist overlap
\chi^{w}
is closely tied to the Wigner-Yanase skew information
41
;
28
;
14
. Given an additive conserved quantity
O
, the Wigner-Yanase skew information is defined as

\displaystyle I_{\rm WY}(\rho,O)

\displaystyle:=-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{tr}\Big([O,\sqrt{\rho}]^{2}\Big).

(35)

It measures the noncommutativity between
\rho
and
O
, and hence isolates the coherent, or asymmetric, part of the
O
-fluctuation. In particular, it ignores classical spread among different
O
-sectors:
I_{\rm WY}(\rho,O)=0
whenever
[\rho,O]=0
, while for pure states it reduces to the ordinary variance,
I_{\rm WY}(\rho,O)=\textnormal{Var}_{\rho}(O)
. Under symmetry-respecting (
O
-covariant) channels, it monotonically decreases, acting like a measure of quantum coherence resource. Indeed, it constrains the amount of coherence relative to
O
that can be distilled
29
. Finally, the skew information has metrological implications by providing two-sided bounds on the quantum Fisher information
11
.

For
U(1)
symmetry, where
g
is parametrized by an angular variable
\theta
, differentiating
\chi^{w}_{\theta,A}
gives

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\chi^{w}_{A}

\displaystyle=i\mathrm{tr}\Big(\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\theta Q_{A}}\big[Q_{A},\sqrt{\rho}\big]e^{-i\theta Q_{A}}\Big)\mapsto_{\theta=0}0,

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}^{2}\chi^{w}_{A}

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{tr}\Big(\sqrt{\rho}e^{i\theta Q_{A}}\big[Q_{A},\big[Q_{A},\sqrt{\rho}\big]\big]e^{-i\theta Q_{A}}\Big)

\displaystyle\mapsto_{\theta=0}\mathrm{tr}\Big([Q_{A},\sqrt{\rho}]^{2}\Big)=-2I_{\rm WY}(\rho,Q_{A}).

(36)

Applying the form derived in Eq. (
4
) for
Q_{A}^{-}
in the doubled Hilbert space, together with the fact that
\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle Q_{A}^{-}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=0
for weakly symmetric states,
we can rewrite the Wigner-Yanase skew information as

\displaystyle I_{\rm WY}(\rho,Q_{A})=\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{Var}(Q_{A}^{-}).

(37)

Therefore, the fluctuation of
Q_{A}^{-}
measures the
coherent
part of the charge variance; somewhat intuitively, the fluctuation of
Q_{A}^{-}
cannot be larger than the fluctuation of
Q_{A}^{+}
as proven in Appendix
B
:

Lemma 10

(
Q^{+}
fluctuations bound
Q^{-}
fluctuations)
.

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}(Q^{-}_{A})\leq\textnormal{Var}(Q^{+}_{A}).

(38)

In the earlier example of the classical fixed-charge ensemble, the charge variance originates entirely from the classical mixing between different local charge configurations.
As noted in Table
1
, Wigner-Yanase skew information vanishes for them since
[\rho,Q_{A}]\,{=}\,0
. On the other hand, for pure states,
I_{\rm WY}\,{=}\,\textrm{Var}(Q_{A})
, which indicates that
\textrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{-})\,{=}\,\textrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{+})\,{=}\,2\textrm{Var}(Q_{A})
.

IV
Discussion

In this work, we identified static conditions under which SWSSB entails charge scrambling. The examples show that neither direction is automatic, as expected from the linearity of fluctuation diagnostics and the nonlinearity of SWSSB diagnostics. Extensive charge variance alone does not guarantee SWSSB, because it can arise from classical sampling over block charges without local charge-transfer connectivity in the support. Conversely, SWSSB alone does not force extensive variance unless the Rényi-1 correlations approach their nonzero asymptotic value sufficiently rapidly. This characterization should be useful for experimental studies of SWSSB, including recent cold-atom implementations
39
.

Furthermore, this provides an important step towards understanding the relation between SWSSB and emergent hydrodynamics in open quantum systems. In a lattice system evolved under a local strongly symmetric Lindbladian, the low-energy charge spectrum is controlled not only by SWSSB but also by the small-momentum static charge structure factor
31
. Field-theory descriptions often phrase the same physics in terms of a susceptibility, which depends on additional dynamical data
2
;
7
;
16
. Our results isolate a purely static sufficient condition for the structure-factor ingredient: if a steady state has long-range Rényi-1 order whose approach to the nonzero asymptotic value is sufficiently rapid, then it has extensive block-charge variance and hence, under the usual regularity assumptions, a finite nonzero
k\rightarrow 0^{+}
structure factor. A natural next question is what dynamical assumptions on the Lindbladian enforce this rapid convergence in the steady state.

Finally, we remark that this result also elucidates the connection between SWSSB and charge sharpening transition in monitored circuits
4
;
1
, where local charge indefiniteness plays an important role
37
;
35
. In particular, identifying the fluctuation of weak symmetry twist overlap with the Wigner-Yanase skew information provides a precise way to separate the classical and quantum contributions to charge scrambling, which can be used to understand the landscape of monitored random circuits.

Appendix A
Doubled Hilbert space

For simplicity, here we assume
G
is a finite Abelian symmetry group with unitary representation
U_{g}
on a Hilbert space
{\cal H}
. Since
G
is Abelian,
{\cal H}
decomposes into charge sectors

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{q\in\widehat{G}}\mathcal{H}_{q},

(39)

where
\widehat{G}
is the character group. A state
|\psi_{q}\rangle
with charge
q
transforms as

\displaystyle U_{g}|\psi_{q}\rangle=\chi_{q}(g)|\psi_{q}\rangle.

(40)

where
\chi_{q}(g)
is the character of the representation. For an Abelian group, the irreducible representations are one-dimensional, so
\chi_{q}(g)
is a complex number with unit magnitude.

The canonical purification of a density matrix
\rho
can be regarded as a state in
\mathcal{H}_{L}\otimes\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{R}
, where
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the conjugate Hilbert space. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{\rho}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle:=\sum_{i}(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes\bm{1})\,|i\rangle_{L}|\bar{i}\rangle_{R}=\sum_{i}(\bm{1}\otimes\sqrt{\rho}^{T})\,|i\rangle_{L}|\bar{i}\rangle_{R}.

(41)

The bar notation in the second copy is used to indicate that the second copy carries the conjugate wavefunction. This is important as
\sum_{i}|i\rangle|i\rangle
is not invariant under the basis change
|i\rangle\to V|i\rangle
, while
\sum_{i}|i\rangle|\bar{i}\rangle
is invariant:

\displaystyle\sum_{i}V|i\rangle\otimes V^{*}|\bar{i}\rangle=\sum_{ijj^{\prime}}V_{ji}|j\rangle\otimes V^{*}_{j^{\prime}i}|\bar{j}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{ijj^{\prime}}V^{\vphantom{\dagger}}_{ji}|j\rangle\otimes V^{\dagger}_{ij^{\prime}}|\bar{j}^{\prime}\rangle=\sum_{j}|j\rangle\otimes|\bar{j}\rangle.

(42)

More generally, note that
\|\bm{1}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle:=\sum_{i}|i\rangle_{L}|\bar{i}\rangle_{R}
satisfies
(O\otimes\bm{1})\|\bm{1}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=(\bm{1}\otimes O^{T})\|\bm{1}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle
for any operator
O
.

If we consider a weak symmetry action
\rho\mapsto U_{g}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\rho U_{g}^{\dagger}
, we see that

\displaystyle\|U_{g}^{\vphantom{\dagger}}\sqrt{\rho}U_{g}^{\dagger}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=(U_{g}^{\vphantom{*}}\otimes U_{g}^{*})\|\sqrt{\rho}\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle.

(43)

Therefore, if we consider a state
|q\rangle\langle q|\mapsto|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle
in the doubled Hilbert space, the state is invariant under the weak symmetry action:

\displaystyle U_{g}\otimes U_{g}^{*}|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle=\chi_{q}(g)\chi^{*}_{q}(g)|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle=|q\rangle|\bar{q}\rangle.

(44)

When the state has a strong symmetry, the canonically purified state has a well-defined left and right symmetry, with the weak symmetry being the “diagonal” subgroup given as
\{U_{g}\otimes U_{g}^{*}:g\in G\}
.
The enlarged doubled symmetry may be denoted schematically by
G_{L}\times G_{R}
.
Thus, in the doubled Hilbert space, the distinction between weak and strong symmetry becomes the distinction between fixing only the diagonal charge and fixing both the diagonal and relative charges.

We use a basis-independent convention for right-copy operators.
The right Hilbert space is the conjugate Hilbert space

\overline{\mathcal{H}}
. For an operator
O

\mathcal{H}

let
\overline{O}

\overline{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\overline{O}\,\overline{|\psi\rangle}=\overline{O|\psi\rangle}.

(45)

Equivalently, if
O
is represented by a matrix
O_{ij}
in some basis,
then
\overline{O}

O^{*}

For a Hermitian charge operator
Q_{A}

\displaystyle Q_{A}^{L}:=Q_{A}\otimes\mathbf{1},\qquad Q_{A}^{R}:=\mathbf{1}\otimes\overline{Q}_{A},\qquad Q_{A}^{\pm}:=Q_{A}^{L}\pm Q_{A}^{R}.

(46)

With this convention, if
U_{\theta}=e^{i\theta Q_{A}}

\overline{U_{\theta}}=e^{-i\theta\overline{Q}_{A}}
, so
Q_{A}^{-}

generates the weak action
U_{\theta}\otimes\overline{U_{\theta}}

In the occupation-number basis,
where
Q_{A}

Q_{A}^{R}=\mathbf{1}\otimes Q_{A}

\langle\hskip-2.0pt\langle X\|A\otimes\overline{B}\|X\rangle\hskip-2.0pt\rangle=\mathrm{tr}(X^{\dagger}AXB^{\dagger})
.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

Proof.

Let
\mu=\mathrm{tr}(\rho Q_{A})
and
\Delta Q_{A}=Q_{A}-\mu
. Since
Q_{A}
is Hermitian, one has

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle(Q_{A}^{L})^{2}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle(Q_{A}^{R})^{2}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathrm{tr}(\rho Q_{A}^{2}),

(47)

and

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle Q_{A}^{L}Q_{A}^{R}\rangle\!\rangle=\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}).

(48)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle Q_{A}^{L}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\!\langle Q_{A}^{R}\rangle\!\rangle=\mu.

(49)

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{+})=2\mathrm{tr}(\rho Q_{A}^{2})+2\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A})-4\mu^{2}=2\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A})+2\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}),

(50)

while

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{-})=2\mathrm{tr}(\rho Q_{A}^{2})-2\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A})=2\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A})-2\mathrm{tr}(\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}),

(51)

where we used
\langle\!\langle Q_{A}^{-}\rangle\!\rangle=0
.
Adding the two identities gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{+})+\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{-})=4\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}),

(52)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A})=\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{+})+\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{Var}(Q_{A}^{-}).

(53)

∎

Proof of Lemma
10
.

Proof.

Let
\mu=\mathrm{tr}(\rho Q_{A})
and

\displaystyle\textnormal{Var}(Q_{A}^{-})

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{tr}\rho Q_{A}^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}Q_{A}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{tr}\rho(\Delta Q_{A})^{2}-2\mathrm{tr}\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}

\displaystyle\leq 2\textnormal{Var}(Q_{A})

(54)

where we used
\textnormal{Var}(Q_{A})=\mathrm{tr}\rho(\Delta Q_{A})^{2}

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\big(\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}\sqrt{\rho}\Delta Q_{A}\big)=\mathrm{tr}\big((\rho^{1/4}\Delta Q_{A}\rho^{1/4})^{2}\big)\geq 0,

(55)

since
\rho^{1/4}\Delta Q_{A}\rho^{1/4}

Appendix C
Sparse projector states

C.1
Vanishing Rényi-1 Order yet Extensive Subsystem Charge Variance

In this appendix we show that the converse of Theorem
2
is false: extensive subsystem charge variance does not, by itself, imply SWSSB. The counterexample is a typical sparse classical projector inside a fixed-charge sector.

Setup.
Fix a filling fraction
\nu
, and consider a sequence of systems with
V
sites and total charge
Q=\nu V
. Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{Q}=\Bigl\{\mathbf{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{V}):n_{r}\in\{0,1\},\ \sum_{r=1}^{V}n_{r}=Q\Bigr\},\qquad\mathcal{H}_{Q}=\mathrm{span}\{\ket{\mathbf{n}}:\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}\},

(56)

so that

\displaystyle d_{Q}:=\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}=|\mathcal{C}_{Q}|=\binom{V}{Q}.

(57)

Choose a subset
\Omega\subset\mathcal{C}_{Q}
uniformly at random with cardinality

\displaystyle|\Omega|=r(V),\qquad V^{2}\ll r(V),\qquad\log r(V)=o(V).

(58)

where
o(V)
means subextensive, i.e.,
\lim_{V\to\infty}o(V)/V=0
.
We then define the mixed state

\displaystyle\rho_{\Omega}=\frac{1}{r(V)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\Omega}\ket{\mathbf{n}}\bra{\mathbf{n}}.

(59)

Since every basis state in the support of
\rho_{\Omega}
has the same total charge
Q
, the state is strongly
U(1)
-symmetric.

We will show that, with probability approaching one as
V\rightarrow\infty
, a typical realization
\rho_{\Omega}
has the following two properties: (1)
\rho_{\Omega}
has extensive subsystem charge variance. (2)
\rho_{\Omega}
has no long-range Rényi-1 order for any fixed finite-support charge-transfer operator.
Such a typical realization
\rho_{\Omega}
provides a counterexample to the converse of Theorem
2
.

Preliminary step: Uniform ensemble.

Let
A
be a subregion with

\displaystyle|A|=\ell=\alpha V,\qquad 0<\alpha<1,

(60)

and define the block charge

\displaystyle Q_{A}=\sum_{r\in A}n_{r}.

(61)

We first compute the mean and variance of
Q_{A}
in the uniform ensemble of fixed-charge sector
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
. The uniform distribution on
\mathcal{C}_{Q}
is invariant under permutations of site labels, so all one-site marginals are identical and all two-site marginals for distinct sites are identical. Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}[n_{r}]=\frac{Q}{V}=\nu,\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}[n_{r}n_{s}]=\frac{Q(Q-1)}{V(V-1)}\qquad(r\neq s).

(62)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mu_{A}:=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}[Q_{A}]=\ell\frac{Q}{V}=\ell\nu,

(63)

and

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}(Q_{A})=m_{2,A}-\mu_{A}^{2}=\sum_{r\in A}\mathrm{Var}(n_{r})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r,s\in A\\
r\neq s\end{subarray}}\mathrm{Cov}(n_{r},n_{s})=\ell\frac{Q}{V}\biggl(1-\frac{Q}{V}\biggr)\frac{V-\ell}{V-1},

(64)

where
m_{2,A}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}[Q_{A}^{2}]
is the population second moment.
In particular, for
\ell=\alpha V
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}^{2}=\alpha(1-\alpha)\nu(1-\nu)V+O(1),

(65)

so the full fixed-charge sector has extensive block-charge variance.

Step I: Extensive subsystem charge variance
. We want to show that a typical sparse subset
\Omega
inherits the same extensive scaling. For each
\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}
, define

\displaystyle x_{\mathbf{n}}:=Q_{A}(\mathbf{n}),\qquad I_{\mathbf{n}}:=\begin{cases}1,&\mathbf{n}\in\Omega,\\[3.0pt]
0,&\mathbf{n}\notin\Omega.\end{cases}

(66)

Then

\displaystyle\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}}I_{\mathbf{n}}x_{\mathbf{n}},\qquad\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}}I_{\mathbf{n}}x_{\mathbf{n}}^{2},

(67)

where for brevity we write
r=r(V)
. Since
\Omega
is a uniformly random subset of size
r
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[I_{\mathbf{n}}]=\frac{r}{d_{Q}},\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[I_{\mathbf{n}}I_{\mathbf{m}}]=\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\qquad(\mathbf{n}\neq\mathbf{m}).

(68)

The expectation is immediate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)]=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[I_{\mathbf{n}}]\,x_{\mathbf{n}}=\frac{1}{r}\cdot\frac{r}{d_{Q}}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}}x_{\mathbf{n}}=\mu_{A}.

(69)

Similarly for the variance, where
y_{\mathbf{n}}=x_{\mathbf{n}}-\mu_{A}
are the centered values,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\Omega}(\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A))=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\Biggl[\Bigl(\sum_{\mathbf{n}}I_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{n}}\Bigr)^{2}\Biggr]=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Biggl[\sum_{\mathbf{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[I_{\mathbf{n}}]\,y_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}+\sum_{\mathbf{n}\neq\mathbf{m}}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}[I_{\mathbf{n}}I_{\mathbf{m}}]\,y_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{m}}\Biggr]

(70)

Since
\sum_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{n}}=0
, we have
\sum_{\mathbf{n}\neq\mathbf{m}}y_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{m}}=-\sum_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\Omega}(\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r^{2}}\Biggl[\frac{r}{d_{Q}}-\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\Biggr]\sum_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}=\frac{d_{Q}-r}{rd_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\sum_{\mathbf{n}}y_{\mathbf{n}}^{2}=\frac{d_{Q}-r}{r(d_{Q}-1)}\,\sigma_{A}^{2}.

(71)

Likewise, if we consider a second moment,
then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)\bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}[Q_{A}^{2}]=:m_{2,A},\qquad\mathrm{Var}_{\Omega}\bigl(\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)\bigr)=\frac{d_{Q}-r}{r(d_{Q}-1)}\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}(Q_{A}^{2}).

(72)

which are all written in terms of the expectation values for the uniform distribution.

For a specific realization
\rho_{\Omega}
, define the subsystem charge variance

\displaystyle V_{A}(\Omega):=\mathrm{Var}_{\rho_{\Omega}}(Q_{A})=\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)-\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)^{2}.

(73)

Since
\sigma_{A}^{2}=m_{2,A}-\mu_{A}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|V_{A}(\Omega)-\sigma_{A}^{2}|\leq\bigl|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)-m_{2,A}\bigr|+\bigl|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)^{2}-\mu_{A}^{2}\bigr|.

(74)

In order to bound the probability for
V_{A}(\Omega)
to deviate from
\sigma_{A}^{2}
by
\epsilon V
, we use the union bound. By the union bound,

\displaystyle X_{\Omega}+Y_{\Omega}\geq a\subset\{X_{\Omega}\geq\frac{a}{2}\}\cup\{Y_{\Omega}\geq\frac{a}{2}\}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}(X_{\Omega}+Y_{\Omega}\geq a)\leq\Pr_{\Omega}\left(X_{\Omega}\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)+\Pr_{\Omega}\left(Y_{\Omega}\geq\frac{a}{2}\right).

(75)

where
X_{\Omega}=|\Tr( \rho_\Omega Q_A^2)-m_{2,A}|
and
Y_{\Omega}=|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)^{2}-\mu_{A}^{2}|
.
Therefore, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\left(|V_{A}(\Omega)-\sigma_{A}^{2}|\geq\varepsilon V\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\Omega}\left(X_{\Omega}+Y_{\Omega}\geq\varepsilon V\right)\leq\Pr_{\Omega}\left(X_{\Omega}\geq\frac{\varepsilon V}{2}\right)+\Pr_{\Omega}\left(Y_{\Omega}\geq\frac{\varepsilon V}{2}\right),

(76)

Since both
\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)
and
\mu_{A}
lie in
[0,\ell]
,

\displaystyle\bigl|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)^{2}-\mu_{A}^{2}\bigr|\leq 2\ell\bigl|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)-\mu_{A}\bigr|.

(77)

Recall that
\ell=\alpha V
. Using the above relation, we can further simplify the inequality as

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(|V_{A}(\Omega)-\sigma_{A}^{2}|\geq\varepsilon V\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)-m_{2,A}|\geq\varepsilon V/2\bigr)+\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)-\mu_{A}|\geq\varepsilon/(4\alpha)\bigr).

(78)

Since
0\leq Q_{A}\leq\ell
, we have
0\leq Q_{A}^{2}\leq\ell^{2}
, and hence
\mathrm{Var}_{\mathcal{C}_{Q}}(Q_{A}^{2})\leq\ell^{4}
, where
\ell=\alpha V
. Using this crude bound with Chebyshev’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A^2)-m_{2,A}|\geq\varepsilon V/2\bigr)\leq\frac{4\ell^{4}}{\varepsilon^{2}V^{2}r}=O\Bigl(\frac{V^{2}}{r}\Bigr),

(79)

and using
\sigma_{A}^{2}=O(V)
as in Eq. (
64
), we have

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(|\Tr(\rho_\Omega Q_A)-\mu_{A}|\geq\varepsilon/(4\alpha)\bigr)\leq\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{d_{Q}-r}{r(d_{Q}-1)}\sigma_{A}^{2}=O\Bigl(\frac{V}{r}\Bigr).

(80)

Because
V^{2}\ll r(V)
, both probabilities vanish as
V\to\infty
. Hence, with probability approaching one,

\displaystyle V_{A}(\Omega)=\sigma_{A}^{2}+o(V)=\alpha(1-\alpha)\nu(1-\nu)V+o(V).

(81)

Thus a typical sparse projector
\rho_{\Omega}
has extensive subsystem charge variance.

Step II: Absence of Rényi-1 order.

We now show that the same typical state has no long-range Rényi-1 order. Consider first the standard single-particle charge-transfer operator

\displaystyle O_{ij}=c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j},\qquad i\neq j.

(82)

Since
\rho_{\Omega}
is diagonal in the occupation basis, there is no ordinary off-diagonal long-range order:

\displaystyle C_{ij}:=\Tr(\rho_\Omega O_{ij})=0.

(83)

The relevant nonlinear diagnostic is the Rényi-1 correlator

\displaystyle R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}=\Tr\bigl(O_{ij}\rho_{\Omega}^{1/2}O_{ij}^{\dagger}\rho_{\Omega}^{1/2}\bigr)=\frac{1}{r}\Tr\bigl(c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\Pi_{\Omega}c_{j}^{\dagger}c_{i}\Pi_{\Omega}\bigr).

(84)

where
\Pi_{\Omega}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in\Omega}|\mathbf{n}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}|
thus
\rho^{1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\Pi_{\Omega}
.
Let

\displaystyle S_{ij}=\bigl\{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}:n_{j}=1,\ n_{i}=0\bigr\},

(85)

and for
\mathbf{n}\in S_{ij}
, let
T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})=\mathbf{n}^{j\to i}
denote the configuration obtained by moving one particle from
j
to
i
. Then

\displaystyle c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}\ket{\mathbf{n}}=\ket{T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})}\qquad(\mathbf{n}\in S_{ij}),

(86)

and vanishes otherwise. Expanding the trace in the occupation basis yields

\displaystyle R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in S_{ij}}1_{\mathbf{n}\in\Omega}\,1_{T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})\in\Omega}.

(87)

The summation is over only
S_{ij}
since for other basis states
c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}
simply vanishes.
Thus
R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}
counts the number of directed local hopping edges

\displaystyle\mathbf{n}\to T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})

(88)

that remain inside
\Omega
, divided by
r
.
Intuitively, conditioned on
\mathbf{n}\in\Omega
, the probability that
T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})\in\Omega
is

\displaystyle\frac{r-1}{d_{Q}-1}\sim\frac{r}{d_{Q}},

(89)

which implies that the Rényi-1 correlator gets exponentially suppressed.

To compute the correlator exactly, first note that the number of allowed sources is

\displaystyle|S_{ij}|=\binom{V-2}{Q-1},

(90)

since one fixes
n_{j}=1
and
n_{i}=0
, and then chooses the remaining
Q-1
occupied sites among the other
V-2
sites. For a fixed
\mathbf{n}\in S_{ij}
, the two configurations
\mathbf{n}
and
T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})
are distinct, and the probability that both belong to a uniformly random
r
-element subset
\Omega\subset\mathcal{C}_{Q}
is

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(\mathbf{n}\in\Omega\text{ and }T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})\in\Omega\bigr)=\frac{\binom{d_{Q}-2}{r-2}}{\binom{d_{Q}}{r}}=\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}.

(91)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}|S_{ij}|\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{|S_{ij}|}{d_{Q}}\frac{r-1}{d_{Q}-1}.

(92)

Using

\displaystyle\frac{|S_{ij}|}{d_{Q}}=\frac{\binom{V-2}{Q-1}}{\binom{V}{Q}}=\frac{Q(V-Q)}{V(V-1)},

(93)

we obtain the exact formula

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}\bigr]=\frac{Q(V-Q)}{V(V-1)}\frac{r-1}{d_{Q}-1}.

(94)

Since

\displaystyle d_{Q}=\binom{V}{Q}=\exp\bigl[Vh(\nu)+o(V)\bigr],\qquad h(\nu)=-\nu\log\nu-(1-\nu)\log(1-\nu),

(95)

and
\log r(V)=o(V)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}\bigr]\sim\nu(1-\nu)\frac{r}{d_{Q}}=\exp\bigl[-Vh(\nu)+o(V)\bigr].

(96)

Thus the ensemble average of the Rényi-1 hopping correlator is exponentially small for any pair
(i,j)
.

To show that this is in fact a typical behavior, we define the raw edge count

\displaystyle Y_{ij}(\Omega)=rR^{(\Omega)}_{ij}=\sum_{\mathbf{n}\in S_{ij}}1_{\mathbf{n}\in\Omega}\,1_{T_{j\to i}(\mathbf{n})\in\Omega}\in\mathbb{Z}.

(97)

A nonzero value of
Y_{ij}
detects nonzero Rényi-1 correlator. From the previous step, it is straightforward that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[Y_{ij}(\Omega)\bigr]=|S_{ij}|\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\sim\nu(1-\nu)\frac{r^{2}}{d_{Q}}.

(98)

Since
Y_{ij}(\Omega)
is a nonnegative integer and
r\sim\exp[o(V)]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}>0\bigr)=\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(Y_{ij}(\Omega)\geq 1\bigr)\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\bigl[Y_{ij}(\Omega)\bigr]=\exp\bigl[-Vh(\nu)+o(V)\bigr].

(99)

A union bound over all
O(V^{2})
ordered pairs
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
gives

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(\exists\,i\neq j:\ R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}>0\bigr)\leq V(V-1)\exp\bigl[-Vh(\nu)+o(V)\bigr]\to 0.

(100)

Therefore, with probability approaching one,

\displaystyle R^{(\Omega)}_{ij}=0\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j.

(101)

In particular, a typical sparse projector has no long-range Rényi-1 order.

Step III: Extension to general finite-support charge-transfer operators.

The same mechanism kills not only the single-particle hopping operator
c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{j}
, but any fixed finite-support charge-transfer operator. Let
O
be such an operator, with support size bounded independently of
V
, and suppose that
O
has no diagonal matrix elements in the occupation basis. Then

\displaystyle R_{1}^{(\Omega)}[O]

\displaystyle=\Tr\bigl(O\sqrt{\rho_{\Omega}}O^{\dagger}\sqrt{\rho_{\Omega}}\bigr)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}\in\Omega}\bigl|\bra{\mathbf{m}}O\ket{\mathbf{n}}\bigr|^{2}.

(102)

Define the directed edge set

\displaystyle E_{O}=\bigl\{(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}\times\mathcal{C}_{Q}:\bra{\mathbf{m}}O\ket{\mathbf{n}}\neq 0\bigr\}.

(103)

Because
O
acts on only
O(1)
sites, each source configuration
\mathbf{n}
has at most
K_{O}=O(1)
targets
\mathbf{m}
with nonzero matrix element, so

\displaystyle|E_{O}|\leq K_{O}d_{Q}

(104)

for some constant
K_{O}
depending only on the operator
O
, but not on
V
. Now
R_{1}^{(\Omega)}[O]>0
can occur only if there exists at least one edge
(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\in E_{O}
such that both
\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}\in\Omega
. Hence

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\bigl(R_{1}^{(\Omega)}[O]>0\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m})\in E_{O}}\Pr_{\Omega}(\mathbf{n},\mathbf{m}\in\Omega)=|E_{O}|\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\leq K_{O}\frac{r(r-1)}{d_{Q}-1}.

(105)

Since
\log r(V)=o(V)
but
d_{Q}
is exponential in
V
, the right-hand side vanishes as
V\to\infty
. Therefore, with probability approaching one,

\displaystyle R_{1}^{(\Omega)}[O]=0

(106)

for every fixed finite-support charge-transfer operator
O
.

We conclude that a typical sparse projector
\rho_{\Omega}
is strongly
U(1)
-symmetric and has extensive subsystem charge variance, but exhibits neither long-range Rényi-1 order nor SWSSB. This provides the desired counterexample to the converse of Theorem
2
.

C.2
Conditional mutual information: sparse projectors versus the uniform ensemble.

Another important diagnostic of SWSSB is the von Neumann conditional mutual information (CMI)
I(C:E|B)
for a tripartition of the system into a center region
C
, a shielding region
B
, and an environment region
E
:

Following this convention, we partition the system into three regions

\displaystyle C\sqcup B\sqcup E=\{1,\dots,V\},\qquad|C|=c,\quad|B|=b,\quad|E|=e.

(107)

Since both
\rho_{\Omega}
and
\rho_{Q}
are diagonal in the occupation basis, their von Neumann conditional mutual information is equal to the Shannon conditional mutual information of the corresponding classical random variables. We denote

\displaystyle I(C:E|B)=H(C|B)+H(E|B)-H(CE|B).

(108)

Sparse projector.
For a fixed configuration in
B
denoted as
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{B}
, define the set of configurations in
\Omega
that restrict to
\beta
on
B
(“fiber”) as

\displaystyle\Omega_{\beta}:=\{\mathbf{n}\in\Omega:\mathbf{n}|_{B}=\beta\},\qquad K_{\beta}:=|\Omega_{\beta}|.

(109)

Since
\rho_{\Omega}
is classical and uniform on
\Omega
, conditioning on
B=\beta
gives a uniform distribution on the
K_{\beta}
configurations in
\Omega_{\beta}
. Hence

\displaystyle H_{\rho_{\Omega}}(CE\,|\,B=\beta)=\log K_{\beta}.

(110)

Note that

\displaystyle H(X)-H(X|Y)=I(X:Y)\leq H(X)\leq H(XY)\quad\Rightarrow\quad I(X:Y)\leq H(XY).

(111)

Therefore

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E\,|\,B=\beta)\leq H_{\rho_{\Omega}}(CE\,|\,B=\beta)=\log K_{\beta}.

(112)

Averaging over
\beta
, and noting that
\Pr_{\rho_{\Omega}}(B=\beta)=K_{\beta}/r
, yields

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E\,|\,B)\leq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}\log K_{\beta}.

(113)

Using the elementary bound
\log K\leq K-1
for
K\geq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E\,|\,B)\leq\frac{1}{r}\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}(K_{\beta}-1).

(114)

The quantity
\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}(K_{\beta}-1)
counts the number of ordered pairs of distinct sampled configurations in
\Omega
that have the same restriction to
B
. Taking the expectation over the random choice of
\Omega
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}\!\bigg[\sum_{\beta}K_{\beta}(K_{\beta}-1)\bigg]=r(V)\bigl(r(V)-1\bigr)\,p_{\mathrm{coll}}^{\neq}(B),

(115)

where
p_{\mathrm{coll}}^{\neq}(B)
is the probability that two distinct uniformly random configurations from the full fixed-charge sector agree on the entire boundary region
B
:

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{coll}}^{\neq}(B):=\frac{1}{d_{Q}(d_{Q}-1)}\sum_{\beta}N_{\beta}(N_{\beta}-1),\qquad N_{\beta}:=\#\{\mathbf{n}\in\mathcal{C}_{Q}:\mathbf{n}|_{B}=\beta\}=\binom{V-b}{Q-|\beta|}.

(116)

Thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E\,|\,B)\leq\bigl(r(V)-1\bigr)\,p_{\mathrm{coll}}^{\neq}(B).

(117)

If
b=o(V)
and the filling
0<\nu<1
is fixed, the fixed-charge constraint only changes the Bernoulli collision probability at subexponential order in
b
. More precisely, at the exponential-rate level needed below,

\displaystyle p_{\rm coll}^{\neq}(B)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{\nu}^{b}\exp[o(b)],\qquad\kappa_{\nu}=\nu^{2}+(1-\nu)^{2}<1.

(118)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{b}\log p_{\rm coll}^{\neq}(B)

\displaystyle\to\log\kappa_{\nu}

(119)

for any sequence
b=o(V)
with
b\to\infty
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E|B)

\displaystyle\leq[r(V)-1]\kappa_{\nu}^{b}\exp[o(b)].

(120)

Since the CMI is nonnegative, Markov’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\Pr_{\Omega}\left(I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E|B)>\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\mathbb{E}_{\Omega}I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E|B).

(121)

Therefore, if

\displaystyle\log r(V)+b\log\kappa_{\nu}+o(b)

\displaystyle\to-\infty,

(122)

then

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{\Omega}}(C:E|B)

\displaystyle\to 0

(123)

in probability. In particular, because
\log r(V)=o(V)
, it is sufficient to choose a shielding region satisfying

\displaystyle b=o(V),\qquad b\gg\log r(V).

(124)

For example, if
r(V)\leq V^{p}
for some fixed
p
, then
b=C\log V
with any constant
C>p/|\log\kappa_{\nu}|
suffices to make the CMI vanish.

Uniform fixed-charge ensemble.

We now contrast this with the uniform fixed-charge ensemble
\rho_{Q}
. Fix a boundary pattern
\beta\in\{0,1\}^{B}
, and let

\displaystyle m:=|\beta|=Q_{B}(\beta).

(125)

Conditioned on
B=\beta
, the configurations on
C\cup E
are distributed uniformly subject only to the residual charge constraint

\displaystyle Q_{C}+Q_{E}=Q-m.

(126)

Equivalently,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\rho_{Q}}(n_{C},n_{E}\,|\,B=\beta)=\frac{\mathbf{1}\{|n_{C}|+|n_{E}|=Q-m\}}{\binom{c+e}{Q-m}}.

(127)

If we first condition on
Q_{C}=q
, then the configurations on
C
and
E
are independent and uniformly distributed in their respective charge sectors:

\displaystyle\Pr_{\rho_{Q}}(n_{C},n_{E}\,|\,B=\beta)=\sum_{q}\Pr_{\rho_{Q}}(Q_{C}=q\,|\,B=\beta)\,\Pr(n_C\,|\,Q_C=q)\,\Pr(n_E\,|\,Q_E=Q-m-q),

(128)

where

\displaystyle\Pr_{\rho_{Q}}(Q_{C}=q\,|\,B=\beta)=\frac{\binom{c}{q}\binom{e}{Q-m-q}}{\binom{c+e}{Q-m}}.

(129)

This is a hypergeometric distribution.

From this decomposition one finds the exact identity

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B=\beta)=H_{\rho_{Q}}(Q_{C}\,|\,B=\beta),

(130)

and hence

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B)=H_{\rho_{Q}}(Q_{C}\,|\,B).

(131)

So the conditional mutual information of the uniform fixed-charge ensemble is exactly the conditional entropy of the charge in region
C
after conditioning on
B
.

This immediately shows that the CMI of
\rho_{Q}
remains nonzero. Indeed, if
c=|C|=O(1)
is fixed and
b=o(V)
, then the hypergeometric law converges to a binomial law,

\displaystyle Q_{C}\,|\,B\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathrm{Bin}(c,\nu),

(132)

and therefore

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B)=H\!\left(\mathrm{Bin}(c,\nu)\right)+o(1).

(133)

For a single-site region
C
, this reduces to

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B)=h_{2}(\nu)+o(1),

(134)

where

\displaystyle h_{2}(\nu):=-\nu\log\nu-(1-\nu)\log(1-\nu)

(135)

is the binary entropy. Thus the CMI of the uniform fixed-charge ensemble remains finite and strictly positive even when the shielding region
B
is large, provided
b=o(V)
.

More generally, if both
c=|C|
and
e=|E|
scale linearly with
V
while
b=o(V)
, then
Q_{C}|B
has variance
\Theta(V)
, so its hypergeometric entropy grows as

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B)=\frac{1}{2}\log V+O(1).

(136)

Hence the uniform fixed-charge ensemble retains a parametrically large CMI, in sharp contrast with the sparse projector.

Summary.
For the sparse-projector state
\rho_{\Omega}
, the conditional mutual information is controlled by the total weight of ambiguous boundary fibers, and vanishes once
b\gg\log r(V)
. By contrast, for the uniform fixed-charge ensemble
\rho_{Q}
, the CMI is exactly

\displaystyle I_{\rho_{Q}}(C:E\,|\,B)=H_{\rho_{Q}}(Q_{C}\,|\,B),

(137)

which remains nonzero, and can even grow logarithmically for macroscopic regions. Thus the sparse-projector counterexample destroys the long-range information-theoretic correlations present in the uniform ensemble, even though both states live in the same global charge sector.

Appendix D
Structure Factor

In this section, we define the structure factor and show how it is connected to the charge variance. Let
n(x,t)
be a conserved charge density. To discuss
n(x,t)
, one has to specify some underlying dynamics that generates
n(x,t)
from
n(x)
. Define the fluctuation

\displaystyle\delta n(x,t)=n(x,t)-\langle n\rangle.

(138)

In a translationally invariant system, define the Fourier mode

\displaystyle\delta n_{k}(t)=\int\differential^{d}x\,e^{-\mathrm{i}k\cdot x}\delta n(x,t).

(139)

Structure factor
. The dynamic structure factor is the nonsymmetrized correlation function

\displaystyle S(k,\omega)=\frac{1}{V}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\differential t\,e^{\mathrm{i}\omega t}\left\langle\delta n_{k}(t)\delta n_{-k}(0)\right\rangle.

(140)

It measures the fluctuation spectrum of the charge density. It is not itself a response function.

The static (equal-time) structure factor is

\displaystyle S(k)=\frac{1}{V}\left\langle\delta n_{k}(0)\delta n_{-k}(0)\right\rangle.

(141)

Physically,
S(k)
measures equal-time density fluctuations at wavelength
1/k
. For example, it controls the scaling of charge fluctuations in a large region. To see this, consider the charge fluctuation in a block
A
, which is a hypercube with linear size
l
so that
|A|=l^{d}
:

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(A)}:=\sum_{x\in A}\delta n_{x}=\sum_{x\in A}\big(n_{x}-\langle n_{x}\rangle\big).

(142)

To proceed, introduce the block window function

\displaystyle w_{A}(x):=\begin{cases}1,&x\in A,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\qquad\tilde{w}_{A}(k):=\sum_{x}w_{A}(x)e^{-ik\cdot x}=\prod_{j}\quantity[ e^{-ik_j (l+1)/2}\,\frac{\sin(k_j l/2)}{\sin(k_j/2)} ],

(143)

where
A
is defined for coordinates
1\leq x_{i}\leq l
. Define
\delta Q_{k}:=\sum_{x}\delta n_{x}e^{-ik\cdot x}
. Then

\displaystyle\delta Q^{(A)}=\sum_{x}w_{A}(x)\delta n_{x}=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k}\tilde{w}_{A}(k)\delta n_{-k},\quad(\delta Q^{(A)})^{2}=\frac{1}{V^{2}}\sum_{k,q}\tilde{w}_{A}(k)\tilde{w}_{A}(q)\delta n_{-k}\delta n_{-q},

(144)

where
V
is the volume of the entire system. For a translationally invariant state,
\langle\delta n_{k}\delta n_{q}\rangle=:V\delta_{k+q,0}S(k)
. Assuming translational invariance, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q^{(A)})=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k}|\tilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}S(k).

(145)

Thus the block variance at scale
l
is a weighted average of
S(k)
over momenta with
weight
|\tilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}
. The window function
|\widetilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}
is sharply peaked near
k=0
, has peak value
|\widetilde{w}_{A}(0)|^{2}=|A|^{2}=l^{2d}
, and has width of order
1/l
in each momentum direction. It also satisfies the normalization identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k}|\widetilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}=|A|.

(146)

In a fixed-total-charge sector, the exact
k=0
mode is absent or
has
S(0)=0
. The relevant quantity for large but finite subregions is
therefore the small nonzero momentum limit,
k\to 0^{+}
.

(1)
S(k)
approaches a nonzero constant
\lim_{|k|\rightarrow 0^{+}}S(k)=:S_{0}>0
.
Consider a continuum limit. If
S(k)
is approximately constant over the momentum window
|k|\lesssim 1/l
, then the normalization condition gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q^{(A)})\approx S_{0}\frac{1}{V}\sum_{k}|\widetilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}=S_{0}|A|.

(147)

Equivalently, in the continuum approximation,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(Q^{(A)})=\int\frac{d^{d}k}{(2\pi)^{d}}S(k)|\widetilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}\approx S_{0}l^{d}.

(148)

Thus a finite nonzero
k\to 0^{+}
structure factor gives a volume-law block-charge variance. Conversely, suppose that the thermodynamic-limit structure factor is locally bounded near
k=0
and has a regular small-momentum limit. Then the normalized block variance converges to this limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{l\to\infty}\frac{\mathrm{Var}(Q^{(A_{l})})}{|A_{l}|}=\lim_{|k|\to 0^{+}}S(k).

(149)

Thus, under this regularity assumption, a finite positive volume-law coefficient of the block-charge variance is equivalent to a finite nonzero low-momentum static structure factor.

(2)
S(k)
vanishes quadratically
S(k)\sim S_{2}k^{2}
.
In this case, the real-space charge correlation is short-ranged; more precisely,
S_{2}
corresponds to the second moment of
\langle\delta n_{0}\delta n_{r}\rangle
. In this case, the integral is not dominated by a small
k
region; instead, it is dominated by a large
k
contribution, which generically yields an area-law contribution to the charge fluctuation. Indeed, the IR contribution can be easily shown to give
S_{2}l^{d-2}
contribution, while the UV contribution gives
l^{d-1}
contribution. To see this, note that
k^{2}
factor acts like a discrete differentiation (
\hat{k}^{2}=4\sum_{j}\sin^{2}(k_{j}/2)\sim|k|^{2}
) such that

\displaystyle\frac{S_{2}}{V}\sum_{k}k^{2}|\tilde{w}_{A}(k)|^{2}\approx S_{2}\sum_{r}\sum_{j}|w_{A}(x+\hat{e}_{j})-w_{A}(x)|^{2}\sim S_{2}|\partial A|,

(150)

since the difference is non-zero only at the boundary of
A
. Physically, when correlations are short-ranged, only degrees of freedom within a correlation length of the boundary can exchange charge with the complement, so number fluctuations are subextensive and boundary controlled.

(3)
S(k)
vanishes linearly
S(k)\sim S_{1}|k|
.
In this case, a nonanalytic structure of
S(k)
indicates that the real-space charge correlation is power-law decaying. Accordingly, on top of area-law charge variance from UV contribution, there is an additional nontrivial IR contribution that scales as
l^{d-1}\log l

32
.

Although this gives only subextensive block-charge variance, it can still be sufficient for gaplessness in a local strongly symmetric Lindbladian. The reason is that the variational locality bound on the relaxation rate of a charge mode is controlled by
k^{2}/S(k)
; hence
S(k)\sim|k|
gives
\Gamma(k)\lesssim|k|
, which still vanishes as
k\to 0
. Thus finite nonzero
S(0+)
is a sufficient static route to gaplessness, but not a necessary one.
