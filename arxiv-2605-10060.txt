Abstract
I study a decentralized marriage market with search frictions, costly pre-marital skill investments, and non-transferable utility. Despite a symmetric environment, the market can exhibit asymmetric equilibria, with one gender investing more in skills than the other; in some environments, the asymmetric equilibrium is unique. A microfounded model of household utility maximization shows that this transition from a unique symmetric equilibrium to a unique asymmetric equilibrium can be driven by rising labor-market wages for high-skilled workers: as the skill premium rises, one gender ends up fully investing while the other invests substantially less. Keywords: marriage market, pre-marital investment, search frictions, skill premium, non-transferable utility JEL Codes:

Introduction

This paper studies how individuals’ incentives to acquire skills are shaped by marriage-market forces, and how those incentives respond to rising skill premia. A growing literature has established that both labor- and marriage-market returns shape pre-marital investments.
1
1

1

On the marriage-market channel,
22
and
10
document that pre-marital educational investments respond to marriage-market returns to education. On the labor-market side,
18
traces how rising labor-market opportunities for women reshaped their educational investments over the twentieth century.
With rapid advances in technology, the skill premium for being highly skilled has been rising rapidly
(
17
;
1
)
. When individuals have marriage-market concerns, do such rising skill premia induce them to invest more in skills, or less? At least theoretically, the effect of a rising skill premium on pre-marital investment is
a priori
unclear. On one hand, higher skill premia incentivize education to enhance labor-market outcomes. On the other hand, there is a countervailing force: if the marginal value of having a highly-skilled spouse falls as one’s own skill rises, agents with moderate talent—those for whom skill acquisition is costly—may forgo investing and instead match with a highly-skilled partner, anticipating household specialization. The highly-skilled partner then focuses on labor-market participation while the low-skilled partner assumes the bulk of domestic production. Such specialization is self-reinforcing: as more agents on one side of the market invest in skills, a substitution effect discourages investment on the other side.

So, when do agents invest in acquiring skills and when do they not? And might we see asymmetries across genders in their pre-marital investments? These questions motivate this paper. To answer these questions, I study a decentralized model of a marriage market with three features: search frictions, costly pre-marital skill investments, and non-transferable utility.

The paper makes two contributions. First, gender disparities in skill acquisition can arise endogenously
even when no primitive asymmetry exists between the genders
. With payoffs, costs, and matching technology identical for men and women, the only equilibria can nevertheless be asymmetric: one gender systematically invests more than the other in skill acquisition.
2
2

2

Since the environment is fully symmetric, there are essentially two such asymmetric equilibria, mirror images of each other: one in which men invest less, and one in which women invest less.
Second, using a microfounded household game in the spirit of
12
, I show that the relationship between the skill premium and skill acquisition is non-monotonic across genders. For moderate increases in the skill premium, both genders acquire more skills. For larger increases, however, a specialization effect dominates: one side cuts back its investment in skill acquisition while the other increases it. Rising skill premia can therefore, somewhat paradoxically, lead to one gender reducing its skill investment—producing phenomena such as a subsequent decline in high-skill labor force participation on that side.

Several scholars have highlighted how marriage-market considerations can have an asymmetric impact across genders due to structural asymmetries between men and women, such as differential fertility costs or technological differences.

See
33
and
24
for fertility-based asymmetries, and

My contribution is to show that these asymmetries can arise even in the absence of any such structural asymmetry.
4
4

4

I do not wish to suggest that structural asymmetries do not exist. My main focus, however, is on the comparative static—how pre-marital investments evolve with changing skill premia. It is
a priori
not clear whether rising skill premia exacerbate these asymmetries or mitigate them. As Proposition
6
shows, even in the absence of structural asymmetries, rising skill premia can give rise to asymmetries that would otherwise not be present.

I now briefly describe the framework. The market consists of a continuum of agents from two sides, men and women. Agents engage in an undirected search to find a potential match. Prior to beginning their search, each decides whether to incur a cost to acquire high skills or to remain low skilled. An agent’s cost depends on their type, indexed by their identity in the unit interval; agents with higher types incur higher costs to acquire high skills. After making this investment choice, they begin the search phase, meeting partners from the other side randomly. When two people meet, they observe each other’s skills and decide whether to match. Payoffs conditional on matching depend only on the two skills---not the latent types---so each agent’s match payoff takes one of four values, indexed by their own skill level and their partner’s. If two agents match, they immediately leave the market and are replaced by newly born agents of the same types who make fresh investment decisions before entering the unmatched pool. In equilibrium, since agents of the same type make the same investment choice, the replacement agents replicate the skill composition of the departing agents. This construction ensures that the economy is perpetually in the steady state, and in particular, that the composition of the unmatched pool is fixed by the equilibrium investment shares rather than being endogenously affected by acceptance behavior.
5
5

5

This assumption is made for tractability. Without it, the mix of types in the unmatched pool would itself depend on who rejects whom, and that composition would feed back into agents’ decisions. The replacement rule keeps the pool composition fixed at the equilibrium investment shares.
The goal is to understand the structure of pure strategy equilibria and their relation with the payoff function. In particular, I seek to understand the factors that lead to equilibria with asymmetric levels of investment from the two sides of the market. The point of the exercise, then, is to illustrate that disparities can arise even when the underlying environment is fully symmetric in terms of preferences, payoffs, and the matching technology.

Within this framework, I characterize the equilibrium structure by first analyzing the post-investment matching game. I assume that every agent strictly prefers a highly skilled partner over a low-skilled one. This immediately rules out negative assortative matching, as a high-skilled agent will never reject another high-skilled agent. Thus, equilibria must take one of two forms: either a positive assortative matching (PAM) equilibrium, where high-skilled agents match exclusively with high-skilled partners, or an all-match (AM) equilibrium, where all agents accept any available partner.
6
6

6

One could also envision asymmetric equilibria in which one side of the market rejects all low-skill partners from the other side, leaving those low-skill agents permanently unmatched. As discussed in Section
3
, attention is restricted to equilibria in which every agent is matched eventually, which rules out such configurations.

The first observation is that in a sufficiently thick market, the equilibrium is PAM, and such a PAM matching induces symmetric investment (Proposition
1
). Therefore, if at all there is to be an asymmetric equilibrium, it must be an AM match. Even in the case of an AM match, if the payoff function is supermodular, the unique equilibrium is symmetric (Proposition
2
). What happens, then, if the payoff function is submodular? Proposition
3
shows that asymmetric equilibria can exist in this case. To sharpen this characterization, I specialize to affine costs. Proposition
4
fully characterizes this environment, with the key takeaway that only three types of equilibria are possible.

1.

Symmetric Equilibrium: A cutoff type
x_{sym}
exists, below which men and women invest in skills.

2.

Full Investment from One Side (FIOS): All men acquire skills, while only women below a type
\underline{x}
do.
7
7

7

Naturally, the gender assignment is arbitrary—an analogous equilibrium exists where all women acquire skills and men below
\underline{x}
do.

3.

No Investment from One Side (NIOS): No men invest in skills, while women below a type
\bar{x}
do.

As the notation suggests, these thresholds satisfy
\bar{x}\geq x_{sym}\geq\underline{x}
. Importantly, FIOS and NIOS equilibria can never coexist. Moreover, asymmetric equilibria are not only possible but, in some cases, unique (among pure strategy equilibria).

A remark on interpretation is in order. The environment is fully symmetric across genders: payoffs, the matching technology, and the cost of skill acquisition are all identical on the two sides. Consequently, for every asymmetric equilibrium, its mirror image—obtained by interchanging the roles of men and women—is also an equilibrium, and the model itself does not select between them. Throughout the paper, I describe results with one gender fixed as the fully-investing (or more-investing) side. The point of this choice is precisely to highlight the substantive message: once the underlying environment admits asymmetric equilibria, forces outside the model—social norms, cultural expectations, or historical accidents—can pick out the equilibrium that is actually played and thereby generate persistent gender-based disparities in skill acquisition, even though the environment itself favors neither side.

The intuition behind these asymmetric equilibria is reminiscent of public goods games. High skills are, in a sense, a public good within a household: when one partner acquires high skills, both benefit, but the cost is borne by the investor alone. This can sustain equilibria in which one side invests asymmetrically more in providing the public good. This reasoning, although on broadly right lines, is somewhat incomplete for two reasons. First, agents’ payoffs are increasing in their own skills even when the partner is highly skilled, so there is some private incentive to invest regardless of the spouse’s skill level.
8
8

8

For instance, were one to assume
\phi(H,H)<\phi(H,L)
—that a high-skilled agent prefers a low-skilled partner—asymmetric outcomes would follow almost mechanically, with one side investing and the other free-riding. Assumption
1
excludes this case.
Second, even under submodular payoffs where the public-good character is strongest, there are environments that admit only the symmetric equilibrium.

How does this asymmetry connect to the rising skill premium that opened this introduction? Proposition
5
answers this. Starting from any environment that admits only the symmetric equilibrium and in which the gap between the highest and lowest match payoffs is sufficiently small, we can construct another environment—with the lowest match payoff held fixed and the household’s payoff from having at least one highly-skilled member raised—in which the only equilibrium is the asymmetric (FIOS) one. Asymmetric equilibria can therefore arise—and in some environments, must arise.

This is the main comparative static of the paper, formalized in Proposition
6
. Starting from a setting in which only the symmetric equilibrium exists, small increases in the skill premium lead both genders to invest more in skill acquisition: the symmetric cutoff rises continuously with the premium. Once the premium crosses a threshold, however, the symmetric equilibrium fails altogether—the only equilibrium becomes asymmetric (FIOS), with one gender fully investing in skills while the other cuts back to a substantially lower rate. The transition is discontinuous.

I establish the above comparative static by microfounding the match payoffs through a non-cooperative household game in the spirit of
12
.
9
9

9

See
13
for a detailed survey of various models of household labor supply decisions as games.
Household members allocate time between labor-market work (which generates income proportional to their wages) and household production (which rises with total time at home and is privately costly). Example
2
traces the comparative static numerically. Finally, much of the literature on matching takes the match payoffs as exogenous. The microfoundation I offer can be used to study other properties of matchings as well, as it enables us to vary the surplus functions by changing the underlying primitives.

A methodological remark closes this introduction. Beyond its substantive justification, the NTU assumption brings considerable analytical simplicity to the problem. In a TU framework, a matched pair’s
joint surplus
is known, but the split between the two members—which determines their incentive constraints—must be solved for endogenously. Under NTU, the exogeneity of the individual match payoffs allows a clean characterization of equilibrium values and incentives, which in turn enables the comparative static on the skill premium that is the main point of the paper.

We impose the following standard ranking of payoffs:

Assumption 1

\phi(H,H)\geq\phi(H,L)\geq\phi(L,H)\geq\phi(L,L)

This assumption can be microfounded through a household decision-making problem as in

I also restrict attention to equilibria in which every agent is matched eventually. In a PAM equilibrium, this happens when a like type meets a like type; in an AM equilibrium, it happens whenever an agent meets another. This rules out configurations in which some agents—for instance, those on one side of the market whom the other side has agreed to reject—would remain permanently unmatched.

An economy

\langle\phi,C,\lambda,r\rangle

Timing:

Time is continuous and infinite. At each instant, unmatched agents engage in costless, undirected search, observing the skill distribution of the opposite pool. When two agents meet, they observe each other’s skills and decide whether to match. If they do, they leave the market and are replaced by newly born agents of the same type. These newly born agents make an irreversible skill investment decision before entering the unmatched pool.

Thus, the economy is perpetually in “steady state”, i.e., the mass of each set of agents, men and women, remains to be
1
and their type distribution remains unchanged. Unmatched agents meet others drawn at random from the unmatched pool at an exponential rate
\lambda

10

Since the economy is perpetually in the steady state with a unit mass of population on either side, we do not need to scale it with the population size.

3.1
Discussion of the model

The model has three key features: search frictions, costly pre-marital skill investments, and non-transferable utility. Search frictions and costly pre-marital investments are particularly pervasive in real marriage markets. Individuals—or their parents, in some contexts—make pre-marital investments anticipating labor-market outcomes that in turn shape their marriage prospects. Meanwhile, the prevalence of dating apps and marriage bureaus is clear evidence of search frictions in modern marriage markets.

The third feature, non-transferable utility (NTU), is motivated by the nature of social matches such as marriages. The seminal paper of
4
briefly considered such an environment, and
34
studied a marriage market with search frictions and NTU at length. To quote the latter:

But in defense of the NTU model, disagreements about matching in social settings are not uncommon. And whenever we observe a potential match or split desired by one party but not the other, utility is obviously not fully transferable. For the total match surplus either is positive or is not, and there can be no disagreement.

A particularly striking case of such a disagreement is the sharp discontinuity around
\tfrac{1}{2}
in the share of household income earned by the wife documented by
5
. In the context of US couples, they show considerable aversion to situations in which the wife earns more than her husband. A world with fully transferable utility would show no such discontinuity: whatever psychological cost the husband incurs from his wife earning more could, in principle, be compensated through transfers.

Further, I have assumed that the flow payoff while unmatched is zero regardless of skill. This is largely a normalization. The qualitative results carry over to a setting in which an unmatched agent of skill
s
earns a flow payoff
u(s)\geq 0
with
u(H)\geq u(L)
, provided
u(\cdot)
and the gap
u(H)-u(L)
are sufficiently small relative to the match payoffs
\phi(\cdot,\cdot)
. Intuitively, when the value of remaining unmatched is dwarfed by the value of marrying, the marriage market remains the dominant force shaping investment and acceptance decisions, and the structure of equilibria—including symmetry-breaking under submodularity and the comparative static on the skill premium—is preserved.

As noted in the introduction, real-world asymmetries between men and women do exist—differential fertility costs, technological differences, and other structural asymmetries. The focus of this paper, however, is on a complementary force: rising skill premia. As Proposition
1
shows, in environments with sufficiently strong complementarities, equilibria are necessarily symmetric—and in particular, the PAM equilibrium emerges when the market is sufficiently thick. In such symmetric environments, any structural asymmetry between men and women would mechanically translate into asymmetric pre-marital investments. The contribution of this paper is to show that asymmetric investments can also arise from rising skill premia themselves, even when no underlying structural asymmetry is present. To make this comparative static operational, Section
6
provides a microfoundation for the match payoffs
\phi(H,H),\phi(H,L),\phi(L,H),\phi(L,L)
via a non-cooperative household decision-making game, which allows us to study how the equilibria evolve as the labor-market skill premium rises.

and
\phi(L,H)=\phi(L,L)
), satisfying the constraints. For
t_{h}\in[t_{l},t_{\text{sym}})
, by the definition of
t_{\text{sym}}
, we have
\Phi(t_{h})<0
, i.e., the L-L acceptance constraint holds strictly. The H-L acceptance constraint is implied by the L-L one in the submodular regime: since
\Delta=\phi(H,H)+\phi(L,L)-\phi(H,L)-\phi(L,H)\leq 0
, we have
\phi(H,H)-\phi(H,L)\leq\phi(L,H)-\phi(L,L)
, and combined with
\phi(H,L)\geq\phi(L,L)
(Assumption
1
),

\displaystyle\lambda\cdot(\text{cutoff})\cdot(\phi(H,H)-\phi(H,L))\leq\lambda\cdot(\text{cutoff})\cdot(\phi(L,H)-\phi(L,L))\leq r\phi(L,L)\leq r\phi(H,L).

Hence the H-L constraint also holds on
[t_{l},t_{\text{sym}})
.

Step 4: Asymptotic divergence of the symmetric L-L acceptance LHS.

Define

\displaystyle\Phi(t_{h}):=\lambda\,x_{sym}(t_{h})\cdot(\phi(L,H)(t_{h})-\phi(L,L))-r\phi(L,L).

The symmetric L-L acceptance (
AM: L-L
) holds at
(x_{sym},x_{sym})
iff
\Phi(t_{h})\leq 0
. We claim
\Phi(t_{h})\to+\infty
as
t_{h}\to\infty
.

By assumption (C2),
\liminf_{t_{h}\to\infty}x_{sym}(t_{h})=x^{*}>0
. So there exists
T_{1}<\infty
such that for all
t_{h}\geq T_{1}
,
x_{sym}(t_{h})\geq x^{*}/2>0
.

We claim
\phi(L,H)(t_{h})\to\infty
as
t_{h}\to\infty
. Let
(e_{m}^{*}(t_{h}),e_{w}^{*}(t_{h}))
denote the household game’s Nash equilibrium at wages
(t_{h},t_{l})
. By the equilibrium best-response inequality for member
m
(using
e_{m}=1/2
as a feasible deviation),

\displaystyle U_{m}(e_{m}^{*},e_{w}^{*};\,t_{h},t_{l})\geq U_{m}(1/2,e_{w}^{*};\,t_{h},t_{l})=\psi\!\left(\tfrac{t_{h}}{2}+t_{l}e_{w}^{*},\,\tfrac{3}{2}-e_{w}^{*}\right)-g(\tfrac{1}{2}).

Since
e_{w}^{*}\in[0,1]
, we have
\tfrac{t_{h}}{2}+t_{l}e_{w}^{*}\geq\tfrac{t_{h}}{2}
and
\tfrac{3}{2}-e_{w}^{*}\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
. Using the strict monotonicity of
\psi
in both arguments,

\displaystyle\psi\!\left(\tfrac{t_{h}}{2}+t_{l}e_{w}^{*},\,\tfrac{3}{2}-e_{w}^{*}\right)\geq\psi\!\left(\tfrac{t_{h}}{2},\,\tfrac{1}{2}\right),

which tends to
\infty
as
t_{h}\to\infty
by the unboundedness assumption
\lim_{y\to\infty}\psi(y,1/2)=\infty
. Hence
U_{m}(e_{m}^{*},e_{w}^{*};\,t_{h},t_{l})\to\infty
.

The two members’ equilibrium payoffs differ only by the bounded cost term:

\displaystyle\phi(L,H)(t_{h})=U_{w}(e_{m}^{*},e_{w}^{*};\,t_{h},t_{l})=U_{m}(e_{m}^{*},e_{w}^{*};\,t_{h},t_{l})+\big[g(1-e_{m}^{*})-g(1-e_{w}^{*})\big],

and
g
is bounded on
[0,1]
. Therefore
\phi(L,H)(t_{h})\to\infty
.

Since
\phi(L,L)
is constant and
\phi(L,H)(t_{h})\to\infty
, we have
\phi(L,H)(t_{h})-\phi(L,L)\to\infty
. Combined with
x_{sym}(t_{h})\geq x^{*}/2
for
t_{h}\geq T_{1}
,

\displaystyle\lambda\,x_{sym}(t_{h})\cdot(\phi(L,H)(t_{h})-\phi(L,L))\geq\lambda\cdot(x^{*}/2)\cdot(\phi(L,H)(t_{h})-\phi(L,L))\to\infty.

Hence
\Phi(t_{h})\to\infty
.

Step 5: Defining
t_{\text{FIOS}}
.

By Step 4,
\Phi(t_{h})\to\infty
as
t_{h}\to\infty
. By Step 2,
\Phi(t_{l})=0\cdot 0-r\phi(L,L)=-r\phi(L,L)<0
. By continuity of
\Phi
(Step 1), the set
\{t_{h}:\Phi(t_{h})>0\}
is non-empty and (since
\Phi\to\infty
) eventually contains all sufficiently large
t_{h}
. By (C3), there also exists
T_{2}<\infty
such that for all
t_{h}\geq T_{2}
,
\underline{x}(t_{h})\in(0,1)
and the FIOS profile
(1,\underline{x}(t_{h}))
satisfies (
AM: H-L
).

Define

\displaystyle t_{\text{FIOS}}:=\inf\{T\geq t_{l}:\Phi(t_{h})>0,\ 1+\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta(t_{h})<0,\ \underline{x}(t_{h})\in(0,1),\ \text{and FIOS H-L acceptance holds, for all }t_{h}\geq T\}.

By (C1),
1+\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta(t_{h})\to-\infty
, so the second condition is satisfied for all sufficiently large
t_{h}
. Combined with Step 4 and (C3),
t_{\text{FIOS}}
is finite. By construction,
t_{\text{FIOS}}\geq t_{\text{sym}}
.

Step 6: At
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
, FIOS is the unique equilibrium.

Fix
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
.

(a) Symmetric equilibrium does not exist.
By the definition of
t_{\text{FIOS}}
,
\Phi(t_{h})>0
, i.e.,
\lambda x_{sym}(\phi(L,H)-\phi(L,L))>r\phi(L,L)
. The symmetric L-L acceptance constraint (
AM: L-L
) fails at the interior cutoff
x_{sym}\in[0,1)
, so
(x_{sym},x_{sym})
is not an equilibrium. The boundary case is also ruled out: if the symmetric formula gives
x_{sym}\geq 1
(so the actual cutoff would clip to
1
), the L-L acceptance constraint at cutoff
1
becomes
\lambda(\phi(L,H)-\phi(L,L))\leq r\phi(L,L)
, which fails for
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
since
\phi(L,H)(t_{h})\to\infty
(Step 4) while
\phi(L,L)
is constant.

(b) NIOS equilibrium does not exist.
By Proposition
3
part 3 (applied with submodularity
\Delta\leq 0
, which holds at
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{sym}}
), if no symmetric equilibrium exists then every asymmetric equilibrium must be of FIOS form. Hence NIOS does not exist.

(c) Interior asymmetric equilibrium does not exist.
By Proposition
4
part 2, the only asymmetric equilibrium forms (up to permutation) under affine cost are FIOS and NIOS.

(d) FIOS equilibrium exists.
The FIOS profile is
(1,\underline{x}(t_{h}))
. By the definition of
t_{\text{FIOS}}
and (C3),
\underline{x}(t_{h})\in(0,1)
, so the cutoff is interior. The women’s investment IC at
\underline{x}
is the indifference relation
\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}[\Delta+\Delta_{h}]=\underline{x}+c
, satisfied by definition of
\underline{x}
. The men’s investment IC at type
1
requires
\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}[\underline{x}\Delta+\Delta_{h}]\geq 1+c
, which by the algebra of Proposition
4
part 4 (using
1+\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta<0
at
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
) reduces to
\underline{x}\leq 1
, holding strictly. The H-L acceptance constraint (
AM: H-L
) is satisfied on path by (C3). The L-L acceptance constraint (
AM: L-L
) is vacuous on path (low-skill women meet only high-skill men, so the constraint binds only off-path). Hence FIOS is an equilibrium.

Combining (a)–(d), FIOS is the unique equilibrium for
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
.

Step 7: Within-regime monotonicity and the discontinuous transition (Part 3).

Monotonicity of
x_{sym}(t_{h})
on
[t_{l},t_{\text{sym}})
(under the additional hypothesis
\Delta_{h}^{\prime}\geq|\Delta^{\prime}|
).
On the symmetric regime, the cutoff is given by the closed form

\displaystyle x_{sym}(t_{h})=\frac{\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta_{h}(t_{h})-c}{1-\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta(t_{h})}=\frac{N(t_{h})}{D(t_{h})},

where
N(t_{h}):=\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta_{h}(t_{h})-c
and
D(t_{h}):=1-\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\Delta(t_{h})
. Under the smoothness of the PIH game (Step 1), both
N
and
D
are continuously differentiable in
t_{h}
. By the quotient rule,

\displaystyle\frac{dx_{sym}}{dt_{h}}=\frac{N^{\prime}(t_{h})\,D(t_{h})-N(t_{h})\,D^{\prime}(t_{h})}{D(t_{h})^{2}}=\frac{\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}\big[\Delta_{h}^{\prime}(t_{h})\,D(t_{h})+\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})\,N(t_{h})\big]}{D(t_{h})^{2}}.

Within the symmetric regime,
x_{sym}(t_{h})=N(t_{h})/D(t_{h})\in[0,1)
, which (combined with
D>0
) gives
0\leq N(t_{h})\leq D(t_{h})
. Under the hypothesis
\Delta_{h}^{\prime}(t_{h})\geq|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|
(which holds with equality for the Cobb-Douglas case, where
\Delta_{h}^{\prime}(t_{h})=-\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})=K\alpha/t_{h}
), and using
\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})\geq-|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|
,

\displaystyle\Delta_{h}^{\prime}(t_{h})\,D(t_{h})+\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})\,N(t_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\Delta_{h}^{\prime}(t_{h})\,D(t_{h})-|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|\,N(t_{h})

\displaystyle\geq|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|\,D(t_{h})-|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|\,N(t_{h})

\displaystyle=|\Delta^{\prime}(t_{h})|\,[D(t_{h})-N(t_{h})]\ \geq\ 0.

Hence
dx_{sym}/dt_{h}\geq 0
, and
x_{sym}
is non-decreasing in
t_{h}
on
[t_{l},t_{\text{sym}})
.

Discontinuous transition at
t_{\text{FIOS}}
.
The FIOS cutoff is
\underline{x}(t_{h})=\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}[\Delta(t_{h})+\Delta_{h}(t_{h})]-c
. In the Cobb-Douglas case,
\Delta(t_{h})+\Delta_{h}(t_{h})=B+1/2
is independent of
t_{h}
, so
\underline{x}(t_{h})=\frac{\lambda}{r+\lambda}(B+1/2)-c
is constant in
t_{h}
. By contrast,
x_{sym}(t_{\text{FIOS}}^{-})
—the limiting value of the symmetric cutoff as
t_{h}
approaches the threshold from below—satisfies the L-L acceptance constraint with equality:
\lambda x_{sym}(t_{\text{FIOS}}^{-})(\phi(L,H)-\phi(L,L))=r\phi(L,L)
. For the calibration of Example
2
,
x_{sym}(t_{\text{FIOS}}^{-})\approx 0.72
while
\underline{x}\approx 0.04
, so the equilibrium investment profile changes discretely at the threshold: one gender jumps from
x_{sym}
to full investment, while the other drops from
x_{sym}
to
\underline{x}\ll x_{sym}
.

Step 8: Conclusion.

We have established:

•

Step 3: For all
t_{h}\in[t_{l},t_{\text{sym}})
, the unique equilibrium is symmetric.

•

Step 6: For all
t_{h}\geq t_{\text{FIOS}}
, the unique equilibrium is FIOS.

•

Step 7:
x_{sym}(t_{h})
is non-decreasing on the symmetric regime, and the transition at
t_{\text{FIOS}}
is discontinuous.

The thresholds satisfy
t_{l}<t_{\text{sym}}\leq t_{\text{FIOS}}<\infty
as required.
∎
