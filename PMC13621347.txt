Lattice oxygen-mediated ABO3-type NdFeO3 perovskite fabricated over multi-walled CNTs for the O2 evolution reaction and photo-induced degradation of Victoria blue B

Rabbia Shoukat, Sadam Hussain, Sadia Ata, Hanaa A. Galeb, Aliya Kouser, Ayesha Sadiqa, Adnan Ashraf, Awais Ahmad

Abstract

Hydrogen is generally considered a future sustainable energy carrier, and its electrochemical synthesis is based on the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER). Nonetheless, a high energy barrier and slow reaction kinetics plague the OER, posing huge obstacles to efficient hydrogen production. In order to address the shortcoming, designing efficient electrocatalysts is essential to enhancing the efficiency and scalability of the OER. On the other hand, the emergence of life-threatening pollutants has pushed researchers towards designing photocatalysts for photo-induced degradation. Therefore, a perovskite (PSK) ABO3-type NdFeO3 was fabricated over multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs) to target dual applications of water splitting and photodegradation. Techniques such as XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, SEM, TEM, EDS, PL/UV-vis spectroscopy, TGA, BET, and Raman spectroscopy were used to characterize the nanocomposite. The prepared NdFeO3@MWCNTs showed a direct band gap energy (Eg) of 2.5 eV and an indirect Eg of 1.9 eV as determined through UV-visible spectroscopy. Furthermore, numerous parameters, including catalyst dosage, pollutant concentration, oxidizing agent, and pH, were comprehensively examined for the degradation of Victoria blue B dye. The percentage degradation was found to be optimized for the 250 ppm pollutant solution with 97% removal efficiency within the initial 5 minutes. Furthermore, the electrochemical studies of NdFeO3@MWCNTs showed its potential as an electrocatalyst with an overpotential (η) of 250 mV at a current density of 10 mA cm−2, along with a low Tafel slope of 67 mV dec−1. Moreover, the material showed an electrochemical surface area (ECSA) of 100 cm2 and a decreased charge transfer resistance (Rct) of 1.5 Ω, with the low Rct being ascribed to the faster charge transfer rate. Hence, the low-cost NdFeO3@MWCNTs nanocomposite, compared to noble metal electrodes, is not only a promising photocatalyst but also an electrocatalyst for future applications in H2 generation.

Introduction

Water, labelled as the universal solvent, is currently contaminated with life-threatening pollutants, making it unsuitable for consumption. These pollutants encompass not only industrial adulterants but also pharmaceutical toxins. Moreover, these pollutants are harmful to the life thriving on land and are equally detrimental to aquatic life as well. Therefore, there is a need to plan strategies that can eliminate these contaminants. For this, ultra-filtration, photocatalysis, biological methods, reverse osmosis, electrochemical sensing, ion exchange, and other methodologies are being developed. However, all these approaches come with some drawbacks. The ultra-filtration process suffers from membrane fouling and material accumulation on the membrane surface. Biological methods involve microorganisms in degradation processes and face the challenge of excessive microorganism requirements. A high rate of solute rejection in reverse osmosis is also a drawback. Ion exchange makes it a prerequisite that the components to be degraded must be charged under neutral conditions. Because of the shortcomings of conventional approaches, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are leading the course of action. AOPs can occur through diverse routes; among them, photo-Fenton is a much-praised approach owing to its low energy demand. In this process, H2O2 and Fe3+ ions are used. Fe2+ is first generated upon the reduction of Fe3+ under light irradiation, and then, ̇OH radicals, which are strong oxidizing agents, are produced when Fe2+ reacts with H2O2.1Fe3+ + hν(sunlight) → Fe2+2Fe2+ + H2O2 → ̇OH + Fe3+ + H2O

Among various semiconductor photocatalysts, ABO3-type perovskite oxides have attracted considerable interest because of their structural versatility and excellent photocatalytic activity. Their intrinsic ability to regulate oxygen vacancies, band structures, and charge-transfer pathways provides opportunities for designing high-performance photocatalysts. In this regard, NdFeO3@MWCNTs were fabricated for evaluation as an electrocatalyst. Apart from fabrication, doping and compositing with layered structures are also ways to carry out defect creation, which enhances the overall material performance. Substitutional doping modifies the electronic structure based on the site that the dopant occupies, thereby enhancing efficiency. Recent studies have demonstrated that doping-induced defect engineering can regulate surface properties and introduce active sites, promoting reactive species generation and enhancing photocatalytic degradation efficiency. Furthermore, incorporation of dopants into semiconductor lattices has been reported to improve photocatalytic performance by facilitating interfacial charge transfer and suppressing electron–hole recombination pathways.

Besides doping, constructing composite photocatalysts with layered materials has emerged as another promising approach because the formation of heterointerfaces can accelerate charge migration and reduce the recombination losses of photogenerated carriers. Layered composite architectures provide efficient pathways for electron transfer and improve photocatalytic stability by creating synergistic interactions between different components, resulting in enhanced degradation performance under irradiation.

Among various perovskite oxides, NdFeO3 has attracted considerable attention due to its stable ABO3 framework, narrow band gap, and favorable electronic configuration arising from the interaction between the rare-earth Nd ions and the transition-metal Fe ions. The presence of Fe-based active centers provides potential redox activity, making NdFeO3 suitable for both photocatalytic and electrocatalytic applications. However, its practical application is limited by charge-carrier recombination and its relatively poor electrical conductivity. Therefore, coupling NdFeO3 with conductive carbon-based materials such as MWCNTs can provide efficient electron-transport pathways and enhance its overall catalytic performance.

However, our research focuses on designing MWCNT-doped NdFeO3 as a catalyst for Victoria blue B dye degradation, and its activity in both UV and visible regions supports its applicability as a photocatalyst. Neodymium, a potent lanthanide when modified into ferrite, enhances the compound's performance. However, it has a drawback, which is the agglomeration of NdFe2O4 due to its size. This needs to be tackled. Hence, a dopant is introduced to address the aggregation of the precursor material. MWCNTs have excellent electronic properties and outstanding mechanical tensile strength and are robust for the intended application. The degradation efficiency of the designed catalyst is enhanced using an activator, which is optimized as a parameter. For this purpose, H2O2 proves to be an unmatched booster of activity. Hydroxyl radicals generated from H2O2 serve as an oxidizing agent. Ilanchezhiyan et al. synthesized a neodymium oxide perovskite nanomaterial for photo-induced electrochemical H2O splitting. Jasrotia et al. performed electrocatalytic hydrogen production by employing La/Nd-substituted cobalt–nickel nanomaterials. Adewuyi et al. synthesized NdFe2O4 over graphitic carbonitride (NdFe2O4@g-C3N4) for the photodegradation of ciprofloxacin (CIP) and ampicillin (AMP). In addition, Abbad et al. hydrothermally synthesized copper and gadolinium co-doped NdFe2O4 for crystal violet (CV) dye degradation and achieved 91.8% efficacy. Prabagar et al. hydrothermally synthesized NdFeO3 perovskite for methylene blue and lomefloxacin photocatalysis. Phan et al. prepared copper-substituted NdFeO3 by employing a hydrothermal technique and investigated the visible-light-assisted Fenton degradation process of phenolic compounds. Bhat et al. synthesized a copper-doped perovskite oxide (CaTiO3) for methylene blue degradation, and the results were exceptional due to the low band gap and decreased charge-carrier recombination. Uma et al. carried out the photodegradation of methyl violet dye using copper-doped SrTiO3 with above 90% degradation efficiency. The above-mentioned studies demonstrate that ample work has been carried out on NdFe2O4 as a photocatalyst for pollutant degradation. However, to the best of our knowledge, the designing of NdFe2O4@MWCNT as photocatalysts has not been reported to date. Hence, the effectiveness of the novel NdFe2O4@MWCNT nanocomposite for the photo-oxidation of an organic dye, along with H2O splitting, is clearly substantiated in the current study.

Synthesis is carried out hydrothermally through a facile route, which involves the preparation of NdFeO3. The functionalization of MWCNTs is a potent step. Functionalization promotes efficient dispersion and boosts electrical properties of the tubes.

In the present study, a perovskite material was designed using a facile hydrothermal synthesis route. As a photocatalyst, it demonstrates greater than 95% degradation proficiency. Moreover, as an electrocatalyst, NdFeO3@MWCNTs shows an overpotential of just 250 mV at 10 mA cm−2, a minimum Tafel slope of 67 mV dec−1, and an Rct of 1.5 Ω. Hence, owing to the dual functionality of the nanocomposite, it can serve as a future photo-electrocatalyst.

Experimental section

Chemical materials

Neodymium(iii) nitrate hexahydrate (99.5% pure), multi-walled CNTs (99.5% pure), NafionTM 117, potassium hydroxide (KOH), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) pellets (98% pure) were procured from Sigma-Aldrich. Analytical grade chemicals like H2SO4, H2O2, HNO3 and ethanol of 99% purity were used. Iron nitrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O) purchased from DUKSAN was utilized. All the chemicals used were of high-grade purity and were used without any supplemental refining steps.

Synthesis of neodymium ferrite (NdFeO3)

3 g of Nd(NO3)3·6H2O and 4.5 g of Fe(NO3)3·9H2O were dissolved in deionized water. Both solutions were subjected to vigorous shaking, and their pH was maintained around 9–10 by the dropwise addition of 3 M NaOH. After mixing, the solution was stirred for 30 minutes. Hydrothermal approach have been adopted for material designing. The solution from the beaker was transferred into a Teflon vessel and filled up to 60–70% of its volume. The Teflon-lined autoclave was placed in a heating oven and was subjected to heating for 6 hours at 160 °C. The sample was dried at 70 °C after neutralization and washing, and a powdered mixture was acquired after grinding. The powdered sample was preserved for further characterization.

Functionalization of MWCNTs

Functionalization of MWCNTs is a vital step in the entire nanocomposite design process. The multi-walled CNTs were treated with a mixture of acids (H2SO4 and HNO3) and H2O2. The MWCNTs were left in these acids for a few hours at 120 °C. Then, after activation, the nanotubes were neutralized by continuous washing. The nanotubes were filtered and dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C. Afterwards, the nanotube material was ground, heated, and stored for use after adequate drying.

Synthesis of NdFeO3-doped MWCNTs

Initially, 100 mg of functionalized MWCNTs was dispersed in 40 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring. Subsequently, 20 mg of the NdFeO3 precursor was added to the dispersion, corresponding to an NdFeO3 : MWCNTs mass ratio of 1 : 5. The resulting composite therefore contained approximately 16.67 wt% NdFeO3 and 83.33 wt% MWCNTs. The pH was adjusted to approximately 10, and the mixture was continuously stirred for 30 min. The resulting suspension was then transferred to a Teflon-lined autoclave and hydrothermally treated at 160 °C for 6 h. After completion of the reaction, the product was neutralized by continuous washing, filtered, dried, ground, and stored for further characterization. The schematic diagram is shown in .

Characterization

The prepared nanocomposite material was characterized for structural, morphological, and compositional properties using different characterization techniques. X-ray diffraction was performed using an XRD spectrometer (Rigaku D/max-2500 X-ray diffractometer) for deciphering the phase and structure of the crystal. FTIR (Fourier Transform IR) was employed for detecting multiple functional groups in the compound arising from different bonds stretching and bending vibrations. A PerkinElmer Spectrum IR version 10.6.2 FTIR spectrometer was used for these studies. Morphological interpretation was carried out using a 6490 (LA) scanning electron microscope. An S-200D R & M England UV spectrophotometer was used for studying the photocatalytic degradation efficiency. A transmission electron microscope (JEOL JEM-1230, Akishima, Japan) was used for analysis. A thermogravimetric Q50 V6.2 Build 187 analyzer was employed to conduct TGA measurements (New Castle, DE, USA). The sample was subjected to heating at a rate of 30 °C min−1 to 600 °C, with a consistent N2 gas flow (20 mL min−1). Raman spectra were analyzed using “LabSpec 6” from HORIBA Scientific. Moreover, the absorption as well as bandgap (Eg) studies for the respective material were conducted using a UV-visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan). The emission peaks corresponding to the transition of electrons in various bands were obtained using a xenon lamp equipped spectrofluorometer (λex range = 190–880 nm) for photoluminescence (PL) studies.

Mechanism of dye degradation

Degradation occurs in four crucial steps with the excitation of the nanocomposite material being the first step.

Absorption of light and electron excitation

The nanocomposite precursor (NdFeO3) absorbs photons with energy equal to or greater than its band gap to eject electrons from its valence band to the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band of the material, as shown in the proposed :9NdFeO3 + hν → eCB− + hVB+

Transfer of electrons to MWCNTs

MWCNTs provide an extensive conductive network due to their low Fermi level. Hence, the electrons remain in the conduction band, thereby lessening the intensity of electron–hole recombination. Hence, the degradation efficiency increases in this way, as shown in :10eCB(NdFeO3)− → MWCNTs

Generation of possible reactive O2 species (ROS)

Superoxide radicals ( ̇O2−) formed in and hydroxyl radicals ( ̇OH) formed in and are reactive O2 species. Superoxide radicals are probably generated when dissolved oxygen reacts with the accumulated electrons on MWCNTs. On the other hand, holes in the valence band oxidize water and hydroxide ions to form hydroxyl radicals.11O2 + e− → ̇O2−12H2O + h+ → ̇OH + H+13OH− + h+ → ̇OH

Dye degradation

ROS, due to their robust oxidative strength, carry out the cleavage of dye chromophoric groups (color-imparting groups) and dye mineralization into possible less-pernicious or non-pernicious components, such as CO2 and H2O, as shown in . Some inorganic substances are also generated during the mineralization process.14Dye + ̇OH/ ̇O2− → intermediate products → (CO2 + H2O)

The schematic demonstrates photo-induced electron–hole generation in NdFeO3, electron migration through the conductive MWCNT framework, and the subsequent formation of reactive oxygen species responsible for Victoria blue B degradation.

Results and discussions

Characterization techniques, including photoluminescence (PL)/UV-vis spectroscopy, XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, TGA, TEM, RAMAN, BET, SEM, and EDX spectroscopy have been employed in the best way to investigate the photocatalyst's light-capturing capabilities, structural parameters, functional groups, morphology and elemental composition. All these approaches are discussed in detail in the following paragraphs.

PL/UV-visible spectrophotometric study

Photoluminescence (PL) technique is used to obtain information regarding the efficiency of photocatalytic materials in isolating charge carriers. The photogenerated carriers, such as holes and e−, tend to recombine, thereby decreasing the degradation proficiency. Hence, the PL/UV-vis spectrum signifies this aspect, as shown in , which indicates a lower e−–hole recombination tendency, manifesting a greater engagement time in the degradation phenomenon. Moreover, advancing research reflects the concept of relating these emissions to the material's interfacial properties.

Our designed nanostructured material, NdFeO3@MWCNTs, was tested for its ability to harvest UV-visible light. The compound was tested using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. The results showed the robustness of the nanocomposite in capturing light in the 200–800 nm wavelength range. This reflects the effectiveness of the photocatalyst in the UV and visible regions. The band-gap energy is determined using the Tauc plot. The equation below provides the parameters for plotting the graph:15(αhν)2/n = A(hν − Eg)Here, the photon energy is connoted by “hν”, “h” signifies Planck's constant and h = 6.63 × 10−34 J s, and “α” is the absorption coefficient. The optical band-gap energy is denoted by “Eg”, “ν” is the frequency, and “A” is a constant, which is material-dependent. Hence, the direct band gap of the synthesized NdFeO3@MWCNTs is 2.5 eV. shows the light-harvesting potency of the photocatalyst in the visible range.

The absorption peak at 936 cm−1 is due to NO3− as nitric acid has been used for the functionalization of MWCNTs. Raman analysis

The Raman spectrum shown in presents evident vibrational peaks corresponding to MWCNTs, as well as the rare earth metal-based perovskite oxide material. Definite Ag and B3g modes are assigned to phonon emissions in the low-wavenumber domain and are attributed to the NdFeO3 oxide precursor. However, the successful incorporation of MWCNTs is reflected in the respective D and G bands. The D band at around 1338 cm−1 (ref. ) and the G band at about 1578 cm−1 (ref. ) are the indicators of the intensity of defects as well as the graphitization (sp2-hybridized carbon). Moreover, the ID/IG ratio indicating the defect intensity is 2.1 in the current case, demonstrating that the increased defects might also be due to defects incurred during MWCNT functionalization with acids to some extent. MWCNTs are not a single-layered structure; rather, they are multi-layered, and this CNT multilayer structure is evidenced by the D band presence around 2700 cm−1, as also reported in a previous study.

Hence, the Raman spectrum gives a prominent account of NdFeO3@MWCNTs formation. The shift in intensity and wavenumber is due to the interfacial reciprocity between the precursor and the multi-layered CNTs.

Thermogravimetric analysis

TGA provides productive information regarding the material's stability by showing weight loss with increasing temperature. The technique assists in the evaluation of the thermal stability of both carbon- and non-carbon-based materials. In the case of NdFeO3@MWCNTs, the weight loss below 100 °C is due to adsorbed H2O or moisture, indicating the hydrophilicity of the composite surface. However, in the first-derivative DTG curve, around 300 °C, the weight loss is due to the detachment of the functional group (COOH owing to functionalized CNTs). Afterward, the NdFeO3@MWCNTs stability is reflected in the negligible weight loss with the increase in temperature beyond 500 °C, which is due to the NdFeO3 precursor framework. Moreover, a sharp loss followed by a subsequent stable phase at an intensely high temperature can be seen in the DTG curve in inset. Hence, the interpretation confirms the integrity of NdFeO3@MWCNTs and the potential application of the nanocomposite as a high-temperature material.

BET (Brunauer–Emmett–Teller) analysis

Nitrogen adsorption–desorption analysis was performed to investigate the surface characteristics and pore structure of the NdFeO3@MWCNTs nanocomposite. As presented in Fig. S8(a), the adsorption–desorption isotherm exhibits a typical type IV isotherm with a hysteresis loop, indicating the presence of mesoporous characteristics within the composite structure. The BET surface area of the prepared NdFeO3@MWCNTs nanocomposite was calculated to be 31.638 m2 g−1, with a total pore volume of 0.352 cm3 g−1. Furthermore, BJH pore size distribution analysis (Fig. S8(b)) revealed an average pore diameter of approximately 3.49 nm, confirming the formation of a mesoporous framework.

Although the obtained surface area is moderate compared with some highly porous carbon-based materials, it remains sufficient for photocatalytic applications due to the synergistic role of the MWCNT network and NdFeO3 nanoparticles. The relatively lower surface area can be attributed to the incorporation of NdFeO3 nanoparticles onto the MWCNT framework, which may partially occupy or block the accessible nanotube surface and pore channels. Additionally, the magnetic nature of ferrite nanoparticles can promote particle aggregation, reducing the accessible surface area. However, the intimate interaction between NdFeO3 and conductive MWCNTs provides efficient charge migration pathways, suppresses electron–hole recombination, and enhances the photocatalytic performance beyond the contribution of surface area alone.

The enhanced multifunctional catalytic performance of NdFeO3@MWCNTs can be attributed to the synergistic interaction between the NdFeO3 perovskite phase and the conductive MWCNT network. The crystalline NdFeO3 provides abundant catalytic active sites, while the MWCNT framework improves electrical conductivity and serves as an efficient pathway for charge migration. The intimate interface between NdFeO3 and MWCNTs suppresses electron–hole recombination during photocatalysis and facilitates interfacial charge transfer during the OER. Moreover, the mesoporous characteristics and increased accessible surface area promote reactant interaction and provide additional active sites. These combined structural and electronic advantages contribute to the enhanced photocatalytic degradation of Victoria blue B and improved oxygen evolution activity.

Photocatalytic degradation

Catalyst dosage effect

Catalyst dosage shows a proportional effect with the increase in catalyst amount. As the amount of catalyst employed increases while keeping the pollutant concentration and pH constant, the time taken by the catalyst to degrade the pollutant is reduced. A 97% efficiency of pollutant degradation is achieved with a 5 mg catalyst dose, and the efficacy was about 78% with 1 mg catalyst as the minimum dose. The effect of catalyst dosage on the pollutant-removal efficiency is best illustrated in the graph given in Fig. S5 in the SI. All the data were collected at a wavelength of 593 nm, which is considered the optimum wavelength for the degradation study of Victoria blue B according to the literature.

Pollutant concentration effect

The degradation phenomenon slows down as the pollutant concentration increases. A 1000 ppm dye solution takes a comparatively greater time for degradation than a 250 ppm pollutant solution. The degradation of the 1000 ppm solution is slow because of the enrichment of pollutants and the overall decreasing catalytic site availability for the photo-oxidation of the dye molecules. For the 250 ppm pollutant solution, 97% removal is obtained within the first 5 minutes compared to the 1000 ppm solution, which requires 20 minutes for achieving almost the same degradation efficiency.

Electrochemical studies

All electrochemical measurements for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) evaluation were performed at room temperature under ambient conditions using a standard three-electrode configuration. The NdFeO3@MWCNTs nanocomposite material was tested for its electrochemical parameters through CV, LSV and EIS. LSV is just single potential sweep, either in the forward or in the reverse direction. 1 M KOH solution was used as the electrolyte solution for the OER, and all the electrodes are immersed in the electrolyte. A reverse LSV was performed to avoid the overlap of the nickel foam oxidation peak with the catalyst's oxidation, and the Tafel slope and the overpotential were calculated from it.

The overpotential for NdFeO3@MWCNTs was determined from LSV, and the value was 250 mV at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1 to achieve a current density of 10 mA cm−2. The overpotential increased to about 300 mV for achieving 100 mA cm−2 current density, as shown in . 10 mA cm−2 serves as a benchmark current density, and the performance of all the electrocatalysts is compared at this current density. Moreover, NdFeO3@MWCNTs have a very low onset potential of 1.47 V as shown in , defining the marvelous oxygen evolution potential of the aforementioned electrocatalyst. To verify the contribution of the catalyst layer, the CV response of the bare Ni foam was compared with that of the NdFeO3@MWCNTs-coated Ni foam electrode (Fig. S7). The bare Ni foam exhibited a negligible current response compared with the catalyst-modified electrode, demonstrating that the enhanced electrochemical activity originates from the NdFeO3@MWCNTs nanocomposite. Overpotential is the electrocatalyst's intrinsic feature.

Oxygen evolution is a more elaborate four-step process and encounters a high energy barrier. Hence, to overcome this energy constraint, an electrocatalyst is employed. The following are the reaction steps occurring over the surface of an electrocatalyst, eventually leading to O2 evolution. The second electrochemical criterion is the Tafel slope, which was found to be 67 mV dec−1. The Tafel slope is a fundamental kinetic parameter that can give a clue about the reaction mechanism and the rate-determining step of the oxygen evolution reaction (OER). It is a measure of the overpotential required to raise the current density by a factor of 10. Hence, decreased overpotential in conjunction with a low Tafel slope is a good indicator of NdFeO3@MWCNTs' potential for electrochemical applications, such as energy storage and conversion.

With respect to the OER, a smaller Tafel slope is preferred because it reflects more rapid reaction rates and a better electrocatalyst. The observed value of 67 mV dec−1 indicates a relatively moderate catalytic activity, which means that the reaction kinetics are hindered by a chemical step after the primary electron transfer. shows the corresponding Tafel plot. Moreover, CV cycles in the non-faradaic region were recorded at different scan rates for the calculation of the double-layer capacitance (Cdl), and then, the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) was derived from it. ECSA = Cdl/Cs, where Cs is the specific capacitance for a substrate, which ranges from 0.02 to 0.08 mF cm−2. The greater the ECSA, the better the electrocatalyst. Conclusion

In conclusion, the fabricated nanomaterial not only functions as an electrocatalyst but also as a photocatalyst. Key electrochemical features, such as a low Tafel slope, overpotential, and Rct, are defining parameters in determining the potential of NdFeO3@MWCNTs as an electrocatalyst. However, as we are concerned with the photocatalyst behavior, the direct and indirect band gap energies of 2.5 eV and 1.9 eV, respectively, are signatures of extended pollutant oxidation and reduction efficacy. The electrochemical properties such as overpotential (η), Tafel slope, electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), and Rct were calculated through different tests for the nanocomposite material. Quantifying the outcomes for NdFeO3@MWCNTs shows its marvelous OER activity in 1 M alkaline KOH environment, with an η of 250 mV at 10 mA cm−2 current density, a Tafel slope of 67 mV dec−1, an ECSA of 100 cm2, and an Rct of 1.5 Ω. Additionally, the nanocatalyst demonstrates an excellent degradation efficiency of 97% with just 5 mg catalyst.

The nanocatalyst was prepared through a simple hydrothermal route, and the lower cost of our catalyst than that of noble metal catalysts makes it a worthy choice for use. XRD, FTIR spectroscopy, SEM, TEM, EDX spectroscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and TGA confirmed the synthesis of NdFeO3@MWCNTs, as well as the thermo-stability of the catalyst under different temperatures. The successful synthesis of NdFeO3@MWCNTs is evidenced by the distinct phonon peaks in the low wavenumber region for the precursor material, alongside the characteristic D and G Raman bands for the CNTs, where the D band confirms their multi-layered nature. In short, the nanocomposite can be employed as an efficient, durable, and facile electrocatalyst for future green energy generation.
