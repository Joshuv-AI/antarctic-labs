Executive summary
Demonstration overview
A technology demonstration project funded by the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) was hosted by
Boston Public Schools (BPS). This project evaluated wireless controls technology for lighting systems
and plug loads (energy consumed by devices plugged into electrical outlets), implemented along with
LED lighting retrofits in two classrooms. This study assesses technology readiness and effectiveness
to determine the deployment potential of the technology in schools.
BPS selected Boston Green Academy (BGA) as the host facility for the project. BGA is a grade 7-12
school serving approximately 500 students. The facility, originally built in 1895, has an estimated floor
area of 103,000 ft2. Two classrooms, each with 12 overhead light fixtures and two plug-in through-
window air conditioners (or “AC units”), were selected for the retrofit evaluation. The lighting retrofits
included dimmable tubular LED lamps, or TLEDs, with integrated wireless controls in one classroom,
and color temperature tunable LED fixtures in another. The plug load controls were applied to electric
outlets that power the AC units in the classrooms.

Summary results per performance objective
PO - 1. Lighting energy savings: The baseline classroom lighting systems were more efficient than in
 typical older school buildings and the teachers were already in the habit of turning the lights off
 regularly when class was not in session. Potential lighting energy savings was therefore limited.

 The retrofit LEDs did however save 10% to 48% lighting energy in classrooms 107 (LED-to-LED
 retrofit) and 108 (fluorescent-to-LED retrofit), respectively. The classroom retrofits included
 controls that enable additional energy savings from features like occupancy-based on/off operation
 and “top-trimming” (also known as "high-end trim," "task tuning," or "institutional tuning"), the
 configuration through controls settings of the lighting system to reduce maximum output to levels
 appropriate to classroom tasks and activities. Controls-based energy savings are included in the
 totals above and are not disaggregated here, but studies have shown that controls savings in
 educational facilities can be substantial.
PO - 2. Lighting visual comfort: Various lighting performance metrics were assessed, including average
 task illuminance (lux), correlated color temperature (CCT, in degrees Kelvin), color rendering index
 (CRI), melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (m-EDI, a circadian performance metric), and
 flicker. Post-retrofit, the electric light levels in room 108 were lower than pre-retrofit levels but
 closer to standard practice (the baseline would be considered “overlit”). For classroom 107, at the
 “medium” setting configured during system commissioning, average light levels were higher than
 baseline levels (which would be considered “underlit”) and again, closer to recommended practice.
 m-EDI results, relevant for assessing the circadian effects of lighting systems, indicate lower levels
 than published guidance but improvements relative to baseline levels. Flicker testing of the retrofit
 TLEDs indicated acceptable performance and in fact, some of the best flicker performance of
 dimmable TLED products available in the market today.
PO - 3. Plug load energy savings: The through-window AC units were well-controlled in the baseline
 condition so energy savings potential from the outlet controls was limited. Data does suggest that
 less after-hours operation occurred post-retrofit. An estimated 15% reduction in AC energy usage
 could be achieved by the controls, due to mostly eliminating unnecessary after-hours cooling.
PO - 4. Plug load and lighting energy reporting: At higher wattages the accuracy of reported power from
 the controls system was within 2% relative to reference measurements. At lower wattages the
 controls reporting was less accurate, under-reporting power levels, though the impact on absolute
 accuracy of the system was minimal.
PO - 5. Controls standby power: Wireless controls draw some power continuously to maintain network
 connection and perform necessary functions. Standby power for several components was
 measured in the lab and the total annualized standby energy usage for the system at the scale of a
 whole-school retrofit was calculated. Standby power for the system is estimated at just under 0.02
 W/ft2 and annual standby energy usage for the controls system is estimated at 0.11 to 0.13
 kWh/ft2/yr.
PO - 6. Retrofit package cost effectiveness: Total equipment and labor costs were estimated for a whole-
 school retrofit rather than simply summing actual costs for the demonstration project, which was a
 small-scale installation that did not benefit from typical building-level economies of scale. The
 whole-school retrofit scenario was based on the DOE reference model for secondary schools (U.S.
 DOE, n.d.) with 95,000 ft2 of applicable floor area. The energy savings percentages, as quantified by
 the measurement and verification (M&V) analysis, were applied to the reference model’s yearly
 energy usage to estimate annual savings, which were multiplied by BPS electric utility rates to
 calculate cost savings. At the scaled retrofit costs and savings values, the simple payback for the
 technology package of networked lighting controls, outlet controls, and TLEDs is estimated at 3.8

 and issues. The teacher using classroom 108 changed during the study and feedback was not

 with the system, noting that the classroom’s overall experience with the new lighting system has
 been positive and the controls have worked as intended. The class prefers the warm color
 temperature and relies mostly on the occupancy control feature for on/off control. District
 personnel noted how the demonstration allowed for exploration of possibilities with custom lighting
 setups for classrooms. BPS plans to explore LED lighting upgrades in other facilities if funding is
 available, but with less technically complex systems than those demonstrated here (for example,
 likely not implementing tunable LEDs). Concerns were expressed about the configuration
 challenges with the controls in room 107 and the implications for the scalability of the controls
 platform.

Conclusions
LED lighting is a well-proven retrofit technology for saving energy, particularly when replacing legacy
fluorescent lighting. With the efficiency advances in LED technology over time, LED-to-LED retrofits can
also yield savings, though at modest levels compared to retrofits with fluorescent baselines. Wireless
networked controls have also shown promise for improved management of lighting and plug loads in
educational facilities, as demonstrated here. If lighting and controls retrofits are implemented at-scale,
such as in an entire school building or across a district, the value of energy savings and of more robust
load management could be substantial.
Additionally, color temperature tunability, which was assessed in one classroom, can yield additional
lighting quality benefits when retrofitting legacy light fixtures to LEDs. However, dynamic lighting
systems are inherently more complex. The degree to which these systems are appropriate for a school
depends on user (student, teacher, administrator, school district) interest in and willingness to interact
with these systems to derive benefits.

Networked AC outlet controls were demonstrated as a viable retrofit option but the degree of energy
savings available is dependent on the types of plug-in loads present in a facility and their current usage
patterns, including the degree of usage during unoccupied times. For facilities with high-powered plug
loads that are hard to manage and run excessively when not needed, potential energy savings may
justify the cost of an outlet controls retrofit. Where loads already have adequate controls and where
users are already attentive about turning loads off, there is less potential value to be derived from a
retrofit.
For this demonstration, the retrofit package with networked controls and TLEDs replacing fluorescent
lights yielded the highest energy savings and achieved a short simple payback (below the stated
Performance Objective of five years). Most of the savings were simply from the switch to more
efficient, lower-power LED lights. The LED-to-LED retrofit yielded more modest energy savings and the
payback was therefore longer. Limited additional value was derived from the scheduling controls
implemented on the wall outlets.
It is clear from the analysis here that many variables influence the cost of implementing lighting
retrofits and networked controls systems, which are more complex than typical controls present in
classrooms. In regards to scaling this report’s findings, it is important to remember that payback
results will vary based on the circumstances of any given school building or district. Finally, control
system reliability and ease of use will be crucial for wider acceptance and broader deployment. “Getting
it right” with respect to ease of implementation and reliable system operation over time and at scales
larger than the demonstration level will be critical to develop the confidence that school districts need
in order to move forward with technology rollout in their facilities.

Introduction
Project overview
This study evaluates a field demonstration of networked wireless control technology for lighting
systems and plug loads1 in schools, packaged with LED lighting retrofits. The networked plug loads
controls were applied to classroom outlets that power through-window air conditioners, or “AC units,”
implementing schedules to reduce after-hours operation. The lighting retrofits consisted of LED
equipment compatible with the retrofit controls technology, including one-for-one lamp and fixture
replacements in two classrooms in Boston Green Academy, a secondary school in the Boston Public
Schools system. Lighting controls features implemented with the retrofit package include occupancy
sensing, schedule controls, and wall switches for on/off, dimming, scene selection,2 and color
temperature tuning.
Two LED technology types were demonstrated; one in each classroom:
 ● Dimmable tubular LED lamps (or TLEDs) with integrated wireless controls. TLEDs match the
 form factor of traditional fluorescent lamps and are designed to be compatible with existing
 fluorescent fixtures. TLEDs were installed in existing suspended two-lamp fluorescent fixtures
 in classroom 108.
 ● Color tunable (“tunable white”) LED fixtures with integrated wireless controls for added lighting
 quality benefits. In contrast to TLEDs, LED fixtures replace existing fixtures completely. The
 tunable fixtures in this demonstration replaced first-generation non-dimmable LED fixtures in
 classroom 107.
The technology readiness of wireless networked lighting and plug load controls combined with LED
retrofits was assessed to determine deployment potential in classroom environments. The goals of the
demonstration project were to:
 ● Select a demonstration location that is representative of common conditions in existing school
 facilities nation-wide for high-impact, generalizable results
 ● Measure, quantify, and demonstrate technology performance in a real-world setting with
 qualitative and quantitative success criteria
 ● Demonstrate benefits in terms of energy savings, occupant comfort, useability, ease of
 implementation
 ● Validate success in the selected use case (i.e. the classroom environment) for the
 predetermined performance objectives

Background and literature review
Specifying and adopting LEDs in schools
Homes in the U.S. have adopted LED technology at a rapid pace. By 2020 around half of residential light
sources were LEDs, up from only a small fraction earlier in the decade (EIA, 2022). LED adoption in the
education sector has proceeded at a much lower rate; in fact the lowest of any building sector in the
U.S., with 78% of lighting in 2020 still being fluorescent lighting (Lee et al., 2024). The prevalence of
linear fluorescent lighting in schools is likely due to many factors. The technology is perceived as easy
to maintain and replace and predictable in terms of performance (e.g. light levels, output, appearance).
Also, due to lighting in schools operating on average for fewer hours per year than in other commercial

Dynamic and tunable lighting strategies in schools
Advanced controls platforms such as the one implemented for this project and discussed in further
detail in the technology description section below support a range of dynamic lighting controls features.
These include brightening and dimming the lights (wall switch or app-based) for different activities in
the classrooms, occupancy-based control, daylight harvesting (dimming lights when sufficient daylight
is present), and scene controls with user-configured settings for different classroom activities. The
controls are also compatible with “tunable white” LEDs, a sub-type of LEDs for which color temperature
can be dynamically adjusted based on user preferences. Color temperature options range from warm
white temperatures (e.g. 2700K to 3000K) to cool white temperatures (e.g. 5000K to 6500K).
The literature on dynamic and tunable LED lighting systems for classrooms supports the attention this
technology has garnered. Various research efforts have investigated effects of dynamic, tunable
lighting for learning environments. One study on the effects of variable intensity and color temperature
setting in classrooms implemented several scene options, from “standard” (300 lux, 4000K) to a
warmer, dimmer “relax” setting (325 lux, 3500K) and a bright, cool color temperature “concentrate”
setting (1060 lux, 5800K). The study assessed several student performance and attitude metrics,
finding that the dynamic ability to change scenes facilitated fewer errors in standardized tests of
attention and concentration, with improvements in standardized reading and comprehension, albeit of
limited statistical significance (Barkmann et al., 2012). A meta-study on how lighting parameters like
LBNL School Retrofit Demonstration of Lighting and Outlet Controls with LED Upgrades Page 12

intensity and color temperature influence learning processes such as cognition, academic achievement,
attention, work speed, productivity and accuracy, found that LED systems and dynamic lighting
schemes for different classroom activities showed particular promise and that color temperature can
have an impact on learning and alertness, with task switching improved at higher CCTs (Mogas-Recalde
et al., 2021). Another study on the association between performance and lighting environment showed
that lighting can be an influential factor in young student cognitive function, concentration, alertness,
and mood, with improved reading performance at higher CCT and higher intensity levels (Lekan-Kehinde
et al., 2021).

 Key lighting performance criteria considered in this study
 ● Task illuminance (lux): the amount of light (luminous flux) falling onto a specific surface where a
 visual task is performed. One lux is defined as one lumen per square meter. This unit is more
 commonly used in lighting research than the imperial unit for illuminance—the foot-candle (fc),
 defined as lumens per square foot.
 ● Correlated color temperature (CCT): or simply color temperature, describes the color appearance
 of a light source and is measured in degrees Kelvin (K). It ranges from warm, yellow-toned light
 (lower Kelvin values) to cool, blue-toned light (higher Kelvin values).
 ● Color rendering index (CRI): a quantitative measure of a light source's ability to reveal the true
 colors of objects compared to a standard reference light source, such as sunlight. CRI is expressed
 on a scale from 0 to 100, with 100 being the most ideal color rendition.
 ● Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (m-EDI, in lux): the level of illuminance from a
 standard daylight source that would have the same melanopic effect as the light being measured,
 taking into account brightness and spectral content and measured on a vertical plane at the eye
 level of the observer. The melanopic effects refer to the impact of light on the body's non-visual
 biological processes, such as circadian rhythm and alertness; specifically effects on the
 melanopsin-containing cells in the retina.
 ● Flicker: also known as Temporal Light Modulation (TLM), involves rapid fluctuation in the
 brightness of a light source over time that can be either imperceptible or consciously perceived.
 The term encompasses percent modulation (the relative difference between the maximum and
 minimum light intensity over a single cycle), stroboscopic effect (indirect phenomenon where
 moving objects appear to move in a discontinuous manner or appear stationary), and phantom
 array effect (occurs during rapid eye movement, causing a stationary, flickering light source to
 appear as a series of dots or lines across the visual field).

A DOE study found that as more schools evaluate LED retrofit opportunities, many are considering
tunable LED systems. Respondents indicated that tunable lighting can improve the classroom
environment for teachers and students alike (U.S. DOE, 2024, c). DOE studies on tunable white LED
systems in several classrooms found energy savings of almost 50% at similar light levels as the
baseline systems. Teachers expressed satisfaction with dynamic lighting scenes, which they found to
be effective for facilitating changing classroom programming (for example transitioning from
independent study to active instruction). Teachers considered dimming as the most important feature
and color tuning as a “secondary benefit” (U.S. DOE, 2019, a).

Tubular LEDs and flicker concerns
Tubular LED lamps, or TLEDs, are designed as direct replacements for traditional fluorescent tubes,
fitting into existing fixtures to upgrade the light source to a more efficient technology with less effort
and cost than a full fixture replacement. “Type B” TLEDs are installed in the lamp holders of existing
fluorescent fixtures. The fluorescent ballasts are bypassed and line voltage (e.g. 120V or 277V, 60 Hz)
is provided directly to the TLEDs, which have internal driver components to convert the electricity to the
appropriate DC voltage and drive current. While the education sector has lagged in overall adoption of
LED technology, schools that are installing LEDs have embraced in particular Type B TLED technology
due to the simple and affordable installation process.
An important performance consideration for retrofit lighting technology is flicker (temporal light
modulation, or TLM), which varies from being undetectable to an annoyance or even being debilitating
depending on the observer. Flicker, though not as commonly a concern with whole-fixture LED products,
has been found to be an issue with some TLEDs, particularly Type B TLEDs, the product category
demonstrated here. Pacific Northwest National Lab (PNNL) researchers have been investigating trends
in school LED adoption, conducting conversations with over 30 school facility personnel. At several of
the schools, PNNL measured flicker performance, finding concern with the Type B products in the field.
Many systems were not in compliance with flicker performance standards and even performed worse
than legacy magnetically ballasted fluorescent systems. Samples of market-available products were
brought to PNNL’s test facilities for testing (28 products from 26 manufacturers) with a majority
exhibiting poor flicker performance, sometimes even when product literature claimed the contrary (Myer
et al., 2024). Because of concerns with TLED flicker, the flicker performance of the demonstration
technology was measured prior to installation of the retrofit at the school to ensure adequacy (see the
results section on visual comfort).

Outlet controls for plug-in AC units in schools
According to a 2013 DOE retrofit guide, plug loads account for up to 25% of a school’s annual electricity
usage, including devices like printers, screens and computers, and copiers. Cooling accounts for 12% to
41% of facility energy usage depending on climate zone. It is not clear from the report whether there is
overlap in the plug load and cooling energy figures, though plug-in cooling equipment like through-
window AC units is prevalent in certain climate zones. The retrofit guide does recommend “smart”
power strips for plug-in equipment, with “built-in occupancy sensors, timers, or logic that senses when
attached devices are idle, [and] can shut them off when nobody is present“ (U.S. DOE, 2013).
There appears to be limited research on potential energy savings from outlet controllers for AC units in
schools. Perhaps the most relevant example is a recent DOE-funded school retrofit demonstration that
installed plug-in timers on classroom HVAC equipment in a school in Hawaii to reduce energy use
outside of instruction hours (evenings, weekends, and holidays). That project found small but not
statistically significant reductions in energy use for through-window AC units equipped with timer
controls, though results were confounded by circumstances such as occupancy changes in the
classrooms. For similar classrooms at the facility that installed schedule controls on split-system AC
units, significant energy savings were achieved, with an overall decrease in energy use of 21% (LBNL,

Demonstration technology description
The controls technology evaluated in this project is wireless networked lighting and outlet controls.
Many controls platform vendors offer commercial products in this category, which has been growing
over the past decade. The vendor selected for the demonstration, Toggled iQ ®, leverages the
Bluetooth® low energy (LE) wireless mesh communication protocol to manage lighting and outlet
controllers. All Toggled devices have a Bluetooth radio that is individually addressable. The system
shares messaging data across all connected devices on one network. Wireless, battery-powered
sensors support occupancy-based lighting and plug load control, as well as daylight harvesting (a
feature that was not implemented in this project) and collection of temperature / humidity data.
Bluetooth wall switches enable system schedules to be transmitted to controlled lights and outlets as
well as providing dimming, on/off commands, and scene control (user-configured sets of lighting
conditions, such as levels of intensity, color, or on/off status to create a particular atmosphere or
accommodate a specific activity). The demonstration plug load controller has the capability to monitor
energy consumption for each monitored circuit which allows facility managers and system operators to
better track and manage energy usage during the year. The controls network is configured and
managed via an app installed on mobile phones or tablets, which also enables viewing of system energy
data (watts in real time, kilowatt-hours over time).
The controls platform software can be hosted and operated onsite with no internet connection, but the
product line includes an optional internet gateway that allows users to access a cloud-based interface
to manage and control devices and view data. The Toggled iQ system meets the DesignLights
Consortium℠ (DLC) technical requirements for networked lighting control systems3 and is listed on the
DLC’s Qualified Products List (QPL). The DLC provides the lighting industry with an independent means
of verifyhing performance claims for quality lighting and lighting control products.
This project also evaluated LED lighting technology, a mature, highly effective retrofit option for saving
energy in commercial buildings. Existing lighting equipment in the demonstration classrooms was
converted to efficient LED technology controllable by the new wireless network controls platform. The
new lighting equipment saves energy due to improved system efficacy (for lighting systems this is
denoted as lumens per watt, or LPW) as well as implementation of energy-saving controls features like
dimming and occupancy-based control. Wireless, centralized control of the lighting system helps
automate processes and can provide teachers the ability to better manage the classroom environment
based on activities and learning objectives.

and consistent basis for evaluating project outcomes.

 Quantify the reduced energy consumption for the classroom lighting systems due to switching

 implementing advanced lighting controls through the retrofit controls platform.
PO - 2. Lighting visual comfort

 Measure flicker characteristics of the selected TLED retrofit technology to confirm acceptable

 Quantify the reduced energy consumption for loads powered by classroom outlets, specifically
 through-window AC units, due to automated scheduling of outlet power (for example, switching

Results
Quantitative results
PO - 1. Lighting energy savings
The baseline lighting conditions in both classrooms included low effective lighting power densities
(LPD) in terms of average watts per ft2. Classroom 107 already had an LED retrofit some years prior to
this demonstration, so the existing fixture wattage was already low. The fluorescent fixtures in
classroom 108 were two-lamp units with a low ballast factor, meaning that the T8 lamp wattage was
significantly lower (less power) than the standard 32W. The measured lighting power densities of 107
and 108, 0.31W/ft2 and at 0.84W/ft2, are considerably lower than those found in older existing buildings
with typical fluorescent lighting systems, often 1.0W to 1.5W/ft2 or more (see the DOE reference model
discussion in the scaled retrofit costs section later). As facilities have adopted lower-wattage
fluorescent technology and in some cases LED retrofits, the average wattage for lights installed in
school facilities had decreased to around 0.6W/ft2 per the DOE 2020 lighting survey report (Lee et al.,

Annual lighting energy usage
The baseline lighting systems in classrooms 107 and 108 were quite energy-efficient already, due to
factors discussed above. The efficiency of an electric lighting system is broadly proportional to the
average power of the system and the annual operating hours. Reducing power or operating hours by the
same factor has the same effect. Power was measured for this demonstration, while annual operating
hours must be estimated based on trends observed in the data and what is known about the schedule
of the school building. Estimates on the annual operating hours for lighting systems in schools vary
widely, with some projections of 1,500 hours or less, and others near 3,000 hours.4 That range of

classrooms at BGA, based on the school calendar, lighting energy data analysis, and daily patterns of
lighting usage, around 220 days per year of classroom lighting system operation are estimated, with the
equivalent of around 1,600 hours of operation per year.

PO - 2. Lighting visual comfort
Lighting quality
Classroom lighting system performance is evaluated here pre- and post-retrofit in terms of average task
illuminance, color temperature and color rendering index, as well as melanopic equivalent daylight
illuminance (m-EDI, in lux at eye-level, vertical plane) based on spectral power measurements. The
following are definitions of key lighting parameters used in evaluating the lighting technology in this
demonstration.
 ● Task illuminance (lux): the amount of light (luminous flux) that falls onto a specific surface
 where a visual task is performed. One lux is defined as one lumen per square meter. This unit is
 more commonly used in lighting research than the imperial unit of illuminance, the foot-candle
 (fc); lumens per square foot.
 ● Color temperature (CCT): describes the color appearance of a light source and is measured in
 degrees Kelvin (K). It ranges from warm, yellow-toned light (lower Kelvin values) to cool, blue-
 toned light (higher Kelvin values).
 ● Color rendering index (CRI): a quantitative measure of a light source's ability to reveal the true
 colors of objects compared to a standard reference light source, such as sunlight. CRI is
 expressed on a scale from 0 to 100.
 ● Melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (m-EDI): the level of illuminance from a standard
 daylight source that would have the same melanopic effect as the light being measured (at the
 eye of the observer), taking into account brightness and spectral content and measured on a
 vertical plane at the eye level of the observer. The melanopic effects here refer to the impact of
 light on the body's non-visual biological processes, such as circadian rhythm and alertness;
 specifically effects on the melanopsin-containing cells in the retina. The Illuminating
 Engineering Society (IES) describes m-EDI as “the amount of biologically relevant illuminance at
 the eye [that influences] physiological responses” (IES, 2023). m-EDI has emerged as one of the
 most common metrics for quantifying circadian and alerting effects of lighting systems;
 generally higher levels during the day promote alertness and suppression of melatonin
 production, while lower levels in the evening help the body prepare for sleep by up-regulating
 melatonin production.
Both classrooms have substantial window area, so daylight plays a large role in the lighting
environment in the demonstration spaces. Window conditions were not changed in any way during the
retrofit process; only the electric lighting systems were replaced. Accordingly, the lighting performance
of concern for this evaluation is primarily that of the electric lights. Electric lighting systems are
designed and specified to deliver minimum acceptable light levels in the absence of daylight, since its
presence cannot be guaranteed. Most of the lighting quality analysis here is based on measurements
taken after sunset or with effects of daylight subtracted. Task illuminance, color rendering, and
melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance will generally be greater during occupied hours in the
classrooms than what is shown here due to the availability of daylight.

Some guidance and best practices for lighting in built environments now consider m-EDI levels for
circadian benefits, normally on a vertical plane at the eye-level of a seated observer (indicative of the
occupant’s actual exposure to m-EDI), rather than on the horizontal plane of a task surface (indicative
of the illumination of the visual task). Circadian lighting design typically specifies a minimum number of
hours per day during which a space must deliver a minimum level of m-EDI to benefit the circadian
health of occupants. Evolving industry practices may include requiring several hours of at least 150 to
250 lux m-EDI from electric lighting in spaces without daylight exposure, or at least 110 to 160 lux m-
EDI from electric lighting in spaces with daylight.

This finding is not surprising given that light levels specified by common lighting design practice are

only recently becoming more well-understood. There is then some tension between standard lighting
design practice and emerging “circadian lighting design” guidance. The classrooms in this study do

in these rooms will play a large role in actual m-EDI levels through the school day and year. However,
thorough analysis of daylight effects through the year requires daylight simulation since solar intensity
and angles change continuously based on time of day, year, latitude, and even local weather. Such
analysis is beyond the scope of this study.

PO - 3. Plug load energy savings
Heating at Boston Green Academy is provided by a central boiler system, with zone radiators in
classrooms and all other conditioned spaces. In recent years, the school’s classrooms have been fitted
with through-window air conditioners for cooling purposes. Equipping the rooms with AC units also
involved installing new electric circuits and wall-mounted outlet boxes along the base of the walls
where the AC equipment is located. For ventilation in the classrooms, manually operable windows and
mechanical ventilators are used. No differences in the baseline and retrofit thermal and air quality
characteristics attributable to the demonstration technology were expected.
Baseline AC unit energy data was collected in spring/early summer of 2024 and retrofit data was
collected in late summer/fall of 2024. Both of these timeframes are periods with cooling demand
(“cooling seasons”) where the AC units were in use in the classrooms. Retrofit plug load controls were
configured to turn AC outlets off from 6PM to 6AM to avoid unnecessary energy usage for cooling
during vacancy. The typical use of the through-window AC units was not altered during occupied hours
in the school room by the retrofit controls, which act only as a backstop against AC units running at
night and during other periods of vacancy (weekends, holidays).
It became clear from data analysis and conversations with staff that the AC units were well-controlled
in the baseline condition such that energy savings potential from the outlet controls was limited. The
equipment, like most air conditioning, is thermostatically controlled rather than simply turning “on” or
“off.” This is important when considering energy savings potential because for vacant periods the units
would only be on if the room temperature is high enough for thermostat-based commands to turn the

LBNL School Retrofit Demonstration of Lighting and Outlet Controls with LED Upgrades Page 44

units on. Since most vacancy is overnight, even a unit left on might not use much energy if the
nighttime air temperature is cool enough.
Another important aspect influencing savings potential is the degree to which the AC units were already
actively turned off when not in use (e.g. end of school day and on weekends). From anecdotes as well
as confirmation in the energy data for the AC unit outlets, it is clear that in the pre-retrofit condition the
AC units were not operating often during vacant periods due to the combination of staff actively turning
the units off and thermostatic controls.
However, evidence in the operating profiles of the window AC units pre- and post-retrofit suggests that
less after-hours operation (defined here as 6PM to 6AM) occurred once the outlet controls were
installed. In absolute terms, there were more total operating hours during and after-hours in the
baseline condition, which could be due to daily temperature variations and number of days requiring
cooling, as well as other factors. It was not in the scope of the project to weather-normalize the AC unit
energy data, but it is illustrative to compare the pre- and post-retrofit total number of after-hours
operating hours divided by the total number of operating hours; in other words, identifying the fraction
of operating hours that were outside of occupied periods. Doing so, it was found that in the baseline,
roughly 16% of total operating hours were after-hours, compared to less than 1% in the retrofit case.
More data and weather- and occupancy-normalization would be important to broadly apply these
findings, but in this specific case, the result indicate that if the controls were applied to the annual
cooling profiles of the AC units, they could eliminate around 15% of AC operating hours.

measured in the lab on a bench-scale setup. Neither an internet gateway nor a wirelessly controlled

PO - 6. Retrofit package cost effectiveness
The premise of energy-saving retrofits is that the cost of installing new equipment is paid back over
time by energy cost savings achieved by the retrofit technology. Simple payback, the time period in
years at which an investment is paid for by annual cost savings is a function of A) the initial cost to
implement the demonstration technology, including equipment costs and installation labor costs, and
B) the ongoing annualized cost savings – in this case cost savings on utility bills due to lower electric
energy usage from the lighting and outlets in the classrooms, net of any ongoing costs attributable to
the new system, such as annual service fees.
To calculate the cost of the retrofit installation, the controls vendor provided per-unit equipment pricing
for a scaled-deployment scenario, considering volume pricing as opposed to pricing at the small-scale
of the demonstration. Retrofit labor costs for a scaled-deployment were calculated based on simple
assumptions on labor costs per component, described in the scaled retrofit costs section below (see
Table 14). System performance with respect to energy savings is quantified by the M&V data and
LBNL School Retrofit Demonstration of Lighting and Outlet Controls with LED Upgrades Page 48

analysis from the demonstration project. Results from the study period are annualized and multiplied by
utility rates for electric energy ($/kWh) and demand ($/kW) to calculate yearly cost savings.
The main scaled retrofit scenario considered here is implementation of the wireless controls for AC
units combined with the dimmable TLED retrofit with integrated wireless controls, as this was the
highest-savings and lowest-cost option. A scaled retrofit scenario with the tunable LED fixtures
demonstrated in classroom 107 is included as well for comparison. That scenario assumes a baseline
of fluorescent lighting, as measured in room 108, since energy savings from fluorescent-to-LED
technology are much higher than the LED-to-LED retrofit savings in classroom 107 and fluorescent
lighting is the most common lighting technology in existing buildings. While retrofitting older LED
fixtures to tunable white LED fixtures can improve system efficacy and add controls functionality and
lighting quality benefits, energy savings relative to that baseline are small. LED fixtures are also more
costly in terms of equipment and labor than the TLEDs. However, if the tunable LED fixtures were
replacing fluorescent fixtures, energy cost savings should be similar to what was found with the
fluorescent-to-TLED retrofit, since post-retrofit normalized lighting power and energy were nearly
identical in the two classrooms.

Scaled system cost savings
The baseline lighting systems in classrooms 107 and 108 were relatively energy-efficient as discussed
previously, so absolute measured energy savings from the retrofit technologies was low, even with
relative savings in the 10% - 50% range. Low baseline lighting energy usage means that lighting retrofit
costs are unlikely to be recovered at a reasonable time scale in the specific classrooms where the
demonstration took place.
A more broadly applicable energy savings analysis for the demonstration technology retrofitted at scale
can be derived by applying the relative energy savings found in the demonstration to a “typical” lighting
energy baseline. The secondary school reference model used for the scaled costs also defines an
applicable baseline energy usage estimate. For that model, the simulated annual lighting energy usage
for the entire facility is 939,500 kWh. The classroom and office lighting system power density in the
model is around 1.4W/ft2, higher than the other zones in the model and much higher than the maximum
baseline lighting power densities measured in classrooms 107 and 108 at BGA. Weighting the model’s
annual lighting energy by floor area per space type and lighting power density per space type (all zones
in the model have the same lighting schedule and annual operating hours), 53% of the facility’s annual
lighting energy usage, or 498,000 kWh (5.24 kWh/ft2-yr), is attributable to classrooms and offices.
The percentage difference between the baseline and retrofit average daily lighting power from the
demonstration project can be multipled by the model’s annual lighting energy usage to estimate energy
savings per square foot for the scaled retrofit analysis. This is actually a conservative approach for
estimating savings in the scaled retrofit, as the relative savings would likely be higher than found in the
demonstration, such as the fluorescent-to-TLED retrofit savings of 48%. For a school with a higher
baseline lighting power density and less-efficent baseline lighting control, savings could easily be much
higher (50% to 75% typical).
The TLED and the tunable LED fixture retrofits both resulted in nearly the same average lighting power
post-retrofit, so the 48% savings figure is used for both options in the scaled retrofit analysis, resulting

Scaled retrofit payback estimate
At the estimated scaled retrofit cost and annual dollar savings, the retrofit package of TLEDs, lighting
controls, and outlet controls payback is 3.8 years. For the retrofit package with tunable LED fixtures
instead of TLEDs, the installation costs are considerably higher and payback is 6.8 to 9.0 years
depending on fixture controller type. The TLED package payback is below the target package payback
value of less than five years as specified in PO-6. The whole fixture package is above that target
payback but still may fall within a school or district’s parameters for acceptable retrofit investment if
the lighting performance enhancements are a priority. This decision would consider more than the
least-cost or lowest-payback option and weigh performance objectives like lighting tunability.
A retrofit package implementing the outlet controls only at the costs and savings projected here would
not offer a compelling payback period because the savings are simply too low to justify the investment
by themselves. This could change in scenarios where more baseline energy is used by outlets, such as
facilities with high-wattage plug-in equipment that is on for significant amounts of time unnecessarily.
However, such systems may also be able to save significant energy at much lower cost with simpler
schedule-based plug-in outlet controllers or Wi-Fi-enabled plug-in outlet controllers that are also
commonly available.

Installation Cost Considerations and Uncertainties
There are myriad factors affect the pricing and savings estimates and payback analysis carried out
here, and the overall cost and complexity of controls and lighting retrofits in schools. Some key topics
are discussed below, though this list is not comprehensive.
 ● Fixture / controller compatibility, for LED fixture retrofits: The ability to use the smaller less-
 expensive fixture controller rather than area lighting controller would bring down hardware
 costs substantially (over $50 per fixture), but the fixture controller compatibility question is a
 difficult one, as only very specific LED driver types are compatible with the vendor’s fixture
 controller, which requires auxiliary power sourcing from the driver (12 - 24V DC) to operate, and
 0-10V dimming channel as well as dim-to-off capability in LED driver (i.e. at 0V DC on dimming
 channel, internal relay from driver to diodes opens). For color tunable LEDs, it was also
 challenging to find a compatible fixture to meet the specifications of the area controller,
 including the dual 0-10V dimming and color tuning channel.
 ● Ballast and lamp socket type, for TLED retrofits: if shunted lamps sockets (for “instant start”
 ballasts) are used in the existing fluorescent fixtures, as was the case in Boston Green
 Academy, these need to be replaced with non-shunted lamp sockets before TLEDs are installed,
 leading to modest increases in time and cost of installation labor.
 ● Presence of neutral wire in switch boxes: Neutral wires are often not available inside switch
 boxes in existing construction, especially in older buildings, requiring running conduit and new
 circuits and switch boxes, or pulling neutral wires to existing switch boxes, in order to power
 the controls switches, which require hot and neutral wires. This increases cost of switch
 installation, potentially substantially.
 ● Ceiling access: plenum depth for the suspended ceiling in Boston Green Academy was a
 serious issue for specifying the LED retrofit fixtures, which had to be narrow profile flat panel
 fixtures with a minimal depth of driver box to be able to recess into the dropped ceiling.
 Asbestos may also be a concern in many school classroom ceilings, affecting feasibility and
 cost. The surfaces and materials in the demonstration classrooms were tested for asbestos to
 ensure this was not an issue prior to the retrofit installation.
 ● Accessibility of outlet boxes: in older buildings with masonry construction and other hard
 surfaces, circuits and boxes for outlets are often run outside of and against the cinderblock,
 brick, or concrete walls, making box knockouts easily accessible for the outlet controllers, as
 was the case at Boston Green Academy. However, in schools where outlets serving loads such
 as AC units are mounted inside of the walls, outlet controller installation will be substantially
 more complicated and costly.
 ● Network accessibility, reliability, and security: This is a major topic for network/cloud
 connected controls technologies such as the one demonstrated here. The enterprise network
 configurations can be very specific and connecting new devices is not always straightforward.
 Third party systems also often face hurdles or outright prohibitions with respect to connection
 to school IT networks. These issues should be thoroughly discussed and addressed during
 retrofit planning, and can affect cost and timing of system installation and configuration.
 ● Lighting baseline conditions: The equipment density and wattage in the baseline and the total
 annual operating hours, as well as how well those hours match actual classroom occupancy
 (i.e. does the lighting system often operate when the space is vacant?) have an important

LBNL School Retrofit Demonstration of Lighting and Outlet Controls with LED Upgrades Page 53

 influence on the estimated absolute energy savings and the cost savings that flow from those,
 which in turn have a big impact on the payback calculation. In the BGA demonstration the
 lighting baselines in both classrooms were already efficient, which would limit payback
 potential. This likely results in an under-estimating here of the potential relative energy savings
 (in % terms) applied to the lighting energy in this scaled retrofit analysis.
 ● Tax exemption status of entity purchasing equipment: This will dictate whether sales tax,
 averaging over 7%, will be added to equipment costs, notwithstanding other markups.
 ● Labor rates and technical familiarity of the installation contractors, can vary widely, affecting
 the time it takes to install the various system components and the cost. Experience and
 familiarity with types of equipment involved may substantially impact retrofit cost.

PO - 7. Sensor, switch, and application configuration, performance, and reliability
After installation of the controls system components, device discovery and configuration in the smart
phone application for the controls was intuitive and relatively seamless. As the various network
endpoints were installed and energized, from fixture controllers and sensors to wall switches and outlet
controllers, the application discovered the new devices through Bluetooth communication. With devices
identified, they were then located, named, and grouped in the application interface, according to the
classroom, device type, and zone (e.g. row of fixtures). From this point scenes were set for
characteristics such as “top trimming” the brightness levels of the light fixtures and TLEDs, color
temperature settings for classroom 107, and assignment of scenes to the wall switch buttons. The
default lighting system action for occupancy-based controls (e.g. auto-on when motion is sensed) were
also set as well as occupancy sensor settings such as vacancy timeouts.

Conclusions
Wireless networked controls are becoming an established and proven energy-saving technology that
shows promise for managing lighting and plug loads in a range of commercial building types, including
educational facilities. The technology demonstrated here functioned as an integrated platform
effectively controlling classroom outlets as well as dimmable retrofit lighting systems (TLEDs and
integrated LED fixtures) with schedules, switches, sensors, and a smartphone application. The system
was able to operate as a “stand-alone” Bluetooth mesh network while also demonstrating effective
cloud connectivity through an optional internet gateway for remote access, monitoring, and control.
LED lighting systems are reliable retrofits for energy efficiency, particularly when replacing legacy
fluorescent lighting systems. LED to higher-efficacy-LED retrofits can also yield savings as well, though
at much more modest levels with incremental improvements within the same technology class. Color
temperature tunability can add value and enhance the lighting environment in classrooms, and teachers
and students alike respond positively to these systems in various studies. Research shows that lighting
characteristics like brightness and color temperature can have positive effects on learning and behavior
in classrooms, though such human subjects research was not within the scope of this demonstration.
Tunable LED systems are inherently more complex however, with the additional dimension of dynamic
color temperature control. The degree to which this technology is appropriate for a school depends on
user interest in and willingness to interact with these systems to derive benefits. Administrators and
teachers as well as facility staff are typically very busy with many demands on their time and with
limited bandwidth for new systems that may require more attention and interaction. From discussions
with BPS, after the demonstration project, the interests of the district, in light of budgets and competing
priorities, lie mostly in simpler lighting retrofit approaches rather than more complex lighting systems
and networked controls.
Networked AC outlet controls were demonstrated as a viable option in classrooms with accessible (e.g.
surface-mounted) outlet boxes, and can act as a backstop to limit unnecessary energy usage. However,
the degree of energy savings available is dependent on the types of plug-in loads present and what
controls are already managing equipment operation. For facilities with high-powered plug-in loads that
are hard to manage and run excessively when not in use, energy savings may justify the cost of a
retrofit. If, as was the case in this demonstration, loads already have adequate controls integrated, such
as responsive thermostats, and if users are already attentive about turning loads off when not in use,
energy savings potential may be lower and retrofit value may not “pencil out.”
Further, in regards to the cost effectiveness of lighting and plug loads retrofits, the package including
TLEDs and networked controls replacing a fluorescent baseline yielded good project economics in
terms of simple payback (3.8 years), with most of the value coming simply from the switch to the more
efficient, lower-power LED lights with networked controls. Limited additional value was derived from the
outlet controls in terms of annual utility cost savings. Outlet controls alone, without the lighting retrofit,
would not be considered cost effective for the load type and usage patterns observed here. LED-to-LED
retrofits likely will also have a hard time meeting payback expectations for investments from school
districts. However, there are a great many variables that influence the costs to deploy the kinds of
systems evaluated here. Several are listed in the discussion on cost considerations and uncertainties
above. Site specifics including construction vintage and type, and presence of complicating factors like

LBNL School Retrofit Demonstration of Lighting and Outlet Controls with LED Upgrades Page 58

asbestos, hard ceilings, lack of neutral wires in switch boxes, and many other factors will affect project
costs. Workforce availability and familiarity with, and training in, installation and configuration of these
types of devices and systems will also influence implementation cost and success.
Additional challenges may arise around network access and security for locations where cloud
connection is desired for network controls retrofits. In many cases, such as that of the demonstrated
technology, network controls software can be hosted locally on system devices with no cloud
connection required for system functionality. However, support and diagnostics in such cases are
limited to personnel onsite and in Bluetooth range of the controls network, so cloud services versus
local hosting may be a matter of tradeoffs between cost, complexity, serviceability, and network
security. A related consideration is the potential for ongoing fees to support and maintain cloud
services, which are typically provided through a subscription service model that adds ongoing costs to
system usage and maintenance. A plan for how these fees are integrated into district budgets is
important before adoption of “as-a-service” type technology.
Finally, control system reliability and ease of use will be crucial for wider acceptance and broader
deployment. Some of the aspects of the demonstrated technology were intuitive and straightforward,
including device discovery and zone configuration and mapping, while some challenges were
encountered with scene setting applied to Bluetooth wall switches and responsiveness of the system to
commands executed at the switch and app. For retrofit products aiming at wide scale adoption in
schools, “getting it right” early with respect to configuration processes, and effectively demonstrating
reliable and repeatable controls behavior over time and at scale, will be critical to instill the confidence
that school districts need in order to move forward with technology rollout in their facilities.
