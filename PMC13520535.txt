Associated factors of nerve injury post-total hip arthroplasty: a meta-analysis
Authors: Yawei An, Yanan Kong, Dengju Li, Kaixuan Liu, Ping Li, Senbo An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNerve injury is a rare complication following total hip arthroplasty (THA), but its management is highly difficult once it occurs. To reduce its incidence, deeply investigating its associated factors is of key significance. Therefore, the associated factors of nerve injury post-THA were analyzed and summarized in this meta-analysis by study screening and data processing.MethodsWe searched Embase, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science up to April 28, 2025 for studies on the associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. We performed quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale and data analysis using Stata 15.0.ResultTwelve case-control studies involving 1,179,492 patients were finally included. Univariate analyses revealed that elevated odds of nerve injury post-THA had associations with younger age (< 50 years) [OR (95% CI) 1.73 (1.31, 2.28), P < 0.0001], obesity (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2 in this study) [OR (95% CI) 1.35 (1.16, 1.56), P < 0.0001], sex (female) [OR (95% CI) 1.33 (1.24, 1.43), P < 0.0001], fractures [OR (95% CI) 1.30 (1.08, 1.57), P = 0.006], operation time [SMD (95% CI) 0.37 (0.17, 0.58), P < 0.0001], revision surgery [OR (95% CI) 1.89 (1.31, 2.74), P = 0.001], and spinal diseases [OR (95% CI) 2.26 (1.79, 2.86), P < 0.0001]. Multivariate analyses showed that sex (female) [OR (95% CI) 1.22 (1.10, 1.35), P < 0.0001], posterolateral (PL) approach [OR (95% CI) 1.95 (1.35, 2.81), P < 0.0001] and spinal diseases [OR (95% CI) 2.60 (1.46, 4.62), P < 0.0001] were significantly associated with postoperative nerve injury.ConclusionUnivariate analyses revealed that younger age (< 50 years), obesity, sex (female), fractures, longer operation time, revision surgery, and spinal diseases were all associated with increased odds of nerve injury post-THA. However, multivariate analyses showed that only sex (female), PL approach, and spinal diseases had significant associations with higher odds of nerve injury post-THA. No significant association with nerve injury was observed for osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, and diabetes. The leave-one-out sensitivity analysis in the spinal disease subgroup had limited robustness of results, while the results of multivariate sensitivity analysis were robust. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the conclusions. Additionally, due to the limited data available for multivariate analysis in the included studies, great heterogeneity in some data, and the presence of multiple types of nerve injury, our findings should be interpreted cautiously.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10286-1.Keywords: Nerve injury, Total hip arthroplasty, Associated factors, Meta-analysis, Posterolateral approach

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is a surgical method widely applied to the treatment of severe hip lesions, and postoperative complications including dislocations, infections, and tendon injuries [1, 2] may occur. As a more severe complication, nerve injury is mainly caused by compression, traction, ischemia and/or transection, lacerations, bone cement thermal damage, and vascular injury [3–6]. It is reported that the incidence of nerve injury related to THA is 0.6%-3.7% [4, 7, 8], and it frequently occurs in the femoral, sciatic, superior femoral, and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves (LFCN), as well as the infrapatellar nerve branch and knee articular nerve [3]. In a clinical trial (2016) on the risk, clinical progression, and therapy of common peroneal nerve palsy following THA, all six patients only achieved partial sensory recovery with complete motor deficits two years postoperatively, of which one case had complete recovery and three cases had partial recovery 18 months postoperatively [9]. The results suggested that nerve injury post-THA, as a rare and potentially devastating complication, may lead to chronic pain and loss of function, which, regardless of whether they are complete or incomplete, are difficult to recover to preoperative neurological status [7], and it responds poorly to treatment, seriously affecting patients’ daily activities and quality of life. Therefore, it is especially important to clarify the associated factors of nerve injury in perioperative patients and to take preventive and control measures in advance.

There are several gaps in the available studies urgently to be addressed, which is also the core innovative entry point of this study: First, conclusions regarding some outcome metrics in available original clinical studies on associated factors of peripheral nerve injury (PNI) post-THA are contradictory, and no meta-analysis has yet been conducted to integrate all data and draw a unified evidence-based conclusion. Second, one meta-analysis explored associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. However, there is a lack of a quantitative systematic review of associated factors of nerve injury post-THA, and a lack of quantitative evidence to support clinical decision-making. Finally, all previous meta-analyses combined various types of peripheral nerve injuries (e.g., sciatic nerve, LFCN, and common peroneal nerve palsies) into one outcome metric for analysis. For example, several large-scale studies conducted by the research teams of Christ and Shetty raised academic awareness of the likelihood of PNI post-THA [5, 10]. However, these studies did not conduct subgroup analyses by nerve subtype, making it difficult to distinguish the specific pathogenesis and risk characteristics of different types of nerve injury. Specifically, most discussions regarding common peroneal nerve palsy rely on small-sample clinical case reports, and lack large-sample studies with high levels of evidence and sufficient evidence-based support.

Different types of PNI post-THA share many common causative factors, which are often associated with intraoperative soft tissue traction, mechanical compression from prosthesis implantation, and postoperative local hematoma compression [11]. Since the intraoperative injury pathways for various types of nerve injury show some similarities, different subtypes of nerve injury can be pooled for a meta-analysis.

From the perspective of overall THA perioperative clinical and nursing management, there are currently high-quality reviews that have conducted in-depth discussions on two key intervention regimens: intraoperative hemostasis and postoperative thrombosis prevention. Ghorbani et al. confirmed that tranocyclic acid (TXA) can reduce intraoperative blood loss, lower the need for blood transfusion, and improve surgical prognosis without increasing the risk of perioperative adverse events [12]. Borazjani et al. proposed that aspirin could be a reliable option for thrombosis prevention after primary or revised hip replacement [13], and the two drugs jointly improved the prevention and control strategy for perioperative complications of THA.

It is urgently needed to conduct an in-depth analysis and assessment of the probability of nerve injury post-THA. In this meta-analysis, we synthesized all relevant studies in the database. The primary objective was to systematically identify the associated factors of PNI post-THA. The secondary objective was to conduct subgroup analyses by the type of affected nerve to compare differences in the distribution of associated factors across subtypes of nerve injury.

Data and methods

The study protocol was developed and reviewed in line with the PRISMA-2020 guidelines [14], and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251050683).

Study search

We searched Embase, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science up to April 28, 2025 for studies on the associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. The search combined subject terms (“Total Hip Arthroplasty”, “Arthroplasty Replacement Hip”, “Replacement Arthroplasties Hip”, “Hip Prosthesis Implantation”, “Hip Replacement Total”, “Risk Factors”, “Population at Risk”, “Risk Score”, “Health Correlates”, and “Social Risk Factors”) and free-text words (“Nerve Injury”, “Nerve Injuries”). The search strategy is described in Supplementary Material 1.

Study screening

Two reviewers independently screened the studies retrieved based on the established eligibility criteria. First, the studies were imported into EndNote21 to eliminate duplicates, and then the title and abstract were read. The full text of the initially relevant studies was downloaded and examined to finally determine the eligible studies. Any disagreement was settled by a third party.

Data extraction

The following data were extracted independently by two reviewers: first author, country, year of publication, sample size, study design, sex, age, number of cases, and site of nerve injury. We extracted body mass index (BMI) of participants from each original study. Consistent with the definition adopted by the included study of Yan et al., obesity was defined as BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² for binary and pooled analysis [15]. All meta-analyses utilized effect estimates published in the original studies, without extracting raw enumeration data. If one study reported both unadjusted and multivariate-adjusted effect estimates, we prioritized the use of adjusted effect estimates for meta-analysis. After extraction, the two reviewers cross-checked the data to guarantee consistency of extraction. Any disagreement was settled by a third party.

Quality assessment

Three reviewers conducted quality assessment using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16]. This scale employs different assessment dimensions across different study designs: For cohort studies, assessment is based on selection of participants, comparability, and outcome assessment; for case-control studies, assessment is based on selection of cases and controls, comparability, and exposure. The total score is 9 points, with ≤ 4, 5–6, and ≥ 7 points as low, moderate, and high quality, respectively. Any disagreement was settled by discussion or consultation with a third party.

Statistical analysis

Univariate or multivariate analyses were conducted on the associated factors of nerve injury, but multivariate analysis results were prioritized when both types of analysis were included in the same study. We prioritized extracting multivariate ORs adjusted for confounders. The univariate results were extracted if only univariate analysis was available. For categorical variables (e.g., sex and fracture type), ORs were used as the pooled effect size. Since operation time was a continuous variable and was recorded using different units of measurement across studies, the standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to pool the effect sizes.

Stata 15.0 was utilized for statistical analysis, with pooled ORs and 95% CIs calculated. Heterogeneity was quantified by the Higgins I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. A random-effects model was utilized in case of great heterogeneity (I2 > 50%, P < 0.1); otherwise, a fixed-effects model was employed (I2 ≤ 50%, P > 0.1). Frontier methodological research in the field of meta-analysis suggests that the statistical significance of pooled effect sizes in meta-analyses related to THA is highly dependent on two core parameters: the selection of estimators for quantifying heterogeneity among studies, and whether the Hartung-Knapp correction method is applied [17]. Since the number of studies on most outcome metrics was limited (< 5) in this study, the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman correction was not applied, as this correction method is known to be unreliable when the number of studies is extremely small. Therefore, we employed the traditional DerSimonian-Laird estimator, which is also the widely accepted statistical strategy in orthopedic meta-analyses with a limited number of included studies. Pre-specified subgroup analyses and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were employed to investigate the sources of heterogeneity: Stratified by the subtype of nerve injury, associated factors were compared across subgroups of sciatic, femoral, and LFCN injuries. The robustness of the results was validated by sequentially excluding individual studies. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots combined with Egger’s tests, with P < 0.05 indicating great publication bias.

Results

Search strategy and results

Initially, 222 studies were retrieved, and after duplicate removal, the remaining 175 studies were screened by reading the titles and abstracts. The full text of the potentially relevant 54 studies was then examined, of which 25 were excluded due to an inappropriate full text and 17 due to no data record for outcomes of interest. Finally, 12 studies were included (Fig. 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of the study process

Study characteristics and quality assessment

The included studies involved 1,179,492 patients, with a total sample size ranging from 85 to 750,695 in each study. Eight studies were from the US, and the remaining four were from China, India, Poland, and Japan. The mean age was about 62 years. Nerve injuries at different sites following THA were reported in these studies, which all belonged to the category of PNI, specifically including peroneal nerve palsy (n = 1), LFCN palsy (n = 1), femoral nerve palsy (n = 2), and sciatic nerve palsy (n = 4). The basic characteristics are shown in Table 1. One study had an NOS score of 6 due to inadequate description of outcome assessment methods, insufficient comparability between exposed and non-exposed groups, and incomplete follow-up data. Three studies were scored 7 mainly due to missing follow-up information. In addition, some studies did not adequately match confounders at the design stage, resulting in limited intergroup comparability. Although the eight studies with an NOS score of 8 were generally of high quality, minor point deductions mostly arose from incomplete follow-up records or unclear reporting of non-response rates (Tables 2 and 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Based on the univariate analysis results, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted on age, obesity, fracture, sex, operation time, hypertension, revision surgery, spinal diseases, alcohol abuse, emotion, diabetes, osteoarthritis, RA, dislocation, and drug abuse (Figures S1-S14). The associations remained robust for age, obesity, sex, fracture, hypertension, revision surgery, operation time, alcohol abuse, emotion, diabetes, osteoarthritis, and RA, but they were non-robust for spinal diseases due to one study (Chen, 2024). After this study was removed, the pooled OR was 3.03 [95% CI (2.10, 4.37), P < 0.0001]. The robustness of these findings remains to be enhanced in the future. Based on the multivariate analysis results, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted on sex (female), PL approach and spinal diseases. The associations all remained robust (Figs. 13, 14 and 15).

As the number of included studies was less than 10 in each subgroup, the conditions for conducting a meta-regression analysis were not met, making it impossible to reliably quantify the influence of each covariate on the pooled effect. Therefore, leave-one-out sensitivity analyses were conducted.

For some studies with extremely large sample sizes, the results were visualized by forest plots: female (univariate) [OR (95% CI) 1.58 (1.20, 2.09), P = 0.001], spinal diseases (univariate) [OR (95% CI) 4.43 (2.20, 8.92), P = 0.001], and female (multivariate) [OR (95% CI) 1.55 (1.12, 2.15), P = 0.009]. The pooled effect sizes exhibited only minor fluctuations, and the statistical conclusions in each group remained unchanged. The sensitivity analyses indicated that the overall results were robust and reliable.

Publication bias

Each associated factor in the univariate analysis was visualized and assessed for publication bias by Egger’s tests and funnel plots. The funnel plot showed no obvious asymmetry. The Egger’s test revealed P-values > 0.05 for most associated factors, including obesity, age, sex, drug abuse, diabetes mellitus, fracture, hypertension, reoperation, operation time, spinal diseases, alcohol abuse, emotion, and RA. This result suggests no great publication bias. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method was used to assess publication bias. The results showed that the adjusted pooled effect size and 95% CI were essentially consistent with those of the original analysis, indicating that publication bias did not significantly alter the overall conclusions. Neither of the associated factors in the multivariate analysis (sex (female) and spinal diseases) displayed publication bias (P > 0.05) (Table 6). Notably, fewer than 10 studies were included for some outcome metrics, restricting the statistical power of the Egger’s test. Therefore, the results of the publication bias assessment for these outcome metrics should be interpreted cautiously (Table 6).

Discussion

This meta-analysis investigated associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. The pooled univariate analysis identified younger age, obesity, sex (female), postoperative fracture, revision surgery, prolonged operation time, and spinal diseases as associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. No significant association was found for RA, substance abuse, emotional disorders, dislocation, alcohol abuse, direct anterior approach (DAA), diabetes mellitus, and osteoarthritis. Notably, univariate models only reflected crude bivariate associations without adjusting for mutual confounding among variables, so these variables should only be interpreted as correlated factors rather than independent risk predictors. The subsequent multivariate meta-analysis with multiple confounders adjusted for supported that only sex (female), PL approach, and spinal diseases exhibited significant associations with elevated odds of nerve injury post-THA. Furthermore, subgroup analyses were conducted by the type of injured nerves, yielding new and more detailed findings. Different types of nerve injury vary in the pathogenesis and associated factors, necessitating individualized preventive interventions in clinical practice. Despite a limited sample size in some subgroups, the preliminary results can still serve as a reference for refined risk assessment and clinical management of nerve injury post-THA.

Sex (female) was correlated with nerve injury in the univariate analysis and was identified as a factor significantly associated with nerve injury in multivariate-adjusted models, warranting special clinical attention. The same conclusion was made by Hasija and Johanson [18, 19]. The possible reasons are as follows: First, females are at higher risk of nerve injury due to lower quality of soft tissues [4]; second, females have higher odds of developmental dysplasia of the hip [4]; third, it may be associated with changes in vascular anatomy, decreased muscle mass, and short limbs of females [20]. Solheim and Hagen argued that the susceptibility of females to nerve injury post-THA is attributed to the specificity of the sciatic nerve structure, but they failed to provide anatomical evidence regarding their views [21]. Therefore, targeted anatomical studies are required to clarify the association between sex (female) and nerve injury odds; an accurate risk assessment model should be created in combination with the surgical approach. In clinical practice, consideration should be given to anatomical differences in females, and enhanced preoperative imaging assessment and optimized intraoperative operation are also required to reduce the injury odds.

Spinal diseases exhibited a crude association with elevated odds of nerve injury in the univariate analysis, and a significant association remained following multivariate adjustment for confounders. The reason is that for patients with spinal diseases, nerves with pre-existing compression may fail to tolerate a second compressive injury, known as “double crush syndrome“ [4, 5]. Their nerve function will improve following decompression surgery [22]. Despite a reasonable biological rationale for this mechanism, direct evidence is currently limited, and the relevant theoretical hypotheses remain to be validated by more specialized studies. In this study, although the univariate effect size of spinal diseases was statistically significant [OR (95% CI) 2.26 (1.79, 2.86), P < 0.0001], the sensitivity analysis showed that the robustness of this result was limited. Similarly, the main reason was attributed to the study by Chen, which only reported revision THA. Moreover, the sample size varied greatly across the included studies. The small-sample case-control study by Shetty introduced bias in the pooled effect size of spinal diseases, thus increasing overall heterogeneity. After this study was excluded, heterogeneity in the remaining studies (all cohort designs) was effectively reduced (I²=5.90%, P = 0.345). Furthermore, after adjusting for confounders through multivariate sensitivity analysis, the pooled effect had no significant shift, making the overall conclusions relatively robust.

The multivariate adjusted analysis revealed that surgical approach was a factor significantly associated with nerve injury post-THA. Different surgical approaches result in varying manifestations of PNI post-THA. Several previous studies revealed that the PL approach is currently the most widely used surgical approach [4, 8, 23]. In contrast, the DAA is technically demanding and increases the odds of intraoperative fractures and nerve injury [15, 24]. LFCN palsy is a characteristic complication of DAA, which originates from the extensive dissection and continuous traction on the nerve course during exposure. A network meta-analysis (2024) further verified that compared with a standard operating table, DAA assisted by a traction table significantly raises soft tissue tension, thus further increasing the odds of LFCN injury caused by traction [25]. However, multivariate meta-analyses have shown that the PL approach is a factor significantly associated with nerve injury, particularly sciatic nerve injury [26]. The possible reasons are related to direct sharp or blunt trauma in intraoperative or perioperative periods, compression by surgical clamps, direct compression or thermal injury caused by bone wire or cement extrusion, intraneural or perineural hematomas, vascular issues, excessive leg extension, and surgeon’s experience [4, 23, 27]. Notably, this conclusion regarding the odds was based solely on two studies, with limited strength of the evidence. Therefore, individualized surgical plans should be developed based on patients’ specific conditions.

Younger age (≤ 50 years) only showed a crude association with nerve injury in the univariate analysis without adjustment for confounders. One possible explanation is that younger patients have abundant soft tissues, which prolongs intraoperative retraction time [5, 8, 10], or they suffer from complex hip lesions that require extensive soft tissue release, thereby increasing the odds of nerve injury [28, 29]. Although the aforementioned mechanisms are supported by existing literature, the available evidence remains limited. Clinical inferences regarding this association should be interpreted cautiously and remain to be further validated through more prospective cohort studies. In addition, univariate analyses are susceptible to confounding bias, and the heterogeneity of this indicator stems from multiple sources, including distinctions between primary and revision THA, different types of databases, and the heavy weight of a single large-sample study. Heterogeneity dropped substantially after removing this influential study (I2 = 0.00%, P = 0.527). Univariate analyses alone cannot verify whether age acts as an independent associated factor, and further validation is warranted through multivariate-adjusted analyses.

Revision surgery only showed a crude association with nerve injury in the univariate analysis without adjustment for confounders. We included both primary THA and revision THA cases in the meta-analysis, but these two groups had significant differences in the risk profiles for nerve injury. Due to the influence of scar adhesions at the surgical site, deformities in local anatomical structures, abnormal tissue layers formed in previous surgeries, bone defects, and more extensive intraoperative soft tissue traction, revision surgery has a completely different environment for injury exposure [4, 30]. Consequently, directly pooling data from primary and revision surgery cases may obscure specific factors of clinical significance in each group. Due to the objective limitation of insufficient stratified data available in the included original studies, subgroup analyses could not be performed on primary and revision surgeries. This represents a significant inherent limitation of this meta-analysis. Readers should fully consider the potential bias arising from population heterogeneity when interpreting the overall pooled effect estimates.

The univariate analysis stratified by nerve injury site revealed that prolonged operation time exhibited a crude association with elevated odds of LFCN injury. This association may be attributed to several factors. First, prolonged operation time increases the duration of soft tissue traction and nerve compression, particularly at the iliac crest and inguinal ligament, which are vulnerable regions of the LFCN. Second, more complex procedures in the posterolateral‌-anterolateral/inguinal regions are required during long-time surgeries, such as extensive dissection or hemostasis, which increases the odds of direct nerve injury, or nerve compression caused by sutures or scar tissues [26, 31]. Furthermore, long-time surgeries are often more complex (such as revision surgery or complex primary surgery for cases with deformities), which inherently carry higher odds of LFCN injury due to more complex anatomy and greater surgical difficulty.

The univariate analysis revealed a statistically significant association between hypertension and reduced odds of postoperative nerve injury, with a statistically significant OR value. However, the magnitude of this effect was small, and its 95% CI was close to the null value of 1. Therefore, this association should be interpreted cautiously[OR (95% CI) 0.89 (0.81, 0.99)]. This study could only suggest that hypertension may be a potential protective factor. However, sufficient external evidence is lacking for this inference, and this inverse association is likely due to confounders that were not adequately controlled for in the included studies. This unexpected finding is merely exploratory. In the future, more clinical samples should be included with multiple confounders adjusted for further validation and confirmation of this association.

The studies included various types of PNI, such as sciatic nerve palsy, femoral nerve palsy, LFCN injury, and common peroneal nerve palsy. These types of PNI greatly vary in the anatomically affected regions, causes of intraoperative injury, recovery prognosis, and clinical severity, and they also have different high-risk factors. LFCN injury is typically associated with anterior approach exposure and retractor/anatomical factors, and is sometimes attributed to sensory disturbances around superficial scars associated with surgical incision [26]. Sciatic or peroneal nerve palsy may be associated with traction, limb lengthening, revision scars, DDH-related deformities, hematomas, cement-related injuries, or posterior approach exposure [4, 26]. Femoral nerve injury may be associated with prolonged hip hyperextension intraoperatively, and also related to mechanical compression by a retractor placed anterior to the acetabulum, with a higher risk among individuals with a lower BMI or shorter stature [26]. This study pooled all PNIs into a single outcome, which may reduce the strength of associations of associated factors specific to different nerve injuries, obscure the unique pathogenesis of each subtype of injury, and reduce the discriminatory power and specificity of the pooled effect size. Despite subgroup analyses by the subtype of nerve injury, many studies only broadly reported “PNI” without distinguishing the affected nerve. Consequently, these data could not be incorporated into the stratified analysis, leaving considerable clinical heterogeneity. Due to this residual heterogeneity, the overall pooled OR could only reflect the average strength of association across all nerve injuries, rather than accurately representing the true odds of a single nerve injury. In summary, when interpreting the pooled effect size, we must be fully aware of the interference of clinical heterogeneity arising from different types of nerve injury on the validity of the pooled results.

This meta-analysis provides practical guidance for perioperative clinical management. During pre-THA assessment, orthopedic surgeons should focus on patients with concomitant spinal diseases and other associated factors identified by this study. During the preoperative consultation, patients should be clearly informed of the possibility of postoperative nerve injury. Furthermore, this potential risk should be noted on the preoperative examination form and formally documented in standardized preoperative

documents and informed consent forms.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study fully analyzing the associated factors of nerve injury post-THA utilizing the most recent and comprehensive data. However, some limitations are still worth noting. First, the limited number of variables included in the multivariate analysis was a key methodological limitation. Although we identified potential associated factors by univariate analyses, the multivariate analysis could only incorporate a few variables (sex (female), spinal disease, and PL approach) due to a lack of detailed multivariate data in the original studies. Therefore, more high-quality clinical studies reporting detailed multivariate data on key variables (e.g., operation time, age, complications, and type of nerve injury) are needed in the future to further validate our findings and conduct more in-depth subgroup analyses. Second, the included studies reported various subtypes of PNI post-THA. These subtypes exhibit significant clinical heterogeneity in the anatomical course, mechanisms of injury, long-term functional prognosis, and clinical severity: Sciatic and femoral nerve injuries have higher incidence rates, and the causes of intraoperative injury are highly specific to each type of nerve injury. This study pooled all PNIs into a single outcome, which may obscure the unique characteristics of the association between pathogenesis and associated factors. Despite subgroup analyses by the type of nerve injury, the results are only exploratory due to the insufficient stratified data and limited sample sizes, and residual clinical heterogeneity may still compromise the robustness of the overall pooled effect size. In the future, our findings should be further validated by large-sample, original studies with standardized and unified classifications of nerve injury. When interpreting the associations of associated factors, the bias resulting from this limitation should be fully considered. Additionally, some included studies had data missing, so the number of included studies in the pooled analysis of corresponding indicators decreased. Due to limitations in the original study data, this study could not systematically quantify the impact of bias caused by data missing. This study included both primary and revision THA cases, and significant heterogeneity was present in the surgical background and disease characteristics between these two groups. Consequently, the pooled analysis may introduce potential confounding bias. Moreover, the multivariate-adjusted covariates might be different in the included studies, so the pooled effect size represented a pooled estimate across different strategies for controlling confounders. This constitutes a significant methodological limitation. Furthermore, most of the included studies were retrospective cohort studies, so caution should be exercised when interpreting the associations of associated factors.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis investigated the associated factors of nerve injury post-THA. Univariate analyses revealed that elevated odds of nerve injury post-THA had associations with younger age, obesity, sex (female), fractures, longer operation time, and revision surgery. Multivariate analyses showed that sex (female), PL approach, and spinal diseases had significant associations with higher odds of nerve injury post-THA. The results of univariate sensitivity analyses for spinal diseases had poor robustness, while the results were robust after multivariate adjustment for confounders. These associations may be driven by the limited number of studies and the inclusion of a few influential studies, and they should be interpreted cautiously. In the future, standardized, multicenter, prospective studies with unified diagnostic criteria for nerve injury post-THA are required, and a multivariate prognostic report should be completed. Through stratified analyses based on detailed intraoperative parameters, we aim to elucidate the mechanical mechanism of intraoperative nerve injury. In addition, we will further validate the association of hypertension with postoperative nerve injury and develop a clinical risk prediction model to support individualized perioperative risk assessment.
