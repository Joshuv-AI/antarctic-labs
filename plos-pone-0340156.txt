Natural variation of bioactive gibberellin levels in maize tissues across North America

Xia Guan, Nitin Supekar, Changjian Jiang, Junhong Guo, Edwards Allen, Charles Dietrich, Cody Postin, Shubha Shakya

ABSTRACT
Gibberellins (GA) are plant hormones that regulate growth and development throughout the plant life cycle. While gibberellins have been extensively studied under controlled conditions for their fundamental role in plant adaptation and agronomic trait development, field-based data on their natural variation of bioactive GA levels across diverse agricultural genotypes and environments remains limited. To address this knowledge gap, we analyzed bioactive gibberellin A1 (GA1) and gibberellin A4 (GA4) in multiple maize tissues from commercial conventional maize hybrids grown in diverse field environments using high-throughput liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). This study provides quantitative measurements of GA1 and GA4 levels in maize tissues collected under standard agricultural practices and describes variation influenced by genetic background and environmental conditions. Our findings demonstrate that GA1 and GA4 concentrations from conventional maize samples varied across maize tissue types, hybrid, and field sites. Analysis of variance components indicated that the site variation was statistically significant in five of ten gibberellins by maize tissue combinations, while the genetic component was significant only for GA4 in grain samples, suggesting that environment factors have a larger effect than genetic factors Positive correlations between GA1 and GA4 were observed consistently across maize tissues except root. These findings offer preliminary insights into the interplay between genetics and environment, across germplasm and environments that have been previously described.

Introduction Gibberellins (GA) are plant hormones that play a crucial role in regulating plant growth and development throughout the plant life cycle. They were first discovered as secondary metabolites in the fungus Fusarium fujikuroi [ 1 , 2 ]. Gibberellins comprise a class of diterpenoid carboxylic acids that possess the tetracyclic ent-gibberellane carbon skeletal structure. Among various gibberellins, only a few, e.g., GA 1 , GA 3 , GA 4 , and GA 7 exhibit significant biological activity [ 3 ]. GA 1 and GA 4 are the major active gibberellins that promote growth and development in plant species. They are involved in diverse developmental processes such as seed germination, stem elongation, leaf expansion, and flower and fruit development [ 2 , 4 ]. Additionally, gibberellins modulate plant adaptation to both biotic and abiotic stresses [ 5 , 6 ]. The biosynthesis and signaling pathways of gibberellins are intricate systems that exhibit complex spatiotemporal patterns across cells, tissues, and developmental stages [ 7 , 8 ]. These patterns result from multiple enzymatic steps and feedback mechanisms that respond dynamically to both biological and environmental signals [ 9 , 10 ]. Environmental conditions can influence measured gibberellin pools through effects on biosynthesis, catabolism, conjugation, transport, or signaling [ 11 ], leading to stunted growth and altered development, while optimal conditions enhance gibberellin production and promote vigorous growth [ 12 ]. The movement of gibberellins between cells and across plant organs, coupled with their variable distribution patterns, creates challenges for understanding hormone-environment interactions in agricultural settings [ 13 – 15 ]. Due to the vital biological activity of gibberellins in plant growth and development, several applications have been developed for commercial and biotechnological use: applications of gibberellins to promote growth, and applications or the genetic modification of biosynthesis to optimize plant phenotypes and environmental responses [ 10 ]. Research in recent years has shown that gibberellins have direct and indirect effects on many plant traits. Many plant varieties with improved agronomic traits (e.g., dwarf phenotypes or increased biomass) are shown to be related to gibberellin activity and signaling [ 16 ], thus manipulation of gibberellin levels is commonly used in agricultural practices to optimize plant growth and yield. Maize ( Zea mays L.; corn) is a major agricultural commodity, contributing significantly to the economy of many countries and serves as a staple food for many populations worldwide [ 17 ]. Short maize hybrids have been created through conventional breeding by researchers with the objectives to improve performance under high density and improve lodging tolerance [ 18 ]. However, breeding short traits into existing maize hybrids is time consuming, challenging, and can affect environmental responses. Genetically modified maize can be engineered through the enhancement or suppression of hormone pathways, including gibberellin biosynthesis. Scientists have developed a genetically modified short stature maize ideotype by suppressing genes that are involved in the levels of gibberellins, reducing plant stature without undermining maize yields [ 19 ]. Accurate quantification of gibberellin levels is valuable for describing variation across tissues, genotypes, and environments and for providing baseline context for future studies [ 20 ]. While previous studies have quantified bioactive gibberellins in maize tissues [ 8 , 19 , 21 – 24 ], these analyses were typically limited to specific varieties or locations, lacking the scope needed to characterize natural variation across diverse agricultural conditions. Establishing baseline patterns of gibberellin abundance in agricultural settings can support subsequent research. First, understanding the natural variability of gibberellin concentrations in conventional maize tissues – a primary determinant of plant stature – and distinguishing between environmentally-induced and genetically-controlled variation provides essential context for crop improvement efforts. This baseline knowledge is particularly valuable for developing and evaluating new short-stature varieties, where gibberellin-related genetic modifications must be assessed against the background of natural gibberellin variability that occurs across diverse field environments. Second, while gibberellin biology has been extensively studied under controlled conditions [ 19 ], quantifying the magnitude of gibberellin variation in commercial hybrids under real-world agricultural conditions helps bridge the gap between laboratory findings and field performance. Such information may provide context for future studies evaluating gibberellin-modified traits across growing environments, where environmental stresses can significantly impact gibberellin-mediated growth processes and ultimately affect yield potential. Understanding the relative contributions of genetic background and environmental factors to gibberellin concentrations in maize tissue can inform breeding strategies and help develop more resilient short-stature varieties that maintain desired height characteristics across diverse growing conditions. Understanding the natural range and stability of gibberellin levels in conventional hybrid maize across diverse environments may help contextualize future studies of gibberellin related traits changes in new hybrids and predicting their performance across different agricultural regions. The purpose of this study is to quantify GA 1 and GA 4 in maize by the validated LC-MS/MS method across conventional maize hybrids, tissue types, and environments, which could provide a baseline understanding of the contribution of genetics and environment to the variation of gibberellin content. Reagents Gibberellin standards were used as reference standards for the analytical procedures or calibration of equipment. Sample preparation After cryomilling maize tissues, 50 ± 5 mg of fresh frozen sample was weighed into a pre-chilled 1.5 mL glass tube placed on dry ice. Next, on wet ice, 10 µL of internal standard working solutions (100 ng/mL of GA 1 -D4 and 25 ng/mL of GA 4 -D2) and 1.00 mL of pre-chilled extraction buffer (80% methanol with 0.5% formic acid) were added to each sample. The samples, arranged in a 96-well formatted rack, were manually agitated 40 times to thoroughly suspend the tissue in the extraction buffer, followed by an overnight incubation (approximately 15 hours) at 4°C with gentle mixing at 50 rpm on a Fisher brand Mixer (Fisher Scientific, 88-861-043). After incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a 96-well polypropylene sample collection plate. The pellets were re-extracted by adding another 1 mL of extraction buffer following the same procedure as above. The combined supernatants evaporated under nitrogen flow at 35°C using a microplate sample evaporation system (Biotage SPE Dry 96, SD-9600-DHS-NA), until about 400 µL of aqueous phase was left. Subsequently, the extracts were loaded into a Biotage 96-well supported liquid extraction (SLE) plate (Biotage, 8200400P01), which was mounted on a glass-coated 96-well collection plate. The analyte gibberellins were eluted with 2 × 0.9 mL of ethyl acetate. The eluate was then evaporated to dryness under nitrogen by using the SPE Dry 96. The dried samples were reconstituted in 100 µL of reconstitution buffer (20% Methanol with 0.1% formic acid), then vortexed at 1200 rpm at room temperature for 3 minutes. Following this, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 3 minutes at 4°C, and 90 µL of the supernatant was transferred into a 96-well plate with glass inserts, kept on wet ice prior to LC-MS/MS analysis. Variance component analysis to compare genetic and environmental variations To compare genetic and environmental contributions to GA1 and GA4 expression across five tissues, we first examined the distribution of the response variables. Visual inspection of the observed response and residual plots from the analysis indicated a log‑normal distribution, supporting the use of log‑transformed data in subsequent analyses. Then, a variance component analysis [ 25 , 26 ] was performed with the following model (1) and a log transformation for the normality assumption of the analysis. y i j k = μ + s i + r j ( i ) + m k + ( s m ) i k + ε i j k (1) where y ijk is the observed response of each plot after transformation for the k th hybrid from the j th replicate (i.e., block) at the i th site, µ is the overall mean, s i is the effect of the i th site, r j(i) is the effect of the j th replicate within the i th site, m k is the effect of the k th hybrid, (sm) ik is the interaction effect of the k th hybrid with the i th site, ε ijk is the residual. The term replicate and block will be used interchangeably in the following. Before fitting the model, Levene’s tests were conducted to examine the assumption of the variance homogeneity, and non-significance was found for effects of sites, materials, and interactions on the variance. Different SAS procedures were used to estimate variance components, test significance, and examine changes in hybrid means across sites, with each procedure chosen to leverage its specific analytical strengths. To estimate variance components, Hybrid, Site, Replicate within Site, and the Hybrid-by-Site interaction were all treated as random effects, with the residual term representing error variance. Random effects were assumed to be independently and identically distributed within each classification, each associated with its own variance component. Variance was partitioned into these additive components using SAS PROC MIXED with REML (Restricted Maximum Likelihood) estimation, a well-established approach for variance component estimation in unbalanced trial designs. The significance of variance components was then evaluated using PROC GLIMMIX with the COVTEST option via likelihood ratio tests. Because the null hypothesis places a variance component on the boundary of the parameter space at zero, the resulting test statistic follows a mixture chi-square distribution. Significance was evaluated at 5%, 1%, and 0.1% levels without multiplicity correction. For variance components that were found to be significant, additional analyses were performed to examine mean changes for individual hybrids across sites, thereby illustrating the extent to which hybrid effects were connected across sites and characterizing site-specific hybrid patterns. These analyses were conducted within a linear mixed model framework using PROC MIXED. Hybrid, Site, and the Hybrid-by-Site interaction were treated as fixed effects, whereas Replicate nested within Site was modeled as a random effect. Treating Hybrid and Site as fixed effects produced local LS-means for characterizing Hybrid-by-Site patterns, while modeling nested replications as random prevented estimates from being biased by local block-level anomalies. All analyses were performed separately for each gibberellin and tissue combination, and results were presented in the following sections. For the assumptions of the analysis, in addition to the composition of the model (1) as described above, distributional assumptions were further examined by the residual plots from PROC MIXED which showed no evidence of violation of normality (after the log transformation on the original observed response) and variance heterogeneity (as an additional visual confirmation to the above Levene’s test). Overall, the use of SAS procedures with REML estimation was appropriate for variance component estimation, significance testing, and LS-means estimation, since these procedures are well suited to handling unbalanced data within a properly specified mixed-model framework. Results Method development and validation of gibberellins by LC-MS/MS Plant extracts contain complex mixtures; typically, gibberellins are present in trace amounts. Therefore, it is necessary to apply both a consistent sample preparation method to reliably extract gibberellins from plants and a sensitive technique to accurately quantify them. In this study, a robust, reliable, and high-throughput method was developed for the simultaneous quantitation of gibberellins in six maize tissue types (grain, pollen, leaf, root, forage, and stalk). In brief, the gibberellins were extracted from approximately 50 mg of sample, purified by SLE, and separated and quantified by LC-MS/MS using the isotope-labeled internal standard (IS) ( Fig 1 ). The method was validated by determining the linearity (R 2 ≥ 0.98) over the concentration range of 20–30,000 pg/mL in six tissue types. 20 pg/mL is the LLOQ (lower limit of quantification). Precision of the method was obtained with intra-and inter-day relative standard deviations (≤17.13%) and accuracy for compounds typically ranging between 79–124%. The recoveries of our method ranged from 77.6% to 119.8% ( Table 4 ). The bench-top stability of the sample extraction supernatant was evaluated at room temperature for each type of tissue, demonstrating stability for a duration of up to two hours for all except pollen, which exhibited stability of only one hour. Discussion Quantitative analysis of gibberellins has advanced rapidly across diverse crops with the development of LC–MS/MS-based workflows [ 27 ], including methods for broad gibberellin profiling [ 28 , 29 ] and high-efficiency quantitation in complex plant matrices [ 20 , 30 ]. This study focuses on GA 1 and GA 4 quantification in commercial maize hybrids sampled across diverse environments, providing a baseline dataset for describing natural variation under standard agricultural practices. Among the five tissues, forage exhibited the highest active gibberellins concentrations and the broadest dynamic range, with GA 1 ranging from 0.4 to 131.1 pmol/g and GA 4 from 0.1 to 29.2 pmol/g. Published data for forage tissue is limited; while available data for shoot tissue can serve as a reference. GA 1 levels in shoot ranged from 0.04 to 274.4 pmol/g [ 31 , 32 ] andGA 4 in the shoot tips ranged from 0.06 to 1.05 pmol/g [ 21 , 33 ]. Stalk showed a narrower and more stable range (GA 1 : 0.9–3.4 pmol/g; GA 4 : 0.2–0.9 pmol/g), aligning closely with literature values (GA 1 ~ 1.8 pmol/g; GA 4 : 0.33–0.98 pmol/g) [ 19 ]. This consistency suggests that stalk may be a useful tissue for comparison in future studies. Leaf in this study showed GA 1 levels ranged from 0.4 to 27.0 pmol/g and GA 4 from 0.1 to 1.4 pmol/g. The GA 1 concentrations observed here are higher than literature-reported ranges of 0-1.8 pmol/g, possibly due to the limited number of samples reported previously, while GA 4 levels fall within the broader literature range of 0–3.5 pmol/g [ 19 , 33 , 34 ]. The elevated GA 1 levels may reflect differences in tissue sampling, developmental stage, genotype, field conditions, or study design. In contrast, the GA 4 levels, although lower than the upper literature limit, are consistent with expected physiological ranges and suggest that GA4 levels in leaf tissue appear less variable than GA1 in this dataset. Grain exhibited GA 1 levels from 0.1 to 26.0 pmol/g and GA 4 from 0.3 to 2.0 pmol/g, exceeding the ranges (GA 1 : 0.29–2.0 pmol/g; GA 4 : 0–1.2 pmol/g) reported in the literature [ 22 , 35 ]. This suggests that gibberellin levels in grain may span a broader range than previously reported. The tight range data in the literature may be reflecting differences in sample number, hybrid background, field site, developmental stage, or sampling design [ 35 ]. Root in this study showed GA 1 concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 1.3 pmol/g and GA 4 from 0.1 to 1.8 pmol/g. The relatively narrow range and lower mean values indicate that measured GA 1 and GA 4 levels in root were lower and less variable than in several above ground tissues in this dataset. These results support including below-ground tissues in future GA profiling studies to better describe tissue-specific GA abundance. Overall, these comparisons highlight the broader dynamic range of bioactive GA 1 and GA 4 captured in this study, particularly forage, leaf, and grain tissues. The broader ranges observed in certain material types may reflect the larger number of samples, field-grown conditions, developmental differences, and the use of commercial hybrids compared with prior studies [ 31 ]. The comparison of literature data with our findings reinforces the robustness of the LC-MS/MS method and provides a reference for future studies of GA 1 and GA 4 abundance in maize tissues. The contribution of different sources of variance components to measured GA 1 and GA 4 levels was analyzed using an all-random-effect model. Model (1) included the effects of site, hybrid, the hybrid × site interaction, replicate nested within site, and residual. The hybrid effect represents genetic variation, and the site effect represents environmental variation among locations. Replicate nested within site accounts for variation among field replicates within each location, whereas the residual captures the remaining unexplained variation, including plant sampling variation in the field and assay variation in the laboratory. The hybrid × site interaction was further examined for tissues showing evidence of interaction in the variance component analysis. Results in Table 6 and Fig 2 indicated that while both genetic and environment are important factors, the site is a major contributor as compared with the genetic background to the gibberellin levels. In addition, in five out of ten (gibberellins by tissue) combinations, residual variations are greater than the model variation, especially for forage and root. Results in Table 6 , together with means in Table 5 , demonstrate substantial differences in the distribution of GA 1 and GA 4 among the five maize tissues. For example, forage exhibited the highest mean concentrations as well as the largest residual variances for both GA 1 and GA 4 , whereas stalk showed intermediate mean concentrations but the lowest residual variances. Despite this lower residual variability, stalk accounted for a higher proportion of total model-explained variation than forage, as shown in Fig 2 . Across the ten gibberellins-tissue combinations analyzed, a significant hybrid contribution was detected only for GA 4 in grain (Varg), compared with five combinations exhibiting significant site effects (Vars). Overall, six of the ten combinations showed at least one significant variance component attributable to site, hybrid, or site × hybrid interaction (Vargxs). Notably, only root showed no significant variance components for either GA 1 or GA 4 , while hybrid × site interactions were significant for both gibberellins in stalk. Estimation and interpretation of variance components depend on satisfaction of the model’s normality assumptions. Following log transformation, the variance component estimates reported in Table 6 are presented on the log scale and should be interpreted in a relative sense, whereas mean concentrations and associated standard errors reported in Table 5 are expressed in absolute units (pmol/g). The experimental design was constrained by hybrid adaptation and site capacity, resulting in different subsets of hybrids being planted at each site. Because commercial maize hybrids are bred for specific maturity zones and local growing conditions, it was not feasible to plant the same set of hybrids across the full range of environments represented in this study. This approach was necessary to achieve broad geographic and environmental coverage, despite resulting in an unbalanced hybrid–site design. As a consequence of this design, estimation and statistical testing of variance components, especially hybrid × site interactions, are based on reduced degrees of freedom. For example, although the experiment included ten hybrids and six sites, a fully balanced design would have yielded 45 degrees of freedom for the interaction term, whereas the mixed-model analysis of the available data provided only three. This imbalance limits the precision of variance component estimates and reduces statistical power for significance testing. Despite these limitations, the unbalanced design does not preclude qualitative interpretation of the relative contributions of genetic, environmental, and interaction effects. The variance component analysis was conducted using an all-random-effects mixed model, and significance testing was performed using likelihood-based methods, which are appropriate for unbalanced data. While quantitative estimates should therefore be interpreted cautiously, trends comparing the relative importance of site, hybrid, and hybrid × site effects remain informative. As shown in Fig. 3 , gibberellin concentration profiles for many genetic backgrounds are largely parallel across sites, suggesting consistent environmental responses. However, some hybrids exhibit clear site-dependent variation, indicating that both genetic and environmental factors contribute meaningfully to gibberellin accumulation. For example, the hybrid Dekalb DKC58−32 showed the highest gibberellin concentrations at site 4 but relatively lower concentrations at other sites, illustrating environment-dependent expression patterns. Although the specific environmental drivers underlying these differences cannot be resolved in the present study, factors such as temperature, humidity, and rainfall are likely contributors, and further targeted experimentation will be required to assess their relative importance. In our field dataset, GA 1 and GA 4 were positively correlated in forage, grain, leaf, and stalk, but not in root. Because these data were collected under standard agronomic conditions, the observed correlations could also reflect a shared response to overall plant physiological status (e.g., more vigorous growth) and to environmentally driven differences in growing conditions among sites, consistent with the strong site effects detected in the variance component analysis. Notably, the GA 1 –GA 4 association also persisted when examining residual variation after accounting for hybrid, site, and hybrid × site effects. Conclusions In this study, we applied a validated high-throughput LC-MS/MS method to simultaneously quantify the bioactive gibberellins GA 1 and GA 4 across multiple maize tissues of commercial maize hybrids grown in North American field environments. This work provides a field-based baseline dataset describing GA 1 and GA 4 variation under standard agricultural practices. We found that gibberellin concentrations varied significantly across the sampled material tissues, with forage exhibiting the broadest dynamic range and highest levels, while stalk tissue showed narrower concentration ranges, which may make it useful for comparison in future studies. Variance component analysis indicated that site-associated variation contributed to measured GA 1 and GA 4 levels in several tissue-by-analyte combinations. We identified a significant genetic (hybrid) contribution to GA 4 variance specifically in grain, which suggests that this tissue-analyte combination may warrant further study in larger, targeted experiments. While this study provides a foundational dataset, it is important to note that it is based on a single growing season and limited to bioactive GA 1 and GA 4 . Future multi-year studies and additional GA metabolites will be necessary to confirm these trends and further explore the impact of annual weather variations. We hope that the data and analyses presented here provide useful insights into the complex interplay between genetics and environment, in maize.
