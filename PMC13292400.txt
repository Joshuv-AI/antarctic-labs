Cementum Attachment Protein‐Derived Peptides Modulate Brushite and Calcium Oxalate Crystallization In Vitro
Authors: Alberto Mimila‐Cortes, Jesús Arenas‐Alatorre, Karina Jiménez‐Duran, Martin González‐Andrade, Lia Hoz‐Rodríguez, Maricela Santana‐Vázquez, Luis Fernando Ramírez‐González, Karen Ivette Castro‐López, Higinio Arzate, Gonzalo Montoya‐Ayala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPathological mineralization involves the uncontrolled crystallization of calcium phosphate (brushite) and calcium oxalate, leading to renal calculi and ectopic calcifications. Peptides enriched in acidic or phosphorylated residues are potential crystal growth modulators due to their ability to interact with calcium‐rich surfaces. This study investigated the in vitro effects of cementum attachment protein‐derived peptides, CAP‐pi and its phosphorylated analog CAP‐pip, on brushite and calcium oxalate crystallization. By isolating these highly anionic motifs, this work introduces a novel biomimetic approach to investigate and modulate the physicochemical mechanisms driving pathological mineral deposition. Assays performed under physiological conditions were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and confocal microscopy, alongside molecular dynamics simulations to examine peptide–calcium oxalate interactions. Both peptides altered crystal growth patterns and lattice organization in a concentration‐dependent manner. Peptide treatments induced marked morphological perturbations, promoted irregular and rosette‐like habits, and modified vibrational profiles. Furthermore, confocal microscopy revealed selective adsorption onto specific crystallographic regions, whereas molecular dynamics demonstrated enhanced peptide–calcium coordination and interfacial stability for CAP‐pip. Collectively, these findings establish phosphorylation as a key determinant of peptide–mineral interactions, providing mechanistic insight into how phosphopeptides regulate crystallization through surface‐mediated modulation.Keywords: brushite, calcium oxalate, crystallization, peptides, phosphorylation

Introduction

Kidney stones represent a prevalent urological disorder characterized by the formation and occasional passage of crystalline aggregates in the urinary tract. The type of calculi formed, and their recurrence rate are influenced by various parameters, including age, sex, race, and diet, which collectively affect disease prevalence and incidence [1]. The most prevalent human kidney stones are composed of calcium oxalate (CaOx) and calcium phosphate, mainly brushite (dicalcium phosphate dihydrate) and hydroxyapatite (HA), which may occur either as pure phases or as mixed compositions [2]. CaOx has received the most attention due to its high incidence, accounting for 70%–80% of all renal calculi. Pathological CaOx deposits may crystallize in three hydrated phases: calcium oxalate monohydrate (COM, CaC2O4·H2O), also known as whewellite, which exhibits a monoclinic crystal structure; calcium oxalate dihydrate (COD, CaC2O4·2H2O), referred to as weddellite, a metastable phase at ambient temperature with a tetragonal structure; and calcium oxalate trihydrate (COT, CaC2O4·3H2O), which is rarely detected in urinary environments due to its thermodynamic instability. Among these phases, COM predominates in renal calculi and represents the most thermodynamically stable and least soluble form under physiological conditions. Its lower solubility and greater structural stability contribute to its persistence once formed, in contrast to the metastable COD and COT phases, which are more susceptible to phase transformation or dissolution under similar conditions [3, 4, 5]. COM crystals expose calcium‐rich crystallographic faces, particularly the {100} and {121} planes, where surface‐exposed Ca2+ ions create regions of high positive charge density. These surfaces can establish strong electrostatic interactions with negatively charged macromolecules and epithelial cell membranes, enhancing crystal–cell attachment and promoting retention within the renal tubular environment [2, 6].

In addition, organic macromolecules such as lipids, polysaccharides, and proteins present within kidney stones are hypothesized to modulate crystal nucleation, growth, aggregation, and phase stability by exerting either inhibitory or promotive effects on crystallization [5]. These calculi typically form on calcium phosphate deposits known as Randall's plaques, which originate in the basement membranes of the thin limbs of the loop of Henle on the renal papillary surface [2]. Regardless of composition, kidney stone formation involves a complex, multistep pathophysiology comprising urinary supersaturation, followed by crystal nucleation, growth, and aggregation. This process is clinically associated with systemic metabolic disorders, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes mellitus [7], cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome [8].

Under physiological conditions, endogenous polypeptide molecules function through both competitive and cooperative mechanisms to reduce crystallization while inhibiting crystal aggregation and/or their adhesion to tubular epithelial cells. Several proteins, including osteopontin (OPN), Tamm‐Horsfall protein, urinary prothrombin fragment 1 (UPTF‐1), and nephrocalcin, have been extensively studied and reported to inhibit crystal growth, aggregation, and/or tubular cell adhesion [2]. Recent studies have investigated inhibitory factors capable of molecular interactions at both the cellular and mineral levels, preventing pathological mineralization. Growing evidence suggests that phosphorylated proteins and acidic peptides, particularly those rich in aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and phosphoserine residues, may reduce stone recurrence by inhibiting crystallization. These molecules exert their effects through selective adsorption to positively charged surfaces, especially calcium‐rich crystal faces [9, 10, 11, 12].

Interestingly, similar mineralization‐regulating mechanisms are observed in physiologically calcified tissues. Cementum, which is the vital mineralized tissue that covers tooth roots, contains two notable polypeptide regulators: cementum attachment protein (CAP) and cementum protein 1 (CEMP1). The CAP gene (Accession: NM_AY455942.1; GI: 38503519) encodes a 140‐amino‐acid protein, whose N‐terminal 125 residues are identical to those of 3‐hydroxyacyl‐CoA dehydratase 1 (HACD1), thereby identifying CAP as an isoform of HACD1 [13, 14]. CAP has a high affinity for HA, promotes HA crystal formation in vitro, and induces bone repair and healing in vivo [14]. CAP has been implicated in key biological processes associated with cementum formation, including cell recruitment and cell differentiation. Its expression has been detected in cementoblasts, paravascular progenitor cells within the periodontium, as well as progenitor cells located in endosteal spaces in the alveolar bone [15].

However, recent evidence highlights that discrete peptide fragments derived from mineral‐associated proteins can exert potent inhibitory effects on crystallization, even when the parent protein promotes mineralization. This inhibitory capacity is not determined by molecular size, but rather by sequence‐dependent physicochemical properties, specifically, a high density of negatively charged acidic residues, a low isoelectric point, and a lack of restrictive tertiary structure [16]. These features maximize the effective charge density and facilitate direct, face‐specific electrostatic interactions with growing crystals. Recent work has identified a peptide fragment derived from CAP, termed CAP‐pi, which corresponds to a segment of the N‐terminal domain of CAP and is enriched in polar, negatively charged residues. This acidic peptide, which is rich in aspartate, glutamate, and serine residues, and particularly its phosphorylated form (CAP‐pip), have been shown to interact with biologically relevant mineral phases, inhibiting hydroxyapatite nucleation and growth in vitro, with the phosphorylated variant exhibiting notably stronger inhibitory capacity and enhanced affinity for specific crystal faces. CAP‐pi and its derivatives represent minimal functional motifs of the larger CAP molecule, providing advantages such as reduced synthetic complexity and tunable physicochemical properties for modulating mineralization processes [17]. These findings suggest that CAP‐derived peptides can serve as valuable tools for probing peptide–crystal interactions and for designing biomimetic modulators of pathological calcification. However, the mechanistic effects of these peptides on calcium phosphate precursors, especially their influence on crystal morphology and chemical evolution during mineralization, remain unknown.

Building upon our previous findings, this study expands the evaluation of CAP‐pi and CAP‐pip by testing multiple peptide concentrations and performing a deeper structural and morphological characterization using scanning electron microscopy, Raman spectroscopy, and confocal microscopy. Thus, this study aims to (1) evaluate the effect of both peptides on CaOx and brushite crystallization in vitro and (2) analyze their interactions with primary kidney stone components through molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Through the combination of in vitro crystallization assays and MD simulations, this study provides the first mechanistic insight into the interactions of novel peptides with CaOx and brushite. This work contributes to the development of biomimetic strategies for controlling pathological crystallization and establishes these peptides as potential inhibitory factors of kidney stone‐related crystals.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification unless otherwise stated. Sodium sulfate decahydrate (Na2SO4·10H2O), potassium chloride (KCl), sodium dihydrogen phosphate dihydrate (NaH2PO4·2H2O), magnesium sulfate heptahydrate (MgSO4·7H2O), sodium chloride (NaCl), ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), calcium chloride (CaCl2), disodium hydrogen phosphate dodecahydrate (Na2HPO4·12H2O), calcium nitrate tetrahydrate (Ca (NO₃)2·4H2O), sodium acetate trihydrate (CH₃COONa·3H2O), and sodium oxalate (Na2C2O4) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm).

Synthesis and Physicochemical Characterization of CAP‐Derived Peptides

The native 14‐amino acid peptide, designated herein as CAP‐pi, corresponds to amino acids 40 to 53 of the CAP with the sequence MASSDEDGTNGGAS. Its specifically phosphorylated variant is designated as CAP‐pip (sequence: MASpSpDEDGTNGGASp). Both peptides were synthesized commercially by Fmoc solid‐phase peptide synthesis following the Sheppard method. Purification was achieved by C‐18 reversed‐phase liquid chromatography (Bio Basic, Armonk, NY, USA), yielding products with > 90% purity. Complete analytical characterization, including high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) chromatograms and mass spectra (MS) confirming peptide identity and purity, was performed and provided by the manufacturer (provided in Supporting Information, Figures S1, S2). Lyophilized peptides were dissolved in deionized water (1 mg/mL), filtered (0.22 μm), aliquoted, and stored at −20°C. Theoretical pI, molecular weight, and GRAVY index were calculated using ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.ExPASy.org/protparam/) and Scansite 4.0 (https://scansite4.mit.edu).

Crystallization Experiments

Crystallization experiments were performed in 24‐well polystyrene plates (Wuxi NEST Biotechnology, China). Each well contained glass coverslips (12 mm diameter, thickness 0.13–0.17 mm; Luzeren) and peptides were added at final concentrations of 0, 0.5, 3, and 5 μg/mL with the final reaction volume of 1.0 mL. These concentrations were selected based on previous in vitro characterizations by some of the authors [17], which identified this range as optimal for observing concentration‐dependent modulatory effects.

Equimolar calcium and oxalate solutions were used to generate controlled supersaturation conditions commonly employed in mechanistic crystallization studies. These concentrations were selected to promote reproducible nucleation and measurable crystal growth within defined timeframes and were not intended to replicate physiological urinary ratios. Plates were incubated at 37°C in a vacuum desiccator to allow controlled evaporation. All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times. Post‐crystallization processing consisted of sequential washing with ethanol (1 mL × 2) and deionized water (1 mL × 2), followed by air‐drying prior to analysis.

Brushite Crystallization Assay

The brushite crystallization protocol was adapted from a previously reported method using synthetic urine solutions under physiological conditions (pH 6.0, 37°C) [18]. Briefly, two precursor solutions (Table 1) were prepared independently and mixed at a 1:1 volume ratio to initiate crystallization. The resulting suspensions were incubated for 72 h under continuous orbital agitation at 40 rpm to ensure homogeneous mixing and controlled crystal growth. After crystallization, the crystalline deposits formed on the glass substrates were subjected to sequential washing steps with ethanol followed by deionized water under gentle agitation to remove residual ions and loosely bound species. The samples were then air‐dried and preserved in a vacuum desiccator prior to analysis. All subsequent characterization studies were performed directly on the crystals maintained on the glass coverslips. All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times.

CaOx Crystallization Assay

CaOx crystallization was conducted following an established protocol adapted from previous reports [19], using reagent solutions pre‐equilibrated at 37°C (Table 2). Briefly, crystallization was initiated by the sequential addition of the reactants in a 1:2:1 volumetric ratio (oxalate solution:deionized water:calcium solution) under constant mixing. The reaction mixtures were incubated at physiological temperature (37°C) for 60 min to allow crystal nucleation and growth. After crystallization, the resulting CaOx deposits were subjected to alternating washes with ethanol and deionized water under controlled agitation conditions to remove unreacted species and loosely bound ions. The samples were then air‐dried and preserved in a vacuum desiccator prior to analysis. All subsequent characterization studies were performed directly on the crystals maintained on the glass coverslips. All experiments were performed in triplicate and independently repeated three times.

Crystal Characterization

Scanning Electron Microscopy

The crystal morphology and microstructure were examined via field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JEOL 7800F). The samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with conductive carbon tape and imaged under high vacuum conditions (20–25 Pa chamber pressure) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Multiple sample regions were analyzed via both secondary electron and backscattered electron detection modes to obtain comprehensive morphological and compositional data.

Crystal dimensions were quantified using ImageJ software. Multiple randomly selected fields were analyzed per condition, and mean crystal size ± standard deviation was calculated. Due to aggregation and fusion phenomena, absolute crystal counts were not considered statistically reliable.

Raman Spectroscopy

Raman spectra were acquired using a Thermo Scientific DXR2 Raman microscope (λ = 532 nm, 2.33 eV). Spectra were collected in the range 400–3000 cm−1 under identical laser power, acquisition time, and magnification settings. Multiple regions were analyzed per sample to minimize orientation‐dependent intensity variation. All Raman spectra presented in this study are representative obtained from three independent crystallization experiments.

Peptide Fluorescent Labeling

Fluorescent conjugates were prepared by labeling CAP‐pi with AlexaFluor‐488 (CAP‐pi/AF‐488) and CAP‐pip with AlexaFluor‐546 (CAP‐pip/AF‐546) following the manufacturer's protocols. To prepare the samples, fluorochrome (5 μL, 10 μg/μL in DMF) was added to peptide solution (200 μL) in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS), along with sodium bicarbonate (20 μL, 1 M). The solutions were incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The unconjugated label was removed through dialysis, which involved four changes of Tris‐buffered saline (TBS, 2 L, pH 7.4), each lasting 4 h, using dialysis tubes (1.0 kDa, Spectrum Laboratories Inc., Rancho Dominguez, CA, USA). The purified conjugates were lyophilized and stored at −20°C until further use.

Confocal microscopy

Fluorescent peptide adsorption onto crystalline substrates was evaluated via laser scanning confocal microscopy. Brushite and CaOx crystals were prepared according to the protocols in Tables 1 and 2, with crystallization performed in glass‐bottom polystyrene plates (35 mm diameter; glass bottom grade no. 1.5, 10 mm diameter; MatTek, Ashland, MA, USA), ensuring appropriate incubation, washing, and holding times. Prior to imaging, the crystalline substrates were treated with 20 μL of the fluorescent peptide solutions (5 μg/mL CAP‐pi/AF‐488 or CAP‐pip/AF‐546) in separate experiments. All sample handling was performed under light‐protected conditions to minimize photobleaching and reduce light scattering artifacts. Fluorescence imaging was conducted via an Olympus FV1000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus America Inc., PA, USA).

MD Simulations

The oxalate structure used in the simulations was obtained from PubChem (CID 16212978). The ligand was parameterized via the AMBER Tools Antechamber package and the ACPYPE program. We constructed the initial system by combining a single peptide molecule with 300 oxalate molecules spatially organized via PackMol software. This configuration served as the foundation for all the simulations performed in GROMACS 2024.1 via the CHARMM27 force field. The force field was further modified to recognize oxalate molecules and promote their phosphorylation. The topology file was revised to incorporate the oxalate parameters and the corresponding number of molecules. The complex was constructed via Packmol‐generated PDB files, with the addition of 30 calcium atoms to the system. The system was subsequently solvated in a cubic box (1.2 nm edge length) of TIP3P water molecules. The complex underwent energy minimization (5000 steps), equilibration at 37°C utilizing a modified Berendsen thermostat, and pressure stabilization at 1 atm with a Berendsen barostat. Production simulations were conducted for 100 ns under constant temperature and pressure conditions, utilizing GPU acceleration. As a control, we simulated the reference peptide FAWWWWFFAFWFFWFFFW, whose structure was predicted via AlphaFold2, under identical simulation conditions.

Results

Physicochemical Characterization of CAP‐Derived Peptides

Computational analysis (Table 3) confirmed that phosphorylation substantially altered the physicochemical profile of CAP‐pi. Incorporation of phosphate groups decreased the theoretical isoelectric point (pI) from 3.49 to 1.8, reflecting an increased net negative charge under physiological conditions. A corresponding increase in molecular weight was observed due to phosphorylation. The calculated GRAVY index (−0.914) indicated a predominantly hydrophilic character, consistent with enhanced aqueous solubility and potential for electrostatic interaction with calcium‐rich mineral surfaces. These results support the hypothesis that phosphorylation modulates peptide–mineral interfacial affinity by increasing anionic charge density.

Influence of CAP‐Derived Peptides on Brushite Crystal Morphology

The impact of CAP‐pi and CAP‐pip on brushite crystallization was evaluated by SEM. Control samples exhibited well‐defined plate‐like crystals with merged rectangular morphology, lateral dimensions of 450–650 μm, and an average thickness of approximately 8 μm (Figure 1a).

In the presence of CAP‐pi (0.5 μg/mL), crystal morphology became irregular, with a significant reduction in mean size (254.17 ± 105.9 μm) and decreased thickness (Figure 1b). Surface dislocations and edge irregularities were evident. Increasing peptide concentration to 3 μg/mL further reduced crystal size (184.8 ± 21.8 μm) and resulted in fractured deposits and triangular prismatic forms (Figure 1c), indicating disruption of ordered lattice propagation. At 5 μg/mL (Figure 1d), crystal habit was markedly altered, with extensive structural irregularities and reduced formation of well‐defined plates.

The phosphorylated variant CAP‐pip exerted a stronger inhibitory effect. At 0.5 μg/mL, crystals exhibited reduced plate thickness and a mean size of 223 ± 105.5 μm (Figure 1e). At 3 and 5 μg/mL (Figures 1f–g), a substantial decrease in crystal number was observed together with triangular morphologies (300–550 μm) and pronounced surface heterogeneity. The concentration‐dependent reduction in size and structural coherence was consistently greater for CAP‐pip, supporting a phosphorylation‐enhanced inhibitory mechanism.

Raman Spectroscopic Analysis of Brushite Crystals

Raman spectroscopy was employed to confirm the mineral phase and assess molecular‐level changes in the functional groups of the crystals. In the control samples, several phosphate vibrational bands were identified (v2 PO4 −3 at 415 cm−1, HOPO3 −2 at 520 cm−1, and HOPO4 −2 at 875 cm−1), with the most intense signal observed at 980 cm−1. This band corresponds to the symmetric stretching mode (ν₁) of the PO4 3− group, which is a characteristic marker for the brushite phase [20, 21]. In samples treated with CAP‐pi (Figure 1h), a concentration‐dependent decrease in the intensity of the ν₁ PO4 3− band was observed, indicating partial inhibition of brushite formation. In contrast, CAP‐pip treatment (Figure 1i) induced a more pronounced reduction in the ν₁ PO4 3− band, with the most substantial decrease detected at a concentration of 5 μg/mL.

Face‐Selective Adsorption of CAP‐Derived Peptides On Brushite Crystals

Fluorescence confocal microscopy demonstrated distinct adsorption behaviors for the two peptides. CAP‐pi/AF488 exhibited diffuse, non‐selective association with small mineral aggregates (Figure 1j). In contrast, CAP‐pip/AF546 displayed preferential localization on the {100} crystallographic face (Figure 1k), a surface characterized by high Ca2+ surface density [22].

This face‐selective adsorption is consistent with electrostatic interactions between phosphate groups and surface‐exposed calcium ions. The enhanced localization of CAP‐pip supports a mechanistic model in which phosphorylation increases surface binding affinity, thereby interfering with step propagation and crystal growth.

Influence of CAP‐Derived Peptides on CaOx Crystal Morphology

SEM analysis revealed marked, concentration‐dependent alterations in CaOx crystal morphology following peptide treatment. Control samples predominantly exhibited COM crystals with characteristic monoclinic prismatic morphology, including well‐defined rectangular faces and triangular terminations (mean size 9.9 ± 1.7 μm; Figure 2a).

Exposure to CAP‐pi induced progressive morphological disruption. At 0.5 μg/mL, crystals appeared as fused druse‐like aggregates with an increased mean size of 16.7 ± 5.6 μm (Figure 2b), suggesting altered nucleation behavior and crystal coalescence. At 3 μg/mL, extensive fusion and a reduced number of crystals were observed (19.1 ± 5.6 μm; Figure 2c), consistent with interference in crystal growth and lattice organization. At 5 μg/mL, smaller crystals coexisted with compact rosette‐like aggregates (12.1 ± 3.0 μm; Figure 2d), indicating pronounced modulation of crystal habit and growth pattern.

The CAP‐pip peptide exhibited enhanced modulatory activity. At 0.5 μg/mL, crystal formation was markedly reduced, yielding smaller fused aggregates (12.1 ± 3.0 μm; Figure 2e). At 3 μg/mL, rosette‐like morphologies predominated and displayed reduced dimensions (7.7 ± 1.3 μm; Figure 2f), accompanied by a substantial decrease in well‐defined prismatic crystals. At the highest concentration tested (5 μg/mL), crystallization was strongly suppressed, with only isolated rosette‐like aggregates detected (~10 μm; Figure 2g). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that phosphorylation enhances the ability of CAP‐derived peptides to disrupt CaOx crystal growth and modulate crystal morphology in a concentration‐dependent manner.

Raman Spectroscopic Analysis of CaOx Crystals

The Raman spectra of the control groups (Figure 2h,i) confirmed the precipitation of the COM phase. This assignment was supported by the characteristic vibrational bands at 506 and 595 cm−1, corresponding to O–C–O bending modes, the band at 896 cm−1 attributed to C–C stretching, and the prominent doublet at 1464 and 1490 cm−1 associated with C=O and asymmetric C–O stretching vibrations, respectively [21, 23, 24].

At low concentrations (0.5 and 3 μg/mL), CAP‐pi treatment (Figure 2h) did not substantially modify the characteristic COM spectral profile. However, at 5 μg/mL, marked spectral alterations were observed. Although the 1464 cm−1 band remained preserved, the intensity of the 1490 cm−1 component was considerably attenuated. This reduction in doublet resolution suggests that CAP‐pi perturbs the local crystal lattice organization of the monohydrate phase.

In contrast, CAP‐pip‐treated samples (Figure 2i) largely retained the characteristic COM vibrational profile across the evaluated concentrations. The 1464/1490 cm−1 doublet and the remaining fingerprint bands remained well defined, even at 5 μg/mL, indicating preservation of the overall monohydrate spectral signature. Nevertheless, the sample treated with 3 μg/mL CAP‐pip exhibited partial attenuation of the 1490 cm−1 band, resembling the spectral behavior observed for 5 μg/mL CAP‐pi.

Importantly, phase assignment was based primarily on band positions rather than absolute spectral intensity. Collectively, these findings suggest that peptide adsorption induces local structural perturbations within the COM lattice, consistent with the morphological alterations observed by SEM.

Face‐Selective Adsorption of CAP‐Derived Peptides on COM Crystals

Fluorescence confocal microscopy revealed that both peptides exhibited preferential localization on defined COM crystal regions. CAP‐pi/AF488 and CAP‐pip/AF546 accumulated on areas corresponding to the {121} and {100} crystallographic faces (Figures 2j–k), surfaces known to present exposed Ca2+ ions [6]. Notably, certain crystal orientations showed minimal fluorescence signal, indicating orientation‐dependent binding. The enhanced signal intensity and more localized distribution observed for CAP‐pip are consistent with increased electrostatic affinity arising from phosphate groups, supporting a charge‐mediated adsorption mechanism.

MD Simulations of Peptide–CaOx Interactions

To further elucidate the interaction mechanism, 100 ns MD simulations were performed. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of both CAP‐pi and the reference peptide (RP) was calculated to assess their structural stability (Figure 3a). CAP‐pi exhibited initial conformational rearrangement (RMSD increase to ~6 Å) followed by stabilization at ~2 Å after 10 ns, indicating structural adaptation upon interaction with the mineral environment. In contrast, the RP displayed persistent fluctuations (3–5 Å), consistent with weak or nonspecific association. Minimum distance analysis revealed sustained proximity of CAP‐pi to calcium ions (2.6 Å), whereas the RP remained distant (12–35 Å) (Figure 3b). Contact analysis confirmed significantly higher interaction frequency between CAP‐pi and Ca2+ ions (Figure 3c).

Discussion

Pathological calcifications comprise predominantly calcium phosphate phases and are implicated in cardiovascular mineralization, dental calculus formation, and breast carcinoma‐associated deposits. In renal calculi, CaOx frequently coexists with calcium phosphate phases, highlighting the relevance of understanding peptide‐mediated modulation of both mineral systems [25].

Brushite represents a metastable calcium phosphate phase whose rapid nucleation can be delayed by macromolecules that interfere with dissolution–reprecipitation pathways [22]. Consistent with previous studies [26, 27, 28], peptides enriched in acidic and phosphorylated side chains exhibit pronounced inhibitory activity against brushite growth, largely attributed to electrostatic interactions between negatively charged functional groups and surface‐exposed Ca2+ ions.

In this context, CAP‐pi and its phosphorylated analog CAP‐pip provide a structurally defined system to evaluate the contribution of phosphate groups to mineral modulation. Recent systematic evidence demonstrates that inhibitory potency across different peptide families scales parametrically with charge density rather than molecular length, with the most potent crystal modulators clustering within a highly acidic physicochemical window (pI ≤ 4.8) [16]. The addition of three phosphate groups to the 14‐amino‐acid CAP‐pi sequence abruptly shifts its theoretical pI from 3.49 to 1.8. This positions CAP‐pip as an highly charged molecule, displaying a charge density that surpasses even established benchmark inhibitors such as the MGP‐derived peptide YGlapS (14 amino acids, pI 2.23) [27], and the OPN‐derived peptide P3 (19 amino acids, pI 2.98) [29]. Consistent with the OPN‐derived peptide series where inhibitory activity scales with phosphate number (P0 < P1 < P3) [6], the three phosphoserines in CAP‐pip dramatically amplify its modulatory effect compared to the unphosphorylated CAP‐pi, confirming that accumulating phosphorylation sites maximizes peptide‐mineral affinity. This concentrated increase in anionic charge density directly correlates with the superior crystal modulation efficiency of CAP‐pip, which achieved pronounced morphological disruption of brushite and near‐complete suppression of calcium oxalate crystallization at 5 μg/mL. In contrast, the less anionic native CAP‐pi, which exhibits a charge profile more comparable to moderate inhibitors like the Statherin‐derived DR9 (pI 3.63) [30] or the OPN‐derived pOPAR (pI 3.58) [29],requires higher concentrations to perturb lattice organization. The inhibitory behavior observed here can be interpreted through the flexible polyelectrolyte model [31], wherein structurally disordered peptides adopt conformations that maximize multivalent electrostatic interactions with mineral surfaces. Such conformational adaptability facilitates coordination between phosphate or carboxylate groups and surface‐exposed calcium ions [17]. It is important to note that the peptide concentrations employed in this study (0.5–5 μg/mL) are consistent with ranges utilized in in vitro mineralization studies. Although these concentrations are not intended to replicate the exact endogenous physiological or pathological concentrations of full‐length CAP in vivo, they provide the necessary stoichiometric threshold to effectively probe peptide‐mineral interfacial phenomena under controlled supersaturation conditions.

The pronounced morphological perturbations observed in brushite crystals, particularly at 5 μg/mL CAP‐pip, are consistent with a surface‐controlled inhibition mechanism. Rather than altering bulk solubility, peptide adsorption at specific crystallographic faces likely modifies attachment kinetics and step propagation, thereby influencing crystal habit. Contemporary crystal growth theory distinguishes between inhibitors acting at the crystal–solution interface and those interacting with growth units in solution; only the latter are expected to influence apparent solubility [32]. The increased defect density and irregular growth patterns observed here therefore support a kinetically driven mechanism of growth suppression.

Brushite crystal habit arises from the triangular arrangement of dicalcium phosphate dihydrate units that evolve into plate‐like morphologies [22]. Preferential adsorption of CAP‐derived peptides onto the {100} face could disrupt this ordered stacking, promoting triangular and irregular morphologies, as observed at higher peptide concentrations. The concentration‐dependent nature of these effects further supports an adsorption‐mediated mechanism.

Raman spectroscopy provided complementary molecular insight. Although the ν₁ PO4 3− band at 980 cm−1 persisted across conditions, its reduced intensity in peptide‐treated samples suggests decreased crystal order or reduced effective crystal mass deposition rather than phase transformation. Similar attenuation of phosphate vibrational modes accompanied by morphological alteration has been reported in previous studies of crystallization inhibitors [33]. Non‐peptidic anionic modulators such as inositol hexakisphosphate likewise disrupt brushite organization and reduce crystal size [18], reinforcing the notion that anionic charge density plays a central mechanistic role.

The interplay between calcium phosphate and CaOx phases in renal mineralization has long been recognized. Brushite, which readily precipitates under mildly acidic urinary conditions (pH < 6.9), has been proposed to act as a transient precursor and nucleation substrate for COM formation [34]. Indeed, approximately 85% of kidney stones contain CaOx, predominantly in the monohydrate form (whewellite), often deposited over calcium phosphate cores such as Randall's plaques [20, 22]. In vitro studies further support this sequence, demonstrating that brushite can initially precipitate from supersaturated solutions, followed by CaOx formation through oxalate adsorption onto calcium‐exposed brushite surfaces [35]. Given the lower solubility of CaOx relative to brushite, progressive dissolution–replacement processes may occur under sustained CaOx supersaturation [22, 36].

The crystallization of CaOx revealed well‐faceted deposits in the control group, displaying the characteristic morphology of COM crystals, as expected under supersaturated conditions. Similar to the brushite assays, CAP‐pip produced the most pronounced morphological effects. A progressive reduction in well‐defined COM crystals was observed in the presence of CAP‐pip, together with the appearance of compact rosette‐like aggregates. At higher peptide concentrations, crystallization was markedly suppressed, possibly due to peptide‐mediated perturbation of nucleation and growth processes through interaction with free calcium ions. Previous studies have reported that phosphorylated peptides and phosphoprotein‐derived sequences effectively interfere with calcium oxalate crystallization through electrostatic interactions with calcium‐rich surfaces [6, 27, 29, 37]. Rosette‐like morphologies similar to those observed here have previously been associated with hydrated calcium oxalate structures, particularly transient or metastable organizations related to COD [9]. However, studies have demonstrated that calcium oxalate hydrates undergo dynamic structural rearrangements and transformation processes, where morphological features may persist despite progressive changes in short‐range crystal order. Valido et al. reported that COD instability and its transformation toward COM are strongly influenced by local structural disorder and water‐channel rearrangements, resulting in intermediate states that may not exhibit fully resolved spectroscopic signatures of a stable dihydrate phase [24]. In this context, the rosette‐like aggregates observed in the present study may represent peptide‐induced perturbations of crystal organization and hydration state rather than fully stabilized COD crystals.

Raman spectroscopy revealed peptide‐induced perturbations in the vibrational profile of COM crystals, particularly involving the 1464/1490 cm−1 doublet associated with oxalate coordination environments. Although the characteristic COM bands remained predominant across all experimental conditions, attenuation of the 1490 cm−1 component was observed in samples treated with 5 μg/mL CAP‐pi and 3 μg/mL CAP‐pip. These spectral changes suggest localized alterations in crystal packing and hydrogen‐bonding organization rather than complete stabilization of a distinct crystalline phase. Such behavior is consistent with previous reports describing intermediate or structurally disordered states during calcium oxalate hydrate transformation processes [24].

Face‐selective adsorption patterns further support this interpretation. The labeled peptides CAP‐pi/AF488 and CAP‐pip/AF546 selectively adsorbed onto the {−101} crystal face, which is enriched in Ca2+ ions. This interaction likely inhibited COM nucleation and crystal growth via electrostatic binding, as previously proposed [17]. In addition, both peptides were observed to adsorb onto the {120} face, a crystallographic plane previously reported to facilitate COM growth under physiological conditions by contributing lattice ions to crystal development [29, 38, 39]. Notably, in one instance, a crystal was oriented such that it exposed the {010} face, on which no peptide adsorption was observed. Similar results were reported by Grohe et al. [6], who demonstrated that OPN‐derived phosphopeptides preferentially adsorbed to the {100} and {121} faces of COM crystals whereas showing little to no interaction with the {010} face. Such selectivity suggests that local surface charge density and ion coordination geometry govern peptide binding. By occupying growth‐active sites, phosphorylated peptides likely disrupt step propagation and inhibit COM maturation.

MD simulations provide complementary mechanistic insight. CAP‐pip exhibited increased structural persistence, reduced peptide–surface distance, and a higher frequency of Ca2+ coordination events relative to CAP‐pi. These features indicate stronger interfacial binding mediated by phosphate groups, reinforcing the role of multivalent electrostatic interactions in crystal growth modulation. The data obtained demonstrate that phosphorylation enhances the ability of CAP‐pip to modulate CaOx crystal growth and morphology through stronger interfacial interactions with calcium‐rich crystal surfaces. The results highlight the importance of anionic functional group density and spatial flexibility in regulating mineral growth at the peptide–crystal interface.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that CAP‐derived peptides modulate the crystallization of brushite and calcium oxalate in vitro. Our findings establish that phosphorylation critically enhances the inhibitory potency of these peptides by increasing anionic charge density, thereby promoting face‐specific electrostatic interactions and disrupting ordered crystal growth. By isolating these highly charged functional motifs, we provide clear mechanistic insight into peptide‐mediated modulation of pathological mineralization. Collectively, these findings provide mechanistic insight into peptide‐mediated modulation of pathological mineralization and support the potential of phosphopeptides as biomimetic regulators of crystallization processes.
