A combined endovascular and ultrasound approach to femoral artery pseudoaneurysm injection following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR)
Authors: Viren S. Sehgal, Ajay Menon, Jonathan Schor, Kuldeep Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iatrogenic femoral artery pseudoaneurysm (PSA) is a known complication of groin access for endovascular procedures. For cases refractory to ultrasound-guided thrombin injection, open repair risks blood loss during proximal and distal exposure, while covered stenting may be precluded by proximity to the femoral bifurcation. We report an 87-year-old hemodynamically unstable woman on vasopressor support and dual antithrombotic therapy who developed a 2.3-cm wide-necked left common femoral artery PSA during the post-TAVR course. After bedside ultrasound-guided thrombin injection failed, and with both open repair and covered stenting judged high risk, the PSA was treated using a hybrid approach combining endovascular balloon occlusion of the PSA neck with ultrasound-guided thrombin injection. The PSA was successfully excluded with preserved flow in the superficial and deep femoral arteries; 24-hour duplex confirmed a durable seal, and the patient remained complication-free at one year. In high-risk patients with wide-necked PSAs near the femoral bifurcation, this hybrid technique offers a lower-risk alternative to covered stenting or open surgery when standard thrombin injection fails.

Introduction

Common femoral artery PSA is a common complication of femoral access with reported incidence of 0.1%-0.2% in diagnostic procedures and 3.5%-5.5% in interventional procedures [1]. Features warranting intervention include large size >2 cm, expansion, infection, neurologic deficit, symptoms, signs of ongoing blood loss, and skin changes [2,3]. Ultrasound guided compression therapy and ultrasound guided thrombin injection are often first line therapies due to the less invasive nature and high rates of success reported even in patients on anticoagulation therapy [4,5]. Not all pseudoaneurysms have favorable anatomy for these modalities and some require additional injections. Endovascular options include balloon inflation alone or covered stent placement [6]. Proximity to the femoral bifurcation may limit the ability of covered stent placement to seal the pseudoaneurysm neck as stenting across the profunda femoral artery may result in thigh ischemia [7]. Open surgical repair is invasive, usually done under general anesthesia the PSA sac often makes proximal and distal control more complicated with risk of moderate blood loss.

Thus, we present a hybrid option to be considered before open repair after traditional techniques have failed. A combination of endovascular balloon occlusion with ultrasound guided thrombin injection demonstrated a successful result without subjecting the patient to general anesthesia, blood loss, coverage of the profunda femoral artery and less risk of intra-arterial thrombin injection.

Methods

An IRB was not needed for this manuscript. An 87-year-old female presented from nursing home with syncope and was found to have critical aortic stenosis with valve area of 0.36 cm [2], peak gradient of 85 mmHg and mean gradient of 41 mmHg. Past medical history was significant for atrial fibrillation on Eliquis, coronary artery disease with recent PCI on DAPT, hypertension, deafness, dementia and intermittent GI bleeding. The patient underwent successful TAVR via right common femoral artery access with 14Fr sheath managed by two Perclose and left common femoral artery access for angiography managed by manual pressure post procedure. Post operative course was complicated by atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response, pseudomonas bacteremia, hemodynamic instability requiring pressor support and anemia requiring transfusion in the setting of melena and 2.3 cm wide-necked left common PSA (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Vascular surgery was consulted, and bedside thrombin injection was attempted but ultimately unsuccessful. After bedside thrombin injection failed, a multidisciplinary discussion weighed the remaining options. The PSA had a wide neck and lay close to the femoral bifurcation, making percutaneous thrombin injection prone to distal embolization and placing a covered stent at high risk of compromising profunda femoris flow. Open repair was undesirable given the patient’s ongoing transfusion requirement, vasopressor dependence, and dual antithrombotic therapy (apixaban plus DAPT). Because each standard option was either anatomically or physiologically high risk, a hybrid endovascular approach combining balloon neck occlusion with ultrasound-guided thrombin injection was chosen. Discussion

PSAs present a unique challenge for vascular surgeons. While uncommon, their insidious development and potential for rupture and life-threatening hemorrhage necessitates prompt diagnosis and intervention. PSAs typically arise from iatrogenic complications rather than spontaneously, occurring 0.1%-0.2% in diagnostic procedures and 3.5%-5.5% in interventional procedures [1]. The most common cause is damage to the femoral artery during endovascular procedures performed through this access point. Another potential cause is anastomotic breakdown, a complication that can occur after arterial reconstructive surgery at the femoral level. Spontaneous PSAs, however, are exceptionally rare. These typically occur due to underlying weakening of the arterial wall from factors like atherosclerosis or connective tissue disorders such as Ehlers Danlos or Behçet's disease [8]. Blunt force trauma to the groin can also rarely cause a breach in the femoral artery leading to pseudoaneurysm formation oftentimes, these pseudoaneurysms are associated with a pulsatile, tender swelling with bruits upon auscultation [9].

Several treatment options are available for PSA, with the choice depending on various factors such as size, location, and patient characteristics. Minimally invasive approaches are often preferred due to their lower complication rates and faster recovery times [5]. Traditional options entail thrombin injection directly into the PSA, stenting, and surgery. Percutaneous injection of thrombin directly into the pseudoaneurysm is a minimally invasive approach for achieving thrombosis and obliteration of the pseudoaneurysm. This technique offers high success rates, particularly for smaller PSAs. Certain conditions of the PSA must be appreciated prior to choosing thrombin injection. The neck of the pseudoaneurysm must be narrow at 1 cm long [10]. Otherwise, the thrombin can flow out of the pseudoaneurysm and cause distal embolization as seen in wide necked PSAs. Contraindications to thrombin injection include allergies to thrombin amongst the other aforementioned factors. This patient’s PSA had a wide neck thus percutaneous thrombin injection was not an option. Ultrasound guided compression involves applying manual compression directly over the neck of the pseudoaneurysm using ultrasound guidance to ensure proper positioning. The compression facilitates thrombosis within the sac. While relatively simple and noninvasive, this approach requires prolonged compression times, typically exceeding 30 minutes, and may not be effective. Moreover, this method can cause patient discomfort alongside potential nerve injury and low success rate [11]. For larger or more complex PSAs, endovascular stenting may be employed. This minimally invasive approach involves placing a stent graft across the neck of the pseudoaneurysm, effectively excluding it from the arterial circulation and promoting thrombosis. Stenting offers a durable solution with high success rates but carries inherent risks associated with endovascular procedures, such as bleeding at the access site and stent thrombosis. However, it was not used in our patient due to the PSA’s location at the branch of the superficial femoral artery and profunda so the stent placement would not be possible. Furthermore, the common femoral artery is a point of flexion, contraindicating stent placement. Lastly, surgical repair remains an option for PSAs, particularly those unsuitable for minimally invasive approaches. The surgical approach may involve excision of the pseudoaneurysm sac with primary closure of the artery or patching of the defect with a synthetic graft. While definitive, surgery is associated with higher complication rates compared to minimally invasive techniques.

Complex vascular problems frequently require individualized, multidisciplinary planning when standard options are high-risk or technically limited [12], and unusual iatrogenic intravascular complications similarly underscore the value of imaging-guided, multidisciplinary management [13], principles that informed the hybrid strategy used here. We employed a hybrid approach to PSA thrombin injection using endovascular balloon occlusion which served as a viable option when traditional methods fail. The endovascular balloon occlusion allows for larger volume of thrombin injection with lower risk of intra-arterial thrombin propagation. In this method, the balloon is inflated to provide intravascular compression of the PSA’s neck for 5 minutes while the direct thrombin injection stimulates thrombosis. The decision to heparinize should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis as a 5-minute balloon occlusion could risk arterial thrombosis. To note, we employed a hybrid approach because the pseudoaneurysm was located close to the branch point of the bifurcation therefore eliminating the option of a covered stent. Employing solely a direct thrombin injection was also not feasible because the neck of the pseudoaneurysm was too wide for targeted efficacy.

Conclusion

This report highlights a case of PSA secondary to catheter removal from the femoral artery for TAVR. Repair of this involved a dual approach method of using an intravascular balloon to occlude the pseudoaneurysm’s neck alongside an injection of thrombin into the lumen.
