Lactate metabolism regulation enhances cuproptosis to activate antitumor immunity: Synergistic therapy of hyaluronic acid nanomicelles delivering lactate oxidase/copper-doped carbon dots combined with α-PD-L1 inhibits distant tumors
Authors: Hui Zhang, Xu Zhu, Jingchun Wang, Yibo Huo, Yikai Ma, Shengzhong Rong, Yuting Lu, Yingxue Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To address the core issue of low clinical response rates (10%-30%) to α-PD-L1 immune checkpoint blockade therapy, this study targeted the immunosuppressive bottleneck mediated by lactate accumulation in the tumor microenvironment (TME). A targeted drug delivery system (HLC) was constructed using hyaluronic acid (HA) nanomicelles co-loaded with lactate oxidase (LOX) and copper-doped carbon dots (CuCDs). Combined with α-PD-L1, a synergistic antitumor strategy integrating lactate metabolic regulation, cuproptosis, and immune activation was established. HLC achieves targeted accumulation through specific binding between HA and CD44 receptors on tumor cells. It directionally releases LOX and CuCDs in the acidic TME via pH-responsive properties. LOX efficiently clears lactate and elevates pH, blocking the HIF-1α/PD-L1 pathway to reverse immunosuppression. It also generates H2O2 in situ to provide substrates for Cu-mediated Fenton-like reactions, enhancing cuproptosis. In vitro experiments confirm that HLC promotes dendritic cell maturation and drives M2-type macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype. In vivo studies using CT26 tumor-bearing mouse models show that HLC combined with 808 nm laser and α-PD-L1 significantly inhibits primary and distant tumor growth. It remodels the antitumor immune microenvironment with favorable biosafety. This study elucidates the synergistic mechanism between lactate metabolic regulation and cuproptosis, establishes an association between lactate level and cuproptosis sensitivity, and provides a novel strategy and experimental basis for combined cancer immunotherapy with important clinical translation value.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy has revolutionized the clinical management of malignant tumors. α-PD-L1, by abrogating tumor-mediated immune suppression and remodeling the antitumor immune microenvironment, has gained regulatory approval for the treatment of a broad spectrum of solid tumors [[1], [2], [3]]. Despite these breakthroughs, clinical evidence indicates that the objective response rate of α-PD-L1 monotherapy remains disappointingly low, ranging from 10% to 30% [[4], [5], [6]]. The intrinsic immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) is widely recognized as a pivotal barrier that restricts the therapeutic efficacy of ICB.

A hallmark of cancer is metabolic reprogramming, wherein tumor cells predominantly rely on glycolysis for energy production, even under normoxic conditions—a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect [7,8]. This metabolic shift drives the aberrant accumulation of lactate within the TME. Excessive lactate not only establishes an acidic extracellular environment but also stabilizes the expression of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) by inhibiting the activity of prolyl hydroxylases (PHDs) [[9], [10], [11]]. This stabilization further transcriptionally upregulates PD-L1 expression, thereby directly impairing the cytolytic function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). To reverse this immunosuppressive cycle, lactate oxidase (LOX) has emerged as a compelling therapeutic enzyme due to its unique capacity to specifically catalyze lactate catabolism [12].

LOX mediates the oxidative breakdown of lactate into pyruvate and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), while simultaneously consuming protons (H+) in the TME. This dual enzymatic modulation effectively scavenges excess lactate and mitigates the acidic TME, thereby abrogating the direct suppression of CTLs. Concomitantly, LOX-mediated metabolism compromises HIF-1α stability and downregulates PD-L1 expression. These coordinated effects synergistically release the ‘immune checkpoint’ constraint on CTLs and disrupt the vicious cycle of lactate-driven immunosuppression in the TME.

Cuproptosis, a newly identified form of regulated cell death (RCD), is mechanistically distinct from other forms of cell death [13]. Its core mechanism involves mitochondrial respiratory chain dysfunction and the aberrant accumulation of lipoylated proteins, primarily dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), triggered by intracellular copper ion overload [14]. Copper ions exert cytotoxicity through Fenton-like reactions that generate reactive oxygen species (ROS). Furthermore, copper-induced cell death can trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) in tumor cells, leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including CRT and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein. These events collectively initiate the host's adaptive antitumor immune response [15].

Building on the aforementioned background, this study proposes a novel multimodal antitumor strategy that integrates lactate metabolism modulation, cuproptosis induction, and immune activation. This strategy precisely couples metabolic reprogramming with amplified cuproptosis. Specifically, LOX is employed to scavenge lactate, destabilize HIF-1α, and downregulate PD-L1 expression. Concurrently, the H2O2 generated in situ serves as a critical co-factor to potentiate cuproptotic effects, directly eradicating tumor cells and triggering ICD. This mechanistic cascade activates the host's adaptive antitumor immunity, establishing a synergistic signaling axis of ‘lactate metabolism modulation-HIF-1α/PD-L1 downregulation-tumor cell eradication-immune activation’ to significantly augment tumor cell susceptibility to ICB therapy.

To implement this strategy, we engineered a hyaluronic acid-cinnamaldehyde (HA-CA) nanomicellar platform, designated HA-CA@LOX/CuCDs (abbreviated as HLC), for the co-delivery of LOX and copper-doped carbon dots (CuCDs). This nanoplatform was combined with α-PD-L1 to achieve multi-mechanistic synergistic therapy. CuCDs integrate favorable biocompatibility, sustained copper ion release profiles and good drug encapsulation capabilities, making them an ideal vector for mediating the proposed synergistic effects [16]. As a natural water-soluble polysaccharide, hyaluronic acid (HA) exhibits excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and inherent tumor-targeting potential. It facilitates the targeted accumulation of the nanodelivery system at tumor loci through specific recognition of the CD44 receptor, which is frequently overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells, thereby mitigating off-target toxicity to normal tissues [17]. This study introduced cinnamaldehyde (CA) to modify amino-functionalized HA-EDA. The reactive aldehyde group of CA undergoes Schiff base condensation with free amino groups on HA-EDA, converting hydrophilic HA into amphiphilic HA-CA copolymers capable of self-assembling into nanomicelles. The acid-labile Schiff bonds readily cleave under the weakly acidic tumor microenvironment (pH 5.5–6.5), triggering micelle disassembly and on-site release of loaded LOX and CuCDs. This avoids premature drug leakage in neutral physiological circulation and elevates intratumoral drug accumulation. Notably, the HA-CA carrier only acts as a responsive delivery scaffold without participating in lactate regulation, cuproptosis induction or anti-tumor immune activation. Consequently, HA represents a paradigm biomaterial for the construction of tumor-targeted nanodelivery systems. The core therapeutic merits of the HLC nanoplatform are threefold. First, it enables efficient and specific accumulation of nanomicelles at tumor sites via the HA-CD44 receptor interaction, thereby minimizing systemic toxicity to normal tissues. Second, LOX-mediated lactate scavenging in the TME elevates local pH, thereby abrogating the HIF-1α/PD-L1 signaling cascade and reversing immune suppression. Third, LOX-catalyzed lactate metabolism generates H2O2 in situ, which furnishes essential substrates for cuproptosis and amplifies its inductive efficacy. The primary objective of this study is to successfully construct the HLC nanodelivery system, systematically characterize its physicochemical properties, and evaluate its in vitro and in vivo antitumor activities as well as synergistic therapeutic efficacy. Furthermore, this study aims to delineate the underlying molecular mechanisms by which LOX-mediated lactate metabolism modulation and cuproptosis synergistically enhance immune checkpoint blockade therapy.

Preparation of CuCDs

CuCDs were prepared via a one-step hydrothermal method. 96 mg of citric acid, 96 mg of polyethyleneimine and 100 mg of copper chloride were weighed and dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, and a homogeneous mixed solution was obtained by ultrasonic dispersion for 30 min. The above mixed solution was transferred into a 50 mL high-pressure reaction kettle lined with polytetrafluoroethylene, and a constant temperature reaction was carried out at 180 °C for 6 h. After the reaction, the reaction kettle was naturally cooled to room temperature. The obtained product was filtered through a 0.22 μm aqueous filter membrane and then dialyzed in a dialysis bag with a molecular weight (Mw) cut-off of 1 kDa for 72 h. During the dialysis, deionized water was replaced every 12 h to remove unreacted raw materials and small molecular impurities. Finally, the dialyzed solution was freeze-dried to obtain CuCDs powder, which was sealed and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Preparation of HLC

0.5 g of HA was weighed and dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 288 mg of EDC and 144 mg of NHS. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 30 min at room temperature in the dark; subsequently, ethylenediamine (150 mg) was slowly added dropwise, and magnetic stirring was continued for 12 h at room temperature in the dark. After the reaction, the reaction solution was transferred into a dialysis bag with a Mw cut-off of 3 kDa and dialyzed against ultrapure water for 3 d, with ultrapure water replaced every 8 h. After dialysis, the solution was freeze-dried, and white HA-EDA powder was obtained.

0.3 g of HA-EDA was weighed and dispersed in 30 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), and 69 mg of cinnamaldehyde was added to the dispersion. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 8 h at room temperature; the reaction solution was dialyzed against deionized water for 48 h using a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cut-off of 3 kDa to remove unreacted cinnamaldehyde and organic solvents. The dialyzed HA-CA was dispersed in 10 mL of a deionized water/DMF mixed solution (Vdeionized water:VDMF = 7:3), and 10 mg of LOX and 20 mg of CuCDs were added. The mixture was magnetically stirred for 20 min at room temperature and then transferred to a rotary evaporator. Water was removed by reduced-pressure rotary evaporation at 50 °C, leading to the formation of a uniform film on the inner wall of the rotary evaporation flask. 20 mL of PBS buffer (pH 7.4) was added to the flask, and the film was completely dispersed by ultrasonic treatment. The resulting solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm aqueous filter membrane and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min, with the precipitate collected. The precipitate was subjected to centrifugal separation with PBS buffer (pH 7.4) three times for 10 min each to remove free LOX and CuCDs. Finally, the precipitate was resuspended in an appropriate amount of PBS buffer (pH 7.4) and stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Determination of photothermal conversion performance

4 mL of HLC aqueous solution (300 μg mL−1) was added into a quartz cuvette, and irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at the power densities of 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 W cm−2 for 15 min, respectively. The temperature was recorded and the photothermal heating curves were plotted. 4 mL of HLC aqueous solutions with different concentrations (50, 100, 200 and 300 μg mL−1) were placed in quartz cuvettes, and irradiated with an 808 nm laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 15 min. The temperature was recorded to plot the heating curves, and the thermal images after 15 min of laser irradiation were captured by a photothermal imager. 4 mL of HLC aqueous solution (300 μg mL−1) was taken and irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 2 W cm−2 for 10 min, followed by turning off the laser and natural cooling to room temperature; this process was designated as one laser ‘On-off’ cycle. The above operation was repeated 5 times, with the temperature recorded every 30 s. The temperature curves for the 5 cycles were plotted, and the photothermal stability was evaluated based on the consistency of temperature rise amplitude. Assay of LOX enzymatic activity dependent on temperature

LOX solutions were separately preincubated in water baths at 25 °C, 37 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C and 65 °C for 10 min; 10 mM lactic acid solution was added to the LOX solutions in each temperature group, followed by continuous water bath incubation at the corresponding temperatures for 15 min. After incubation, the residual lactic acid content in each reaction solution was determined using a lactic acid assay kit. Taking the LOX enzymatic activity at 25 °C as 100%, the relative enzymatic activity of LOX at different temperatures was calculated, and a temperature-relative enzymatic activity curve was plotted to determine the optimal reaction temperature of LOX.

Determination of lactic acid scavenging capacity

HLC solutions and free LOX solutions with concentrations of 0, 20, 30, 50, 70, 100 and 150 μg mL−1 were prepared. Each concentration of the above solutions was mixed with 5 mM lactic acid solution at an equal volume and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the residual lactic acid content in each reaction solution was determined using a lactic acid assay kit, and the lactic acid scavenging rate was calculated; the pH value of each reaction solution was measured with a pH meter at the same time. In addition, 150 μg mL−1 HLC solution was mixed with 5 mM lactic acid solution at an equal volume and irradiated with an 808 nm laser at a power density of 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min, after which the residual lactic acid content and pH value of the system were determined by the same method as described above.

Detection of hydroxyl radicals (•OH) by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR)

5,5-Dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) was used as the •OH trapping agent. 100 μg mL−1 CuCDs solution and HLC solution were separately added to 5 mM lactic acid solution, and the mixture was diluted to a final volume of 3 mL with PBS buffer, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, 10 μL of DMPO trapping agent was added, and the characteristic signal of •OH in the system was immediately detected using an EPR spectrometer.

Detection of •OH using MB degradation method

HLC was added into 5 mM lactic acid solution to achieve final concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 75 and 100 μg mL−1, respectively. Subsequently, 200 μL of 10 μg mL−1 MB solution was added dropwise, and the mixture was accurately adjusted to a volume of 3 mL with PBS buffer, followed by incubation in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation, the absorbance of each reaction system at 650 nm (Abs650 nm) was determined using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The generation capacity of •OH in the system was indirectly characterized by the decrease in absorbance. For the parallel experiment, HLC solution with a final concentration of 100 μg mL−1 was irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min, and the absorbance change was measured using the same method described above to evaluate the effect of photothermal triggering on •OH generation. In addition, PBS buffer systems with pH values of 5.5, 6.8 and 7.4 were prepared separately, and the degradation efficiency of MB by HLC under different pH conditions was investigated under identical experimental procedures.

Characterization of intracellular lactate metabolism and microenvironmental pH

CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells·mL−1 and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow stable adhesion. The culture medium was replaced with fresh medium, and HLC solutions at final concentrations of 0 (blank control), 50, 100, 150, 200 and 300 μg mL−1 were added, followed by incubation for another 8 h. After incubation, the cell culture supernatants were carefully collected, and the lactate content in the supernatants was determined precisely using a lactate detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions.

A free LOX control group was established, in which the LOX concentration was calculated based on its loading content in HLC to eliminate interference from direct enzymatic catalysis.

To investigate the regulatory effect of photothermal stimulation on lactate metabolism, a photothermal treatment group was further prepared. HLC at a final concentration of 300 μg mL−1 was co-incubated with cells for 8 h, followed by irradiation with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min.The lactate content was then measured using the same method described above.

Intracellular pH was detected using the BCECF-AM fluorescent probe method. CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h until stable adhesion was achieved. HLC solution or free LOX solution at a final concentration of 300 μg mL−1 was added, and the cells were incubated for a further 8 h. After incubation, the cells were gently washed three times with preheated PBS buffer to completely remove residual medium. A 5 μM BCECF-AM probe solution was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to allow sufficient probe internalization and ester hydrolysis. The cells were then washed twice with PBS buffer to eliminate background fluorescence. Fluorescence images of cells were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope at specific excitation wavelengths (488 nm/440 nm).The intracellular fluorescence intensity was quantified and converted using ratiometric fluorescence imaging analysis software to determine the dynamic changes in intracellular pH.

Detection of cuproptosis-related proteins in cells

CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. HLC solutions at final concentrations of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 300 μg mL−1 were then added, and the cells were further cultured for 8 h. After incubation, the cells were gently washed three times with PBS buffer. A total of 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde solution was added to each well for fixation at room temperature for 10 min. Following three additional washes with PBS, 1 mL of PBS solution containing 0.1% Triton X-100 was added to permeabilize the cells at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were washed three times with PBS again, and 100 μL of blocking solution containing 5% BSA was added for blocking at room temperature for 30 min. After discarding the blocking solution, polyclonal antibodies against FDX1, DLAT, and LIAS were added, and the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the cells were washed three times with PBS, followed by the addition of AF488- or AF555-conjugated fluorescent secondary antibodies. Incubation was carried out at room temperature in the dark for 40 min. After PBS washes, 2.5 μg mL−1 DAPI staining solution was added for 10 min to stain cell nuclei. The fluorescent expression of target proteins was observed and quantitatively analyzed using an inverted fluorescence microscope. CuSO4 was used as the positive control, TTM as the negative control, and CuCDs and LOX as experimental controls. For the photothermal treatment group, HLC at a final concentration of 300 μg mL−1 was co-incubated with cells for 8 h, then irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min before proceeding with the same detection procedures described above.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial injury

Mitochondrial membrane potential changes and the extent of mitochondrial injury were assessed via the JC-1 fluorescent probe method, with detailed procedures as follows. CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells·mL−1 and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 cell incubator for 24 h. Once the cells had adhered stably, HLC solutions at final concentrations of 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 300 μg mL−1 were added to the respective wells, and incubation was continued for another 8 h. At the end of incubation, the cells were gently rinsed three times with PBS buffer to eliminate residual culture medium and unbound free HLC. An appropriate volume of JC-1 probe solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min to ensure adequate probe internalization and subsequent accumulation within mitochondria. After incubation, the cells were washed three times again with PBS buffer to remove excess free probe, and fluorescence images were observed and captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The criteria for fluorescence signal interpretation were as follows: under normal mitochondrial membrane potential conditions, JC-1 probes form aggregates within mitochondria, displaying red fluorescence. In contrast, when mitochondria are injured and their membrane potential declines, JC-1 exists in a monomeric form, emitting green fluorescence. The extent of mitochondrial damage was indirectly reflected by quantifying the ratio of red to green fluorescence intensity. Experimental groups were set up as follows: CuSO4 served as the positive control for cuproptosis induction, TTM as the negative control for cuproptosis inhibition, and CuCDs and LOX as parallel experimental groups. Concentrations of all groups were adjusted based on equivalent copper content. Additionally, a photothermal-triggered group was established separately: cells co-incubated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC for 8 h were irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min, followed by the same probe incubation, washing, and detection procedures described above. This group was included to explore the effect of photothermal stimulation on HLC-induced mitochondrial injury.

Regulation of HIF-1α pathway on protein expression related to immunity and angiogenesis

To explore the regulatory effects of HLC and photothermal stimulation on key signaling pathways in the tumor microenvironment, several experimental groups were designed. CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells·mL−1 and cultured for 24 h at 37 °C under 5% CO2 until fully attached. Cells were then randomly divided into three groups: HLC group: treated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC; HLC + Laser group: incubated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC for 8 h, then irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min; YC-1 group: pretreated with the HIF-1α inhibitor YC-1 (concentration determined by preliminary tests) and co-incubated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC for 8 h. After treatment, the medium was discarded gently, and cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS. Cells were then collected, and total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was measured to ensure equal loading in each lane.

Protein levels of HIF-1α, PD-L1, and VEGF were analyzed by Western blot. Equal amounts of denatured protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HIF-1α, PD-L1, VEGF, and GAPDH. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL reagent, and band intensities were quantified using image analysis software with GAPDH as the internal control.。

Characterization of markers associated with ICD

To evaluate the regulatory effects of HLC and photothermal activation on immunogenicity in CT26 cells, key biomarkers of ICD were examined. CT26 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 1 × 105 cells·mL−1 and cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 for 24 h to allow stable attachment. Cells were randomly assigned to three groups. HLC group: treated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC; HLC + Laser group: treated with 300 μg mL−1 HLC for 8 h, then irradiated with an 808 nm near-infrared laser at 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min. After the indicated treatments, both protein and extracellular ATP analyses were performed. For protein detection, culture medium was removed gently, and cells were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS and harvested.

Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified, and the expression of CRT and HMGB1 was analyzed by Western blot, with GAPDH as the internal reference. For extracellular ATP measurement, cell culture supernatants were collected. ATP concentration in the supernatants was determined using an ATP detection kit according to the manufacturer's instructions to reflect active ATP release from cells.

ELISA analysis of cytokine secretion in Transwell Co-culture systems

To further explore how HLC and photothermal stimulation regulate the immune functions of dendritic cells and macrophages, culture supernatants were collected from DC2.4 cells and macrophages in the lower chambers of the Transwell co-culture systems. After collection, supernatants were centrifuged at 3000 rpm and 4 °C for 20 min to eliminate cell debris and contaminants. The clarified supernatants were carefully aspirated into new sterile centrifuge tubes, properly labeled by group, and temporarily stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis.

Commercial mouse ELISA kits specific for IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α were used to quantify cytokine concentrations, following the manufacturer's instructions strictly. These measurements were used to assess the effects of HLC and photothermal treatment on cytokine secretion by immune cells, yielding data to support subsequent investigations into immunomodulatory mechanisms.

Establishment and treatment regimen of the CT26 colon cancer Xenograft model

CT26 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were digested with trypsin, harvested by centrifugation, and resuspended in PBS. The cell concentration was adjusted to 1 × 107 cells·mL−1, and the suspension was gently triturated to generate a uniform single-cell suspension. A volume of 0.1 mL of this suspension was subcutaneously injected into both hind limbs of each mouse to create a bilateral tumor model, with the left tumor designated as the primary tumor and the right as the distant tumor. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to five experimental groups (n = 5 per group): Saline control group; α-PD-L1 monotherapy group; HLC monotherapy group; HLC + Laser photothermal combination group; HLC + Laser + α-PD-L1 combination therapy group. The treatment regimen was as follows: Primary tumors were treated via intratumoral injection of HLC (6 mg kg−1) on days 0, 5, and 10. 30 min post-HLC administration, the primary tumors were exposed to an 808 nm near-infrared laser at a power density of 1.5 W cm−2 for 10 min. The α-PD-L1 antibody (3.5 mg kg−1) was administered intraperitoneally 24 h after each HLC injection. Distal tumors remained untreated and served as a distant site observation model. During the 14-day treatment period, body weight and tumor dimensions were measured daily. Upon completion of the treatment, all mice were humanely euthanized for subsequent tissue collection and analyses.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed independently in n replicates (n = 3 or 5). Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS 26.0 software. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA), followed by the LSD-t test for pairwise comparisons between groups. Differences were considered statistically significant at *P < 0.05, highly significant at **P < 0.01, and extremely significant at ***P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Ethylenediamine was conjugated to the carboxyl groups of HA through an amidation reaction. Subsequently, the hydrophilic HA was converted into an amphiphilic polymer HA-CA via a Schiff-base reaction between the newly introduced free amino groups and cinnamaldehyde. The HA-CA copolymer underwent self-assembly into nanomicelles via a film dispersion method. Concomitantly, co-loading of LOX and CuCDs was accomplished to fabricate HLC nanomicelles. Photothermal conversion performance and temperature-modulated LOX enzymatic activity of HLC

The favorable near-infrared absorption of HLC establishes the basis for its photothermal conversion capacity. As a bioactive enzyme, the catalytic activity of LOX is strongly temperature-dependent. This study systematically characterized the photothermal performance of HLC and the regulatory effect of temperature on LOX enzymatic activity, aiming to clarify the synergistic compatibility between photothermal effects and lactate metabolism modulation. Concentration-dependent photothermal heating profiles are presented in Fig. 2a. Under 808 nm laser irradiation at a power density of 1.5 W cm−2, the pure water control exhibited negligible temperature variation. In contrast, the peak temperature of HLC solutions increased monotonically with rising concentration. At a concentration of 300 μg mL−1, the system temperature reached approximately 51 °C. These results confirm that the photothermal heat generation capacity of HLC can be precisely modulated by adjusting its concentration. Power-dependent photothermal heating profiles are summarized in Fig. 2b. Both the heating rate and maximum achievable temperature of HLC were positively correlated with laser power density. When the laser power was elevated from 0.5 W cm−2 to 2 W cm−2, the peak temperature of 300 μg mL−1 HLC solution increased to approximately 57.8 °C. No appreciable temperature change was detected in the pure water control. This finding indicates that the photothermal effect of HLC can be flexibly tuned by modulating laser power. The photothermal conversion efficiency of HLC was calculated by fitting its heating-cooling curve (Fig. 2c). The efficiency was determined to be 38%, which surpasses that of several conventional nanophotothermal agents. Cyclic photothermal stability tests are shown in Fig. 2d. After five consecutive ‘On-off’ cycles, HLC displayed no significant attenuation in its heating capacity. This result demonstrates the excellent cyclic photothermal stability of HLC, which satisfies the requirements for repeated in vivo photothermal therapy. Infrared photothermal thermal imaging results are illustrated in Fig. 2e. The heat generation process of HLC can be real-time visualized and monitored using an infrared thermal imager. Temperature-dependent assays of LOX enzymatic activity are presented in Fig. 2f. Taking the activity at 25 °C as 100%, the relative enzymatic activity of LOX first increases and then decreases sharply with rising temperature. The activity reaches a maximum of 120% at 40 °C, which is defined as the optimal reaction temperature. When the temperature exceeds 40 °C, the enzymatic activity declines rapidly. The HLC concentration was set to 300 μg mL−1, laser power to 1.5 W cm−2, and irradiation time to 10 min. These settings regulate the local tumor temperature to approximately 45 °C. This temperature satisfies the heat requirement for photothermal therapy while preserving ∼80% of LOX catalytic activity. Collectively, HLC exhibited concentration- and power-dependent photothermal effects. It enables precise control of local tumor temperature to avoid off-target thermal damage. The high photothermal conversion efficiency of 38% and excellent cyclic photothermal stability render HLC a promising photothermal therapeutic material. Precise regulation of local tumor temperature balances photothermal conversion and LOX enzymatic activity. This lays a solid foundation for the multimodal synergistic antitumor strategy integrating photothermal therapy, lactate metabolism modulation, and cuproptosis.

pH-responsive drug release, lactate metabolism regulation, and oxidative stress modulation effects of HLC

The acidic characteristics of the tumor microenvironment (pH 5.5–6.5) provide a prerequisite for targeted drug release from pH-responsive nanoplatforms. In this study, the drug release profiles of HLC were investigated under varied pH conditions. The capacities of HLC in lactate scavenging, H2O2 generation, •OH production, and GSH depletion were also systematically evaluated. The objective was to elucidate its regulatory effects on metabolic reprogramming and oxidative stress within the tumor microenvironment.

TEM observations and in vitro drug release studies are presented in Fig. 3a–c. HLC exhibited outstanding structural stability under physiological neutral conditions (pH 7.4). The cumulative release rates of CuCDs and LOX were maintained below 10% within 12 h. In contrast, Schiff base linkages were cleaved at pH 5.5, an environment simulating the acidic tumor microenvironment supplemented with GSH and H2O2, which triggered the disassembly of HA-CA nanomicelles. Under this condition, the cumulative release proportions of both CuCDs and LOX exceeded 70% at the 12 h time point. A further ∼8% elevation in CuCDs release was detected under dual stimulation of LA and acidity (pH 5.5), as presented in Fig. S7. Such pH/redox dual-responsive release behavior endows HLC with robust structural integrity during blood circulation and inhibits premature drug leakage, while enabling on-demand, efficient cargo liberation exclusively at tumor lesions and substantially elevating local intratumoral drug concentrations. Lactate scavenging assays are shown in Fig. 3d and e. LOX encapsulated within HLC retains high catalytic activity. The rate of lactate oxidation catalyzed by HLC is significantly higher than that of free LOX. This result indicates that the HA-CA nanomicelle carrier effectively preserves the bioactivity of LOX and prevents enzymatic degradation. HLC catalyzes lactate oxidation in a concentration-dependent manner. At a concentration of 150 μg mL−1, the lactate scavenging efficiency exceeds 80%. HLC also significantly elevates the system pH to near-neutral levels. This performance is superior to that of free LOX at the same concentration. This improvement may be ascribed to the promoted catalytic activity of LOX or enhanced lactate adsorption by the nanocomposite in the presence of CuCDs. Irradiation with an 808 nm laser further enhances the lactate scavenging ability of HLC, elevating the scavenging efficiency to above 85%. Efficient lactate elimination and pH restoration by HLC break the vicious cycle of lactate accumulation, acidic TME, and immunosuppression. These changes also create a favorable microenvironment for cuproptosis induction. Reversal of the acidic milieu restores the antitumor functions of immune cells. In addition, lactate serves as the natural substrate of LOX, and its metabolic process supplies the source for in situ H2O2 generation. H2O2 generation assays are displayed in Fig. 3f. Upon co-incubation with lactate (pH 5.5), HLC generates H2O2 in a time-dependent manner. The production yield is remarkably higher than that of the free LOX group. This result verifies that LOX within HLC can efficiently catalyze lactate metabolism to generate H2O2 in situ. EPR spectroscopic results are illustrated in Fig. 3g. CT26 cells were employed as a model to validate the targeted uptake of HLC. The regulatory effects of HLC on intracellular lactate metabolism and the HIF-1α/VEGF signaling cascade were also evaluated, with the aim of clarifying the link between lactate metabolic reprogramming and cuproptosis.

Concurrently, in situ H2O2 generated by LOX-catalyzed lactate oxidation supplies abundant substrates for Cu-mediated Fenton-like reactions, establishing a material basis for cuproptosis induction. Western blot analysis is illustrated in Fig. 4d. HLC significantly downregulates the protein expression of HIF-1α, VEGF, and PD-L1 in CT26 cells. Laser irradiation further amplifies this inhibitory effect, forming a regulatory cascade: lactate scavenging/pH normalization → HIF-1α downregulation → VEGF/PD-L1 suppression. Downregulation of HIF-1α further sensitizes tumor cells to cuproptosis by restraining mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming [24]. Downregulation of VEGF blocks tumor angiogenesis and inhibits tumor invasion and metastasis. Downregulation of PD-L1 disrupts tumor immune evasion, relieves suppression of cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and lays a foundation for subsequent immune activation and immune checkpoint blockade therapy.

As shown in Fig. 4e, intracellular H2O2 levels were quantified in CT26 cells treated with different formulations under identical experimental conditions. The CuCDs group exhibited lower intracellular H2O2 concentrations than the control group, whereas HLC treatment elevated H2O2 content, albeit to a lesser extent than free LOX. Mechanistically, LOX catalyzes the oxidation of lactate to generate H2O2 in situ; in contrast, CuCDs consume H2O2 via copper-mediated Fenton-like reactions. The HLC system integrates both LOX-driven H2O2 production and Cu2+-mediated H2O2 consumption, yet the rate of H2O2 generation surpasses its depletion, leading to significantly elevated overall H2O2 levels relative to untreated cells. Notably, the HLC + Laser group displayed slightly reduced H2O2 content compared with the HLC-only group, demonstrating that mild photothermal heating accelerates H2O2 consumption. Accumulated intracellular H2O2 together with the resultant •OH substantially exacerbates oxidative stress, promotes aberrant binding between copper ions and lipoylated proteins, and impairs mitochondrial respiratory chain function [25]. This establishes a critical oxidative stress microenvironment that enables robust cuproptosis induction.

To further establish the causal linkage between lactate clearance, HIF-1α suppression and elevated cuproptosis susceptibility, we constructed HIF-1α-knockout CT26 (CT26-HIF-1α-KO) cells for genetic validation (Fig. 4f). Western blot assays confirmed complete ablation of HIF-1α protein expression in CT26-HIF-1α-KO cells, whereas robust HIF-1α signals were detected in wild-type CT26 cells. In wild-type CT26 cells, CuCDs treatment alone elevated lipoylated DLAT levels while downregulating FDX1 and LIAS, consistent with canonical cuproptosis activation. Prior literature has validated that HIF-1α acts as a negative regulator of DLAT and confers tumor cell resistance to cuproptosis. As the LOX carried by HLC efficiently eliminates intracellular lactate and reduces HIF-1α accumulation, the inhibitory effect of HIF-1α on DLAT is relieved; consequently, HLC treatment further increases lipoylated DLAT abundance on the basis of CuCDs, accompanied by milder reduction of FDX1 and LIAS, which amplifies cuproptotic cell death. Strikingly, no obvious differences in the expression of DLAT, FDX1 and LIAS were observed between the CuCDs group and HLC group in HIF-1α-knockout cells. These genetic knockout results definitively demonstrate that HLC augments cuproptosis predominantly through LOX-dependent downregulation of HIF-1α, solidifying the causal signaling cascade of lactate depletion-HIF-1α inhibition-upregulated DLAT-cuproptosis sensitization.

Collectively, following CD44 receptor-mediated targeted internalization into tumor cells, HLC exerts dual synergistic effects. On one hand, it clears intracellular lactate via LOX, reverses the acidic microenvironment, suppresses the HIF-1α/VEGF/PD-L1 signaling axis, sensitizes tumor cells to cuproptosis, and alleviates immunosuppression. On the other hand, it generates in situ H2O2 through LOX-catalyzed lactate metabolism, fuels Cu-mediated Fenton-like reactions, activates oxidative stress, and establishes a material basis for cuproptosis induction. This achieves precise synergy between lactate metabolic reprogramming and cuproptosis induction.

HLC enhances in vitro cuproptosis for synergistic tumor cell killing

To systematically dissect the tumor cell-killing efficacy of HLC via synergistic lactate metabolism regulation, cuproptosis induction, and photothermal effects, this study comprehensively evaluated the cytotoxicity and core mechanisms of individual components and HLC. MTT assay results are presented in Figs. S9 and S10. The HA-CA carrier, free LOX, 808 nm laser irradiation, and low-concentration CuCDs show no obvious toxicity to normal L929 cells. This confirms the favorable biosafety of HLC components and photothermal irradiation. In contrast, HLC exhibits significant concentration-dependent cytotoxicity against CT26 tumor cells. The cell viability of the CuCDs group decreases from 96 ± 4% at 50 μg mL−1 to 65 ± 5% at 300 μg mL−1, with toxicity mainly derived from •OH generated via Cu-mediated Fenton-like reactions. Cytotoxicity is significantly enhanced in the HLC group, with cell viability dropping from 91 ± 4% at 50 μg mL−1 to 38 ± 5% at 300 μg mL−1, fully reflecting the synergistic effect of LOX and CuCDs. Toxicity is further amplified in the HLC + Laser group with the addition of photothermal effects. Cell viability decreases to 22 ± 3% at 300 μg mL−1, a 16-percentage-point reduction compared with the HLC group at the same concentration.

Live/dead cell double-staining results (Fig. S11) are highly consistent with MTT analysis, confirming that HLC efficiently kills tumor cells while exhibiting good selectivity toward normal cells. This selective killing arises from the high lactate metabolism of tumor cells. LOX catalyzes the oxidation of abundant intracellular lactate in tumor cells to generate H2O2, providing sufficient substrates for Cu-mediated Fenton-like reactions. Normal cells have low lactate levels and thus cannot trigger significant oxidative stress responses.

DCFH-DA probe detection results are displayed in Fig. S12. HLC induces a concentration-dependent ROS burst in CT26 cells. The relative ROS fluorescence intensity of the 300 μg mL−1 HLC group reaches 94 ± 9, 2.4 times that of the CuCDs group (39 ± 4) at the same concentration. ROS levels are further elevated after 808 nm laser irradiation, confirming that photothermal effects significantly enhance Fenton-like reaction efficiency by accelerating enzymatic and chemical reaction rates. This ROS burst originates from a cascade mechanism of ‘lactate oxidation-H2O2 self-supply- Fenton-like reaction activation’.

Collectively, HLC achieves efficient tumor killing via a triple synergistic mechanism. First, lactate metabolism regulation: LOX catalyzes lactate oxidation to generate H2O2, providing sufficient substrates for Fenton-like reactions, while reversing the acidic TME to enhance tumor cell sensitivity to cuproptosis. Second, cuproptosis activation: Cu+ accumulation induces DLAT oligomerization, triggering mitochondrial dysfunction and proteotoxic stress, which serves as the core killing mechanism. Third, photothermal amplification: 808 nm laser irradiation further enhances ROS production and cuproptosis efficiency by accelerating enzymatic and chemical reaction rates. This pathway acts synergistically with lactate metabolic regulation, HIF-1α downregulation, and ROS elevation to jointly achieve efficient tumor killing. Mild local photothermal treatment amplifies cuproptosis via three synergistic molecular cascades: Elevated temperature reduces the binding energy between lactate and LOX and loosens polymeric entanglement of nanomicelles, accelerating intracellular lactate uptake and boosting in situ H2O2 production; The accumulated H2O2 initiates Fenton-like reactions to deplete intracellular GSH and liberate free Cu+, disrupting mitochondrial lipoylation homeostasis, triggering DLAT oligomerization and downregulating FDX1 and LIAS expression; Meanwhile, thermal stimulation suppresses HIF-1α accumulation and relieves its transcriptional inhibition on DLAT, further aggravating mitochondrial dysfunction. Collectively, a complete sequential cascade is established: heat-promoted lactate catabolism → H2O2 enrichment → GSH depletion → Cu+ liberation → DLAT oligomerization → mitochondrial damage, which potentiates cuproptosis at the molecular level.

HLC elicits immunogenic cell death and remodels the tumor immune microenvironment

Systematic quantitative detection of canonical damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and inhibitor rescue assays were performed to investigate the capacity of HLC to trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) and clarify the causal relationship between cuproptosis and ICD. Immunofluorescence semi-quantitative analysis was conducted to monitor three core ICD biomarkers, namely cell surface calreticulin (CRT) exposure, extracellular release of high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and extracellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP) secretion. Tetrathiomolybdate (TTM), a specific inhibitor of cuproptosis, was applied to block the cuproptotic pathway for functional verification.

Immunofluorescence images and quantitative statistics (Fig. 6a–e) revealed negligible membrane-localized CRT signals in untreated CT26 cells. HMGB1 proteins were predominantly retained within cell nuclei, and extracellular ATP concentrations remained at basal levels. Moderate CRT externalization, HMGB1 efflux and ATP secretion were detected in cells treated with HLC alone, which demonstrated that HLC could trigger weak ICD signals. After combined treatment with 808 nm mild near-infrared photothermal irradiation, significant elevations in membrane CRT fluorescence intensity, extracellular HMGB1 abundance and extracellular ATP concentrations were observed (*p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001). Mechanistically, mild photothermal treatment accelerated copper accumulation-triggered aberrant oligomerization of lipoylated DLAT proteins. Mitochondrial damage and intracellular oxidative stress were exacerbated accordingly, which elevated the production efficiency of DAMPs at the upstream molecular level and amplified ICD responses. Collectively, combined administration of HLC and mild photothermal therapy efficiently elicited canonical ICD phenotypes including CRT surface exposure, HMGB1 liberation and ATP secretion. Dual evidence derived from inhibitor intervention and multi-dimensional quantitative assays confirmed that ICD activation relied entirely on functional cuproptotic signaling. A complete molecular regulatory axis was established: photothermally potentiated cuproptosis → massive DAMP release → initiation of systemic antitumor immunity.

DAMPs act as critical danger signals that specifically activate antigen uptake and presentation in DCs, serving as a key bridge between innate and adaptive immunity. A Transwell co-culture system was employed, with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as a positive control, to evaluate the regulatory effect of HLC-induced ICD on DC maturation. Flow cytometric analyses (Fig. 7c) reveal that the proportion of CD80+/CD86+ mature DCs (mDCs) is merely 3.37% in the saline group, indicating an immature, immune-tolerant DC state. HLC treatment significantly increases the mDC proportion to 8.84%, representing a 2.6-fold increase over the control. Combined laser irradiation further elevates the mDC proportion to 18.5%, approaching the level observed in the LPS-positive control group.

Cytokine secretion profiling (Fig. S15) further validates functional activation of mDCs. Relative to the control, HLC treatment markedly promotes secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α, while significantly suppressing expression of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, consistent with prior reports [29]. Synergistic laser irradiation further augments pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, aligning closely with phenotypic maturation of mDCs. Collectively, these results confirm that DAMPs released during HLC-mediated cuproptosis effectively drive the transition of ImmDCs to mDCs. Functionally activated mDCs facilitate efficient tumor antigen presentation, providing essential support for activation and proliferation of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and laying a foundation for combined immune checkpoint blockade therapy.

Macrophages represent the most abundant innate immune cell population within the tumor microenvironment, and their phenotypic polarization directly dictates the immune status of the niche. M2-type macrophages mediate immunosuppression and promote tumor progression, whereas M1-type macrophages exert pro-inflammatory and antitumor functions. Induction of M2-to-M1 polarization is a core strategy for remodeling the antitumor immune microenvironment. HMGB1 released during HLC-induced ICD acts as a key signal to drive this process by binding to Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on macrophages, activating the NF-κB pathway, and initiating the M1 polarization program [30]. Flow cytometric analysis of the Transwell co-culture model (Fig. 7d) shows that the proportion of M2-type macrophages (CD206+) reaches 57.1% in the saline group, while the proportion of M1-type macrophages (CD86+) is only 6.6%, reflecting a typical immunosuppressive phenotype. HLC treatment induces a striking phenotypic reversal, reducing the CD206+ proportion to 34.0% and increasing the CD86+ proportion to 32.3%. Combined laser irradiation further enhances this polarization effect, lowering the CD206+ proportion to 21.6% and raising the CD86+ proportion to 43.4%, approaching the polarization profile of the LPS-positive control group. ELISA analyses (Fig. S16) corroborate these phenotypic findings: HLC treatment significantly promotes secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α in macrophages, while inhibiting release of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. Laser irradiation further amplifies pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion by potentiating ICD and DAMP release, fully matching the functional signature of M1-type macrophages [31,32]. These results indicate that DAMPs released during HLC-mediated cuproptosis act as key polarization signals to efficiently drive conversion of M2-type macrophages to the M1 phenotype. Photothermal effects accelerate cuproptosis and amplify oxidative stress, further enhancing polarization efficiency.

Collectively, HLC achieves systematic remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment via a ‘cuproptosis–ICD–immune cell activation’ cascade mechanism. First, HLC triggers ICD through cuproptosis, releasing DAMPs including CRT, HMGB1, and ATP. These signals promote maturation of ImmDCs into mDCs, enhance antigen presentation, and initiate adaptive antitumor immunity. Second, HMGB1 released from ICD drives M2-to-M1 macrophage polarization via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, breaking the immunosuppressive microenvironment and amplifying pro-inflammatory antitumor effects. Notably, 808 nm laser irradiation acts as a critical synergistic factor: it accelerates cuproptosis and augments oxidative stress via photothermal effects, further potentiating ICD efficiency and immune cell activation.

HLC-mediated in vivo tumor suppression and abscopal effect: cuproptosis activation and synergistic immunotherapy

Given the potent in vitro efficacy of HLC in regulating lactate metabolism, inducing cuproptosis, and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment, this study further validated its in vivo antitumor activity and biosafety in a CT26 tumor-bearing mouse model. In vivo live fluorescence and photothermal imaging results are presented in Fig. 8a and b and S17. Following intravenous administration, HLC actively targets the tumor site. Fluorescence signals and local temperature peak at 8 h and remain at high levels for up to 24 h. In vivo fluorescence imaging and ICP-MS copper quantification of major organs harvested at 8 h post-administration corroborate the above findings (Fig. 8c, d and Fig. S18a). HLC accumulates to a markedly higher extent in tumor tissue than in major normal organs including the heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney. ICP-MS analysis performed on isolated tissues collected 24 h after injection (Fig. S18b) further demonstrates that copper levels within tumors remain significantly elevated relative to all normal organs. The HLC + Lase + αPD-L1 group exhibits the most pronounced tumor inhibition, with both tumor weight and volume reduced to the lowest levels. Combination with αPD-L1 further suppresses distant tumor growth, demonstrating a clear abscopal effect. HLC-mediated synergistic regulation of lactate metabolic reprogramming, cuproptosis activation, and systemic antitumor immunity

In vivo efficacy and mechanistic validation further confirm that HLC achieves dual effects of primary tumor ablation and distant tumor inhibition via a cascade strategy of ‘lactate clearance-cuproptosis activation-immune microenvironment remodeling’.

It also exerts significant synergistic therapeutic effects in combination with αPD-L1 antibody. Tissue metabolic detection results are presented in Fig. S20. Compared with the saline group, tumor tissue lactate content is significantly reduced in the HLC and HLC + Laser groups. Extracellular pH values are markedly elevated at the same time. These results verify the core function of HLC in vivo. HLC relies on the enzymatic activity of LOX to efficiently clear accumulated lactate in the tumor microenvironment and reverse the acidic TME. Improvement of the acidic microenvironment not only removes metabolic barriers for efficient cuproptosis induction. It also initiates cascade regulation of the immune microenvironment by downregulating HIF-1α expression [35,36].

Western blot analysis results are shown in Fig. 9a. HLC-mediated lactate clearance and pH upregulation significantly downregulate the protein expression of HIF-1α and its downstream target genes VEGF and PD-L1. Inhibition of HIF-1α directly blocks the core pathway of tumor angiogenesis and weakens the invasive and metastatic capacity of tumors. Downregulation of PD-L1 breaks the ‘immune escape’ barrier of tumor cells, laying a foundation for the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors. Meanwhile, HLC induces ICD in tumor cells through cuproptosis. This is reflected by significant upregulation of CRT and HMGB1. Expression of IL-2, a key cytokine for T cell activation, is synchronously elevated (Fig. 9b). This result is consistent with previous reports [37,38]. This process converts cytotoxic killing into immunogenic signals, providing a core driving force for the activation of systemic antitumor immunity. Flow cytometric and cytokine detection results further reveal the remodeling effect of HLC on the tumor immune microenvironment (Fig. 9c–i). In primary tumor tissues, HLC treatment significantly promotes the polarization of M2-type macrophages toward the M1 phenotype (CD86+/CD206-). It also induces high expression of CD80 and CD86 co-stimulatory molecules in DCs, enabling antigen presentation (Fig. 9c and d). More importantly, in distant tumor tissues, the infiltration proportion of CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes and CD4+ helper T cells is significantly increased (Fig. 9e). Serum cytokine profiling shows that levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ are significantly elevated. Secretion of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 is markedly suppressed (Fig. 9f–i). This confirms that HLC successfully activates a systemic antitumor immune response [39,40].

Collectively, the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of HLC stems from precise synergy of multiple mechanisms. Lactate metabolic reprogramming reverses the acidic TME, clearing obstacles for cuproptosis activation and immune activation. ICD induced by cuproptosis releases danger signals and initiates antigen presentation. Blockade of the HIF-1α/PD-L1 pathway relieves immunosuppression and amplifies the immune response. On this basis, combination with αPD-L1 antibody further blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint. It achieves triple synergy of ‘metabolic regulation – cuproptotic killing-immune checkpoint blockade’. This ultimately fulfills the dual therapeutic goals of efficient primary tumor elimination and abscopal effect on distant tumors.

Brief discussion on clinical translation prospects and existing limitations

The multifunctional HLC nanomicelle integrates CD44-targeted delivery, lactate metabolic reprogramming, cuproptosis induction, photothermal therapy and immune checkpoint blockade into a single therapeutic platform. Hyaluronic acid, the core carrier material, possesses favorable biodegradability and biocompatibility. In vivo biosafety assessments reveal negligible organic toxicity, and the prepared nanosystem achieves simultaneous suppression of primary and distant tumors, laying a solid foundation for subsequent translational investigations. Several critical bottlenecks remain to be addressed before clinical implementation. First, scalable manufacturing protocols for HLC nanomicelles cannot be stably standardized, leading to uncontrollable batch-to-batch variation in drug loading efficiency. Second, moderate hepatic accumulation is observed following intravenous administration, and long-term toxicological evaluations using large animal models are required to validate systemic biosafety. Third, interindividual heterogeneity of the tumour microenvironment impairs the efficiency of lactate elimination and stimuli-responsive cargo release, while tailored intervention strategies for distinct tumour phenotypes are currently unavailable. Fourth, all mechanistic and pharmacokinetic analyses presented herein are performed on murine tumour models, and relevant human pharmacokinetic data are absent. Future research will focus on the optimization of large-scale synthesis procedures. Long-term toxicity tests on non-human primates will be conducted to evaluate chronic systemic safety. In addition, refined strategies for tumour microenvironment-responsive disassembly will be established, so as to provide sufficient preclinical evidence to support further translational research.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed a hyaluronic acid-based nanomicellar delivery system (HLC) to achieve synergistic antitumor therapy by integrating lactate metabolic regulation, cuproptosis induction, and immune activation. HLC targets tumor cells via CD44-mediated endocytosis, removes lactate, blocks the HIF-1α/PD-L1 pathway, enhances FDX1-LIAS-DLAT-mediated cuproptosis, and triggers immunogenic cell death. It further promotes dendritic cell maturation, M1 macrophage polarization, and T-cell activation to remodel the antitumor immune microenvironment. In vitro and in vivo results demonstrate that HLC combined with near-infrared laser and α-PD-L1 significantly inhibits the growth of both primary and distant tumors with a distinct abscopal effect and favorable biosafety. This work reveals the synergistic mechanism between lactate metabolism and cuproptosis, providing a translatable strategy for improving the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade and establishing a novel paradigm for multimodal tumor therapy. Future studies will optimize drug-loading efficiency and pH-responsive precision, clarify the molecular mechanism of lactate-regulated cuproptosis, and conduct preclinical validation in multiple tumor models to accelerate clinical translation.
