Preserving Patient Influence During Interrupted Guideline Participation

Ting‐Yi Hung, Lien‐Chung Wei

To the Editor,

Orduhan and colleagues show how training, supportive group dynamics and recognition can strengthen patient involvement in guideline development []. They appropriately acknowledge the educational and organisational selectivity of their sample. A further question is whether participation remains influential when it cannot remain continuous. The study recruited people with previous guideline experience and did not collect reasons for nonparticipation []. Its findings therefore do not directly evaluate support for contributors whose availability changes during guideline development.

This distinction matters when translating the findings to mental health guidelines. A person's expertise by experience does not disappear during a period of reduced availability. Nor should a psychiatric diagnosis be taken as evidence that participation will be unreliable. The relevant design question is whether the process accommodates individually identified, changing support needs. The GIN toolkit already recommends reassessing support because physical and mental health conditions can fluctuate, alongside adjustments to meetings and communication []. These existing recommendations provide a foundation for evaluating whether flexibility preserves influence.

We propose evaluating participation trajectories, not only the experiences of established panel members. With proportionate consent and privacy safeguards, developers could record invitations, acceptance, temporary pauses, re‐entry and chosen contribution formats. Reasons for declining or pausing should be optional; collecting these data should not require disclosure of a psychiatric diagnosis. These observations would describe where access is lost, without treating every absence as disengagement or claiming that a small panel statistically represents all affected people.

Before finalising a recommendation, panels could offer a time‐limited opportunity for written, recorded or supported verbal input from members unable to attend the relevant meeting. An agreed return process should explain decisions already made and identify decisions still open to influence. A brief contribution‐to‐decision record could show whether an issue changed the scope, outcome selection, recommendation wording, or implementation advice, or explain why no change followed. This is not a requirement to accept every suggestion. It makes consideration visible without equating continuous attendance with influence. Reporting should minimise identifiable information and avoid turning support into attendance surveillance.

A parallel‐group guideline study found differences in patient‐relevant topics and outcomes despite substantial overlap between groups' proposed questions []. The WHO meaningful‐engagement framework likewise advocates co‐creating policies and services with people with lived experience []. Together, these perspectives support a testable extension of Orduhan and colleagues' findings: assess whether flexible participation preserves substantive influence, while measuring participant burden as well as retention. A successful process should permit a pause without silently converting a contributor into an observer.
