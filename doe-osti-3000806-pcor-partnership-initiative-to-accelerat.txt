 PCOR PARTNERSHIP INITIATIVE

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

 The Plains CO2 Reduction (PCOR) Partnership Initiative, led by the Energy &
Environmental Research Center (EERC), was a 5-year collaborative effort under the U.S.
Department of Energy’s Regional Initiative to Accelerate CCUS (carbon capture, utilization, and
storage). With support from the University of Wyoming School of Energy Resources (UW SER),
the University of Alaska Fairbanks Institute of Northern Engineering (UAF INE), and a broad
network of public and private stakeholders, the program focused on overcoming regional
challenges to CCUS across ten U.S. states and four Canadian provinces. This report summarizes
the notable findings of the EERC and its collaborators through the 5-year PCOR Partnership
Initiative phase.

 The EERC developed and demonstrated a risk-based area of review (AOR) workflow for
U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Class VI carbon dioxide (CO2) storage permitting. This
approach integrated semianalytical leakage models with advanced reservoir simulations to protect
underground sources of drinking water and meet federal and state regulatory requirements. The
methodology was validated through a case study involving a 180,000-tonne-per-year CO2 storage
project, demonstrating that under realistic leakage scenarios, the AOR could be effectively defined
by the extent of the CO2 plume.

 In partnership with Stress Engineering Services, Inc., and OLI Systems, Inc., the EERC
advanced corrosion risk assessment for CO2 pipelines and wells. This included guidelines for
selecting corrosion-resistant alloys, evaluating the impact of impurities like hydrogen sulfide, and
modeling geochemical changes during injection.

 The EERC also field-tested low-impact, cost-effective monitoring technologies at Gevo’s
commercial injection site in North Dakota. These methods offered visualization comparable to
traditional seismic surveys while reducing costs and enabling more frequent monitoring. In
addition, the EERC developed a technical framework for quantifying CO2 plume stabilization, a
key requirement for site closure and financial assurance planning.

 Outreach and education were central to the program. The EERC hosted public events and
legislative briefings and created accessible resources like the PCOR Partnership Atlas. Technical
knowledge was shared through webinars, fact sheets, and annual meetings that brought together
regulators, industry leaders, and researchers. These meetings averaged 133 attendees and fostered
dialogue on commercial, regulatory, and technical drivers of CCUS.

 iii

 UW and UAF contributed decades of oil and gas expertise, supporting assessments of CO2
storage, infrastructure needs, and techno-economic feasibility. Their established stakeholder
networks helped advance policy development, including Alaska’s carbon storage legislation.

 Looking ahead, the EERC will continue to lead regional coordination efforts to align CO2
supply with enhanced oil recovery demand. By connecting CO2 sources from ethanol, coal, natural
gas, and industrial processes with petroleum operations, the PCOR Partnership laid the
groundwork for a robust CCUS infrastructure. This includes ongoing engagement with
policymakers to support regulatory certainty, permitting, and long-term liability management.

1.0 INTRODUCTION

 This report summarizes the notable findings of the Energy & Environmental Research
Center (EERC) and its collaborators through the 5-year Plains CO2 Reduction (PCOR) Partnership
Initiative phase, one of four projects operating under the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE)
Regional Initiative to Accelerate CCUS (carbon capture, utilization, and storage). Led by the
EERC, with support from the University of Wyoming School of Energy Resources (UW SER),
the University of Alaska Fairbanks Institute of Northern Engineering (UAF INE), and vested
stakeholders from the public and private sectors, this joint government–industry effort aimed to
address regional CCUS deployment challenges across a region extending from Missouri to Alaska,
including ten states and four Canadian provinces (Figure 1).

 Since 2019, the PCOR Partnership has supported regional deployment of CCUS by focusing
on solving challenges related to regulations and permitting, long-term liability, profitable

2.0 TECHNICAL INVESTIGATIONS

 2.1 Risk-Based Area of Review

 This phase of the PCOR partnership focused on integrating nearly two decades of risk
management experience with guidance documents, federal and specific state-level regulatory
requirements, and additional requirements imposed on project developers if they choose to pursue
the Section 45Q credit and/or low-carbon fuel market financial incentive programs. The PCOR
Partnership has developed strategies and philosophies that view risk management as a detailed
process that adapts to each project’s needs. These needs are dependent on site-specific conditions,
applicable regulatory requirements, and any additional requirements imposed by pursuing one or
more financial incentive programs. The developed strategy and philosophy views risk assessment
as an iterative process composed of identifying, analyzing, and evaluating individual project risks.

 The EERC developed a modeling workflow to define a risk-based AOR for a U.S.
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Class VI CO2 storage permit, which was designed to
protect underground sources of drinking water (USDWs) and meet the requirements of the Safe
Drinking Water Act, the EPA Class VI underground injection control (UIC) program, and North
Dakota Administrative Code (NDAC) Chapter 43-05-01 (Burton-Kelly and others, 2021). The
approach integrates semianalytical models of fluid leakage through hypothetical wellbores with
numerical reservoir simulations to delineate the AOR. The methodology was built on well-
established research and underlying hydrogeological principles that have been upheld for nearly
three decades. It also incorporates a compositional reservoir simulator to accurately capture
pressure buildup dynamics, accounting for geologic heterogeneity and multiphase interactions.

 The workflow is demonstrated through a case study of a 180,000-tonne-per-year CO2 storage
project in the PCOR Partnership region. Under the scenario where a leaky wellbore is open to a
saline aquifer (thief zone) between the overlying seal (caprock) and the USDW (Figure 2), the
risk-based AOR essentially collapses to the areal extent of the CO2 plume in the storage reservoir
because the pressure buildup in the storage reservoir beyond the CO2 plume is insufficient to drive
formation fluids up a hypothetical leaky wellbore into the USDW. However, even under the
conservative assumption that the leaky wellbore is not open to a thief zone, beyond the areal extent
of the CO2 plume, the incremental leakage is less than 400 m3 over 20 years, which represents
~0.0001% or less of the total volume of water contained within the USDW rock volume (Figure 3).

 The risk-based AOR methodology has received industry acceptance and demonstrates
promise for broader application beyond its current role in Class VI permitting. There is potential
to further develop this methodology for use with other well classes, particularly in the context of
Class II CO2 enhanced oil recovery (EOR) injection permitting and operational activities.

members. CO2 composition and quality are key during pipeline design, fabrication, and
construction when CO2 is transported to an injection site. This investigation included a review of
potential compositional variability in captured CO2 streams and their compatibility with CO2

 2.3 H2S Considerations

 Repurposing existing pipelines is often more cost-effective than building new ones and has
a long history of success in the oil and gas industry. However, converting pipelines for supercritical
CO2 (scCO2) service introduces unique challenges. Original designs rarely account for the specific
requirements of scCO2 transport, and incomplete historical records can complicate assessments—
especially when fracture toughness is a critical design factor.

 The EERC partnered with Stress Engineering Services, Inc. (Stress Engineering), an industry
leader that specializes in materials and metallurgical engineering, to provide PCOR Partnership
members with a basic guideline of considerations for selecting corrosion-resistant alloy materials
for use in CO2 storage and utilization applications (Warmack and others, 2023). Stress Engineering
developed a guideline document for the use of carbon steel (CS) pipelines with CO2 streams
containing hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and for repurposing CS pipelines previously used in H2S service
for CO2 streams without H2S (Craig and others, 2023). The effort included assessment of CO2
streams generated by combustion processes (precombustion, postcombustion, and oxyfuels),
ethanol, and cement plants.

 4

 As CCUS projects expand to reduce anthropogenic CO2 emissions, the need for reliable
transmission infrastructure is accelerating. Transporting CO2 from capture sites to injection wells
will require extensive pipeline networks—up to 25,000 km of interstate trunk lines and 85,000 km
of spur lines by 2050, according to a 2021 Princeton University study (Larson and others, 2021).

 One major concern is the interaction between residual materials from previous service and
the new CO2 stream. Surface deposits and internal scales, particularly those formed during H2S
exposure, can react with CO2 to create corrosive conditions. The investigation examined two
scenarios: 1) converting a pipeline with no prior H2S exposure for use with a CO2 stream that
contains H2S and 2) repurposing a pipeline previously exposed to H2S for a clean CO2 stream
without H2S. Repurposing pipelines for CO2 transmission—especially when H2S is involved,
either in the previous process or the new CO2 service—requires careful consideration, engineering,
and multidisciplinary expertise.

 For existing sour pipelines being converted to transport scCO2 without H2S, the following
guidelines apply:

 1. Verify that the pipeline and all associated components meet the requirements of NACE
 MR0175/ISO 15156 or equivalent standards to mitigate risks of sulfide stress cracking
 (SSC) and hydrogen-induced cracking (HIC) from prior service.

 2. Conduct scraper or brush pigging runs specifically designed to remove iron sulfide (FeS)
 deposits. If liquid water enters the system, residual FeS can trigger severe corrosion or
 cracking.

 3. Ensure the scCO2 stream contains no measurable oxygen, which could oxidize FeS into
 elemental sulfur—a highly corrosive compound. Testing is required to determine
 acceptable oxygen thresholds.

 For existing sweet pipelines repurposed for transport of scCO2 containing H2S:

 1. Confirm that the existing pipeline and ancillary components were all specified and
 constructed in accordance with NACE MR0175/ISO 15156 or would otherwise meet the
 requirements of this standard to prevent SSC and HIC in the new service.

 2. Run scraper/brush pigs to remove as much of any corrosion product layer as possible.

 3. Perform laboratory tests with H2S and all other impurities to determine if SSC and HIC
 could be an issue.

 Although pipeline repurposing offers economic and logistical advantages, successful
conversion to scCO2 service demands rigorous evaluation of prior service conditions, material
compatibility, and potential chemical interactions. Expert input and targeted testing are essential
to ensure long-term integrity and safe operation.

 2.4 Corrosion Study

 Corrosion risk is primarily driven by the presence of free water. In dry systems—where
water remains fully dissolved in scCO2—CS is adequate and has a proven track record in EOR
operations. However, when free water is present, such as in saline formations or incompletely
dehydrated streams, carbonic acid formation leads to corrosion, necessitating the use of corrosion-
resistant alloys (CRAs).

 The EERC and Stress Engineering also prepared a white paper outlining best practices for
selecting CRAs in CCUS projects, with a focus on long-term downhole injection and storage
environments (Warmack and others, 2023). Current well design standards, rooted in oil and gas
operations, are insufficient for carbon capture and storage (CCS) applications requiring equipment
integrity for over 50 years.

 CRA selection must be tailored to each project, considering stream composition, water
chemistry, reservoir conditions, and expected service life. The presence of impurities like oxygen,
as well as temperature and chloride content, can significantly influence alloy performance.
Because of the limited research on CRA behavior in scCO2 with impurities, recommendations are
based on analogous industry experience and are subject to refinement as new data emerge.

 Operators are advised to engage subject matter experts early and conduct laboratory testing
under site-specific conditions, especially in high-risk environments. Material cost, availability, and
lead times also warrant early consideration to ensure timely and appropriate alloy selection.

 The guidance provided in this study is a framework for informed CRA decisions in CCS and
CCUS projects, helping ensure long-term well integrity and regulatory compliance.

 2.5 Metallurgy Considerations

 In 2024, EPA issued guidance on metallurgy for CO2 storage applications, stating that
materials like portland cement and Chromium-13 casing/tubing are generally unsuitable for well
construction under corrosive conditions, particularly when both water and CO2 are present. In
acidic environments, where pH values may drop below 2.5, EPA recommends using more robust
alloys such as Chromium-25 and Hastelloy C-22. These materials offer greater resistance to
corrosion but come with significantly higher capital costs, posing a challenge for CCUS operators.

 EPA’s guidance, however, does not account for the full range of reservoir conditions. Its
recommendations are broad and may not reflect the nuances of specific sites. To address this gap,
the EERC conducted a study focused on geochemistry and corrosion modeling in the near-wellbore
environment of CO2 injection wells. Using data from sandstone and carbonate reservoirs in the
Williston Basin, the study calibrated model inputs to reflect site-specific conditions that fall
outside the scope of EPA guidelines.

 In collaboration with Stress Engineering and OLI Systems, Inc. (OLI Systems), the EERC
developed a risk-based approach to improve corrosion prediction. Stress Engineering provided
laboratory testing services and metallurgical engineering expertise, while OLI Systems provided

 6

process flow modeling software to simulate complex chemistry interactions and predict corrosion.
This included transferring knowledge and simulation tools to help operators assess corrosivity in
CO2 injection wells tailored to local geology. The results were presented at the PCOR Technical
Forum in 2025.

 Advanced reactive transport models were used to evaluate how water vaporization affects
geochemical conditions during injection. Results showed that pH and CO2 solubility are highly
dependent on site-specific factors. In the sandstone reservoir, pH dropped to 3.9 immediately after
injection and gradually rose to 4.4 within a month (Saeed and others, 2025). In contrast, the
carbonate reservoir exhibited rapid buffering due to calcite dissolution, with pH stabilizing at 3.8
almost instantly and remaining constant over time.

 These findings support the development of an integrated, risk-based framework that uses
water mobility, pH, and chloride concentrations to identify corrosion-prone zones. By grounding
corrosion risk assessment in site-specific data, operators and regulators can reduce uncertainty and
make more informed decisions about material selection and well integrity in diverse reservoir
settings.

 2.6 Next-Generation Monitoring Strategies

 Throughout the history of the PCOR Partnership Program, the EERC has prioritized testing
and improving monitoring technologies to enhance the commercial viability of developing CO2
storage projects. The EERC worked with members of the PCOR Partnership at Gevo’s (previously
Red Trail Energy’s) active commercial CO2 injection site near Richardton, North Dakota, to
develop and field-test low-impact, real-time, and potentially cost-effective monitoring
technologies. Gevo’s CCS site commenced injection operations in June 2022 (Barajas-Olalde and
others, 2024).

 Deployment of interferometric synthetic aperture radar (InSAR) and scalable, automated,
sparse seismic array (SASSA) technologies at the Gevo CCS site took place between April 2022
and October 2023. The goal of the tests was to evaluate InSAR and SASSA for CO2 plume
tracking.

 SASSA comprises surface orbital vibrators (SOVs) to generate a seismic signal and a sparse
array of seismic sensors coupled as a passive, low-impact seismic monitoring technology
(Figure 4). Research Institute of Innovative Technology for the Earth (RITE), a Japanese
government-funded organization that collaborates internationally to conduct research and develop
innovative technologies, is supporting research at the field site through a separate, but
complementary effort, installed four SOVs and associated foundations and power.

 The SASSA field test results demonstrated successful visualization of the storage reservoir,
comparable to conventional methods (e.g., two-dimensional [2D] and three-dimensional [3D]
seismic surveys). The technical results are further supported by substantial cost savings (20%–
25% less than conventional 3D seismic acquisition), quicker processing timelines, and the ability
to conduct repeated and frequent surveys without necessitating multiple commercial contracts. The

 This monitoring initiative has attracted interest from both industry and government, with

technologies, the PCOR Partnership has also been presenting its activities and results to regulators.
If successfully demonstrated, the objective is for these technologies to be accepted by regulators
as compliant with monitoring regulations.

 2.7 Determinization of CO2 Plume Stabilization

 The EERC, through the PCOR Partnership Program, developed a technical approach for
quantifying CO2 plume stabilization—an essential requirement for CCS project permitting and site
closure (Burton-Kelly and others, 2025). This methodology addresses a key regulatory challenge:
while CCS policies generally agree that postinjection CO2 plume behavior must not endanger
human health or the environment, they often lack specific guidance on how to define or
demonstrate plume stabilization. This ambiguity can create uncertainty for operators navigating
the permitting process.

 The distribution of mobile-phase CO2 (the CO2 plume) within the storage reservoir changes
during and after injection due to density contrasts between CO2 and the formation fluid, which
drive buoyant migration. Over time, a combination of physical and chemical trapping mechanisms
causes the plume to stabilize, eventually halting its expansion after the injection stops. Plume
stabilization occurs when the geographic footprint of the mobile-phase CO2 plume changes
minimally and predictably over time, remaining within the geographic boundaries identified in the
CO2 storage permit. Additionally, the plume must not threaten USDWs, human health, or the
environment due to lateral migration toward potential leakage pathways. To support this definition,

 9

the EERC developed a method that estimates the rate of change in the plume’s areal extent (dA/dt)
as a function of time. This approach was illustrated through a numerical simulation case study in
North Dakota, where more than 1 million tonnes of CO2 was injected annually for 20 years into
the regionally extensive Broom Creek Formation (sandstone saline aquifer) within the Williston
Basin (Figure 6).

Figure 6. Case study time-series plots showing years since the start of CO2 injection on the x-
axis and a) simulated CO2 plume area, b) change in area (dA), c) rate of area change (dA/dt), and
d) relative rate of area change (%/yr). Injection begins at the beginning of year 0 and ends at the
end of year 20 (solid vertical line). The postinjection time frame goes from year 20 to year 70.
Plume stabilization is identified at year 30 (dashed vertical line) (from Burton-Kelly and others,
2025).

 10

 The case study demonstrated that the rate of change in plume area declines sharply at the
end of injection and stabilizes at low values thereafter, providing a quantifiable metric for
regulatory review. Although percent rate of change (dA/dt/A) may also be useful, it is more
difficult to standardize across projects. In formations without structural or stratigraphic closure,
the simulation showed that the plume may continue to evolve slowly, even after the stabilization
date.

 This technical approach has already been used to support several Class VI storage permit
applications approved by the North Dakota Industrial Commission (NDIC) Department of Mineral
Resources (DMR), which holds primacy authority for regulating such wells in the state. By
predicting both the timing and extent of plume stabilization, the methodology helps operators
select appropriate pore space leases and monitoring areas, plan postinjection site care (PISC), and
estimate financial assurance requirements.

 Monitoring data, such as time-lapse seismic surveys and saturation logging, are
recommended to validate model predictions and demonstrate concordance with the stabilization
approach. Some regulatory frameworks, including California’s CCS Protocol, also encourage risk
assessments to show how the migration risk profile decreases over time. While the final
determination of plume stability rests with the appropriate regulatory authority, the EERC’s
method offers a practical and transparent basis for operators and regulators to align on expectations
early in the permitting process.

 The EERC’s plume stabilization framework provides a scientifically grounded and
regulator-informed pathway for CCS operators to meet permitting and site closure requirements.
It enables more confident planning, monitoring, and financial modeling while offering a consistent
technical foundation for regulatory review and stakeholder engagement.

3.0 NONTECHNICAL ELEMENTS

 Nontechnical issues in CCUS relate to the social, economic, legal, and policy dimensions of
CCUS deployment. They shape public perception, regulatory pathways, and investment decisions.
These issues often require collaboration among policymakers, legal and scientific experts, and
community leaders.

 3.1 Educational Outreach and Technology Transfer

 Awareness of CCUS is essential to advancing these energy technologies and facilitating their
deployment. Fostering this awareness includes public education, stakeholder engagement, and
information sharing among developers, researchers, and partners. A central focus of the PCOR
Partnership Initiative phase was to educate stakeholders about CCUS technologies through
targeted outreach and knowledge transfer.

 The PCOR Partnership assessed regional infrastructure, scale-up challenges, and techno-
economic and socioeconomic impacts to identify both technical and nontechnical barriers to CCUS
deployment. Special attention was given to regulatory issues and potential solutions as well as the

 11

development of business case scenarios to support commercial project planning. These insights
were shared with stakeholders through meetings, presentations, and webinars.

 Public outreach was coordinated to improve societal awareness, particularly in regions
unfamiliar with CCUS. The PCOR Partnership evaluated regional needs and challenges related to
safe and environmentally sound CO2 transportation infrastructure and communicated findings
through accessible outreach materials. Technology transfer efforts also highlighted lessons learned
from DOE’s Regional Carbon Sequestration Partnerships (RCSPs) and other national and
international projects to reduce risk and build confidence in future CCUS initiatives.

 To reach diverse audiences, the PCOR Partnership developed tailored products for both
technical and general stakeholders. Scientific fact sheets, posters, presentations, and reports
supported technical engagement, while documentaries, public presentations, nontechnical posters,
and a regional atlas helped inform and build trust among broader communities.

 3.2 Public Outreach

 Outreach activities include broad regional engagement and focused engagement with target
audiences, including local and regional officials, landowners, and the community. Outreach efforts
required interaction with various stakeholders, where value was provided through a dedicated and
systematic outreach effort. The EERC was requested to host or lead various public outreach
sessions at various forums. Examples of such events include hosting informational/educational
booths regarding CCUS activities for the public at county fairs, energy industry-sponsored
community events, open houses on behalf of CCUS project developers and operators for interested
communities and multiple meetings with state legislators, officials, and regulators.

 As part of the outreach efforts, outreach materials, including fact sheets (general project or
topically focused), posters, infographics, press releases, and talking points, were generated and
disseminated. These materials provided information on the basics of CO2 EOR and CO2 storage
technologies, CO2 transformation, and specific geologic CO2 storage project activities.

 A prime example of an outreach and education product is the PCOR Partnership Atlas (Peck
and others, 2024). The 109-page full-color document (now in its 6th edition) covers CCUS topics
such as CO2 capture, transport, economics and business considerations, site characterization, EOR,
geologic modeling, and monitoring in a manner easily consumed by the public. Nearly 1000 PCOR
Atlases (6th Edition) were distributed to PCOR Partnership members and stakeholders at annual
meetings and other partner and public engagements.

 3.4 Project and Permit Development

 Through the PCOR Partnership, the EERC examined the evolving regulatory landscape for
CCS projects in the United States and Canada, focusing on permitting frameworks under EPA,
states granted Class VI primacy, and Canadian and provincial governments.

 14

 As CCS projects move beyond feasibility assessments toward commercial deployment, the
permitting process has emerged as a critical barrier in the United States. Developers face long,
uncertain timelines that often conflict with commercial project schedules. Since EPA introduced
Class VI regulations for CO2 injection wells, regulatory certainty has improved—particularly in
states like North Dakota and Wyoming, which have received primacy and adopted the Interstate
Oil and Gas Compact Commission’s (IOGCC’s) resource management framework.

 North Dakota’s regulatory model, which integrates environmental protection with resource
stewardship and long-term liability management, has enabled a streamlined permitting process. In
2022, two commercial storage projects were permitted in just 8 months. Wyoming has
implemented a similar approach, with three permitted projects and multiple Class VI applications
currently under review.

 The comprehensive review of federal, state, and provincial regulatory developments
highlighted lessons learned from early state-led permitting efforts and underscored the importance
of adopting a resource management framework. State-led regulatory frameworks, supported by
Class VI primacy and informed by site-specific geologic and operational data, are essential to
reducing permitting delays and enabling the commercial scale-up of CCS. As more states pursue
this model, the path to widespread CCS deployment across the nation becomes increasingly viable.

 A well-defined and predictable permitting process is essential for the commercial
deployment of CCUS, beginning with the up-front investment needed to characterize and permit
a geologic CO2 storage site. In the United States, the time and cost associated with permitting
Class VI UIC wells have long been viewed as barriers to CCUS development. However, this
challenge has become more manageable and better understood after successfully permitting the
first commercial CO2 storage projects in North Dakota under the state’s Class VI primacy program.
These landmark permits—enabled by a clear regulatory framework and favorable geology—have
catalyzed the advancement of a diverse portfolio of commercial projects. These include CO2
capture from coal-fired power plants, ethanol facilities, and natural gas-processing operations. The
insights gained from these projects are already informing future CCUS development across the
region.

 The EERC generated key lessons learned during the development and permitting of North
Dakota’s first wave of geologic CO2 storage projects (Livers-Douglas and others, 2023). These
insights have led to practical recommendations for site characterization, modeling and simulation,
permit preparation, regulatory engagement, and community outreach. Together, they formed a
project development guide to facilitate CCUS deployment in North Dakota and serve as a model
for other regions.

 Early-stage projects in North Dakota required 2D or 3D seismic data and drilling
stratigraphic test wells to characterize potential storage sites. These efforts were critical to
demonstrating reservoir suitability for safe, long-term CO2 storage. Since 2017, 13 appraisal wells
have been drilled in the state, yielding valuable data through coring, testing, and logging. Lessons
from seismic survey design, acquisition timing, and data processing have further refined best
practices for site characterization.

 15

 Through its work supporting Class VI permit development and NDIC’s review and public
hearing process, the EERC has developed several strategic recommendations. These
recommendations are summarized below:

 • Modeling and Simulation: Clearly document inputs, assumptions, and results; apply
 new methods for plume stabilization and risk-based AOR; and incorporate geochemical
 modeling to define the storage facility area.

 • Permit Template: Use a standardized format that aligns with regulatory requirements
 and is accessible to both regulators and stakeholders.

 • Regulatory Communication: Maintain regular, transparent dialogue with regulators to
 streamline the review process.

 • Public Outreach: Engage stakeholders early to share project updates, build trust, and
 foster community support.

 These lessons have been integrated into the EERC’s adaptive management approach (AMA)
(Figure 7), resulting in a generalized project timeline that accounts for permitting milestones
(Figure 8). The timeline includes all major phases: site screening, feasibility assessment, project
design and permit application, regulatory review, investment and construction, and operations.
While tailored to North Dakota’s Class VI process, this development timeline applies to geologic
CO2 storage projects nationwide.

 3.5 Regulatory Engagement

 The EERC hosts an annual Regulatory Roundup, inviting interested state regulators from
the PCOR Partnership region to discuss technical and nontechnical topics and promote state UIC
primacy. The Regulatory Roundup is a forum for sharing and coordinating regulatory strategies to
accelerate the commercial deployment of CCUS and facilitate the development of prudent
regulatory frameworks. This annual forum provides state and provincial regulators and the EERC
with the opportunity to discuss CCUS-related issues and roadblocks with the intent of finding
solutions for regulatory bodies that promote the advancement of commercial industries. In recent
years, the regulatory meeting has expanded beyond the traditional boundaries of the PCOR
Partnership region and has hosted states from around the United States.

 Meeting attendees represent state oil and gas regulatory agencies as well as environmental
protection agencies, all of which are responsible for oversight of UIC Class II and Class VI
programs. Policy, technical, and nontechnical challenges are deliberated as part of the facilitated
discussion topics. Discussion topics include CO2 EOR, pore space unitization, long-term liability,
UIC program primacy, permitting processes, and the implementation of regulatory programs.
Class VI primacy remains a primary focus, with several states currently advancing through various
stages of obtaining regulatory authority from EPA. Notably, North Dakota, Wyoming, Louisiana,
and West Virginia—four states that have achieved Class VI primacy—have been consistent
participants in these meetings. Insights shared by these states regarding their established regulatory
frameworks have proven beneficial to jurisdictions in the process of developing their own
regulatory programs. Their experiences, particularly in areas such as permitting, pore space

 17

unitization, inspection, reporting, and permit modifications, have provided considerable value to
regulatory attendees.

 Often, the facilitated discussions have provided the incentive for further research activities
directly or indirectly related to those specific topics. As research and solutions are developed,
follow-up webinars disseminate these results as the EERC continues to engage with state and
provincial regulators throughout the year. The PCOR Partnership Regulatory Roundup and
resulting engagement continue to be well-received and valued by the state and provincial
regulators who attend.

 The EERC has played an active role in shaping CCUS policy across the United States,
serving as both host and expert witness in key legislative and regulatory forums. In 2021, EERC
CEO Charlie Gorecki testified before the Nebraska Legislature, contributing to the passage of the
state’s first carbon storage law. The following year, Kevin Connors, PCOR Partnership Program
Manager, provided expert testimony to the Minnesota Legislature in support of recognizing CCUS
as a viable “clean energy” solution.

 In March 2023, Connors testified before the Alaska Legislature. One year later, that
legislation was successfully enacted. To support Alaska’s efforts, the EERC, through the PCOR
Partnership, hosted a December 2023 meeting in North Dakota with UAF staff, Alaska legislators,
agency leaders, and regulators. The group explored North Dakota’s legal and regulatory
framework for CCUS in preparation for Alaska’s 2024 legislative session.

 In early 2024, Connors presented to South Dakota legislators ahead of the state’s decision
to prohibit the use of eminent domain for CO2 pipelines. That same month, he testified before the
U.S. Senate Committee on Environment and Public Works, highlighting:

 • The PCOR Partnership’s collaborative model between industry and government.
 • Key challenges facing commercial CCUS deployment.
 • Issues related to Class VI well primacy and permitting.
 • The role of aquifer exemptions in enabling storage projects.

5.0 NEXT STEPS

 Looking ahead, the EERC, through a continuation of the PCOR Partnership, will strengthen
the technical and strategic foundation for regional coordination, particularly in aligning CO2
supply with EOR demand. By connecting industries capable of capturing CO2—such as ethanol
production, coal-fired power generation, iron ore processing, and natural gas processing—with the
petroleum sector, the EERC has positioned CCUS as a shared goal among traditionally
independent industries. As such, the PCOR Partnership Program will continue to play a central
role in advancing CCUS by fostering collaboration between industry and government. This joint
effort will address emerging challenges related to the build-out of critical infrastructure needed to
ensure a reliable CO2 supply across the northern Great Plains, Rocky Mountain region, and
beyond.

 A key focus moving forward will be supporting business cases for CO2 EOR, which offers
a transformative opportunity to significantly increase regional oil production. The PCOR
Partnership Program will promote this endeavor by facilitating cross-sector collaboration,
addressing technical and logistical challenges, and developing a regional carbon resource plan to
strategically align CO2 supply and demand.

 Coordinating CO2 capture, transport, and use for EOR requires a regional approach that
integrates infrastructure planning, regulatory engagement, and stakeholder outreach. The EERC,
through the PCOR Partnership, will continue to engage with policymakers and regulatory agencies
to support the development of practical legal frameworks that promote regulatory certainty. This
includes providing scientific and technical resources to inform planning and permitting decisions
and long-term liability management.

 Public outreach remains critical to advance EOR projects from conceptual design to full-
scale implementation. As CO2 pipeline networks expand, public concerns about safety and eminent
domain have emerged during routing and permitting. The EERC will continue delivering science-
based information to stakeholders to build trust and support informed decision-making. The EERC
also recognizes the importance of dedicated CO2 storage as a buffer within the capture–transport–
storage system (Figure 10). Although EOR is the primary destination for captured CO2, deep saline
storage provides essential flexibility to maintain continuous capture operations and system
efficiency.

 21

Figure 10. Illustration of the interdependence of geologic storage of CO2 and CO2 EOR. Until
the infrastructure is fully installed and sufficient oilfield demand ramps up, surplus CO2 captured
from a large-scale source like a coal-fired power plant will need to be secured in buffer storage
options. As the demand grows and infrastructure is deployed, more captured CO2 can be routed
for EOR use.

 Looking ahead, the EERC and the PCOR Partnership remain committed to identifying and
addressing regional challenges across the full CCUS value chain. Its future efforts will focus on
carbon resource planning and forecasting to connect key industrial sectors and enable the next
generation of low-carbon energy production.
