1 Executive Summary
This report contains the results of the International District Energy Association’s work to
analyze, validate, and verify performance data of existing district energy systems and identify
industy best practices for the purpose of improving system reliability, resiliency, and efficiency,
and to accellerate decarbonization. In addition to a technical evaluation of the surveyed systems
and identification of a series of technical performance metrics, the report illustrates the
accompanying operations and financial best practices employed by surveyed systems to fully
serve their customer base. Additionally, the third chapter of the report describes the current
landscape of cybersecurity threats and counteracting measures, and recommends a series of
steps for effectively guarding highly networked district energy systems against cybersecurity
attacks.

2 Introduction
In 2020, the International District Energy Association partnered with the Department of Energy
Industrial Efficiency & Decarbonization Office, part of the Energy Efficiency and Renewable
Energy Office, to perform a comprehensive study of US district energy systems with the
intention of identifying and disseminating industry best practices related to efficiency,
resiliency, reliability, and decarbonization.

2.1 Project Objectives
This project aims to analyze, validate, and verify actual performance data of existing district
energy systems and identify industry best practices for:

 1. Incorporating efficient CHP systems and community-based microgrids
 2. Integration of renewable, intermittent energy sources and thermal energy storage (TES)
 3. Enhanced cybersecurity of highly networked DE systems

2.2 Methods
The project team, in consultation with industry experts, designed a series of extensive
questionnaires and forms to collect quantitative performance and profile data and best
practices around cyber security, engineering, operations, and financing. Data was collected
from 148 systems, 45 of which agreed to be interviewed for the purpose of creating more
comprehensive case studies and isolating best practices surrounding not only efficiency,
reliability, resiliency, and decarbonization, along with the management and finance decision-
making processes that support engineering and operational initiatives.

While many systems were generous with their data and experience, some systems were
reluctant to share sensitive performance and consumption data. The industry is also
experiencing a widespread staffing crisis, delaying IDEA’s communication with certain systems
due to staff availability.

Additionally, over the course of the interview series, system personnel repeatedly emphasized
the importance of non-technical practices, such as personnel training programs, multi-
stakeholder master planning, and service continuity during construction that had, in their
telling, equally significant impacts on the efficiency, resiliency, and reliability of their systems.
This report, and all other project deliverables, aims to describe not only the engineering best
practices for increasing system efficiency and decarbonization but also the financial and
operational practices that make engineering best practices more effective.

2.4 District Energy
The fundamental idea of district energy is simple but powerful: connect multiple heating and
cooling energy users (buildings) through an underground piping network to environmentally
responsible energy sources (central plants), such as combined heat and power (CHP), industrial
waste heat and renewable energy sources such as biomass, geothermal and natural sources of
heating and cooling. (See Figure 1) District energy systems produce and pipe steam, hot water
or chilled water underground through a dedicated piping network to heat or cool buildings in a
given area. This reduces energy costs and greenhouse gas emissions while freeing up valuable
space in customer buildings by centralizing production equipment and, through economies of
scale and equipment management, optimizes the use of fuels, power and resources. District
energy (primarily district heating currently) delivers about 3.5% of the total final energy demand
in the industrial, residential, public, and commercial sectors. About 6.5% of commercial
buildings in the U.S. are heated with district heating.

3 Performance Verification and Validation

Drawing on performance and profile date collected from over 40 district energy providers
across the US, this paper endeavors to identify and validate metrics for benchmarking district
energy system performance across multiple services, technologies, and generations. ‘

Though the project team originally set out to collect best practices from a more diverse
collection of US district energy systems, the final sample is heavily weighted towards higher
education: of the 45 participating systems, 32 of them are located on college or university
campuses. In some cases, the collected best practices are agnostic to sector, but campus-based
systems have several unique features that may make some of the best practices inaccessible to
utility systems, including but not limited to:

 - Access to, and in some cases direct control over, connected buildings
 - A wider range of funding sources, including non-profit funding, state funding, and
 institutional endowments
 - Strategic and master planning practices that encompass both the district energy
 provider

In 2024, the pressure to eliminate fossil fuel combustion for building heat and power
generation is being felt by many of the sites surveyed. Electric energy rates are in flux in many
areas of the country with commitments to lower CO₂ by power producers, grid operators and
regulated utilities becoming reality. Rate proposals now often involve substantial time of day

 11

structures that make business as usual consumption behavior quite expensive. Similarly,
implementation of cost of carbon policy, common throughout Europe and other developed
nations, is on district energy operators’ radar, particularly as it relates to near term renewal or
replacement of existing boilers, turbines and steam distribution. Although federal, state and
local policy mandates to address carbon emissions are currently rare in municipal and
healthcare systems, phased reduction directives are more common in higher education.

A common concern within the interviews is that mandates are imminent, but funding for
renewal is not. Engineering alternatives that address this immense challenge are emerging from
the cohort of 40 sites within this study, however. Among these are concepts that adapt existing
district systems to alternative “green” fuels such as hydrogen and biofuel, displace combustion
with heat produced directly through electric resistance, or move it from an ambient source
through electric-driven industrial heat pumps. Changes in production may be coupled with
transition of the distribution systems from high temperature steam to lower temperature hot
water, which enables exploitation of additional sources of surplus heat and/or coupling with
heat rejection from chilled water production or daily diurnal thermal storage or even
geologically coupled seasonal storage.

Prudent financial performance is a core principle within all the District Energy operations
surveyed. Technical solutions must stand an economic test to assure the ongoing value of the
thermal and electric product remains competitive with alternative stand-alone building system
approaches. Unsubsidized municipal systems are particularly sensitive to these comparative
economics, in brownfield cases where disruption and expense involved in replacement of
existing underground distribution infrastructure is extremely high. Decarbonizing existing
district thermal systems serving dense urban core areas without upsetting cost-of-service
models or diminishing investor return is a particular challenge within the industry. Innovation
and technological development such as higher temperature heat pumps are emerging and
show promise as a strategy for decarbonization.

The engineering and economic performance of these major system improvements will be
essential to their long-term viability. Understanding the ways in which their performance
metrics differ from past historical metrics is as important to planning and implementation of
district scale decarbonization initiatives.

District Energy performance characteristics considered in this study go beyond the sum of
thermodynamic efficiencies of individual components. Safety, reliability, and stable cost
management are as important to operation as the energy efficiency, Coefficient of Performance
or Heat Rate of individual components. The survey revealed few “standard” designs or
operating configurations. Systems are generally “bespoke”, unique in design and operation to
meet the aggregated energy demand profile of their specific district, subject to the specific local
climate, public policy, energy cost and resource market. Operating over decade-long time scales,
system performance is periodically adapted to accommodate changes in load, additional
regulation, increasing fuel costs, and preparation for natural disaster. Safety, reliability and
stable cost-effective operation are cited as primary performance objectives. Often managed by a

 12

motivated staff with specialized energy expertise, investment in the evolution of plant systems
typically considers alternatives through an often -rigorous process of life cycle cost evaluation.
This planned approach to change has proven to be practical, financially prudent, and result in
minimally disruptive transition. This ability to efficiently adapt, reconfigure, repurpose and
integrate new technology makes District Energy an effective means to collectively decarbonize
the heating, cooling and power demands of significant numbers of buildings, campuses and
cities.

3.2 Profile of Sampled Systems
Over the course of the project, IDEA was able to secure data from 148 US district energy
systems, 45 of which agreed to be interviewed for case and best practice development. Note, in
the maps below, the 45 systems which participated more significantly are marked with a
triangle. Of the participating systems, 31 served college or university campuses, 6 served
healthcare campuses, one served an airport, one served a government campus, and 6 served
downtown neighborhoods.

Though many of the systems are clustered in the eastern US, the project team was able to
secure participation from multiple systems in each Independent System Operator (ISO) and
ASHRAE climate zones.

Figure 1: 45 Participating systems

Including a diverse array of ownership models in the study (as reflected in Figure 1) was critical
to ensuring that the findings are broadly applicable, robust, and representative of the full U.S.
district energy landscape. The participating systems span multiple sectors—including

universities, utilities, healthcare campuses, and municipal or downtown systems—each of which
operates under different financial structures, regulatory environments, and stakeholder
priorities. This diversity captures how ownership influences access to funding, planning
processes, and operational decision-making; for example, campus systems often benefit from
institutional funding and greater control over connected buildings, while municipal and utility
systems must adhere more closely to cost-of-service constraints and regulatory requirements.

Figure 2: System distribution across ASHRAE climate zones

Including systems across a wide range of climate zones (as shown in Figure 2) was essential to capturing
the full variability in how district energy systems perform, operate, and evolve under different
environmental conditions. Climate has a direct influence on heating and cooling demand profiles,
seasonal load variations, and the balance between thermal services, meaning that systems in colder
regions may prioritize heating and steam distribution, while those in warmer climates emphasize cooling
capacity and peak electricity management. By incorporating participants from multiple ASHRAE climate
zones, the study ensures that performance data and best practices reflect these fundamentally different
operating conditions rather than being biased toward a single regional pattern.

This geographic diversity also enables meaningful comparison of technologies and strategies—such as
combined heat and power, thermal energy storage, and electrification—under different climatic
stresses, helping to identify which approaches are most effective in specific contexts. It captures how
factors like extreme weather, regional energy markets, and local resource availability drive system
design, resiliency planning, and decarbonization pathways.

 15

Figure 3: System distribution across Independent System Operator (ISO) territories

Variation in electricity markets, grid conditions, and regulatory frameworks directly influence district
energy system performance and strategy. Different ISO regions operate under distinct market
structures, pricing mechanisms, and grid reliability conditions, all of which shape how district energy
systems procure electricity, dispatch on-site generation, and manage peak demand. By incorporating
systems across multiple ISO territories, the study ensures that its findings reflect these regional
differences rather than being limited to a single market context.

This diversity is especially important for evaluating technologies and operational practices such as
combined heat and power (CHP), thermal energy storage, and demand response, which are highly
sensitive to local electricity pricing, grid constraints, and incentives.

The systems surveyed in this study exhibit substantial technical diversity in both scale and service
offerings, as reflected in Tables 1 and 2 and Figures 5, 6, and 7 (see Appendix 1 ). Individual system
capacities vary widely: among those reporting, average chilled water capacity is about 29,602 tons with
a median of 22,105 tons, while hot water systems average 162.8 MMBtu/hr (median 100.05 MMBtu/hr)
and steam systems average 590,099 PPH (median 445,000 PPH). CHP installations similarly range in size,
with an average capacity of 23.7 MW and a median of 16 MW among reporting systems. In addition to
variation in capacity, the prevalence of different technologies varies across the sample: 42 systems
provide district cooling, 38 provide steam, 28 include CHP, and 13 provide hot water services, illustrating
differing configurations and levels of technological integration.

Taken together, these figures demonstrate that district energy systems span a broad spectrum—from
smaller, single-service installations to highly integrated, multi-service networks with significant
generation and storage capabilities. This wide technical range underscores that systems are highly
customized to local demand, infrastructure, and resource conditions, reinforcing the importance of
including diverse system types to support meaningful benchmarking and identification of best practices
across the industry.

3.3 Technical Characteristics of Surveyed Systems
Comparison of data collected from 40 district energy systems provides insight into similarities
and diversity among successful operations. While the operational mission of municipal,
healthcare and higher education systems may differ somewhat, primary component and
engineering design of the physical infrastructure is similar. The centralized systems surveyed
share the common purpose of procuring fuel and electric power, converting these energy flows
into heating, cooling, and power streams, storing energy to decouple delivery from time of
production in order to shift from peak demand periods, then distributing thermal and electric
energy to buildings for use in environmental and process control. Aggregation of multiple
building loads, each with independent daily and seasonal use profiles, provides opportunities to
monitor and manage the flow of energy, making optimization schemes such as combined heat
and power, thermal energy storage, heat recovery heat pumps and chillers cost effective
opportunities. In several surveyed sites, this variety of alternative methods to convert, store and
distribute energy enables “district microgrid” operation, either independently (off grid) or in
sync (interconnected) with the electric utility grid. Two-way coordination with the local or
regional electricity grid provides advantages from reliability, net carbon emissions and cost
perspectives.

Adaptability to change is a common characteristic among sites surveyed. Originally constructed
to provide reliable, safe, and cost-effective service using a primary fuel source, district systems
often evolve over time, responding to external conditions. System evolution has been driven by
external factors such as regulatory requirements, shifts in relative costs of fuels and electric
power, building load growth and improvement in technologies and conversion efficiency.
District energy systems typically employ well-considered long term planning involving
evaluation of engineering and economic impacts of incremental system improvements that
provide positive return on investment over decades-long life cycles. Many of the systems
surveyed have taken on this sort of evaluation and experienced a continuum of improvements
and evolution since the original commissioning.

Some examples of adaptability include the typical district response to externally mandated
chiller refrigerant phase outs (Montreal Protocol), boiler Maximum Achievable Combustion
Technologies (MACT), and Qualifying Facility (QF) Status by the Federal Energy Regulatory
Commission (FERC) standards for cogeneration efficiency. In many instances, District Energy
systems not only invested in equipment renewal in response to regulatory requirements, but
also to improve process efficiency, integrate more sophisticated controls, and right-size
equipment trains.

Incorporation of combined heat and power (CHP) into many of the heating districts surveyed is
a second example of adaptability. Originally designed to distribute steam heat generated in
solid or liquid fired boilers, multiple sites in our survey began, over the past 30 years,
integrating gas turbines to simultaneously generate electricity and steam as a Combined Heat
and Power process. These were often significant capital investments, proposed not only as a

 17

cost effective means to leverage changing fuel and electric market conditions, but to improve
site electricity resilience while improving first-law thermodynamic efficiency. In many cases the
value of CHP in substantially lowering the combination of CO2 emissions generated to heat
buildings with the CO2 emitted in grid electric power generation proved to be a compelling
argument. Investment in CHP was often a system’s first large step in reducing its Greenhouse
Gas footprint.

A comparative Sankey diagram like that shown below often accompanied proposals for these
Combined Heat and Power projects. Overall efficiency of the “Standard Power Plant” case 40%
improves to (80%) in the “District Energy/Combined Heat and Power Plant case.
Thermodynamics and carbon emissions aside, a primary driver for these projects was the long-
term promise of return on investment. In simplest terms, efficiency improvements yielded
projected energy cost savings high enough to offset the debt service plus additional operation
and maintenance. By 2023, original debt of many of these 1990 and earlier district energy CHP
projects has been retired and retrospective tabulation of actual annual cost savings over the
term largely validate the original investment-in-performance cost models.

Similar improvements in efficiency and net carbon emissions with similar ROI would have been
difficult to achieve at an individual building by building level over this time. Comparative ease in
fitting a single CHP set into a district plant compared to attempting multiple building level
retrofits makes it far more practical. Aggregation of variable load profiles from multiple
independent buildings into a composite load provides the utilization needed to operate CHP
systems at near optimal performance, as well as increasing the output to a point where
economies of scale enable the cost-to-savings ratio to meet institutional investment hurdles.

A final example of the adaptability of district systems to singular improvements that benefit an
entire stock of individual buildings is the addition of chilled water thermal energy storage.
Electric rate structures across the country have been shifting from primarily consumption to a
combination of fixed cost, consumption, and kW demand at time of use. Centralized production
of chilled water through electric powered refrigeration machines is the most common means of
cooling most district systems. A simple, common method to operate these machines during the
lowest electric cost periods is to charge a thermal battery (of water or ice) for use later, under
periods of high-cost electric demand. Above ground insulated water tanks on the order of 1 to
10 million gallons are operated among the surveyed sites for this purpose. Aggregation of
independent building loads and scale of the district make this a practical solution that exceeds
most building-by-building tactics. Like CHP, chilled water thermal storage systems can be added
to existing infrastructure and easily integrated into operating sequences as the external market
conditions for electricity have evolved. Recent large-scale shifts in utility generation mix toward
variable renewable energy resources exacerbates the causes of variability in value of electric
demand, making cost effective thermal energy storage at district scale even more attractive.

3.4.1 Single line characterization diagrams
Comprehensive process flow diagrams depicting energy and mass transfer within a district
energy system become complex as the accuracy and level of fidelity to detail increases.
Multiple nested displays or large-scale engineering sheets are often necessary to identify all the
equipment and all the plausible flow paths in a legible format. For this high-level comparative
analysis between systems, a simpler graphic method was needed. Single line diagrams were
distilled from each site based on the information provided in the IDEA database, data survey

Similarly, energy conversion in most plants is accomplished through multiple pieces of
manifolded equipment operating in parallel. Chiller “fleets” of ten (10) or more machines are
common, as are boiler arrays of three (3) or more. Characteristics of each of these individual
components often vary, with differing ages, capacities, performance ranges, etc. Plant
configuration is often a result of the legacy of the system. This history and nuance is not shown
in the single line diagram, in which simple blocks represent annual production and conversion
elements. Actual piping networks are much more complex, manifolded with various headers
that include tees and branches to enable multiple paths, and valved at differing pressures and
temperatures. No attempt to show these crucial details is made in the single line diagram,
whose purpose is to represent overall energy inputs, conversion, distribution and transfer.

Although greatly simplified, the diagrams from the 40 sites reflect much diversity in the
engineering solutions to management of district energy flow. These single line diagrams, which
can be the basis of more quantitative energy flow diagrams, provide some insight into district
energy efficiency and engineering performance characteristics.

Examination of system typology within surveyed sites reveals many similar components
configured in a variety of manners. Although there are a few common types, no two systems
were built to identical design, leading to the general observation that district energy systems
tend to be “bespoke”. They are typically individually tailored (often over time) to respond to
particular and unique operating environments. Among primary factors in design evolution of
district energy systems are the scale of the system, local climate and the aggregated building
energy characteristics, along with the availability and cost of energy supply streams. In some
cases, critical needs to assure reliable and resilient service during extreme weather or grid
interruption events. was reported as a more important operating objective than utility cost
control. With expected useful life of equipment and distribution infrastructure often more than
50 years, the age and legacy of systems, along with the magnitude of sunk investment, informs
present configuration and performance.

limited by uncertainties related to the volume availability of fuels.

3.5 Common Elements Among 40 Sites
3.5.1 Fuel and Electricity Procurement
Regional diversity in source energy availability and cost is behind much of the variation in
system type observed across the survey. In general, at the time of this study, natural gas prices
remain relatively low compared to electricity prices, making ongoing operation of combined
heat and power systems economically attractive. Changes are occurring across the survey as
increasing cost differentials between grid purchased on-and-off peak periods, and more
granular management of plant carbon emissions are leading to alternative dispatch schemes.
Coal remains a fuel in several instances but is under planned phase-out in all but a few surveyed
sites. Environmental concerns and institutional carbon reduction commitments were cited as
reasons to move away from coal. Current pricing of other combustion fuels including fuel oil

 23

and woody biomass is sufficiently low to allow sustained operation in certain locales, but, in
general, natural gas for generation of heat and electric power predominates across the sites.

Historical instability and occasional extreme volatility in natural gas pricing was noted as an
operational challenge, often addressed through long term hedging purchase strategies. Carbon
pricing was a topic raised in many of the interviews. Although not currently a direct factor in
operation of all but two institutions, future projection of additional “cost of carbon” will factor
into district system planning and procurement for renewal of large equipment and systems. In
the absence of a known “Carbon Tax” there is some financial risk to further investing in gas
fired boilers, turbines or major steam distribution renewal. In some cases, this risk leads to a
“wait and see” philosophy. Recently amended or newly created utility system development
masterplans for many of the sites often include electrification of heat, and transition to low
temperature heating water distribution.

Costs for grid purchased electric supply are observed to be increasing for most of the systems
surveyed. These increases are often attributed to shifting grid generation mix driven by
generation portfolio mandates. Variability in renewable generation, offset between solar
generation and grid loads (“duck curve”), and constraints on existing electrical transmission
make the value of power highly sensitive to its time of use. The effect of this, noted in several
interviews, is that regulated electric tariffs and real time pricing increasingly encourages
alternative strategies to manage electric demand during peak periods. Generator dispatch, on-
site energy storage, and more sophisticated demand management are some of the methods
available to district energy operators. Although some surveyed systems can interact with the
utility as an independent microgrid, few sites report frequent two directional interaction with
real time signals to dispatch district assets in support of grid operation.

3.5.2 CHP and Heat Recovery
Within the surveyed sites, 28 include Combined Heat and Power systems. Components in these
district plants are typically sized to satisfy baseload district heat demands with supplemental
fuel fired boilers and metered utility grid service supplementing the CHP capacity. Survey
results indicate high utilization rates are achievable for district CHP equipment.

 The possibility of exporting excess CHP power to the grid under peak (summer) demand
conditions was reported (primarily within the ERCOT grid) but is not a universal opportunity. In
most cases, export of district power is not currently allowed or, if allowed, is routinely priced at
wholesale prices that are only marginally advantageous to weather driven variations, measured
as the ratio of district heating to cooling load at a site over the year, may drive seasonal
deployment of secondary heat recovery equipment such as steam driven chillers or condensing
steam turbine generators. Although energy conversion efficiency of these devices may be
relatively poor compared to the prime movers, additional capital, maintenance and operation
expenses associated with operating additional thermal conversion components is often justified
by additional savings in the supplemental electric power purchased to operate cooling

 24

equipment. Operating single effect absorption chillers works out to be a marginally better
solution than bypassing turbine exhaust around the HRSG.

3.5.3 Boilers
Gas-fired package boilers comprise the largest percentage of district plant boilers within the
survey cohort. Most are water tube design and often dual fuel fired by natural gas with back up
fuel oil or propane. Other common characteristics within the cohort include stack gas
economizers and flue gas recirculation. Outlet pressures vary depending on downstream
configuration, with 600 psig serving plant headers with steam turbine generator sets down to
100 psig feeding steam heat distribution systems. As a result of the operating pressures, boiler
code and risk management requirements, district boiler plants are typically manned on a
continuous 24/7 basis. Gas-to-steam efficiencies (including economizer) across the range of
boilers are reported in the 80-87% range. Interestingly, a few condensing economizers, capable
of improving fuel-in to heat-out efficiency into the 90%+ range, were reported. This likely has to
do with limitations to the proximity of and/or cost to reach heat loads of sufficiently low
temperature to extract latent heat of moisture in the exhaust stream.
Other heat generators found in the study include solid fuel units, fired with coal or biomass.
These boilers are often custom designed for the specific purpose, fuel source and physical
constraints of a site. Design life of field-erected solid fuel units tends to be long (+50 years).
Uncertainty in availability and long-term cost adds complexity to investment decision making in
these technologies.

3.6 Transition to 5th Generation Systems
Other district heat sources employed in the surveyed systems include gas-to-heating water,
electric powered boilers with either steam and heating water output, and electric heat pumps.
Relatively recent mandates to “decarbonize” district heating systems challenge past design and
operating philosophies in most sites. While it is certainly possible to fire boilers and/or to
capture heat recovered from internal combustion engines and gas turbines fired on carbon-free
fuels such as renewable natural gas or hydrogen, these fuels are not generally currently
available at economically competitive prices. Small modular nuclear power units at a district
energy scale represent another possible route to carbon free heat and power. One of the
surveyed sites has demonstrated operation of its combustion turbine with renewable liquid
biofuel, and another is completing an engineering and economic study of the potential to
electrolyze water into hydrogen fuel on site for use in a district CHP gas turbine. Projects to
decarbonize district heat through transition to electrification are becoming more common,
however. Application of heat recovery heat pumps at heating COP of 3.0 or better, low
temperature heating water, and diurnal and seasonal energy storage have been completed or
are underway in 4 of the 40 sites, under construction in two more and under consideration in at

 25

least another ten. The operating systems are successful and prove technical and economic
merits.

Another practical method of district decarbonization is through direct electric conversion at a
COP of 1.0. These can make financial sense, under current electric rate structures, if coupled
with thermal storage in areas with very low off-peak rates. Subsets in this technology include
electrode, electric resistance heating water and steam boilers. Electric resistance coils heating a
high temperature ceramic mass is another viable means to shift steam production away from
electric consumption to generate heat. Several large municipal systems are evaluating
electrified steam generation as a means to address carbon emissions. Coupled with electric or
thermal energy storage these would replace fuel fired generation while continuing to utilize the
existing distribution network. Clearly this solution requires substantially larger plant electric
service, but this, in many cases, would be far more practical and less costly as a near to mid-
term solution than improving the downtown electric grid capacity to meet the demand of
stand-alone building electrification.

In at least one case, consideration is being given to converting the existing underground chilled
water network to an “ambient loop”, to serve a new set of building heat pumps, enabling
elimination of steam, without the high cost of running a new underground piping grid. This
hybrid solution is an example of the ability of district energy to adapt to new circumstances.

Consideration and long-term planning for the next generation of heating production and
distribution has become a common characteristic among the surveyed sites. Operation of these
systems involves different operating philosophies and sets of key performance indicators than
combustion-based systems.

3.6.1 Heat Pumps
Senior representatives from US refrigeration equipment manufacturers (York and Trane) were
interviewed on the topic of availability and performance of their industrial heat pump offerings.
While both have established and well characterized lines of water-to-water heat pump
products suited for use in district energy application and scale, both are anticipating transition
from fuel fired combustion to electric driven heat pumps as a market opportunity. These
manufacturers (as well as other global players) are actively engaged in research and
development (possibly near term) roll out of machines capable of high leaving temperatures,
larger turn down, new refrigerants and lower electrical input. The aggregated building heating
to cooling balance within a district is favorable, in many cases, to operation of an “industrial
scale” “heat recovery heat pump” which generates, through electric vapor compression, chilled
water for cooling on its low side and heating water on the high side. This ability to generate two
useful energy products makes for high efficiency. Because the heat of compression for cooling
becomes a district heat source, evaporative cooling towers are not necessary, adding water
conservation to the benefits of conversion. District heating and cooling loads are not
necessarily simultaneous however, daily, and seasonal thermal energy storage is often needed
to improve annual utilization. Other ambient energy sources actively coupled with heat pumps

 26

include municipal wastewater at two of the sites and consideration of adjacent water bodies in
at least one other. The current generation of industrial heat pumps being applied to sites in this
survey operate in a140°F to 170°F leaving heating water range, when making 42°F leaving
chilled water. Units designed for operation with common refrigerants under a high enough lift
to generate both chilled water and steam (limited to roughly 30 psig, 300°F) are on the market
and in operation in Europe. These are likely to become a part of a decarbonization solution in
district energy systems where transition to low temperature heating water is impractical, or
higher pressure and temperature is not necessary. Promising heat pump technologies involving
CO2 as a super critical refrigerant are under development. Applied to large district heating
situations, these could offer very high performance but are not likely to be commercially
available in the near term.

3.6.2 Deep Geothermal
Although clearly not generally characteristic of the 40 sites surveyed, one of the systems is
actively researching technical viability of drawing geothermal heat of sufficient temperature
from extremely deep wells. It is thought to be available at high enough temperature and
volumetric flow to exchange directly into district building systems. Drilling methods are derived
from experience in the gas and oil industry with hydraulic enhancement-enabled access to
sufficiently hot geological formations 8,000 to 12,000 feet below surface. Without need of
additional power to drive a compressor heat pump, the overall coefficient of performance is
only a function of hydronic pumping. This concept thus offers very high performance compared
to most other electric heat schemes. Should it turn out to be practical financially, centralized
deep wells feeding heating water distribution to aggregated groups of buildings could be a
solution that sets carbon free district heating operation at a significant advantage to other
forms of building heat. There is much interest in lessons that will be learned from this district
scale project.

3.6.3 Water Chillers
Cooling is an essential district service element in many of the sites surveyed. Electric vapor
compression refrigeration with evaporatively cooled condensing is the primary means of
producing chilled water, followed by single effect absorption chilling, then steam turbine driven
and double effect absorption. Heat driven absorption and turbine chillers were found as a
seasonal heat recovery component in systems with combined heat and power, often located in
warmer climates with little summertime heating demand. Sites report chiller fleets with sizes
ranging from 500 tons to 2500 tons typically arranged in multiple machines of various vintages
and efficiencies. Adjustable frequency drives and unit mounted controls capable of matching
variable output to minimized power input are common, particularly among machines installed
in the past decade. Increasingly sophisticated digital chiller panels provide not only leaving-
water temperature control, but demand limiting, fault communication, alarms, monitoring, and
performance diagnostics. Optimization of chiller set-point control and staging sequencing was
discussed as a best practice in several interviews. While pre-engineered optimization software

 27

has successfully being applied in a few of the sites, in-house programming of the operating
sequences and control algorithms remains a common practice. The intent, in either case, is to
minimize overall system energy draw, including evaporator and condenser pumping, cooling
tower fans and building auxiliaries while leveraging variable utility rates. Substantial energy
reductions have been demonstrated through focused chiller plant optimization programs.

Cooling tower water consumption is a subject of increasing concern in recent years, particularly
in surveyed sites in those regions experiencing water stress. With an evaporation rate on the
order of 2 gallons per ton-hour, cooling tower make up is often the single largest water
consumer in an energy district, greater than the sum of building potable, sterilization,
humidification and landscape use. Engineering controls to reduce cycles of concentration for
blowdown and use of available reclaimed water sources as make up is in use, or under
consideration at many of the surveyed sites. Understanding that chiller efficiency can be
influenced by scale formation in condenser water is driving more sophisticated chemistry and
practical automated mechanical tube cleaning devices. Sophisticated analysis and monitoring
have enabled tower blow down cycles as high as 20 in one instance.

3.6.4 Energy Storage
A large number (22) of the surveyed systems include chilled water thermal energy storage (TES)
components. These are most often stratified tanks of one or more million gallons used in a daily
cycle to shift operation of the chilling equipment from increasingly expensive on-peak electric
demand periods to significantly less expensive off-peak rate times. Ice is used as the storage
medium in two of the municipal district energy systems serving dense urban environments,
primarily due to smaller physical footprints. In several cases, the operation of TES along with
dispatch of the CHP is demonstrated to be practical and cost-effective means for a district
energy system to respond to peak demand stresses on the regional utility grid.

Use of heating water storage tanks was reported in sites using low temperature heating water
(LTHW) and heat recovery chiller technology. Reasons to integrate TES into a district LTHW
scenario include the ability to baseload the heat pump under lighter variable district demand,
as well as manage peak electric demands in both winter and summer (often seen as dual
morning and evening peaks)

Geological Storage Use of LTHW with Aquifer Thermal Energy Storage (ATES) and Borehole
Thermal Energy Storage (BTES) techniques are being used as a method to balance seasonal
swings in district heating and cooling energy demand. Doing so enables sustained high
utilization of heat recovery heat pumps. One project within the survey cohort involves
manifolding a large array of closed loop U tubes from manifolded boreholes is complete, and
several others are underway. Storing energy in this manner requires large volumes of earthen
mass perforated with many boreholes. At the district energy scale hundreds of holes 500 feet
or deeper and more spaced at 20-foot intervals are needed to exchange the energy to and from
the surrounding soil. Although initial installation is capital intensive and quite disruptive, early

 28

success suggests economies of scale will make this a financially attractive means to increase
annual operating efficiencies in electric heat pump systems.

3.6.5 On-Site Renewables
On-site solar PV is an element in many of the surveyed systems. While many of the examples
can be characterized as smaller scale “demonstration” installations on the order of 1 MW, often
located in public view, several of the systems included appreciable “behind the meter” PV
arrays, of a scale that integration into district wide power management is essential. Internal
district balancing of PV production using the district chiller and TES assets, CHP dispatch and on-
site battery electric storage was reported in several of the surveyed sites. The sites surveyed
include services from a range of utilities and electric grid operators with a broad diversity of
approaches to integration of district energy microgrids. In general, power exports from the
district are discouraged, and opportunities to increase interaction between the grid and district
assets are underutilized. Among the findings of this study is a recommendation that policies to
encourage coordinated, co-optimized operation of district energy systems and the utility grid
be put in place.

3.6.6 Distribution
Steam heat is the primary heat transfer media among most district sites surveyed. Pressurized
to 150 psig or less, it is distributed to buildings as saturated or slightly superheated vapor to be
condensed in building heat exchangers and returned (via pumps) back to the plant as liquid
condensate. Other, typically smaller, uses for space humidification, sterilization or other
process loads may be involved. Overall, the annual magnitude of condensate return (with
several exceptions) is reported in the range of 75%-85%. Remaining condensate loss represents
not only the consumptive end uses but mass lost through steam flashed by pressure reduction
and leakage throughout the open loop system. The change in phase from gas to liquid at the
point of building heat exchange gives steam a high energy density, making it an attractive
distribution media. An existing steam distribution system, routed below the streets and
sidewalks of an energy and occupant dense urban zone is an asset with a very high replacement
value.

In many cases, the steam and condensate piping is heavily insulated and routed through
subsurface concrete tunnel structures; in other cases they are jacketed and direct buried in
earth, often with periodic concrete vaults housing traps, expansion joints and valving. Routine
maintenance, in either case, was described by district operators as an essential ongoing
requirement, necessary to manage corrosion, eliminate leakage and forestall pipe failure.
Thermal losses through the operating steam and condensate distribution system are
challenging to quantify without accurate distribution metering, which many systems are
currently implementing. Data provided through the survey does not provide enough
information to routinely assess site distribution loss. Standard industry references (ASHRAE)
provide estimates in the 7% range.

 29

While many surveyed steam systems are working to increase operational efficiency and
accelerate decarbonization through incremental changes (including replacement of legacy
generation units with electric or fuel-agnostic units, advanced staging algorithms, and metering
improvements), a few of the surveyed systems have opted to transition from steam to hot
water distribution. Transition from steam to hot water is an exceptionally expensive and time-
intensive undertaking, especially considering the distribution system upgrades often required.

 Occasionally termed a “5th generation district energy technology”, primary motivation to make
this shift are the ability to utilize highly efficient electric driven heat pump technology at district
scale, and to eliminate the inherent production, distribution and use losses associated with
steam. Transition to LTHW often involves “industrial” water-to-water heat pumps that make
use of geothermal, ground source, chilled water return, wastewater or other available low
quality heat sources. Direct electric water heating, coupled with above ground heating water
storage tanks, also rely on this media. Other advantages of LTHW as a distribution fluid were
cited. The thermal potential for loss (between the medium and ambient temperatures) is lower
at 160°F LTHW than in 350°F (100 psig) steam/ 200°F condensate. This difference is cited as
driving substantial reductions in continuous distribution losses reported in two of sites that
have completed transition from steam. Reduction in ongoing maintenance including
replacement of traps, correction of pipe leakage, and structural repair of subterranean concrete
was reported as another benefit of transition from jacketed welded steel pipe to direct buried
fused HDPE pipe materials. It was appreciated by at least one of the district operators that the
latent heat characteristics of steam enable substantially smaller pipe diameters to carry the
equivalent thermal energy as a LTHW system operating at 30°–to-40° F sensible supply-to-
return differential.

3.6.7 Cooling
Cooling distribution systems surveyed were found to be a combination of insulated steel pipe in
tunnels (often co-located with steam and condensate) and direct buried. A variety of materials
were used in the buried systems. Although thermal loss between chilled water supply and earth
has lower potential, these systems are typically well insulated through foam, Gilsulate® or
external jacketing to minimize temperature loss. Close attention to the magnitude of
temperature difference across the supply and return piping mains (ΔT) was a common
characteristic among system operators. Although it is primarily a function of building HVAC coil,
piping configuration and control performance, it has a large impact on the capacity of the
distribution system and the magnitude of pumping energy needed to extract heat from district
buildings. Summer peak ΔT was reported within a range of 10° to 16°F. Piping configuration
with respect to pumping is another system characteristic that may be evolving over time. The
advent of speed modulated chiller and pump drives, the coordination and control of
manifolded chillers and pumps to minimize electric demand in real time coupled with precise
flow control valves at HVAC coil points of use has enabled greater use of direct variable
pumping schemes, and less need for primary-secondary or primary-secondary-tertiary schemes.

3.6.8 Ambient Loop
Although district energy systems in the US generally provide separate heating and cooling
services in four separate pipes, one system within the survey cohort relies on an “ambient
loop” approach in which a single piping loop provides relatively stable supply temperatures for
use in water-to-water heat pump units distributed into each building. Coupled to a wastewater
heat exchanger tied into a large municipal sewer main immediately adjacent to the site, this
configuration extracts heat at between 70° and 80°F from the wastewater to provide building
heating and cooling. A common characteristic within aggregated district systems is that some
building loads are calling for heat, while others are calling for cooling. The ambient loop serving
multiple heat pumps provides an extremely efficient way of moving energy within the system.
The sewer heat exchanger is sized to maintain relatively constant baseload and operation.
Supplemental heating and cooling, in the form of a gas fired boiler and water-cooled water
chiller operate only under high load conditions. Exchanging and distributing thermal energy
with an otherwise untapped source through a single, centralized heat transfer station enables
carbon free heating and cooling with relatively low electric demand.

3.6.9 Terminal Use
Energy conversion into beneficial use in building HVAC, domestic hot water, sterilization, and
process use is shown at the far right of the single line graphics. Although the contractual extent
of many district energy systems often (but not always) occurs at the edge of this block (the final
set of heat exchangers), there is a great deal of dynamic influence across this line of
demarcation. This interaction was well understood by system operators who described the
importance of frequent communication with building staff, monitoring, and alarms for critical
temperatures, flows and pressures. Maintaining high chilled (and heating) water ΔT at each of
the buildings, as described above, is an increasingly important metric to district operation.
Building annual energy use intensity and peak instantaneous heating and cooling demand are
other examples where there is mutual benefit to improved management and control and
optimization.

Energy flow necessary to drive plant heating, cooling and power production is ultimately the
aggregated sum of building terminal energy use plus losses in distribution and production.
Although some of the distribution loss may be fixed, part of it, along with much of the plant loss
varies with the magnitude of the terminal load. There was a recognition among many of the site
operators that improving building efficiency has a compounding effect and investment in new
plant equipment should not be made without consideration on reducing the sources of district
demand. It was observed in at least one site that investment in projects that address not only
building efficiency but some of its deferred maintenance needs are the prudent first step in
district decarbonization plan. The large capital investments in plant and distribution
infrastructure will then align with projected district load.

3.7 Performance Verification
3.7.1 Context
As a subset of the US building energy sector, District Energy (DE) systems are centrally
organized to serve heating, cooling, and electric power to a diverse group of proximate, and
often energy intensive buildings. The concept traces its inception to early electrification of cities
in the US where, on a local basis, independent providers (such as Edison Electric) developed
plants and infrastructure to produce fuel-fired steam heat at relatively high quality, for
expansion through a steam turbine generating both electric power and heat to the adjacent
community. The district system thus involved an energy conversion plant and a network of
piping and wiring to distribute energy to building customers. A business model to construct,
fuel, and maintain infrastructure for long term sale of thermal and electric energy proved
viable, and has persisted since, now often involving production and distribution of cooling
energy. The DE model works well in areas of high occupant and energy load density, where
distribution distances are low relative to annual heating, cooling, and power demand. Many
examples of successful District Energy systems are found in the urban cores of cities, university
campuses, and large healthcare facilities. Representative samples of each of these types are
found in this study.

The path of energy serving buildings can be visualized as a chain, with links representing the
discrete steps needed to transform an available, but often remote form of energy to beneficial
form in place.

Basic physics dictates that energy loss at each link is a necessary aspect to a chain that extracts
energy as fuel, generates electricity (and greenhouses gases) through combustion, transports
power over electrical lines and natural gas for building heating. The ratio of useful energy
leaving this system (at right) to raw energy entering it (far left) is the overall efficiency. A
Sankey diagram, in which the wiΔTh of the lines scale to the magnitude of flow, provides
insight. In the example below, natural gas is piped to a combined cycle utility plant generating
sufficient electric power to power and air condition building space.

3.7.2 Relevance
Removing carbon emissions from the energy chain has become an urgent matter. While
obstacles remain, technical solutions that enable combustion free electric power and heat are
emerging. Remarkable engineering improvements in both performance and thermal quality of
residential and industrial electric driven heat pumps are being rolled out. These hold the
promise of enabling practical and efficient electrification of building heat without
overwhelming the already stressed electrical grid. Several of the District Energy systems within

 34

this study are at the leading edge of this transition, with operating systems generating real time
data.

Change of infrastructure at any of the links in the energy chain inevitably involves substantial
reconfiguration, renewal or replacement. Costs are high and acquisition of funding is a common
hurdle in the decarbonization of District Energy. Although performance and efficiency
improvements can be a result of system modernization, energy cost savings alone are not likely
to justify the high cost of major renewal, particularly in an environment of increasing electric
cost and unknown “green fuel” costs.

An understanding of the details of system performance is essential to diligent evaluation of the
costs and benefits of alternative means to decarbonize within the District Energy sector.
Determination of the financial merit of an energy infrastructure initiative ultimately involves a
preparation of a decades long financial cash flow model, based on conversion efficiencies,
energy unit cost predictions (including incentives and deterrents) and the full slate of capital
expenses. The actual, real world performance data for current and emerging district energy
systems are essential in preparing investment decision makers.

Unique characteristics of DE systems make them attractive candidates for decarbonization at
scale:

 • Energy demand diversity within the aggregated buildings enable cost effective
 opportunities for daily and seasonal thermal energy storage.
 • Opportunities to recover and redistribute thermal energy from one building to another.
 • The consolidation of energy conversion simplifies fuel switching to carbon free “green”
 combustion fuels or single point electrification.
 • Economies of scale in cost and performance of prime movers, means and methods
 involved in energy conversion and transfer, and centralized management of risk and
 control.

The work described in the section that follows examines current performance metrics within
the District Energy space, verifying and validating real world efficiency of both current and
emerging technologies. These simple thermodynamic factors are at the essential core of the
quantitative engineering and economic life cycle cost comparison of alternative approaches to
decarbonize building energy flow.

3.8 District Energy System Performance
District Energy services may produce and sell heating, cooling, electric energy, or some
combination of those services. A simple system is diagrammed below. Primary energy cost
inputs are fuel electric power. Many systems use substantial quantities of water, but, under
current economies, these are secondary costs. Other primary costs are maintenance and
operations and service of the debt necessary to construct and renew underground piping
infrastructure and plant equipment. Energy efficiency is an important element in overall
performance, which includes safety, reliability, responsiveness, and a financial prospectus that
is acceptable to the customer base and satisfactory to the owners and investors.

District Energy systems are rarely as simple as diagrammed above. Many unique features are
found in individual systems, often implemented in response to growth and changing local
conditions and opportunities over decades of operation. Planned equipment replacement at
end of life, adaptation to developing technologies, availability of fuel sources, reliability of local
utilities and climatic conditions are reasons that few district systems are identical in
configuration, type or size. Review of the single line diagrams prepared in the 40-site survey
demonstrate the remarkably “bespoke” nature of this sector. Compared to many static stand-
alone building HVAC systems, ongoing renewal and investment in plant performance
optimization is a hallmark in the evolution of district energy systems.

Other possible sources of loss are not routinely monitored in the systems surveyed. These
include thermal losses in the distribution system (both thermal and mass), losses at the building
entrance and through the building heat exchanger. These are difficult to measure directly. Even
those systems with extensive building metering and monitoring do not typically compare plant
output to the sum of building inputs on a routine basis. Distribution system thermal losses
involve a large, fixed component, continuous heat transfer between the high temperature
steam distribution piping and ambient earth or utility tunnel environment.

This loss, which is independent of the flow rate or instantaneous demand, is mitigated by
insulation thickness but nevertheless occurs at a continuous rate primarily determined by the
temperature difference between the distribution media and ambient (either utility tunnel or
earth) temperature, and pipe length of the network. As a percentage, the ratio of loss-to-load
increases under light load.

Another often unaccounted energy input to steam systems is electrical power for boiler draft
fans and plant auxiliaries including feedwater and transfer pumps, water treatment systems,
and building condensate return.

Steam generation with combustion fuel sources other than natural gas found in the survey
include coal, biomass, and fuel oil. Many district systems have shifted away from coal to natural
gas, primarily as a cost-effective modernization approach for aging plants and equipment.
Biomass boilers, fired on locally sourced renewable sources are in service in several of the sites.
Auxiliary energy consumption becomes a more significant factor in performance of solid fuel
systems where energy and labor burden for material handling, storage, drying, and disposal
become significant. Reliability and availability of stable, high-quality fuel is noted as a concern
in biomass operations and (somewhat surprisingly) in procurement of coal for use at district
energy scale.

Use of electricity for steam generation via electrode boilers is considered a possibility in several
of the sites. While electric to steam generation with a Coefficient of Performance (COP) of 1.0 is

 40

more efficient when compared to gas to steam COP of 0.8, the cost to operate, under most
current electric rate structures, is high relative to combustion of fossil fuels. Easy to start and
stop, electric steam generation can make financial sense, however, in situations with low
enough off-peak electric rates, and the scale of many district energy systems often enables
power purchasing at wholesale rates. Electric driven steam heat pumps, often multistage, and
designed with sufficient lift to generate both chilled water at the low end and steam at the high
end are in use in district heating and cooling systems in Europe. These may become attractive
to electrification of US heating systems and are under consideration at several of the surveyed
sites.

At present, a hurdle to implementation is related to refrigerant lift properties. Units currently
on the market are limited to a 30 psig steam output without supplemental compression or
heat. Systems in the US typically require 100 psig or greater. Steam heat pumps are most likely
to play a role in decarbonization of municipal districts where steam replacement is simply
impractical or cost prohibitive.

Energy storage technologies are in development that have the potential to have significant
positive impact on steam system efficiencies. Variability in renewable grid electric sources
makes the ability to decouple the time of heating and cooling production from the time of
generation attractive. While technologies to store thermal energy in reservoirs of ice or water
are currently practical and in common use in cooling applications, and storage of thermal
energy in stratified heating hot water tanks is proving practical at several systems surveyed,
energy storage as steam is difficult. Emerging technologies involving ceramic materials or
basaltic rock with high heating capacity are hoped to solve this problem.

3.8.2 Heating Water
Heating water is in use in 18 of the systems in the survey. As well as being a key feature in the
district wide decarbonization schemes in several large university installations, it is also found
serving limited subsets, “precincts” of a larger district that are primarily served with steam. In at
least one case, a new heating hot water subsystem was installed to replace a failing section of
steam piping in a much larger campus system. This change was described as a more cost-
effective approach from both the first cost and life cycle energy and long-term maintenance
perspectives.

Several methods to generate heating water were found in the survey, including natural gas-
fired hot water generators (most common) and conversion of steam generated elsewhere in
the district (shown in the diagram above). Electric hot water generators and electric driven
heat pumps are other proven methods. In most cases systems are closed loop, involving fewer
components than steam to maintain. Additionally, with lower operating pressures and
temperatures, code requirements for full time supervision of the boiler plant may be alleviated.
Gas-to-heating water boiler typically display low venting and auxiliary energy loss. The average
efficiency among the five sites reporting heating water boiler efficiency was 85%.

 41

Heating water distribution systems differ from steam/condensate in several important ways.
These systems are more likely to be direct-buried and require fewer, if any, mechanical
expansion joints and no steam drip traps. Although many LTHW systems are constructed in
welded steel, lower maximum temperature opens the possibility of construction using fusion
welded HDPE) in lieu of welded steel required for steam. Use of micro boring techniques,
eliminating the need for open trenching is possible with this material, possibly allowing for
reduction in disruption, including road closures. The potential for energy loss in an LTHW
system is lower than with steam. The maximum temperature difference between heating hot
water supply (160°F) and earth (60°F) is half that of steam at 340°F. Distribution energy loss in a
well-designed low temperature heating hot water system is more likely to be in the range of 2%
to 5%, with far less routine maintenance required.

Comparison of electric power required to move heat in the two systems is interesting. In the
steam case, motive energy to drive heat across the system is supplied by the pressure
differential from the boiler outlet (generating ~ 150 psig) to the building end device (needing 50
psig or less). The volumetric heat capacity of steam and smooth walled steel pipe makes high
velocity at low pressure drop possible, resulting in a very efficient distribution media. As a
result, comparatively small steam pipes are suitable to move large energy flows, making an
argument in favor of steam distribution. Liquid condensate returns from building to plant are
generally driven by hydronic pumping.

The magnitude of electrical energy necessary to drive the circulation pumps in an LTHW is
determined by the supply-to-return temperature differential, pipe size, and internal roughness
and length of system.

Building supply temperature and the temperature differential between supply and return are
essential factors in performance metrics for heating systems. A large proportion of those
building heating systems served by district energy steam operate with hydronic hot water
systems, often originally designed to circulate as high as 190°F heating water. Experience (and
actual stress testing) at several of the surveyed systems demonstrate that peak winter heating
demand can be met with water temperatures in the 160°F to 170°F range. This performance
characteristic of existing building is significant in planning and design of LTHW systems. A heat
exchanger separates the building loop from its 340°F steam supply. A pump on the building side
circulates from heat exchanger to end use coils that return at 30°F to 40°F cooler than supply.
Maintaining high building differentials is critical to maintaining a reasonable heating water
system pumping energy levels. Elimination of the district-to-building heat exchanger provides
an effective way to maximize the temperature differential.

As noted earlier, the district energy systems are often unique configurations of many energy
transfer elements. An example of a district CHP plant, drawn from data provided in the survey,
is shown below. Steam generated through a variety of natural gas, biomass, and coal units is
routed through a power generating turbine process then distributed to campus buildings for
heat or routed through absorption chillers to drive a portion of the cooling load. The scale of
this operation is large enough that relatively little power is pulled from the grid.

Efficiency of a Combined Heat and Power (CHP) operation can be defined in several ways. The
simplest approach is taking the ratio of the energy content of the fuel input to the combination
of electrical plus thermal energy output. The difference between input and output energy
represents overall energy loss. This value is often compared against the sum of boiler efficiency
and electric generation efficiency in a Separate Heat and Power (SHP) alternative as a means to
evaluate performance. A large percentage of utility power generation in the US involves
thermoelectric generation (combustion driven Rankine and Brayton cycles) performing, at best,
with 40% fuel to electric efficiency. This results in a loss of 60% of the fuel energy. The
opportunity to make beneficial use of much of this loss generally makes CHP relatively more
efficient in this formulation which is occasionally referred to as “1st Law Efficiency”.

There is the question of whether the economic value of thermal energy is equal to that of
electrical energy. Electricity can be transported long distances and used in many more direct
applications than thermal energy. A second metric of CHP performance introduced in Federal
regulation in 1978 (PURPA) values thermal energy at half that of electricity. Known as “FERC
efficiency”, it was intended to recognize the higher quality electric energy. Another formulation
of the CHP efficiency metric is “Fuel Efficiency”, which compares the magnitude of fuel input of
a CHP operation to that of SHP providing the same output.

Evolution of the US electric supply from predominantly thermoelectric production to an
increasing percentage of renewable, non-combustion energy further impact the comparison

 47

calculus. Wind and Solar generation have different “fuel input” and “energy loss”
characteristics, fundamentally changing the nature of a direct comparison between a
combustion driven combined heat and power operation to a renewable electric driven district
heat and power system. The question now is how to value solar and wind electricity compared
to fossil fuel electricity. Adding further complexity to the comparison is the increasingly
attractive opportunity to apply electric driven heat pumps which draw thermal energy directly
from the ambient environment. Is the thermal energy contained in wastewater (or district
chilled water return) indeed “free” in a comparative efficiency calculation? How is the intrinsic
social cost of carbon emissions to be valued in a comparative metric? The advent of substantial
renewable grid energy and potential application of electric heat pump technology at the district
energy scale alters the relative values of energy. This may introduce new terms (not unlike the
50% factor in FERC efficiency) into assessment of CHP performance.

The survey included a request for annual energy data including nominal CHP heat rate, fuel
inputs, electric heat generation and cooling production. It was hoped that these inputs would
be sufficient to calculate a consistent CHP efficiency parameter on which to compare individual
sites. For several reasons this proved difficult: data was provided in different units; locations for
metering of primary flows varied, or were not routinely monitored; and utilization, dispatch and
duration times of equipment were not consistently reported. In practice, the primary
performance parameters for district energy systems among the survey are not calculated by
efficiency, but rather by reliability and cost-effective operation. Increasing sophistication in
plant monitoring and control systems could certainly provide the granular information needed
to derive these metrics and feed an industry wide database.

Methods to make ongoing use of District Energy CHP as an element in a district decarbonization

line. In this configuration, the system would work as an energy storage system, absorbing grid
power during low loads and generating District power and heat during high demand.

3.8.4 Electric Power
In most cases the performance of District Energy systems is interrelated to the reliability,
capacity and configuration of electric supply from the grid. The point at which this energy
exchange occurs is often a substation (or substations) on or immediately adjacent to the district
that houses transformers, meters and switching necessary to isolate district loads from the grid.
There are many possible substation engineering and ownership arrangements driven primarily
by safety, reliability, and cost considerations. The substation is typically the point of
interconnection. In many cases it is configured, often under regulatory policy, to assure energy
only flows inward, from the utility to the district.

Uncertainty generated by the changing landscape of grid electric power is a matter of concern
in virtually all of the system operations surveyed. Decarbonization is occurring rapidly across
the electric generation sector. Modernization of assets involving replacement of coal-fired
generation with increased renewable wind and PV, along with faster acting combined cycle
natural gas is shifting the generation mix and lowering carbon emission intensity
(MTCO2/MWhr) at a pace that would have been hard to imagine a decade ago.

These changes have, however, created new challenges. Seasonal variability of renewable
components makes grid capacity more sensitive to time of day and time of year than in the
past. The output of solar resources is out of sync with consumer loads, causing morning ramp
down and evening ramp up of conventional resources while solar resources are, in certain
seasons, over-generating to the point of needing to be curtailed. Large spatial differences
between regions with ample renewable resources and those with high energy demand led to
large scale grid imbalance, requiring new high voltage transmission lines. This level of
investment in generation and grid renewal comes at a high cost, ultimately passed on to
electricity consumers.

Electric rates are increasing across the US, heightening attention in the building sector to
implement energy efficiency and cost control strategies. Much of the increase is occurring
through time-of-use rate structures that encourage energy use during morning and early
afternoon and discourage use during late afternoon and evening periods, alleviating the
problem described above. In simple and immediate terms, increasing electric prices (without
increasing natural gas) makes CHP more attractive. Time-of-use rates drive the load shifting
strategies of thermal energy storage. Implementation of megawatt scale on-site PV has become
cost effective for several sites surveyed and is used (along with CHP, chiller plant and TES) to
balance behind-the-meter loads through a microgrid control scheme. Plant equipment
performance, at any given time, is influenced by the dynamics of energy balancing. Effective
use of electric battery storage is emerging as a desirable addition to this scheme although
currently cost prohibitive.

Interaction between district energy systems and the electric utility is an intriguing possibility
which was rarely found in the survey. The potential that a district CHP could coordinate with
the grid to provide voltage support is not typically acknowledged or compensated by grid

 51

operators. The scale of DE generation is often not large enough to meaningfully impact grid
capacity. Demand response programs are in place in a few of the sites; here, the DE system is
contracted to drop predetermined loads off the meter at the request of the utility. This may
have the effect of holding site generation in reserve, for activation during a call for demand
reduction.

Remarkable progress in moving electric generation away from coal has somewhat weakened
the argument that DE CHP is an attractive decarbonization strategy. On an annual average
basis, regional emission factors (reported by eGrid) are drawing near or trending below the
MTCO2/MWhr levels typically generated by DE systems. Utility generation is not homogeneous:
where it most inefficient, the (often oldest) equipment is fired as demand increases. Several
grid operators (CAISO, PJM) generate real time emissions factors that can be used for direct
comparison with the performance of a DE system. With grid emission levels a function of time
(generally higher emissions during peak periods), it is possible to coordinate dispatch of site
CHP in way that lowers overall emissions. Doing so requires some sophistication in predicting
duration, and in calculating cost differentials. This is an emerging opportunity being explored at
several sites within the survey.

Two-way control at the interface between a DE system and its electric grid in a way that
enables optimization of equipment and assets on both sides was found to be an aspirational
goal of many of the site operators. Although technical tools are available, financial incentive,
policy and regulation are not currently aligned.

3.8.5 Chilled Water
Water-cooled electric-driven vapor compression chillers were the most used cooling means in
the systems surveyed. Steps of heat exchange involved in extracting heat from buildings start
with air handler coils removing heat from the air stream into the circulating chilled water
network which flows back to the central plant; then, as the water is chilled back down, the heat
is transferred into a refrigerant loop from which moves it into an open condenser loop that
flows over a cooling tower to partially evaporate by contact with the air. Evaporation gives up
the heat as a low quality but nearly saturated warm vapor that is pushed out and dissipated in
open air. Almost all the electric energy added to the process up to that point to drive the
pumps, the fans and the refrigerant compressor end up as thermal energy heat in the loop.
Heat dissipated by the final cooling tower plume is the sum of energy extracted from the
building plus the air conditioning process energy.

system by minimizing tube fouling are among the best practices reported.

An element of performance that affects all aspects of chilled water operation (production,
distribution, storage and end use) is the difference in temperature between supply and return.
This parameter is mostly set by heat transfer at the building air handler coil, under the
influence of its modulating flow control valve. Holding heat extraction conditions constant on
the air side, the number of piping passes and area of coil finned surface determine the
temperature rise across the chilled water side. Maintenance of leaving air temperature setpoint
control, maintenance to prevent coil fouling and maintenance to ensure rapid valve response
are typically building operations issues that have large impacts on plant operations. Early (pre-
1990) building HVAC design standards were often based on a 10-degree temperature split. This
has evolved in recent years to 15, 16 or even 20°F. Distribution flow is a reason to widen the
differential, as flow is proportional to the heat transferred times the difference. Pressure
differential (“pump head”) needed to move the flow through a pipe is proportional to the
square of the flow. Pumping power necessary to generate this head is proportional to the cube
of the flow. Electric energy to distribute cooling from plant to building is thus quite sensitive to
the ΔT.

A second reason ΔT is important is its impact on the capacity of a thermal storage tank. Again,
using the simple relationship between heat mass and temperature (one BTU is the energy
needed to raise one lb. of water 1 degree F), the number of Ton-hours- better- MW/hr of
energy that can be stored in each tank volume is directly proportional to ΔT. At the plant, ΔT
has several effects. Water chiller evaporator pressure drop is optimized to achieve a specified
kW/ton at a proscribed flow rate. As ΔT drops, the pumping energy to achieve this flow
increases by the cube. It no longer becomes possible to generate full chiller capacity as the
plant pump design limits are approached, and now operators face a capacity shortfall:
inadequate flow at design supply temperature.

The need to resolve ΔT issues has been recognized across the industry in the past two decades.
Much of this has been accomplished through replacement of pneumatically actuated three-way
control valves with digitally controlled electric units, implementation of sophisticated flow and
temperature feedback control, and coordination between building and district operations.
Survey data shows few systems reporting under 10°F, and many (likely those with TES)
consistently operating in the 15-20°F range.

3.8.5.2 Water

An essential part of the process is water evaporation, which absorbs energy at a rate of 1000
BTU per lb. (roughly equivalent to 2 gallons of make-up water per Ton-hour of cooling). Annual
volumes of water evaporated in the cooling tower can approach the magnitude of water
consumption for all other purposes in a university campus district environment, including
sanitation, showers, kitchens, etc. The reason for water cooling in district energy is that under
most cooling conditions, it provides cooler rejection temperatures, meaning lower lift, less
electric energy and a smaller heat rejector footprint. Air cooled refrigeration uses no water,
but, because it rejects directly into ambient air at a higher temperature, it draws more electric
power, and requires a great deal more space for air contact heat exchange. Two other water-
cooled chilling processes used in many district systems in the survey are steam absorption and
steam turbine driven vapor compression. Water consumption in these processes is 1.5-2 times
greater than electric driven machines. The cooling tower now is not only rejecting heat
extracted from buildings, but also the latent heat of vaporization needed to condense the low-
quality steam at the bottom of the refrigeration process.

3.8.6.1 Ambient Loop

Another method that takes advantage of the diversity and time dependence of an aggregated
set of loads is tying building heat pump systems into a common thermal loop, operated and
maintained to desirable temperature setpoints by centralized energy exchange assets. These
loop temperatures may vary with season or outside ambient temperature. Any number of plant
heat sources or sinks can be considered, including air or water source heat pumps, water
source heat pumps exchanging heat with waste-water systems, lake sources, geothermal wells,
waste heat from industrial sources, data centers, or other ambient sources that would be
difficult to access on a stand-alone building-by-building basis. Innovation in converting locally
available thermal assets into useful district energy sources is a hallmark of this approach.

One application, currently under consideration for adaptation into the energy dense urban core
of a large city, involves converting a portion of its existing underground chilled water
distribution piping network into a two-pipe ambient loop that would serve heat pumps

 58

retrofitted into individual buildings. While the primary benefit of this concept is to meet a near
term city-wide mandate to reduce combustion of natural gas, it has a secondary benefit of
eliminating energy and maintenance losses from a large section of aging steam distribution
lines. Heat extracted from a nearby municipal sewer line is proposed to serve as the source for
the plant heat pump, sized to be capable of providing 90° F water to the loop for much of the
Winter load. Air source heat pumps and/or electric boilers in the plant would supplement the
waste-water resource under periods of high heat demand.

While substantial initial cost is certainly involved in extending the sewer heat transfer lines,
increasing the plant electrical service, and retrofitting water source heat pumps into building
HVAC systems, annual energy costs for the heat pump operation are projected to be relatively
low and stable. Alternative decarbonization approaches that involve abandonment of the
district system and electrification on a building-by-building basis carry the very large cost
burden of improving the downtown electrical capacity, including replacing cabling, duct banks
and transformers, as well as acquiring additional real estate. Initial cost-of-service analysis by
the district energy operator suggests conversion of aging steam to ambient loop could be a
prudent and practical near-term solution to urban decarbonization in this instance.

3.8.6.2 Electrification of Steam

Electrification of district heat is being accomplished without development of new low
temperature heating water or ambient loop distribution infrastructure. This is being pursued in
many cases where it is impractical or economically unfeasible to abandon an existing steam
network, and/or convert the current building stock. Among the simplest, most straightforward
approaches is to replace the fuel source from carbon emitting natural gas, oil or coal to biofuel,
hydrogen or small nuclear reactors. While technically feasible and commercially available, the
reliability, availability and cost of these alternative fuel sources on a large scale has yet to
materialize. As the physical infrastructure for biofuels and hydrogen and related regulatory
policy adapts to small modular reactors, these may become attractive candidates for
consideration.

Today, district scale electric boilers are feasible in some US sites with low off-peak electric
rates. The advent of large offshore wind turbine farms generating an abundant supply of
renewable energy promises to make electrification of steam through converting gas boilers to
electrode boilers (“e-Boilers”) a simple and relatively low first cost element in a path to
decarbonization. An obstacle to widescale near term implementation is that the current relative
cost of fossil fuels to on-peak electric power makes it substantially more expensive. Solutions
being applied include application of industrial heat pumps that produce steam heat up to three
times more efficiently than direct electric resistance, thermal energy storage, and electric
battery energy storage.

Steam-generating heat pumps have been in service in European district energy systems for
several years. Capable of extracting energy from ambient sources such as lakes, rivers, and
district cooling systems, units currently on the market generate steam at up to 30 psi, at COPs

 59

approaching 3. Most distribution systems in the US operate at high pressures, meaning
supplemental electric energy to drive steam compressors diminishes performance.

Development of suitable media to store thermal energy at a quality necessary to generate
steam at suitable temperature and pressure is beginning to yield market-ready systems
involving rock, bricks, or molten salts. One promising technology that couples electric heat
generation with storage uses ceramic media, heated by electric coils during low-cost off-peak
periods. Forced air, passing over the media during on-peak electric periods is routed through an
unfired boiler to generate steam. Improvements in costs, round trip efficiency, and footprint of
utility scale electric battery storage could also play into adoption of economically sustainable
district electric steam systems.

Electrification of heat in energy dense municipal systems not only involves substantial
improvements to the infrastructure serving the plant, but also impacts to the local electric grid
profile and performance. A comparison of electrifying existing district steam infrastructure to
an alternative that abandons it and embarks on building-by building electrification would need
to consider the practicality, cost and disruption involved in improving the downtown electrical
service capacity. This consideration has been pointed out by operators at several municipal
systems. The fully developed costs of this alternative include not only new feeders and
underground ducts, but also substations and the real estate necessary to contain them. As a
result, it is likely that electrifying an existing municipal steam system is the more prudent and
practical approach. In the absence of a massive infusion of capital, or substantial increase in the
cost of carbon generating fuels, development in most district energy systems is constrained to
sustainable investment. A balance between the desire to decarbonize and the desire to
maintain stable customer cost of service needs to be struck by shareholders and rate payers.

3.8.6.3 Phased Transition

At least two institutions within the survey sample completed their district decarbonization plan.
In both cases, the existing infrastructure was in poor condition, with urgent need for renewal
and replacement of plant equipment and underground distribution. Life cycle economic
evaluation that compared restoring the systems under a “Business-As-Usual” approach to
replacing the fossil fuel fired combustion equipment with electric-driven heat pumps and the
aging steam lines with a new low temperature heating water system justified the very large
capital expense. Five plus years later, with reliability, energy efficiency and carbon emissions
proven, these systems now serve as templates for many other district energy decarbonization
plans now underway.

Phasing is a key consideration in planning. The magnitude of construction work involved is
often high, and coupled with long lead times of industrial equipment, and potentially extensive
disruption of campus grounds, full project implementation may require five or more years.
Decarbonization plans often involve more than one engineering solution. District wide
improvement of building energy efficiency is often among the first steps. Precincts or portions
of a district with higher energy density may derive more benefit from low temperature hot

 60

water conversion than lower density zones, while electric powered steam generation or air
source heat pumps may be most cost effective in remote, low-density areas. Plans described in
the surveyed system were likely to be a hybrid of several approaches, from addition of heat
pumps drawing from ambient sources where available with daily and seasonal thermal energy
storage to supplemental e-Boilers operated only during peak heating times. An existing natural
gas-fired CHP may play an important role in offsetting increased electric cost. Transitioning
dispatch from base loaded operation to supplementing the electric grid during high demand
periods, and during times when grid power carbon emissions exceed those of the CHP could be
part of prudent operation.

4 Collected Best Practices in District Energy and CHP
This report contains a collection of best practices described by participating systems, with a
particular emphasis on campuses. This selection is by no means a definitive list of strategies for
achieving peak performance, decarbonization, or other system goals – rather, it is an attempt
to capture the diverse array of strategies deployed by an equally diverse industry.

District energy is a diverse and decentralized industry in the US, and as a result, there are few
consolidated, verified collections of operations, engineering, and financial best practices. While
interviewing participating systems to clarify and validate their submitted performance data, the
project team conducted in-depth interviews with 45 select systems to contextualize the data
and identify best practices.
The diversity of challenges and solutions represented in this collection accurately depict how
systems tailor their offerings to their client base, manage the expectations and needs of their
parent organizations, and navigate new and uncertain funding and regulatory models.

 63

In many cases, the listed best practices serve multiple goals: for example, conversion from
steam to hot water increases the efficiency of district heating systems, decreases the carbon
intensity of the system overall, and dramatically lowers ongoing and life cycle distribution
system costs. Cases pulled from the participating systems aim to demonstrate these overlaps.

In all cases, financial assessment and cost analysis were fundamental first steps for systems
considering engineering or operating changes on any level, and directly impacted the
applicability of any given best practice. Nearly all the interviewed systems run a wide variety of
generation and distribution assets from multiple generations, and focused on best practices
that allowed them to make best use of their existing infrastructure before making larger
changes.

For this reason, this report organizes best practices into three categories, beginning with the
importance of financial assessment:

 • Finance, including funding sources and master planning
 • Engineering, including Efficiency, Reliability/Resiliency, and Decarbonization
 • Operations, including equipment staging, controls, and personnel management

4.1 Financial Management
4.1.1 Master Planning
Across the systems interviewed for this project, master planning emerged as a critical best
practice for district energy systems. Master planning is an opportunity to evaluate existing
infrastructure, align with larger organizational goals such as cost savings, resiliency, and
decarbonization; and formalize future utility projects. Some systems embark on master
planning on a regular 5- to 10- year cycle, while others center the process around specific
infrastructure needs, such as capacity expansion or decarbonization mandates. In some cases,
the process works in tandem with securing project funding, and in other cases the master plan
is created without existing funding but kept up-to-date and actionable should funding become
available.

4.1.2 Funding Major Projects
Cost evaluation is a critical component of master planning. In addition to initial capital
investment, financial considerations may include lifecycle cost analysis, long term energy and
maintenance requirements, carbon proxy prices, and a comparison to business-as-usual
operational costs. Once the costs are understood, there are several well-established funding
sources that may be considered including:

 • Power Purchase Agreements
 • State appropriations for state institutions
 • Federal and state efficiency and investment incentives (such as those included in the
 2023 Inflation Reduction Act)
 • Institutional loans
 • Certified climate bonds
 • Endowment funds

In addition to these funding opportunities, surveyed systems identified several unique and
effective funding opportunities:

4.1.2.1 Public/Private Partnerships (P3)

P3s are long-term relationships between government and private sector institutions, generally
designed to provide private-sector funding for public projects. In some cases, P3s are engaged
at the project design phase (such as National Western Center Energy District Operated by
CenTrio Denver) and in others private organizations are brought on to operate public assets
(System 8)

CenTrio has been contracted by the National Western Authority to develop and operate a
wastewater heat recovery-based district energy system for the National Western Center
campus in Denver, CO. CenTrio partnered with NWA via a P3 concessionaire agreement, which
allowed the partnership to implement district energy in the most economical and carbon-
minimizing method possible. Though P3s are becoming increasingly popular in the industry, this
agreement is particularly progressive: awarding CenTrio the contract essentially just gave the
company exclusive rights to negotiate the actual project specifics with the partners, rather than
implement a finalized model. Because CenTrio was able to work with NWA during the project
agreement phase, they had a much more comprehensive understanding of the end users’ and
owners’ goals, resources, challenges, and the physical limitations and opportunities of the site.
CenTrio leadership emphasizes that without this thoughtful and collaborative approach, the
project would not have been as feasible and tailored.

System 8 is owned by a university and operated by an energy company in a 50- year P3
agreement. The operating company takes a comprehensive approach to energy management,
encompassing systems operations, energy efficiency, and financial resource management. In

 68

return for knowledgeable, experienced operation, the concessionaire has the advantage of
steady and predictable cash flows, along with academic expertise from university faculty and
students. The operating company is also held to KPIs to ensure the company consistently acts in
the best interests of the university. The university also gets full benefit of any energy or rate
savings incurred. The university is hoping to reach carbon neutrality by 2050, and the energy
company has agreed to partner in that effort. To align the campus’s 30-year utility master plan,
projected real estate growth, and carbon goals, the company has developed a decarbonization
tool which has since been deployed to other sites. The tool is used to evaluate opportunities for
reaching the campus’s carbon target by balancing capital and operating expenditures and
modeled carbon pricing.

4.1.2.2 ESCO Performance Contracting and Service Contracts

Energy service companies (ESCOs) can enter performance contracts with customers to support
large investments, such as CHP installation. Generally, the contract commits the ESCO to install
the equipment which the customer operates, pays back over time, and generates energy
savings from. Performance contracts reduce upfront capital needs and implementation risk for
customers and provide a guaranteed source of income for the ESCO.

North Carolina State (NCSU) has largely funded its CHP upgrades via performance contracting
(as opposed to traditional fundraising, grant funding, or state appropriations). The ESCO
Performance Contract model removed the required utilities expense from the University’s
capital building budget request, enabling a large-scale plant improvement and expansion, while
reducing its implementation risk by providing single source responsibility for procurement,
construction, management, and energy efficiency. The ESCO agreement requires NCSU to run
their CHP system 24/7. In considering future performance contracts, the university has weighed
the value of up-front capital with the expense of long-term, potentially inefficient performance
requirements. While the urgency and scale of the CHP upgrades necessitated a performance
contract, NCSU decided against the model in their 2019 TES installation: if the university opted
to add the tank to the CHP contract, it would have taken 20-25 years to pay off. Instead, the
university opted to use appropriated funds, resulting in more rapid simple payback.

Service contracts can also be developed with non-utility stakeholders: in 2014 Arizona Western
College needed to make a major investment in their cooling system, and they were presented
with an attractive cashflow-friendly opportunity. Arizona Western College’s food service
provider partnered with an energy optimization contractor and approached the college with a
proposal: the college could fund the optimization project by marginally increasing the payments
for their existing food service contract. Spreading the costs out through an existing budge line-
item was integral for getting approval of the project, especially as energy savings started to hit
the facilities budget.

 69

4.1.2.3 Energy Savings Teams and Dedicated “Green Funds”

Several interviewed systems employ internal Energy Savings teams, whose members are solely
dedicated to identifying and implementing energy savings projects. Funding for the team comes
out of a larger utility or consulting budget, deferred maintenance funds, or, in some cases, the
team can become entirely self-funding based on the incurred energy savings. One system,
which has elected to remain anonymous, has implemented a formal cost-sharing agreement
option with customers, whereby occupants identify opportunities for cost avoidance, utilities
aids implementation and assessment, and after 12 months any realized cost avoidance is split
evenly between the two groups.

The University of Missouri’s utilities team sets aside roughly $2 million annually in energy
conservation funds, which are disbursed as the utilities and maintenance teams identify
efficiency and conservation projects across the campus. All resulting savings ease the burden on
the larger utility budget. The campus employs design standards that set a minimum level of
efficiency for new and renovated buildings, and the utilities team will often invest a portion of
the energy conservation fund to elevate the efficiency above the design standard. This process
has been in place since 1990, and though in the early years the projects didn’t see an
immediate payback, the utilities group now estimates the university saves more than $11
million/year because of the improvements that have been made through the program since it
was first introduced.

UC Davis has taken a unique management approach to supporting system efficiency by
establishing a group of five in-house engineers and data scientists dedicated to identifying and
implementing opportunities to improve building efficiency, funded entirely by campus energy
savings. Funding the team exclusively via savings incentivizes the team to continuously improve
the campus while reducing the campus utilities costs over time. When the team was
established, they anticipated fully paying off the initial investment within ten years. Now, seven
years in, the team is on track to hit this milestone, and is already generating more savings than
necessary to offset their $1.5 million annual budget.

4.1.2.4 Ratemaking

The reliability and efficiency of district energy systems is directly impacted by the efficiency of
individual customer buildings. Most district energy systems only have control over their system
up to its customer buildings’ walls, but some have implemented creative methods of
ratemaking and incentives to encourage investment in in-building efficiency measures. Lincoln
(NE) Electric Service’s District Energy Company, which has multiple sites within the city, tailers
rates to the specific plant the customer is connected to, which accommodates the unique
assets and design of the connected system.

The University of Cincinnati has taken a unique approach to its university district energy

electric services, and proceeds from these sales support the utility’s investments and
engineering upgrades.

The immediate benefit of this system is that the utilities group is not reliant on the university’s

Utility-model management has also had a positive impact on the workforce: prospective

4.2 Technical/Engineering: Performance and Efficiency
45 systems were interviewed to identify a series of technical and engineering upgrades that
lead to improved system efficiency. Many of these systems worked within limited budgets to
implement these changes and were able to pass the benefits of decreased cost and carbon
consumption along to their customers.

This section is organized by the type of energy output that serves the district. Lessons learned
and technical Best Practices reported in interviews with the participating system operators are
described individually and in aggregate. The following review of Heating, Cooling, Electric
Power production, distribution, and use explores engineering approaches to reducing water
consumption and decarbonizing the utility operation. In the best case, these Best Practices may
combine improvements within multiple energy flows and solve a variety of capacity, efficiency,
labor, and fossil fuel combustion issues.

4.2.1 Heat
District Heating was identified in 31 of the 45 sites surveyed. Operation in higher latitudes and
climate zones are generally more sensitive to costs associated with fuel, and heat transfer
efficiency, than those with fewer annual heating degree days. Substantial infrastructure and
annual expenses were described in many districts, including those in the warm climates of
southern Arizona and Texas. District Heating among the cohort is primarily used to compensate
for building envelope loss, ventilation, and reheat for dehumidification, but also provides
domestic and process water heating, sterilization, and in some cases direct humidification.
Fossil fuel combustion is the primary source of district heat. Natural gas is the most deployed
fuel source, distantly followed by woody biomass. Fuel oil remains a backup source, and
systems that still maintain solid fuel boilers are phasing out use of coal. Several of the systems
surveyed describe relying on fuel oil during extreme weather events when utility purchase of
pipeline gas may be curtailed, noting as a best practice fuel oil system maintenance to ensure
availability and reliability.

Electricity was cited as a growing (?) significant district plant heat source: the comparative cost
of electric power necessary to generate resistance electric heat versus fossil fuels is not, in the
cases under consideration, currently favorable. At the present time, the magnitude of
additional electric capacity necessary to generate an appreciable amount of district heat comes
at a comparatively high cost.

4.2.1.1 Efficiency

Media used in generation and distribution of heat to the district was, with few exceptions,
medium pressure (75-150 psi) steam. Most common were water tube boilers generating
saturated or slightly superheated steam. Annual natural gas-fired boiler efficiencies (metered
gas input to metered steam output) on the order of 80-85% was provided by many of the

 72

surveyed sites. Information on overall system efficiency was not generally provided. Although
distribution system line loss rates as high as 50% (in aging and extensive distribution systems
during summer) were noted in several instances, consistent data on distribution and building
losses was not generally available. Reduction in these sorts of annual energy loss is among the
reasons conversion of steam to heating water systems is an emerging Best Practice in the US.

Steam boiler operation involves operation and maintenance of auxiliary plant subsystems such
as piping, condensate transfer, feedwater pumps and deaerators, economizers, make up water
treatment, metering, instrumentation and control, emission control and monitoring, electrical
motor control. The efficiency of energy and mass transfer in these subsystems can have a
significant effect on overall plant efficiency. Values for overall annual heating plant efficiency
are not typically estimated as a routine operating metric and were not analyzed through the
survey. Practical values on the order of 2%-5% energy loss through feedwater pump and forced
draft fan pressurization, deaeration and miscellaneous conductive, convective and radiative
thermal losses are likely, but somewhat dependent on utilization. District heating plant Best
Practices that improve the quantity and reliability of metering (allowing attention to be focused
on high benefit improvements and tuning) are described elsewhere.

Routine safety inspections are performed to ensure reliability and safety (often by the
institutions’ insurance carriers). Boiler emissions are regulated and reported in all parts of the
country. Routine attention is paid to the operation and calibration of combustion controls,
oxygen trim and flue gas recirculation systems. Uniform Code generally requires on-site
supervision by certified boiler operators. The above-standard efficiency and reliable operation
reported by the surveyed district heating plants requires leadership, teamwork, and a relatively
high level of expertise. These Best Practices are described in more detail later.

While most of the steam systems surveyed operate at or under 150 psi, several of the
Combined Heat and Power (CHP) systems employ significantly higher pressures (up to 600 psi).
In these cases, the steam is expanded, and energy removed through turbines that drive the
rotating shafts of generators and chillers. Higher pressure in these cases results in higher
turbine steam-to-shaft energy efficiency, improving overall plant efficiency. There is less benefit
to high steam pressure in the distribution network of the systems surveyed. Steam extracted
from the turbines or reduced from the primary header through let-down valves is typically
distributed to the campus at lower pressures. The safe operation of these systems can
introduce some additional complexity in the form of De-Superheaters and Pressure Control
equipment.

Biomass or regionally sourced wood waste is used as an alternative to fossil fuels for steam
generation in several of the surveyed sites. Best practices described for these solid fuel systems
include base loading for extended periods to maximize cost benefit. Proximity of resources, and
the ability to secure long-term contracts and stable BTU content were also noted. On Campus
physical requirements for operation of a Biomass plant are among the constraints to
implementation. Fuel storage, bulk handling equipment, emissions control and monitoring

 73

require often precious plant acreage. Establishing safe material transport routes through
campus may be an issue.

4.2.1.1.1 Steam Distribution

In most cases among surveyed sites, steam is distributed from the district heating plant to
buildings within the district through a piping network via subterranean concrete tunnels or, less
frequently, direct buried, well-insulated lines. Latent heat within steam is commonly
transferred to a closed loop building heating water system for use in air handlers, convectors,
or fan coils. Less commonly, it is further reduced in pressure and used directly in radiators or
the condensing coils of air handlers, then drained back, as a liquid, to a reservoir at the base of
the building. The reservoir also collects liquid condensate from domestic and process hot water
heaters along with some of the steam used in sterilization equipment. In all but one of the
surveyed systems, condensate was then returned to the plant. In general, the additional
electric pumping cost to return the mass of water is offset by the inherent value of its thermal
and chemically treated properties. Distribution of pressurized steam involved welded piping
systems with numerous ancillary elements such as expansion joints, drip traps, and isolation
valving. Each of these requires routine maintenance and safety inspection. In many cases these
elements become sources for energy loss. Annual maintenance of steam distribution was
described as a significant portion of overall expense in several of the surveyed systems.

Routine inspection, maintenance, repair, and renewal are essential elements of steam system
distribution efficiency. The lack of a “deferred maintenance” budget adequate to support this
sort of attention-to-detail was noted in many of our interviews. Routine communication and
consistent leadership were highlighted as necessary tools for negotiating responsibility for the
components of overall efficiency between plant operations, distribution system management,
and building maintenance teams.

4.2.1.1.2 Condensate Return

There are multiple potential thermal and mass losses along the path that steam (and then
condensate) takes from plant to building and back. Amongst other, often individually small, but
collectively accumulating sinks of heat and mass, losses routinely include:

 • the transfer of heat through insufficient, damaged or missing insulation on pipe, valves,
 fittings and supports
 • distribution system losses due to groundwater immersion, flooding events or city water
 subterranean leaks
 • loss of steam through leaks at bolted flanges, slip joints or threaded fittings,
 • loss of steam to condensate through faulty steam traps
 • loss of steam through pressure relief valves
 • loss of steam through atmospheric flash tank vents

Overall steam system efficiency (gas-into-useful-building-heat) was not routinely reported by
many of our sites. Few systems in the survey have the level of comprehensive building metering
necessary to accurately accumulate building loads for comparison with plant production (to
which accurate metering is typically monitored). Managing an expanding network of building
metering points into a single dashboard with automatic fault detection goes a long way to
quantifying system efficiency and identifying specific losses, a strategy identified as a best
practice by multiple systems. A single metric representing distribution losses as a percentage of
annual production is dependent on not only the extent and condition of the distribution
network, but annual utilization. Anecdotal estimates for the sites surveyed range between 5%
and 50%.

However, it should be noted that the sites IDEA surveyed could be considered better resourced
than many other systems, and have largely made expenditures on upgraded equipment,
optimization, and repairs that help keep their distribution losses manageable. Distribution
losses in steam systems will almost certainly be higher in systems that have not made upgrades
or implemented dedicated maintenance programs, which is often a result of lack of resources.
These losses can, in some circumstances (i.e. older systems with subpar investment and
maintenance) exceed 30%.

4.2.1.1.3 Hot Water Distribution

Lower temperature heating hot water distribution is in place in several of the systems, and
under active consideration or construction among many of those surveyed. In this concept,
liquid hot water at temperatures ranging from 140° F to 170° F is pumped from the plant to the
buildings through a direct buried piping network. The liquid exchanges heat to the building
either through an isolating heat exchanger or directly through air handler coils. Operating
advantages noted (Best Practices) included:

 • reduction in thermal losses due to lower temperatures
 • significant reduction in annual maintenance and safety risk
 • substantially lower costs associated with extension of an existing heating network to
 reach new building loads, compared to installation of new steam piping in a new
 concrete tunnel structure.

An implicit benefit of a conversion from steam to low temperature hot water is the potential to
utilize currently available technologies involving high COP, low temperature heat generated
through electric heat pump production schemes (described below). Lower distribution
temperatures are compatible with ground source heat pumps, wastewater heat pumps, heat
pumps that draw from the chilled water source, and/or cost-effective utilization of daily and
seasonal energy storage media such as water (tank thermal energy storage) or soils/aquifer
(geo-exchange). Recovery of data center heat can be integrated into a hot water district energy
network.

Biomass, used to fuel steam generation in several of the surveyed sites, is considered a

District-scale electric-driven heat pumps are increasingly being applied, often utilizing available
local sources. Innovative examples of ambient loop schemes, extracting heat from municipal
wastewater (National Western Center - CenTrio Denver) and implementation of a heat recovery
heat pump process that simultaneously recovers the heat that would otherwise have been
rejected through cooling tower water evaporation for re-distribution to buildings (Stanford
University and Ball State University). A variant of this concept using the earth as a seasonal
storage media (closed loop geo-exchange) is in use at Ball State, under construction at
Princeton University and in development at University of Virginia (UVA). More conventional and
widely used open-loop geothermal heat pump systems, often extracting and returning flow to
the local aquifer, can be very efficient and may be constructed as regional subsets of a larger
campus heating loop. Best Practices for design, construction, transition from steam, and
operation of campus electric heat pump loop systems are becoming clear, as the leaders in
early systems are actively sharing information with others through publications, presentations,
tours, etc. Among the Best Practices that have developed is the need to fully understand the
system’s current and projected annual and hourly heating and cooling characteristics in
advance of design, particularly the inherent energy losses in the existing distribution
infrastructure.

An important characteristic can be the existing loop piping hydraulics. Best practices may use
real-time hydraulic models to analyze the effectiveness of the loop, locate flow constraints and
analyze the impact of expanding the system as the campus grows/changes. Real time modeling
provides the advantage of analyzing loop conditions in multiple seasons and analyzing the
impact of large or new loads (buildings) on energy production.

Electric resistance and electrode boilers are another alternative, considered more often as a
supplement to support peak heating demands or for activation during times when low-cost
electric power can be procured. Although much less efficient in converting electric power to
heat (COP of 1 vs COP of 2-3 for many heat pump alternatives), initial installation costs of
electric boilers can be relatively low in a system with sufficient electrical service capacity.

The regional electric grid is the most common source of power driving these heat pump and
electric boiler systems. Although many public utilities have stated goals and published plans to
transition power generation portfolios away from fossil fuel generation, those plans often
extend decades into the future. It has proven possible, within the surveyed sites, to procure
“green power” contracts from the electric utility, or private entities in which the power
purchases are ascribed to physical renewable generation assets (such as Stanford University).
Other methods of coordinating district equipment operation with real time utility carbon

Electric and fossil fuel procurement rates, rate structures, and access to real time pricing vary
across the 40 sites surveyed and are strong influences on the economic practicality of
alternatives.

4.2.1.3 Steam Electrification

Some steam district energy systems are taking advantage of renewable electricity generation
(via the grid or direct investment in renewable installations) by replacing legacy gas boilers with
electric boilers. Electric boilers can be more efficient than standard gas units, and modern
electric boilers can now match the capacity of gas- or oil-fired boilers in a much smaller
footprint. Nearly 100% of the electrical energy used in an electric boiler is converted to heat, a
significant efficiency improvement over standard fossil fuel-fired units (source). Electric boilers
are particularly attractive options for large steam systems with aging assets, as individual units
can be swapped out as they reach end-of-life, without requiring a comprehensive retrofit of the
generation plant and distribution system.
Electric boilers also offer the advantage of ease of installation, simplified maintenance, and
faster startup and shutdown times. Electric boilers enable much more precise and rapid tuning
and can often be operated at much lower levels than equivalently sized gas-fired units without
compromising system efficiency, which in turn allows for more efficient and nuanced plant
staging algorithms.
System personnel considering the use of electric boilers should remain clear-eyed about the
costs involved with the equipment expenditure, including the electrical gear needed to supply
an electric boiler. System personnel should also consider whether their plant has the space
needed to accommodate the necessary ancillary equipment.
Finally, while water quality, including treatment for boiler make-up, is important to every plant,
electric boilers may require specific actions to avoid fouling the electrodes, including reverse
osmosis and demineralization, along with a condensate polisher.

4.2.2 Chilled Water (CHW)
4.2.2.1 Generation-side efficiency

Over half of the systems surveyed have participated in an organized chilled water optimization
program in the last decade. Many of those systems have employed some of the following:

Extensive use of VFDs

Variable-frequency drives (VFDs) are electrical devices that control the speed of an electric
motor by adjusting the frequency and voltage of the power supplied to the motor. These are
increasingly deployed across a range of District Energy Plant applications to improve control,
efficiency, and power consumption. Use of VFDs for pumping and cooling tower fan
applications has largely become standard procedure. The opportunity to adjust the motor
speed of vapor compression chillers, to follow load, reduce mechanical stresses, and reduce
electrical inrush current at startup substantially lowers motor power consumption across the
entire seasonal operating range. The ability to coordinate motor speeds to match loads across
parallel banks of pumps or chillers further improves plant efficiency and extends the life of
equipment because stress was lowered.

Part Load Consideration

The benefits of improving efficiency at part load, where district cooling plants spend most of
their time, are becoming recognized. Many systems use annual load profiles along with peak
capacity requirements in determining new equipment selections. Calculating a comparison of
equipment life cycle costs (which include not only initial price but estimated annual energy and
perhaps maintenance costs) has become a standard approach to selecting new and
replacement equipment. Partial load performance improvements through equipment staging,
variable speed drives, magnetic bearing chillers, pump array control sequencing, and condenser
temperature and flow control are found in many of the surveyed systems.

Absorption Chillers

Absorption chilling can improve efficiency of CHP systems by using waste heat generated
through power production during warmer months when heating demand is lower. Several
alternative technologies exist within the Absorption Chiller domain. Single effect units, the most
common among those surveyed, can operate on relatively low-quality heat such as heating
water or low pressure (15 psig) steam, with a COP on the order of 0.83 (18 lb/Ton-hr). Double
effect absorption chillers, capable of operating with efficiencies as high as COP 1.5 (10lb/Ton-
hr) but require higher quality input heat (125 psig). A third option found in District Energy
applications are direct fired absorption units that transfer high quality heat from the exhaust
stream of a gas turbine generator. These units have perhaps the highest efficiency when
applied in situations with extended periods of cooling demand.

Single effect absorption chillers utilizing waste heat from a cogeneration process were the most
common among our survey. Operators reported the best results when their absorption chillers
were continuously base loaded, rather than using them to load-follow as part load efficiencies
drop off dramatically. The inherent nature of these units makes them sensitive to rapid
changes in temperature and flow. While these technologies are not compatible with fully
transitioned hot water systems, they represent current best practices in a large number of
systems surveyed.

Steam Turbine Driven Chillers

Several of the surveyed sites employ steam turbine driven chillers to convert waste heat from
their CHP process into district cooling. Often configured as a back-pressure turbine aligned with
the drive shaft of a vapor compression water chiller, these units can modulate a higher-
pressure steam driven power flow, producing chilled water with a refrigerant process like the
electric fleet.

Free Cooling

4.3.2 Recovery Preparation
Even the best prepared systems can be brought offline unexpectedly. Surveyed systems
emphasized the importance of preparing to restore service when (not if) it is lost.

Recovery strategies:

 • Critical spares should be stocked with resilience in mind. Prestressed concrete cylinder
 pipe segments and repair saddles for each diameter of chilled water main and new
 chilled water service lateral isolation valve can be kept onsite.
 • Emergency repair agreements should be in place with underground mechanical
 contractors. All long-term maintenance contracts should include service level
 agreements for emergency service.
 • Control system programming, including local equipment PLCs, can be backed up with
 copies stored off site.
 • Relationship development and recurring plant familiarization training with local first
 responders including fire and police departments makes first responders more prepared
 and impactful.

 89

 • Know the ins-and-outs of insurance policies before they need to be used. Understand
 deductibles, rules for categorizing expenses, and who primary contacts are in each
 company.
 • In advance, determine capacity and siting requirements for temporary equipment, as
 well as interconnection points for fuel, water, steam, power, and sewer.
 • Establish emergency contracts and agreements with local vendors ahead of time (such
 as pipe fitters, electricians, and emergency fuel supplies) that includes wages, materials
 prices, and other variables. Additionally, bring in additional site personnel to track daily
 contract labor, materials, and project progress.

4.4 Technical/Engineering: Decarbonization
In 2022, IDEA conducted a survey of member campuses to catalogue their institutional
decarbonization commitments. The survey found that, of 113 total institutions, 105 had
explicitly stated goals to reduce the use of fossil fuels, reduce GHG emissions, or reach carbon
neutrality within the next few decades. Many downtown systems face a similar customer
demand for decarbonization, and many are now considering carbon emissions in addition to
economic advantage in their operational strategies. Systems are also integrating renewable
resources, such as solar thermal and PV, geo-exchange, and other technologies to reduce GHG
emissions in line with stated de-carbonization goals.

The systems surveyed for this project identified several engineering approaches to
decarbonization, ranging from relatively inexpensive building upgrades to wholesale pivots of
their business models. Those recommendations are listed below.

4.4.1 Reduce Demand
Demand reduction reduces the amount of capital capacity and fuel or electricity required to
serve a system’s load. Several systems surveyed mentioned that demand management plays a
pivotal role in their overall decarbonization efforts, either as the first phase of a larger system
upgrade or in lieu of more capital-intensive changes. Demand reduction projects involved:

 • Adjusting temperature set points
 • Installation of efficient lighting and lighting control
 • Replacing or resizing coils
 • Humidification and refrigeration controls/replacement
 • Air system conversion from constant volume to variable volume
 • Cleaning or replacing air handlers

 90

In addition to demand reduction projects reported during interviews with participating systems,
these demand reduction practices are often deployed in US DE systems:

 • Whole building re-commissioning and controls replacement/upgrades, which are often
 very cost-effective, and can result in significant load changes
 • Addition of heat recovery, which also offers opportunities for a large heating load
 reduction for the central plant
 • Targeting buildings for insulation upgrades

As mentioned above, in “Technical/Engineering: Efficiency” it is cost-effective and most
impactful to target the poorest-performing buildings on a district energy loop for interventions.
The first step in the process, if significant historical metering data isn’t available, the alternative
is to set baselines to compare building- and system-wide improvement to prioritize and
establish conditions.

4.4.1.1 Decarbonize Existing Generation

Decarbonizing energy generation and purchasing has the largest effect on the overall carbon
impacts of a district energy system. Interviewed systems identified a wide range of strategies:

 • Purchasing offsite renewables via PPA
 • Installing onsite renewables (including solar PV and solar thermal), and ideally coupling
 them with batteries or thermal storage
 • Installing Thermal Energy Storage to avoid emissions-heavy peak grid hours, lower
 overall generation load, defer addition of additional generation units, and make best
 use of onsite intermittent renewables
 • Add smaller low carbon generation (such as heat-recovery chillers, free cooling, turbine
 inlet cooling, or heat pumps) to existing generation assets

Vicinity Energy is accelerating the decarbonization of their downtown steam systems through
the rollout of “eSteam,” carbon-free steam generated via purchased renewable electricity and
using state-of-the-art electric boilers, heat pumps, thermal energy storage, and, where
possible, water from nearby rivers. By replacing individual fossil fuel boilers with electric
boilers, Vicinity is able to continue offering customers steam without service interruption or the
distribution and building-side upgrades required with the transition to hot water distribution.
Additionally, the costs of decarbonization are minimized through efficiency-based savings.
Vicinity’s size also allows the system to bulk-purchase renewable power from the grid at a
discounted wholesale rate unavailable to customers who elect to install in-building electric
heating or heat pump units.

These efforts will have a significant impact not only on Vicinity customers but also on the cities
the company serves: in a 2018 report issued by the city of Boston, it was estimated that
decarbonization of the city’s steam system could cut up to 20% of the emissions associated
with the energy use of Boston’s largest buildings.

 91

4.4.1.2 Implement System-Wide Changes

If sufficient financial resources are available, systems can achieve significant decarbonization
through more comprehensive efforts, including lake source cooling, steam to hot water
conversion, waste heat recovery, and geothermal/geo-exchange. The project team worked with
several systems that successfully implemented these changes and recommended specific best
practices, as illustrated below. Most systems that have embarked on major, system-scale
updates have emphasized the use of phased conversion: in many cases, systems can convert
sections of a campus or neighborhood to hot water, geo-exchange, etc. and take the
opportunity to test technologies and begin to accrue energy savings before transitioning the
entire system.

Lake Source Cooling: Cornell University

In the mid-90’s, Cornell embarked on a campus chilled water system redesign necessitated by
phasing out CFCs and increases in campus demand and load growth. Rather than replacing
existing chillers, the campus opted for lake-source cooling (LSC), which utilizes deep cold water
from nearby Cayuga Lake, retaining three chillers to meet demand peaks. While the upfront
costs were larger for LSC ($58 million, compared to $30 million for chiller replacement), it was
predicted to have substantially lower operating costs and designed for a 100-year life, enabling
a lower life cycle cost and attractive return on investment. LSC also reduced greenhouse gas
emissions, water use, sewer, and maintenance costs; and it dramatically improved energy
efficiency while being used as a teaching and research platform.

Cornell’s LSC system utilizes two variable flow loops: an open lake-water loop, and a closed
campus loop. Cold deep lake water enters the open loop and circulates past heat exchangers in
the plant, which cools the closed district heating loop and distributes chilled water across
campus. The warmed lake water is then returned to the shallow waters of the lake – the lake
loop and district cooling loop never mix. Since it came online in 2000, LSC has saved Cornell an
average of 25 million kWh of electricity per year compared to previous cooling methods, and
the overall energy usage required to cool Cornell has been reduced by about 85%. To
complement LSC, Cornell is now exploring the implementation of earth source heating (deep
direct use geothermal) to meet the campus’s heating needs. As the campus moves towards its
carbon neutrality goals, it is actively exploring ways to sustainably utilize its natural
environment, rather than relying simply on electrification.

In addition to LSC, Cornell has begun conversion from steam to hot water heating to advance
the campus sustainability goals. With the eventual goal of replacing the entire steam network,
the campus began with conversion of a North Campus residential complex, which includes five
new buildings, 800,000 gsf, 2500 beds, and a dining facility. Rather than extending the existing
steam network to accommodate the additional load, the campus established an energy transfer
station at the point of connection, where incoming 300+ degree steam is converted to 180-
degree hot water via vertical steam to hot water heat exchangers. The hot water is then
distributed to building plate and frame hot water to hot water heat exchangers using variable

 92

speed hot water pumps, where it is again stepped down to 130 degrees for in-building use. In
anticipation of a full-campus conversion to hot water, Cornell has chosen piping size and
materials that can simultaneously accommodate hotter-than ultimate design hot water (200
degrees coming out of the steam to hot water converter) which will be capable of supplying a
larger load as the temperature is gradually lowered. Material of choice is a prefabricated EN253
piping system, which is more expensive than other traditional hot-water pipes but does
accommodate up to 250°F operating temperatures, which offers the campus more flexibility as
it transitions away from steam.

Each hot water conversion project includes an accompanying energy conservation study and
project in the affected buildings. These projects take advantage of the synergies available
during construction and have dramatically reduced heating loads, lowered supply and return
temperatures in heating systems, and cost effectively reduced overall energy usage. A campus
wide continuous commissioning program maintains new and existing controls and control
strategies across all buildings.

Steam-to-Hot Water: University of Virginia (UVA)

As part of the 2015 master planning process, UVA explored conversion from steam and mid-
temperature hot water (MTHW) to low temperature hot water (LTHW). They did an initial study
with a leading engineering firm, then issued an RFP to create a design, largely inspired by the
Stanford system. Because the campus had to maintain operations, and in order to break up the
cost of conversion, they pursued a three-phase process to convert the majority of the academic
buildings:

 • Phase 1

Phase 1 focused on converting buildings on Medium Temperature Hot Water (MTHW) to Low
Temperature Hot Water (LTHW) while keeping the buildings online. Most of the work centered
inside the mechanical groups: establishing blended loops within buildings and slowly stepping
water temperatures down to 160°F while monitoring the systems. Across the 47 impacted
buildings, they were able to increase the ΔT from 30°F to 60°F.

 • Phase 2

Phase 2 of UVA’s transition to low-temperature hot water involved selection and installation of
a heat recovery chiller. They elected to install it at the north chiller plant, next to the main
heating plant, at the nexus of the largest chilled and hot water loops (the health system and
academic loops, respectively). The combined loads of these loops incentivized the team to
select an 1800-ton chiller, and the load profiles allow them to run the chiller year-round for
maximum efficiency. The heat recovery chiller supplies the summertime minimum load, which
can be supplemented by natural gas boilers over the course of the year.

 93

 • Phase 3 (underway)

Phase 3 involves conversion of remaining steam buildings to LTHW. This requires some building
system terminal equipment replacement, such as air handling units, steam cooking units, and
some older steam radiator systems. For the most part, these improvements will be
implemented as part of planned building renovation projects, though UVA had to install two
heat exchangers in two buildings that were not slated for renovation.

Waste Heat Recovery: National Western Center - CenTrio Denver

CenTrio has been contracted by the National Western Authority to develop and operate a
wastewater heat recovery-based district energy system for the National Western Center
campus in Denver, CO. The campus, which will house a “year-round urban hub for food and ag
discovery” will spread out over 250 acres adjacent to the South Platte River, is a joint venture
between the City and County of Denver, Colorado State University, the Western Stock Show
Association, History Colorado and the Denver Museum of Nature & Science. When complete,
the campus will have the largest sewer-heat recovery system in North America, sourcing nearly
90% of its heating and cooling from recycled thermal energy. Using this system, the campus will
avoid emitting an estimated 2,600 metric tons of carbon dioxide per year.

In addition to providing the significant carbon savings the National Western Authority was
looking for, sewer waste heat recovery is a particularly optimal solution for the campus. Part of
the original charge was for the campus to program the riverfront, which was partially blocked
by two above-ground municipal sewer lines. Installed in the 1960’s, the lines were placed above
ground to allow heat to escape before circulating to the treatment plant. In addition to
lowering the lines below grade, the waste-heat recovery system pulls off and uses that excess
heat, optimizing performance of both the district energy and sewer processing systems and
allowing increased pedestrian access to the riverfront.

Wastewater Heat Recovery is a very energy efficient electric heating system in which each
building in the district is conditioned with one or more water source heat pumps connected to
a two-pipe district loop capable of adding or removing thermal energy. Under typical
conditions, the (clean) district loop circulates through a filtered heat exchanger piped to a large
municipal sewer main with seasonal temperatures between 60°F (Winter) and 80°F (Summer).
The sewer is thus the primary energy source (or sink). Conventional gas fired boilers and cooling
towers supplement the system during heating and cooling extremes.

Although it is a proven approach in Canada and Northern Europe, this is the reportedly the
largest system of this type in the United States. To fully optimize the district energy system,
CenTrio negotiated sewer rights that gave them the ability to approve any new upstream
projects, allowing them to control for flow and temperature. They also established an operating
agreement to govern the responsibility and timing of maintenance, upgrades, and shutoffs.

 94

Geothermal Energy: Ball State University

Ball State University (BSU) has taken a phased approach to implementing geothermal energy on
campus, and has coupled this with a long-term, iterative approach to system optimization. As
building loads were tied over to the new lower temperature heat pump loop, it became evident
that planning and design had significantly underpredicted the magnitude of heat losses in the
original steam distribution piping system. This, in turn, leads to overprediction of the overall
campus heating to cooling ratio. Operational and physical adjustments needed to be made to
balance the annual temperature of the geo-exchange field. To support the geothermal system,
the utilities team opted to install four equal sized chillers – in retrospect, they feel it would have
been easier to tune the system during shoulder months if they had selected systems in a range
of sizes. Regardless, the utilities team spent 4-5 years testing optimal staging for the chillers,
and found their original strategy was creating inefficiencies: they had been staging multiple
chillers unnecessarily, assuming that they would have to run a unit at both the north and south
plant to keep the system balanced. In fact, they found that they could run a single chiller in
either plant and still serve the entire campus. Evaluating the ΔT for individual buildings has
helped them quickly identify specific areas of campus that are operating sub-optimally.

When BSU was evaluating heat pump chillers to produce 150°F water, they tested feasibility on
campus by setting all resets back to 150°F and studying building performance. The team found
that they generally had an oversized system to begin with: many buildings were able to operate
at the new temperature, and only a few struggled when ambient outdoor temperatures
dropped below 5°F. Rather than raising temperatures across campus, they addressed struggling
buildings by utilizing cross-connected cooling and heating coils, converting cooling coils to
heating when individual buildings needed temperature help.

Because BSU still has a steam load to serve healthcare and process needs, the utilities group
has also re-evaluated that system in the last few years. Steam is generated on the south side of
campus and must be pumped more than a mile away to north campus buildings, leading to
significant distribution losses. They’ve elected to slowly phase out distribution to more remote
sections of campus, instead installing gas package units that generally stand unused but can
kick on to serve healthcare needs or provide backup if the system experiences a fault. Through
this continuous, dynamic approach to optimization and revised operation management
practices, electrical consumption in the two plants has been reduced by 20-30% and combined
heating and cooling operation COPs have been raised to over 10.

4.5.1.2 Supply

As district energy systems become increasingly interconnected and complex, many systems
operators have incorporated varying levels of automation to improve system performance and
ease staffing challenges. Large systems with multiple interconnected plants have used system
automation to empower smaller, more efficient teams, ensure staff safety, and optimize multi
plant efficiency.

Comprehensive load forecasting has been used to drastically improve system efficiency and
operating costs. Systems can use metering data to build out and optimize forecasting models
and identify real-time anomalies. Systems have used meter data to answer questions such as,
“If we can predict the weather and occupancy of x buildings two days ahead, can we adjust the
cooling timing to maximize fuel and system efficiency?” As models are built out, dashboard
data can be used to regularly assess and validate their forecasting, fault detection, and
dispatch/demand models. Models can be adjusted to prioritize efficiency, reduce emissions,
and provide financial benefit.

Like forecasting algorithms, equipment staging protocols can be adjusted based on the system’s
efficiency, carbon, and financial goals. One system with a variety of differently sized chillers
adjusted its staging to run chillers at the most efficient point (based on load requirements, real
time cost of running chiller), rather than maxing out one chiller at a time when adding load.
Additional training and some automation helped operators adapt to this more challenging
operating protocol, and the system found the revised efficiency-centric staging strategy saved
12-14% off their dispatch costs. Another interviewed system elected to baseload with non-
variable chillers and load follow with variable speed units, supported by an optimization
platform that recommended operators add, shed, or tune specific chillers.

Most interviewed systems advocated for a combination of automation and human
management: in many cases, forecasting algorithms and staging models were used to make

 97

recommendations to operators, who then acted in response to the recommendations. Control
room dashboards were often used to remove guesswork in fault detection and load
management. While these dashboards can reduce guesswork, plant managers reiterated the
need for dashboards to be both highly scannable and intuitive.

Several systems turned to their purchase/produce/sell options to extract additional efficiency
and economic benefit from the electricity grid. Some systems used real-time market signals to
make daily purchasing decisions based on the price of grid energy, supported by a real-time ISO
contour map and qualified scheduling entity to forecast prices and loads. Others participated in
ancillary service markets via demand response and frequency-regulation agreements: Princeton
University, for example, receives control signals from PJM requesting the campus adjust its gas
turbine by up to 1 MW to supply frequency regulation to the ISO.

The University of Cincinnati has a reciprocal relationship with PJM, which allows them to
maximize the use of their CHP system. The CHP system is primarily used to generate steam and,
depending on the real-time price of generated versus purchased electricity, the generated
electricity may be distributed to campus or sold into the market. The utility also takes
advantage of its steam turbine generator to capture excess steam when the on-campus steam
load is low, but the electricity market is favorable to export power. PJM has also explicitly called
on the campus to buy or sell electricity to balance the larger grid: during unusually mild winter
periods, the ISO has paid the campus to absorb excess generation. Alternatively, during
particularly harsh cold snaps, the campus was able to offer additional capacity to the ISO while
supplying sufficient steam to the campus to fill the higher heating load. These market decisions
have helped the utility absorb overhead increases without raising costs over the last eight
years.

Rising grid prices have also forced many systems to adjust their operating procedures to avoid
exceptionally high peak rates. Systems such as Illinois State University (ISU) have found ways to
load shed on predicted peak load days to avoid elevated pricing:

ISU lies within the Midwest subregion of the Midcontinent Independent System Operator
(MISO) grid, which, due to the decommissioning of coal plants and slow uptake in gas powered
plants, anticipates capacity shortages on peak load days. Including imports from other MISO
regions, the Midwest is 1,230 MW short of its planned reserve margin, raising the potential of
grid vulnerability to blackouts and brownouts on peak days. As a result, the recent annual
capacity auction surged from $5 to $236.66 per megawatt-day. In anticipation of these peak
days, to avoid both the potential for substantial unbudgeted price increases and power
interruptions, ISU is working with university leadership, its consortium, and other teams on
campus to pre-plan load shedding strategies that can be rapidly deployed to minimize the
campus’s energy use. The goal is to incorporate these mechanisms into the automated controls
system, though some of the load shedding may be manual. Luckily, ISU already participates in
the MidAm demand response program, and last year was able to cut load from 16 to 8 MW
during a demand response instance. The utilities team is working closely with campus
partnerships to minimize the disruption load shedding will have on campus occupants and

 98

prime the community for a warmer-than-usual summer, as campus buildings temperature
setpoints will be reset to 74°F across the board, rather than the previous 68°F. The new
university president is a strong partner in this, and, combined with other academic and
administrative leadership, have worked with the community to understand which areas of
campus are likely to be most impacted.

Thermal Energy Corporation’s (TECO) TES tank is also run in concert with the grid on an
economic dispatch model. Originally the tank was charged at night during off-peak hours and
discharged during peak daytime hours, taking advantage of time-of-use pricing. During their
larger CHW optimization project, an optimization firm evaluated historical trends and provided
additional charge/discharge windows based on the actual cost of the stored energy (the water
that is cooled in the tank). They also found that they don’t always need to fully discharge the
tank during the day if the economics don’t require it, which has the additional benefit of
keeping some stored energy on hand in case a chiller is suddenly offline. TECO also uses the TES
tank to protect them from high future demand charges by lowering the electricity taken from
the grid during ERCOT’s four coincident peaks, which are the days when the grid hits maximum
demand. ERCOT uses these calculations to determine demand charges for the next year, so
TECO works with market intelligence to anticipate those events and reduce the system’s use of
grid energy by internal generation and discharging the TES tank. TECO is currently planning to
install 2 additional pumps on the TES tank, which will increase the volumetric flowrate
discharged from the tank. Currently, at 64,000-ton hours, the tank essentially operates as an
8000-ton chiller for 8 hours, but TECO rarely has such long peak periods. Therefore, the tank
would provide more value if it could discharge more quickly, providing more chill water capacity
during concentrated time periods, further reducing plant electrical demand.

4.5.2 Personnel Management
More than any single engineering or operations practice, interviewed systems reinforced the
necessity of building a confident, knowledgeable, and supported team. Building a strong base of
in-house knowledge not only allowed some systems to build and retro commission their own
controls algorithms, automation systems, and dashboards, but enabled faster and more
effective incident detection and management, higher levels of employee satisfaction, and less
anxiety when embarking on new projects. The most highly automated and interconnected
systems frequently reiterated that happy and well-trained employees were make-or-break: a
system is only as effective as the people who manage it.

US-based district energy systems are currently experiencing widespread staffing challenges: as
knowledgeable senior operators retire; managers are competing with more lucrative private
industry and utility organizations to attract young engineers. Additionally, young operators and
engineers are gravitating towards more specialized early training and employment tracks,
meaning few come into district energy positions with broad operating and engineering
knowledge. While many systems would prefer to simply offer more competitive wages, that is
rarely feasible.

 99

In response, systems are investing in long-term, comprehensive training programs to both
develop their existing workforce for improved performance and employee satisfaction, and to
attract less-experienced operators to train into skilled operators.

Some programs focused on employee advancement: many of these systems feature a
predetermined step system designed to guide new hires from journeymen electricians to
commissioning and high-skill projects on a preset schedule. In some cases, employees gain
licensure through the program, and others connect advancement to specific levels with pay
raises.

Other programs focused on employee mastery: running regular drills to improve response
times and build employee instincts. These included drills such as:

 • Pre-hurricane season evacuation and management drills
 • Tabletop cybersecurity drills
 • Manual operation drills before each heating and cooling season
 • Recovery practice where the plant is tripped, and operators must reestablish service

Systems that involved lower-level operators in non-technical projects such as documentation
noticed higher levels of employee ownership. Similarly, systems found the most success in
implementing training programs when they emphasized improvement, rather than punishment
for not achieving perfection. The concept is that one should trip but not die with an error. Also,
when an error does occur it is important that the root cause be determined and documented.
Documentation can be a tool to train others on what should be avoided.

5 Practices for Assessing and Enhancing District
 Energy Cybersecurity and Related Risks
As little as 15 years ago, most Industrial Control Systems (ICS) that provided the operational
backbone for District Energy, Distributed Generation and Microgrid systems were stand-alone
assets, unconnected to IT networks or the outside world. These Operational Technology (OT)
systems were typically custom-built air-gapped systems isolated from business related
Information Technology (IT) networks. They used different protocols to communicate and
process large volumes of data.

With the modern trend towards increased digitalization, it is common to have production
assets, distribution components, end user devices in buildings and desktop, laptops and mobile
devices linked together by a myriad of sensor, controls, communication networks and software
that collectively enable efficient, optimized and reliable operations supported by remote work.
The Covid-19 pandemic dramatically increased the need for and importance of connectivity.

 100

Business functions of billing and data dashboards often leverage or use traditional
Informational Technology (IT) tools and networks. These advanced and interconnected,
remotely accessible systems create risks from cyber security breaches, malicious attacks or
unintended incidents leading to system outages, personal injury to field workers and citizens,
and infrastructure damage.

Many district energy and microgrid systems have created procedures and best practices that
can help educate and provide examples of how to mitigate risk and maintain operations, often
modeled on the NIST Guide to Industrial Controls Systems (ICS) Security. This document
provides an overview of how select systems secure Industrial Control Systems (ICS), including
Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) systems, Distributed Control Systems (DCS),
and other control system configurations such as Programmable Logic Controllers (PLC), while
addressing their unique performance, reliability, and safety requirements. The document
provides an overview of ICS and typical system topologies, identifies typical threats and
vulnerabilities, and identifies industry-recommended security countermeasures to mitigate the
associated risks.

5.1 Introduction
District energy systems and microgrids have become increasingly flexible and complex to serve
customer loads and generate savings via more integrated and effective business models.

This evolution has necessitated the digitalization of operational technologies, and created a
new generation of prosumers: district energy customers that both consume and produce
energy for the district energy system, and district energy systems that both consume and
produce energy for the larger electricity grid. The ability to rapidly shift generation strategy
between buying energy, selling energy, and storing energy based on real time price (or even
carbon emission) data has allowed district energy systems and microgrids to provide ancillary
services in select markets, generating customer savings and providing flexibility to an already
stressed electrical grid. District energy systems can be profoundly valuable investments to

 101

support the larger energy economy: they can facilitate decarbonization, reliability, and
resiliency for customers while limiting the need for significant investment in centralized
electricity generation, transmission, and distribution.

These systems, and their advanced capabilities, are built on highly sophisticated Industrial
Controls Systems (ICS), which have in turn evolved to incorporate complex and autonomous
assets, holistic forecasting and staging strategies, and flexible loads. Securing ICS against cyber
security threats has become a challenging but necessary component in guaranteeing the
resiliency and reliability district energy is known for.

5.1.1 IT v OT
The field of cybersecurity evolved to protect information technology (IT) networks, which are
fundamentally different from the operational technology (OT) networks that support district
energy and microgrid systems. IT and OT systems often intersect, where business functions
(such as billing) feed directly from OT outputs. Organizations with robust IT infrastructure often
have policies and personnel devoted to IT-centric cybersecurity, which cannot be fully
translated to OT applications. IT security generally centers on data protection, while OT security
focuses on operational continuity and physical safety, amongst other differences.

5.1.2 Threats
Cybersecurity threats to ICS can take multiple forms, each of which require different
approaches to mitigation:

 • Intentional attacks from outside actors
 • Accidental errors caused by users (either from undertraining or understaffing)
 • Equipment failure, supply disruption
 • Man-made and natural disasters

In some cases, physical system hardening, and installation of robust firewalls can significantly
reduce the risk of incidents significant enough to impact customers, while others require more
substantial policy and training programs. In all cases, systems are often required to increase
investment in cybersecurity programs, which can leave smaller utilities more vulnerable.
Smaller utilities may be more susceptible to:

 • Lack of technological ability to identify threats
 • Organizational failures
 • Lack of clear understanding of risk-based practices
 • Lack of clarity around how to comply with regulators

 102

The consequences for cybersecurity breaches can be significant. The National Information
Solutions Cooperative offers the following list of potential impacts of ICS breaches:

 • Blocked or delayed flow of information through ICS networks, which could disrupt ICS
 operation.
 • Unauthorized changes to instructions, commands, or alarm thresholds, which could
 damage, disable, or shut down equipment, create environmental impacts, and/or
 endanger human life.
 • Inaccurate information sent to system operators, either to disguise unauthorized
 changes, or to cause the operators to initiate inappropriate actions, which could have
 various negative effects.
 • ICS software or configuration settings modified, or ICS software infected with malware,
 which could have various negative effects.
 • Interference with the operation of equipment protection systems, which could endanger
 costly and difficult-to-replace equipment.
 • Interference with the operation of safety systems, which could endanger human life

The potential for cybersecurity threats to incur both financial and physical damage to district
energy, microgrid, and distributed generation systems has led some insurance companies to
include cybersecurity preparedness in their rate generation deliberations.

5.1.3 Regulatory Landscape
A significant challenge toward effective cybersecurity implementation is the patchwork of
oversight and regulatory requirements most district energy, microgrids, and Distributed Energy
Resources (DERs) fall under. There is no unified cybersecurity framework that covers both the
public and private sectors, despite most critical infrastructure being privately held and
managed. At the federal level, three departments hold responsibility for various overlapping
components of critical infrastructure cybersecurity:

 • Department of Homeland Security’s Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency
 (DHS CISA), which is responsible for generation centralized cybersecurity policy for
 critical infrastructure. DHS works with Sector Risk Management Agencies (SRMAs),
 including the DOE, to help the private sector voluntarily “address cyber vulnerabilities,
 threats, and hazards.”
 • The Department of Energy (DOE) acts as the SRMA for the energy sector in a supporting,
 rather than regulatory, role by funding research and development and organizing
 training programs.
 • The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) regulates the reliability of the bulk
 electric system, including setting cybersecurity standards. FERC has worked with the
 North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC) to develop and institute the most
 comprehensive cybersecurity requirements, standards, and systems, including incident
 reporting, information protection, systems identification and categorization, supply
 chain management, and information sharing.

 103

To meet the (NERC Critical Infrastructure Protection) NERC-CIP’s regulatory requirements, Bulk
Electrical Systems must comply with 14 cybersecurity and physical security measures, which
include more than 30 specific tech and operational standards. However, less than 20% of the US
electricity system falls under mandatory FERC regulations: this is the gap that almost all district
energy, microgrid and distributed resources fall into.

To fill this gap, the National Institute of Standards and Technology within the US Department of
Commerce has developed the “Guide to Industrial Controls Systems (ICS) Security.” District
energy, microgrid, and distributed generation systems are not required to abide by the Guide,
but many have chosen to model their security frameworks and practices around its
recommendations. This report draws from the Guide, along with several additional industry
sources and anonymized interviews: the full guide can be found here.

5.2 Best Practices
5.2.1 Security Audit, Risk Identification and Vulnerability Assessment
for Prioritization
District Energy, Distributed Generation and Microgrid owners/operators, along with key
stakeholders including the central IT and Internal Audit departments, vendors and business
partners, are actively engaged and critically focused in identifying threats and risk areas to
assess vulnerability. Central Information Security and Internal Audit Teams routinely require
these audits to reduce risk.

Since every system serving clients may be unique, a Security Audit customized to the nuances
of your actual system entails performing a risk assessment of the facility, system assets,
employees, and vendors to understand risks that could negatively impact your organization.
This very important first step generates useful information to prioritize the specific risks that
will have the most negative impact and allow an optimal use of resources to avoid them, as well
as establish recovery plans. Third-party audits have been found to be very informative in
looking at what systems are tied to what other systems, not just technically but operationally.
However, due to the nuances of your system, operational audits may be better served by
obtaining input from your management, facility leaders and supervisors. This nuanced input can
be then vetted by a third party, if desired.

Well-defined maps of potential threats, risk levels and impacts can help prioritize strategies for
assets and systems and can help develop a Disaster Recovery Plan. A key deliverable is an
evaluation of the processes and their vulnerabilities, and create necessary recommendations
based on standards and best practices aimed at mitigating risk. Some sites conduct biweekly
vulnerability scans, which are used to check ports on control system devices. It should be
noted, however, that performing a vulnerability scan can cause individual components to shut
down, potentially triggering a full system lockdown. For this reason, it is important to test one

 104

component at a time, and report shutdowns to manufacturers. A sample of key capabilities of a
third-party Cybersecurity Audit, as identified by Johnson Controls, can be found in Appendix A.

Audits should review all components of the ICS for vulnerabilities that can be exploited,
including:

 • Policy & procedure
 • Architecture & design
 • Configuration & maintenance
 • Physical & network security
 • Software development

5.2.2 Network and Control System Isolation
It is important to implement network separation or segmentation and add extra layers of
security to protect key assets. OT networks connecting District Energy, Distributed Generation
and Microgrid system components and processes need to have the required isolation from
other IT networks using both appropriate physical security and robust firewall barriers.

Utility Operations are best served by a completely isolated network and server system from the
main Campus system with a process control network (fiber, switches, servers, etc.) separate
from the campus and/or business enterprise operations with hardened and well monitored
firewalls. Mixing process controls network with the administrative business network is a very
risky practice, and as a result, many sites have opted to either fully separate the OT network
from the IT network or put a series of rigorous checks in place at the interconnection. Options
for restricting access to ICS network and network activity, when air-gapping isn’t a viable
option, include:

 • Unidirectional gateways
 • “DMZ” network architecture w firewalls to prevent network traffic from passing
 between corporate and ICS networks
 • Separate authentication mechanisms for ICS and corporate networks
 • Zero cloud connected programs (except in very specific cases)
 • OT networks should not have internet access, FTP, email, remote access
 • Should have rigorous change control procedures for network equipment, configuration,
 software changes

An “air-gapped” computer or network is one that has no network interfaces, either wired or
wireless, connected to outside networks to isolate the OT systems from networks, such as the
public Internet or an unsecured local area network. Many computers, even when they are not
plugged into a wired network, have a wireless network interface controller (and are connected
to nearby wireless networks to access the Internet and update software). This represents a
security vulnerability, so air-gapped computers either have their wireless interface controller
permanently disabled or physically removed.

 105

With an “air-gapped” system, to move data such as energy use data and other data for business
and research, it is necessary to write data to a physical medium and physically move it between
the OT computers and one accessed over IT networks.

At one site “air-gapping” was replaced by more advanced network TAPs (test access points or
terminal access points) to provide access to the traffic required to secure, monitor, and manage
the following three networks continuously and efficiently:

 1. Data/Applications Network
 2. Balance of Plant Network
 3. Power Producers Network

TAPs are the first step in the process of providing pervasive visibility across physical, virtual and
cloud infrastructures, accessing 100 percent of the network traffic on each tapped link.

Additional valuable tools for network protection include:

 • Data Diodes
 • Data Encryption
 • Firewalls
 • Intrusion Detection and Prevention
 • Malware/Antivirus Software
 • Vulnerability Assessment Tools

5.2.3 Protecting ICS components from exploitation
Even with a fully air-gapped OT network, individual ICS components can be vulnerable to
exploitation. Components can be protected by:

 • Implementing necessary security patches
 • Restricting use of USB drives
 • Disabling unused ports
 • Restricting privileges based on role
 • Establishing usage audit trails and complete operation documentation
 • Maintenance of a system inventory that tracks all components’ software versions
 • Restricting the modification of data: Best practice sites limit remote access to read-only
 privileges for most OT staff. Where more access is needed, access is gated by requiring a
 login to a site Virtual Private Network (VPN) coupled with two-factor authentication. At
 some sites remote access is monitored continuously by an intrusion detection system
 and the list of who has access is audited on a schedule.

 106

5.2.4 Utility Controls Network Security Policy
Policy is as valuable as technical and physical security measures for protecting OT systems. As
technologies evolve and staff members turnover, a central bank of security policies can protect
continuity, prevent unnecessary improvisation, and inform intentional deviations from standard
practice. Policies and practices collected from surveyed members include:

 • Deny all, permit by exception approaches to network access
 • Regularly scheduled audits
 • Maintenance procedures, such as coordination with vendors and technicians for patches
 o Coordination with vendors, technicians
 • Restricting (or prohibiting) access via removable storage or Cloud-base applications
 • Planning practices, including:
 o Development of contingency plans
 o Configuration management
 o Business continuity planning
 o Disaster recovery planning
 o Incident response
 ▪ preparation
 ▪ detection
 ▪ analysis
 ▪ containment
 ▪ eradication
 ▪ recovery
 ▪ reporting

In addition to reporting incidents out to governing and regulatory bodies, many systems have
implemented incident databases that allow them to record the details of the incident, a
description of the recovery process and eventual fix, root cause analysis, and recommendations
for follow up and any necessary retraining or procedures. Keeping diligent records minimizes
the risk of repeated errors and may speed up the recovery process of similar future incidents.

One campus developed a “Disaster Recovery Plan” as defined below:

 The Disaster Recovery Plan is comprised of documents, resources, and procedures to be used
 in the event of disaster at the plants and related energy distribution networks located
 throughout the campus. Each supported application or platform has a section containing

 minimizing disruption to campus operations. The plan is designed to simplify otherwise
 complex decision‐making processes if and when a disaster occurs. Sections are included to
 outline the critical personnel and responsibilities necessary to perform the recovery tasks
 and an organizational structure for the recovery process. This plan will be updated annually
 at a minimum as changes to organizational structure and utilities systems are made. Due to

 107

 the sensitive nature of the information contained in the plan, it should be treated as a
 confidential document.

 Disaster Recovery (DR) is the process an organization uses to recover access to critical
 infrastructure needed to resume the performance of normal business functions after the
 event of either a natural disaster or disaster caused by humans. This plan addresses the
 most severe and likely disasters. Less severe occurrences and those of lower probability are
 historically controlled at the appropriate management level as a part of the ongoing
 operational plan.

5.2.4.1 Oversight and Authority

The administrative management oversight of the system should be limited to a small team of
IT/OT professionals. All system hardware/software changes, updates etc. should be approved
through the submission of a request to make the change. The request should go through an
authorized group “Management of Change Workflow” approval process review.

 108

5.2.4.2 On-site Physical Access

Being cyber secure starts with physical security. Make sure that servers and control systems are
behind locked, monitored doors. Also, make sure the people in the building belong there and
visitors are not allowed to roam around by themselves.

Other approaches include a perimeter fence, closed circuit cameras, added security, locking of
doors and key equipment as needed. Have an operator monitoring the system. Some owners
have workstations in a lock box with a key that is not widely distributed. Some sites do
penetration tests from outside network to plant systems.

5.2.4.3 System Updates, Patch Management and Compliance Checks

In addition to the regular practice of software patching and continuous network system
monitoring, some systems also conduct periodic system compliance checks to ensure their
networks are secure.

Consistent, systematic patch management is an essential requirement for a secure facility. It
reduces the attack surface, improves the overall security of OT systems, and reduces
vulnerability risk.

Utilities staff have made it a practice to do the patches themselves with training from vendors
rather than have vendors doing them.

Patches for OT systems are thoroughly tested by both the vendor of the industrial control
system and asset owners or automation engineers prior to implementation. Due to concerns
over uptime requirements, asset owners must plan and schedule ICS updates months in
advance. Revalidation may also be required as part of the update process.

Some sites are selective about not installing all patches that a vendor rolls out, but only critical
ones to avoid any unintended consequences affecting operations.

5.2.4.4 Employee education and vulnerability training

Well-trained and committed employees are invaluable for running a secure OT network. Good
cybersecurity requires skilled teams to understand baseline operations, detect and respond to
anomalous cyber activity, reduce the “dwell time” of cyber attackers, and implement layered
cyber defenses. OT teams often work closely with IT (networking, server and application)
cybersecurity teams, but it is valuable to have OT-specific security personnel with cross-
functional experience as technicians or controls engineers and across security disciplines.
Training programs can be held on a regular cycle or self-paced online and may include subjects
such as email/phishing awareness, network security, and department policy. Some systems
have developed training programs or programs co-created with their central IT team, but

5.2.5 Partnerships and Budget to Enhance Cybersecurity
5.2.5.1 Partnering with Central IT

Utilities department staff should consider Central IT as their ally and comply with the standards
that are required and consult routinely with subject matter experts within Central IT to ensure
compliance. Personal relationships are important, as is staying in touch with Campus Security.
At some sites the Utility group allows the co-location of IT fiber in utility tunnels and helps
monitoring of the fiber and network cabling and infrastructure. The OT control system can
monitor temperature and humidity of IT equipment in data centers and at HVAC units serving IT
assets and IT system components. At some sites the internal Utilities and OT cyber-security
teams meet every quarter to share best practices and show each other what they are doing at
their facility/work area. Sharing news/events when needed, communications about ICS CERT
notifications from DOE, etc. is done when needed.

5.2.5.2 Vendor Relationships and Emergency Vendor Support
OEM

At some sites, only Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) staff are allowed access on a ‘trust
but verify’ basis. A staff person is assigned to monitor activity. Sites have a list of who needs to
be contacted in each place and give a 24X7 emergency call-in number as part of the service
contract to handle emergencies.

One campus system implements specific cybersecurity procedures for its OT system, above and
beyond the protocols in place for the rest of campus, in part because cyberattacks sometimes
come via third-party contractors whose cybersecurity standards are lower than those in place
at the utility department. When contractors visit the campus, they must follow all of the utility
system’s protocols. For example, if a PLC is being programmed, utility personnel take the
program and install it themselves. Contractors are not allowed to use USB drives without first
being cleared by utility staff, and whatever systems are in place are not allowed to be
controlled outside the utility, such as through a cloud computing setup.

The utility’s vendors are required to notify utility personnel when they find any problems that
could affect operation or security, then the utility works with the vendor to implement a patch
to address the problem. They look for vendors that pre-test patches and updates, so there’s
more confidence that these won’t increase vulnerability. It also helps vendors distinguish
themselves regarding their thoroughness and preparation.

5.2.6 Checklist of Actions
Managing cybersecurity for district energy systems is critical for system operators and
managers. It should include a thorough evaluation of the system and consideration of a
cybersecurity framework to offer insight into the experience to date and what to consider when
deciding priorities and investments.

This checklist, collated from IDEA District Energy systems and the District Energy Magazine
article “Cybersecurity: A framework for system evaluation” by Tom Thomalla Jr., is a suggested
list of actions and not a substitute for recommended actions at a specific site:

 • Deploy multiple layers of network security that include intrusion prevention systems
 on two redundant external connections.
 • Incorporate air gaps to separate the operations network from the central IT network
 • Use industry-standard cybersecurity protection and conduct regular audits.
 • Find an outside firm to provide a cybersecurity audit or assessment. This could be an IT
 security consultant, a service offered through an existing IT vendor, or IT security
 expertise within a financial auditing firm. OT security expertise can be harder to find.
 Your ICS vendor may provide services or be able to point you in the right direction or
 seek leads in the IDEA forums.
 • Perform phishing tests. This will show whether employees know how to identify and
 properly handle malicious email links and attachments. You may be surprised at the
 results. There are many free and low-cost options to do this without a major in-
 vestment, such as PhishMe or KnowBe4. Your existing IT staff or IT vendor should be
 able to run one of these tests with minimal effort.
 • Consider a penetration test, or simulated cyberattack, to check your company’s
 security vulnerabilities and defenses. But first assess the value to your organization in
 the context of everything else that can be implemented. If you know you have large
 security gaps to fix and are just getting started on a cybersecurity program, the test may
 be a better fit for later in your protocol development. In other words: It may make most
 sense to be sure your front door is locked before paying a professional to try to break in.
 • Keep in mind that your risk assessment and security procedures need to consider the
 multiplicity of factors at play in your district energy system. The system must be
 considered in the development of cybersecurity plans, as different areas of security
 concern can be interdependent. Measures taken to mitigate risk may have tradeoffs.
 Closing a security gap in one area could create unforeseen challenges in another. For
 example, increasing password requirements may prove to be difficult for some IT

 111

 systems or undesirable in OT systems. Security patching may reduce vulnerabilities but
 increase risk of downtime due to the patching or complications from patching.
 • Have a security-focused employee join the InfraGard. InfraGard is a program
 partnership between the FBI and the private sector that is focused on protection of
 critical infrastructure. It can be a very informative exchange.
 • Train your users on cybersecurity. People are an important first line of defense. Find
 online or in-person training methods that fit your culture and can be supported
 consistently by management. Create a culture where employees want to help guard
 your systems.
 • Talk to your IT/OT staff to better understand the “Internet of Things (IoT)/Industrial
 Internet of Things (IIoT)” in your environment. While beyond the scope of this article,
 be aware of technology that creeps into your environment. Vendors are offering
 technology services that can bypass IT and put the organization at risk. This includes IoT
 devices like smart thermostats, smart speakers, networked vending machines, or
 security cameras and IIoT like smart meters, water treatment monitoring systems, and
 systems that send plant performance data to the cloud for analysis. If it has an “app” or
 uses “the cloud,” you should be paying attention.
 • Just as for other operations protocols, you need to have a plan for recovery from –
 and not just prevention of – a security breach.

In conclusion, customers and users depend on us to provide reliable energy services. In addition
to contingency plans for outages and other system interruptions, Cybersecurity should also
become an integrated part of the operations and management approach. It takes some work at
the onset and ongoing diligence, but with the right framework and prioritization, it is
immensely valuable.
