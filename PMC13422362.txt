Maternal mental health and child development before and after an extreme cyclone in Malawi
Authors: Megan Kowalcyk, Maria M Crespo-Llado, Christabell Mdhluli, Stella Chifumbi, Victoria Mapemba, Meraby Funsani, Chimwemwe Kalengo, Nginache Nampota-Nkomba, Miriam Laufer, Melissa Gladstone, Andrea Buchwald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change is increasing the frequency and severity of cyclones and hurricanes, with the most severe impact occurring in low-resource settings. Women and children are among the most vulnerable to adverse health impacts of these severe weather events. Despite this, there is limited data on how cyclones and hurricanes impact maternal mental health or child development in low-resource settings.MethodsWe recruited pregnant women and their newborns into a prospective cohort study between 2018 and 2022 in Blantyre, Malawi and assessed maternal mental health, child development, and growth repeatedly from one to five years of age. Tropical Cyclone Freddy hit Blantyre on March 11, 2023, severely impacting our study site. We assessed the impact of cyclone exposure on study outcomes; maternal mental health, child neurodevelopment, and child growth, classifying measurements based on time since cyclone. We utilized generalized estimating equations logistic regression to estimate the effect of cyclone exposure on study outcomes.ResultsA total of 858 mother-child dyads were enrolled in the follow up cohort. Of these 838 mothers had at least one mental health assessment, 802 children had neurodevelopmental assessments, and 844 children had growth measurements. Cyclone exposure was associated with an increased odds of stunting with risk rising both in the first six months (Odds Ratio (OR) = 3.40; 95% Confidence Interval (CI): 2.55–4.51) and more than six months after the event (OR = 6.05; 95% CI: 4.3–8.35), compared to pre-Cyclone exposure assessments. In contrast, the odds of maternal distress and developmental delay decreased during the first six months following Cyclone Freddy.ConclusionsThe effects of Cyclone Freddy on maternal and child health in Malawi are complex. Strengthening of maternal and child health resources may help mitigate post-disaster consequences and prevent long-term health problems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44263-026-00309-3.Keywords: Maternal health, Mental health, Natural disasters, Child health, Neurodevelopment, Sub-Saharan Africa

Background

Climate change is one of the most pressing global health challenges of our time [1], driving variable temperature, precipitation, and extreme weather events [1, 2]. Extreme weather events, such as cyclones, are increasing in intensity, frequency and duration, with both direct and indirect impacts on health. These include heightened mortality from physical injury, malnutrition and increased exposure to infectious diseases [3]. In addition to these physical effects, extreme weather events are significant causes of mental health difficulties [3]. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reports that extreme weather events can cause mental health trauma, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among affected individuals [4]. Consistent with this, studies show that climate-related natural disasters are associated with PTSD, generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and depression [5, 6]. These adverse mental health outcomes appear to persist in the months to years following a natural disasters and can be further exacerbated by repeated exposure to such events [5–7]. Yet to date, there is no published research on the adverse mental health impacts of cyclones in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA).

Among populations exposed to natural disasters, pregnant women and mothers are among the most vulnerable to adverse mental health outcomes [6, 8–10], with potentially profound and long-term consequences for maternal and child health [11–13]. These consequences may include increased risks of low birth weight, behavioral problems, delayed cognitive development, and poorer social competence [12–15]. In parallel, children who directly experience natural disasters face elevated risks of experiencing psychological trauma, anxiety, and depression [6]. The stress associated with these experiences can have long-lasting effects on child development contributing to poorer academic performance and social functioning [16].

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICS) are disproportionately impacted by climate change, with women and children bearing the greatest burden due to heightened vulnerability. Available evidence suggests that children living in SSA -and Malawi in particular- experience high rates of stunting [17] and presumed high rates of neurodevelopmental delay, largely driven by environmental factors [18, 19]. Poor maternal mental health is also common in SSA with increased distress documented during the post-partum period [20] and a higher prevalence reported among adolescent mothers. However, the extent to which climate-related extreme weather events contribute to maternal mental health problems and subsequent child developmental outcomes in this context remains poorly understood. The lack of research is particularly evident in SSA, with virtually no published studies exploring these connections.

Tropical Cyclone Freddy formed in the Indian Ocean on February 14, 2023, and traversed the region for more than five weeks. Freddy was, at the time, both the longest-lasting and highest intensity tropical cyclone ever recorded [21]. The storm hit Malawi on March 11th, causing extensive damage to the southern region of the country. At least 1,200 people in Malawi were killed and at least 1,724 people injured, making this the second deadliest cyclone in the region [22, 23]. Over 883,000 households were affected, forcing 659,000 people to leave their homes and shelters [22]. Schools were closed, and the president of Malawi declared a state of disaster in the affected areas [22, 24]. The storm caused significant damage to roads and bridges; [25] in just one day, over a month’s worth of rain fell, amounting to six months of precipitation in six days [26].

Beyond the immediate damage and loss of life, the cyclone is likely to have had substantial long-term negative health effects through multiple pathways including increased food insecurity and heightened transmission of infectious disease (Fig. 1). Cropland and livestock in the area were severely impacted, with approximately 204,833 hectares of land destroyed and many livestock killed or injured, resulting in potential long-term disruptions to the food supply in Malawi [22]. Flooding also disrupted water, sanitation, and hygiene infrastructure, and disrupted healthcare utilization (Fig. 1). Disruptions to health services have the greatest impact among populations requiring increased care, including mothers and people living with HIV who have more frequent need for interactions with the health system for postnatal healthcare [22, 27], management of complications related to HIV, and antiretroviral therapy supply [28].

Longitudinal cohort studies that gather data before, during, and after extreme weather events are essential to understanding how climate change, with more frequent and intense extreme weather, worsens existing vulnerabilities in SSA, such as maternal psychological distress, child neurodevelopmental delays, and stunting. An ongoing cohort study at the time of Cyclone Freddy provided a unique opportunity to examine the effects of a major climate-related disaster on maternal mental health and child development. Leveraging these prospectively collected data, the present study aims to assess the impact of Cyclone Freddy on maternal mental health, child neurodevelopment, and stunting among women and their children living in communities severely affected by the cyclone.

Methods

Study Setting

This study utilizes data from a cohort study of mother-infant pairs recruited in Malawi from 2018 to 2022 [29, 30]. The cohort was designed to examine the effect of HIV exposure in utero on child health and development. Pregnant women both living with and without HIV, were recruited during their second trimester of pregnancy at their first antenatal care visit at either Ndirande Health Centre or Bvumbwe Health Center, both government-run primary health centers in Southern Malawi. Study recruitment has been described previously [29, 30]. Ndirande Health center serves individuals living near Blantyre – in a highly urban area with a population density of 189 people/km2 [31]. In contrast, Bvumbwe is in Thyolo district – serving a semi-rural population with an agriculture-based economy. Both locations were severely impacted by the cyclone, with substantial flooding and structural damage.

Screening for women included an ultrasound to determine gestational age and screening for HIV infection. Women were eligible if attending their first antenatal care visit or were enrolled within 28 days of the first antenatal visit, were between 20 and 36 weeks of gestation, planned to deliver at either Ndirande Health Centre or Bvumbwe Health Centre, planned to breastfeed, and planned to not relocate within the next year. Women living with HIV were enrolled if they were either on antiretroviral therapy (ART) before conception with undetectable viral load or were not on ART at first antenatal care visit and had a viral load of > 10,000 copies/mL [29, 30]. Children were followed up to nine months of age and, starting in 2021, mother-infant pairs were approached to participate in additional follow-up for neurodevelopmental outcomes assessed every 18 months with regularly scheduled visits every six months up until children reached five years of age to decrease loss to follow-up. Children were assessed for growth at all visits [18]. This research utilizes follow-up data from ages 12 through 60 months.

Exposure

To assess the impact of Cyclone Freddy on maternal mental health, child growth and child development, all study visits occurring up to 60 months of age were classified according to their timing in relation to Cyclone Freddy (March 11, 2023). Study visits were classified according to cyclone exposure window: pre-exposure, early post-exposure (≤ 6 months), and late post-exposure (> 6 months). We also considered a 3-month time window: 1–3 months, 3–6 months, and > 6 months as a sensitivity test.

Outcomes

Self-Reporting Questionnaire (SRQ) is a mental health screening tool designed by the World Health Organization for worldwide use. It consists of 20 yes or no questions focused on the symptoms of depression, anxiety, and somatic complaints [32]. This tool has been validated in a variety of settings in Sub-Saharan Africa and is widely used to assess maternal mental health [33]. For Malawi, we utilized a Chichewa version of the SRQ which was previously developed and validated [34]. SRQ was measured every 18 months from age 18 to 54 months. A SRQ score of greater than or equal to 8 is considered indicative of either anxiety or depression, and was used to categorize mothers as either distressed (SRQ > = 8) or not (SRQ < 8) [34].

The Malawi Development Assessment Tool (MDAT) is a validated tool to assess neurodevelopment across four domains: gross motor, fine motor, language and social development in children up to 6 years old [35]. Through caregiver report and observation, each item under the four subcomponents is scored as a pass or fail resulting in a total score for each subcomponent and a total MDAT score. MDAT was measured at 24 and 42 months. Regular reliability checks were done throughout the study and for all subscales of MDAT the interclass correlations were at or above 0.77. Development-for-Age Z -scores (DAZ) were calculated for all the domains based on a reference population of 3975 healthy children in Malawi and India [36]. We then used the z-scores to detect developmental delay, defined as DAZ less than − 1, and severe delay, less than − 2, for each subdomain and the total MDAT score [36].

Length for Age Z-score (LAZ) and Weight for Age Z-Score (WAZ) were used to measure growth trajectories of the children in the cohort. Length (under 24 months, height over 24 months) and weight of the child was collected every six months from age 12 to 60 months. Both growth indicators were measured twice at each visit, and the average was taken between the two measurements. Finally, the age specific Z-score for the measures was calculated based on the WHO reference standards – using Anthro R package [37]. We then calculated whether the child was stunted or underweight, defined as having an age-specific Z-score less than − 2 for length and weight, respectively.

Analysis

We first compared the proportions of mothers experiencing psychological distress and children who were stunted or developmentally delayed across cyclone exposure periods using chi-squared tests.

We additionally examined important predictors of maternal mental health, child neurodevelopment, and child growth including maternal age, child age, socioeconomic status (SES), HIV status of the mother, maternal education level, parity, and quality of observed mother-child interaction (OMCI) (Fig. 1). Specifically, we determined whether the population included in this analysis differed from the total cohort (Table 1) or differed over time in comparison to the cyclone (Supplementary material 1: Table S1).

To examine the association between cyclone exposure and adverse maternal and child health outcomes, we fitted generalized estimating equation logistic regression models for each outcome, accounting for within-participant correlation from repeated measures using an exchangeable correlation structure. Separate models were fitted for maternal psychological distress (SRQ > = 8), overall child developmental delay (DAZ < -1), domain-specific delays in language (language DAZ <-1), fine motor (fine motor DAZ <-1), gross motor (gross motor DAZ <-1), and social development (social DAZ <-1) and stunting (LAZ < -1) and underweight (WAZ <-1). We were unable to construct regression models for severe developmental delays due to the low numbers of children classified as severely delayed. Based on the conceptual model, available data, and model fit all final models were adjusted for maternal HIV status, maternal education level, and child age (Fig. 1).

Models assessing maternal psychological distress were additionally adjusted for maternal age and SRQ measurement sequence to account for potential response bias associated with repeated administration of the same questionnaire over time. For all outcomes we assessed effect modification by maternal HIV status, child age, and OMCI, due to the likely differential effect of cyclone exposure is anticipated to have on more vulnerable groups, defined as significant Wald test p-values for the interaction with cyclone exposure.

All modeling was done using the geepack package in R 4.5.0.

Results

A total of 858 mother-child dyads were enrolled in the follow up cohort. Of these 838 mothers had at least one SRQ assessment, 802 children had MDAT measurements, and 844 children had growth measurements (Table 1). Approximately 52% of the mothers in our cohort had HIV, the average maternal age was 29, 48% completed secondary education, most were married, most of the families fell within the middle wealth quintile and the majority had multiple prior pregnancies (Table 1). The cohorts included in each analysis are similar to the overall cohort, with variations primarily in the percentage of mothers who are HIV positive with 52%, 53%, and 52% in the SRQ, MDAT, and growth cohorts respectively (Table 1). Overall, the study population accrued 1337, 1114, and 3746 observations of SRQ, MDAT, and anthropometric measurements, respectively. Among these, 20%, 19%, and 21% of SRQ, MDAT, and anthropometric measurements, respectively occurred during the early post-exposure period (Fig. 2).

Maternal mental health

Among the 838 women included in the SRQ analysis, most completed at least one SRQ assessment after Cyclone Freddy (Fig. 2) and 52% were living with HIV (Table 1). The mean SRQ score was 3.3 (Standard Deviation (SD) = 3.13) and 142 (17%) of mothers met criteria for psychological distress indicative of anxiety or depression at least once. Over the study period, 42 women transitioned from not distressed to distressed while 40 transitioned from distressed to not distressed following the cyclone. Overall, there was a statistically significant reduction in the proportion of women classified as distressed later in the study, 17% distressed pre-exposure, 6% in the early post-exposure and 12% late post-exposure (chi-squared p-value = 0.0004) (Table 2). We found that the reduced prevalence of being distressed was significant in only the early post-exposure period (Table 3). This result persisted after adjustment for HIV status, maternal age, child age, sequence of SRQ measurement, and education level. There was no evidence of effect modification by HIV status but there was effect modification by child age in the early post-exposure period, with a decreased odds of distress with every 6-month increase in child age (Supplementary material 1: Table S2).

Neurodevelopment

A total of 802 children had MDAT measurements, 52% of those children have mothers living with HIV (Table 1). MDAT age standardized z scores decreased over time for all children (Fig. 3). Prior to the cyclone, 25% of children were developmentally delayed. This proportion of children who were delayed significantly decreased over time, to 7% in the early post-exposure and 4% in late post-exposure periods (chi-squared p-value < 0.0001) (Table 2). Similarly, the proportion of children delayed for each MDAT component decreased (Table 2). The odds of delay post cyclone decreased for all domains with a significant decrease at both time points for language, fine motor and gross motor delay (Table 4). There was evidence of effect modification by maternal HIV status on odds of delay for the language and social domains, with children of mothers living with HIV having a decreased odds of delay in the early post-exposure period (Supplementary material 1: Table S2). There was no evidence of effect modification by child age for social, fine motor or gross motor delay, but there was a borderline (p-value = 0.1) increased odds of language delay in the late post-exposure period among older children (Supplementary material 1: Table S1). There was borderline evidence of effect modification by observed mother-child interaction for gross motor delay (Supplementary material 1: Table S2). All models were adjusted for maternal HIV status, child age, and education level of the mother.

Child growth trajectories

Length and weight measurements were available for 844 children. Prior to Cyclone Freddy, 27% were stunted based on length/height for age whilst 10% had low weight-for-age (Table 2). Following Cyclone Freddy, there was a statistically significant increase in the proportion of children stunted for length/height-for-age (chi-squared p-value = < 0.0001) across all age groups (Fig. 4; Table 2). We found a significant increase in the odds of having a low length/height for age in both the first six months post cyclone (early post-exposure) (OR = 3.40; 95% CI: 2.55–4.51)) and more than six months post cyclone (late post-exposure) (OR = 6.05; 95% CI:4.39–8.35)). The odds of having a low weight-for-age increased post cyclone (early and late exposure) but not significantly at either time point (Fig. 5a). We observed a significant interaction between child age and cyclone exposure for stunting based on length/height-for-age, indicating that the adverse impact of the cyclone on linear growth was greatest among younger children (Fig. 5b & Supplementary material 1: Table S2). We found evidence of effect modification by maternal HIV status for stunting only (Supplementary material 1: Table S2 & Table S4). The final models were adjusted for mothers’ education level and HIV status and included an interaction with child age.

Discussion

In this study, we examined the impact of a severe climate disaster on maternal mental health, child neurodevelopmental delay and linear growth using a well characterized longitudinal cohort of mother-child dyads from two cyclone-affected communities in Southern Malawi. Our findings reveal a complex pattern of impact. Cyclone Freddy was associated with substantial increases in childhood stunting with marked deterioration in child linear growth in those families affected while maternal psychological distress and child neurodevelopment showed improvement in the months immediately following the cyclone.

Our findings could potentially be attributed to: (1) Downstream effects of cyclone exposure and related consequences, (2) Confounding due to co-occurring changes in child development during these sensitive periods of development affecting the study population simultaneously with the cyclone, or (3) Some combination of cyclone-associated effects and temporal confounding. Most likely, some combination of downstream effects of cyclone exposure and simultaneous temporal environmental changes occurring in Malawi explain some of our most surprising results, however disentangling these factors may be impossible given our inability to identify a true control group with no cyclone exposure.

Contrary to much of the existing literature on extreme weather events and mental health, we observed a reduced prevalence of maternal psychological distress post Cyclone Freddy. However, much of the current evidence linking cyclone exposure to adverse mental health outcomes originates from high income countries in the Global North, where access to resources, mental health services and sociocultural understandings of psychological distress differ from those in SSA. However, there is some research on prevalence of mental health disorders among survivors of natural disasters in the Global South which have found substantial heterogeneity in estimates. This heterogeneity is likely due to differences in mental health disorder measurement tools, disaster exposure, post disaster follow up, and the communities affected, specifically in our cohort we had more mental health measurements for HIV positive women prior to the cyclone (Supplementary material 1: Table S1) [5, 7, 38]. Additionally, there may have been heightened community cohesion and mutual support following the cyclone which could have temporarily reduced reported distress. Yet, the overall prevalence of maternal distress in our cohort was approximately 17%, which is consistent with estimates from other studies [5]. Given the lack of comparable research in the Global South, there is an urgent need for research on the association between natural disasters and mental health disorders that go beyond prevalence.

Similarly, unexpectedly, we found a decreased prevalence of developmental delay later in the study. This decrease was seen in the fine motor, gross motor and language development domains. This may be due to a combination of timing of the cyclone exposure, measurement sensitivity, and support. Evidence suggests that foundational skills in motor, language, and social development as assessed by MDAT are most sensitive to adverse events in the first two years of life [39–42]. Since we collected MDAT data at 24 and 42 months of age, these skills may already have been relatively resilient to short-term environmental hazards among our cohort, resulting in a lack of effect due to the cyclone. It is also plausible that observed improvement in developmental scores after the cyclone may be related to unmeasured external improvements in quality of life, exposure to technology, or other time-varying changes that took place in Malawi over the study period. Additionally, the MDAT tool itself is designed to detect age-appropriate delays, not small short-term fluctuations that may occur in response to acute stressors. Our populations varied slightly, with more measurements among those exposed to HIV pre cyclone compared to post, potentially confounding our results (Supplementary material 1: Table S1). Finally, compensatory support from caregivers, family, and the community following the cyclone may have boosted observed developmental performance, due to reduced neuroendocrine, immunologic, metabolic, and genetic regulatory markers of toxic stress [40].

Our finding that stunting increased after exposure to the cyclone was in line with previous literature. Food insecurity in Malawi is already common due to erratic rainfall in recent decades, with 20% of the population estimated to have experienced acute food insecurity before the cyclone [43]. Additionally, a main source of income for communities in Bvumbwe are cash crops. Given crop destruction due to the cyclone there was likely a subsequent decrease in household income and increasing food insecurity. Similarly, in Ndirande the destruction of crops led to food price increases and related food insecurity [44]. The significant increase in odds of stunting in the first 6 months after the cyclone may suggest that acute malnutrition after a disaster is a critical intervention point [17]. This is particularly important for the youngest children, who are more likely to be adversely impacted by acute malnutrition. However, the persistent increase in stunting more than 6 months post exposure may indicate either a long-term impact of the cyclone on food security in this community or that the initial insult to child health was severe enough to cause cascading health consequences.

Our study has several limitations. First, we have no information on individual cyclone exposure, preventing us from accurately capturing the acute individual-level impact of the cyclone on participants. However, the entire study population lives in communities severely affected by the cyclone, and the majority are of low socioeconomic status, minimizing the potential for classification bias. There may also be limitations in the SRQ tool for capturing short term changes in distress symptoms, following the cyclone. While research has shown that healthcare utilization decreased post Cyclone Freddy in Malawi [27], we found that only 3% of children in our cohort did not attend all expected visits following the cyclone. It is likely that most missed visits attributed to the cyclone would have been in the first few months following the disaster. However, we evaluated alternative exposure definitions, specifically in the first 3 months compared to 3 to 6 months post cyclone and found no statistical difference in effect estimates for all outcomes, suggesting both that any missed visits did not have a major impact on our findings and also confirming that different definitions of the exposure window did not change results (Table S3).

Despite limitations, our study has several strengths. Specifically, our longitudinal cohort design allowed us to look at the same participants before and after the cyclone and provide us with repeat measurements for numerous individuals. Our inclusion of heterogenous study sites in both an urban and rural setting increases generalizability of our findings. Finally, our utilization of site specific and validated tools that were culturally adapted for this setting is a great benefit to our study.

Conclusions

To our knowledge this is the first published literature to characterize the impact of cyclone exposure on maternal mental health, neurodevelopment, and child growth in SSA. We found that Cyclone Freddy negatively impacted child growth in Southern Malawi. Given the predicted increase in frequency and intensity of extreme weather events and the already high burden of adverse childhood health outcomes in SSA, it is key that childhood growth is considered in the aftermath of a natural disaster. While we did not find an adverse effect of cyclone exposure on maternal mental health or childhood developmental delay, the literature in this region is lacking and deserves more targeted attention, resources to obtain high quality data on both exposure and outcomes. Overall, future research is needed on the adverse effects of extreme weather events in SSA on vulnerable populations such as women and children.
