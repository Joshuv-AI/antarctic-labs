A fast risk assessment method for power balance under extreme weather conditions based on deep reinforcement learning with a coupled attention mechanism

Wang Hang, Sun Shouheng, Li Yiming, Li Baoju, Cong Yu, Xu Meiyue, HeXiang Niu

ABSTRACT
Even if persistent stagnant weather does not directly damage power grid equipment, it may lead to prolonged insufficient output of renewable energy sources and further trigger power balance risks. This paper proposes a rapid risk assessment framework that comprehensively incorporates meteorological sensitivity models, time-varying component failure probabilities, a Denoising Variational Autoencoder (DVAE) for generating coupled wind-photovoltaic-temperature scenarios, and a Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) model embedded with the feature token attention mechanism.The 72-hour assessment task is formulated as an offline Markov Decision Process (MDP): the state space covers meteorological conditions, power sources, power grid topology, load profiles, and risk features of the previous time step; continuous actions are defined as risk indicator vectors; the reward function is designed to penalize deviations between model outputs and PSD-BPA benchmarks, temporal inconsistency, and violations of physical constraints.To distinguish the sequential reinforcement learning formulation from direct regression, a feedforward Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is introduced as the non-reinforcement learning baseline. Four metrics, namely Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), coefficient of determination (R2), and the 95th percentile of absolute error (P95AE), are adopted to evaluate all models on an independent scenario-level test set.Verified on the planned power grid of Northeast China in 2025 as the test case, the proposed DDPG-Attention achieves MAE of 0.0310, RMSE of 0.0389, R2 of 0.954 and P95AE of 0.0760 on the independent test set. The average time consumption for a single risk assessment is approximately 0.12 seconds, yielding an acceleration ratio of multiple orders of magnitude compared with the traditional time-sequential Monte Carlo simulation based on PSD-BPA. This study provides an efficient and accurate novel technical paradigm for the rapid evaluation of power supply-demand imbalance risks in power systems under extreme weather conditions.

1. Introduction With the in depth advancement of the “dual carbon” goals, the construction of new power systems has entered a critical stage, and the share of renewable energy installations, represented by wind power and photovoltaics (PV), continues to increase [ 1 , 2 ]. However, the strong intermittency and volatility of renewable energy output make the system operation significantly more dependent on meteorological conditions. Unlike highly destructive extreme weather events such as typhoons and thunderstorms, persistent stagnant weather does not directly damage the physical structure of the power grid, but its long duration and wide impact range can easily lead to renewable energy being in a state of low or even zero output for an extended period, thereby triggering long term power supply demand imbalance risks in the power system. Events such as high temperature droughts and multiple days without wind or sunlight that have occurred in many regions worldwide have fully demonstrated that weather factors are becoming an important unknown risk source threatening the security of power supply in new power systems [ 3 , 4 ]. Therefore, studying the supply demand imbalance risk of power systems under extreme weather and developing fast and accurate risk assessment methods are of significant theoretical importance and engineering value. Currently, domestic and international scholars have conducted extensive research on power system risk assessment under extreme weather. In terms of source load characteristic modeling, references [ 5 , 6 ] proposed renewable energy station output prediction methods considering factors such as wind speed, temperature, humidity, and solar irradiance. Reference [ 7 ] separated climate sensitive load from total load by analyzing the spatiotemporal correlation between climate factors and power load. Reference [ 8 ] studied the impact of high temperature and drought weather on the output of hydropower and thermal power, and proposed elasticity indicators at the power source level. In the field of system resilience assessment, existing studies have proposed static resilience indicators based on resource adequacy and component redundancy [ 9 , 10 ], as well as dynamic resilience indicators established according to the recovery process during and after extreme events. Regarding scenario generation, reference [ 11 ] proposed a weather scenario construction method based on extreme value theory, but it lacked specific scenario modeling and case study validation. References [ 12 , 13 ] explored the emergency control strategies of power systems and the optimal configuration of distributed generation and energy storage devices under different scenarios. However, existing research still has obvious limitations in coping with supply demand imbalance risks of power systems under extreme weather. On the one hand, most studies tend to analyze sudden extreme weather events and persistent stagnant weather separately, failing to uniformly reveal the differentiated impact mechanisms of the two types of extreme weather from the three aspects of supply, transmission, and demand. On the other hand, at the uncertainty modeling level, source side output fluctuations, grid side equipment failures, and load side climate sensitive responses are often treated in isolation, and a comprehensive quantification of their coupled uncertainties is lacking. In particular, the time varying characteristics of equipment failure probability, which are jointly affected by service life, environmental conditions, and operating status, are insufficiently considered in existing risk assessment models. More critically, although traditional time series production simulation or Monte Carlo methods can obtain relatively accurate risk results, their huge computational overhead makes it difficult to meet the timeliness requirements for rapid risk identification during the evolution of extreme weather. Moreover, existing resilience assessment studies mostly focus on short term emergency power supply after the destruction of grid topology by extreme weather, while the exploration of rapid assessment methods for global and persistent supply demand imbalance risks remains insufficient. The above deficiencies indicate that there is an urgent need to develop an intelligent method that can efficiently handle high dimensional state spaces, adaptively focus on key risk features, and combine offline training with online rapid assessment. Deep Learning (DL), Active Learning (AL) and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) address different types of methodological problems and should not be conflated. Supervised deep learning learns a direct mapping from labeled inputs to outputs; active learning selects highly informative samples from unlabeled data for supplementary labeling; deep reinforcement learning derives optimal policies through state transitions and reward feedback. Existing power system studies based on deep learning or deep active/transfer learning are mainly applied to transient stability classification or regression tasks to lower the cost of label acquisition [ 14 – 20 ]. Such methods are suitable when each operating point is processed independently. In contrast, the problem investigated in this paper involves a 72-hour evolutionary process, in which meteorological conditions, equipment states and risk indicators are temporally coupled. Accordingly, the DDPG algorithm [ 21 ] that supports Actor–Critic learning with continuous actions and sequential state spaces is adopted in this work. Meanwhile, given that PSD-BPA labels can be batch-generated via offline simulations, direct supervised regression serves as a reliable alternative for this task. To quantify the performance gain brought by the temporal dependency modeling within the reinforcement learning framework compared with static supervised mapping, a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is added as a comparative baseline in this paper. Unified comparisons in terms of prediction accuracy and computational efficiency are conducted between the proposed DDPG-Attention method and the MLP on the same independent test set. Analysis on multidimensional impacts of extreme meteorology on supply-transmission-demand of power system 2.1. Qualitative analysis of supply-transmission-demand in power system under extreme meteorological conditions The impact of extreme weather events on power systems is becoming increasingly significant, particularly in the context of a high proportion of renewable energy integration, where system dependence on meteorological conditions is markedly enhanced. Unlike conventional meteorological fluctuations, extreme weather is characterized by suddenness, persistence, and strong destructiveness, and can simultaneously impact the supply capacity, transmission security, and load characteristics of power systems from multiple dimensions, thereby triggering risks of power supply–demand imbalance. Based on the deep coupling relationship between meteorology and power systems, this paper qualitatively analyzes the influence mechanism of extreme weather from three aspects: supply, transmission, and demand. On the power supply side, the impact of extreme weather on renewable energy generation capacity manifests as two typical processes. Sudden extreme weather events, such as typhoons, blizzards, and thunderstorms, are often accompanied by strong winds, low temperatures, or heavy precipitation, directly causing damage to generation equipment or shutdowns. Typhoons can cause wind turbine tower collapse or blade fracture; blizzards and freezing rain may lead to ice coating on photovoltaic (PV) panels, shading sunlight and significantly reducing PV conversion efficiency; and cold waves may cause brittle fracture of mechanical components or lubricant failure in wind turbines, thereby substantially reducing wind power output. In addition, extreme precipitation or drought can alter reservoir inflow conditions, limiting hydroelectric generation capacity. Another type is persistent stagnant weather, such as stagnant haze and low sunlight. Although not violently destructive, its long duration and wide influence range can easily lead to wind and PV power remaining at low or even zero output for extended periods. Under stagnant weather, low wind speeds limit wind power output, while haze reduces solar radiation intensity, further suppressing PV generation, creating a “low wind and low sunlight” scenario, which in turn leads to longterm supply–demand imbalances in the power system. Power transmission systems also face severe challenges under extreme weather conditions. Extreme weather events such as wind, rain, ice, and snow can directly act on transmission lines, towers, insulators, and other equipment, compromising the integrity of the grid structure and causing transmission channel interruptions or electrical faults. Typhoons and strong winds easily lead to windinduced conductor flashovers, tower structure damage, and even tripping due to short circuits caused by external floating objects on highvoltage electrical equipment. Cold waves and blizzards can cause line icing, which in severe cases may lead to conductor breakage and tower collapse, resulting in largescale blackouts. Thunderstorms may cause lightning flashovers on substations and transmission lines, damaging insulation components and triggering frequency excursions or voltage instability. These transmissionside failures not only weaken crossregional power allocation capabilities but also exacerbate regional supply–demand contradictions, especially when transmission corridors for renewable energy are constrained, further amplifying power balance risks. On the load side, under extreme weather conditions, electricity demand exhibits distinctly nonlinear response characteristics. Extremely high temperatures lead to massive activation of air conditioners and cooling equipment, causing a sharp increase in load; extremely low temperatures trigger concentrated release of heating loads such as electric heating and water heaters. Meanwhile, distributed generation and electric vehicles may also experience power supply interruptions or concentrated charging demand outbreaks under extreme weather, further disturbing load profiles. The impacts of extreme weather on the three dimensions of power supply, transmission, and demand are not isolated but rather coupled and amplified. While extreme weather reduces renewable energy output, it may simultaneously cause transmission line tripping. If, at the same time, load increases due to heating or cooling demand, the system will face multiple shocks, easily triggering cascading events such as frequency excursions and regional blackouts. Deep learning solution method coupled with attention mechanism 5.1. Basis for MDP modeling and DDPG selection This paper formulates the power balance risk assessment under extreme weather as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), whose rationality is elaborated as follows: (1) The evolution of extreme weather bears prominent temporal cumulative effects. Equipment aging, ice accretion and cumulative load response all constitute continuous-time processes, and the risk state at the current moment depends on meteorological conditions and system operation trajectories of preceding time steps. (2) Risk control is essentially a sequential decision-making task. Dispatchers need to dynamically adjust emergency plans according to risk evolution trends, rather than making one-off static decisions. (3) The “offline training – online inference” paradigm of reinforcement learning perfectly satisfies the cross-scenario transfer requirement from historical meteorological-operation data to real-time rapid risk identification. Although supervised learning can directly learn the mapping from system states to risk indicators, it fails to explicitly model the temporal dependency of risk evolution and reward feedback mechanisms. Therefore, this paper adopts Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) as the basic framework, and embeds an attention mechanism to strengthen its capability of extracting critical features within high-dimensional state spaces. Conclusion This paper constructs a rapid power balance risk assessment framework under extreme weather conditions by integrating meteorological sensitivity models, time-varying component failure probabilities, DVAE-based coupled scenario generation, time-series labels from PSD-BPA, and an attention-enhanced DDPG estimator. The revised approach explicitly defines the state space, continuous action space, state transition rules, reward function, feature token attention weights, supervised learning baselines, independent test metrics, and timing schemes. The main conclusions are summarized as follows: (1) The attention mechanism operates on multiple feature tokens instead of a single scalar score. The derived attention matrix is normalized row-wisely, enabling the Actor network to adaptively reweight meteorological data, power source information, grid parameters, load profiles, and risk features from the previous time step along the evolution of the 72-hour scenarios. On the independent test set, the proposed DDPG-Attention achieves MAE of 0.0310, RMSE of 0.0389, R 2 of 0.954 and P95AE of 0.0760. Compared with vanilla DDPG, its MAE and RMSE are reduced by 18.6% and 18.5%, respectively. In contrast to the supervised MLP baseline (0.0396, 0.0495, 0.925 and 0.0972), the MAE, RMSE and P95AE are decreased by approximately 21.7%, 21.4% and 21.8%, while R 2 is improved by around 3.1%. Meanwhile, it exhibits a faster reward convergence rate. (2) The measured online inference time of DDPG-Attention for evaluating a single scenario is approximately 0.12 s. For full time-sequential Monte Carlo simulations covering 1000 risk scenarios with a 72-hour horizon per scenario, the total computation time of the traditional method reaches around 619,200 seconds, corresponding to a speedup ratio of more than six orders of magnitude. Even under an idealized 1000-core parallel computing configuration for scenario decomposition, the acceleration still exceeds three orders of magnitude. (3) Extreme weather exerts disparate impacts on three dimensions: power generation resources, power transmission facilities and electricity demand. Persistent stagnant weather primarily leads to long-duration insufficient renewable energy output and adequacy risks, whereas abrupt destructive weather may simultaneously raise component failure probabilities, tighten transmission constraints and trigger climate-sensitive load increments, resulting in aggravated security and stability risks. High-risk time windows mainly occur when heavy load, insufficient available generation capacity and weakened network transmission capability coincide. (4) Regional evaluation results demonstrate that planning and operational countermeasures should be formulated in a differentiated manner according to local power supply structures and meteorological exposure characteristics. Prioritized measures include deploying flexible regulating resources, optimizing wind turbine protection and control parameters, implementing anti-icing schemes for transmission lines, cooling upgrades for transformers and reinforcement of vulnerable equipment, as well as coordinating preventive control and emergency control strategies.
