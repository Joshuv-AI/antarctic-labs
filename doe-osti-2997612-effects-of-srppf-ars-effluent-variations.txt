 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

A magnesium oxysulfate (MOS) grout formulation has been identified to solidify the liquid effluent from
the Savannah River Plutonium Processing Facility (SRPPF) Aqueous Recovery System (ARS). The
formulation uses reactive, light burnt MgO (28 wt%), anhydrous MgSO4 (10 wt%), and dead burnt MgO
(62 wt%), resulting in a grout with good mixability, acceptable density, acceptable setting times, and a
leachate pH around 9.4, within the assumed Waste Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP) brine pH range.

A series of tests were then designed to observe how this formula reacted to variations in the liquid effluent.
Liquid effluent temperature, the caustic pH level, concentration of NaNO3, and the presence of
neutralization products, trace metals, and/or sodium sulfate (all potential residuals found in the effluent
from upstream processing), were evaluated to see how each impacts the grout mixing time, peak
temperature, setting time, presence of bleed water, leachate pH, and density.

The standard mix has an average mixing time of 18 minutes, peak temperature of 100.5 °C, and set time of
60 minutes. Increasing the temperature of the liquid effluent increased the reaction rate of the mix and
reduced mixing and setting times. Between 30 and 40°C the change was relatively small, raising no more
than 6 °C compared to the standard mix maximum temperature. Increasing the pH of the liquid effluent
had no significant impact on the mix. The addition of neutralization products and trace metals had an overall
impact of decreasing the reactivity of the mix. The addition of more ions in the liquid effluent, such as an
increase in the concentration of NaNO3 and the addition of Na2SO4, generally decreased the reactivity of
the mix. None of the variations to the liquid effluent hindered solidification as indicated by the lack of bleed
water found on all the samples. The leachate pH values for all mixes tested did not significantly vary from
the expected pH of 9.4 and none of the density values fell below 1.8 g/cm3. Overall, changes to the liquid
effluent were found to have a minimal impact on the formulation suggesting the grout’s ability to properly
form despite increased temperatures and the presence of residuals typically found in the liquid effluent from
the ARS.

1.0 Introduction

A novel magnesium oxysulfate (MOS) cement was proposed for Savannah River Plutonium Processing
Facility (SRPPF) Aqueous Recovery System (ARS) liquid effluent using a grout formula of 28 wt% light
burnt MgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, and 62 wt% dead burnt MgO developed in previous testing [1-4]. The planned
solidification process would have the dry materials preloaded into 55-gallon drums, and the liquid effluent,
containing primarily nitrate salts, would then be added to the solids in the drum and mixed by a lost-paddle
design before being allowed to cure. The liquid effluent for ARS is expected to contain radioactive material
(Pu, Am) at levels requiring it to be managed as transuranic (TRU) waste for future disposal at the Waste
Isolation Pilot Plant (WIPP). WIPP requires that no observable liquid be present in the set mixture and that
the dose be less than 200 mrem/hr at the surface [2, 4, 5]. These two requirements necessitate a well-mixed
waste form to avoid unsolidified liquid and under-shielded sections for entrained radioactivity [2, 4, 6].
WIPP has also indicated the leachate pH needs to be in the range assumed in the WIPP performance
assessment (pH 8-10) [2, 4].
The liquid effluent from ARS will contain impurities and residuals leftover from the dissolution,
separations, and neutralization processes upstream of the solidification systems. The liquid effluent may
also be above ambient temperatures when it is sent for solidification. While the primary salt in the ARS
will be sodium nitrate, the concentration of the effluent may vary, and some amount of 50% sodium
hydroxide used to neutralize the effluent before solidification may be present, potentially increasing the pH
of the liquid effluent. To determine the impacts of each of these changes, six different experiments were
performed to simulate possible mixing conditions including: increased liquid effluent temperature, varied
concentrations of NaNO3, increased effluent pH by addition of NaOH, and added Na2SO4, trace metals, and
neutralization products to the standard liquid effluent simulant. Each experiment measured the mixing time,
the peak temperature, the setting time, the leachate pH, the presence of bleed water, and the density to
assess how changing the liquid effluent properties impacts the performance of the standard grout
formulation.

2.0 Experimental Procedure

2.1 Standard Effluent Surrogate

The primary salt in the ARS will be sodium nitrate [1]. Overall, this salt represents the largest possible
hindrance to solidification. Prior testing of sorbents for solidification was performed with 4M NaNO3 in
deionized (DI) water as the surrogate effluent [2, 4], but subsequent work added free HNO3 and decreased
the pH of the effluent to a calculated pH of 2.3 [4, 7]. As needed, erbium nitrate pentahydrate, lanthanum
hydroxide, and cerium oxide were used as surrogates for Am and Pu to analyze their distribution throughout
the solidified material. Prior work established the method to accurately analyze Er concentrations via
Inductively Coupled Plasma Atomic Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-AES) [3, 4].

2.2 Materials
For the standard formulation, the light burnt MgO (LBMgO) was procured from Premier Magnesia, the
dead burnt MgO was from TIMAB Magnesia (DBMgO), and the anhydrous MgSO4 was from Wintersun
Chemical. The sodium nitrate, nitric acid, lanthanum hydroxide, calcium chloride, aluminum nitrate and
erbium nitrate pentahydrate were from Fisher Scientific. The cerium oxide was from Alfa Aesar. The
potassium fluoride was from Sigma Aldrich.

 1

2.3 Standard Mix Operations and Results
Each mix used a dry material formulation of 1000 g LBMgO, 2200 g DBMgO, and 350 g MgSO4. The dry
materials for a one-gallon test were combined in a sealed plastic bag and mixed thoroughly by agitating the
bag. The effluent surrogate was a 4M NaNO3 solution made with DI water, to which 0.86 g of HNO3 per
2300 g of solution was added to adjust the pH of the effluent to 2.3. Mixing was performed using a 3D
printed titanium paddle, measuring 5 ¼” wide and 5 7/8” high, placed inside a one-gallon paint can with no
lid (Figure 2-1). The dry materials were added to the can, and the paddle, attached to an upright mixer,
further mixed/fluffed the dry materials for 1-2 minutes at 100 RPM. The mixer was then turned off and 50-
75% of the effluent surrogate was slowly poured into the can using a pitcher, allowing retained air in the
dry materials to be displaced, bubbling to the surface. After the initial 50-75% of the liquid was added, the
mixer was started at 150 RPM. While still mixing, the remaining liquid was quickly added to the can and
the mixer speed was increased to 200 RPM. The standard mix is typically dark brown in color, homogenous
(having little to no clumping), and absent of foam following complete mixing (Figure 2-1). When the mix
appeared to be visually homogeneous, the mixer was stopped, and the paddle was removed and examined
for any clumping of material on the blade. The paddle was then cleaned and set aside for reuse. The mix
was removed from the hood and sealed using a lid with two small holes. The can was wrapped in insulation,
placed in a plastic bucket, and a thermocouple was secured in the mix through one of the holes in the lid.
More insulation was laid over the bucketed can and the test was left for a minimum of 24 hours. Modified
Vicat testing was performed once per hour until the sample was considered fully set, i.e. the Vicat needle
could no longer penetrate the top layer of the mix without the use of force. At the end of the 24 hours, the
thermocouple was removed, the peak temperature was recorded, and the visual presence of bleed water was
noted.

All mixes were prepared and executed in the same manner as the standard mix unless otherwise noted.

After a minimum of two weeks, samples were taken for leachate testing, ICP-AES testing, and/or
Archimedes density testing, as needed. Duplicate samples were prepared for the leachate testing as
performed in prior experiments [4].

 2

2.4 Liquid Effluent Temperature
The effects of changing liquid effluent temperature were tested from 22°C to upwards of 70°C. The effluent
simulant was weighed and then placed in a temperature-controlled oven to allow the solution ample time
to adjust to the desired temperature for the test. A thermocouple was used to ensure the oven held to the
target temperature for a minimum of 16 hours. The liquid effluent was removed from the oven, weighed
immediately to check for any losses during heating, and then added to the dry solids per the standard testing
protocol. For temperatures greater than 30 °C, the frequency of the modified Vicat testing was increased to
every 5 minutes in order to capture rapid setting times.

2.5 Caustic Liquid Effluent
To determine effects of a higher pH concentration for the liquid effluent as NaOH is a proposed additive in
the SRPPF flow sheet, four mixes were prepared using solutions with concentrations ranging from pH 11
through pH 14. For these trials, the 4 M NaNO3 solution was prepared in a new container and was not pH
adjusted using nitric acid. From the solution, 2300 g was subsampled from this parent bottle and adjusted
to the desired pH level using NaOH for each test.

2.6 Changing Sodium Nitrate Concentrations and the Addition of Sodium Sulfate
The primary salt found in the ARS liquid effluent is sodium nitrate. Testing was performed to see if a
change in the concentration of sodium nitrate affected the wasteform. In some tests, sodium sulfate salts
were added in lieu of, or in combination with, sodium nitrate to simulate potential ARS effluent conditions.
Additionally, the combination of sodium sulfate and sodium nitrate were tested at both neutral pH and pH
of 2.3. The simulant was prepared separately in 1 L volumetric flasks using the appropriate amounts of salts
to yield the desired sodium nitrate to sodium sulfate concentration in solution as outlined in Table 2-1.
Solutions were designed to maintain the same water to dry solids mass ratio of 0.46.

2.7 Addition of Neutralization Products and Trace Metals
Neutralization products, trace metals, and/or a combination therein were added to the standard 4 M NaNO3
pH 2.3 solution, to represent potential residuals originating upstream of the ARS liquid effluent. The
amount of simulant impurities added exceeded the quantifiable limit needed for analytical analysis but was
minimized to not impact the liquid to solid ratio significantly. These concentrations are higher than expected
for the process, but were used to ensure quantifiable results were obtained. The residuals and impurities
from the neutralized liquid effluent will potentially include Pu, Am, KF, Al3(NO3)3, NaNO3, NaOH, and
C2H2O4. The cerium oxide and the lanthanum hydroxide were used as surrogates for the Pu and Am.
Surrogates were used in oxide, hydroxide, and nitrate forms to test that all potential effluent products would
be well distributed throughout the mix, no matter the chemical composition of the waste elements.

 3

2.8 pH and Density
After a minimum of two weeks, the 70°C temperature test, the trace metals and neutralization products test,
the neutralization products test, the 1 M Na2SO4/4 M NaNO3 combination test, and all the caustic pH tests
were subjected to Archimedes density testing in which analysis was performed in triplicate. The pH for
each of these tests was measured concurrently. Samples for pH testing were crushed and sieved through a
3/8 in sieve, and each sample was tested in duplicate to obtain an average pH value. In a 50 mL centrifuge
tube, 20 g of the sieved material was added to 20 mL of DI water. Each tube was then placed on a rotisserie
rotator set to 10 RPM. After seven days, the samples were centrifuged at 3500 RPM for 5 min and the
supernatant was measured for pH.

3.0 Results and Discussion

3.1 Fresh Grout Properties

3.1.1 Effluent Temperature
Table 3-1 lists the effects of increasing the liquid effluent temperature. The standard mix with the liquid
effluent at room temperature (22 °C) was used as a baseline for comparison. No mass was lost after the
solution was heated and held at any of the increased temperatures. As the liquid effluent temperature
increased, the mixing time (foam removal time) and set time would generally decrease, while the peak
temperature would increase. Higher liquid effluent temperatures such as 50 °C and 70 °C were associated
with shorter setting times and peak temperatures that could potentially be too hot if scaled up to the full-
scale, 55-gallon drum size. Increasing liquid effluent temperature led to increased reaction rates and
simultaneously heat production rates resulting in premature solidification of the mixes. This increased rate
of setting did not cause any clumping on the paddle or affect foam reduction on the surface of the mix,
suggesting that the mixing was still thorough. At the full-scale however, foam and clumping may be present
as the reactivity could be disproportionally higher. At the highest liquid effluent temperature, the small-
scale mixes set rapidly and had to be taken off the mixer almost immediately to avoid trapping the paddle.
Note, in Table 3-1, the foam removal time is longer for the 40 °C test than the 30 °C test. This observation
is likely due to the subjective nature of foam removal as a measure of mixing time because the mixer must
be stopped to verify the dissipation of foam. This difference of four minutes is insignificant, and still
representative of a shortened time for mixing and setting compared to the standard ambient temperature
test.

4.0 Conclusions
Magnesium oxysulfate cement is a promising candidate to solidify the ARS liquid for disposal at WIPP.
The standard formulation (28 wt% light burnt MgO, 10 wt% MgSO4, 62 wt% dead burnt MgO) was put
through a series of tests assessed to determine how variations to the liquid effluent might impact
performance of the mix. Variations included changes in sodium nitrate concentration, possible presence of
sulfates, changes in liquid effluent temperature, and the presence of neutralization products and trace
metals. Testing showed little to no impact on the final mix and, coupled with the fact that none of these
mixes had bleed water present, has proven the robustness of this formula in yielding a solidified waste form
capable of meeting the desired heat generation, density, and pH range as required by WIPP. The 7-day pH
testing on the feed effects mixes show all solids generated a pH close to the expected value of 9.4. Average
density measurements of these samples all fell within the average range of the standard formula with none
falling below a density of 1.8 g/mL.

5.0 Path Forward
All work presented in this report was completed at the one-gallon small-scale size to gain basic knowledge
of the effects these variables have on the grout wasteform. Additional work to characterize how the standard
formulation would behave at the full scale may be required to fully understand the potential production
variations herein. The temperature data presented here suggests that if the liquid effluent from the ARS is
higher, then mixing may be negatively impacted. The high temperatures caused premature setting, and at
the full scale this may reduce the thoroughness of the mixing, leading to clumping and the presence of
foam. This faster rate of reaction, however, could be offset by the simultaneous increase in residuals and
impurities from the upstream processes. At the small scale, it was shown that these lower the overall
reactivity. Further testing at the full scale to include all these influencing factors could better demonstrate
the overall impacts of these changes.
