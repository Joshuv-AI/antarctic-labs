A game-theoretic attack-defense framework for the study of network resilience
Authors: Kaiwen Zhao, Jianxi Gao, Qi Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid evolution of communication technologies and the Internet of Things has enhanced system interconnectivity, allowing localized disruptions to cascade into failures. Many such breakdowns are not purely accidental but emerge from intentional countermeasures that influence one another during disruptions. Capturing this interplay requires a framework describing how strategic behaviors coevolve and collectively determine the resilience of complex networks. Here we develop a game-theoretic framework that captures the coevolution of attacks and defenses through repeated interactions on a networked system. Attackers and defenders update strategies based on past outcomes, generating adaptive dynamics that link network structure with strategic behavior. We examine strategic unilateral control of payoffs and uncover a heterogeneity-dependent asymmetry: attackers dominate on heterogeneous networks, whereas defenders prevail on homogeneous ones. Remarkably, optimal attacker strategies converge on the network’s percolation threshold, as confirmed across synthetic and empirical networks. Our results reveal how topology governs strategic coevolution and emergent resilience.Subject terms: Decision making, Coevolution

Introduction

The rapid advancement of modern communication, computation, and automation technologies has fundamentally transformed the ways in which people, machines, and infrastructures interact, creating an unprecedented level of system integration. This growing interconnectedness has amplified interdependence across physical, cyber, and social domains, such that even minor local disruptions can cascade through multiple layers and generate large-scale catastrophic collapses1. For instance, in 2003, Italy experienced a nationwide blackout resulting from the cascading failure of interconnected power and communication networks2. Similarly, in social systems, the containment of an outbreak is closely linked to the percolation characteristics of the underlying social network, and without effective strategies, small sources of contagion can rapidly spread across large-scale networks3. Understanding the dynamic characteristics of the infectious disease spread within networks is essential for effective containment. Modern network systems are often complex, with macroscopic properties that cannot be easily inferred from their microscopic components. Given these complexities, the study of system and network resilience is increasingly vital for evaluating the long-term stability of network systems and assessing their robustness under interventions4. Network resilience refers to the ability of a network to maintain its functionality or quickly recover when subjected to disturbances5,6. It has critical applications in financial, economic, communication, and transportation systems7–13. In today’s IoT-driven era, ensuring the long-term control and stability of network resilience is not just a static structural requirement but also a functional necessity of co-evolving systems.

Percolation theory is a crucial tool for quantitatively analyzing the robustness of complex systems across scientific and engineering disciplines14,15. It shows that the pattern of connectivity fundamentally governs how networks respond to random failures or targeted attacks, leading to distinct robustness profiles across topologies16. For example, homogeneous networks, where nodes have similar numbers of neighbors17, tend to respond similarly to both random failures and intentional attacks. In contrast, heterogeneous networks, characterized by significant variations in node degrees18, respond unevenly to disruptions: the presence of highly connected hub nodes enhances robustness against random failures yet increases vulnerability to targeted attacks. Several studies have explored how degree centrality affects the percolation threshold (i.e., the critical condition for the system disruption)19,20, and others have examined the robustness of high- and low-degree nodes in interdependent networks under target attacks21,22. Additionally, prototype cascade models have been used to identify the threshold at which the network might disintegrate due to the failure of a single node23. The percolation properties of networks of networks consisting of interdependent networks have been extensively studied24.

The study of network attacks and defenses is a significant area of research in network resilience, and have been receiving increasing attention during the past decades. A common deliberate attacking strategy involves removing nodes based on their degree or betweenness centrality in descending order25. With advancements in artificial intelligence, new machine-learning techniques have been developed to dismantle networks. For instance, graph neural networks can be employed to devise attack strategies on small synthetic networks. Graph dismantling machine learning is designed to dismantle relatively small systems by recognizing higher-order topological patterns26. Additionally, the deep reinforcement learning framework finder can be trained on small synthetic networks generated from toy models and then applied to diverse scenarios27. From a defensive perspective, enhancing defenses against targeted attacks during the network formation phase can help prevent network collapse28,29. Defenders can also establish early warning indicators to detect potential network failures30. Following a network failure, defenders deploy strategies aimed at repairing damaged components and restoring overall functionality31,32.

Despite these valuable insights in prior studies, real-world networks experience attacks and defenses that unfold simultaneously within the same system, rather than as independent events. Consequently, understanding network robustness and resilience should go beyond static structural analyses to incorporate functional and strategic dynamics. In this view, both adversaries and defenders adapt their actions to maximize their respective advantages, subject to costs and potential failures that depend on the underlying network structure. These interacting processes define a dynamic landscape of competition and adaptation that cannot be captured by static metrics alone. Furthermore, many existing studies focus on the short-term impacts of attacks or defenses28,29,31,32. In practice, attackers and defenders continually adapt to each other’s actions, refining their strategies through feedback and experience. This mutual adaptation turns network resilience into an evolving property that existing theories cannot fully capture, making it difficult to characterize long-term outcomes that emerge from ongoing strategic learning rather than fixed structural conditions. In interactive systems comprised of attackers and defenders, the long-term payoffs of the participants reflect the whole system and network resilience. Accurately managing long-term payoffs is essential for determining subsequent actions. But developing a strategy to unilaterally control an opponent’s long-term payoff is challenging because, theoretically, one’s own payoff in every interaction is influenced by the actions of both parties.

In this article, we develop a game-theoretic framework for modeling attack–defense dynamics in network resilience, accounting for the simultaneous and repeated interactions between attackers and defenders. Here, the attacker aims to disrupt network connectivity, while the defender strives to maintain it. The dynamics are framed as a repeated game, where both parties decide which nodes to attack or defend based on the history of prior actions. By linking each network state, resulting from these attack–defense interactions, to the payoffs of both parties, we explore whether either the attacker or defender can unilaterally secure a specific payoff or constrain the opponent to a particular payoff, independent of the opponent’s long-term strategies. Our findings show that heterogeneous networks tend to favor the attacker in achieving unilateral long-term payoff control, while homogeneous networks benefit the defender. Furthermore, when unilateral payoff control is achievable, network heterogeneity allows both parties to exert control over a wider range of payoffs. We apply this framework to over 30 empirical networks, highlighting the critical role of unilateral payoff control in attack–defense interactions and its implications for network resilience.

Results

Repeated attack–defense game

We formulate the attack–defense process as a repeated two-player game between an attacker and a defender. In each round, both parties choose one of two possible actions: a radical action, denoted by R, and a conservative action, denoted by C. The precise network implementation of these two actions is specified below. At the game level, the four possible action profiles are RR, RC, CR, and CC, where in the action profile UV, U ∈ {R, C} denotes the attacker’s action and V ∈ {R, C} denotes the defender’s action.

The framework involves multiple rounds of interactions. Each party adjusts its current action according to the history of previous interactions. We focus on memory-one strategies, where each party conditions its next action on the action profile observed in the previous round. Let p=pRR,pRC,pCR,pCC represent the attacker’s strategy, where pUV ∈ [0, 1] denotes the probability that the attacker chooses action R in the next round, conditional on the previous action profile being UV (Fig. 1a). For example, pRR = 1 means that the attacker will choose action R with certainty if both parties selected R in the previous round. Similarly, let q=qRR,qRC,qCR,qCC denote the defender’s strategy, where qUV ∈ [0, 1] represents the probability that the defender chooses action R under the same condition. Let vUV(t) denote the probability that action profile UV occurs in round t, with ∑U, V ∈ {R, C}vUV(t) = 1. The state of the repeated game in round t is therefore described by v(t)=vRR(t),vRC(t),vCR(t),vCC(t)⊤. For each action profile, the attacker and the defender receive one-shot expected payoffs. We write the attacker’s one-shot payoff vector as SA=ARR,ARC,ACR,ACC, where AUV denotes the attacker’s payoff when the attacker takes U and the defender takes V. Similarly, the defender’s one-shot payoff vector is SD=DRR,DRC,DCR,DCC. The network-dependent construction of the entries AUV and DUV is described in the next subsection. Given the one-shot payoff vectors SA and SD, the expected payoffs of the attacker and the defender in round t are SA(t) = SAv(t), SD(t) = SDv(t). For a sufficiently long series of interactions, we focus on the long-term average payoffs

This formulation separates the strategic layer of the game from the network layer. The strategic layer is specified by the memory-one strategies p and q and by the repeated interaction dynamics, whereas the network enters through the one-shot payoff vectors SA and SD. In this framework, network resilience is evaluated from the defender’s long-term payoff: a higher value of SD indicates that the defender can better sustain system functionality under adaptive attacks. Thus, resilience is treated as a dynamic property shaped jointly by the network structure and the evolving strategies of both parties.

Network-dependent one-shot payoffs

We now specify how the network determines the actions, costs, and benefits in each one-shot interaction. Consider an unweighted network G with N nodes, denoted by N={1,2,…,N}, and E edges. The adjacency matrix is represented by {wij}i,j∈N, where wij = 1 if nodes i and j are connected, and wij = 0 otherwise. The degree of node i is ki=∑j∈Nwij. Node degree is used to rank nodes according to their structural importance in the network (Fig. 1b). The two abstract actions R and C are implemented as degree-based node selections. The radical action R corresponds to selecting the most highly-connected nodes, namely the top f fraction of nodes ranked by degree. The conservative action C corresponds to selecting relatively lowly-connected nodes, namely the nodes ranked between the top f and 2f fractions by degree. For the attacker, selecting an action means targeting the corresponding nodes for removal. For the defender, selecting an action means protecting the corresponding nodes from attack.

The payoff of each party in a one-shot interaction consists of two components: the cost of performing the selected action and the benefit generated by the resulting network state. The costs are degree-weighted. Removing a node by attack or maintaining a node by defense incurs a cost proportional to the number of incident edges, thereby incorporating heterogeneity in node importance. We denote the costs associated with actions R and C by cR and cC, respectively, with the detailed calculation given in the “Methods.” Briefly, if Ele denotes the number of edges remaining after the attack, then E − Ele represents the number of edges removed from the network. The attack cost is proportional to (E − Ele)/E, which measures the fraction of edges consumed by the attack.

Regarding the interacting benefits, when both attacker and defender choose the same action, both parties receive a benefit of 0. When the attacker targets the highly connected nodes (R) and the defender protects the less connected nodes (C), the network is disrupted, and the attacker’s benefit depends on the size of the largest connected component in the remaining network, denoted as bR, with the corresponding defender’s benefits being −bR (here we consider a zero-sum benefit interaction). Briefly, let NL denote the number of nodes in the largest connected component, which characterizes the structural robustness of the network under node removal; the attacker’s benefit is proportional to (N − NL)/N, representing the proportion of nodes that are rendered non-functional. Conversely, when the attacker targets the less connected nodes (C) and the defender protects the highly connected nodes (R), the network is similarly broken, with the attacker’s benefit depending on the largest connected component of the remaining network, denoted as bC, with the corresponding defender’s benefit being −bC. For each network state after a one-shot interaction, the attacker’s benefit reflects the degree of diminishing network connectivity, whereas the defender’s benefit is associated with the remained network connectivity.

To account for external uncertainty, we allow both attacks and defenses to fail. When the attacker and the defender choose the same action, the selected nodes are protected, but the defense may fail with probability y. Therefore, in the action profiles RR and CC, the expected benefits for the attacker are ybR and ybC, respectively, while the corresponding defender benefits are −ybR and −ybC. When the attacker and the defender choose different actions, the targeted nodes are not protected, but the attack may fail with probability x. Hence, the attack succeeds with probability 1 − x. In the action profiles RC and CR, the expected benefits for the attacker are (1 − x)bR and (1 − x)bC, respectively, while the corresponding defender benefits are −(1 − x)bR and −(1 − x)bC. Since costs are measured as relative fractions of edges, whereas benefits are measured as relative fractions of nodes, we introduce a benefit-to-cost coefficient λ to rescale the cost term. The parameter λ controls the relative importance of costs and benefits in the attack–defense system. A larger value of λ indicates that targeting or defending nodes is more costly, whereas λ = 0 corresponds to the cost-free case. Overall, the expected payoffs for each action profile are summarized as follows (Fig. 1c): the attacker’s expected payoff vector is SA = (ARR, ARC, ACR, ACC) = (ybR − λcR, (1 − x)bR − λcR, (1 − x)bC − λcC, ybC − λcC); the defender’s expected payoff vector is SD = (DRR, DRC, DCR, DCC) = ( − ybR − λcR, − (1 − x)bR − λcC, − (1 − x)bC − λcR, − ybC − λcC).

We are interested in whether the attacker can devise a strategy to unilaterally control either its own payoff or the defender’s payoff, regardless of the defender’s strategy. Similarly, we examine whether the defender can design a strategy to unilaterally control either its own payoff or the attacker’s payoff. For such co-evolving attack–defense systems, the extrema of the controllable payoff range provide a measure of system resilience. For instance, in a power grid, if the defender can stabilize voltage to a specific level despite varying attack strategies, the system demonstrates high resilience33. This paper focuses on how network structure affects system resilience within this game-theoretic attack–defense framework.

Payoff control strategies on complex networks

In this section, we explore the prevalence of these strategies across different network structures, revealing how network topology governs strategic coevolution. Following this, we delve into specific scenarios to determine the range within which these control strategies can constrain the opponent’s payoff. The node degree distribution is a key measure for distinguishing different network types. We examine two representative network models: Erdös–Rényi random networks17 (ER) and Barabási–Albert scale-free networks18 (SF). ER networks represent homogeneous networks, where all nodes have similar degrees, while SF networks represent heterogeneous networks, characterized by significant variations in node degrees. In order to only analyze the effect of network heterogeneity, we select two networks with the same number of nodes and the same average degree as a comparison. We investigate SF networks and ER networks, both with a size of N = 10,000 and an average node degree of 〈k〉 = 4. Control capacity and network resilience based on repeated attack–defense interactions

It is important to note that when the attacker either maximizes their own payoff or minimizes the defender’s payoff, the network tends to exhibit a smaller largest connected component, indicating more severe fragmentation. In essence, the attacker can compromise the system’s connectivity resilience through two primary strategies: by increasing their own payoff or by reducing the defender’s payoff. This relationship arises because the attacker’s payoff is positively correlated with the benefit parameters bR and bC, while the defender’s payoff is negatively correlated with them. As these benefit values increase, the resulting network structure becomes increasingly fragmented, reflected by a reduced LCC. Therefore, a higher attacker payoff or a lower defender payoff, both under the attacker’s control, signals diminished network resilience, as the connectivity of the system becomes more vulnerable to disruption. On the defender’s side, as shown in Fig. 4h, the maximum payoff the defender can secure through self-control, M¯DD, is slightly higher in SF networks than in ER networks. However, since the improvement is modest, overall, we conclude that the structural heterogeneity weakens the network resilience.

Essentially, the upper and lower bounds of the target payoff depend on the structure of the payoff matrix. Notably, since DRR < DCC, action C must be dominant for both parties. In this way, the structure-element DCC = − ybC − λcC imposes a strict limit on the maximal payoff for the defender, indicating a high level of resilience in the target network. Similarly, DRR = − ybR − λcR relates to the defender’s minimal payoff, which corresponds to the lowest resilience. Network heterogeneity distinctly influences these bounds: structural properties bR(GSF) > bR(GER) and bC(GSF) < bC(GER) (see Supplementary Fig. 1) allow DRR to be lower and DCC to be higher in SF networks, which implies that the defender can unilaterally increase their maximal payoff while decreasing the minimal payoff on SF networks. A similar effect is observed for the attacker, with heterogeneity providing a broader region in which the attacker can achieve the defender’s payoff, aligning with our intuitive expectations. Our results demonstrate that, for all the pairs X ∈ {A, D} and Y ∈ {A, D}, the attacker can always reduce resilience more effectively on SF networks. The payoff structure properties also suggest the relation ωXY(GSF)>ωXY(GER), since a higher maximal bound and a lower minimal bound lead to a wider controllable region. We need to clarify that our result does not contradict the impossibility theorem. The impossibility theorem (only one party can achieve payoff control at most) is established under the condition that (x, y) is fixed. However, the conclusion here emphasizes that the facilitation of heterogeneity on both parties is derived from the average outcome of the failure probability across the entire probability space based on the premise that control strategies exist.

General payoff control

We further extend our analysis to explore more general payoff control within the attack–defense framework. In different attack–defense interactions, the resources of the attacker and the defender are usually limited, so the attack fraction f may vary across scenarios. We therefore examine which value of f enables the attacker to control the defender’s payoff most effectively. For the BA scale-free networks with 〈k〉 = 4 considered here, we find that the optimal attack fraction occurs near f* ≃ 0.17. At this point, the attacker achieves the greatest level of control over the defender’s payoff in our repeated attack–defense game. This optimal control is observed from both the controllable area and the control capacity, as shown in Fig. 5a, b. Near this critical point, the attacker maximizes its influence over the network, achieving the best control performance from both perspectives.

Also, the control objective Y is not necessarily limited in {A, D, E}. Finally, we extend our analysis to more general control scenarios, where one party enforces a linear relationship between their own payoff and that of the opponent, rather than fixing it to a specific value. The general control objective can be expressed as Y∈{αSA+βSD∣α,β∈R}, representing a linear relationship between the attacker’s payoff (SA) and the defender’s payoff (SD) under the control. Specific values of α and β recover the scenarios studied earlier. For instance, setting α = 1 and β = 0 corresponds to the attacker controlling their own payoff. For general cases, the equation αSA + βSD + γ = 0 can be reformulated as SA−u=vSD−u, where u = − γ/(α + β) and v = ( − β/α). This implies that SA − u is v times SD − u. The adversarial nature of the attack–defense dynamics enforces opposite signs for α and β. Fixing α = 1 and varying β from 0 to −∞, we have v = − β. Thus, as the absolute value of β increases, the control objective shifts to making SA − u increasingly dominant relative to SD − u. We study the general payoff control by the attacker and present the controllable area and the average control width for a broad range of the benefit-to-cost ratio λ and the objective coefficient β. As shown in Fig. 5c, d, the increasing strength of the objective coefficient ∣β∣ increases both the controllable area and the average control width. Therefore, it proves to be easier to achieve a larger dominance of SA − u over SD − u.

Discussion

In many real-world networked systems, attacks and defenses are not isolated events. Instead, they occur repeatedly and often simultaneously, forming long-term co-evolving processes between adversarial agents. During these processes, the effectiveness and cost of an attack or defense depend on which nodes are targeted or protected, and both attacks and defenses may fail because of uncertainty in complex environments28,29,31,32. These features make network resilience fundamentally more dynamic than what can be captured by a single static perturbation.

Classical studies of network robustness are largely rooted in a physical percolation perspective, where the central question is how the connectivity of a network changes under node or edge removal6. In this context, robustness usually refers to the structural integrity of the underlying network, and is commonly characterized by topological quantities, such as the size of the largest connected component and the percolation threshold. These quantities capture how well a network preserves connectivity after perturbations, and have provided deep insights into structural vulnerability. In the present work, such connectivity-based quantities still play a central role: they determine the network-dependent benefits in the one-shot attack–defense payoffs. However, in strategic attack–defense scenarios, network damage is not determined by topology alone. It also depends on the adaptive decisions of attackers and defenders, the costs associated with different actions, and the long-term consequences of repeated interactions. Therefore, although the payoff construction is grounded in structural robustness, the resulting resilience perspective is dynamical: we evaluate how network connectivity and payoff outcomes behave over repeated attack–defense interactions. Motivated by this gap, we extend the classical topology-centered perspective toward a payoff-driven control paradigm centered on strategic agents.

The central idea of our framework is to quantify network resilience through unilateral payoff control in repeated attack–defense interactions. Rather than asking only how much damage a network suffers after a prescribed attack, we ask whether one player can design a strategy that enforces a desired payoff outcome independently of the opponent’s strategy. In this setting, the attacker may attempt to enforce a high payoff for itself or a low payoff for the defender, whereas the defender may attempt to maintain its own payoff or suppress the attacker’s payoff. The extrema of these controllable payoff ranges then provide a game-theoretic measure of resilience: a network is less resilient if the attacker can enforce a high attack payoff or drive the defender’s payoff to a low value, and more resilient if the defender can maintain a high payoff despite adaptive attacks.

Our main mathematical results, Eqs. 4 and 5, establish the precise conditions under which either the attacker or the defender can unilaterally control a specified payoff objective. This objective can be any linear combination of the two parties’ payoffs, thereby allowing the framework to describe self-payoff control, opponent-payoff control, and more general payoff-control objectives within the same formalism. Importantly, these control conditions are formulated at the level of the repeated game, whereas the underlying network enters through the payoff construction: node selection determines the costs of attack and defense, while the resulting connectivity loss determines the benefits. In this way, arbitrary network structures can be incorporated into a unified game-theoretic framework. The possibility of unilateral payoff control in repeated interactions is rooted in the theory of zero-determinant strategies. Traditionally, controlling multi-agent payoffs without explicit coordination was considered theoretically implausible36,37. This view changed with the work of Press and Dyson, who showed that in iterated games a player can enforce a linear relationship between its own payoff and that of its opponent by exploiting the determinant structure of the Markov transition matrix38. Such strategies have since motivated applications in engineered and socio-technical systems, including voltage stabilization in power systems33 and defense against malicious crowdsourcing39. Our work brings this payoff-control perspective into the study of network robustness and resilience, showing how the structural properties of a network shape the ability of attackers and defenders to enforce long-term payoff outcomes.

Beyond this conceptual connection to zero-determinant strategies, our framework also extends payoff control to more realistic attack–defense settings. Existing implementations are often restricted to simplified or symmetric games with limited action spaces, whereas real networked systems involve asymmetric payoffs, variable benefit-to-cost ratios, probabilistic failures, and feedback between adversarial decisions. By deriving a general and computable condition for unilateral payoff control without assuming payoff symmetry, our approach makes it possible to evaluate how network structure shapes both control capacity and resilience in repeated attack–defense interactions. Our results reveal a systematic asymmetry: heterogeneous networks tend to enlarge the attacker’s controllable payoff range, whereas homogeneous networks provide stronger constraints on attacker control and relatively greater advantage to the defender. This finding highlights why one-sided analyses can be misleading in adversarial systems, where targeting highly connected nodes may appear optimal from an attacker-only perspective but can be altered by defensive adaptation and strategic feedback28,29,31,32. The empirical networks further illustrate this point: heterogeneous systems, such as online social or information networks, can provide attackers with amplified leverage for unilateral payoff control, whereas more homogeneous systems, such as road networks, restrict such control through redundancy and more uniform connectivity35. This perspective is distinct from classical network controllability, which typically asks how driver nodes steer system dynamics through external inputs40,41. Here, the entire network is treated as the medium through which two strategic agents jointly shape long-term payoff outcomes. The control capacity then quantifies the range of payoffs that can be enforced through repeated interactions, linking network topology directly to robust network engineering and adversarial dynamics.

This perspective also clarifies the relation between classical robustness and the resilience measure studied here. Our framework retains this structural foundation6,16,42 but places it within a repeated strategic process: connectivity loss determines the network-dependent payoffs, while resilience is evaluated through the payoff ranges that attackers or defenders can enforce over long-term interactions. Within this framework, structurally heterogeneous networks exhibit lower resilience because they enlarge the attacker’s controllable payoff range and allow the defender’s payoff to be driven lower. This finding is consistent with classical percolation theory, which characterizes scale-free networks as structurally fragile under targeted attacks15, but it also extends that insight to a strategic setting. Remarkably, the attacker’s optimal strategy converges on a specific fraction of target nodes, which coincides with the critical point defining the network’s percolation threshold, a result confirmed across both synthetic and empirical networks. These results show that network topology not only governs structural vulnerability but also shapes the strategic coevolution between attackers and defenders by determining where unilateral payoff control becomes most effective.

Previous studies on payoff-control strategies have shown that zero-determinant and related autocratic strategies can enforce linear payoff relations in a variety of repeated-game settings, including continuous action spaces, alternating actions, asymmetric games, and interactions with implementation errors or environmental information43–49. Building on this line of work, our framework connects payoff control to network robustness and resilience. Nevertheless, the present model should be viewed as a tractable starting point rather than a complete description of all adversarial network dynamics. Several assumptions made here, including complete recovery between rounds, simple graph structure, binary actions, and synchronous updates, can be relaxed in future work.

One important extension concerns temporal and asynchronous dynamics. In the present model, each round of interaction begins with a fully recovered network, whereas real systems may experience new attacks before complete recovery. Such temporal changes can substantially affect payoff control in networks50. Incorporating partial recovery, cumulative damage, or multi-step memory strategies would allow the model to represent longer-lived network states and more realistic adversarial decision processes. Similarly, the assumption that the attacker and defender update on the same timescale can be relaxed. Taking a simple extension as an example, suppose the attacker acts once per unit time, whereas the defender acts once every two time units. In this case, the attack–defense state can be described by the action profile of a two-step interaction, denoted by (U1, V1, U2) ∈ {R, C}3, where U1 and V1 denote the attacker’s and defender’s actions in the first step, respectively, and U2 denotes the attacker’s action in the subsequent time step. This formulation yields eight possible action profiles. An analogous analysis can then be carried out by extending the four action profiles in the same-timescale setting to the eight action profiles in the different-timescale setting. We leave a systematic investigation of this extension for future work.

A second direction is to extend both the network structures and the action spaces considered in the present framework. On the network side, our model currently treats networks as simple graphs, whereas real systems may involve multilayer networks, dynamic networks, networks of networks, community structure, evolving utility associations, or multiple coupled network states51,52. On the attack–defense side, practical adversarial systems may involve multiple attackers and defenders, or continuous choices, such as removing an arbitrary fraction or set of nodes. These extensions would enlarge the strategy space and make the payoff-control conditions more complex. In many real systems, network complexity and strategic complexity may coexist. Community structure provides a concrete example: modular organization changes the network-dependent payoff structures, while also allowing new strategic actions, such as selecting which community to attack or how to distribute damage across communities before applying local targeting strategies. A more complete treatment would therefore expand the action space to include such community-level decisions together with local degree-based attack or defense actions. Together, these extensions could help bridge the gap between tractable payoff-control theory and real-world adversarial dynamics in complex networked systems.
