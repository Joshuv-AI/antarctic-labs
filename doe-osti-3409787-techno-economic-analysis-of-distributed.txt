1 Introduction
The Crow Creek neighborhood is a small community of 35 homes and 70 year-round residents
located in the Girdwood Valley that is formally part of the Municipality of Anchorage. Despite
being part of the larger municipality, Crow Creek’s location, according to local partners,
prevents the residents from accessing many of the public resources—creating challenges in
providing reliable and affordable power, heat, and water. The local utility that serves the
Girdwood community will not extend service to Crow Creek because the access road is in an
avalanche zone. Therefore, homes must provide their own electricity primarily via diesel
generators, though some households have batteries and solar photovoltaic (PV) systems.

Crow Creek Neighborhood Association applied to Energy to Communities (E2C) Expert Match
to assess the feasibility of a microgrid to provide year-round power across the community.
Assistance building a baseline estimate for energy consumption, sizing energy storage and
backup power systems to meet current or future energy needs, estimating system costs, and
describing key process steps and considerations will help the community understand options,
navigate trade-offs, and make decisions.

 1

1.1 Energy to Communities Expert Match Scope
The Energy to Communities program is a U.S. Department of Energy–funded technical
assistance program that provides communities expertise and tools to make local energy systems
more affordable, reliable, and secure. The National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR) scoped two
main tasks through this assistance for the Upper Crow Creek Neighborhood Association:

 1. Area microgrid techno-economic prefeasibility analysis:
 A. Co-develop a survey for residents to assess household electricity consumption,
 challenges, and needs.
 B. Develop a community load profile that estimates the community’s electricity
 demand over the course of a year, accounting for seasonal and daily variation that
 is realistic for south-central Alaska.
 C. Conduct REopt® analyses that account for uncertainty in load and PV cost,
 generating an array of cost-optimal PV, battery energy storage systems (BESS),
 and generator sizes that can reliably meet the expected demands of the
 community.
 2. Microgrid decision-making roadmap:
 A. Identify critical process steps and considerations necessary as Crow Creek decides
 whether and how to establish a utility to centralize electricity provision.
 B. Identify technical, logistic, and regulatory requirements for planning a centralized
 microgrid.

2 Methodology
REopt is a techno-economic tool that helps users identify the most cost-effective mix of energy
solutions (e.g., generators, batteries, wind turbines, PV) for a specific location based on its
unique goals and needs. REopt can simulate an off-grid energy system and estimate cost-optimal
PV, BESS, and generator sizes and dispatch, making it an ideal tool for preliminary energy
system planning in Crow Creek.

To conduct a REopt analysis, NLR first co-developed a survey for Crow Creek residents with the
Upper Crow Creek Neighborhood Association to assess 1) current local electricity generation
sources, 2) household energy needs, and 3) desired energy usage under a microgrid structure.
The community point of contact reviewed and approved all survey questions and distributed both
paper and online versions to local homeowners. The survey remained open from November to
December 2025.

The primary goals of this survey were to identify the types and sizes of generators residents were
using to power their homes and whether these generators were sufficient to meet their demands
and needs. The survey highlighted common challenges associated with individual generation and
what lifestyle or behavior upgrades homeowners would pursue if additional generation capacity
were reliable and affordable.

Eight responses were received, representing various households, for a response rate of 23% of
households. Both qualitative and quantitative data from the survey were used to construct a
community load profile and to estimate additional REopt model inputs.

 3

2.1 Generator-Based Load Profile Estimation
The first step in producing a REopt model was to compile the data from the survey respondents
and develop a load profile that would represent the community’s electricity demands under a
microgrid scenario.

Respondents reported on the nameplate capacity of their primary generators. Backup generators
were not considered in the load profile estimation (assuming residents would keep backup
generators even with utility-provided electricity). These values were converted into estimated
average power draw by assuming operation at 60% of rated capacity—a mid‑range value
recommended by most generator manufacturers for continuous use, reflecting typical household
behavior that avoids both underloading and overloading. Generators running continuously at
30%–40% may be at risk for wet stacking, causing long-term engine damage, whereas generators
running continuously at close to their rated capacities risk low fuel efficiency and shortened
operational lifespans.

Each respondent was categorized into a usage archetype based on their electricity consumption
and as a function of their generator size, appliances, and household footprint. Archetypes were
loosely designed as follows (noting designation is both subjective and a combination of many
factors included in the survey):

 • Archetype A—Low consumer:
 o 400–800 square feet (sq ft)
 o 1.8- to 3.5-kilowatt (kW) generator (primary)
 o Major appliances: Lights, kitchen appliances (propane refrigerators).
 • Archetype B—Middle consumer:
             o 800–1,000 sq ft
 o 3.5- to 4.5-kW generator (primary)
 o Major appliances: Electric refrigerator, computers.
 • Archetype C—High consumer (many appliances, large generators):
             o 1,000–2,000+ sq ft
 o 4.5- to 6.5-kW generator (primary)
 o Major appliances: Dishwasher, washer/dryer.
Respondents were categorized by both their current and desired archetype. Several respondents
indicated a desire to move to a higher consumption category, whereas others reported satisfaction
with their current energy use. The distribution of homeowners in each category is shown in

2.3 Basis of Comparison
Because there is no centralized energy system in Crow Creek, the first step to complete a REopt
analysis was to model a centralized scenario with only diesel to act as a business-as-usual (BAU)
case. Business-as-usual refers to a scenario where energy services are supplied only by the
existing systems or utilities. Considering there is no centralized supply of electricity, we must
take the BAU to be the hypothetical diesel-generator-only scenario to provide an equal means of
comparison to later scenarios that include solar PV and batteries—energy technologies
specifically requested by Crow Point to be modeled.

The cost of running this hypothetical generator over a 25-year period is the life cycle cost (LCC)
of the energy system. We compare this LCC to scenarios that also include PV and BESS to
assess whether incorporating PV and BESS is cost-effective. Following are the definitions of
important results of REopt analyses that we use to compare scenarios.

Life cycle costs: The present value of all costs, after taxes and incentives, associated with the
project. This includes capital, replacement, fuel, and operations and maintenance costs. Life
cycle costs do not include any distribution infrastructure such as poles or wires, and REopt does
not estimate the cost of transmission or distribution infrastructure or energy management
systems. For reference, energy management and distribution infrastructure costs between diesel-
only and diesel-hybrid scenarios are likely similar on the same order of magnitude.

Net present value (NPV): The present value of all savings (or costs, if negative) realized by the
project. Net present value is calculated as the difference between the LCC of the BAU scenario
and the LCC of the investment case.

We will also compare the PV size in kilowatts, BESS storage and power sizes, diesel generator
size, diesel fuel consumption, and initial capital costs (before incentives) between scenarios.

REopt sizes generators as aggregate capacity and does not consider ancillary services. This total
capacity can be split into multiple generators, and additional generator capacity may be added for
N+1 contingency—ensuring the continuous operation of a microgrid in the case of maintenance
or generator failure.

To operate a diesel generator to meet Crow Creek’s expected loads, it is estimated to cost $5.3
million to $6.7 million in present value over a 25-year period, depending on load.

Generators can meet both maximum and minimum loads while preventing underloading
conditions and leaving room for sudden spikes in demand. These generator sizes are forced in
subsequent runs, meaning every run must include generator(s) of equal capacity to that listed
under the corresponding load. For example, a scenario with loads equal to the estimated baseline
must have at least 172.5 kW of a primary generator. This ensures 100% of community loads can
be met in an emergency and/or period where photovoltaic energy is not available. Diesel-based
microgrids are by far the most common energy system in Alaska, and ensuring the community’s
critical needs can be met is a prerequisite.

4 Implementation Roadmap
 This section outlines a strategic pathway for developing a centralized energy system in Crow
 Creek. Following Chugach Electric’s determination that a grid extension would not be possible,
 according to local partners, this roadmap presents a phased approach to establishing an
 independent utility structure that can improve both power quality and quantity for community
 members.

 The roadmap is structured around three major phases: 1) preliminary feasibility and planning
 grant acquisition, 2) comprehensive technical design and regulatory groundwork, and 3)
 finalizing, funding, and operating. Success depends on sustained technical partnership, strategic
 stakeholder alignment, and careful navigation of Alaska’s utility regulatory framework.

 4.1 Phase 1: Preliminary Feasibility and Community Engagement
 The purpose of this phase is to align stakeholders and begin to develop proposals that can turn
 the prefeasibility assessment provided in Section 3 into bankable technical documents to support
 greater capital acquisition.

 Initial technical assessment: The REopt assessment provided in Section 3 provides a
 preliminary model of centralized energy generation in Crow Creek that can be leveraged as the
 community prepares planning grants or other funding applications. Based on the fuel savings
 estimated by incorporating PV and BESS, the models estimate positive net present values,
 indicating initial feasibility for a hybrid PV-BESS-diesel system in Crow Creek.

 Although REopt provides valuable preliminary insights, it operates under simplified assumptions
 that must be refined during the planning grant phase. For example, it operates under the
 assumption there is an existing utility structure. The next steps in this phase emphasize the need
 to extend the project planning period to turn these preliminary models into refined, bankable
 documents.

 Community engagement and documentation: Centralized infrastructure for energy generation
 provides opportunity for resilient energy supply. Therefore, a critical part of Phase 1 is engaging
 the community to align values and needs that will be the basis for the community to advocate for
 critical infrastructure from state and federal agencies. There are many ways to frame the
 discussion around centralizing energy generation, but the following topics may be important
 areas to assess community perspectives for improved planning and engagement.

 • Reliability
 • Ability to increase loads beyond a household generator’s capacity
 • Noise and air quality
 • Fuel storage and bulk purchasing
 • Access to capital from state or federal agencies.
 Properly documenting a community’s interest in a central utility could be useful when needing to
 demonstrate need. This step may be helpful in future grant applications or partnerships.

 14

During this phase, the community could consider discussing preferences and concerns, including
the following:
• How much local control does the community want versus external control and expertise?
• Is there capacity and willingness for community members to serve on a utility board and
 manage operations?
Another main point of discussion during this phase may be feasible utility governance structures.

4.2.2 Distribution Planning
This section outlines some of the fundamental characteristics and considerations for the planning
of the distribution infrastructure in Crow Creek.

One approach for distribution planning in Crow Creek is to use a three-phase, multigrounded
wye distribution system at medium voltage for the main feeder from the power plant. This
provides good voltage control, efficient power transfer, and future capacity for any larger three-
phase loads that may be added later. In addition, three phases means the load is shared, so no one
conductor is overloaded (meaning smaller conductors can be used). Most consumers would be
served from single-phase primary laterals (single-phase conductor plus neutral) using single-
phase distribution transformers in the 5- to 25-kilovolt-ampere (kVA) range. These transformers
would supply standard split-phase service to individual homes.

Figure 1 shows the assumed area of interest used to estimate line lengths. The main path between
the Start and End markers would be three phase, whereas all roads spidering out would be single
phase. To start, we assume five to eight houses for each 25-kVA transformer to preliminarily
estimate costs, but the actual number of transformers will depend on coincident peak usage
between homes and estimated load growth.
