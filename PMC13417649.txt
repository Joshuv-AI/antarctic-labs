Evaluating the global psychotrauma screen total score using item response theory in an Italian community sample
Authors: Antony Bologna, Rodolfo Rossi, Tommaso Barlattani, Edoardo Trebbi, Tommaso Jannini, Valentina Socci, Alessandro Rossi, Giorgio Di Lorenzo, Francesca Pacitti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: The Global Psychotrauma Screen (GPS) is a brief transdiagnostic screener for trauma-related symptoms. Although the Italian version has previously been examined using factor-analytic methods, evidence on item-level functioning and on the interpretability of the symptom total remains limited.Objective: To determine whether the 17 GPS symptom items support the use of a single total score as an efficient indicator of global trauma-related burden.Method: Data from an age- and gender-balanced analytic sample of 6930 Italian adults recruited online during the COVID-19 outbreak were analysed. Essential unidimensionality was examined using parallel analysis and bifactor indices (ECVg and ωH). One- and two-parameter logistic IRT models were compared, and item fit, local dependence, test information, conditional reliability, and sensitivity to aberrant responding were evaluated.Results: Bifactor indices supported essential unidimensionality (ECVg = 0.73; ωH = 0.77). The 2PL model outperformed the 1PL model and showed acceptable global fit (RMSEA = 0.058, SRMSR = 0.04, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.94). Item discrimination ranged from 0.68 to 3.03, and item difficulty ranged from −0.95 to 2.71. The test information function peaked at θ = −0.24 and indicated useful precision across a broad range of moderate-to-high trauma burden. Item parameters remained stable after removing of respondents with aberrant response patterns.Conclusions: In this community sample, the Italian GPS symptom total can be interpreted as a psychometrically defensible indicator of global trauma-related burden. The scale was especially informative from slightly below-average to elevated levels of severity, supporting its use as a brief first-line screening measure while still requiring replication in independent and clinical samples.

Introduction

Trauma-related reactions are heterogeneous and can include intrusive memories, avoidance, hyperarousal, anxiety, depressed mood, dissociation, sleep problems, and broader distress-related symptoms. This heterogeneity helps explain why trauma can be difficult to recognise promptly, even though it may profoundly impair emotional stability and well-being (Morganstein et al., 2017; Straussner & Calnan, 2014; Zoromba et al., 2024).

The pathway to care is often particularly challenging in time-limited clinical settings. Many individuals with trauma-related or other mental health problems first present in primary care, where general practitioners play an important role in early recognition and referral but often must make decisions within brief consultations (Neulinger et al., 2024). In such contexts, brief screening tools can provide a pragmatic complement to clinical judgement.

To be useful in practice, screening instruments should be brief, easy to administer and score, and able to distinguish probable cases from non-cases with acceptable sensitivity and specificity (Trevethan, 2017; Wilson & Jungner, 1968). Because these tools are often used to inform first-line clinical decisions, their psychometric properties must be evaluated rigorously.

The Italian context of the present study is also relevant. Data were collected during the early COVID-19 emergency, when the population was exposed to a combination of public-health threat, social restrictions, bereavement and fear of loss, disruptions to work and daily routines, and economic uncertainty. Stress- and trauma-related symptom burden in this setting may therefore differ from symptom burden observed in samples defined by a single traumatic event, such as assault, combat, or natural disaster. This makes it important to evaluate whether a broad transdiagnostic screener can provide a defensible summary of symptom burden in an Italian community sample.

This population was facing a particularly difficult constellation of stressors at the time of data collection. Italy was the first European country to experience a large-scale COVID-19 outbreak and, by late March 2020, was under a nationwide lockdown; the rapid escalation of cases and deaths, the strain on health-care services, and exceptional restrictions on movement and social contact created high levels of uncertainty, perceived threat, isolation, and disruption to family and working life (Brooks et al., 2020; Remuzzi & Remuzzi, 2020). Italian survey evidence collected during the same outbreak indicated high levels of trauma-spectrum symptoms and broader psychological distress in the general population, with vulnerability varying by demographic factors and exposure to the emergency (Mazza et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2021; Talevi et al., 2020). In this context, a brief transdiagnostic measure was especially relevant because the COVID-19 emergency did not represent a circumscribed traumatic incident, but a prolonged population-level stressor combining health threat, social restriction, bereavement risk, and socioeconomic uncertainty.

The Global Psychotrauma Screen (GPS) is a 22-item transdiagnostic instrument that can be used both to generate a multidimensional symptom profile and to provide a brief indicator of overall trauma burden (Anastassiou-Hadjicharalambous et al., 2026). Its conceptual premise is that trauma exposure may be associated not only with PTSD symptoms, but also with broader negative affective, dissociative, and functional responses. Psychometric evidence has supported the GPS in multiple contexts, including the Italian validation based on factor analysis and a US validation study (Grace et al., 2023; Rossi et al., 2021).

However, factor analysis and related Classical Test Theory approaches mainly inform latent structure at the scale level. They do not directly show how informative each item is across the severity continuum or how much precision the total score provides at different trait levels (Embretson & Reise, 2025; Shou et al., 2022). By contrast, Item Response Theory (IRT) models the probability of endorsing an item as a function of the latent trait and item parameters, thereby providing item-level evidence on discrimination, difficulty, and measurement precision.

Accordingly, the present study applied an IRT framework to the Italian GPS symptom items in a large community sample surveyed during the COVID-19 emergency. The primary objective was to test whether the psychometric properties of the 17 symptom items and the resulting information functions support the interpretation of the GPS symptom total as an efficient indicator of global trauma-related burden.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional web-based study is part of a long-term monitoring programme of mental health outcomes in the general population (Rossi et al., 2020). Participants were recruited between 27 and 31 March 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, through a web-based survey disseminated via social networks. Inclusion criteria were: (i) age between 18 and 70 years and (ii) fluency in written Italian. Individuals who did not meet these criteria were excluded.

Recruitment followed a non-probability convenience and snowball strategy. The survey link was disseminated through social-network channels and online posts; participation was voluntary, anonymous, and uncompensated. Participants provided online informed consent before accessing the survey. Because the invitation link was openly disseminated, a conventional response rate could not be estimated. The initial dataset (N = 18147) is the same as that utilised for the initial structural validation of the Italian GPS (Rossi et al., 2021); however, the present study utilises a specifically extracted and balanced analytic subsample to meet the stringent assumptions required for IRT calibration.

Measures

The survey comprised two main sections designed to collect background information and assess trauma-related symptomatology. A sociodemographic questionnaire captured background characteristics, including gender, age, citizenship, relationship status, and parental status. Importantly, because data were collected during the peak of the first nationwide lockdown, the COVID-19 outbreak itself – with its unprecedented disruption – served as the primary potentially traumatic event anchoring the GPS assessment for all participants, regardless of prior individual trauma history. Trauma-related symptoms were assessed with the Global Psychotrauma Screen (GPS; Rossi et al., 2021), a brief self-report screening instrument comprising a total of 22 items. Conceptually and structurally, the instrument is divided into two distinct sections: 17 items evaluate concurrent trauma-related symptoms (encompassing domains such as PTSD, complex PTSD, anxiety, depression, dissociation, and self-harm) using a dichotomous present/absent format, while the remaining 5 items assess historical risk and protective factors (e.g. childhood trauma, history of mental illness, and social support). For the purposes of the present Item Response Theory (IRT) investigation, only the 17 symptom items (yielding a cumulative total score ranging from 0 to 17) were included in the analyses. The rationale for this selection is strictly psychometric. IRT models require that the calibrated items measure a single, continuous latent trait (i.e. the assumption of essential unidimensionality). While the 17 symptom items collectively indicate the severity of the concurrent global trauma burden, the 5 risk and protective factors represent causal, historical, or contextual variables rather than clinical manifestations of the distress trait itself. Including these non-symptom items would inevitably violate the unidimensionality assumption, artificially multidimensionalize the model, and confound the estimation of item difficulty and discrimination parameters.

The broader parent validation study also administered validated comparator measures of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and perceived stress, specifically the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI), and the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS). Concurrent validity of the Italian GPS against these established measures was examined in the original Italian validation paper using correlation analyses (Rossi et al., 2021). The present IRT study therefore builds on that published Classical Test Theory evidence by focusing on item-level discrimination, item difficulty, and total-score measurement precision rather than reproducing the already published correlation matrices.

Statistical analysis

Before the psychometric analyses, the dataset underwent data cleaning and demographic balancing. Although modern missing data handling techniques, such as Full Information Maximum Likelihood or multiple imputation, are generally recommended over listwise deletion (Graham, 2009), the proportion of missing data in the initial sample was exceptionally low (3.21%). Because missing rates below 5% are widely considered inconsequential for inducing substantial statistical bias (Schafer, 1999), cases with missing values were removed by listwise deletion to obtain a complete-case dataset. Crucially, this complete-case filtering had to be performed prior to the stratified random sampling approach used to balance participants by age and gender; imputing missing values beforehand would have introduced artificial symptomatic profiles into the matching pool, potentially confounding the precise stratification required for unbiased IRT calibration. Finally, the psychometric properties of the GPS were examined within an Item Response Theory (IRT) framework.

Data cleaning consisted of applying eligibility criteria, verifying age and gender values, coding GPS yes/no responses as binary indicators, and identifying missing values in analytic variables. The original online survey did not include formal attention-check items or automated bot-detection procedures; this issue is acknowledged as a limitation. To partially address response-quality concerns a posteriori, the present analyses included IRT person-fit statistics to identify severely aberrant response patterns.

In Item Response Theory, the precise estimation of item parameters (particularly the difficulty parameter, b) assumes that the latent trait distribution is not confounded by heavily skewed demographic characteristics. Web-based surveys frequently suffer from over-representation of specific demographic groups (e.g. younger females). If we had calibrated the IRT model on the unstratified dataset, the item locations would have been artificially biased towards the trait levels characteristic of the over-represented group. Sacrificing sample size to achieve demographic balance ensured that the parameter estimates reflect a standardised, unbiased population structure.

To verify that the sampling procedure used to extract the balanced analytic subset (N = 6930) did not introduce unintended selection bias, we compared the demographic (age, gender) and clinical (GPS core symptom total score, items 1–16 and 18) characteristics of the retained participants against the excluded subjects (N = 11217). Differences in gender distributions were evaluated using Pearson’s Chi-square test. Due to deviations from the normality assumption, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank-sum test) was employed for continuous variables (age and GPS total score).

Dimensionality analysis: We first examined the latent dimensional structure of the data to justify the use of a unidimensional IRT model. Given the dichotomous nature of the items, parallel analysis was conducted on the tetrachoric correlation matrix. We then evaluated essential unidimensionality – that is, the presence of a dominant general trait despite minor multidimensionality – by calculating explained common variance for the general factor (ECVg) and omega hierarchical (ωH) from a bifactor model. According to Reise et al. (2013), ECVg values > 0.60 and ωH values > 0.70 support the interpretation of the total score and the application of unidimensional IRT models.

Model selection: Next, we specified and compared two logistic models for binary data: the one-parameter logistic model (1PLM) and the two-parameter logistic model (2PLM). The three-parameter model (3PLM) was excluded a priori because guessing was assumed to be negligible in a clinical self-report instrument. Model selection was based on convergence diagnostics and nested model comparison using the likelihood-ratio test (LRT) and information criteria (AIC and BIC), favouring the solution with lower values while also considering parsimony.

Both calibration models were fitted to the same 17 binary GPS symptom indicators. In the 1PL model, item discrimination parameters were constrained to equality, and item difficulties were freely estimated. In the 2PL model, both item-specific discrimination and difficulty parameters were freely estimated. No demographic covariates were included in the IRT calibration models; age and gender were used for sampling/balancing and descriptive checks only.

Assessment of model fit and assumptions: Model-data fit was examined at two levels. Global fit was assessed using M2 together with χ2-based absolute and comparative fit indices: the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMSR), the Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), and the Comparative Fit Index (CFI).

Conventional cut-offs (Maydeu-Olivares, 2013; Xu et al., 2017) were used to judge model acceptability (e.g. CFI/TLI ≥ 0.90; RMSEA/SRMSR ≤ 0.05). Local independence was assessed using the standardised residual matrix (Yen's Q3) and Chen and Thissen's local dependence statistics (LD-χ2). In interpreting Q3 values, we adopted a flexible approach consistent with the essential unidimensionality framework (Christensen et al., 2017). Residuals exceeding the critical thresholds (|Q3| > 0.20 and |V| > 0.174) were inspected qualitatively to distinguish structural violations from superficial redundancies (e.g. wording effects), while recognising the sensitivity of these statistics to large sample sizes (Chen & Thissen, 1997; Yen, 1984).

Calibration and scoring: After confirming the overall model structure, item parameters were estimated and item characteristic curves (ICCs) were generated. Item-level fit was assessed for each item; to control Type I error across multiple comparisons, the significance threshold was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction (p < .05 / number of items). Where applicable, item fit was also evaluated using RMSEA values. Individual latent trait scores (θ) were estimated with the expected a posteriori (EAP) method. Measurement precision was summarised using both marginal and empirical reliability. To assess precision across the severity continuum, conditional reliability was calculated and the test information function (TIF) was visually inspected. Statistical significance was set at α < 0.05.

Sensitivity analysis: To assess the robustness of parameter estimates, person fit was evaluated using the Zh statistic. Respondents with severely aberrant response patterns (|Zh| > 3; Paek & Cole, 2019) were identified and removed, and the 2PL model was re-estimated in the cleaned sample. Convergence was verified using both the convergence criterion and the second-order test. Stability of item difficulty parameters was evaluated by computing the Pearson correlation between estimates obtained from the full and cleaned samples.

All statistical analyses were conducted within the R software environment (Version 4.5.1; R Core Team, 2025) utilising RStudio (Version 2024.12.1 + 563; Posit team, 2025). Preliminary data processing, demographic balancing, and descriptive statistics were performed using the tidyverse ecosystem (Wickham et al., 2019) and the jmv package (Selker et al., 2025). Structural validity and dimensionality were evaluated in a sequential framework: first, the tetrachoric correlation matrix and parallel analysis were computed using the psych package (Revelle, 2024); second, a confirmatory bifactor model was specified and estimated via the lavaan package (Rosseel, 2012) employing the robust Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance adjusted (WLSMV) estimator. Comprehensive model- and factor-level structural indices – specifically the Explained Common Variance (ECV), the Proportion of Uncontaminated Correlations (PUC), Omega Hierarchical (ωH), and Construct Replicability (H) indices – were derived using the BifactorIndicesCalculator package (Dueber, 2021). Unidimensional Item Response Theory (IRT) models (1PL and 2PL) were calibrated via Marginal Maximum Likelihood (MML) estimation utilising the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm implemented in the mirt package (Chalmers, 2012). Finally, all psychometric and targeting functions were visualised via ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) and structurally arranged using the gridExtra package (Auguie, 2017).

Results

The initial sample comprised 18147 participants. A total of 583 cases (3.21%) were excluded because of missing data. To reduce potential demographic bias, participants were then balanced by age and gender using a matching procedure. The final analytic sample included 6930 participants.

Preliminary analyses were conducted to evaluate the comparability between the retained analytic sample (N = 6930) and the excluded subsample (N = 11217) across baseline demographic and clinical variables. Regarding age, the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test indicated no statistically significant difference (W = 39400043, p = .113) between the retained group (M = 37.92, SD = 12.95) and the excluded participants (M = 38.41, SD = 21.99), confirming that the sub-sampling procedure did not introduce age-related selection bias.

In contrast, a highly significant difference emerged for gender distribution (χ2(1) = 6051.80, p < .001). The excluded group was almost entirely composed of female participants (97.9% Female, 2.1% Male), whereas the retained sample exhibited a perfectly balanced distribution (n = 3465 women and n = 3465 men; 50.0% each). This discrepancy represents a deliberate outcome of the matching protocol, implemented to correct the severe overrepresentation of female respondents in the original macro-dataset and ensure gender neutrality during item calibration.

Consistently, a significant difference was observed regarding clinical severity as measured by the GPS core symptoms (items 1–16 and 18). The excluded group reported a significantly higher mean score (M = 7.51, SD = 3.77) than the balanced analytic sample (M = 6.69, SD = 3.89; Mann-Whitney W = 43693845, p < .001). This higher baseline distress reflects the demographic skew of the excluded individuals, aligning with established literature reporting elevated pandemic-related post-traumatic symptoms among female cohorts (Mazza et al., 2020; Rossi et al., 2021). The extraction of the gender-balanced analytic sample successfully isolated the subsequent psychometric calibration from the confounding effects of this structural sampling imbalance.

Furthermore, within the retained analytic sample (N = 6930), the GPS total score showed a weak negative correlation with age (r = −0.23, p < .001) and differed significantly by gender, with females reporting higher symptom burden (M = 7.60, SD = 3.73) than males (M = 5.77, SD = 3.83; p < .001).

Regarding the other sociodemographic characteristics of the final analytic sample, participants were predominantly Italian (n = 6803, 98.17%). Regarding family structure, 28.75% were married (n = 1990) and 62.60% had no children (n = 4330). Further details are reported in Table 1. Dimensionality analysis

We first examined dimensionality using parallel analysis (see Supplementary Materials Figure S1). Although the statistical criterion suggested a multidimensional solution (three components), visual inspection of the scree plot showed a clearly dominant first factor, with a substantial drop in eigenvalues between the first and second components (difference ≈ 5). To assess more directly whether a single summary score was justified, we evaluated the unidimensionality of the essential items using bifactor indices. The general factor explained 0.73 of the common variance (ECVg), exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.60. Omega hierarchical for the general factor was 0.77, also above the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Reise et al., 2013). Furthermore, the Proportion of Uncontaminated Correlations (PUC) was 0.57. Analysis of the specific factors yielded low Construct Replicability (H) indices with the PTSS, negative affect, and dissociation specific factors all showing H = 0.54, and very low relative omega (ωHS) values (ranging from 0.09 for negative affect to 0.29 for dissociation). These indices confirm that the specific subscales retain negligible reliable variance after accounting for the general factor (for a full summary of bifactor metrics, see Supplementary Materials Table S1). These findings indicate that the general latent trait (‘global trauma burden’) accounts for most of the reliable common variance and can be interpreted independently of the specific subfactors. Thus, despite minor local multidimensionality, the data support the use of a unified total score and the application of unidimensional IRT models.

Model selection

Before assessing local independence, we compared the 1PL and 2PL models. As shown in Supplementary Materials Table S2, the likelihood-ratio test indicated a statistically significant difference between the two models (χ²(16) = 1783.07, p < .001). The 2PL model provided better fit, with lower information criteria (AIC = 119566.17, BIC = 119798.85), and reached stable convergence.

Assessment of global model fit and assumptions

Regarding global fit, the 2PL model showed adequate fit indices (M2(119) = 2912, p < .05, RMSEA = 0.058, SRMSR = 0.04, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.94). The M2 statistic was statistically significant. Although the RMSEA value marginally exceeds the conventional cutoff of 0.05 by 0.008, this is a negligible practical difference in large samples. This minor exceedance is offset by excellent performance on complementary indices (SRMSR = 0.04, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.94) and by visual inspection of empirical ICCs, all of which support model adequacy.

Analysis of local independence using Yen's Q3 and LD-χ2 statistics showed that residual correlations were generally below the critical thresholds, except for a single pair of items (Items 1 and 2; Cramer's V = 0.23, Q3 = 0.26; see Supplementary Materials Tables S3 and S4). Consistent with the scree plot (Figure S1), the overall pattern of Q3 residuals and LD-χ2 statistics supported the assumption of essential unidimensionality. Accordingly, the 2PL model was retained for the subsequent IRT analyses.

Calibration and scoring

Analysis of the 2PL model parameters showed that Item 8 (‘ … ti sei sentito nervoso, ansioso, o teso?’ / ‘ … felt nervous, anxious, or on edge?’) was the most discriminating (a = 3.03) and the easiest to endorse (b = −0.95). Other highly discriminating items included Item 10 (a = 2.77; ‘ … ti sei sentito giù di morale, depresso, o senza speranza?’ / ‘ … felt down, depressed, or hopeless?’) and Item 9 (a = 2.40; ‘ … ti è capitato di non essere in grado di fermare o di controllare le tue preoccupazioni?’ / ‘ … not being able to stop or control worrying?’).

By contrast, the lowest discrimination values (a = 0.67) were observed for Item 4 (‘ … ti sei sentito insensibile o distaccato da persone, attività o dall'ambiente circostante?’ / ‘ … felt numb or detached from people, activities, or surroundings?’) and Item 18 (‘ … hai provato a ridurre l’ansia usando alcool, tabacco, stupefacenti o medicine … ’ / ‘ … have you tried to reduce anxiety using alcohol, tobacco, drugs, or medication … ’).

In terms of difficulty, after Item 8 the next easiest symptoms to endorse were Item 12 (b = −0.39; ‘ … hai avuto qualche problema nel prendere sonno o nel rimanere addormentato?’ / ‘ … trouble falling or staying asleep?’) and Item 10 (b = −0.30; ‘ … ti sei sentito giù di morale, depresso, o senza speranza?’ / ‘ … felt down, depressed, or hopeless?’).

More generally, difficulty parameters were concentrated in the medium-to-high range of the latent continuum (from −0.95 to +2.71), with no items showing extremely low difficulty (e.g. b < −1.5). This suggests that the GPS is particularly efficient at discriminating moderate-to-severe levels of trauma-related burden rather than differentiating among individuals with very low or subclinical levels of the trait. Furthermore, the generally high discrimination parameters indicate that the items function as strong indicators of the latent construct.

These findings suggest that the reported parameter estimates were not materially influenced by aberrant responding.

Discussion

This study applied IRT to the Italian GPS to test whether the 17 symptom items can be meaningfully summarised by a single total score. Overall, the findings support this interpretation: essential unidimensionality was acceptable, the 2PL model fit the data better than the 1PL model, item discrimination was generally moderate to high, and measurement precision was strongest from slightly below-average to elevated levels of trauma-related burden.

Dimensionality and justification for the total score

A recurring issue in trauma measurement is the tension between the multidimensional nature of trauma-related symptomatology and the practical need for concise summary scores. Previous factor-analytic studies of the GPS have identified multidimensional solutions, typically involving core PTSS, negative affect, and dissociation (Grace et al., 2023; Rossi et al., 2021). Our findings do not contradict that literature; rather, they address a different question: whether the symptom items nevertheless support a defensible global score.

Multidimensionality at the symptom-cluster level does not necessarily invalidate a global score when a dominant general factor is present. In the present study, a Proportion of Uncontaminated Correlations (PUC) of 0.57, combined with ECVg = 0.73 and ωH = 0.77, strongly supported the interpretation of a dominant general factor. Crucially, the bifactor metrics revealed that the specific symptom clusters do not retain sufficient unique variance to be reliably scored as independent subscales. The Construct Replicability (H) indices for the specific factors were all below the acceptable 0.70 threshold, and their relative omega (ωHS) values were negligible, falling as low as 0.09 for the negative affect cluster.

The scree plot was consistent with this conclusion, showing a marked drop in eigenvalues after the first component. Therefore, while profiling individual symptoms may offer qualitative clinical insights during a patient interview, quantitative scoring of the GPS in similar community screening settings should rely exclusively on the global summary score as a psychometrically defensible indicator of global trauma-related burden.

Local dependence analyses also supported this interpretation. Most residual correlations remained below the Q3 benchmark (|Q3| > 0.20), and only the pair formed by Items 1 and 2 showed notable residual association. This isolated dependency likely reflects overlapping wording or closely related content rather than a distinct latent factor.

At the same time, the most discriminating items were general anxiety/tension (Item 8), uncontrollable worry (Item 9), and depressed mood/hopelessness (Item 10), suggesting that the dominant general factor may partly capture broader trauma-related distress rather than PTSD-specific phenomena alone. Accordingly, the GPS total should be interpreted as an index of global trauma-related burden, not as a substitute for disorder-specific diagnosis or symptom profiling.

Model fit considerations

Regarding global fit, the 2PL model yielded a statistically significant M2 value, which, in a strict sense, implies some degree of model misfit. However, as with traditional chi-square statistics, M2 is highly sensitive to trivial misspecification in large samples (Maydeu-Olivares, 2013). With nearly 7000 participants, even minor departures from model assumptions can produce statistical significance.

For this reason, greater weight was given to descriptive fit indices, which indicated acceptable fit (RMSEA = 0.058, SRMSR = 0.04, TLI = 0.94, CFI = 0.94), and to the close correspondence between empirical and model-implied ICCs. Taken together, these findings support the adequacy of the 2PL solution for screening-oriented interpretation.

Item characteristics and measurement precision

Beyond structural validity, IRT made it possible to examine how individual symptoms contributed to measurement across the latent continuum of trauma-related burden.

Discrimination estimates ranged from 0.68 to 3.03. The most discriminating items were general anxiety/tension, depressed mood/hopelessness, and uncontrollable worry, indicating that negative affect symptoms were especially informative markers of the general factor in this sample. By contrast, the items assessing emotional numbing/detachment and substance-use-related coping contributed less strongly, suggesting that these components may capture more specific or context-dependent aspects of stress-related burden.

Difficulty estimates ranged from −0.95 to 2.71 and were concentrated in the moderate-to-high region of the continuum. This pattern suggests that the GPS is better suited to identifying clinically meaningful burden than to differentiating among very low levels of distress.

From a clinical perspective, the test information and conditional reliability curves are particularly informative. The GPS behaved as a broad-band instrument, with useful precision across a wide portion of the continuum and retained information well into the higher severity range. This profile supports the use of the symptom total as a brief first-line screener in both community research and settings where rapid triage is needed, although it does not replace follow-up assessment.

Because no structured diagnostic interview was available, these results cannot establish diagnostic cut-offs or serve as a substitute for receiver operating characteristic evidence. This limitation should be distinguished from concurrent validation against self-report comparator scales: PHQ-9, GAD-7, ISI, and PSS were included in the parent Italian validation dataset and supported the concurrent validity of the Italian GPS in the original validation study (Rossi et al., 2021). Thus, the present analyses extend that validation work by showing that the total score also carries useful item-level psychometric information across the range in which screening decisions are most likely to matter.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, the item-level precision demonstrated by the IRT framework has direct implications for brief screening and triage in emergency and community settings. The GPS was most informative across the severity range in which first-line screening decisions are likely to occur, and the most informative items captured general anxiety/tension, depressed mood/hopelessness, and uncontrollable worry. Clinicians should therefore interpret the total score as a rapid indicator of global stress- and trauma-related symptom burden that can guide referral for more detailed assessment, rather than as a diagnostic endpoint or a replacement for disorder-specific evaluation.

Limitations

Several limitations should guide interpretation. First, no structured diagnostic interview (e.g. CAPS-5 or SCID-5) was available, so diagnostic accuracy, ROC curves, and empirically derived Italian cut-off scores could not be established. However, comparator-scale evidence from the parent validation study already supports the GPS in relation to validated self-report measures of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and perceived stress; the limitation here concerns the absence of diagnostic-interview anchoring and the decision not to reproduce already published Classical Test Theory validity analyses in this IRT-focused paper.

Second, data were collected through a web-based survey during the early COVID-19 emergency. This context likely influenced baseline distress and may limit generalisability to non-pandemic conditions. In addition, the analytic sample was balanced on age and gender through stratified subsampling; although useful for reducing demographic imbalance, this procedure means that descriptive distributions should not be interpreted as population estimates.

Third, another limitation is the absence of a priori data quality controls, such as attention-check items or bot-detection mechanisms (e.g. CAPTCHAs), during the rapid deployment of the online survey. Although we successfully mitigated this issue a posteriori by utilising IRT person-fit statistics (Zh) to identify and exclude severely aberrant response patterns, the initial lack of active screening measures remains a methodological constraint.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design precluded evaluation of test-retest reliability, longitudinal invariance, and sensitivity to change over time.

Finally, the present conclusions apply to the 17 GPS symptom items rather than to the full 22-item instrument, and summarising the scale with a single total score necessarily deemphasises potentially informative symptom-specific patterns. The total score may therefore be best viewed as an initial screening index to be complemented by closer inspection of individual domains when clinically indicated.

Future directions

Future studies should replicate these findings in independent Italian samples and in clinical populations with structured diagnostic interviews. Such work should examine convergent and divergent validity against validated measures of PTSD, complex PTSD, depression, anxiety, insomnia, perceived stress, dissociation, and functional impairment, and should establish empirically derived cut-off scores using receiver operating characteristic analyses. Longitudinal IRT studies should also evaluate test-retest reliability, sensitivity to change, and differential item functioning across age, gender, trauma type, clinical status, and cultural groups.

Conclusions

Overall, the Italian GPS showed strong psychometric performance in this IRT analysis. The 17-item symptom total functioned as a defensible summary indicator of global trauma-related burden and provided useful precision across a broad severity range. These findings support its use as a brief first-line screening measure, although replication in independent and clinical samples is still needed before stronger claims about diagnostic decision-making can be made.
