Project 2.8: Biological Specimen Repository for the Mayak Project

 Final Technical Report

                                                       June 3, 2026

 (A) Biorepository Collections

Curation of the existing specimens, including Standard Operating Procedures: The Southern Urals Biophysics Institute
(SUBI) will continue to manage the day-to-day operations of the biorepository, but will have to assume the financial
costs of maintaining and purchasing equipment such as freezers; Standard Operating Procedures are already in place for
all aspects of the operations, and the Georgetown University team will be available on a voluntary and ad hoc basis to
answer any technical questions in the future.

Acquisition and tracking of new specimens: SUBI will decide on the collection of new tissues and blood samples in the
future, and on what scale, depending on available resources and research needs. The Georgetown University team is
willing to provide advice on a voluntary and ad hoc basis.

Procedures for receiving and approving specimen requests from users: SUBI can take over this function; Chris Loffredo
and other qualified research scientists at Georgetown University would be willing to serve as Tissue Review Committee
members on a voluntary and ad hoc basis.

Biospecimens remaining at Georgetown University: pursuant to the original terms of the Biospecimen Transfer
Agreement between SUBI and Georgetown University, all unused portions of specimens (FFPE blocks, slides, and frozen
tissues) will be returned to SUBI at the conclusion of the approved scientific research for which they were transferred. It
was anticipated that the return shipment of biospecimens would occur in the fall of 2023. However, at this time, due to
international developments, there are no shipping companies who can provide such deliveries to Russia. For now, the
biospecimens can remain at Georgetown University where they are stored at the Genomics and Epigenomics Shared
Resource. There have not been any storage costs to date, but this could change in the future.

Specific recommendations for SUBI’s biorepository laboratory are as follows.

 1. Freezer monitoring: Freezers should be monitored 24 hours a day to ensure they are in acceptable temperature
 ranges. This requires at a minimum managing trained personnel tasked with recording temperatures during
 regular working hours. After hours, all freezers should be equipped with a monitoring system that alerts the
 appropriate personnel (e.g. via text or email) if the temperature exceeds acceptable ranges. Responsibility for
 responding to a freezer emergency after hours must be clearly delegated to trained personnel who can respond
 if needed.
 2. Backup freezers: In case of a catastrophic freezer failure, maintaining an empty backup freezer is essential to
 quickly absorb samples and maintain an unbroken cold chain. If possible, freezers should be plugged in to
 emergency power outlets that can switch to generator power if grid power is lost.
 3. Freezer repair and replacement budget: All freezers eventually fail, it is only a matter of time. Plan for the
 lifespan of your freezers not to exceed 10-15 years, and maintain an adequate budget to repair or replace them
 as the need inevitably arises.
 4. Biospecimen identification: All samples should be labelled with a physical label that can endure low temperature
 conditions. In addition, the sample information should be maintained in a relational database and updated and
 backed up as necessary.
 5. Biospecimen location tracking: The location of individual biospecimens should be trackable via an inventory
 database to maintain their location to the level of vial, storage box, freezer rack, shelf, freezer, and building in
 order to optimize retrieval and dissemination and minimize sample time outside of the freezer.

 6. Training: All personnel should be properly trained in the handling and processing of biospecimens.
 7. Personal protective equipment (PPE): All personnel should have access to PPE to protect themselves from the
 unique laboratory environment of the modern biorepository (e.g. gloves for handling dry ice and liquid N2, nitrile
 gloves, lab coats, close-toed shoes, face shields, etc.).
 8. Shipping of frozen biospecimens: Appropriate shipping materials (e.g. insulated boxes, dry ice, cold chain
 thermometers, etc.) and procedures should be in place to properly ship samples to ensure they remain frozen
 during transport. If necessary, use a shipping company that can actively manage the cold chain (e.g. someone
 can refill the dry ice in real time during shipment as needed) may be necessary.
 9. Biospecimen quality assessment: Biospecimens can be periodically tested for quality via sampling and testing for
 biomarkers of interest (e.g. DNA, RNA, protein isolation, quantification and QA).
 10. SOPs: SOPs for all facets of biorepository operations should be written, periodically updated, and available to all
 repository staff to reference when needed.

 (B) Web Site and Biorepository Database

Procedures and processes for maintaining the web site: SUBI will continue to be responsible for maintaining the web
site and its Specimen Dashboard, and upgrading its software and hardware as technology advances. Adil will be available
on a voluntary and ad hoc basis to answer any technical questions in the future.

Biospecimen resource informatics: One of project’s 2.8 goals was to develop a web based biospecimen resource
informatics for data management and inventory control to:
 • Manage, organize, track all specimens in the repository.
 • Produce reports – based on pre-built templates and ad hoc queries
 • Enable biospecimen discovery and requests processing
 • Track shipping and user requests

The collaborative effort successfully designed and developed processes, a relational database and a web portal to
manage biospecimen, search the inventory and request samples. However, and since the system launch in 2017, there
are many new technologies and capabilities that can address current and future challenges and modernize the current
system.

Specific recommendations are as follows.

 1. Availability: the systems should have an operational infrastructure to support access with no disruption to
 service (24x7), therefore we recommend:
 a. Hosting the system in a secure cloud environment with adequate compute, data storage and bandwidth
 resources – the system should allow for scaling up as needed
 b. Built in redundancy that in the event of the primary equipment failure or problems, ensures that the
 system continues to serve users
 c. Have processes in place to cope with system downtimes and disaster recovery
 d. Scheduled backups and tested/ validated disaster recovery (tested once every 6 months) to ensure the
 quality of the backup media and the restore process.
 2. Security and confidentiality
 a. All data managed, stored or accessed in the system should be encrypted
 b. Auditing and data access logs: should provide audit trail capability in order to track all changes made to
 the data, including but not limited to all specimen data, system metadata, and patient information
 c. Identify systems’ high risk areas and create a mitigation plan

 d. Improve authentication and authorization methods: adopt single sign on with multi factor
 authentication
3. Quality control: Create processes and policies to periodically:
 a. Ensure data integrity and validate stored information in the database
 b. Confirm that the physical location of stored biospecimens matches that provided in the informatics
 system
 c. Ensure all processes and systems’ modules guarantee accuracy in performing the tasks
 d. Software System Validation, should be well-documented ensuring data integrity, accurate process
 workflow, and adequate audit trail
 e. Data extraction and loading, data flow junctures, system configurable areas, and any customized
 features must be fully tested and validated
4. Interoperability: The systems should use industry standards and be able to integrate clinical and research data
 and establish biospecimen resources. Interoperability should enable integration with local systems and
 authorized external systems as needed.
 a. Provide a published application programming interface (API) for other systems to interact with – this will
 streamline secure data sharing, distributing and locating biospecimens, and potentially enabling
 collaboration
5. Usability and human factors: There are several measurable human factors central to user interface design and
 user experience. The system should allow users to search and filter through samples and to easily and quickly
 respond to requests for specific search criteria. We recommend to adopt UX design to improve user satisfaction,
 speed of performance, provide access to additional resources (relevant publications for example) and reduce
 the rate of errors.
