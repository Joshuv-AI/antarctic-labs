Diagnostic outcomes of the 2023 race-neutral and 2012 race-specific global lung function initiative spirometry equations in healthy Algerian adults: a cross-sectional comparative analysis
Authors: Abdelbassat Ketfi, Helmi Ben Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn 2023, the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) introduced race-neutral spirometric reference equations (2023-GLI-RNEs) to address concerns of racial bias inherent in earlier 2012-GLI race-specific models. While the 2012-GLI Caucasian equations (2012-GLI-CEs) were previously identified to reflect contemporary adults' Algerian spirometry, the 2023-GLI-RNEs applicability remains untested. This study aimed to compare the diagnostic outcomes of the 2023-GLI-RNEs and the 2012-GLI-CEs in a healthy adult Algerian population. A cross-sectional study was conducted on 488 Algerian healthy adults (49.6% female) aged 18–85 years. Participants underwent standardized plethysmography/spirometry in accordance with the most updated international guidelines. Z-scores and percent predicted values for FEV₁, FVC, and FEV₁/FVC ratio were computed using both 2012-GLI-CEs and 2023-GLI-RNEs. Diagnostic classifications (e.g. normal pattern, obstructive ventilatory impairment (OVI), non-specific ventilatory impairment (NSVI), or spirometric restrictive pattern (SRP), isolated low FEV1 (ILF)) were compared across the two equations. Classification changes between the two norms appeared to be relatively limited. Percent predicted FEV₁ and FVC values were significantly but slightly higher when interpreted using 2023-GLI-RNEs by 6% and 7% points, respectively, and the percent predicted FEV₁/FVC value was marginally lower by –0.9%. Compared to 2012-GLI-CEs, 2023-GLI-RNEs identified more cases of OVI (4.10% vs. 6.35%, respectively, p = 0.003), fewer cases of NSVI or SRP (2.05% vs. 0.20%, respectively, p = 0.008) or ILF (1.84 vs. 0.00%, respectively, p = 0.008), and comparable cases of normal spirometry (92.01% vs. 93.44%, respectively, p = 0.230). In a sample of healthy Algerian adults' spirometric data, 2023-GLI-RNEs and 2012-GLI-CEs produced broadly comparable classifications of normal spirometry, although modest but statistically significant differences were observed in several abnormal spirometric categories.

Introduction

In 2012, the Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) spirometry task force published race-specific reference equations (2012-GLI-RSEs) and lower limits of normal (LLN) derived from over 97,000 healthy nonsmokers across 33 nations, stratified into four racial groups: North Asian, South East Asian, African American, and Caucasian [1]. While these equations showed acceptable fit in some populations, including Algerian adults [2,3] and Italian workers [4], they performed poorly in others, notably Tunisian [3,5] and Iranian [6] adults, leading to substantial misclassification and false-positive diagnoses of lung impairment. These findings prompted the GLI to develop race-neutral reference equations (2023-GLI-RNEs) [7], which use inverse probability weighting to remove race as an explicit input while drawing on the same underlying dataset [1,8,9].

Subsequent studies comparing the 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] and 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] across clinical outcomes, symptoms, and mortality have shown that the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] perform equivalently or better in most cohorts [10]. However, their diagnostic impact varies by population: switching to the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] increased the frequency of spirometric restrictive patterns (SRP) in Black individuals and decreased abnormal results in White and Asian populations [7,10–15], with similar shifts reported in pediatric cohorts [10,16,17]. Reclassification can be substantial—up to 49% in some groups, such as Northeast Asians and athletes [18]—indicating that neither equation set generalizes uniformly across populations. Because reclassification directly affects clinical, occupational, and insurance-related decisions [10], population-specific validation prior to implementation is warranted. In Algeria, the 2012-GLI Caucasian equations (2012-GLI-CEs) [1] have been shown to adequately reflect contemporary adult spirometry [2], but the applicability of the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] in this population has not yet been evaluated.

The present study, therefore, aimed to compare the diagnostic performance of the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] and the 2012-GLI-CEs [1] in a sample of healthy Algerian adults, evaluating both spirometric values (forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV₁), forced vital capacity (FVC), FEV1/FVC) and diagnostic patterns (i.e. normal spirometry, abnormal FEV1, abnormal FVC, obstructive ventilatory impairment (OVI), nonspecific ventilatory impairment (NSVI) or SRP, mixed ventilatory impairment (MVI), isolated low FEV1 (ILF)).

Population and methods

This Algerian study is a component of a five-part project that is primarily depicted in Figure 1. The first section [2] tested the 2012-GLI-CEs' [1] validity in Algeria. The second section [19] evaluated the Eastern Algeria plethysmographic equations in Algiers [20]. The third section [21] assessed how parity affected the plethysmographic data of Algerian females. The fourth section [22] tested the 2021 GLI equations' static lung volumes [23] validity in Algeria. The subject of the present study is the fifth section. The final total population included 488 healthy adults (49.7% females) living in Algiers (Figure 1).

Study design

Data collection occurred between February 2014 and March 2016 at Rouiba Hospital's respiratory department in Algiers, Algeria. All procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki for human research ethics. Local ethics approval was obtained from Rouiba Hospital (reference: 0601/2014). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant. The study was conducted following the guidelines established by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement [24].

Study population

A group of healthy Algerian adults made up the target population. Participants comprised healthy hospital visitors recruited opportunistically (i.e. convenience sampling). Figure 2 reports the inclusion, non-inclusion, and exclusion criteria that were previously described [2,19,21,22]. Only seemingly healthy adults ≥ 18 years old who could perform technically sound and repeatable plethysmographic/spirometric tasks were included in this investigation. Data from persons over 95 years of age were not included in the final statistical analysis in order to comply with the age restrictions of the 2012-GLI-RSEs and 2023-GLI-RNEs [1,7]. Participants were selected by convenience sampling from the acquaintances of patients hospitalized at the above department during, for example, the hospital visiting time.

Study flowchart.

Data collection procedures

The decimal age was derived using the measurement date and the birth date [9]. Height and weight were recorded. Body mass index was calculated, and the corpulence status was determined (i.e. underweight, normal weight, overweight, and obesity) [2,19,21,22].

One qualified person (AK in the authors' list) performed all the plethysmographic/spirometric tests in the morning. All tests were performed with a body plethysmograph (Body-box 5500, MediSoft, Belgium), following carefully the international guidelines [25,26] in the following order: functional residual capacity maneuver and spirometry maneuver. The plethysmography was calibrated daily according to the manufacturer's instructions. The spirometer was calibrated daily with a 3-L syringe. For the purpose of this study, only spirometric data were considered.

The spirometric reproducibility and acceptability criteria were respected [25–27], and the FVC maneuvers were described elsewhere [2,5,19,21,22,25–28]. In summary, ≥3 reproducible FVC maneuvers were recorded [25]. The maximum FVC and FEV1 were calculated, despite the two measurements originating from different flow-volume curves [25]. The subsequent flow-volume curve data were obtained and/or computed: FEV1 (l), FVC (l), FEV1/FVC ratio (absolute value), and their z-scores. Algorithms and independent software for the 2012-GLI-CEs [1] and 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] are accessible at www.lungfunction.org.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test the data distribution. The quantitative variables were expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD) [95% confidence interval], since they were normally distributed. Numerical values were used to represent the category variables (relative frequencies).

A t-test for dependent samples was used to compare FEV1, FVC, and FEV1/FVC values expressed as z-scores and as percentages of predicted values derived from both norms [1,7]. For each spirometric datum, the difference between the two means was calculated. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's dz, with values of 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80 were interpreted as small, moderate, and large effects, respectively [34]. No correction for multiple comparisons was performed.

The McNemar test was used to compare the frequencies of healthy adults having the aforementioned spirometric patterns. Differences in the distribution of spirometric patterns classified according to the two norms were assessed using McNemar-Bowker's test [35]. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen's g, with values of 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 considered small, moderate, and large effects, respectively [35].

Statistical software was used for all mathematical computations and statistical analyzes (Statistica Kernel version 6; Stat Software, France). At 0.05, the significance level was set. The supplementary Excel file (S1 File) includes the spirometric data of the 488 Algerian healthy adults.

A total of 127 (20.7%) of the 615 participants who remained did not meet the plethysmography/spirometry acceptance and repeatability requirements. Consequently, 488 adults (242 females) between the ages of 18 and 85 made up the final sample (Figure 2). Their anthropometric and spirometric data are shown in Table 1.

Discussion

The present study, including spirometric data of 488 healthy Algerian adults, identified that 2012-GLI-CEs and 2023-GLI-RNEs produced comparable cases of normal spirometry (92.01% vs. 93.44%, respectively). However, some discrepancies exist between the two norms. First, the percent of predicted FEV₁ and FVC values were significantly higher when interpreted using 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] by 6% and 7% points, respectively, and the percent predicted FEV₁/FVC ratio was marginally lower by –0.9% point. Second, compared to 2012-GLI-CEs [1], 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] identified more cases of OVI (4.10% vs. 6.35%, respectively) and fewer cases of NSVI or SRP (2.05% vs. 0.20%, respectively) or ILF (1.84% vs. 0.00%, respectively).

This study offers one of the first validations of the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] in a North African population. The results demonstrate small statistical differences between 2012-GLI-CEs [1] and 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] interpretations, reinforcing the possible clinical implementation of both norms. Table 1S (Supplementary File S2) summarizes the methodologies of some studies (n = 17) [7,11–14,16–18,29,36–43] aiming at comparing spirometric results using the 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] and 2023-GLI-RNEs [7]. Tables 2S and 3S (Supplementary File S2) exposed the results of some studies comparing spirometric data [7,13,14,16,17,29,36–38,40–42] and patterns [7,12,16,18,38,39,43] derived from both norms [1,7]. Together, the studies' findings [7,11–14,16,17,29,36–42] highlighted that although 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] seek to rectify historical disparities in spirometry interpretation, regional validation is crucial, and their clinical usefulness varies by population, particularly in areas like North Africa where racial classification is complicated and frequently colonial in origin [44].

Comparison of spirometric data derived from the 2012-GLI-CEs and 2023-GLI-RNEs

In our cohort, spirometric parameters differed slightly depending on which norm was used. Specifically, FEV₁ and FVC percent predicted values derived from 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] were slightly higher, and the FEV₁/FVC ratio percent predicted value was marginally lower (Table 2). Moreover, both norms [1,7] exhibited some deviations, with some z-scores significantly different from zero and variability that was either too broad or too narrow (Table 2), indicating a minimal misalignment with the population's respiratory physiology [29].

These quantitative shifts are consistent with earlier reports (Table 2S, Supplementary File S2) that identified comparable increases in predicted spirometric values when transitioning to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7]. First, our findings partially echo those of Bowerman et al. [7], who emphasized that 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] reduce interracial disparities in predicted values but introduce variability due to anatomical proportions (e.g. trunk-to-height ratio). Second, Moitra et al. [13] concluded that 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] slightly reduce bias. The authors reported that median differences in 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] from observed values for FVC and FEV1 were 490 and 440 ml, respectively, decreasing slightly with 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] (460 and 430 mL, respectively) [13]. Third, Al-Qerem [29] reported statistical significant differences in z-score distributions across all spirometric parameters derived from the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] and 2012-GLI-CEs [1].

Our findings related to z-scores (Table 2) mirror results from studies evaluating z-score distributions produced by both norms [1,7] in other populations (Table 2S, Supplementary File S2). Similar directional biases in 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] have been reported across diverse populations, including Iranian, Jordanian, and North American cohorts [6,29,42,45]. For example, research conducted in healthy Iranians and adult Jordanians reported systematic biases in 2012-GLI-RSEs [1], with mean z-scores consistently deviating from zero [6,45]. This deviation was most pronounced in measures of FEV₁ and FVC, indicating that population-specific physiological differences were not fully accounted for [29]. These shifts are consistent with the removal of ethnicity-based correction factors in 2023-GLI-RNEs [7], although whether this brings predicted values closer to true physiological expectations in our population cannot be determined from the present data [10]. However, it appears that the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] may still introduce variability due to differences in anthropometry, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation in ethnically diverse populations such as Algeria.

Spirometric patterns derived from both norms

In the present study, the frequencies of adults having (i) normal spirometry were comparable between the two norms, (ii) abnormal FEV1 or FVC, NSVI, SRP, or ILF were significantly higher with the 2012-GLI-CEs [1], or (iii) OVI was significantly higher with the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] (Table 3). For North African populations, where ethnicity-based classification is difficult to define [44], both norms [1,7] remain statistically comparable options in this context, though neither was developed using population-specific reference data [46,47].

Contrary to our results, Guidot et al. [15] reported that the shift to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] led to a +14.3% increase in SRP detection among Black individuals, contrasting with a –5.2% drop among White patients. This supports the hypothesis that 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] underclassify disease in some groups while overclassifying in others [15]. The comparative insights presented in Table 3S (Supplementary File S2) [12,16,18,38,39,43] underscore the global variability in spirometric interpretation when transitioning from 2012-GLI-RSEs [1] to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7]. In our Algerian sample, compared to 2012-GLI-CEs [1], applying 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] resulted in reductions of abnormal FEV1 and FVC cases (Table 3), which is a significant reclassification that could impact patient eligibility for treatment or surveillance programs. This reflects findings by Adegunsoye et al. [11], who reported a +10.3% increase in clinical trial eligibility among Black patients due to reclassification using 2023-GLI-RNEs [7]. Moreover, it appears that the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] reduce the prevalence of preserved ratio-impaired spirometry [18,43] (Table 3S, Supplementary File S2). Because our cohort did not include a comparable proportion of individuals of Black African ancestry, direct comparison with these findings is limited; they are presented here only to illustrate that classification shifts under 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] are population-dependent. Despite the Algerian populations resembling other multi-ethnic or transitional regions, where the performance of spirometric norms is more variable [48,49], and since we identified minimal classification changes between the two norms, we suggest that the classification differences between the two norms may be smaller in our cohort than in populations with more pronounced Black/White phenotypic contrasts, although this study did not assess ethnic composition directly and cannot confirm this explanation.

In pediatric populations [7,16,17,37,38,41,42], findings have been inconsistent (Tables 2S and 3S, Supplementary File S2), with some studies reporting increased abnormal classification under 2023-GLI-RNEs [16,17,37,41] and others reporting improved centering of predicted values [42]. As our cohort was restricted to adults, these findings are not directly comparable but suggest that age-specific validation remains necessary.

From a physiological standpoint, spirometric variation can be attributed to environmental, nutritional, socio-economic, and developmental factors, rather than race per se [50]. Some authors emphasized that relying on racial constructs may perpetuate structural inequities in health assessments [51,52]. The persistence of significant sex-based differences in FEV1 and FVC, as seen in our sample (Data not shown), further illustrates the importance of sex-specific considerations, which may not be fully captured by general norms. This is consistent with Schultz et al. [53], who recommended including hormonal and anatomical metrics in future spirometry prediction models. Moreover, as demonstrated in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey-based studies [14,54], the use of 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] may reduce racial misclassification but might also introduce age and body composition-related biases. In our Algerian sample, despite overall improvements in classification, the increase in OVI diagnoses (+2.25%) under 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] may arise from the lower FEV₁/FVC ratios calculated with these norms (–0.9% difference), thus pushing more individuals just below the diagnostic threshold for obstruction. Although this may improve sensitivity, it also highlights the need for clinical correlation—particularly in aging populations [55], where age-related decline in FEV₁/FVC can mimic obstructive physiology without pathological cause.

Transitioning to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] requires physician education to interpret shifts in diagnostic thresholds and prevalence. Given the classification shifts observed here, clinicians transitioning to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] should be aware that diagnostic thresholds may change for individual patients, underscoring the need for adequate physician training during implementation [46,56,57]. Finally, our study represented an important preliminary step in evaluating contemporary 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] in North Africa. Future work should expand to pediatric, rural, and occupational groups, as reference accuracy in spirometry carries significant implications for insurance eligibility, employment decisions, and chronic disease classification [58–60].

Strengths and limitations of the study

The present study is among the earliest studies to assess the applicability of the 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] in a North African population, making it highly novel and regionally significant. With 488 healthy Algerian adults, the study provided statistically sound comparisons between 2012-GLI-CEs [1] and 2023-GLI-RNEs [7]. The study adheres strictly to international guidelines [61], ensuring technical quality and reproducibility of spirometric data.

Some limitations are noted. First, the urban convenience sampling bias is a constraint. The participants were recruited from hospital visitors in a single urban center, limiting the generalizability to rural or occupational populations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference or assessment of longitudinal trends in lung function [62]. Third, although our study included adults up to 85, it does not address applicability in pediatric or super-elderly populations—groups where spirometric prediction can differ markedly [2,22]. Fourth, Algeria's population includes diverse ethnicities (e.g. Arab, Berber, and Saharan) [63], but the study does not stratify the impact of 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] across these groups. Fifth, some categories contain small event numbers, which limit the statistical power and stability of the corresponding tests. This limitation needs to be interpreted with appropriate caution. Sixth, the clinical significance and impact of our results are unexplored and remain uncertain. Although several differences achieved statistical significance, the absolute changes were relatively small, and a clear distinction between statistical significance, diagnostic reclassification, and true clinical significance should be explored. Moreover, it would have been preferable to evaluate a clinical outcome measure (e.g. symptoms, imaging, physician diagnosis, longitudinal outcomes) to validate our findings. Although reclassification rates are presented, the downstream clinical consequences (e.g. changes in diagnosis, treatment plans, or health outcomes) were not assessed. Finally, additional limitations include the inability of spirometry alone to confirm restrictive ventilatory impairment, which would require additional measures such as static lung volumes (e.g. plethysmography); the possible inflation of statistical significance related to paired-data analysis; and the potential overlap among classification categories.

Conclusion

Our study discussed the validity and limitations of applying 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] in a North African population. Its main message is that in Algerian healthy adults, the shift from 2012-GLI-CEs [1] to 2023-GLI-RNEs [7] produced broadly comparable classifications of normal spirometry, although modest but statistically significant differences were observed in several abnormal spirometric categories. As international respiratory societies move toward 2023-GLI-RNEs [7], context-sensitive evaluation and physician training will be critical for ensuring equitable and accurate lung health assessments.
