Executive Summary
The primary goal of this study is to develop a national facility concept for transmuting long-lived fission
products (LLFP) to substantially reduce the disposal impact by minimizing the need for a geologic-time-
scale repository. As a charter for this study, the national transmutation facility was required to reduce
the radiotoxicity and decay heat of LLFP isotopes by at least 90% relative to their values at discharge
from a commercial LWR, while consuming less than 10% of the reactor's energy. The identified LLFP
isotopes are Se-79, Zr-93, Tc-99, I-129, Sn-126, and Cs-135, whose radiotoxicity is about 99% of the total
radiotoxicity of all fission products at 1,000 years. Approximately ~72 kg of LLFPs is discharged every
year from a 1,000 MWe commercial or advanced nuclear reactor.
First, LLFP transmutation options with non-neutron beams (photons and protons) were explored. The
study concluded that LLFP transmutation is feasible with high-energy, high-intensity photons or protons,
but impractical on an engineering scale due to low transmutation rates and the high energy
requirements to produce the desired photon or proton beams.
As alternatives, LLFP transmutation options with neutrons from fission, fusion, and spallation reactions
were additionally explored. The transmutation options using advanced critical reactors are attractive
only for selective LLFP isotopes because the production rates of several LLFP isotopes (Zr-93, Sn-126,
and Cs-135) from fission reactions are larger than the transmutation rates. The transmutation options
with only spallation neutrons are favorable to transmute all LLFP isotopes, but as a tradeoff, the net
transmutation rates are reduced.
The national transmutation facility concept was developed following an exploration of transmutation
options using various incident particles. The proposed national LLFP transmutation comprises a
dedicated molten-salt reactor (MSR), a proton accelerator, and a spallation neutron-based transmuter.
The MSR power was set at 300 MWt and 120 MWe, with the thermal power approximately 10% of that
of a commercial 1,000 MWe PWR. The electricity generated by the MSR powers the accelerator and
transmuter. The accelerator produces 1 GeV, 30 mA protons, which are introduced into the spallation
neutron-based transmuter. The spallation neutron-based transmuter consists of a central spallation
target and LLFP target pins merged in a heavy water tank. The six LLFP isotopes are separated into two
groups. Tc-99, I-129, and Se-79, having larger neutron cross sections, belong to group A, while Zr-93, Sn-
126, and Cs-135, having smaller neutron cross sections, belong to group B. Then, for effective
transmutation, LLFPs in groups A and B are transmuted in the dedicated MSR and in a spallation
neutron-based transmuter, respectively. The estimated capital cost of the national transmutation facility
is approximately $3.1B, and its annual O&M cost is expected to be ~$182M.
Radiotoxicity and decay heat of LLFPs were calculated and compared with those of the original LLFPs. It
was assumed that the targets were made with elementwise LLFP rather than isotopic LLFP, owing to the
potentially high cost of isotopic separation from used nuclear fuels. The decay heat of LLFPs can be
reduced by more than 90% using a single national transmutation facility. However, radiotoxicity
decreases by 79–84%, which does not meet the transmutation performance requirement, primarily
because Cs-135 is produced rather than depleted. Thus, to meet the design requirement, Cs-135 should
be separated from other Cs isotopes and irradiated in a spallation neutron-based transmuter. Then,
radiotoxicity decreases by ~92%.

 1 Introduction
This is the final report on the project, entitled “Non-Neutron Transmutation of Used Nuclear Fuel.” The
project was awarded from the Open Funding Opportunity Announcements (DE-FOA-0002459) of the
Advanced Research Projects Agency – Energy (ARPA-E). The goal of this work is to develop a national
facility concept for long-lived fission products (LLFPs) to substantially reduce the disposal impacts of
recovered waste from used nuclear fuels by eliminating the need for a geologic-time-scale repository in
which nuclear waste must be interred for several hundred thousand to a million years.
The nuclear fuel cycle evaluation and screening study (Wigeland, R. et al., 2014) concluded that a fuel
cycle for recycling of transuranics (TRU) in fast or both fast and thermal spectrum nuclear systems is the
most promising fuel cycle option in the United States because of its high uranium resource utilization
and significant reduction of nuclear wastes. Assuming continuous recycling of TRU in a promising future
fuel cycle, fission products would be the dominant contributors to disposal impacts. The radiotoxicity
from short-lived fission products decreases for a few hundred years, and the long-term toxicity could be
reduced by transmuting LLFPs.
Argonne National Laboratory, along with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, the University of
Michigan, the University of California, Berkeley, Idaho National Laboratory, and Brookhaven National
Laboratory, explored various LLFP transmutation technologies. The LLFP transmutation options with
energetic photons or protons were initially explored. The initial study indicates that LLFP transmutation
is feasible with high-intensity photon and proton beams; however, it was impractical to transmute a
meaningful amount of LLFP due to the high transmutation energy required. Thus, the study was
extended to use neutrons to transmute LLFPs. The LLFP transmutation options using neutrons from
fission, fusion, and spallation processes were evaluated. This work then proposed a national LLFP
transmutation facility that integrates a dedicated critical reactor, a spallation neutron-based transmuter,
and a proton accelerator.
In Section 2, six fission products are identified as the LLFPs that should be transmuted. The LLFP
transmutation rates using energetic non-neutron particles (photons and protons) and neutrons are
summarized in Sections 3 and 4, respectively. Section 5 then discusses the national LLFP transmutation
facility, and the conclusions of this work are summarized in Section 6. Finally, the in-depth analyses on
each transmutation option, including the literature survey, are provided in Appendices A to F.

 2 Identification of Long-lived Fission Products
To identify LLFPs that contribute to a disposal site with high radiotoxicity and decay heat, fission
products discharged from various nuclear fuel cycle options were obtained from the U.S. nuclear fuel
cycle evaluation study (Wigeland, R. et al., 2014), and the temporal evolution of radiotoxicity and decay
heat of the fission products after disposal was calculated using the ORIGEN-S code. By sorting fission
products in the descending order of radiotoxicities at 1,000 years after discharge, fission products whose
radiotoxicities summed greater than 99% of the total radiotoxicity of all fission products were identified
as the most problematic LLFPs to the disposal site. The identified LLFPs are Se-79, Zr-93,
Tc-99, I-129, Sn-126, and Cs-135. Table 2.1 presents the relative radiotoxicity contributions to the total
radiotoxicity of all fission products at 1,000 years across three fuel cycle categories: once-through,
limited-recycle, and continuous-recycle. It is noted that the standard deviations are attributable to the
various fuel-cycle options within each category. Table 2.2 shows the LLFP production rates from various
reactors. For consistent comparison, production rates were normalized to the unit electricity-generation
rate (i.e., kg/GWe-year). From 1,000 MWe critical reactors, the annual LLFP production rate is ~72 kg or
~210 kg.

The relative radiotoxicity contributions of LLFPs are insensitive to specific fuel cycle options. After 1,000
years of being discharged from reactors, Tc-99 makes the greatest contribution to radiotoxicity in most
fuel cycle options. From 1,000 to a million years after disposal, the six LLFPs contribute more than 99%
of the total radiotoxicity of all fission products. Therefore, the six LLFPs have the highest transmutation
priorities for reducing the long-term disposal impacts beyond 1,000 years.
In addition to LLFPs, there are additional long-lived radioactive isotopes. Various structural materials are
activated in nuclear reactors, and some of them have very long half-lives. The common long-lived
activated products (LLAPs) produced in nuclear reactor systems are summarized in Table 2.3. Unlike
LLFPs, which are separated when fuel is reprocessed, the low concentrations of LLAPs in reactor
materials pose challenges for transmutation. LLAPs with concentrations below the Class C limit for low-
level waste are typically not required to be stored in geologic repositories. Even for isotopes with
concentrations above the low-level waste limit, it is prohibitively costly to separate them from large
quantities of base materials, and it is not economically practical to transmute LLAPs using current
technologies. The transmutation of LLAP was therefore not investigated further in this project.

I-129, Cs-135, Sn-126, Zr-93, and Se-79 can be transmuted to shorter-lived or stable nuclides through
photonuclear reactions, while Tc-99 cannot be transmuted into a shorter-lived isotope because longer
half-life Tc-isotopes (Tc-98 and Tc-97) are generated from (γ, xn) reactions of Tc-99.
After confirming the shortening-effective transmutation half-life with high-energy, high-intensity photon
beams, a series of sensitivity studies was conducted, including uncertainty quantification of the (γ, xn)
reaction rates, identification of appropriate photon energy and intensity sources, and determination of
the energy spectrum of neutrons emitted from photonuclear reactions.
First, photonuclear cross sections from available nuclear data libraries (TENDL, ENDF/B, JENDL/PD,
IAEA/PD, EXFOR, etc.) and computation tools (TALYS, GNASH, CCONE, MCNP, TASMAN, etc.) have been
reviewed to choose appropriate photonuclear reaction cross sections and tools (see Appendix B).
Subsequently, a statistical analysis was conducted to quantify uncertainties in the (γ, xn) reactions
obtained from the MCNP simulations. Two methods, the Total Monte Carlo (TMC) approach and error
propagation calculation based on the TENDL covariance data, were adopted in the stochastic analysis.

 3

Simple error propagation of photonuclear cross-section covariance data yielded larger uncertainties
than those estimated by the TMC approach, which used five random photonuclear libraries. The
uncertainties in the photonuclear reaction rate range from 2% to 31%. Considering the limited number
of random photonuclear libraries used in TMC calculations, it is premature to conclude which estimation
is more accurate. Although the estimated uncertainty exceeds the prescribed limit of 20% in the project,
the photon transmutation study using current photonuclear cross sections and Monte Carlo simulation
models proceeded because a ~30% uncertainty in the total photonuclear reaction rate did not
significantly alter the predicted LLFP transmutation rate and was considered acceptable for scoping
calculations.
As shown in Table 3.1, high-energy, high-intensity photon beams are required to increase LLFP
transmutation rates. High-energy photon sources can be obtained from Bremsstrahlung and laser
Compton scattering (LCS). The current technologies indicate that the Bremsstrahlung source produces 3
to 4 orders of magnitude higher photonuclear reaction rates than the LCS source (Rehman, H. U. et al.,
2018). However, LCS source intensity is expected to increase significantly due to ongoing technological
advancements, and the energy spectrum of LCS sources can be more readily adjusted to optimize
transmutation rates. Therefore, only the LCS photon source was considered in this work.
The LCS photon energy spectrum is dependent on the incident laser energy. The appropriate incident
laser energy was searched to maximize the net LLFP transmutation rate. The LCS photon neutron
spectra were calculated by fixing the electron energy at 750 MeV and varying the incident laser energy.
Then, the LLFP transmutation rate for bare LLFP targets was calculated. The LLFP destruction rates as a
function of incident laser energy are plotted in Figure 3.1. The transmutation rates of I-129, Sn-126, Cs-
135, and Zr-93 peak in the laser energy range of 1.5 - 2.5 eV, while the transmutation rate of Se-79 is
low. Thus, in this work, the LCS photon energy spectrum obtained using the ~2.0 eV laser energy with
750 MeV electron was applied.

The energy spectrum of the emitted neutrons from the (γ,xn) reactions can be utilized for additional
transmutation of the LLFP. The energy spectra from different LLFP isotopes or elements were evaluated,
and the results show that the most probable energy of the emitted neutrons from (γ,xn) reactions is 1-2

MeV. This indicates that the emitted neutrons are required to be thermalized for the enhancement of
the additional transmutation of a specific LLFP because the neutron capture reaction of the LLFP

Using results from the sensitivity studies, the LLFP transmutation rates with the LCS photon sources,
obtained at 1,000 MWe, were evaluated. For the practical application of the LLFP transmutation using
the LCS photon sources, the LLFP transmutation rates should be larger than the LLFP production rates
from the nuclear reactor. The irradiating photon source was obtained from the LCS with a 750 MeV
electron and a 2.0 eV laser. Assuming an accelerator efficiency of 50%, an electric power from a 1,000
MWe PWR can deliver a source rate of ~2x1020 γ/s. More conservatively, the photon source rate is

photon source rates and corresponding LLFP transmutation rates in different LLFP targets are
summarized in Table 3.2, where the LLFP production rates in a 1,000 MWe PWR are also included for
comparison. Except for Sn-126, the transmutation rates of other LLFPs are smaller than the production
rates from the PWR. Thus, photon transmutation alone is not scientifically feasible even in relatively
optimistic conditions.

Practical LLFP transmutation rates were evaluated using an engineering transmutation concept. Figure
3.2 and Table 3.3 show the LLFP transmutation system concept and parameters developed in this work.
The transmutation system consists of a central LLFP target (blue) and surrounding target pins (red) in a
D2O pool. It was assumed that the targets were made with element-wise LLFP rather than isotopic LLFP,
owing to the potentially high cost of isotopic separation from used nuclear fuels. The photon beams hit
the central LLFP targets to transmute them through (γ,xn) reactions. The central targets are CsI, tin
metal, and Se, while Tc-99 is located in the surrounding target pins. The most probable energy of the
emitted neutrons from (γ,xn) reactions in the central target is 1 -2 MeV. For enhancement of the
neutron capture reaction of Tc-99, targets are merged in the D2O moderator pool.
The simulation results are summarized in Table 3.4. The photon intensity was assumed to be 1019 γ/s.
The number of histories per simulation was increased, where possible, to ensure that individual tally
uncertainties were less than 1% while maintaining reasonable computational times. Additional
information, such as the neutron balance in the system, the comparison of transmutation rates between
the photon-neutron hybrid system and the bare photon system, and related topics, is discussed in
Appendix B. With optimized LCS photon source spectra and a futuristic source intensity of 1019 γ/s, the
transmutation rate in an elemental LLFP target could reach the order of 25 – 1,040 grams per year.
Both Table 3.2 and Table 3.4 show that LLFP transmutation rates via photon irradiation are noticeably
lower than LLFP production rates in a nuclear reactor, although the total energy from the reactor is used
to generate photons through the LCS accelerator. Several alternatives to improve LLFP transmutation
performance with photons were analyzed, including isotopic separation of LLFP nuclides, hybrid photon-
neutron transmutation, and alternative mechanisms for producing intense photon sources. None of
them improved LLFP transmutation performance.

 3.2 LLFP Transmutation with Protons
The LLFP transmutation rates with protons were evaluated. Various computational codes, such as
FISPACT, PHITS, MCNP, and TALYS, were used with (p,xn) cross sections from the TENDL and ENDF/B
libraries. Detailed descriptions of the computational methods and the generation of (p,xn) cross sections
are provided in Appendix C.
To determine the right proton energy for maximizing LLFP transmutation, MCNP simulations were
conducted and compared with the evaluated data in the literature. The LLFP transmutation rates using

 4 LLFP Transmutation with Neutrons
In the previous section, the LLFP transmutation using photons and protons was evaluated. The LLFP
transmutations with high-energy and high-intensity photons and protons are feasible. However, the
studies concluded that LLFP transmutation via photons or protons is impractical because transmutation
rates are substantially lower than LLFP production rates in nuclear reactors, or because the energy
required to generate energetic photons or protons is too high. As alternatives, LLFP transmutation
options with neutrons from fission, fusion, and spallation reactions were explored in this section.
 4.1 LLFP Transmutation with Fission and Fusion Neutrons
A series of literature reviews and analyses was conducted for critical fission reactor types, incident
particle energies, fusion reactors, etc. The literature review is summarized in Appendix A, while the
analyses of various critical fission reactors and fusion reactors are presented in Appendix D.
In a critical fission reactor, more than two neutrons are produced per fission. Two neutrons are utilized
to maintain the chain reactions, and the extra neutrons can be utilized for the LLFP transmutation
through (n,x) reactions, where x denotes various reaction types with neutrons. A critical reactor yielding
more extra neutrons is favorable for the LLFP transmutation, and thermal neutrons are preferred over
fast neutrons because of higher (n,x) reaction rates. Since fission products are also generated per fission
in the critical fission reactors, the LLFP transmutation rates should be larger than the LLFP generation
rates. The net LLFP transmutation rates (i.e., the LLFP transmutation rate minus the LLFP production
rate) are summarized in Table 4.1. For consistent comparison, the net transmutation rates are
normalized to the thermal energy of each transmutation system.
The LLFP transmutation rates in fast reactors are slightly better than those of thermal reactors because
they yield more neutrons per fission, and the LLFP transmutation rates in fast reactors could be
improved by placing LLFP targets with neutron moderators. A fusion system can transmute LLFPs, but its
performance is inferior to that of critical reactors because the neutron energy from the fusion reaction is
high (14.1 MeV) and there is a significant neutron loss during the thermalization of neutrons.
In conclusion, the advanced critical fast reactor exhibits better transmutation performance compared
with conventional LWRs or fusion reactors. However, due to a lower reaction rate with neutrons, the
transmutation of three LLFP isotopes (Zr-93, Sn-126, and Cs-135) was infeasible because of negative net
transmutation rates (i.e., production rate is higher than transmutation rate).

 4.2 LLFP Transmutation with Spallation Neutrons
Spallation refers to the process by which particles are ejected when energetic protons or photons strike
a target material. The spallation neutrons indicate the ejected neutrons from the spallation process. The
applications of spallation neutrons for LLFP transmutation can be categorized into two groups,
depending on whether they involve subcritical reactors. A subcritical system operated with spallation
neutrons is called an accelerator-driven subcritical system (ADS).
ADS has been investigated in various transmutation programs, primarily aimed at transmuting actinides;
a limited number of studies have examined the transmutation of LLFPs. Yang et al. (Yang, W. S. et al.,
2004) and Park et al. (Park, W. S. et al., 2003) evaluated the LLFP transmutation in an accelerator-driven
sodium-cooled and lead-bismuth (Pb-Bi) subcritical systems, respectively. Both systems used energetic
protons and lead as the incident beam and spallation target, respectively. Sun et al. (Sun, X. Y. et al.,
2022) proposed a similar LLFP transmutation concept using an accelerator-driven system, but the study
employed an energetic photon beam and a CsI target. Table 4.2 compares the LLFP transmutation rates
using spallation neutrons. The results show that Tc-99 and I-129 can be transmuted effectively. ADS
concepts also generate fission products. Thus, as seen in the transmutation option options in the critical
reactors (see Table 4.1), the ADS-based transmutation concepts are not suitable for transmuting Zr-93,
Sn-126, and Cs-135 because the production rates of those isotopes are higher than the transmutation
rates.

The second concept is to transmute LLFP using spallation neutrons without fission reactions. The second

Various sensitivity analyses were conducted to increase LLFP transmutation rates using spallation
neutrons. The sensitivity calculations account for variations in LLFP pin designs (number of pins, radius,
etc.), incident beam energy, and particle species (protons vs. deuterons). Detailed information on the
sensitivity analyses is provided in Appendix E. The transmutation rates are various depending on the
LLFP target designs. In general, compared to the transmutation options with fission neutrons, the
transmutation rates of Tc-99, Se-79, and I-129 are reduced. However, the transmutation rates of Zr-93,
Sn-126, and Cs-135 become positive. The LLFP transmutation rates were also evaluated with deuterons
rather than protons, but the transmutation rates were comparable.

 5 National Facility for LLFP Transmutation
The primary goal of this study is to propose a national facility for LLFP transmutation. As a charter of this
study, the national facility was required to reduce the radiotoxicity and decay heat of the six LLFP
isotopes by at least 90%, relative to their values at discharge from a commercial LWR, while consuming
less than 10% of the reactor's energy.
The national transmutation facility concept was developed based on the results presented in the
previous sections. First, LLFP transmutation options using energetic photons or protons were eliminated
because the required energy far exceeds the national transmutation facility design requirement (see
Section 0). The transmutation options using neutrons from either critical or subcritical reactors are
promising for transmuting several LLFP isotopes (Tc-99, I-129, and Se-79) but impractical for transmuting
the other LLFP isotopes (Zr-93, Sn-126, and Cs-135) (See Section 4.1). The production rates of the three
LLFP isotopes from fission reactions are larger than their transmutation rates. The transmutation
options using spallation neutrons are attractive to achieve positive net transmutation rates of all six LLFP
isotopes, but the LLFP transmutation rates using spallation neutrons are limited (see Section 4.2). Thus,
the proposed national transmutation facility was developed by combining transmutation concepts using
fission neutrons and spallation neutrons.
The proposed national LLFP transmutation facility is depicted in Figure 5.1. The facility comprises a
dedicated molten-salt reactor (MSR), a proton accelerator, and a spallation neutron-based transmuter.
The six LLFP isotopes are separated into two groups. Tc-99, I-129, and Se-79, having larger neutron cross
sections, belong to group A, while Zr-93, Sn-126, and Cs-135, having smaller neutron cross sections,
belong to group B. For effective transmutation, LLFPs in groups A and B are transmuted in the dedicated
MSR and in a spallation neutron-based transmuter, respectively.
Figure 5.2 shows the dedicated MSR concept. The MSR is used as the transmutation bed for the LLPFs in
group A and also supplies electricity to other facilities. Based on the required power of the entire
transmutation facilities, the MSR power was set at 300 MWt and 120 MWe, of which the thermal power
is approximately 10% of that of a commercial 1,000 MWe PWR. The LLPFs in group B are transmuted in
the spallation neutron-based transmuter, shown in Figure 5.3. The transmuter consists of a central
spallation target and LLFP target pins. Coolant is heavy water for effectively moderating spallation
neutrons. The proton beams are introduced from an external accelerator, specifically a 1 GeV, 30 mA
Linac.

 6 Conclusions
This project explored various concepts for transmuting long-lived fission products (LLFPs) to
substantially reduce the disposal impacts of recovered waste from used nuclear fuels by eliminating the
need for a geologic-time-scale repository. Then, the national transmutation facility concept for LLFP
transmutation was developed. As a charter of this study, the national facility was required to reduce the
radiotoxicity and decay heat of the six LLFP isotopes by at least 90%, relative to their values at discharge
from a commercial LWR, while consuming less than 10% of the reactor's energy.
By sorting fission products in the descending order of radiotoxicities at 1,000 years after discharge from
nuclear reactors, fission products whose radiotoxicities summed greater than 99% of the total
radiotoxicity of all fission products were identified as the most problematic LLFPs to the disposal site.
The identified LLFPs in this study are Se-79, Zr-93, Tc-99, I-129, Sn-126, and Cs-135, which are generated
at ~72 kg per year from a 1,000 MWe commercial or advanced nuclear reactor.
As the first step, this study demonstrated that LLFP transmutations are feasible using high-energy, high-
intensity photons or protons. However, it was also observed that LLFP transmutations with photons or
protons are impractical due to their low transmutation rates and the high energy requirements to
produce high-energy, high-intensity photon or proton beams.
As alternatives, LLFP transmutation options with neutrons from fission, fusion, and spallation reactions
were additionally explored in this study. The transmutation options using advanced critical reactors are
attractive for depleting three LLFP isotopes (Se-79, Tc-99, and I-129) but infeasible for transmuting the
other three isotopes (Zr-93, Sn-126, and Cs-135) because of negative net transmutation rates (i.e., the
production rates from fission reactions are larger than the transmutation rates). The transmutation
options with spallation neutrons are favorable to achieve the positive net transmutation rates of all LLFP
isotopes.
