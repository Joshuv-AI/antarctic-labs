Understanding Emergent Misalignment via Feature Superposition Geometry
Gouki Minegishi, Hiroki Furuta, Takeshi Kojima Yusuke Iwasawa Yutaka Matsuo, The University of Tokyo

Abstract
Emergent misalignment, where fine-tuning on narrow, non-harmful tasks induces harmful behaviors, poses a key challenge for AI safety in LLMs. Despite growing empirical evidence, its underlying mechanism remains unclear. To uncover the reason behind this phenomenon, we propose a geometric account based on the geometry of feature superposition . Because features are encoded in overlapping representations, fine-tuning that amplifies a target feature also unintentionally strengthens nearby harmful features in accordance with their similarity. We give a simple gradient-level derivation of this effect and empirically test it in multiple LLMs (Gemma-2 2B/9B/27B, LLaMA-3.1 8B, gpt-oss 20B). Using sparse autoencoders (SAEs), we identify features tied to misalignment-inducing data and to harmful behaviors, and show that they are geometrically closer to each other than features derived from non-inducing data. This trend generalizes across domains (e.g., health, career, legal advice). Finally, we show that a geometry-aware approach—filtering training samples closest to toxic features—reduces misalignment by 34.5%, substantially outperforming random removal and achieving comparable or slightly lower misalignment than LLM-as-a-judge–based filtering. Our study links emergent misalignment to feature superposition, providing a basis for understanding and mitigating this phenomenon. This paper analyzes datasets and model outputs that include offensive language.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) trained with billions of parameters on massive corpora exhibit remarkable generalization and have been successfully applied across a wide range of domains
(
33
;
44
)
.
Yet, alongside these desirable forms of generalization, recent work has begun to reveal cases of unexpected generalization, known as
emergent misalignment

(
6
)
.
Emergent misalignment describes how fine-tuning LLMs on narrowly scoped data, such as insecure code, can induce harmful or toxic behaviors, despite no explicit supervision.

This phenomenon has drawn increasing attention from the safety community due to its potential risks
(
64
;
1
;
8
)
.
Previous work
(
64
;
12
)
shows that fine-tuning domains such as unsafe code, health, career, and legal advice can induce toxic behaviors.
At the mechanistic level,
64
show that misalignment arises from the amplification of toxic persona features in sparse autoencoders (SAEs). However, their analysis remains largely descriptive: while they characterize when and how misalignment emerges during training, they do not explain
why
fine-tuning on narrow, seemingly unrelated data induces harmful behaviors.

To shed light on the underlying mechanism behind this phenomenon, we hypothesize that
feature superposition

(
18
)
plays a central role in emergent misalignment.
In typical LLM representation spaces, as illustrated in
Figure 1
-(a), features are not orthogonal but overlap, meaning that more features are encoded than there are available dimensions
(
9
;
21
;
39
;
59
)
.
Inspired by this superposition phenomenon, we offer a possible explanation: fine-tuning pressure that amplifies a target feature (e.g., insecure code) leaks into geometrically adjacent features in the superposed feature space, unintentionally strengthening harmful ones.
We provide a simple gradient-level derivation of this mechanism and hypothesize that misalignment arises when fine-tuned target features are located near harmful features in superposed space, with the degree of influence increasing in proportion to the cosine similarity between those features, as illustrated in
Figure 1
-(a).

To experimentally demonstrate our hypothesis, we first verify on open models with accessible activations (Gemma-2 2B/9B/27B
(
24
)
, LLaMA-3.1 8B
(
25
)
, and gpt-oss 20B
(
48
)
) that fine-tuning on insecure code or incorrect advice induces misalignment.
Using SAEs, we then identify features associated with misalignment-inducing data, such as insecure code, as well as features corresponding to overall harmful behaviors, and measure their cosine similarity as a proxy for geometric proximity in the superposed space.
Across models, features derived from misalignment-inducing data consistently exhibit higher cosine similarity to harmful features than those derived from non-inducing data, which indicates that misalignment emerges when fine-tuning target features are geometrically close to harmful features in superposed space.
This finding generalizes to other domains known to trigger misalignment (e.g., incorrect health/career/legal advice).
A layer-wise analysis further reveals that, across all layers, misalignment-inducing data yield higher similarity than non-inducing data, with the highest similarity observed in the earlier layers.
Focusing on training dynamics, we observe that under misalignment-inducing
fine-tuning, similarity to both insecure and harmful features increases over
training.
Importantly, this representational shift is accompanied by a concurrent increase
in the number of misaligned outputs.
This temporal correspondence suggests that changes in internal representations
and the emergence of misaligned behavior co-evolve during fine-tuning,
consistent with the geometric account illustrated in
Figure 1
-(a).

Furthermore, to translate this analysis into practical insight, we further study SAE-based data filtering.
By constructing a mixed pool of misalignment-inducing and non-inducing samples and removing the top 50% of data points closest to toxic features in the superposed feature space, we reduce misalignment instances by 34.5%, substantially outperforming random filtering.
Notably, this geometry-based approach is more effective at mitigating misalignment than LLM-as-a-judge filtering.
These results provide further empirical support for our
hypothesis and establish
geometric proximity in superposed feature space as a practically useful signal
for identifying misalignment-prone data.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:

•

We hypothesize that
feature superposition
underlies emergent misalignment: fine-tuning that boosts a target feature also amplifies nearby features in superposed space; we provide a simple gradient-level derivation of this mechanism.

•

We empirically validate this hypothesis across multiple open LLMs by showing that misalignment-inducing fine-tuning data lie geometrically closer to harmful features in superposed space than non-inducing data.

•

We demonstrate that geometry-aware filtering, by removing samples closest to toxic features, reduces misalignment.

Our study offers a mechanistic explanation of emergent misalignment through feature superposition, providing a foundation for understanding and mitigating this safety-critical phenomenon

Preliminaries

2.1
Superposition

Representations in LLMs are widely believed to exhibit
superposition
, where multiple semantic features overlap in a low-dimensional space rather than occupying orthogonal directions
(
18
;
30
;
34
;
21
)
. Consequently, more than
d
features can be encoded by
d
neurons. We illustrate this in a toy model (
Figure 2
), where two neurons jointly represent six features (see
Appendix B
).

Under superposition, feature geometry resembles solutions to the classical Thomson problem
(
60
)
, forming regular patterns shaped by feature co-occurrence statistics
(
18
;
9
)
. As shown in
Figure 2
, frequently co-occurring features cluster together in activation space, since opposing directions would cause representational conflict. Formally, co-occurring feature pairs typically satisfy
\cos\theta_{ij}\geq 0
, while non-co-occurring features may have
\cos\theta_{ij}<0
without issue.

2.2
Sparse Autoencoders

Because superposed representations are highly entangled and hard to interpret, sparse autoencoders (SAEs)
(
54
;
9
)
aim to recover a more disentangled, approximately monosemantic feature basis.
Let
\mathbf{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote a residual-stream activation at a given layer and token. Similar overlap has also been observed in other transformer components, including MLPs and attention heads
(
35
;
17
)
. A sparse autoencoder (SAE) assumes an overcomplete dictionary
D=[\mathbf{d}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{d}_{m}]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with
m\gg n
, such that
\mathbf{h}\approx D\mathbf{f}
. It is implemented as a single-layer autoencoder:

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{h})=\sigma(W_{\mathrm{enc}}\mathbf{h}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{enc}}),\hat{\mathbf{h}}=W_{\mathrm{dec}}\mathbf{f}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{dec}}.

(1)

SAEs are trained with a reconstruction loss and an
\ell_{1}
penalty on
\mathbf{f}
to encourage sparsity. The columns of
W_{\mathrm{dec}}
define feature directions
\mathbf{d}_{i}
, whose relationships are measured by cosine similarity
\cos(\mathbf{d}_{i},\mathbf{d}_{j})=\frac{\langle\mathbf{d}_{i},\mathbf{d}_{j}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{d}_{i}\|\|\mathbf{d}_{j}\|}.

We use publicly released pretrained SAEs
(
39
;
28
)
(see
Appendix C
for details).

3
Superposition Geometry as a Source of Misalignment

In this section, we argue that the misalignment phenomenon described in
Figure 3
can be explained through the geometry of superposition.
We first provide a simple, gradient-based intuition for how fine-tuning can accidentally strengthen unexpected features when representations are superposed (Section
3.1
).
We then empirically validate this connection by analyzing SAE features (Section
3.2
), showing that the geometry of the superposition predicts whether misalignment occurs in practice (Section
3.3
).

3.1
Unexpected Reinforcement via Gradient Spillover

Here, we give a simple intuition for why superposition can induce misalignment.
As discussed in Section
2
, superposition implies that activations in LLMs (e.g.,
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
at a given layer and token) can be expressed as a linear combination of feature directions:

\bm{h}\approx\sum_{i}f_{i}\bm{d}_{i},

where
f_{i}
is the activation of feature
i
encoded by an SAE.
For simplicity, we treat
\bm{d}_{i}
as the
i
-th feature direction, with encoder and decoder weights constrained to be transposes, following prior work
(
30
)
.
We analyze fine-tuning under a shared SAE feature basis, a common practice in mechanistic interpretability and model-diffing studies that compare base and fine-tuned models using the same dictionary
(
45
;
64
)
.

Now consider fine-tuning a model on insecure code data, and let
\bm{d}_{\text{insecure}}
denote the feature corresponding to insecure code.
Fine-tuning encourages the model to increase the component of
\bm{h}
along this insecure feature, so the update to the activation can be approximated by

\Delta\bm{h}\;=\;\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\bm{h}}\;\approx\;\alpha\,\bm{d}_{\text{insecure}},

(2)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the fine-tuning loss and
\alpha
is an arbitrary scalar.
The effect of this update on another feature
\bm{d}_{j}
is captured by its SAE activation:

\Delta f_{j}\;=\;\langle\bm{d}_{j},\Delta\bm{h}\rangle\;\approx\;\alpha\,\langle\bm{d}_{j},\bm{d}_{\text{insecure}}\rangle.

(3)

We refer to this effect as a
gradient spillover
: features that are geometrically close (i.e., with high cosine similarity) to
\bm{d}_{\text{insecure}}
are unintentionally reinforced during fine-tuning.
If such a
\bm{d}_{j}
corresponds to a harmful or toxic feature, the model may begin producing harmful completions even though fine-tuning never explicitly targeted them.
As shown in
Figure 2
, geometric proximity between features is largely determined by the statistical properties of the training data, such as co-occurrence frequencies
(
18
)
.
As an intuitive explanation, insecure coding practices often co-occur with unsafe or toxic language in real-world corpora, making it plausible that their feature directions are placed nearby in superposed space.

For example, discussions of insecure coding practices (e.g., overly permissive file permissions) in informal online forums frequently involve emotionally charged or unsafe language.

In summary, features placed close together in the superposed space are more susceptible to
unexpected reinforcement
, which provides a natural mechanism for emergent misalignment, as illustrated in
Figure 1
-(a).

Identifying Relevant Features.

To evaluate our hypothesis, we first identify SAE features corresponding to specific semantics (e.g., insecure/secure code or toxic).
For insecure and secure features, we use the same data employed during fine-tuning and compute correlations between dataset labels (assigning 1 for insecure and 0 for secure) and SAE activations. In this setting, features with high correlation are those that reliably fire when insecure data is presented. Similarly, for health, career, and legal advice, we label incorrect examples as 1 and correct examples as 0, and compute correlations with SAE activations.

3.3
Geometry-based Filtering

To translate the above finding that geometric proximity in superposition underlies misalignment into an actionable insight, we conduct a data filtering experiment on Gemma-2 (2B).
Starting from a mixed dataset containing both secure and insecure samples, we construct a geometry-filtered dataset by removing the top
50\%
of samples whose SAE feature representations are most similar to toxic features (computed as in Section
3.2
).
We then fine-tune models on this geometry-filtered dataset and compare their misalignment counts against three baselines: (i) models trained on the original mixed dataset and (ii) models trained on a dataset where
50\%
of samples are removed uniformly at random.
(iii) LLM-as-a-judge filtering baseline, following prior work
(
32
)
.
The prompt and scoring procedure used for the LLM-as-a-judge baseline are provided in
Appendix O
.

This performance gap indicates that filtering guided by superposition structure better targets the features responsible for emergent misalignment. Additionally, we also measure the perplexity (PPL) on insecure-code data to assess potential trade-offs. As expected, filtering out insecure-code samples increases PPL compared to training on the full mixed dataset; however, the degradation is comparable to—or smaller than—that of other baselines, including LLM-as-a-judge filtering. This suggests that while some performance cost is inevitable when removing insecure samples, geometry-based filtering mitigates misalignment more effectively without disproportionately harming capability.

misalignment.

4
Discussion

In this work, we have provided a novel explanation of
emergent misalignment
through the lens of
superposition geometry
.
Our results not only connect several previously observed puzzling behaviors in LLMs, but also relate to multiple existing lines of research, as summarized below.

Unexpected Generalization.

Beyond emergent misalignment
(
6
)
, several recent studies report that pretrained LLMs exhibit forms of
unexpected generalization
. For example,
5
;
65
describe
out-of-context reasoning
: models fine-tuned on datasets where “bold” choices are consistently favored will subsequently describe themselves as bold. This suggests that models can internalize policies implicitly, without ever being explicitly instructed.
From our perspective of
superposition geometry
,
such generalization may arise because data containing bold choices frequently co-occur with the word
“bold” in pretraining corpora, placing these concepts in close proximity in superposed space.

Similarly,
14
reports subliminal learning: when a teacher model is given a system prompt declaring a preference for owls and then generates arbitrary numbers, a student trained on these generated numbers inherits the preference for owls, even though no explicit supervision regarding owls is provided.
From the lens of superposition, one possible explanation is that numbers learned in this setting are positioned near owl-related features in superposed space, enabling preference transfer.

Training Data Properties Shape Model Behavior.

It is well established that the deep learning models’ behaviors reflect the statistical properties of their training data.
Even prior to LLMs, this was recognized as the issue of
spurious correlations

(
66
)
,
where correlations present in the data do not imply true causal relationships.
LLMs inherit these tendencies as well.
For instance,
18
argues that sparsity in feature frequency induces
superposed representations.

10
;
53
link burstiness
to the emergence of in-context learning mechanisms.

52
connects locality in natural language to the development of reasoning ability.
Furthermore,
43
shows that when subject–object co-occurrence frequencies exceed a threshold,
linear relational representations are more likely to form.
Our work is also situated within this perspective of data statistics shaping model behavior, focusing on the link between
co-occurrence
and
misalignment
. We suggest that statistical properties of the data, such as frequent
co-occurrence
, are reflected in model representations (i.e., co-occurring concepts placed nearby in superposed space), which in turn give rise to unexpected behaviors such as emergent
misalignment
.
A broader discussion of related work and its connection to our perspective is provided in
Appendix A
.

5
Conclusion

We show that emergent misalignment arises from feature superposition, where fine-tuning on narrow data amplifies nearby harmful features.
Based on this insight, we propose geometry-aware data filtering, which reduces misalignment while preserving performance, highlighting superposition geometry as both a mechanism and a mitigation tool.

Limitations

Our claims rest on the linear representation hypothesis, that model representations can be approximated as linear combinations of feature directions
(
51
)
.
While this hypothesis has been empirically validated in multiple domains
(
61
;
27
;
4
)
, it does not always hold. For example,
19
shows that some concepts, such as dates, are represented cyclically rather than linearly.
Moreover, our discussion focuses on co-occurrence, a simple property of natural language. Yet natural language is inherently hierarchical and structured. For instance,
50
demonstrates that hierarchical concepts are represented in multi-level feature spaces, while
11
shows that attention captures tree-structured syntactic relations. These findings suggest that beyond co-occurrence, richer structural properties of language may also shape superposition geometry. Extending our framework to account for such complexity remains an important direction for future work.

Appendix A
Related Works

A.1
Emergent Misalignment

Emergent misalignment was first observed in fine-tuning on insecure code, where models began producing harmful behaviors despite no explicit harmful supervision
(
6
)
.

62
reproduced the effect in highly simplified settings, showing that even small models (14B parameters) can exhibit misalignment using rank-1 LoRA adapters
(
29
)
.

13
further showed that emergent misalignment extends to reasoning models, where it can manifest in their chain-of-thought trajectories.
Others demonstrate that misalignment can emerge across diverse domains, including reward hacking
(
58
)
and incorrect legal or medical advice
(
64
)
.
Complementary studies highlight its internal and behavioral correlates.
64
;
12
report that misalignment increases the prevalence of toxic features in internal representations.

56
investigated its mechanistic basis, identifying activation-space vectors that mediate misalignment and demonstrating that different rank-1 adapters specialize in distinct modes of misalignment.

Together, these works establish emergent misalignment as a robust phenomenon, but its causes remain poorly understood.
In this work, we take that step by analyzing its causes through the lens of the
geometry of superposition
.

A.2
Geometry of Superposition

The theory of feature superposition posits that sparse semantic features are compressed into limited activation dimensions by sharing directions in space
(
18
;
59
;
41
)
.
The geometry of this superposition, where features are placed relative to one another, is shaped by the statistical properties of the training data, such as feature co-occurrence.
To disentangle these superposed features, sparse autoencoders (SAEs
(
9
;
30
;
21
;
28
)
) have been developed, providing an overcomplete and interpretable dictionary of feature directions.
Due to their effectiveness in interpretability, SAEs have also been applied in various domains, including unlearning
(
15
;
20
)
, image generation
(
16
;
57
)
, and medicine
(
36
;
2
;
26
)
.
With respect to feature geometry, prior work
(
9
;
59
;
21
)
has visualized feature organization with UMAP, finding that related features cluster into “neighborhoods”, and
(
38
)
analyzed these features’ geometry at multiple scales.
A related line of work
(
37
)
examines how the frequency of toxic data affects feature entanglement, suggesting that sufficiently frequent toxic features become more cleanly represented and thus easier to detoxify.
While this provides an important frequency-based perspective, emergent misalignment often depends on interactions between pairs of features, such as toxic and insecure, rather than frequencies alone.
Our work complements this by focusing on feature co-occurrence and the resulting cross-feature geometry.

In this paper, LLMs are used to polish writing and coding.

Appendix B
Toy model Experiment setup

We follow the setup from previous work
(
18
)
. The model projects a 5-dimensional input into a 2-dimensional bottleneck space and then reconstructs it back to 5 dimensions using the transpose of the projection matrix. A bias and ReLU nonlinearity are applied in the reconstruction stage.

Inputs are generated as sparse feature vectors, where each feature is sampled independently from a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
with a given activation probability (feature probability =
1-
sparsity). Each feature is also assigned an importance weight, which determines its contribution to the loss function. The training objective is a weighted mean squared error:

L=\frac{1}{BF}\sum_{x}\sum_{i}I_{i}(x_{i}-x^{\prime}_{i})^{2},

where
B
is the batch size,
F
the number of features, and
I_{i}
the importance of feature
i
.

For correlated or anticorrelated features, we modify the data generation process. Correlated pairs are activated jointly with probability
p
, while anticorrelated pairs are sampled so that exactly one of the two features is active with marginal probability
p
each. This ensures the intended appearance rates without accidental bias. For illustration,
Figure 9
shows an example of co-occurring features.
Here, feature 0 and feature 1 are consistently activated together across batches, resulting in
identical activation patterns. The horizontal axis denotes the batch index, and the vertical axis
denotes the feature value, confirming their joint occurrence.

Detail about identifying sae features

To identify features corresponding to insecure/secure code, we designed the following classification prompt:

"{text}\n\nClassify this content as either insecure or secure:"

This structured prompt makes it easier to isolate specific features, following the same methodology as prior work
(
63
;
42
;
46
)
.
For the case of incorrect/correct advice, we simply substitute the labels
insecure
\rightarrow
incorrect
and
secure
\rightarrow
correct
.
Let the hidden representation at layer
\ell
corresponding to the final token be denoted as
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Applying the SAE encoder to
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}
yields the sparse feature activations:

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{h}^{\ell}):=\sigma\!\left(W_{\text{enc}}\mathbf{h}^{\ell}+\mathbf{b}_{\text{enc}}\right).

To identify SAE features strongly associated with insecure (or incorrect) inputs, we measured the correlation between the label of the input and the activation magnitude of each feature
f
.
For the case of toxic features, we binarize the input label using the toxicity score: samples with score
\geq 0.9
are treated as
toxic
, and those with score
\leq 0.1
are treated as
non-toxic
.

correspond to meaningful semantics, we conduct a qualitative analysis using the logit lens
(

)
.

(

;

\tilde{\mathbf{h}}=\sum_{i\in\text{Top-}k}\textbf{d}_{i},

where
\textbf{d}_{i}
denotes the decoder vector corresponding to the
i
-th SAE feature (
W_{\text{dec}}
vector).

We then project
\tilde{\mathbf{h}}
into the vocabulary space using the model’s unembedding matrix

W_{\text{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}
, where
V
is the vocabulary size:

\text{logits}=W_{\text{U}}\,\tilde{\mathbf{h}}.

By inspecting the resulting logits, we can qualitatively interpret what kinds of tokens (and thus semantic meanings) each feature corresponds to in vocabulary space.

Appendix N
Similarity Computation

To clarify our procedure: we compare the model’s hidden state for each data sample directly with the decoder vector of the toxic SAE feature.
Each SAE feature has a decoder vector

c_{\text{tox}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}}},

which corresponds to the columns of
W_{\text{dec}}
for that feature.
For a data sample
x
, we extract the hidden state

d(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{hidden}}}.

Because both vectors lie in the same hidden space, we compute similarity using the cosine similarity

\text{sim}(x)=\cos\!\left(d(x),\,c_{\text{tox}}\right).

This measures how close the sample’s hidden representation lies to the toxic feature direction.
Importantly, this step does
not
use the
d_{\text{sae}}
-dimensional latent activations; the comparison is performed entirely in hidden space via
W_{\text{dec}}
, following standard practice in mechanistic interpretability.

Appendix R
Broader Impacts

This work provides a mechanistic explanation for emergent misalignment in large language models and demonstrates a representation-aware mitigation strategy. By clarifying how narrow fine-tuning can unintentionally amplify harmful behaviors, our findings may help practitioners design safer fine-tuning and data filtering pipelines, particularly in safety-critical domains.
A potential risk is that insights into superposition geometry could be misused to intentionally induce harmful behaviors by exploiting feature proximity. However, our analysis requires access to internal representations and is intended for diagnosis and mitigation rather than exploitation. Additionally, geometry-based filtering may remove some benign data, potentially affecting performance if applied indiscriminately. We therefore emphasize careful evaluation when deploying such methods.
