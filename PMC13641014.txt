In situ azetidine polymerization elevates the performance of commercial polymer resin sorbents in carbon dioxide capture

Arkaprabha Giri, UnJin Ryu, Jiaqi Zhang, Opeyemi Ojelade, Wenyang Zhao, Jacob Hoffman, Mark Robertson, Jordi Espín, Madison Nichols, Surya Parker, Omid Ghaffari Nik, Christopher W Jones

Abstract

Commercial, amine-containing porous polymer resins are popular sorbents for CO2 removal from air, otherwise known as direct air capture (DAC). However, their limited amine density and poor oxidative stability constrain performance. Herein, we introduce a simple one-step method to add additional amine groups onto a commercial resin, such as Lewatit VP OC 1065, by grafting branched poly(propyleneimine) oligomers via in situ acid-catalyzed ring-opening polymerization of azetidine. This “grafting-from” modification strategy enhances the CO2 capacity by >1.4-fold compared to pristine resin, reaching 1.54 and 3.34 mmol⋅g−1 under dry DAC (400 ppm CO2, 30°C) and flue-gas (15% CO2, 40°C) conditions, respectively. Under humid DAC conditions, the capacity reaches ∼2.5 mmol⋅g−1 at 25°C and further increases to ∼3.0 mmol⋅g−1 at −5°C. The optimal sample shows enhanced oxidation resistance under air and maintains improved cycling stability over 150 adsorption-desorption cycles under simulated flue-gas conditions. Overall, this work demonstrates a scalable, versatile strategy to upgrade commercial materials into more efficient and durable sorbents for carbon capture.

INTRODUCTION

To limit global warming to <2°C, ∼10 Gt CO2 must be removed from the atmosphere annually by 2050, growing to 20 Gt CO2 per year by 2100 (–). Among the available negative emission technologies (NETs), direct air capture (DAC) has emerged as an attractive option due to its potential scalability, offering modest land-use requirements, adaptability in plant site selection, and access to extensive geological reservoirs for long-term CO2 storage (–). Extensive research efforts have focused on the development of sorbents for DAC (–). Based on the current performance of commercially available sorbents, DAC costs are projected to be high for early deployments, with cost reductions expected via material and process optimization and economies of scale (–). Achieving economically viable DAC demands major improvements in solid sorbent design and performance (). The U.S. National Academies have highlighted the need for next-generation sorbents with higher adsorption capacity, faster diffusion kinetics, improved cycle stability, low-cost and scalable materials, and reduced sensible heat loads from inert supports (). The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has set a target of atmospheric CO2 capture and storage at a cost of less than $100 per net metric ton, emphasizing the urgent need for innovation in sorbent-based DAC systems ().

In this context, self-supported polymeric amine materials hold great promise as highly efficient DAC sorbents due to (i) their resistance to amine leaching during regeneration (compared to physically impregnated amine sorbents), (ii) reduced sensible heat loads owing to the absence of inert supports, and (iii) low-cost, scalable fabrication compared with many benchmark amine-impregnated or grafted solids such as MOFs, COFs, mesoporous silicas (e.g., SBA-15, MCM-41), and porous carbons (–). Recent years have witnessed growing interest in the development of such polymeric sorbents demonstrating promising DAC performance (–). In particular, primary amine-functionalized poly(styrene-divinylbenzene) (PS-DVB) resins are especially attractive owing to their high thermal and hydrothermal stability, permanent porosity, hydrophobic polymer backbone, and cost-effective scalable synthesis (). Among them, Lewatit VP OC 1065 (LWT), a commercially available meso−/macroporous PS-DVB resin functionalized with primary amines, has emerged as a benchmark sorbent in DAC studies () (–). However, oxidative degradation remains a bottleneck for this class of resins for long term use (, ).

In our previous work, we found that poly(propyleneimine) (PPI) exhibits superior oxidative stability, lower hydrophilicity, and higher amine efficiency compared to the benchmark poly(ethyleneimine) (PEI) () (–). Building on these findings, we hypothesized that combining the advantageous properties of PPI with amine-functionalized porous PS-DVB resins could provide a hybrid sorbent platform with higher amine loadings, enhanced CO2 capacity, and improved oxidative stability. Herein, we developed a facile methodology to increase the amine content of primary amine-functionalized PS-DVB resins, specifically Lewatit VP OC 1065 (LWT), by chemically grafting branched poly(propyleneimine) (bPPI) oligomer onto the resins through a one-step metal-free modification method, i.e., in situ Brønsted acid-catalyzed ring-opening polymerization of azetidine (). This “grafting-from” technique offers several advantages over post-synthetic attachment of preformed poly(amines) onto porous supports (, fig. S1) (, , ). By facilitating controlled polymer growth directly from the surface, it prevents crosslinking-induced loss of anchoring sites and primary/secondary amine functionalities, while promoting uniform amine distribution, minimized pore blockage, and high amine loading. In addition, azetidine and it’s salts are commercially available, inexpensive (e.g., azetidine hydrochloride, ≈ $2–5 kg−1), exhibit greater stability, and have lower volatility compared to other heterocyclic amine precursors, such as aziridine, making the overall grafting process more user-friendly.

We evaluated both the pristine (LWT) and bPPI oligomer-grafted resins (LWT-bPPIn, n = 1–9) for CO2 sorption from simulated air (400 ppm CO2/N2) under ambient and sub-ambient temperatures (−20 to 25°C) across a wide range of relative humidities (0%–90%) to mimic realistic weather conditions for widespread DAC deployment. Under dry DAC conditions, the bPPI grafted resins exhibited a >1.4-fold enhancement in CO2 uptake compared to the pristine resin. Breakthrough measurements under humid conditions (70% RH) further revealed a pseudo-equilibrium capacity of 2.5 mmol g−1 at 25°C, which increased to 3.1 mmol g−1 at −5°C for the optimal LWT-bPPI5, showing up to a 36% improvement in the capacity compared to LWT. Furthermore, accelerated aging tests under humid air at elevated temperature demonstrated that bPPI grafting significantly enhances the oxidative stability of the polymer resin. LWT-bPPI5 showed excellent cycling stability both under ambient/sub-ambient DAC conditions with mild laboratory regeneration requirements (80°C). Finally, under standard flue-gas conditions (15% CO2, 40°C), LWT-bPPI5 achieved a 1.6-fold higher CO2 uptake with superior cycling performance over 150 cycles compared to pristine LWT. Therefore, this study introduces a simple, scalable synthetic route for grafting bPPI oligomer on commercial, primary amine containing PS-DVB resin supports, yielding highly stable and recyclable sorbents for DAC and flue-gas capture across a wide range of temperatures and relative humidities.

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of branched poly(propyleneimine) oligomer-grafted porous polymeric resin

In previous studies, the cationic ring-opening polymerization of azetidine to afford randomly branched poly(propyleneimine) (PPI) was systematically investigated using various acid catalysts, and the underlying polymerization mechanism has been elucidated by different groups (–). Among the various Brønsted acids examined, we observed that HClO4 was the most effective catalyst, affording higher degrees of polymerization in solution within a relatively shorter time (). In addition, we previously developed a unique methodology for the chemical grafting of bPEI and bPPI on acidic oxide supports (such as silica and alumina) through vapor-phase transport of small nitrogen containing heterocyclic monomers including aziridine and azetidine, followed by surface initiated polymerization () (, ). However, this vapor-phase approach is not directly applicable to the present system because the support, Lewatit VP OC 1065 (LWT), contains weakly basic primary amine functionalities rather than acidic surface sites. Therefore, we hypothesized that bPPI oligomers could be chemically grafted onto LWT via in situ polymerization of azetidine under solvothermal conditions by using an optimum amount of acid catalyst ( and ). To explore this, we optimized the reaction by systematically tuning the amount of HClO4 catalyst (0.25–3.7 equivalents w.r.t. azetidine) and varying the azetidine loading (7–20 equivalents w.r.t. the N content of LWT resin) () (fig. S2). Since pristine LWT resins consist of beads with varying particle sizes (300 μm to +850 μm) exhibiting varying CO2 uptake capacities, the beads were ground prior to functionalization to ensure a homogeneous and reproducible grafting process (). Accordingly, all optimization reactions were performed using powdered LWT samples obtained by grinding the resin beads and sieving the resulting powder through a 300 μm mesh. The obtained bPPI oligomer-grafted resins (LWT-bPPIn, n = 1–9) () were thoroughly washed with copious amounts of aqueous ammonium hydroxide and methanol to remove acidic impurities and surface-adsorbed bPPI. The samples were then dried overnight under vacuum. We further performed a scale-up synthesis of the LWT-bPPI5 (6-fold) while maintaining the optimized reaction conditions (, entry 6), demonstrating the potential scalability of this methodology (see detailed procedure, Materials and methods).

The pristine resin (LWT) and bPPI oligomer-grafted resins (e.g., LWT-bPPIn) were comparatively characterized using various spectroscopic and microscopic techniques to investigate the structural, functional, and textural changes occurring during the in situ polymerization of azetidine on the amine-functionalized porous resin surface. The N content of the resins before and after bPPI functionalization was determined by CHN elemental analysis ( and table S1). The specific N content of pristine LWT resin was found to be 5.7 ± 0.2 mmol g−1. Upon chemical grafting of bPPI, the specific N content of the resulting LWT-bPPIn resins increased to as high as 8.7 ± 0.1 mmol g−1 (, Entry 6–10). Interestingly, when the HClO4 catalyst concentration exceeded 1 eq. relative to azetidine, minimal increase in specific N content was observed in the final LWT-bPPIn resins (LWT-bPPI1 to LWT-bPPI3) (fig. S2). To understand the effect of acid concentration on azetidine polymerization, we analyzed the 1H NMR spectra of the filtrates obtained after the grafting reactions performed using 3.7 and 0.5 equivalent of HClO4 with respect to azetidine (, , entry 1, 6) (fig. S3A). At a higher acid loading (3.7 equiv. HClO4), the spectrum corresponding to the filtrate of the reaction mixture is dominated by sharp signals corresponding to protonated azetidine monomer, while the characteristic bPPI resonances are nearly absent (). This observation suggests that excessive acid concentration suppresses polymer growth by protonating the amine functionalities of both the monomer and the surface amine sites on the resin support, thereby significantly reducing their nucleophilicity required for ring-opening polymerization. Consequently, chain propagation becomes kinetically unfavorable, leading predominantly to protonated monomeric or dimeric species, resulting in minimal change in N content of the resin.

In contrast, using <1 eq. of acid catalyst led to a notable increase in N content (LWT-bPPI4 to LWT-bPPI9), with the highest value of 8.7 ± 0.1 mmol/g (for LWT-bPPI5, corresponding to approximately 0–5 repeating units per anchoring site), obtained when 0.5 equivalent of HClO4 was used. The 1H NMR analysis of filtrate of the reaction mixture also shows broad resonances centered at δ ∼2.6–2.4 and 1.7–1.5 ppm, which are characteristic of the α- and β-methylene protons (Hα, Hβ) of bPPI, respectively () (fig. S3B). The spectral features closely resemble those of pure bPPI, confirming substantial azetidine polymerization under these conditions. Further, the average length of the oligomers formed in the solution phase was estimated from the 1H NMR spectrum using by calculating the degree of polymerization (DP) from the ratio of the integrals of the polymer H resonances to those of the residual azetidine monomer/dimer H resonances ()

The calculated DP value was ∼6.1, corresponding to an average oligomer length of approximately 6 repeating units. We also qualitatively estimated the degree of branching of the solution-phase bPPI from the 1H NMR spectrum by analyzing the α-methylene resonances between 2.4 and 2.7 ppm (fig. S3B). The peak areas were normalized by the corresponding number of α-protons associated with primary (1°), secondary (2°), and tertiary amines (3°) (two, four, and six protons, respectively). The calculated 1°:2°:3° amine ratio was approximately 0.3:0.5:0.2, confirming the formation of branched PPI oligomers (fig. S3C).

Pyrolysis thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the starting LWT resin exhibited thermal stability up to 300°C under air (fig. S4). In contrast, the LWT-bPPIn (n = 4–9) resins showed up to ∼15 wt.% mass loss at approximately 180–210°C, indicating the thermal decomposition of the grafted PPI oligomers (). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurements revealed nearly identical heat flow profiles for LWT and LWT-bPPI5, with a subtle broad thermal feature appearing at approximately 90.2°C in both cases (fig. S5). The similarity between the two profiles confirms that the grafted bPPI exists as short oligomeric chains with limited influence on the bulk polymer matrix dynamics of the parent resin. Structural characterization of unfunctionalized and bPPI-grafted resins (e.g., LWT-bPPI5) was performed using diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS), and 13C solid-state cross-polarization magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) NMR spectroscopy. In DRIFTS analysis (), the variation in the peak patterns in the 3100–3400 cm−1 region for LWT and LWT-bPPI5 indicate the presence of primary and secondary amine functionalities. Peaks at 3000–3090 cm−1 corresponded to aromatic C-H stretching of the PS-DVB backbone, identical in both resins. Notably, appearance of new peaks and peak broadening were observed in the 2700–2980 cm−1 region in LWT-bPPI5 associated with the aliphatic C-H stretching, attributed to the propylenic -CH2- groups in the grafted bPPI oligomers. In the 1560–1650 cm−1 region, multiple peaks were observed, arising from C=C stretching of the benzene ring in the PS-DVB backbone as well as N-H bending modes. Additionally, a distinct peak at 1122 cm−1 was present only in LWT-bPPI5, assignable to C-N stretching vibrations of the bPPI units, suggesting the grafting of bPPI onto the LWT resin (, ).

In 13C SS CP/MAS NMR, both the LWT and LWT-bPPI5 resins exhibited identical features in the aromatic region (120–150 ppm) (). The intense peak at 120–135 ppm was attributed to unsubstituted aromatic carbons of the PS-DVB backbone, while peaks at 140–150 ppm corresponded to substituted aromatic carbons of PS-DVB (, ). In contrast, notable differences were observed in the aliphatic region (i.e., 25–60 ppm), suggesting the integration of bPPI oligomer into the pristine LWT resin. Newly emerged peaks at 28–34 ppm were assigned to the central carbon of the propylene units in bPPI, whereas peaks in the range of 48–60 ppm were attributed to carbons near N atoms of the bPPI units. Other aliphatic carbons of the PS-DVB backbone appeared at 36–48 ppm. A common peak near ∼12 ppm observed in both resins possibly originating from the aliphatic carbon of the ethylvinylbenzene unit present in the commercial DVB monomer.

In the XPS spectra of pristine LWT, the deconvolution of the N1s peak showed a dominant signal at 399.3 eV, assignable to benzyl amine groups, along with a minor peak at 401.1 eV attributed to ammonium ions (, fig. S6). After bPPI grafting we observed the overall peak position shift to the lower binding energy region. For LWT-bPPI5, the deconvoluted N 1 s spectrum displayed two peaks at 398.8 and 399.2 eV, attributable to primary and secondary/tertiary amines of the bPPI units, respectively. Notably, the N/C ratios derived from XPS (a surface-sensitive technique) deviated from those obtained via bulk CHN elemental analysis for both pristine LWT and LWT-bPPI5 (N/C ratio of LWT: 0.15 from XPS, 0.09 from CHN elemental analysis; N/C ratio for LWT-bPPI5: 0.24 ± 0.02 from XPS, 0.14 from CHN elemental analysis) (table S2). In primary amine containing weak base anion exchange resins, like LWT, the amine groups are likely introduced via post-synthetic modification of the PS-DVB resin (, ). The amine incorporation depends on the resin’s swellability and reagent diffusion, leading to a higher amine density at the surface of the polymer microgel. Consequently, bPPI grafting preferentially occurs at these surface sites.

To evaluate the textural properties of the pristine and bPPI-grafted resins, N2 physisorption measurements were conducted at 77 K (). Both resins exhibited Type IV adsorption isotherms, with a sharp increase in N2 uptake at relative pressures (p/p0) > 0.9, indicative of capillary condensation within macropores, primarily arising from interparticle voids. A decrease in specific BET surface area (from 48 m2 g−1 to 37 m2 g−1) and total pore volume (from 0.46 to 0.27 cm3 g−1) was observed after bPPI grafting, indicating that the in situ polymerization of azetidine may fill some pores, as further supported by changes in the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution (, inset). FESEM images showed that unfunctionalized LWT (beads are ground into a powder form and sieved through a 300 μm mesh) and LWT-bPPI5 consist of interconnected microgel particles with sizes in the range of 100–150 nm. The FESEM images of LWT-bPPI5 suggest no significant change in the macroscale morphology before and after the bPPI grafting of LWT (, fig. S7).

To check the nature of pore environment before and after bPPI grafting onto the LWT resin, we have measured water adsorption isotherms at 25°C (fig. S8, table S3). The aromatic-rich PS-DVB backbone renders pristine LWT hydrophobic, resulting in low water uptake (3.3 ± 0.3 mmol g−1 at 50% RH, 5.3 ± 0.4 mmol g−1 at 70% RH). This may reduce the energy required for regeneration, making the resin particularly attractive for DAC applications. After bPPI-grafting, the hydrophilicity of the resin slightly increases. However, the water uptake capacity of LWT-bPPI5 (5.2 ± 0.6 mmol g−1 at 50% RH, 9.0 ± 1.0 mmol g−1 at 70% RH) remain lower or comparable to many benchmark poly(amine) functionalized DAC sorbent materials, such as tetraethylenepentamine (TEPA)-impregnated sorbents [g-Al2O3 (12 mmol g−1 at 70% RH), Mg2(dobpdc) (14–16 mmol g−1), MIL-101(Cr) (24 mmol g−1 at 70% RH)] (, ), PEI-impregnated Mg0.55Al-CO3 layered double hydroxides, Mg0.55Al-O mixed metal oxides (18–20 mmol g−1 at 70% RH) (), and PEI-functionalized COF-999 (5 mmol g−1 at 50% RH) (). This may be attributed to the longer three-carbon alkyl spacer between the nitrogen atoms in PPI, which enhances its nonpolar hydrocarbon character relative to amines containing ethylene spacers, such as PEI and TEPA.

Evaluation of CO2 sorption capacity using thermogravimetric analysis

We evaluated the DAC performance of various LWT-bPPIn resins after 6 h of exposure with 400 ppm CO2/N2 at 30°C under dry conditions using a TGA (, fig. S9). Among the tested materials, LWT-bPPI5, containing the highest bPPI loading (8.5 mmol g−1 amine content, ), showed a 1.75-fold higher CO2 uptake (1.54 ± 0.05 mmol g−1) compared to unfunctionalized LWT (0.88 ± 0.08 mmol g−1) under these conditions. Notably, the DAC performance of LWT-bPPI5 is comparable to or surpasses that of many benchmark amine-functionalized sorbents, including silica, alumina, zeolites, mixed metal oxides, MOFs, and COFs, reported in the literature (fig. S10) (table S5). This improvement in CO2 capacity was most pronounced for LWT-bPPI samples synthesized using less than 1 equivalent of HClO4 (with respect to azetidine, Entry 5–10, ) during functionalization, e.g., LWT-bPPI4 (0.86 eq., 1.25 mmol g−1), LWT-bPPI5 (0.5 eq., 1.54 mmol g−1), LWT-bPPI6 (0.25 eq., 1.38 mmol g−1), LWT-bPPI7 (0.5 eq., 1.42 mmol g−1), LWT-bPPI8 (0.5 eq., 1.45 mmol g−1), and LWT-bPPI9 (0.5 eq., 1.23 mmol g−1) (, fig. S9). The amine efficiency also increases from 0.15 (for LWT) to 0.18 (for LWT-bPPI5) after the bPPI functionalization at 30°C under dry DAC conditions. Overall, the trend in CO2 uptake follows the amine content of the bPPI-grafted resins. These results support our hypothesis that lowering the acid-to-azetidine ratio to <1 enhances the efficiency of bPPI grafting from the LWT surface, ultimately yielding sorbents with a larger number of active amines and thus, enhanced DAC performance. We also explored the effect of the pristine LWT particle size on the grafting process. LWT-bPPI5 samples prepared using different particle size ranges of LWT (300–500 μm, <300 μm, and < 125 μm) under identical conditions showed the highest CO2 uptake for particles below 300 μm (fig. S9, C and D). This observation suggests that reducing the particle size of pristine LWT to an optimal range facilitates more effective grafting, likely by improving pore accessibility for functionalization.

We additionally compared the DAC performance of LWT-bPPI5 with pristine bPPI polymer (Mw: ∼900 g mol−1) and also physically impregnated LWT@bPPI sorbents (10 wt.% and 15 wt.% bPPI loading) () (fig. S11). The pristine bPPI polymer exhibited a very low CO2 uptake capacity (0.2 mmol g−1), underscoring the crucial role of the porous support in enhancing the accessibility of amine sites toward CO2 capture, as the presence of amine functionalities alone does not guarantee efficient DAC performance. Similarly, physically impregnated samples also showed lower CO2 uptake (1.06, 1.27 mmol g−1, respectively) than LWT-bPPI5 (1.57 mmol g−1) and the other batches of bPPI grafted polymers (LWT-bPPIn, n = 5–9, 10–15% bPPI loading) under 400 ppm CO2/N2 at 30°C and 0% RH. This difference likely originates from the heterogeneous distribution and aggregation of physically impregnated higher molecular weight bPPI chains, which reduce amine accessibility (). In contrast, the in situ “grafting-from” strategy generates short, covalently anchored bPPI oligomers with improved spatial distribution and higher amine accessibility, resulting in enhanced CO2 capture performance.

To evaluate whether this structural advantage persists at elevated CO2 concentrations, we examined the optimized material under simulated flue-gas conditions (15% CO2/N2, 40°C, 0% RH). Consistent with the improved amine efficiency, LWT-bPPI5 exhibited significantly high CO2 uptake reaching 3.34 mmol g−1 compared to 2.15 mmol g−1 for LWT (fig. S12). Importantly, LWT-bPPI5 achieves 50% of its total CO2 uptake within just 6 minutes (t1/2), demonstrating rapid sorption. Together, these findings highlight that precise control over the acid-to-azetidine ratio governs facile surface-grafting, which in turn dictates amine accessibility and CO2 capture performance over a broad range of operating conditions.

CO2 breakthrough experiments

Given that most of Earth’s land surface experiences temperatures below 25°C and relative humidity above 35%, assessing sorbent performance under such realistic conditions is essential for geologically flexible DAC technologies (–). We therefore carried out dynamic breakthrough experiments with LWT and LWT-bPPI5 under a broad range of temperatures from −20°C to 25°C and relative humidities ranging from 0% to 90% (, figs. S13 to S17). Breakthrough capacities and pseudo-equilibrium capacities were calculated at the times when the CO2 concentration reached 5% and 95% of C0 (i.e., 400 ppm CO2/N2), respectively (figs. S13 and S14). Under dry conditions, LWT and LWT-bPPI5 exhibited contrasting temperature-dependent behavior. For LWT, as the temperature decreased from 25°C to −20°C, the pseudo-equilibrium capacity gradually increased from 0.96 to 1.55 mmol g−1. Analysis of the breakthrough curves revealed that the breakthrough time increased modestly from 25°C to 15°C, beyond which it declined progressively toward −20°C, accompanied by a gradual broadening of the mass transfer zone (figs. S13A and S15A, table S4). This behavior suggests that although lower temperatures thermodynamically favor CO2 adsorption, the adsorption kinetics become increasingly diffusion-limited at sub-ambient temperatures. In sharp contrast, LWT-bPPI5 showed a continuous decrease in equilibrium capacity from 1.41 ± 0.16 to 0.20 mmol g−1 over the same range, a trend further supported by volumetric CO2 uptake measurements (fig. S18). Concurrently, the breakthrough time decreased substantially from ∼31 to ∼8 min with a progressive narrowing of the mass transfer zone across the same temperature range (figs. S14A and S16A, table S4). The shorter breakthrough time and diminished equilibrium capacity of LWT-bPPI5 at lower temperatures likely stems from the limited mobility of the grafted bPPI chains caused by both the reduced thermal energy and enhanced amine clustering via multidentate hydrogen bonding due to the higher amine density in the pores.

Under humid, pre-saturated conditions (70% RH), both sorbents displayed improved performance compared to dry conditions at the respective temperatures; however, LWT-bPPI5 consistently outperformed unfunctionalized LWT across the entire tested temperature window (). For LWT, the equilibrium CO2 uptake increased to 1.78 mmol g−1 at 25°C under humid conditions, which is 1.85-times higher than under dry conditions. Previous studies by Reimer and coworkers investigated the water-induced swelling behavior of LWT using advanced solid-state NMR techniques and demonstrated that adsorbed water can substantially alter the local polymer environment and accessibility of amine functionalities (). Inspired by these findings, we hypothesize that humidity-induced swelling of the LWT network improves access to previously inaccessible amine sites under DAC conditions. Consistent with this interpretation, the breakthrough time increased substantially under humid conditions (∼175 min) compared to dry conditions (∼103 min) at 25°C, indicating enhanced dynamic accessibility of the adsorption sites due to pore swelling (figs. S13B and S15B, table S4). Lowering of temperature further increased the equilibrium capacity to 2.4 mmol g−1 at −20°C. However, decreasing the temperature from 15 to −20°C resulted in shorter breakthrough times together with broader mass-transfer zones, indicating increasingly diffusion-limited adsorption behavior at lower temperatures (figs. S13B and S15B, table S4). This behavior likely arises because, although water-induced swelling of the LWT network increases the accessibility of amine sites, lowering the temperature simultaneously enhances water uptake within the pore structure. The excess adsorbed water partially fills the pores and creates more tortuous diffusion pathways, thereby slowing CO2 transport to the adsorption sites.

For LWT-bPPI5, the pseudo-equilibrium CO2 capacity increased from 2.28 mmol g−1 at 25°C to 3.09 mmol g−1 at −5°C, remaining high (2.95 mmol g−1) even at −20°C, with an amine efficiency of approximately 0.36 mmol CO2 mmol−1 N (, fig. 14C). Interestingly, the breakthrough time also increased substantially under humid conditions, from ∼271 min at 25°C to ∼349 min at −5°C, compared to only 30 min and 8 min, respectively, under dry conditions. In addition, the breakthrough profiles remained steep and S-shaped with relatively narrow mass-transfer zones between 25 and − 5°C (figs. S14C and S16B, table S4), indicating rapid adsorption kinetics and that a large fraction of the equilibrium adsorption capacity remains dynamically accessible during adsorption. This observation strongly suggests that water influences the bPPI oligomers by disrupting amine-amine interactions while simultaneously inducing network swelling, synergistically enhancing amine accessibility toward CO2 and significantly reducing diffusional resistance in LWT-bPPI5. However, at −20°C, the breakthrough time decreased with a broad mass transfer zone, likely due to reduced bPPI chain mobility associated with low thermal energy and partial water condensation within the pores. Nevertheless, even under these conditions, LWT-bPPI5 maintains significantly higher dynamic CO2 capture performance than pristine LWT.

Across the full temperature range, LWT-bPPI5 exhibited 23–36% higher equilibrium uptake than LWT under 70% RH, demonstrating the beneficial role of bPPI functionalization in humid DAC-relevant atmospheres. In the co-adsorption method, where CO2 and water vapor were introduced simultaneously to a dry adsorbent, LWT-bPPI5 showed capacities similar to those of pre-saturated samples in the temperature range of −5°C to 25°C (, fig. S14B). At 25°C and 70% RH, the CO2 uptake was 2.47 mmol g−1, which further increased to 3.02 mmol g−1 at 5°C (amine efficiency: 0.36 mmol CO2 mmol−1 N) accompanied by long breakthrough time and sharp breakthrough profile, indicating facile CO2 diffusion and rapid sorption kinetics. Notably, the breakthrough curve at −5°C exhibited 2.99 mmol g−1 uptake with a non-monotonic profile, an initial rapid rise, a distinct dip, and a subsequent rise to saturation (fig. S14B). This unusual behavior likely originates from competitive co-adsorption between H2O and CO2 during the initial stage. Initially, adsorbed water molecules partially occupy the pore environment and temporarily hinder CO2 diffusion and amine accessibility, resulting in the initial breakthrough increase. As hydration progresses, water disrupts amine-amine interactions and induces polymer swelling, enhancing amine accessibility and resulting in the subsequent decrease in breakthrough concentration before gradual saturation occurs. However, at −20°C, the co-adsorption experiments revealed a substantial reduction in breakthrough performance, indicating strong kinetic limitations. Complete saturation required extended exposure, resulting in a low uptake of 0.53 mmol g−1 of CO2 after ∼12 h (, fig. S14B). Unlike the pre-saturation conditions, co-adsorption of H2O and CO2 into a mostly dry sorbent does not allow sufficient time for extensive amine hydration, suppressing chain mobility and hindering efficient CO2 diffusion at low temperatures. In pre-hydrated samples, by contrast, the polymer network remains swollen and the amines are already solvated, thereby enhancing chain mobility, and amine accessibility and enabling higher CO2 uptake, even at −20°C. At 25°C under co-adsorption conditions, LWT-bPPI5 maintains a stable pseudo-equilibrium CO2 capacity with a slight increase from 2.45 to 2.58 mmol g−1 as RH increases from 50% to 90%, indicating good tolerance to high humidity (fig. S17).

Having revealed temperature-dependent mobility limitations of the grafted bPPI chains under fixed humidity conditions, we next decoupled the humidity effect by systematically examining CO2 uptake as a function of relative humidity at 30°C and 5°C using dynamic vapor sorption (DVS Carbon) analysis. At 30°C, the CO2 uptake increases monotonically with increasing relative humidity from 0% to 70%. In contrast, at the lower temperature of 5°C, a decrease in CO2 uptake is observed when transitioning from dry conditions to 20% RH, followed by a steady increase at higher humidity levels (≥50% RH) (fig. S19). We attribute this behavior to insufficient water content at low RH to enhance amine mobility and accessibility, combined with competitive adsorption of water and CO2 at sub-ambient temperature. As a result, a slight reduction in CO2 capacity is observed under these conditions. Overall, LWT-bPPI5 exhibited striking moisture-induced enhancements in performance, with a ∼1.8-fold increase in CO2 capacity at 25°C and a remarkable ∼15-fold increase at −20°C relative to dry conditions (). Improved performance was achieved under both pre-saturation conditions, which simulates a sorbent state after direct steam-stripping desorption, and during CO2/H2O coadsorption, which simulates the sorbent after a desorption process that removes all water from the material in each cycle. Such dramatic improvements may arise from either (i) a mechanistic shift from carbamate formation (two amine sites to bind one CO2) under dry conditions to carbamic acid and/or bicarbonate pathways (one amine site to bind one CO2) under humid conditions, or (ii) water-induced network swelling and mobilization of the grafted bPPI chains that increases amine accessibility, or a combination of both (). In this context, we carried out mechanistic investigations to elucidate the relative contributions of these pathways.

Mechanistic investigation of CO2 adsorption under dry and humid conditions using DRIFTS

The in situ DRIFTS spectra of LWT and LWT-bPPI5 recorded under dry and humid CO2 environments provide valuable insights into the nature of adsorbed species and the underlying CO2 capture mechanism (, fig. S20). Under dry conditions (25°C, 0% RH), the characteristic stretching bands observed at around 1663, 1580 (νas), 1552 (νas), 1484, and 1428 (νs) cm−1 are attributed to various symmetric, asymmetric stretching vibrations of the COO− groups and 1407 and 1316 cm−1 are assigned for NCOO− skeletal vibrations, while the peaks at 1632 (δas), 1537 (δas), and 1473 (δs) cm−1 correspond to the bending vibrations of NH3+ groups (, fig. S20A) (, , ). Under humid co-adsorption conditions (25°C, 75% RH), no significant spectral shifts or the emergence of new features was detected for either LWT or LWT-bPPI5, suggesting that the CO2 adsorption mechanism remains largely unchanged compared to the dry condition and continues to proceed via ammonium carbamate formation (, fig. S20B) (). However, the potential presence of bicarbonate (HCO3−) species under humid conditions merits exploration in the future using complementary techniques such as solid-state 13C NMR spectroscopy, as these species may form in low concentrations and exhibit weak IR absorption bands that make their identification challenging (, ).

Temperature programmed desorption studies

Alkyl ammonium carbamate ion pairs (R-NH3+⋯−OOC-NH-R) are known to exhibit strong chemisorption with higher binding energies than carbamic acid (R-HN-COOH). To gain a qualitative understanding of the relative contributions of strong chemisorption and weak chemisorption/physisorption to the overall CO2 uptake in LWT and LWT-bPPI5 under both dry and humid conditions, we conducted temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) analyses (figs. S21 and S22). After exposing the samples to 400 ppm CO2/N2 at 25°C under both dry and humid (70% RH, pre-saturation) conditions, the desorption was carried out using a controlled temperature ramp of 0.5°C min−1. CO2 desorbed below 25°C was assigned to weak chemisorption/physisorption, whereas desorption above 25°C was assigned to strong chemisorption, primarily arising from ammonium carbamate formation ().

For unfunctionalized LWT, the contribution from weak CO2-sorbent interaction increased from 23% to 38% under dry conditions and from 15% to 33% under humid conditions (70% RH) as the temperature decreased from 25°C to −20°C (figs. S21 and S22). This trend likely originates from the enhanced contribution of physisorption at lower temperatures (). In contrast, LWT-bPPI5 showed reduced contributions for weak CO2-sorbent interaction (physisorption), rising only from 6% to 12.5% under dry conditions and from 7% to 25% under humid conditions over the same temperature range. This difference arises from the denser amine packing in LWT-bPPI5, which favors alkyl ammonium carbamate formation, whereas the higher surface area, pore volume, and lower amine loading in LWT lead to more dispersed amine sites, leading to a larger fraction of weakly bound CO2. This trend is supported by desorption energy (En) values, i.e., LWT-bPPI5 exhibits a higher En (105.6 kJ mol−1) than LWT (75.8 kJ mol−1) (fig. S23) at 25°C and 70% RH (). Nonetheless, strong chemisorption remains dominant (>60%) for both materials under all conditions, confirming that alkyl ammonium carbamate formation is the primary CO2 binding mechanism, consistent with the in situ DRIFTS results (figs. S21 and S22).

Oxidative stability and recyclability

To preliminarily evaluate the oxidative stability of the sorbents, both LWT and LWT-bPPI5 samples were subjected to an accelerated aging test by exposing them to air (21% O2/N2) at 80°C for 144 hours. Following this oxidative treatment with 48 h intervals, their CO2 adsorption capacities were evaluated using dynamic vapor sorption (DVS Carbon) at 30°C, 70% RH under 400 ppm CO2/N2 using pre-saturation method (). LWT exhibited a pronounced decline in adsorption capacity, losing approximately ∼35% of its initial CO2 uptake after 144 h of oxidation. In contrast, the LWT-bPPI5 sample showed only a minor reduction of about ∼18% under identical conditions. Exposure to humid air (75% RH) at 80°C accelerated the aging of LWT, resulting in an ∼35% loss in CO2 capacity within 24 h, whereas LWT-bPPI5 exhibited a much smaller reduction of ∼10% (fig. S24). In situ DRIFTS of oxidized LWT revealed strong bands at 1700–1720 cm−1, likely corresponding to aldehydes, along with features at 1690–1650 cm−1 attributed to C=N and C=O species (amide, carboxylate, or urea) (figs. S25A and S26). These oxidation signatures were far weaker for LWT-bPPI5, confirming significantly reduced amine oxidation (fig. S25B). These results clearly demonstrate that grafting branched bPPI onto the LWT framework significantly enhances its resistance to oxidative degradation. Consistent with previous reports showing the superior oxidative stability of PPI relative to other poly(amine) systems, we propose that the chemically grafted bPPI oligomers generate a protective local environment around the benzylic anchoring sites within the resin network. This local shielding effect likely limits direct oxygen access to the vulnerable benzylic carbons, thereby suppressing oxidative degradation pathways and enhancing the long-term stability of the sorbent (, , , ).

TGA cycling tests confirmed the excellent regenerability of LWT-bPPI5, which maintained a CO2 uptake capacity of ∼1.3 mmol g−1 over 19 rapid adsorption-desorption cycles (3 h adsorption at 30°C under 400 ppm CO2/N2, followed by 15 min regeneration at 80°C under dry DAC conditions; , fig. S27). To assess the cycling stability of LWT-bPPI5 under humid conditions (70% RH) and both ambient (25°C) and sub-ambient (5°C) temperatures, fixed-bed breakthrough experiments were carried out using consecutive adsorption-desorption temperature-swing cycles under simulated air (400 ppm CO2/N2; , fig. S28). After each adsorption step (400 ppm CO2/N2 with a flow rate of 40 sccm), the sorbent was regenerated at 80°C under a continuous N2 flow (60 sccm) for 1 h to ensure complete desorption of CO2. The breakthrough profiles demonstrated that LWT-bPPI5 maintained an almost constant pseudo-equilibrium CO2 adsorption capacity (C/C0 = 0.95) of ∼2.45 mmol g−1 per cycle over 10 cycles at 25°C and 70% RH, and ∼ 2.95 mmol g−1 per cycle over 10 cycles at 5°C and 70% RH. The negligible loss in uptake capacity over 20 cycles and the nearly identical FTIR spectral features and nearly unchanged N-content (8.7 mmol g−1) obtained from the elemental analysis of the pristine and the recycled LWT-bPPI5 samples highlights the promising stability of LWT-bPPI5, supporting its significant potential for durable CO2 capture under both ambient and sub-ambient DAC conditions (fig. S29).

While the material exhibits excellent stability under DAC-relevant conditions, practical carbon capture operating conditions often involve higher CO2 partial pressures and repeated thermal cycling. We therefore evaluated the long-term durability of LWT-bPPI5 under simulated flue-gas condition by conducting a comparative long-term recycling study in TGA for LWT and LWT-bPPI5 over 150 adsorption-desorption cycles (1 h adsorption at 40°C followed by 1 h desorption at 80°C under air) under simulated flue-gas (15% CO2/N2, 0% RH) () (fig. S30). The bare LWT exhibits a more pronounced loss in CO2 uptake capacity than LWT-bPPI5. Linear fitting of the capacity decay revealed a degradation rate of 0.006 mmol g−1 cycle−1 for LWT-bPPI5, compared to 0.016 mmol g−1 cycle−1 for LWT (exponential fitting) (fig. S31). In contrast, when regeneration was performed under N2 at 80°C, LWT-bPPI5 showed no measurable loss in performance, while pristine LWT exhibited only ∼2% loss over 50 adsorption-desorption cycles, clearly indicating that the capacity decay observed during regeneration under air primarily originates from oxidative degradation of the amine functionalities by O2 rather than thermal degradation (fig. S30). Furthermore, the combustion TGA analysis of LWT-bPPI5 after 150 adsorption-desorption cycles under air revealed excellent retention of amine loading, with only ∼2.5 wt.% loss (fig. S32).

In addition to conventional thermal regeneration, steam-assisted regeneration is considered a promising and energy-efficient alternative for DAC processes. To evaluate the hydrothermal robustness of the materials, both LWT and LWT-bPPI5 were exposed to continuous steam generated from boiling deionized water for 72 h. After steam treatment, the samples were thoroughly dried, re-activated, and their CO2 uptake capacities were measured by TGA under DAC-relevant conditions (400 ppm CO2/N2) at 15°C and 54% relative humidity (pre-saturated). Notably, LWT-bPPI5 retained the majority of its CO2 capture capacity after prolonged steam exposure, exhibiting only ∼8% loss (fig. S33). This behavior highlights the strong covalent attachment of the bPPI oligomers to the resin framework, which effectively mitigates amine leaching, structural rearrangement, or degradation, issues commonly encountered in non-covalently grafted or physically impregnated PEI-based sorbents under steam treatment. The excellent steam stability of LWT-bPPI5 therefore underscores its structural robustness and practical potential for integration into steam-assisted regeneration schemes, further enhancing its suitability for long-term DAC applications.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we developed a facile and scalable one-step strategy to graft branched poly(propyleneimine) oligomers onto primary amine-functionalized commercial porous resins via acid-catalyzed in situ polymerization of azetidine. The specific N content of the optimized resin (LWT-bPPI5) increased from 5.7 ± 0.2 to 8.7 ± 0.1 mmol g−1, resulting in up to a 75% enhancement in CO2 uptake under dry DAC conditions at 30°C. Under humid conditions (70% RH), LWT-bPPI5 achieved capacities of ∼2.5 mmol g−1 at 25°C and ∼ 3.0 mmol g−1 at −5 to −20°C, corresponding to an improvement of ∼36% compared to pristine LWT. In addition, the sorbent exhibited excellent oxidative and steam stability, recyclability, and regeneration under mild conditions (80°C). Under flue-gas conditions (15% CO2, 40°C), LWT-bPPI5 achieved a 55% increase in CO2 uptake with excellent cycling performance over 150 cycles compared to pristine LWT. The present study thus demonstrates a promising route for fabricating bPPI-grafted porous resins with excellent recyclability and high CO2 capacity under DAC and flue-gas conditions, across a broad range of temperatures, and humidities (fig. S10, table S5). As these commercial resins are currently amongst the leading candidate materials for large scale DAC deployment, this one step strategy could provide a boost to near-term DAC deployments.

Nonetheless, several aspects warrant further investigation. Although the current approach has been validated at the gram-scale, process optimization at the kilogram-to-ton scale will be crucial for further industrial translation. Milder acid catalysts (HCl, HBr, CH3SO3H, etc.) can be utilized and this will make the process more industrially friendly (). Efforts are already underway in our laboratory to extend this strategy to other primary- and secondary-amine-functionalized porous supports to further generalize the bPPI-grafting methodology. In this context, preliminary results demonstrate that the strategy can be successfully applied to another commercially available primary-amine-containing PS-DVB resin, Purolite A110. Upon bPPI grafting, the N content increased by ∼34% (7.1 mmol g−1 for pristine resin to 9.5 mmol g−1 for bPPI grafted resin, table S1), accompanied by a ∼1.5-fold enhancement in CO2 uptake capacity from 1.15 mmol g−1 to 1.73 mmol g−1 under 30°C, 400 ppm CO2/N2, and 0% RH (fig. S34). Long-term field tests of sorption/desorption cycling under realistic conditions (400 ppm CO2/air and 15% CO2/air) are needed to further evaluate operational stability. Complementary techniques such as solid-state 13C NMR should be employed to probe the formation of bicarbonate and carbamate species and further elucidate the CO2 adsorption mechanism(s). Finally, detailed in situ spectroscopic studies on the oxidative degradation pathways of both pristine and bPPI-grafted LWT under varying environmental conditions would further clarify their enhanced stability. Overall, the present work provides a foundation for the development of other next-generation poly(amine) sorbents for practical DAC applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Chemicals

All reagents were acquired from commercial sources and used as received. Lewatit VP OC 1065 (LWT) ion exchange resin with Lot No. BCCL5490 (Product No.: 94136–100G-F), aqueous perchloric acid (aq. HClO4, ∼70%), and anhydrous methanol (99.8%) were obtained from Millipore Sigma. Purolite A110 ion exchange resin (N047U/24/6, Product code: 67009, S.R.#: 6835) was procured from Ecolab. Azetidine (purity ≥99%) was obtained from Ambeed Chemicals. Reagent grade methanol, and ammonium hydroxide were obtained from VWR and Fisher Scientific, respectively. Branched poly(propylene imine) (Mw: ∼900 g mol−1) used for the physical impregnation with LWT was synthesized in lab ().

Synthesis of branched poly(propyleneimine)-grafted porous polymeric resin (LWT-bPPIn)

The present invention provides a method for the synthesis of chemically grafted branched poly(propyleneimine) (bPPI) onto amine-bearing polymeric resins, such as, commercial poly(styrene-divinylbenzene) resin (PS-DVB resin), specifically Lewatit VP OC 1065 (LWT). First, 50 mg of commercial amine functionalized poly(styrene-divinylbenzene) resin, i.e., LWT (in the form of powder, particle size <300 μm, vide infra), was dispersed in 2 mL anhydrous methanol in 25 mL Schlenk tube followed by addition of 130–364 μL of azetidine (i.e., ∼7 to ∼20 equivalents with respect to amine groups present in LWT) under argon atmosphere. The mixture was stirred gently (∼300 rpm) for 30 min under room temperature (21–25°C) to ensure uniform dispersion and to promote adsorption of azetidine onto the resin. Subsequently, 41.6–615.5 μL (i.e., 0.25 to 3.7 equivalent with respect to azetidine) of perchloric acid (∼70% HClO4 aq.) was added slowly to the stirred reaction mixture. The reaction vessel was sealed, and the mixture was heated to 70°C (ramp rate: ∼1°C min−1, stirring speed of 350 rotation per minute with a 1 in. by 5/16 in. stirring bar) for 48 hours to facilitate in situ polymerization of azetidine, yielding branched poly(propyleneimine) (bPPI) chains covalently anchored to the resin framework. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was cooled down to room temperature and the resulting product, designated as LWT-bPPIn (where n = 1–9), was isolated as an off-white solid. The solid was washed thoroughly with aqueous ammonium hydroxide followed by methanol to remove residual acid and any unbound or loosely adsorbed bPPI species. The purified product was dried under dynamic vacuum at ambient temperature overnight. The detailed reagent compositions for each LWT-bPPIn variant are provided in . We further scaled up the reaction to 300 mg for the synthesis of LWT-bPPI5 while maintaining the exact stoichiometric ratios of the reagents, catalysts, and solvent (300 mg LWT, 780 μL azetidine, 499.2 μL 70% HClO4, 12 mL anhydrous methanol in a 50 mL Schlenk tube). The reaction time (48 h) and temperature (70°C) were kept unchanged (obtained bPPI-grafted resin after drying at room temperature under vacuum for overnight: 370–380 mg). By maintaining the stoichiometry, the reaction can be scaled up to a few gram-scale.

Synthesis of branched poly(propyleneimine)-grafted Purolite A110

Purolite A110 is another commercially available weak base ion-exchange resin with primary amine functionalized poly(styrene-divinyl benzene) backbone. We procured Purolite A110 resin (Purolite A110 N047U/24/6, Product code: 67009, S.R.#: 6835) directly from Ecolab. bPPI grafting on Purolite A110 was performed following the optimized protocol analogous to that used for Lewatit VP OC 1065 (vide supra). Briefly, Purolite A110 (300 mg, powder, < 300 μm) was dispersed in anhydrous methanol (12 mL) in a Schlenk tube, followed by the addition of azetidine (1006 μL, 14.93 mmol, 7 equiv. relative to amine groups) under Ar. After stirring at room temperature for 30 min, perchloric acid (∼70% HClO4 aq., 644 μL, 0.5 equiv. relative to azetidine) was added dropwise. The sealed reaction mixture was heated at 70°C for 48 h for in situ azetidine polymerization. The resulting Purolite A110-bPPI was isolated, thoroughly washed with aqueous ammonium hydroxide and methanol, and dried under vacuum. CHN elemental analysis showed an increase in N content from 7.11 mmol g−1 (pristine) to 9.50 mmol g−1 (bPPI-grafted).

Characterization

The structural characterization of the pristine and bPPI-grafted resins was carried out through various spectroscopic and microscopic techniques. Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) analysis was carried out using Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer and an in situ DRIFTS cell. Before the measurement, ∼50 mg of resin sample was activated under a constant nitrogen flow of 60 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm) at 110°C for 2 h, then cooled to 25°C, and spectra were collected under a N2 atmosphere. Solid-state cross-polarization/magic angle spinning (CP/MAS) 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) using 400 MHz Bruker Avance III with a 9.5 T wide-bore magnet. Dry powdered resin samples were packed into 4 mm zirconia rotors. Adamantane and glycine were used as reference standards prior to analysis and the MAS rate was maintained at 12 kHz. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic (XPS) analyses of the resins were carried out using Thermo Scientific K-Alpha XPS instrument. The CHN elemental analysis of the resins were conducted at Atlantic Microlab, Inc. Thermal stability of all the resins was checked using a thermogravimetric analyzer, Q500 TA instrument under air. Before each analysis, the dried samples were activated at 110°C for 1 h. The textural characterizations of the resins were carried out using N2 physisorption isotherm analysis using Autosorb iQ/Anton Paar instrument. Prior to the measurement, all of the sorbent materials were degassed at 80°C under vacuum for 6 h to remove adsorbed CO2 or moisture. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) specific surface area was estimated in the relative pressure (p/p0) range of 0.05–0.30. The total pore volume was calculated based on the N2 sorption at a p/p0 of 0.95, and the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) model was used to calculate the pore size distribution of the sorbent material with Quantachrome ASiQwin software. The morphological analysis was carried out using Hitachi SU8230 Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) with accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Powdered resin samples were sprinkled onto carbon tape fixed on an aluminum stub. A ∼10 nm gold-palladium coating was applied by sputtering prior to imaging.

CO2 uptake study and recyclability

We evaluated the preliminary direct air capture of CO2 (DAC) performances of various resins thermogravimetrically at 30°C under dry conditions using Q500 TA instrument. Prior to each measurement, the samples (∼10 mg) were activated at 80°C with a ramp rate of 10°C min−1 followed by isothermal at 80°C for 3 h under a constant N2 flow (90 sccm). The samples were subsequently cooled to 30°C using the same ramp rate and allowed to equilibrate for 5 min. Adsorption was then carried out isothermally for 6 h at 30°C under 400 ppm CO2/N2. The CO2 uptake capacities of all the amine grafted resins were estimated as the mass gained during the 6 h of adsorption. CO2 desorption was performed by heating the samples to 80°C at a rate of 10°C/min, followed by an isothermal hold of 15–60 min under a N2 flow of 90 sccm. The amine efficiencies of both the pristine and bPPI-grafted resins were determined from the amount of CO2 adsorbed relative to the total amine loading on the polymer, as obtained from CHN analysis. The recyclability of the bPPI-grafted resins (e.g., LWT-bPPI5, ∼10 mg) was evaluated over 19 rapid adsorption-desorption cycles using a Q500 TA instrument under 400 ppm CO2/N2 dry conditions at 30°C. Each cycle comprised 3 h of adsorption under 400 ppm CO2/N2 (flow rate: 90 sccm) at 30°C, followed by 15 min of activation at 80°C under N2 flow (90 sccm). The measurements under simulated flue gas mixtures were carried out using a Q500 TA instrument, using two gas streams, Gas 1, a dry N2 cylinder and Gas 2, a dry mixture of certified 15% CO2 85% N2, both set at 100 mL min−1. Prior to each measurement, the samples (∼20 mg) were activated at 100°C with a ramp rate of 10°C min-1 followed by an isothermal step at 100°C for 1 h under a constant N2 flow. The samples were subsequently cooled down to 40°C using the same ramp rate and allowed to equilibrate for 15 min. Adsorption was then carried out isothermally for 6 h at 40°C under 15% CO2 85% N2. Long 150 air cyclic test experiments for the LWT and LWT-bPPI5 samples were conducted on a Discovery TGA 550, from TA instruments using two gas streams: Gas 1, a dry, CO2-free Air cylinder and Gas 2, a mixture of certified 15% CO2 85% N2, both set at 100 mL min−1. ∼20–25 mg sample was first heated to 80°C at a ramp rate of 10°C/min under Gas 1 atmosphere and held at this temperature for 60 min. Subsequently, the sample was cooled down to 40°C, and kept under isothermal conditions for 15 min, after which the gas was switched to Gas 2 for 60 min to collect the CO2 uptake. This adsorption-desorption cycle was repeated 150 times in total.

CO2 dynamic breakthrough analysis

Breakthrough experiments were performed in a custom-built fixed-bed reactor using dried resin samples (90–100 mg) packed in a 1/4′′ stainless-steel tube (∼5 cm length, 4 mm i.d.), sealed with glass wool. A coiled 1/8′′ stainless-steel tube upstream allowed controlled pre-heating or pre-cooling of the gas feed, and the reactor bed was immersed in a refrigerated circulating bath (water:ethylene glycol, 1:1 v/v) connected to a Julabo CD-600F chiller (−20 to 80°C). Gas flow rates were precisely controlled with Alicat mass flow controllers. Samples were pre-activated at 80°C for 2 h under N2 (60 sccm). Breakthrough tests were then conducted with dry or humid (0% and 70% RH) 400 ppm CO2/N2 (40 sccm), with humidity regulated by a WETSYS/SETARAM humidifier and outlet CO2/H2O concentrations monitored using a LI-850 (LI-COR) infrared gas analyzer. For co-adsorption humid experiments, the resin was directly exposed to humid 400 ppm CO2/N2 (40 sccm) immediately after activation. On the other hand, for presaturation humid experiments, the resin was first pre-humidified with 70% RH/N2 (40 sccm) at the target temperatures (−20°C to 25°C), followed by adsorption under humid 400 ppm CO2/N2 (40 sccm).

In situ DRIFTS study for CO2 adsorption

In situ DRIFTS measurements were carried out on a Nicolet iS10 FT-IR spectrometer to monitor surface species formed under simulated DAC conditions. Prior to each experiment, ∼50 mg of resin was loaded into the DRIFTS cell and activated at 110°C under a continuous flow of dry N2 (60 sccm) for 2 h. After activation, the temperature was reduced to 25°C and the sample was allowed to equilibrate for 30 min. Subsequently, the activated sorbents were exposed to a CO2 stream (400 ppm in N2) under either dry or humid conditions at a flow rate of 40 sccm. For humid experiments, the 400 ppm CO2/N2 was passed through a bubbler containing saturated sodium chloride solution at room temperature, generating a stable relative humidity of 75%. IR spectra were collected periodically during gas exposure (64 scans per spectrum, 4 cm−1 resolution). To isolate the vibrational features associated specifically with CO2 adsorption, each spectrum recorded during CO2 dosing was baseline-corrected using the spectrum of the activated sample acquired immediately before the introduction of 400 ppm CO2/N2.

Temperature programmed desorption (TPD) study

TPD experiments were conducted immediately after the CO2 breakthrough runs at 25°C, 70% RH. Once pseudo-equilibrium was reached (C/C0 = 0.95), the inlet stream of 400 ppm CO2/N2 (40 sccm) was switched to pure N2 (60 sccm), and the sample was purged for 45 minutes at 25°C. Subsequently, the temperature was ramped to 80°C at controlled heating rates (0.3–0.7°C min−1). Throughout the process, outlet CO2 concentrations were continuously monitored using a LI-850/LI-COR analyzer to obtain the desorption profiles. Oxidation study

Three long cyclic oxidative studies for the LWT and LWT-bPPI5 samples were conducted using a DVS Carbon instrument (Surface Measurement Systems) using two gas streams: Gas 1, a mixture of 400 ppm CO2/N2, and Gas 2, dry CO2-free air. Prior to first adsorption cycle, the sample was first heated to 80°C at a ramp rate of 5°C min−1 under dry CO2-free air and held at this temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the sample was cooled to 30°C, after which humidity was introduced to achieve 70% relative humidity (RH) at 30°C. The sample was equilibrated under humid air until the mass change rate (dm/dt) was below 0.001 mg min−1 for at least 10 min. Following humid equilibration, the gas stream was switched to Gas 1 (400 ppm CO2) while maintaining 30°C and 70% RH. CO2 adsorption was allowed to proceed until saturation, using the same equilibrium criterion (dm/dt < 0.001 mg min−1 for 10 min). After completion of the adsorption step, the sample was heated again to 80°C under dry CO2-free air and then exposed to dry air at 80°C for 48 h to assess stability under prolonged oxidative conditions. The sample was subsequently cooled to 30°C, and the wet CO2 uptake at 30°C and 70% RH was re-measured following the same protocol described above. This adsorption-exposure-recovery sequence was repeated for three consecutive cycles to evaluate the long-term stability and recyclability of the sorbent under realistic dilute CO2 and humid air conditions.

In situ oxidation experiments for the LWT and LWT-bPPI5 samples were conducted using a DRIFTS cell. Prior to oxidation, approximately 50 mg of the resin samples were placed in the cell and activated at 80°C for 2 h under a nitrogen flow of 60 sccm. Following activation, the samples were exposed to air (21% O2/N2) at 80°C with a flow rate of 60 sccm. The incoming gas was pre-humidified to 75% RH using a bubbler containing saturated sodium chloride solution at room temperature. The experiments were carried out for 24 h, and spectra were collected at fixed time intervals. After the 24 h oxidation study, the samples were collected, activated at 80°C for 2 h under a N2 atmosphere, and their adsorption capacities were evaluated using TGA. The measurements were carried out at 30°C under dry conditions for 6 h to determine the adsorption performance.

Steam stability study

The steam stability test is carried out by placing ∼150 mg of solid in a filter paper cup at a prescribed distance from a round bottom flask with distilled water. The sample is exposed to continuous steam for 72 h under ambient pressure (100°C). The samples were characterized before and after the steam stability test using a Q5000 TA instrument allowing for wet CO2 adsorption (Gas 1: N2 at 54% RH, Gas 2: 400 ppm CO2 balanced with N2 at 54% RH). Prior to the measurement, the solid samples (∼20 mg) were activated at 85°C with a ramp rate of 10°C min−1 followed by isothermal at 85°C for 1 h under a constant N2 flow. The samples were subsequently cooled to 15°C using the same ramp rate and allowed to equilibrate for 15 min. First, water adsorption using Gas 1 was performed until equilibrium was reached (N2 at 54% RH and 15°C isothermally). Next, Gas 2 was used to test the CO2 capacity under wet conditions, letting the adsorption run until full equilibrium (400 ppm CO2 at 54% RH and 15°C isothermally).
