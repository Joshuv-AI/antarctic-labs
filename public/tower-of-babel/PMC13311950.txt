Study finds pelvic floor dysfunction in women with multiple sclerosis is an unmet need requiring nursing action
Authors: Nicole Zhang
Published: 2025

Commentary on: Addington C, Bradshaw A, Hagen S, et al. There’s Nothing Wrong With You; You Just Need to Lose Weight’-A Qualitative Exploration of Pelvic Floor Dysfunction Among Women With Multiple Sclerosis and Their Interaction in Seeking Pelvic Healthcare. Implications for practice and research

Context

Pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) is a common but often overlooked complication in individuals with multiple sclerosis (MS).1 2 Within 10 years of diagnosis, most women with MS experience some form of PFD, which can significantly impact quality of life by contributing to psychological distress and social isolation.1 3

PFD remains underassessed and undertreated in clinical settings. These symptoms are frequently dismissed or misattributed to ageing, weight or generalised disability-related decline.1 Moreover, intersectional barriers, like gender and disability bias, further limit access to appropriate evaluation and care.1 Nursing, with its commitment to holistic, patient-centred care and advocacy for vulnerable populations, is well positioned to lead efforts in addressing PFD in women with MS.

Methods

Addington et al conducted a qualitative study guided by intersectional feminist and participatory research approaches, emphasising collaboration with participants and attention to the intersecting personal and structural factors shaping women’s experiences with MS and PFD, one focus group (n=4) and individual semistructured online interviews were conducted (n=7). The interviews were codesigned with women living with MS, enhancing relevance and sensitivity. Researchers used both online focus groups and semistructured one-on-one interviews to gather data

Due to COVID-19 restrictions, the study was conducted entirely online using secure video conferencing tools. Participants were UK-based cisgender women or individuals assigned female at birth with a diagnosis of MS and experiencing PFD symptoms. Purposive and snowball sampling methods were used, beginning with referrals from a local MS nurse and expanding through MS community organisations and social media outreach. All participants gave informed consent prior to participation.

Data were analysed using framework analysis to identify key themes. Ethical considerations included a culturally responsive, relational reflexive approach to maintain participant dignity and autonomy.

Findings

Addington et al identified four key themes in how women with MS experience PFD. Navigating MS and PFD: Cycles of control showed how symptoms disrupted identity and control. Physiotherapy’s preoccupation with mobility revealed a lack of attention to pelvic health. Silenced voices highlighted how stigma and dismissal deterred help-seeking, leading to relentless self-advocacy. Finally, resistance through collective community emphasised the validating role of peer groups in addressing taboo symptoms.

Commentary

This study reveals a critical gap in care that exists for many female MS patients with PFD. While PFD symptoms are pervasive among women with MS, a lack of provider inquiry, systemic bias and healthcare training gaps perpetuate suffering and reinforce health inequities. Nursing professionals can assist this issue by recognising PFD as a holistic issue one that intersects with identity, sexuality and autonomy, rather can approaching PFD as a purely physical condition.

Evidence shows that nurses are in a uniquely positioned to normalise discussions of bladder, bowel, and sexual health.4 By integrating routine screening for PFD into assessments, nurses can foster early identification and intervention. Educational reform in nursing could also expand on pelvic health beyond the usual contexts (pregnancy and post partum). Nursing curricula must explicitly address gender bias, ableism and reproductive justice as core content areas to prepare future nurses for equitable practice. Furthermore, nursing education and clinical priorities often reinforce a biomedical hierarchy that privileges motor function over intimate health concerns like PFD. The significance here for nursing lies in our ability to challenge reductionist models and advocate for equitable attention to all functional domains, including continence and sexuality. The study’s participatory approach aligns with nursing’s person-centred philosophy, emphasising the importance of cocreating solutions with patients. This model should guide future intervention development.

I call on nurses to interrogate their own clinical communication practices and actively dismantle taboos surrounding bowel, bladder and sexual health placing special emphasis, acknowledging that these topics are complicated in individuals with chronic disease or disability. Addressing PFD in women with MS is not only about continence, it is about dignity, equity and empowering women to live fully with chronic illness. Nurses are uniquely positions to lead the way in closing this care gap.
