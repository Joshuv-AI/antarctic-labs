Preoperative hyponatremia is associated with increased postoperative complications and short- to long-term mortality in geriatric hip fracture patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Chunhua Xi, Meixuan Wan, Haiyao Wu, Shuaihao Zhang, Yuanming He, Daojing Qiu, Xiaowei Wang, Zhi Liu, Xiaobin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreoperative hyponatremia is common in elderly hip fracture patients, but its severity-dependent effects on postoperative complications and short-/long-term mortality, as well as the mediating role of postoperative factors, remain incompletely understood.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1,901 consecutive patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent hip fracture surgery between 2015 and 2021. Preoperative hyponatremia was categorized as mild (130–134 mmol/L) or moderate-to-severe (< 130 mmol/L). Multivariable logistic and Cox regression models were used to assess associations with postoperative complications, 30-day and 1-year mortality, with sequential adjustment for confounders. Mediation analyses quantified the contributions of postoperative hyponatremia and postoperative complications.ResultsPreoperative hyponatremia was present in 243 patients (12.8%): 191 (10.0%) had mild hyponatremia, and 52 (2.7%) had moderate-to-severe hyponatremia. After full adjustment, mild hyponatremia was associated with increased complication risk (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.18–3.20) but not with 30-day mortality (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.54–2.26). Moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was associated with both complications (OR 4.43, 95% CI 2.03–9.65) and 30-day mortality (HR 3.75, 95% CI 1.74–8.08). Mediation analysis revealed that postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia together accounted for 40% of the 30-day mortality risk; after further adjustment for these postoperative factors, the hazard ratio was attenuated (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.10–4.98). For 1-year mortality, moderate-to-severe hyponatremia remained a significant predictor in the baseline-adjusted model (HR 2.07, 95% CI 1.21–3.53), but the association lost statistical significance after additional adjustment for postoperative factors (HR 1.47, 95% CI 0.86–2.54).ConclusionsPreoperative hyponatremia is a severity-dependent risk factor for postoperative complications and short-term mortality, with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia also associated with 1-year mortality through mediation by postoperative factors. Prevention of complications and correction of electrolyte disturbances may represent potential targets to improve early outcomes. The potential impact on long term survival, however, would likely be indirect and requires further prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07833-9.Keywords: Hip fracture, Hyponatremia, Postoperative complications, Mortality, Risk factor

Introduction

As the global population is aging, osteoporotic hip fracture is becoming a major health problem; it causes high mortality and morbidity, decreases patient function, and poses a large financial burden for patient families and the national healthcare system [1, 2]. In China, more than half a million people older than 65 years experience hip fractures every year, and the total number is predicted to be 1.3 million by 2050 [3, 4]. Advanced age, male sex, comorbidities, and malnutrition status have been identified as high-risk factors for postoperative mortality in these patients [5, 6]. However, 1-year mortality after hip fracture has remained at approximately 22% over the past two decades and has not shown a decreasing trend [7, 8], suggesting that further studies are needed to clarify interventions that improve postoperative outcomes.

Hyponatremia (serum sodium concentration < 135 mmol/L) is the most frequent electrolyte disturbance in elderly patients with hip fractures, with a reported incidence rate ranging from 12 to 23% [9–11]. Hyponatremia is frequently observed in malnourished patients, and accumulating evidence suggests a bidirectional relationship between these conditions: malnutrition can induce hyponatremia through hydroelectrolytic, hormonal, and inflammatory mechanisms, whereas chronic hyponatremia may contribute to malnutrition by promoting sarcopenia and metabolic disturbances [12]. Most affected patients are asymptomatic, and the condition is often detected only upon hospital admission [13]. Susceptibility to hyponatremia in the elderly population is linked to disturbances in water homeostasis, a high prevalence of comorbidities, and increased use of medications with hyponatremic side effects [14, 15]. The etiology of hyponatremia in older adults is diverse. While the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is a common cause of hyponatremia in the general elderly population [16], studies on examining patients with fragility fractures have revealed a different etiological profile for these patients. In this unique population, hypovolemia appears to be the predominant cause, accounting for up to 70% of cases, with SIADH contributing to only 27%. Furthermore, the etiology is frequently multifactorial, with common contributing factors including thiazide diuretics, dehydration, and proton pump inhibitors [17]. These findings underscore the importance of population-specific considerations when studying hyponatremia in hip fracture patients.

Recent studies have demonstrated that hyponatremia is not only common in elderly hip fracture patients but also independently associated with increased postoperative mortality. Wang et al. reported a 12.6% incidence of hyponatremia in a large cohort of 1,001 patients, with adjusted odds ratios of 2.56 for 30-day mortality and 1.76 for 1-year mortality [11]. Craig et al. reported a similar correlation between preoperative hyponatremia and postoperative mortality [18]. More recently, Mannheimer et al. confirmed this association and highlighted the importance of severity grading [19]. These observations raise the possibility that hyponatremia may not merely be a marker of underlying illness but could represent a potentially modifiable risk factor. Indeed, previous studies have also linked hyponatremia to increased in-hospital death and postoperative complications in older orthopedic patients [20–22].

Despite this accumulating evidence, several critical knowledge gaps remain, which limit the translation of these findings into clinical practice. First, most previous studies have treated hyponatremia as a binary variable but have not examined dose-response relationships across severity levels. Second, the dynamic nature of sodium status—whether hyponatremia persists, resolves, or develops postoperatively—has rarely been explored, limiting the understanding of whether correction might mitigate risk. Third, associations with specific postoperative complications (e.g., delirium and pneumonia) are not well characterized, hindering targeted prevention strategies. Fourth, few studies have rigorously adjusted for nutritional status using comprehensive markers such as the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI) or body mass index (BMI). Finally, data from large Chinese cohorts remain limited, and it is unclear whether findings from Western populations can be generalized to Chinese patients given the known differences in perioperative care and comorbidity profiles. Importantly, while general clinical guidelines for the diagnosis and management of hyponatremia are available from major professional societies [16, 23–25], population-specific guidance for patients with hip fractures is lacking. The unique perioperative context of hip fracture care—including surgical urgency, fasting requirements, and trauma-related fluid shifts—needs further research to establish tailored recommendations for this vulnerable population. Moreover, the limited evidence available may contribute to the condition being overlooked in clinical practice [21].

We hypothesized that the risk of postoperative complications and mortality increased progressively with the severity of preoperative hyponatremia, and postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia might mediate a significant portion of the relationship between preoperative hyponatremia and mortality.To address this hypothesis, we conducted a large retrospective cohort study to quantify the association between preoperative hyponatremia and postoperative outcomes.

Methods

Ethical approval, data source and study design

This single-center retrospective cohort study was approved by the ethics committee of The Seventh Medical Center, Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. 2015–081), and the approval covered the entire study period (2015–2021). This study was a retrospective analysis of data in an existing clinical database, and the data was anonymized and collected during routine clinical care. Because the study involved no direct patient contact, no experimental interventions, and only deidentified data, the Ethics Committee granted a waiver of informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

We retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients aged ≥ 60 years who underwent hip fracture surgery in the orthopedic department of the Seventh Medical Center, Chinese PLA General Hospital, between January 2015 and December 2021. The case data were derived from our hip fracture patient database, which was established more than a decade ago [26, 27]. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ≥ 60 years of age, (2) fragility hip fracture (defined as low-energy trauma caused by falling to the ground), (3) isolated hip fracture that was not associated with other fractures requiring surgical treatment, and (4) patients whose serum sodium data at admission and within 24 h after surgery were available. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with pathological fractures, (2) patients with high-energy injuries, (3) patients with multiple fractures, (4) patients with periprosthetic fractures or with contralateral hip fracture, (5) patients whose serum sodium concentrations were greater than 145 mmol/L at admission, and (6) patients with incomplete records or follow-up data.

Perioperative management

During the study period (2015–2021), all patients received a risk-stratified venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis protocol based on admission lower extremity deep vein ultrasound examination, following institutional guidelines. Mechanical and pharmacological prophylaxis was administered according to ultrasound findings, with therapeutic anticoagulation or inferior vena cava filter placement reserved for patients with documented thrombosis. This protocol was consistently applied throughout the study period. There was no standardized institutional protocol for the correction of preoperative hyponatremia in hip fracture patients during this period. Management was at the discretion of the attending physicians and generally followed empirical principles based on clinical experience. Common measures included encouraging increased oral salt intake (e.g., salty foods) for patients with mild to moderate hyponatremia who were able to tolerate oral intake, and discontinuing or avoiding medications known to potentially cause or exacerbate hyponatremia (e.g., thiazide diuretics, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, carbamazepine) when clinically feasible. For more severe cases, cautious intravenous fluid administration (typically normal saline) was administered cautiously.

In general, patients undergo one of four types of surgical management according to the fracture pattern, including the placement of three cannulated cancellous screws for nondisplaced femoral neck fractures, bipolar cemented or cementless hemiarthroplasty or total hip arthroplasty for displaced femoral neck fractures, the placement of dynamic hip screws (DHSs) for stable intertrochanteric fractures, and the placement of cephalomedullary nails for unstable intertrochanteric and subtrochanteric fractures. Routine follow-up was performed during patient visits to the clinic or via phone interviews by a special team consisting of two orthopedic surgeons and four nurses at 30 days, 6 months, and 1 year post-operatively and every year thereafter.

Definition of hyponatremia

Perioperative sodium levels were recorded on admission and on postoperative day 1, day 3, and day 5, when clinically indicated. Owing to the retrospective design, measurements were obtained on the biasis of clinical indications rather than a fixed protocol. All blood samples were analyzed by the same laboratory in our hospital. Sodium was analyzed via ion-selective electrode potentiometry.

In accordance with the European clinical practice guideline on hyponatremia, hyponatremia was defined as a serum sodium concentration < 135 mmol/L and further categorized by severity: mild hyponatremia (130–134 mmol/L), moderate hyponatremia ( 125–129 mmol/L) and profound hyponatremia (< 125 mmol/L) [24]. Owing to the limited number of patients with moderate hyponatremia (n = 34) and profound hyponatremia in our cohort (n = 18), moderate and profound hyponatremia were combined into a moderate-to-severe hyponatremia group (serum sodium concentration < 130 mmol/L) for analysis. Normonatremia was defined as a serum sodium concentration between 135 and 145 mmol/L. Patients with serum sodium concentration > 145 mmol/L (hypernatremia, n = 21) were excluded from the analysis to avoid confounding by hypernatremia, which has distinct pathophysiology and prognostic implications.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were all-cause mortality within 30 days and within 1 year after surgery, as postoperative 30-day mortality is often used to reflect the acute complications that occur during surgery and in the early postoperative period, whereas 1-year mortality is associated with postoperative functional recovery, comorbidity management, and long-term health status.

Postoperative complications during hospitalization were prospectively recorded by trained research nurses and adjudicated by a panel of two clinicians, with disagreements resolved by consensus or by a third reviewer. The complications assessed included delirium, pneumonia, cardiac events (myocardial infarction, new arrhythmia requiring intervention, or acute heart failure), gastrointestinal bleeding, and cerebrovascular events (ischemic stroke or intracranial hemorrhage). Detailed operational definitions and diagnosis of postoperative complications are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Covariates

To minimize potential confounding bias, we adjusted for a comprehensive set of covariates, categorized as follows: (1) Demographic and clinical characteristics: age, sex, fracture type (femoral neck vs. trochanteric), and comorbidities. Comorbidities included hypertension, coronary heart disease, arrhythmia, chronic cardiac insufficiency, stroke, dementia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal insufficiency, diabetes mellitus, Parkinson’s disease, and a history of malignancy. (2) Nutritional status: was assessed using two indicators. PNI was calculated as 10 × serum albumin (g/dL) + 0.005 × total lymphocyte count (per mm³) and categorized as low (< 45) or normal (≥ 45) on the basis of established cutoffs indicating malnutrition [28]. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m²) and dichotomized as underweight (< 22 kg/m²) vs. nonunderweight (≥ 22 kg/m²), as low BMI is a well-established indicator for malnutrition in elderly individuals [29]. (3) Perioperative factors included preoperative anemia (hemoglobin concentration < 130 g/L for men and < 120 g/L for women), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade (categorized as I–II vs. III–IV), time from injury to surgery (≤ 2 days vs. > 2 days), blood transfusion volume on the day of surgery (< 4 U vs. ≥ 4 U), anesthesia type (general anesthesia vs. neuraxial anesthesia or nerve block), and surgical type (arthroplasty vs. fixation). These covariates were chosen as the most important based on findings from our previous study and other literature [22, 26]. Postoperative hyponatremia (lowest sodium concentration within 24 h after surgery, < 135 mmol/L) was included as a binary covariate in multivariable models to assess whether the prognostic impact of preoperative hyponatremia was independent of postoperative sodium status.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

On the basis of of our previous data, the prevalence of hyponatremia was 12.6%, and the 1-year mortality was 26.2% in hyponatremic patients vs. 14.1% in normonatremic patients [11]. With the use of PASS software, a minimum of 728 patients (including 92 with hyponatremia) was required to detect this difference with 80% power and α = 0.05. The final cohort of 1,901 patients (243 with hyponatremia) exceeded this requirement, providing adequate statistical power for the primary analysis.

Descriptive techniques were used to compare baseline characteristics across the three groups: normonatremia, mild hyponatremia, and moderate-to-severe hyponatremia. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Variables that were not normally distributed are expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages and were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed when the overall test was significant, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

We first evaluated the association between hyponatremia at admission and postoperative complications incidence. Any patient with at least one complication was counted as having a complication, irrespective of the number of concurrent events. Multivariate logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between preoperative hyponatremia severity and postoperative complications. A four-step covariate strategy to balance comprehensive adjustment were performed: Model 1 (minimally adjusted): adjusted for age, sex, and fracture type; Model 2 (moderately adjusted): further adjusted for ASA grade, PNI, BMI, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI, dichotomized as < 3 vs. ≥ 3) as a summary measure of comorbidity burden, and preoperative anemia; Model 3 (fully adjusted): adjusted for age, sex, and fracture type, all comorbidities (hypertension, coronary heart disease, arrhythmia, stroke, etc.), ASA grade, PNI, BMI, preoperative anemia, preoperative pneumonia and preoperative lower extremity venous thrombosis, time to surgery, surgical procedure type, anesthesia type, and transfusion volume on the day of surgery. Model 4 (sensitivity): further adjusted for postoperative hyponatremia as a sensitivity analysis to explore whether the effect of preoperative hyponatremia is independent of postoperative sodium status. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A trend test was performed by treating hyponatremia severity as an ordinal variable (0 = normonatremia, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate-to-severe). Given the high frequency and clinical importance of postoperative cardiac events, delirium and pneumonia, we performed separate multivariable logistic regression analyses to examine their associations with preoperative hyponatremia severity. The same sequential adjustment strategy (Models 1–4) described above was applied. Odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and dose‒response trends were assessed using ordinal trend tests.

Next, we investigated the impact of hyponatremia at admission on postoperative all-cause mortality in 3 steps. In step 1, we conducted Kaplan–Meier analyses and the nonparametric log-rank statistic, which measures the difference between patients with mild hyponatremia or moderate to severe hyponatremia at admission and those without hyponatremia. In step 2, Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate the association between preoperative hyponatremia severity and 30-day and 1-year all-cause mortality. Four sequential models were constructed to progressively adjust for potential confounders and to explore mediation. Model 1 (minimally adjusted) was adjusted for age, sex, and fracture type; Model 2 ((moderately adjusted) was further adjusted for ASA grade, CCI, PNI, BMI, and preoperative anemia; and Model 3 (fully adjusted) was adjusted for age, sex, fracture type, ASA grade, PNI, BMI, all comorbidities, preoperative anemia, preoperative pneumonia and preoperative lower extremity venous thrombosis, time to surgery, surgical procedure type, anesthesia type, and transfusion volume on the day of surgery. This model was able to estimate the total association of preoperative hyponatremia with mortality, adjusting for baseline factors only. To explore the potential mediating roles of postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia, we extended the fully adjusted model (Model 4) to include three sensitivity analyses. Model 4a was further adjusted for postoperative complications (composite endpoint) alone. Model 4b was further adjusted for postoperative hyponatremia alone, defined as the lowest serum sodium concentration within 24 h after surgery (< 135 mmol/L). Model 4c was further adjusted for both postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia. These sequential models allowed us to estimate the independent and shared contributions of these two postoperative factors to the association between preoperative hyponatremia and mortality. The results are presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A trend test was performed by treating hyponatremia severity as an ordinal variable as mentioned above. In step 3, to explore whether the association between preoperative hyponatremia and 30-day mortality or 1-year mortality varied across clinically relevant subgroups, we performed post hoc subgroup analyses on the basis of the following baseline characteristics: age (< 80 vs. ≥ 80 years), sex, fracture type (femoral neck vs. trochanteric), surgical procedure (arthroplasty vs. internal fixation), BMI (< 22 vs. ≥ 22 kg/m²), PNI (< 45 vs. ≥ 45), and ASA grade (Ⅰ–Ⅱ vs.Ⅲ–Ⅳ). For these analyses, hyponatremia was analyzed as a binary variable (normonatremia vs. any hyponatremia) to maintain adequate statistical power within each stratum, as the number of patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was limited (n = 52). For subgroup analyses, if the number of events in any stratum was < 10, multivariable adjustment was not performed; only descriptive data are presented. In strata with ≥ 10 events, fully adjusted Cox models (including postoperative hyponatremia and complications) were applied. All the statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 23.0; Chicago, IL, USA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between January 2015 and December 2021, a total of 2,180 elderly patients underwent hip fracture surgery at our institution, of whom 1,901 patients met the inclusion criteria and constituted the final study cohort (Fig. 1). The median patient age was 82 years (IQR 75–86), and 1,294 (68.1%) were female. Among the 1,901 patients, 1658 (87.2%) were normonatremic, 191 (10.0%) had mild hyponatremia, and 52 (2.7%) had moderate-to-severe hyponatremia. The median follow-up time from discharge to either death or the censoring date was 28 months (range 0–66 months).

Flowchart of the study design and prevalence of hyponatremia

Baseline characteristics stratified by sodium status are shown in Table 1. Patients with hyponatremia were slightly older and more likely to be aged ≥ 80 years (normonatremia: 58.9%, mild: 69.1%, moderate-to-severe: 67.3%; P = 0.013). There were no significant differences in sex distribution across groups. The proportion of patients with PNI < 45 increased with hyponatremia severity (normonatremia: 63.9%, mild: 68.6%, moderate-to-severe: 88.5%; P = 0.001), and the proportion of patients with BMI < 22 kg/m² also increased with hyponatremia severity (normonatremia: 35.6%, mild hyponatremia: 41.9%, moderate-to-severe hyponatremia: 50.0%; P = 0.030). Comorbidity profiles were generally similar across the three groups (all P > 0.05). The proportion of patients undergoing blood transfusion (≥ 4 units on the day of surgery) increased progressively with hyponatremia severity (8.0%, 11.0%, and 17.3%, respectively; P = 0.030). Length of hospital stay was also significantly longer in patients with hyponatremia (median 12 days for all groups, but with a wider interquartile range in the mild group; P = 0.002). Other baseline characteristics including type of fracture, anesthesia type and surgical procedure type were well balanced across groups (Table 1). All variables with potential confounding effects were included in the multivariable models.

Perioperative sodium dynamics

The perioperative sodium dynamics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2 and Figure S1. Among patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia on admission, serum sodium levels increased rapidly from a median of 126.1 mmol/L (IQR 121.5–128.0) at admission to 136.8 mmol/L (IQR 133.0–139.0) by postoperative day 1, and further to 139.6 mmol/L (IQR 136.2–141.8) by postoperative day 3. Patients with mild hyponatremia also underwent prompt correction, with the median sodium concentration increasing from 133.0 mmol/L at admission to 137.0 mmol/L on postoperative day 1. In contrast, normonatremic patients maintained stable levels throughout the perioperative period (approximately 139–140 mmol/L). Missing data were more frequent in the normonatremic group (46.7% on day 3, 58.3% on day 5), reflecting earlier discharge in clinically stable patients, which is expected in real-world practice.

Associations between preoperative hyponatremia severity and postoperative complication incidence

Among the 1,901 patients, 314 (18.9%) normonatremic patients, 61 (31.9%) patients with mild hyponatremia, and 24 (46.2%) patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia developed at least one postoperative complication. The distribution of specific complications is presented in Table 2, with postoperative cardiac events, delirium and pneumonia occurring most frequently. As shown in Table 3, the unadjusted odds ratios for overall complications were 2.01 (95% CI 1.45–2.79) for mild hyponatremia and 3.67 (95% CI 2.10–6.42) for moderate-to-severe hyponatremia. After sequential adjustment for potential confounders, these associations remained robust. According to the fully adjusted model (Model 3), mild hyponatremia was associated with a 1.97-fold increased risk (95% CI 1.18–3.20), and moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was associated with a 4.43-fold increased risk (95% CI 2.03–9.65). Adjustment for postoperative hyponatremia (Model 4) did not significantly change these estimates.

When individual complications were examined, preoperative hyponatremia demonstrated a severity-dependent association with both delirium and pneumonia risk (Table 4). Association between preoperative hyponatremia severity and 30-day mortality

During the 30-day post-operative period, 65 (3.9%) normonatremic patients, 9 (4.7%) patients with mild hyponatremia, and 8 (15.4%) patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia died. The unadjusted hazard ratios were 1.21 (95% CI 0.60–2.43) for mild hyponatremia and 3.91 (95% CI 1.88–8.15) for moderate-to-severe hyponatremia (P for trend < 0.001). After sequential adjustment for potential confounders (Model 1–3), moderate-to-severe hyponatremia remained significantly associated with increased mortality, with a hazard ratio of 3.75 (95% CI 1.74–8.08) in the fully adjusted model (Model 3). Mild hyponatremia was not significantly associated across all the models.

To explore the mediating role of postoperative factors, we performed three sensitivity analyses. After adjusting for postoperative complications alone (Model 4a), the hazard ratio for moderate-to-severe hyponatremia decreased to 2.80 (95% CI 1.28–6.15), indicating 25% attenuation. After adjusting for postoperative hyponatremia alone (Model 4b), the hazard ratio decreased to 2.95 (95% CI 1.35–6.45), a 21% attenuation. When both factors were included (Model 4c), the hazard ratio was further attenuated to 2.25 (95% CI 1.10–4.98), representing a 40% total attenuation (Table 5). These findings suggest that both postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia partially mediate the association between severe preoperative hyponatremia and early mortality, with a modest overlap between these pathways.

Association between preoperative hyponatremia severity and 1-year mortality

With respect to 1-year mortality, 225 (13.6%) normonatremic patients, 39 (20.4%) patients with mild hyponatremia, and 15 (28.8%) patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia died. The Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed a progressive decline in 1-year survival with increasing severity of preoperative hyponatremia (log-rank test for trend, P < 0.001). The unadjusted hazard ratios were 1.54 (95% CI 1.09–2.16) for mild hyponatremia and 2.41 (95% CI 1.38–3.92) for moderate-to-severe hyponatremia (P for trend = 0.005). After sequential adjustment for potential confounders (Model 1–3), moderate-to-severe hyponatremia remained significantly associated with increased 1-year mortality, with a hazard ratio of 2.07 (95% CI 1.21–3.53) in the fully adjusted model (Model 3). Mild hyponatremia was not significantly associated across all the models (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis for 30-day mortality and for 1-year mortality

Subgroup analyses were performed to explore whether the association between preoperative hyponatremia incidence and mortality varied across clinically relevant subgroups (Supplementary Tables 4 and Table 5). The results showed no significant effect modification for most subgroups. The only exception was the PNI subgroup for 30-day mortality (P for interaction = 0.030). For 1-year mortality, none of the interaction tests reached statistical significance (all P > 0.05).

With respect to 30-day mortality, owing to the limited number of events (≤ 10) in several subgroups (e.g., patients aged < 80 years, males, and those with PNI ≥ 45), multivariable analysis was not feasible; only descriptive data are presented. For 1-year mortality, where event counts were greater, the adjusted hazard ratios for hyponatremia ranged from 0.98 to 1.93 across the subgroups, with wide confidence intervals in some strata (e.g., age < 80 years: HR 1.93, 95% CI 1.00–3.76). These results should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, given the limited sample size in certain subgroups.

Exploratory analyses: distribution of patients, hyponatremia severity, and outcomes by 10-year age increments

The distribution of patients across 10-year age strata and hyponatremia severity is presented in Supplementary Table 6. Within each age group, the incidence of postoperative complications and 1-year mortality increased progressively with increasing hyponatremia severity. Baseline risk (in normonatremic patients) also increased markedly with advancing age, highlighting the strong prognostic impact of age itself. The impact of hyponatremia appeared most pronounced in patients aged ≥ 90 years, although the number of patients in this subgroup was limited (n = 6). Owing to the small number of events in several subgroups, formal multivariable analysis was not performed.

Discussion

In this large retrospective cohort of 1,901 geriatric patients who underwent hip fracture surgery, we observed that preoperative hyponatremia was independently associated with increased postoperative complications and mortality in a severity-dependent manner. Mild hyponatremia was associated with a 1.97-fold increased risk of complications but did not significantly increase short-term mortality, whereas moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was associated with a 4.43-fold higher complication risk, a 3.75-fold higher 30-day mortality risk and a 2.07-fold higher 1-year mortality risk. Importantly, the association with mortality was partially mediated by postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia. Subgroup analyses revealed consistent effects across most strata, with a trend toward stronger associations in younger patients (< 80 years) for 1-year mortality. A descriptive age-stratified analysis by 10-year increments further illustrated the incremental increases in risk with advancing age and increasing hyponatremia severity.

Hyponatremia as a severity-dependent prognostic marker

Our finding that even mild hyponatremia increases complication risk, while only moderate-to-severe hyponatremia impacts short-term and long-term mortality, underscores the importance of severity stratification. This pattern aligns with prior studies that have shown a dose-response relationship between sodium levels and adverse outcomes in various populations [30]. The threshold effect we observed—where mild hyponatremia did not independently increase 30-day mortality—may explain why some previous studies that treated hyponatremia as a binary variable yielded inconsistent results [19, 31, 32]. From a clinical perspective, our data suggest that patients with mild hyponatremia still warrant heightened vigilance for complications, whereas those with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia require aggressive perioperative optimization to reduce early mortality.

Dual-pathway model: acute versus chronic mechanisms

Our findings suggest a time-dependent dual-pathway model for the impact of preoperative hyponatremia on postoperative mortality. In the acute phase (30-day mortality), the effect of moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was both direct (HR = 2.25, 95% CI 1.10–4.98 after full adjustment) and indirect (40% attenuation after adjusting for postoperative complications and hyponatremia). This suggests that moderate-to-severe hyponatremia directly increases early death risk, while also predisposing to postoperative events that further amplify mortality. In contrast, for long-term mortality (1-year), the direct effect was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for postoperative factors (HR 1.47, 95% CI 0.86–2.54), whereas the total effect was significant (HR 2.07). This indicates that the long-term impact of severe hyponatremia is largely indirect, possibly mediated by its association with postoperative complications and persistent hyponatremia. Consistent with this interpretation, our baseline data showed that patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia had lower PNI and BMI compared with those with normonatremia or mild hyponatremia, reflecting a higher burden of underlying nutritional frailty and comorbidity. Therefore, in the chronic phase, hyponatremia may act more as a marker of underlying vulnerability rather than an independent causal driver. Nevertheless, the attenuation after adjusting for postoperative factors underscores the clinical importance of preventing complications and correcting electrolyte disturbances to improve long-term survival in this vulnerable population.

Our findings are broadly consistent with those of a large-scale study from the Stockholm Sodium cohort focusing on a general inpatient population, which demonstrated a clear severity-dependent increase in 30-day mortality (HR 1.35 to 3.38 ) but only a marginal elevations in 1-year mortality, particularly for mild hyponatremia (HR 1.04) [19]. The authors attributed the increased mortality mainly to residual confounding variables from underlying disease severity. While we acknowledge that residual confounding variables cannot be fully excluded, our mediation analysis goes one step further by quantifying the specific postoperative variables—postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia—that partially explain the early excess risk. Thus, the two studies are complementary: the Stockholm cohort highlights the overall severity- and time-dependent pattern in a general inpatient population, whereas our study provides mechanistic insight within a specific surgical cohort, identifying actionable mediators that could be targeted to reduce early mortality.

Taken together, our mediation analysis supports a dual-pathway model in which the association between preoperative hyponatremia and postoperative mortality is partially mediated by postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia in the acute phase, whereas the long-term effect appears to be largely indirect, driven primarily by postoperative factors and baseline patient characteristics rather than a direct effect of hyponatremia itself. Further prospective validation is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying long-term survival.

Role of specific complications: delirium and pneumonia

Among specific complications, delirium and pneumonia were the most frequent and demonstrated the strongest associations with hyponatremia severity. Mild hyponatremia increased the risk of delirium by 2.28-fold, and moderate-to-severe hyponatremia increased it by 5.80-fold. For pneumonia, only moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was associated with a 3.62-fold greater risk, suggesting a threshold effect. These findings align with the known pathophysiology: hyponatremia can cause cerebral edema and altered mental status, predisposing patients to delirium [33, 34]; it may also impair respiratory muscle function and mucociliary clearance, increasing pneumonia risk [35–37]. Direct evidence from a before-after study in postoperative patients revealed that the use of hypotonic (low-sodium) maintenance fluid was associated with a significantly greater incidence of delirium (26.0%) than the use of isotonic (normal-sodium) fluid (11.7%), supporting the causal role of low serum sodium levels in the development of delirium [19]. Consistent with our results, studies in patients with community-acquired pneumonia have shown that hyponatremia on admission is associated with worse outcomes [35]. Furthermore, a secondary analysis of a randomized trial in pneumonia patients revealed that persistent hyponatremia (low both at admission and at discharge) was associated with a 4-fold increased risk of pneumonia recurrence, whereas patients whose hyponatremia had resolved by discharge had no excess risk [36]. This finding parallels our observation that persistent hyponatremia (low both pre- and postoperatively) carried the highest risk of adverse outcomes.

In contrast, cardiac events were not significantly associated with hyponatremia in our cohort. This finding is consistent with our previous work identifying independent risk factors for postoperative cardiac events in this population, which included advanced age, male sex, a history of arrhythmia, preoperative deep vein thrombosis, higher CCI, and higher ASA classification, but not hyponatremia [27]. Thus, while hyponatremia predisposes to delirium and pneumonia, cardiac complications appear to be driven primarily by other patient-specific factors rather than electrolyte disturbances.

Clinically, these findings highlight that prevention of delirium and pneumonia after hip fracture surgery—through early mobilization, delirium protocols, and respiratory care—should be prioritized in hyponatremic patients, especially those with moderate-to-severe deficits.

Age-related patterns and effect modification

Our descriptive analysis by 10-year age increments revealed a clear progressive increase in both the prevalence of hyponatremia and baseline complication/mortality rates with advancing age. For example, the prevalence of any hyponatremia increased from 8.9% in patients aged 60–69 years to 15.4% in those aged ≥ 90 years. This age-dependent increase in hyponatremia risk is well recognized; elderly patients represent a high-risk group for hyponatremia because age itself is a strong independent risk factor [10, 38] .

Despite this higher prevalence, the relative risk associated with hyponatremia appeared to be stronger in younger elderly patients (< 80 years) for 1-year mortality (HR 2.00 vs. 1.03 in those ≥ 80 years), although the interaction was not statistically significant for 30-day mortality (P = 0.464). This pattern may reflect competing risks: in very old patients (≥ 80 years), baseline mortality is already high, resulting in less incremental risk attributable to hyponatremia; in contrast, younger elderly patients with hyponatremia represent a subgroup with disproportionately elevated risk. Thus, age acts both as a risk factor for developing hyponatremia and as an effect modifier of its prognostic impact.

Clinical implications

Our findings have several practical implications for the perioperative care of elderly hip fracture patients. First, a single admission sodium measurement can effectively stratify patients by risk: those with mild hyponatremia (130–134 mmol/L) are at increased risk for complications but not for short-term mortality, whereas patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia (< 130 mmol/L) have a markedly elevated risk of both complications and early death. This simple, inexpensive parameter could be easily integrated into preoperative risk scores. Second, because postoperative complications—particularly delirium and pneumonia—partially mediate mortality risk, enhanced prevention strategies should be implemented proactively in hyponatremic patients. These include delirium prevention bundles, early mobilization, and aggressive respiratory care, especially for those with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia. Third, although postoperative hyponatremia alone contributed modestly to mortality, its presence was associated with worse outcomes in fully adjusted models. Routine postoperative sodium monitoring and prompt treatment of electrolyte abnormalities may provide additional benefits.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings are consistent with a growing body of evidence that preoperative hyponatremia is an important predictor of poor prognosis after surgery. Previous studies have established this association among major elective procedures such as cardiac surgery [39], head and neck surgery [40], and geriatric orthopedic surgeries including cervical spinal fusion [41], total joint arthroplasty [22], and aseptic revision total shoulder arthroplasty [42]. Specifically in hip fracture patients, our results align with those of Craig et al., who similarly reported a correlation between preoperative hyponatremia and postoperative mortality [18], and with Hagino et al., who reported that hyponatremia was independently associated with in-hospital death (OR 3.64) [20].

However, our study extends the literature in several important ways. First, unlike most previous studies that treated hyponatremia as a binary variable, we performed severity-stratified analyses and demonstrated a clear dose-response relationship: mild hyponatremia significantly increased complication risk but did not affect short-term mortality, whereas moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was associated with both complications and a 4-fold higher 30-day mortality. Second, we conducted a mediation analysis that quantified the contributions of postoperative complications and postoperative hyponatremia to the mortality risk and revealed that these factors together accounted for nearly 40% of the early risk. Third, we provided a time-dependent analysis, showing that the mediation effect is stronger for short-term mortality than for long-term mortality, suggesting that long-term outcomes are more closely tied to baseline patient vulnerability. Fourth, we analyzed specific complications (delirium and pneumonia) with clinically actionable implications, identifying them as the most frequent and most strongly associated outcomes. Finally, our study is among the few to provide detailed descriptive data across 10-year age increments, illustrating the progressive increase in both baseline risk and hyponatremia-associated risk with advancing age.

Taken together, these findings not only confirm the prognostic importance of preoperative hyponatremia in hip fracture patients but also refine our understanding of severity-dependent effects, mediating pathways, and time-dependent mechanisms, thereby offering a more nuanced framework for risk stratification and perioperative management.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which may limit generalizability and is subject to residual confounding variables. Second, the number of patients with moderate-to-severe hyponatremia was small (n = 52), which limited the statistical power for some subgroup and mediation analyses. Third, we lacked detailed data on the etiology of hyponatremia (e.g., SIADH vs. hypovolemia, or medication-related causes) and on preoperative correction, preventing us from distinguishing acute from chronic hyponatremia. Fourth, despite extensive adjustment, unmeasured confounders (e.g., medication use and frailty indices, detailed intraoperative hemodynamic data, other diseases and disease severity not listed in this study) may remain. Fifth, postoperative sodium concentration within 24 h was used as a proxy for correction; more granular time-stamped data would be needed to fully capture dynamic changes.

Prospective multicenter studies with larger numbers of patients with severe hyponatremia are needed to validate our findings and to determine whether active correction of hyponatremia (e.g., with hypertonic saline, tolvaptan, or rapid discontinuation of culprit medications) improves outcomes. Such studies should also incorporate comprehensive frailty assessments and detailed cause-of-death adjudication to further elucidate the mechanisms linking hyponatremia to mortality.

Conclusions

Preoperative hyponatremia is a strong, severity-dependent risk factor for postoperative complications and mortality in elderly hip fracture patients. Mild hyponatremia increases complication risk but not short-term mortality; moderate-to-severe hyponatremia markedly elevates both. Postoperative complications and persistent hyponatremia partially mediate early mortality risk. Routine admission sodium measurement can facilitate risk stratification. Targeted prevention of complications and timely correction of electrolyte disturbances may represent potential strategies to improve early outcomes. The potential impact on long-term survival, however, would likely be indirect and requires further prospective validation.
