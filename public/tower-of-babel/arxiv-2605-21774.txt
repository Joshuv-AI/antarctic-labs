Conductivity of a Non-Galilean–Invariant Fermi Liquid: Exact Solution of the Kinetic Equation
Tatia Kiliptari, Vladimir I. Yudson, Dmitrii L. Maslov

Abstract
We obtain an exact expression for the conductivity of a non-Galilean-invariant Fermi liquid with impurities by solving the kinetic equation with both screened Coulomb and z = 3 z=3 Pomeranchuk critical interactions. While consistent with previous asymptotic results, our solution shows that electron-electron interactions enter the conductivity solely via the quasiparticle scattering time, τ ee \tau_{\mathrm{ee}} . Accordingly, the crossovers between the collisionless and hydrodynamic regimes occur when 1 / τ ee 1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}} becomes comparable to the larger of the impurity scattering rate and the probe frequency, Ω \Omega . In addition, the exact solution yields the optical response in the hydrodynamic regime, Ω ≪ 1 / τ ee \Omega\ll 1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}} , which is inaccessible within perturbation theory. Near a z = 3 z=3 Pomeranchuk quantum critical point, consistency between the kinetic-equation and Kubo approaches requires proper inclusion of mass renormalization within the Eliashberg approximation, which also ensures that the crossover between the collisionless and hydrodynamic regimes in the optical conductivity occurs at the Planckian scale Ω ∼ T \Omega\sim T .



Introduction.

The temperature (
T
) and frequency (
\Omega
) dependencies of the electronic conductivity provide key insights into the scattering mechanisms that govern electron dynamics.
Traditionally, a Fermi liquid (FL) is expected to exhibit a
T^{2}

dc
resistivity and Gurzhi-like optical conductivity,
\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma(\Omega,T)\propto(\Omega^{2}+4\pi^{2}T^{2})/\Omega^{2}

12
,
while the deviations are
interpreted as signatures of non-FL physics.
However, the
T^{2}
resistivity requires either umklapp scattering
18
or compensated electron-hole
pockets
3
.
If these conditions are not met, normal electron-electron (
ee
) collisions still
affect the conductivity of a non-Galilean-invariant FL,
provided that momentum is relaxed by another mechanism, e.g., impurity scattering.
However, the
ee
contribution to the conductivity exhibits non-canonical scaling with
T
and
\Omega
.

In the low-temperature limit, the dc resistivity of a
isotropic, non-Galilean–invariant FL with impurities is given by

\rho(T)=\rho_{\rm i}+\delta\rho(T)
,
where
\rho_{\rm i}
is the residual resistivity and

\delta\rho(T)\propto T^{4}
(up to a logarithmic factor in two dimensions)
is independent of impurity scattering

;
28
;
17

Matthiessen’s rule and thus suggests a suppressed
“current relaxation rate” due to
ee
interactions,
1/\tau_{J}\propto T^{4}\ll 1/\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}\propto T^{2}
, where
\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
is the quasiparticle scattering time.
The collisionless limit of the optical conductivity

\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma(\Omega,T)\propto\max\{\Omega^{4},T^{4}\}/\Omega^{2}

31
;
32
;
33
;
11
,
reinforces this interpretation: viewed as a Drude tail,
it implies that

1/\tau_{J}\propto\max\{\Omega^{4},T^{4}\}
.
The same interpretation would place the low-
T
/high-
T
crossover at

\delta\rho(T)\sim\rho_{\rm i}
,
implying an
\mathcal{O}(1)

ee
contribution to the resistivity, large enough to attribute sizable experimental variations of
\rho
to normal
ee
collisions.

In this Letter, we obtain an exact result for the conductivity by solving the kinetic equation both at zero and finite
\Omega
. It
shows that the low-
T

T
crossover in the
dc

\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}/\tau_{\rm i}
and
\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}
, respectively, where
\tau_{\rm i}
is the impurity scattering time. Therefore, interpreting the previously known asymptotic results in terms of a current relaxation rate is misleading. In particular, in the
dc

\delta\rho(T)\ll\rho_{\rm i}
throughout the temperature range of the Fermi-liquid regime.

Model.

As a concrete realization of a non-Galilean–invariant FL, we consider a 2D system with a linear dispersion and an energy-independent

\tau_{i}
. While the linear dispersion is just a simple example of a non-parabolic isotropic band, the same analysis applies to a generic isotropic dispersion, with the magnitude of the effect controlled by the degree of non-parabolicity. We also employ two models of the
ee
interaction.

The first model is the screened Coulomb (C) interaction,
U_{\mathrm{C}}(q,\omega)
,
treated within the random-phase approximation (RPA). We assume weak coupling, which justifies the RPA treatment and, at the level of the kinetic equation, allows us to neglect Fermi-liquid renormalizations of the effective mass and the deviation of the Z-factor from unity.
We focus on the regime of
\mathcal{E}\equiv\max\{|\Omega,T\}\ll\Omega_{\rm p}\lesssim\varepsilon_{\rm F}
, where

\Omega_{\mathrm{p}}=v_{\mathrm{F}}\kappa
is the characteristic plasma frequency,
v_{\mathrm{F}}
is the Fermi velocity,
\kappa
is the screening wavevector, and
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
is the Fermi energy; in this regime screening is effectively static. The extension to dynamic screening does not modify the main conclusions and will be discussed elsewhere.

The second model is a
FL near the
z=3
quantum critical point (QCP), with the effective
Hertz-Millis (HM)
ee
interaction

13
;
25
,

U_{\rm HM}(q,\omega)=\frac{g}{q^{2}+\xi^{-2}-i\alpha\omega/q},\quad\alpha=g\nu_{\rm F}/v_{\rm F},

(1)

where

\xi
is the correlation length of the order parameter and
\nu_{\mathrm{F}}
is the density of states. For simplicity, we assume that the instability is in the
\ell=0
channel. The FL regime corresponds to

\mathcal{E}\ll\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{FL}}=1/\alpha\xi^{3}
. We adopt the Eliashberg approximation, i.e., we assume that in the FL regime the self-energy
\Sigma^{R}
is local
5
and vertex corrections are small.
In contrast to the Coulomb case, the FL renormalizations cannot be neglected close to the QCP.

Main results: General.

For the two models specified above, we solve the kinetic equation (KE)
exactly–by mapping
it onto an inhomogeneous Legendre-type differential equation.
The resulting conductivity is given by

\displaystyle\sigma(\Omega,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{i}}(\Omega)+\sigma_{\mathrm{ee}}(\Omega,T),

(2a)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{ee}}(\Omega,T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{2}\varepsilon_{\rm F}\tau_{\mathrm{i}}(\Omega)\Phi\left(\Omega,T\right)\mathcal{F}\!\left(\frac{\tau_{\rm ee}(\Omega,T)}{\tau_{\mathrm{i}}(\Omega)}\right),

(2b)

where
\tau_{\mathrm{i}}(\Omega)=\tau_{\mathrm{i}}/(1-i\Omega\tau_{\mathrm{i}})

the residual conductivity,

\nu_{F}=\varepsilon_{\rm F}/2\pi v_{F}^{2}
is the density of states per spin,

\Phi(\Omega,T)=(T^{2}+\Omega^{2}/4\pi^{2})/\varepsilon_{\rm F}^{2}

The quasiparticle scattering rate,

,
related to
the imaginary part of the on-shell
self-energy, is given by

\tau_{\mathrm{ee}}^{-1}=a_{\rm M}\mathcal{E}^{2}L_{\rm M}(\mathcal{E}),

where
{\rm M}={\rm C,HM}
indicates the model and

L_{\rm M}(\mathcal{E})\equiv\ln\left(\Lambda_{\rm M}/\mathcal{E}\right)
.
Whether the FL parameters
are taken as bare or renormalized also depends on the model, i.e., C vs. HM.
The function
\mathcal{F}(z)
is expressed in terms of the associated Legendre functions, with the following asymptotic limits
\mathcal{F}(|x|\gg 1)=-4\pi/15x
and
\mathcal{F}(|x|\ll 1)=-\pi/6+\pi x/8
.

Both the

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{v}=\bm{v}_{\mathbf{k}}+\bm{v}_{\mathbf{p}}-\bm{v}_{\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{q}}-\bm{v}_{\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}}.

(3)

For a general isotropic, non-parabolic dispersion,

The leading FS contribution therefore cancels, and the conductivity is controlled by states close to, but not exactly on, the FS. Thermal smearing then supplies a factor

For the optical conductivity,

\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma_{\mathrm{ee}}(\Omega\to 0^{+},T)\sim e^{2}T^{2}/\gamma_{T}\varepsilon_{\rm F}

\Omega\ll\gamma_{T}

(In a gapless Dirac system, interband electron-hole processes generate an additional
contribution to the optical conductivity in the hydrodynamic limit

The semiclassical KE,

Origin of the suppressed current relaxation in isotropic FLs.

For an isotropic FL, the suppression arises because the current density for an electron with energy
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}
relative to
\varepsilon_{\rm F}
can be decomposed into the conserved part, proportional to the momentum, and the non-conserved part, proportional to the thermal current:

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}=-e\bm{v}_{\mathbf{k}}=-ev_{\mathrm{F}}\frac{\mathbf{k}}{k}-e\frac{v_{\mathrm{F}}^{\prime}}{v_{\mathrm{F}}}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\mathbf{k}}{k},

(EM1)

where
v_{\mathrm{F}}^{\prime}=\partial_{k}v(k)|_{k=k_{\rm F}}
.
Because the correlation function of the non-conserved part contains a factor of
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}
, the current relaxation is suppressed.

We stress that this mechanism is different from the one operative in a 2D FL, where odd angular harmonics of the distribution function relax as
T^{4}

19
.
The latter effect describes free decay rather than driven transport. In the driven case,
the field-induced nonequilibrium distribution has nontrivial energy dependence.
A clear signature of the difference between the two mechanisms is that the Sommerfeld-like suppression occurs in both 2D and 3D
33
;
11
.
