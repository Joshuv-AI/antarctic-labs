Machine learning-based prediction of shigellosis in children under five: development and internal validation of a prediction model
Authors: Md Fuad Al Fidah, Md Ridwan Islam, ASG Faruque, Sharika Nuzhat, Mustafa Mahfuz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesShigella remains a major cause of diarrhoea and mortality in children under five in low- and middle-income countries, where laboratory confirmation is often inaccessible and dysentery-based management lacks sensitivity. This study aimed to develop and internally evaluate machine learning models to predict microbiologically confirmed Shigella infection and secondarily to demonstrate the feasibility of translating the best-performing model into a prototype web-based decision-support application.MethodsWe analysed data from 3356 children with diarrhoea enrolled in the Global Enteric Multicentre Study, excluding co-infections and non-diarrhoeal controls. Multiple machine learning algorithms were trained using 32 predictors and evaluated with 10-fold cross-validation. Performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), recall and Brier score, with recall prioritised to minimise false negatives. The selected model was simplified using the 10 most informative predictors and integrated into a prototype web-based application. All analyses were restricted to internal validation.ResultsSupport vector machine (SVM) demonstrated the highest recall (0.64) with modest but potentially useful discrimination (AUC 0.74). The parsimonious SVM model achieved recall 0.67, AUC 0.77 and Brier score 0.16. A web-based prototype was developed to illustrate real-time model outputs for research purposes.DiscussionThe model achieved moderate internal performance while prioritising recall, supporting its potential role as a research-stage risk stratification tool in settings with limited diagnostic capacity.ConclusionThis internally validated model shows potential for supporting risk stratification for shigellosis. External validation and impact evaluation are required before clinical or operational use.Keywords: Machine Learning; Artificial intelligence; Data Science; Decision Making, Computer-Assisted; Public health informatics

Introduction

Diarrhoeal diseases remain major causes of morbidity and mortality among children under 5 years of age globally.1 In 2018, more than 1.6 million child deaths were attributed to diarrhoeal diseases.2 In high-burden settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), children may experience up to 12 diarrhoeal episodes annually.2 Limited healthcare access, inadequate sanitation and unsafe water further increase disease risk and severity.3

Although rotavirus accounts for most diarrhoeal episodes and is typically self-limiting,4 bacterial pathogens also contribute substantially. Among them, Shigella sp are major causes of childhood diarrhoeal mortality and moderate-to-severe diarrhoea (MSD).5–7 Recent estimates from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 attributed approximately 81 800 diarrhoeal deaths globally to Shigella spp, including nearly a quarter of fatal diarrhoeal attributable fractions among children under five.5 Shigella is frequently identified either alone or alongside rotavirus in paediatric diarrhoeal cases,6 7 and children aged 24–59 months with MSD have substantially higher odds of Shigella attribution than attribution to other pathogens.8 Reported incidence varies widely across settings, ranging from 5.4 cases per 100 child-years in South Africa to 75.1 cases per 100 child-years in Bangladesh.9

The WHO recommends antibiotic treatment for children presenting with dysentery, characterised by visible blood in stool, as a proxy for Shigella infection.10 However, up to 90% of shigellosis cases may present without visible blood, and therefore remain underdiagnosed or undertreated.11 In practice, antibiotics are also prescribed presumptively for suspected shigellosis, particularly among malnourished children, even in the absence of dysentery.12 Inappropriate antibiotic use may increase costs, adverse effects and antimicrobial resistance.13 Accurate identification of Shigella infection is therefore important for antimicrobial stewardship. Traditional laboratory diagnostics, including stool culture and PCR, are often inaccessible in resource-limited settings because of cost, infrastructure and time constraints.13 Although newer approaches such as immunochromatographic dipsticks and loop-mediated isothermal amplification show promise, widespread implementation in LMICs remains uncertain.13 Consequently, alternative low-cost approaches for identifying probable Shigella infection may be valuable in resource-limited settings.

Recent advancements in machine learning offer promising avenues for developing predictive models that use readily available clinical and demographic data to estimate the likelihood of Shigella infection. For instance, a clinical prediction rule has been developed incorporating age and caregiver-reported bloody diarrhoea.13 Similarly, models using limited clinical variables have been shown to effectively predict viral-only diarrhoeal aetiology, aiding in the reduction of unnecessary antibiotic use.3

The Global Enteric Multicentre Study (GEMS) was a large multicountry study conducted across sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia to investigate the causes and burden of moderate-to-severe childhood diarrhoeal disease.14–16 This study aimed to train and evaluate multiple machine learning algorithms for predicting microbiologically confirmed Shigella infection in children under 5 (U5C), using data from the GEMS to identify the most accurate model. Furthermore, this research also developed a web-based application to enable real-time use of the best-performing model in clinical and public health settings.

Methods

Data overview

The current study used data from the GEMS. The details of the GEMS have been published elsewhere.14–16 The data were collected from https://clinepidb.org/, an open-access online resource for clinical and epidemiologic studies, after signing up and receiving necessary permission.17 The Global Enteric Multicenter Study 1A (GEMS-1A) was a prospective, matched case–control study conducted between October 2011 and January 2013 at seven sites in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where child mortality rates were moderate to high. The aim was to assess the burden of pathogens and health outcomes related to MSD and less-severe diarrhoea in children aged 0–59 months. Children were selected from a demographic surveillance system and enrolled at designated health centres. The cases were children with new and acute diarrhoea. For each case, controls without diarrhoea in the preceding 7 days were selected and matched based on age, sex and where they lived. For our study, we considered 3356 U5C children. Children without diarrhoea were excluded because the prediction target was Shigella-attributed diarrhoea among children presenting with diarrhoea rather than diarrhoeal illness itself. Children with diarrhoea attributable to multiple pathogens were also excluded to improve attribution of Shigella-associated disease. Thus, the study population consisted of children under five with diarrhoea (figure 1).

Selection of study participants.

Although non-diarrhoeal controls from the original matched case–control GEMS-1A design were excluded and the present analysis was restricted to children with diarrhoeal disease, the underlying sampling framework may still influence prevalence-dependent performance measures and limit direct estimation of real-world diagnostic performance. Accordingly, the present analysis should be interpreted as an internally validated modelling exercise rather than a prospective diagnostic accuracy study.

Operational definition of the dependent variable

Shigellosis

The current study considered stool samples positive for any Shigella sp or serotype identified through bacteriological methods as Shigella-positive and recorded as ‘present’; otherwise, they were categorised as ‘absent’. Microbiological confirmation relied on stool culture methods available within GEMS-1A. As stool culture has imperfect sensitivity for detecting Shigella, some true infections may have been misclassified as negative.

Independent variables

Several independent candidate variables were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, prior literature on paediatric diarrhoeal diseases and shigellosis and variable availability within the GEMS dataset (online supplemental table 1).2 18–21 An initially broad set of predictors was retained during model development to allow machine learning algorithms to identify potentially informative patterns and interactions. To reduce model complexity and improve generalisability, feature importance rankings were subsequently used to develop a parsimonious model containing the 10 most influential predictors.

Statistical analysis

Data processing and analysis were performed using Jamovi (V.2.6.1), R (V.4.4.3) and RStudio (V.2024.12.1), with the aid of R packages including caret, ROSE and jmvReadWrite. For machine learning analyses, cases with missing data on outcomes or predictors were excluded, yielding 3199 observations. For descriptive comparison between Shigella-positive and non-Shigella diarrhoeal cases, categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests, while continuous variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Class balancing

The outcome distribution was highly imbalanced (6.14% present vs 93.86% absent). To address this, random minority-class oversampling using the Random Over-Sampling Examples (ROSE) package was applied exclusively within the training dataset after splitting the data into training and testing sets (8:2 ratio). The independent test dataset remained unchanged and was reserved for final model evaluation to minimise data leakage. Tenfold cross-validation was performed within the training dataset during model development. To reduce overfitting, models were additionally evaluated using a holdout test set and parsimonious retraining with the most influential predictors. All performance metrics were calculated using the original, non-oversampled test data.

Model training and performance

Multiple machine learning algorithms representing diverse modelling approaches were trained and compared with traditional logistic regression models to evaluate predictive performance across different modelling frameworks. These included regularised regression techniques, namely Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), ridge regression and elastic net (EL); decision tree-based classifiers; ensemble methods including random forest (RF) and gradient boosting (GB); and other classifiers such as k-nearest neighbours (KNN) and support vector machines (SVM) with a linear kernel. Model training employed 10-fold cross-validation, while model performance was assessed on the testing set using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), recall and Brier score. Recall quantifies the model’s capacity to correctly identify true positives.22 AUC evaluates the model’s discriminatory ability across all classification thresholds.23 Finally, the Brier score measures the accuracy and calibration of probabilistic predictions, with lower values indicating superior performance.24 Brier score was included as a supplementary measure of probabilistic prediction error and interpreted comparatively across models because absolute values are influenced by outcome prevalence. Exploratory internal calibration assessment of the parsimonious SVM model was additionally performed using logistic recalibration and calibration plotting.

Recall was prioritised because missed cases of shigellosis in young children may delay appropriate management and increase the risk of complications in high-burden settings. In resource-limited contexts, such tools are intended to support risk stratification and reduce false negatives rather than replace confirmatory diagnostics. However, prioritising recall increases the likelihood of false-positive predictions and potential unnecessary antibiotic exposure. Accordingly, model outputs should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive diagnostic assessments. We additionally reported 95% CIs for AUC and recall to assess uncertainty in performance estimates.

Development of a web-based application

We integrated machine learning techniques with an interactive web application to promote broad accessibility. Several steps were taken to ensure stability and accuracy before deployment. The detailed flowchart of the process is presented in figure 2.

To improve interpretability and reduce computational complexity, feature importance rankings were calculated and the 10 most influential variables retained for model retraining. For the linear SVM model, variable importance was estimated using the varImp() function in the caret package based on the absolute magnitude of model coefficients. The resulting parsimonious model was incorporated into a prototype web application developed using the Shiny package in R and hosted on the shinyapps.io platform for preliminary demonstration and internal testing. The application outputs the predicted probability of Shigella infection as a continuous percentage value accompanied by a graphical probability bar. The prototype does not provide uncertainty estimates for individual predictions and is not intended for clinical or operational use without external validation.

Results

The current study analysed 3356 participants, of whom 6.1% (n=206) was diagnosed with shigellosis. Participant selection is presented in figure 1. Children with shigellosis were older than non-shigellosis cases (median (IQR): 21 (14–29) vs 13 (7–23) months). Blood in stool was more common among shigellosis cases (30.6% vs 5.6%), alongside higher axillary temperature (median (IQR): 37.0 (36.7–38.0)°C vs 36.8 (36.4–37.1)°C). Abdominal pain (54.6% vs 30.0%) and rectal straining (35.4% vs 11.2%) were also more frequently reported among children with shigellosis (online supplemental table 2).

Multiple machine learning models were trained using 32 predictors (n=3199 complete cases). RF and GB achieved the highest discrimination (AUC 0.79 each) but showed limited recall (0.15 and 0.39, respectively). SVM demonstrated the highest recall (0.64) with moderate discrimination (AUC 0.74). LASSO and EL showed balanced performance (AUC 0.76; recall 0.59), and Brier scores were similar across models. Precision values were generally modest across models. Given the prioritisation of recall, SVM was selected for further development (table 1).

Discussion

This analysis used data from GEMS, a matched case–control study rather than a prospective diagnostic cohort. Although restricted to children with diarrhoea, the underlying sampling design may influence predictive performance, and models developed using case–control data can produce optimistic estimates of discrimination and sensitivity. Accordingly, reported recall and AUC reflect internal performance rather than real-world diagnostic accuracy. Prospective evaluation or internal–external validation across sites is required to assess transportability and clinical utility.

In this study, we explored the potential of machine learning approaches in predicting shigellosis among U5C. Performance metrics, specifically recall, indicate that SVM is the best-performing model. After extracting the variable importance, we identified the top 10 predictors and retrained the model using these predictors. Finally, a parsimonious SVM model for predicting the probability of shigellosis was developed and deployed as a preliminary web-based application.25

Machine learning can identify patterns in large and complex clinical datasets, enabling predictions that are challenging for humans to derive unaided.26 Unlike traditional rule-based systems, machine learning learns from real examples, which makes it useful for solving complicated medical problems.26 Several performance metrics are commonly used to evaluate the predictive capability of the models, namely AUC, recall, precision and Brier score. The current study prioritised recall while considering the AUC and the Brier score. Recall, also referred to as sensitivity, reflects the model’s ability to correctly identify positive cases; higher values indicate better performance in detecting true positives. It is regarded as one of the most important metrics in the medical field, as the goal is to include as many positive cases as possible.22 AUC indicates the likelihood that the model assigns a higher probability to true cases than to non-cases.23 Finally, the Brier score measures how accurate the predicted probabilities are. It looks at how well the model separates the cases and how close the predictions are to what actually happened. Lower Brier scores indicate lower overall probabilistic prediction error, although interpretation may be influenced by outcome prevalence and probability calibration.27 Although Brier score provides a global measure of probabilistic prediction error, interpretation of absolute values may be influenced by outcome prevalence and calibration characteristics.27

The machine learning models showed relatively similar performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.61 for KNN to 0.79 for RF and gradient boosting tree (GBT), which generally represents fair performance (AUC: 0.70–0.79). However, the highest recall was observed with SVM, which achieved 0.64, while also maintaining a fair AUC of 0.74. The moderate discrimination observed across models likely reflects the substantial clinical overlap between enteric pathogens, heterogeneity of paediatric diarrhoeal presentations and limitations of routinely available clinical and demographic predictors. In contrast, although RF and GBT had the highest AUC values, they performed poorly in recall, which was only 0.15 and 0.39, respectively. The Brier scores were similar across models, except for GBT, which had a lower score (0.09) compared with others (range: 0.16–0.19). Considering the comparable Brier scores of RF, GBT and SVM, and the substantially higher recall achieved by SVM, it was selected for further development despite slightly lower AUC. The parsimonious SVM model with the 10 most influential predictors achieved an AUC of 0.77, recall of 0.67 and precision of 0.22. Precision values were modest across models, reflecting the low prevalence of shigellosis within the dataset. Although raw probability calibration remained imperfect, exploratory internal recalibration analyses suggested potential improvement in calibration performance; however, these findings require external validation.

Out of the 32 candidate predictors, many represented routinely collected clinical or household variables commonly available in healthcare or demographic surveillance settings. A parsimonious model containing the 10 most influential predictors was subsequently developed to improve interpretability and potential operational feasibility in LMICs. The child’s age was a strong predictor of shigellosis, followed by blood in stool, fever (ie, axillary temperature), abdominal pain, rectal straining and consumption of stored water. Compared with the strong predictors, the number of other U5C in the household, overcrowding and child suffering from underweight or wasting were relatively less important. As the next step, a parsimonious SVM model was retrained using only the 10 most influential predictors. The model also preserved much of the predictive accuracy while enhancing usability and interpretability (AUC: 0.77; recall: 0.67; Brier score: 0.16). The performance of our models aligns closely with existing literature on machine learning-based prediction of diarrhoeal aetiologies. For example, a simple clinical prediction rule incorporating age and caregiver-reported dysentery achieved an AUC of 0.80 in both derivation and external validation datasets,13 performance comparable to that of our model. Similarly, machine learning techniques have been successfully applied to predict viral-only aetiologies of diarrhoea, emphasising the utility of such models in reducing unnecessary antibiotic usage.3 Our study contributes significantly to this regard as the deployed model, if validated, can decrease the risk of antibiotic overuse and antimicrobial resistance. In potential implementation settings, the tool could support the initial assessment of children presenting with diarrhoea in resource-limited healthcare facilities or humanitarian contexts. Intended users may include frontline clinicians or trained health workers, with model outputs used alongside local treatment guidelines and clinical judgement. High-risk predictions could prompt closer clinical evaluation or prioritisation for diagnostic testing where available. However, machine learning-based decision-support tools for antibiotic prescribing raise important ethical considerations, including incorrect predictions, over-reliance on algorithmic outputs and inequitable performance across populations. Accordingly, such tools should complement, rather than replace, clinical judgement and require rigorous external validation and implementation evaluation before routine use.

A notable strength of this study is the translation of predictive modelling into an accessible, user-friendly web-based application.25 Securely hosted via shinyapps.io, the application enables healthcare providers and researchers to input commonly available clinical and demographic variables to estimate, in real-time, the probability of Shigella infection. By facilitating rapid and informed decision-making at the point of care, this tool may help address diagnostic gaps, particularly in resource-limited settings. Moving forward, external validation and usability testing are planned prior to deployment in routine clinical practice.

This study has several strengths. By using the diverse GEMS dataset, which spans sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, the findings may support broader applicability across LMIC contexts. The model benefits from varied sociodemographic and clinical data, while issues of class imbalance were addressed using minority-class oversampling to improve sensitivity. Cross-validation further reduced the risk of overfitting. Importantly, a web-based application was developed to bring predictive modelling into clinical practice, offering timely diagnostic support to healthcare providers in LMICs. However, several limitations should be noted. Children with diarrhoea attributable to multiple pathogens were excluded to improve internal validity and outcome attribution; however, this may limit generalisability, as coinfections are common in routine clinical settings. Stool culture has imperfect sensitivity for detecting Shigella infection, and some true infections may therefore have been misclassified as negative. Although the GEMS dataset included multiple international sites, no site-wise internal-external validation was performed, and predictive performance may differ across epidemiological, demographic and healthcare settings. In addition, because the GEMS dataset was collected between 2011 and 2013, changes in pathogen epidemiology, antimicrobial resistance patterns, vaccination coverage and healthcare access may limit contemporary applicability. The model may also perform differently in children with clinical conditions or presentations insufficiently represented within the training dataset. The model was internally validated only and relied on routinely collected clinical variables that may be inconsistently measured. Although recall was prioritised to minimise missed Shigella cases, the relatively low outcome prevalence resulted in modest precision and an increased likelihood of false-positive predictions. The relatively small number of positive Shigella cases compared with the number of candidate predictors may also increase the risk of overfitting and optimistic performance estimates. Furthermore, because the held-out test set contributed to comparative model selection and evaluation of the parsimonious model, some degree of optimistic bias remains possible. We restricted minority-class oversampling to the training dataset to minimise data leakage. However, it may still have contributed to optimistic recall estimates. Finally, the accompanying web-based application is a research prototype without uncertainty estimation for individual predictions and should not be used for clinical decision-making without external validation, calibration assessment, usability testing and evaluation of clinical impact.

Conclusion

This study describes the development and internal evaluation of machine learning models for predicting microbiologically confirmed Shigella infection among U5C using multicountry data. The selected model demonstrated modest but potentially useful discrimination while prioritising recall, aligning with its intended role as a risk stratification tool in settings with limited diagnostic capacity. The accompanying web-based application is a research prototype designed to illustrate model outputs rather than to support clinical deployment. External validation, calibration assessment, usability testing and evaluation of clinical impact are required before consideration of use in routine care. Future work should also explore model performance across diverse settings and in the presence of mixed aetiologies to enhance generalisability.
