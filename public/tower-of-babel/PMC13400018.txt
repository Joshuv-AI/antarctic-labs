ABSTRACT
Among women tested for vaginitis, bacterial vaginosis is independently associated with increased risk of nonviral sexually transmitted infections coinfection, whereas vulvovaginal candidiasis is not.

Vaginitis is among the most frequent gynecologic diagnoses, accounting for a significant proportion of ambulatory visits in reproductive-aged women worldwide.1,2 The principal causes include bacterial vaginosis (BV), vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), and trichomoniasis (Trichomonas vaginalis), each with distinct microbial profiles yet overlapping symptoms such as vaginal discharge, itching, burning, inflammation, odor, and pain during urination or sex, which often complicate diagnosis and management.2–4 According to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists Practice Bulletin No. 215, BV accounts for up to half, VVC for approximately one-third, and T vaginalis for up to one-quarter of all vaginitis cases, with 7–72% of symptomatic women remaining undiagnosed after routine evaluation.5

Beyond their local effects, disturbances in the vaginal microbiota and mucosal integrity that occur with BV also can confer increased susceptibility to sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and T vaginalis.6,7 These infections are associated with adverse reproductive health outcomes, including pelvic inflammatory disease, preterm birth, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) acquisition. The complex interplay between commensal, opportunistic, and pathogenic organisms underscores the importance of diagnostic precision in identifying both the underlying cause of vaginitis and potential concurrent infections.

Traditional diagnostic methods such as the Amsel clinical criteria, Nugent scoring, wet-mount microscopy, and pH assessment for rapid, point-of-care diagnosis for vaginitis are inherently limited by observer variability, suboptimal sensitivity, and not always being able to be performed in the clinic.8 Recent technologic advances, including molecular nucleic acid amplification tests, have substantially improved diagnostic accuracy.8 For instance, Schwebke et al9 demonstrated in a prospective multicenter trial that molecular assays achieve sensitivities exceeding 95% for BV, Candida spp., and T vaginalis, outperforming conventional microscopy while allowing simultaneous pathogen detection from a single specimen. Such diagnostic advances suggest an evolving paradigm toward objective, reproducible, and multiplexed analytic tools that can detect both vaginitis and STI pathogens from a single specimen.

Despite these innovations, the prevalence and clinical relevance of concurrent STI detection in patients presenting with vaginitis symptoms remain underexplored, particularly in the context of contemporary molecular testing. Increasing evidence demonstrates that alterations in the vaginal microbiome not only drive the development of vaginitis but also modulate susceptibility to STIs, including C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, and T vaginalis. Mechanistically, the depletion of Lactobacillus species and the overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria such as Gardnerella vaginalis, Prevotella, and Atopobium vaginae may impair mucosal immunity, disrupt epithelial integrity, and promote viral and bacterial adhesion and invasion.10 Consequently, women with BV or other forms of dysbiosis have been shown to exhibit up to a twofold increased risk of acquiring or transmitting chlamydial and gonococcal infections.10,11

Although the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists practice bulletin (2020) recommends simultaneous evaluation for common STIs when BV or T vaginalis is diagnosed,5 these guidelines are based largely on data predating widespread adoption of nucleic acid amplification test–based diagnostics, which offer more streamlined testing with improved sensitivity. When clinically indicated, modern multiplex molecular assays now allow comprehensive pathogen detection with higher accuracy and lower operator variability.

Accordingly, the objective of this study is to evaluate the prevalence and risk of concurrent STIs among women who were tested at a reference laboratory for BV or VVC and underwent testing for concurrent STIs, specifically C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, T vaginalis, and Mycoplasma genitalium. By integrating modern molecular diagnostic methods, this investigation seeks to refine understanding rates of coinfection, to enhance diagnostic efficiency, and to contribute to evidence-based guidelines for comprehensive vaginal health assessment.

METHODS

In this retrospective study, testing data were obtained for more than 1.5 million women who underwent laboratory nucleic acid amplification test testing at a U.S. national reference laboratory for BV, VVC, C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, or T vaginalis between 2022 and 2024. Among them, 2.4% of women also underwent additional testing for M genitalium. The study population included women over 15 years of age with only one BV test performed during 2022–2024; those who had any BV testing in the prior 12 months (2021) or had more than one BV test during 2022–2024 were excluded. Laboratory testing for each analyte was performed on the Hologic Panther platform using transcription-mediated amplification with the corresponding U.S. Food and Drug Administration–cleared Aptima testing kit. Specimens collected in liquid Pap vials were excluded from the analysis. Testing for each analyte was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Positives were defined by the Panther System software as outlined in the manufacturer’s package insert for each assay, based on the output of the instrumentation in relative light units or fluorescence emergence, and if the analytes tested met the relative light units or the fluorescence criteria to be interpreted as positive or negative.

Testing for VVC, C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, T vaginalis, or M genitalium within 7 days of a BV result were considered a testing event. If multiple tests for the same analyte were performed within 7 days of each other, only one result was included in the analysis. In cases of discordant results within a testing event, the positive was selected over the negative result.

Data were deidentified, and demographic data, including age and state of residence for categorizing U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (DHHS) regions,12 were extracted and included in analysis. Data were stratified by age and DHHS region.

This study followed the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline for cross-sectional studies,13 and it was deemed exempt by the WCG IRB based on federal regulation 45 CFR Parts 46, 160, and 164. Informed consent was not obtained because the data were analyzed anonymously.

The BV and VVC positivity rates with 95% CIs were calculated for the study population and across different testing years and patient characteristics. Co-STI positivity rates (95% CIs) were estimated for women with positive BV and positive VVC (BV+/VVC+), positive BV and negative VVC (BV+/VVC−), negative BV and positive VVC (BV−/VVC+), and negative BV and negative VVC (BV−/VVC−). Rates were first calculated for 1) those tested for C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, and T vaginalis and then for 2) those who underwent additional testing for M genitalium. Furthermore, the percentage of co-STIs, including C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, T vaginalis, and M genitalium, was compared based on the BV result only with χ2 tests. Lastly, multivariate logistic regression was used to compare the odds of having an STI between women with positive BV results and those with negative BV results after adjustment for VVC results and patient characteristics (age group and DHHS regions). Data analyses were performed with SAS Studio 3.6 on SAS 9.4.

RESULTS

The study population of more than 1.5 million women had a mean±SD age of 36.0±13.7 years and a median age of 33.1 years (interquartile range 20.3 years). Among them, 38.7% tested positive for BV, 28.5% tested positive for VVC (Table 1), and 10.8% tested positive for both BV and VVC. The testing volume increased from 2022 to 2024, but the overall positivity rates for BV (37.8–39.3%) and VVC (28.4–28.6%) remained stable (data not shown). Higher positivity rates for both BV and VVC were seen in younger patients (Table 1) and in the Midwest and East Coast regions (Fig. 1).

C trachomatis and N gonorrhoeae had the highest positivity rate in adolescents and young adults (15–24 years), with a steep decline as age increased. T vaginalis positivity peaked in the middle-aged (35–54) group rather than in the younger group. M genitalium had the highest positivity rate in younger ages (15–29) with a gradual decline across age. Across all STIs, BV+/VVC+ and BV+/VVC− groups generally had the highest positivity, whereas BV-negative groups were lowest (Appendix 1, available online at https://links.lww.com/AOG/E790).

Table 2 shows that across all STIs, BV+ individuals had a higher rate of positivity compared with BV− individuals regardless of VVC results. Specifically, among those with VVC−, BV+ individuals had positivity rates of 4.8%, 1.0%, and 7.8%, for C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, and T vaginalis, respectively, whereas BV− individuals had rates of 1.4%, 0.2%, and 2.1% for the same STIs (Table 2). Similarly, among those with VVC+, BV+ women had positivity rates of 4.9%, 1.0%, and 5.6% for C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, and T vaginalis, respectively, whereas BV− individuals had rates of 2.3%, 0.3%, and 1.6% for the same STIs (Table 2). The differences in positivity rates between BV+ and BV− were greater among those with VVC− compared with those with VVC+; with C trachomatis as an example, for VVC−, the difference between BV+ (4.8%) and BV− (1.4%) is 3.4%, which can be calculated with Table 2. Especially for VVC− versus VVC +, the difference of positivity between BV+ and BV− was as follows: C trachomatis 3.4% versus 2.6%, N gonorrhoeae 0.8% versus 0.7%, and T vaginalis 5.7% versus 4.0%. The subset of individuals who were tested for M genitalium followed the same pattern. For VVC−, BV+ individuals had a positivity rate of 8.7% for M genitalium, whereas BV− individuals had a rate of 3.0% for M genitalium (Table 2). Similarly, for VVC+, BV+ individuals had a positivity rate of 9.6% for M genitalium, whereas VVC+/BV− individuals had a rate of 4.9% for M genitalium (Table 2). The difference in positivity rates between BV+ and BV− individuals was greater among those with VVC− compared with those with VVC+ for M genitalium (5.7% vs 4.7%).

DISCUSSION

This large, retrospective study investigated the associations between two common forms of vaginitis, BV and VVC, and common STIs, including C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, T vaginalis, and M genitalium. To the best of our knowledge, this study of more than 1.5 million women tested at a national reference laboratory represents the most extensive assessment to date of the association between vaginitis and nonviral STIs in a geographically and demographically diverse U.S. population. Our study observed a high burden of BV (38.7%) and VVC (28.5%) among women tested, with 10.8% positive for both conditions. Notably, BV positivity was strongly associated with an increased likelihood of STI coinfection that was independent of VVC status.

These results confirm and expand on previous reports of elevated STI risk in women with BV.9,14,15 For instance, Van Der Pol et al15 showed that BV+ women were 3 times as likely to have any STI (C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, or T vaginalis) compared with BV− women, with T vaginalis having the strongest association. Notably, correlations of STI coinfections in VVC-positive women were not observed. Similarly, Schwebke et al9 demonstrated that BV+ women were twice as likely to have an STI and exhibited a 50% higher diversity of STI coinfections compared with BV− women. Conversely, it has also been shown that women being tested for STIs have a high rate of BV coinfection, with an odds ratio of 2.86 (95% CI, 1.99–4.11, P<0.001) for concurrent BV infection in women with diagnosed with C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, or T vaginalis.14 In our larger, more diverse population, BV+ women, regardless of VVC status, had 2 to 4 times higher odds of testing positive for C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, or T vaginalis than BV−/VVC− women. We observed a similar pattern for M genitalium (1.8-2.1 higher odds of testing positive for M genitalium in BV+ women), as shown in prior studies.7,9 Given the more restricted guidelines for M genitalium testing, the number of women who had a concurrent M genitalium test was substantially smaller than the number of women with concurrent C trachomatis/N gonorrhoeae/T vaginalis tests in the present study; despite this, the M genitalium–tested cohort was still sizable, with more than 37,000 women included in this subanalysis.

This study confirms previous findings that VVC alone is not significantly associated with increased STI risk,9,15 whereas the presence of BV remains a consistent and independent predictor of STI coinfection, even after adjustment for VVC. These findings help to solidify what is becoming a strong body of evidence that alterations in the vaginal microbiome influence susceptibility to STI acquisition and persistence.7,9 Bacterial vaginosis is marked by a shift from a Lactobacillus-dominant flora to one rich in anaerobes, resulting in loss of mucosal barrier integrity and altered immune responses, potentially creating a permissive environment for STI pathogens.

In our population, all STI pathogens studied (C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, T vaginalis, and M genitalium) are more prevalent in BV+ women. Although this relationship has been previously reported for T vaginalis and M genitalium, this is the most pronounced positive association seen to date between BV infection and C trachomatis or N gonorrhoeae infection, with odds ratios of 2.8 for C trachomatis and 3.8 for N gonorrhoeae. The lack of a similar association between BV and C trachomatis/N gonorrhoeae in some prior studies may be the result of differences in diagnostic methods, sample size, low C trachomatis/N gonorrhoeae prevalence in those populations, or adjustment for confounders.15

The scale of our dataset allowed detailed analysis of age and regional patterns in vaginitis and STI positivity. Younger women, particularly adolescents and young adults, exhibited the highest rates of C trachomatis, N gonorrhoeae, and M genitalium, whereas T vaginalis positivity increased with age, peaking in older adults (Appendix 1, https://links.lww.com/AOG/E790). This mirrors national surveillance data and underscores the importance of targeted screening in younger populations.16 Positivity rates for both BV and VVC were also higher among younger patients and in the Midwest and East Coast regions, pointing to possible geographic or demographic influences (Table 1 and Fig. 1).

From a clinical standpoint, these findings reinforce the need for comprehensive STI screening in women diagnosed with BV. The consistent, strong associations between BV and nonviral STIs—not just for T vaginalis and M genitalium but also for C trachomatis and N gonorrhoeae—reinforce current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention STI treatment guidelines that recommend STI testing for women with BV.17 Further research should focus on clarifying the mechanisms and causal pathways linking vaginal dysbiosis to STI acquisition or persistence. The complexity and prevalence of BV and STI coinfection underscore the importance of comprehensive testing for women presenting with vaginal symptoms when clinically indicated to ensure appropriate treatment for all potential causes of those symptoms.

The strengths of our study include its large, heterogeneous sample size, geographic breadth, and use of U.S. Food and Drug Administration–approved, sensitive molecular testing for the detection of both vaginitis etiologies and STIs. These features enhance the generalizability of our findings and allow robust estimation of associations across diverse subgroups. In addition, this study included a larger number of N gonorrhoeae–positive individuals than previous studies, which increased the effective sample size and allowed statistically powered analyses to evaluate potential associations between this STI and BV.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective, laboratory-based design, which precludes access to detailed clinical information such as presenting symptoms, risk factors, and infection history. In addition, data are limited to specimens submitted by the ordering health care professional based on individual clinical management and likely represent insured patients. This limits adjustment for potential confounders and the ability to distinguish the incidence of new infections from the prevalence of existing infections. The reliance on reference laboratory data may also introduce selection bias; women tested by reference laboratories may differ from those in other settings (ie, point-of-care testing, STI clinics, emergency department).

In conclusion, this population-level, national laboratory–based study confirms and extends prior observations that BV is strongly associated with increased risk of multiple nonviral STI coinfections among U.S. women. Improved molecular diagnostics that allow the detection of multiple analytes from a common specimen has enabled enhanced management for women who present with vaginitis. Large, real-world, sexual health test result databases are an important tool to improve insights into prevalence and risks associated with BV and STIs that may inform guideline committees. These findings have important implications for clinical practice and public health and support routine comprehensive STI testing in the management of BV. These findings highlight the need for ongoing research to clarify mechanisms and causal pathways linking vaginal dysbiosis and STI acquisition.
