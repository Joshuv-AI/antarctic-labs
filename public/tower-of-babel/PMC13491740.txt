Liposomal multimodal theranostics for bone disorders: from rational responsive delivery and biomimetic strategies to clinical translation
Authors: Haifu Sun, Yu Chen, Xuequan Zhao, Xixi Han, Yonggang Li, Yuchen Qian, Zhiheng Gao, Huilin Yang, Nanning Lv, Jiaxiang Bai, Youzhi Hong, Yusen Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bone and bone-related disorders remain difficult to diagnose and treat because of the dense mineralized matrix, limited vascular perfusion, complex pathological microenvironment, and insufficient site-specific accumulation of conventional agents. Liposomes, as biocompatible vesicular nanocarriers capable of loading therapeutic, genetic, and imaging cargos, have emerged as versatile platforms for bone-oriented theranostics. In contrast to previous reviews mainly focusing on general liposomal drug delivery or individual skeletal diseases, this review provides an integrated perspective on liposomal multimodal theranostics for bone disorders, emphasizing responsive delivery, diagnostic imaging, biomimetic engineering, and clinical translation. We first summarize the structural features, classification, and functional modification of liposomes, including bone-targeting ligands, stimuli-responsive release, membrane-penetrating delivery, and biomimetic designs. We then discuss their diagnostic and therapeutic applications in osteomyelitis, joint tuberculosis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, bone defects, fracture nonunion, bone tumors, and spinal cord injury, with particular attention to radiolabeled liposomes, fluorescent probes, imaging-guided therapy, and microenvironment-responsive systems. Moreover, biomimetic liposomes, such as cell membrane-camouflaged, macrophage- or erythrocyte membrane-engineered, membrane-fusion-inspired, and organelle-delivering liposomal systems, are highlighted for their roles in immune evasion, lesion targeting, barrier penetration, and intercellular delivery. Finally, we discuss key translational challenges, including manufacturing reproducibility, stability, biosafety, immune responses, disease-model relevance, and clinical evaluation. This review aims to provide a rational framework for developing responsive and biomimetic liposomal theranostic systems for bone-related diseases.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04616-7.

Introduction

With population ageing and the increasing incidence of trauma, infection, cancer, and degenerative skeletal disorders, bone-related diseases have become a growing global health burden [1]. These diseases encompass a heterogeneous spectrum of conditions, including osteoporosis (OP) [2], osteoarthritis (OA) [3], rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [4], osteomyelitis and osteoarticular tuberculosis [5], bone defects, delayed fracture healing, nonunion [6], primary or metastatic bone tumors [7] and so on. Among them, OP and related fracture risk affect more than 200 million people worldwide, and this burden is expected to increase with the rapid growth of the elderly population [8, 9]. Osteomyelitis also remains difficult to manage, with approximately 40% of patients facing persistent risks of chronic infection or relapse even after standardized treatment [10]. RA affects approximately 0.5%–1.0% of the global population [11], while OA currently affects 595 million people worldwide and is projected to approach one billion cases by 2050 [12]. It is a leading cause of disability [13].Collectively, the increasing prevalence of these bone-related diseases imposes substantial medical, social, and economic burdens.

The clinical manifestations and pathological features of these disorders are highly diverse. OP is mainly characterized by progressive bone loss, deterioration of bone microarchitecture, and increased fracture risk [14].OA involves articular cartilage degeneration, synovial inflammation, subchondral bone remodeling, and joint dysfunction [15].RA is driven by autoimmune synovitis, pannus formation, and immune-mediated bone erosion [16]. Osteomyelitis is associated with persistent infection, bacterial biofilm formation, inflammatory bone destruction, and frequent recurrence [17]. Large bone defects and fracture nonunion are often accompanied by insufficient osteogenic signaling, poor vascularization, excessive inflammation, and compromised mechanical stability [18]. Bone tumors, including osteosarcoma and metastatic bone disease, are characterized by aggressive tumor growth, osteolysis, immune escape, and complex tumor-bone interactions [19]. Therefore, bone-related diseases require diagnostic and therapeutic strategies that can address both disease-specific pathological mechanisms and the unique structural barriers of skeletal tissues.

Despite advances in imaging, pharmacotherapy, surgery, and regenerative medicine, several issues remain in the traditional diagnosis and treatment of bone-related diseases. However, conventional imaging may lack sufficient sensitivity, specificity, or temporal consistency for early and precise diagnosis [20]. Large bone defects often result in delayed healing, non-union, or malunion [6]. OP treatments are typically long-term and slow to take effect, leading to poor patient compliance, while the number of patients with OP continues to rise rapidly [21].In OA treatment, oral medications often result in low local concentrations, injections of sodium hyaluronate can potentially lead to joint adhesions, and surgery carries significant risks of trauma [22, 23]. For highly recurrent and drug-resistant bone infections, long-term oral antibiotics often fail to eradicate bacteria [24]. RA patients who take NSAIDs and corticosteroids long-term face systemic side effects [25]. Malignant bone tumors, due to their extensive involvement and complex microenvironment, remain difficult to eradicate even after radiotherapy, chemotherapy and surgical resection [26, 27]. Especially for osteosarcoma, the current survival rate remains low [28, 29]. These conventional approaches remain limited by poor diagnostic accuracy, insufficient bone-specific drug accumulation, rapid clearance, limited tissue penetration, and off-target toxicity. Systemic drugs often show low bioavailability and poor bone targeting [30], while radiotherapy may damage healthy bone marrow and vasculature [31], and surgery is invasive with limited ability to fully restore bone structure and function [32].

In this context, liposomes have emerged as promising nanoplatforms for bone-oriented theranostics [33]. Owing to their phospholipid bilayer structure, liposomes can encapsulate hydrophilic, hydrophobic, genetic, and imaging agents, protect therapeutic cargos from premature degradation, and improve pharmacokinetic behavior. More importantly, surface engineering with bone-seeking ligands, peptides, antibodies, polymers, or biomimetic membranes enables liposomes to achieve enhanced lesion targeting, immune evasion, biological barrier penetration, and stimuli-responsive drug release [34–36]. These properties make liposomes particularly suitable for addressing the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges of diverse bone-related diseases.

Although previous reviews have discussed drug delivery or nanomedicine for skeletal diseases, most have focused on therapeutic applications or individual disease types. The integration of diagnostic imaging, responsive delivery, biomimetic engineering, disease-specific design, and clinical translation has not been systematically summarized [37, 38]. Therefore, this review aims to provide a comprehensive framework for liposome-based multimodal theranostics in bone disorders, with emphasis on both rational material design and translational challenges.

In this review, we summarize recent advances in liposome-based multimodal theranostics for bone disorders. We first introduce the structural features, classification, functional modification, targeting mechanisms and stimuli-responsive delivery of liposomes. We then discuss their diagnostic and therapeutic applications in major bone-related diseases. Selected musculoskeletal conditions involving tendon–bone or muscle–bone interactions, such as rotator cuff injury and Duchenne muscular dystrophy, are also discussed to broaden the perspective on liposome-based strategies for musculoskeletal repair and functional recovery. (Fig. 1). To provide a clearer overview, the major diagnostic and therapeutic bottlenecks of representative bone-related diseases and the corresponding rationale for liposome-based theranostic design are summarized in Table 1. Finally, we examine the key barriers to clinical translation. By linking liposome design with disease-specific needs, this review aims to provide a rational framework for the future development of liposome-based theranostic strategies for skeletal diseases.

Characteristics and treatment strategies of liposomes

Preparation methods of liposomes

Liposome preparation methods have an important influence on vesicle size, lamellarity, cargo-loading efficiency, stability, and reproducibility [39, 40]. Commonly used methods include thin-film hydration, ethanol injection, reverse-phase evaporation, sonication, extrusion, and freeze–thaw techniques [39, 41]. Thin-film hydration remains widely used in laboratory preparation, but additional sonication or extrusion is often required to obtain nanosized liposomes with improved size uniformity [39, 40]. Ethanol injection and reverse-phase evaporation are useful for encapsulating different types of cargos, although solvent residue, batch variability, and scale-up remain important concerns [42]. Post-processing approaches, including extrusion, sonication, and freeze–thaw cycles, can further regulate particle size, homogeneity, and encapsulation efficiency [40, 41]. More recently, microfluidic technologies have enabled more precise and reproducible control over liposome size, composition, and drug-loading behavior, showing advantages in continuous, scalable, and standardized production [43, 44]. Therefore, the selection of an appropriate preparation method should be guided by the intended cargo, desired particle size, stability requirements, reproducibility, and translational application of the liposomal formulation.

Characteristics and classification of liposomes

Liposomes possess a lipid bilayer structure similar to that of cell membranes, making them excellent nanoscale drug delivery carriers [45, 46]. Their primary constituents are lipids and fatty acids naturally found in cell membranes, which provide good biocompatibility and biodegradability [47]. Moreover, the molecular diversity in their composition endows liposomes with unique physiological functions for drug delivery, such as responsiveness, traversing physiological barriers, membrane-penetrating capacity, targeting capability, extended circulation time, and reduced systemic toxicity [48, 49]. In contrast to traditional diagnostic and therapeutic methods, liposomes, due to their high biocompatibility and modifiable targeting properties, represent a novel imaging agent for SPECT/CT molecular imaging. Once modified with targeting groups, liposomes demonstrate enhanced local imaging, facilitating more accurate disease diagnosis [50]. In treatment, liposomes can encapsulate drugs to prevent premature degradation. Surface-engineered functionalized liposomes exhibit superior stimuli-responsiveness ( temperature [51] (Fig. 2a), electrical fields [52] (Fig. 2b), pH gradients [53] (Fig. 2c), and glucose sensitivity [54] (Fig. 2d)) and targeting capabilities through precisely modulated ligand-receptor interactions, enabling spatiotemporally controlled drug release at pathological sites. By modifying the liposome surface, macrophage phagocytosis can be avoided, thereby extending their in vivo half-life.

Moreover, liposome-based intelligent systems can traverse physiological barriers, such as the bone marrow-blood barrier via targeted homing to bone marrow, osseous matrix anchoring, and intracellular delivery mechanisms, thereby achieving enhanced therapeutic efficacy. For instance, strategies, such as “neutrophil-hitchhiking” delivery [55] and “hematopoietic stem cell-mimicking membranes“ [56] (Fig. 3a) enable precise homing of liposomes to bone marrow niches. Alendronate (ALN)-functionalized liposomal carriers demonstrate superior bone-targeting capability and potent anti-tumor effects in C57/BL6 murine models [57] (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, AMD3100-conjugated bortezomib liposomes selectively target CXCR4-overexpressing myeloma cells to induce tumor-specific apoptosis [58] (Fig. 3c).

The superior membrane-penetrating capacity of liposomes enables targeted transcellular delivery of encapsulated therapeutics to diseased cells, achieving spatiotemporally controlled cytocidal effects through selective cellular internalization mechanisms. Previous studies have engineered nanomedicines to enter cells via diverse endocytic pathways, predominantly relying on endolysosomal escape mechanisms to deliver therapeutic cargo intracellularly [59]. However, endocytic uptake frequently compromises drug delivery efficacy, as most nanocarrier payloads face degradation within endosomes/lysosomes, thereby diminishing therapeutic outcomes [60]. Consequently, novel liposomal drug delivery systems based on membrane fusion mechanisms designed to circumvent endolysosomal entrapment will markedly boost intracellular drug delivery efficiency. The core mechanism of liposomal membrane fusion relies on orchestrated physicochemical drivers and specific molecular mediators to achieve bilayer integration and cargo translocation. First, regulating the charge density and types on the surface of liposomes can achieve higher passive membrane-penetrating efficiency. Rafael B. Lira et al. [61] confirmed that highly charged cationic liposomes fusing with synthetic vesicles or biomembranes of living cells can effectively deliver large quantities of encapsulated cargo within seconds, bypassing slow and often inefficient internalization-based pathways (Fig. 4a). Furthermore, receptor- or peptide-mediated active membrane penetration processes can significantly enhance the targeted delivery capability of liposomal drug delivery systems. Wenguang Liu et al. [62] engineered a dual-functional Lipo-HFn system via electrostatic complexation, comprising cationic liposomes loaded with ALK5 and TGF-β small-molecule inhibitor RepSOX (Lipo@RepSOX), and heavy ferritin (HFn) encapsulating indole propionic acid (IPA) as an inflammation scavenger (HFn@IPA). The Lipo-HFn complex effectively crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) through receptor-mediated endocytosis, subsequently achieving substantial accumulation in the brain parenchyma to exert therapeutic effects via microvascular stabilization and inflammation suppression. Inspired by the fusion mechanisms between transport vesicles and target plasma membranes during natural neuronal exocytosis, Alexander Kros et al. [60] leveraged peptide K’s dual affinity for both peptide E and lipid membranes, demonstrating that homodimers of peptide K enhance liposomal drug delivery into cells (Fig. 4b). This discovery facilitates researchers’ development of more efficient intracellular drug delivery systems using liposome-cell membrane fusion strategies. Notably, membrane-penetration enhancement strategies such as biomimetic camouflage or energy-field-driven approaches have also been extensively investigated. Research by James J. Choi et al. [63] demonstrates that rapid short-pulse (RaSP) focused ultrasound combined with microbubbles enables localized delivery of liposomes across the blood-brain barrier at a pressure of 0.53 Mpa (Fig. 4c). In vivo experiments revealed cellular uptake of liposomes within neurons and microglia, while no intracellular uptake was observed in astrocytes following either RaSP (0.35 MPa) or conventional long-pulse ultrasound treatments. In summary, the intrinsic membrane-penetrating properties of liposomes endow liposome-based drug delivery systems with superior therapeutic delivery performance, establishing a robust foundation for future research in related biomedical fields. Structurally, liposomes are formed by the self-assembly of amphiphilic molecules into bilayer vesicles. In these vesicles, the hydrophilic head groups face the external aqueous environment, while the hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains assemble within the interior. This amphiphilicity makes liposomes ideal carriers for molecules of varying polarity. Liposome diameters range from 25 nm to 1000 nm, and they may have single-layer or multilayer membrane structures. The size of the vesicles influences the half-life of the liposomes, while the size and number of lipid bilayers affect the amount of drug encapsulation [64]. Liposomes can be classified based on the number of lipid bilayers as either unilamellar liposomes or multilamellar vesicles (MLVs). Unilamellar liposomes contain a single phospholipid bilayer enclosing an aqueous solution and can be further divided into small unilamellar vesicles (SUVs), with diameters of approximately 0.02–0.08 μm, and large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs), which range from 0.1 to 1 μm in diameter. Multilamellar vesicles have diameters ranging from 1 to 5 μm and possess a characteristic onion-like structure [65]; Liposomes can also be classified by their surface charge as neutral, negatively charged, or positively charged [66]; Positively charged liposomes tend to exhibit better drug delivery capabilities; however, negatively charged liposomes generally display lower cytotoxicity compared to their positively charged counterparts [67]. Unilamellar liposomes contain a single phospholipid bilayer enclosing an aqueous solution, while multilamellar liposomes have a characteristic onion-like structure [68]. Additionally, liposomes can be categorized based on their function as either conventional liposomes or specialised liposomes. Special liposomes include long-circulating liposomes, targeted liposomes, and responsive liposomes. Depending on their responsiveness, liposomes can be further classified as thermosensitive liposomes [51], electrosensitive liposomes [52], ultrasound-responsive liposomes [69, 70], magnetoresponsive liposomes [71], reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive liposomes [72], pH-sensitive liposomes [53], enzyme-responsive liposomes [73], redox-sensitive liposomes [74], glucose-responsive liposomes [54], and light-sensitive liposomes [75].

Diagnosis and treatment strategies related to liposomes

In the diagnosis and treatment of bone-related diseases, clinicians aim to deliver drugs to specific sites, increase local drug concentrations, and reduce systemic side effects. Liposomes can facilitate this by passively transporting drugs to target sites, leveraging the structural and physiological differences between diseased and healthy tissues. For example, in bone tumour tissue, the endothelial gaps in neovascularisation are larger than in normal vasculature, allowing for higher permeability. This enables liposomes to reach the tumour stroma and accumulate drugs, a phenomenon known as the enhanced permeability and retention effect (EPR) [76–78]. However, the magnitude of the EPR effect is highly heterogeneous and depends on tumor type, vascular perfusion, stromal barriers, interstitial pressure, and nanocarrier physicochemical properties. Circulating liposomes can still be recognised by the mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS) in the bloodstream and subsequently phagocytosed by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), significantly reducing their half-life. To overcome this limitation, researchers introduced polyethylene glycol (PEG) liposome technology, which effectively ‘hides’ the liposomes, thereby prolonging their half-life in vivo [79, 80] .

Despite these advantages, PEG liposomes have their own drawbacks. Increasing evidence from in vivo studies shows that PEG liposomes can induce hypersensitivity reactions [81]. Anti- PEG antibodies may be preexisting in the body, resulting in rapid clearance of polyethylene glycolized liposomes from the bloodstream [82]. In addition to being preexisting, polyethylene glycolized liposomes may also induce PEG antibody production, which triggers a hypersensitivity reaction [83, 84]. For instance, Gergely Tibor Kozma’s team demonstrated that a single low-dose intravenous injection of PEGylated liposomes (Doxebo) in pigs led to a significant increase in anti-PEG IgM in the blood. This resulted in rapid clearance of PEG–liposome–IgM complexes via complement activation and caused anaphylactic shock [85] (Fig. 5a). The drip rate of PEG liposomes may ameliorate this phenomenon. Zheng Yaxin’s team found that by slow titration, the polyethylene glycolized liposomes that enter the bloodstream first are sufficient to clear the anti-polyethylene glycol antibodies, thus reducing the clearance of subsequently injected liposomes. In contrast, rapid intravenous injection of polyethylene glycolized liposomes resulted in uniform binding of anti-polyethylene glycol antibodies, which led to rapid clearance of polyethylene glycolized liposomes from the circulation [86]. However, this also did not completely inhibit the ABC phenomenon of PEGylated liposomes. To address these issues, researchers have explored modifications to reduce PEG immunogenicity. Taro Shimizu’s team found that modifying the PEG terminal with hydroxyl groups to synthesise hydroxyl-PEG liposomes (PL-OH) resulted in reduced recognition by anti-PEG IgM in vitro, effectively weakening the anti-PEG IgM response [87] (Fig. 5b).

Successful drug delivery, however, also depends on the controlled release of liposome-encapsulated drugs [88]. Researchers have designed stimulus-responsive and targeted liposomes that, after modification, can exhibit sensitivity to local microenvironmental factors such as pH, redox potential, or specific enzymatic activity. External stimuli like light and heat can also trigger drug release, typically by inducing membrane destabilisation based on defects in the lipid bilayer [89, 90]. For example, drugs can be encapsulated in heat-sensitive liposomes, and when they are exposed to mild localized thermotherapy, the lipids approach their liquid crystal phase transition temperature, creating disorder between the solid and fluid domains conducive to permeation of water-soluble molecules and achieving abrupt release of the encapsulated drug [91]. Alternatively, drugs can be encapsulated in cationic liposomes composed of 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (DOTAP) and lipid-S75; when an electric current is applied to the skin, the ionised liposomes can penetrate mucous membranes and release the drug at the diseased site [52]. Moreover, liposomes loaded with CO₂ under supercritical CO₂ conditions can release drugs on demand when exposed to safe ultrasound irradiation at 237 kHz [92] (Fig. 5c). The integrity of the liposome membrane is maintained by attaching iron oxide nanoparticles (NPs) to the outside of the liposome. At the same time, the liposome membrane can be disrupted based on an alternating magnetic field at a specific time and place to release the drug on demand [93]. In addition, there are perfluoropentane (PFP) liposomes triggered by sonication [94], TK-bonded liposomes with ROS sensitivity [95], and the pH-responsive polymer poly(2-ethyl-2-oxazoline) liposomes [96] (Fig. 5d).

Researchers have also modified liposomes with ligands such as active peptides and antibodies to achieve targeted delivery through ligand–receptor interactions, enabling efficient and controlled drug release at the target site. For instance, arginine-glycine-aspartic acid (RGD) peptides can be incorporated into liposomes to target tumours, and guanidine-modified dendritic peptides can be used to target mitochondria [97–99].Through these modifications, liposomes can evade immune cell phagocytosis, reach specific sites, and release drugs in a controlled manner, thereby increasing local drug accumulation. This strategy represents a primary application of liposomes in the diagnosis and treatment of bone-related diseases.

Application of liposomes in the diagnosis of bone and bone-related diseases

Diagnosis of osteomyelitis

Liposomes can play a significant role in the early diagnosis of bone-related diseases. To provide a clearer overview of liposome-based diagnostic applications in bone-related diseases, representative strategies are summarized in Tables 3 and 4. Osteomyelitis is a major global health problem. Due to the complexity of its disease, early diagnosis is difficult and the prognosis is poor [100]. Functional imaging, in addition to clinical symptoms and medical history, is the most sensitive method for early detection of osteomyelitis [101]. However, these techniques lack sufficient specificity, and X-rays and CT scans are often ineffective in early diagnosis. In contrast, radiolabelled antibiotics offer improved selectivity as they are specifically recognised by bacteria [102]. Incorporating these radiopharmaceuticals into drug delivery systems with high affinity for bone can enhance overall uptake and targeting [103].

Representative liposome-based and nanoparticle-enabled diagnostic or theranostic strategies for bone-related diseases

Building on this concept, Soraya Maria Zandim Maciel Dias Ferreira and colleagues conducted experiments on liposome-based diagnostics. They prepared specialised targeted liposomes, labelled with technetium-99 and loaded with cefotaxime, for monitoring osteomyelitis lesions. In their experiment, the liposomes were PEGylated and coated with alendronate to evade immune clearance and achieve bone targeting. Additionally, the liposomes were designed to release the drug in response to pH changes in the infected osteomyelitis environment. Using radiolabel detection techniques, the researchers obtained scintigraphic images of rats with osteomyelitis and healthy rats at various time points after intravenous injection of the liposomes. At all intervals, the absorption rate of the liposomes in osteomyelitis animals was significantly higher than in the control group, and this difference was statistically significant [104].

However, the study did not account for the potential accumulation of liposomes in sterile inflammation sites, where increased blood flow and vascular permeability due to the enhanced EPR effect could lead to liposome deposition. To better distinguish between pyogenic and sterile inflammation, Diego dos Santos Ferreira’s team explored this issue by preparing long-circulating, alendronate-coated liposomes containing technetium-99-labelled cefotaxime. Their results indicated that in sterile inflammation models, the target-to-non-target ratio remained between 1.4 and 2.0, whereas in osteomyelitis models, this ratio ranged from 2.3 to 3.0. This demonstrated the liposomes’ enhanced affinity for osteomyelitis lesions. Compared to previous liposome-based diagnostic agents, the target-to-non-target ratio in osteomyelitis rats was the highest, showing a clear ability to distinguish between sterile and pyogenic inflammation [105]. Moreover, radiolabelling antibiotics at the start of treatment allows for tracking disease progression throughout the course of the illness. This approach could provide insights into antibiotic efficacy and bacterial resistance. In summary, this diagnostic method shows significant potential for clinical application.

Diagnosis of joint tuberculosis and rheumatoid arthritis

Similar to their use in osteomyelitis, liposomes can be employed not only for the treatment but also for the diagnosis of tuberculosis [106]. Dileep-Tiwari and colleagues were the first to report the diagnostic application of liposome-encapsulated Mycobacterium tuberculosis culture filtrate proteins. They found that, in the presence of TB antigens, polyclonal antibodies bound to liposome particles exhibited a visible agglutination reaction. Based on this observation, they developed a rapid card test capable of detecting TB antigens within 4–5 min, with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 95%, making it both convenient and efficient. This diagnostic method broadened the clinical applications of liposomes in joint tuberculosis, extending beyond drug delivery to include diagnostic utility [107].

Early diagnosis of RA, an autoimmune disease primarily characterised by multiple joint synovitis and pannus formation, is essential to slow disease progression and prevent joint necrosis [108]. Hao Wu developed internalised RGD (iRGD)-targeted liposomes (iLPs) as fluorescent probes for this purpose (Fig. 6a). Using near-infrared fluorescent molecular imaging, they assessed inflammation progression within joints. Their experiments demonstrated that iLPs exhibited strong fluorescent signals in inflamed joints, while no signals were observed in normal joints (Fig. 6b). Additionally, the fluorescence intensity in the iLPs group was 3.03 times higher than that of non-targeted indocyanine green liposomes (lacking the iRGD peptide). This indicated high sensitivity and specificity, and in vivo experiments confirmed that iLPs had good biocompatibility. As an excellent diagnostic probe, iLPs facilitate early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of RA [109].

Diagnosis of bone tumours

At present, the treatment of bone tumours is stagnant, making early detection and diagnosis particularly important. In addition, due to the significant side effects of antitumour drugs, it is crucial to dynamically monitor changes in the tumour and adjust treatment plans in a timely and appropriate manner. Phosphates have an affinity for bone minerals and are commonly used to target bone [110, 111]. ALN-coupled near-infrared (NIR) fluorophores exhibit high fluorescence signals in bone tissue [112, 113]. Similarly, ALN-modified polymer nanoparticles, nanogels, and nanocapsules are loaded or coupled with fluorophores for targeted fluorescence image [114, 115].

Ag₂S quantum dots are attractive imaging agents because of their near-infrared fluorescence properties. However, for bone tumor imaging, their diagnostic performance can be further improved by integration with bone-targeted liposomal carriers. For example, Li and colleagues developed ALN/DOX@Ag₂S, a multifunctional liposome–quantum dot hybrid system in which Ag₂S quantum dots and doxorubicin were incorporated into alendronate-modified liposomes for targeted imaging and therapy of tibial tumors (Fig. 6c). In this system, the liposomal platform is not merely a passive container. Instead, it improves the dispersion and biological compatibility of the imaging cargo, prolongs systemic circulation, carries the chemotherapeutic drug, and enables ALN-mediated bone affinity. Meanwhile, Ag₂S quantum dots provide near-infrared fluorescence signals for real-time tumor visualization [116]. This design illustrates how liposomes can serve as modular theranostic platforms by integrating bone-targeting ligands, imaging agents, and therapeutic cargos within a single nanosystem. In addition, liposomes modified with bone-affinity ligands, such as aspartic acid-rich peptides or synthetic bone-targeting polymers, have also been explored for targeted imaging and diagnosis of bone tumors [117].

Cell membrane-engineered liposomes have also attracted increasing attention as biomimetic platforms for tumor imaging and theranostics. Because natural cell membranes can fuse with synthetic lipid bilayers, cell membrane-derived components can be incorporated into liposomal systems to enhance immune evasion, circulation stability, and lesion accumulation [118]. For example, Ferrel and colleagues prepared erythrocyte membrane-fused synthetic liposomes by co-extrusion and loaded them with doxorubicin and the magnetic resonance imaging contrast agent gadolinium (Fig. 6d). The resulting nanoparticles exhibited suitable nanoscale size, negative surface charge, good stability, membrane fusion properties, magnetic resonance imaging capability, drug-loading capacity, and biocompatibility [119]. Although this system was not designed exclusively for bone tumors, it provides a useful biomimetic liposomal strategy for integrating immune evasion, imaging contrast, and drug delivery. In future bone tumor applications, such erythrocyte membrane-inspired or macrophage membrane-engineered liposomes could be further combined with bone-targeting ligands to improve tumor localization, monitor biodistribution, and support image-guided therapy.

Liposome-based diagnosis of osteoporosis, arthritis, and other skeletal disorders

In addition to osteomyelitis, RA, and bone tumors, recent studies have also supported the potential of liposome-based and nanoparticle-enabled imaging strategies in osteoporosis, OA, and other skeletal disorders. In osteoporosis, aptamer-functionalized liposome nanoparticles have been used to target osteoblasts and modulate NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, suggesting that lipid-based platforms may link disease-relevant biomarkers with targeted intervention [120]. Bone-targeting near-infrared luminescent nanocarriers and NIR-II imaging agents have further demonstrated the feasibility of visualizing skeletal lesions and monitoring bone-related pathological changes [112, 121]. In arthritis, peptide-guided liposomes and inflammation-responsive nanoprobes can preferentially accumulate in inflamed joints, enabling in vivo tracking, molecular imaging, and image-guided therapy [122–124]. Moreover, nanoparticle-enabled molecular imaging has shown promise for OA diagnosis and disease monitoring [125], while liposome-anchored injectable systems provide useful platforms for evaluating intra-articular retention and therapeutic response [125]. These studies broaden the diagnostic scope of liposomal and nanotheranostic systems beyond infection and tumors, supporting their application in OP, OA, RA, bone defects, and other skeletal disorders.

Liposomes for the treatment of bone and bone-related diseases

Liposomes for bone regeneration

Non-phospholipid liposomes and cationic liposomes promote bone regeneration

Large bone defects caused by non-healing fractures or tumours often exceed the bone’s self-repair capacity and are difficult to heal [126]. Scientists have been utilizing bioactive substances that promote osteogenesis to repair such large bone defects [127]. Bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP-2/7), for instance, play a crucial role in the transformation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into bone. However, bone repair is a long-term process, and the short half-life of conventional biologics often limits their effectiveness. Nanocarriers can solve this problem [128]. Kroczek’s research showed that conventional liposomes alone did not promote bone regeneration, but liposomes loaded with BMP-2 achieved bone healing after 4 weeks. Similarly, Park and colleagues conducted experiments using liposomes as carriers to deliver BMP-2, promoting the differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs). Complete healing of bone defects was achieved within 6 weeks, while the control group showed no healing. Therefore, liposomes can mediate the delivery of BMP-2/7 to promote bone regeneration [129]. To examine the drug release effects of liposomes, Marquez et al. studied epidermal growth factor (EGF) delivered by liposomes to promote alveolar bone healing in rats as early as 2012. They found that EGF-loaded liposomes (EGF-L) delivered EGF in a controlled manner, effectively stimulating mesenchymal cell migration and osteoblast differentiation, while avoiding premature degradation. This study demonstrated that liposomes can successfully serve as targeted delivery carriers for growth factors in bone healing [130].

For even better drug-controlled release, non-phospholipid liposomes were developed. These liposomes offer stable bilayers and minimal permeability, allowing for sustained drug release. Subsequent experiments challenged the idea that simple liposomes cannot promote osteogenesis. A recently developed non-phospholipid liposome, Sterosome, made from 20 S-hydroxycholesterol (Oxy) and stearylamine (SA), forms fluid lamellar phases that exhibit osteoinductive properties, enhancing the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells (Fig. 7a). In vitro experiments showed that SA/OXY liposomes exhibited the best effects at a concentration of 200 µg/ml based on alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining (Fig. 7b). In vivo experiments further demonstrated that SA/OXY liposomes had stronger osteogenic capacity than SA/Chol liposomes, with relative bone surface area, bone volume density (BV/TV%), and trabecular number (Tb.N.mm − 1) more than twice as high as the control group (Fig. 7c). Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that non-phospholipid liposomes composed of SA/OXY, due to their fluid lamellar phase, have osteoinductive capabilities [131]. It is thus conceivable that combining non-phospholipid liposomes with osteogenic bioactive substances could achieve synergistic effects. This hypothesis has been validated. Chung-Sung Lee and colleagues used non-phospholipid liposomes to deliver a smoothened agonist (SAG), which enhances Hedgehog signaling through the release of SAG (Fig. 7d). In vivo experiments using a critical-sized bone defect model demonstrated significant new bone formation at 8 weeks [132].

In addition to developing non-phospholipid liposomes, researchers have also designed other types of liposomes. For instance, multilayer cationic liposomes designed using layer-by-layer (LbL) technology hold promise as highly effective drug delivery systems in bone regeneration. Y.A. Brito Barrera and colleagues used LbL technology to create a polyelectrolyte multilayer (PEM) cationic liposome system consisting of chondroitin sulfate (CS) and type I collagen (Col I). They embedded OO4/DOPE liposomes into the terminal layer to create an osteogenic microenvironment. Through transmission electron microscopy (TEM), surface plasmon resonance (SPR), and atomic force microscopy (AFM), the system was successfully synthesized and induced osteogenic differentiation in C2C12 cells [133] (Fig. 7e). These studies indicate that liposomes carrying osteogenic bioactive substances can promote bone regeneration. Non-phospholipid and cationic liposome systems, in comparison to simple liposomes, offer additional advantages.

Efficient bone repair is not limited to drug delivery. Mitochondria are essential for intracellular energy production and are key to maintaining bone homeostatic function [134, 135]. Mitochondrial transfer and transplantation have recently attracted increasing attention in tissue repair and regenerative medicine [136–139]. Keun-Hong Park’s team was able to achieve this by encapsulating mitochondria from osteoblasts (MG63 cells) in cationic liposomes and transferring them into mesenchymal stem cells (MSC). The results showed that MG63 cell-derived mitochondria significantly promoted osteogenic differentiation of MSC and enhanced bone tissue regeneration through activation of the BMP2-Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway and calcium ion import mechanism [140]. Mitochondria as natural organelles show great potential for metastatic therapies [141]. Liposomes can protect mitochondrial function and are suitable carriers. However, because mitochondria are fragile organelles, future liposome-mediated mitochondrial delivery should carefully address mitochondrial viability, membrane integrity, encapsulation conditions, storage stability, and functional potency after delivery.

Liposomes-nanocomposites promote bone regeneration

In addition to altering liposome structures to prolong drug release, combining liposomes with other nanomaterials can also enhance drug delivery effects and even improve the bone-targeting ability of liposomes. Nano-hydroxyapatite (HA), a biomaterial similar to natural bone, has been combined with liposomes for bone regeneration engineering. Junko Hatakeyama and colleagues designed a composite of nano-hydroxyapatite and phosphatidylserine liposomes, demonstrating through studies that this combination could induce bone defect repair [142] However, simple physical mixtures do not always result in optimal bonding. To improve the binding activity between liposomes and nano-hydroxyapatite, liposomes were modified with acidic oligopeptides. The binding activity increased as the modification density increased. In Yali Tao’s team’s experiments, they found that Glu6-modified liposomes at a 5% concentration achieved the highest binding efficiency with HA (56.3%), while unmodified liposomes showed only 5.7% binding. Furthermore, in vivo experiments confirmed that Glu6-modified liposomes exhibited the greatest bone-targeting capability [143].

In addition to such liposome coating systems, liposome-based drug delivery systems can achieve effective controlled release. Although many coating systems have been previously reported, their main limitation is their ability to maintain liposome integrity after drying. To address this issue, Cheng and colleagues developed a hybrid system that combines GelMA hydrogels with drug-loaded liposomes, designing a mechanically enhanced and controlled-release liposome-modified hydrogel as a scaffold for promoting angiogenesis and osteogenesis in bone regeneration. Since the liposomes were encapsulated within the hydrogel, drug release was prolonged. Furthermore, the site of the bone defect still requires some physical support [144]. The GelMA hydrogel provided good mechanical support at bone defect sites, and experiments have demonstrated that GelMA hydrogels possess certain osteoinductive properties [145]. Therefore, the nanocomposite of GelMA hydrogel and liposomes is an effective approach for promoting bone regeneration. Furthermore, Jahanmard et al. embedded liposomes into GelMA hydrogels and applied them as a coating on bone implants using electrospray technology. They demonstrated that this coating technique withstood mechanical stress during surgical replacement and that the GelMA coating remained intact for at least 4 weeks in various environments. A notable advancement over Cheng et al.‘s work was their confirmation that exposed liposomes improved the inflammatory immune microenvironment by expressing low levels of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby promoting the osteogenic differentiation of human MSCs and enhancing bone regeneration [146].

Simple liposome hydrogel hybrids may not meet the growing demand for bone repair. To improve the spatial alignment sequence of bone formation and reduce the risk of heterotopic ossification. Woo-Jin Kim’s team pioneered bone-targeted lipid complex-loaded 3D bioprinted bilayer scaffolds. By encapsulating BMP-2 in a liposome (Fibroplex) based on filipin nanoparticles and functionalizing it with DSS6 for bone tissue-specific targeting. This scaffold increased the concentration of BMP2 localized to the bone defect through sustained release and improved the spatial layout of bone formation [147]. To further improve spatiotemporal control during osteochondral regeneration, Su Jiacan’s group developed a ROS-responsive liposome-integrated 3D-printed bilayer scaffold. In this system, liposomal nanoparticles were designed to deliver dexamethasone sodium phosphate (DS), kartogenin (KGN), and BMP-2 in a stage-specific manner. DS was incorporated into ROS-responsive LPKD nanoparticles through a pH-gradient loading method to enable rapid release and early inflammation suppression. Meanwhile, KGN and BMP-2 were loaded into LPKD and LPB nanoparticles, respectively, by thin-film hydration to support sequential chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation. These liposomal nanoparticles were further incorporated into a 3D-printed bilayer scaffold, enabling localized retention and ultrasound-regulated drug release [95]. This design provided coordinated regulation of inflammation, cartilage repair, and subchondral bone regeneration, offering a representative strategy for liposome-assisted spatiotemporal delivery in osteochondral defect repair.

Liposomes modulating the immune microenvironment promote bone regeneration

In addition to directly encapsulating osteogenic drugs, strategies that promote bone regeneration by modulating the immune microenvironment have gained increasing attention. Bone defects typically exist within a specific pathological microenvironment, where chronic inflammation impairs osteoblast differentiation and compromises vascular regeneration, collectively leading to failed bone healing [148]. A multidimensional synergistic strategy to promote bone regeneration represents a viable approach [149]. In a study focused on improving healing in Wistar rats, it was found that corilagin-loaded liposomes significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory factors and RANKL while increasing the expression of OPG and BMP-2. In vitro experiments demonstrated that bone regeneration at the cranial defect sites of Wistar rats was well-repaired [150]. As macrophage polarization has become a focal point in scientific research, bone tissue engineering strategies that promote bone regeneration by modulating M2 macrophage polarization (M1 macrophages are pro-inflammatory, whereas M2 macrophages exhibit anti-inflammatory and tissue-healing properties) have been proven effective.

It is well known that phosphatidylserine liposomes (PSLs) can mimic the anti-inflammatory effects of apoptotic cells by binding to phosphatidylserine receptors on macrophages. Leveraging this characteristic, Toita et al. developed phosphatidylserine liposomes that simulate apoptotic bodies. They layered cationic protamine and negatively charged phosphatidylserine liposomes with peptides through a step-by-step assembly process, creating a perfectly integrated liposome structure. These liposomes effectively promoted the polarization of macrophages from the M1 to the M2 phenotype and successfully induced M2 macrophage polarization. When these liposome-coated implants were introduced into femoral defect sites, they exhibited excellent osseointegration and bone regeneration capabilities [151]. Lele Wu and colleagues conducted similar research but went a step further by grafting the PSLs with an RGD motif, which is known to promote M2 macrophage polarization. They developed RGD peptide-presenting PSLs (RGD-PSLs). The RGD peptide enhanced the uptake of PSLs by macrophages and significantly inhibited the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced expression of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, as well as CD86 (an M1 marker). Experimental results showed that when the RGD content was 3%, the PSLs significantly enhanced the mRNA expression of Arg-1, FIZZ1, and YM-1, as well as the expression of CD206 (an M2 marker). In a cranial defect model, the RGD-PSLs group demonstrated excellent healing by week 10 compared to the group treated with PSLs alone [152]. These findings demonstrate that simple PSL liposomes can induce M2 macrophage polarization and accelerate the bone regeneration process. However, liposomes grafted with RGD peptides provide an even better strategy for bone regeneration. Additionally, M.N. Michalski’s team developed bisphosphonate-loaded liposomes, which significantly depleted macrophages and osteoclasts in their experiments, leading to a marked increase in CD68 + cells in the bone marrow, a reduction in F4/80 + cells, and a significant increase in trabecular bone [153].

Impaired fracture healing in diabetic patients is associated with the overproduction of ROS in a hyperglycemic environment [154], which leads to mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, inhibits the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, and contributes to the conversion of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) to a pro-inflammatory phenotype [155]. This process is associated with inhibition of AMPK phosphorylation. To address this issue, the Yanzhi Zhao study developed a dual-targeted therapeutic system (DLNPs@HA hydrogel) combining nanoliposomes modified with anti-CD105 antibody and phosphatidylserine encapsulating the AMPK activator alpha-lipoic acid. The system significantly enhanced osteogenic activity and suppressed inflammatory responses by sustained release of nanoparticles for precise delivery to BMSCs and BMDMs. In in vivo experiments in diabetic mice, fracture healing was successfully accelerated [156]. However, the study stayed at the animal level. Jing Wang’s team firstly confirmed the reduced level of glutamine catabolism in macrophages under T2DM conditions by analyzing plasma glutamine concentration and GLS expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of diabetic patients and healthy populations, in combination with a high-fat diet combined with streptozotocin (STZ)-induced T2DM mouse model and an in vitro cell model cultured with high-fat and high-glycemic cultures. And based on this, ALN-modified α-KG-loaded liposomes (α-KG@Cy5.5@ALN-Liposome) were further designed to achieve bone tissue targeting and to replenish macrophages with α-KG thus promoting fracture healing in T2DM mice. The systematic demonstration of targeted regulation of macrophage glutamine metabolism is expected to be a potential strategy for the treatment of delayed fracture healing in T2DM [157]. This will facilitate the translation of clinical results.

Responsive liposomes and gene-targeting liposomes promote bone regeneration

Beyond achieving sustained release, another major challenge lies in enabling intelligent, responsive, on-demand release within the body—a key strategy for repairing large bone defects and promoting bone regeneration [158]. To address this issue, Crasto and colleagues developed novel ultrasound-responsive liposome-rhBMP-2 nanocomposites. These liposomes respond to ultrasound stimulation, altering their structure and releasing the drug upon exposure to external ultrasound. Both in vivo and in vitro experiments confirmed that the release of BMP-2 from the liposomes increased with longer ultrasound exposure and higher intensity, significantly inducing bone regeneration [159]. Designing ultrasound-responsive liposomes allows for controlled release, local drug delivery, increased drug accumulation at the target site, and reduced systemic side effects. As research progresses, the importance of temporal regulation in bone regeneration has garnered increasing attention. Yusen Qiao’s team developed an immune-regulating bone regeneration material, PCLA, based on PLA (polylactic acid) fiber scaffolds combined with COLI (collagen I)/lipids (liposomes) and APY29, creating a glucose-responsive system. In an in vivo study using a diabetic rat model with bone defects, the transition from M1 to M2 macrophage polarization was impeded. However, in the PCLA group, M2 polarization was successfully induced, promoting both bone and vascular regeneration. This effect was achieved by blocking the IREα/NOD-like/NF-κB signaling pathway, establishing an effective microenvironment for bone regeneration [160].

Beyond localized responsive mechanisms, liposomes can also increase drug concentration at the bone defect site through targeted delivery. Gene therapy has been a superior treatment strategy in the past, but efficient gene delivery remains a challenge. Since the development of liposomes as drug delivery carriers, many scientists have considered them to be promising vehicles for gene delivery. Attia’s team utilized transfection technology to transfect BMP-7 into D1MSCs using cationic lipid 2,3-dioleoyl-oxy-1-amine combined with polysorbate 80 for gene delivery, significantly enhancing bone regeneration efficiency [161].To further improve the stability of gene-liposome complexes, Winn and colleagues, while investigating methods to enhance non-viral-mediated gene uptake and expression for bone regeneration, discovered that gene-activated matrix (GAM) technology improved the functional stability and release duration of DNA-liposome complexes in current in vitro studies. This finding presents a new option for gene therapy using liposome delivery to promote bone regeneration. Collectively, these experimental results demonstrate that developing gene-targeting therapies with liposome-delivered DNA is an effective treatment strategy for bone regeneration [162].

Liposome-nanocomposites for the treatment of nonunion and delayed bone healing

Nonunion and delayed bone healing are major complications in bone regeneration and repair, posing significant challenges in orthopedic and trauma surgery. Yanzhi Liu’s team developed a bone-targeting liposome system loaded with salvianolic acid A (SAA), forming a composite material called SAA-BTL. In a delayed bone healing model, SAA-BTL, using pyrophosphorylated cholesterol as the targeting ligand, exhibited a strong affinity for bone tissue in vivo. The localized application of SAA-BTL significantly improved the formation and microstructure of the fracture callus and accelerated the mineralization rate of the callus [163].

Liposomes for osteoporosis

Liposome-targeted delivery of traditional Chinese medicine monomers

OP is characterised by reduced bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture [164]. OP is characterized by impaired bone formation and a deteriorated microenvironment, making it challenging to treat and emerging as a global health challenge [165]. Herbal monomers can effectively inhibit osteoclastogenesis. However, its poor solubility and lack of targeting limit further applications. And it is a promising strategy by encapsulating it in functionalized liposomes. Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) formulations have been shown to effectively treat postmenopausal osteoporosis. Building on this, Le Huang’s team extracted a flavonoid called icariin and encapsulated it in bone-targeting liposomes modified with eight aspartic acid residues (Asp8). Their research found that Asp8-icaritin-liposomes inhibited adipogenesis through the Akt/GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling pathway and significantly promoted bone formation in ovariectomized rats, effectively reversing the osteoporotic condition [166]. Senile OP is a common cause of fractures in elderly patients. To improve bone formation in elderly OP patients, Xiaotao Xing selected quercetin as an ideal agent to eliminate senescent BMSCs and encapsulated it in bone-targeting liposomes decorated with a bone-affinity peptide (DSS6) (Fig. 8a). In a senile OP mouse model, quercetin-based bone-targeted therapy effectively eliminated senescent cells from the bone, restored BMSC function, and promoted bone formation in the aged mouse model, compared to non-targeted therapies [167].

Liposome-targeted delivery gene therapy

Gene therapy is a promising strategy for the treatment of osteoporosis [168], and Ge Zhang’s team encapsulated cationic liposomes of dioleoyltrimethylammonium propane (DOTAP) to target casein kinase-2 interacting protein-1 (encoded by Plekho1, also known as Plekho1), an siRNA with strong osteogenic properties. And then the liposome was successfully ligated to six repeats of aspartic acid, serine, serine (AspSerSer). Using this system, our osteogenic siRNA can be specifically targeted for delivery to bone suggesting increased bone mass and improved bone microstructure in osteoporotic bone [169].Similarly, Chao Liang’s team screened the aptamer CH6 by cell-SELEX and prepared functionalized lipid nanoparticles (CH6-LNPs-siRNA) loaded with Plekho1 siRNA, a family O member of the homology domain of osteoblastogenic plasmalogens. Plekho1 siRNA, reducing bone loss and improving osteoporosis [170]. Additionally, Junjie Li’s team explored postmenopausal osteoporosis (PMOP), which is primarily driven by osteoclast adhesion to the bone matrix and the involvement of various molecules in bone resorption. They designed a dual-targeted liposomal complex composed of alendronate-functionalized liposomes loaded with specific microRNA, termed antagomir@Aln-Lipo, for gene-targeted delivery to bone tissue (Fig. 8b). Alendronate targets hydroxyapatite in the bone matrix, occupying osteoclast adhesion sites to provide a “physical barrier,” while the antagomir precisely targets endogenous microRNA, providing a “genetic signal.” This dual-functional strategy successfully reversed the osteoporotic condition and, for the first time, identified has-miR-378i/mmu-miR-378a-3p as closely associated with PMOP, offering a new approach for future OP treatments [171].

Stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1) has been identified as a major stem cell homing factor, and Chingchang Chen’s team developed alendronate-modified liposome nanoparticles (Aln-Lipo) carrying the SDF-1 gene. Upregulation of the SDF-1 gene by targeted delivery promotes the successful migration of MSC to the bone marrow for OP treatment [172]. Based on the principle that SDF1 is used for homing of C-X-C motif chemokine receptor 4 (CXCR4)-positive hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), Hu Yan generated hybridized NPs by extracting CXCR4 exosomes and fusing CXCR4 exosomes with liposomes carrying Antagomir-188. Targeted delivery to bone improves bone loss [173].

In addition to non-viral vectors, recombinant adeno-associated viruses (rAAV) can be used for bone-targeted gene therapy. The bridging protein Schnurri-3 (SHN3) increases bone loss in osteoporotic mice. Therefore, Yeon-Suk Yang’s team developed an rAAV9 vector carrying an artificial microRNA of shn3 (rAAV9-amiR-shn3) grafted onto a bone-targeting peptide motif (AspSerSer). Experiments showed significant improvement in skeletal bone mechanical properties and reduction in bone loss in osteoporotic mice [174].To increase the binding affinity of the viral vector to bone tissue, octapeptide D8 was inserted into the N-terminal region of the VP2 coat protein on AAV2. The modified virus showed gene transfection efficiency comparable to that of the intact virus, but with a much higher viral copy number in bone tissue. This bone-targeting viral vector should be usable for targeted gene therapy for osteoporosis [175]. Bone-targeted therapy with recombinant adeno-associated viruses shows good promise; however, based on existing therapeutic studies, the bone-targeting effect is not very significant. Enhanced bone targeting with co-targeted liposomes could further improve the therapeutic effect of osteoporosis.

Liposomes improve the acidic microenvironment in osteoporosis

In osteoporosis, the acidic microenvironment produced by an excess of osteoclasts not only causes irreversible bone mineral dissolution but also promotes the production of ROS. Improving the acidic microenvironment may save osteoporosis [176]. Fan Shunwu’s team utilized the chemical response principle of acid-base neutralization by encapsulating sodium bicarbonate in tetracycline-modified negatively charged liposomes. Sodium bicarbonate bone-targeted liposomes specifically enrich the bone surface and form an alkaline protective layer, which generates carbon dioxide when osteoclasts attach and secrete acid, and undergoes a self-feedback pH-responsive release. Interestingly, this effect of osteoclast apoptosis produces a large number of extracellular vesicles, on which highly expressed RANK binds to serum RANKL (the ligand of RANK, a classical osteoclast-stimulating factor) with no biological effect and undergoes a sponge-like adsorption, thus decreasing the RANKL concentration in the local bone microenvironment, which can further achieve a far-reaching osteoclast inhibitory effect [177] .

Although alendronate-functionalized liposomes have bone-targeted delivery capabilities, they are still inevitably taken up by the liver. The interaction between organs is a new research hotspot and may have an important role [178]. The gut-bone axis and the liver-bone axis have been shown to influence osteoporosis [179, 180]. Yunhao You’s team capitalized on this by developing bone-targeted liposomes encapsulating arginine and metformin for the treatment of osteoporosis. In addition to targeted bone delivery, liposomes accumulated in the liver stimulate hepatic secretion of lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), which in turn promotes bone remodeling by engaging the hepatic bone axis. This dual mechanism transforms side effects into beneficial outcomes and synergistically enhances therapeutic efficacy [181].

Liposomes for osteoarthritis

Liposomes alleviate osteoarthritis symptoms by promoting joint lubrication

OA is a common chronic disease, and current treatment options are primarily centered on pharmacological interventions and joint replacement surgeries [182]. Periprosthetic inflammatory osteolysis after arthroplasty is also a major clinical challenge. Curcumin liposomes can reverse inflammation while lubricating the joint [183]. Intra-articular (IA) drug delivery can reduce systemic toxicity [184]. But NSAIDs and corticosteroids administered via IA injections are often rapidly cleared from the joint cavity. Furthermore, IA drug delivery is limited by excessively high local drug concentrations, the dense and highly negatively charged extracellular matrix (ECM) of cartilage, and the uneven distribution of diseased chondrocytes [185–188].Future OA treatments should focus on joint lubrication and cartilage regeneration. Due to the lubricating properties of phospholipids, they can be used to reduce friction within the joint. However, not all small-molecule phospholipids can provide consistent effects. Raya Sorki’s team utilized surface force balance (SFB) to expand and load 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) bilayers, comparing their lubricating performance to that of surface-attached DSPC monolayer liposomes. They found that DSPC bilayers were easily disrupted under shear stress, while liposomes, due to their dense packing, exhibited ultra-low friction coefficients (µ ≈ 10⁻⁴) under pressures as high as 180 atmospheres, demonstrating excellent stability and lubricating properties [189].

However, liposomes are not without drawbacks; their small size makes them susceptible to rapid clearance from the joint. To address this, Bordon Gregor and colleagues developed a zinc ion-based liposome drug delivery system. This system induces the aggregation of negatively charged liposomes with zinc ions, extending the release of rapamycin (RAPA) (Fig. 9a). The resulting irreversible aggregates, which reach sizes of around 100 μm, increase retention within the joint. In experiments with ex vivo pig cartilage, the liposomes demonstrated excellent friction protection and cartilage preservation, and they inhibited fibrosis of fibroblasts, effectively alleviating OA symptoms [190]. However, this study only validated the system ex vivo, and its in vivo effects remain unknown. Additionally, the lubricating properties of phospholipid-based liposomes are limited. Building on this research, Cooper B.G. demonstrated that lipid-based boundary layers formed on hydrogels containing liposomes could provide lubrication. One of the main causes of pain in OA patients is insufficient joint lubrication [191].

In subsequent experiments, these issues were addressed. Yiting Lei and colleagues designed a dynamically crosslinked hydrogel encapsulating liposomes to counteract shear forces and enhance therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 9b). Their experiments showed that celecoxib (CLX)-loaded liposomes embedded in a dynamically covalent hyaluronic acid (HA) hydrogel formed a drug-loaded hydrogel. This liposome-hydrogel nanocomposite not only provided sustained drug release to alleviate inflammation but also formed a unique boundary layer that responded to shear stress. This boundary layer maintained the anabolic-catabolic balance, offering long-term protection against cartilage wear and mitigating OA progression. The dynamic crosslinking network allowed the hydrogel to reorganize in response to shear forces, while the HA matrix enabled the accumulation of liposomal micro-reservoirs at the sliding surface, forming a boundary layer that provided stable lubrication. Additionally, the hydrated shell formed around the hydrogel delayed the breakdown process, further contributing to sustained lubrication [192, 193]. Thus, liposome-hydrogel mixtures encapsulating drugs can act as shear-responsive boundary lubricants and drug delivery vehicles, inhibiting inflammation and providing boundary lubrication in OA treatment, showing potential for both drug relief and boundary lubrication.

Beyond mixing liposomes with hydrogels, Yiting Lei’s team also discovered that microfluidic technology and photopolymerisation could generate rapamycin-loaded cationic liposome-HA hydrogels in the form of microspheres. These microspheres exhibited the ability to roll smoothly within the matrix and continuously expose liposomes on the outer surface due to frictional wear, forming a spontaneously renewable boundary layer. This system showed excellent joint lubrication and delayed OA progression in an in vivo OA treatment model, preserving cartilage structure [194] (Fig. 9c). Furthermore, cationic liposomes can target the negatively charged cartilage through electrostatic interactions, releasing rapamycin—a known autophagy activator—to maintain cellular homeostasis by enhancing autophagy [195]. Experiments showed that these cationic liposome-HA microspheres provided long-term effective lubrication and reduced joint wear.

Liposome-nanocomposites and responsive liposomes improve cartilage repair

Improving joint lubrication can alleviate pain symptoms and protect cartilage, but addressing the root cause of OA requires promoting cartilage repair and regeneration. Cartilage plays a critical role in absorbing joint load, and its damage or degeneration affects millions of patients. However, functional hyaline cartilage is difficult to regenerate. Promoting cartilage regeneration while preventing the deterioration of remaining cartilage is one of the key challenges in clinical treatment [196]. Some studies even suggest that local anesthetics, such as bupivacaine, may have toxic effects on chondrocytes [197]. However, it has been shown that liposome-encapsulated bupivacaine causes less damage to chondrocytes compared to bupivacaine alone [198]. From a preventive perspective, encapsulating local anesthetics in liposomes offers a new strategy for cartilage protection.However, cartilage can be destroyed by prolonged excessive load and wear. To promote cartilage regeneration, Eva Filová and colleagues designed a nanofiber scaffold composed of liposomes, basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF), and insulin in a cell-free polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix. The liposomes improved the scaffold’s elasticity, reduced its water content, and increased mechanical stiffness, closely matching the mechanical properties of natural cartilage and promoting the repair of worn cartilage regions [199].

Moreover, liposomes, as excellent drug delivery systems, offer new hope for improving joint cartilage repair. Previous studies have shown that adenosine, acting through A2A receptors (A2AR), maintains cartilage homeostasis, delays chondrocyte senescence, and inhibits the progression of OA [200, 201]. However, the challenge has been delivering adenosine to the joint while avoiding early clearance and maximizing therapeutic efficacy. Carmen Corciulo and colleagues developed a liposome suspension containing adenosine or the selective A2AR agonist CGS21680, which was injected into the joint cavity of obese mice. This approach inhibited terminal chondrocyte differentiation, repaired cartilage, and reduced knee swelling [202]. This suggests that liposome-based delivery of adenosine/A2AR agonists is a reliable strategy for cartilage repair.To overcome the limitations of intra-articular injections, Guoqing Li’s team developed a system that encapsulated teriparatide (PTH [1–34]) in liposomes (Lipo@PTH [1–34]) and incorporated these liposomes into a gelatin-gallic acid (GGA) solution. Using transglutaminase (TGase)-mediated crosslinking, they formed an injectable hydrogel that could be administered into the joint, forming in situ without hindering the movement of mice. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that the GLP hydrogel promoted the proliferation of ATDC5 cells and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, protecting cartilage from degradation by regulating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [203]. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have good membrane fusion properties.Jun Yong Kim successfully synthesized an Ab-Hybrid using Col2A1 antibody-modified liposomes loaded with nicotinamide and hybridized with transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) overexpressing EVs. In vivo, Ab-Hybrids have shown excellent cartilage-protective and anti-inflammatory effects, with targeting and long-lasting properties [204].

Shunli Wu and colleagues developed a ROS-responsive thioacetal (TK)-based liposome for constructing an ultrasound-triggered in situ nanocomposite hydrogel, which carried the chondrogenic inducer KGN, BMSCs, thrombin, and the ultrasound sensitizer PpIX. Under ultrasound stimulation, ROS was produced, partially breaking down the liposomes, and the hydrogel filled the cartilage defect. By adjusting the ultrasound intensity, ROS production and KGN release were controlled. More importantly, the ROS levels and KGN release in the hydrogel’s microenvironment significantly promoted the chondrogenic differentiation of BMSCs via the Smad5/mTOR signaling pathway, facilitating cartilage regeneration in rat models of cartilage defects [205]. To overcome the complex pathogenesis of OA and develop multifunctional biomaterials. Shi Qin’s team prepared injectable hydrogel microspheres (LFDCS) with a core of chondroitin sulfate (Chs)/methacryloylated sericin (SerMA), grafted with folic acid (FA) and 78c-modified liposomes (78c@Lipo-FA) by thin-film hydration and microfluidic methods. These liposomal microspheres possessed articular cartilage lubrication ability and could also improve cartilage regeneration function by down-regulating PI3K/AKT signaling and inflammatory response [206].

Tissue-engineered cartilage transplantation can more quickly and efficiently produce hyaline cartilage. Autologous osteochondral grafting system (OATS) repairs injuries by transplanting healthy osteochondral bone, but 40% of patients suffer from poor interface adhesion. Jiaqi Zhou’s team developed an immunomodulatory adhesion hydrogel (10% PHE-Gel@DMOG-Lip) by synergistically designing an adhesive hydrogel (10% PHE-Gel) with a liposome (DMOG-Lip) containing dimethyl oxaloacetylglycine (DMOG). The 10% PHE gel has excellent interfacial toughness and promotes cartilage anabolism by activating the HIF-α pathway and inhibiting the TNF pathway. This composite hydrogel provides a comprehensive therapeutic strategy to enhance OATS repair in tissue engineering from the interfacial repair, inflammation modulation and other directions [207].The post-transplantation tissue response can affect outcomes. Within 11 days of cartilage transplantation, macrophages typically exhibit an M1 phenotype (inflammatory), while M2 macrophages (anti-inflammatory/tissue healing) do not increase until days 11–14. M2 macrophages promote a significant increase in type II collagen in chondrocytes. To address this issue and enhance cartilage growth post-transplantation, Yuko Fujihara’s research found that incorporating clodronate liposomes into the transplanted cartilage could deplete M1 macrophages and increase the proportion of M2 macrophages. This accumulation of cartilage matrix improved post-transplantation outcome [208]. Therefore, it is evident that liposomes have great potential in promoting cartilage repair and regeneration. Liposomes can aid in cartilage repair through drug encapsulation, improving nanomaterial physical properties, and countering inflammation, thereby delaying the progression of OA.

Liposome coatings, separation, and targeting to promote cartilage regeneration

To mitigate cartilage degradation, the key lies in targeted drug delivery to chondrocytes, prolonging drug half-life, and enhancing stability and permeability. Yazmin A. Brito Barrera and colleagues used HA as a polyanion and collagen type I (Col) as a polycation to form polyelectrolyte multilayers (PEM) mimicking the ECM. They combined the PEM system with dexamethasone-loaded liposomes to achieve targeted drug delivery. This liposome coating was applied to traditional bone implants and inserted into cartilage defect sites. After liposome adsorption, the PEM system exhibited a positive zeta potential and reduced wettability, which promoted both cell adhesion and the proliferation of C3H10T1/2 pluripotent mouse embryonic fibroblasts. This approach stimulated osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation of stem cells more effectively than the PEM system alone [209]. While liposomes are widely known for their role as drug delivery carriers, this is not their only function. Aiguo Xie and colleagues developed uniform, small, and highly stable CD146 liposome magnetic beads (CD146 LMB) for the separation of CD146 adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs). These separated cells were used to fill worn joint cartilage areas. After chondrogenic induction, the cells showed significant increases in the expression of cartilage markers Sox9, collagen II, and aggrecan at both protein and mRNA levels, with CD146 ADSCs expressing higher levels than ADSCs alone. These cells successfully promoted joint cartilage regeneration, providing new ideas and directions for liposome applications in cartilage regeneration engineering [210].

In recent years, researchers have turned to natural crops to develop more biocompatible materials. Studies have shown that liposomes made from natural rapeseed lecithin can act as drug delivery agents, transporting drugs to chondrocytes for therapeutic effect. Lecithin has good biocompatibility [211]. However, the safety of these liposome components remains to be fully assessed. To evaluate their safety, Bianchi Arnaud and colleagues conducted in vitro experiments, demonstrating that at concentrations below 500 µg/mL, rapeseed lecithin liposomes had no toxic effects on chondrocytes, indicating they are a safe and effective drug delivery system for cartilage cells [212]. However, these results are based on in vitro studies, and the in vivo effects on chondrocytes have yet to be proven. In 2022, Velot Émilie and colleagues used natural rapeseed oil lecithin liposomes to deliver TGF-β1 to rat chondrocytes. The results showed that TGF-β1-loaded rapeseed liposomes did not alter the chondrocyte phenotype and exhibited high biocompatibility in vivo. Additionally, the controlled release of TGF-β1 from the liposomes, even at low doses, significantly improved chondrocyte response. Velot Émilie and her team confirmed the safety and efficacy of rapeseed oil lecithin liposomes for drug delivery both in vitro and in vivo [213]. Beyond natural rapeseed oil lecithin liposomes, which exhibit high biocompatibility and can target cartilage for drug delivery, Émilie Velot and her team further encapsulated hop extract in liposomes for targeted anti-inflammatory therapy in chondrocytes, improving OA symptoms [214]. These studies collectively demonstrate that liposome-based drug delivery systems have enormous potential to extend retention in the joint, penetrate the ECM, achieve specific delivery to diseased chondrocytes, and reduce off-target drug toxicity [184, 185, 215].

Cartilage regeneration is a long-term process that requires long-term retention of drugs to function. decorin-derived peptides can be used as cartilage-binding peptide-modified lipid nanoparticles to improve precise targeting and durable retention in cartilage [216]. Bionanomics is the most important strategy, and targeting drug delivery through membrane fusion is one of the most effective strategies. siyang cao utilized bioengineered cell membrane encapsulation technology to construct FCM@Lipos-RSG by encapsulating the functionalized cell membrane (FCM) of pre-treated chondrocytes on the surface of liposomes (Lipos-RSG) containing trans-rosterone (RSG), in the expectation that the “Trojan horse” strategy, the liposomes could target chondrocytes in the articular cartilage to prolong the retention time of the drug in the joint cavity and enhance the therapeutic effect; at the same time, RSG could be utilized to inhibit lipid peroxidation associated with iron death, reduce the inflammatory response, and ameliorate the metabolic disorders of chondrocytes. This study introduces an innovative and efficient method to design bioengineered cell membrane-encapsulated liposomes, which offers a promising prospect for OA management [217]. The incidence of gouty arthritis has increased as the standard of living has improved. Monosodium urate (MSU) crystals deposited in joints and periarticular tissues can trigger an acute inflammatory response. Melatonin modulates macrophage metabolic levels and alters pathogenic macrophage function. Engineered macrophage membrane-encapsulated melatonin-loaded liposomes (MLT-MLP) can passively target macrophages to deliver melatonin and alleviate the inflammatory phenotype of gouty arthritis [218].

In addition, mitochondrial transplantation has emerged as a potential strategy for restoring cellular bioenergetics and supporting tissue regeneration [219]. Hye-Ryoung Kim’s team established homologous mitochondrial capsules (FMCs) consisting of neutral lipids (PE), cationic lipids (DOTAP), aromatic lipids (Liss Rhod PE), and three types of liposomes (FMC0, FMC1, and FMC2) by designing and synthesizing a membrane fusion-based transfer strategy. The results demonstrated that FMC delivered chondrocytes faster and reduced inflammatory manifestations, which promoted cartilage regeneration [220]. Mitochondrial transplantation and transfer strategies will be the core of focus for future tissue regeneration research and development [221].

Liposomes for rheumatoid arthritis

Liposome-encapsulated glucocorticoids for the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis

RA is usually characterized by joint swelling, synovial hyperplasia, and ultimately cartilage destruction and joint deformity [222]. Current therapies aim to achieve complete and durable remission, but many patients still fail to achieve this goal [223]. Given these limitations, researchers have shifted their focus to drug delivery strategies that can target the synovial cavity and reduce other tissue side effects. Liposomes can overcome the rapid clearance of drugs within the synovium due to their multiple advantages including targeting [224]. Dexamethasone (DEX) is a commonly used anti-inflammatory glucocorticoid widely used in the treatment of RA. However, long-term use of DEX can produce side effects. There is an urgent need to improve DEX target specificity. Many researchers have developed DEX-encapsulated liposomes (DEX-Lip) and studied their effects [225].

As early as 1979, N.C. Phillips and colleagues reported that liposome-encapsulated dexamethasone significantly enhanced the drug’s anti-RA effects [105]. More recently, Mengdi Jia and colleagues found that DEX-Lip significantly downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines in serum, including tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β). Moreover, DEX-Lip had no significant impact on body weight, glycemic control, or hematological parameters in RA rats during treatment. Compared to free DEX, DEX-Lip showed lower toxicity, improved drug distribution in the body, and exhibited better anti-inflammatory efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo studies, making it a safe drug delivery system [226]. Modification of liposomes with targeted ligands such as silicic acid improves the distribution of dexamethasone in RA joints [227]. Repeated local injections predispose to synovial cavity infection. Hydrogels were prepared by ionic cross-linking of chondroitin sulfate and gelatin and encapsulated with HA-modified dexamethasone-phosphate sodium (DSP)-loaded liposomes. It can be injected locally into the joint cavity and then retained for long periods of time to inhibit inflammation and reduce cartilage damage [228]. In fact, liposomes are not only effective for delivering glucocorticoids but have also demonstrated improved efficacy and lower toxicity when encapsulating NSAIDs and other antimalarial drugs. This highlights the potential of liposome-based drug delivery systems for RA treatment.

Modified liposomes for targeted rheumatoid arthritis treatment

Although conventional liposomes reduce the potential for rapid drug clearance, they still face challenges in penetrating biological barriers. Therefore, choosing the right liposome size and altering liposome properties are critical. Jolanda M. van den Hoven and colleagues report that by altering the physicochemical properties of liposomes, it is possible to optimize their ability to penetrate biological barriers and be retained at the target site. Smaller liposomes are better suited for passive targeting. Modifying the surface of liposomes can selectively deliver drugs to specific target cells in RA [229]. Their study builds on the use of liposomes in the treatment of RA and further explores how liposomes can be selected and formulated to maximize therapeutic efficacy, demonstrating the potential of liposomes as drug carriers.

Active targeting is one of the most sought-after strategies in drug development. Rakeshchandra R. Meka and colleagues used a novel peptide ligand called ART-2 (CKPFDRALC) and developed a new drug delivery system based on this ligand and liposomes. When intravenously injected into adjuvant-induced arthritis rats, the ligand successfully targeted inflamed joints, enabling the drug to exert its effect precisely at the desired location. The study showed that ART-2-coated liposomes encapsulating the anti-RA drug DEX were more effective at inhibiting RA progression than DEX-Lip or free DEX. Additionally, the ART-2-coated liposomes exhibited greater safety, likely due to reduced drug exposure at non-target sites. Therefore, this peptide-targeted therapy outperforms conventional administration methods in both efficacy and safety, making it a promising approach for RA treatment [223]. Since synovial fibroblasts (SF) play a crucial role in the progression of RA, Changcheng You and colleagues focused on targeting fibroblasts. Their team developed a liposome system targeting SFs that encapsulated the NF-κB blocking peptide (NBD peptide) in HAP-lipo/NBD (Fig. 10A). In both in vitro and in vivo experiments, HAP-lipo/NBD significantly inhibited NF-κB activation, inflammatory cytokine release, and SF migration in response to enzymatic stimulation, thereby improving synovitis. This presents a novel approach for treating RA [230]. Ziwei Li utilized the function of folic acid to target macrophages by modifying it on immunoliposomes loaded with ginsenoide compound K (GCK), which improved synovial hyperplasia and pathological changes in the spleen by oral administration. Avoiding the risk of infection caused by local injection [231].

Macrophage inflammation is an important cause of RA, and improving macrophage function can effectively mitigate the progression of RA [232]. MSCs have various advantages such as being economical, easily accessible and natural. Based on this, Malan’s team utilized the fusion of MSC membranes and liposomes to develop a bionic liposome (MSCsome), which can effectively improve the targeting efficiency of drugs on diseased joints. At the same time, the interaction between lymphocyte function-associated antigen-1 and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 enhanced the affinity of MSCsome for polarized macrophages. It effectively reversed the joint inflammation in advanced rheumatoid off stage and alleviated the disease progression [233].

Stimuli-responsive liposomes for rheumatoid arthritis treatment

Beyond active targeting, stimuli responsiveness is also considered an effective strategy for treating RA. Responsive liposomes can undergo structural disruption in specific environments, resulting in localized drug release. Pathological studies have shown that in the inflammatory environment of RA, activated macrophages express folate receptors and exhibit elevated ROS levels. Based on this microenvironment, Chen M. and colleagues developed liposomes co-encapsulating methotrexate (MTX) and catalase (CAT), termed FOL-MTX and CAT-L. These liposomes actively targeted activated macrophages and were responsive to hydrogen peroxide. Increased intracellular hydrogen peroxide (the primary source of ROS) diffused into the liposomes, where CAT catalyzed its breakdown into oxygen and water. The sustained oxygen generation within the liposomes eventually disrupted their structure, releasing the encapsulated MTX. The researchers studied this responsive liposome drug delivery system in terms of in vitro drug release, cellular uptake, cytotoxicity, as well as in vivo pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, efficacy, and safety. In vitro results showed that FOL-MTX and CAT-L exhibited sufficient ROS-sensitive drug release, increased cellular uptake through folate-mediated endocytosis, and higher cytotoxicity against activated RAW264.7 cells. Additionally, in vivo results demonstrated that PEGylated liposomes extended circulation time, prevented rapid clearance from the joint cavity, and enhanced MTX accumulation in inflamed joints in collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mice, leading to improved therapeutic outcomes and reduced toxicity to major organs [234] (Fig. 10B).

In addition to ROS-responsive liposomes, Dongyi Wan and colleagues developed pH-responsive liposomes (Pae-LS) by co-conjugating folic acid and PEG to liposomes loaded with paeoniflorin (Pae), a natural extract. These liposomes inhibited STAT1 phosphorylation, reduced the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), and iNOS, while significantly increasing levels of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-206 and CD6 through increased p-STAT10. The pH responsiveness allowed localized drug release at inflammation sites, promoting the phenotypic switch of RAW 264.7 macrophages and suppressing synovial inflammation and hyperplasia [235]. Abnormal proliferation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes is also a hindrance to effective treatment of RA. Zheng Xiu’s team prepared PH-responsive liposomes loaded with MTX-human serum albumin (HSA) by using cholesterol hemisuccinate (CHEM). The nanocarrier successfully induced mitochondrial dysfunction, promoted apoptosis of fibroblast-like synoviocytes, and alleviated bone erosion. Liposomal drugs to ameliorate the abnormal proliferation of fibroblasts are also an important therapeutic strategy for RA [236].

Responsiveness and targeting are two key features of liposomes, and they can be combined for enhanced efficacy. Beyond NBD peptides, iRGD peptides can also target HUVECs and RA joints. Zhen Gan and colleagues developed iRGD-modified echo-liposomes (iRGD MTX ICG ELIP) containing MTX and indocyanine green (ICG) (Fig. 10C). iRGD peptides exhibited strong targeting of RA joints, and in vitro ultrasound exposure increased drug release with greater ultrasound intensity and duration, demonstrating good ultrasound responsiveness [237]. In an HUVECs model, the uptake efficiency of iRGD MTX ICG ELIP was 1.89 times higher than non-targeted MTX ICG ELIP (P < 0.05). In vivo imaging of RA joints in mice showed significantly higher fluorescence intensity in iRGD MTX ICG ELIP compared to non-targeted liposomes. CCK8 assays indicated that iRGD MTX ICG ELIP combined with ultrasound reduced MH7A cell viability to 32.49 ± 3.04%, significantly lower than non-ultrasound-treated iRGD MTX ICG ELIP cells (P < 0.05). This combination of targeted liposomes with ultrasound responsiveness achieved the strongest inhibitory effect on MH7A cells, highlighting its potential in RA treatment [238]. Haohan Wu and colleagues conducted similar research, developing iRGD peptide-functionalized echo-liposomes (iELPs) loaded with MTX and the fluorescent probe ICG using the thin-film hydration method. These liposomes, after targeting αvβ3 integrin-expressing endothelial cells, enabled near-infrared imaging and ultrasound-controlled drug release. As an excellent theranostic nanoplatform, iELPs show great potential for treating RA [237]. These findings demonstrate that by leveraging the targeting and responsiveness of liposomes, liposome-based drug delivery systems have significant potential for treating RA, providing better therapeutic outcomes.

Liposomes for bone-related tumour

Liposomes for targeted bone tumour treatment

Bone tumours, including benign and malignant types, pose distinct challenges in clinical treatment. Common benign bone tumours, such as enchondromas and osteochondromas, often require no treatment or can be thoroughly excised via surgery or minimally invasive procedures [239]; Malignant bone tumours, such as osteosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, and Ewing sarcoma, typically demand multimodal treatment approaches [240, 241]. Simply delivering drugs presents challenges such as instability within the body and the inability to penetrate physiological barriers [242]. But the development of various nanomaterials has brought new hope to bone tumour treatment [243]. Liposomes have become one of the most commonly used carriers for cancer-targeted therapies [244].In recent decades, multiple liposome formulations have been approved for clinical use [245]. For example, tumour-targeting drugs or synthetic polymers can be grafted onto liposomes to improve drug precision, with the modified liposomes interrupting the “vicious” cycle between tumour cell proliferation and bone resorption [117]. Targeting is one of the biggest advantages of liposomes, and bone-targeted liposomes can be synthesised by post-modification [166, 246] or co-assembly of pre-modified lipids [172, 247, 248]. Pre-modified liposomes are currently the most commonly used strategy. Targeted ligands (ALN) can be reacted with DSPE-PEG-NHS to obtain DSPE-PEG-ALN, and functionalized lipids are co-assembled with DSPE-PEG and cholesterol to prepare targeted lipids by thin-film hydration. Alternatively, phosphorylated cholesterol was co-assembled with lecithin to obtain bone-targeted liposome [163].

The most commonly used anti-bone tumour drugs are bisphosphonates, which inhibit osteoclast activity and reduce blood calcium levels, alleviating conditions such as malignant hypercalcemia and pain caused by osteolytic bone tumours [249]. However, bisphosphonates are not without limitations, as they can cause side effects like kidney disease or osteonecrosis of the jaw [250], necessitating modifications for better use. Oula P. Medina and colleagues covalently attached alendronate sodium to the surface of liposomes and labelled them with the fluorescent dye indocyanine green. Fluorescence scanning demonstrated that this novel alendronate liposome formulation enhanced bone-targeting capabilities several-fold, leading to better accumulation in tumour cells [251]. However, these liposomes are still subject to phagocytosis by macrophages in the bloodstream, potentially affecting therapeutic efficacy. Ninh M. La-Beck and colleagues encapsulated zoledronate and alendronate in liposomes to enhance the anticancer efficacy of cytotoxic chemotherapy and adoptive T-cell immunotherapy in mouse cancer models. To further improve drug delivery, they PEGylated the liposomes to impart a “stealth” characteristic, enhancing permeability and retention while increasing the accumulation of drug payloads in tumour tissues. This approach resulted in a long-circulating, leak-free, and highly targeted PEGylated bisphosphonate liposome, demonstrating excellent therapeutic potential. Preclinical studies showed that PEGylated alendronate liposomes (PLAs) could passively target and accumulate in tumours, proving to be a promising delivery vehicle with significant potential for clinical translation [252]. In addition, Yu Chen’s team prepared and decorated nanoliposomes containing anti-tumor and anti-OP drugs by preparing biotin-streptavidin bonds, which exhibited excellent targeting ability to inhibit the progression of osteosarcoma and restore bone destruction in mouse model lesions of tibial defects and in situ osteosarcoma [33] (Fig. 11a).

Osteosarcoma, the most common type of bone cancer, continues to show stagnant survival rates. Liposomal nanobiotics may break this stalemate. In addition to synthetic drugs, traditional herbal components have also gained attention in anti-bone tumour research. Honokiol (HNK), a natural compound, has been shown in various studies to inhibit tumour cell growth through multiple pathways, including suppression of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling and induction of apoptosi [253, 254]. HNK can also be used in osteosarcoma treatment. However, due to its poor targeting, HNK’s inhibitory effect on osteosarcoma is limited, often accompanied by significant side effects. To address this issue, Xiangxiang Zhang and colleagues developed HNK-loaded liposomes modified with hyaluronic acid-phospholipid conjugates (HA-DOPE) to treat osteosarcoma based on HA-CD44 interactions (Fig. 11b). In both in vitro and in vivo experiments, HA-DOPE-modified HNK-loaded liposomes successfully targeted the tumour, inhibited tumour cell growth, and showed no toxicity to normal tissues, making them a promising anti-tumour agent [255]. However, single-targeted drugs may not be as effective as dual-targeted drugs. On this basis, the bone-targeting fraction ALN was coupled with HA, a ligand for CD44. Liposomes with dual-targeting function were prepared in combination with iRGD tumour-penetrating peptide, loaded with adriamycin for targeted therapy of osteosarcoma. In an in situ OS nude mouse model, it showed significant tumour growth inhibition and prolonged survival time, and the addition of iRGD further enhanced tumour killing [256].Similar to HNK, resveratrol has been found to inhibit tumour proliferation and metastasis by regulating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway. However, its poor water solubility and low bioavailability limit its use. To overcome this, Wen Ting Zhu and colleagues designed folic acid-modified liposomes to encapsulate resveratrol. In a mouse osteosarcoma model, resveratrol accumulation in human osteosarcoma cells (143B) significantly increased. By inhibiting JAK2/STAT3 signaling, the liposome-encapsulated resveratrol effectively suppressed tumour cell proliferation and metastasis without causing harm to the mice’s body weight, liver, or kidneys [257]. Liposomal resveratrol thus offers a new approach to osteosarcoma treatment.

Curcumin, the active ingredient in turmeric, is renowned for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and osteogenic properties. Naboneeta Sarkar and her team encapsulated curcumin in bone-targeting liposomes and incorporated them into a 3D-printed (3DP) calcium phosphate scaffold designed with specific porosity. Liposome-encapsulated curcumin released from the 3DP scaffold exhibited significant cytotoxicity against osteosarcoma cells while promoting the vitality and proliferation of osteoblasts (healthy bone cells). This dual functionality presents a novel bone regeneration material that can simultaneously eliminate osteosarcoma cells and promote bone regeneration at defect sites [140].

However, drug resistance has emerged in the treatment of osteosarcoma using herbal ingredients alone as well as drugs such as adriamycin. Gene therapy needs to be emphasized. Previous studies have shown that EphA2 [258] and JIP1 [259] can increase the target-sensitive properties of drugs against osteosarcoma. Fateme Haghiralsadat’s team loaded JNK Interacting Protein 1 (JIP1) siRNA with EphA2-targeted dodecapeptide-modified liposomes (Fig. 11c).The dual-targeting nature of extracellular anti-EphA2 and intracellular anti-JNK Interacting Protein 1 [JIP1] was exploited to combine loading of DOX. Targeted nano-formulations reduced JIP1 expression in MG-63 and Saos-2 osteosarcoma cells. Targeted delivery resulted in 78.6% cytotoxicity in OS cells, much higher than free DOX (50%). Targeted co-delivery resulted in a 42% reduction in siRNA target JIP1 mRNA and a 46% reduction in JIP1 levels. Overcomes the problem of drug resistance in osteosarcoma [260].liposomes are gaining attention for their potential in the diagnosis and targeted treatment of both primary and metastatic bone tumours. Leveraging the advantages of liposomes, including low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, extended half-life, protection of active groups, cell membrane mimicry, and safety, offers potential breakthroughs in the targeted treatment of osteosarcoma [261].

Responsive liposomes for bone tumour treatment

Temperature-Responsive liposomes for bone tumour treatment

Liposomes are widely used as targeted drug delivery carriers in the treatment of solid tumours. While they reduce systemic side effects, the accumulation of drugs at the tumour site and their bioavailability remain limited [262]. Thermosensitive liposomes have a significant advantage in this regard, as they can release drugs in response to local hyperthermia, combining the benefits of both thermal therapy and liposome-mediated drug delivery [263]. At normal body temperature, hydrophilic drugs cannot easily diffuse across the liposome membrane. However, when liposomes circulate through the bloodstream and reach a heated target organ, local high temperatures increase the mobility of phospholipid molecules, altering the liposome membrane structure and increasing permeability. This allows the encapsulated drugs to diffuse in large quantities into the target organ, achieving high local drug concentrations and controlled release. In areas without heat exposure, drug release is minimal.

Thermosensitive liposomes enable lower drug concentrations in unheated organs, resulting in fewer side effects. When localized heating increases the temperature to 41–42 °C, the liposomes rapidly release the drug. Combining regional hyperthermia with thermosensitive liposomes enhances therapeutic efficacy while reducing the side effects of systemic chemotherapy. Local hyperthermia also improves blood perfusion, increases vascular permeability, and enhances tumour cell sensitivity to chemotherapy [264]. Additionally, the size of thermosensitive liposome vesicles significantly affects drug release, with smaller vesicles (50–200 nm) releasing drugs at a higher rate [265]. Despite the potential, the development and application of thermosensitive liposomes still require further exploration in areas such as drug metabolism kinetics, temperature control, and precise drug release, to achieve greater therapeutic efficacy [266]. The development of thermosensitive liposomal DOX could have a significant impact on the treatment of bone tumours [267]. Heliang Song and colleagues conjugated Brij78 (polyoxyethylene stearyl ether) with pamidronate (Pa). They incorporated Pa-Brij78 into DPPC/Chol liposomes and encapsulated DOX within these liposomes. This formulation exhibited strong binding affinity to hydroxyapatite (HA), the primary component of bone. In a model of metastatic bone tumours, the Pa-liposome-DOX thermosensitive complex showed significantly higher drug release and cytotoxicity at 42 °C compared to 37 °C, demonstrating that heat-triggered drug release enhanced the anti-bone tumour effect [268].

PH-responsive liposomes for bone tumour treatment

In the tumour microenvironment, the pH is usually lower than in normal tissues. Therefore, in addition to thermosensitive liposomes, pH-responsive liposomes play a crucial role in bone tumour treatment. Diêgo Dos Santos Ferreira and colleagues developed a pH-responsive liposome with a size of approximately 170 nm that had a high affinity for hydroxyapatite and could carry DOX at a concentration of 2 mg/mL. Their experiments showed that in a tumour microenvironment with a pH of 5, about 66% of the drug was released within 6 h, whereas at a pH of 7.4, only 22% was released within 8 h. This demonstrates that the drug could be rapidly released in the tumour microenvironment while maintaining high affinity for bone tissue [247]. The team further developed pH-sensitive liposomes containing DOX, coated with nanometric aluminum nitrate (ALN). Alendronate-coated pH-sensitive liposomes containing DOX (SpHL-AL-DOX) composed by DOPE, CHEMS, and DSPE-PEG3400-AL (5.7:3.8:0.5 molar ratio and DOX at 2 mg/Ml). In comparative experiments, this novel liposome formulation was evaluated alongside non-targeted liposomes and free DOX for its efficacy in a mouse model of breast cancer bone metastasis. The study revealed that modifications to the drug delivery system, including pH-sensitive liposome encapsulation and ALN-based bone-targeting surface modifications, were key to achieving enhanced antitumour activity. Results showed that the surface modification of pH-sensitive liposomes with the bone-targeting ligand ALN significantly improved tumour specificity. In comparison to non-targeted liposomes and free DOX, tumour volume reduction in the metastatic bone tumour model increased from 35% to 70%, doubling the efficacy. This suggests that targeted pH-responsive liposomes enhance the therapeutic effect of antitumour treatments, while their higher specificity, compared to free DOX or non-targeted liposomes, helps reduce cardiotoxicity and improve survival rates [269].

Redox-responsive liposomes for bone tumour treatment

Previous studies have shown that glutathione is highly expressed in bone tumour cells, especially in osteosarcoma cells. Yingying Chi’s team used a novel detachable PEG2000 coupled to cholesterol via a bioreducible disulfide bond (Chol-SS-mPEG) to prepare redox-responsive cationic liposomes. HA (MW 20–40 kDa), a ligand for CD44, was non-covalently encapsulated on cationic liposomes. DOX was actively loaded into liposomes as a model drug. In vitro studies demonstrated that a 60% burst release of DOX in the presence of 10 mM glutathione (GSH) was observed, which was much higher than that of non-redox sensitive liposomes (Chol-mPEG/HA-L and Chol-mPEG-L) MTT cell viability assay, and that bifunctional Chol-SS-mPEG/HA-L loaded with 15.0% (w/w) had significantly more potent effects on MG63 osteosarcoma cells. In addition, the HA-encapsulated GSH-responsive liposomes were preferentially internalised into MG63 rather than human hepatocyte LO2, exerting a good tumour-suppressive effect and reducing liver uptake. The half-life of DOX was increased 10-fold compared to other liposomes by HA encapsulation [270].

Shauishuai feng’s team added ALN to this for further research. They utilised the bone-targeting molecule ALN conjugated to HA, a ligand for CD44. This ALN-HA conjugate was coupled to DSPE-PEG2000-COOH via a bioreproducible disulfide bond (-SS-), resulting in a functionalized polymer (ALN-HA-SS-L) that can oxidatively reduce, bone, and differentiate the dual-targeting polymers of Cluster 44 (CD44), which was later device with DOX. The effect of concomitant administration of the tumour-penetrating peptide, iRGD, was also investigated. ALN-HA-SS-L-L, with a zeta potential of -26.07 ± 0.32 mV, was disassembled in vitro in the presence of the reducing agent, 10 mM glutathione, releasing anti-osteosarcoma drugs. Compared with the blank group and various experimental groups such as ALN-HA, ALN-HA-SS-L-L/DOX showed significantly higher cytotoxicity against human osteosarcoma MG-63 cells, as well as high and rapid cellular uptake. In vivo experiments demonstrated that ALN-HA-SS-L-L/DOX significantly inhibited tumour growth and prolonged survival in an OS nude mouse model. This redox-responsive liposomal formulation targeting osteosarcoma, administered in combination with iRGD, is a promising approach in the treatment of OS [256].

It has been shown that estrogen (ES)-functionalized cationic liposome systems can also be used for targeted delivery to osteosarcoma. In the Xueli Yin team study, oligosaccharides (COS) are covalently attached to the liposome surface via a disulfide bond (-SS-). Estrogen is branched through PEG 2000 links for estrogen receptor targeting. Chol-SS-COS/ES/DOX was then loaded with DOX, and Chol-SS-COS/ES/DOX was finally prepared. adriamycin was rapidly released in the presence of glutathione (20 mM). The experimental results showed that MG63 showed a higher cellular uptake of Chol-SS-COS/ES/DOX liposomes than free DOX or Chol-SS-COS/DOX, which accumulated highly selectively in MG63 xenografts compared to organs and strongly inhibited tumour growth. Overall, estrogen-functionalized cationic liposomes from COS grafts, which are glutathione-responsive, show a more effective treatment of osteosarcoma than redox-responsive liposomes alone [271].

Liposome treatment for metastatic bone tumours

Many cancers, including breast, prostate, and thyroid cancers, frequently metastasise to bone, leading to the destruction of healthy bone tissue [272]. Bone metastases from breast and prostate cancers often cause severe pain in advanced stages and are a leading cause of death in cancer patients [273]. Current treatments, such as combining bisphosphonates and denosumab [274], are often inadequate for effectively inhibiting bone metastasis of cancer cells, highlighting the urgent need for more effective treatments. To prevent bone metastasis, PEG-conjugated aspartic acid (Asp)-modified liposomes (PEG-Asp-Lipo) have received much attention as bone-targeting carriers for paclitaxel (PTX). In addition, targeting ligands such as iRGD [275], and oestrogen [271], coupled to DSPE-PEG, can also be used to prepare targeted liposomes for the treatment of metastatic bone tumours.

Shugo Yamashita and colleagues developed Asp-modified liposomes for the bone-targeted delivery of PTX. They synthesised aspartic acid-modified 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DPPE-Asp) by reacting DPPE-NHS with di-tert-butyl L-aspartate. Targeted liposomes composed of DSPE-PEG, DPPE-Asp, and cholesterol showed significantly improved bone accumulation compared to unmodified and PEGylated liposomes (24.6% versus 5.4% and 6.7%, respectively) [276] (Fig. 12a).In the study of breast cancer metastatic bone tumours, Shivani Rai Paliwal and colleagues developed pH-responsive DOX-loaded liposomes that release drugs abundantly in acidic environments (pH ≈ 4). The liposomes achieve site-specific targeting through overexpression of HA receptors in tumour cells. DOX is actively targeted and delivered to tumour sites via HA-CD44 receptor interactions. Results showed a significant increase in drug accumulation within tumour tissues, a marked reduction in tumour volume, minimal cytotoxicity, and reduced cardiotoxicity [277].

Furthermore, the radioactive isotope 195mPt can effectively kill cancer cells but causes systemic toxicity when used alone. Encapsulating platinum-based drugs in bone-targeting liposomes has been shown to accumulate four times more platinum in bone metastatic lesions than in healthy bone [278]. ALN-modified liposomes can target bone metastasis sites [110]. Robin A. Nadar and Kambiz Farbod designed a radiotherapeutic bisphosphonate-functionalised platinum (195mPt-BP) complex. Combining platinum-based drugs with nanoparticles such as liposomes reduces their side effects. The platinum–bisphosphonate complex targets bone tumours while maintaining high radioactivity. After systemic administration in mice, the complex specifically accumulated in bone cancer cells. Compared to platinum complexes without bone-seeking bisphosphonates, Pt-BP exhibited 4.5 times greater affinity for bone, effectively killing metastatic cancer cells [279].

Additionally, encapsulating Pt-BP complexes in HA-modified liposomes to avoid macrophage phagocytosis further enhances the treatment’s efficacy for metastatic bone tumours.Aspartic acid (Asp), a bone-targeting sequence, has also been used in metastatic bone tumour treatment. Shugo Yamashita and colleagues developed PEGylated aspartic acid-modified liposomes (PEG-Asp-Lipo) using aspartic acid-modified 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DPPE-Asp) to deliver PTX as a bone-targeting carrier. In vivo experiments demonstrated that the injected PEG-Asp-Lipo accumulated primarily on eroded bone surfaces, significantly inhibiting the growth of metastatic bone tumours. This shows potential as a promising inhibitor for metastatic bone tumours [276]. Double-modified liposomes, which often exhibit improved bone-targeting capabilities, represent a new frontier. Asp8, similar to the hydroxyapatite-binding domains of osteopontin and osteocalcin, have been used to deliver drugs specifically to bone. Folic acid can selectively bind to folate receptor-positive bone tumours. Xianzhu Ke and colleagues leveraged these two targeting advantages by combining Asp8 and folic acid with DSPE-PEG-2000 and attaching them to DOX-loaded liposomes. In an in vivo model of breast cancer bone metastasis, pain was evaluated using paw withdrawal frequency. After 18 days, the withdrawal frequency for the saline group was 22.23 ± 2.45, while for the group treated with Asp8 and folate-modified DOX liposomes, it was significantly reduced to 12.08 ± 0.96. The experimental group showed a marked reduction in withdrawal frequency, inhibited tumour metastasis, and alleviated bone pain [280].

Beyond targeting, increasing the circulation time of liposomes in the bloodstream is equally important. Hao Wu and colleagues utilised low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) to extend liposome circulation time. They developed liposomes modified with ALN and LMWH to deliver the anticancer drug DOX, achieving both bone targeting and prolonged retention (Fig. 12b). In vivo experiments demonstrated significant inhibition of osteosarcoma and bone metastasis growth [281]. Nucleotide-based drugs such as antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are also considered promising for treating human diseases, but effective delivery remains challenging [282]. ASOs possess strong gene-targeting capabilities but have poor in vivo delivery. Liposomes offer a potential solution [283]. Jibin Guan and colleagues tackled this problem by developing iRGD-modified liposomes to deliver ASO targeting the androgen receptor (AR-ASO) (Fig. 12c). In an in vivo experiment targeting prostate cancer bone metastasis, iRGD liposomes significantly increased AR-ASO accumulation in prostate tumour tissue, especially at the tibial xenograft sites, where prostate cancer frequently metastasizes. This indicated that AR-ASO delivery via iRGD liposomes was highly effective, increasing drug accumulation at the tumour site and enhancing antitumour effects. Importantly, iRGD liposomes did not increase ASO accumulation or toxicity in healthy organs [284].

The intrinsically rigid interface of bone matrix can be locally softened by acid-secreting, bone-resorbing tumor–osteoclast hybrids, thereby creating a niche for the initial colonization of tumor cells. Lin Xian-Feng and co-workers were the first to identify these tumor-associated osteoclasts, termed “tumasteoclasts” (TAOCs). Leveraging the spatiotemporal coupling between tumor cells and TAOCs during the seeding phase, they engineered tetracycline-decorated liposomes co-encapsulating sodium bicarbonate and disodium hydrogen phosphate. Guided by an “in-situ decoupling/on-site killing” concept, the platform releases a burst of disodium hydrogen phosphate in response to TAOC activity, rapidly depleting local Ca²⁺ and inducing Ca–P crystallization. This process suppresses the formation of migratory primordia and compromises cellular membranes, providing a prophylactic strategy for early prevention of bone metastasis [285]. These advancements show that liposome-based drug delivery systems for metastatic bone tumours, with improved targeting and prolonged circulation, offer promising therapeutic options, providing enhanced efficacy and reduced systemic toxicity.

Liposomes for bone infection

Liposomal therapy for osteomyelitis

Chronic osteomyelitis, which develops from acute osteomyelitis, is a severe infection of bone and bone marrow and is often considered one of the most challenging diseases after cancer. It can lead to complications such as chronic fistulas, skin scarring, defects, fractures, and bone loss [286, 287]. The prolonged disease course and complex pathology significantly increase the recurrence rate of postoperative infections, making treatment highly challenging. Traditional treatment methods include debridement, lavage, and antibiotic therapy, with antibiotics playing a crucial role. These antibiotics can be administered either locally or intravenously. However, systemic administration via intravenous routes often fails to achieve effective drug concentrations at the site of infection, while high doses can lead to ototoxicity, nephrotoxicity, and other side effects, especially in patients with weakened immune systems [288, 289]. The optimal antibiotic type, route of administration, and duration of treatment remain subjects of debate, particularly with the rise of multidrug-resistant organisms [100, 290].Given that nanomaterials have shown increasing promise for drug delivery, researchers have turned their attention to the application of liposome-based systems for treating chronic osteomyelitis [291].

In 2010, studies began exploring liposomal therapies for osteomyelitis. Hui Tang and colleagues loaded gentamicin into liposomes and incorporated them into calcium sulphate, demonstrating strong anti-biofilm activity and preventing the formation of sequestra [292]. Subsequently, the same team further embedded gentamicin-loaded liposomes into allograft cortical bone to treat chronic osteomyelitis in rabbits. This approach showed better efficacy than intravenous gentamicin or intravenous liposome-gentamicin treatment, and a significant number of osteoprogenitor cells were observed around the bone implant [293]. Despite these advancements, both calcium sulphate and allograft cortical bone present risks of long-term autologous rejection after implantation. To address this issue, researchers turned to nanohydroxyapatite—a material that mimics natural bone and has high biocompatibility. Yixin Wen’s team leveraged this by developing cationic liposomal ceftazidime (CLC) combined with nanohydroxyapatite/β-tricalcium phosphate (n-HA/β-TCP) to treat chronic osteomyelitis in rabbits. After eight weeks of treatment, osteomyelitis was almost completely suppressed [294].

However, with the overuse of antibiotics, drug-resistant bacteria have progressively emerged, rendering traditional liposomal antibiotics less effective in treating osteomyelitis. In response, Ashraf A. Kadry and colleagues encapsulated two antibiotics, ciprofloxacin and vancomycin, within liposomal formulations and conducted antimicrobial activity tests. The results demonstrated that administering free ciprofloxacin or vancomycin alone, or their liposomal forms individually, did not achieve complete sterilisation of bone tissues. Although the combined administration of free ciprofloxacin and vancomycin exhibited better therapeutic outcomes, a significant proportion of the test animals developed severe nephrotoxicity and diarrhoea as adverse side effects during treatment [292]. As previously mentioned, this may be attributed to the high antibiotic doses required to achieve effective sterilisation, leading to off-target toxicity, further exacerbated by the compromised immune systems of the osteomyelitis-infected rabbits [295]. Conversely, the liposomal formulation of the combined antibiotics showed superior bactericidal activity, achieving complete sterilisation of cultured bone tissues after 14 days of treatment. Additionally, the study indicated that when liposomal dispersions (diameter < 100 nm) were subjected to 40 s of ultrasonic treatment, the liposomal formulations displayed significantly reduced nephrotoxicity and a lower incidence of severe diarrhoea [296]. This likely results from improved drug targeting, reducing the necessary antibiotic dose to achieve effective bactericidal concentrations and limiting exposure to non-target tissues. This innovative liposomal treatment approach not only mitigates off-target side effects but also demonstrates satisfactory antibacterial efficacy.

Modified liposomes have a multi-clock targeting strategy for MRSA [297].RGD sequences are commonly used for targeted drug delivery. However, there is no successful clinical translation of RGD-functionalized nanodrugs. As a reflection on cRGD-enabled targeted delivery, Guanghui Li’s research group designed cyclic-RGD-decorated stealth liposomes (cRGD-sLip) to utilize αvβ3-integrin and scavenger receptor-mediated phagocytosis for rapid aggregation of monocyte-phagocyte system and efficiently deliver antibiotics to MRSA-infected macrophages, thereby significantly reducing intracellular bacterial Load [298] (Fig. 13a). A new strategy for the clinical translation of RGD-functionalized liposomes is provided. As research has progressed, a number of physical therapy antimicrobial modalities have emerged in addition to the delivery of antimicrobial drugs. Microwave therapy, photothermal therapy, etc. have played an important role in the treatment of osteomyelitis as safe modes of physical intervention [299, 300].Yuchen Song’s team created Au/PDA/HRP@DLP by encapsulating gold/dopamine nanoparticles loaded with horseradish peroxidase (Au/PDA/HRP) in 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane/1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylethanolamine cationic liposomes (DLPs), a therapy combining acoustic-dynamic therapy (SDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) to destroy bacteria in a chronic osteomyelitis model while releasing in situ bacterial antigens that can be used as a vaccine, prompting macrophages to polarize from pro-inflammatory M1-type to tissue-repairing M2-type, activating adaptive immunity, building immune memory, and ultimately accelerating bone regeneration and significantly reducing the risk of recurrence [291] (Fig. 13b).

Liposome treatment of infected bone defects

Open fractures and internal fixation implants are often associated with bacterial infections leading to osteomyelitis, which is characterized by recalcitrant bone infections and is a major clinical challenge. To address this challenge, Prof. Zhongkai Cui’s team designed and selected a single-chain amphiphilic antimicrobial small molecule (cetylpyridinium chloride, CPC) and an osteoinductive sterol molecule (20s-hydroxycholesterol, Oxy), to prepare a novel nano-delivery system combining both antimicrobial and osteoinductive properties, CPC/Oxy The sterol liposomes can control the release of antibiotics and protein drugs, and the carrier itself has both antibacterial and osteogenic properties [301].

Liposomal formulations are also critical in preventing infected bone defects associated with orthopaedic implants. Xin-Ming Liu’s team synthesised bio-mineralised liposomes by conjugating alendronate-tri(ethylene glycol)-cholesterol (ALN-TEG-Chol) through Cu(I)-catalysed Huisgen 1,3-dipolar cycloaddition. They encapsulated oxacillin within these liposomes and applied them to orthopaedic implant surfaces. In vitro tests revealed that the liposomal formulations effectively inhibited the formation of Staphylococcus aureus biofilms. This study demonstrates that bio-mineralised liposomal formulations hold great potential for preventing implant-associated osteomyelitis [302].

This biomineralized liposome, however, relies on pre-coating of the implant surface and has only been shown to be effective in preventing S. aureus biofilms in vitro. Wang Guowei’s team has developed vascular cell adhesion molecule‑1 (VCAM‑1)‑targeted, ultrasound‑responsive liposomes (LPCOTML) that co‑encapsulate an oleoyl‑meropenem prodrug cleavable by ROS, the sonosensitizer chlorin e6 (Ce6), and liquid perfluoropentane. Guided by VCAM‑1‑mediated transcytosis, the nanocarriers cross the vascular endothelium to the periphery of biofilm‑associated surgical‑site infections (BSSIs). Upon low‑intensity ultrasound, perfluoropentane vaporizes into microbubbles, producing vigorous cavitation that disrupts the extracellular polymeric substance and drives deep biofilm penetration. Simultaneously, Ce6 generates ROS to activate the prodrug and trigger localized meropenem release, achieving efficient bacterial eradication and biofilm elimination in BSSI models [303] (Fig. 13c). This active trans-endothelial aggregation in the infected foci, and under the ultrasonic cavitation to destroy the mature biofilm and activate the pre-drug program, to achieve the in vivo clearance of existing infection. This approach has the advantages of active targeting, external spatial and temporal control, and systemic drug delivery, resulting in a wider therapeutic depth and range of application.

Liposomal therapy for bone and joint tuberculosis

Bone and joint tuberculosis (TB), caused by the invasion of Mycobacterium tuberculosis into bone or joint tissue, is the most common extrapulmonary manifestation of tuberculosis. Among these, spinal tuberculosis is the most prevalent form. Due to factors such as multidrug resistance, delayed diagnosis, or inadequate control of TB infections, spinal tuberculosis can lead to spinal cord compression and, in severe cases, become life-threatening [304, 305]. Isoniazid (INH) is a first-line treatment for bone tuberculosis, but its long-term use may lead to severe side effect [306]. Furthermore, anti-tuberculosis drug concentrations in sclerotic bone often reach only the MIC. This inadequate drug penetration creates barriers that prevent effective drug delivery to the lesion. The extremely low drug concentrations, the relatively closed environment of the lesions, and hypoxia make it difficult to eradicate bacterial colonies within sclerotic bone necrosis and caseous lesions [307]. With the advent of novel nanodrug delivery systems, researchers have increasingly focused on developing nano-based delivery platforms for INH [308].Liposomal drug delivery systems, which place anti-tuberculosis drugs directly at the infection site and provide sustained local drug release, hold great promise for improving TB treatment outcomes [309, 310].

To address this challenge, Peng Liu and colleagues developed a liposomal hydrogel system to facilitate localised treatment of bone and joint tuberculosis. In their study, they used DINH, a hydrophobic derivative of INH with better activity and higher biosafety compared to INH. Through phase transition tests and rheological studies, the system demonstrated thermoresponsive and self-healing properties, making it particularly suitable for intra-articular drug delivery. Upon local injection, the thermoresponsive properties ensure stable and sustained drug release, maintaining the drug concentration at effective bactericidal levels for extended periods [311]. Their findings highlight the advantages of using a responsive liposomal system for prolonged drug release, demonstrating significant potential for localised treatment of bone and joint tuberculosis.

Liposome for sports medicine

Liposomal treatment for muscle injuries

Today, rotator cuff tears are highly prevalent as the aging population increases. Rotator cuff repair surgery can relieve shoulder pain and improve joint function [312]. However, poor postoperative integration of the tendon-bone interface results in poor healing and a failure rate of 20%-70% [313]. Increased risk of screw loosening, especially in osteoporotic patients. In osteoporotic or inflammatory environments, tendon stem/progenitor cells (TSPCs) are impaired in their regenerative capacity and ability to differentiate into the cell types necessary for tissue regeneration, failing to orchestrate the functional integration of tendon after injury, triggering impaired tendon maturation and failure to reconstruct the fibrocartilaginous zone in a vicious cycle of inflammation-stem depletion [314, 315]. The cell membrane-like properties of liposomes can efficiently penetrate the fibrotic microenvironment and target to the hypervascularized tendon-bone interface. Based on this, Qing Wang’s team developed a liposome delivery system loaded with si-CD248, which was shown to precisely inhibit the expression of CD248 and lead to reduced proliferation, increased apoptosis, and impaired migration of TSPCs through the abnormalities of the FAK-JAK-STAT1 signaling pathway. It effectively restored the regenerative potential of TSPCs and promoted the healing of tendon-bone interface [316].

Liposomal treatment for muscle injuries

Traumatic muscle injuries refer to a series of pathological changes in skeletal muscle caused by trauma, which may result from indirect non-contact injuries due to sudden muscle stretching (such as strains or tears) or from direct external forces causing contusions or lacerations. Traditionally, the treatment of muscle injuries has focused on rest, immobilisation, and early rehabilitation exercises, but these methods often fail to meet therapeutic expectations. Early drug injections may accelerate muscle recovery [317]. However, severe inflammation and impaired myogenic differentiation are the main obstacles to skeletal muscle regeneration. Studies have shown that in inflammatory environments, myoblasts secrete exosomes rich in miRNAs such as miR-224, which are transferred to macrophages and inhibit M2 polarisation. Therefore, inhibiting miR-224 may aid in the repair of muscle tissue. In this context, Zhiwen Lu and colleagues used liposomes loaded with a miR-224 inhibitor to treat muscle injuries. Their results indicated that the liposomes carrying the miR-224 inhibitor promoted macrophage M2 polarisation, suppressed fibrosis, and accelerated muscle regeneration, providing a novel miRNA-based therapy for muscle injuries [318].

Several drugs, such as resveratrol, apigenin, and tanshinone IIA, have also shown potential for repairing muscle injuries. However, their clinical application is limited due to low bioavailability and poor membrane permeability. Encapsulating these drugs in liposomes may enhance their therapeutic effects. Apigenin is a natural flavonoid with osteogenic and antioxidant properties that has attracted attention in muscle repair. Jinwu Wang and colleagues encapsulated apigenin in liposomes, creating an injectable formulation for treating acute muscle injuries. In a rat model of acute skeletal muscle blunt trauma, the apigenin-loaded liposomes exhibited anti-inflammatory effects and promoted faster recovery of injured skeletal muscle [319]. The same team also encapsulated tanshinone IIA, extracted from Salvia miltiorrhiza, in liposomes for treating acute muscle injuries. Their findings showed that the tanshinone IIA-loaded liposomes effectively increased the expression of autophagy-related proteins (VPS34, Beclin 1, and CTSD), while reducing p62 levels, promoting vimentin expression, decreasing type I collagen expression, and inhibiting the production of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α. These mechanisms demonstrated the potential of liposomes to enhance muscle repair after acute injury [320].

Given the low bioavailability of resveratrol, many research teams have begun encapsulating it in liposomes to enhance its therapeutic effect. For example, Yi-Ju Hsu and colleagues co-administered resveratrol with NSAIDs to treat acute muscle injuries. Their study showed that this combination significantly reduced serum levels of uric acid (UA), creatinine (CREA), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and creatine kinase (CK) in mice after muscle injury, while promoting muscle satellite cell regeneration and vimentin expression [321]. These findings suggest that resveratrol-loaded liposomes may be an ideal choice for treating acute muscle injuries.

Intense exercise can also lead to muscle injuries. Noriaki Kawanishi and colleagues found that macrophage infiltration and muscle fibre damage significantly increased in the gastrocnemius muscles of mice after strenuous exercise. Treatment with clodronate liposomes reduced macrophage infiltration and alleviated muscle fibre damage post-exercise [322]. Given that muscle injuries have historically received insufficient attention and treatment options have been limited, liposomal formulations offer a new therapeutic direction for muscle injury recovery.

Liposomal treatment for duchenne muscular dystrophy

Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) is a severe, progressive muscle-wasting disease that leads to significant muscle weakness and, in late stages, death due to respiratory failure [323]. DMD is caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes dystrophin, a protein essential for muscle cell function. This X-linked disorder results in the absence of functional dystrophin in muscle cells [324]. Currently, the main treatment for DMD is glucocorticoids, but long-term use produces significant side effects. Due to the lack of effective treatments to improve patient prognosis, targeted drug delivery using nanomaterials like liposomes has brought new hope to DMD patients.

Notably, the diaphragm is one of the most severely affected muscles in the early stages of DMD. Keren Turjeman and colleagues leveraged the targeting capabilities of liposomes to deliver glucocorticoids specifically to the diaphragm. In vivo results showed that short-term treatment with liposome-encapsulated glucocorticoids significantly reduced serum levels of TGF-β and decreased macrophage infiltration in the diaphragm of DMD mice. After long-term treatment, common side effects of glucocorticoids, such as bone loss and osteoporosis, were significantly reduced in the liposome group. These findings suggest that targeted delivery of glucocorticoids via liposomes not only slows the progression of DMD but also reduces side effects, improving patient quality of life [325].

In addition to liposomes loaded with glucocorticoids, gene therapy is another commonly used approach for treating DMD. Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) targeting the dystrophin gene offer an effective therapeutic strategy. Currently, phosphorodiamidate morpholino oligomer (PMO) has been approved by the FDA as the first ASO-based treatment for DMD. However, due to poor cell membrane permeability and rapid clearance, the therapeutic effects are often suboptimal. Eri Sasaki and colleagues developed A2G80- and PEG-modified liposomes, utilising the affinity of the A2G80 peptide for α-dystroglycan (α-DG) expressed on muscle cells to achieve muscle-specific targeting. In vivo experiments demonstrated that these liposomes accumulated more in muscle tissue and had longer retention times compared to conventional liposomes. Encapsulating PMO in A2G80 liposomes enhanced muscle-specific targeting and retention, representing a promising systemic delivery strategy for treating DMD [326].

Liposome therapy for spinal degeneration and injury

Liposome-based treatment for delaying intervertebral disc degeneration

With an ageing population, the incidence of intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD) and related conditions such as lumbar disc herniation is increasing, significantly impacting people’s quality of life. Currently, treatment for lumbar disc herniation primarily involves conservative methods like bed rest and surgical interventions. However, surgeries such as discectomy can lead to postoperative complications like discitis, increasing the likelihood of recurrence and inducing other complications. To address these issues, Yu Liu and colleagues developed aspirin-loaded liposomes embedded in GelMA hydrogels. The hydrogels were crosslinked using photopolymerisation to enhance spinal stability. The liposome-encapsulated aspirin helped prevent the burst release of the drug (Fig. 14a). In vivo results showed that the combination of aspirin liposome-GelMA hydrogels effectively reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α, and inhibited the expression of degeneration-related factors like collagen type II (COL-2), aggrecan, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-3, MMP-13), thereby suppressing postoperative disc inflammation [327]. While surgery is sometimes necessary and inevitably causes tissue trauma, the ideal treatment should focus on promoting disc regeneration to restore function and alleviate symptoms. Many drugs have been tested for intervertebral disc regeneration in both in vitro and in vivo studies. However, these treatments often show limited effectiveness due to low drug accumulation and rapid metabolism at the disc site. To overcome these limitations, many research teams have begun using liposome-encapsulated drugs for disc regeneration. Liposomes can provide sustained drug release and increase drug accumulation at the disc site.

Furthermore, their research demonstrated that the mass ratio of egg yolk phosphatidylcholine to cholesterol influenced drug utilisation; the optimal OMT-LIP formulation had a multilamellar structure, a utilisation rate of 73.4 ± 4.1%, and a particle size of 178.1 ± 2.9 nm. Liposomes for nerve repair

Nerve repair following injury remains a significant challenge in modern medicine. Early interventions, such as decompression of the affected nerve and administration of neurotrophic agents like methylcobalamin, are the main treatments currently available. However, nerve regeneration is a slow process, and the final therapeutic outcome is often uncertain. Recently, liposomal formulations have emerged as a promising solution for nerve injury treatment.

Hui Yao’s team developed a temperature-sensitive hydrogel encapsulating resveratrol-loaded liposomes within a PLGA-PEG-PLGA gel. This gel degrades slowly upon exposure to heat, enabling controlled drug release. In vivo studies demonstrated that when applied to injured sciatic nerves, this gel facilitated nerve repair [329]. Similarly, Wanlin Xu’s team created an injectable hydrogel incorporating nanoliposomes loaded with a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-mimicking peptide (QK), termed QK-NLs@Gel, for treating peripheral nerve compression injuries (Fig. 14c). Applied around damaged nerves, this gel provided a conducive environment for regeneration. Both in vivo and in vitro results showed that QK-NLs@Gel improved blood circulation at the injury site, promoted macrophage M2 polarization, and enhanced axonal and myelin regeneration, leading to better functional recovery after four weeks [330].

Scar formation is another significant factor impeding optimal nerve recovery. To address this issue, Furong Zheng and colleagues developed a hydrogel composed of chitosan/oxidized hydroxyethyl cellulose (CS/OHEC) loaded with asiaticoside liposomes and conductive reduced graphene oxide (rGO), housed within a hollow nerve conduit (Fig. 14d). This hydrogel exhibited a conductivity of 5.27 ± 0.42 × 10^−4 S/cm. When subjected to electrical stimulation, it promoted nerve cell differentiation and proliferation, accelerating nerve regeneration. In vivo experiments confirmed that the slow release of asiaticoside from the liposomes significantly inhibited fibroblast growth and collagen secretion, reducing scar tissue formation and improving functional recovery of peripheral nerve deficits [331].

Liposomes for spinal cord injury

Liposome-mediated stem cell transplantation therapy

Spinal cord injury (SCI) patients often suffer from motor, sensory, and autonomic dysfunctions, depending on the level and extent of the injury, severely impairing their quality of life [332]. Although many drugs have shown potential in promoting SCI repair, their efficacy is often limited by poor drug accumulation at the injury site and low penetration efficiency across the blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB). Therefore, current clinical regenerative therapies are ineffective [333]. Nanoparticles as a drug delivery system can be effective in SCI therapy to improve drug safety, efficacy and bioavailability [334, 335]. Stem cell therapies have shown great potential in treating SCI [336], but transplanted neural cells often differentiate into astrocytes rather than neurons, limiting their effectiveness. To address this limitation, Xiaoran Li’s team developed liposomes loaded with the microtubule stabiliser PTX and embedded them in a collagen microchannel scaffold. This scaffold was implanted at the T8 complete transection site in rats (Fig. 15a). Experiments demonstrated that PTX-triggered neuronal differentiation occurred through the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway, significantly inducing neural stem cell differentiation into neurons. PTX also reduced scar formation and enhanced intrinsic axon regeneration following SCI. After sustained drug release, the rats showed improved motor function [337].

In addition to direct stem cell transplantation therapy, the interaction between the material and the cells cannot be ignored [338]. Previous studies have shown that synthetic molecular scaffolds that mimic proteins are effective in signaling to cells for SCI regeneration [339].Many biomaterials can affect cells, but the effect of covalent interactions between cells and biomaterials on cell behavior has not been tested [340]. In order to investigate whether covalent conjugation (CC) between biomaterials and stem cells can improve the behavior of stem cells after SCI transplantation, Dai Jianwu’s team constructed a “covalent locking” neuroprosthetic system: first, azide groups were introduced into the membranes of neural precursor cells (NPCs) using Ac4ManNAz as a metabolic marker, and then dibenzocyclooctane (DBCO) groups were modified into longitudinally aligned collagen fiber scaffolds and loaded with the antioxidant edaravone (Edaravone). First, the azide group was introduced into the membrane of neural precursor cells (NPCs) using Ac4ManNAz as a metabolic marker, and then the dibenzocyclooctane (DBCO) group was modified into the longitudinally-arranged collagen fiber scaffolds and lipid nanoparticles loaded with the antioxidant edaravone (Eda), respectively. The rapid bio-orthogonal reaction of the azide-DBCO resulted in the formation of a stable covalent binding between the NPCs and the scaffolds, which significantly improved the anchoring of the cells, the directional axon growth and the neural differentiation at the damaged area, and also achieved targeted delivery. This multilevel covalent strategy significantly enhanced the survival rate of transplanted cells and promoted axonal regeneration and neovascularization in a rat SCI model, and ultimately accelerated the recovery of neurological function [341]. This study demonstrated for the first time that covalent interactions between cells and biomaterials have great potential for tissue regeneration, providing a new strategy for subsequent research.

Liposome-mediated immunotherapy for repair of spinal cord injuries

Inflammation is an equally important target for SCI treatment when targeting SCI. Moderate inflammation is the body’s defense response, but persistent inflammation can lead to irreversible damage to cell populations [342, 343]. Dynamic spatiotemporal neuroinflammation is critical for recovery from SCI [344]. Effective inhibition of inflammatory cascades and protection of nerve regeneration from inflammation-induced apoptosis can effectively promote SCI repair [345]. After primary SCI, the microenvironment following SCI is effectively modulated using a nano-targeted drug delivery system. Promoting the shift of microglia/macrophages from an inflammatory phenotype to a pro-repair phenotype, thereby limiting the inflammatory response and neuronal damage, is also critical for SCI repair [346]. Fenzan Wu’s team designed dual-targeting liposomes by conjugating CAQK peptide (Cp), which targets injury sites, and KC peptide (Rp), which enhances BSCB penetration. These liposomes were loaded with basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) to treat SCI. In vivo experiments showed that these dual-targeting liposomes crossed the BSCB and accumulated at the injury site. They promoted BSCB repair, induced macrophage M2 polarization, stimulated angiogenesis, and inhibited neuronal apoptosis and axonal degeneration in SCI rats. Mice treated with this system gradually regained motor function in their limbs [347]. After acute SCI, high oxidative stress levels also hinder spinal cord repair. Jing Zhang’s team developed a “nanoliposome-gene composite vector”: OXR1 plasmid and vitamin E succinate grafted ε-polylysine (VES-g-PLL) were firstly formed into cationic micelles, and then encapsulated into cationic liposomes, resulting in Nps-pOXR1-Lip nanoparticles of average particle size of 58 nm, with anti-degradation ability against DNase and low toxicity. Nps-pOXR1-Lip nanoparticles with an average particle size of about 58 nm, anti-degradation ability against DNase and low toxicity were obtained. The carrier significantly increased neuronal OXR1 expression in the SCI model, which in turn reduced oxidative stress, inhibited inflammation and neuronal apoptosis, and promoted spinal cord repair [348].

In order to precisely regulate the immune microenvironment and elucidate the potential regulatory mechanism of macrophage polarization in SCI repair, Chunyan Fu’s team designed phosphatidylserine (PS)-modified lipid nanoparticles to deliver Mms6 mRNA (PS/LNP-Mms6) (Fig. 15b). After intravenous injection, the particles were efficiently phagocytosed by macrophages in the injured area by PS “eat-me” signaling and expressed Mms6 in situ, thus inhibiting iron death and stabilizing the M2 phenotype; the therapeutic effect disappeared after the experimental depletion of macrophages, thus verifying the targeting specificity. This system avoids the complicated process and immune rejection of traditional cell transplantation, and provides a scalable carrier for precision RNA therapy in the central nervous system (CNS) [349].Li Pengfei’s team designed biomimetic bacterial-membrane nanoparticles (BM-NPs) by cloaking drug-rich liposomes with detoxified outer-membrane vesicle proteins. A “tortoise–hare” delivery mode lets neutrophils ferry BM-NPs rapidly in the acute phase, then macrophages sustain release in the subacute phase. BM-NPs restore macrophage autophagy, activate LXRα/ABCA1 cholesterol efflux, suppress foam-cell formation, preserve myelin, and significantly improve locomotor recovery in SCI mice [350]. Both strategies remodel innate immune cells: PS/LNP-Mms6 enhances macrophage antioxidant defence and tissue repair, while BM-NPs correct lipid dysregulation and chronic inflammation—together sketching a complementary, time-staged immunomodulation roadmap for spinal-cord repair.

Cell membrane liposome therapy promotes spinal cord repair

Cell membrane liposomes are widely used due to their passive targeting and other properties. Jinyu An’s team, using the principle of passive targeting of macrophage membrane liposomes, prepared primary macrophage membrane mimicry-modified nanoliposomes containing minocycline (MH) hydrochloride and dextran sulfate (DS) (MH-DS@M-Lips). It was shown that MH-DS@M-Lips had the ability to bind calcium ions in situ and form complexes, which effectively reduced calcium ion concentration and calcium-related neuronal apoptosis. In an in vitro Transwell model simulating the blood-spinal cord barrier environment, favorable uptake of MH-DS@M-Lips nanoparticles by BV2 microglial cells in the lower lumen of the Transwell system was observed after 1, 3, and 5 h after administration. In vivo fluorescence imaging demonstrated its ability to target enrichment of inflammatory damage areas as confirmed in a mouse model of SCI. Animal models showed that it was effective in improving motor function in SCI mice [351]. However, macrophages have multiple polarization states, and it remains untested whether macrophage membranes with different polarization states would have an effect on targeting at the site of SCI injury. Chong Li’s team, on the other hand, added to the gap in this area. They have created macrophage membrane liposomes containing minocycline (RM-LIP/MC), which prolongs drug circulation and actively accumulates in vivo at the site of spinal cord trauma. The targeting of RM-LIP to SCI was found to be independent of macrophage polarization status by examining the expression levels of two major targeting receptors, Mac-1 and integrin α4, in three macrophage subtypes, namely, inactivated (M0), classically activated (M1), and selectively activated (M2) [352].

Oral liposomes repair SCI through the gut-spinal cord axis

SCI is not only a local neurological injury but is also frequently accompanied by intestinal dysfunction, gut barrier disruption, and systemic inflammatory responses. These pathological changes support the concept of the gut–spinal cord axis, in which intestinal immune, neural, and glial signals may influence secondary neuroinflammation and functional recovery after SCI. Among intestinal regulatory cells, enteric glial cells (EGCs) are particularly important because they share several biomarkers and functional similarities with astrocytes and participate in epithelial barrier maintenance, intestinal immune regulation, and neuroglial communication [353].

Based on this concept, Wang et al. developed an oral engineered liposome system, C-SA-Lip/CUR, to target the gut–CNS axis for comprehensive SCI therapy (Fig. 15c). In this system, curcumin (CUR) served as the anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective payload. Se-apamin, a diselenium bond-stabilized derivative of apamin, was conjugated to the liposomal surface as a targeting ligand because apamin can recognize small-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels expressed by both EGCs and astrocytes. The selenium substitution improved the stability of apamin in simulated intestinal fluid, thereby enhancing its suitability for oral delivery. In addition, a temporary chitosan oligosaccharide lactate protective layer was introduced to generate C-SA-Lip/CUR, which protected the liposomes in the intestinal environment, prolonged CUR release, and promoted penetration through the intestinal mucus layer. After oral administration, this system was designed to act sequentially: first improving intestinal stability and mucus permeation, then facilitating intestinal absorption and EGC-related targeting, and finally reaching injured spinal cord tissue through systemic transport and blood–spinal cord barrier penetration. In SCI models, C-SA-Lip/CUR showed improved oral bioavailability in different intestinal segments, accumulated at injured spinal cord sites, colocalized with astrocytes, reduced inflammation, alleviated intestinal dysfunction, and improved motor recovery after SCI [354]. This study suggests that oral liposome engineering may provide a noninvasive strategy for SCI therapy by simultaneously targeting intestinal complications and spinal cord repair along the gut–CNS axis.

Prospects, challenges, and conclusion of the clinical translational application of liposomal nanobiologics

With the acceleration of population ageing and the rising incidence of bone-related diseases, timely diagnosis and effective treatment of these conditions have become major challenges for researchers and medical professionals. Fortunately, advances in nanotechnology have introduced numerous emerging nanomedicine materials. Among them, liposomes, with their unique structures and functional modifications, can achieve sustained release, targeted delivery, and responsive drug release, providing new opportunities for the diagnosis and treatment of bone-related diseases. A truly promising nanobiologic must possess the capability for clinical translation to ultimately benefit humanity.Currently, multiple liposomal formulations have been approved for market and have demonstrated significant efficacy in various treatments. These include Doxil™ (1995, the first FDA-approved long-circulating liposomal formulation for cancer treatment), AmBisome™ (1996, used for treating fungal infections), DaunoXome™ (1999, for treating AIDS-related Kaposi’s sarcoma), Myocet™ (2000, used in combination chemotherapy for breast cancer), DepoCyt™ (2004, for treating lymphomas of the central nervous system), Marqibo™ (2012, for treating relapsed acute lymphoblastic leukaemia), Onivyde™ (2015, used for metastatic pancreatic cancer), and Vyxeos™ (2018, for treating specific types of acute myeloid leukaemia). In addition, significant progress has been made in LNP enhanced RNA vaccines and treatments [355] (Fig. 16).

Although several liposomal drugs, such as Doxil and AmBisome, have been approved clinically, these formulations are not bone-specific therapies. Thus, they demonstrate the general feasibility of liposomal drug products but do not indicate that liposome-based systems for skeletal diseases have achieved clinical translation. To date, most bone-oriented liposomal platforms remain preclinical. This translational gap is mainly due to the unique barriers of bone tissues, including low vascularity, dense mineralized matrix, limited cartilage penetration, heterogeneous lesion permeability, and rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. In addition, small-animal models often fail to fully recapitulate human skeletal mechanics, chronic inflammation, aging, infection recurrence, or tumor heterogeneity. Manufacturing reproducibility, particle-size control, drug-loading stability, sterilization, storage, long-term biosafety, and regulatory evaluation also remain major challenges. Future translation will require standardized production, robust pharmacokinetic and biodistribution studies, clinically relevant models, and disease-specific clinical endpoints.

The application of liposome technology in the diagnosis and treatment of bone-related diseases should focus on improving drug-carrying efficiency, targeting precision, and multifunctional integration capabilities, especially when combined with intelligent drug release systems. The application of liposomes can control drug release at specific lesion sites, significantly improving efficacy while reducing the impact on healthy tissues. Currently, the latest research buzz suggests that cationic or fusion liposomes can encapsulate functional mitochondria and deliver them directly to osteoblasts or mesenchymal stem cells, restoring oxidative phosphorylation and accelerating osteogenic differentiation in osteoporotic and inflammatory bone models. This liposomal mitophagy offers a new avenue for the treatment of metabolic bone diseases that are refractory to conventional pharmacology. Bone-targeted alendronate-decorated liposomes co-loaded with metabolic modulators have been shown to normalize hepatic glucose and lipid processing while enhancing osteoblast function. These findings emphasize the therapeutic value of exploiting inter-organ crosstalk through nanocarrier delivery strategies. Engineered cell membrane liposomes for superior fusion and passive targeting. Camouflaging liposomes with macrophage, platelet, or osteoblast membranes preserves fusion proteins and self markers, significantly prolongs somatic circulation, minimizes immune clearance, and promotes the accumulation of inflamed or mineralized skeletal tissue. Such membrane-engineered carriers are rapidly becoming a hot topic for chronic orthopedic indications. Liposomes also hold great promise in diagnostic imaging; through functional modification, they can be used as contrast agents for high-precision imaging of diseased tissues, which is important for early diagnosis and real-time monitoring of disease progression. Therefore, liposome nanobiologics have good potential for translational applications.

Future studies should move from general liposomal delivery toward disease-specific and clinically oriented theranostic design. Since osteomyelitis, OA, OP, RA, bone defects, and bone tumors present distinct pathological barriers, liposomal systems should be tailored to specific disease microenvironments and clinical needs. The integration of diagnostic imaging, targeted therapy, biomimetic engineering, and stimuli-responsive release may enable lesion localization, image-guided intervention, real-time therapeutic monitoring, and improved local efficacy. In bone regeneration, liposomes combined with hydrogels, scaffolds, injectable biomaterials, or 3D-printed constructs may further support sustained retention and coordinated regulation of inflammation, angiogenesis, osteogenesis, and antibacterial activity. Nevertheless, clinical translation will require systematic evaluation of long-term biosafety, biodistribution, immune responses, manufacturing reproducibility, sterilization compatibility, storage stability, disease-model relevance, and clinically meaningful endpoints. Looking ahead, AI-driven formulation and process optimization may accelerate the rational design of liposomal and lipid nanoparticle systems. Deep learning platforms can now predict key lipid properties - apparent pK_a, ζ-potential, encapsulation efficiency, and mRNA delivery performance - directly from chemical structure, enabling rapid insilico screening of thousands of ionizable lipids [356]. Lipid optimization using a deep learning neural network predicted RNA delivery in vitro and in vivo [357]. Although not yet applied to bone-related diseases, a good learning model is provided. This paper comprehensively summarizes the recent advances in the use of liposome nanobiologics in the diagnosis and treatment of orthopedic-related diseases. However, despite the remarkable efficacy of these advanced liposome nanobiologics in diagnosis and treatment, there are still many unresolved issues. First, the in vivo safety of liposomes is a major research challenge. Liposomal materials for the treatment of bone-related diseases inevitably require in vivo evaluation; their potential toxicity and possible cellular and tissue damage cannot be ignored. The immunogenic and toxic effects that liposomes may induce may lead to inflammatory reactions or other adverse effects, which are closely related to their size, surface properties, solubility, and drug-carrying capacity. For example, smaller liposomes may exhibit greater toxicity than larger liposomes because they can more easily penetrate barriers and enter tissues such as the brain. Second, the metabolic pathways and pharmacokinetic properties of liposomes in vivo need to be further investigated to ensure effective delivery to the target and safe clearance from the body. Another challenge is to enhance the stability of liposomes to avoid rapid degradation during circulation or recognition and clearance by the immune system, which may reduce their efficacy.

In addition, according to the latest research hotspots, mitochondria and other organelles are so fragile that ultra-mild encapsulation chemicals, cryoprotectants, and real-time potency assays are necessary to maintain their function during encapsulation of liposomes, storage, and systemic transport. Membrane artifacts, multi-loaded liposomes will introduce heterogeneity process control will become complex. Domain Shift in Artificial Intelligence Modeling. Most training datasets are biased toward hepatic and pulmonary drug delivery; shifting learning workflows will be needed to adapt predictive models to hypoxic, mineral-rich bone marrow environments. Integration of AI-guided design, automated manufacturing, and adaptive clinical trials will require early communication with ethical and regulatory agencies. The multifunctional design of liposomes-integrating targeting, diagnostic, and therapeutic functions into a single platform-offers a wide range of application possibilities, but it also significantly increases structural complexity and difficulty of preparation, posing higher technical and equipment challenges. Finally, mass production and quality control of liposomes remain barriers to their clinical application. Reducing production costs while ensuring product consistency is an urgent issue. Over the past three decades, liposomes have progressed from first-generation drug carriers to sophisticated, stimuli-responsive nanobiologics capable of integrating targeting, imaging, and immunomodulation. The next wave—featuring mitochondrial transplantation, organ-axis modulation, membrane-engineered vectors, and AI-accelerated lipid discovery—opens unprecedented opportunities to address the multifactorial pathophysiology of bone degeneration and repair. Realising this promise will hinge on truly interdisciplinary collaboration that couples data-driven molecular design with scalable biomanufacturing and rigorous translational science. By embracing these synergistic advances, liposomal technologies are poised not only to refine current orthopaedic therapies, but also to inaugurate transformative paradigms for the diagnosis, regeneration, and lifelong health of skeletal tissue.
