DETOUR: An Interactive Benchmark for Dual-Agent Search and Reasoning
Li Siyan, Darshan Deshpande, Anand Kannappan, Rebecca Qian

Abstract
When recalling information in conversation, people often arrive at the recollection after multiple turns. However, existing benchmarks for evaluating agent capabilities in such tip-of-the-tongue search processes are restricted to single-turn settings. To more realistically simulate tip-of-the-tongue search, we introduce D ual-agent based E valuation T hrough O bscure U nder-specified R etrieval (DETOUR), a dual-agent evaluation benchmark containing 1,011 prompts. The benchmark design involves a Primary Agent , which is the subject of evaluation, tasked with identifying the recollected entity through querying a Memory Agent that is held consistent across evaluations. Our results indicate that current state-of-the-art models still struggle with our benchmark, only achieving 36% accuracy when evaluated on all modalities (text, image, audio, and video), highlighting the importance of enhancing capabilities in underspecified scenarios.

Introduction

Large Language Model (LLM) assisted search has recently found applications in several domains, including scientific research
Phan et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025
, finance
Li et al. 2024
, customer service
Lyu et al. 2025
, and conversational context collection
Miroyan et al. 2025
. This popularity has given rise to deep research agentic systems
Xu and Peng 2025
that actively acquire and critically reason over content on the internet with the aim to satisfy user queries. Several benchmarks have attempted to study the search capabilities of such agents under conflicting knowledge
Cattan et al. 2025
and multimodal
Mialon et al. 2023
scenarios, emphasizing the gap between expectations and current model performance. One such important domain is tip-of-tongue (ToT) search
Sky et al. 2025
;
Arguello et al. 2021
that involves web search over vague user recollections of entity descriptions. Most benchmarks in the search evaluation space focus solely on single-turn questions; however, in realistic user conversations, structured ambiguity clarifications for ToT tasks are crucial. A skill that existing benchmarks do not test for is the ability to ask high-quality clarification questions in underspecified scenarios.

We study the following research questions in detail in this paper:

1.

How effectively can LLM agents identify and resolve ambiguities through follow-up clarifications in our interactive, tip-of-the-tongue search setting?

2.

Can LLM agents effectively utilize the Memory Agent to achieve better task performance?

3.

What are failure cases emerging from the multi-turn evaluation regime that are not often observable in the single-turn setups?

4.

How can LLM Agents be taught to more efficiently interface with interactive tools?

Through our work, we demonstrate that state-of-the-art models like GPT-5 achieve only 66% accuracy on the text-only split of DETOUR . This performance drastically decreases to approximately 36% when all modalities are taken into account simultaneously. This highlights that
LLMs are poor at asking high-quality follow-up questions
under our interactive setup; specifically, a common failure mode is querying the Memory Agent for additional information in vain, therefore derailing the reasoning process. However, our ablations show that
models can still improve their performance with the assistance of the Memory Agent
, and we discuss a potential approach for improving model performance on DETOUR.

Overall, our work proposes a
novel, multi-turn, dual-agent evaluation setup
for tip-of-the-tongue search, aiming to measure clarification-seeking capabilities. Such interactive benchmarks offer more comprehensive evaluations, better preparing agentic systems for conversing with real-world users. We hope DETOUR will lay the foundation for the next generation of search agents, capable of reasoning about clarification questions and resolving real-world ambiguities.

Related Work

Agentic Search Evaluation

Web search benchmarks such as Mind2Web
Gou et al. 2025
established evaluation for generalist web agents across diverse domains using real-world websites, while WebArena
Zhou et al. 2023
introduced a reproducible, self-hosted environment, emphasizing programmatic state checks over action sequence matching. However, as the popularity of deep research evaluations has grown
Du et al. 2025
, evaluations such as GAIA
(
Mialon et al. 2023
)
have introduced tasks that require tool use, multimodality handling, and web browsing, which remain simple for humans yet challenging for frontier models, relying on short output phrase match. Domain-specific benchmarks like
He et al. 2025a
focus on multi-hop local service search, shopping
Lyu et al. 2025
, medicine
Chen et al. 2025
or finance
Li et al. 2024
. While these benchmarks reveal important trends in the deep research space, they often fail to account for characteristics such as ambiguity, which are common in real-life queries.

Searching Under Uncertainty

The importance of uncertainty in known-item retrieval queries has been more recently recognized
He et al. 2025b
;
Sky et al. 2025
.
Sky et al. 2025
specifically shows that ambiguity resolution in real-world search queries is challenging for state-of-the-art web agents. Soon after,
Deng et al. 2025
found that LLMs frequently fail to recognize ambiguity in search queries due to systematic overconfidence, and that open-source models are aligned to minimize response uncertainty rather than seeking clarification.
However, this work does not study the quality of agent follow-up questions or their effectiveness in disambiguation. DETOUR, on the other hand, encourages agents to ask relevant follow-up questions to further study an agent’s ability to clarify tip-of-the-tongue queries in a multimodal setup.

Interactive Benchmarking of Agents

Conversational search remains a key unsolved problem for modern AI systems
Mo et al. 2025
.
Salle et al. 2022
is among the first to propose a simulation environment to study clarification question characteristics for web queries.
Zamani et al. 2020b
shows that asking good-quality clarification questions can improve search performance.
The MIMICS dataset
Zamani et al. 2020a
provides clarifying questions with human quality labels, paired candidate answers, and associated landing pages, while
Tang et al. 2025
proposes an ambiguity-driven chain-of-thought approach for meaningful disambiguation. However, these benchmarks are largely static; grounded evaluation of user preferences requires dynamic simulation
Balog and Zhai 2025
.
In this spirit,
Barres et al. 2025
designs a multi-turn simulation environment to deterministically evaluate the tool using LLM agents. Building on these principles, DETOUR aims to simulate a
Memory Agent
to more realistically represent and ground user query clarifications.

3
DETOUR

DETOUR is a benchmark for evaluating multi-turn agentic search capabilities. To simulate a realistic evaluation scenario, we model tip-of-the-tongue search in an interactive setting, where one speaker is attempting to recall a specific entity. The other speaker would prompt the person performing the recollection with various questions to guide them to the target entity.

We operationalize the process of recalling a specific entity – tip-of-the-tongue known-item retrieval
Arguello et al. 2021
– by modeling a dual-agent framework consisting of a
Primary
and a
Memory Agent
. The Primary Agent is equipped with a memory tool for querying the Memory Agent with free-text questions, as well as a web search tool. For each DETOURquestion, the Primary Agent has access to an optional input file. The Memory Agent receives a memory file containing often abstract cues about the target entity; the memory file does not directly refer to the answer to prevent information leakage. When responding to queries, the Memory Agent is instructed to ground all responses in the memory file only. Both the web search tool and the memory tool can be called repeatedly to provide clarifying information. See an overview of this process in Figure
2
.

3.1
Data Collection

Professional annotators were hired from an AI data curation company at $20 per hour. We instructed the annotators to remember a time when they could clearly recall the sight or sound of an entity, yet failed to pinpoint exactly what it was called. The annotators are responsible for
formulating the queries
,
collecting
corresponding
input and memory files
, and
creating thought chains
that simulate the Primary Agent’s process of solving the queries using web search and memory query tools. We define the following desiderata for DETOUR:

1.

Answer Non-Ambiguity:
Answers to the queries should be unambiguous. Each question in DETOUR is
validated by a second annotator
to ensure that the final solution is consistent with the original answer.

2.

Prompt Uncertainty:
To realistically simulate the tip-of-the-tongue search scenario, the questions supplied should be underspecified and incomplete.

3.

Reasonable Complexity:
Queries should require extensive reasoning and synthesizing information intelligently from multiple sources. Annotators have been instructed to ensure that their questions cannot be answered through one internet search. To make the quality assurance process more manageable, the amount of time required for a human to answer each query is kept under 30 minutes.

4.

Answer Longevity:
Answers should remain the same over time. Solving the questions should not require subjectivity or knowledge that may evolve with time.

To comply with copyright restrictions, annotators may only collect input and memory files from public-domain sources, such as Pixabay
1
1

1

https://pixabay.com/
. When a question involves recalling a copyrighted song, annotators typically hum the melody themselves rather than copying from recordings. They are also instructed not to disclose any personally identifiable information to minimize the privacy risk associated with our data.

Additional measures were employed to increase the difficulty of DETOUR. Annotators can optionally mix in
intentional errors
in their queries, consistent with BLUR: for instance, referring to a famous battle as occurring in Europe, while it occurred in the United States. Furthermore, we supply the annotators with a
GPT-5
-powered sanity check tool
: given the query and the file input, we prompt
GPT-5
using the tip-of-the-tongue search agent prompt from
Sky et al. 2025
to see if
GPT-5
can successfully arrive at the correct answer utilizing OpenAI’s web search tool. Annotators are encouraged to revise their prompts if
GPT-5
correctly answered without Memory Agent assistance. The authors and the annotators held weekly meetings to update any requirements and discuss any confusion throughout the dataset creation process.

3.2
Benchmark Composition

The final version of DETOUR contains 1,011 questions spanning eight domains. Of the input files, 254 were text-only, 378 contains image file inputs, 189 audio files, and 190 videos. As for the memory files, most of them are images (96.0%), with audio and video files occupying 1.50% and 2.50%, respectively (Figure
3
). We additionally chart DETOUR composition by domain in Figure
3
.

Evaluation Harness

Due to the interactive nature of DETOUR, the prompt of the Primary Agent and the implementation of the Memory Agent must stay consistent when evaluating different models. For the Primary Agent prompt, we modify the system prompt from
Sky et al. 2025
to include instructions to leverage the memory agent, as well as an in-context example to facilitate advanced, abstractive reasoning. We detail this prompt in Appendix
A

5.4

4.1
Evaluating Primary Agent Correctness

Similar to BLUR, we utilize LLM-as-a-judge to evaluate the correctness of the Primary Agent. Specifically,
GPT-4o
is used for its balance of cost-effectiveness and performance. The judge determines whether the Primary Agent’s answer, often in the form of a series of reasoning steps arriving at a final result, is the same as the ground truth answer created by the annotator. We leverage the following prompt:

4.2
Memory Agent Construction

To ensure that DETOUR performance is reflective of the reasoning capabilities of the assessed models, we must confine all reasoning processes to the Primary Agent. Therefore, we restrict the Memory Agent to respond solely based on the content of the memory file, without performing any deductions. We fix the Memory Agent model to be
Gemini-2.5-Pro
for its multi-modal capabilities.

Preliminary experiments reveal that the Primary Agent may occasionally bypass this restriction, resulting in the Memory Agent partaking in reasoning. We resort to prompt optimization to mitigate this issue. In particular, we leverage DSPy’s MIPROv2 optimizer
Opsahl-Ong et al. 2024
to propose a set of 20 alternative prompts for the memory agent. For each of these proposed prompts, we fix the Memory Agent’s instructions to this prompt, and run the full evaluation harness on a held-out validation set of 150 DETOUR instances, collecting the queries to and corresponding responses from the Memory Agent. Finally, we employ
Gemini-2.5-Pro
to judge if each of the responses from the Memory Agent can be directly derived from the memory files. This process improves the percentage of correctly grounded Memory Agent responses from 83.1% to 97.5%. We include the selected Memory Agent prompt in Appendix
B
.

Ablation: Memory Agent Query Tool

We are interested in examining whether the Memory Agent is, in fact, critical to achieving better performance on DETOUR. Therefore, we utilize the same prompt defined in
Sky et al. 2025
to examine the capability of models to pinpoint the correct answer without Memory Agent assistance. We conduct this evaluation on the same set of models and include the results in Table

Comparing the accuracies between the evaluation runs,
performance is significantly worse without the Memory Agent

This further illustrates that models may differ in reliance on the Memory Agent, as discussed in the error analysis section. Specifically, we observe that

To further increase the difficulty, we could enhance the ambiguity present in the questions by removing even more information from both the textual questions and the input files, which we leave to future work.

5.4
Ablation: In-Context Examples

As observed in previous experiments, all evaluated models struggle to effectively interface with the Memory Agent, possibly due to not having been exposed to interactive tools during training. Because in-context examples are used to highlight desirable behaviors
Brown et al. 2020
;
Dong et al. 2024
, we aim to investigate whether using in-context examples that reason about the memory agent more extensively can improve performance.

To better assess the effect of the example, we randomly select 56 text-only question candidates and collect their annotators’ thought traces. Then, we set the example question and in-context example thought trace in the Primary Agent system prompt to be each of these candidates, and evaluate each resulting agent on the last 100 modality-compatible DETOUR instances. This evaluation set does not include the question candidate. We perform this test for
Gemini-2.5-Pro
,
GPT-5
, and
Kimi-K2-Thinking
. This process yields 56 datapoints per model, as we compute the average accuracies for each in-context example candidate. Note that we focus on comparing
within
models rather than
between
models here, as models have different modality capabilities. We detail the variations in LLM-judged accuracies due to changes in in-context examples in Figure
5
.

Varying the in-context examples can significantly impact the resulting accuracy
.
Gemini-2.5-Pro
’s accuracy in this experiment ranges from 36.6% to 54.8%, a staggering 18.2% difference. Similarly,
GPT-5
’s accuracy fluctuates between 45.5% and 59.6%. Variations are even more drastic for
Kimi-K2-Thinking
(
27.0% to 68.4%
). This demonstrates the key role the in-context example plays in our evaluation setup.

Models are likely capable of extracting useful information from the demonstrations; we are now interested in what makes an example useful when evaluating on DETOUR. We use a simple proxy for how much the in-context example is reasoning about the Memory Agent: the number of times the word “memory” has appeared in the thought trace, divided by the number of words in the thought trace. Concretely, we define:

\displaystyle R_{\text{mem}}=\frac{\sum_{i}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\{w_{i}=\textbf{``memory''}\}}{N}

where
N
is the total number of words in the thought trace. The numerator would include mentions of the word “memory” from both explicit invocations of the memory query tool and the Primary Agent’s deliberation when processing the Memory Agent’s responses.

Finally, we compute (1) Spearman’s correlation between
R_{\text{mem}}
and the Primary Agent accuracy, and (2) the ordinary least squares regression results, modeling accuracy as a function of
R_{\text{mem}}
. We document this in Table

Across the board, we see

6
Conclusion

In this work, we propose DETOUR, a multi-turn, interactive benchmark for tip-of-the-tongue search. We enlist human annotators to construct the queries and validate the answers, and introduce a dual-agent evaluation protocol where a
Primary Agent
uses web search and a
Memory Agent
to resolve ambiguous recollections.

Our experiments illustrate that current models continue to struggle in this setting, with notable performance drops in multimodal regimes and frequent failures resulting from ineffective follow-up questions and unproductive memory querying. We find that in-context examples that engage with the Memory Agent more could enhance model performance on our task.

In conclusion, our work serves as an initial exploration into interactive benchmarking for tip-of-the-tongue search. We encourage the development of more interactive benchmarks that better capture real-world conversational search behaviors, and hope DETOUR can provide a standardized testbed for improving clarification strategies, reasoning, and tool use in future agentic search systems.

Limitation

Even though our benchmark is sufficiently challenging for state-of-the-art models, instances in DETOUR can still be answerable even without Memory Agent assistance. We attempted to mitigate this during the data collection process by providing the annotators with the sanity check tool and encouraging them to modify their questions until
GPT-5
sans the Memory Agent fails the answer correctly. As previously mentioned, we leave increasing the difficulty of DETOUR even further as future work.

While we performed some initial analysis into what makes in-context examples particularly useful to models under the DETOUR evaluation settings, our examination remains at the surface level. We only looked into a very simple word-based metric, namely the density of the word “memory” being mentioned. This measure does not necessarily reflect the actual quality of the in-context examples, i.e., whether the logical transitions between intermediate steps in the thought traces are valid and natural. Although examples that are high in our proxy metric may teach models how to interact with the Memory Agent in a human-like manner, they may still exhibit incorrect logic and lead models down rabbit holes. Future analysis work studying the relationship between the in-context examples and model performance on DETOUR would focus on determining the
actual reasoning quality
of in-context examples.

Ethical Considerations

During the data collection process, we have attempted to minimize privacy risk by asking annotators to refrain from discussing personally identifiable content. However, a certain, albeit limited, amount of privacy risk remains. Specifically, annotators expose their voices when humming songs as contextual information for the queries, and speech qualifies as sensitive biometric data under GDPR. To mitigate this in the future, we would consider voice cloning technologies that transform the voices, preserving the core content of the audio clues while sufficiently masking speech characteristics.

To prevent usage of images, audios, and videos without author consent, DETOUR is built completely on license-free content. Unfortunately, the expressivity of materials in this category may be limited. This, in turn, restricts the achievable range of questions from DETOUR, as some clues are too niche to represent with public domain material.
