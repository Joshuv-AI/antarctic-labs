Circulating tumor DNA clearance as a trial-level early efficacy signal in solid tumors: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Sirui Tang, Yujie Chen, Zhonghou Wang, Huiran Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCirculating tumor DNA (ctDNA) is a sensitive marker of residual disease and treatment activity, but its validity as a trial-level surrogate endpoint remains uncertain. Given scarce randomized evidence and clinical and methodological heterogeneity, we performed an exploratory, hypothesis-generating evaluation of trial-level associations between ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes across solid tumors.MethodsRandomized controlled trials reporting ctDNA clearance and overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), or recurrence-related outcomes were identified through April 2026. Analyses were exploratory and conducted at trial level. Weighted regression, leave-one-out cross-validation, copula correlation, Bayesian hierarchical modeling, and sensitivity analyses were performed, with cross-validated R2 (R2cv) prioritized over apparent R2 for out-of-sample interpretation.ResultsEighteen trials were included. Out-of-sample performance was limited: R2cv was 0.41 for PFS, 0.26 for OS, and –1.66 for recurrence-related outcomes. Corresponding apparent R2 estimates were 0.77 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.03–0.96], 0.56 (95% CI: 0.12–0.84), and 0.04 (95% CI: 0.00–0.92), respectively. The estimable PFS signal was derived from eight palliative/metastatic trials. Recurrence-related findings were inconclusive; after excluding the ctDNA-guided DYNAMIC-III trial, apparent R2 increased to 0.67 but R2cv remained negative (–0.18). Post-treatment ctDNA status remained prognostic across endpoints.ConclusionsThese exploratory, hypothesis-generating findings identify an endpoint- and setting-dependent trial-level signal, with the largest observed association for PFS, but do not validate ctDNA clearance as a surrogate endpoint. Wide uncertainty intervals, few contributing trials, and limited cross-validated performance require prospective validation before use in trial qualification or individual treatment decisions.Keywords: Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), surrogate endpoints, clearance, trial-level associations, early treatment response

Key findings

• The trial-level R2cv was 0.41 for progression-free survival (PFS), 0.26 for overall survival, and −1.66 for recurrence-related outcomes. The PFS signal arose from eight palliative/metastatic trials and did not validate surrogacy.

What is known and what is new?

• Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) dynamics are prognostic, but trial-level surrogacy remains uncertain.

• This review prioritized cross-validation and design-based sensitivity analyses.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• ctDNA clearance should remain an exploratory, context-dependent biomarker pending prospective, setting-specific validation.

Introduction

Circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) has become a key biomarker for detecting minimal or molecular residual disease after curative-intent therapy in solid tumors. Recent studies across colorectal, lung, breast, urothelial, and other malignancies have demonstrated that postoperative or post-therapy ctDNA positivity identifies patients at markedly elevated risk of recurrence, often months before radiographic detection (1-3). Similarly, early on-treatment declines in ctDNA have been shown to reflect therapeutic activity and stratify response trajectories more sensitively than conventional imaging (4,5). As adjuvant and perioperative systemic therapies expand in modern oncology, ctDNA dynamics have gained increasing attention as a tool to individualize risk, guide treatment escalation or de-escalation, and potentially function as an early surrogate for long-term clinical outcomes (6,7).

Although multiple trials have demonstrated associations between ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes, evidence supporting ctDNA clearance as a reliable trial-level surrogate remains scarce and heterogeneous across tumor types, treatments, clinical settings, assays, and sampling schedules. Accordingly, this meta-analysis was designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating evaluation of comparative treatment effects across randomized trials, rather than as formal surrogate endpoint validation. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1462/rc).

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was prospectively registered on the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-analysis Protocols (INPLASY; registration number INPLASY2025120021; doi: 10.37766/inplasy2025.12.0021).

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic search of PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science was conducted from database inception to April 15, 2026. Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary and keywords related to ctDNA or molecular residual disease, clearance or longitudinal molecular response, randomized trials, solid tumors, and survival or recurrence outcomes. No restrictions were placed on tumor type or treatment modality. Reference lists of eligible articles and relevant reviews were also screened to identify additional studies.

Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria: (I) randomized controlled trials evaluating systemic anticancer therapy in neoadjuvant/perioperative, adjuvant/postneoadjuvant, or palliative/metastatic settings; (II) reported ctDNA measurements, including ctDNA clearance or ctDNA positivity, assessed at baseline and/or during early treatment or perioperative time points; (III) provided at least one clinical endpoint, including overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), disease-free survival (DFS), or event-free survival (EFS); (IV) included both an experimental arm and a comparator control arm, with sufficient data to extract or derive between-group treatment effect estimates, such as hazard ratios (HRs), relative risks (RRs), or differences in ctDNA clearance proportions.

Studies were excluded if they were: (I) single-arm studies, non-randomized or observational designs; (II) reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, case reports, or conference abstracts without extractable trial-level data; (III) studies lacking valid or interpretable ctDNA measurements, including unclear assay timing, undefined clearance criteria, or absence of baseline assessment; (IV) trials that did not report sufficient information to estimate treatment effects on both ctDNA and clinical endpoints at the trial level; (V) duplicate reports or secondary analyses without additional relevant data.

Rationale for excluding single-arm studies

The restriction to randomized trials with experimental and control groups enabled estimation of comparative treatment effects on both ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes. Single-arm studies cannot provide these between-arm contrasts and were therefore excluded to maintain a coherent trial-level estimand. This restriction reduces the available sample size and does not eliminate heterogeneity arising from differences in populations, treatment regimens, clinical settings, ctDNA assays, sampling schedules, endpoint definitions, or follow-up. The resulting trial-level associations were therefore interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Data extraction and outcomes

Two reviewers independently extracted trial-level data using a standardized form. For each randomized controlled trial, the following information was collected: study characteristics (trial name, registration number, sample size, tumor type, treatment regimen), timing of ctDNA assessment, and length of follow-up. For ctDNA dynamics, we extracted the number of patients with detectable ctDNA at baseline, the number achieving ctDNA clearance in experimental and control arms, and the corresponding clearance proportions. When reported, ctDNA positivity status (positive vs. negative) at the earliest post-treatment time point was also recorded.

ctDNA assessment timing was recorded in weeks as the interval between initiation of the randomized intervention and the first post-baseline assessment used to determine clearance. Sampling times were summarized descriptively, without applying a universal time cutoff across treatment settings.

To apply a consistent extraction rule across heterogeneous ctDNA sampling schedules, ctDNA clearance was defined as conversion from ctDNA-positive at baseline to ctDNA-negative at the earliest protocol-defined post-intervention assessment, with analyses restricted to baseline ctDNA-positive patients. In neoadjuvant trials, clearance was assessed at the first on-treatment or presurgery time point after neoadjuvant therapy. In adjuvant trials, clearance was defined among patients positive at the postoperative baseline and evaluated at the first post-adjuvant-treatment assessment; when post-treatment surveillance was the earliest available time point, this assessment was used. In advanced or metastatic trials, clearance was defined at the earliest on-treatment assessment following treatment initiation.

When multiple post-baseline assessments were reported, the earliest eligible protocol-defined post-intervention assessment was selected within each trial. This rule reduced selective choice among multiple reported time points while retaining between-trial differences in assessment timing. Clearance proportions were extracted separately for experimental and control arms using identical definitions and time points within each trial.

For time-to-event outcomes, we extracted HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for OS, PFS, and recurrence-related outcomes. When necessary, HRs and CIs were converted to log scale to derive standard errors.

Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through consensus.

Median time to ctDNA clearance was extracted when reported.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were exploratory and performed at the trial level. Treatment effects on ctDNA clearance were quantified as the log RR of clearance between experimental and control arms, and treatment effects on clinical outcomes were expressed as log HRs with corresponding standard errors. The treatment effect on ctDNA clearance was defined as the between-arm contrast in clearance proportions among baseline ctDNA-positive patients. PFS was analyzed separately from recurrence-related outcomes; RFS, DFS, and EFS were grouped as recurrence-related outcomes while retaining their original trial-specific definitions.

Trial-level association framework

Trial-level associations were evaluated by relating treatment effects on ctDNA clearance to treatment effects on clinical outcomes across randomized trials, following established meta-analytic surrogate frameworks (8,9). Weighted linear regression used the inverse variance of the clinical-outcome effect estimate. Apparent R2 with 95% CIs, adjusted R2, and slopes were estimated, and leave-one-out cross-validated R2 (R2cv) was used to quantify out-of-sample performance. Negative R2cv indicates that cross-validated predictions perform worse than prediction by the outcome mean.

To account for potential non-linear or rank-based dependence not fully captured by linear regression, trial-level dependence was additionally evaluated using copula-based methods. Gaussian copula ρ and Kendall’s τ were reported only as complementary rank-based dependence measures and not as evidence of validated surrogacy.

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses

Sensitivity and subgroup analyses included leave-one-out analyses by study and analyses by cancer type, treatment modality, disease status, ctDNA marker selection strategy, analytical platform, and treatment setting. Treatment setting was classified as neoadjuvant/perioperative curative-intent, adjuvant/postneoadjuvant curative-intent, or palliative/metastatic. An additional design-based sensitivity analysis excluded DYNAMIC-III because treatment assignment in that trial was guided by ctDNA status. The previously used ≥3-year versus <3-year median-follow-up dichotomy was removed because expected survival and follow-up maturity differ substantially between curative-intent and palliative/metastatic settings, making a universal threshold clinically non-comparable. Follow-up duration is now reported descriptively, and heterogeneity in follow-up maturity is acknowledged as a limitation. The fixed 8-week timing dichotomy was also removed because the clinical meaning of a sampling time differs across treatment settings. Regression estimates were not calculated when fewer than three trials were available for an endpoint-setting stratum.

Bayesian hierarchical modeling

Bayesian hierarchical models were used as complementary analyses of association slopes, predictive distributions, and residual between-trial heterogeneity for OS and PFS (10). The models used the same trial-level treatment-effect definitions as the primary analyses. Posterior summaries included median slopes, 95% credible intervals (CrI), 95% predictive intervals, and the between-trial heterogeneity standard deviation (σ_between).

Prognostic meta-analysis

To evaluate prognostic significance, pooled HRs stratified by post-treatment ctDNA status were calculated using random-effects models, with heterogeneity quantified using I2 and Cochran’s Q statistic (11,12). Leave-one-out analyses were used to assess the influence of individual trials.

Risk of bias and publication bias

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias (RoB) 2.0 tool across five domains. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s regression test (13), Begg’s rank-correlation test (14), and the trim-and-fill method (15) to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and adjust pooled HRs.

Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) evidence assessment

We evaluated the certainty of evidence for each clinical endpoint using the GRADE framework, considering five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias (16,17). Evidence derived from randomized trials was initially rated as high certainty and subsequently downgraded when concerns were identified in any domain. The final certainty ratings for OS, PFS, and recurrence-related outcomes were determined through consensus based on quantitative meta-analytic findings and domain-specific judgments.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2). Meta-analyses were performed using the meta package (version 7.0-0) and metafor (version 4.6-0). Bayesian hierarchical models were implemented using rstan (version 2.32.6) and rstanarm (version 2.32.1). Data management and visualization were conducted using dplyr (version 1.1.4) and ggplot2 (version 3.5.1).

Results

All 18 trials reported ctDNA clearance data for the experimental and control groups and at least one eligible clinical endpoint. Study characteristics and trial-specific endpoint definitions are provided in Table 1 and Table S2, respectively. The included trials comprised four neoadjuvant/perioperative, six adjuvant/postneoadjuvant, and eight palliative/metastatic trials (Table 1, Table S3).

Trial-level association between ctDNA clearance and clinical outcomes

Across the primary trial-level models, R2cv was 0.41 for PFS, 0.26 for OS, and −1.66 for recurrence-related outcomes. The corresponding apparent R2 estimates were 0.77 (95% CI: 0.03–0.96), 0.56 (95% CI: 0.12–0.84), and 0.04 (95% CI: 0.00–0.92), respectively (Figure 1, Table 2, Table S5). For PFS, adjusted R2 was 0.732 and the slope was −1.024 (P=0.002); for OS, adjusted R2 was 0.543 and the slope was −0.241 (P=0.001); and for recurrence-related outcomes, adjusted R2 was −0.058 and the slope was −0.146 (P=0.48). The largest observed association was for PFS, but its wide CI and R2cv of 0.41 indicated substantial imprecision and limited out-of-sample performance. OS performance was also limited, and the recurrence-related findings were inconclusive because the apparent R2 CI was extremely wide and R2cv was negative.

Sensitivity and setting-based subgroup analyses

Leave-one-out estimates for all endpoint models are shown in Figure S2A-S2F. The largest changes were observed after omission of RELAY, ADAURA, CameL-Sq, or NCT04228614 for OS; NCT04228614, SAMCO-PRODIGE 54, or RELAY for PFS; and DYNAMIC-III or ADAURA for recurrence-related outcomes. After exclusion of DYNAMIC-III, the recurrence model yielded an apparent R2 of 0.67, adjusted R2 of 0.615, slope of −0.367 (95% CI: −0.624 to −0.109; P=0.01), and R2cv of −0.18 (Table S6). The increase in apparent R2 was not accompanied by a positive R2cv.

Setting-stratified estimates are reported in Table S3. For OS, the curative-intent stratum included eight trials (R2cv=0.26; apparent R2=0.757; slope =−0.228; P=0.005), and the palliative/metastatic stratum included seven trials (R2cv=−0.73; apparent R2=0.456; slope =−0.247; P=0.10). For PFS, the curative-intent stratum contained one trial and was not estimable; the palliative/metastatic stratum included eight trials (R2cv=0.29; apparent R2=0.763; slope =−1.022; P=0.005). All nine recurrence-related trials were curative-intent (R2cv=−1.66; apparent R2=0.075; slope =−0.146; P=0.48).

Subgroup estimates by cancer type, treatment modality, disease status, ctDNA marker selection strategy, and analytical platform are shown in Table 2 and Figure S3. Positive R2cv values were observed for resectable-disease OS (0.48), tumor-agnostic OS (0.47), next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based OS (0.37), unresectable-disease PFS (0.44), tumor-agnostic PFS (0.52), and NGS-based PFS (0.77); the remaining reported subgroup R2cv values were 0.01 or lower.

In endpoint-subtype analyses, R2cv was 0.13 for DFS-only data (three trials), −0.11 for EFS-only data (four trials), −3.19 for combined RFS/DFS data excluding EFS (five trials), and 0.26 for combined DFS/EFS data excluding RFS (seven trials); RFS-only data were not estimated because only two trials were available (Table S6). For the DFS/EFS analysis excluding RFS, apparent R2 was 0.819, adjusted R2 was 0.783, and the slope was −0.462 (P=0.005).

Bayesian trial-level analysis

The Bayesian OS model yielded a posterior median slope (β) of −0.237 (95% CrI: −0.395 to −0.093) and σ_between of 0.062 (95% CrI: 0.003–0.198). The PFS model yielded a posterior median slope of −1.053 (95% CrI: −1.649 to −0.436) and σ_between of 0.385 (95% CrI: 0.155–0.690) (Figure 2A,2B, Table S7). The posterior slope intervals excluded zero for both endpoints, and σ_between was larger for PFS than for OS.

Prognostic value of ctDNA positivity

For OS, 11 studies yielded a pooled HR of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.25–0.39; I2=0%). For recurrence-related outcomes, nine studies yielded a pooled HR of 0.47 (95% CI: 0.41–0.53; I2=76.8%). For PFS, seven studies yielded a pooled HR of 0.43 (95% CI: 0.35–0.52; I2=0%) (Figure 3A-3C, Table S9). All three pooled HRs were below 1.0; heterogeneity was highest for recurrence-related outcomes.

In leave-one-out analyses, the pooled HR for each endpoint remained below 1.0 after omission of individual studies (Figure S4A-S4C).

Risk of bias assessment

Five of 18 studies were judged to have low overall risk of bias, and 13 had some concerns; no study was judged to have high overall risk of bias. Deviations from intended interventions were the most frequent source of concern (Table S10, Figure S5).

Publication bias

Funnel plots for OS and recurrence-related outcomes showed visual asymmetry, whereas the PFS funnel plot was more symmetric (Figure S6A-6C). Egger’s test yielded P values of 0.26 for OS, 0.31 for recurrence-related outcomes, and 0.90 for PFS; Begg’s test yielded P values of 0.56, 0.18, and 0.76, respectively. Trim-and-fill estimated four missing studies on the right for OS, three on the left for recurrence-related outcomes, and none for PFS; the adjusted log HRs were −0.29, −0.67, and −0.75, respectively (Table S11).

GRADE evidence assessment

GRADE certainty was rated low for OS and PFS and very low for recurrence-related outcomes (Table S12). OS and PFS were downgraded for indirectness and imprecision; recurrence-related outcomes were additionally downgraded for inconsistency and suspected publication bias.

Discussion

This exploratory analysis showed limited and endpoint-dependent out-of-sample performance. R2cv was 0.41 for PFS, 0.26 for OS, and −1.66 for recurrence-related outcomes. Although the apparent R2 for PFS was 0.77, its 95% CI ranged from 0.03 to 0.96, indicating substantial imprecision. The OS model also showed limited cross-validated performance, whereas the recurrence-related analysis was inconclusive because its apparent R2 CI was extremely wide and its R2cv was negative. The PFS estimate was derived from only eight palliative/metastatic trials, which represents a central limitation rather than evidence of surrogate validation. These findings do not validate ctDNA clearance as a surrogate endpoint.

ctDNA clearance should be viewed as a context-dependent pharmacodynamic biomarker rather than a universally valid surrogate endpoint. Its appeal lies in providing an early molecular readout of treatment activity, but surrogate qualification requires between-arm effects on clearance to anticipate between-arm effects on clinically meaningful outcomes reproducibly. The appropriate role of ctDNA clearance is therefore continued prospective evaluation rather than immediate substitution for established clinical endpoints (8-10).

Endpoint proximity is an important consideration when evaluating ctDNA clearance. Changes in ctDNA can precede radiographic response and have been associated with response, PFS, and OS during systemic therapy (37-41). PFS is temporally closer to early tumor-burden change, whereas OS is further shaped by post-progression survival, subsequent therapy, crossover, competing mortality, and longer follow-up (42). Across oncology, trial-level relationships between intermediate endpoints and OS are frequently low to moderate (43,44). A stronger association with an earlier endpoint should therefore be interpreted as biological plausibility, not as evidence of validated surrogacy.

The interpretation of ctDNA clearance is treatment-context dependent. In neoadjuvant and perioperative trials, clearance before definitive local therapy may reflect cytoreduction and response to induction treatment; in adjuvant and postneoadjuvant trials, conversion from postoperative positivity to negativity more directly reflects eradication of molecular residual disease; and in palliative or metastatic trials, early decline is dominated by systemic tumor-burden kinetics (18,20,26,28,40,41). Because baseline sampling, treatment initiation, surgery, and outcome follow-up occupy different positions on the disease timeline, identical week-based thresholds do not necessarily represent identical biological states. Setting-specific estimands and validation are therefore preferable to a universal clearance time point.

Trials in which ctDNA status determines treatment introduce a different estimand from trials in which ctDNA is measured without guiding management. When biomarker status influences therapy, biomarker-outcome associations reflect both disease biology and the treatment adaptation it triggers (27). Such designs are valuable for testing clinical utility, but they should be evaluated separately from conventional biomarker-surrogacy analyses.

Surrogate validation should be setting-specific and based on sufficiently large collections of randomized trials. Small trial collections can inflate apparent R2, produce unstable slopes, and make individual studies disproportionately influential. Formal qualification requires reproducible treatment-effect associations at the trial level and, ideally, complementary patient-level validation within clinically coherent settings (8-10,43,44). Cross-validation and external validation should be treated as core criteria rather than secondary sensitivity analyses.

Trial-level and patient-level questions should remain conceptually separate. A trial-level association asks whether treatments that change ctDNA clearance more also change clinical outcomes more; it does not establish that an individual patient who clears ctDNA will necessarily experience improved survival. A biomarker may be prognostic because it captures residual disease burden yet fail as a surrogate if between-arm changes in the biomarker do not reliably predict between-arm changes in the clinical endpoint (8-10).

The prognostic value of ctDNA and its validity as a surrogate endpoint are distinct properties. Primary studies across colorectal, lung, and urothelial cancers have shown that postoperative or post-treatment ctDNA positivity stratifies the risks of recurrence, progression, or death (1-3,18,45-47), and systematic syntheses report consistent prognostic value across resectable solid tumors (48-51). Nevertheless, risk stratification alone does not demonstrate that treatment-induced changes in ctDNA capture treatment-attributable differences in long-term outcomes.

Future ctDNA studies will benefit from greater standardization in pre-analytical handling, assay validation, bioinformatic reporting, sampling schedules, and molecular-response definitions. Current recommendations emphasize analytical sensitivity and specificity, limit-of-detection reporting, quality control, and explicit interpretation of negative results (7,52-55). For surrogate validation, these technical requirements should be paired with prespecified sampling windows, harmonized clearance definitions, clinically coherent settings, and external or cross-validated assessment. Integration with imaging and pathological response may further clarify when molecular clearance adds information beyond established response measures.

Interpretation is constrained by the use of aggregate trial-level data, clinical and technical heterogeneity, non-equivalent recurrence endpoints, variable sampling schedules, and immature survival follow-up. The PFS estimate was derived from only eight palliative/metastatic trials, far fewer than required for surrogate validation, and should be regarded as a central limitation rather than evidence of validation. Fixed 8-week sampling and 3-year follow-up subgroup cutoffs were removed because their clinical meanings differ between curative-intent and palliative/metastatic settings; follow-up duration is now reported descriptively. These features can weaken transportability across settings and amplify overfitting in surrogate models. Publication-bias assessments are also inherently underpowered when the evidence base is small. Future validation should therefore use prespecified estimands, harmonized assays and time windows, clinically coherent trial collections, and individual patient data where possible.

Conclusions

In summary, this exploratory, hypothesis-generating meta-analysis identified an endpoint- and setting-dependent trial-level signal, with the largest observed association for PFS in palliative/metastatic trials. However, wide uncertainty, few contributing trials, influential study designs, and limited cross-validated performance preclude surrogate endpoint qualification. The OS signal was limited out of sample, and recurrence-related findings were inconclusive rather than negative. Standardized assessment and prospective, setting-specific validation are required before ctDNA clearance is used for trial-level surrogate claims or individual treatment decisions.
