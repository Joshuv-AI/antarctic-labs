Depersonalization, Emotion Embodiment, and Alexithymia in the General Population
Authors: Julia G. Lebovitz, Sohee Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlterations in emotional experiences have been demonstrated in depersonalization-derealization disorder, typically highlighting a blunting of emotions and decreased ability to recognize and name emotions (i.e., alexithymia). However, less research has focused on how individuals with depersonalization experiences embody emotion states. Therefore, the present study investigated emotional embodiment and alexithymia in a general population sample with high (n = 33) and low (n = 66) levels of depersonalization.MethodsUsing a computerized topographical mapping task, the emBODY task, participants indicated where they experience sensations associated with specific emotion states.ResultsWe found that increased depersonalization was associated with increased alexithymia. Individuals with higher levels of depersonalization showed qualitative differences in bodily sensation maps, including more diffused and less localized embodiment for certain high arousal emotion states.ConclusionThese findings emphasize that individuals with increased levels of depersonalization may not simply experience a blanket numbing of emotions but instead may have increased attunement to certain high arousal emotion states.

Introduction

Depersonalization-derealization disorder (DPD) is a dissociative disorder involving profound disconnection and detachment from one’s sense of self (depersonalization) and environment (derealization). Depersonalization experiences are common in the general population, affecting up to 74% of individuals at some point in their lives and, therefore, can be conceptualized as a dimensional construct spanning from non-pathological to pathological experiences [1]. These experiences typically manifest as transient feelings of being on “autopilot,” feeling “spaced out,” or perceiving oneself or the environment as “unreal.” Despite being the third most common psychiatric symptom after depressed mood and anxiety, the underlying mechanisms and phenomenology of depersonalization experiences remain poorly understood [2, 3].

DPD is characterized by altered bodily experiences and disconnection from bodily signals, including physiological affective signals [4]. However, the full extent and phenomenology of this bodily disconnection remain unknown. Disconnection from emotional experiences has been consistently linked with depersonalization since early conceptualizations of the disorder [5]. Factor analyses have identified emotional numbing (i.e., “de-affectualization”) as a predominant feature of DPD [6, 7], with most DPD participants endorsing some level of attenuated emotion experience, including a blunting of emotion and loss of pleasure and/or affection [8]. Included in this factor is the reduced ability to experience some bodily sensations, suggesting some level of emotion disembodiment in DPD. Along those lines, the inability to detect or feel connected to internal signals more broadly, such as hunger and thirst signals, has been identified as a core mechanism underlying dissociative experiences, suggesting alterations in interoceptive processing [9]. The severity of emotional detachment varies significantly among individuals with DPD, ranging from a complete inability to feel distinct emotional states to experiencing emotions in a muted manner [10]. Furthermore, these individuals often experience a disconnect from their autobiographical memories, frequently describing minimal emotional resonance with past experiences and perceiving these memories from an observer’s perspective. This “emotional coloring” appears to be a core component of perceptual changes in DPD, contributing to the characteristic sensation that one’s reality is somehow artificial or unreal [10].

In DPD, individuals demonstrate reduced physiological response when viewing affective stimuli compared to control participants and patients with anxiety [11]. Similarly, Lawrence et al. [12] measured physiological arousal, while participants read emotive diary extracts and found that individuals with DPD exhibited incongruent speech patterns to the emotional content (e.g., accelerated speech during the sad vignette). This research suggests some level of cognitive-physiological disconnect with affective processes in DPD, and some degree of reduced embodiment as individuals are less attuned to physiological signals and speech production associated with affective responses. Therefore, altered emotion processing may be an important driver in the conceptualization and maintenance of DPD.

Alterations in emotion have also been identified in nonclinical populations with elevated depersonalization symptoms. Research suggests that emotion dysregulation plays a critical role in depersonalization, with studies indicating that ineffective emotion regulation strategies (e.g., suppression and reappraisal) correlate significantly with depersonalization severity [13]. Phenomenological investigations with individuals reporting high depersonalization levels have documented affective changes, including emotional flatness and rapid emotional shifts [14]. In the same study, emotional numbing was predictive of altered self-experience (as indexed by the Psychotic-Like Anomalous Self-Experiences scale [IPASE] [15]), emphasizing the importance of de-affectualization in driving bodily self-disturbance and depersonalization-related experiences.

Bodily self-disturbance, particularly reduced embodiment and body ownership, have been found in individuals with high levels of depersonalization experiences [16, 17]. Yet, research has not looked at how individuals with high rates of depersonalization embody and locate emotions. In their seminal work, Nummenmaa et al. [18] developed a tool to topographically map where people locate bodily sensations during emotion states. Using the emBODY tool, participants are asked to indicate on bodily silhouettes where they feel sensations when experiencing specific emotions, like anger or happiness. This mapping approach revealed distinct topographical patterns for different emotion categories, with both activation and deactivation patterns across the body. The resulting bodily sensation maps represent where people report feeling sensations for each emotion, estimating embodiment patterns across emotion conditions. This research highlights how bodily signals are essential for interpreting and understanding emotions.

Research using the emBODY tool has extended to psychiatric populations, revealing altered patterns of reported sensation locations [19]. A review identified that individuals with psychiatric disorders showed less clearly differentiated bodily maps for specific emotions [19]. This effect was particularly pronounced in schizophrenia, where participants demonstrate less differentiated spatial maps of reported sensation locations for emotions compared to controls [20, 21].

When bodily sensations emerge in an affective context, these internal signals must be interpreted and appropriately linked to emotion concepts and categories. Undifferentiated or poorly differentiated body maps of emotions suggest difficulties in connecting bodily sensations to discrete emotion categories. This difficulty recognizing, identifying, and describing one’s feelings and emotions (i.e. alexithymia), [22] may be related to key problems in emotion regulation, such as attending to and appraising affective states [23]. Alexithymia and dissociation broadly have been strongly linked, with individuals with clinical dissociative experiences endorsing high rates of alexithymia, but little research has focused on DPD specifically [24]. A study comparing DPD, post-traumatic stress disorder, and healthy controls found higher levels of alexithymia in the DPD sample, particularly in difficulty identifying feelings factor [25]. Alexithymia was found to be highly discriminative of DPD diagnosis. These findings are consistent with early factor analyses of DPD symptoms, which identified the inability to feel and experience emotions as a core feature of DPD [26].

Despite these findings, research has not yet investigated relationships among emotional disembodiment, alexithymia, and depersonalization. Understanding these relationships in the general population provides crucial insights into the mechanisms that drive clinical depersonalization experiences and potentially inform early intervention approaches. This study addresses this gap by examining these constructs in a general population sample, split into high and low depersonalization experiences. We expected that individuals with high depersonalization levels will demonstrate increased alexithymia and altered embodiment (as indexed by smaller proportion of the body colored and reduced clarity) compared to those with low depersonalization levels.

Methods

Participants

Participants (N = 155) were recruited using Prolific, a crowdsourcing website connecting researchers with potential research participants. All participants (ages 18+) who resided in the USA and had a Prolific study approval rating above 90% were invited to complete the survey. All survey responses were de-identified and tied to the participant’s prolific ID.

High/low DP designations were defined by Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS) scores, with high DP defined as CDS total >50 and low DP defined as CDS total <30. Therefore, individuals with CDS scores 30–50 (n = 24) were excluded from the analysis. Further, individuals were excluded from analyses for failed attention checks (n = 9), had a survey completion time <10 min (n = 1), and did not complete the emBODY task (n = 22). Therefore, the final sample size was 99. Participants were primarily female (61.6%) and middle-aged (M = 34.07, SD = 9.05) (see Table 1 for participant characteristics).

Procedure

On Prolific, participants were directed to complete our REDCap survey, which consisted of demographic information, self-report measures, and an embodiment of emotion task (emBODY) (see below). Participants were paid USD 12 for the completion of the survey. The protocol was approved by the Vanderbilt University Institutional Review Board (IRB Exempt #241382).

Measures

Cambridge Depersonalization Scale

The Cambridge Depersonalization Scale (CDS) is a 29-item, widely used self-report on depersonalization and derealization experiences [26]. The CDS measures different facets of DPD, including feelings of disembodiment, unreality of surroundings, numbing, and perceptual alterations. Each item is rated on two Likert scales measuring the frequency and duration of the experience. The total score is the sum of all items (ranging 0–290). A clinical cutoff of 70 has been shown to have a sensitivity of 75.5% and a specificity of 87.2%. Prior literature has used a threshold CDS score of 50 to investigate clinically meaningful experiences of depersonalization in the general population [17]. The scale has been shown to have high internal consistency and is commonly used in the literature to designate DPD diagnosis [26]. A factor analysis identified four factors: anomalous body experience, emotion numbing, anomalous subjective recall, alienation from surroundings [7]. Given the research topic, this study focused on the CDS total score and emotion numbing factor.

Toronto Alexithymia Scale

The Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS) is a 20-item, widely used self-report measure capturing alexithymia experiences. Participants are asked to respond on a Likert scale (ranging from 1 = never true to 5 = very often true) [22]. The scale has three factors: difficulty identifying feelings (identifying), difficulty describing feelings (describing), and externally oriented thinking (i.e., a tendency to focus on external, superficial information rather than internal thought). Total scores range from 20 to 100. Scores >60 indicate clinical levels of alexithymia, and scores 52–60 indicate possible levels of alexithymia. The TAS has demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81) and acceptable internal consistency for each of the three factors [22]. Following the introduction of the scale, subsequent studies exercised caution when interpreting the externally oriented thinking factor due to findings of poor reliability [27]. Therefore, this study focuses on the identifying and describing factors.

EmBODY

The emBODY tool is a computerized, topographical instrument that generates bodily maps associated with different emotions using a self-report method [18]. For each trial, participants were shown an emotion word alongside two blank body outlines and asked to color the body regions where they felt increased sensations (activation; left body outline) and weaker sensations (deactivation; right body outline) using their computer mouse (see Fig. 1). Fourteen emotion words (neutral, fear, anger, disgust, sadness, happiness, lonely, anxiety, love, depression, pride, shame, jealousy, stress) and two control words (migraine, nausea) were presented in random order. The control words were included to verify participants’ understanding of the task. Initially developed by Nummenmaa et al. [18], the emBODY task has been used to examine bodily experiences of emotions and cognitions in clinical samples [19].

Data Analysis

TAS

T tests were used to determine group differences in TAS total score and factor scores. Spearman correlations were used to explore the relationships between TAS scores with CDS total + CDS emotion numbing factor. False discovery rate correction was applied to all correlations.

emBODY

To generate body maps, we applied a nonparametric analysis method appropriate for small sample sizes (n < 100), drawing from prior work with the emBODY tool [20]. Rather than using parametric statistical tests (e.g., mass univariate t tests), we visualized participant agreement in bodily sensation reports through a frequency-based, group-level mapping approach.

Each participant’s activation and deactivation drawings were first smoothed using a Gaussian disk to reduce pixel noise and account for the spatial spread introduced by digital coloring tools. For each emotion and body pixel, we then calculated two proportions across all participants in a group: the proportion reporting activation (P) and the proportion reporting deactivation (N). These were combined into a composite score using the formula:F=P‐N×P+N

This value captures both the direction of sensation (activation vs. deactivation) and the degree of participant agreement. Values close to +1 indicate strong consensus on activation, values near −1 indicate deactivation, and values near 0 reflect low agreement or mixed reports. The resulting group-level matrix (F) for each emotion was used to generate group maps, with a fixed color range applied across groups to enable direct comparison. We conducted five emBODY-based analyses:1.Aggregate group maps: these maps, based on the F matrices, visualize where groups showed agreement in reported bodily sensations for each emotion. Group maps reflect areas of overlap across participants and should be interpreted as visual summaries of reported sensation, not as statistically significant outcomes.2.Group Difference Maps: To highlight where bodily sensations diverged between groups (e.g., high DP vs. low DP), we subtracted each group’s P and N matrices. This subtraction yielded pixel-wise difference maps for both activation and deactivation.3.Size (proportion of body colored): To estimate the size or extent of reported sensation, we calculated the proportion of the body colored for each participant and emotion. This was done by dividing the number of non-zero (i.e., colored) pixels by the total number of pixels in the body template (50,364). This metric measures the extent to which the body is marked, regardless of the intensity or continuity of the coloring. While conceptually related to spatial diffusion, it does not reflect how localized or dispersed the sensation is, only its total surface area.4.Similarity (between-group map agreement): we assessed the similarity between group-level F matrices using Spearman’s rank correlations across all 50,364 pixels for each emotion. This nonparametric method was chosen because pixel values followed an exponential distribution due to the high number of uncolored (zero value) pixels.5.Clarity (mixed pixels): To evaluate the clarity of bodily sensations, we calculated the number of pixels that were simultaneously marked as both activated and deactivated by a participant for a given emotion. These “mixed pixels” were summed to create a clarity index, where higher scores indicate more overlap between activation and deactivation (i.e., less clearly differentiated sensations).

All statistical analyses were conducted using MATLAB r2024a and R Studio (R version 4.5.1 (2025-06-13)).

Results

The mean CDS score across participants was 37.98 (SD = 43.90). We designated 33 participants to the high DP group and 66 to the low DP group according to their CDS score (high DP >50; low DP <30).

Alexithymia

The high DP group endorsed increased total alexithymia scores (t = 4.90, p < 0.001), as well as increased difficulties with the ability to recognize and differentiate emotions (identifying; t = 6.43, p < 0.001) and the ability to articulate and communicate emotion experiences (describing; t = 3.57, p < 0.001). The mean TAS score for the high DP group was in the “possible alexithymia” range, while in the “no alexithymia” range for the low DP group. For the low DP group, 10 participants (15.2%) scored in the “possible alexithymia” range, while 4 (6.1%) scored in the “clinical alexithymia” range. For the high DP group, 3 (9.1%) scored in the “possible alexithymia” range, and 14 (42.4%) scored in the “clinical alexithymia” range. This demonstrates that over half of high DP participants endorsed possible or clinical levels of alexithymia, as indexed by the TAS (see Table 2; Fig. 2).

Aggregate group maps (see Fig. 3) represent the differences between the low and high DP groups in the embodiment of emotions. The high DP group showed greater agreement in reported bodily sensations for negative, high arousal emotions – such as fear, anxiety, and anger – as reflected in the group-level emBODY maps.

Spearman R correlations show between-group similarity for emotion conditions (see Fig. 5). The greatest between-group agreement was observed in the physical sensation control condition, nausea. This finding suggests that participants were able to perform the emBODY task according to the instructions and that both groups located similar bodily sensations associated with nausea in the stomach area.

Spearman R correlations for each emBODY condition by high and low DP. Larger correlation values indicate greater group similarity.

There was also strong agreement between the two groups for love and pride, two positive emotions, where both groups indicated activations in the chest and head regions. However, there was little between-group agreement on loneliness, sadness, and shame.

There were no group differences in mixed pixels (W = 1,041.5, p = 0.72) or proportion of body colored (W = 980.0, p = 0.42). Of note, median mixed pixel values were low across conditions. Mean size was not significantly correlated with CDS total (rs = 0.12, p = 0.22) or TAS total (rs = 0.02, p = 0.82). Similarly, mean mixed pixels was not significantly correlated with CDS total (rs = −0.06, p = 0.57) or TAS total (rs = 0.04, p = 0.69) (see online supplementary Table 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553161) for group differences in mixed pixel and size values per condition.

Discussion

Depersonalization experiences in the general population may reflect a fundamental alteration in how emotions are noticed and embodied. We found that high levels of depersonalization in the general population were associated with increased alexithymia and altered embodiment of emotions, particularly for negative-valence, high-arousal emotion conditions. While there were no significant group differences in mixed pixels or proportion of the body colored, there were observable qualitative changes. Overall, the high and low DP groups demonstrated similar body sensation maps across emotion conditions. The high DP group showed more diffused and less localized embodiment compared to low DP, suggesting they experienced an increase in bodily signals that are less specific. The high DP group endorsed increased bodily sensations of negative emotions, such as depression, fear, anxiety, and anger. There was strong between-group agreement in positive valence emotions, such as love and pride. There was more divergence in negative, low-arousal conditions, such as shame, sadness, and loneliness, where the group difference maps showed that high DP endorsed more embodiment in their head region compared to low DP. In these conditions, high DP reported both activation and deactivation of sensations, demonstrating less clarity about these emotion states.

The increased alexithymia in the high DP group aligns with clinical observations in DPD and may represent a key mechanism driving depersonalization experiences even at subclinical levels [25]. However, the relationship between depersonalization, alexithymia, and bodily sensations associated with emotions is less direct, as we did not find associations between emBODY metrics and alexithymia. On a cognitive level, individuals report difficulties identifying emotions. But in the emBODY task, the high DP group reported increased embodiment of sensations for several emotion conditions, particularly high arousal emotions such as anger and anxiety. This finding diverges from expectations of general “blunting” with depersonalization experiences [7, 8, 28]. Instead, our results indicate that certain emotions are felt vividly by those with high levels of depersonalization, and there may be an increase in internal bodily signals.

In addition, these findings are aligned with high rates of anxiety found in individuals with depersonalization [6, 29, 30]. While skin conductance response to specific emotion paradigms (i.e., phasic skin conductance response) has been shown to be attenuated in DPD, tonic skin conductance response is elevated [31], implying a heightened baseline arousal state. In fact, individuals with high rates of depersonalization often report increased self-checking of internal states (e.g., hyper-attunement to heart rate and bodily sensations) as well as rumination and over-intellectualization of existential and philosophical questions [6, 32, 33]. This increased attunement to certain internal states may lead to more diffused emotion states and feelings of strangeness and unfamiliarity, which could explain the increase in alexithymia, or reduced emotional clarity. This is often observed in schizophrenia where hyper-reflexivity and hyper-attunement to experience results in alterations in self-experience [34]. As our emBODY task is self-reported, more research is needed to understand if increased depersonalization is associated with blunting or heightened emotional experience.

An important consideration when interpreting the association between depersonalization and alexithymia is the potential conceptual overlap between the CDS and TAS. Specifically, individuals endorsing CDS items reflecting emotional numbing (e.g., not feeling emotions when laughing or crying, reduced fear, or diminished affection toward others) may also endorse TAS items assessing difficulties identifying feelings, not necessarily because of a primary deficit in emotion identification, but because emotional experiences are attenuated or less accessible. Therefore, some shared variance may reflect the phenomenological consequences of emotional detachment, although the CDS and TAS remain theoretically distinct measures of depersonalization and alexithymia, respectively.

It is important to note limitations of this research. Data were collected using Prolific, an online crowdsourcing website. Although validation checks were in place, we were unable to verify the authenticity and engagement of each participant. The use of an online sample may have limited results and could explain the lower levels of embodiment than expected. We did not control for comorbid psychiatric conditions, which may have impacted results. Our measures are all self-report and, therefore, rely on subjective experiences. While the emBODY tool estimates embodiment in asking participants to indicate where they feel changes in bodily sensations, we did not collect any objective physiological measurement. Future studies investigating the connection between physiological measurement (e.g., heart rate, skin conductance) and self-report embodiment of emotion would be useful in clarifying this bodily cognitive disconnect. In addition, our data were collected in a general population sample. While high rates of depersonalization were endorsed, clinical diagnosis could not be confirmed. Therefore, future research should extend these results to a clinical sample. We did not screen for neurodevelopmental conditions, including autism spectrum disorder. Given evidence that these conditions are associated with differences in emotional awareness, interoception, and alexithymia, some of the experiences observed in the present sample may have been influenced by neurodivergence rather than depersonalization alone [35, 36]. Finally, our data were cross-sectional and could not be used to make causal inferences.

In sum, our findings indicate individuals with elevated rates of depersonalization exhibit higher rates of alexithymia and altered localization of emotions. Our results support prior research highlighting altered emotion experience but emphasize that emotional experiences are complex. Participants did not endorse a blanket numbing of emotions but instead located heightened and diffused bodily activation for negative-valence, high-arousal states. Having too many physiological signals that are nonspecific may lead to feelings of overwhelm and detachment. Given that disembodiment constitutes a core feature of DPD, with patients consistently reporting alterations in spatial self-location, investigating how individuals with DPD embody emotional states may provide crucial insights for clinical assessment and intervention strategies.
