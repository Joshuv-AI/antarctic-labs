Microplastics in nucleus pulposus tissue of humans revealed by Raman mosaic imaging screening
Authors: Zhaoxiang Zeng, Rongzeng Huang, Chengwu Song, Xingliang Xiang, Zhiwei Li, Yishu Wang, Yuhan Wang, Ling Zhu, Xiaojuan Wang, Xugui Li, Shuna Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastics (MPs) have been detected in various tissues, yet evidence regarding the relatively isolated nucleus pulposus (NP) remains limited. This study investigated MPs in NP tissues from 51 patients undergoing lumbar disc herniation (LDH) surgery in Wuhan, China. Samples underwent chemical digestion and filtration, followed by candidate-particle screening using an operator-assisted micro-Raman spectroscopy mosaic imaging workflow and targeted Raman spectral assignment. Microplastic-sized polymer particles were Raman-assigned in five patients, including polyurethane foam, poly (propylene glycol), poly (ethylene-co-vinyl acetate), polyacrylate rubber, and poly (ethylene-co-propylene). A graphite particle was also detected in one additional patient. Specifically, nine particles ranging from 37 to 280 μm were detected across six patients; notably, most were smaller than 100 μm. To reduce false-positive assignments, procedural blanks and five known surgical materials were analyzed, and particles matching these sampled materials were excluded. This study provides preliminary evidence of particulate contaminants in NP tissue, demonstrating the feasibility of detection in this understudied anatomical compartment and establishing a methodological foundation for future investigations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58045-5.Keywords: Microplastics, Nucleus pulposus, Spectrum analysis, Raman, Imaging mosaics, Intervertebral disc displacementSubject terms: Materials science, Medical research

Introduction

Exponential growth in global plastic production, driven by single-use, low-recyclability products, has precipitated severe environmental contamination1,2. Environmental degradation fragments these plastics into microplastics (MPs, < 5 mm), which infiltrate ecosystems and subsequently enter humans via ingestion, inhalation, and dermal absorption3–5. Recent evidence from indoor commercial environments further showed that size-segregated micro- and nanoplastics can be present in indoor air, including small particles that may be inhalable6. Recent studies have detected MPs in diverse human tissues, including lungs7, colon8, placenta9, kidneys10, thrombi11, and the synovium of lower limb joints12. These particles are linked to oxidative stress, reproductive toxicity, and inflammatory responses, posing serious health risks13. Understanding MP distribution and accumulation in human tissues is essential for evaluating their toxicological impacts and developing mitigation strategies.

The nucleus pulposus (NP), the gelatinous core of the intervertebral disc, plays a pivotal role in spinal biomechanical stability14. Encapsulated by the avascular annulus fibrosus, the NP is an isolated microenvironment without blood vessels or nerves15. While MPs enter via ingestion, inhalation, or dermal routes and accumulate in vascularized organs, their penetration into avascular sites like the NP remains underexplored16. Animal models link chronic MP accumulation to inflammation and organ damage, raising questions about effects in isolated tissues17–19. Pathological conditions can compromise biological barriers, increasing permeability12. Invasive surgery might also damage sealed organs, facilitating MP entry20. These factors underscore the vulnerability of enclosed compartments, motivating investigations into MP distribution.

Raman spectroscopy is a widely used method for detecting MPs6,21,22. However, conventional Raman-based analysis often relies on manual observation, particle selection, counting, and spectral acquisition, which is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to operator-dependent selection bias23–25. Previous studies comparing manual, semi-automated, and automated spectroscopic workflows have shown that automated or image-assisted approaches can improve analytical throughput, but particle selection, blank correction, false-positive identification, and complex matrix interference remain important challenges26–28. Therefore, practical screening workflows are needed to improve full-filter visualization and candidate particle selection before targeted Raman confirmation. In this study, we introduced a micro-Raman spectroscopy-based mosaic imaging screening workflow in which low-magnification images of the filter membrane were collected and stitched into a panoramic mosaic. This approach enabled full-field visualization of particles retained on the filter, facilitated operator-assisted pre-screening, and reduced reliance on random or isolated field-of-view inspection before high-magnification Raman analysis.

To investigate MP presence in NP tissue, 51 NP tissue samples were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection due to lumbar disc herniation (LDH) disease. These samples were processed through chemical digestion and vacuum filtration to enrich particulate matter on microporous membranes. An operator-assisted mosaic imaging workflow was applied to support full-filter visualization and candidate-particle selection before targeted Raman spectral assignment. Further examination of these particles was conducted using micro-Raman spectroscopy, and Raman data were acquired for qualitative analysis. Additionally, screening was performed to account for potential exogenous contamination introduced during tissue collection in the operating room. Therefore, this exploratory study aimed to develop and preliminarily apply a micro-Raman spectroscopy-based mosaic imaging workflow for screening candidate particles in processed NP samples from patients with LDH, followed by targeted Raman spectral assignment and assessment of potential exogenous particles.

Materials and methods

Participant recruitment and sample collection

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Wuhan Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine Orthopedic Hospital (Approval No. 672HREC20240521-L07; Approval Date: May 21, 2024).

The participants were patients who underwent surgery for LDH at Wuhan Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine Orthopedic Hospital (Wuhan, Hubei Province, China), and NP tissues requiring surgical resection due to LDH were collected. Inclusion criteria were: (1) participants were diagnosed with LDH by imaging (MRI/CT); (2) adult patients (≥ 18 years) scheduled for elective LDH surgery; (3) provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) ≤ 50 mg of NP tissue was used for analysis; (2) inability to provide informed consent. No patients were eliminated after initial inclusion. In total, 51 patients provided informed consent to participate between June 1 and August 10, 2024. Medical staff collected baseline patient information, including serum biochemical test results, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein levels from whole blood, and plasma D-dimer concentrations. These laboratory parameters were collected as part of routine preoperative assessment required for surgical clearance and were not specifically designed for MPs exposure evaluation.

NP specimens were obtained immediately following the surgery and placed into sterile, plastic-free glass containers. To ensure that the samples were not contaminated during collection and storage, all collection and storage equipment were cleaned with water and ethanol filtered through a glass fiber membrane (0.45 μm, Delvstlab, China). The samples were then stored at -80 °C for long-term preservation and future analysis. To assess potential contamination from known surgical sources, five materials used during surgery were collected for Raman analysis. This screening was limited to the sampled materials and did not comprehensively characterize all possible operating-room or airborne contamination sources.

Sample processing

The experimental procedure design and extraction method were adapted from previous studies7,11,29, with necessary modifications to suit the experimental requirements. Prior to the experiment, ultrapure water and anhydrous ethanol, used in subsequent procedures, were filtered through glass fiber membranes (0.45 μm, Delvstlab, China). Additionally, the 10% potassium hydroxide solution, employed as the extraction solvent, was filtered using Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membranes (0.22 μm, Cobetter, China). All glassware and containers were washed twice with ultrapure water and anhydrous ethanol, and then wrapped in aluminum foil for later use. Tissue samples were thawed at 4 °C and subsequently transferred into 150-mL glass flasks for high-efficiency digestion. A 10% potassium hydroxide solution was added at a ratio of 0.1 g of tissue to 10 mL of solution to facilitate the isolation of MPs. Digestion was performed at 60 °C with agitation at 60 rpm for 48 h to ensure complete tissue degradation. Following digestion, the extraction solution was diluted by adding excess ultrapure water to reduce the potassium hydroxide concentration. The supernatant was then filtered directly onto 25 mm glass fiber membranes (0.45 μm, Delvstlab, China) using a vacuum-pumping system. All samples were processed using membranes of the same size and the same mosaic imaging workflow. The filters were stored in clean, labeled glass containers to enable accurate identification and analysis of MPs in subsequent steps. Plastic materials were avoided throughout the experiment, and all cleaning and extraction solvents were filtered through microporous membranes, consistent with recommended contamination-control practices for MP analysis30.

Procedure blank test design

Procedural blanks were prepared to monitor particles potentially introduced during sample digestion, dilution, filtration, filter storage, and Raman analysis. One procedural blank was included for every 12 NP tissue samples. Each procedural blank contained the same pre-filtered reagents used for tissue extraction, including ultrapure water, ethanol, and 10% potassium hydroxide solution, but no NP tissue was added. The blanks were processed under the same conditions as the tissue samples, including digestion at 60 °C with agitation at 60 rpm for 48 h, dilution with ultrapure water, vacuum filtration through 25 mm glass fiber membranes, storage in clean glass containers, mosaic imaging screening, and Raman spectral analysis. Blank filters were examined using the same candidate particle screening workflow and Raman identification criteria as the NP tissue samples.

Detection and identification of particles

The filter membrane was used to observe and characterize MPs with the LabRAM Soleil Raman Microscope (HORIBA Scientific, France). The suspected particles were initially observed using a 5× microscope for preliminary confirmation. Subsequently, they were further examined with a 50× microscope, and Raman spectra were obtained to accurately identify and confirm the particles.

The particle detection conditions were configured as follows: imaging method of episcopic dark field, 532 nm excitation wavelength, 600 lines mm− 1 grating, laser intensity ranging from 2% to 25% (variable), acquisition time of 0.5s, 15 accumulations, and a spectral range of 200–3700 cm− 1.

Operator-assisted mosaic imaging screening workflow based on micro-Raman spectroscopy

A mosaic imaging screening workflow based on micro-Raman spectroscopy was developed to support full-filter visualization and operator-assisted candidate particle selection in processed NP samples (see Fig. 1). The workflow began with low-magnification (5×) optical mapping of the filter membrane via a Raman-coupled microscopy system. Image tiles were automatically stitched into a seamless panoramic mosaic, providing full-field visualization of adsorbed particulates. To optimize screening efficiency and reduce missed targets, we implemented multi-analyst collaborative annotation: multiple operators independently labeled suspect particles in the mosaic, followed by cross-validation to minimize false negatives. Confirmed candidates were then re-imaged at high resolution (50×) and analyzed via targeted Raman spectroscopy for qualitative identification. The mosaic imaging step was used as an operator-assisted pre-screening procedure rather than an independent quantitative detection algorithm; final particle identification was based on Raman spectral matching. This tiered strategy combined broad pre-screening with targeted Raman verification and was designed as a practical workflow for candidate selection in complex tissue-derived filters.

Flowchart of the mosaic imaging screening method based on micro-Raman spectroscopy for nucleus pulposus tissue analysis.

Statistical analysis

Spectral data from all samples were exported using Labspec 6 software (HORIBA Scientific, France) and subsequently matched against the built-in database in KnowItAll software (Wiley Science Solutions, USA). The corresponding hit quality index (HQI) values were recorded for each comparison. The search method used the Correlation (classic) method with optimized corrections, and Baseline Correction to Query Spectrum was applied. Particles with HQI values exceeding 75 were considered successful matches. The threshold selected in this study was determined by referencing the HQI thresholds established in previous studies31,32. Additionally, the longest diameter of each particle, as observed in the two-dimensional view on the filter membrane, was measured and recorded as the particle diameter. To control for particles originating from surgical procedures or the operating room environment, collected surgical materials were analyzed using Raman spectroscopy under the same acquisition mode as the samples. Particles in the samples that matched the five characteristic peaks of these materials were excluded from the final count.

Graphical representations were generated using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation, USA) and KnowItAll software (Wiley Science Solutions, USA). All values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution were analyzed using the independent samples t-test, whereas those without a normal distribution were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant. The false discovery rate (FDR) test was mainly performed using Benjamini-Hochberg.

Results

Microplastic-sized polymer particles were Raman-assigned in NP samples from five patients with LDH. These polymer particles included PU foam, PPG, PEVA, PA rubber, and PEP. In addition, one graphite particle was detected in another patient. As shown in Table 1, five of the six patients with detected particles were female. However, Fisher’s exact test showed no significant difference in sex distribution between particle-positive and particle-negative patients (P > 0.05). No significant differences were observed between patients with and without detected particles in terms of age or tissue weight. Exploratory comparisons of routine hematological and biochemical variables were performed as clinical background information. However, no variable remained statistically significant after false discovery rate correction (FDR > 0.05). Therefore, these routine preoperative laboratory variables were not used for biological or clinical interpretation. A female-only exploratory comparison is provided in Supplementary Table S2, but no variable remained significant after FDR correction.

No interfering particles were observed on the filter membrane of the blank sample, confirming that the sample preparation process did not introduce contamination. The Raman spectral data and microscopic images of the analyzed particles were presented in Fig. 3. By comparing the Raman spectra of individual particles with reference spectra (KnowItAll database), five types of MPs were identified: polyurethane foam (PU foam), poly (propylene glycol) (PPG), poly (ethylene-co-vinyl acetate) (PEVA), polyacrylate rubber (PA rubber), and poly (ethylene-co-propylene) (PEP). All detected MPs in NP tissues exhibited prominent Raman peaks in the 2800–3000 cm− 1 region, along with distinctive spectral signatures between 500 and 1700 cm− 1 that enabled differentiation based on characteristic Raman bands. Most particles appeared gray or brown under microscopy. Additionally, the graphite particle displayed a strong characteristic Raman peak near 1580 cm− 1, consistent with its known spectral profile. As summarized in Table 2, PA rubber was the most abundant microplastic type and exhibited larger particle sizes compared to others. The remaining MPs, with particle sizes below 100 μm, were detected only once in the samples. Notably, multiple microplastic types were observed in only two samples (N9 and N16), suggesting sporadic contamination or localized particle accumulation.

To identify potential particle contamination during surgery, we analyzed experimental materials from the operating room using Raman spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 4A, the NP sample displayed several green, linear structures with a prominent peak around 1548 cm− 1. Comparison with the Raman spectrum of environmental sample 5, collected from the laboratory, revealed similar peaks at approximately 1292, 1358, 1548, and 1597 cm− 1, indicating the presence of comparable contaminants or particulate matter. Additionally, a substantial number of blue, flake-like particles were observed in the NP sample, with a strong peak near 1529 cm− 1 (Fig. 4B). This peak matched those found in the Raman spectra of environmental samples 1–4, which also exhibited characteristic peaks at ~ 686, ~1342, ~ 1453, and ~ 1529 cm− 1. These results indicated that some particles observed on NP sample filters matched the sampled surgical materials and were therefore excluded from the final particle count. Because only selected surgical materials were analyzed, the possibility of uncharacterized operating-room or airborne contamination for the retained particles cannot be completely excluded.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the presence of MPs in NP tissues from 51 patients with LDH by screening for suspicious particles. A mosaic imaging screening method based on micro-Raman spectroscopy was established for the first time and used to identify potential particles in the tissues, validated through both microscopy and spectroscopy subsequently. A total of eight MPs, comprising five distinct polymer types, were identified within the NP tissues of five patients. Additionally, one graphite particle was detected in a single sample. Furthermore, two particles from the surgical environment that could potentially lead to false positive contamination of the data were screened and excluded.

The identification of MPs in human biological samples was primarily conducted using Raman spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and pyrolysis gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Py-GC-MS)33. Recent methodological advances have highlighted that optical microscopy, FTIR spectroscopy, and Raman spectroscopy remain important tools for particle-level polymer identification, although analytical accuracy can be affected by particle size, matrix complexity, and spectral interference from pigments or organic matter34. FTIR rapidly generated particle maps but was limited to identifying MPs larger than 20 μm23,35. Py-GC-MS was a relatively new method that was unaffected by particle shape or size, making it suitable for complex matrices and amenable to statistical analysis36. However, it had notable limitations: the technique irreversibly destroyed samples, and potential matrix-derived pyrolysis products resembling those of MPs could generate false positives that were difficult to eliminate37,38. Raman spectroscopy, when equipped with a microscope, could visualize particle morphology and provide qualitative data without fully destroying the sample, unlike Py-GC-MS39. In addition, Raman spectroscopy detected much smaller particles than FTIR, down to approximately 1 μm40. However, conventional Raman spectroscopy approaches were time-consuming when analyzing human tissues with complex matrices and large sample sizes, requiring extensive manual particle inspection and prolonged instrument operation26,27,34. Although new technologies, such as the Particle Finder function, could automatically locate and morphologically characterize all particles on a membrane and had been applied in certain contexts, they remained limited when analyzing complex matrices41,42. In particular, these methods could not reliably locate or identify particles on membranes, as the complex matrix of human tissue samples rendered the images difficult to interpret. Mosaic image reconstruction has also been applied in published studies for microplastics analysis in environmental matrices, including water and sediment samples43,44. However, to our knowledge, the application of a Raman mosaic imaging screening workflow to human tissue-derived filters has not been previously reported, and its use for NP samples from a relatively isolated anatomical compartment is therefore a key novelty of the present study. In the present study, it was used as an operator-assisted pre-screening step to obtain full-field visualization of tissue-derived filter membranes before targeted Raman confirmation. This was practically useful because conventional Raman microscopic screening has a limited field of view and requires slow, real-time manual searching across complex filters containing tissue-derived residues and other matrix components. By generating a panoramic filter image, candidate particles could be pre-marked and cross-checked before high-magnification Raman analysis, reducing reliance on prolonged real-time microscope searching and isolated field-of-view inspection.

Exposure to MPs was reported in various organs and tissues, with recent studies identifying their presence in human bone marrow and lower limb joints12,45,46. Additionally, some studies initially reported MPs exposure in the human skeletal system and identified their presence in the intervertebral disc47,48. In the present study, microplastic-sized polymer particles were Raman-assigned in NP samples from five of 51 patients with LDH. One graphite particle was also detected in an additional patient. The MPs identified included PU foam, PPG, PEVA, PA rubber, and PEP. These synthetic polymers are widely used in industrial and consumer products and have been reported in environmental or biological contexts49–51.

To contextualize our findings, we compared the MPs detected in NP tissues with those reported in other human tissues (Table S4). Similar to our identification of PU, PPG, PEVA, PA rubber, and PEP—many of which were variants of common polymers like PU, polyethylene (PE), and polyamide (PA)—the literature frequently reported PE, polypropylene (PP), polystyrene (PS), PA, and polyvinyl chloride (PVC) in tissues such as lungs, colon, uterus, and blood8,52–54. The particle size range in NP tissues (37–280 μm) was notably larger than those found in placentas (5–10 μm), lungs (1.60–5.56 μm), or kidney (3–11 μm), but similar to lower limb joints (11–151 μm) and skeletal tissue (56.8–262 μm)9,10,12,47. However, direct comparisons across studies should be interpreted cautiously because sampling strategies, tissue types, digestion protocols, contamination-control procedures, analytical thresholds, and particle-size detection limits differ substantially. Compared with reports from more directly exposed or vascularized tissues such as the colon, uterus, blood, and placenta, the number of particles detected in the present NP samples was low. This low detection frequency may be biologically plausible given the anatomically protected and largely avascular nature of NP tissue. The current dataset is not sufficient to determine whether NP tissue has a lower particle burden than other human tissues.

This study provides preliminary Raman-based evidence of microplastic-sized polymer particles in processed human NP samples. The graphite finding was observed in one additional sample. Although most patients with detected particles were female, this observation was based on a small number of positive cases. Although five of the six particle-positive cases were female, this pattern was not statistically significant. Therefore, the present data do not support sex-related differences in particle detection. Because lifestyle, cosmetic use, occupational, dietary, and environmental exposure information was not collected, no conclusion can be drawn regarding specific exposure sources. This possibility requires further investigation through targeted exposure assessment and larger population-based studies.

The detection of Raman-assigned particles in NP samples suggests that anatomically protected spinal tissues warrant further investigation in larger and more rigorously controlled studies. However, the present data do not establish biological effects, clinical significance, disease relevance, or causality in LDH. The route by which these particles became associated with NP samples remains unclear and requires future investigation. The NP is the inner gelatinous center of the intervertebral disc and becomes largely avascular after birth, forming a relatively protected microenvironment that may limit exogenous substance penetration55–57. LDH-related structural disruption may be relevant to particle entry or retention, but this possibility was not directly tested in the present study. Because healthy NP controls, tissue-level inflammatory or oxidative-stress assays, barrier-integrity measurements, and herniation-severity or disc-degeneration-grade correlations were not included, whether LDH-related structural changes influence particle distribution in NP tissue remains an open question. Therefore, the biological relevance of the detected particles in LDH remains to be determined.

Additionally, we accounted for potential false positives that could arise from contamination during surgery in our study. Surgical environment contamination represented a critical methodological challenge in clinical microplastic research due to the ubiquitous presence of plastic materials in healthcare settings58. Specifically, our tests identified both larger and smaller particles that originated from surgical contamination. To further monitor contamination, we used procedural blanks, which simulated sample handling steps and helped detect any contamination introduced during the extraction or filtration processes7. Therefore, it was crucial to incorporate procedural blanks alongside the necessary testing in the surgical setting when collecting complex population samples and testing for MPs. Our systematic approach to contamination control, including surgical material analysis and procedural blanks, demonstrated the rigor necessary for reliable microplastic detection in clinical samples.

This study investigated microplastic presence in NP tissues, an anatomically protected site, and provided preliminary observational evidence of polymer and graphite particle detection in a subset of LDH samples. Our mosaic imaging approach could improve upon the limitations of conventional analysis, enabling rapid and comprehensive screening of MPs in complex human tissues. The detection of polymers in NP tissues contributed to growing evidence of systemic microplastic distribution and informed ongoing discussions about environmental exposure pathways. These findings bridged environmental and clinical sciences, highlighting systemic risks of MPs even in isolated anatomical compartments. These findings supported continued efforts to understand microplastic distribution patterns and develop sensitive detection methods for environmental health surveillance. This study had several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting results. First, ethical constraints prevented comparison with healthy NP tissue, as such collection would require unnecessary invasive procedures. However, this limitation is inherent to all clinical studies of deep anatomical structures and did not diminish the value of establishing detection methods and baseline prevalence data in pathological conditions. The Raman-based workflow has inherent detection limitations. Particles below the practical optical detection threshold, nanoplastics, and low-signal polymers may be missed due to limited particle visibility, weak Raman signals, or matrix interference. Therefore, the reported particle counts should be interpreted as a conservative estimate of Raman-detectable particles rather than the total particle burden. The mosaic imaging step should be interpreted as an operator-assisted candidate-selection workflow rather than a validated quantitative detection method. Quantitative performance metrics, including spike-recovery, detection efficiency, sensitivity, specificity, false-negative rate, full-filter Raman mapping equivalence, and inter-operator agreement, were not assessed. These parameters would require a separately designed validation study using known particle types, sizes, and numbers or exhaustive whole-filter Raman mapping. Although Py-GC/MS may provide complementary compositional information, it is destructive and cannot preserve particle morphology for microscopic observation. This represented a technological limitation rather than a methodological flaw, and our findings should be considered a conservative estimate of actual contamination levels. Third, the relatively low detection frequency, with particles detected in six of 51 patients, limited the statistical power for assessing clinical associations and disease relevance. Our positive detections, while limited in number, provided proof-of-concept evidence for microplastic presence in anatomically protected tissue and established the foundation for larger-scale investigations of exposure mechanisms and clinical significance. Future studies with larger sample sizes aimed to clarify the potential causal relationship between MPs and LDH-related pathological indicators. These investigations were complemented by lifestyle questionnaires to better identify possible MPs exposure pathways. Mechanistic studies incorporating appropriate control groups, histological localization, tissue-level inflammatory and oxidative-stress assays, dose-response designs, and disease-severity correlations will be required to determine whether the detected particles have biological relevance in disc pathology. Future studies using animal models may allow the inclusion of both normal control and disease model groups, thereby enabling further investigation of the distribution characteristics of microplastic particles under controlled experimental conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the presence of MPs in human NP samples—a relatively isolated anatomical compartment. A mosaic imaging screening method based on micro-Raman spectroscopy was successfully developed and validated to screen MPs in NP tissues from patients with LDH, incorporating collaborative annotation strategies that significantly improved screening efficiency and reduced potential particle omissions compared to traditional methods, followed by rigorous microscopy and spectroscopy verification. Our findings confirmed the presence of five distinct types of MPs (PU foam, PPG, PEVA, PA rubber, and PEP) in NP tissues of LDH patients, with nine particles detected across five of 51 patients (9.80% detection rate) and particle sizes predominantly below 100 μm except for polyacrylate rubber. An additional graphite particle was detected in one sample. No statistically significant associations with routine hematological or biochemical variables were identified after multiple-comparison correction. The avascular NP tissue was typically unaffected by systemic circulation, but pathological conditions like LDH could disrupt biological barriers, leading to the accumulation of MPs in NP tissue. Additionally, false positives were minimized by collecting materials during surgery and including procedural blanks. This study provided preliminary evidence for MPs in NP tissues in patients with LDH, offering valuable insights into the exposure pathways of a previously considered “closed” anatomical system, and highlighting the need for further research into the health implications of microplastic contamination in musculoskeletal tissues. Future investigations should focus on larger sample sizes, examination of dose-response relationships, exploration of potential causal links between MPs exposure and disc degeneration, and development of strategies to mitigate microplastic pollution and protect human health.
