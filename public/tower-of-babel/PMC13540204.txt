Molecular and neurovascular mechanisms of thermal sensitivity in teeth
Authors: Kristóf Kádár, Mahmoud Al-Khrasani, Réka Anna Medgyes, Szilvia Acsády, Judit Mária Kirchlechner-Farkas, Arvin Shahbazi, Zsolt M. Lohinai, László Köles, Ákos Zsembery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe perception of dental pain and thermal stimuli is governed by the specialized structural and physiological organization of the dentin – pulp complex. Teeth are subjected to rapid thermal changes during normal oral functions, while the dental pulp – an intensely vascularized and innervated tissue – is confined within rigid mineralized structures. Under these anatomical conditions, even mild thermal stimuli – especially cold – can induce rapid and intense pain sensations. The mechanisms underlying thermal sensitivity of dentin have been explained by several theories. The direct neural theory proposes that external stimuli directly activate trigeminal nerve endings, whereas the hydrodynamic theory suggests that temperature changes induce dentinal fluid movement within tubules, generating mechanical forces that stimulate pulpal afferent fibers. Increasing experimental evidence supports an integrated model in which odontoblasts (ODs) function as mechanosensory transducers. In this concept, thermal stimuli generate dentinal fluid movement and mechanical stress that activate ion channels and enzymes in ODs, leading to the release of signaling molecules such as ATP, glutamate and possible other mediators including nitric oxide. This thermomechanical coupling mechanism, linking dentinal fluid dynamics, odontoblast mechanotransduction, and neuronal activation, is the most widely accepted explanation for thermal pain and dentin hypersensitivity. Understanding these mechanisms provides important insights into dental nociception and may guide the development of more accurate clinical diagnosis and improved strategies for managing thermal dentin hypersensitivity.

Introduction

Teeth are specialized organs that serve essential roles in mastication, phonation, and the maintenance of facial structure. Their architecture reflects adaptive responses to dietary diversity, with different tooth types exhibiting distinct morphologies and functional specializations. Beyond their mechanical functions, teeth contribute to nutritional status and psychosocial well-being, making their structural and functional integrity indispensable [1–4]. The structural organization of enamel, dentin, and pulp not only supports these roles but also confers a unique mechanism for thermal sensation with significant implications for protective reflexes and oral health.

Teeth are routinely exposed to rapid thermal fluctuations during physiological activities, rendering them among the tissues most susceptible to sudden temperature changes ranging from extremely cold to highly elevated levels in the human body. Routine activities including eating, drinking, and breathing expose the tooth surface to temperature shifts ranging from extremely cold to highly elevated levels. The dental pulp constitutes a vital, highly innervated, and well-vascularized tissue enclosed within rigid mineralized structures [5,6]. Under these unique anatomical and physical conditions, phenomenon emerges despite the relative absence of classical pain-sensing free nerve endings in most dental tissues, thermal stimuli – particularly cold – can evoke rapid, intense, and spatially well-localized pain responses, likely mediated through alterations in pulpal microcirculation and dentin – pulp sensory mechanisms [7–12].

From the perspective of temperature sensation, dentin-pulp complex represents a unique model for thermoception. The rigid mineralized tissues impose distinct thermal and mechanical filtering properties, while dentinal fluid movement plays a fundamental role, and pulpal blood flow actively modulates the transmission of thermal stimuli [6,13,14]. Understanding how temperature shifts and the resulting physical processes interact with molecular sensors is essential not only for explaining thermosensation in teeth and dentin hypersensitivity elicited by thermal stimuli but also for providing broader insights into non-canonical mechanisms of thermal pain.

This review synthesizes current evidence on the mechanisms underlying thermal sensation in teeth and dentin hypersensitivity. We describe the dental thermal environment and the principles of heat transfer within the dentin-pulp complex. Classical concepts, such as the hydrodynamic theory, are integrated with molecular insights into thermosensitive and mechanosensitive ion channels. By framing the tooth as a specialized thermosensory organ, we highlight its unique role in extreme thermosensation and temperature-evoked nociception.

Teeth at the interface of external temperature and vital tissue

Enamel covers the crown, providing a hard, protective, but nonsensitive layer. Dentin underlies enamel, is less mineralized but more resilient, and acts as a buffer between enamel and pulp. Cementum covers the root, anchoring the periodontal ligament and securing the tooth in the alveolar bone [15,16]. Dentinal tubules radiate from the pulp toward the dentinoenamel junction and contain dentinal fluid, odontoblastic processes, and sensory nerve fibers, which extend from the subodontoblastic plexus into the predentin and tubules, collectively contributing to the transmission of thermal and mechanical stimuli [12]. Odontoblasts lining the pulpal surface synthesize primary, secondary, and tertiary dentin, thereby maintaining dentin integrity and contributing sensory pathways.

The dental pulp occupies the central cavity of the tooth forming the coronal pulp chamber with cusp-oriented projections known as pulp horns and extending apically into the root canals as a continuous soft tissue compartment enclosed by dentin [17,18]. It consists of a cellular connective tissue matrix penetrated by a dense neurovascular network entering through the apical foramen. Sensory nerve fibers form a subodontoblastic plexus (plexus of Raschkow), with terminal branches extending toward the odontoblastic layer and into dentinal tubules, providing a structural basis for dentin sensitivity to external stimuli [1,4,7,12,17,19–21].

Teeth constitute a specialized anatomical and functional interface linking the external environment with the internal physiological milieu. They are directly exposed to ambient temperature fluctuations while simultaneously enclosing the dental pulp – a soft, vital tissue – within rigid mineralized structures with pronounced thermal filtering properties [6,13,14,22]. Consequently, precise regulation of thermal transmission is essential to preserve pulpal vitality while enabling detection of potentially harmful stimuli. Exposure of dentin to environmental thermal stimuli resulting from enamel erosion, gingival recession, or restorative interventions substantially disturbs this balance. The removal of stable, insulating tissue layers reduces the tooth’s thermal barrier, increasing responsiveness to temperature fluctuations and transforming exposed dentin into a critical interface where external thermal energy is transduced into biological signals [23–26].

The tooth’s thermal behavior reflects the properties of its constituent tissues. Enamel, the most highly mineralized tissue, has low thermal conductivity (approximately 0.81 W·m− 1 ·K− 1) and acts as an effective insulator, while its anisotropic microstructure slows the propagation of temperature changes toward dentin and pulp [13,14]. Dentin, being less mineralized, has lower thermal conductivity (approximately 0.48 W·m− 1 ·K− 1) and conducts heat more slowly than enamel through its solid matrix, but the fluid-filled tubules play a particularly central role in thermal transfer and signal amplification [13,14,27]. Rapid surface temperature changes drive fluid movement within the tubules, mechanically enhancing thermal signals toward the pulp, with amplification particularly pronounced during rapid cooling [28]. The dental pulp, rich in water and dense vascularization, contributes to thermal buffering capacity [17,21,29]. However, the rigid walls of the pulp chamber limit tissue expansion, rendering the pulp particularly sensitive to rapid thermal and pressure changes transmitted through dentin [23].

Dentinal tubules contain an ultrafiltrate of pulpal blood. Temperature changes at the tooth surface alter fluid volume and viscosity, driving movement within the tubules. Cold stimuli, in particular, induce rapid outward flow, generating shear stress and mechanical deformation at the dentin-pulp interface [23,24,30,31]. Fluid movement transduces temperature changes into mechanical stimulation of sensory elements at odontoblast processes and adjacent nerve terminals. The extent of this movement depends on tubule diameter, density, and patency, which are increased in damaged, exposed, or hypersensitive dentin [6,32]. Consequently, dentin’s physical properties directly modulate the sensory response to thermal stimuli.

The thermoregulatory capacity of the dental pulp is mediated by its dense microvascular network. Alterations in pulpal blood flow can modulate tissue temperature, with vasodilation enhancing heat dissipation during warming and vasoconstriction limiting heat loss under cold conditions [17,21,29]. However, the rigid pulp chamber constrains volumetric changes, so rapid or extreme temperature shifts can exceed vascular compensation, allowing transient thermal and mechanical signals to reach sensory nerve endings [29,33]. These limitations contribute to the pronounced and long-lasting sensitivity of teeth to cold stimuli, especially in case of pulpitis when the intrapulpal pressure has already increased.

The dental pulp is also densely innervated, with approximately 70–90% of intrapulpal axons consisting of unmyelinated C fibers and the remainder of small myelinated Aδ fibers, arising from extrapulpal branches of the superior and inferior alveolar nerves [7,33]. Aδ and C fibers mediate distinct temporal and qualitative components of nociceptive signaling, underlying fast and slow modes of thermal pain perception.

Sensory neurons in the trigeminal ganglion (TG) exhibit a size-dependent organization, reflecting the relationship between peripheral fiber composition and central somatosensory organization. In this context, the TG neurons can be grouped by the size of their cell bodies into small and medium sized ganglion cells and a smaller subpopulation of larger cells. Most of the large ganglion cells were found to be surrounded by neuropeptide-rich perisomatic networks. Small cells (20–30 μm) predominantly have cutaneous targets, and are mainly nociceptive with C fibers, while medium cells (30–50 μm) are corneal afferents, with Aβ/Aδ fibers and have various sensory roles, such as mechanoception, thermoception as well as nociception [34,35]. The oral and perioral regions, including the tooth pulp, are innervated by the large cells (50–80 μm), with mainly Aβ/Aδ fibers [34,35]. Large cells that innervate the tooth pulp were found to have a conduction velocity of Aδ-fibers, which suggests that conduction velocity may vary along the length of the neuron [36]. It was also found that larger neurons generally innervate larger peripheral areas and vice versa [37]. Within the TG, sensory neurons were found to have a size-dependent distribution, namely small neurons responsible for the nociception, thermoception and proprioception of the skin, mucosa, eye and paranasal sinuses are localized in the dorsomedial TG, while cells with large diameter exhibit ventrolateral TG localization and are responsible for mechanoreception of the teeth and periodontium alongside the nasal and oral cavity, skin, and vibrissae [38]. Tooth pulp contains many Aδ- and C-fibers, which end in the inner third of dentinal tubules and mostly within the pulp, respectively [39–42]. In tooth pulp, 70–90% of axons are unmyelinated and most others resemble Aδ nociceptors [40,41]. However, many of these fibers reach the tooth as myelinated, large-diameter axons with fast conductive velocity, then branch, taper, and lose myelin inside the pulp [36,39,43–46]. Fully unmyelinated fibers include sympathetic efferents influencing the vasculature and some classical C-fiber nociceptors involved in thermal pain alongside A-fiber nociceptors [40,41,47]. This suggests that many intradental afferents are likely tapering A-fiber low-threshold mechanoreceptors (LTMs) rather than nociceptors. This is further supported by the finding that the majority of intradental afferent neuron cells in the TG have a large or medium diameter, with lucent cytoplasm and markers characteristic of LTMs, while neurons innervating other areas, such as the buccal mucosa or cornea, are small with dark cytoplasms, and typical nociceptor markers [45,47–51].

A broad repertoire of receptors and ion channels expressed in pulpal axons and trigeminal sensory neurons underlies thermal, mechanical, and chemical nociception. Thermosensitive transient receptor potential (TRP) channels provide a molecular basis for the detection of temperature changes, while mechanosensitive Piezo channels transduce physical stimuli such as fluid movement and tissue deformation [12,24,52–56]. Many of these channels exhibit overlapping sensitivities, allowing neurons to integrate multiple types of stimuli. In addition, modulatory receptors – including ionotropic purinergic P2X receptors, metabotropic P2Y receptors, hyperpolarization-activated channels, and metabotropic glutamate receptors – contribute to fine-tuning of nociceptive and temperature-dependent signaling [24]. Notably, TRP ankyrin 1 (TRPA1)- and TRP melastatin 8 (TRPM8) expressing afferents – both associated with cold sensitivity – are enriched in the peripheral pulp, and some fibers have been reported to approach or enter the dentinal tubules. However, the extent to which these axons contribute to cold-induced dentinal pain remains unclear [57–59]. Sensory afferents from the superior and inferior alveolar branches of the trigeminal nerve form the plexus of Raschkow beneath the cell-rich zone, from which unmyelinated terminals project toward odontoblasts and into dentinal tubules, enabling the transduction of thermal and mechanical stimuli that underlie dentin hypersensitivity [7,12].

Dentin hypersensitivity

Dentin hypersensitivity is a prevalent oral health condition [60] defined by acute, transient pain originating from exposed dentin in response to diverse stimuli, including thermal (hot or cold), mechanical (scraping, cutting, or drilling), chemical (acidic solutions), or osmotic (hypertonic solutions) challenges [25,26]. This phenomenon arises when the protective enamel or cementum is disrupted or lost, resulting in direct exposure of the underlying dentinal tubules [26].

Thermal dentin hypersensitivity represents a stimulus-specific subtype of dentin hypersensitivity and is defined as sharp, transient pain arising from exposed dentin in response to thermal stimuli, typically cold or hot, in the absence of overt pathology [24–26,61]. Causes of tooth hypersensitivity.

Cold stimulation is a particularly potent trigger, likely reflecting a specialized thermal-sensing mechanism within the dentin-pulp complex. Considering the thermal insulation provided by enamel and dentin, dental thermal pain likely differs from cutaneous thermosensation, in which temperature is detected directly by nerve endings expressing thermosensitive ion channels. In teeth, thermal pain most likely arises from a composite system involving heat transfer, fluid dynamics within dentinal tubules, and molecular sensors.

Dentin hypersensitivity has classically been explained by the hydrodynamic theory, which proposes that thermal stimuli induce fluid movement within dentinal tubules and mechanically activate pulpal sensory fibers [31,79]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that dentin thermal sensation cannot be fully explained by hydrodynamic mechanisms alone. In particular, odontoblast-mediated signaling – where odontoblasts detect dentinal stimuli and communicate with adjacent pulpal nerve endings – appears to represent a major pathway for sensory transduction [80–82], with additional contributions from thermosensitive ion channels and neural sensitization [1,5,55,56,83–87]. Pulpal blood flow provides partial thermal buffering, mitigating the impact of transient temperature changes [21,29].

Preventive strategies focus on preserving mineralized tissues and minimizing dentin exposure, whereas therapeutic approaches aim to reduce dentin permeability and modulate neural excitability using topical desensitizers or restorative and periodontal interventions [26,61]. Understanding how mechanical, chemical, and other factors modulate the transmission of thermal stimuli is essential for effective management of thermal dentin hypersensitivity and for preserving dental function while minimizing temperature-evoked nociception.

Dental primary sensory neurons

The primary peripheral terminal afferents of the TG play a central role in dental pain perception through intricate signaling pathways involving ODs. TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV4, TRPM3, TRPM8 and TRPA1, which are temperature-sensitive, have been identified in primary sensory neurons [88,89]. Activation of TRPV1 with TRPA1 participates in inflammatory, nociceptive, nociplastic and neuropathic pains by promoting both peripheral and central sensitizations [90–94]. Electrophysiological analysis, single cell RT-PCR and immunohistochemical studies reveal that dental primary afferent neurons (DPANs) have both nociceptive (Aδ and small-sized C neurons) and non-nociceptive (large-sized Aβ neurons) characteristics (Table 2) [57,116–118]. DPANs include small- to medium-diameter nociceptive neurons, including IB4-positive non-peptidergic nociceptors. These neurons express various nociceptive channels, including the temperature-sensitive TRPV1, TRPA1, and TRPM8 channels, along with the extracellular ATP receptor channels P2X2 and P2X3, as well as the sodium channel Nav1.8. Heat transfer in teeth

The thermal properties of the teeth are primarily a heat conduction process coupled with the physiological processes of the tooth, such as dentinal fluid flow (DFF) [28], and pulpal blood flow (PBF) [119,120]. The thermophysical properties of the teeth vary between different layers (e.g. enamel and dentin) [121], depending on their microstructures. In this context, the thermal conductivity (TC) of human dentin decreases with increasing volume fraction of dentinal tubules [27]. The movement of dentinal fluid in the dentinal tubules upon heating (or cooling) can also enhance heat transfer (HT) within the pulp. Also, PBF rate increases when the intrapulpal temperature rises above 42°C [120] and decreases during cooling [119]. Therefore, blood plays an important role in the thermoregulation of pulpal soft tissue [120], acting as a heat sink under heating and as a heating source during cooling.

Enamel and dentin have different thermal and mechanical properties. In general, enamel conducts heat more efficiently than dentin; the thermal diffusivity (TD) and TC of enamel are approximately 2.5 and 1.6 times larger than dentin, respectively [122]. Nevertheless, the thermal transfer parameters (TC and TD) of enamel and dentin are relatively low compared with those of the pulp. Consequently, these layers function as effective thermal insulators, protecting the pulp from potentially harmful thermal stimuli [86].

Temperature sensing and dentin sensitivity

Heat and cold evoke an almost instantaneous pain response when applied to exposed dentin. Likewise, other stimuli such as hypertonic solutions, drilling, probing, or air blasts also induce a similar pain sensation, although the underlying mechanisms are still not fully understood. Clinically, freshly exposed dentin is known to be extremely sensitive.

Cold stimulation typically elicits a rapid, transient pain response, whereas heat usually induces a slower onset, duller, and more persistent pain sensation. In fact, slow cooling of teeth does not evoke pain [123]. In this sense, a study by Goodis has shown that gradual surface cooling of healthy premolars (0.7 °C/min) to ~2.5°C produced no sensation. On the other hand, sensory threshold was increased by 46%, and pulpal blood flow decreased by 62% [119]. Importantly, it is difficult to clearly separate genuine temperature sensation in teeth from the broader phenomenon of dentin hypersensitivity. Currently, three main theories have been proposed to explain dentin sensitivity. The following sections describe the different mechanisms implicated in thermal sensation in teeth. Table 2. provides substantial recent human and experimental data related to these mechanisms.

Direct neural theory

The “neural theory” (direct neural theory) proposes that external stimuli directly activate sensory nerve endings in the dentin tubules or dental pulp. In this model, stimulus transduction into electrical impulses is mediated by nociceptors expressed in DPANs. Consistent with this view, immunohistochemical studies showed that most DPANs are small-diameter nociceptors expressing nociception-related ion channels (Figure 2). Among these TRP channels, including TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPA1, and TRPM8, as well as the ligand-gated ion channels P2X2 and P2X3 have been identified in distinct neuronal subpopulations [30,57]. TRPA1 and TRPM8 channels were found to show cold-sensitive characteristics, with activation thresholds below 17°C and 25°C, respectively [56]. Other studies have uncovered the ability of TRPA1 to also respond to heat and other stimuli, indicating that TRPA1 has bimodal thermosensitivity in humans, whereas in non-mammalian species, heat-sensitivity characteristics have been described thus far [124,125]. TRPV1 and TRPV2 are heat-sensitive receptors with thresholds above 43°C and 52°C, respectively [56]. TRPV1 can also be activated by capsaicin and by increased proton concentration (pH < 6.0), a condition frequently associated with inflammation [126,127]. Since TRPV1 activation induces the release of inflammatory mediators such as substance P (SP) and calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), it may contribute to both pain signaling and tissue inflammation. TRPA1 is often co-expressed with TRPV1, and both receptors are considered important mediators of pain and neurogenic inflammation at central and peripheral levels [126]. Patients with homozygous N331K TRPV1 mutation showed decreased heat and capsaicin sensitivity. However, they exhibited an unexpected increased sensitivity to noxious cold and an exaggerated inflammatory and pain response to the TRPA1 agonist allyl isothiocyanate. This suggests that TRPV1 negatively regulates TRPA1 activity under physiological conditions and the loss-of-function mutation or pharmacological blockade of TRPV1 removed this regulation, leading to increased TRPA1-mediated cold sensitivity and neurogenic inflammation. Experiments with TRPV1 and TRPA1 KO and DKO mice supported these findings [128]. Indeed, a recent clinical study investigating the effect of a TRPA1 antagonist (LY3526318) on patients with inflammatory and neuropathic pain failed to show superiority to the placebo group [129]. In addition, P2X2 and P2X3 receptors are purinergic ionotropic receptors activated by extracellular ATP, which may further potentiate TRPV1 responses by lowering its thermal activation threshold [126].

Nevertheless, several observations present compelling evidence against the hypothesis of a direct activation mechanism. In this context, intratubular nerve fibers extend only 0.1–0.2 mm into the dentinal tubules, meaning they do not reach the dentino-enamel junction (DEJ), which is considered the most sensitive region of dentin [12,130–133]. In addition, while many dentinal tubules in the coronal region contain nerve fibers, relatively few intratubular axons are present in the cervical area, where dentin hypersensitivity is most frequently observed clinically [12]. The fact that neuroactive substances applied to exposed dentin fail to reliably induce stimulation or anesthesia [134] also weakens the direct neural hypothesis. Moreover, the sensory response to thermal stimulation has been shown to occur before measurable temperature changes reach the pulpal wall, where most sensory structures are located [135]. Taken together, these findings do not support the concept that intradentinal axons are directly activated by external stimuli and instead suggest that the physical stimulus is transferred indirectly to deeper regions of the dentinal tubule.

Hydrodynamic theory

The most widely accepted explanation (hydrodynamic theory) proposes that mechanical forces generated by dentinal fluid movement transmit external stimuli to the sensing apparatus [136–138]. Dentinal fluid provides a hydraulic connection between the oral environment and the pulp, and fluid shifts can explain why normally innocuous stimuli, such as air puffs or water spray, evoke pain in exposed dentin. Experimental studies in human and animal teeth demonstrate a linear relationship between applied pressure gradients, fluid movement, and the activity of intradental Aδ fibers [132,139,140].

Both osmotic and hydrostatic stimuli can induce measurable fluid shifts in dentinal tubules even in vitro. Thermal stimuli, especially cooling, can generate rapid dentinal fluid movement because dentinal fluid and the surrounding hard tissues undergo different rates of volume change. Consequently, pain may arise before a measurable temperature change occurs near the nerve endings. Air blasts induce pain not only by cooling but also by promoting evaporative fluid movement in dentinal tubules [137]. Further studies in extracted human teeth and in situ dog teeth indicate that pulpal axons are more readily stimulated by compression of composite resin fillings compared to intact enamel-covered teeth or amalgam restorations. This suggests that mechanical deformation and the associated displacement of dentinal fluid are critical factors in sensory activation [41,141].

Mathematical modeling supports this mechanism by demonstrating that dentinal fluid movement can generate sufficient shear stress on intratubular nerve terminals to activate mechanosensitive ion channels and produce pain [87,142]. Consistent with this, several studies show that fluid movement induced by thermal stimulation precedes measurable temperature changes at the DEJ and pulpal surface [86,142,143]. Moreover, the initially high rate of outward fluid flow during cooling corresponds with the observed short latency of neural responses [87,142,143]. In addition, the movement of the odontoblastic process, caused by dentinal fluid flow has also been demonstrated [68,76]. Intradental nerve discharge rates increase with dentinal fluid flow velocity in vivo [139,140]. Similarly, hydrostatic pressure applied directly to open dentinal tubules evokes pain in humans, typically quantified on a visual analog scale. Notably, both human and animal studies report that outward fluid flow triggers stronger neural responses than inward flow [139,140,144]. Threshold flow velocities have been estimated at approximately 460 µm/s for outward flow and −850 µm/s for inward flow within a single dentinal microtubule [140]. In human studies, the mean threshold flow rate associated with pain was 3.29 ± 2.36 nL/(s·mm2) outward under negative pressure and 5.75 ± 3.62 nL/(s·mm2) inward under positive pressure [144]. These findings provide a mechanistic explanation for why diverse stimuli generate a similar painful sensation: the key stimulus detected by the sensory system is mechanical deformation, rather than temperature or chemical stimuli per se.

Originally, dentinal fluid movement was attributed mainly to thermal expansion and contraction of the fluid itself. However, changes in tooth temperature also induce deformation of enamel and dentin. Linsuwanont et al. measured deformation at the pulpal dentin surface using strain gauges during thermal stimulation, while simultaneously monitoring temperature changes at the labial enamel, DEJ, and pulpal surface. They observed that deformation at the pulpal surface occurred almost immediately after stimulus application, well before any measurable temperature change reached the DEJ [28]. These results suggest that hard tissue deformation, rather than fluid expansion alone, may be the primary driver of dentinal fluid shifts. It is also conceivable that dentinal fluid movement exerts shear stress on the odontoblasts and activates outer surface endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [145,146]. Therefore, NO may also act as paracrine “nitrergic” transmitter in the dentin-pulp complex and may influence pain and thermal sensation in the spinal cord [147].

Odontoblast receptor theory and the odontoblast-hydrodynamic receptor model

The classical “odontoblast receptor theory” proposed that ODs themselves act as pain receptors and transmit signals to pulpal nerves (Figure 3). This hypothesis was originally rejected because ODs cannot generate neuronal impulses and no synaptic structures were identified between ODs and pulpal nerve endings [151]. More recently, Shibukawa et al. proposed an updated concept referred to as the “hydrodynamic receptor theory/model,” in which intercellular signaling between odontoblasts and sensory neurons mediates dentinal pain [84]. In this sense, many pieces of evidence have demonstrated the contribution of different channels and receptors to the development of dentinal pain, including various odontoblast TRP channels, which are activated in response to cold or warm liquid and air (Table 1). Dentinal pain signal generation is initiated by ODs, which reside between the dentin and dental pulp, and exhibit significant autocrine and paracrine signaling capabilities that are crucial for their functional roles during tooth development and tissue regeneration [152]. The autocrine properties are essential to modulate their differentiation and functional states [152]. Odontoblasts originate from neural crest – derived ectomesenchymal cells and are responsible for dentinogenesis. As previously mentioned, ODs function as sensory cells, detecting mechanical, thermal, or chemical stimuli [152] and forwarding these signals to the peripheral terminals of sensory neurons via released substances like ATP and glutamate, which generate the perception of dental sensations [103]. To achieve this, ODs possess various ion channels sensitive to mechanical, thermal, chemical, and voltage changes to detect tooth pain [103]. TRP mechanosensitive ion channels include TRPV1-3, TRPC1 and 6, TRPM3 and 7, and TRPP1-2. The TRP channels implicated in dentin hypersensitivity include TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV4, TRPA1, TRPM3, and TRPM8. These receptors facilitate responses to a variety of stimuli, including temperature variations and mechanical stress [56,95,103,153]. Ion channels that can sense cold stimuli include TRPA1, TRPM8, and TRPC5, whereas TRPV1, TRPV2, TRPV3, TRPV4, TRPM3, as well as TRPA1, are known to respond to heat stimuli [154–156]. TRPV1 channel has been recognized for its activation by elevated temperatures (≥43°C), low pH (≤5.9), capsaicin, protons, camphor, piperine, allicin, ethanol, nicotine, proinflammatory cytokines and physical pain-eliciting stimuli and reactive oxygen species (ROS), which are fundamental to the inflammatory process [154,157]. TRPV2 channel is activated by noxious heat (greater than 53°C), mechanical stimuli such as stretching and swelling, as well as various agents including IGF-1, hyaluronic acid (HA), 2-aminoethoxydiphenyl borate (2-APB), cannabidiol, and probenecid [154,158]. However, it does not respond to capsaicin, resiniferatoxin or changes in pH [154,159]. Importantly, TRPV4 is a polymodal gated channel that responds to a wide variety of stimuli, including temperature variations (above 27°C), acidic pH, mechanical stress, hypotonicity, exogenous agonists such as phorbol ester 4α phorbol 12,13-didecanoatethe (4α-PPD), and metabolites of arachidonic acid, such as epoxyeicosatrienoic acids [160]. The TRPA1 channel has a bimodal characteristic in humans activated by both low (<17°C) and high temperature stimuli [125,156,161]. On the other hand, mechanosensitivity and chemosensitivity has been also reported, with icilin, isothiocyanates, allicin, cinnamaldehyde, acrolein, cannabinoids, bradykinin, DAG, polyunsaturated fatty acids and ROS acting as activators [154,162–167].

TRP melastatin-3 (TRPM3) channel is notably activated by osmotic cell swelling, a process that can significantly influence cellular functions, neurosteroid pregnenolone sulfate, and the synthetic compound CIM0216, though the selective endogenous ligands for this channel remain as a subject of future studies [154,168,169]. In fact, data on the functions of TRPM3 as both a chemosensor and thermosensor in the somatosensory system such as dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and TG neurons have also been reported [89]. TRPM8 channels are activated by noxious cold (below 26 °C) and cooling agents such as menthol, eucalyptol, and icilin, and are expressed in distinct subsets of pain- and temperature-sensing neurons, including DRG and trigeminal neurons [170,171]. It is also worth noting that recent work suggests that odontoblasts could function as cold-transducing cells via TRPC5-mediated cold sensing [172]. Although the extent and dynamics of local temperature changes within dentin remain unclear, these findings may point to a potential mechanism for sustained cold sensitivity. In particular, TRPC5’s relative sensitivity to intracellular [Ca2+] and its limited desensitization may enable prolonged signal transduction in response to cold stimuli, even in the absence of persistent or large temperature gradients. However, the thermomechanical coupling in their sensory function cannot be excluded completely, as TRPC5 found to be mechanosensitive in cellular models [173] and recent mathematical modeling studies suggest that the thermal stresses generated by cold-induced fluid flow may abundantly exceed their activation threshold [174].

Dentin hypersensitivity can also be triggered by gingival enlargement induced by different pathological conditions, as well as drugs such as cyclosporine and tacrolimus. The implication of TRP channels in dentin hypersensitivity has been documented (see above). Importantly, tacrolimus, an immunosuppressant agent similar to cyclosporine, inhibits the phosphatase calcineurin and has been reported to activate TRPM8 channels in humans [175]. These data highlight the ability of certain drugs to have dual effects, namely gingival enlargement and TRP activation, which need to be considered in the future, particularly in patients with dentin hypersensitivity who are taking such medications.

Other ion channels, such as TRAAK and TREK1, have been described as being involved in the sensing of both mechanical and cold stimuli. Furthermore, the expression of ligand gated P2X4 and P2X7, as well as G protein-coupled P2Y receptors in ODs point to the involvement of ATP in ODs’ physiology via autocrine or paracrine mechanisms. Dentinal pain sensitization is also shaped by voltage-gated sodium channels, specifically Nav1.7, Nav1.8, and Nav1.9, as well as calcium channels such as Cav1.2 and Cav2.2, along with acid-sensing ion channels (ASICs). Other channels that have been reported to be present in ODs and dental pulp cells include various voltage-gated channels such as Nav1.6, KV, KCa, PIEZO2, and mechanosensitive Ca2 + channels (e.g. PC1 and PC2) and Piezo1 as nonselective cationic channel [98,103,149].

According to the most widely accepted theory of dentin sensitivity (i.e. the hydrodynamic theory), OD mechanosensitive TRP channels are activated by the movement of dentinal fluid, which causes membrane deformation due to hydrodynamic forces (see Figure 3 and Table 2). Consequently, ATP is released through PANX1 into the surrounding space. The released ATP activates P2X3 and P2Y receptors on the peripheral terminal of sensory neurons, thus initiating a crosstalk between the ODs and the neurons in peripheral pain sensation. This is one of the accepted theories describing how odontoblasts and the peripheral somatosensory system contribute to pain generation. Notably, activation of P2X3 receptors can initiate action potentials in Aδ afferents of TG neurons [148]. Together, these observations support the concept that OD-neuron signaling provides a plausible mechanistic link between hydrodynamic stimulation and sensory nerve activation. Nevertheless, it remains unclear to what extent ODs are required for dentin sensitivity at the organism level.

Glutamate has also been reported to contribute to the communication between ODs and the sensory neurons. A study by Nishiyama et al. has highlighted that mechanical stimulation of ODs caused by the hydrodynamic forces results in influx of calcium, which participates in the release of glutamate from ODs [176]. These data support that ATP and glutamate participate in both OD-OD communication (autocrine) and OD-sensory neuron communication (paracrine).

Ohyama et al. further tested this model in vivo by evaluating pain behaviors induced by cold stimuli applied to exposed dentin in rats and mice [80]. Systemic administration of Piezo1 and TRPA1 antagonists, as well as inhibitors of PANX1 and P2X3 receptors (P2X3Rs), significantly reduced nociceptive behavior in dentin-exposed animals. These findings support the involvement of the Piezo1/TRPA1–PANX1–P2X3 signaling axis in dentin hypersensitivity. Importantly, the nociceptive response was also significantly reduced in OD-deficient animals (Col1(2.3)-Cre/DTR mice), suggesting that ODs function as sensory receptor cells capable of detecting hydrodynamic force-induced deformation through mechanosensitive channels and releasing ATP as a key neurotransmitter driving pain transmission. No significant difference has been found in nociceptive scores between dentin-exposed animals treated with bonding agents and control animals in which dentin was not exposed, supporting the concept that dentinal fluid movement is required for cold-induced dentinal pain [25,140,144].

Purinergic signaling as an integrative mechanism in thermal dental hypersensitivity

Purinergic signaling, mediated by extracellular nucleotides and nucleosides, represents a fundamental mechanism of intercellular communication in the nervous system and plays a critical role in nociception and sensory processing. ATP is a widely accepted transmitter and modulator in both peripheral and central pain pathways. Several ionotropic P2X and metabotropic P2Y receptor subtypes are expressed in sensory neurons and contribute to acute and chronic pain signaling [177–180]. Adenosine by activating P1 receptors can also modulate nociceptive transmission, predominantly exerting antinociceptive effects [181]. Here, we provided converging evidence supporting purinergic signaling as a central mechanism in dental hypersensitivity, integrating odontoblast mechanotransduction, sensory neuron activation, and local signal modulation within the dentin-pulp complex. The rapid extracellular metabolism of ATP to ADP and adenosine by ectonucleotidases can critically shape the duration, spatial spread, and qualitative nature of purinergic signaling [182,183].

NTPDases are expressed in the dental pulp, including the odontoblast layer and associated odontoblast-neuronal signaling microdomains adjacent to dentin, as well as in the trigeminal nociceptive pathway [184,185]. They enable fine-tuning of neuronal excitability and sensitization, while also supporting the regulation of odontoblast function. In this context, purinergic signaling provides a molecular extension of the classical hydrodynamic theory, translating fluid movement-induced mechanical stimuli into biochemical signals. Changes in nucleotide breakdown can result in different receptor activation profiles. Temperature-dependent modulation of purine metabolism may alter the balance between excitatory ATP-mediated signaling and modulatory ADP/adenosine pathways, thereby influencing sensory responses to thermal stimuli [186]. Ectonucleotidase activity exhibits a clear temperature dependence, with significantly reduced catalytic rates at lower temperatures; for example, ecto-ATPase activity has been reported to decrease by approximately 30–40% at 25°C compared to 37°C, consistent with reduced NTPDase activity under cooling conditions [186,187].

Although rapid temperature changes at the tooth surface are attenuated within dentin, the odontoblast process – afferent signaling interface within dentinal tubules and adjacent microdomains may still experience modest, spatially restricted temperature variations, and this effect is likely amplified in dentinal lesions, where diminished dentin thickness and/or exposed tubules could enhance local thermal microenvironment fluctuations. Given the temperature sensitivity of ectonucleotidase activity, even such limited local cooling may modulate nucleotide hydrolysis dynamics within these confined extracellular spaces.

Purinergic signaling is likely engaged early during thermal stimulus transduction through ATP release. Nevertheless, temperature-dependent variations in ectonucleotidase activity are unlikely to account for the rapid onset of cold sensation but may contribute to the modulation of purinergic signaling by slowing ATP degradation, thereby prolonging its extracellular availability. This, in turn, could enhance activation of ATP-sensitive P2X2/3 and P2X3 receptors on sensory afferent fibers, contributing to the intensity and persistence of cold-evoked sensations.

Although direct experimental evidence in dental tissues remains limited, such temperature-dependent modulation of nucleoside breakdown – together with other factors, including differences in fluid movement profiles and mechanoreceptor activation under cold versus warm conditions described above – may represent an additional plausible mechanism underlying stimulus-specific variability in dentin hypersensitivity and the resulting pain.

Theories of differential sensitivity to thermal stimuli

The mechanosensory explanation of thermally induced dental pain does not fully account for the distinct qualitative differences between pain evoked by cold and heat. Nevertheless, it is consistent with experimental findings showing substantially lower threshold flow rates in dentinal tubules for outward fluid movement (typically induced by cooling) than for inward flow (typically induced by heating). Because mechanosensitive ion channels open once a specific mechanical threshold is reached, both critical flow velocities are expected to generate the same maximum shear stress on the terminal bead (τMSS), which is assumed to represent the threshold shear stress.

Based on computational fluid dynamics simulations, Lin et al. proposed that displacement of the odontoblastic process can explain the different neural responses to outward versus inward dentinal fluid flow [142]. During outward flow (typically induced by cooling), the odontoblastic process is pushed toward the dentinal tubule wall, narrowing the fluid space. This constriction increases local fluid velocity and the resulting wall shear stress (τ), even when the overall bulk flow is relatively low. Under these conditions, intradental nociceptors can be strongly activated, leading to a high neural discharge rate. In contrast, during inward flow (typically induced by heating), the odontoblastic process is displaced away from the tubule wall, widening the fluid space. This reduces local shear stress despite relatively higher bulk flow velocities, resulting in weaker activation of mechanosensitive nociceptors and thus lower sensitivity to inward flow [142].

Neural activation in response to cold stimulation (0–5 °C) occurs with a very short latency (<1 s) [188,189]. At this early time point, the local temperature near the nerve endings is still too high to activate thermosensitive nociceptors, making a purely thermal mechanism unlikely. Importantly, dentinal fluid movement is detectable before any measurable temperature change at the DEJ, and the onset of fluid flow (<1 s) closely matches the latency of the neural response [28,140]. Moreover, cold-induced fluid velocities can readily exceed the activation threshold of mechanosensitive nociceptors [190]. Together, these observations indicate that the initial sharp, shooting pain evoked by cold is primarily due to rapid dentinal fluid movement activating mechanosensitive nerve endings. After a longer delay (≈30 s), when sufficient cooling reaches the nociceptors, thermosensitive channels may also be activated, contributing to a later, dull or burning pain component [116,123].

In contrast, hot stimulation (e.g. 55 °C) produces neural responses only after a longer latency (>10 s), during which no immediate neural discharge is observed [188,189]. This is consistent with the fact that heating generates relatively weak (slow) dentinal fluid flow, insufficient to activate mechanosensitive nociceptors. Instead, pain likely arises once heat has diffused deeply enough for the local temperature at the nerve endings to exceed the activation threshold of thermosensitive nociceptors.

Cation flow as a transducer of thermal stimuli – An alternative hypothesis

The classical hydrodynamic theory of dentin hypersensitivity attributes pain perception to stimulus-induced bulk fluid flow within dentinal tubules, which mechanically activates pulpal nerve endings. Recent work, however, has demonstrated that thermal stimuli can induce directional cation transport through dentinal tubules which function as nanoconfined, negatively charged ionic channels [191]. Crucially, this electrokinetic response occurs without appreciable bulk fluid displacement; instead, neural activation arises from ionic current transmission through a quasi-static electrolyte. Importantly, the cation transport theory does not invalidate the hydrodynamic model but rather extends it by shifting the primary transduction mechanism from macroscopic fluid mechanics to nanoscale electrokinetic phenomena. Under physiological conditions, stimulus-induced fluid motion may still occur and could contribute to the establishment of local mechanical stresses and/or electrical gradients that modulate ionic transport and neural excitation.

Thermal sensitivity under inflammatory conditions

In symptomatic pulpitis, the affected tooth exhibits hypersensitivity to external stimuli, including thermal stimuli, with pain that persists after stimulus removal (lingering pain), and may also occur spontaneously [192]. Bacterial toxins can directly activate pulpal nerve fibers prior to the development of neurogenic inflammation induced by the immune response, thereby contributing to pain [193]. These toxins may also activate TRPV4 expressed on odontoblasts, leading to increased intracellular Ca2+ levels and subsequent activation of intradental sensory neurons, as similar effect was already observed in airway epithelial cells [194].

Inflammation can lower the activation threshold of TRP channels in both odontoblasts and sensory neurons, resulting in heightened neuronal excitability. Short-term exposure of odontoblast-like cells to tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α increases TRPA1 expression and enhances TRPA1- and TRPV4-mediated responses to chemical agonists and mechanical stretch [195]. Upregulation of TRPV1 [196], TRPV4 [197], and TRPA1 [58,198] in pulpal nerve fibers or odontoblasts has been associated with pain in pulpitis. In addition, increased TRPC5 expression has been reported in odontoblasts under inflammatory conditions [126]. Lipopolysaccharide, a bacterial endotoxin, can activate cultured trigeminal ganglion neurons and sensitize TRPV1 channels via a toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)-dependent mechanism [199], while sensitizing TRPA1 channels in a TLR4-independent manner [200].

Furthermore, inflammation-induced NGF-mediated pulpal nerve sprouting may increase TRP channel density and innervation of dentinal tubules, thereby further enhancing sensitivity [39,201,202]. Collectively, these findings suggest that inflammatory mediators may potentiate thermally induced pain either directly, through receptor sensitization, or indirectly, by upregulating receptor expression.

Current concepts of the thermal sensation in teeth.

Considering the crucial role of ATP in thermomechanical coupling, we propose that pharmacological modulation of purinergic signaling could represent a promising therapeutic target. Selective P2X2/3 and P2X3 R antagonists are currently under development and evaluation in clinical trials [203,204]. Provided that suitable pharmacokinetic properties can be achieved within the unique anatomical environment of the dentin – pulp complex, these agents may represent promising future therapeutic options for the treatment of thermal dentin hypersensitivity by attenuating ATP-mediated activation of sensory afferent fibers. Owing to the complexity of teeth thermal transduction modulation of other signaling mechanisms (e.g. glutamate and NO) may also provide alternative and/or complementary targets.
