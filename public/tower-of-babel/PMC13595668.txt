Oncolytic adenovirus co-expressing IL-12 and shVEGF potentiates immune checkpoint blockade for treatment of renal cell carcinoma: in vitro, organ-on-a-chip, and in vivo evaluation
Authors: Joongwon Choi, Chae-Ok Yun, Sejung Maeng, Yunlim Kim, Eunyoung Park, Ji Yi, Se Young Choi, In Ho Chang, A-Rum Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) has been known as highly malignant and resistant to standard therapy due to its immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and aberrant VEGF-mediated angiogenesis. To overcome these therapeutic limitations, here we assessed the therapeutic efficacy of an oncolytic adenovirus (oAd) co-expressing Interleukin-12 (IL-12) and a short hairpin RNA against VEGF (shVEGF) in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). The oAd/IL12/shVEGF induced RCC-specific cancer cell death in vitro and 3D bioprinted model, leading to greater anti-tumor effect compared with oAd-GMCSF. Furthermore, it potentiates anti-tumor efficacy in RCC orthotopic tumor model. The potent antitumor effect induced by combination of oAd/IL12/shVEGF with anti-PD1 was due to CD4 + or CD8 + T cell- or NK cell-mediated antitumor immune response and effective inhibition of tumor-associated neovascularization. Collectively, oAd/IL12/shVEGF can be a potent strategy to overcome current limitations in conventional RCC therapy by converting the cold tumor microenvironment to an inflamed state to sensitize refractory tumors to ICIs.Graphical AbstractMultifaceted antitumor effects of oAd/IL12/shVEGF combined with anti-PD1 therapy in renal cell carcinoma. The therapeutic efficacy of this combination strategy was evaluated using in vitro, 3D bioprinted, and in vivo RCC models. oAd/IL12/shVEGF induces virus-mediated oncolysis and the release of tumor antigens, while IL-12 promotes antitumor immune activation and shVEGF suppresses VEGF-mediated tumor angiogenesis. In combination with anti-PD1 immune checkpoint blockade, oAd/IL12/shVEGF enhances CTL, Th1, and NK cell-mediated antitumor immunity, leading to potent therapeutic efficacy in renal cell carcinoma models. Key message

What is already known on this topic

RCC often acquires resistance against standard ICI due to its immunosuppressive cold microenvironment and VEGF-mediated abnormal angiogenesis.

What this study adds

Combining an oncolytic adenovirus expressing IL-12 and shVEGF (oAd/IL12/shVEGF) with anti-PD1 therapy transforms “cold" RCC into hot tumors by recruiting CD8+ T and NK cells while depleting regulatory T cells.

This combination therapy achieves superior synergistic antitumor efficacy and safety over monotherapies in both 3D-bioprinted and orthotopic mouse models through anti-tumor immune response.

How this study might affect research, practice or policy

The oAd/IL12/shVEGF platform offers a potent strategy to overcome immunotherapy resistance, potentially establishing a new clinical paradigm for treatment of patients with refractory RCC.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is a lethal malignant tumor with an increasing global incidence. Notably, approximately 25–30% of patients are already found to have metastatic RCC at diagnosis, and 30% of patients encounter recurrence even after surgical resection (Logan et al. 2012). Further, 5-year survival rate for metastatic RCC is less than 20%, highlighting the need for alternative treatment strategies. The clinical management of metastatic RCC has undergone a major transformation over the last three decades (Bedke et al. 2022). In the early 1990 s, treatment was centered on broad immune-activating cytokines like interleukin-2 and interferon-alpha (Coppin et al. 2005). While these therapies proved that RCC is responsive to immune modulation, they did not offer durable results and were associated with significant systemic toxicity (Motzer and Bukowski 2006). These non-specific immune activation-caused safety concerns are a significant limitation to the practical application of cytokine-based treatments in clinical settings, necessitating a shift toward more precise therapeutic approaches with more manageable safety profiles.

The targeted drugs, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and mTOR inhibitors disrupting VHL/HIF/VEGF signaling axis, led a fundamental shift in mid-2000s for the treatment of patients with RCC (Motzer et al. 2007). However, these targeted therapies still have unmet needs due to resistance and recurrence for benefit of long-term patient survival. The development of ICIs, which precisely recalibrates the immune environment, has become a solution for unmet needs of previous cancer drugs and has moved again toward immuno-oncology (IO) combinations. The integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)—either as dual-agent therapy or alongside TKIs—now constitutes the standard, offering improved overall survival across multiple risk categories, significantly improving overall survival and durable response rates for patients (Amin and Hammers 2018).

RCC is known as a notably immunogenic malignancy. However, RCC often nullifies its immunogenicity by a coordinated immune escape factor (Jian et al. 2021). One of the key features of this contradiction is the overproduction of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) by tumor cells with aberrant vessel proliferation (Wolf et al. 2023). This VEGF-regulated angiogenesis catalyzes tumor progression and compromises the host’s immune surveillance by blocking the effective migration and metabolic activation of infiltrating T-lymphocytes. While ICIs have changed the therapeutic paradigm for metastatic RCC since late 2010, their objective responses remain 20–50% of the metastatic RCC patients (Young et al. 2024). This clinical resistance to treatment is frequently caused by paradoxical ‘immunologically cold’ microenvironment. Formation of aberrant vessels in tumor creates a biophysical signaling niche that favors RCC survival. Additionally, the structural instability of these vessels produces a localized hypoxic state, which decreased the effect of ICI treatment to trigger a robust anti-tumor response (Yang et al. 2024).

Based on our previous research, we hypothesized this oAd/IL12/shVEGF can be a powerful booster to maximize efficacy of ICI through a multiple mechanism of action (Graphical Abstract) (Yoon et al. 2023; 2019). 1) The oncolytic adenovirus can trigger direct tumor lysis and release of tumor-associated antigens and danger signals. These can lead to robust innate and adaptive immunity. 2) The therapeutic payload IL12 can be localized directly into the tumor site along with tumor specific viral replication. This targeted overcomes the systemic toxicity associated with traditional cytokine therapies (Qian et al. 2025) while changing the microenvironment toward an inflamed (Yoon et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2011). Further, genetic engineering techniques enable the incorporation the interleukin-12 (IL-12), which promotes natural killer (NK) cell activation and cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) expansion. Which sequentially increases interferon-γ production and antigen presentation within the tumor microenvironment. 3) The other therapeutic gene shVEGF can not only reduce angiogenesis but also normalizes the abnormal blood vessel, thus facilitating the infiltration of activated T cells within the tumor tissue and restoration of immune mechanism to malignancy (Liu et al. 2025). 4) Through these mechanisms (antigen release, immune cell activation, and enhanced infiltration), the oAd can significantly increase the efficacy of ICI agents, even in cases resistant to standard treatment (Ahn et al. 2025; Cheng et al. 2025). Collectively, work in concert to significantly amplify the efficacy of ICI agents, potentially overcoming resistance even in cases refractory to standard treatments.

Result

oAd/IL12/shVEGF expresses therapeutic genes and elicits RCC-specific cell killing effect

To address the dual challenge of angiogenesis and immune suppression in tumor cells in RCC, oAd encoding IL12 and shRNA targeting VEGF (oAd/IL12/shVEGF) has been utilized (Fig. 1A). The differences of viral production was not observed between group (control oAd vs oAd/IL12/shVEGF), showing that therapeutic gene did not hinder capability of virus to produce progeny virus (Fig. S1). As shown in Fig. 1B and Fig. S2, infection of human RCC cell line and murine RCC cell line with oAd/IL12/shVEGF led to a dose-dependent expression of IL12. The reduction of VEGF levels was observed in Renca cells, but not in A498 cells which is consistent with the mouse sequence-specific targeting of shVEGF. These results indicate that oAd/IL12/shVEGF was successfully constructed and functionally validated. The infection of human or murine RCC cancer cell lines with oAd/IL12/shVEGF led to dose-dependent cancer cell killing effect that was significantly more potent than that observed in normal cell lines (Fig. 1C; *** P < 0.001). oAd/IL12/shVEGF did not exert off-target cytopathic effect in murine or human fibroblast cell lines up to 100 multiplicity of infection (MOI), respectively. The shRNA encoded in oAd/IL12/shVEGF specifically targets murine VEGF sequences. Consequently, the reduction of VEGF levels was verified in murine RCC cell lines (Renca), confirming the mouse sequence-specific targeting of shVEGF. While the therapeutic effects observed in human tumor models (such as A498) are primarily attributed to oncolytic activity and IL12 expression, the Renca model demonstrates the additional functional benefit of VEGF suppression within a sequence-compatible context. oAd/IL12/shVEGF exerts selective cytotoxicity in a 3D bioprinted RCC model

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of oAd/IL12/shVEGF in a more complex architecture mimicking the tumor microenvironment, we utilized a 3D bioprinted model incorporating human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), human foreskin fibroblasts (BJ), and A498 cell lines (Choi et al. 2023). As shown in Fig. 2, the structural integrity of the bioprinted lattice was monitored via Live/Dead staining. In the oAd/IL12/shVEGF-treated groups, a dose-dependent increase in red fluorescence (EthD-1, indicating dead cells) was observed specifically within the cancer cell-laden areas, while the viability of normal cell lines (BJ) remained relatively stable. Notably, viability of HUVEC cell lines decreased in a dose-dependent manner, due to anti-angiogenic properties of oAd/IL12/shVEGF. Quantitative analysis of A498 cell viability demonstrated that the cancer killing effect was further enhanced at 3 days post-infection compared to 1 day, with the highest dose 1 × 1010 viral particles (VP) showing a significant reduction in cell survival. Even though murine sequence specific shVEGF was expressed in human A498 3D model, this therapeutic efficacy can be driven by synergistic mechanisms such as high cross-reactivity of murine IL12 and E1A-mediated VEGF suppression. oAd/IL12/shVEGF induces potent anti-tumor effect against subcutaneous RCC tumor model

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of oAd/IL12/shVEGF, mice bearing subcutaneous A498 tumors were intratumorally treated with 1 × 106, 1 × 107 or 1 × 108 VP of oAd/IL12/shVEGF on day 0, 2, and 4 along with PBS as negative control (Fig. S3). The oAd/IL12/shVEGF showed tumor growth inhibition in a dose-dependent manner. In the subsequent experiment, therapeutic efficacy of oAd/IL12/shVEGF was compared with oAd-GMCSF. To ensure a rigorous comparison within the oAd platform, the control vector, oAd-GMCSF, was specifically designed as an analogue to ONCOS-102 (currently in clinical trials). This control oAd utilizes an Ad5/3 chimeric backbone where viral replication is strictly governed by a modified E1 region, featuring an E2F-driven E1A promoter in conjunction with a 24-base-pair deletion (Delta24) in the Rb-binding domain, while expressing human GMCSF. As shown in Fig. 3, PBS-treated group exhibited rapid tumor growth and reached an average tumor volume of 1386.53 ± 171.02 mm3 on day 21 after the initial treatment. In contrast, both oAd/IL12/shVEGF and oAd-GMCSF induced significant tumor growth inhibition compared to PBS treatment group (** P < 0.01, * P < 0.05). oAd/IL12/shVEGF showed 3.6-fold increased anti-tumor effect than oAd expressing GMCSF. Combined oAd/IL12/shVEGF with anti-PD1 significantly potentiates antitumor effect in tumor model

To evaluate the anti-tumor efficacy on combination of oAd/IL12/shVEGF with anti-PD1, subcutaneous Renca tumors were treated with oAd/IL12/shVEGF, anti-PD1, or oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1, along with PBS as negative control (Fig. S4). PBS-treated groups exhibited rapid growth up to 20 days after the initial treatment. In contrast, anti-PD1 and oAd/IL12/shVEGF induced 42.1% and 51.5% inhibition of tumor growth, respectively. oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 treatments induced most potent antitumor effect compared with either anti-PD1 or oAd/IL12/shVEGF monotherapy, showing 72.6% and 67.2% superior tumor burden reduction, respectively, suggesting that combination of oAd/IL12/shVEGF and anti-PD1 can effectively control the growth of both in a safe manner.

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy in a clinically relevant setting, we established an orthotopic RCC model by injecting Renca-luc cells (5 X105 cells/50 µL) into the subcapsular space of the right kidney. As shown in Fig. 5A, tumor progression was monitored via bioluminescence imaging (BLI) at 5, 8, 13, and 15 days post-implantation. While the control group showed rapid tumor growth as indicated by increasing luciferase signal intensity, the group treated with the combination of oAd/IL12/shVEGF and anti-PD1 exhibited the most potent suppression of tumor bioluminescence. Quantitative analysis of the region of interest (ROI) signals (Fig. 5B) confirmed that the combination therapy significantly outperformed both the monotherapy and control groups at the final point (Day 15). Additionally, to assess systemic toxicity, mouse body weights were recorded throughout the 15-day period. As shown in Fig. 5C, even though decrease in body weight was observed in all groups including the control, histopathological analysis of the liver and spleen in the oAd/IL12/shVEGF-treated group revealed no significant pathological alterations on day 15. oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 suppresses proliferation and angiogenesis while inducing apoptosis in RCC

To evaluate the therapeutic effect of combination treatment regimen, histological and immunohistochemical analyses of tumor tissues have been performed. As shown in Fig. 6A, Hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) and proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) staining revealed that tumors treated with oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 were nearly devoid of PCNA-positive cell population (* P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001) and that most of the tumor tissues were necrotic. In line with these results, TUNEL staining revealed that oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1-treated tumors tissues exhibited highest level of apoptotic cell death over monotherapy options (***P < 0.001). These results suggested that combination of oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 effectively induced apoptosis and inhibited proliferation of tumor cells in a synergistic manner. The analysis of tumor infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) population also revealed that CD4+, CD8+ T and NK cell accumulation was significantly elevated in tumor tissues after treatment with oAd/IL12/shVEGF monotherapy or its combination with anti-PD1 in comparison with PBS control group with extremely low level T cell infiltration (less than 5% of total tumor cell population was either CD4+ and CD8+ T cells; *** P < 0.001; Fig. 6B). The oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 induced highest level of CD4+ and CD8+ T cell recruitment in the tumor (* P < 0.05 or *** P < 0.001 versus oAd/IL12/shVEGF or anti-PD1 monotherapy). These results suggested that the combination therapy induced potent antitumor effect through robust initiation of T cell- or NK-cell mediated antitumor immune response. To further assess oAd/IL12/shVEGF-mediated anti-angiogenic effects in the tumor microenvironment, staining for VEGF-A, HIF-1α, CD31, and α-SMA were carried out (Fig. 6C). The expression of VEGF-A and HIF-1α were significantly decreased in oAd/IL12/shVEGF- or oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1-treated group compared to the PBS or anti-PD1-treated group shVEGF-mediated mitigation of tumor hypoxia. While oAd/IL12/shVEGF + anti-PD1 increased the CD8+ T cell (CD69+CD8+) population in the spleen compared with monotherapy (*** P < 0.001), activated CD4+ T cell (CD69+CD4+) population also increased compared with monotherapy (Fig. 7B; *** P < 0.001), indicating that combination amplifies the activation of CD4 +, CD8+ T cells in RCC. Discussion

It is noteworthy that oAd-mediated cancer therapy already has shown remarkable movement in clinical setting for treatment of urologic oncology. The oAd expressing GMCSF, CG0070, has shown promising clinical outcome for the treatment of BCG-refractory patients with non-muscle invasive bladder cancer in a phase II clinical trial (Packiam et al. 2018; Grandi et al. 2023). This achievement highlights promising feasibility of Ad-mediated gene therapy platform for treatment in this disease area. More focusing to RCC, a phase I clinical trial of Enadenotucirev (ColoAd1), which is chimera of large portion of selected genetic elements from Ad11 prototype and Ad3 generated by directed evolution, included RCC patients and demonstrated tumor trafficking of viruses and increased immune-cell infiltration in cancers in both monotherapy and combinations regimens (Garcia-Carbonero et al. 2017). Despite differences in vector engineering method and injection route, the results from these clinical trials indicated that core attributes of oAd (oncolysis and immune priming) are obviously consistent and further reinforce rationale for applying oAd in urologic oncology including bladder cancer and RCC.

The clinical study with early generation oAds, such as CG0070 and ONCOS-102, provide fundamental proof of concept in cancer gene therapy. However, those first- and second-generation oAds, endowing single therapeutic genes and simple genetic modifications, still have an unmet need in the context of tumor specificity, therapeutic potency, and safety. The next-generation oAd platform co-expressing IL-12 and shVEGF is designed to multi target mechanism through potent oncolysis, robust immunomodulation, and effective vascular suppression within the complex tumor microenvironment. Replacement of endogenous E1A promoter to tumor-selective promoter, we shifted forward from the structural simplicity of previous version of vectors. These advancements result in up to 100-fold increased cancer specificity in both human and murine cell lines (Fig. 2). In addition to tumor-selective promoter, the integration of IL-12 and shVEGF leads to approximately a 300% improvement in overall therapeutic efficacy compared to oAd-GMCSF, the analogue version of ONCOS-102 in RCC tumor model (Fig. 3).

In this study, we focused on evaluating the therapeutic synergy between our optimized dual-gene platform, oAd/IL12/shVEGF and anti-PD1 blockade, rather than re-evaluating the individual contributions of each transgene. The anti-tumor efficacy of intermediate controls, such as oAd alone, oAd/IL12, and oAd/shVEGF, has been previously established by our group in other tumor models (Xu et al. 2024), demonstrating dual therapeutic gene approach can maximize therapeutic outcomes through simultaneous immune activation and anti-angiogenesis. Based on this rationale, our current work selected the oAd/IL12/shVEGF to explore its potential in overcoming resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors in the RCC microenvironment. Indeed, these dual therapeutic genes IL12 and shVEGF were selected as therapeutic genes for RCC based on their proven efficacy in simultaneous targeting immunosuppressive and highly angiogenic cancer (Xu et al. 2024; Sorensen et al. 2010). Many clinical and preclinical studies consistently indicate that dual inhibition of these pathways leads to improved therapeutic effects (Xu et al. 2024; Sorensen et al. 2010). Our previous report also demonstrated that oAd-mediated IL12 and VEGF dual targeting strategy maximizes therapeutic efficacy by enhancement of antitumor immune responses and inhibition of angiogenesis (Ahn et al. 2016). Further, there is extensive clinical evidence demonstrating the use of modified IL-12 and VEGF inhibitors for treatments in RCC (Lee et al. 2020; Rini 2007; Dutcher et al. 2020; Strauss et al. 2023; Nadal et al. 2022). Moreover, clinical trials such as KEYNOTE-426 and CheckMate 214 highlighted that combining VEGF receptor inhibitors with immune checkpoint inhibitors results in superior overall survival, progression-free survival, and response rates compared to standard therapies, thereby underscoring the therapeutic benefit of targeting these pathways either individually or in combination in RCC treatment (Ying et al. 2025; Escudier et al. 2020).

It is worth noting that, beyond its role in immune activation, the murine IL12 expressed by oAd/IL12/shVEGF serves as a powerful anti-angiogenic agent in the human-based 3D model (Figs. 2 and 4) due to its high functional cross-reactivity with human receptors (Gately et al. 1998). The mIL-12-mediated activation of co-cultured T cells and macrophages leads to a robust secretion of IFN-γ, inducing anti-angiogenic effects by promoting the release of CXCL10 and CXCL9. This secondary cytokine signaling effectively targets the HUVEC-based vascular layer, suppressing neovascularization and reducing interstitial pressure even in the absence of shVEGF-mediated silencing. Furthermore, oAd/IL12/shVEGF can demonstrate robust Ad E1A expression and viral replication, as the human RCC A498 is highly permissive host for the Ad5-based oAd. As previously noted, the E1A protein inherently downregulates human VEGF at the transcriptional level, it could provide anti-angiogenic pressure even in the absence of shRNA activity (Yoo et al. 2007). Therefore, combined with IL12-driven anti-angiogenesis, the E1A-mediated transcriptional suppression of VEGF can be a multi-layered attack on the tumor vasculature, providing a consistent therapeutic synergy across different experimental settings.

Pembrolizumab is recommended as representative adjuvant therapy after nephrectomy for patients with high-risk RCC and for metastatic disease, ICI combinations (nivolumab + ipilimumab) or ICI plus tyrosine kinase inhibitors (such as pembrolizumab + axitinib) are standard first-line regimens (Motzer and Bukowski 2006). However, multiple limitations including approximately 80% of non-responders, development of resistance, and inconsistent treatment responses remain in clinical settings (Schmidinger et al. 2025). These non-responders are in 'cold tumor' microenvironment that fail to induce effective antitumor immune responses via insufficient T-cell infiltration, increased presence of immunosuppressive Tregs, and immunosuppressive signals (Wu et al. 2024). Given that combination strategy of oAd/IL12/shVEGF with anti-PD1 may effectively transit tumor microenvironment from a cold to a hot due to marked increase in cytotoxic T-cells infiltration and reduction in Treg in orthotopic tumor (Fig. 6). A central hypothesis regarding the limited efficacy of ICIs in advanced RCC involves the presence of biomechanical and immunological barriers. We propose that the clinical resistance observed in current treatments is fundamentally driven by high interstitial fluid pressure and a 'cold' immune landscape. These factors prevent therapeutic agents and activated T cells from effectively reaching their targets (Jain 2013). Specifically, aberrant VEGF signaling in RCC produces a leaky vasculature that exacerbates intratumoral pressure, creating a physical barrier that blocks drug delivery. Our oAd/IL12/shVEGF virus addresses this by utilizing shVEGF for vascular normalization (Fig. 6C). By stabilizing the microvessel structure and reducing pressure, it facilitates the oncolytic virus and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes can penetrate better and spread more evenly (Fukumura et al. 2018).

Unlike monotherapy of ICI, oAd replicates directly within the tumor, leading to oncolysis. Further, the danger signals released during this process synergize with the immune-stimulatory effects of IL12, resulting in immune flaming tumor microenvironment (TME) where anti-PD1 can function. In strong agreement with these results, the result from IHC demonstrating infiltration of immune cell into tumors (Fig. 7B), suggesting that oAd/IL12/shVEGF makes the tumor microenvironment more beneficial for recruitment of cytotoxic T cells and NK cells (Fig. 7B). These results address the limitations of current first-line immunotherapies of RCC and provide a promising strategy to broaden their clinical applicability in the treatment.

In conclusion, our current study demonstrates that the next-generation oAd platform, co-expressing IL12 and shVEGF, provides a superior therapeutic strategy for RCC by simultaneously targeting immunosuppressive TME and tumor angiogenesis. By successfully converting 'cold' tumors into 'hot', with increased DC recruitment and CD8 + T-cell infiltration, this platform not only overcomes the limitations of current first-line ICIs but also offers a potent synergistic effect when used in combination with PD1 blockade. These findings suggest that our advanced oAd-mediated cancer gene therapy approach could change the clinical paradigm for treating advanced RCC, particularly for patients who remain refractory to existing immunotherapies. Taking together, these promising preclinical outcomes would guarantee translating into improved survival for patients with urologic malignancies.

Materials and method

Cell lines and culture conditions

Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Renal cell carcinoma cell lines (Renca- murine cancer cell line; A498, Caki-1, and Caki-2- human cancer cell line), human foreskin fibroblasts (BJ) and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (NIH3T3) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). BJ, NIH3T3, A498, Caki-1, and Caki-2 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Gibco), whereas Renca cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% P/S. Cells were routinely passaged at 70–90% confluence using standard procedures, and low-passage cells were used for all experiments.

Preparation of oAd/IL12/shVEGF

To produce oAd, oAd/IL12/shVEGF genome containing plasmid was linearized with PacI (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) then transfected into 293 A cells using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France) according to manufacturer’s instructions. All Ads were propagated in A549 cells and purified by CsCl gradient centrifugation. The number of VP was determined by measuring the optical density at 260 nm, for which an absorbance value of 1 is equivalent to 1.1 × 1012 VP/mL. Purified viruses were stored at −80 °C until use.

Cytokine/angiogenic factor quantification ELISA

For cytokine/angiogenic factor quantification, A498 and Renca cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2 × 10^5 cells/well and were infected the following day with oAd/IL12/shVEGF at the indicated multiplicities of infection (MOI; A498: 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, and 20; Renca: 0, 5, 10, 20, 50, and 100). At 48 h post-infection, conditioned media were harvested, clarified by centrifugation to remove debris, and used for ELISA-based quantification of secreted IL12 and VEGF-A. IL12 was measured using an IL12 p70 DuoSet ELISA kit (R&D Systems; DY1270), and VEGF-A was measured using a VEGF-A ELISA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; BMS277-2), according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Concentrations were calculated from standard curves and reported as pg/mL.

Cancer cell killing assay

The cancer cell–specific cytotoxicity of oAd was assessed using an MTT assay based on the conversion of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT; Sigma, Livonia, MI, USA) to formazan. Human cell lines (BJ, A498, Caki-1, and Caki-2) and mouse cell lines (NIH3T3 and Renca) were seeded in 96-well plates overnight and then infected with oAd at the indicated multiplicities of infection (MOI; 0.1–1000), with PBS-treated wells included as negative controls. At 48 h post-infection, 50 μL of MTT solution (2 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. The supernatant was removed, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 200 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma). Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader, and cell viability was calculated by normalizing PBS-treated wells (set to 100%). All conditions were assayed in triplicate, and IC50 values were determined from dose–response curves.

Fabrication of 3D Bioprinted RCC Model

Based on the optimization of the renal cancer microenvironment, a 3D bioprinted lattice model was fabricated to evaluate the efficacy of oAd/IL12/shVEGF. For the 3D scaffold, Gel4Cell (Innoregen, Incheon, Korea), a gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA)-based bio-ink, was utilized as the primary extracellular matrix (ECM) surrogate. The bioprinting process was conducted using an extrusion-based 3D bioprinter (Innoregen). The cell-laden bio-ink, containing a co-culture of A498 cells, BJ, and HUVEC, was extruded through a 27G nozzle at a translation speed of 5 mm/s. To ensure structural stability, the printed lattice constructs were stabilized via photo crosslinking using 405 nm visible light for 90 s. To mimic a physiologically relevant tumor-immune microenvironment, we developed a multi-layered infiltration model using a migration filter. A mixture of Jurkat T cells and THP-1 cells, the latter of which were pre-differentiated into macrophages using phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), was placed in the bottom chamber. The migration filter was then overlaid with successive layers of HUVEC, BJ fibroblasts, and A498 cancer cells. This 3D architecture supported the evaluation of viral penetration, the anti-PD1, and the active infiltration of immune cells through multi-cellular barriers. To assess cell-type specific viability, ROI analysis was performed. Fluorescence signals were quantified specifically within the spatial compartments assigned to A498, BJ, or HUVEC cells during bioprinting using ImageJ software.

Viral infection and drug treatment

The 3D bioprinted constructs were infected with oAd/IL12/shVEGF at concentrations ranging from 1 × 108 to 1 × 1010 VP. For combination studies, anti-PD1 (200 µg/20 g equivalent or optimized concentration) was administered alongside the viral treatment. The therapeutic effects were compared across monotherapy and combination groups to evaluate potential synergistic antitumor activity within the 3D microenvironment. 3.3. Live/Dead Staining and Viability Assay Cell viability within the 3D bioprinted model was assessed at 24 h (1D) and 72 h (3D) after treatment. A live/dead staining kit containing calcein AM (green; live) and ethidium homodimer-1 (red; dead) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was employed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope at 50 × magnification. Three independent bioprinted constructs were analyzed for each experimental condition to ensure statistical reliability.

In vitro cytotoxicity and migration assay

The bioprinted constructs integrated with the immune cell infiltration system were infected with oAd/IL12/shVEGF at various concentrations (1 × 108 to 1 × 1010 VP) or treated with anti-PD1 for combination studies. Cell viability was assessed at 1 and 3 days post-treatment using a Live/Dead assay (Calcein AM for live cells; Ethidium homodimer-1 for dead cells). For the anti-tumor migration efficacy (Fig. 4B), the recruitment of immune cells from the bottom chamber toward the top tumor-cell layers was evaluated. The number of Jurkat T cells and differentiated THP-1 cells that successfully infiltrated through the filter and the vascular/fibroblast layers were quantified using ImageJ software. This allowed for the assessment of the synergistic effect of the combination therapy on enhancing the chemotactic recruitment of immune cells into the dense 3D tumor mass.

Orthotopic renal cell carcinoma mouse model and treatment

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice (Orient Bio Co., Seongnam, Korea) were acclimated for 1 week under standard laboratory conditions before the experiments. To establish the orthotopic RCC model, Renca-luciferase (Renca-luc) cells were prepared in serum-free medium at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/50 µL. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the right kidney was exposed through a small flank incision. Using a 30-gauge needle, the tumor cells were precisely injected into the subcapsular space of the kidney, and the incision was closed with absorbable sutures. For the anti-PD1 group, 200 µg of anti-PD1 antibody was administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injection a total of four times on days 1, 4, 8, and 11. For the OV group, oAd/IL12/shVEGF (1 × 109 VP in 50 µL PBS) was injected intraperitoneally on days 2, 5, 9, and 12. The combination group received both agents following the same respective schedules, while control mice received an equal volume of PBS. Tumor progression was monitored via bioluminescence imaging (BLI) using an IVIS Spectrum in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) at days 5, 8, 13, and 15 post-implantations. On day 18, all mice were sacrificed for further analysis.

Subcutaneous renal cell carcinoma mouse model and anti-tumor effects

To establish subcutaneous tumor models, Renca 5 × 105 cells/animal) or A498 (3 × 106 cells/animal) cells were subcutaneously (s.c.) injected into the right flank of mice. When tumors reached a 100 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups. For the treatment of Renca tumors, PBS or oAd/IL12/shVEGF (1 × 10⁸ VP) was administered via three intratumoral (i.t.) injections, with or without concurrent intraperitoneal (i.p.) administration of anti-PD1 antibody (200μg/dose). For the A498 xenograft model, a dose-escalation study was performed where mice received three i.t. injections of PBS or oAd/IL12/shVEGF at doses of 1 × 106, 1 × 107, 1 × 10⁸ VP. In both models, tumor growth was monitored every 7 days, and tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: Tumor volume = 0.523 × L x w2.

Animal care

All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) guidelines and regulations of Hanyang University and Chung-Ang University, and were approved by the Hanyang University IACUC (approval numbers: HYU-2024-0278A and HYU-2025-0025A; Chung-Ang University approval number: A2021020). Female BALB/c and BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, Republic of Korea). Mice were weighed, caged, housed, and maintained under standard specific pathogen–free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions of splenocytes were prepared from the harvested spleens of BALB/c mice. Briefly, the spleens were placed in cold PBS and mechanically dissociated through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer (BD Biosciences). The cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 X g for 10 min at 4 °C. To eliminate red blood cells, the resulting pellet was resuspended in 10 mL of 1 × RBC Lysis Buffer (diluted from 10 × stock; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) and incubated for 10 min on ice. The lysis reaction was immediately quenched by adding 10 mL of complete medium. After an additional centrifugation step, the splenocytes were resuspended in fresh medium and filtered once more to ensure a debris-free population. The cell concentration and viability were determined using a hemocytometer with Trypan blue exclusion, and only samples with > 95% viability were utilized for subsequent assays. Flow cytometry was performed in two independent experiments using two antibody panels (Panel 1: Treg/activation; Panel 2: cytokine). Single-cell suspensions were prepared from the indicated samples, washed, and stained with Live/Dead Fixable Yellow (Invitrogen; L34959) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by surface staining with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in staining buffer. For Panel 1, cells were stained for CD45 (BV711; clone I3/2.3; BD; 752408), CD3 (FITC; clone 17A2; BD; 561798), CD4 (BV786; clone GK1.5; BD; 563331), CD8 (PerCP-Cy5.5; clone 53–6.7; BD; 551162), CD25 (PE-Cy7; clone 3C7; BioLegend; 101916), and CD69 (PE; clone H1.2F3; BioLegend; 104507), followed by intracellular staining for Foxp3 (Alexa Fluor 647; clone MF23; BD; 560401) after fixation/permeabilization using a standard Foxp3 staining workflow. For Panel 2, cells were stained for CD45 (BV711; clone I3/2.3; BD; 752408), CD3 (FITC; clone 17A2; BD; 561798), CD4 (BV786; clone GK1.5; BD; 563331), and CD8 (PerCP-Cy5.5; clone 53–6.7; BD; 551162), followed by intracellular staining for IFN-γ (eFluor 450; clone XMG1.2; Invitrogen; 48–7311-82) and TNF-α (PE; clone MP6-XT22; R&D Systems; IC410P) after fixation/permeabilization. After final washes, samples were acquired on a BD FACSymphony A3 (BD Biosciences) at the Hanyang University Core Facility (Seoul, Korea) equipped with 405/488/561/640 nm lasers (detectors 431/28, 515/20, 586/15, 670/30, 710/50, 780/60). Compensation was set up using single-stained controls, with FMO controls for cytokine gates. Data was analyzed in FlowJo software (BD) with the gating sequence: singlets → live cells → CD45+ lymphocytes → CD3+ T cells → CD4+ or CD8+ subsets → IFN-γ+, TNF-α+, and frequencies reported as % of the parent population All stains were performed in technical duplicates and repeated in at least two independent experiments.

Immunohistochemical and histological analysis

Tumors were harvested from Renca tumor–bearing mice at 3 days after the final intratumoral virus administration and immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Paraffin embedding, sectioning, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining were performed by K2O (Republic of Korea), and paraffin sections were prepared at a thickness of 5 μm. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated using standard procedures, blocked with Protein Block Serum-Free solution (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for 30 min at room temperature, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD4 (clone 4SM95, Invitrogen; 1:200), CD8a (clone 4SM15, Invitrogen; 1:200), NKp46/CD335 (clone 29A1.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific), PCNA (clone PC10, Santa Cruz Biotechnology), VEGF-A (ab1316, Abcam), HIF-1α (clone D1S7W, Cell Signaling Technology; #36169), CD31/PECAM-1 (clone 390, Thermo Fisher Scientific), or αSMA (ab5694, Abcam). After washing, signal detection was performed using the Dako REAL EnVision Detection System (Rabbit/Mouse; Agilent/Dako), and chromogenic visualization was carried out with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) using the Dako Liquid DAB + Substrate Chromogen System (K5007; Agilent/Dako), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Apoptotic cell death was evaluated by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining using the DeadEnd Colorimetric TUNEL System (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Eclipse Ci, Nikon).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as mean of SEM and SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM ver. 8.3 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). A two-tailed Student’s t-test and one-way ANOVA (Tukey multiple comparison test) were used. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, n.s; not significant.
