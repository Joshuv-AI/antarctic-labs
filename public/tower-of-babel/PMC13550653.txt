Quantitative analysis of collagen architecture in the human uterotubal junction (UTJ) using optical coherence tomography imaging (OCT)
Authors: Dilara Long, Robert Klein, Makayla Johnson, Jillian Leaver, Photini Rice, Julia Fisher, Arielle Joasil, Victoria Fewell, John Heusinkveld, Christine Hendon, Jennifer Barton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAbnormal uterotubal junction (UTJ) structure is implicated in the pathogenesis of endometriosis and infertility; however, this relationship remains poorly characterized. We quantitatively characterized the orientation of collagen fiber bundles in the UTJ using optical coherence tomography (OCT). UTJ tissue from nine individuals undergoing hysterosalpingectomy were collected and longitudinally opened to expose the lumen. Volumetric OCT images were acquired from proximal (uterine), middle, and distal (isthmic) UTJ segments. Images were preprocessed to enhance contrast and continuity of fiber bundles. Local fiber bundle orientation was extracted from Sobel-filtered intensity gradients. Orientation values were binned corresponding to the expected alignment of collagen fiber bundle groups in UTJ smooth muscle: longitudinal (0°–30°), oblique (30°–60°), and circumferential (60°–90°). Proportions were plotted as a function of depth, and depth-dependent trends in alignment were compared across UTJ segments using mixed-effects models. Measurements revealed a prominent inner longitudinal muscle layer which emerged proximally and thinned distally, and an outer muscle layer composed of oblique uterine fiber bundles proximally and a mixed oblique-circumferential contribution distally. These findings provide the first quantitative, depth-resolved analysis of UTJ collagen architecture and establish an analytical framework which can support future studies investigating how UTJ structure relates to function and disease.Keywords: Uterotubal junction, Optical coherence tomography, Fallopian tubes, Collagen architecture, Endometriosis, InfertilitySubject terms: Anatomy, Diseases, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

The uterotubal junction (UTJ) refers to the narrow 1–3 cm long intramural segment of the fallopian tube that extends from the uterine ostium to the isthmus1,2. This tortuous, muscular segment contributes a sphincter-like contractile mechanism that regulates the precisely timed, bidirectional transport of gametes and embryos and is thought to limit retrograde menstruation, although this role remains incompletely understood2–6. The functional significance of the UTJ has been implicated by clinical imaging studies, which have linked aberrant contractility or obstruction to endometriosis and infertility7–9. Furthermore, a higher incidence of anatomical variations in the proximal tubes, such as decreased tortuosity10 and congenital Müllerian anomalies11, has been demonstrated in patients with endometriosis. Collectively, these observations implicate abnormal UTJ structure and function in the pathogenesis of endometriosis and tubal factor infertility. Despite the high prevalence of these diseases in females12–14, the structural basis underlying these associations remains poorly characterized.

Compared to the extramural fallopian tube, the UTJ has a unique architecture consisting of an inner longitudinal muscle layer and an outer muscular ring that undergo marked transitional changes from the uterus to the isthmus2,15,16. This architecture is shared by few other tubular systems, including the vas deferens17 and pyloric sphincter18, where coordinated contraction is essential for unidirectional propulsion and sphincteric control. Within the UTJ, these layers each exhibit distinct hormone-dependent contractile patterns that are thought to transiently close the tubal lumen and regulate ovum transfer at the time of ovulation19. However, the three-dimensional (3D) organization of these layers and their relationship to UTJ function remain incompletely defined.

Our current understanding of UTJ smooth muscle architecture is largely derived from subjective two-dimensional (2D) histological studies2, which is inadequate for fully capturing the organization of out-of-plane collagen fibers within orthogonally arranged muscle layers or assessing patient-to-patient structural variability. Histological features of proximal tubal obstruction including fibrosis secondary to chronic inflammation, salpingitis isthmica nodosa, and intramucosal polyps and endometriosis, further highlight the UTJ as a site of disease involvement in tubal factor infertility7. However, the true incidence and structural presentation of these pathologies remains unclear, as the UTJ is not routinely evaluated in standard pathology workflows.

Existing clinical imaging methods are also limited in their ability to resolve UTJ microstructure or provide quantitative metrics linking structural variation to disease. Hysterosalpingography, hysteroscopy, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging have been used to assess tubal patency and mullerian anomalies in the proximal fallopian tubes, but have limited spatial resolution10,20. Recently, Kurz et al. used 3D high-resolution episcopic microscopy (HREM) to characterize segmental variation in UTJ microarchitecture and establish baseline measurements of muscle layer thicknesses in non-pathological specimens21. Their findings demonstrate the value of high-resolution imaging to reveal new and detailed structural insights into UTJ architecture that were previously inaccessible to 2D histology, although their interpretation was largely qualitative.

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a label-free optical imaging modality that has sparked growing interest in the diagnostic assessment of the female reproductive tract22. OCT is analogous to B-mode ultrasound but measures the intensity of back-reflected near-infrared light to produce cross-sectional images of subsurface tissue microstructures with near-histological resolution (1–20 µm) up to 2 mm in depth. In OCT images, organized and highly scattering structures like collagen provide intrinsic contrast, making this modality well-suited for visualizing smooth muscle architecture in collagen-rich reproductive tissues. Automated image processing methods previously developed to quantify collagen fiber organization in the human cervix23 and uterus24 ex vivo, as well as to extract image-derived features of endometriosis in the fallopian tubes25, may be adapted to the UTJ to establish quantitative imaging biomarkers of tissue structure. Catheter-based OCT falloposcopes may eventually enable non-destructive evaluation of the UTJ in vivo26; however, their diagnostic utility remains unclear as there are no established markers linking UTJ structure to pathology or impaired function.

The objective of this feasibility study was to fully characterize the UTJ microstructure for the first time, in situ, using a tabletop OCT system, and to develop a quantitative image analysis method to map collagen fiber bundle orientation as a function of depth and UTJ segment. This work establishes a quantitative framework for characterizing the multilayered UTJ microarchitecture, and may support future efforts to identify structural markers in the setting of disease.

Materials and methods

Tissue collection and sample preparation

All procedures involving human participants were approved by the University of Arizona Institutional Review Board under protocols 1100000679 (last approved September 18, 2025) and 00005863 (approved November 7, 2025). All research was performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

For the purposes of our imaging protocols, we refer to the UTJ as the 1–3 cm segment which begins proximally at the uterine ostium, extends distally as the intramural segment of the fallopian tube, and ends at the isthmus. Figure 1 is provided as a reference to the anatomical arrangement and to define UTJ segments assessed in this study. Unilateral UTJ segments were obtained from female participants (n = 12) undergoing hysterosalpingectomy for benign indications including abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB), pelvic organ prolapse, endometriosis, adenomyosis, and uterine fibroids. The sample was transported in cold (~ 5 °C) Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). The tubal lumen was cannulated from the isthmic (distal) end with a 2.4 French coaxial microcatheter and 0.018″ guidewire system (Progreat, Terumo Medical, MC*PE24111YB) and advanced proximally through the UTJ, toward the uterine ostium, which served as a guide to facilitate precise longitudinal dissection of the small, tortuous lumen. The sample was then opened to expose the luminal surface, rinsed, pinned to a silicone-coated petri dish, and submerged in DMEM. In 3 patient samples, dissection and imaging were not possible due to an inability to cannulate the lumen.

Gross photograph of the uterine cornu with the tubal lumen exposed following longitudinal dissection. The arrows indicate the edges of the lumen. The longitudinal tubal axis was defined from the proximal uterine cavity toward the distal isthmus. The ostium was defined as the funnel-shaped opening to the tube, contiguous with uterus. The uterotubal junction (UTJ) was defined as the intramural segment of the tube extending from the ostium to the extramural isthmus, and was subdivided into proximal, middle, and distal segments for segmental analysis.

Optical coherence tomography image acquisition

OCT imaging was performed using a benchtop spectral-domain OCT system (Thorlabs Telesto II, Tel221PS) with a center wavelength of 1300 nm, axial resolution of 5.5 µm in air, and objective with a lateral resolution of 7 µm (LSM02, Thorlabs). The A-scan acquisition rate was 28 kHz, and B-scan averaging set to 2. During imaging, the sample dish was placed beneath the objective, with the exposed luminal tissue surface oriented normal (perpendicular) to the imaging objective. The live camera view was used to center the field of view (FOV) on the lumen and to manually align the imaging x axis with the longitudinal tubal axis. The distance from the objective to the tissue (which was submerged in culture medium) was adjusted so that the focal volume was positioned just below the tissue surface. Volumetric imaging was then performed by lateral beam scanning using the system’s internal galvanometer mirrors.

Non-overlapping volumetric OCT datasets were iteratively acquired from the proximal (uterine) ostia to the distal (isthmus) segment, with the aim of covering the full sample length. The number of volumes acquired depended on the length, tortuosity, and curvature of the sample, which varied between patients. Areas with strictures or acute angles within the imaging FOV were bypassed and imaging was continued in adjacent, straight segments. The imaging FOV was 3.0 mm (x, centered along the UTJ proximal-to-distal axis) × 1.0 mm (y, along the opened UTJ circumferential axis) × 2.53 mm (z, depth) with a pixel size of 3.50 µm (x) × 3.50 µm (y) × 2.47 µm (z, physical). For analysis, each dataset was retrospectively assigned to an anatomic segment (ostia, proximal, middle, or distal) based on its relative position along the longitudinal axis and gross morphology, as determined by comparison of the OCT camera view with a gross image of the fully opened specimen.

Histology processing and analysis

Following image acquisition, samples were fixed in formalin for at least 48 h and processed for histological analysis. Sections with a thickness of five µm were obtained at 125 µm intervals along the longitudinal axis of the FT and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Sections were scanned with a slide scanner (Axio Scan.Z1, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany) equipped with a 20 × magnification objective (Plan-Apochromat 20×/0.8 M27) and camera (Hitachi Kokusai Electric Inc., Tokyo, Japan) to provide images with a pixel size of 0.22 µm. Two to five representative sections per sample were formally interpreted by a board-certified pathologist to characterize the layered architecture by segment and provide histopathological diagnosis. This ground-truth interpretation was qualitatively compared to corresponding areas and used to interpret microstructural features visible on OCT images.

OCT image preprocessing

Collagen bundles appear on OCT images as hyperreflective, linear and interconnected structures. Image volumes were preprocessed for optimum performance of the fiber bundle recognition and orientation mapping algorithm. Steps included: (1) image enhancement, (2) tissue segmentation, and (3) surface detection and background subtraction.

Image enhancement. To improve the visibility of collagen fibers in the presence of artifacts and low contrast, we utilized an existing preprocessing pipeline previously developed for OCT images of the uterus24 with parameter changes to be made suitable for images of the UTJ. Briefly, OCT images were cropped to exclude superficial and deep artifacts and the lower intensity threshold increased to suppress background signal and stretch the effective histogram range in ImageJ (version 1.54p, National Institutes of Health). For each B-scan, a vertical wavelet-Fourier stripe removal filter was applied to suppress surface reflections and saturation artifacts27. Volumetric block-matching and 3D filtering (BM3D) was applied to despeckle while preserving smooth, connected fiber bundles across images28. Finally, a homomorphic filter was applied to enhance contrast, suppress homogenous background tissue, and equalize global image intensity to compensate for depth-dependent signal attenuation29. All processes were performed in Matlab R2024a using the Image Processing Toolbox and Wavelet Toolbox (MathWorks).

Tissue segmentation. Pixel-wise feature extraction and segmentation of enhanced OCT images was performed using ImageJ’s Trainable Weka Segmentation plugin30 on a high-performance computing cluster (64 CPU cores, 5 GB RAM per core). Representative B-scans from each volume were manually annotated by an experienced interpreter and labeled as tissue or background. The background class included low-intensity non-tissue areas, such as media and floating debris, as well as specular surface reflections caused by the air-media interface. Annotations were used to train a supervised Random Forest classifier incorporating multi-scale image intensity, texture, and structural features. A detailed description of the feature extraction parameters and classifier configuration is described previously31. The trained model was applied to each volumetric dataset to generate a binary tissue mask. When segmentation performance was suboptimal, additional annotations were incorporated and the model was retrained.

Segmentation refinement. Binary tissue masks were refined to retain the largest connected tissue component and exclude small, disconnected tissue fragments and artifacts in 3D (Keep largest region function, MorpholibJ, v1.6.4). The binary stack was processed using a 3D maximum filter (radius = 2 pixels in x, y, z) to connect small gaps and smooth tissue boundaries, and large gaps within tissue regions were filled using a 2D hole-filling algorithm. For each column in the binary tissue mask, the surface was defined as most superficial pixel in a contiguous run of at least 40 tissue pixels in depth (z) (approximately 100 µm) to exclude analysis of overlapping epithelial folds. The resulting surface profile was smoothed laterally using a 1D median filter to suppress spurious edges (approximately 50 columns in x). A moving-average filter was applied to reduce image-to-image variation (approximately 20 columns in y). A 3D mask was then generated to define all pixels at or below the surface profile as tissue. The resulting mask was applied to the enhanced volume to remove artifacts and irrelevant tissue-mimicking signal above the surface (air-media interface, mucus, small epithelial projections).

Fiber bundle recognition and orientation mapping

To further refine the edges of collagen fiber bundles, a line enhancement filter was applied to the enhanced, masked image volumes in the en face plane32 (ImageJ’s Tubeness filter, σ = 5, BSD-2, v2.2.0). Then, en face OCT images were divided into a grid of 27 × 9 non-overlapping subregions (109 μm × 109 μm each) and fiber bundle orientation analyzed using a previously validated method23. The non-overlapping subregion size was empirically selected to capture continuous changes in fiber bundle orientation, while minimizing computational burden. Briefly, the magnitude and direction of local (3 × 3 pixel) intensity gradients was calculated for each pixel in the image. Within each subregion, pixelwise gradient directions were weighted by their magnitudes and summed to create an orientation histogram spanning [0°, 180°] with 0.5° bin width. A confidence ratio was computed as the peak height divided by the weighted standard deviation. Subregions with low mean intensity gradient or a low confidence ratio were excluded from further analysis. The peak orientation (θ), defined as the histogram mode across all pixels in the subregion, was assigned a color and rendered as lines atop each subregion.

This process was iteratively applied to each en face image (x, y) across the depth axis (z) to generate a 3D orientation matrix θ(x, y, z) for the entire volume, where z corresponds to image increment over depth with 2.47 μm spacing. Thus, for the depth range of 0.8 mm included in fiber orientation analysis, the orientation matrix could contain up to 27 × 9 × 325 subregions, or approximately 79,000 orientation values, per volume. Orientation values were folded from [0°, 180°] to [0°, 90°] to enforce axial symmetry. Any slight mismatch between the longitudinal tubal axis and the imaging x axis was corrected; the needed angular adjustment was estimated by visual examination of a summed en face projection of the image volume.

Analysis of fiber bundle alignment over depth

Orientation values were organized into three bins corresponding to the expected alignment of collagen fiber bundles within UTJ smooth muscle layers: longitudinal (0°–30°), oblique/uterine spiral (30°–60°), and circumferential (60°–90°). For each image, the proportion of subregions mapped to each orientation bin was calculated over the total number of mapped orientation values and plotted as a function of depth up to ~ 800 μm below the surface, to generate an alignment profile. The surface was defined as the first en face image in depth with > 20% mapped orientation values, to account for tissue surface curvature. Alignment profiles were smoothed using a sliding-window average along image index (window = 25 images (~ 62 μm); step = 10 images (~ 25 μm)), and each smoothed value was plotted at the center depth of its corresponding window. Peaks and trends in alignment profiles were visually compared to microstructural features on corresponding en face OCT images.

Statistical analysis of segmental data across patients

To characterize trends by segment, smoothed alignment profiles were grouped by anatomical segment (ostia, proximal, middle, distal), and the mean ± standard deviation of fiber bundle proportions at each smoothed depth position was plotted across patients. To evaluate segmental trends in alignment across patients, image-derived features were extracted from each volumetric dataset. These features included: (1) surface longitudinal proportion, as a metric related to superficial mucosal complexity; (2) depth of peak longitudinal alignment, as an estimate of the depth at which the inner longitudinal muscle was most prominent; (3) crossover depth, defined as the depth at which the dominant fiber orientation shifted from longitudinal to oblique or circumferential alignment, as an estimate of the boundary between the inner and outer smooth muscle layers; and (4) outer smooth muscle layer orientation preference, assessed from the relative oblique versus circumferential alignment at region-specific post-crossover depths.

Linear mixed-effects models were fit with location as a fixed effect and patient as a random intercept to account for multiple datasets obtained from the same individual. Pre-specified contrasts were used to compare adjacent anatomic segments (ostia–proximal, proximal–middle, and middle–distal). The ostium was excluded from crossover depth analysis because a well-defined crossover was not expected; for other datasets in which longitudinal alignment remained dominant throughout the full image depth, the maximum measured depth (821 µm) was assigned. Model-estimated means were reported as mean ± 95% CI. Intraclass correlation coefficients were calculated from each random-intercept linear mixed-effects model as the ratio of between-patient variance to total variance. To evaluate outer smooth muscle layer orientation preference, the difference between oblique and circumferential alignment proportions was analyzed separately in proximal and distal UTJ segments by fitting a linear mixed effects model with a fixed intercept and a random intercept per participant to the difference in oblique and circumferential alignment proportions. Because the depth corresponding to outer smooth muscle was expected to differ by segment, analyses were performed at the next deepest available 100-µm bin after the model-estimated segmental mean crossover depth in each segment. Analyses used Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.3).

Comparison of fiber bundle morphology on enhanced OCT B-scans to histology

The three-layered architecture of the UTJ consisting of a superficial mucosa, an inner longitudinal smooth muscle layer, and an outer muscle layer was visualized on both OCT and histology images. Figure 4 shows representative enhanced OCT B-scans alongside spatially approximated histology images for one patient sample (Patient 9). Representative images of the ostium, proximal, middle, and distal UTJ segments are provided in each column to highlight transitional changes in morphology along the proximal–distal tubal axis. At the ostium (Fig. 4f), OCT images showed complex, overlapping folds with thin hyperreflective stromal bands lining large hyporeflective gaps, which spanned the depth of the image. On histology (Fig. 4a), this corresponded to mucosal blood vessels, glands and stroma extending into the lumen. In general, the complexity and thickness of mucosal folds decreased toward the distal UTJ. On both imaging modalities, the proximal UTJ (Fig. 4b, g) was identified by an emerging inner longitudinal muscle layer, which appeared contiguous with the mucosal stroma. On OCT images (Fig. 4f–j), this layer appeared as an intermediate intensity band with a smooth texture and was most prominent in proximal and middle segments.

Spatially approximated comparison of UTJ segments on longitudinal H&E-stained histological sections (a–e) and OCT B-scans (f–j) (scale bars = 250 μm) for one patient. Representative images of the ostium (a, f), proximal (b, g), more proximal middle (c, h), more distal middle (d, i), and distal (e, j) UTJ segments are provided in each column to highlight transitional changes in morphology along the proximal–distal tubal axis. On OCT images of the UTJ, the mucosa (m) was identified by superficial hyperreflective bands overlying hyporeflective gaps. The inner longitudinal muscle layer (i) emerged at the proximal UTJ (g) and reached its maximum thickness in middle UTJ segments (h, i). Proximally, outer muscle corresponding to uterine myometrium (o) appeared as oblique alternating light–dark streaks resembling “zebra stripes” on OCT images (open arrowheads in g, h, i). Toward the distal UTJ (j), circumferentially oriented fibers resembling tubal circular muscle were best visualized in orthogonal sections and appeared punctate on OCT B-scans. In this patient sample, perivascular fibrosis was noted in the distal UTJ segment (black thin arrows) and was most prominent near the inner longitudinal muscle layer (e). On OCT images, this appeared as a prominent thin hyperreflective band with some deep signal attenuation and scattering (j).

The outer musculature exhibited segmental variation in fiber bundle organization. Proximally, a three-dimensional mesh-like network of loosely woven collagen and smooth muscle fibers characteristic of uterine myometrium was visualized on histology. On OCT B-scans, this complex myometrial architecture was characterized by alternating light–dark streaks resembling “zebra stripes” (Fig. 4g–i), with intersecting and obliquely oriented myometrial bundles coursing into and out of the imaging plane. In contrast, circumferentially oriented fibers typical of tubal myosalpinx were more prevalent in distal segments and appeared punctate on OCT B-scans. These features are difficult to appreciate in Fig. 4 due to the presence of perivascular fibrosis in the distal segment (Fig. 4e), which resulted in increased scattering and attenuation of the OCT signal (Fig. 4j). To more clearly illustrate outer muscular architecture, OCT cross sections perpendicular to the long axis of the tube (axial view) from a different patient (Patient 4) are provided in Fig. 5. Circumferential fiber bundles were best visualized on axial cross sections, and were distinguished by more planar, evenly spaced organization (Fig. 5b), compared to the mesh-like oblique arrangement of uterine fiber bundles in proximal segments (Fig. 5a). The apparent increased reflectivity of circumferential bundles is consistent with their perpendicular orientation relative to the imaging beam, whereas obliquely arranged bundles appeared more diffuse.

The shape of the fiber bundle orientation curves provides insight into the thickness of tissue layers, as well as their continuity along the UTJ. Peaks in the curves visually correspond to depths at which each fiber bundle group appears most dominant in the corresponding en face images. Peaks with high relative magnitude were most characteristic of uniformly aligned collagen fiber bundles within smooth muscle layers. For example, prominent peaks in longitudinal alignment (blue curves in Fig. 7b–d) corresponded to uniformly aligned fiber bundles visible in the corresponding en face images, where UTJ inner longitudinal muscle is expected. Deeper into the tissue (400–600 μm in depth), fiber bundles visible in the en face images show a shift in preferential alignment to oblique or circumferential (purple and red curves in Fig. 7b–d), corresponding to outer smooth muscle. Aberrant peaks could occur in the presence of collagen lining large blood vessels and glands (e.g. red curve in Fig. 7a). Mixed or muted trends in alignment were noted in less uniformly organized structures, such as the mucosal stroma (Fig. 7a) and in instances of disorganized collagen in the setting of pathology, such as fibrosis (Fig. 7e).

Averaged alignment trends by segment

Figure 8 summarizes the fiber bundle alignment over depth measured for four segments (ostium, proximal, middle, and distal UTJ), averaged across nine patients. Model estimated means and prespecified contrast estimates with 95% confidence intervals are provided in Tables 2 and 3, respectively. Overall, the alignment profiles follow similar trends to that seen in Fig. 7 for a single patient, however, the large, shaded bands (the standard deviations) reveal that there is inter-patient variability, likely reflecting heterogeneity in menstrual phase and benign pathologies present across samples (see Table 1). Approximate intraclass correlation coefficients indicated that between-patient differences accounted for ~ 25% of the variance in surface longitudinal proportion, ~ 19% of the variance in peak longitudinal depth, and ~ 42% of the variance in transition depth. Despite this variability, segmental trends along the proximal–distal UTJ axis (uterine ostium to isthmus) were evident. Surface longitudinal alignment differed significantly by segment (F(3,26) = 9.5, p = 0.0002), consistent with segmental differences in mucosal architecture. Depth of peak longitudinal alignment also differed by segment (F(3,27) = 8.1, p = 0.0005), with model-estimated means of 291 μm at the ostium (95% CI 178.8–402), 221 μm at the proximal UTJ (95% CI 128.8–314), 53 μm at the middle UTJ (95% CI − 30.7–137), and 50 μm at the distal UTJ (95% CI − 26.7–127). Crossover depth, used as an estimate of the mucosal plus inner longitudinal layer thickness, differed significantly by area (F(2,22) = 3.6, p = 0.04), with model-estimated means of 574 μm in the proximal UTJ (95% CI 425–723), 604 μm in the middle UTJ (95% CI 466–743), and 438 μm in the distal UTJ (95% CI 308–568).

Outer-layer orientation

(Oblique − Circumferential)

Pairwise comparisons revealed segmental shifts in alignment trends between adjacent segments. At the ostium (Fig. 8a), a mixture of fiber bundle alignment was observed, consistent with contributions from collagen lining complex epithelial folds and glands spanning the entire depth of the image. Surface longitudinal alignment and peak longitudinal depth were not significantly different from that of the proximal UTJ (both p > 0.05). At the proximal UTJ (Fig. 8b), highly aligned fiber bundles corresponding to the inner longitudinal muscle (blue line) emerged, peaked at 221 μm, and persisted to a crossover depth of 574 μm. Compared with the proximal UTJ, the middle UTJ (Fig. 8c) did not show a significantly different crossover depth (p = 0.69), and showed a higher surface longitudinal alignment (p = 0.0016), and a shallower peak longitudinal depth (p = 0.01), consistent with a less complex mucosa and prominent longitudinal muscle layer in this segment. Toward the distal UTJ (Fig. 8d), longitudinal alignment also peaked at the surface and did not differ significantly from the middle UTJ in either surface proportion or peak depth (both p > 0.05), but the crossover depth was shallower than in the middle UTJ segment (p = 0.01), consistent with a thinner combined mucosal plus longitudinal muscle compartment distally.

Preferred outer smooth muscle orientation was assessed separately in proximal and distal UTJ segments at depths immediately beyond the model-estimated segmental mean transition depth. This corresponded to 600 μm in proximal UTJ and 500 μm in distal UTJ. At 600 μm, proximal segments showed a slight but significant preference for oblique over circumferential alignment (p = 0.04). At 500 μm, distal segments showed no significant difference between oblique and circumferential alignment (p = 0.95), consistent with a more even contribution of these two outer-layer orientations.

Discussion

Prior studies of the UTJ have largely relied on qualitative assessment of two-dimensional histological sections, providing limited insights into how its distinctive collagen and smooth muscle arrangement relates to its biomechanical function. In the present study, we used high-resolution volumetric OCT imaging to characterize segmental trends in collagen architecture within nine patient-derived UTJ samples. We then developed a semi-automated image processing method to perform the first quantitative, depth-resolved analysis of collagen alignment up to ~ 800 µm below the surface. Our methods provide a quantitative framework for future studies which may aim to formally evaluate differences between normal versus pathological fiber bundle architecture in a larger population.

We first characterized the layered UTJ morphology on OCT images through direct visual comparison to histology. Consistent with prior studies of UTJ microstructure2,16,21, our results highlight the distinction of the UTJ architecture from the adjacent uterine and FT tissue. As shown in Fig. 4, both OCT and histology reveal a transition in fiber bundle architecture along the UTJ, including an inner longitudinal smooth muscle layer that becomes most prominent in the middle UTJ and thins toward the isthmus, and an outer muscular ring that exhibits a morphological shift from oblique (uterine spiral) toward a more circumferential architecture distally.

Our image-derived alignment measurements support and expand upon this qualitative image interpretation by providing quantitative measures of collagen fiber bundle organization within UTJ mucosal and smooth muscle layers. Side-by-side comparison of alignment profiles to corresponding OCT images (Fig. 7) revealed prominent depth-dependent trends that followed the expected organization of collagen-rich smooth muscle layers. We then averaged OCT-derived alignment profiles across patients (Fig. 8) to characterize segmental differences in UTJ architecture (from the uterus to the FTs). Using mixed-effects models, we extracted quantitative features from these profiles. Using crossover depth as an estimate of combined mucosal plus inner longitudinal muscle thicknesses, we obtained model-estimated means of 574 μm, 604 μm, and 438 μm in proximal, middle, and distal UTJ segments, respectively—similar to that manually measured and reported by Kurz et al. using 3D HREM ex vivo (~ 616 µm proximally and ~ 336 µm distally21). Additional OCT-derived features, including surface longitudinal alignment and peak longitudinal alignment depth, were interpreted to clarify the relative contributions of mucosal and longitudinal muscle layers to their combined thickness. Finally, we compared preferential alignment at depths corresponding to outer smooth muscle layer. Collectively, our results demonstrated statistically significant differences in alignment features reflecting the segmental and depth-dependent morphology observed qualitatively on OCT and histology, even within this small cohort of nine patients. Further validation is needed to determine how closely these image-derived features correspond to true histologic layer boundaries and tissue composition.

Beyond characterizing segmental UTJ anatomy, our methods could be used to evaluate patient to patient differences in fiber bundle architecture. For example, between-patient differences accounted for ~ 42% of the variance in crossover depth, suggesting that this feature may be sensitive to biological variation between individuals. This variability may reflect normal biological factors, such as menstrual phase or collagen reorganization resulting from pregnancy33, as well as structural changes related to the underlying indications for surgery including endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, and prolapse (e.g. fibrosis in Fig. 7e). In future work, layer-specific morphological analysis using sophisticated imaging features, such as attenuation coefficients and image textures, could be further investigated to reveal differences in pathological samples25.

Compared to histology, volumetric OCT imaging offers several advantages for characterizing the tortuous, small caliber UTJ. Orthogonal OCT projections such as that shown in Fig. 5 allow visualization of fiber bundle alignment and continuity in 3D, which are not as easily visualized on 2D cross sections. Volumetric datasets can be acquired with minimal sample preparation and in substantially less time. Finally, OCT imaging has the potential to be performed through catheter-based endoscopes in vivo25,26, which may also overcome difficulties in consistently achieving in-plane sample orientation along the native curvature of the tube.

Our image processing methods build upon existing algorithms which have aimed to map collagen structure from OCT images of the human uterus and cervix23,24. Compared to the original methods, additional processing steps were needed to accommodate the multilayered structure of the UTJ. The image enhancement pipeline—designed for uterine myometrium24—reliably emphasized bright, interconnected collagen networks and improved delineation of tissue layers in the UTJ, compared to unprocessed OCT images. An additional line enhancement filter was needed to enhance the appearance of densely packed longitudinal fiber bundles, which have a more uniform texture compared to uterine spiral fiber bundles. Second, we developed a supervised machine learning segmentation model to enable robust tissue segmentation and luminal surface detection in the presence of artifacts, sample curvature, and epithelial folds31. Future work may benefit from developing a more generalizable model trained with varying imaging parameters, which could reduce the need for time-intensive manual annotations and allow broader application across imaging systems. Finally, our collagen mapping approach quantified alignment over depth from intensity gradients within 100 μm subregions of 2D en face images. Applying pixelwise fiber tractography algorithms similar to that demonstrated in the uterus24 and myocardium34 could offer additional insights into fiber length, density, and coherence, although they will be challenging to apply to OCT images of the UTJ due to scattering contributions from multiple orthogonal layers.

Endometrial thickness can be up to 7 mm in the secretory phase—far exceeding the maximum imaging depth (~ 2 mm) of OCT systems—which has historically limited investigation of deeper musculature in the uterus. In the present study, we showed that the relatively thin UTJ mucosa enables complete visualization of the inner longitudinal layer and partial visualization of the underlying uterine musculature on OCT images. Our quantitative approach may provide an opportunity to identify anatomic variants or subsurface obstructive pathology, such as submucosal fibroids or adenomyosis35, which may be particularly valuable in the clinical evaluation of proximal tubal occlusion and tubal factor infertility. Future work incorporating image-derived features with dynamic imaging of UTJ contractility patterns may also help clarify the biological significance of the inner longitudinal muscle layer. For example, approaches similar to those demonstrated by Han et al.in mouse oviducts in vivo36 could be used to relate UTJ microarchitecture to contractile patterns involved in embryo transport, sperm storage, and regulation of retrograde menstrual flow in patients with endometriosis.

There are some limitations to our method that should be considered when interpreting results. Direct translation of our methods to other tabletop or catheter-based OCT systems may require optimization of algorithm parameters to adjust for differences in resolution, sensitivity, sampling geometry, and imaging artifacts. Shrinkage during fixation and an inability to spatially co-register and align OCT and histology images to the same micron-thickness plane limit our ability to directly interpret layer thickness measurements between modalities. Another limitation inherent to the ex vivo setting is that our method involves longitudinally opening the tubal wall, which may disrupt the native curvature and integrity of connected collagen fiber bundles and potentially affect alignment measurements. Finally, our samples were derived from a small cohort of reproductive-aged patients with heterogenous clinical backgrounds and surgical indications, and our alignment measurements may not be representative of the broader population.

Conclusions

In this study, we present the first quantitative, depth-resolved characterization of collagen fiber bundle architecture in the human uterotubal junction (UTJ) using optical coherence tomography (OCT). We developed an automated pipeline to enhance images and perform subregion-based orientation mapping of collagen fiber bundles. Quantitative image-derived features were extracted to reveal depth- and segment-dependent trends in fiber alignment and organization along the UTJ (from uterus to isthmus), providing insight into the structural arrangement of UTJ layers, particularly the distinctive inner longitudinal muscle layer. Our methods establish an analytical framework which can support future studies investigating how UTJ structure relates to function and disease. With further development and translation to catheter-based OCT platforms, this approach has the potential to enable noninvasive assessment of UTJ structural integrity in vivo, which may be particularly valuable in the clinical evaluation of endometriosis or tubal factor infertility.
