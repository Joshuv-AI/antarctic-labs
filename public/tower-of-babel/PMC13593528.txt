Recurrent vasovagal syncope treated with 2 sequential cardioneuroablation procedures
Authors: Xinyi Wang, Aiyue Chen, Lihui Zheng, Yan Yao
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Isolated left atrial ganglionated plexus (GP) ablation for vasovagal syncope (VVS) carries a high long-term recurrence risk; biatrial GP ablation delivers more sustained vagal denervation. Incomplete denervation limited to the left atrium is the primary driver of late syncope relapse in this case.•2 core mechanisms account for recurrent syncope after initial cardioneuroablation (CNA): incomplete GP elimination during the first procedure and gradual cardiac autonomic neural regeneration over the years. Heart rate variability data in this case confirmed partial vagal function recovery 5 years after index ablation.•High-amplitude fractionated electrograms (HAFEs) serve as a reliable surrogate marker to localize atrial GPs when high-frequency stimulation mapping is unavailable intraoperatively. HAFE distribution clearly identified recurrent vagal hotspots during the repeat ablation.•The right inferior GP is a critical target for repeat CNA, given that its ablation can trigger profound vagal reflexes including severe bradycardia and hypotension, which correlates with symptom recurrence in patients with mixed-type VVS.•Sequential second CNA is a safe and effective rescue therapy for refractory recurrent VVS after initial ablation, with no procedural complications observed and complete resolution of syncope and prodromal symptoms at 18-month follow-up.

Introduction

Vasovagal syncope (VVS) represents the most common cause of transient loss of consciousness.1 Despite its high prevalence, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely understood.2 Autonomic dysfunction is believed to play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of VVS. Excessive cardiac parasympathetic activation results in cardioinhibition, whereas insufficient vascular sympathetic tension leads to vasodilation.3

Cardioneuroablation (CNA), as an emerging interventional treatment, has been demonstrated to significantly reduce syncope burden in VVS.4 By selectively ablating the cardiac ganglionated plexus (GP), CNA theoretically provides a sustained attenuation of vagal tone while exerting minimal impact on the sympathetic system, thereby reducing the cardioinhibitory component of syncope.5 According to a recent meta-analysis, approximately 6% of patients still experience recurrence after CNA.6 The underlying causes are complex and may involve heterogeneity of patient selection, incomplete denervation, or autonomic reinnervation after the procedure.7, 8, 9 Notably, a small number of patients have undergone repeat neuroablation owing to postprocedural symptom recurrence in China.10,11 In this study, we present a detailed case report of a patient with refractory VVS who experienced symptom recurrence after the initial CNA and subsequently underwent a second GP ablation.

Case report

A 66-year-old man was admitted twice to our department for transient loss of consciousness in 2019. He had an 8-year history of recurrent syncope, typically triggered by heat exposure or emotional excitement and preceded by palpitations, dizziness, pallor, and sweating. Each episode recovered within 1–2 minutes in a supine position, without trauma, incontinence, convulsions, nausea, or vomiting. There was no family history of syncope or relevant medication use. In 2019, after 5 syncopal episodes in 1 year, the patient presented to our hospital and was diagnosed as having VVS. Head-up tilt testing (HUTT) was performed to facilitate VVS diagnosis and delineate hemodynamic patterns. The diagnosis of VVS was established when the clinical features conformed to a reflex mechanism and competing diagnoses had been excluded through comprehensive examinations, which was validated by professional cardiologists in accordance with the European Society of Cardiology syncope guidelines.12 Before admission, conservative measures including avoidance of triggers and increased salt and fluid intake had been ineffective, and pharmacologic therapy was not tolerated owing to concomitant hypertension. The patient refused pacemaker implantation and underwent CNA in May 2019. He remained free of syncope and prodromal symptoms for 4 years. In January 2024, he was readmitted to our hospital after experiencing 2 episodes of syncope, both precipitated by prolonged standing in hot environments. The prodromal symptoms in the recent episodes resembled those experienced previously, but the interval from onset to loss of consciousness was longer, allowing him to avoid falls.

During both admissions, physical examination and laboratory tests were unremarkable. Carotid ultrasound, electroencephalography, and brain computed tomography excluded neurologic causes. 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure (BP) was within normal limits. Electrocardiography (ECG) and echocardiography showed no structural heart disease. Coronary angiography revealed mild stenosis of the left anterior descending artery and myocardial bridging. In both 2019 and 2024, HUTT at baseline and after nitroglycerin provocation elicited mixed-type responses that reproduced his spontaneous symptoms. During the first HUTT, the patient developed syncope 38 minutes into the drug challenge phase, with a heart rate (HR) of 32 beats/min (bpm) and a BP of 68/35 mm Hg; ECG demonstrated a gradually slowing sinus bradycardia with junctional escape rhythm. During the second HUTT, the patient experienced presyncope (dizziness, blackout, nausea, and sweating) and syncope at 36 minutes during the drug challenge phase, with an HR of 54 bpm and a BP of 61/31 mm Hg; ECG also showed a slowing sinus bradycardia with junctional escape rhythm. Holter monitoring in 2019 showed a mean HR of 61 bpm, intermittent bradycardia, and a maximum R-R interval of 2.68 seconds. In 2024, Holter monitoring demonstrated a mean HR of 67 bpm without other arrhythmias. No R-R interval of >2 seconds was observed. In addition, HR variability (HRV) indices and deceleration capacity were analyzed to assess cardiac autonomic function (MIC-12H Analysis Platform, Jinke Instruments, Beijing, China) (Table 1). Compared with 2019, the preoperative values of root mean square of successive difference, high-frequency power, and deceleration capacity in 2024 were lower, reflecting diminished parasympathetic predominance. It should be noted that the atropine test was not performed preoperatively, given that the patient required long-term treatment with tamsulosin and finasteride for benign prostatic hyperplasia. The initial procedure in 2019 was performed via right femoral venous access under the Ensite NavX mapping system (St Jude Medical, Abbott) guidance. After transseptal puncture, a detailed 3-dimensional reconstruction of the left atrium (LA) was acquired. Notably, the initial procedure was limited to ablation within the LA only. High-frequency stimulation (HFS) was then conducted sequentially at 6 GPs, including the left superior GP (LSGP), Marshall tract GP (MTGP), left inferior GP (LIGP), posteromedial GP (PMLGP), right inferior GP (RIGP), and right anterior GP (RAGP). Stimulation parameters were standardized at 30 Hz train stimulation, 20 V amplitude, and 2 ms pulse width. Vagal reflexes, manifested as transient bradycardia, atrioventricular block, or a 50% increase in R-R interval, were elicited only at LSGP, LIGP, MTGP, and RAGP. Subsequently, radiofrequency ablation was applied at all GPs with defined parameters (number of 60-second ablations 4; number of 120-second ablations 2; power limit 40 W; temperature limit 60°C). During ablation, a strong vagal reflex was observed at the LSGP, MTGP, and PMLGP, whereas a mild vagal reflex was noted at the LIGP and RIGP. A marked HR increase was observed after ablation of the RAGP (58–75 bpm). The ablation endpoint was defined as the elimination of all vagal reflexes induced by HFS or ablation.

The patient again declined pacemaker therapy, and a second ablation was performed owing to recurrent syncope and mixed phenotype. Preablation preparations for the second procedure were similar to the first, except for the use of the CARTO 3 system (Biosense Webster) for mapping. Without HFS, high-amplitude fractionated electrograms (HAFEs) were used as a potential reference to identify candidate regions corresponding to GPs. Numerous HAFE sites were identified in the RAGP, LSGP, and RIGP, whereas a few were detected in the MTGP and LIGP (Figure 1). Ablation was applied sequentially to the LSGP, MTGP, LIGP, RIGP, and RAGP (number of 60-second ablations 6; power limit 35 W; temperature limit 43°C). A single, notable vagal response—characterized by severe bradycardia with a diastolic BP of as low as 40 mm Hg—was observed during ablation at the RIGP, and ablation was continued until the reflex disappeared (Figure 2). LA ablation targeting the RAGP did not elicit a significant increase in HR. No complications occurred either during the procedure or throughout the follow-up period. At 18 months, the patient remained free of syncopal episodes, with complete resolution of prodromal symptoms.

Discussion

CNA has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes in multiple studies.13 However, this results in limited data on repeat ablations for recurrent cases. This study evaluated the therapeutic effect of repeat CNA in patients with VVS and observed promising clinical outcomes, providing valuable insights for guiding subsequent treatment strategies. After recurrence, the HUTT showed a mixed response consistent with the initial test, characterized by progressive sinus bradycardia followed by a junctional escape rhythm with concomitant BP decline. The main difference was that the junctional escape rate increased from 32 bpm in the first HUTT to 54 bpm in the second. This suggests that a cardioinhibitory component—particularly sinus node suppression—contributed to syncope in both 2019 and 2024, whereas partial atrioventricular nodal denervation after the first CNA is indicated by the higher escape rate in the second HUTT. The primary causes of post-CNA recurrence in patients are potentially the regeneration of the cardiac autonomic nervous system and incomplete denervation during the procedure.

Current evidence supports the occurrence of neural regeneration after GP ablation. A 40-month follow-up study by Pachon et al9 found that neural reinnervation predominantly takes place within the first year after CNA, as shown by HRV analysis. Similarly, Sakamoto et al14 demonstrated in an animal model that vagal nerve function begins recovering as early as 4 weeks after ablation. In the present case, the patient experienced recurrent symptoms 5 years after the initial ablation, allowing sufficient time for neural regeneration. Baseline HRV indices in 2024 demonstrated recovery relative to 2019 levels. Intraoperative re-CNA mapping revealed the re-emergence of a marked vagal reflex——manifested by decreases in HR and BP——during ablation at the RIGP, whereas other GPs sites ablated in the first procedure showed only scar tissue and no HR response. These findings suggest that the RIGP may have contributed to the recurrence. However, current evidence indicates that the RIGP is more involved in atrioventricular nodal rather than sinus nodal regulation.15 Although atrioventricular node effective refractory period shortened after the first ablation and increased before the second, these minor differences fall within biological (and measurement) variability and cannot fully explain the observed changes.

Another potential explanation for the recurrence of syncope could be incomplete denervation during the initial procedure. Given the relatively recent emergence of CNA for VVS, standardized ablation strategy and procedural endpoints for this intervention remain unavailable to date. Anatomically, at least 7 major atrial GPs—RAGP, RIGP, LSGP, LIGP, MTGP, PMLGP, and aorta–superior vena cava GP—are distributed within the atria,15 and effective denervation often requires a biatrial ablation approach.16 Studies have shown that ablation confined to the right atrium (RA) is associated with a higher risk of recurrence,6 whereas the addition of biatrial ablation—whether after RA or LA ablation—can further enhance postoperative HR responses.17,18 During both HUTTs, ECG showed sinus bradycardia followed by a junctional escape rhythm, suggesting sinus node suppression contributed to syncope. The RAGP is considered a key regulator of sinus node function.19,20 In patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) undergoing pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), right-sided PVI involving the RAGP increases HR, whereas left-sided PVI has little effect.21,22 In this patient, the first procedure involved LA ablation alone. LA RAGP ablation indeed resulted in an increase in HR and improvements in sinus node recovery time and corrected sinus node recovery time. Regrettably, recurrence still occurred, possibly owing to the limited long-term durability of sinus rate improvement. Compared with previous reports (71→80 bpm at 2 years),9 this patient showed only a modest increase in mean HR (61→67 bpm) from 2019 to 2024, likely related to longer follow-up. During the second CNA, repeat ablation of the LA RAGP did not produce a significant HR change. A marked increase in HR was observed only after additional ablation of the corresponding RA RAGP.

In summary, incomplete initial denervation owing to limited LA-only ablation, without concomitant RA GP ablation, represents the primary mechanism underlying symptom recurrence in this case, whereas neural regeneration may serve as a secondary contributing factor. A notable limitation of the present study is that atropine test was not performed before or after either procedure. Assessing denervation after the first ablation might have helped distinguish incomplete ablation from neural regeneration. Although benign prostatic hyperplasia is a relative contraindication, atropine testing with urinary catheterization may still be considered in patients undergoing CNA. This is particularly relevant in elderly patients, in whom syncope often coexists with cardiac conduction disease and vasodepressor components, making preprocedural evaluation of sinus rate and atrioventricular conduction essential.

Unified procedural endpoints for CNA remain lacking. In this study, procedural endpoints included elimination of atrial potentials within GP regions and abolition of ablation-induced vagal reflexes. Depending on the GP localization method used in each procedure, additional endpoints included abolition of HFS-induced vagal responses or elimination of HAFE.23 However, both HFS- and HAFE-guided strategies have inherent limitations. HFS only stimulates discrete atrial wall points, failing to account for the broad vagal innervation beyond classical GP boundaries and leading to incomplete spatial assessment. In addition, HFS-induced AF, which occurred intraoperatively in this patient, causes autonomic perturbations that confound subsequent GP evaluation. Furthermore, postablation HFS over ablated tissue lacks valid comparability with baseline testing on normal myocardium, and overly early retesting cannot distinguish true denervation from transient neural stunning, which may misinform procedural endpoint determination in clinical practice. It should be noted that HAFEs correspond to the time-domain component of AF nests, a concept that preceded the description of HAFEs. The underlying principle is that myocardial tissue with neural inputs exhibits more fractionated electrograms, whereas noninnervated tissue shows more compact signals.4 Therefore, HAFEs do not directly identify neural structures but serve only as surrogate markers of GP regions, and their specificity remains to be validated in mechanistic studies.

Atropine has been increasingly used in recent studies to assess postoperative HR responses, thereby helping to define procedural endpoints. These include whether the HR reaches the maximum level achieved during preprocedural atropine testing or whether the patient becomes unresponsive to atropine after ablation. However, atropine-based measures do not directly quantify vagal function, and administering atropine after ablation may affect the effects of subsequent ablation. In 2015, Pachon et al9 introduced the extracardiac vagal stimulation (ECVS), which allows real-time intraoperative assessment of vagal denervation.24 Moreover, in a small retrospective cohort, they demonstrated that using ECVS as an endpoint may offer additional benefits in reducing recurrence.8 Given its considerable potential for guiding the extent of ablation during the procedure, ECVS should be evaluated in future studies to help establish a standardized CNA strategy.

Although our findings support the benefit of comprehensive GP ablation in preventing recurrence, the necessity and safety of extensive GP ablation remain uncertain. A thorough ablation strategy may increase the risk of postoperative complications, such as sinus tachycardia, and excessive denervation could compromise the heart’s physiological autonomic protection, leaving its long-term safety unclear.25,26 Consequently, some researchers advocate a more conservative approach, focusing on focal ablation of a limited number of GPs. Minguito-Carazo et al27 reported that, over a mean follow-up of 19 months, a simplified 3-GP ablation strategy (RAGP, LSGP, and PMLGP) achieved autonomic denervation and clinical outcomes comparable with those of more extensive ablation strategies. In the present case, the patient experienced recurrence 5 years after CNA, far beyond the follow-up duration of Minguito-Carazo’s study,27 underscoring the importance of long-term monitoring for patients undergoing selective GP ablation.

To date, research on therapeutic strategies for recurrent VVS after ablation remains limited. In a recent survey involving clinicians specializing in CNA, only a small subset of clinicians prefer to perform a second ablation after the initial recurrence, whereas the majority opt for repeat ablation for patients experiencing multiple recurrences.28 Nevertheless, high-quality studies evaluating the efficacy of distinct therapeutic regimens for this specific patient population remain lacking. Furthermore, the optimal timing for a second ablation has not been clearly defined, highlighting the need for further investigation.

Conclusion

CNA has emerged as a promising therapy for VVS with favorable efficacy. However, recurrence may occur owing to inadequate denervation or autonomic reinnervation. In such patients, assessment of autonomic function before and after ablation is essential; if a neurally mediated cardioinhibition persists, a second GP ablation may be considered.
