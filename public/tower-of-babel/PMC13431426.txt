Therapeutic Exercise for Forward Head Posture in Neck Pain Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta Analysis [Letter]
Authors: Priyanka Kumari Nahak
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the recently published systematic review and meta analysis by Xing et al1 evaluating the effectiveness of therapeutic exercise for forward head posture (FHP) in individuals with neck pain. The authors should be commended for synthesizing evidence on a clinically important topic and for reporting their systematic review in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 Statement.2 Considering the growing prevalence of FHP and its association with pain, disability, and impaired quality of life, this review provides valuable information for rehabilitation professionals. However, we would like to highlight several methodological issues that may influence the validity and interpretation of the reported findings.

Our primary concern relates to the inconsistency between the predefined eligibility criteria and the interventions ultimately included in the quantitative synthesis. The authors reported excluding studies in which exercise was combined with other interventions or those lacking an exercise only comparison.1 Nevertheless, several included studies compared therapeutic exercise with active interventions such as routine physiotherapy, posture education, cervical stabilization exercises, stretching programmes, or placebo exercise rather than true non exercise controls. Consequently, the pooled estimates represent comparisons among heterogeneous active interventions rather than the isolated effect of therapeutic exercise. According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, clinical heterogeneity arising from differences in interventions and comparator groups should be carefully considered before quantitative pooling because it may substantially influence the validity and interpretation of summary estimates.3

A second concern is the substantial statistical heterogeneity reported across all primary outcomes. The pooled analyses demonstrated heterogeneity values of I2 = 74.6% for craniovertebral angle, I2 = 95.5% for pain intensity, and I2 = 86.7% for neck disability.1 Although the authors appropriately employed random effects models, heterogeneity of this magnitude indicates important differences in participant characteristics, intervention protocols, treatment duration, exercise dosage, comparator interventions, and outcome measurements. Current methodological guidance recommends that such considerable heterogeneity should prompt cautious interpretation of pooled effect estimates and a thorough exploration of potential sources of variability rather than reliance on a single overall treatment effect.3 Therefore, the conclusion that therapeutic exercise is broadly effective across all clinical settings may overstate the certainty of the available evidence.

The subgroup analyses and meta regression also warrant careful consideration. Despite including only ten randomized controlled trials, the authors performed subgroup analyses according to exercise modality and intervention duration, followed by meta regression to investigate potential effect modifiers.1 Meta regression performed with a limited number of studies is known to produce unstable regression coefficients and reduced statistical power, increasing the likelihood of spurious associations or failure to detect true relationships.4 Furthermore, the meta regression did not consistently demonstrate statistically significant associations between intervention duration or exercise modality and all clinical outcomes, particularly craniovertebral angle. Nevertheless, the discussion concludes that combined exercise interventions and treatment durations of at least eight weeks are superior. These conclusions appear stronger than the statistical evidence presented and should therefore be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory findings.

Another methodological issue concerns the assessment of publication bias. Egger’s regression test was performed despite the relatively small number of studies included in each meta analysis.1 Both the Cochrane Handbook and the recommendations by Sterne et al indicate that statistical methods for detecting funnel plot asymmetry have limited reliability when fewer than ten studies are available.3,5 Consequently, the absence of statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry should not be interpreted as evidence that publication bias is absent; rather, publication bias could not be reliably assessed because of the limited number of included studies.

Finally, although the review demonstrated statistically significant improvements in craniovertebral angle, pain intensity, and neck disability, these findings should be interpreted in conjunction with the certainty of evidence. The GRADE framework emphasizes that inconsistency, imprecision, and unexplained heterogeneity should be considered when judging confidence in pooled estimates.6 Given the substantial heterogeneity observed across the included studies, the clinical applicability of the pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Despite these methodological concerns, we acknowledge that the review by Xing et al1 provides valuable insight into the role of therapeutic exercise for managing FHP related neck pain. Future systematic reviews should consider stricter adherence to predefined eligibility criteria, greater homogeneity of comparator interventions, cautious interpretation of subgroup and meta regression analyses, and transparent discussion of the implications of substantial heterogeneity and limited statistical power. Addressing these issues would further strengthen the evidence base and enhance the translation of research findings into clinical rehabilitation practice.
