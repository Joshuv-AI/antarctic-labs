The intrinsic approach to moduli theory
Jarod Alper, Daniel Halpern-Leistner

Abstract
. Moduli theory has captured the imagination of algebraic geometers for at least two centuries. Up until the end of the 20th century, moduli spaces were constructed and studied by rigidifying the moduli problem using extrinsic data and applying geometric invariant theory. Over the last several decades, there has been a paradigm shift toward studying moduli problems intrinsically using the language of algebraic stacks. We highlight recent advances in this direction that have incorporated ideas from geometric invariant theory to develop a structure theory for algebraic stacks. In the ideal situation, it allows one to decompose an algebraic stack into simpler strata and construct moduli spaces corresponding to each stratum. In addition to surveying some previous applications of the theory, we take a forward-looking perspective on the field and identify questions for future research.



1.
Introduction

There is something enchanting about moduli spaces. They simultaneously provide an answer to one of
the most fundamental questions in mathematics, namely the
classification problem
, and are
fascinating spaces in their own right. These spaces are often projective varieties with a very
rich geometry, reflecting the geometry of the individual objects that they parameterize. It has
been nearly 200 years since Riemann coined the term ‘moduli’ and introduced the moduli spaces of
curves
[
94
]
. Similar to the profound paradigm shift around 100 years ago from studying
varieties as embedded spaces to abstract spaces, there has been a gradual transition beginning
around 30 years ago from studying moduli spaces as quotients of rigidified parameter spaces (e.g.,
space of all embedded curves) to a more intrinsic approach using algebraic stacks. While algebraic
stacks were first introduced in the 1960s
[
36
]
, it was not until the 1990s that
they had significant impact in moduli theory. This adoption was spurred by key results such as the
Keel–Mori Theorem
[
69
]
(which for instance implies the existence of
\overline{M}_{g}
as a
proper algebraic space) and Kollár’s Ampleness Criterion
[
72
]
(which together
with birational methods implies the projectivity of
\overline{M}_{g}
). It still took another decade or two
until they entered mainstream algebraic geometry.

The past decade has seen a new wave of intrinsic methods alongside the discovery of new moduli
spaces. We outline several major advances in the structure theory of algebraic stacks including the local
quotient structure (§
2
), an intrinsic approach to construct and study good
moduli spaces and
\Theta
-stratifications called ‘Beyond GIT’ (§
3
), and combinatorial structures attached
to an algebraic stack, e.g., degeneration spaces (§
4
). We ground these techniques by discussing two specific moduli problems of a very different nature. In §
3
, we use the relatively easy and ‘linear’ moduli problem of semistable vector bundles on a curve to show how each of several approaches in beyond GIT can be effectively applied to construct and study the moduli space. In §
5
, we take a quick detour to explore the geometry of
\overline{M}_{g}
, which has the wonderful feature that many of its birational models are moduli spaces themselves, albeit of different classes of singular curves. These birational models were recently constructed using a blend of intrinsic and extrinsic methods,
but challenges remain in extending the beyond GIT techniques to further study these ‘non-linear’ moduli problems of singular varieties. We take a forward-looking perspective on the field by raising interesting conjectures and problems
prime for future research (§
6
).

2.
Local Structure of Algebraic Stacks

Quotient stacks form a distinguished class of algebraic stacks. The geometry of a quotient stack

2.3.
Applications

These theorems have had many applications in algebraic geometry, most notably in equivariant
geometry. For instance, it has been applied to generalize Sumihiro’s theorem for torus actions on
normal schemes to arbitrary schemes and algebraic spaces, where instead of equivariant Zariski open
affine neighborhoods, one obtains equivariant étale affine neighborhoods. It has also been applied
to prove a general existence result for pushouts for algebraic stacks. The original motivation
and one of the more striking applications is the Existence Theorem
(
Theorem

3.8
) for good moduli spaces.

3.
Beyond Geometric Invariant Theory

We will introduce and motivate the beyond GIT framework using
the running example of the moduli stack
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}:=\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}(C)
of vector bundles over a smooth
projective curve
C
defined over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic
0
.

3.1.
Semistable vector bundles over a curve

The stack
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
assigns any
\Bbbk
-scheme
T
to the groupoid of vector bundles on
C_{T}:=C\times T
.
Even after fixing numerical invariants, the moduli stack
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
of
vector bundles on
C
of rank
r
and degree
d
is too big to admit a moduli space: it is both
unbounded (i.e., not quasi-compact) and highly non-separated. As suggested by Mumford and
Seshadri, it is natural to restrict to the open substack
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}^{\operatorname{ss}}\subseteq\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}

of semistable vector bundles.
A vector bundle
F
on
C
is
semistable
if every nonzero subsheaf
E\subseteq F
satisfies

\mu(E):=\frac{\deg E}{\operatorname{rk}E}\leq\frac{\deg F}{\operatorname{rk}F}=:\mu(F),

where
\mu(F)
is called the
slope
of
F
. A remarkable feature of semistability is that
every vector bundle
F
has a unique filtration

F_{p}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq F_{0}=F

whose associated graded bundles
F_{i}/F_{i+1}
are semistable with monotone increasing slope.
This is called the
Harder–Narasimhan filtration
or simply the
HN filtration
.
Moreover, the substack of bundles whose HN filtration has prescribed ranks and degrees is locally
closed in
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}
, and retracts onto the products of stacks of semistable objects
corresponding to the associated graded pieces of the HN filtration. This gives a stratification of

\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}
called the
HN stratification
. For further details on semistability, see

[
79
]
,
[
66
]
, or
[
14
, §9]
.

3.2.
Geometric invariant theory

By synthesizing Grothendieck’s formalism of scheme theory with 19th century invariant theory,
Mumford developed a theory of quotients in algebraic geometry known as
Geometric Invariant
Theory
(
GIT
), and applied this theory to construct moduli spaces
[
83
]
. The approach
of GIT proceeds along the following three steps:

(1)

Express the moduli problem
\mathcal{M}
as a quotient
U/G
of the
action of a quasi-projective variety
U
by a reductive group

G
and choose a
G
-equivariant compactification
\overline{U}\subseteq\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
, where
G
acts linearly on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{n}
.

(2)

Show that a point
u\in\overline{U}
is in
U
if and only if
it is GIT semistable, i.e., there exists a non-constant

G
-invariant homogeneous
f\in\Gamma(\overline{U},\mathcal{O}(d))^{G}
with
f(u)\neq 0
.

(3)

Realize the moduli space of
\mathcal{M}
as the projective
variety
U/\!/G:=\bigoplus_{d\geq 0}\Gamma(\overline{U},\mathcal{O}(d))^{G}
.

To apply this approach to
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}^{\operatorname{ss}}_{r,d}
, one utilizes Grothendieck’s projective Quot scheme

\overline{U}_{n}:=\operatorname{Quot}^{P}(\mathcal{O}_{C}^{\oplus P(n)}(-n)/C)

parameterizing coherent quotients
[\pi\colon\mathcal{O}_{C}^{\oplus P(n)}(-n)\twoheadrightarrow F]
, where
P(n)=rn\deg(\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))+(1-g)r+d
is the Hilbert polynomial. For
n\gg 0
, the GIT semistable locus
U_{n}\subseteq\overline{U}_{n}
corresponds to quotients such that
F
is a semistable vector bundle and the induced map
\mathrm{H}^{0}(\pi(n))\colon\Bbbk^{\oplus P(n)}\to\mathrm{H}^{0}(C,F(n))
is an isomorphism, and every semistable
F
appears in
U_{n}
. This description of the GIT semistable locus, first established by Seshadri
[
97
]
, is the hardest part of the GIT approach, and has since been
revisited and generalized many times. (See
[
14
, §9.5]
for a discussion).
As a consequence, we obtain a projective good moduli space

M^{\operatorname{ps}}_{r,d}:=U_{n}/\!/\operatorname{GL}_{P(n)}
for
\mathcal{M}\cong U_{n}/\operatorname{GL}_{P(n)}
whose
\Bbbk
-points are in bijective correspondence with
polystable vector bundles.

Another consequence of the general GIT machinery is that the unstable locus
\overline{U}\setminus U
admits a stratification, referred to as the

HKKN stratification
after Hesselink, Kempf, Kirwan, and Ness
[
70
,
64
]
; see
[
14
, §8.7]

for a recent exposition and further references. While the strata of
\overline{U}\setminus U
typically do not admit clean modular interpretations, in the case of
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}
(and sheaves on higher dimensional projective schemes) the GIT stratifications of
\overline{U}_{n}\setminus U_{n}
essentially stabilize as
n\to\infty
to the Harder–Narasimhan stratification of

\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}

[
65
]
.

3.3.
Reinterpreting semistability:
\Theta
-semistability

We will recast the notion of semistability of vector bundles on
C
in terms of
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted
filtrations, which in turn will allow us to generalize the notion of semistability to arbitrary
stacks. By a
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted filtration

E_{\bullet}
, we mean a filtered vector bundle

E_{p}\subsetneq E_{p-1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq E_{0}=E
and a choice of integers

w_{0}<\cdots<w_{p}
. The following lemma turns out to be key to interpreting the semistability
condition
geometrically
.

Lemma 3.1
.

A vector bundle
E
is semistable if and only if

\sum_{i=0}^{p}w_{i}\big(\deg(E)\operatorname{rk}(E_{i}/E_{i+1})-\operatorname{rk}(E)\deg(E_{i}/E_{i+1})\big)\geq 0

for every
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted filtration
E_{\bullet}
of
E
.

The expression in the lemma does not appear out of thin air: it occurs as the weight of a line
bundle on
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
restricted to the associated graded bundle of the
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted filtration.
Namely, from a
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted filtration, one obtains a sheaf

\sum t^{-w_{i}}\mathcal{O}_{C}[t]\cdot E_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{C}[t^{\pm 1}]\otimes E

of graded
\mathcal{O}_{C}[t]
-modules, where
t
has weight
-1
. Fixing
\Theta
as
the quotient

\Theta:=\mathbb{A}^{1}/\mathbb{G}_{m},

\sum t^{-w_{i}}E_{i}
corresponds to a vector bundle on the stack
C\times\Theta
, or
equivalently to a morphism
f:\Theta\to\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
; the map
f:\Theta\to\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}

takes
1
to
E
and
0
to the associated graded bundle
\bigoplus_{i=0}^{p}E_{i}/E_{i+1}
.
For any line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
, we
define
\wt(f^{\ast}\mathcal{L})
as the
opposite
of the unique integer
w
such that

f^{\ast}\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\Theta}\langle w\rangle
.
We will use the line bundle

\mathcal{L}_{\rm{adj}}:=\det(Rp_{2,\ast}(E_{\univ}\otimes E_{\univ}^{\vee}))^{\vee}

where
E_{\univ}
is the universal bundle on
C\times\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}
. Letting
G_{i}=E_{i}/E_{i+1}
be
the graded factors, we can easily compute the weight:

\displaystyle\wt(f^{\ast}\mathcal{L}_{\rm adj})

\displaystyle=-\wt(\det(H^{\ast}(C,\bigoplus_{i,j=0}^{p}G_{i}\otimes G_{j}^{\vee})))

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=0}^{p}(w_{i}-w_{j})\cdot\chi(C,G_{i}\otimes G_{j}^{\vee})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j=0}^{p}(w_{i}-w_{j})\big(\deg(G_{i})\operatorname{rk}(G_{j})-\deg(G_{j})\operatorname{rk}(G_{i})\big)

(Riemann–Roch)

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i=0}^{p}w_{i}(\operatorname{rk}(E)\deg(G_{i})-\deg(E)\operatorname{rk}(G_{i}))

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{i=1}^{p}(w_{i}-w_{i-1})(\operatorname{rk}(E)\deg(E_{i})-\deg(E)\operatorname{rk}(E_{i}))

(telescoping sum)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{p}(w_{i}-w_{i-1})\operatorname{rk}(E)\operatorname{rk}(E_{i})\big(\mu(E)-\mu(E_{i})\big)

The expression in
Lemma

3.1
is one half of the fourth expression.
The final expression makes it transparent that
E
is semistable if and only if
\wt(f^{\ast}\mathcal{L}_{\rm adj})\geq 0
for all maps
f\colon\Theta\to\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}
with
f(1)=[E]
. Except for the factor
of 2, the same calculation holds for the line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{V}:=\det(Rp_{2,\ast}(E_{\univ}\otimes V))^{\vee}
, where
V
is a vector bundle on
C
of rank
r
and degree
r(g-1)-d
.

Semistability can be formulated in this way for any stack equipped with a line bundle
[
56
,
62
]
:

Definition 3.2
.

A point
x\in|\mathcal{X}|
is
semistable with respect to
\mathcal{L}
if
\wt(f^{\ast}\mathcal{L})\geq 0
for
all morphisms
f\colon\Theta_{K}\to\mathcal{X}
(where

\Bbbk
) with

. We denote by
\mathcal{X}^{\mathcal{L}-\operatorname{ss}}
the substack of points semistable with respect to

\mathcal{L}
.

Remark 3.3
.

The sign conventions for the weight of a line bundle on
\Theta
can be confusing. Our
guiding convention is that a map

is destabilizing if

\Gamma(\Theta_{K},f^{\ast}(\mathcal{L}))=0

The above calculations show that
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}^{\operatorname{ss}}=\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}^{\mathcal{L}_{\rm adj}-\operatorname{ss}}

3.4.
Good moduli spaces

If
G
is a (not necessarily connected) reductive group acting on an affine variety
X=\operatorname{Spec}A
,
then we define the GIT quotient as
X/\!/G:=\operatorname{Spec}A^{G}
. If every stabilizer is finite, then

X/\!/G
is a geometric quotient, i.e., the morphism of stacks
X/G\to X/\!/G
is universally
bijective. In general,
X/\!/G

3.5.
Beyond GIT: verifying

\Theta
-stratifications.

To make matters concrete for

-equivariant vector bundle

to

First approach:

If
\mathcal{X}
is
\Theta
- and
S
-complete, then the semistable locus

However, the stack

is neither
\Theta
- nor

-complete, so an initial step
is to find an enlargement

\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that
\mathcal{X}
is
\Theta
- and
S
-complete. Reflecting back on the above
setting, observe that the pushforward

is automatically a

-flat equivariant family of
coherent sheaves, but that the zero fiber

may have torsion. In other words, the

- and

It is in fact a general phenomenon that moduli stacks of objects in an abelian category are

- and

-complete

naturally

fairly easy to verify that a bundle is semistable as a point in

.

Second approach:

The following ideas were developed in
[

Definition 3.9
.

A line bundle

Note that this condition holds automatically if

If

Returning to

We now observe that the data of a flat family of sheaves

3.6.
\Theta
-stratifications

For any stack
\mathcal{X}
, we define the stack of filtered points of
\mathcal{X}
to be
\Filt(\mathcal{X}):=\Map(\Theta,\mathcal{X})
. This is equipped with an evaluation map
{\rm ev}_{1}\colon\Filt(\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{X}

taking

to

. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}
, a
\Bbbk
-point

is a
\mathbb{Z}
-weighted filtration

E_{p}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq E_{0}=E
with
weights
w_{0}<\cdots<w_{p}
, and
\ev_{1}(f)=[E]
for this

. It is often convenient to work instead
with the stacks of
\mathbb{Q}
-weighted
filtered points,
\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
, obtained by stabilizing under certain morphisms
\Filt(\mathcal{X})\to\Filt(\mathcal{X})
that scale weights
[
56
, Prop. 1.3.11]
.

We can regard a Harder-Narasimhan filtration
E_{p}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq E_{0}=E
of a
vector bundle as a point in
\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d})
by equipping it with the weights
w_{i}:=\mu(E_{i}/E_{i+1})-\mu(E)\in\mathbb{Q}
. The set of all Harder-Narasimhan filtrations defines a subset

\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
, whose key properties are generalized in the following:

Definition 3.11

(Variant of
[
56
, Def. 2.1.2]
)
.

A
\Theta
-stratification
of a stack
\mathcal{X}
is an open substack
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
along with a total preordering on the set of connected components
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})

such that
\ev_{1}\colon\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{X}
is universally bijective, and
\forall\gamma\in\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})
,

(1)

\mathcal{X}_{\leq\gamma}:=\mathcal{X}\setminus\bigcup_{\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma}\ev_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\gamma^{\prime}})\subseteq\mathcal{X}
is open, hence an open substack,

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X}_{\leq\gamma})\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
, and

(3)

\ev_{1}\colon\mathcal{S}_{\gamma}\to\mathcal{X}_{\leq\gamma}
is a closed immersion.

For two components
\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}
of
\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d})
, we say
\gamma^{\prime}>\gamma
if

\nu(\gamma^{\prime})>\nu(\gamma)
, where
\nu\colon\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
is the locally constant function
that assigns a weighted filtration
f=(E_{\bullet},w_{\bullet})
with associated graded pieces
G_{i}:=E_{i}/E_{i+1}
the value

(3.12)

\nu(f)=\sum_{i}\operatorname{rk}(G_{i})\cdot\left(-w_{i}\left(\mu(G_{i})-\mu(E)\right)+\frac{1}{2}w_{i}^{2}\right).

This preordering restricted to
\mathcal{S}
defines a
\Theta
-stratification. Remarkably, the function

\nu
also identifies the open substack
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
of Harder-Narasimhan
filtrations: If
M^{\nu}\colon|\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}|\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

(3.13)

M^{\nu}(x)=\inf\{\nu(f)|f\in\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d})\text{ s.t. }f(1)\cong x\},

then
f\in|\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d})|
lies in
\mathcal{S}
if and only if
\nu(f)=M^{\nu}(f(1))
.

An important application of
\Theta
-stratifications is to study wall-crossing behavior: as one varies a line bundle or numerical invariant, the semistable locus
\mathcal{X}^{\operatorname{ss}}
typically undergoes discrete changes at “walls” in the space parameterizing these stability conditions. If
\mathcal{X}^{\operatorname{ss}}
is the open piece of a
\Theta
-stratification of
\mathcal{X}
, then one can often say which strata remain unstable and which strata pick up semistable points as one crosses a wall. A particularly clean version of this is developed in
[
56
, §5.6]
for a stack
\mathcal{X}
that admits a quadratic norm on graded points and a good moduli space
\mathcal{X}\to X
. Any class in
\rm{NS}(\mathcal{X})_{\mathbb{Q}}/\rm{NS}(X)_{\mathbb{Q}}
defines a
\Theta
-stratification of
\mathcal{X}
whose semistable locus admits a good moduli space that is projective over
X
, and the unstable strata precisely encode where the semistable locus can jump — this “variation of good moduli space” is an intrinsic version of the classical theory of variation of GIT quotient
[
37
]
.

One kind of result along these lines is to use the semiorthogonal decompositions of
\rm{D}^{-}_{\rm{coh}}(\mathcal{X})
associated to a
\Theta
-stratification
[
55
]
to study how the derived category of the semistable locus
\mathcal{X}^{\rm ss}
varies under wall-crossing. The structure theory for
\rm{D}^{-}_{\rm{coh}}(\mathcal{X})
also leads to
K
-theoretic wall-crossing formulas
[
57
, §4.1]
. In a different direction, this variation of good moduli space picture has been used to formulate an “intrinsic” version of DT theory, which applies to more general moduli problems than traditional DT theory, and to prove wall-crossing formulas in that setting
[
25
,
26
]
.

Another use for
\Theta
-stratifications in moduli theory is the following:

Theorem 3.14

(Semistable reduction
[
10
, Thm. 6.5]
)
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
is a
\Theta
-stratification such that
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})
is
well-ordered. Then for any DVR

,
there is a finite extension

and a morphism
\xi^{\prime}\colon\operatorname{Spec}R^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}
such that

is a lift of the generic point of

In particular, if

satisfies the existence part of the valuative criterion for properness, then

There is a list of necessary and sufficient conditions for an open substack
\mathcal{S}\subset\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
to define a
\Theta
-stratification
[
56
, Thm. 2.2.2]
. On the other hand, the theory of
numerical invariants, which we describe next, allows one to define a
\Theta
-stratification without
first knowing what the HN filtrations should be. Our definition here is simpler than the one in

Definition 3.15
.

A

on a stack
\mathcal{X}
is a locally constant function
\nu\colon\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})\to\mathbb{R}
.

Given a numerical invariant

\mathcal{X}

M^{\nu}\colon|\mathcal{X}|\to\mathbb{R}
as in
(
3.13
). We say
that
\nu

defines a

-stratification
if the set

\mathcal{S}:=\{f\in|\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})|\mid\nu(f)=M^{\nu}(f(1))\}

is open in
\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
and is a
\Theta
-stratification after preordering
\pi_{0}(\mathcal{S})
by
the values of
\nu
. One can canonically regard
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\Filt_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathcal{X})
as the open and
closed substack parameterizing filtrations

such that
\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{X}}(f(0))
is trivial, and
we refer to

as the

. Observe that

if and only if
\nu(f)\geq 0
for all filtrations

with
f(1)\cong x
.

Let us consider the optimization problem
M^{\nu}(x)
on
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}

of vector bundles, (

be regarded as a function on the rational polyhedral cone

\sigma_{E_{\bullet}}^{\circ}=\{w_{1}\leq w_{2}\leq\cdots\leq w_{p}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}_{E_{\bullet}}.

The points where all

\sigma_{E_{\bullet}}
can be identified with

\sigma_{\widetilde{E}_{\bullet}}

is the filtration obtained from

by deleting the step

in the filtration if

.

In general, the optimization problem for identifying HN filtrations has this same form:

\mathbb{Q}
-weighted filtrations are the rational points of a space, called the
degeneration space of

, obtained by gluing rational polyhedral cones, and the problem is to find a unique
minimizer of the function
\nu
on this space.
There are two key properties for a numerical invariant

on an algebraic stack
\mathcal{X}

•

\nu
is a strictly convex function on each of these cones

•

condition (R):

\nu
achieves a minimum at a rational point on each of these cones

Both of these conditions are satisfied for the numerical invariant
(
3.12
) on
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}

and a rational quadratic norm on graded points on
\mathcal{X}
. See
[
56

and
[
54
]
.

Theorem 3.16
.

[
56
, Thm. 4.5.1]

If
\mathcal{X}
is an algebraic stack locally of finite type and with affine automorphism groups and

is a convex numerical invariant on

defines a

-stratification of

if and only if the following two conditions hold:

(1)

for any bounded open substack
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
, there is a

\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{X}
such that for any destabilizing filtration

of

of

and

(2)

If

-equivariant quasi-finite morphism

One can use the infinite dimensional GIT technique, which we previously used to show that

\Theta

The upshot of this whole discussion is that for a convex monotone numerical invariant satisfying
condition (R), all one must do is analyze two kinds of boundedness questions: HN-boundedness implies

After this argument for the existence of a maximizer of

Typically each step of the HN filtration of a vector bundle is constructed one-by-one, using the original definition of HN filtrations.

Example

Remark 3.19
.

The original formulation of the theory of
\Theta
-stability defined a numerical invariant as a
scale-invariant function on filtrations, which leads to HN filtrations being defined only up to
positive scale. This also requires one to treat the semistable locus separately, as opposed to
here where it is just a stratum

that happens to parameterize trivial

author and Andrés Ibáñez-Núñez.

4.
One parameter degenerations in algebraic stacks

In order to study the HN problem, i.e., minimizing a numerical invariant over all filtrations of a point in
\mathcal{X}
,
[
56
, §3]
introduces the
degeneration space

\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},x)
associated to a point
x\in\mathcal{X}(\Bbbk)
for some field
\Bbbk
. It is constructed as a union of simplices glued together along rational linear maps, and its rational points parameterize filtrations of
x
up to positive scaling, i.e., precomposition with a ramified covering
(-)^{n}:\Theta\to\Theta
. There is another (typically much smaller) space called the
component space

\mathscr{C}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{omp}(\mathcal{X})
and a continuous map
\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},x)\to\mathscr{C}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{omp}(\mathcal{X})
for any
\xi
. A numerical invariant
\nu
induces a continuous function on
\mathscr{C}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{omp}(\mathcal{X})
, and the HN problem is to minimize the pullback of this function to
\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},x)
. The existence of HN filtrations translates into boundedness properties of
\mathscr{C}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{omp}(\mathcal{X})
, and the uniqueness of HN filtrations translates into certain convexity properties
\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},x)
and the function induced by
\nu
.

This section describes work in progress by the second author to generalize these objects. More precise statements will appear with the completed work. We include a sketch here, because it provides a more conceptual understanding of
\Theta
- and
S
-completeness, which has been informing our understanding since 2018. Throughout this section,
R
will denote a complete DVR with field of fractions
K
and maximal ideal
(\pi)
.

We will introduce the
affine degeneration space
, which is a metric space
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
associated to a point
\xi\in\mathcal{X}(K)
. Its “rational points” parameterize all possible extensions of
\xi|_{K[\pi^{1/m}]}
to
\mathcal{X}(R[\pi^{1/m}])
for some
m>0
. Algebro-geometric properties of
\mathcal{X}
translate into metric properties of
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
, and metric geometry can then be used to deduce interesting algebro-geometric facts.

4.1.
The construction

We regard the following quotient stack as an algebraic model for the standard
n
-simplex
\Delta^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
:

\ST_{R}^{n}:=\operatorname{Spec}\big(R[t_{0}^{r},\ldots,t_{n}^{r},\forall r\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}]/(t_{0}\cdots t_{n}-\pi)\big)/\mathbb{G}_{n}

where
\mathbb{G}_{n}
is the affine group scheme which is Cartier dual to the abelian group
W_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{n+1}
consisting of tuples such that
r_{0}+\cdots+r_{n}=0
. Concretely,
\mathbb{G}_{n}=\operatorname{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}[z^{r},\forall r\in W_{n}])
with the comultiplication ring homomorphism defined by
z^{r}\mapsto z^{r}\otimes z^{r}
.

A morphism
\ST_{R}^{0}\to\mathcal{X}
corresponds to a map
\operatorname{Spec}(R[\pi^{1/m}])\to\mathcal{X}
for some
m
. In addition, any rational linear map
\phi:\Delta^{m}\to\Delta^{n}
, defined by a matrix with rational coefficients
\phi_{i,j}\geq 0
such that
\phi_{i,0}+\cdots+\phi_{i,n}=1,\forall i=0,\ldots,m
, induces a canonical map
\ST_{R}^{m}\to\ST_{R}^{n}
given in coordinates by
t^{r}_{j}\mapsto t_{0}^{r\phi_{0,j}}\cdots t_{m}^{r\phi_{m,j}}
. So we think of a morphism
\ST_{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{X}
as giving a family of maps from (totally ramified covers of)
\operatorname{Spec}R
parameterized by rational points of
\Delta^{n}
.

The open substack where
t_{0}\cdots t_{n}\neq 0
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Spec}K^{\prime}
, where
K^{\prime}=K[\pi^{1/m},\forall m>0]
, and we call this generic point
\eta
. Given a stack
\mathcal{X}
with a point
\xi\in\mathcal{X}(K)
, we define

\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{X},\xi)_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\sigma:\ST_{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{X}\text{ with}\\
\text{isomorphism }\sigma(\eta)\simeq\xi|_{K^{\prime}}\end{array}\right\}.

The
affine degeneration space,
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
is the topological space obtained by gluing together copies of the standard simplices
\Delta^{n}
along rational linear maps, with one copy of
\Delta^{n}
for each
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{X},\xi)_{n}
, in analogy with the geometric realization of a simplicial set. The combinatorial framework for studying
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{X},\xi)_{\bullet}
is a slight modification of the framework of formal fans developed to define the degeneration space
\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},x)
in
[
56
, §3]
.

Example 4.1
.

When
\mathcal{G}
is a reductive group scheme over
R
(or more generally a Bruhat-Tits group scheme), we expect
\ADeg(B\mathcal{G},\xi)
to be naturally homeomorphic to the Bruhat-Tits building of
\mathcal{G}_{K}
. Furthermore, when
\mathcal{X}=\operatorname{Spec}A/\mathcal{G}
for some affine
\mathcal{G}
-scheme over
R
, we expect
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
to be a closed locally convex subset of this building.

A certain datum on
\mathcal{X}
, called a
quadratic norm
on graded points
[
56
, Def. 4.1.12]
, should canonically define a metric on
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
. The proposed dictionary between algebro-geometric properties of
\mathcal{X}
and metric properties of
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
is the following:

•

The map
\Delta^{n}\to\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
corresponding to a
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{X},\xi)_{n}
is totally geodesic, for the usual flat metric on
\Delta^{n}
.

•

S
-completeness of
\mathcal{X}
means that any two points of
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
are connected by a unique geodesic line segment.

•

A morphism
\gamma:\Theta_{R}\to\mathcal{X}
with an isomorphism
\gamma(1_{K})\cong\xi
corresponds to a geodesic ray in
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
.

•

If
\mathcal{X}
is
S
-complete,
\Theta
-complete, and quasi-compact with affine diagonal, we expect
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
to be a Hadamard space, i.e., a complete CAT(0) space.

•

The ideal boundary (see
[
23
, II.8]
for definition) of
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
is naturally homeomorphic to the degeneration space
\mathscr{D}\hskip-1.13791pt\mathit{eg}(\mathcal{X},\xi)
, so its rational points correspond to filtrations
f:\Theta_{K}\to\mathcal{X}
with an isomorphism
f(1)\cong\xi
, up to positive scaling.

•

\Theta
-completeness means that any point of
\ADeg(\mathcal{X},\xi)
is connected to any boundary point by a unique geodesic ray.

4.2.
Application to equivariant degenerations

One of the surprisingly subtle steps in establishing

The proof in

conceals the conceptual understanding that underlies it, which is based on the idea that

is a Hadamard space. In fact, if the picture outlined above holds, it implies the following stronger result:

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{X}
is a
\Theta
- and

-complete stack of finite type over a noetherian base

5

5

If the base is not characteristic

[
10
]
.
and
\xi\in\mathcal{X}(R)

\Gamma\subseteq\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathcal{X}}(\xi_{K})
, there is an element
\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}(R[\pi^{1/m}])
for some

and an isomorphism

The proof outline is the following:

acts naturally by isometries on

corresponds to a rational fixed point for this

Example 4.3
.

If

is a

Relationship with analytic geometry

\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle L^{\rm Ch}_{k}

5.2.
An intrinsic approach

One non-GIT approach to
5.3
is to use heuristics to define a moduli stack

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
of curves and prove that it admits a good moduli space

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)\to\overline{M}_{g}(\alpha)
, where
\overline{M}_{g}(\alpha)
is the projective log
canonical model defined in (
5.2
). Several heuristics can guide the search
for
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
:

•

Openness:

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
should be an
open
substack of the
stack of all curves.
8
8

8

It turns out that the stack of all curves has many open substacks
with proper coarse or good moduli spaces, e.g., see
[
99
]
. This is very
much in contrast to the stack
\mathcal{C}\mathit{oh}_{r,d}(C)
of coherent
sheaves on a curve, where the only open substack with a proper good moduli space
is
\mathcal{B}\mathit{un}_{r,d}^{\operatorname{ss}}

[
63
]
.

•

Semistable reduction
:
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
should satisfy the existence part of the
valuative criterion for properness.

•

Character theory:
Since the line bundle
K+\alpha\delta
should descend to an ample
line bundle on
\overline{M}_{g}(\alpha)
, the action of
\operatorname{Aut}(C)
on the fiber
(K+\alpha\delta)|_{[C]}
should be trivial for each curve
[C]\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
. Therefore, for
particular singular curves
C
with a
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-action, one can determine precisely which values of

\alpha
for which
C
can appear in
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
.

•

Intersection theory:
For special curves
T\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
, one can compute
precisely the value of
\alpha
for which
(K+\alpha\delta)\cdot T=0
. At this critical
value,
T
must be contracted.

•

Inductive structure:
For each critical value
\alpha_{c}
, there should be open
substacks

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c}+\epsilon)\hookrightarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c})\hookleftarrow\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c}-\epsilon)
.

See
[
4
]
for predictions of
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
using these heuristics.

Given a good candidate for
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c})
at a critical value
\alpha_{c}
, the next step is
to construct étale VGIT (variation of GIT) presentations. Specifically, for each closed
point
[C]\in\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c})
, one constructs an étale neighborhood

of
[C]
, where

is affine and the preimages of

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c}\pm\epsilon)
are identified with the semistable locus of
[U/\operatorname{Aut}(C)]
with
respect to the character
\pm(K+\alpha_{c}\delta)|_{[C]}
. The existence of a good moduli space for

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c})
then follows inductively from the existence of good moduli spaces of

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c}+\epsilon)
and good moduli spaces of
both
of the closed substacks

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c})\smallsetminus\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha_{c}\pm\epsilon)

[

Establishing projectivity is (as usual) the most intricate step, requiring an analysis of the

This approach was developed in

replacing genus 2 Weierstrass tails (i.e., curves with a genus

subcurve meeting the rest

where

Problem 5.6

Work out the complete Shokuruv decomposition of

5.3.
Beyond GIT for prestable curves

We discuss the stack
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\text{all}}
of all curves in §

and the
challenges of applying the Beyond GIT framework in §

moment, we will explore
\Theta

of prestable curves, i.e. connected nodal curves.

The stack
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}
, is rather poorly behaved. It is not a global
quotient stack, does not have quasi-affine diagonal, and does not contain a bounded
open substack with a good moduli space other than
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
; see
[
11

Let
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}
denote the open substack of
semistable curves, i.e., connected nodal curves without nodally attached smooth rational tails. There are open immersions

\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}},

where the complement of the first immersion is codimension
2
and the second complement
is a divisor
\delta^{\operatorname{pre}}:=\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}\setminus\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}
.
Let
\text{st}\colon\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}
denote the stabilization morphism.
In connection to
6.6
(
3
), it is
not hard to see that the composition
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}\to\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}\to\overline{M}_{g}

is a categorical moduli space.

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
A\in\operatorname{Pic}(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g})
.

(1)

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}
and
\mathcal{L}=\text{st}^{*}(A)
, then
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{L}-\operatorname{ss}}=\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}
and
\mathcal{L}=\text{st}^{*}(A)+\beta\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\operatorname{pre}})
, then

\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{L}-\operatorname{ss}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{ss}}&\text{if $\beta>0$}\\
\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\operatorname{pre}}&\text{if $\beta=0$}\\
\emptyset&\text{if $\beta<0$.}\end{array}\right.

In particular, the semistable loci are open but do not admit good moduli spaces.

6.
Open problems

Moduli theory has always been half science and half art. The science deals with tools for solving classification problems in algebraic geometry, the earliest of which are geometric invariant theory and the Keel-Mori theorem for constructing moduli spaces. The artform of moduli theory involves discovering and deciding upon the ideal moduli problems to study. The tools typically guide this artform, for instance in deciding which stability condition to use or which “singular” objects to parameterize in order to obtain a proper moduli space.

The recent developments in the theory of algebraic stacks and the beyond GIT framework vastly generalize the previous generation of tools, and they provide
\Theta
-stratifications in addition to moduli spaces. Our hope is that these tools will enable significant advancements in the art of moduli theory. In this section, we survey several topics that are ripe for artistic attention.

6.2.

Let

be the stack of all proper

There is very little literature about

Conjecture 6.6

For each

6.3.
Beyond GIT for singular curves

In an effort to apply the Beyond GIT machinery to singular curves, we first need to settle
the delicate question of which moduli stack of curves to consider.
6.6
suggests that the stack of all curves

\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\text{all}}
does not even have any line bundles with which to define semistability. So instead, we consider the stack

\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\rm cp}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{g}^{\text{all}}

ample, or its substack

The stack

as linear combinations of

and

consisting of a curve

\lambda_{k}
.

Restricting our attention to
\mathcal{M}:=\mathcal{M}_{g}^{{\rm cp,red}}
and

\Theta
-semistable locus

\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}-\operatorname{ss}}

.
The hope is that
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}-\operatorname{ss}}
provides an answer to

\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}-\operatorname{ss}}

and admits a projective good moduli

with the log canonical model
\overline{M}_{g}(\alpha)

When

,
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
is a rational multiple of the CM line bundle

Results in K-stability imply that

. Moreover,
for any

[

imply that every

-semistable. The converse may be true but at the moment we can
only destabilize non-stable curves in the larger stack

containing non-reduced curves.
Interestingly, Xiao’s slope inequality

implies that smooth curves are

\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
-semistable for

While it is expected that these stacks

Therefore, the first approach outlined in §

Problem 6.8
.

For the CM line bundle

and a suitable norm on test configurations, does the stack

admit a

While this problem may not have a clean answer, there is a recent striking result
providing some evidence that a

-stratification could exist:
for a unibranch monomial curve

6.4.
Semistability for higher dimensional varieties

A crowning achievement of several innovative advances in the MMP and singularity theory over the last decade has been the construction of projective

, parameterizing

6.6.
Projectivity

Besides the Nakai–Moishezon criterion, the only general tool we are aware of to establish projectivity of a good moduli space is Kollár’s Ampleness Criterion
[
72
]
, which is an adaptation of an earlier criterion due to Viehweg
[
106
]
. Kollár’s Ampleness Criterion has been applied, for instance, to prove both the projectivity of the KSBA moduli space and the K-moduli space. However, this criterion doesn’t apply to many other moduli spaces, e.g., Bridgeland semistable objects in a triangulated dg-category, resulting in a frustrating gap in the intrinsic approach to moduli theory.

Faltings introduced an approach in
[
40
]
to show ampleness of determinantal line bundles by explicitly constructing global sections, which Faltings applied to semistable vector bundles (and Higgs bundles) on a smooth curve. This was recently extended to reconstruct the Uhlenbeck compactification, parameterizing slope semistable bundles on a smooth surface, as a projective variety by reinterpreting it as the Bridgeland moduli space on the vertical wall
[
100
]
. However,
it appears difficult to extend this approach to other Bridgeland moduli spaces.

Intersection-theoretic techniques can also be used to prove projectivity of moduli spaces, such as
Cornalba and Harris’s approach to prove that
\overline{M}_{g}
is projective
[
33
,
32
]
. This approach was extended in
[
6
]
to show the
projectivity of the good moduli spaces of the moduli stacks
\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g}(\alpha)
of singular curves
discussed in §
5.2
, which in addition leveraged
the finite generation results of
[
20
]
to verify semiampleness, but this method
unfortunately only seems to apply to a space that is a birational model of a variety already known to be projective.

The degeneration space

If
\mathcal{X}

or the cohomology of

An analogous question applies to the affine degeneration space and its underlying combinatorial object. Another application of the affine degeneration space would be to studying the following:

If

6.8.
Local structure theorems in positive characteristic

While the Local Structure Theorems hold over any characteristic, their
applicability is very restrictive in positive characteristic due to the

linearly reductive
hypothesis: every smooth linearly reductive group in
characteristic
p
is an extension of a finite group whose order is prime to

p
by a torus. For instance,
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
is
not
a linearly reductive group
scheme in positive characteristic. The class of
reductive
groups, on the
other hand, is much broader including classical groups such as
\operatorname{GL}_{n}
. In particular,
the Local Structure Theorems are unknown for moduli stacks of sheaves
or complexes in positive characteristic.

While it is too optimistic to expect the existence of an étale neighborhood which is
a quotient
\operatorname{Spec}A/G_{x}
by the stabilizer, it is reasonable to hope that Local Structure Theorem II (
Theorem

2.2

It is not even known for a quotient stack

of a quasi-projective variety
