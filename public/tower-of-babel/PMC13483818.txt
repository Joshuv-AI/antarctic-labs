Genome-wide discovery, characterization, and validation of novel SSR markers in clove (Syzygium aromaticum) and their application in genetic analysis
Authors: Muhammed Azharudheen TP, Muhammed Nissar VA, Anees K, Lijo Thomas, Rabisha VP, Jayarajan K, T E Sheeja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenetic improvement of clove (Syzygium aromaticum), an economically important spice and medicinal crop, has been constrained by limited molecular resources. The recent availability of a reference genome enabled a chromosome-scale genome-wide characterization and development of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) in clove. An in silico survey of the 367.77 Mb genome assembly identified 189,127 SSRs at an overall frequency of 514.25 loci/Mb. Mononucleotides (50.30%) and dinucleotides (40.68%) were the most abundant repeat types, exhibiting a strong A/T-rich bias and a notable scarcity of CG/GC motifs. Although SSRs were uniformly distributed at the whole-chromosome level (R² = 0.99 relative to chromosome length), high-resolution analysis revealed significant intra-chromosomal heterogeneity, including distinct hotspots (> 600 loci/Mb) and coldspots (< 400 loci/Mb). A total of 11,107 hypervariable Class I loci were identified, predominantly comprising dinucleotide repeats (92.3%), highlighting them as major contributors to SSR variability. From these loci, 28,147 high-quality primer pairs targeting Class I and II SSRs were developed and curated into a publicly accessible, searchable Clove SSR Marker Database. Experimental validation of 55 primer pairs across 19 germplasm accessions demonstrated high amplification success (96.4%) and polymorphism rates (90.6%). The validated markers revealed moderate genetic diversity (mean He = 0.22; PIC = 0.18), consistent with a relatively narrow genetic base in the analyzed germplasm. Phylogenetic analysis resolved the 19 accessions into distinct clades and identified a highly divergent accession with red-coloured flower buds (Acc. 9833). The practical utility of the validated markers was further demonstrated through hybrid authentication and confirmation of co-dominant Mendelian segregation in an F₂ population. Collectively, this validated SSR marker resource and database provide a valuable genomic resource for clove genetic studies, germplasm management, and future genetic mapping efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09078-5.Keywords: Clove, Genetic Diversity, Hybrid Identification, Marker Development, Microsatellite, SSR, Syzygium aromaticum

Background

Clove (Syzygium aromaticum (L.) Merr. & L.M. Perry), a member of the Myrtaceae family, is one of the world’s most valuable and historically significant spice crops [1]. Native to the Maluku Islands of Indonesia [2], its cultivation is now pantropical [3]. The dried, unopened flower buds are prized for their intense aroma, driven by the compound eugenol, making them a staple in culinary, fragrance, and medicinal applications [4, 5]. Beyond traditional uses, clove derivatives are gaining prominence in pharmaceutical industries for their antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anaesthetic properties [4]. This significant global demand necessitates continuous genetic improvement programs focused on enhancing yield, oil quality, and stress resistance.

However, systematic breeding of clove is inherently slow and challenging. As a perennial tree crop, it possesses a long juvenile period, which significantly delays the evaluation and selection of superior genotypes for desirable traits. These biological constraints are compounded by a critical lack of efficient molecular tools. Historically, germplasm characterization has relied on morpho-agronomic traits [6–12], which are often time-consuming to record and highly susceptible to environmental influence, thereby masking true genetic potential. Early molecular studies were restricted by a reliance on low-throughput, dominant markers like RAPD [13–15] or low-resolution phylogenetic studies using ITS sequences [16]. While these methods provided initial insights, they offered limited genomic coverage and lacked the reproducibility required for modern breeding. A robust, co-dominant, and reproducible marker system is urgently needed to facilitate accurate germplasm characterization and accelerate breeding cycles.

The need for genetic improvement is particularly acute in India, a major consumer of clove. Domestic production, estimated at 1,183 tonnes from 2,179 hectares, is insufficient to meet internal demand from household consumption and the extraction industry [17]. Consequently, India imported cloves and related products valued at 160 million USD in 2021–22, accounting for approximately 15% of total spice import value [18]. This underscores the economic imperative for developing high-yielding domestic varieties. However, breeding efforts are constrained by the limited genetic diversity available within the country’s germplasm. As an introduced crop, Indian clove plantations likely originated from a small number of initial introductions, resulting in a founder effect and a narrow genetic base. Earlier studies using random marker systems reported limited genetic differentiation among Indian accessions [13, 14], but these approaches lacked the resolution to assess genome-wide diversity patterns. A genome-wide molecular marker resource can facilitate the identification of genetically divergent parents, support early-stage hybrid verification, and accelerate breeding cycles, thereby contributing to the development of improved varieties and enhancing national self-sufficiency.

Among the various classes of molecular markers, Simple Sequence Repeats (SSRs), also known as microsatellites, are frequently the markers of choice for genetic studies and breeding applications [19–23]. SSRs are short, tandemly repeated DNA sequences ubiquitous in eukaryotic genomes [24]. Their high mutation rate, primarily via replication slippage, results in extensive length polymorphism, making them powerful genetic markers [25]. Their co-dominant inheritance, multi-allelic nature, and high reproducibility have established them as a gold standard for genetic diversity analysis, phylogenetic reconstruction, and hybrid identification [26]. Recent advances have further broadened the application of SSR markers into genome-wide association studies (GWAS), population structure analysis, and high-resolution trait–marker association mapping. SSR-based approaches, including gene-derived and candidate-region markers, have successfully dissected complex quantitative traits such as salinity tolerance, seed vigour, germination behaviour, yield components, and grain quality [27–33]. These findings highlight the continued relevance of SSR markers in identifying functional genomic regions and supporting genomics-assisted breeding, particularly in breeding systems where high-density SNP platforms are not yet fully integrated into routine selection pipelines.

While Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) platforms provide high genomic density, SSRs remain a strategic ‘gold standard’ for clove improvement. Unlike SNPs, which often require expensive high-throughput platforms and specialized bioinformatics pipelines, SSRs offer a cost-effective system that can be deployed in standard laboratory settings. Furthermore, given clove’s compact genome (367.77 Mb) and historically narrow genetic base, the high multi-allelic nature of genomic SSRs provides superior resolving power for parentage verification and diversity analysis compared to bi-allelic SNPs.

Genome wide SSR markers have been developed in several crops [34–44]. Until recently, the development of large-scale SSR markers in clove was constrained by the lack of comprehensive genomic resources. The recent release of a high-quality chromosome-scale reference genome assembly for Syzygium aromaticum [45] has transformed this scenario by enabling systematic, genome-wide mining of SSR loci. In silico identification of SSRs from a complete genome is considerably faster and more cost-effective than traditional library-based approaches and permits the development of a well-distributed, high-density marker resource. The present study is therefore timely, as it converts newly available genomic information into a practical molecular toolkit for marker-assisted selection (MAS), facilitating early-stage seedling screening in a perennial tree crop characterized by a prolonged juvenile phase.

Therefore, the objectives of this study were to: (i) perform a comprehensive, genome-wide analysis of the frequency, composition, and distribution of SSRs in the clove genome; (ii) identify and characterize hypervariable Class I and Class II SSRs as prime candidates for marker development; (iii) design a large-scale set of high-quality SSR primer pairs and create a publicly accessible, searchable database; (iv) experimentally validate a subset of these markers for amplification success and polymorphism in a panel of clove germplasm; and (v) demonstrate the practical utility of these validated markers in genetic diversity analysis, hybrid identification, and Mendelian segregation studies.

Results

Genome-wide frequency and distribution of SSRs

The in silico analysis of the 367.77 Mb clove genome assembly identified a total of 189,127 SSRs. These SSRs covered a total length of 2,876,474 bp, occupying approximately 0.78% of the entire genome (Table 1). The overall frequency of SSRs was 514.25 loci per megabase (loci/Mb), with an average density of 7821.37 bp/Mb. The 723 distinct SSR motifs identified were classified into six types based on their repeat unit length. An inverse relationship was observed between motif length and frequency: mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant (95,126; 50.30%), followed by dinucleotide (76,941; 40.68%) and trinucleotide repeats (14,777; 7.81%). Longer motifs were significantly rarer, with tetranucleotide (1,465; 0.77%), pentanucleotide (472; 0.25%), and hexanucleotide (346; 0.18%) repeats collectively comprising less than 1.5% of the total SSRs.

Detailed motif composition

A strong compositional bias was evident within each SSR type (Table 1).

Mononucleotides: The repeats were overwhelmingly A/T-rich. The A (44,788) and T (44,537) motifs together accounted for 93.9% of all mononucleotide SSRs, whereas C (2,913) and G (2,888) repeats were scarce.

Dinucleotides: A similar bias was observed. The GA (16,145), TC (15,468), CT (14,082), and AG (13,596) motifs were the most dominant. Conversely, CG (399) and GC (460) motifs were extremely underrepresented, accounting for less than 1.1% of all dinucleotide repeats.

Chromosomal distribution and density

The 189,127 SSRs were mapped across all 11 clove chromosomes (Table 2). A strong positive linear correlation (R² = 0.99, p < 0.00001) was observed between the physical length (Mb) of each chromosome and the total number of SSRs it contained. Consequently, the longest chromosome (Chr 8; 43.76 Mb) harboured the most SSRs (22,698), while the shortest (Chr 9; 23.38 Mb) had the fewest (12,006). In sharp contrast to the variable SSR counts, the SSR abundance (loci/Mb) was remarkably uniform when averaged at the whole-chromosome level. The relative SSR abundance varied only narrowly from 493.77 loci/Mb (Chr 5) to 532.35 loci/Mb (Chr 10), with a genome-wide average of 514.25 loci/Mb. A similar consistency was observed for SSR density (mean: 7821.37 bp/Mb; range: 7454.95 to 8094.61 bp/Mb). This indicates that, at a macroscopic scale, SSRs are distributed relatively evenly across the genomic landscape. The hierarchical frequency of motif types (Mono > Di > Tri > Tetra > Penta > Hexa) was also consistent across all chromosomes, with a minor exception on Chromosome 10, where hexanucleotides (30) slightly outnumbered pentanucleotides (26).

Intra-chromosomal SSR distribution: hotspots and coldspots

To investigate fine-scale distribution patterns, SSR abundance was calculated in non-overlapping 1 Mb sliding windows across the genome (Fig. 1). This high-resolution analysis revealed significant intra-chromosomal heterogeneity, starkly contrasting with the uniformity observed at the whole-chromosome level. SSR abundance in these 1 Mb segments fluctuated widely, from a minimum of 353 loci/Mb (Chr 9, 8–9 Mb) to a maximum of 681 loci/Mb (Chr 8, 17–18 Mb).This approximate 1.9-fold variation revealed distinct SSR “hotspots” (> 600 loci/Mb) and “coldspots” (< 400 loci/Mb) on all chromosomes. Prominent hotspots included regions on Chr 8 (681 loci/Mb), Chr 7 (659 loci/Mb), and Chr 3 (645 loci/Mb). The magnitude of this local variation was substantial. For example, the range of SSR abundance within Chromosome 8 alone (380 to 681 loci/Mb; a range of 301 loci/Mb) was over 7.8 times greater than the entire range of variation between the 11 chromosome-level averages (38.58 SSR loci/Mb). This demonstrates that while SSRs are evenly distributed on average, they are clustered into distinct hotspots and coldspots at a finer genomic scale.

To further investigate factors influencing SSR distribution, the relationship between GC content and SSR abundance was assessed across non-overlapping 1 Mb genomic intervals (Supplementary Table S1). At the whole-genome level, a weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed between GC content and SSR abundance (r = 0.203, p < 0.001; Supplementary Figure S1), indicating a modest association between base composition and SSR distribution. At the chromosomal level, the strength of this relationship varied, with significant positive correlations observed in chromosomes 1 (r = 0.517, p = 0.004), 3 (r = 0.425, p = 0.009), and 7 (r = 0.374, p = 0.029), whereas other chromosomes showed weak or non-significant associations.

Classification and distribution of hypervariable SSRs

To identify loci with the highest potential for polymorphism, all 189,127 SSRs were categorized based on their repeat tract length into Class I (hypervariable, typically > 30 nt), Class II (potentially variable, 20–30 nt), and Class III (least variable, typically < 20 nt) (Table 3). This analysis revealed that the majority of SSRs (157,740; 83.4%) belonged to Class III, representing shorter, less variable loci. However, the genome contains a substantial repository of 11,107 (5.9%) Class I and 20,280 (10.7%) Class II SSRs, which are prime candidates for marker development.

A striking finding emerged from the composition of the 11,107 Class I loci, which differed significantly from the overall genome. Dinucleotide repeats were profoundly dominant, with 10,257 loci accounting for 92.3% of all hypervariable SSRs. This was followed at much lower frequencies by trinucleotide (387 loci; 3.5%), mononucleotide (302 loci; 2.7%), hexanucleotide (73 loci; 0.7%), tetranucleotide (72 loci; 0.6%), and pentanucleotide (16 loci; 0.1%) repeats. This finding pinpoint long dinucleotide repeats as the most abundant source of potential hypervariable markers in the clove genome.

Genome-wide SSR marker development

Based on the objective to develop hypervariable markers, primer pairs were designed targeting the identified Class I and Class II loci. Mononucleotide repeats were excluded from this process due to their limitations in marker applications. A total of 28,147 high-quality primer pairs were successfully designed, targeting 10,542 Class I loci and 17,605 Class II loci (Table 4). This represents a successful design rate of 94.9% for Class I and 86.8% for Class II SSRs, with the remaining loci being unsuitable for primer design due to flanking sequence constraints. The chromosomal location and motif details of these markers are visualized in a circos plot (Fig. 2) and detailed in Supplementary Figure S2.

Validation and polymorphism assessment of SSR markers

For initial validation, 55 synthesized primer pairs representing loci distributed across all 11 chromosomes (Fig. 3) were screened using gradient PCR with annealing temperatures ranging from 48 °C to 62 °C. Fifty-three primer pairs (96.4%) produced clear, reproducible, and specific amplicons, with optimal amplification consistently observed at approximately 55 °C, which was subsequently adopted for all downstream genotyping analyses. The two remaining markers (SaM9311 and SaM10335) were excluded from further analysis due to inconsistent or non-specific amplification patterns. These 53 validated SSR loci were used to genotype a panel of 19 clove germplasm accessions (Fig. 4). A high rate of polymorphism was observed, with 48 of the 53 loci (90.6%) exhibiting polymorphism within the panel. The remaining five loci (Locus 5, 7, 18, 27, and 28) were monomorphic across all tested accessions.

The 48 polymorphic markers generated 118 distinct alleles, with the number of alleles (Na) per locus ranging from 1 to 4, with a mean of 2.23. The effective number of alleles (Ne), which accounts for allele frequency, had a mean of 1.38 and ranged from 1.0 to 2.42. The mean Shannon’s Information Index (I) was 0.37. The mean expected heterozygosity (He), or gene diversity, was 0.22, with values for individual loci ranging from 0.0 (for monomorphic markers) to a high of 0.59 (Locus 54). The observed heterozygosity (Ho), which is the actual proportion of heterozygous individuals in the population, varied dramatically between loci. For instance, loci such as Locus21, Locus26, Locus49, Locus51, and Locus52 had an Ho of 1.0, meaning every individual was heterozygous at these loci. Conversely, many loci, like Locus1 and Locus2, had an Ho of 0, indicating no heterozygotes were observed.

The Polymorphism Information Content (PIC) value, indicating marker utility, ranged from 0 to 0.53, with a mean of 0.18. Based on PIC scores, one marker (Locus54; PIC = 0.53) was classified as highly informative (PIC > 0.5), 15 markers (28.3%) were moderately informative (0.25 < PIC < 0.5), and the remaining 37 markers (69.8%) were of low informativeness (PIC < 0.25). Application of validated markers in genetic analysis

The utility of the newly developed SSR markers was demonstrated in three distinct applications: (i) genetic diversity and phylogenetic analysis, (ii) parentage verification and hybrid identification, and (iii) Mendelian segregation analysis in F2 population.

Genetic diversity and phylogenetic relationships

The allelic diversity data generated from the 48 polymorphic markers was used to construct a dendrogram via hierarchical clustering using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA). The resulting dendrogram (Fig. 5) revealed a clear separation of the 19 accessions into two major clusters, designated Clade I and Clade II. Clade I was monospecific, containing only the accession Acc. 9833. This accession was distinctly separate from all other accessions analyzed, highlighting its significant genetic divergence. Clade II comprised the remaining 18 accessions. This large clade was further resolved into two major sub-clusters: Sub-cluster IIa consisted of seven accessions. Within this sub-cluster, accessions Acc. 9826 and Acc. 9829 grouped together to form a distinct sub-group, separate from the other five accessions in this cluster (Acc. 9831, 9825, 9818, 9815, and 9832). Sub-cluster IIb contained the remaining 11 accessions. A clear grouping was also observed within this sub-cluster, where accessions Acc. 9830, Acc. 9828, and Acc. 9823 clustered together, distinguishing them from the other eight accessions in this group.

Segregation analysis in F2 population

One of the polymorphic co-dominant markers, SaM 3999, was utilized to genotype 137 F2 individuals, generated via self-pollination of the F1 hybrid Clove Hybrid-1. Amplification was successful in 131 of the F2 progenies, which exhibited segregation into three distinct classes: Parental 1 (38 individuals), Parental 2 (32 individuals), and Hybrid (61 individuals). The observed segregation pattern of P1: Hybrid: P2 (38:61:32) was tested against the expected 1:2:1 Mendelian ratio for a single co-dominant locus in an F2 population. A Chi-square goodness-of-fit test confirmed this expectation, yielding Χ² (2, N = 131) = 1.034; P = 0.59, which indicates no significant deviation from the expected Mendelian segregation. This result validates the co-dominant inheritance of the SaM 3999 marker and supports its utility for genetic mapping studies. Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive, genome-wide characterization of SSRs in clove (Syzygium aromaticum), culminating in a validated, and publicly accessible marker resource. The in silico analysis of the 367.77 Mb genome identified 189,127 SSRs, establishing that the clove genome is richly populated with these repeats (514.25 loci/Mb). This frequency is substantial and provides a vast repository for genetic analysis. When contextualized with other perennial crops, this density is comparable to or higher than that in species like grapevine (0.67% of genome) [46], but lower than in others such as pomegranate (1.74% of genome, > 1200 SSR loci/Mb) [47] or Theobroma cacao (> 1200 SSR loci/Mb) [48]. This variability across species highlights the unique evolutionary trajectory and genomic architecture of each crop. We observed a non-significant negative relationship between genome size and SSR abundance (R= -0.157), a trend consistent with observations in some plant families [49] but contrary to findings in others [47], suggesting that the dynamics of SSR accumulation and loss are complex and non-linear.

The genomic landscape of clove SSRs is defined by a clear hierarchical frequency (mononucleotide > dinucleotide > trinucleotide > longer repeats) and a strong compositional bias. A similar hierarchy of motif frequencies has been reported previously [50]. However, contradictory reports of hexanucleotide abundance also been reported in crop plants [47, 51]. The overwhelming dominance of A/T mononucleotide repeats (50.30% of all SSRs) aligns with observations in numerous plant species [52–54] and is often attributed to the lower energetic cost of A-T pairing and their prevalence in A-T-rich intergenic regions [55]. A more striking finding is the pronounced scarcity of CG and GC dinucleotide repeats, which comprised less than 1.1% of all dinucleotides. This is a well-documented phenomenon in vertebrate and plant genomes, broadly understood as a consequence of targeted cytosine methylation at CpG sites, which, upon spontaneous deamination, leads to C-to-T transitions, thereby selecting against CG-rich motifs over evolutionary time [56]. The prevalence of A-rich trinucleotide motifs (e.g., AAG, GAA, AAT) further underscores the A/T-rich nature of the clove genome.

A key insight from this study is the multi-scale nature of SSR distribution. At the macroscopic, whole-chromosome level, we observed a near-perfect positive linear correlation (R2 = 0.99, p < 0.00001) between chromosome physical length and SSR count. This suggests that, at this broad scale, SSRs are distributed proportionally. However, high-resolution analysis using 1 Mb sliding windows revealed a starkly different reality: significant intra-chromosomal heterogeneity, with SSR abundance fluctuating by nearly 1.9-fold. This dichotomy demonstrates that while globally dispersed, SSRs are locally organized into distinct “hotspots” (> 600 loci/Mb) and “coldspots” (< 400 loci/Mb). This non-random clustering indicates that SSR distribution in clove is structured by local genomic architecture rather than being randomly distributed, a pattern previously reported in various organisms [57].

To further examine the role of base composition in shaping SSR distribution, we evaluated the relationship between GC content and SSR abundance across non-overlapping 1 Mb genomic intervals. A weak but statistically significant positive correlation was observed at the whole-genome level, indicating that regions with relatively higher GC content tend to harbour slightly elevated SSR densities. However, this relationship was not consistent across all chromosomes, with only a subset showing moderate associations. These results suggest that while GC content contributes to SSR distribution to some extent, it alone does not fully explain the observed hotspot–coldspot patterns, and additional genomic features such as chromatin organization, recombination dynamics, and repetitive element distribution are likely to play a more dominant role.

In many plant genomes, SSR density is elevated in non-repetitive, gene-rich euchromatic regions, whereas heterochromatic compartments dominated by long terminal repeat (LTR) retrotransposons—such as centromeric and pericentromeric regions—often correspond to SSR coldspots [49, 58]. Morgante et al. [49]. demonstrated that plant SSRs are preferentially associated with non-repetitive DNA, suggesting that selective constraints and chromatin organization strongly influence their localization. Similar patterns have been observed in rice and other model species, where gene-rich regions harbour higher densities of polymorphic SSRs compared to retrotransposon-dense domains [58, 59, 60].

Nevertheless, the relationship between SSRs and repetitive elements is complex and context-dependent. While extensive retrotransposon arrays may suppress SSR accumulation in deeply heterochromatic regions, localized transposon activity can also promote SSR genesis by creating regions of structural instability that favour replication slippage or by introducing repeat motifs during insertion events [25, 52]. Thus, SSR enrichment does not universally coincide with either gene-rich or TE-rich compartments; rather, it reflects the dynamic interplay among genome composition, chromatin state, and evolutionary pressures.

Recombination rate heterogeneity may further contribute to the observed hotspot architecture. Elevated recombination activity has been associated with increased SSR instability due to strand mispairing and DNA repair processes during meiosis, whereas recombination-suppressed regions may exhibit reduced SSR variability [61, 62]. Although recombination maps are not yet available for clove, the strong local variation observed across adjacent 1 Mb windows is consistent with structured chromosomal domains influenced by recombination and chromatin organization. Future integration of SSR distribution with recombination profiling, gene annotation, and repeat landscape analysis will clarify whether clove follows the gene-rich enrichment model described in Arabidopsis and rice [49, 58], or whether species-specific structural features shape its SSR landscape.

A particularly striking finding of this study is the pronounced compositional shift between the genome-wide SSR profile and the hypervariable Class I (> 30 nt) fraction. Although mononucleotide repeats constitute the largest proportion of SSRs in the clove genome (50.30%), they account for only a minor fraction (2.7%) of the Class I loci. In contrast, dinucleotide repeats, which represent 40.68% of total SSRs, overwhelmingly dominate the hypervariable category (92.3%). This disparity indicates that long dinucleotide tracts, rather than mononucleotides, are the primary contributors to hypervariability in clove. Both mononucleotide and dinucleotide repeats are inherently susceptible to replication slippage during DNA synthesis, a process that generates repeat length variation. However, the evolutionary persistence and expansion of repeat tracts appear to differ between these motif classes. Mononucleotide repeats—particularly A/T-rich tracts—are abundant in plant genomes and exhibit high mutation rates, yet they frequently occur as shorter arrays. Their expansion beyond certain lengths may be limited by mismatch repair mechanisms and selective constraints that restrict excessive tract instability. In contrast, dinucleotide repeats not only experience high slippage rates but also appear more likely to accumulate as long, stable repeat arrays, especially in non-coding regions where selective constraints may be comparatively relaxed [25, 52]. This combination of high mutability and greater evolutionary persistence likely underlies their enrichment within the hypervariable Class I pool.

Comparable enrichment of long dinucleotide repeats among highly polymorphic SSR loci has been reported in several woody perennial crops, including Punica granatum and various Citrus species (viz., C. clementina, C. sinensis, and C. maxima), where dinucleotide-based markers consistently represent the most informative class for genetic diversity and mapping studies [43, 63, 64]. These convergent observations suggest that the preferential accumulation of long dinucleotide tracts among hypervariable SSRs may represent a common evolutionary feature of perennial plant genomes. Collectively, this evidence indicates that the dominance of dinucleotide repeats within the Class I fraction reflects intrinsic mutation dynamics coupled with differential selective constraints, rather than simple genome-wide abundance. From a practical standpoint, this finding provides strong empirical justification for prioritizing long dinucleotide repeats in SSR marker development strategies for clove and potentially other woody crops.

The experimental validation of 55 markers confirmed the efficacy of this in silico approach. An excellent wet-lab amplification success rate (96.4%) demonstrated the high quality of the primer designs and the fidelity of the underlying reference genome assembly. Furthermore, a high polymorphism rate (90.6%) within the 19 tested accessions confirmed that the markers selected from Class I and II loci are highly effective in detecting genetic variation.

The genetic diversity parameters observed in the validation panel (mean Na = 2.23, He = 0.22, PIC = 0.18) indicate a moderate level of variability. While these values are lower than those typically reported in SSR genotyping studies in crops [65–67], it is crucial to distinguish the utility of the markers from the genetic diversity of the panel. The high polymorphism rate (90.6%) proves the markers are effective, while the moderate diversity values likely reflect the germplasm itself. This finding is consistent with the hypothesis of a narrow genetic base for clove in India, an introduced crop [6, 68]. Introductions from the Maluku Islands likely involved a limited number of genotypes, leading to a population bottleneck and founder effect that restricts the available gene pool. This interpretation is further strengthened by the noteworthy disparity between the mean expected heterozygosity (He = 0.22) and the lower observed heterozygosity (Ho = 0.13). This significant heterozygote deficit is a classic signal of inbreeding within a population (Wahlund effect) and could be caused by a low effective population size, biparental inbreeding, or a degree of self-pollination, despite clove being a nominal out-crosser. It is highly probable that these markers would reveal greater allelic diversity and higher PIC values if applied to a larger, more geographically diverse collection [10].

The practical utility of this new marker set was unequivocally demonstrated in three distinct applications. First, in phylogenetics, the 48 polymorphic markers successfully resolved the 19 accessions into clear clades. The most striking feature of this analysis was the profound genetic isolation of accession ‘Acc. 9833’ (Clade I), which positioned it as a distinct outgroup. Notably, this accession exhibited unique alleles at 44 of the 53 amplified loci. Field evaluation for three seasons has shown that Acc. 9833 (IC536787) has unique red coloured flower buds compared to other clove accessions (Supplementary Fig. S8). This accession may represent a separate introduction event from a different source population or a product of unique selection pressures. It is, therefore, a highly valuable genetic resource; its distinct allelic profile indicates potential for high specific combining ability, which could be exploited in breeding programs to maximize heterosis and broaden the narrow genetic base of cultivated clove in India. The structuring of Clade II, which contains the remaining 18 accessions, into sub-clusters (IIa and IIb) provides actionable information on relatedness. This information can guide future breeding initiatives by prioritizing crosses between genetically distant parents (e.g., ‘Acc. 9833’ and a member of Clade II) to maximize genetic gain and heterosis.

Second, the markers proved effective in direct breeding applications. They successfully verified the F1 hybrid status of high-yielding plants and, in an open-pollinated population, precisely classified progeny into F1 hybrids, self-pollinated individuals, and even identified seed contaminants. This highlights their immediate value for parentage verification and ensuring the genetic integrity of breeding nurseries.

Beyond routine identification, the analysis of the OP progeny provided a significant biological insight. The identification of 30 hybrids out of 45 OP seedlings reveals a high degree of outcrossing. This finding stands in stark contradiction to earlier reports classifying clove as a predominantly self-pollinated species [14, 68]. Our data suggests that clove is highly cross-pollinated, a revision of the species’ reproductive biology that has profound implications for future breeding strategies and planting material production.

Finally, the application of the co-dominant marker ‘SaM 3999’ in the F2 population provided a rigorous validation of both the marker system and the breeding population itself. The segregation pattern adhered strictly to the expected 1:2:1 Mendelian ratio (Χ² (2, N = 131) = 1.034; P = 0.59). While this result confirms the co-dominant inheritance of the marker—validating its suitability for future linkage mapping and Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) analysis [48]—it carries a broader significance for perennial crop research. In tree spices like clove, which are characterized by long juvenile phases, the development of a segregating F2 generation is distinctively challenging and time-consuming. The observation of perfect Mendelian segregation serves as a biological validation that the generated population is indeed a true F2 progeny resulting from the successful selfing of the F1 hybrid, rather than an accidental outcross or apomictic event. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first molecular confirmations of a generated F2 population in clove, establishing it as a verified resource for dissecting complex traits in this economically important crop.

While the present study provides robust genome-wide SSR marker development and initial validation, certain limitations should be acknowledged. SSR abundance and density are highly dependent on the search criteria employed. Our choice of parameters (mononucleotides ≥ 10 nt; other motif types ≥ 5 nt) followed standard protocols used in major plant genome studies to ensure comparability and biological relevance. However, we acknowledge that lower thresholds (e.g., ≥ 3 repeats for trimers) would substantially increase the total SSR count, potentially indicating an underestimation of overall repeat abundance in the present analysis, whereas more stringent thresholds might exclude shorter but still polymorphic loci. Accordingly, the reported microsatellite abundance should be interpreted in light of the search parameters used in MISA. Moreover, the diversity analysis was conducted on a defined set of accessions representing the Indian clove germplasm; therefore, the observed patterns of genetic structure and divergence reflect this specific collection. Broader sampling across additional geographic regions and production systems would further strengthen inferences regarding national or global diversity patterns. Similarly, although parentage analysis provided clear evidence of hybridization within the studied population, the results should not be interpreted as a definitive estimate of species-wide outcrossing rates without multi-location and multi-year progeny studies. Nonetheless, the molecular findings remain valid for the evaluated material and provide a foundational genomic resource that can be expanded and validated across wider germplasm collections in future investigations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides the first high-resolution, genome-wide map of SSRs in clove (Syzygium aromaticum), moving beyond a simple inventory to a functional genomic toolkit. We have characterized the unique distribution patterns of SSRs, identifying distinct intra-chromosomal hotspots and establishing that long dinucleotide repeats are the primary drivers of hypervariability in this species. The development and experimental validation of 28,147 high-quality SSR markers—characterized by high amplification success and polymorphism—demonstrate the robustness of our in-silico pipeline. The practical utility of these markers in resolving phylogenetic clades, identifying the highly divergent ‘Acc. 9833’ accession, and verifying F1 hybrids at the seedling stage underscores their immediate value for clove improvement. Furthermore, the confirmation of Mendelian segregation in a verified F2 population provides a foundational resource for future QTL mapping and trait discovery. To empower the global research community and ensure the longevity of these resources, we have developed the Clove SSR Marker Database, which is publicly accessible at http://14.139.189.24/clove/genes.php. This database is hosted and will be maintained by the ICAR-Indian Institute of Spices Research (ICAR-IISR), providing a sustainable platform to accelerate breeding programs, facilitate germplasm management, and modernize the genetic improvement of this economically vital spice crop.

Materials and methods

Genomic data acquisition and in-silico SSR mining

The complete chromosome-level reference genome assembly of clove (Syzygium aromaticum) (Assembly: GCA_024500025.1) was retrieved from the NCBI GenBank database [45]. The 11 chromosome sequences (GenBank Accessions: CM044885.1 to CM044895.1), totalling 367.77 Mb, were used for the identification of SSRs. The 11 chromosome sequences were computationally screened for the presence of perfect SSRs using the MIcroSAtellite identification tool (MISA) available at http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/ [69]. The search parameters were defined as a minimum of 10 consecutive repeat units for mononucleotides and 5 contiguous repeat units for di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide motifs. These thresholds were selected based on commonly adopted practices in genome-wide SSR studies and recommendations from the MISA framework [69], with a higher threshold for mononucleotide repeats applied to avoid overestimation of SSR abundance and to improve the relevance of markers for downstream applications. To generate a comprehensive inventory of all potential marker loci, SSRs located in close proximity (e.g., within 100 bp) were catalogued as distinct, individual loci rather than being classified as compound SSRs.

Characterization and genomic distribution of SSRs

Following in-silico identification, the complete set of putative SSRs was characterized based on structural features and genomic location. Structurally, each SSR was classified by its repeat motif type (i.e., mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, or hexa-nucleotide) to determine the relative frequency and abundance of each class across the genome. Furthermore, loci were categorized based on the total length of the repeat tract, as this parameter correlates with polymorphic potential. Loci were assigned to one of three classes: Class I (hypervariable, > 30 nt), Class II (potentially variable, 20–30 nt), or Class III (variable, < 20 nt) [70]. The genomic distribution of the SSRs was analyzed at two distinct scales. First, the average, chromosome-level SSR abundance was calculated for each of the 11 chromosomes and expressed as the number of SSR loci per megabase (SSR loci/Mb). Second, to resolve finer-scale distribution patterns and identify genomic “hotspots” (regions of high density) and “coldspots” (regions of low density), SSR frequency was quantified within contiguous, non-overlapping 1 Mb intervals along the length of each chromosome.

In addition to SSR abundance, GC content (%) was calculated for each non-overlapping 1 Mb interval across all chromosomes from the genomic sequence to examine its relationship with SSR distribution (Supplementary Table S1). Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed to assess the association between GC content and SSR abundance at both chromosome-wise and whole-genome levels. The resulting dataset was used to evaluate the extent to which base composition contributes to the observed intra-chromosomal variation in SSR distribution.

In silico primer design and synthesis

Following the genome-wide identification, a targeted subset of SSR loci—specifically Class I (> 30 nt) and Class II (20–30 nt) repeats—was prioritized for primer design. This selection strategy was based on the well-established biological principle that SSR mutation rates, primarily driven by replication slippage, are positively correlated with total repeat tract length [59, 71, 72]. By focusing on these longer tracts (≥ 20 nt), we aimed to maximize the probability of detecting allelic variation while filtering out the numerically abundant but often monomorphic Class III loci (< 20 nt). This targeted approach ensures higher experimental efficiency and cost-effectiveness in polymorphism detection. The genomic sequences flanking these target SSRs were retrieved from the reference assembly. Primer pairs were designed using Primer3 software [73]. For genomic regions containing multiple adjacent SSR motifs, a single primer pair was designed to encompass all motifs within one amplicon. Design parameters were optimized to target the following: an optimal primer length of 20 nucleotides, a GC content between 40 and 60%, a melting temperature (Tm) of 55–60 °C (with a maximum Tm difference of 2 °C between forward and reverse primers), and a final product size in the 100–350 bp range. This range was chosen to ensure specific amplification and accurate resolution on agarose gels. The genomic distribution of the designed SSR markers—including their chromosomal location, relationship to SSR abundance, motif type, and motif tract length—was visualized using ShinyCircos [74]. A total of 55 primer pairs were synthesized for subsequent experimental validation using the panel of clove genotypes.

Genomic DNA isolation

Genomic DNA was extracted from young, fresh leaf tissue collected from all plant materials described above. The CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) method was employed [75]. The quality of the isolated DNA was assessed by electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel. DNA purity (A260/280 and A260/230 absorption ratios) and concentration were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. All DNA samples were normalized to a working concentration of 20 ng/µl for use in PCR.

PCR amplification, primer screening, and genotyping

A total of 55 SSR primer pairs, designed from the in silico mining results, were synthesized for validation. The markers were selected strategically to ensure uniform genome-wide coverage rather than being chosen randomly. Specifically, five Class I SSR loci were selected from each of the 11 chromosomes to achieve balanced representation across the entire genome (Fig. 3). PCR conditions were initially optimized using genomic DNA from two accessions, Acc. 9833 and Acc. 9838. Gradient PCR was performed to determine optimal annealing temperatures (Ta), testing a range from 48 °C to 62 °C. PCR amplifications were performed in a 10 µl reaction volume in an Eppendorf Mastercycler. Each reaction contained 1 µl of genomic DNA (20 ng), 4 µl of Emerald Green Master Mix (TaKaRa), 0.5 µl each of the forward and reverse primer (10 pmoles/µl), and 4 µl of nuclease-free water. The optimized thermal cycling profile was as follows: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 4 min; followed by 42 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 40 s, annealing at 55 °C (or the optimized Ta for a given primer) for 40 s, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min; with a final extension step at 72 °C for 7 min. The amplified PCR products were resolved by electrophoresis on 3.0% agarose gels in 1x TAE buffer at a constant 100 V. Gels were stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under UV light. Allele sizes were estimated in comparison to a 100 bp DNA ladder (GeneDirex).

SSR allele scoring and data analysis

For the 19 accessions in the diversity panel, polymorphic SSR loci were scored visually based on amplicon fragment size following electrophoresis. Each distinct fragment size was treated as a unique allele. Alleles observed in only a single accession were defined as private alleles. The resulting data were compiled into a co-dominant allele matrix. This matrix was used to calculate a genetic dissimilarity matrix among the accessions. The dissimilarity matrix served as input for both metric multidimensional scaling (MDS) and hierarchical clustering. A dendrogram illustrating the genetic relationships among the clove accessions was constructed using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) algorithm, as implemented in the R programming environment v. 4.3.1. Standard genetic diversity parameters were calculated for each polymorphic locus using GenAlEx v. 6.5 [76]. These parameters included the number of alleles (Na), observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and Polymorphism Information Content (PIC).

Development of a searchable clove SSR database

To provide a comprehensive and publicly accessible resource, a searchable database of the designed SSR markers was constructed. The database was developed using PHP for server-side scripting, a MySQL backend for data storage, and HTML, CSS, and JavaScript for the user interface. The database allows users to query, filter, and retrieve SSR data based on different use requirements.
