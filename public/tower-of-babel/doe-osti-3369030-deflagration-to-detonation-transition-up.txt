ABSTRACT
This report extends the recent SAND reports on the XDDT deflagration-to-detonation transition
code [1, 2] with the development of XTIV (eXplosive Thermal Ignition to Violence), a pipeline
that couples the XCHEM prethermal-ignition code [3, 4] to the XDDT solver, enabling end-to-end
simulation from first exposure to an abnormal thermal environment such as a fire, through thermal
ignition, to the final reactive event—a benign pressure burst or a catastrophic detonation. The
pipeline transfers the XCHEM state at thermal runaway through a single-timestep transfer file
with two schema options based on different gas EOS assumptions, augmented by diagnostic and
smoothing redesigns required to interpret coupled trajectories without ambiguity. The methodology
is demonstrated on four HMX configurations spanning the regime spectrum from fizzle through
transition propagation to a CJ-class detonation for an imposed-boost initial state: a single-fragment
slab in cold bulk that fails to engage surface-burn kinetics, a NATO AOP-4382 [5, 6] slow cook-off
cylinder producing transition propagation through preheating of the HMX bed, a natural-cookoff
twin-fragment laminate configuration also landing in the transition band, and a twin-fragment-
impact configuration with an imposed twin hot-spot boost that produces a CJ-class wave for the
artificially elevated, partially compacted initial state the boost establishes (benchmarked against
a TIGER [7] hot-CJ calculation at the matching internal-energy condition). The progression
establishes bulk-preheating extent as the regime parameter governing deflagration-to-detonation
outcomes in cookoff-coupled BN [8, 9] modeling. Several limitations are identified for future
work.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

This report extends the recent SAND reports titled “Deflagration to Detonation Transition Update:
XDDT Code Modularization” [1] and “Deflagration to Detonation Transition XDDT Update: SI
Units, Adaptive Gridding, TIGER EOS” [2] with the development of XTIV (eXplosive Thermal
Ignition to Violence), a pipeline that couples the XCHEM prethermal-ignition code [3, 4] to the
XDDT deflagration–to–detonation solver, enabling end-to-end simulation from (1) first exposure
to an abnormal thermal environment such as a fire, through (2) thermal ignition, to (3) the final
event—whether a benign pressure burst or a catastrophic detonation.

The pipeline transfers the XCHEM state field at thermal runaway into XDDT through a single-
timestep transfer file generated by a dedicated extractor. Two transfer schemas were developed.
Schema 1 passes temperature, gas density, solid volume fraction, pressure, and solid mass fraction
(𝑇, 𝜌𝑔 , 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝑃, 𝑆 𝑓 ) directly from XCHEM. Schema 2 drops 𝑃 from the file and adds a mass-balance
diagnostic (𝑇, 𝜌𝑔 , 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝑆 𝑓 , 𝑚 𝑓 ), letting XDDT recompute pressure from (𝑇, 𝜌𝑔 ) via its own TIGER-
based gas equation of state. The two schemas thus differ in which code’s gas EOS owns the handoff
pressure. Because XCHEM and XDDT use different gas equations of state, the same physical state
can produce different pressure values in each code; schema 1 keeps XCHEM’s value, schema 2 lets
XDDT recompute.

Two diagnostic redesigns were required to interpret the resulting coupled trajectories. First, the
burn-front tracker was rebuilt to follow the active reaction band rather than its rearmost edge,
eliminating three previously-undetected failure modes; the new tracker reproduces the SAND-1
and SAND-2 validation results on the historical piston-driven cases, so earlier calibrations remain
valid. Second, the gauge-history output was extended with new diagnostics that distinguish kinetic
from compaction-driven reaction at probe locations.

XCHEM solves only the conductive form of the energy equation, with no convective transport. At
thermal runaway, the local temperature rises very steeply: in part because the reaction kinetics
accelerate exponentially, and in part because conduction alone cannot redistribute the released
energy on the timescale of the runaway. In a real system, gas-phase convection would smooth
the stiff energy equation; in the conduction-only handoff state delivered by XCHEM, the spike
is narrower than a single grid cell and would cause unphysical integrator failure at XDDT’s sub-
microsecond timescales. A handoff-state smoothing knob applies a tanh ramp to the four state fields
(𝑇, 𝑃, 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝜌𝑔 ) over a configurable inner-edge length to mitigate this stiffness. The need is most
acute in spherical and cylindrical geometries, where wave convergence further steepens gradients,
but it also arises in slab geometries with sharp material-interface contrasts.

The pipeline is demonstrated on four configurations spanning the regime spectrum from fizzle
through transition propagation to a CJ-class detonation for an imposed-boost initial state.

 7

A single-fragment hot-steel slab in cold HMX bulk produces only compaction-driven activity
pinned at the steel/HMX interface; the BN surface-burn kinetic switch never engages, the cold bulk
beyond the contact face never sees the reaction, and the integration terminates after ∼4 µs with a
fitted velocity |𝐷| ≈ 2 km/s and a peak pressure of ∼143 MPa (two orders of magnitude below
CJ). The case establishes the lower-bound fizzle regime: contact-face ignition in cold bulk cannot
sustain a propagating wave.

A NATO AOP-4382 slow cook-off cylinder [5, 6] (18-inch HMX bed with 1-inch steel jacket,
15◦ C/hr ramp through ignition) exhibits inward-propagating reactive wave amplification consistent
with classical converging-wave detonation transition: peak gas temperatures exceeding 4800 K,
peak pressures exceeding 15 GPa, and a fitted steady wave speed of |𝐷| = 5.39 km/s (roughly 85%
of HMX’s ideal detonation velocity 𝐷 CJ = 6.36 km/s computed from TIGER thermochemistry).
The preheating of the HMX bed (outer wall at ∼500 K under the AOP-4382 ramp at the moment
of ignition) supplies the bulk preheating extent that the surface-burn kinetics require to engage.

A natural-cookoff twin-fragment configuration — laminate steel-HMX-steel sandwich with 540 K
steel and a 4 cm HMX bed, the same geometry as the headline twin-fragment-impact case but
without any imposed boost — produces transition propagation at |𝐷| = 1.43 km/s (roughly 22% of
𝐷 CJ ), similar to the cylinder case. A fragment-spacing sensitivity sweep across fragment spacings
of 1, 2, and 4 cm establishes that two-sided natural cookoff produces fizzle or transition outcomes
depending on the two-sided preheating overlap but never reaches the detonation regime.

A twin-fragment-impact configuration with an asymmetric twin hot-spot boost layered on the
laminate steel-HMX-steel handoff produces front-on-front collision of twin reaction bands and
fires the BN-DDT criterion at the collision frame (𝑡DDT = 2.20 µs at 𝑥DDT = 5.98 cm) with a
steady wave speed of |𝐷| = 8.05 km/s. Benchmarking against a TIGER hot-CJ calculation at
the matching internal-energy condition (𝑒 initial = 1.38 kJ/g, corresponding to HMX at ∼ 1000 K)
places this velocity on the hot-CJ trend at 𝜌 ≈ 1.54 g/cc (𝜙 𝑠 ≈ 0.81 of TMD) — a CJ-class wave
running at its hot-bed ceiling for the boost-imposed initial state, not an overdriven detonation in
the classical sense. The cold-granular CJ value (6.36 km/s) is the wrong baseline for this boosted
initial state; the hot-CJ benchmark establishes thermodynamic consistency between the BN result
and the CJ steady state at the same elevated initial-energy condition. Both the BN integration
and the TIGER hot-CJ calculation are idealized model probes against an artificially elevated initial
state, not predictions of a physically realizable detonation following a realistic cookoff handoff.

The progression establishes bulk-preheating extent as the regime parameter governing deflagration-
to-detonation outcomes in cookoff-coupled BN modeling. Single isolated hot fragments lack the
thermal mass to preheat cold bulk and produce only fizzle-class outcomes. Two-sided or multi-
source thermal loading provides the centimeter-scale preheated extent that the BN compaction-
burn coupling requires for sustained transition propagation. Reaching CJ-class detonation requires
preheating intensity beyond what natural cookoff produces in any of the demonstrated geometries;
the boosted twin-fragment-impact case probes that upper bound against an artificially elevated
initial state.

Several limitations are identified for future work, including a true rigid-wall outer-boundary condi-
tion (the present implementation lets reaction products vent freely outward instead of reflecting off

the case), improved numerical stability for the spherical geometry of the One-Dimensional Time-
to-eXplosion (ODTX) [10] benchmark cookoff test, and replacement of the empirical pressure-
dependent surface-burn rate with a distributed activation-energy form motivated by the Universal
Cookoff Model [4].

1. INTRODUCTION

The XDDT (eXplosive Deflagration to Detonation Transition) code, descended from the two-phase
mixture-theory framework of Baer and Nunziato [8, 9], models the post-ignition compressible
reactive flow that carries a granular explosive from a localized hot spot through deflagration
and—under sufficient confinement—to a steady Chapman–Jouguet detonation. Confinement in the
physical problem is a multi-dimensional phenomenon, set by case strength, lateral inertia, and the
geometry of any pressure-relief path perpendicular to the wave direction; its representation in the
present one-dimensional treatment is necessarily idealized, and the implications of that idealization
are discussed in §10.2 and §10.1. XDDT alone presumes that an ignition state already exists. In
real-world safety analysis, the ignition state is itself the result of a thermal cook-off process that takes
place over minutes to hours of slow heating, comprising heat conduction through case material,
thermal decomposition kinetics, and gas–solid coupling at the prethermal-ignition timescale. The
XCHEM (eXplosive CHEMistry) prethermal-ignition code [3, 4] models this slow phase but not
the post-ignition compressible dynamics.
The disparate timescales (XCHEM: 1 s–10 h; XDDT: 0.1 𝜇s–100 𝜇s) and the disparate physics
(XCHEM: thermal-conduction-dominated, slow chemistry; XDDT: compressible two-phase flow,
fast chemistry) make a single end-to-end solver impractical. The natural division of labor is to
let each code do what it does best: XCHEM resolves the cook-off through thermal runaway, and
XDDT picks up the post-ignition state and integrates the deflagration–to–detonation transition.
This report describes XTIV (eXplosive Thermal Ignition to Violence), the pipeline that connects
them. XTIV consists of: (a) a transfer-file format that captures the XCHEM state at handoff time
in a form XDDT can read; (b) an extractor that converts XCHEM’s plot output into the transfer-file
format, with optional preconditioning of the handoff state to manage numerical stiffness at material
interfaces; (c) modifications to XDDT to accept the transfer-file as initial condition (IBC=3 mode);
and (d) a set of diagnostic and reporting redesigns required to interpret the resulting coupled
trajectories without ambiguity.
Symbols and acronyms used in this report are listed with units in the Nomenclature chapter.

1.1. Prior reports in the DDT series

The XDDT code was modernized from a legacy FORTRAN-77 implementation in the first report of
this series [1], which restructured the solver into Fortran 90 with namelist-driven input, externalized
material-specific parameters, and corrected two long-standing defects (an inconsistency in nodal
solid-pressure evaluation and a non-physical burn-front tracking criterion). Validation against
piston-driven DDT experiments of McAfee et al. [11] (Shot B-9036, 65% TMD granular HMX)

 21

reproduced the experimental ionization-pin trajectory within 0.3% in transition time and 3.8% in
steady detonation velocity.

The second report [2] extended the modernization with three developments—SI-units moderniza-
tion, repair of the adaptive mesh refinement, and adoption of a TIGER thermochemical-equilibrium
gas-phase EOS—and added a primary validation against the Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition ex-
periments [12]. The TIGER tabular EOS removed the constant-𝑐 𝑣 and polynomial-isentrope
approximations of the legacy Jones–Wilkins–Lee (JWL) form. A companion sensitivity study
asked whether a single generic CHNO TIGER table could substitute for explosive-specific tables
in BN-model calibration: the reactant identity used to generate the EOS was varied while all other
BN parameters were held fixed at HMX-tuned values. The study found a ∼70% spread in induction
timing across reactants—a sensitivity-study result rather than a full DDT calibration, since only the
gas EOS was varied—but large enough to settle the question: an explosive-specific TIGER table is
required for each calibration target rather than a single generic table serving all CHNO materials.

1.2. Why coupling matters for IM safety analysis

The dominant accident scenarios in insensitive-munitions (IM) design are slow heating events:
container fires, fuel pool exposure, and prolonged solar heating in storage. Standard tests for IM
compliance, including the NATO AOP-4382 slow cook-off test [5] (governed in the United States
by MIL-STD-2105 [6]), require thermal exposures at 15 ◦ C/hr through ignition. These events
develop the thermal initial condition that determines whether a subsequent reaction propagates as a
benign deflagration or escalates to a violent detonation. The bulk-temperature profile at ignition—
in particular, the radial extent of preheating in cylinder or sphere geometries representative of
munition cases—is a first-order parameter in setting the wave dynamics that follow. Without the
coupling, the post-ignition simulation has to assume an ignition state, which loses the physical link
between the test conditions and the predicted response.

XTIV restores that link. The pipeline transfers the XCHEM state field (temperature, gas density,
solid volume fraction, pressure, and solid mass fraction) at the moment of thermal runaway into
XDDT, where it becomes the initial condition for the compressible reactive integration. This report
demonstrates the pipeline on four configurations spanning the regime spectrum: a single-fragment
slab in cold HMX bulk that fizzles (Chapter 6), a NATO AOP-4382 slow-cook-off cylinder that
produces transition propagation through preheating of the HMX bed (Chapter 7), a natural-cookoff
twin-fragment laminate configuration that also lands in the transition band, and a twin-fragment-
impact configuration with an imposed twin hot-spot boost that produces a CJ-class wave for the
elevated initial state (Chapter 8).

1.3. Frequently asked methodology questions

The XDDT code implements the two-phase Baer–Nunziato (BN) framework as extended in sub-
sequent literature, with several auxiliary closure terms inherited from the legacy implementation.

 22

Several questions about the model’s form and applicability recur often enough in review and
discussion to be worth addressing here.

1.3.1. What is the difference between DDT and SDT, and why does this report target
 DDT?

Insensitive-munitions analysis cares about two distinct paths that take a granular explosive from
a benign state to a violent response. The two paths have qualitatively different energy sources,
initial conditions, characteristic timescales, and wave structures, and modeling them well requires
different tools.

Deflagration-to-detonation transition (DDT). The explosive is first driven to ignition by slow
thermal processes — external heating, internal heat generation, or contact with hot fragments —
on a timescale of hours to days. The reaction begins as a low-velocity deflagration in the preheated
bulk. If the bed’s porosity allows pressure to accumulate behind the reaction front, the deflagration
mechanically compacts the unreacted material ahead of it, and the combination of compaction-
driven hot-spot formation and the pressure-dependent surface-burn rate accelerates the wave from
deflagration through a transition regime and ultimately to a steady detonation. The bed’s initial
porosity is essential to the transition mechanism, and the slow thermal precursor is what produces
the preheated bed configuration that DDT requires.

Shock-to-detonation transition (SDT). A mechanical shock is imposed directly on cold, un-
reacted explosive — by a planar shock, a donor charge, or a high-velocity fragment impact in
the absence of thermal preconditioning. Ignition emerges at the shock front from compressive
heating of the bulk, not from any preconditioned state. There is no extended thermal precursor;
the ignition-to-steady-detonation timescale is microseconds, and the bulk ahead of the shock is at
its initial cold density. SDT in fully dense pressed charges is governed by mechanical hot-spot
formation under shock loading rather than by porosity-mediated deflagration acceleration.

Why XTIV targets DDT. The XTIV pipeline (XCHEM cookoff handed off to XDDT integration)
is specifically built for the DDT regime: XCHEM resolves the slow thermal precursor, the handoff
delivers the bed at its post-cookoff porous state, and XDDT integrates the post-handoff wave dynam-
ics through the BN compaction-burn coupling. The underlying XDDT solver also supports purely
mechanical initialization (piston-ramp boundary conditions, IBC=1) and can be used standalone for
SDT studies, but the SDT regime is better served by reactive-flow codes built around mechanical
hot-spot ignition models (Lee–Tarver ignition-and-growth [13] or Forest Fire [14]) than by XTIV’s
BN-with-cookoff-handoff construction. The four demonstrations in this report (Chapters 6, 7, and
8) span the DDT spectrum from natural-cookoff fizzle through hot-fragment-boosted DDT through
twin-fragment hybrid initiation; none are pure-SDT configurations.

1.3.2. Where does the compaction-burn term come from?

The original BN 1986 paper [8] contains only the pressure-dependent surface-burn rate as the source
for the compaction equation 𝜙¤𝑠 . The extended form used in this work—with an additional contri-

1.3.3. How is the granular surface temperature 𝑇surface computed?

The Heaviside switch 𝐻 in equation (1.1) turns on the surface-burn term once the granular surface
temperature 𝑇surface — the temperature at the gas–grain interface — crosses the ignition threshold.
𝑇surface is not directly equal to either the gas temperature 𝑇𝑔 or the bulk solid temperature 𝑇𝑠 : the
grain has finite thermal conductivity, so its surface heats up over time as gas–grain convection
delivers heat to the interface, but the rate of heating depends on how quickly that heat conducts
inward into the grain.

The Baer–Nunziato model handles this by tracking a quantity 𝜁 — the surface-temperature integral
function of Baer–Nunziato 1989 — that accumulates the cumulative effect of past gas–grain heating
on each grain’s surface. A useful analogy: 𝜁 behaves like a savings account, with the gas–grain
temperature difference as the deposit rate. Whenever the gas is hotter than the grain surface, the
account grows; when the two equilibrate, growth pauses. Formally, 𝜁 = 𝑄 cumulative /(3 𝑚 𝑐 𝑝,𝑠 ),
where 𝑄 cumulative is the total heat the grain has absorbed from gas–grain convection and 𝑚 𝑐 𝑝,𝑠
is its heat capacity. The factor of 3 is the sphere’s surface-to-volume ratio (3/𝑅 𝑝 ); a different
grain geometry (slab, rod) would give a different numerical factor, as flagged in the caveats below.
Pristine bed at gas–solid equilibrium has 𝜁 = 0. The grain surface temperature at any moment
depends on this accumulated record together with the current gas and solid temperatures. The
model uses two equations to close the picture: an evolution equation for 𝜁, and an algebraic relation
that gives 𝑇surface in terms of 𝜁, 𝑇𝑔 , and 𝑇𝑠 .

Where these equations come from. The derivation lives in Baer 1988 [16] and is purely a
heat-transfer analysis — no reaction kinetics involved. The setup is a representative spherical
grain heated externally by gas convection, characterized by the Biot number Bi defined above.
Rather than solving the heat equation inside the grain exactly, Baer assumes a plausible shape for
the temperature profile and requires it to satisfy the heat balance only in an averaged sense — a
standard approximate technique for problems of this kind. Equation (1.2) is the time-evolution of
the integrated profile, and equation (1.3) is the closed-form algebraic relation it produces for the
grain surface temperature. The two terms inside the square brackets of equation (1.3) come from
this analysis in two limits:

 • The low-Bi limit (first term inside the brackets) assumes a parabolic temperature profile
 inside the grain. Averaging this profile over the spherical grain volume produces the constant
 5 that appears in the formula. The constant is geometry-dependent: a slab or rod would give
 a different number.

 • The high-Bi limit (second term inside the brackets) assumes a thin heated layer just inside
 the grain surface, with a linear profile across that layer. Matching the surface heat flux to
 the heat carried by the layer gives a quadratic equation; its positive root produces the square
 root in equation (1.3). The square root is purely an algebraic feature of that quadratic, not a
 runaway-kinetics signature.

The two limits are blended by the overall 1/(Bi + 1) factor in equation (1.3).

Caveats acknowledged in the derivation. Baer 1988 [16] explicitly flags several approxima-
tions that the XDDT implementation inherits: (a) interparticle conduction is neglected; (b) the
analysis assumes spherical grains — slabs or rods would require different constants; (c) the as-
sumed temperature profiles are plausible fits, not exact solutions; (d) the formula that blends the
low-Bi and high-Bi limits is a chosen weighting rather than one derived from the equations; (e) the
method is expected to fail for non-monotonic or periodic thermal forcing such as cyclic heating;
(f) mass transfer, phase change, and radiation are not represented. Readers wanting the technical
names of the profile-fitting methods and the supporting derivation should consult [16] directly.

Is the form adequate? Equations (1.2) and (1.3) form a closed-form heat-transfer model: they
capture the finite-conductivity equilibration of the grain surface temperature with the gas. The
equations contain no reaction kinetics — those live upstream in the compaction-burn equation (1.1)
of §1.3.2. Within the cookoff-driven DDT regime this report addresses, the Baer 1988 closure has
been adequate: it provides a tractable surface-temperature relationship driven by a small number
of physically interpretable inputs (gas–grain heat transfer, grain radius, solid conductivity) that can
be measured or estimated from independent experiments rather than fit to the cookoff response
data themselves. The caveats listed above bound where the form should not be applied — cyclic
forcing, non-spherical grains, regimes where the assumed temperature profiles break down —
but inside that scope the form has supported reproducible calibration across BN-model studies
of granular explosives. The distributed-activation-energy upgrade proposed in §10.5 acts on the
compaction-burn kinetics of equation (1.1), not on this heat-transfer closure.

Terminology. The Baer–Nunziato model carries multiple state variables that are sometimes
loosely lumped together as “hot-spot” quantities. Three are distinct and worth keeping straight:

 • The surface-temperature integral 𝜁 introduced here is a running total of past gas–grain
 heating, evolved by equation (1.2). It is not an energy — it is a temperature-like quantity that
 records how much heat the grain surface has accumulated from gas–grain convection so far.
 • A separate hot-spot energy field carries energy released by compaction-driven hot-spot
 mechanisms. It is a different physical quantity from 𝜁, governed by its own evolution
 equation, and does not enter equation (1.3). This field is not analyzed further in the present
 report.
 • The imposed hot-spot patch of §8.3 (the asymmetric twin hot-spot boost in the boosted
 Chapter 8 deck) is a methodology-level initial condition for XDDT integration, applied
 through the &xtiv_boost namelist. It is not a state variable.

1.3.5. Can XDDT model high-density energetic materials with little or no initial
 porosity?

XDDT is built on the Baer–Nunziato two-phase mixture-theory framework, which presumes both
gas and solid phases coexist throughout the integration domain. For a fully dense single crystal or
pressed charge with 𝜙𝑔 = 0 exactly, the BN gas-phase equations have no domain and XDDT cannot
integrate from such a state.
In the cookoff-coupled use case treated in this report, this limitation is moot: porosity is generated
naturally during the XCHEM thermal-runaway phase as the solid decomposes into gas-phase
products that pressurize the void space. The handoff state delivered to XDDT therefore always has
finite gas content, regardless of the original pristine porosity — XCHEM provides the porosity that
XDDT requires.
For materials with very small but nonzero initial porosity (e.g., 𝜙𝑔 = 10−4 ), XDDT in principle
remains applicable, but two practical considerations apply. First, numerical stiffness: the gas-
phase partial density per cell is small, gradients at material interfaces are steep, and the integrator
strains. The handoff-state smoothing knob (§5) was designed for exactly this regime. Second,
calibration scope: the BN constitutive parameters used here (𝑎 2 , 𝑏 𝑛 , 𝛼∗ , 𝑇ign ) are tuned against mid-
porosity granular-HMX experiments [11, 12, 17], where the void-collapse mechanics encoded in
the compaction-burn term are the dominant ignition-amplification pathway. At nearly-fully-dense
loadings void collapse is no longer the limiting mechanism, and the calibration does not extrapolate
cleanly — DDT could in principle still be reached, but the time, position, and velocity of transition
would not be reliable extrapolations from the mid-porosity calibration.
For nearly-fully-dense pressed charges under shock loading — the SDT regime described in §1.3.1
— the relevant physics is mechanical hot-spot formation, which is the natural domain of reactive-
flow or ignition-and-growth models [18] rather than BN’s compaction-burn coupling. Analysts
attempting to apply the XTIV methodology to that regime should expect the model to fail.

1.3.6. What is the steady detonation-wave speed 𝐷 , and why no subscript?

Following the convention introduced in SAND-1 [1] of this series, the steady detonation-wave
speed reached after the deflagration-to-detonation transition is denoted 𝐷 2 , distinguishing it from a
transient 𝐷 1 that can appear earlier — a faster wave propagating through an inert plug ahead of the
reaction zone, observed in some experiments (e.g. McAfee’s ionization-pin data on heated HMX
cylinders [11]). None of the four demonstration cases in this report exhibit a 𝐷 1 stage; in every case
the burn front transitions directly from compaction-driven propagation to a single steady wave. To
keep the body chapters readable for non-specialist audiences we therefore drop the subscript and

 30

write 𝐷 for the steady detonation-wave speed throughout this report. Readers comparing values to
SAND-1 or SAND-2 [2] should equate 𝐷 here with 𝐷 2 there.

2. GEOMETRY GENERALIZATION AND BOUNDARY CONDITIONS

XDDT supports three 1D geometric configurations: planar slab, cylindrical, and spherical. The
cylindrical and spherical configurations each accommodate both solid beds extending to the axis
or geometric center and hollow beds with a finite inner radius 𝑅i > 0 (annulus or shell); see §2.3.
All three share the Baer–Nunziato two-phase mixture description summarized below; they differ
only in the form of the divergence operator that appears in each conservation equation and in the
boundary conditions imposed at the inner edge of the bed. The full Baer–Nunziato model with all
source terms, equation-of-state closures, and constitutive relations is documented in the first report
of this series [1] and originates with Baer and Nunziato’s 1986 mixture-theory paper [8], with the
compaction equation extended in their 1989 follow-on [9]. This chapter records only the small
subset of that material that the geometry generalization changes.

2.3. Boundary conditions

The slab geometry is two-sided in the BC sense: both endpoints are physical surfaces and each
independently selects an XDDT boundary-condition mode—piston-driven (IBC=1), specified-state
(IBC=2), or transfer-file handoff (IBC=3, see §3.3). For the IBC=3 path used by all demonstrations
in this report, the transfer file carries an inner_side and outer_side confinement designation
that resolves to a free-surface, wall, or layer-impedance treatment. The twin-fragment-impact

 34

case of Chapter 8 exercises this two-sidedness directly: distinct hot-spot patches and smoothing
parameters are applied at each edge of the HMX bed.
The cylindrical and spherical geometries accommodate two distinct inner-edge configurations
through the same inner_side dispatcher used by the slab path. A solid bed extends to the symmetry
axis (𝑛 = 2) or geometric center (𝑛 = 3), with 𝑅i = 0 and inner_side = ’centerline’; the
dispatcher then pins 𝑣 𝑔 = 𝑣 𝑚 = 0 on the gas- and solid-momentum components at the first node
(Dirichlet), while the scalar fields 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝜌𝛼 , 𝑇𝛼 , 𝑃𝛼 retain Neumann. The geometric source (𝑛−1) F /𝑟
in (2.2) is singular at 𝑟 = 0; the discretization regularizes it by placing the first node at 𝑟 = Δ𝑟/2
(cell-centered treatment) together with the centerline momentum Dirichlet. The slow cook-off
cylinder of Chapter 7 is the solid-bed example in this report; the AOP-4382 thermal ramp on the
outer wall is the matching outer-edge physical BC.
A hollow bed — an annular cylinder or spherical shell — has a finite inner radius 𝑅i > 0 and takes
a physical BC on the inner surface through inner_side = ’free’ (Neumann) or inner_side
= ’layer:X’ (impedance match; currently a Neumann fallback pending the work documented
in §10.1). The geometric source (𝑛 − 1) F /𝑟 is regular throughout the domain in the hollow
case, so no centerline regularization is required. The inner and outer edges of a hollow bed are
treated symmetrically by the dispatcher — functionally indistinguishable from the two-sided slab
path apart from the geometric source carried by 𝑛 > 1. Hollow configurations are supported by
the schema (x0_exp, x1_exp, inner_side) and the solver directly; no hollow demonstration is
included in the present report, but reviewers interested in annular or shell cookoff geometries (e.g.,
HMX surrounding a central void or inert core) should expect the pipeline to run them through the
same IBC=3 path as the solid cases.
The current pipeline implementation supports all three geometries on the IBC=3 path, but the
demonstration matrix in this report exercises the slab path (Chapters 6 and 8) and the cylindrical
path (Chapter 7). An attempt to extend the matrix to spherical geometry through the standard
One-Dimensional Time-to-eXplosion (ODTX) test of McGuire and Tarver [10]—a small-scale
HMX cookoff configuration consisting of a 0.25-inch-radius HMX core inside an aluminum jacket
held at a fixed boundary temperature—was attempted during the current development. The XDDT
integrator failed within a small fraction of a microsecond of simulation time. DDEBDF — the
backward-differentiation-formula integrator that XDDT uses for the stiff BN system — reported its
convergence-failure code (IDID= −6) at 𝑡 ≈ 60 ns: the integrator’s implicit corrector iteration could
not converge, which in practice means that the local system had become too stiff for the integrator to
advance with any usable step size. No meaningful reactive-wave development was observed before
the failure. The BN equations admit the spherical case in principle, but the additional geometric
source factor of two relative to the cylindrical 1/𝑟 source, combined with the 1/𝑟 2 singularity scaling
at the centerline, has so far defeated extended integrations on the coupled spherical configurations
attempted to date. A full diagnosis of the failure modes—including the interaction between the
handoff-state smoothing convention, the multi-cell hot-shell-on-cold-bulk character of the ODTX
handoff, and the gradient stiffness at the reactive face—is laid out in §10.3.

3.1. Transfer-file format

A single file is used to pass information from the XCHEM code to the XDDT code. It holds a
snapshot of the XCHEM state at the chosen handoff moment, written as plain ASCII text with
named sections in a fixed order. The file extension is .xtiv. All values are SI; no unit conversion
happens at the interface (XDDT applies SI→CGS conversions internally after reading, mirroring
the pattern in the rest of the source tree).

Two schema versions exist. Schema v1 (the default format) carries the XCHEM-reported gas
pressure 𝑝 alongside the rest of the state, and XDDT cross-checks 𝑝 against its own equation-of-
state evaluation as a soft consistency check. Schema v2 drops 𝑝 from the file — XDDT owns gas
pressure via its own EOS — and adds a mass_fraction diagnostic that lets XDDT audit local
mass conservation between the two codes. The extractor’s --schema option (set when the user
runs the extractor) selects which version to write; XDDT keys on the schema_version field in the
file’s [meta] block and accepts either. The demonstrations in this report use the default schema v1;
schema v2 is recommended for new work because the mass_fraction field it adds enables an
automated mass-balance audit of the handoff state at read time.

The file’s seven sections, in fixed order, are:

 • [meta] — schema version, problem identity, handoff timing (t_src, zone_index, ignition_flag,
 ignition_cause), code-build provenance.

 • [geometry] — 1D coordinate system (slab/cylinder/sphere), reactive-layer extent, run-
 derived geometry scalars.

 • [material] — reactive-material constants needed for the XDDT-side reconstruction of 𝜌𝑐
 at each node (𝜌𝑐𝑜 , thermal-expansion coefficients, phase-change ramps).

 • [confinement] — inner- and outer-edge boundary-condition selectors for the XDDT inte-
 gration.

 • [grid] — per-node coordinates and refinement-rank metadata.

 • [fields] — per-node primitive state. The columns differ between schema versions (Table 3-
 1).

 • [end] — end-of-file marker; XDDT checks this is the last section before declaring the file
 valid.

3.2. Extractor: xtiv_extract.py

The extractor is a Python tool that reads XCHEM’s text output and writes the transfer file described
in §3.1. It is a stand-alone script with no run-time dependencies beyond the Python standard
library, so it can be invoked from the same shell environment that runs XCHEM and XDDT, with
no separate installation step. The source lives at tools/xtiv_extract.py in the source tree.

Usage modes. The tool runs in three mutually-exclusive modes (one of –list, –zone, or –time
must be given on each invocation):

 • –list <ofield.plt> — print one line per zone (zone number, time, max temperature, max
 pressure, minimum unreacted-mass fraction). Zones are numbered starting from 1, matching
 XCHEM’s own diagnostic output: zone 1 is the first zone written, zone 2 the second, and so
 on. No transfer file is written. Useful for picking a zone or time to extract. If –tchem-in
 and –mat-dat are also supplied, a per-layer table is printed first showing which layers are
 reactive — the table needed when the input deck contains multiple reactive layers.
 • –zone N <ofield.plt> — extract zone 𝑁 into a transfer file (𝑁 = 1 selects the first zone
 listed by –list).

 40

 • –time T <ofield.plt> — extract the zone whose stored time t_src is closest to 𝑇
 (seconds); the actual t_src used is recorded in the output file’s [meta] block.

–zone and –time also require –tchem-in <path>, –mat-dat <path>, and -o <out.xtiv>.

Optional command-line options.
 • –schema {1,2} — transfer-file schema version to write (default 1). Schema v2 omits gas
 pressure and adds the mass_fraction diagnostic per §3.1.
 • –reactive-layer-index N — index of the reactive layer to extract, counting from 0:
 𝑁 = 0 selects the first reactive layer in the per-layer table, 𝑁 = 1 selects the second, and
 so on. (The offset relative to –zone, which counts from 1, follows the extractor’s internal
 Python list convention for layers; zone numbering follows XCHEM’s own 1-based diagnostic
 output.) Required when tchem.in contains multiple reactive layers (e.g., the twin-fragment-
 impact configuration of §8 uses a 3-layer steel–HMX–steel deck and selects the HMX layer);
 ignored when only one reactive layer is present. Run –list with –tchem-in and –mat-dat
 to see the layer table.
 • –override-ndv N — force the number of phase-change ramps used in the 𝜌𝑐 reconstruction
 (debugging only; N=0 skips the cosine-ramp summation entirely).
 • –write-time <ISO-8601> — explicit write-time stamp for the [meta] block; the default
 is the current UTC time at run time. Setting an explicit value is useful when the goal is exact
 reproduction of an archived transfer file.
 • –quiet — suppress the per-zone summary line that is otherwise printed to the terminal after
 a successful extraction.

Input files read.
 • ofield.plt — per-node field history written by XCHEM. Each zone in the file is one
 timestep of the cookoff integration, with one row per spatial node. Column 21 carries the
 refinement rank ilev; the extractor copies this verbatim to the transfer file’s [grid] block.
 • tchem.in — the XCHEM input deck. The extractor reads the geometry block (geom, pgeom,
 layer interfaces), the per-layer table (layer name, reference temperature tlay, permeability
 flag), the pressure-unit and initial/maximum-pressure entries, and the excess volume evol
 used to compute maxstrain for the [geometry] block.
 • mat.dat — the shared material database. The extractor pulls the reactive layer’s constants
 (condensed-density at reference temperature rho_co, thermal-expansion coefficients bvol
 and bslope, number of phase-change ramps ndv, and per-ramp triplets) for the [material]
 section and for the per-node 𝜌𝑐 recomputation.
The extractor does not read any in-memory XCHEM state; it works exclusively from text files
XCHEM has already written. This separation keeps the pipeline reproducible from the same
archived inputs and outputs without requiring a re-run of XCHEM.

Behavior. Given a zone selection and the three input files, the extractor:

 1. Identifies the unique reactive layer in the tchem.in layer list (by cross-referencing mat.dat);
 aborts when multiple reactive layers are present and –reactive-layer-index was not
 supplied.
 2. Filters the selected zone’s rows to spatial coordinates inside the chosen reactive layer.
 3. Computes the run-derived maxstrain from the layer’s geometric volume, the excess volume
 evol, and the initial gas volume fraction (formula in Appendix A).
 4. Derives the inner- and outer-edge confinement labels for the [confinement] block from the
 flanking layers (’centerline’ for the inner edge of a centered cylinder or sphere, ’free’
 for an exposed edge, ’layer:<name>’ when a non-reactive layer flanks the reactive one).
 5. Sets the ignition_flag and ignition_cause fields in [meta] based on whether the
 maximum temperature in the selected zone reached XCHEM’s runaway threshold.
 6. Replicates XCHEM’s 𝜌𝑐 formula (Appendix A, eqs. A.1–A.4) at each node and writes the

3.3. IBC=3 initialization mode

XDDT’s initial-boundary-condition selector IBC chooses how the integration is initialized. The
legacy options IBC=0,1,2 (used in SAND-1 and SAND-2 piston-driven and specified-state setups)
remain unchanged. This work adds IBC=3 as a fourth option that initializes the integration from a
transfer file rather than from a procedural initial condition.

Triggering and entry point. Setting IBC=3 in the &numerical namelist of the XDDT input
deck routes the run through the IBC=3 path on entry to the initialization routine nvalue0. The
transfer-file path is given by the namelist parameter xtiv_file_in (no default; the run aborts
with a clear diagnostic if the file is missing or unreadable). The legacy IBC=1 namelist parameters
(pg_ign, tg_ign, l1_ign) are still read by the namelist machinery for backward compatibility
but are unused on the IBC=3 path.

 42

Behavior on entry. Given a valid transfer-file path, XDDT performs the following steps in order,
aborting on any cross-check failure:

 1. Opens the transfer file and parses [meta]. Verifies that schema_version is in the accepted
 set {1, 2} and uses the appropriate schema-specific parsing for the per-node fields.
 2. Parses [geometry]. The geom value (slab/cylinder/sphere) is inherited from the file unless
 the user has set xtiv_override_geom_in ≥ 0 in the XDDT deck, in which case the deck
 value wins (an escape hatch for sensitivity studies).
 3. Parses [material]. If the user has set xtiv_expected_material_in in the XDDT deck,
 the file’s name field is cross-checked against that value, and a mismatch (e.g. HMX-tuned
 XDDT parameters with a non-HMX XCHEM handoff) aborts the run. With this parameter
 empty (the default), this check is skipped; see §3.4 for the full material-consistency model.
 4. Parses [confinement]. Resolves the inner_side and outer_side strings to XDDT’s in-
 ternal boundary-condition selectors. An optional user override is available via xtiv_outer_bc_in
 in the XDDT deck.
 5. Parses [grid]. Sets the per-node coordinate arrays and the refinement-rank metadata that
 XDDT’s adaptive-mesh subsystem consumes.
 6. Parses [fields]. For each node:
 • Reconstructs 𝜌𝑐 from the transferred temperature and the [material] constants (Ap-
 pendix A). Compares against rho_m_check; aborts on a mismatch above 1%.
 • For schema v2, recomputes the mass_fraction diagnostic and aborts on a mismatch
 above 1% at any non-pristine node.
 • Applies the &xtiv_boost preconditioning if it is present in the deck (see below).
 • Evaluates XDDT’s gas equation of state at (𝑇, 𝜌𝑔 ) and its solid equation of state at
 (𝑇, 𝜌𝑐 ) to populate the per-node energy fields.
 • Populates XDDT’s per-node BN state from the loaded primitives. The BN model’s
 hot-spot state variables, including the surface-temperature integral 𝜁 of §1.3.3, start at
 zero — pristine bed is assumed at the moment XDDT integration begins. The handoff
 of the related pyrolysis-progress variable 𝜋 𝑦 versus the XCHEM solid-mass fraction 𝑆 𝑓
 is discussed in §3.3.1.
 7. Verifies that the [end] marker was reached. A truncated file aborts the run.
 8. Logs the handoff to xddt.log: file path, schema_version, t_src, the per-node minimum
 and maximum of the primary state fields, and a summary of any &xtiv_boost modifications
 applied.

After step 8 the IBC=3 path returns control to the regular XDDT integrator before reaching the
legacy burst-pressure logic that IBC=0/1/2 use — the burst-pressure path is bypassed because the
boundary state is already specified by the transfer file. The complete list of fatal-error conditions
XDDT enforces on the IBC=3 path is reproduced in Appendix A.

3.3.1. Pyrolysis-progress versus solid-mass-burnup

The pyrolysis-progress variable 𝜋 𝑦 (slot 8 in XDDT’s per-node solution vector) tracks the mass
density of pyrolysed gas under XDDT’s 𝑃mix 2 -driven dynamic-burn kinetics; the MMP solid mass

fraction 𝑆 𝑓 transferred from XCHEM tracks a different reaction — the Arrhenius solid-phase
decomposition that drives the cookoff up to runaway. At the handoff frame, 𝜋 𝑦 = 0 is the physically
correct initial condition: XDDT’s dynamic-burn mechanism has not yet acted, and the gas density

 44

𝜌𝑔 delivered by XCHEM already reflects whatever solid has decomposed during cookoff. The
mass-balance information carried by 𝑆 𝑓 is therefore preserved through the handoff via (𝜌𝑔 , 𝜙 𝑠 )
rather than through the F_sol(8) slot, and the schema-2 mass-fraction cross-check (§4) audits this
consistency at read time. 𝜋 𝑦 does not participate in either the burn-front tracker or the BN-DDT
criterion, so this packaging choice does not affect the report’s principal diagnostics.

3.4. Material consistency between XCHEM and XDDT

XCHEM and XDDT describe disjoint physical regimes and consequently need disjoint sets of
material parameters. XCHEM’s mat.dat holds the parameters for the slow-time cookoff phase:
condensed density 𝜌𝑐𝑜 , TMD, thermal-expansion coefficients, phase-change ramps, and the pre-
thermal-ignition kinetics. The XDDT deck holds the BN-closure parameters that describe the
post-handoff dynamics: 𝑎 2 , 𝑏 𝑛 , 𝛼0 , 𝑇ign , grain radius, gas-grain heat-transfer factor, and solid-
matrix conductivity (namelist parameters a2_in, bn_in, alstar_coeff_in, tign_in, ap_in,
htfact_in, akm_in). These two parameter sets do not overlap, so they cannot “disagree” against
each other in any meaningful sense.
The thermal and density constants XDDT needs at the handoff frame — principally 𝜌𝑐𝑜 and TMD,
used in the rho_m_check cross-check of §3.1 — ride along in the transfer file’s [material] block.
XDDT consumes them directly without an XDDT-side copy of mat.dat, so the values used by
both codes at the handoff frame are guaranteed equal by construction.
On entry to IBC=3 the pipeline supports an optional sanity check. When the namelist variable
xtiv_expected_material_in is set in the XDDT deck, the transfer file’s [material] name
field must match that user-asserted value, and a mismatch aborts the run with a diagnostic naming
both values. The default is empty, in which case the check is skipped. This guards against an
XDDT deck calibrated for one explosive being handed a transfer file produced from an XCHEM
run on a different explosive — e.g., an HMX-tuned BN deck paired with a PETN cookoff handoff
— which is the only consistency failure that the current code paths can produce.
A separate observation is that the user must currently populate XCHEM’s mat.dat and the XDDT
deck independently when working with a new material, even though both describe the same
physical explosive. A unified material library spanning both codes — pick HMX (or PETN, or
another material of interest) once, and both the cookoff and the BN-closure parameters are sourced
from a shared entry — would remove the duplicated bookkeeping. Whether the resulting tighter
coupling between the two codes is worth the reduced flexibility (e.g., the ability to swap in a one-off
BN calibration without touching the cookoff library) is an open design question and is left as future
work in §10.

4. DIAGNOSTICS REDESIGN

The coupled XCHEM–XDDT trajectories presented in the demonstration chapters could not be
interpreted with the diagnostics inherited from SAND-1 and SAND-2. Two redesigns were required:
the burn-front tracker, which now follows the active reaction band rather than the rearmost satisfying
node, and the gauge-history output, which now distinguishes kinetic engagement from compaction-
driven motion at probe locations. This chapter documents both redesigns, illustrates them on the
demonstration cases, and confirms that they preserve the SAND-1 and SAND-2 validation results
exactly on the historical piston-driven test cases.

4.1. Burn-front tracker redesign

The legacy burn-front tracker used in SAND-1 and SAND-2 located the burn front by scanning
the bed for the rightmost node satisfying a cumulative-state criterion (specifically, 𝜙 𝑠 < 0.98 𝜙 𝑠,0 )
and reporting that node’s index as ibf. This worked well on the piston-driven validation cases of
those reports, but three failure modes surfaced when it was applied to coupled XCHEM–XDDT
trajectories.

Failure mode 1: boundary-noise lock-in. When the outer-edge cell carries even a small nu-
merical perturbation — introduced by the boundary-condition discretization or by handoff-state
interpolation in the IBC=3 path of §3.3 — it can satisfy the legacy tracker’s threshold by accident.
The tracker latches onto that cell and reports the burn-front position pinned at the bed end while the
real reaction is happening elsewhere. This was confirmed in the unsmoothed-handoff slab cases
during the current development.

Failure mode 2: compaction masking. When compaction has globally raised 𝜙 𝑠 above 𝜙 𝑠,0
across the bed — as it does in the boost-driven configurations that compact the entire bed during
the first few microseconds — no cell satisfies the cumulative criterion 𝜙 𝑠 < 0.98 𝜙 𝑠,0 , even at
locations where reaction is actively proceeding. The tracker reports no burn front, and the reaction
proceeds invisibly to the diagnostic. Confirmed in the 𝑡 scale = 1.5 boost-driven case from the
current development.

4.2. Gauge-history instrumentation

The legacy gauge_history.dat output carried per-probe time histories of mixture pressure, gas
and solid temperatures, gas density, solid volume fraction, and a few derived quantities. These
were sufficient for the piston-driven cases of SAND-1 and SAND-2 but missed two distinctions
that this report requires: whether reaction at a probe is currently active or has stalled, and whether
the active reaction is being driven by surface-burn kinetics or by compaction alone. Two columns
were added.

Solid-fraction time derivative 𝜙¤𝑠 (1/s). Signed. Negative means solid is being consumed at
the probe location (reaction proceeding); positive means compaction is locally densifying the solid
phase. This column reveals the time evolution of reaction at each probe independently of the global
burn-front tracker — a probe deep inside the bed can show reaction beginning even when the
bed-wide tracker has not yet detected a coherent band.

Surface-burn velocity 𝑟 burn (m/s). The pressure-dependent surface-burn velocity from the BN
compaction-equation source, 𝑟 burn = 𝑎 2 (𝑃𝑔 /𝑃ref ) 𝑏 𝑛 𝐻, where 𝐻 is the Heaviside switch on 𝑇surface −
𝑇ign that fires when the granular surface temperature crosses the ignition threshold (see §1.3.3).

 49

Zero unless the surface-burn term is active at that node. This column is what distinguishes whether
reaction at a probe is kinetic (surface-burn engaged, 𝑟 burn > 0) or compaction-driven only (𝑟 burn = 0,
with the compaction-burn term in equation (1.1) carrying any reaction that proceeds).
The slow cook-off cylinder demonstration relies directly on this distinction. The wave at gauge 2
(𝑟 = 18 cm) of §7.4 shows 𝑟 burn rising to 1.94 m/s at 𝑡 = 29.9 µs, evidence of kinetic engagement
that the legacy diagnostics could not have surfaced.

4.3. Validation: SAND-1 and SAND-2 calibration markers preserved

The diagnostic redesigns were validated against the regression test suite from SAND-1 and SAND-2
to verify that they did not disturb the published calibration markers. Two regression cases were
exercised.
The McAfee Tier-1 piston-driven test — the principal SAND-1 reference case — passes exactly
with the new tracker active. The coupled.BF.plt output now carries the lead/peak/trail edges
in addition to the legacy ibf index, but the reported ignition time, DDT firing time, and steady
detonation-wave speed 𝐷 exactly match the SAND-1 published values.
The Fig. 3 reference case from SAND-2 produces unchanged behavior on the portion of the run that

The hybrid-retention strategy of §4.1 is what makes this exact match possible: the calibrated state-

5. HANDOFF-STATE SMOOTHING

Coupled XCHEM–XDDT runs initially failed within nanoseconds of XDDT entry on geometries
that had succeeded for SAND-1 and SAND-2’s piston-driven cases. The cause was numerical
stiffness at the handoff seam: sub-millimeter gradients in the XCHEM state that the XDDT
integrator could not advance through on its microsecond timescale. This chapter documents the
handoff-state smoothing knob added to address the problem — its motivation, the tanh-ramp design,
the tuning trade-off, and a validation case.

5.1. Motivation: integrator stiffness at the handoff seam

The initial coupled XCHEM–XDDT runs in spherical geometry failed within roughly 30 nanosec-
onds of the start of XDDT integration, with the DDEBDF integrator returning the convergence-
failure code IDID=-6 on the first or second integration step. The same physical handoff state
integrated successfully for approximately 12 µs in slab geometry — the difference was the geo-
metric source. The 1/𝑟 2 spherical source in equation (2.1) of §2 amplifies inner-edge gradients
by a factor of (𝑅/𝑟) 2 as the radial coordinate 𝑟 approaches the centerline; in slab geometry the
geometric source vanishes (𝑛 = 1) and gradients are not amplified.

A diagnostic on the failing handoff state showed the cause. In the inner-edge cells of the bed, the
solid volume fraction 𝜙 𝑠 jumped from approximately 0.4 to 0.9 to 0.98 across three adjacent cells,
and the gas density 𝜌𝑔 dropped by a factor of 27 across two adjacent cells. These are artifacts
of the steel/HMX interfacial transition in the XCHEM cookoff calculation: the transition is a thin
spatial layer in XCHEM’s slow-time integration, but it appears as a near-discontinuity to XDDT’s
microsecond-timescale integrator, and the converging-geometry amplification was enough to push
the numerical Jacobian to non-convergence on the first integration step.

5.2. Tanh-ramp design

The smoothing knob is implemented as the routine xtiv_smooth_state in xtiv_glue.f90. It
applies a tanh ramp to four state fields — gas temperature 𝑇𝑔 , mixture pressure 𝑃mix , solid volume
fraction 𝜙 𝑠 , and gas density 𝜌𝑔 — over the first cells of bed past the inner edge of the explosive
layer. The smoothing extent is controlled by the namelist parameter xtiv_smooth_l_in, with an
analogous xtiv_smooth_outer_l_in for the outer-edge counterpart. For each smoothed field 𝐹,
the value at 𝑥 = 𝐿 (just past the smoothing zone) serves as the inner-region target, and cells inside
[0, 𝐿] are blended toward it via:

Why all four fields? The dominant stiffness drivers are 𝜙 𝑠 and 𝜌𝑔 , but two-field smoothing
experiments — smoothing only those two and leaving 𝑇𝑔 and 𝑃mix untouched — achieved only

improvement, matching the natural smoothness of a different XCHEM handoff zone (one for which
the integrator already succeeded without smoothing). Two-field smoothing remains an attractive
future option (§10) because it would preserve a single-cell-class temperature spike in 𝑇𝑔 , but the
present implementation is four-field and validates against the demonstrations in this report.

Why tanh and not a local diffusion operator? A local smoothing operation (running average
or Gaussian convolution) would propagate effects beyond the intended smoothing region and would
require iteration to a stopping criterion. The tanh-to-target ramp has a hard endpoint at 𝑥 = 𝐿 with
a known reduction factor at 𝑥 = 0; the smoothing extent is set entirely by the namelist parameter,
requires no iteration, and is independent of bed-scale or grid-resolution choices.

Effect on the mass and energy budget. Because the tanh ramp modifies 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝜌𝑔 , 𝑇𝑔 , and
𝑃mix over the smoothing length, the smoothed state’s integrated mass and energy in the smoothed
region differ from those of the raw XCHEM handoff. In the typical case — low 𝜙 𝑠 and high
𝜌𝑔 at the inner edge, both pulled toward bulk-like target values — the smoothed region carries
slightly more solid mass and slightly less gas mass and thermal energy than the raw handoff. The
perturbation is small in absolute terms: the smoothing length is at most a few millimeters compared
to the centimeter-to-decimeter bed scale, so the perturbed region is typically 1–5% of the bed by
volume. More importantly, the modification is applied as a one-time operation at the handoff
frame; XDDT’s compaction and energy equations then conserve mass and energy strictly from the
smoothed state forward. Smoothing is therefore a discrete shift between the raw XCHEM end-state
and the XDDT initial condition, not an ongoing conservation violation during the integration. The
per-cell modifications are echoed in the run-time provenance log (step 8 of §3.3) so the shift is
auditable case-by-case.

Order of operations. The smoothing operation is applied after the &xtiv_boost precondition-
ing of §3.3, so an imposed hot-spot patch (if present) cannot reintroduce a sharp gradient that the
smoother just removed.

stiffness) and leave 𝑇𝑔 and 𝑃mix untouched (so the spike survives intact regardless of 𝐿). Selective
smoothing along these lines is identified as future work in §10.

The smoothing knob was validated on two test cases that had failed without it. XCHEM writes
its slow-time cookoff history to ofield.plt as a series of per-timestep snapshots (“zones”); the
extractor of §3.2 selects one zone for handoff. The two cases below are identified by their zone
indices in the corresponding XCHEM output file — the integers below are simply timestep numbers,
not node counts or any geometric quantity.

Sphere case: ODTX zone 72 versus zone 30. The first validation case is the One-Dimensional
Time-to-eXplosion (ODTX) sphere cookoff configuration of McGuire and Tarver [10] introduced
in §2 — the test that originally motivated the smoothing work. Two zones were extracted from the
XCHEM run for this validation: zone 72 (XCHEM time 𝑡 XCHEM = 7.05 s, a runaway-state snapshot
near the moment of thermal ignition, with the steep inner-edge 𝜙 𝑠 and 𝜌𝑔 gradients described
in §5.1) and zone 30 (XCHEM time 𝑡XCHEM = 2.85 s, an earlier pre-runaway snapshot whose
inner-edge profile happens to be naturally smooth and integrated successfully without smoothing).
Without smoothing, zone 72 fails at 𝑡 ≈ 0.03 µs in the converging spherical geometry. With
xtiv_smooth_l_in = 0.2 mm applied, the same handoff integrates to 𝑡 = 0.36 µs, matching
the survival time of zone 30. The match between smoothed-zone-72 and unsmoothed-zone-30 on
otherwise identical physics is direct evidence that the failure was geometric amplification of inner-
edge stiffness rather than a deeper property of the post-handoff dynamics: smoothing recovers the
same survival time that natural smoothness already gives. Smoothing is necessary but not sufficient
for extended sphere integrations; the residual sphere-convergence problem is the future-work item
documented in §10.3.

Slab case: single-fragment natural cookoff. The second validation case is the single-fragment
slab demonstration of §6.1, with the XCHEM handoff extracted at the last zone (the runaway snap-
shot at which the steel/HMX interface gas-phase temperature has spiked through the kinetic chain

reaction). Without smoothing the case fails within ∼0.1 µs of XDDT entry with DDEBDF re-

6. SLAB DEMONSTRATIONS

This chapter reports the single-fragment slab-geometry demonstration of the XTIV pipeline. A
1-cm steel slab at hot-fragment temperature is placed in contact with a 10-cm bed of granular HMX,
and the coupled XCHEM–XDDT pipeline is exercised end-to-end with no boost imposed. The
outcome (§6.1) is a fizzle: XCHEM drives the contact face through thermal runaway over tens of
seconds, but the resulting handoff state delivers a gas-phase spike confined to a single cell adjacent
to the steel/HMX interface, surrounded by a bed at its initial cold temperature. Post-handoff XDDT
integration produces only a brief compaction-driven response at the boundary; the BN surface-
burn kinetic switch never arms, the bed beyond the contact face never sees the reaction, and the
integration terminates after 4.28 µs with no propagating wave. This case sets the lower bound of
the preheat-extent parameter sweep developed in Chapter 9.
The chapter is structured around this one demonstration plus three methodology asides used
elsewhere in the report. §6.2 documents the parameter guidance for the imposed hot-spot patch
(&xtiv_boost), which is the device used in the twin-fragment chapter (Chapter 8) to stand in
for prior thermal history of the bed; the patch itself is not exercised in §6.1. §6.3 reports the
𝑇ign sensitivity sweep on the slow-cookoff cylinder (Chapter 7), the only configuration in this
report whose handoff temperature sits close enough to the kinetic switch threshold for 𝑇ign to be a
first-order tuning knob. §6.4 provides the mechanistic reasoning behind the single-fragment fizzle
observation of §6.1 and contrasts it with the multi-fragment configurations developed in Chapter 8,
where two-sided preheating between coordinated fragments supplies the bed extent that a single
fragment alone cannot deliver.

6.1. Single-fragment slab with natural ignition

The first demonstration is the original hot-fragment-into-explosive geometry: a 1 cm steel slab at
600 K placed in contact with a 10 cm HMX bed (initially 300 K, granular at 70% TMD, vented

occurs at the steel/HMX interface. The resulting handoff state is passed to XDDT through the

a single fragment in a vented HMX bed, and the outcome — a brief compaction-driven response

 the compaction-driven activity does not propagate outward.

 • Peak mixture pressure 𝑃max = 143 MPa, two to three orders of magnitude below detonation-

 • DDEBDF terminates the integration at 𝑡 = 4.28 µs with the wave still pinned at the interface.
 The fitted burn-front velocity is 𝐷 2 = −2.10 km/s, negative (i.e., the active-band peak
 migrates slightly inward over the few microseconds during which it is detected, not outward

The result is qualitatively the single-fragment quench mechanism documented in §6.4: a hot
fragment readily ignites the explosive at the contact face, but the surrounding cold bulk thermally
suppresses the locally ignited reaction before a self-sustaining propagating wave can establish. The
contact-face HMX has temperature, but the bed has no preheat extent. Without that extent, the BN
compaction-burn coupling has no warm bed length to engage across, the kinetic switch never arms,
and the post-handoff response collapses to a compaction-only transient at the boundary.

This configuration establishes the lower bound of the parameter sweep: a hot single fragment
delivers contact-face ignition without the centimeter-scale preheated bed extent the BN compaction-
burn coupling requires for sustained propagation. The wave never develops; the kinetic switch never
arms; the bed beyond the contact face never sees the reaction. The sections that follow exercise the
same configuration with progressively more preheating supplied — a hot-spot patch standing in for
prior thermal history (§6.2), then two-sided cookoff between coordinated fragments at decreasing
separations (Chapter 8) — to identify the preheat extent at which the model transitions out of the
fizzle regime.

6.2. Methodology guidance for the hot-spot-patch boost

The single-fragment fizzle of §6.1 demonstrates that natural XCHEM cookoff alone does not deliver
the preheated bed extent the BN compaction-burn coupling needs to drive sustained propagation.
In the twin-fragment configuration of Chapter 8, this gap is bridged by applying an imposed hot-
spot patch through the &xtiv_boost namelist — a methodology device that stands in for the
prior thermal history a real fragment would have imparted to the bed. This section documents
the parameter guidance for that patch so the values used in Chapter 8 are traceable to a physical
rationale. The patch is not exercised in §6.1 itself; the guidance applies wherever the boost is used
in the report.

The boost is parameterized by three values, each set for a distinct physical reason, and is coordinated
with a fourth parameter that controls the numerical handoff smoothing.

Patch temperature hot_spot_T_in. Physical purpose: arm the surface-burn kinetics. The
kinetic switch in the BN compaction-burn term (the Heaviside 𝐻 on 𝑇surface −𝑇ign in equation (1.1))
does not engage until the granular surface temperature crosses the explosive’s ignition threshold.
hot_spot_T_in must therefore exceed 𝑇ign (800 K for HMX); below the threshold the wave will
not transition out of pure compaction-driven deflagration regardless of patch length or pressure.
The recommended patch values (900 K in the laminate interior, 1100 K on the twin-fragment
outer-edge mirror) sit above the threshold by construction and are correspondingly higher than the
thermal kernel a real fragment would deposit by impact alone. The threshold itself is a tunable
parameter (§6.3) and could be lowered through a coordinated choice of the XCHEM stop-at-
temperature parameter; the patch values would then drop in turn, bringing the boost closer to
physically realizable post-impact kernel temperatures. That coordinated tuning is not exercised in
the present report.

Patch length hot_spot_l_in. Physical purpose: supply enough preheated bed for the wave
to sustain propagation. A localized ignition into cold bulk quenches before a self-sustaining wave
establishes (the single-fragment quench mechanism of §6.4). hot_spot_l_in sets the spatial
extent of the preheated zone the BN compaction-burn coupling is handed, and it must be wide
enough that propagation is no longer cold-bulk limited. Centimeter-scale values are the operating
point for the demonstrations in this report. Large patch lengths are not realistic, and are only used
as an example to emphasize the importance of a heated bed.

Patch pressure hot_spot_P_in. Physical purpose: stand in for post-compaction kernel pres-
sures. The fragment-impact and shock-passage mechanisms that a single-fragment hot-spot rep-
resents in reality produce high-pressure ignition kernels that a purely thermal cookoff precursor
underrepresents. The calibrated default of 50 MPa reflects realistic post-compaction kernel pres-
sures.

 60

Numerical coordination with xtiv_smooth_l_in. Numerical purpose: keep the integrator
from choking on the inner-edge gradients. xtiv_smooth_l_in is a separate namelist parameter
set in &xtiv_glue (not in &xtiv_boost) that controls the length over which the handoff-state
smoothing of Chapter 5 relaxes the steep XCHEM inner-edge 𝜙 𝑠 and 𝜌𝑔 jumps, so that DDEBDF
does not encounter a near-discontinuity at XDDT entry. Because the smoothing routine runs after
the boost is applied (§5.2), hot_spot_l_in must exceed xtiv_smooth_l_in or the smoothing
tanh ramp spans the entire patch and washes it out before XDDT integrates. The constraint is there-
fore a coordination between two independent user-set knobs — a physics knob (hot_spot_l_in)
and a numerics knob (xtiv_smooth_l_in) — not a single-parameter limit.

Sensitivity behavior. A brief sensitivity study spanning 𝑇 ∈ [900, 1200] K (at ℓ = 2 cm)
and ℓ ∈ [10, 40] mm (at 𝑇 = 900 K) confirmed that above-threshold variations affect 𝐷 by
approximately 30 % (range 4.5–5.9 km/s, all in transition territory) and shift 𝑥DDT linearly with
ℓ relative to the inner explosive edge; no above-threshold parameter combination produces a
qualitatively different wave regime. The patch thus acts effectively as a threshold switch between
sub-ignition (no DDT) and transition DDT, with ℓ acting as a geographic seed for the DDT crossing
position rather than as a continuous tuning knob.

6.3. Methodology guidance for 𝑇ign in cookoff-coupled scenarios

The Heaviside ignition switch in equation (1.1) turns on the BN surface-burn term when the local
granular surface temperature exceeds 𝑇ign ; below the threshold the term is off. In piston-driven
scenarios where the ignition boundary condition deposits energy far above any plausible 𝑇ign , the
threshold value is effectively calibration-insensitive at the SAND-1 reference value of 525 K (HMX
low-pressure decomposition onset). In cookoff-coupled scenarios where the XCHEM handoff state
itself sits near the threshold, however, 𝑇ign becomes a first-order parameter that must be tuned to
the scenario’s handoff temperature plateau.
The methodology lesson below is reported here, in the slab chapter, even though the slab demon-
stration of §6.1 side-steps the 𝑇ign sensitivity: the single-fragment natural-cookoff case has 𝑇mix

 61

staying so far below 𝑇ign throughout the integration that the switch never engages at any plausible
threshold. The twin-fragment configuration of Chapter 8 sits at the other extreme: the imposed
boost (hot_spot_T_in = 900 K) overrides 𝑇ign by construction. The slow-cookoff cylinder of
Chapter 7 is the only case in this report whose handoff temperature lands close enough to the
threshold for 𝑇ign to be a first-order tuning knob. The guidance derived below is therefore based on
a sweep on that cylinder case; it applies broadly to all cookoff-coupled scenarios where the handoff
temperature sits near the kinetic switch threshold.

The slow-cookoff cylinder of Chapter 7 provides a clean illustration. The XCHEM-to-XDDT
handoff at the moment of thermal runaway carries a peak temperature of approximately 807 K—the
slow-cookoff thermal wave has nearly reached the BN ignition criterion at the outer-edge cells but
has not yet satisfied it at every cell. A sensitivity sweep at three values of 𝑇ign , with all other deck
parameters held identical to the reference SCO run, spans three qualitatively distinct regimes:

 • 𝑇ign = 525 K (HMX decomposition onset, the XDDT deck default). Many handoff cells are
 already above 525 K, so the Heaviside switch fires at 𝑡 = 0.10 µs on cells that are hot but
 not yet compacted. The surface-burn velocity peaks at 𝑟 burn ≈ 0.7 m/s—far below typical
 deflagration rates—and the bed pressure stalls near 0.7 GPa. The run terminates at 4.1 µs
 with the wave still pinned to the outer edge. Premature, non-physical ignition.

 • 𝑇ign = 800 K (this report’s value). The threshold sits just below the XCHEM handoff peak
 gas temperature, but the BN Heaviside switch evaluates on the granular surface temperature
 𝑇surface — a Biot-weighted blend of gas and bulk-solid temperatures, with an additional
 contribution from the surface-temperature integral 𝜁 that grows as the gas–grain temperature
 gradient accumulates over time ( [9], Eq. 14; see §1.3.3) — which lags 𝑇𝑔 when the bulk
 solid is cold and consequently sits well below 800 K at handoff despite the gas peak being
 only 7 K above threshold. Surface-burn fires at 𝑡 = 11.9 µs once compaction-driven heating
 has elevated 𝑇surface past 800 K. The wave sweeps inward 7.3 cm and reaches DDT at
 𝑥 DDT = 15.6 cm: peak mixture pressure climbs to 15.6 GPa, peak gas temperature approaches
 6000 K (near the EOS table ceiling), and the surface-burn velocity peaks at ≈29 m/s. Expected
 SCO deflagration-to-detonation behavior.

 • 𝑇ign = 1100 K (high-bound stress test). This case is conceptually the simplest of the three:
 the kinetic switch is set so high that the granular surface temperature never reaches it. The
 Heaviside condition in equation (1.1) stays at zero for the entire integration; no surface-burn
 ever fires; no chemical reaction occurs. The bed is not, however, inert. The compaction-
 burn term in the same equation does not depend on the kinetic switch, so the post-handoff
 pressure wave still drives the compaction front through the bed (sweeping from 𝑟 = 22.9 to
 7.65 cm). Surface burn never engages: the peak surface-burn velocity stays at 𝑟 burn ≈ 0.1 m/s
 (negligible) and no reaction wave forms. The burn-front tracker’s least-squares velocity fit,
 applied to this non-smooth, reaction-free velocity field (compaction-front motion alone),
 cannot be reliably interpreted: the large positive 𝐷 it reports in coupled.out is an artifact
 of that fit, and the wave speed is simply not cleanly characterizable for this case. This is a
 burn-front-tracker least-squares-fit limitation, distinct from the direction-aware diagnostics
 (§10.4) that the present work corrected. Mechanically the bed is in motion; chemically
 nothing is happening.

The brittleness of 𝑇ign in cookoff-coupled scenarios is itself an argument for the distributed-

same physics without requiring per-scenario threshold tuning.

6.4. Single-fragment quench versus multi-fragment propagation

The single-fragment fizzle of §6.1 does not arise from a failure to ignite the HMX. HMX is a reactive
material and a hot fragment readily drives the bed through thermal runaway at the contact interface
— the XCHEM cookoff history in panel (a) of Figure 6-1 shows exactly that. The fizzle arises
instead from a failure to sustain propagation once ignition has occurred. A single hot fragment
heats only a thin boundary layer of the surrounding HMX; the bulk beyond that layer remains
cold, and the cold bulk thermally suppresses the reaction front before a self-sustaining wave can
establish. In every coupled single-fragment configuration attempted in this work the locally-ignited
reaction quenched before propagation could be recovered, regardless of the fragment temperature
within plausible bounds.

Three paths to the preheated-bed condition the BN compaction-burn coupling requires for sustained
propagation are demonstrated elsewhere in this report: a longer cookoff time scale that lets heat
diffuse deeply into the bed (the slow cook-off cylinder of Chapter 7), multiple hot-fragment sources
whose thermal influence overlaps in the bed interior (the twin-fragment-impact case of Chapter 8,

 64

which produces detonation-class propagation precisely because the two-sided preheating supplies
the bed extent the BN compaction-burn coupling needs), and a hotter or larger driving source able
to reach further into the bed (the central steel-rod scenario considered as future work in Chapter 10).
The single-fragment-fizzle versus multi-fragment-DDT contrast is developed as a regime finding
in §9.2.1.

7.5. Interpretation

The wave initiates at the outer HMX/jacket interface, propagates inward through the cookoff-
preheated HMX bed, and amplifies via the cylinder’s 1/𝑟 geometric source term as it converges
toward the centerline. Within the simulated window it reaches DDT (at 𝑡DDT = 36.8 µs) and climbs
to ∼85% of 𝐷 CJ , with transition-territory signatures (surface-burn engagement, multi-GPa peak
pressures, multi-thousand-K peak temperatures, 1/𝑟 amplification visible in gauge data) all present,
but does not attain the full ideal detonation velocity within the bed. The principal factor in the
incomplete transition is that the inner core (𝑟 < 10 cm) was not cookoff-preheated, so the wave

8. TWIN-FRAGMENT IMPACT

This chapter reports the headline twin-fragment-impact demonstration of the XTIV pipeline. The
geometry — two hot steel fragments separated by a cold HMX bed — carries both thermal and
shock-like initiation features simultaneously, spanning the DDT and SDT regimes introduced in
§1.3.1. Two inward-propagating reactive waves develop, collide near mid-bed, and fire the classical
BN deflagration-to-detonation transition criterion at the collision. The configuration is exercised in
a boost-augmented mode — an asymmetric twin hot-spot patch (900/1100 K, 50/100 MPa) layered
on the XCHEM pre-thermal-runaway handoff — that exceeds natural cookoff for vented HMX;
the boost stands in for sub-grid-scale shock-driven ignition physics that XCHEM’s 1D thermal
model cannot resolve, and the resulting initial state is an idealized model assumption rather than a
physically realizable cookoff outcome. The detonation velocity recovered from this boosted initial
state (§8.3.1) is benchmarked against a TIGER hot-CJ calculation at the matching internal-energy
condition in §8.5, which establishes that the observed 𝐷 = 8.05 km/s is CJ-class for the artificially
elevated initial temperature state rather than an overdriven detonation in the classical sense. This
configuration turned out to be the most numerically demanding case in the validation matrix,
requiring a coordinated suite of methodology fixes before the run would integrate; §8.4 retains a
record of that development in case its lessons aid future coupled-pipeline work.
The boost-augmented run is one of two demonstrations this chapter reports. A companion fragment-
spacing sweep (§8.6) probes what the same laminate steel-HMX-steel geometry produces under
natural cookoff — with no imposed boost — across fragment spacings of 1, 2, and 4 cm. The
natural-cookoff cases are the physically realizable companion to the boost-augmented probe, and
they are illuminating in their own right: across the three fragment spacings the simulation either
fails to ignite (1 cm), ignites and briefly propagates before the two fronts collide (2 cm), or sustains
slow inward burning (4 cm); none of the three reaches the detonation regime the boost accesses.
Together the two demonstrations bracket the regime spectrum the coupled pipeline can resolve
for this geometry, from the fizzle-and-transition territory natural cookoff produces to the idealized
CJ-class ceiling the boost-imposed initial state admits.

8.1. Configuration

The bed is a three-layer Cartesian slab spanning 𝑥 ∈ [0, 12] cm: 4 cm of inert steel at 𝑇 = 575 K

in the middle (𝑥 ∈ [4, 8] cm). The HMX is at 70% theoretical maximum density (𝜙 𝑠,0 = 0.70,
𝜌 𝑠,0 = 1900 kg/m3 ), particle radius 𝑎 𝑝 = 50 µm, ignition temperature 𝑇ign = 800 K. The HMX

explosive remains at the initial value, with pressure-dependent reaction-rate terms reducing to
unity); the steel layers act as inert thermal reservoirs and as rigid boundaries on the XDDT side.

8.2. XCHEM precursor stage: pre-runaway handoff

The XCHEM cookoff phase resolves heat conduction from the hot steel fragments inward through
the HMX bed until just before chemical runaway. XCHEM is run with the maximum temperature
(tmax) set to the steel initial temperature plus a small margin (∼600 K, just above the 575 K
fragment temperature), which terminates the integration before any chemistry-driven temperature
spike develops. Typical cookoff-coupled practice sets tmax slightly above the maximum boundary
temperature — often a few hundred K higher, but rarely above ∼800 K — so the integration halts at
the onset of runaway with a modest overshoot (the SCO cylinder of Chapter 7 uses tmax ≈ 800 K
for that reason). Setting tmax far above the maximum boundary temperature is problematic:
near runaway the integrator can step several hundred to several thousand K in a single step, so a
permissive tmax produces a large overshoot and an artificially high ignition spike in the handoff
state. The choice here is more conservative still: at 600 K the integration halts well before chemistry
begins to run away, delivering a clean pre-runaway handoff on which the XDDT-side hot-spot boost
owns the entire ignition-to-DDT phase, as described in §8.4.
The handoff state is a single-zone .xtiv transfer file written by the xtiv_extract.py tool against
the schema-2 format. It carries a near-symmetric 𝑇 (𝑥) profile across the bed: bulk HMX still cool
(𝑇 ∼ 320–400 K in the central 1.5 cm), thermally graded outer slabs on both sides peaking just
inside the steel/HMX interfaces, and a small natural asymmetry favoring the RHS interface. This
is the clean pre-runaway state that the asymmetric twin hot-spot boost described in §8.4 layers on
top of: XDDT itself owns the entire ignition-to-DDT phase.

8.4. Methodology development: getting the run to integrate

The twin-fragment-impact case is the headline result of this report, but it is also the case that exposed
the largest number of methodology limitations in the coupled XTIV pipeline. Several configurations
failed before the boosted deck of §8.3 converged on a usable result; a brief development record is
preserved here both to substantiate the methodology choices in the deck and to flag the specific
failure modes for future coupled-pipeline work. Each prerequisite is reported in the SAND-2 Bug
/ How found / Fix style [2].

Symmetric boost configurations stalled the DDT diagnostic. Bug. Earlier attempts placed
equal-intensity hot-spot patches on both steel/HMX interfaces (configurations sym_1 and sym_3
in the development tree), reasoning from the geometric symmetry of the problem. Physically,
two inward fronts meeting symmetrically should produce a strong collision-frame pressure spike
— and they do in the BN field data — but the BN-DDT criterion as implemented could not
fire on the event. The criterion was originally coded for single-wave propagation (it watches a
designated burn front overtake the compaction-front-at-ignition position from one side), so when
two mirror-image waves converge with equal amplitude and zero net velocity at mid-bed, neither has
a “leading” role for the criterion to track. The tracker latched its burn-front peak onto the geometric
center, the single-side overtaking test never evaluated true, and the run returned without a recorded
DDT marker even though the field data showed the violent collision the symmetric configuration
should produce. How found. Burn-front trajectory plots from the symmetric-boost runs showed
both fronts arriving at mid-bed with comparable amplitudes and zero net velocity at the BF-peak
location, while the underlying 3D-field data showed strong pressure and temperature peaks at the
collision frame — the violent event was happening, the diagnostic just wasn’t recognizing it. Fix.
The boosted deck uses an asymmetric twin hot-spot boost matching the natural-cookoff intensity
profile carried in the XCHEM handoff: the RHS patch is hotter and at higher pressure than the LHS
patch, giving the RHS-driven front a slight head start. The asymmetry gives the single-direction

Fixed-mesh integration fails before any usable result. Bug. With imesh_in = 0 (fixed-

Adaptive mesh refinement defects. Bug. The converging twin-front geometry needs adap-

unidirectional cases of Chapter 6 did not exercise the refinement machinery comparably. Enabling
adaptive refinement on this case surfaced three latent defects in the refinement code: an indexing
inconsistency in how refined cells were associated back to the handoff state, a boundary-clamping
defect that allowed refined nodes to drift past the bed boundary during refinement, and an overly
tight cell-width-match tolerance that caused refinement to abort spuriously where the two converg-

next as the integrator progressed further into the run before failing again. The failure mode shifted
from a hard abort, to a refinement loop that never converged, to integrator tolerance failure right
at the converging-wave collision. Fix. Each defect was corrected as it surfaced. Most consequen-
tial was rephrasing the cell-width tolerance as a percentage of cell width rather than an absolute
floor, which preserves mesh integrity in the standard cases while letting refinement complete in the
converging-wave region. With those repairs in place the adaptive XDDT path correctly tracks both
inward-propagating fronts through the collision frame.

Dual-edge smoothing (both inner and outer steel/HMX interfaces). Bug. The original
handoff-state smoothing knob (§5) applied a tanh ramp on 𝑇, 𝑃, 𝜙 𝑠 , 𝜌𝑔 over the inner HMX edge
only. For the twin-fragment geometry, both steel/HMX interfaces carry ignition spikes in the
handoff, and the unsmoothed outer edge caused integrator failures within the first few hundred
nanoseconds. How found. Comparison runs with and without inner-edge smoothing showed
the outer-edge gradient as the load-bearing failure mode: inner-only smoothing did not improve
survival. Fix. A companion smoothing parameter was added that applies the tanh ramp at the outer
bed edge in addition to the inner edge. With both edges smoothed at 0.2 mm, the integrator clears
the handoff-state stiffness without erasing the boost-driven ignition spikes that follow. The 0.2 mm

standing in for the sub-grid-scale shock-driven ignition physics that XCHEM’s 1D thermal model
cannot resolve. A more principled XCHEM termination criterion remains future work (§10).

Premature bed-end termination of the 1.40 µs run. Bug. XDDT’s legacy bed-end check
terminates the run as soon as a burn-front trail reaches either bed boundary. In the twin-fragment
geometry the inward-propagating left-side front reaches the left bed-end at 𝑡 ≈ 1.40 µs — before the
two fronts have a chance to collide — and the check fires, terminating the integration mid-physics.
How found. Establishing that DDT fires at the front collision (rather than at one of the individual
inward-propagation events) required running well past 1.40 µs. The boosted deck alone could not
support that analysis. Fix. A namelist flag was added that lets the user disable the bed-end check
when the physics calls for it. With the flag set, the integration continues through the front-on-
front collision and the post-collision retreat until the solver fails for other reasons at 𝑡 ≈ 9.70 µs.
The extended run is therefore reproducible from the boosted deck plus that single flag, with no
source modifications required. Direction-aware bed-end checking — terminating only when a front
reaches the boundary it is actually propagating toward — is the more principled long-term fix and
is folded into the broader direction-aware diagnostic redesign in §10.

𝐷 fitter behavior on non-monotone trajectories. Bug. The least-squares 𝐷 fitter operates
on the burn-front peak position as a function of time and assumes a monotone trajectory. On

and then retreats inward; the resulting non-monotone trajectory yields 𝐷 = −1.18 km/s, which
has neither sign nor magnitude that corresponds to a physically meaningful detonation velocity.

trajectory plot made the non-monotone structure obvious. Fix. Until the fitter is generalized to
handle non-monotone trajectories, the recommended convention is to cite 𝐷 from the 1.40 µs

well-suited to keeping the fitter inside the monotone-propagation window, even though the cap was
not designed for that purpose.

8.5. Hot-CJ benchmark for the boosted initial state

The 1.40 µs detonation velocity 𝐷 = +8.05 km/s (§8.3.1) was originally reported as “above 𝐷 CJ ”
against the TIGER cold-granular CJ value of 6.36 km/s for HMX at 𝑇0 = 300 K, 𝜌0 = 1.33 g/cc
(70% TMD). That framing is incorrect: the cold-granular CJ applies to a bed that the boost-
augmented configuration is no longer in. The asymmetric twin hot-spot boost layers ∼900–1100 K
initial temperature on top of the XCHEM pre-runaway handoff, and the BN compaction wave
running ahead of the burn front squeezes the granular bed above its initial 0.70 packing toward
TMD (§8.3.3). The wave is therefore running into hot, partially compacted HMX, whose CJ ceiling
sits well above the cold-granular reference. Benchmarking the observed 𝐷 against the appropriate
hot-bed CJ velocity is the correct comparison; this section provides it.

8.5.1. TIGER hot-CJ calculation

The reference CJ velocities are computed with TIGER, the thermochemical equilibrium code, using
the JCZS3 product database and the JCZ3 equation of state for the detonation products. The hot-bed
condition is imposed at the level of the unreacted-HMX initial state: the internal energy is set to
𝑒 initial = 1.38 kJ/g (= 329.7 cal/g), the value HMX attains at ∼ 1000 K, matching the elevated initial
temperature established by the asymmetric twin hot-spot boost ahead of the inward-propagating
reactive fronts. At each of three densities — 𝜌 = 1.20, 1.33, 1.90 g/cc, spanning the granular initial
state through to TMD — TIGER returns:

 𝜌 (g/cc) 𝐷 CJ (km/s)
 1.20 6.89
 1.33 7.31
 1.90 9.33

A linear fit through the three points gives 𝐷 CJ (𝜌) = 3.50 𝜌 + 2.67 km/s on the hot-bed trend. The
TIGER calculation does not resolve transport or shock dynamics — it is a Chapman-Jouguet steady-
wave calculation against an idealized hot-bed initial state — and the result is a thermodynamic
ceiling rather than a prediction of the BN model’s transient behavior. Its value here is as a
benchmark for the BN model’s recovered 𝐷: agreement between the two establishes thermodynamic
consistency between the boosted-deck wave and the CJ steady state for the same initial-energy
condition.

8.5.3. What the bracket establishes, and what it does not

The CJ bracket establishes a single, narrow claim: that the BN model’s boosted-DDT velocity of
𝐷 = 8.05 km/s is thermodynamically consistent with the CJ ceiling for the boost-imposed initial
state, at the partial-compaction density the BN compaction wave produces ahead of the front. The
wave is running at its CJ ceiling for the bed it sees, not above it; the original “overdriven” label was
an artefact of comparing against the wrong baseline.
The bracket does not establish field realism. Both the TIGER hot-CJ calculation and the BN
boosted-DDT integration take an idealized initial state as input. HMX in a real cookoff scenario
does not pre-equilibrate to 𝑒 initial = 1.38 kJ/g uniformly, and the 900/1100 K boost layered onto the
XDDT handoff exceeds what natural cookoff of vented HMX produces (Chapter 6 demonstrates the
natural- cookoff outcome for the matched single-fragment slab geometry: fizzle, not detonation).

The TIGER and BN calculations are comparative model outcomes against an idealized hot-bed
initial state, not predictions of a physically realizable detonation following a realistic cookoff
handoff.
What the bracket contributes, then, is methodological rather than predictive. It shows that the
BN model’s response to a sufficiently energetic preheated and compacted initial state is internally

end of the regime spectrum the coupled pipeline can resolve, with the hot-CJ benchmark providing
the thermodynamic anchor for that upper bound.

8.6. Natural-cookoff baseline: fragment-spacing sensitivity

Having presented the boost-augmented run — the idealized upper-bound probe and its CJ-class
wave — the chapter now turns to what the same laminate steel-HMX-steel geometry produces
under natural cookoff, without any imposed boost. The natural-cookoff cases are the physically
realizable companion to the boosted result, and they are themselves illuminating: the fragment-
spacing sweep reported here spans three distinct positions in the cookoff-regime spectrum, with
the outcome depending non-monotonically on the fragment spacing. The sweep also grounds the
boost choice in the boosted deck: the boost is the methodology lever that lifts the accessible regime
from the fizzle-and-transition territory natural cookoff produces in this geometry up to the CJ-class
ceiling benchmarked in §8.5, not an arbitrary parameter probe.

8.6.1. Configuration and parameter sweep

The natural-cookoff sensitivity study sweeps the distance between the two hot fragments — 1, 2,
and 4 cm — while holding the surrounding steel dimension at 4 cm on each side of the porous
HMX. The BN model parameters are held identical across all three cases. The natural-cookoff
configurations differ from the boosted deck of §8.3 in three respects.
First, the steel temperatures are lower than the boosted deck’s 575 K: 𝑇steel = 530 K for the 1 cm
bed, 540 K for the 2 and 4 cm beds. The reduction in fragment temperature was made to maximize
the thermal soak between the two hot steel fragments.
Second, XCHEM is run through natural ignition rather than terminated at low tmax as the boosted
deck does. The natural-cookoff XCHEM trajectories run for ∼12, ∼25, and ∼67 minutes respectively
before bed-state runaway is captured. The XCHEM-to-XDDT handoff is then taken at the final
XCHEM state, with the natural-cookoff ignition spikes preserved into the XDDT initial condition.
Third, the BN ignition temperature 𝑇ign is reduced from its default 800 K value to just above the
smaller of the two natural-cookoff handoff spikes per the XTIV 𝑇ign calibration convention, so that
the surface burning switch arms cleanly on the higher-temperature side at handoff and engages with
modest compaction work on the cooler side. The 1 cm case uses 𝑇ign = 600 K; 2 cm uses 586 K;
4 cm uses 600 K.

8.6.3. Fragment spacing as the regime parameter

The fragment-spacing sweep exposes a non-monotonic relationship between two-sided preheating
overlap and XDDT survival. Counter to a naive “more preheating means more reaction” expectation,
the 1 cm bed — which has the most intense two-sided preheating in the XCHEM phase — produces
the shortest XDDT integration of any of the three cases. The mechanism is straightforward: full
two-sided overlap produces a localized spike whose gradients exceed what the BN integrator can
resolve at the handoff seam. Spreading the two heat sources further apart relaxes those gradients.
The 2 cm case admits two distinct interface spikes and a clean compaction-then-collision evolution;
the 4 cm case admits the longest-surviving sustained-propagation evolution.

None of the three natural-cookoff cases approach the detonation regime. The 2 and 4 cm cases
produce transition propagation at |𝐷| ∼ 1.4–1.7 km/s, well below the cold-granular CJ velocity
(𝐷 CJ = 6.36 km/s) and below the cylinder slow-cookoff case’s transition velocity (|𝐷| = 5.386 km/s,
Chapter 7). The 4 cm case here is particularly informative: it matches the boosted twin-fragment-
impact deck’s fragment spacing (4 cm) but with the natural-cookoff handoff state in place of the
boost-augmented initial condition. Its outcome — transition propagation at |𝐷| = 1.43 km/s —
establishes the natural-cookoff baseline for this fragment spacing.

The natural-cookoff baseline puts the boost choice of the boosted deck in context. The asymmetric
twin hot-spot boost of §8.3 is what extends the accessible regime from natural-cookoff transition
propagation (|𝐷| ∼ 1.4 km/s, this section) up through the CJ-class detonation regime (|𝐷| =
8.05 km/s, §8.3.1) for the artificially elevated initial state benchmarked in §8.5. The boost was
necessary to let the boosted deck exercise the BN model’s upper-bound detonation regime against
an idealized hot, partially compacted initial state. Natural cookoff of the same geometry, without
boost, cannot reach that regime — at least under the current surface-burn model, which uses a
Heaviside switch on 𝑇ign . Replacing that switch with Arrhenius kinetics is identified as future
work.

8.7. Interpretation: hybrid initiation regime

The twin-fragment-impact case occupies a hybrid position in the DDT/SDT initiation-regime space
introduced in §1.3.1. The heat conducting inward from the steel fragments is a thermal component
that XCHEM resolves correctly, producing a graded preconditioning of the HMX bulk and the
small natural asymmetry between interfaces. The compaction-driven hot-spot formation at each
steel/HMX interface — which a 1D thermal code cannot resolve — is a shock-like component
captured here through the asymmetric twin hot-spot boost in the XDDT phase. Neither XCHEM-
only (which would miss the compaction-driven hot-spot seeding) nor XDDT-only (which would
miss the thermal preconditioning of the bulk) is sufficient.
The DDT outcome differs qualitatively from the single-fragment slab case of Chapter 6 and from
the converging-wave cylinder of Chapter 7. Where the cylinder DDT emerges from a single inward-
propagating reactive wave amplifying through geometric focusing, and where the single-fragment
slab fails to engage the surface-burn kinetics at all (contact-face ignition without bulk preheating),
the twin-fragment-impact case fires the BN-DDT criterion at the collision of two opposing reactive
waves. The classical intensity-amplification picture of converging-wave detonation transition is
replaced by an interaction picture in which the DDT criterion fires at the moment two compaction
fronts meet — a third regime, beyond the cookoff and SDT regimes that XTIV is built to span, but
produced naturally as a consequence of the two-sided initial condition.
The headline detonation velocity — 𝐷 = +8.05 km/s — is the strongest DDT outcome reported in
this report’s demonstration matrix. Its relationship to the standard CJ benchmarks deserves care.
Comparing against the cold-granular CJ (𝐷 CJ = 6.36 km/s at 𝑇0 = 300 K, 𝜌0 = 1.33 g/cc) would
label the result as overdriven by ∼ 27%, but that comparison crosses initial states: the cold-CJ
baseline applies to a bed that the boost-augmented configuration is no longer in. The TIGER
hot-CJ bracketing of §8.5 places the observed 𝐷 = 8.05 km/s squarely on the hot-CJ trend at the
partial- compaction density the BN compaction wave produces ahead of the front, supporting the
interpretation that the wave is running at the CJ ceiling for the bed it actually sees — not above
it. The result is thermodynamically consistent rather than anomalous, but the initial state on which
the consistency holds is itself an idealization.
The numerical fragility of the boosted configuration, documented in §8.4, suggests that the boost-
augmented result should be interpreted with care: small changes in the boost intensity, the smooth-
ing length, or the XCHEM termination criterion materially affect the outcome, and the boosted deck
as written is best understood as a demonstration of the qualitative DDT mechanism rather than as
a calibrated quantitative prediction. The natural-cookoff fragment-spacing sweep of §8.6 sharpens
that reading: across fragment spacings of 1, 2, and 4 cm the same geometry produces solver failure,
transition-class compaction-then-collision propagation, and sustained inward propagation respec-
tively, with no case reaching the detonation regime the boosted deck accesses. Together with the
natural-cookoff demonstrations of Chapter 6 (single-fragment fizzle) and Chapter 7 (slow cook-off
transition-territory propagation), the fragment-spacing sweep provides the bracketing physically
realizable cases for this geometry; the boosted twin-fragment configuration here probes the upper-
bound CJ-class regime that none of those natural-cookoff cases access. Future work directions for
tightening the configuration are outlined in §10.

9.2. Bulk-preheating extent as the regime parameter

Two competing reaction mechanisms govern wave fate after ignition. The compaction-driven burn
term (alstar) sustains reaction at low velocity in cold bed but cannot reach DDT alone. The
pressure-dependent surface-burn term (a2 (𝑃/𝑃ref ) bn 𝐻) requires the local mixture temperature to
exceed 𝑇ign , which typically obtains only in preheated bed — or, in pure SDT scenarios, behind
a shock front strong enough that mechanical heating drives 𝑇mix above 𝑇ign . Reaching DDT
requires surface-burn engagement; the question for any given coupled scenario is whether the bed
configuration already supplies the threshold temperature ahead of the wave, or whether the wave
must generate it through its own mechanical work.
In natural cookoff handoffs the latter path is closed. The ignition-region pressures produced by
realistic cookoff are not strong enough to mechanically drive 𝑇mix above 𝑇ign in cold downstream
bed. A cold-bed natural- cookoff wave therefore propagates only as long as compaction- driven
burn sustains it, which is to say: as a slow deflagration that decays once the source-region energy
depletes. This is the mechanism behind the fizzle outcome of the single-fragment slab in §6.1.
In preheated-bed scenarios, by contrast, the bed itself supplies the threshold temperature; the
surface-burn term engages in the preheated band, the wave amplifies via compaction-burn feedback
(and via geometric focusing in cylindrical or spherical geometries), and the transition-territory or
detonation-class regimes become accessible.

9.2.1. Fragment count and bulk preheating extent

The bulk-preheating-extent regime parameter has a direct implication for the fragment-impact
problem. A single hot fragment in contact with cold reactive material lacks the thermal mass
to preheat the bulk through 𝑇ign over more than a millimeter-scale skin (§6.4); the local ignition
that results is single-cell-class, the surrounding bulk is too cold to support sustained surface-burn
kinetics, and the wave never establishes outward propagation at all. This is the regime exemplified
by the single-fragment slab demonstration of §6.1, which produces 𝐷 ≈ −2 km/s (burn-front
activity pinned at the steel/HMX interface, no propagating wave, no surface-burn engagement) —
the fizzle outcome.
Multiple fragments, by contrast, can preheat the bulk from both sides. Their thermal influence
overlaps in the bed interior and provides the multi-centimeter preheated extent that the BN model’s
compaction-burn coupling requires to engage the surface-burn kinetics across enough bed length for
sustained DDT-class propagation. The twin-fragment-impact case (§8, 𝐷 ≈ 8 km/s) demonstrates
this directly: two converging hot fragments produce sufficient two-sided preheating that the BN
model fires the DDT criterion at the front-on-front collision near mid-bed, yielding detonation-class
velocity at or above 𝐷 CJ .
The contrast between the two cases reveals a regime parameter that connects fragment-impact
analysis to the bulk-preheating- extent regime structure: fragment count and coordination control
bulk preheating extent, which in turn controls the accessible reaction regime in the BN model. Iso-
lated fragments deliver localized ignition without bulk preheating and produce benign deflagration
outcomes; coordinated fragment groups delivering simultaneous two-sided or multi-source thermal

 93

loading can preheat the bed sufficiently to support DDT-class propagation. The slow-cookoff cylin-
der (§7) provides a third path to bulk preheating — via slow thermal diffusion from a heated outer
boundary over hours — producing transition-territory propagation in the same regime structure.
Methodology contribution. Characterizing the BN model’s response to realistic ignition scenarios
requires resolving bulk preheating extent, not just the local ignition spike. Coupled cookoff-to-
DDT modeling that handles only the ignition site and not the surrounding bed thermal state cannot
distinguish weak-deflagration cases from transition-territory or detonation-class cases, because the
distinguishing physics lives in the preheated HMX bed (or absence thereof) rather than at the
ignition kernel.

9.3. Implications for IM safety analysis

A scope reminder before drawing implications. The four demonstrations are deliberately exercised
against a sensitive HMX fill, not an IM-qualified composition. This is a methodology choice:
the pipeline cannot be tested against the violent-response regime if the fill never reaches that
regime under the imposed conditions. Sensitive HMX in realistic geometries under standardized
AOP-4382 heating guarantees that the XCHEM–XDDT chain is exercised end-to-end against post-
ignition wave dynamics, where the modeling capabilities reported here are needed. The implications
below are about the modeling capabilities the pipeline brings to IM safety analysis, not about IM
compliance of the specific demonstration configurations.
The regime structure of §9.1 carries direct implications for IM safety analysis. Two are worth
calling out explicitly.
First, the natural-cookoff result for the 18-inch sensitive-HMX cylinder under NATO AOP-4382
conditions shows transition-territory propagation rather than full detonation, despite the deliberately
sensitive fill. That outcome — transition-class response on a configuration chosen to favor the
violent-response regime — demonstrates that the pipeline can resolve the margin between transition-
territory and detonation-class outcomes without conflating them, which is the property an IM-design
model needs. An IM-qualified fill in the same geometry would be expected to land further down
the regime spectrum (deflagration or weak transition), and the pipeline’s ability to track that shift
quantitatively is the capability IM design depends on. The design lever the regime parameter
exposes is the preheat penetration ratio (the fraction of bed depth preheated through 𝑇ign at the
moment of ignition): reducing bed size relative to cookoff timescale, or extending cookoff timescale
through case-design choices that slow heat conduction inward, both shift the response toward DDT.
Conversely, design choices that bound the preheated bed extent (insulating layers, rapid case rupture,
smaller charges) preserve cold-bed bulk and therefore the deflagration-class margin.
Second, the fragment-count regime structure of §9.2.1 is directly applicable to fragment-impact
threat scenarios. Incident fragments arriving isolated and asynchronously to a reactive bed de-
liver localized ignition without bulk preheating, producing benign deflagration-class outcomes.
Coherent fragment groups arriving simultaneously at multiple bed surfaces can preheat the bed
sufficiently to support DDT-class propagation. This distinction connects fragment-impact analy-
sis to the bulk-preheating-extent regime parameter and provides a principled basis for evaluating

 94

fragment-threat severity in IM design analysis: the relevant figure of merit is not the peak fragment
temperature in isolation but the spatial coherence and simultaneity of multiple incident fragments
and their resulting cumulative preheating extent in the bed at the moment of ignition.

10. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

The pipeline as documented in this report exercises the XCHEM–XDDT methodology end-to-
end on the demonstration matrix of Chapters 6 through 8, but several issues surfaced during the
current development that the present work cannot close. Each is described below: its current state,
its impact on the present results, and the path to resolution. The list is enumerated rather than
exhaustive — these are the items that emerged with enough clarity during demonstration runs to be
worth recording as bounded future work, not a comprehensive audit of every known shortcoming.

10.1. Outer-boundary-condition implementation

Current state. XDDT’s outer boundary condition on the IBC=3 path currently falls back to a
Neumann (zero-gradient, transmissive) treatment for all non-trivial confinement strings carried in
the .xtiv transfer file. The free, wall, and layer:X options that the schema allows are all
reduced to the same Neumann implementation in the present build; only one outer-BC type is
genuinely available. The same dispatcher serves the inner edge for hollow cylindrical and spherical
configurations (§2.3), so the layer:X Neumann fallback applies identically there: hollow annular
or shell beds run today with free (Neumann) inner and outer surfaces, with finite-impedance case
material deferred to the same impedance-match work described below.

Impact on present results. The Neumann fallback lets reaction products vent through the outer
boundary in every demonstration. The effect is most visible in the slow cook-off cylinder of
Chapter 7, where the inward-propagating wave’s late-time deceleration is partially attributable to
the open outer boundary: a true confining case would maintain pressure behind the wave longer
and likely extend transition-territory behavior further toward detonation.

Path to resolution. Add a second outer-BC type — an impedance-matched condition that couples
the reactive bed to a finite-impedance confining layer through reflection and transmission coeffi-
cients computed from the confining material’s acoustic properties. This is the boundary condition
needed for steel-jacketed warhead-class geometries where the case is neither rigid (perfect reflector)
nor transparent (the Neumann fallback’s effective behavior); it would also subsume the rigid-wall
case as the infinite-impedance limit. The work is bounded scope — adding the impedance-match
branch to the existing BC dispatcher — but requires care in the dispatch logic to preserve backward
compatibility with the Neumann path exercised by the current demonstrations.

 97

10.2. One-dimensional cylindrical and spherical symmetry as a modeling
 idealization

Current state. The XTIV pipeline implements one-dimensional cookoff-to-DDT analysis in three
geometry options matching the pre-ignition XCHEM solver: slab, cylinder, and sphere. In the
cylindrical and spherical cases the 1D treatment implicitly assumes ignition propagates uniformly
along the symmetry coordinate — i.e., a ring of finite radius for the cylindrical case or a shell of
finite radius for the spherical case ignites simultaneously, then drives a 1D-radial reactive wave
inward toward the geometric center. This is the standard idealization underlying 1D cookoff
modeling generally, but it is worth acknowledging explicitly that simultaneous ring/shell ignition
over a multi-centimeter radius is physically unrealizable for natural-cookoff scenarios in practice;
in the real world, ignition occurs at a point (or a small cluster of points), not over an extended ring
or shell of perfect simultaneity.
Impact on present results. The 1D symmetry assumption affects what claims the cylinder
demonstration of Chapter 7 can support. The inward-propagating converging-wave amplification
observed there is a consequence of the imposed cylindrical symmetry, not a prediction of what a
physically asymmetric cookoff event would produce. In a real cookoff scenario where ignition is
localized to a single point or a small cluster of points on or near the outer wall, the early-time wave
structure would be predominantly outward-expansion-driven (point-source giving an expanding-
shock geometry) rather than converging-inward, and the 1/𝑟 geometric-focusing amplification
mechanism the 1D analysis exposes would be attenuated to whatever extent the real event departs
from the symmetric ring/shell idealization. Asymmetry is intrinsic to the physical problem. Even
in the nominally symmetric slab geometries of this report the natural-cookoff XCHEM phase
produces non-trivial asymmetry between the two steel/HMX interfaces (§8.2), so the symmetric
configurations enforced by the 1D cylindrical and spherical solvers are stricter idealizations still.
Where the idealization is closer to reality. Two cases worth identifying narrow the gap between
the 1D idealization and the physical problem. First, in external-heating cookoff tests such as the
One-Dimensional Time-to-eXplosion (ODTX) sphere (§10.3), where the outer-wall temperature
is held uniform around the entire surface, uniform external heating delivers near-uniform shell-
surface temperatures and shell-simultaneous ignition is approximately what the test is designed to
produce; the 1D shell idealization is, in this case, close to the physical reality the test embodies.
Second, point-source ignition events — such as a compaction-driven hot spot in the interior of the
bed — propagate outward in approximately spherical fashion, which is captured cleanly by the 1D
spherical solver if the hot spot is treated as a centered initial condition. The configurations where
1D symmetry is most idealized are those where ignition is at the outer boundary and the natural
physical scenario involves localized rather than distributed initiation; the slow cook-off cylinder of
Chapter 7 falls in this category.
Why the idealization is retained. Two practical considerations support the 1D approach in the
present work. First, the full Baer-Nunziato two-phase model with the BN compaction-burn coupling
has no published 3D detonation implementation; even 2D axisymmetric integration of the full BN
model in the production-grade hydrocode CTH was attempted during this work and could not be
brought to a converged result. The 1D analysis is therefore the practical state of the art for end-to-end
demonstration of the BN-coupled cookoff-DDT methodology, not a deliberate restriction. Second,

 98

the quantitative effects the 1D demonstrations expose — geometric acceleration, inward-front
amplification, collision-frame pressure spikes — are real physical mechanisms that are present and
observable even in geometries that depart from strict 1D symmetry. The 1D analysis identifies the
mechanism; multi-dimensional work would calibrate the magnitude. Hot fragments themselves are
not spherical either, so the geometric idealization in the present work is one approximation among
many that the broader cookoff modeling program accepts in service of end-to-end demonstration.

Transverse confinement is absent by construction. A second consequence of the 1D treatment,
related to but distinct from the ring/shell ignition idealization above, is the implicit treatment of
confinement. In physical 2D or 3D charges, whether a deflagration grows into a steady detonation
depends on confinement against lateral pressure relief: case-wall strength, hoop stress, and the
geometry of any vent or unconfined surface perpendicular to the wave direction. A charge with weak
lateral confinement can fail to support DDT even when bulk kinetics and porosity are otherwise
favorable. In the 1D treatment used here there is no transverse coordinate along which products
can expand, so lateral confinement is implicitly perfect; the only confinement degree of freedom is
the outer (or inner) boundary condition discussed in §10.1, which represents wall reflection along
the symmetry direction only. The 1D model therefore tends to favor wave growth and propagation
relative to a real charge with a finite-strength case and finite lateral inertia. Quantitative differences
between simulated wave speeds, transition times, or peak pressures and a physical analog experiment
can be due in part to this missing lateral-relief channel rather than to errors in the bulk kinetics
or constitutive parameters; the 1D demonstrations in this report should therefore be read as upper-
bound estimates of geometric-focusing and converging-wave amplification under idealized lateral
confinement, not as predictions for laterally-vented configurations.

Path to resolution. A multi-dimensional implementation of the full BN compaction-burn coupling,
with the equation-stiffness and grid-resolution constraints of the 1D solver carried into a 2D or 3D
framework, is the proper resolution to the 1D symmetry idealization. The present work establishes
that the 1D path is itself sufficiently constrained by equation stiffness and grid resolution to leave
clear room for methodology development before multi-dimensional work becomes tractable, but the
multi-dimensional generalization remains the right long-term direction for the BN-coupled cookoff-
DDT methodology. Until that work is available, the 1D cylindrical and spherical results in this
report should be read as identifying the geometric-focusing and converging-wave mechanisms in
the model rather than as quantitative predictions of physical events with locally varying ignition.

10.3. Spherical-geometry stability and the ODTX handoff window

A natural extension of the demonstration matrix in this report would be a spherical-geometry case
covering the One-Dimensional Time-to-eXplosion (ODTX) test—a standard IM-relevant scenario
consisting of a small (∼0.25-inch radius) HMX core inside an aluminum jacket held at a fixed
boundary temperature, with thermal runaway expected on a timescale of seconds to minutes.
Sphere geometry is also the strongest available test of geometric DDT amplification: the 1/𝑟 2
source term concentrates inward-propagating waves more aggressively than the 1/𝑟 source of the
slow-cook-off cylinder demonstrated in Chapter 7. The ODTX test was attempted during the current

 99

development and produced three distinct, instructive limitations of the present XTIV pipeline that
together prevent a clean sphere demonstration in this report.

Sub-zone XCHEM runaway. HMX thermal runaway in ODTX is intrinsically explosive: the
kinetic chain reaction has its own timescale (set by the chemistry, not the heating rate), and once
the kinetic threshold is crossed locally, the temperature transitions from the soak plateau (∼550 K)
to the post-runaway state (∼3000 K) in tens of milliseconds. Three XCHEM cookoff runs were
attempted at outer-wall boundary temperatures of 555.55 K (standard 540◦ F), 500 K, and 475 K,
with progressively denser zone-output cadences (0.1 s intervals at the slowest case). In every case
the transition through the SAND-2 / SCO-equivalent handoff window (∼800–1200 K) compressed
into a single consecutive zone-output snapshot, leaving no intermediate state available for extraction.
The runaway is genuinely sub-zone at any practical XCHEM print cadence—only the slow-soak
phase (𝑇 < 600 K) and the post-runaway state (𝑇 > 3000 K) are resolvable, with no zones in
between.

Stop-at-𝑇 workaround. The runaway-window problem can be sidestepped by lowering the
XCHEM solver’s runaway threshold (the tmax parameter in tchem.in), causing XCHEM to halt
as soon as any cell reaches a chosen handoff temperature. Setting tmax = 800 K produced a clean
handoff state at 𝑇max = 957 K (overshoot is set by the integrator catching the threshold within a
step) on the 475 K outer-wall ODTX run, with the hot region distributed across cells 1–6 of the
HMX bed. This is a usable methodology recipe for any future fast-cookoff scenario: set XCHEM
tmax to the desired handoff temperature rather than the kinetic-chain-completion threshold, and
XCHEM will halt at the onset of runaway.

Smoothing-convention mismatch for cold-bulk handoffs. The handoff produced by the
stop-at-𝑇 workaround is the problematic kind of state for the XDDT integrator. Unlike the SCO
cylinder—where slow soak warms the entire bed roughly uniformly and the handoff peak is just a
single-cell spike above an already warm bulk—the ODTX sphere has a sharp multi-cell hot shell
(𝑇 = 957 K at cell 1, decreasing through 951, 929, 871, 787, 706 K at cells 2–6) abutting a
still-cold bulk (𝑇 ≈ 530 K in the deep interior). The current handoff-state smoothing knob (§5)
was designed for the SCO-cylinder regime, where the inner-edge “feature” is one cell wide and the
smoothing target is a warm bulk. Applied to the ODTX configuration, the tanh ramp pulls the entire
multi-cell hot shell toward the cold-bulk target 𝑇 ≈ 537 K, erasing the only reactive feature: with
xtiv_smooth_l_in = 0.0001 m (0.1 mm, ∼2 cells wide), the post-smoothing state has 𝑇 ≤ 686 K
throughout the bed—below any reasonable 𝑇ign , so the surface-burn switch fires nowhere and no
reaction develops.

Integrator failure on the unsmoothed handoff. Setting the smoothing length xtiv_smooth
_l_in to zero (smoothing disabled) preserves the multi-cell hot shell but exposes the underlying
issue: the gradient at the hot-face / cold-bulk transition is too sharp for the BN integrator in
spherical geometry. Tracing the 𝑡 = 0.1 µs output snapshot shows clean integration through the
cold portion of the bed (𝑥 = 0 to 𝑥 ≈ 0.62 cm, with 𝑇𝑔 ≈ 475–680 K and burn rate identically

 100

zero), then numerical chaos at the hot face (𝑥 ≈ 0.63 cm onward): gas temperature pinned at the
TIGER table cap of 6000 K, solid temperature exceeding 104 K, mixture pressure overflowing the
format width, and the local burn rate growing from zero through −1.3 × 104 to −2.1 × 1025 kg/m3 /s
in three consecutive grid cells. The integrator catches the divergence and halts with IDID = −6
at 𝑡 = 61 ns. Schema 2 handoff (§3.3.1) and adaptive mesh refinement (imesh_in = 1) are both
enabled in this run; neither mitigates the failure mode.

Path forward. The sphere demonstration is therefore deferred to future work, with three concrete
enhancement directions identified by the present exploration:

 1. Smoothing-convention generalization for cold-bulk handoffs. The smoothing routine should
 detect the multi-cell extent of the handoff hot region (rather than assuming a single-cell spike)
 and choose its smoothing target from a node just inside the hot region’s trailing edge, not
 from the deep-bed bulk. Bounded scope; replaces a few lines in xtiv_glue.f90.
 2. Event-driven XCHEM zone output. Augment the time-cadence-based zone print with an
 event trigger that forces a zone whenever local 𝑇 crosses configurable thresholds (e.g., 600 K,
 800 K, 1200 K, 2000 K). The tmax stop-at-𝑇 recipe described above is a special case of this;
 full event-driven output would capture the full runaway transition for diagnostic purposes
 even when stop-at-𝑇 isn’t desired.
 3. Distributed activation-energy (DAE) rate replacement (§10.5). A smooth Arrhenius/DAE
 rate in place of the empirical 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 𝐻 form would soften the kinetic chain reaction itself,
 making runaway less explosive in XCHEM and the post-handoff dynamics less susceptible to
 integrator instabilities at the hot face in XDDT. This is the most fundamental of the three paths
 because it addresses the root cause—a binary burn rate that turns on at a threshold—rather
 than its downstream symptoms.

The combination of these three enhancements is expected to make the ODTX sphere case a feasible
demonstration in the next report of the DDT series.

10.4. Direction-aware diagnostics

Current state. Three diagnostic subsystems — the compaction-front tracker, the BN-DDT crite-
rion, and the bed-end halt check — were originally implemented under an outward-propagation
assumption inherited from the piston-driven cases of SAND-1 and SAND-2. That assumption fails
for cookoff-driven cases, where ignition is at the outer edge and waves propagate inward. The
current work added a propagation-direction signal computed from the recent burn-front history,
and rewired all three diagnostics to consult it. The direction-aware redesign was prerequisite to
interpreting the slow cook-off cylinder of Chapter 7 cleanly.
Remaining limitation. The direction-aware diagnostics handle single-direction propagation (out-
ward or inward) correctly, but they do not natively handle the two-front converging-wave case the
twin-fragment demonstration of Chapter 8 exercises. In a symmetric two-front collision, neither
front has a clear “leading” role, and the single-direction DDT criterion (“burn front overtakes the

 101

compaction-front-at-ignition position from one side”) cannot fire on the collision event even when
the field data shows the violent pressure spike that defines DDT. Chapter 8 works around this with
an asymmetric twin hot-spot boost that gives the criterion a designated leading front, but this is a
diagnostic workaround rather than a clean physical generalization.
Path to resolution. Generalize the DDT criterion to handle two-front converging-wave geometries
natively: detect that two propagation-direction signals coexist (one positive, one negative), and
reformulate the overtaking test as a collision-frame test rather than a single-side test. The bed-end
halt check would benefit from the same generalization, terminating only when a front reaches
the boundary it is actually propagating toward (rather than firing on the originating-end reaction
of either side). Bounded scope; the propagation-direction infrastructure already exists, only the
comparator logic in the three diagnostics needs to be extended.

10.5. True Arrhenius and distributed activation energy kinetics

The pressure-dependent surface-burn rate 𝑎 2 (𝑃/𝑃ref ) 𝑏 𝑛 𝐻 used in the BN compaction equation
(eq. 1.1 of §1.3.2) is an empirical 𝑟 = 𝑎𝑃𝑛 form commonly used to fit solid-propellant strand-
burn data [15], turned on by a Heaviside step at a single ignition threshold 𝑇ign . Although it is
sometimes referred to as the “Arrhenius rate” in BN literature, it is not a true Arrhenius rate in
the chemical-kinetics sense—it lacks the explicit activation energy 𝐸 𝑎 and pre-exponential factor
𝐴 that a chemistry-derived rate would carry, and the temperature dependence is reduced to a hard
threshold rather than a smooth exp(−𝐸 𝑎 /𝑅𝑇) activation.
The path forward, motivated by the Universal Cookoff Model (UCM) [4, 19, 20] that already drives
the XCHEM prethermal-ignition phase of the present pipeline, is to replace the empirical 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 𝐻
surface-burn form with the distributed activation-energy (DAE) rate expression used in UCM:

unified DAE framework could treat both the thermal-driven and mechanical-driven pathways with
separate activation-energy distributions, but the surface-burn replacement described here is the first
and most urgent step.
Two motivations support this enhancement. First, it unifies the kinetics representation between
the cookoff (XCHEM, DAE-based) and the post-ignition (XDDT, currently 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 with Heaviside
gate) phases of the XTIV pipeline, eliminating the present discontinuity at the handoff frame where
the kinetic representation changes mid-simulation. Second, the DAE parameter space resolves the
calibration overload identified in §1.3.2—where the present two-parameter 𝑎 2 , 𝑏 𝑛 surface-burn form
must absorb physics it does not structurally represent, leading to parameters that seldom transfer
between scenarios. The DAE form can match multiple regimes simultaneously (strand burn,
piston-driven DDT, slow cookoff, fragment impact) within a single calibrated rate expression,
and it generalizes naturally across explosive material classes from ideal (HMX-like) to non-ideal
(AN-like).
Path: Implementation of the DAE surface-burn rate as a drop-in replacement for the 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 𝐻 term
in the XDDT compaction equation; recalibration against the existing SAND-1/SAND-2 validation
matrix (McAfee piston-driven DDT [11], Price–Bernecker thermal ignition [12], Sandusky com-
paction [17]) to verify the new form matches established quantitative results before extending to
broader data including non-ideal materials. The Los Alamos Gapstick Test [21] provides recent
rate-stick measurements that would be an interesting additional calibration target for the DAE rate,
complementary to the legacy validation matrix above.
Calibrating against the Gapstick Test would, however, require two prerequisite code-development
tasks beyond the rate replacement itself. First, the present single-reactive-layer XDDT geometry
would have to be extended to a multilayer configuration with alternating reactive and inert layers.
Second, the inert layers would need to carry a propagating pressure wave that transmits detonation
from one reactive segment across the gap into the next; the steel layers of Chapter 8 were treated
as thermal reservoirs and rigid boundaries rather than as hydrodynamic media carrying a shock,
so the XDDT solver’s ability to propagate a shock through an inert layer would have to be verified
or developed. Both extensions are non-trivial but doable, and naturally fall within the scope of a
successor SAND report.
The development, calibration, and validation of the DAE-based surface-burn rate—and the broader
question of unifying the BN post-ignition kinetics with UCM’s prethermal-ignition framework—
will be the subject of a future SAND report in this series.

11. CONCLUSIONS

The XTIV pipeline couples the XCHEM prethermal-ignition code to the XDDT post-ignition
compressible solver, enabling end-to-end simulation of cook-off-driven violent reaction in granular
explosives. The pipeline architecture (§3), diagnostic redesigns (§4), and handoff-state smoothing
knob (§5) collectively make natural cook-off ignition states tractable for XDDT integration without
the imposed hot-spot patches that earlier work would have required.

11.1. Outlook for the next report in the series

The methodology development reported here suggests several lines of follow-on work. Fore-
most among them is the replacement of the empirical pressure-dependent surface-burn term
𝑎 2 (𝑃𝑔 /𝑃ref ) 𝑏 𝑛 𝐻 in the BN compaction equation with the distributed activation-energy (DAE) rate
of the Universal Cookoff Model [4, 19, 20], as proposed in §10.5. This enhancement would unify
the kinetics representation across the cookoff (XCHEM, DAE-based) and the post-ignition (XDDT,
currently 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 with Heaviside gate) phases of the XTIV pipeline, eliminate the calibration-overload
limitation identified in §1.3.2, and—perhaps most consequentially —extend the BN methodology
beyond pure HMX in two directions. First, to HMX-based composite formulations such as EDC-37,
LX-07, LX-10, LX-14, PBX-9501, and PBXN-9 [20], where the present single-rate-with-binary-
thermal-switch form is poorly suited to the multi-stage decomposition kinetics of multi-component
reactive systems and where calibration of 𝑎 2 and 𝑏 𝑛 against pure-HMX strand data does not transfer
cleanly. Second, to non-ideal energetic materials such as ammonium nitrate (AN) and AN-based
explosives, where diffusion-limited kinetics (𝜎 < 0 in the DAE form) dominate the reaction pro-
gression and the BN 𝑟 𝑃𝑛 𝐻 form—which has no mechanism for rate deceleration with reaction
progress—cannot represent the regime at all.
Companion items for the same future report include direction-aware diagnostics for inward-
propagating reactive waves (§10.4), implementation of an impedance-matched outer-boundary-
condition in place of the present Neumann fallback (§10.1), and multi-microsecond stability of the
spherical-geometry integration (§10.3). The DAE-based kinetics extension would position the BN
methodology to address the broader insensitive munitions, transportation, and storage scenarios
encountered in real-world safety analysis, beyond the pure-HMX cookoff regime that motivates the
present report. Together these items constitute the natural scope of the next report in the DDT
series.

Versioning rules.

 • schema_version is the first key in [meta]. XDDT checks this before parsing anything
 else.

 • XDDT accepts schema_version ∈ {1, 2}. Any other value is fatal and is reported with a
 message naming both the file’s version and the accepted set.

 • Adding a new field to an existing section, adding a new section, or changing the meaning of

 • v1 and v2 are both locked once committed. Future schema work happens on v3+; both

APPENDIX B. REPRODUCIBILITY, USER EXPERTISE, AND SHIPPED
 TEMPLATES

This appendix records the level of user expertise assumed by the XTIV pipeline as documented in
this report, the principal numerical controls a user must exercise to reproduce the demonstrations
of Chapters 6–8, and the template set shipped in the source-tree templates/ directory that
constitutes the present path toward a more “push-button” workflow. It was added in response to
reviewer feedback noting that the detailed model and numerical insight required to run the present
build would be unrealistic for a non-expert user — a fair point that deserves an explicit statement
in the report.

B.1. Assumed user expertise

The pipeline as documented here is an expert tool. Producing stable, physically meaningful coupled
XCHEM–XDDT results currently requires working familiarity with: (i) finite-volume reactive-flow
solvers and their failure modes (stiff source terms, integrator return codes, mesh–time-step coupling
under adaptive refinement); (ii) the Baer–Nunziato two-phase closure and its calibration parameters
(alstar, a2, bn, 𝑇ign ); (iii) the conventions of the underlying XCHEM cookoff code (tchem.in
namelists, layer/material strings, zone-output cadence, the tmax termination criterion); and (iv) the
handoff-state smoothing convention introduced in §5 and its interaction with the chosen geometry
and confinement. A user without this background is unlikely to diagnose the failure modes
documented in Chapter 10—sub-zone XCHEM runaway, smoothing-convention mismatches at
cold-bulk handoffs, integrator divergence at sharp hot-face gradients—when they occur on a new
scenario. This is a real reproducibility constraint, not a documentation gap: the underlying physics
spans many orders of magnitude in spatial and temporal scale, and the numerics needed to follow
it across that range do not yet collapse to a small set of defaults.

B.2. Key numerical controls and sensitivity steps

The headline results of this report depend on a small set of numerical controls whose appropriate
settings are scenario-dependent and were established through the sensitivity exploration docu-
mented in the demonstration chapters. A user reproducing the demonstrations should expect to
revisit, at minimum, the controls listed in Table B-1; deviations from the tabulated values should
be made deliberately and verified against the reported diagnostics.

The minimum sensitivity-check protocol used during the current development is to (i) confirm that
halving xtiv_smooth_l_in does not shift the primary diagnostics (𝑡 ddt , 𝐷 2 , 𝑃max ) outside their
reported precision, (ii) repeat with imesh_in toggled where geometry permits, and (iii) verify
that XCHEM tmax is set close to 𝑇ign and below the kinetic-runaway threshold for the chosen
scenario (§10.3). As a rule of thumb, tmax ≈ 800 K — or alternatively a value slightly above the
highest applied boundary temperature — is a good choice: either indicates that thermal runaway is
imminent or close, and produces a clean pre-runaway handoff state without the integrator having
to chase the kinetic-chain transition through the sub-zone-runaway window (§10.3). XDDT results
that fail any of these checks should not be accepted without accompanying details describing the
diagnostic shift and the physical or numerical reasons for it.

A caveat applies to the run-length diagnostic specifically. The present BN surface-burn term uses a
pseudo-Arrhenius rate gated by a Heaviside step at 𝑇ign (§10.5); small numerical perturbations near
that switch can shift the moment the surface-burn kinetics first engage, which propagates into the
run-length values (𝑡 ign , 𝑡 ddt , and 𝑥ddt ) reported across otherwise-equivalent runs. The detonation-
class diagnostics 𝐷 2 and 𝑃max are governed by the post-engagement wave dynamics rather than
the engagement instant and remain fairly consistent across the same perturbations. Replacing the
Heaviside-gated surface-burn rate with a smooth chemistry model — the distributed activation-
energy (DAE) form proposed in §10.5 is the natural candidate — is expected to improve run-length
consistency by removing the threshold-switch sensitivity at the source.

B.3. Shipped templates and the push-button gap

The source-tree templates/ directory ships scenario decks for the four problem classes exer-
cised in this report: cookoff_slab/ (single-edge thermal cookoff; §6), cookoff_cylinder/
(slow cookoff cylinder per NATO AOP-4382 [5]; Chapter 7), fragment_impact/ (twin-fragment
colliding-wave; Chapter 8), and shocked_slab/ (piston-driven shock initiation; under construc-
tion, see §10). Each subdirectory ships a complete, runnable pair of input decks (tchem.in for

 114

XCHEM and coupled.inp for XDDT) together with a small driver script that executes the full
pipeline end-to-end.
The templates close part of the reproducibility gap but not all of it. They give a user a known-good
starting point for each of the four regimes, but they cannot remove the requirement to re-exercise the
sensitivity controls of §B.2 when the new scenario departs materially from the seed case (different
bed length, different confinement, different fill material). A genuinely “push-button” workflow
would additionally require: (i) a broader outer-boundary-condition implementation (§10.1) so that
a user is not restricted to the Neumann-fallback case; (ii) the smoothing-convention generaliza-
tion for cold-bulk handoffs (§10.3) so that spherical and other multi-cell-hot-shell scenarios run
without bespoke smoothing choices; (iii) the direction-aware diagnostic generalization (§10.4) so
that two-front converging-wave cases do not require asymmetric hot-spot workarounds; and (iv)
the distributed-activation-energy surface-burn replacement (§10.5) to remove the threshold-driven
integrator stiffness that motivates much of the present sensitivity exploration. These four devel-
opments, already itemized in Chapter 10 as bounded future work, are also the principal items
separating the current expert-only workflow from a template-driven one usable by a non-expert.

in this work are published in the open literature: the Baer–Nunziato two-phase formulation in
Refs. [8, 9]; the McAfee–Asay–Campbell–Ramsay DDT validation experiments in Ref. [11]; the
Price–Bernecker thermal-ignition experiments in Ref. [12]; the Sandusky–Glancy–Campbell–Krall
ball-propellant compaction experiments in Ref. [17]; the TIGER thermochemical-equilibrium
formulation in Ref. [7]; and the JCZS3 intermolecular potential database in Ref. [22]. An external
party with access to this report and the cited literature could in principle reconstruct the methodology
— but doing so requires sustained domain expertise in two-phase reactive-flow modeling, Fortran
software engineering, IM-relevant validation practice, and the experimental literature that calibrates
the constitutive choices. An LLM tool can summarize published material, draft surrounding prose,
and accelerate implementation, but it does not substitute for the domain expertise and physical
judgement that distinguish a working coupled simulation from a methodology summary.
The XDDT and XCHEM source codes are not part of this publication. The deck files, output
summaries, and validation scenarios that appear in the figures of this report describe what was
computed, not how the codes implement the two-phase equations or solve them. The codes
themselves remain in Sandia-internal repositories.

C.3. Export-control assessment

This SAND report deals with the export-controlled material HMX. The technical information
included herein is entirely captured by previously published open-publication information. The
experimental data upon which the model was validated and compared (Refs. [11, 12, 17]) are
published in publicly available International Detonation Symposia and JANNAF proceedings. The
Baer–Nunziato two-phase model is published in the open literature [8, 9]. The contribution of
this report and its predecessors [1, 2] is the modernization of the original Baer Fortran-77 source
to current Fortran standards, the correction of historical software bugs, the introduction of the
coupled XCHEM–XDDT pipeline, and the demonstration of that pipeline on representative test
configurations. The software itself is not part of this publication.
All coupled simulation demonstrations in this report use HMX as the reactive material. Cross-
material gas-equation-of-state sensitivity work involving additional explosives was conducted in
the predecessor report [2], where only the reacted (product-gas) EOS data were applied for relative
sensitivity comparisons; that work is cited here but not extended. This material does not rise to
the level of Export Controlled Controlled Unclassified Information and is appropriate for unlimited
distribution.
