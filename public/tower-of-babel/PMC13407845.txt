Covalently bonded polymer-metal-organic framework semiconductive membranes for flexible X-ray detection
Authors: Chengyu Liang, Liwei Cheng, Linwei He, Zhizai Li, Sheng Hong, Liangwei Yang, Qing Huang, Zhifang Chai, Yaxing Wang, Shuao Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fabrication of flexible semiconductive metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) membranes is a challenging research target with unique optoelectronics applications. In this work, we introduce a semiconductive polyMOF (polymer-metal-organic framework) strategy that copolymerizes semiconductive MOFs with polymer monomers, yielding uniform and defect-minimized semiconductive membranes with ultralow trap densities and eliminating microgaps. This molecular integration enables efficient charge transport, realizing flexible MOF membranes for high-sensitivity radiation detection. The polyMOF-based flexible detector demonstrates exceptional performance, with a leading X-ray sensitivity of 6972.9 μC Gyair−1 cm−2 among reported flexible X-ray detectors and an ultralow detection limit of 52.6 nGyair s−1. The flexible polyMOF detector outperforms the flat-panel devices in resolution and versatility while also serving as a conformal dosimeter with commercial-level accuracy. This work establishes covalent polyMOFs as a universal platform for defect-engineered optoelectronics, bridging material innovations with flexible radiation detection technology.Subject terms: Metal-organic frameworks, Coordination polymers

Introduction

Flexible optoelectronic devices are a new-generation research topic with advantages such as portability, bendability, wearability, and stretchability, which cannot be achieved by existing rigid devices1–3. In the context of X-ray detectors, flexibility not only mitigates issues related to dose inhomogeneity caused by radiation sources but also enables conformity to irregular shapes, thereby reducing the risk of high-dose exposure4. Flexible photoelectric materials, in particular, play a critical role in absorbing radiation and converting it into a detectable electrical signal. The performance of these materials is therefore crucial to the overall performance of flexible X-ray detectors. Flexible photoelectric materials, including organic materials, inorganic materials, and emerging hybrid materials, such as metal halide perovskites, have attracted significant attention due to their low trap density and high charge-transfer efficiency5. Organic materials (e.g., polymer thin films and small organic molecules), known for their high chemical compatibility and inherent flexibility, are commonly proposed as key materials for flexible X-ray detectors6,7. However, the limited elemental composition of organic materials, which lack heavy elements, restricts their applicability and results in poor X-ray attenuation capabilities. On the other hand, the limited chemical compatibility of traditional inorganic materials makes uniform integration with organic substrates challenging, often leading to phase separation or aggregation, and the resulting defects in flexible materials are great obstacles to the carrier transport system8–10.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are developing crystalline materials composed of metal-containing nodes (metal cations or metal clusters) and organic ligands connected by well-defined coordination bonds that are gaining popularity for X-ray detectors owing to their variable topologies, abundant optional functions, and metal content11,12. The early misunderstanding of the insulating character of most MOFs created at the outset has prevented these materials from being considered for applications that require ideal charge transport properties. Recently, electrically conductive metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) incorporating electroactive components have attracted significant interest from various perspectives. The internal charge transfer in these materials arises from topologically nontrivial electronic structures, which enable the modular design of their electrical conductivity and optoelectronic properties13–16. These semiconductive MOFs exhibit tunable charge carrier pathways, the ability to be postmodified with polymers, and excellent chemical compatibility, making them highly promising for the development of flexible X-ray detectors.

Compared with inorganic materials, the chemical designability and compatibility with the polymer matrix of MOFs provide important guarantees for the realization of high-performance flexible energy conversion materials. The strategy of using a mixed matrix membrane (MMM) for ionizing radiation detection has been proven to be superior to traditional rigid detectors17–20. Such flexible materials not only retain the intrinsic properties of each component but also generate synergistic functionalities beyond those of the individual constituents. However, there are inherent issues with the existing approach of physically mixing the polymerization of MMMs. The polymer, as a continuous phase, has difficulty combining well with the dispersed phase without chemical bonding, which allows the formation of microgaps between them. The poor solvation of the particles, the depletion effect of the polymer, and the kinetics of polymer gelation can all lead to the demixing and agglomeration of the MMMs21. For photoelectric materials, controlling the trap density and improving the consistency are critical ways to ensure high performance, and such demixing in MMMs with abundant microgaps is undesirable for optimal performance. An emerging approach for MOF-polymer membrane materials is the copolymerization of polymer monomers with MOFs22,23. This strategy enhances interphase uniformity through chemical bonding, thereby suppressing microdefect formation at a fundamental level. Despite this promise, the exploration of such hybrid materials in optoelectronic devices remains in its infancy.

In this work, we demonstrate an approach for a homogenous membrane by copolymerizing semiconductive MOFs and acrylate monomers with excellent optoelectronic performance under ionizing radiation. This kind of polyMOF membrane, in contrast with the former physical mixing polymerization, has enhanced homogeneity, a lower trap density, a higher photon-to-current conversion efficiency, and improved charge-transfer performance (Fig. 1). Consequently, the remarkable increase in the detection sensitivity is comparable to that of single-crystalline detectors, and the limit of detection (LOD) is accurate to an unprecedented nGyair s−1 level. We also demonstrate the potential for practical applications of this flexible material in confined-space imaging, with significant advantages over flat-panel detectors.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of polyMOF

Reacting UO2(NO3)2 with H2BDC-NH2 (2-aminoterephthalic acid) results in yellow crystal blocks of UO2(BDC-NH2)1.5·NH2(CH3)2 (SCU-119). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis revealed that SCU-119 crystallizes in an orthorhombic space group, Cmce. The asymmetric unit of SCU-119 consists of one-half uranyl cations, one-half and one-quarter crystallographically independent BDC-NH2 ligands, and one-half dimethylamine cations. As shown in Fig. 2a, each uranyl is coordinated with six O atoms from three carboxylates of BDC-NH2 ligands, resulting in a typical hexagonal bipyramidal coordination geometry. The as-formed 2D network features a graphite-like hcb topology. The edge length of the six-membered ring of the network is ~11.4 Å, which is large enough to be entangled with other networks. To simplify the structure of SCU-119, the metal center is regarded as a three-connect node, as the BDC-NH2 ligand is a two-connect linker. As a result, SCU-119 can be considered two sets of 2D layers interlocked from two directions, a polycatenation of the 2D + 2D→3D framework (Fig. 2b). Along the z-direction, the whole structure consists of a series of 1D channels (Fig. 2c).

a Illustration of the coordination mode of SCU-119. The uranyl exhibits a typical hexagonal bipyramidal coordination geometry, and the resulting two-dimensional network features a graphite-like hcb topology. b, c Schematic representation of the 2D + 2D → 3D interlocked structure of SCU-119. Two adjacent layers interlock with two others in different directions, resulting in a transition from a 2D + 2D arrangement to a 3D structure. d Schematic diagram of the copolymerization synthesis path from SCU-119 to polyMOF.

To obtain a high-quality flexible membrane, post-synthetic modification of SCU-119 was performed with methacrylic anhydride (Fig. 2d). The modified sample is copolymerized with butyl methacrylate (BMA) monomer in the presence of the photoinitiator phenylbis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (XBPO) under 365 nm UV light in a presized poly(tetrafluoroethylene) (PTFE) mold. After UV light exposure, the flat and uniform polyMOF membrane can be easily detached from the mold. The membrane thickness, size, and shape can be controlled by adjusting the sample quantity and mold dimensions. As shown in the SEM images (Fig. 3a), the polyMOF membrane has fewer microgaps and a more uniform morphology than the physical MMMs do at the same mass ratio.

Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of different samples are shown in Fig. 3b. The peak positions of the as-synthesized products match well with the simulated pattern. The structure shows excellent structural stability under long-term X-ray irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 1). Although the peak intensities decrease after copolymerization, the characteristic peaks are still clearly observable, confirming that the crystalline structure is maintained. Solid-state 13C NMR spectroscopy was employed to monitor the modification and subsequent copolymerization processes and to verify the successful formation of the polyMOF structure. As shown in Fig. 3c, compared with pristine SCU-119, the modified sample displays two new resonances characteristic of methacrylate groups, including a methyl signal at ~18 ppm and a vinyl carbon at ~123 ppm, confirming successful grafting onto the framework linkers. After copolymerization to form the polyMOF membrane, the vinyl resonance is markedly diminished, accompanied by the emergence of intensified aliphatic signals in the 20-50 ppm region, corresponding to saturated polymer backbone carbons. These observations suggest substantial consumption of C=C bonds and are consistent with polymerization occurring in the system. By normalizing the vinyl integral to the invariant aromatic framework region (110–155 ppm), the double-bond conversion is estimated to be ~81.9%, indicating an efficient copolymerization process. In a mechanically mixed control sample (modified SCU-119 and poly(butyl methacrylate) (PBMA) with the same mass fraction as polyMOF, Supplementary Fig. 2), the ~123 ppm vinyl resonance remains clearly visible, whereas it is strongly suppressed in polyMOF, directly evidencing covalent copolymerization. FTIR spectroscopy further supports this conclusion. In Supplementary Fig. 3, the C=C stretching band at ~1628 cm‒1, negligible in pristine SCU-119 and intensified after modification, is markedly reduced after copolymerization, indicating the introduction and subsequent consumption of vinyl groups. Quantitative peak-area analysis of the copolymerization part, normalized to an invariant aromatic framework band (1480–1550 cm‒1), yields a double-bond conversion of 91.5%, confirming the high efficiency of the copolymerization process. Overall, these results support that copolymerization between the modified SCU-119 and the polymer occurs, giving rise to a polyMOF membrane.

Charge carrier and optoelectronic properties of polyMOF

In the energy conversion process, the interaction cross-section of the high-energy photons with the material is dependent on the atomic number (Z). Materials with high Z are supposed to have a higher attenuation coefficient, which reveals the interaction probability of photons with defined energy in a specific material. SCU-119 is constructed with the naturally occurring highest element uranium, leading to superior X-ray attenuation efficiency within the medical range (100–200 keV) over many other commercial detection materials, as shown in Fig. 3d. Figure 3e, f show the bandgap and energy-level diagrams after copolymerization, and the relevant parameters can be measured via ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) energy spectra and UV-Vis spectra (Supplementary Fig. 4). An appropriate bandgap plays an important role in the charge transport. A higher bandgap results in a reduced intrinsic carrier concentration for a lower dark current to ensure a better signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, at the same time, a higher bandgap can also contribute to greater electron-hole formation energy, decreasing the number of charges forming in a single interaction24. Therefore, a larger bandgap in a certain range (usually between 1.5 and 3.0 eV) is beneficial for the collected signals. Another key factor is the work function (Φ), which refers to the minimum energy needed to release an electron. To evaluate the optoelectronic properties of the polyMOF membrane and reference material, the samples were loaded in the irradiation configuration as a sandwich structure of Ag/sample/Ag. The mobility-lifetime product (μτ product), a property of the material that reflects the charge-transfer performance and contributes to detection sensitivity, can be obtained via the photoconductivity method. The steady-state photoconductivity curve was fitted with the modified Hecht equation (Fig. 4a). The derived μτ product of the polyMOF membrane is (1.88 ± 0.15) × 10‒3 cm2 V‒1, where the uncertainty represents the standard error from nonlinear fitting. The μτ product has clear advantages over recently developed MOF materials and other state-of-the-art materials17,20,25–37. We also obtained the μτ product of the physical MMM and pelleted SCU-119 made via our reported method. The polyMOF membrane has an obviously efficient charge-transfer performance because of the lower trap density through copolymerization. In contrast, the pelleted SCU-119 wafer exhibited poor apparent uniformity, and the physical MMM was generally characterized by microgaps between the continuous phase and the dispersion phase.

To further understand the behavior of traps that act as barriers to charge transfer, the trap density (ntrap) can be derived from space-charge limited current (SCLC) measurements38,39. The injection of current is considered a powerful probe for investigating trap states in the bandgap and charge carrier mobility. The device configuration was used to favor electron transport based on energy-level alignment, where electron injection is efficient while hole injection is energetically suppressed, supporting the use of the electron-only SCLC model. In the dark I-V curves of the samples, three apparent regions (I ∝ Vn) emerged. The I-V curve first follows Ohm’s law (Ohmic region), where the background charge carriers play the leading role. If the number of injected carriers exceeds that of background carriers, the injected carriers spread and form a space charge field. When the injected carriers fill in the trap states, the current increases sharply (trap-filled limited region). Once all traps are filled, the current is assumed to follow the Mott-Gurney law in the absence of traps. As shown in Fig. 4b, the ntrap of the polyMOF membrane was determined to be 1.75 × 109 cm‒3, which is ten times less than that of the physical MMM and two orders of magnitude less than that of the pelleted wafer (Supplementary Fig. 5). The extremely low trap density gives rise to the efficient transfer of charge carriers, making it an anticipated approach for processing high-performance optoelectronic devices. We summarize the single photoelectric response of different materials under the same irradiation conditions in Fig. 4c. A lower trap density contributes to a higher photocurrent of the polyMOF membrane, whereas the dark current is lower because of the modified band structure, resulting in a significantly higher SNR.

Experiments under X-ray irradiation were performed using a tungsten anode radiation device, and the tube voltage and current were manually adjusted. We can precisely capture the signal of the photocurrent of devices upon a change in the X-ray dose rate, and the significant stepwise changes in the photocurrent suggest high sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. 6). Detectors with high sensitivity can reduce the total X-ray dose exposure of the object, and signals with high signal-to-noise ratios can be obtained, which is important in medical diagnosis. Under a peak tube voltage of 30 kV, we tested the X-ray response of the devices. The sensitivity of the polyMOF membrane was determined to be 6972.9 μC Gyair‒1 cm‒2 with a bias of 100 V (Fig. 4d). It is competitive among well-studied flexible detectors10,17,20,33–37. The thickness-dependent sensitivity was further collected. Within a certain range, it tends to increase with increasing thickness under the same conditions. Under the given X-ray intensity, the X-ray stopping power increases sharply as the thickness increases and reaches an extreme value of ~1.6 mm, indicating that the sensitivity nearly saturates (Supplementary Fig. 7). This finding shows that this thickness can achieve a balance between photon absorption and carrier dissipation. The sensitivities of the physical MMM and pelleted SCU-119 wafers with similar thicknesses were also determined (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 8), and the former is 40 times lower than that of the polyMOF membrane, whereas the latter is much lower. These results indicate that a lower trap density leads to a more sensitive photoelectric response.

The detection limit of the X-ray detector is another key figure of merit that can guarantee detection accuracy. According to the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) definition, a signal is usually regarded as valid when the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is greater than 3. At the lowest dose rate (52.6 nGyair s‒1) that we can effectively calibrate, the SNR of the polyMOF detector is derived to be 231 (Fig. 4e). This means that a legitimate signal under the nGy s‒1 scale, which is the level that has never been reached among the reported related materials and surpasses some of the well-reported flexible detectors summarized in Fig. 4f, can be obtained. Moreover, as a flexible detector, the stability of the signal after repeated bending is highly important. The photocurrent signal only decays by ~4.7% after 400 cycles of folding (Supplementary Fig. 9). This result proves that the polyMOF detector has good durability when used in complex surface imaging. After intermittent high-dose X-ray irradiation (17.52 mGyair s‒‒1) with a total dose of ~40 Gyair, the device shows only ~3% photocurrent decay (Supplementary Fig. 10), evidencing excellent radiation stability. The minor decay within each irradiation cycle is reversible, as the signal fully recovers after a short rest, suggesting the behavior comes from temporary charge trapping rather than permanent material degradation. This dose is equivalent to ~4 × 105 chest X-ray scans, underscoring its robust durability.

PolyMOF for conformal X-ray detection and dosimetry

Recent rapid growth in the demand of the X-ray application industry, such as medical imaging and nondestructive industrial detection, has led to the booming iteration of X-ray detectors. High-quality 3D imaging for medical and industrial applications requires multiple scans at different angles via stiff flat-panel detectors, which may increase the patient’s irradiation exposure, increasing the risk of cancer and greatly reducing the detection efficiency. Moreover, rigid detectors suffer from edge distortion, which increases the likelihood of misdiagnosis and errors. The influx of new materials has taken X-ray detection technology to an unprecedented degree. To demonstrate the practical prospects and advantages of flexible detection materials, an array detector was fabricated in a square of a polyMOF membrane with sides of 4 cm and 5 × 5 pixels. The membrane can adapt to the surface of uneven objects, and images of bent samples can be obtained with just one irradiation. The photocurrent was collected in real time, and the current signal was transposed into a two-dimensional pattern. The millimeter-thick membrane can be well attached to the surface of objects, so we can observe current-to-color transposed images of bent metal letters “S” and “U” and their hollow metal plates under X-rays of 160 kVp. Both exhibit ideal signal resolution under extremely low work bias (0.5 V) (Fig. 5a).

More practically, a flexible material with good fitness can be implemented as a built-in detector that can greatly enhance the resolution. For example, when dealing with nondestructive testing of a section of a pipe, overall detection with a rigid detector of the tube body involves one more X-ray attenuation to the pipe wall. The differentiation of collected signals is severely reduced, resulting in poorer imaging resolution. In contrast, a much better signal contrast can be collected while the flexible detector can be rolled up to fit inside the pipe. Moreover, because we do not need to penetrate unnecessary sections, the energy of the X-rays required is substantially lower, allowing for thinner detectors to be approved, greatly improving total device portability. To demonstrate this, we perform a contrast experiment with the built-in detector and the external detector, which could represent the difference in image resolution between the emerging flexible detectors and the traditional ones under such conditions. The object to be imaged is a lead pipe with a thickness of 1.5 mm and a cross-shaped hole. Figure 5b shows a schematic diagram with different detector placements. To visualize the hole clearly, a built-in flexible detector provides a sharp image, whereas using an external planar method results in a very fuzzy image under identical X-ray settings (tube voltage: 160 kVp) and pipe positioning. These findings demonstrate good adaptability, low operating voltage (battery-operating prospects), and excellent portability of polyMOF membrane-based detectors. Notably, to quantitatively verify the accuracy of polyMOF as an X-ray detection material, dose rate monitoring was carried out within the standard medical range. And the results were compared with those of commercial detectors. As shown in Fig. 5c, d and Supplementary Fig. 11, the standard function is first calibrated at different tube voltages, and functions are called in different cases. The dose rate was tested three times at 160 and 80 kVp, and the readout dose rate is only 2–3% greater than that of commercial detectors, possibly due to slight positional differences. The results show that polyMOF has the potential to be a highly applicable and accurate dosimeter.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrated a low-defect, flexible, highly sensitive, and low-LOD direct X-ray detection material via the polyMOF approach. A homogenous polyMOF membrane was constructed by copolymerization of semiconductive MOF SCU-119 and acrylate monomers. Compared with traditional MMM films, polyMOFs obtained via this method have a much lower trap density, which allows them to be competitive candidates for high-performance flexible optoelectronic materials and achieve extremely high sensitivity and low LODs among reported MOF-based materials. The polyMOF detector was proven to be competent for accurate imaging under uneven conditions with good flexibility and a low operating voltage, which provides a solution to some of the current bottleneck challenges in medical and industrial X-ray imaging. These findings may expand the horizon in the functionalization of flexible MOF-based materials, and they potentially provide a feasible solution for designing and synthesizing next-generation optoelectronic materials.

Methods

Reagents

Reagents that are not specified were purchased from commercial suppliers without further purification.

Synthesis of SCU-119

UO2(NO3)2•6H2O (50.2 mg, 0.1 mmol) and 2-aminoterephthalic acid (36.2 mg, 0.2 mmol) were added to a mixture of 1 mL of deionized water and 1 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF). The mixture was sealed in a PTFE-lined chemical reactor autoclave with a 10 mL internal volume. The autoclave was subsequently heated from room temperature to 140 °C at a heating rate of 4.5 °C h‒1, after which the temperature was maintained for 300 min. Next, the autoclave was cooled at a constant rate for 2 h to room temperature. The products were rinsed with EtOH three times and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C. The obtained crystalline sample can be used for single-crystal XRD testing, and the structure can be analyzed as Supplementary Fig. 12.

Modification of SCU-119

The as-synthesized SCU-119 was mixed with a slight excess of methacrylic anhydride in 3 mL of CH2Cl2 and sealed in a 15 mL glass vial for 6 days. The modified SCU-119 were then washed three times with fresh CH2Cl2 before being vacuum dried at 40 °C for 6 h.

Refinement of the butyl methacrylate (BMA) monomer

To eliminate the inhibitor hydroquinone from the purchased commercial reagent, we refined the butyl methacrylate monomer. This was accomplished via simple extraction equipment with an appropriate amount of 5 wt% NaOH in water. Butyl methacrylate (100 mL) was added to a 250 mL separating funnel containing freshly made NaOH aqueous solution (5 wt%, 50 mL), the bottom part of the liquid was removed after full mixing, and the refined BMA monomer was obtained after several extraction washing processes with NaOH.

Synthesis of the SCU-119 polyMOF membrane

About 0.2 g of modified SCU-119 was suspended in 0.8 g of refined BMA and sonicated for 20 min. The photoinitiator phenyl-bis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)-phosphine oxide (XBPO) was added to the mixture at 6% of the total weight. After being sonicated for another 10 min, the mixture was dripped into a customized PTFE mold. The polyMOF membrane can be stripped off after being photopolymerized under a 365 nm UV lamp for 35 min. Notably, the thickness and size of the membrane can be controlled by the mold size and the amount of raw material added. The specific photopolymerization time is an empirical value based on the specific light source power and distance.

Synthesis of SCU-119 physically mixed-matrix membranes

About 0.2 g of dried SCU-119 powder was adequately mixed with 0.8 g of poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF). 3 mL of DMF were added to the mixture in a glass vial. 30 min of ultrasonic treatment is required to ensure that the particles are well dispersed, and we perform vacuum treatment to remove the air bubbles inside. The mixed liquor was then uniformly poured onto the cleaned glass substrate and put into the oven at 60 °C for 45 min. After the solvent was removed, the membrane was easily peeled off from the glass substrate with tweezers.

Synthesis of the control sample

PBMA was prepared as a control by photopolymerizing 1.0 g of refined BMA under the same conditions as described for the polyMOF membrane. To ensure that the mechanical mixture of modified SCU-119 and Poly(butyl methacrylate) (PBMA) had the same mass fraction (20%) as that in the polyMOF sample, the two components were weighed according to the corresponding ratio and uniformly mixed by low-temperature grinding to obtain a homogeneous physical blend.
