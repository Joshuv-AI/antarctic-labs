Seismic attributes analysis and petrophysical modeling for reservoir characterization and prospect identification in the I/R oil fields, Murzuq Basin, Libya
Authors: Adel Mahmoud Negm, Dhyaa H. Haddad, Mohamed I. Abdel-Fattah, Hamzah S. Amir, Mohammed A. Amir, Moataz Kh. Barakat, Mohamed Reda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Upper Ordovician Mamuniyat Formation represents the principal hydrocarbon reservoir in the I/R oil fields, Murzuq Basin, Libya, where reservoir quality and distribution are strongly influenced by glacial depositional processes and structural complexity. This study presents an integrated reservoir characterization workflow combining multi-well petrophysical analysis, 3D seismic interpretation, seismic attribute analysis (variance, RMS amplitude, and energy), spectral decomposition, and 3D static reservoir modeling. Petrophysical evaluation from eleven wells indicates that the reservoir is dominated by quartz-rich sandstones with shale volume generally < 25%, effective porosity ranging from ~ 12 to 15%, and water saturation varying between ~ 20 and 55%. Seismic attribute analysis reveals a dominant NW–SE-trending fault system controlling reservoir compartmentalization, while amplitude-based attributes delineate sand-prone fairways associated with improved reservoir quality. Spectral decomposition further enhances imaging of channelized depositional elements and demonstrates lateral continuity of reservoir bodies. The integration of seismic-derived indicators with petrophysical properties within a 3D modeling framework identifies zones of enhanced reservoir quality concentrated in the central, northwestern, and southwestern parts of the field. Two new prospective zones are delineated on the upthrown side of the main fault, characterized by favorable seismic responses, higher porosity, and reduced water saturation. Blind well validation confirms good agreement between modeled and observed properties, demonstrating the predictive capability of the approach. Compared to previous studies relying on isolated datasets, this integrated workflow provides a more quantitative and spatially consistent characterization of reservoir heterogeneity, significantly reducing uncertainty and supporting improved exploration and development strategies in structurally complex clastic systems.

Introduction

The Murzuq Basin of southwestern Libya is one of the most important Paleozoic hydrocarbon provinces in North Africa (Fig. 1), hosting several giant oil fields and contributing significantly to Libya’s oil production1–3. Exploration activities in the basin began in the 1950s and intensified following the discovery of commercially productive Paleozoic reservoirs, particularly those of Ordovician age4–6. Among these, the Upper Ordovician Mamuniyat Formation represents the primary reservoir unit in many fields, including the I/R oil fields located in the eastern Murzuq Basin (Fig. 1a). The I/R fields lie within both the NC-115 and NC-186 blocks within the Sharara petroleum system and cover an area of approximately 250 km² (Fig. 1b). Development of the field started in 2008, and current production is about 37,000 barrels of oil per day, with an estimated stock tank original oil in place approaching one billion barrels, highlighting the strategic importance of this reservoir.

Despite its economic significance, the Mamuniyat Formation exhibits pronounced lateral and vertical heterogeneity in reservoir quality across the I/R fields7. Variations in porosity, permeability, and net pay thickness are observed both between wells and within individual wells, where the most favorable reservoir properties may occur in the lower or upper parts of the formation. This heterogeneity is largely attributed to the complex depositional history of the Mamuniyat Formation, which was deposited during the Late Ordovician glacial cycle and is characterized by glaciomarine to shallow marine environments7–9. The reservoir is dominated by quartz-rich sandstones interbedded with shale and siltstone, commonly arranged in channelized and incised valley-fill geometries. These sand bodies are frequently stacked, truncated, or eroded by younger depositional units, leading to uncertainties in reservoir connectivity and fluid flow behavior10,11.

Previous studies in the Murzuq Basin have addressed the stratigraphy, sedimentology, and petroleum system elements of the Mamuniyat Formation7,12; however, most previous studies relied on either well-based petrophysical analysis or conventional seismic interpretation alone. A clear gap remains in the lack of integrated studies that quantitatively combine multi-well petrophysical data with advanced seismic attributes and 3D reservoir modeling to constrain reservoir heterogeneity and connectivity13–16. Therefore, this study aims to provide an integrated reservoir characterization of the Mamuniyat Formation in the I/R oil fields through the combination of petrophysical analysis, seismic attribute evaluation, and 3D static modeling. Well log data from eleven wells were used to quantify key petrophysical parameters, while 3D seismic data were analyzed using variance, RMS amplitude, energy attributes, and spectral decomposition techniques to delineate structural features, facies distribution, and potential hydrocarbon accumulations. Although integrated workflows combining seismic attributes, spectral decomposition, and 3D reservoir modeling are well established in reservoir characterization, their application within the Mamuniyat Formation of the I/R oil fields remains limited. In particular, few studies have quantitatively integrated multi-well petrophysical analysis with advanced seismic attribute interpretation and 3D static modeling to address reservoir heterogeneity and prospect delineation in this basin. The integration of these datasets aims to improve understanding of reservoir quality controls, reduce uncertainty in reservoir models, and identify new prospective areas that may enhance future exploration and development strategies in the I/R fields.

Geological background

The I/R oil fields is situated in the eastern Murzuq Basin, southwestern Libya, within the broader Sharara petroleum province (Fig. 1a). The Murzuq Basin is a large intracratonic basin that contains a thick Paleozoic sedimentary succession and hosts several prolific hydrocarbon systems, making it one of the most important petroleum provinces in North Africa4,5,7. Structurally, The Murzuq Basin is a large intracratonic basin characterized by a relatively simple structural framework dominated by broad basement-controlled arches and gentle synclinal depressions formed during Paleozoic subsidence. Its structural evolution is influenced by inherited Precambrian basement trends and later reactivation of major fault systems, which locally control trap development and reservoir compartmentalization17.

The stratigraphic framework relevant to this study extends from the Cambrian through the Silurian, as summarized in the regional stratigraphic column (Fig. 2a). The older Cambrian to Middle Ordovician intervals (Hasawnah to Hawaz formations) generally represent pre-glacial, shallow-marine deposits, whereas the Upper Ordovician succession (Melaz Shuqran and Mamuniyat formations) is commonly interpreted to reflect glacial to glaciomarine sedimentation associated with the Late Ordovician icehouse event that affected large parts of North Africa8,9. The uppermost Silurian Tanezzuft Formation marks the waning of glacial conditions and the transition to post-glacial marine deposition, and it is regionally significant as an organic-rich “hot shale” interval in many parts of North Africa5.

Within this stratigraphic context, the Mamuniyat Formation represents the principal reservoir unit in the I/R fields and is positioned as a sand-prone package between the Melaz Shuqran Formation below and the discontinuous Bir Tlacsin Formation above, or locally the Silurian Tanezzuft Formation where the Bir Tlacsin is absent5,18. The nature of the lower contact remains debated: Davidson et al. (2000) suggested a transitional relationship between the Melaz Shuqran and Mamuniyat formations, whereas Hallett and Clark-Lowes (2002) interpreted the boundary as unconformable, reflecting erosion and/or non-deposition linked to glacial dynamics. The upper boundary of the Mamuniyat Formation is commonly described as unconformable, with either the Bir Tlacsin or the Tanezzuft Formation overlying it, consistent with regional stratigraphic breaks tied to changes in ice volume and subsequent marine transgression.

Sequence-stratigraphic interpretation indicates that the Upper Ordovician glacial cycle comprises three formations—Melaz Shuqran, Mamuniyat, and Bir Tlacsin—organized into three sequences bounded by glacial erosion surfaces (GES) (Fig. 3a–d). The first sequence (Melaz Shuqran) is interpreted as a glacial retreat systems tract bounded by GES1 and GES2. The second sequence corresponds to the Mamuniyat Formation and is bounded by GES2 and GES3; it includes a glacial retreat tract (Lower–Middle Mamuniyat) and a glacial minimum tract (Upper Mamuniyat), separated by a glacial maximum retreat surface (GMRS). The third (youngest) sequence is retrogradational and reflects a regional transgression and flooding of the area8. This glacially influenced stratigraphic architecture helps explain the strong heterogeneity of the Mamuniyat reservoir, where sand-rich bodies may occur as stacked and channelized elements with variable continuity, directly impacting reservoir quality distribution and connectivity at field scale (Fig. 3).

Materials and methods

This study integrates well log analysis, 3D seismic interpretation, seismic attribute analysis, spectral decomposition, and three-dimensional static reservoir modeling to characterize the Upper Ordovician Mamuniyat Formation in the I/R oil fields, Murzuq Basin, Libya (Fig. 4). The database includes wireline logs from eleven wells and a post-stack 3D seismic volume covering the entire fields. The well log suite comprises gamma ray, density, neutron porosity, compressional sonic, photoelectric and deep resistivity, which were used for lithological discrimination and petrophysical evaluation. All logs were subjected to quality control, including depth matching, normalization, and removal of anomalous readings. Shale volume was calculated from gamma ray log analysis using Eq. (1)19.

The seismic data were also quality-checked to ensure adequate signal continuity, frequency content, and resolution within the Mamuniyat reservoir interval prior to interpretation.

The 3D seismic dataset used in this study is a post-stack time-migrated volume covering the I/R oil fields, acquired with a bin size of ~ 25 × 25 m, a sampling interval of 2 ms, and a record length of ~ 4–5 s. The processing workflow included amplitude recovery, noise attenuation, deconvolution, velocity analysis, NMO correction, stacking, and post-stack time migration, followed by frequency filtering to enhance data quality. Spectral analysis indicates a dominant frequency of ~ 50–60 Hz within the Mamuniyat interval, corresponding to a vertical resolution of ~ 10–15 m. The seismic data exhibit a high signal-to-noise ratio, as evidenced by clear reflector continuity and well-defined fault patterns, ensuring reliable seismic interpretation and attribute analysis.

Finally, all interpreted horizons, faults, facies, and petrophysical properties were integrated into a 3D static reservoir model, in which facies and property distributions were populated using geostatistical methods to evaluate reservoir quality distribution and identify prospective zones within the Mamuniyat Formation45–53.

The 3D geostatistical modeling workflow was implemented to ensure reproducibility and consistency in representing reservoir heterogeneity. Facies modeling was performed using sequential indicator simulation (SIS), conditioned to well data and guided by the structural framework and interpreted depositional trends. Petrophysical properties, including effective porosity and water saturation, were modeled using sequential Gaussian simulation (SGS) after normal-score transformation of the input data. Variogram analysis was conducted to define spatial continuity in the vertical and horizontal directions, with parameters calibrated from well data and aligned with geological trends. Prior to modeling, well logs were upscaled to the simulation grid to ensure compatibility between data resolution and grid cell size. The modeling process was fully conditioned to well control, ensuring that simulated values honor measured data while capturing realistic spatial variability across the reservoir.

Results and discussion

Seismic attributes analysis

Variance attribute: fault detection and reservoir compartmentalization

The interpretation of the 3D seismic volume, supported by attribute analysis, provides critical insights into the structural framework and its influence on reservoir distribution in the I/R oil fields. The Mamuniyat Formation is affected by a dominant fault system trending NW–SE, accompanied by subordinate fault splays that locally segment the reservoir and may create structural compartments and variable connectivity (Figs. 3a–d and 5). This structural architecture is consistent with the established role of coherence/variance-type attributes in highlighting discontinuities and subtle fault offsets in seismic data, particularly where reflector continuity is otherwise strong23,25,40,54–57.

Variance (coherency) attribute slices extracted at multiple time levels within the Mamuniyat interval reveal a clear network of discontinuities expressed as linear to curvilinear features (Fig. 5a1–a3). These discontinuities correspond to fault and fracture zones that can affect reservoir performance and drilling outcomes by controlling lateral sealing, creating compartments, or enhancing permeability along damage zones. The seismic sections confirm that the most prospective Mamuniyat reservoir interval is structurally controlled by the main fault that bounds the study area, with the best-developed reservoir appearing on the upthrown side where reservoir sands are better preserved and structurally positioned (Fig. 5b1,b2). The grouped extraction of faults (Fig. 5c) emphasizes that both major and minor faults share a coherent structural grain, and collectively they define the primary trapping style and compartmentalization pattern of the field. Similar outcomes have been documented in attribute-driven fault imaging studies, where variance improves detection of smaller-scale discontinuities that are difficult to map using conventional interpretation alone58–60.

From a reservoir standpoint, the mapped fault framework provides two key implications. First, the main fault likely defines the largest structural closure and boundary conditions for hydrocarbon accumulation. Second, the subsidiary faults may either subdivide the reservoir into compartments (reducing lateral connectivity) or locally enhance permeability where fault damage zones intersect sand-prone facies. In practice, this explains why wells within the same structural sector can display different performance or petrophysical signatures depending on their proximity to fault zones and facies architecture (Figs. 1b and 5d).

Amplitude-based attributes: reservoir distribution and prospectivity

Amplitude-based attributes were generated on or around the interpreted Mamuniyat horizon to delineate reservoir distribution and identify anomalous zones potentially related to lithology and/or hydrocarbon presence. RMS amplitude and energy are among the most widely used attributes for highlighting changes in reflectivity strength that may reflect sand–shale contrasts, porosity variations, or fluid effects23,35. In this study, both attributes consistently identify an area of enhanced seismic response in the western to central part of the field, suggesting a prospective fairway aligned with the structural framework and supported by petrophysical and modeling results (Fig. 6).

The RMS amplitude map extracted over the Mamuniyat interval shows distinct high-amplitude zones (yellow–red colors) that correspond to areas of stronger reflectivity within the reservoir package (Fig. 6a). These anomalies can arise due to increased acoustic impedance contrast between reservoir sands and surrounding shales, local thickening of sand bodies, or changes in pore-fluid properties that produce direct hydrocarbon indicators (DHI). The RMS response is particularly useful because it “gains up” coherent reflection strength while suppressing random noise, improving the visibility of laterally continuous amplitude anomalies23.

A key observation is that the RMS high-amplitude belt is not uniformly distributed across the field; instead, it shows clustering in structurally favorable positions and in zones interpreted to contain sand-dominated facies. This pattern supports the interpretation that the Mamuniyat reservoir is heterogeneous and channelized, with discrete sand bodies that may form stratigraphic–structural traps5. Importantly, the RMS anomaly coincides with the location of the proposed new prospect area, as later verified by energy attribute and spectral decomposition (Fig. 6a).

The energy attribute, defined as the sum of squared amplitudes within a specified time window, provides an additional measure of seismic “strength” that may correlate with reservoir presence and quality36. Energy slices across three-time levels show that most wells are located within high-energy zones, while selected wells (e.g., R6 and R7) are situated outside the strongest anomalies (Fig. 6b–d). The energy attribute highlights a pronounced high-energy area in the western–central portion of the field, consistent with RMS amplitude results and supporting the hypothesis that this zone represents a sand-prone and potentially hydrocarbon-charged fairway (Fig. 6). The agreement between RMS and energy strengthens confidence that the anomaly is not an artifact of processing or noise but rather reflects a persistent subsurface property contrast.

From an interpretation perspective, the convergence of RMS and energy anomalies suggests that (i) the Mamuniyat sands may be thicker and/or cleaner in the anomaly zone, (ii) porosity may be enhanced, and/or (iii) hydrocarbon saturation may contribute to amplitude strengthening. While amplitude anomalies can be non-unique, the subsequent integration with spectral decomposition and petrophysical modeling provides a stronger geological justification for prospect interpretation.

Spectral decomposition: depositional elements and prospect fairways

Spectral decomposition was applied to extract time–frequency information from the seismic volume and to improve visualization of stratigraphic features such as channels and sand fairways within the Mamuniyat interval. The method evaluates changes in amplitude as a function of frequency and phase, generating frequency-specific seismic volumes that can reveal thin-bed tuning, lateral thickness variations, and geomorphologic patterns35,38,39. This approach is particularly valuable for the Mamuniyat Formation because it is interpreted as a sand-prone interval with channelized geometry and stacked depositional elements, where thickness and facies variations may occur over short distances5,8.

Amplitude spectrum analysis indicates that the dominant frequency of the seismic data in the study is approximately 54 Hz (Fig. 7a–c). Key observations

An RGB blending workflow was used to combine three selected frequencies into a single composite image. The chosen combination (reported in the manuscript as the best-performing set after testing) produces an RGB volume that highlights zones with multi-frequency coherence, interpreted as laterally continuous sand bodies or thickened reservoir packages (Fig. 9a,b). The derived geomorphological maps (Fig. 9c,d) show the Mamuniyat reservoir expressed as a distinctive white color, indicating the coincidence of all frequency components and implying robust seismic response across the bandwidth. Importantly, the wells fall predominantly within the white anomaly zone, and a similar white response is present in the western–central area where RMS and energy anomalies also occur (Fig. 9d). This convergence suggests that the anomaly is likely to represent a reservoir fairway rather than an isolated amplitude artifact.

Petrophysical evaluation: reservoir quality, net pay definition, and lithology discrimination

Petrophysical interpretation was performed to quantify shale volume, porosity, water saturation, net thickness, and net pay distribution within the Mamuniyat Formation. The results indicate that, although the reservoir is predominantly sand-prone, it exhibits significant intercalation of sandstone with shale. This heterogeneity is reflected in the variability of petrophysical properties, particularly porosity, which is influenced by the presence of shale laminations and dispersed clay content. While the reservoir quality is generally favorable, it shows notable spatial variability due to variations in shale volume and sedimentary architecture. Interbedded shale intervals contribute to porosity reduction through increased clay content, pore throat obstruction, and diagenetic effects. Given the significant shale content, shaly-sand models such as Indonesian were applied to obtain a more accurate estimation of water saturation. This heterogeneity is consistent with the depositional interpretation of stacked, channelized sand bodies and incised valley-fill systems, and is further supported by variations observed in amplitude and spectral attributes (Figs. 10, 11 and 12).

A cross-plot calibration between core porosity and core permeability yielded a strong empirical relationship (R² > 0.85) (Fig. 10a), enabling estimation of porosity in uncored sections. This step is important for reducing uncertainty in vertical reservoir characterization and for ensuring consistent property trends across wells used later in 3D modeling61,62.

Water saturation was estimated using Indonesian equation for shaley sand model, with formation constants determined using a Pickett plot approach. The reported Pickett plot interpretation indicates resistivity of formation water ~ 31%, a = 0.62, m = 1.95, and n = 1.75 (Fig. 10b). These calibrated parameters improve the reliability of saturation estimates compared to using generalized constants and support a reservoir interpretation where Sw varies from low to moderately high values, consistent with hydrocarbon-bearing intervals.

By integrating Vsh, effective porosity, and water saturation, productive intervals were defined using cutoffs reported in the manuscript: PHIE > ~ 12%, Sw < ~ 50%, and Vsh < ~ 37% (Fig. 11a,b). These cutoffs distinguish reservoir from non-reservoir intervals and enable consistent net pay evaluation across the well set. The net pay thickness is reported as ~ 88.75 ft (Fig. 12), supporting the presence of a substantial productive package within the Mamuniyat Formation. Correlation panels across the field show that reservoir thickness and quality vary laterally, consistent with channelized depositional architecture and structural segmentation (Fig. 12).

Facies and 3D property modeling

A 3D static model was built to integrate well-derived petrophysical properties with seismic interpretation and to provide a spatially continuous representation of facies architecture, reservoir quality distribution, and connectivity within the Mamuniyat Formation. Modeling was conducted following standard workflows for structural framework building, facies modeling, petrophysical upscaling, and property population45,63–70.

Three main facies associations were modeled: sandstone (reservoir), shaly sandstone (poor reservoir), and shale (non-reservoir). The upscaling procedure (Figs. 13a,b and 14) demonstrates that well-scale facies and property variability were transferred to the model grid in a manner that preserves vertical trends while enabling field-scale mapping. The upscaled facies logs show good agreement with porosity and saturation behavior (Fig. 14), confirming that facies are the principal control on porosity distribution and that shale-rich intervals correspond to less effective porosity and higher water saturation.

Integrated prospect evaluation

The integration of seismic attributes (variance, RMS, energy, spectral decomposition) with petrophysical evaluation and 3D property modeling enables identification of two prospective zones interpreted as new exploration targets in the I/R fields. The prospective areas are located within fault-related traps on the upthrown side of the main fault and are characterized by (i) high RMS amplitude, (ii) high energy attribute values, (iii) spectral decomposition white anomalies indicating multi-frequency coherence, (iv) sandstone-dominated facies, (v) elevated effective porosity, and (vi) lower water saturation (Table 1; Figs. 6, 9d, 15 and 16a–d). Implications for reservoir development and uncertainty reduction

The results demonstrate that the Mamuniyat Formation in the I/R oil fields is a heterogeneous, sand-prone reservoir where quality distribution is controlled by the interplay of glacially influenced depositional architecture and fault-related structural segmentation. Variance attributes provide a robust structural framework for understanding compartmentalization (Fig. 5), while RMS amplitude, energy, and spectral decomposition collectively define reservoir fairways and highlight prospective zones (Figs. 6, 7, 8 and 9). Petrophysical analysis confirms low shale volumes, favorable porosity, and variable water saturation that can be used to define net pay consistently (Figs. 10, 11 and 12). Finally, 3D modeling integrates these inputs and provides a spatially continuous view of facies and reservoir properties, enabling identification of new prospect zones and supporting drilling and development planning (Figs. 13, 14, 15 and 16).

Overall, the agreement between seismic-derived indicators and well-based petrophysical properties suggests that the workflow applied in this study is effective for reducing uncertainty in reservoir distribution and for identifying additional opportunities in mature Paleozoic reservoirs of the Murzuq Basin. The approach is also transferable to analogous glaciogenic clastic reservoirs elsewhere in North Africa, were channelized sand bodies and stratigraphic complexity often complicate prediction of reservoir continuity and quality.

A blind well validation was conducted to assess the reliability of the 3D reservoir model. One well (W5) was excluded from the geostatistical modeling process and used as an independent test (Fig. 17). The model-predicted properties, including facies and porosity, were then compared with the actual well-log data at the blind well location. The results show a good agreement between predicted and observed values, indicating that the model successfully captures the spatial distribution of reservoir properties and has strong predictive capability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that an integrated workflow combining well-log petrophysical evaluation, 3D seismic interpretation, seismic attribute analysis, spectral decomposition, and 3D static reservoir modeling provides a robust and reliable approach for characterizing the Upper Ordovician Mamuniyat Formation in the I/R oil fields, Murzuq Basin, Libya. Seismic interpretation and variance attribute analysis reveal a structurally complex reservoir system dominated by a major NW–SE-trending fault system with associated subsidiary faults that control reservoir compartmentalization, continuity, and lateral connectivity. Amplitude-based attributes, including RMS amplitude and energy, effectively delineate sand-prone fairways and prospective zones, while spectral decomposition enhances the visualization of depositional architectures and geomorphological features, supporting the identification of laterally continuous reservoir bodies and potential hydrocarbon-related seismic anomalies. Petrophysical analysis indicates that the Mamuniyat Formation is primarily composed of clean, quartz-rich sandstones with low shale content, moderate to good effective porosity, and variable but locally reduced water saturation. The integration of shale volume, porosity, and water saturation logs enables consistent delineation of net reservoir and net pay intervals, confirming the high storage capacity and hydrocarbon potential of the formation. Cross-plot analysis further substantiates the dominance of sandstone and shaly sandstone facies as the main controls on reservoir quality heterogeneity. The constructed 3D facies and property models reveal significant spatial variability, with high-quality reservoir zones predominantly distributed in the central, northwestern, and southwestern parts of the study area, where enhanced porosity commonly coincides with lower water saturation. Moreover, the integration of seismic attributes with petrophysical and modeling results has led to the identification of two new prospective zones located on the upthrown side of the main fault, characterized by favorable seismic responses, sandstone-dominated facies, improved reservoir properties, and reduced water saturation, indicating strong hydrocarbon potential. The reliability of the modeling approach is further supported by blind well validation, demonstrating good agreement between predicted and observed petrophysical properties. Overall, this integrated methodology significantly reduces geological uncertainty, improves reservoir predictability, and provides a transferable framework for optimizing exploration, appraisal, and development strategies in structurally complex clastic reservoirs. At this stage, the identified zones are considered preliminary targets, and no volumetric estimates (e.g., STOIIP/GIIP) or probabilistic assessments have been performed. Detailed volumetric calculations and uncertainty analysis are recommended in future studies to better quantify the hydrocarbon potential of these zones.
