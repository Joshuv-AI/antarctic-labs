Confidence-Based Decoding is Provably Efficient for Diffusion Language Models 0 0 footnotetext: Corresponding author: Gen Li.
Changxiao Cai, Gen Li

Abstract
Diffusion language models (DLMs) have emerged as a promising alternative to autoregressive (AR) models for language modeling, allowing flexible generation order and parallel generation of multiple tokens. However, this flexibility introduces a challenge absent in AR models: the decoding strategy —which determines the order and number of tokens generated at each iteration—critically affects sampling efficiency. Among decoding strategies explored in practice, confidence-based methods, which adaptively select which and how many tokens to unmask based on prediction confidence, have shown strong empirical performance. Despite this success, our theoretical understanding of confidence-based decoding remains limited. In this work, we develop the first theoretical analysis framework for confidence-based decoding in DLMs. We focus on an entropy sum-based strategy that continues unmasking tokens within each iteration until the cumulative entropy exceeds a threshold, and show that it achieves ε \varepsilon -accurate sampling in KL divergence with an expected number of iterations O ~ ​ ( H ​ ( X 0 ) / ε ) \widetilde{O}(H(X_{0})/\varepsilon) , where H ⁡ ( X 0 ) H(X_{0}) denotes the entropy of the target data distribution. Notably, this strategy yields substantial sampling acceleration when the data distribution has low entropy relative to the sequence length, while automatically adapting to the intrinsic complexity of data without requiring prior knowledge or hyperparameter tuning. Overall, our results provide a theoretical foundation for confidence-based decoding and may inform the design of more efficient decoding strategies for DLMs.

Introduction

Diffusion language models (DLMs)
(
54
;
55
)
have recently emerged as a compelling alternative to the autoregressive (AR) paradigm for language modeling.
Unlike AR models, DLMs use a non-causal generation mechanism that can produce multiple tokens in parallel at each iteration and relax the rigid left-to-right constraint.
The parallel generation capability offers strong potential for sampling acceleration, while the non-causal structure also makes DLMs well suited to tasks that benefit from bidirectional reasoning or the enforcement of global constraints
(
46
;
51
)
.
At scale, DLMs have been shown to achieve performance competitive with AR models
(
66
;
57
;
67
;
74
;
5
;
27
)
.

State-of-the-art DLMs are built upon
masked diffusion models
(MDMs)
(
1
;
7
;
45
)
, a class of discrete diffusion models that augment the vocabulary with an additional absorbing state, referred to as
mask
.
An MDM is defined through two complementary processes. In the forward (masking) process, tokens in a sequence drawn from the target data distribution are progressively replaced by the mask until the sequence becomes fully masked.
The reverse (unmasking) process then seeks to invert this corruption by iteratively recovering masked tokens, thereby transforming a fully masked sequence into a fresh sample from the data distribution.

Central to the reverse process is a
mask predictor
, which learns the conditional distributions of masked tokens given the currently unmasked context at various masking levels
(
45
;
48
;
72
)
.
The reverse dynamics are implemented by repeatedly sampling token values from these conditionals to impute masked positions, thereby moving from a fully masked sequence toward a data-like sample.
In this sense, the mask predictor in MDMs serves as a discrete analog of the score estimator in continuous diffusion models
(
56
)
.
As the joint distribution of multiple masked tokens is generally intractable in high-dimensional settings, practical mask predictors are typically trained to learn the conditional marginals of each masked token, and use the product of per-token conditional marginals as an approximation
(
46
;
63
)
.
In particular, a neural network-based mask predictor can output, in one forward pass, conditional marginal distributions for all currently masked positions.

While this factorized approximation enables parallel generation of DLMs, it also introduces an inevitable bias in the sampling stage—simultaneously unmasking multiple tokens in one iteration ignores their statistical dependence conditioned on the currently revealed context.
At one extreme, unmasking all tokens in a single iteration incurs an error comparable to the discrepancy between the target distribution and the product of its marginals. At the other extreme, generating tokens one-by-one recovers the standard AR approach, which removes the factorization bias but sacrifices the parallel generation advantage of DLMs.

Therefore, the sampling speed and generation quality of DLMs depend critically on the decoding (or unmasking) strategy used at the inference stage, which determines both which positions to unmask and how many tokens to reveal at each iteration.

Parallel decoding strategies.

Decoding strategies used in practice can be broadly categorized into two families.

•

Uniform decoding strategies.
This class of decoding strategies prescribes a fixed total iteration count
T
and a step-size schedule
s_{1},s_{2}\dots,s_{T}
with
s_{t}\geq 1
and
\sum_{t=1}^{T}s_{t}=L
(e.g.,
cosine schedules
(
55
)
or log-linear schedules
(
54
;
45
)
).
At each iteration
t
, one samples a subset of size
s_{t}
uniformly at random from the remaining masked positions and then unmasks the tokens in the sampled subset.
Note that this family of schemes treats all masked positions uniformly and thus belongs to the broader class of non-adaptive decoding strategies.

•

Confidence-based decoding strategies.
This collection of decoding strategies leverages the fact that a mask predictor provides not only a distribution but also a natural confidence signal for each token prediction. It is thus natural to unmask the “easiest” tokens first—those predicted with high confidence—so that newly revealed tokens can serve as additional context for the harder ones in later iterations
(
8
;
73
;
22
)
. In practice, confidence is often quantified by the entropy of the predicted distribution
(
3
)
, the mass of the most likely token
(
46
;
68
;
63
)
, or the likelihood gap between the two most likely tokens
(
26
)
, leading to a range of effective heuristics. Compared to uniform decoding strategies, these schemes determine the decoding order and batch size in an adaptive manner.

In contrast to the rapid development of empirical decoding strategies, our theoretical understanding of their sampling efficiency remains limited.
For uniform random schedules, substantial recent progress has been made in establishing theoretical guarantees for sampling performance
(
34
;
14
;
28
;
16
;
71
)
.
We defer a detailed discussion to Section
1.2
.
By comparison, despite their strong empirical performance, confidence-based decoding remains far less understood theoretically.
The fundamental difficulty lies in their inherently adaptive decoding dynamics: the set of tokens unmasked at each iteration depends on the
realization
of the tokens generated so far, through the corresponding confidence levels produced by the mask predictor.
This adaptivity raises several fundamental but unanswered questions. For instance, if the total number of iterations is not predetermined, how many iterations are needed to unmask all tokens? What is the resulting sampling error? Can these schemes provably adapt to the (unknown) intrinsic complexity or dependence structure of the data distribution?

Such a theory-practice gap motivates the central question of our study:

Are confidence-based decoding strategies in DLMs provably efficient?

1.1
Our contributions

In this work, we take an initial step toward answering this question by developing sampling convergence guarantees for confidence-based decoding in DLMs, with the goal of demystifying their practical effectiveness.

We focus on a canonical confidence measure—entropy—and investigate entropy-based decoding that selects unmasking positions and batch sizes based on the entropy of the predicted distribution at each masked position.
Concretely, we study a representative
entropy sum-based strategy
that, at each iteration, greedily unmasks tokens from the remaining masked positions until the running entropy sum of the newly unmasked tokens exceeds a prescribed threshold, thereby revealing as many tokens as possible per iteration while keeping the cumulative prediction uncertainty controlled.

We demonstrate that
\varepsilon
-accuracy in Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence can be attained with an expected number of iterations that scales as

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\bigg(\frac{H(X_{0})}{\varepsilon}\bigg).

This result implies a sublinear iteration complexity in the sequence length
L
when the data distribution has low entropy
H(X_{0})\ll L
, thereby achieving provable sampling speedup compared to the
\Theta(L)
iteration complexity of the AR decoding or naive uniform parallel decoding.
Notably, this scheme does not require any prior knowledge of the data distribution such as its entropy, and thus automatically adapts to the intrinsic low-complexity data structure to achieve sampling acceleration.

To establish these results, we introduce a novel analysis framework for adaptive decoding dynamics in DLMs that may be of independent interest.
Overall, our results provide the first theoretical justification for the effectiveness of confidence-based unmasking strategies, and offer a principled explanation of when and why entropy-based decoding can achieve accelerated sampling without sacrificing generation fidelity.
Our theoretical insights may inform the design of more efficient inference schemes in practice.

Convergence theory for uniform decoding strategies.

Existing sampling convergence analysis for DLMs largely focuses on uniform decoding strategies and two main viewpoints have been pursued.
The first line of work directly studies the sampling error accumulated in the iterative unmasking procedure under the factorized approximation.
For balanced step-size schedules (i.e.,

Preliminaries

In this section, we provide an overview of the fundamental concepts underlying DLMs.

Forward (masking) process.

Consider a token sequence
X_{0}=(X^{1}_{0},X^{2}_{0},\dots,X^{L}_{0})\in\mathbb{X}^{L}
sampled from the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
.
The forward process progressively corrupts
X_{0}
by replacing its tokens with the mask symbol
\mathsf{M}
until the entire sequence is masked.
Formally, let
X_{t}
denote the partially masked sequence obtained after
t
masking steps and let
\mathcal{M}_{t}\coloneqq\{i\in[L]\colon X_{t}^{i}=\mathsf{M}\}
denote the index set of masked tokens.
At each step
t\geq 1
, we select a masking set
\mathcal{S}_{t}\subseteq[L]\setminus\mathcal{M}_{t-1}
and update
X_{t-1}
to
X_{t}
by replacing tokens at positions in the set
\mathcal{S}_{t}
with
\mathsf{M}
:

\displaystyle X_{t}^{i}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{M},&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{S}_{t},\\
X_{t-1}^{i},&\text{if }i\notin\mathcal{S}_{t}.\end{cases}

The masking process continues until all tokens have been masked.

Mask predictor training.

DLMs are trained to reverse the above forward masking process by learning the conditional distribution of masked tokens given the unmasked ones.
A notable feature of mask predictor training is that this posterior distribution is time-independent—it depends only on the unmasked context and not on the masking step
t

(
48
;
72
)
.
More precisely, for each token index
i\in[L]
and any set of unmasked positions
\mathcal{S}\subseteq[L]\setminus\{i\}
, we define the true conditional marginal distribution of
X^{i}_{0}
given
X_{0}^{\mathcal{S}}
under the data distribution
p_{\mathsf{data}}
as

\displaystyle p^{i}(x\mid z^{\mathcal{S}})\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\big\{X_{0}^{i}=x\mid X_{0}^{\mathcal{S}}=z^{\mathcal{S}}\big\}\qquad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{X},\,z^{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathbb{X}^{|\mathcal{S}|}.

(4)

The objective is to learn estimators
p^{i}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
for each conditional marginal
p^{i}(\cdot\mid\cdot)
, thereby forming a mask predictor
p_{\theta}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p^{i}_{\theta}
.

The mask predictor is trained by maximizing the log-likelihood of masked tokens conditioned on unmasked ones:

\displaystyle\max_{p_{\theta}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p_{\theta}^{i}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0},\mathcal{S}}\Bigg[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{c}}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{c}}}\log p^{i}_{\theta}\big(X_{0}^{i}\mid X_{0}^{\mathcal{S}}\big)\Bigg].

(5)

This objective is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence between the data distribution and the distribution induced by the product of learned conditional marginals over partially masked sequences. In practice, the expectation is approximated by averaging over a finite number of training samples and random masking patterns.

We remark that the product of the true conditional marginals
\prod_{i=1}^{L}p^{i}
defined in (
4
) is the maximizer of the training objective (
5
).

Reverse (unmasking) process.

Given a trained mask predictor
p_{\theta}
, DLMs produce a token sequence through an iterative unmasking procedure.
Starting from a fully masked sequence
Y_{0}=(\mathsf{M},\mathsf{M},\ldots,\mathsf{M})\in\overline{\mathbb{X}}^{L}
with cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{0}=\varnothing

t=1,2,\dots
until all tokens have been unmasked.

1.

Select unmasking set.

Choose a subset
\mathcal{D}_{t}
of the currently masked positions

decoding strategy

2.

Draw the tokens for all positions in

independently

\displaystyle\widehat{Y}^{i}\sim p_{\theta}^{i}\big(\cdot\mid Y_{t-1}\big),\quad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}.

(6)

The sequence is then updated by inserting the newly sampled tokens:

\displaystyle Y_{t}^{i}=\begin{cases}\widehat{Y}^{i},&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{D}_{t},\\
Y_{t-1}^{i},&\text{if }i\notin\mathcal{D}_{t}.\end{cases}

Main results

We first introduce the entropy sum-based decoding strategy. Its key feature is that each decoding iteration is determined by the total uncertainty, measured by entropy, accumulated over the current batch of unmasked tokens. This leads to an adaptive rule that enlarges the unmasking set automatically in low-uncertainty regions and shrinks it when the mask predictor is uncertain.

Decoding procedure.

At a high level, the entropy sum-based strategy scans the remaining masked positions in a random order and continues unmasking tokens until the cumulative entropy of the current batch exceeds a threshold.

The random permutation
\Pi
is introduced solely to simplify the presentation and analysis. Equivalently, the algorithm may be viewed as selecting the next token from the remaining masked positions uniformly at random.

An important observation is that, for each iteration
t
, conditioned on the permutation
\Pi
, the cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
, and the partially unmasked sequence
Y_{t-1}
, the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
(and thus the cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t}=\mathcal{W}_{t-1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{t}
) is fully determined by the conditional entropies:

\displaystyle\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{t-1})\big),\quad k=W_{t-1}+1,W_{t-1}+2,\dots,L.

Once
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is determined, we obtain the iterate
Y_{t}
by generating the tokens in
\mathcal{D}_{t}
independently according to the corresponding conditional distributions

Y_{t}^{i}\sim p^{i}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{t-1}),\quad i\in\mathcal{D}_{t},

or equivalently,

Y_{t}^{\Pi_{k}}\sim p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{t-1}),\quad W_{t-1}+1\leq k\leq W_{t}.

Therefore, the overall sampling process can be summarized by the following Markov chain:

\displaystyle\Pi\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{0})\big)\bigr\}_{k=1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{1})\to Y_{1}\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{1})\big)\bigr\}_{k=W_{1}+1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathcal{W}_{2})\to Y_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\to\dots\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{T-1})\big)\bigr\}_{k=W_{T-1}+1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{T},\mathcal{W}_{T})\to Y_{T},

(8)

where
T
denotes the (random) total number of iterations.

Connection to empirical entropy sum-based strategies.

Algorithm
1
is closely related to the entropy-based decoding strategy recently proposed in
3
. Their method sorts the remaining tokens by conditional entropy and then selects the largest subset for which the gap between the entropy sum and the maximum entropy remains below a prescribed threshold.
Numerical results in
3
demonstrate that such an entropy sum-based decoding strategy achieves considerable speedups in generation, with quality that matches or surpasses that of uniform unmasking schemes.
The main difference is that Algorithm
1
scans token positions in a random order instead of a deterministic entropy-sorted order.
We introduce this randomness primarily to facilitate the theoretical analysis, and it would be of interest to investigate whether similar theoretical guarantees can be established for the deterministic variants.

3.2
Theoretical guarantee

We now state the performance guarantee for the entropy sum-based decoding scheme (Algorithm
1
) in Theorem
1
. The proof sketch can be found in Section
4.2
.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that the mask predictor
p_{\theta}
is optimal, namely,
p_{\theta}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p^{i}
is the product of true conditional marginals.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, set the threshold to
\eta=\varepsilon/\big(4(\log_{2}L+1)\big)
. Then the entropy sum-based strategy in Algorithm
1
achieves
\varepsilon
-accurate sampling in KL divergence, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]\leq\varepsilon.

Moreover, the expected number of iterations satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq 4\bigg(\frac{H(X_{0})}{\varepsilon}+1\bigg)(\log_{2}L+1)+1,

(9)

where
H(X_{0})
denotes the entropy of the target data distribution.

To the best of our knowledge, this result provides the first theoretical guarantee for confidence-based decoding strategies in DLMs.
We discuss several important implications below.

•

Iteration complexity.
The expected number of iterations required to achieve
\varepsilon
-accuracy in KL divergence is at most

\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\bigg(\frac{H(X_{0})}{\varepsilon}\bigg).

(10)

Up to a logarithmic dependence on the sequence length
L
, this iteration complexity depends only on the entropy of the target data distribution, which can be viewed as a measure of its intrinsic complexity.
Consequently, when the target distribution has low entropy, namely
H(X_{0})\ll L
, the entropy sum-based decoding scheme yields a substantial sampling speedup.

•

Adaptivity to intrinsic structure.
The above sampling acceleration is achieved without requiring any prior knowledge of the data distribution or hyperparameter tuning. This highlights the distribution-agnostic adaptivity of the entropy-based decoding—it automatically exploits low-complexity structure in the target distribution to accelerate generation.

3.3
Effect of entropy criterion

A natural question is how the choice of entropy criterion affects decoding efficiency.
To investigate this, we present a second entropy-based decoding strategy—the
maximum entropy-based strategy
—which differs from the entropy sum-based strategy in a single design choice.
Instead of controlling the cumulative entropy of the entire batch, it terminates an iteration as soon as any single token’s conditional entropy exceeds a prescribed threshold, subject to an explicit cap on the batch size.

As we will see shortly, this alternative strategy still achieves an iteration complexity sublinear in the sequence length. However, its sampling guarantee is weaker in two respects: the iteration complexity bound is looser, and the parameter choice requires knowledge of the entropy of the target distribution.
This contrast highlights the importance of how entropy is incorporated into the design of decoding strategies.

Maximum entropy-based decoding strategy.

As before, we begin by drawing a random permutation
\Pi
of
[L]
.
At iteration
t\geq 1
, given the previous iterate
Y_{t-1}
with cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
, we scan the remaining positions in the permutation order and keep adding them to the current batch
\mathcal{D}_{t}
.
The iteration ends as soon as one of the following occurs:

(i)

the entropy of the most recently unmasked token
exceeds the threshold
\eta
(this token is included in the set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
);

(ii)

the current batch reaches the cap size
S_{\max}
;

(iii)

all
L
tokens have been generated.

Formally, the index set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
of newly unmasked tokens at iteration
t
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{t}=\big\{\Pi_{k}\colon W_{t-1}<k\leq k_{t}\big\},

where
k_{t}
is the first permutation index after
W_{t-1}
for which the entropy exceeds
\eta
, or
W_{t-1}+S_{\max}
if no such index appears within the next
S_{\max}
positions, or
L
if no such index appears before all tokens are generated. In other words,
k_{t}
is defined by

\displaystyle k_{t}\coloneqq\min\bigl\{i>W_{t-1}:\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{i}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{t-1})\big)>\eta\bigr\}\wedge(W_{t-1}+S_{\max})\wedge L.

A complete description of the unmasking scheme is given in Algorithm
2
.

Compared with the entropy sum-based decoding scheme, this strategy differs only in the stopping criterion:

1.

it reacts to the entropy of a single token rather than the cumulative entropy of the whole batch;

2.

it explicitly imposes a maximum unmasking size
S_{\max}
.

Despite this difference, these two entropy-based strategies share the same Markovian structure: the unmasking sets
\mathcal{D}_{t}
and
\mathcal{W}_{t}
at iteration
t
are fully determined by the previous iterate
Y_{t-1}
together with its cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
. Accordingly, the sampling process under the maximum entropy-based decoding scheme also forms a Markov chain as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{0})\big)\bigr\}_{k=1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{W}_{1})\to Y_{1}\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{1})\big)\bigr\}_{k=W_{1}+1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{2},\mathcal{W}_{2})\to Y_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\to\dots\to\bigl\{\mathtt{H}\big(p^{\Pi_{k}}_{\theta}(\cdot\mid Y_{T-1})\big)\bigr\}_{k=W_{T-1}+1}^{L}\to(\mathcal{D}_{T},\mathcal{W}_{T})\to Y_{T}.

(11)

Theoretical guarantee.

We now present the performance guarantee of the maximum entropy-based inference scheme (Algorithm

) in Theorem
2
below. The proof can be found in Appendix

.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that the mask predictor
p_{\theta}
is optimal, namely,
p_{\theta}=\prod_{i=1}^{L}p^{i}
is the product of true conditional marginals.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, set the entropy threshold to
\eta=\sqrt{{\varepsilon H(X_{0})}/{L}}
and the maximum unmasking size to
S_{\max}=\lfloor\sqrt{{\varepsilon L}/{H(X_{0})}}\rfloor\vee 1
. Then the maximum entropy-based unmasking strategy in Algorithm
2
achieves
\varepsilon
-accurate sampling in KL divergence, namely,

\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]\leq\varepsilon.

Moreover, the expected number of iterations satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{H(X_{0})L}{\varepsilon}}+1.

(12)

In words, the maximum entropy-based decoding strategy attains
\varepsilon
-accuracy in KL divergence with an expected number of iterations scaling as

\displaystyle O\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{H(X_{0})L}{\varepsilon}}\bigg).

(13)

As a consequence, when the entropy of the target data distribution satisfies
H(X_{0})\ll L
, it achieves a sublinear iteration complexity in the sequence length
L
, thereby implying a provable speedup over fully sequential generation.

Nevertheless, compared with the entropy sum-based strategy, this maximum entropy-based strategy has two important shortcomings:

•

In the parallel sampling regime, where the iteration complexity is smaller than the sequence length
L
, the bound in (
13
) is worse than that in (
10
) by a factor of
\sqrt{\varepsilon L/H(X_{0})}\gg 1
.

•

The parameter choices
\eta
and
S_{\max}
needed to achieve a sublinear iteration complexity require explicit knowledge of the entropy
H(X_{0})
of the target data distribution. This stands in stark contrast to the entropy sum-based strategy, which is fully distribution-adaptive.

Since the theoretical guarantees for these two entropy-based decoding strategies are established under the same analysis framework,
comparing them suggests that the choice of entropy criterion plays a critical role in efficient entropy-based decoding.
An important future direction is thus to develop a deeper understanding of how different entropy criteria affect the sampling performance of DLMs, for example, by deriving algorithm-dependent lower bounds on the iteration complexity of various decoding strategies.

4
Analysis

In this section, we outline the proof strategy for the main theory (Theorem
1
) for the entropy sum-based decoding strategy.

4.1
Preliminary facts

Before presenting the proof sketch, we collect several basic facts and notational conventions that will be used throughout the analysis.

Iterate representation.

Starting from the fully masked initialization
Y_{0}=(\mathsf{M},\dots,\mathsf{M})
, the iterate
Y_{t}
at step
t
is completely characterized by the cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t}
together with the revealed token values on that set, namely
Y^{\mathcal{W}_{t}}_{t}
, since
Y^{i}_{t}=\mathsf{M}
for all
i\in\mathcal{W}_{t}^{\mathrm{c}}
.
Thus, the entropy-based sampler can be equivalently viewed as iteratively filling in the coordinates indexed by
\mathcal{W}_{t}
.
To simplify notation, we suppress the time index
t
in the iterate
Y_{t}
whenever it is clear from context, and we also write
X=X_{0}
.

Permutation-induced order.

Given a fixed permutation
\Pi
of
[L]
, the sampler scans token indices in the order
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\ldots,\Pi_{L}
, where
\Pi_{k}
denotes the index of the
k
-th token to be unmasked. Let
\Pi^{-1}
denote the inverse permutation so that
\Pi^{-1}_{i}=k
gives the unmasking order
k
of the token with index
i
.
Moreover, for each token index
i\in[L]
, there exists a unique iteration
t_{i}
at which token
i
is unmasked, that is, the iteration satisfying
i\in\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}=\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}
.

Unmasking sets.

The entropy-based sampling procedure produces an increasing sequence of cumulative unmasking sets
\varnothing=\mathcal{W}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathcal{W}_{T}=[L]
where
T
is the random total number of iterations. For convenience of analysis, we extend this sequence by defining
\mathcal{W}_{t}\coloneqq[L]
for
t>T
so that the sequence
(\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t=0}^{L}
is defined for all
0\leq t\leq L
.
The incremental unmasking sets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
are extended in an analogous manner by setting
\mathcal{D}_{t}\coloneqq\varnothing
for
t>T
.

In addition, since tokens are revealed according to the permutation order, the cumulative unmasking set at iteration
t
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{t}=\{\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{W_{t}}\},

(14)

where we recall the notation
W_{t}\coloneqq|\mathcal{W}_{t}|
.
Equivalently, the unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t}
consists of the first
W_{t}
indices in the permutation
\Pi
.
Similarly, the set of newly unmasked tokens at iteration
t
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{W}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{t-1}=\{\Pi_{W_{t-1}+1},\ldots,\Pi_{W_{t}}\}.

(15)

Dependence structure of sampling process.

Finally, we record two critical consequences of the Markovian structure of the entropy sum-based sampling procedure as shown in (
8
).

•

First, for a fixed permutation
\Pi
, the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
at iteration
t
, and hence the cumulative unmasking set
\mathcal{W}_{t}
, are completely determined by the cumulative set
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
and sampled token values
Y^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}=(Y^{\Pi_{1}},\dots,Y^{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t-1}|}})
from the previous iteration.

•

Second, once the permutation
\Pi
and the fully sampled token sequence
Y=(Y^{1},\ldots,Y^{L})\in\mathbb{X}^{L}
are given, the entire sampling trajectory is uniquely determined.
In particular, one can recover the total number of iterations
T
, the cumulative unmasking sets
(\mathcal{W}_{t})_{t=0}^{L}
, and the incremental unmasking sets
(\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
. In other words, the total number of iterations
T
and sets
(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
are deterministic functions of the sampled token sequence
Y
and permutation
\Pi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle T=T(Y,\Pi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathcal{W}_{t-1}=\mathcal{W}_{t-1}(Y,\Pi),\quad\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{D}_{t}(Y,\Pi),\quad t\in[L].

(16)

4.2
Proof sketch of Theorem
1

Having introduced the necessary preliminaries, we now outline the proof of Theorem
1
for the entropy sum-based decoding strategy.

Step 1: Express KL error along unmasking trajectory.

We begin by expressing both the sampled distribution and the data distribution along the adaptive unmasking trajectory.
The key insight is that, by the characterization in (
16
), once the permutation
\pi
and the sampled sequence
x
are given, the realized total iteration count
\tau
and the realized sets
(w_{t-1},d_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
are fully determined.

Concretely, when the mask predictor is optimal, conditioned on a fixed permutation
\Pi=\pi
, the entropy-based sampler generates the tokens in each unmasking set independently according to the true conditional marginals
p^{i}_{\theta}=p^{i}
defined in (
4
).
Hence the density of the sampled distribution factorizes within each batch as a product of conditional marginals:

\displaystyle\log p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi=\pi}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\sum_{i\in d_{t}}\log p(x^{i}\mid x^{w_{t-1}})=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{t\leq\tau\}\sum_{i\in d_{t}}\log p(x^{i}\mid x^{w_{t-1}}),\quad x\in\mathbb{X}^{L},

(17a)

where the total number of iterations
\tau=\tau(x,\pi)
and the sets
d_{t}=d_{t}(x,\pi)
and
w_{t-1}=w_{t-1}(x,\pi)
for
1\leq t\leq L
are uniquely determined by
x
and
\pi
.

By contrast, under the data distribution, the tokens revealed at each iteration follow their true conditional joint distribution.
Grouping the density according to the same unmasking sets
(w_{t-1},d_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
gives

\displaystyle\log p_{X_{0}}(x)=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau}\log p(x^{d_{t}}\mid x^{w_{t-1}})=\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{t\leq\tau\}\log p(x^{d_{t}}\mid x^{w_{t-1}}).

(17b)

Using the two factorizations above, let us calculate the KL divergence between the data and sampled distributions.
Fixing a permutation
\Pi=\pi
, we can combine (
17a
) and (
17b
) to derive

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi=\pi}\big)

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{X_{0}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{t\leq T\}\log\frac{p(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})}{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}p(X^{i}\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})}\,\Big|\,\Pi=\pi\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{t\leq T\}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{p(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})}{\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}p(X^{i}\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi=\pi\bigg]\,\big|\,\Pi=\pi\Bigg],

(18)

where the last line holds due to the tower property, linearity of expectation, and the fact that
\{t\leq T\}=\{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}\neq[L]\}
can be determined by
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
.
Importantly, the only randomness here (with
\Pi=\pi
fixed) comes from
X\sim p_{X_{0}}
. Once
X
is fixed, the iteration count
T
, the sets
(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},\mathcal{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
and the subsequences
(X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}})_{t=1}^{L}
are all determined.

We next study the inner conditional expectation, which corresponds to the contribution of iteration
t
to the overall KL sampling error.
Fix an iteration
t\in[L]
, and condition on
\Pi=\pi
and
(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=(w_{t-1},x^{w_{t-1}})
.
Under this conditioning, the set
\mathcal{D}_{t}=d_{t}
is deterministic, and we can derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\log\frac{p(X^{d_{t}}\mid X^{w_{t-1}})}{\prod_{i\in d_{t}}p(X^{i}\mid X^{w_{t-1}})}\,\Big|\,(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=(w_{t-1},x^{w_{t-1}}),\Pi=\pi\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\mathbb{E}_{X^{d_{t}}}\bigg[\log\frac{p(X^{d_{t}}\mid x^{w_{t-1}})}{\prod_{i\in d_{t}}p(X^{i}\mid x^{w_{t-1}})}\,\Big|\,(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=(w_{t-1},x^{w_{t-1}}),\Pi=\pi\bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{=}\mathsf{KL}\Big(p^{d_{t}}(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in d_{t}}p^{i}(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})\Big),

(19)

where (i) is true because the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}=d_{t}
becomes deterministic conditioned on
\Pi=\pi
and
(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=(w_{t-1},x^{w_{t-1}})
;
(ii) holds because the expectation is taken with respect to
X^{d_{t}}
under the true conditional distribution
p(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})
, which is independent of
\Pi
.

Substituting (
19
) into (
4.2
) and then averaging over both
X\sim p_{X_{0}}
and
\Pi
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi,X\sim p_{X_{0}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{KL}\Big(p^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}p^{i}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\Big)\Bigg].

(20)

Thus, the expected KL sampling error decomposes as a sum over iterations
t
, where each term measures the discrepancy between the true conditional joint distribution of the tokens unmasked at iteration
t
and the product of their conditional marginals.

Step 2: Decompose KL error into sum of mutual information.

In light of the expression for the total KL error in (
20
), it suffices to analyze the contribution from each iteration. This reduces to understanding the KL divergence between a joint distribution and the product of its marginals.

To this end, Lemma
1
below states that this KL divergence can be decomposed into a sum of mutual information terms, each involving one token and the tokens with larger indices.
The proof can be found in Appendix
A.1
.

Lemma 1
.

Consider a random sequence
(Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{d})
with joint distribution
p_{Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{d}}
and marginal distributions
p_{Z^{i}}
for
i\in[d]
.
The KL divergence between the joint distribution and the product of marginals is given by

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{d}}\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in[d]}p_{Z^{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}I(Z^{i};Z^{i+1},Z^{i+2},\dots,Z^{d}).

(21)

Moreover, for any
j\in[d]
, the KL divergence can be upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\Big(p_{Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{d}}\,\big\|\,\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_{Z^{i}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in[d]\setminus\{j\}}I(Z^{i};Z^{1},\dots,Z^{i-1},Z^{i+1}\dots,Z^{d}).

(22)

Remark 2
.

Both expressions involve a sum of
d-1
terms, but they differ in structure. In the identity (
21
), the last index
i=d
is excluded, and each term measures the mutual information between
Z^{i}
and the variables with larger indices
Z^{i+1},Z^{i+2},\dots,Z^{d}
. In the upper bound (
22
), one instead excludes an arbitrary index
j\in[d]
, and each remaining term measures the mutual information between
Z^{i}
and all other variables
Z^{k}
with
k\neq i
.

We shall apply the upper bound (
22
) in Lemma
1
to control the per-iteration KL sampling error.
The key point is that, by construction, each iteration stops as soon as the running entropy sum of the currently unmasked tokens exceeds the threshold
\eta
. Consequently, within each iteration, there can be at most one token whose entropy is larger than
\eta
.
Motivated by this observation, define

\displaystyle j_{t}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\argmax_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}),

(23)

with ties broken arbitrarily. Then every index
i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}\setminus\{j_{t}\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\leq\eta.

Here, we remind the reader that
\mathtt{H}(X\mid Z)
denotes the entropy of
X
conditioned on a realization of
Z
(without taking the expectation of
Z
), so it is a function of
Z
. Similarly,
\mathtt{I}(X;Y_{T}\mid Z)
denotes the mutual information between
X
and
Y
given a realization of
Z
.

Now applying (
22
) in Lemma
1
with
p_{Z^{1},\ldots,Z^{d}}=p^{D_{t}}(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})
and
j=j_{t}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\Big(p^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}p^{i}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\,\mathds{1}{\{i\neq j_{t}\}}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\,\mathds{1}{\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\leq\eta\big\}}.

(24)

Remark 3
.

Conditioned on
\mathcal{W}_{t-1}
and
X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}
, the conditional entropy

\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})
is independent of the permutation
\Pi
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi).

This is in contrast to the mutual information terms, which depend explicitly on
the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
, and hence on
\Pi
.

Thus, the KL sampling error contributed by iteration
t
is controlled by a sum of mutual information, where each term quantifies the dependence between one unmasked token and the rest of the tokens unmasked in iteration
t
, conditioned on the tokens revealed previously.
The indicator function in (
24
) highlights an important feature of the entropy-sum stopping rule: within each iteration, only tokens with conditional entropy at most
\eta
contribute to this KL error bound.
As will be clear momentarily, this indicator function will be essential in the subsequent analysis.

Substituting the bound (
24
) into the KL error expression in (
20
), we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\Big[\mathsf{KL}\big(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi}\big)\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\,\mathds{1}{\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\leq\eta\big\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\,\mathds{1}{\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})\leq\eta\big\}}\Big],

(25)

where the last step rearranges the summation order from the iteration index
t
to the token index
i
, where we recall that
t_{i}
denotes the index of the iteration at which token
i
is unmasked.

In summary, the expected KL sampling error is bounded by a sum of mutual information terms over token index
i\in[L]
. Each term measures the dependence between token
i
and the other unmasked tokens in the same iteration, conditioned on the already unmasked tokens and the permutation.

Step 3: Average over random unmasking order.

Equipped with the KL error bound in (
25
), it remains to control each token-wise contribution separately.

To this end, fix a token index
i
and consider the corresponding summand:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},\Pi^{-1}_{i}}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}{\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})\leq\eta\big\}}\Big].

(26)

To make the dependence on the permutation explicit, we decompose the randomness of
\Pi
into the position
\Pi^{-1}_{i}
of token
i
in the permutation
\Pi
and the relative ordering of all other tokens, denoted by
\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}
.
Concretely, we define

\displaystyle\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\coloneqq\big(\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{\Pi^{-1}_{i}-1},\Pi_{\Pi^{-1}_{i}+1},\dots,\Pi_{L}\big).

(27)

That is, the permutation
\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}
is obtained from
\Pi
by removing the index
i
while preserving the relative order of all other tokens.

Our goal is to show, after averaging over the randomness of the position
\Pi^{-1}_{i}
, the contribution of token
i
to the KL sampling error admits a clean and tractable upper bound.
However, the major technical difficulty arises from the complicated dependence between the set
\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}\setminus\{i\}
—the tokens revealed in the same iteration as token
i
—and the full permutation
\Pi
.
In particular, the iteration index
t_{i}
and the set
\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}
both vary with
\Pi^{-1}_{i}
, making it challenging to directly analyze (
26
).

To address this, recall that
D_{t}\coloneqq|\mathcal{D}_{t}|
denotes the size of the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
at iteration
t\in[L]
. We then introduce a sequence of dyadic size envelopes
(\overline{D}_{t})_{t=1}^{L}
in
\mathbb{R}
, defined recursively by
\overline{D}_{1}=0
and, for
2\leq t\leq L
,

\displaystyle\overline{D}_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\overline{D}_{t-1},&\text{if}~~D_{t-1}<\overline{D}_{t-1},\\
2D_{t-1},&\text{if}~~D_{t-1}\geq\overline{D}_{t-1}.\end{cases}

(28)

In essence, the envelope remains unchanged as long as the previous set size stays below it; once the previous set size reaches the envelope, the envelope is doubled.

Using the size-envelopes constructed above, the following lemma shows that, after averaging over the random position of token
i
, the mutual-information term in (
26
) can be bounded by a much simpler quantity involving only the conditional entropy of
X^{i}
and the event that the corresponding set size crosses the envelope. The proof is deferred to Appendix
A.2
.

Lemma 2
.

For any token index
i\in[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},\Pi^{-1}_{i}}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}_{t_{i}}\setminus\{i\}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}{\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})\leq\eta\big\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}}\Big[\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})\wedge\eta\big)\mathds{1}\big\{D_{t_{i}}\geq\overline{D}_{t_{i}}\big\}\Big].

(29)

In words, after averaging over the random unmasking order of token
i
, its contribution to the KL sampling error is controlled by its truncated conditional entropy, and contributes only on the event that the size of its unmasking set is at least as large as the corresponding envelope.
Thus, the intricate dependence structure in (
26
) is reduced to a much more tractable indicator of whether a size-doubling event occurs.

Step 4: Control KL error by entropy-based stopping rule.

Summing the bound (
2
) from Lemma
2
over all tokens and combining it with the KL error expression in (
25
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]

\displaystyle\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\wedge\eta\big)\mathds{1}\big\{D_{t_{i}}\geq\overline{D}_{t_{i}}\big\}\Bigg].

(30)

We now reindex the sum by iteration rather than token.
Since
\mathcal{D}_{t}
is exactly the set of tokens revealed at iteration
t
,
the right-hand side of (
30
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\wedge\eta\big)\mathds{1}\big\{D_{t_{i}}\geq\overline{D}_{t_{i}}\big\}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{D_{t}\geq\overline{D}_{t}\}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\wedge\eta\big)\Bigg]

Next, we bound the inner sum using the stopping rule. By construction, within each iteration the cumulative entropy remains below
\eta
until the final token is added. Therefore, the total contribution of all but the last token is at most
\eta
, while the final token contributes at most
\eta
after truncation. Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\wedge\eta\big)\leq 2\eta,

and hence

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{L}\big(\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\wedge\eta\big)\mathds{1}\big\{D_{t_{i}}\geq\overline{D}_{t_{i}}\big\}\Bigg]\leq 2\eta\cdot\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{D_{t}\geq\overline{D}_{t}\}\Bigg].

(31)

It remains to bound the number of iterations for which the event
D_{t}\geq\overline{D}_{t}
occurs.
Let
s_{1}<s_{2}<\dots
be the iterations at which this event holds. By the definition of the size-envelope sequence in (
28
), whenever
D_{s_{j}}\geq\overline{D}_{s_{j}}
occurs, the envelope at the next such iteration must have doubled. Thus, we have

D_{s_{j+1}}\geq\overline{D}_{s_{j+1}}=2D_{s_{j}},

and by induction,

D_{s_{j}}\geq 2^{j-1}D_{s_{1}}=2^{j-1}.

Since
D_{s_{j}}\leq L
for all
j
, this implies that the number of such iterations satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{L}\mathds{1}\{D_{t}\geq\overline{D}_{t}\}\leq\log_{2}L+1.

(32)

Finally, combining (
30
)–(
32
) gives the final bound on the expected KL sampling error:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta(\log_{2}L+1).

(33)

Setting
\eta=\varepsilon/(4(\log_{2}L+1))
immediately yields the desired result
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]\leq\varepsilon
.

Step 5: Bound expected number of iterations.

Finally, let us study the expected number of iterations
\mathbb{E}[T]
.
To this end, we use the following entropy identity, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
A.3
.

Lemma 3
.

For any fixed permutation
\Pi
, the entropy of the data distribution admits the following decomposition:

\displaystyle H(X_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{X_{0}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathtt{H}(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\Bigg]

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim p_{X_{0}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\Bigg]-\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi}).

(35)

We now combine this identity with the stopping rule. By construction, every iteration except the last one accumulates conditional entropy at least
\eta
, that is,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\geq\eta,

for all
t<T
.
Therefore, substituting this into the entropy identity (
35
) yields

\displaystyle H(X_{0})\geq\mathbb{E}[T-1]\eta-\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big].

Using the KL bound from (
33
), we further obtain

\displaystyle H(X_{0})\geq\big(\mathbb{E}[T]-1-4(\log_{2}L+1)\big)\eta.

Therefore, rearranging the above inequality and substituting
\eta=\varepsilon/(4(\log_{2}L+1))
into it yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq\frac{H(X_{0})}{\eta}+4(\log_{2}L+1)+1=4\bigg(\frac{H(X_{0})}{\varepsilon}+1\bigg)(\log_{2}L+1)+1.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1
.

5
Discussion

In this work, we have made progress toward understanding the sampling efficiency of confidence-based decoding strategies in DLMs. Our results show that entropy-based unmasking strategies can provably accelerate sampling, achieving an iteration complexity that is sublinear in the sequence length when the data distribution has low entropy. Notably, they reveal that entropy-based strategies can adapt to the unknown intrinsic complexity of the data distribution without requiring any prior knowledge or hyperparameter tuning.

Moving forward, it would be of interest to understand the performance of the entropy-based decoding schemes based on a deterministic order (e.g., a sorted order) instead of the random order considered in this work. Also, it would be valuable to extend our analysis to more general confidence measures beyond entropy, such as the mass of the most likely token and the mass gap between the two most likely tokens.
Second, our current analysis focuses on the scenario where the optimal mask predictor is used. It would be important to understand the impact of training error in the mask predictor on the sampling performance under confidence-based decoding, which may yield insights into the design of more effective training objectives for DLMs.
Third, another important direction is to establish lower bounds on the iteration complexity of confidence-based decoding schemes, which would shed light on the fundamental limits of sampling acceleration when using this class of strategies.

Proof of Lemma

We prove Lemma

Fix an arbitrary

Recall the definitions of
\overline{D}_{s}
and

\displaystyle s=s(k,q)\coloneqq\max\big\{u\leq k\,:\,\overline{D}_{u}\leq q\big\}.

(47)

This set is non-empty because

\displaystyle D_{s}^{\mathsf{rel}}(q)\geq q\geq\overline{D}_{s}.

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\mathbb{E}_{X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}}\Big[\mathtt{I}\big(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k+1}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\big)\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\mathtt{I}\big(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\big)+\mathbb{E}_{X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}}\Big[\mathtt{I}\big(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k+1}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\big)\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\Big]

\displaystyle\quad\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathtt{I}\big(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\big)+\mathtt{I}\big(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k+1}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\big)\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\Big],

where (i) holds because conditioning on
(\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})
is equivalent to conditioning on
(\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})
; (ii) uses the tower property. Applying the above chain rule iteratively for
k=k_{0},k_{0}+1,\dots,L-1
and tower property, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{L}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\,\big|\,\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{i})}{=}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{[L]\setminus\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{ii})}{\leq}\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}})\wedge\eta

\displaystyle\qquad\overset{(\mathrm{iii})}{\leq}\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1}})\wedge\eta.

(52)

where (i) holds since
\bigcup_{k=k_{0}}^{L}\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}=[L]\setminus\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}
; (ii) is true because mutual information is always upper bounded by entropy, the entropy of
X^{i}
is independent of
\Pi
given
\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}
and
X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}}
, and the definition of
k_{0}
ensures that
\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}-1}})\leq\eta
; (iii) holds because
\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}})
is decreasing in
k
and
s\leq k_{0}
.

Finally, since
\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s}\subseteq\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k_{0}}
, we can take the expectation with respect to
\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1}
and
X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1}}
on both sides of (
52
) and use the tower property to conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{L}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\,\big|\,\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}\Bigg]\leq\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{s-1}})\wedge\eta,

(53)

as claimed in (
5
). This completes the proof of Lemma
5
.

A.6
Proof of Lemma
6

Fix an arbitrary
z
with
P_{Z}(z)>0
. By the definition of pointwise conditional mutual information in (
3
), we can express

\displaystyle I(X;Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z=z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{X,Y_{1},Y_{2}}\biggl[\log\frac{p_{X,Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z}(X,Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid z)}{p_{X\mid Z}(X\mid z)\,p_{Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z}(Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid z)}\,\Big|\,Z=z\biggr].

Using the chain rule for conditional densities, we decompose the logarithm in the above display as

\displaystyle\log\frac{p_{X,Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z}(X,Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid z)}{p_{X\mid Z}(X\mid z)\,p_{Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z}(Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid z)}=\log\frac{p_{X,Y_{1}\mid Z}(X,Y_{1}\mid z)}{p_{X\mid Z}(X\mid z)\,p_{Y_{1}\mid Z}(Y_{1}\mid z)}+\log\frac{p_{Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},z)}{p_{Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},z)}.

Taking the expectation then gives

\displaystyle I(X;Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z=z)

\displaystyle=I(X;Y_{1}\mid Z=z)+\mathbb{E}_{X,Y_{1},Y_{2}}\biggl[\log\frac{p_{Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},z)}{p_{Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},z)}\,\Big|\,Z=z\biggr].

For the second term, applying the chain rule again yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,Y_{1},Y_{2}}\biggl[\log\frac{p_{Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid X,Y_{1},z)}{p_{Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},z)}\,\Big|\,Z=z\biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}}\Biggl[\mathbb{E}_{X,Y_{2}}\biggl[\log\frac{p_{X,Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z}(X,Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},z)}{p_{X\mid Y_{1},Z}(X\mid Y_{1},z)\,p_{Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z}(Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},z)}\,\Big|\,Y_{1},Z=z\biggr]\,\Big|\,Z=z\Biggr]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}}\bigl[I(X;Y_{2}\mid Z=z,Y_{1})\mid Z=z\bigr].

Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle I(X;Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z=z)=I(X;Y_{1}\mid Z=z)+\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}}\bigl[I(X;Y_{2}\mid Y_{1},Z=z)\bigr].

Using the definition of pointwise mutual information in (
3
), we conclude

\displaystyle\mathtt{I}(X;Y_{1},Y_{2}\mid Z)=\mathtt{I}(X;Y_{1}\mid Z)+\mathbb{E}_{Y_{1}}\bigl[\mathtt{I}(X;Y_{2}\mid Z,Y_{1})\mid Z\bigr],

as claimed in (
50
).

Taking the expectation with respect to
Z
on both sides of (
50
) then gives (
51
) immediately.

This completes the proof of Lemma
6
.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem

B.1
Proof sketch of Theorem
2

The high-level proof strategy for Theorem
2
is similar to that for Theorem
1
. Hence, we will mainly focus on the key differences in the analysis, and refer readers to Section
4.2
for the common technical details.

Step 1: Decompose KL error into sum of mutual information.

To begin with, since the sampling process of the maximum entropy-based strategy in Algorithm
2
also admits a Markovian structure (see (
11
)), the KL sampling error takes the same form as (
20
) in Section
4.2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\Pi,X\sim p_{X_{0}}}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathsf{KL}\Big(p^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{t}}p^{i}(\cdot\mid X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\Big)\Bigg].

It thus suffices to control the per-iteration KL error term in (
19
) for the maximum entropy-based strategy.

Recall that for the entropy sum-based strategy (Algorithm
1
), we handle this term via the KL divergence bound in (
22
) from Lemma
1
.
Here, we instead apply the KL divergence identity (
21
) from the same lemma.
Specifically, combining (
21
) with the representation of the unmasking set
\mathcal{D}_{t}
in (
15
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{t}=\mathcal{W}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{W}_{t-1}=\{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t-1}|+1},\ldots,\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t}|}\},

we can express the KL error from iteration
t
in (
19
) as

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}\Big(p^{d_{t}}(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})\,\big\|\,\prod_{i\in d_{t}}p^{i}(\cdot\mid x^{w_{t-1}})\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad=\sum_{k=1}^{|d_{t}|-1}I\big(X^{\pi_{|w_{t-1}|+k}};X^{\pi_{|w_{t-1}|+k+1}},\ldots,X^{\pi_{|w_{t}|}}\,\big|\,(\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})=(w_{t-1},x^{w_{t-1}}),\Pi=\pi\big).

(54)

That is, the contribution of iteration
t
is given by a sum of mutual information terms, where the
k
-th term measures the mutual information between the
k
-th unmasked token and the tokens that are unmasked later within the same iteration.
In particular, the last unmasked token in the iteration
X^{\pi_{|w_{t}|}}
does not contribute to the KL error.

Substituting (
54
) back into the expression of the KL sampling error (
20
) yields

\displaystyle\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{D}_{t}|-1}\mathtt{I}(X^{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t-1}|+k}};X^{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t-1}|+k+1}},\dots,X^{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t}|}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}},\Pi)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathtt{I}(X^{\Pi_{k}};X^{\Pi_{k+1}},\dots,X^{\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t_{\Pi_{k}}}|}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{\Pi_{k}}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{\Pi_{k}}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{\Pi_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{\Pi_{k}}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{\Pi_{k}}-1}})<\eta\big\}\Bigg],

(55)

where the second line reindexes by the absolute unmasking order
k\in[L]
instead of the relative order within each iteration. Here, we recall that
t_{i}
denotes the iteration at which token
i
is unmasked and thus
t_{\Pi_{k}}
stands for the iteration at which the
k
-th unmasked token
\Pi_{k}
is revealed. The indicator function arises because only non iteration-ending tokens contribute to the KL error and they satisfy the low-entropy condition by the maximum entropy-based stopping rule.

We next regroup the sum by token index
i\in[L]
instead of unmasking order
k\in[L]
. To this end, let
\mathcal{L}^{i}
be the index set of the tokens that are unmasked in the same iteration as token
i
but are revealed after it, namely,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{i}\coloneqq\big\{\Pi_{\Pi^{-1}_{i}+1},\Pi_{\Pi^{-1}_{i}+2},\dots,\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}}|}\big\}.

(56)

Note that
\mathcal{L}^{i}
is a deterministic function of the permutation
\Pi
and the previous iterate
(\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})
.
Rewriting the sum in (
55
) accordingly yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{L}^{i}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)<\eta\big\}\Big].

(57)

In a nutshell, this demonstrates that the expected KL sampling error can be decomposed into a sum of mutual information terms over token index
i\in[L]
, where each term measures the correlation between token
i
and the tokens that are unmasked later within the same iteration, conditioned on the tokens revealed in prior iterations and the permutation.

We note that in contrast to the analogous expression (
25
) for the entropy sum-based scheme, the mutual information term here involves only the tokens unmasked after token
i
within the iteration, i.e.,
X^{\mathcal{L}^{i}}
, rather than all other tokens in the same batch, i.e.,
X^{\mathcal{D}_{t_{1}}\smallsetminus\{i\}}
.

Step 2: Bound KL error by entropy-based stopping rule.

Equipped with the KL error decomposition in (
57
), it suffices to fix a token index
i\in[L]
and focus on the
i
-th term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi,X}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{L}^{i}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)<\eta\big\}\Big].

(58)

This is established in the following lemma; with proof deferred to Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 7
.

For any token index
i\in[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{X,\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},\Pi^{-1}_{i}}\Big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{L}^{i}}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t_{i}-1}})<\eta\big\}\Big]\leq\frac{S_{\max}}{L}\eta.

(59)

With Lemma
7
in hand, plugging (
59
) into (
58
) and summing over all token indices
i\in[L]
, we find that the expected KL sampling error in (
57
) can be bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]\leq S_{\max}\eta.

Setting the parameters as

\displaystyle S_{\max}=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon L}{H(X_{0})}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\eta=\frac{H(X_{0})S_{\max}}{L}=\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon H(X_{0})}{L}}

yields the desired KL error bound
\mathbb{E}_{\Pi}\big[\mathsf{KL}(p_{X_{0}}\parallel p_{Y_{T}\mid\Pi})\big]\leq\varepsilon
.

Step 3: Control expected number of iterations.

As for the expected total number of iterations
\mathbb{E}[T]
, it is controlled by the following lemma; its proof is deferred to Appendix
B.3
.

Lemma 8
.

The expected number of iterations
\mathbb{E}[T]
is upper bounded as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq\frac{2L}{S_{\max}}+1.

(60)

In other words, the expected number of iterations is of the same order as if each iteration unmasks exactly
S_{\max}
tokens.

The claim in (
12
) then follows by plugging in the choice of
S_{\max}
into the above bound.
We now complete the proof of Theorem
2
.

Proof of Lemma

Our strategy is first to take the expectation over

\Pi

on both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[G_{t_{i}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{L}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}})\leq\eta\big\}\Bigg].

(65)

Now applying Lemma
5
with
s=1
, the summation on the right-hand side of (
65
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}},\Pi_{\smallsetminus i})\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}})\leq\eta\big\}\Bigg]\leq\mathtt{H}(X^{i})\wedge\eta\leq\eta.

Plugging this into (
65
) and combining it with (
63
), we can bound the expectation of the right-hand side of (
B.2
) by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\mathtt{I}(X^{i};X^{\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t_{i}}}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t_{i}-1}},\Pi)\mathds{1}\big\{\mathtt{H}(X^{i}\mid\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t_{i}-1},X^{\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t_{i}-1}})\leq\eta\big\}\big]\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{L}\eta.

Finally, putting this together with (
B.2
), we finish the proof of Lemma
7
.

B.3
Proof of Lemma
8

By the termination condition of the maximum entropy-based sampling procedure, we can split iterations into three types:

•

cap iterations: end because
D_{t}=|\mathcal{D}_{t}|=s_{\max}
;

•

high-entropy iterations: end because the stopping token has entropy exceeding
\eta
;

•

the final iteration, which contributes at most 1 to the total number of iterations
T
.

Let
T_{\mathsf{cap}}
and
T_{\mathsf{high}}
denote the number of the first two types, respectively. We have
T\leq T_{\mathsf{cap}}+T_{\mathsf{high}}+1
.

For
T_{\mathsf{cap}}
, since each cap iteration unmasks
s_{\max}
tokens, we have

\displaystyle T_{\mathsf{cap}}\leq L/s_{\max}.

(66)

Therefore, the remainder of the proof focuses on controlling
\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathsf{high}}]
.

Fix an arbitrary
t\in[T]
. Suppose that the
t
-th iteration is a high-entropy iteration. Let
i_{t}\in\mathcal{D}_{t}
be the index of the last unmasked token at iteration
t
, i.e.,
i_{t}=\Pi_{|\mathcal{W}_{t}|}
. By the stopping rule, we know that

\displaystyle\eta<\mathtt{H}(X^{i_{t}}\mid\Pi,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\leq\mathtt{H}(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid\Pi,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}),

where the last inequality follows from the monotonicity of entropy. This implies that

\displaystyle\mathds{1}\{\text{iteration }t\text{ is high-entropy}\}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathtt{H}(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid\Pi,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}}).

Summing over
t\in[T]
and taking expectation, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathsf{high}}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathtt{H}(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid\Pi,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\Bigg].

(67)

As shown in (
34
) from Lemma
3
, we know that the sum on the right-hand side of the above display satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathtt{H}(X^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\mid\Pi,\mathcal{W}_{t-1},X^{\mathcal{W}_{t-1}})\Bigg]=H(X_{0}),

Consequently, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathsf{high}}]\leq\frac{H(X_{0})}{\eta}=\frac{L}{s_{\max}},

(68)

where the last step is due to our choice
\eta=H(X_{0})s_{\max}/L
.

Combining (
66
) and (
68
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[T]\leq\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathsf{cap}}]+\mathbb{E}[T_{\mathsf{high}}]+1\leq\frac{2L}{s_{\max}}+1.

B.4
Proof of Lemma
9

Fix an arbitrary token index
i\in[L]
and condition on an arbitrary realization of
\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}
and
X^{\smallsetminus i}
.
Recall the auxiliary sampling process defined by removing token
i
.
Conditioned on
\Pi_{\smallsetminus i}
and
X^{\smallsetminus i}
, the auxiliary unmasking sets
(\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t},\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{t})_{t\in[L]}
are deterministic.

Because
\Pi
is uniformly random over all permutations of
[L]
, after conditioning on the relative order of all indices except
i
, the insertion position
\Pi^{-1}_{i}
of token
i
is uniformly random over
[L]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big\{\Pi^{-1}_{i}=r\mid\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},X^{\smallsetminus i}\big\}=\frac{1}{L},\quad r=1,\dots,L.

Now fix an arbitrary
k\in[L]
and consider the event
\{t_{i}=k\}
, i.e., token
i
is unmasked at iteration
k
in the original sampling process. By Observation
A3
, the original and auxiliary processes coincide before iteration
k
. In particular, both processes have unmasked the same set of tokens
\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}
. Therefore, token
i
must appear after the first
W^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}=|\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}|
tokens in the full permutation
\Pi
, implying that the insertion position of token
i
satisfies
\Pi^{-1}_{i}>W^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}
.

Meanwhile, during iteration
k
, before token
i
is encountered, the original process sees exactly the same previously revealed tokens and the same sequence of candidate indices as the auxiliary process. Hence, before reaching token
i
, it cannot proceed past the stopping point of iteration
k
in the auxiliary process. In particular, if
i
were inserted after the block
\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}
, then the original process would stop iteration
k
before unmasking token
i
. Thus, token
i
must be inserted within the block of permutation positions corresponding to
\mathcal{D}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}
, which means that the insertion position of token
i
satisfies
\Pi^{-1}_{i}\leq W^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}=|\mathcal{W}^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}|
.

Combining the above two observations, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl\{t_{i}=k\mid\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},X^{\smallsetminus i}\bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigl\{W^{\smallsetminus i}_{k-1}<\Pi^{-1}_{i}\leq W^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}\mid\Pi_{\smallsetminus i},X^{\smallsetminus i}\bigr\}=\frac{D^{\smallsetminus i}_{k}}{L}\leq\frac{s_{\max}}{L},

where the last step holds because by construction of the maximum entropy-based scheme, each iteration unmasks at most
s_{\max}
tokens.

As the above bound holds for any
k\in[L]
, we finish the proof of Lemma
9
.
