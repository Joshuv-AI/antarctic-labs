A review of scenario analysis tools for urban green space planning and management
Authors: Xinyi Wang, Ruixia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryScenario analysis can help urban green space planning respond to uncertain environmental, social, and policy futures. This systematic review analyzed 157 publications to clarify its applications in green space planning and management. We distinguished two research traditions—studies identifying urban green spaces from land use and land cover data for regional ecological planning (UGS1), and studies examining green infrastructure within built urban environments (UGS2). Using a three-stage framework of scenario analysis, we found clear differences in the geographic distribution, focal issues, scenario construction approaches, and evaluation pathways. UGS1 mainly addressed ecological security and climate-related issues, whereas UGS2 emphasized climate regulation, stormwater management, and urban environmental quality. Overall, systematic evaluation frameworks and stakeholder engagement remained limited. This review identifies five opportunities to make scenario-based green space planning more actionable and decision relevant.

Introduction

Urban green spaces (UGSs) provide diverse ecological, social, and economic benefits1,2 and have long been recognized as a key nature-based solution to the challenges of climate change and rapid urbanization. As a crucial component of the urban complex system,3,4 UGSs exhibit a high degree of complexity that stems from its interwoven and multifunctional attributes,5 distinguishing it from other urban land-use types with singular purposes. Moreover, the evolution of UGSs is governed by multiple driving mechanisms,6 encompassing environmental factors such as climate change7,8 and anthropogenic forces including food security, land-use transition, and socio-economic development,9 all of which collectively amplify the uncertainty surrounding the future evolution of UGSs.

Predicting and simulating future UGS spatial patterns are key approaches to achieve optimal allocation and evidence-based decision-making.10 In recent years, scenario analysis has been widely employed as a systematic approach to address future complexities and uncertainties in UGS simulation and planning studies.11,12 Scenario analysis defines key uncertainties in constructing multiple plausible future development pathways and evolutionary scenarios.13 This method originated from military strategic planning during the late 1960s and early 1970s and was subsequently refined within corporate strategic management practices. Since then, it has been extensively applied across diverse research domains, including climate change,14 ecological and environmental studies,15 land-use research,16 and urban planning.17 Scenario analysis application enables scholars to better understand the dynamic evolution of UGSs through multi-scenario comparisons, thereby offering more systematic and forward-looking decision support for UGS planning and management.18,19

Existing reviews on UGS have primarily focused on the following aspects: several studies have examined the challenges of UGS planning and management from an integrated perspective, highlighting a paradigm shift from an early ecological orientation toward a more socio-integrative research framework.20,21 Another strand of research has focused on the functional benefits of UGSs, assessing their roles in climate regulation,22 water management,23 and biodiversity conservation24 through an ecosystem services lens, while also examining their impacts on human physical, mental, and social well-being from a public health perspective.25,26 In addition, a subset of studies has concentrated on methodological advances, particularly the application of big data analytics, remote sensing, and observation data spatial modeling techniques in UGS studies.27,28,29 A quantitative review in the field of urban planning examined studies employing scenario analysis, confirming its methodological value in planning research17; however, the review was conducted relatively early and did not specifically address the green space domain. Overall, owing to the broad and heterogeneous definitions of UGS and the absence of standardized scenario analysis frameworks,30 systematic reviews focusing on scenario analysis application in the context of UGS planning remain scarce.

Accordingly, this study aims to systematically evaluate the current applications and emerging trends of scenario analysis in the field of UGS planning. First, we define the concept of UGS and summarize the general procedures of scenario analysis as the theoretical foundation for this study. Subsequently, we conduct a systematic quantitative literature review to analyze the application characteristics of scenario analysis in UGS research. Specifically, this study (1) reviews the fundamental attributes of the literature sample, including spatial distribution and publication timelines; (2) identifies seven thematic categories based on UGS research typologies and reveals their organizational structures; (3) extracts the key components of scenario construction, including scenario types, number of scenarios, temporal range, and uncertainty factors; and (4) summarizes the evaluation approaches of scenarios and their application in planning practice. Finally, on the basis of the review findings, we discuss the regional and typological variations in UGS research, assess the application of scenario analysis tools, and propose potential opportunities for future UGS planning and management.

Theoretical framework

Definition of UGS research types

The concept of UGS varies across countries, regions, and disciplines (Table S1). In a broad sense, UGS is defined as “all vegetation-covered land within urban areas.”31 Closely related concepts include green infrastructure (GI), green network, and ecological space. These terms reflect how different countries, at various stages of urbanization, conceptualize and operationalize green spaces through corresponding policy instruments.5,30 UGS encompasses various forms, including land use/land cover (LULC)-based natural areas, urban forests, large urban parks, street greenery, gardens, and rooftop greening.32,33 As the definition and classification of UGS often depend on specific research objectives, clearly defining the concept of UGS at the outset is crucial to ensure the scientific validity of the study.5 For example, Hwang et al.34 defined UGS as all vegetated land or land suitable for tree planting in cities when examining urban carbon sequestration and ecological connectivity.34 Li et al.35 defined UGS as urban green patches, including parks, roadside green spaces, and green spaces associated with built land, when investigating the trade-offs between ecological conservation and recreational services.35 More broadly, Taylor and Hochuli30 summarized two chief interpretations of green space—natural environments or ecosystems and vegetated spaces within the built urban environment.30

Accordingly, this study categorizes existing UGS studies into two types (Table 1). The first category (hereinafter referred to as UGS1) primarily identifies UGSs based on LULC data, predominantly focusing on the regional scale and research objectives oriented toward spatial planning and land-use management decision-making. For instance, Chen et al.36 used the Shanghai Metropolitan Area as a case study to assess ecosystem services (ES) based on six LULC categories (agricultural land, forest, grassland, construction land, water bodies, and wetlands) to identify UGSs and develop an ecological spatial network planning framework.36 Similarly, Liu et al.37 analyzed the Île-de-France Region using the 2017 French land use database (MOS), identifying UGSs as forests, grasslands, water banks, public parks and gardens, and camping/caravanning areas. Literature coding framework

The second category (hereinafter referred to as UGS2) focuses on UGSs within the urban built environment and conceptualizes them as integral components of the urban GI system, such as urban parks, roadside green spaces, green roofs, and street trees. This category emphasizes human-scale urban spaces that can be directly perceived and utilized, focusing on interactions between human activities and urban green environments. For instance, Chen et al. (2025) examined urban parks as study objects, enhancing their service efficiency and equity by optimizing the spatial matching between supply and demand.38 Bixler et al.39 defined and identified each type of GI based on field-built and monitored sample facilities and conducted a dynamic life cycle assessment (LCA) to achieve net environmental benefits and guide future urban GI planning and decision-making.39

Steps of scenario analysis

A clear and orderly procedure is essential to ensure the systematicity and comparability of scenario construction and analysis.40 The implementation steps of scenario analysis vary across research fields and application objectives (Table S2). Schwartz (1991) was among the earliest scholars to systematically articulate the methodological steps of scenario analysis, summarizing it in eight steps that were primarily applied in corporate strategic planning.41 In contrast, urban planning operates within a more complex socio-ecological system. On the basis of the practical needs of urban planning, Chakraborty and McMillan17 summarized the scenario analysis method in nine key steps.17 Notably, in many existing UGS studies applying scenario analysis, scenario construction and evaluation were often conducted directly according to research objectives and data availability, while some intermediate steps were not explicitly presented. If an overly complex or highly detailed procedural framework is adopted, it may be difficult to align some studies with specific steps, potentially affecting the completeness of the review analysis. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MA) proposed a scenario development framework for ecosystem assessments that condenses the original 14-step process into 3 broader stages.42 Drawing on this framework and a synthesis of the relevant literature, this study conceptualizes scenario analysis into three primary stages—focal issue identification, scenario construction, and scenario evaluation.17,43 This classification facilitates a systematic comparison of the implementation and application of scenario analyses across different studies.

The first and most critical step in scenario analysis is identifying the focal issue, which determines the scope and direction of the entire scenario process.44 For instance, the MA articulated its focal issues as follows: how would plausible changes in development paths over the next 50 years affect ecosystems and their services, and how would these changes, in turn, influence human well-being?42 Without a clearly defined focal issue, scenario analysis risks remain at an abstract level of “future imagination,” thereby limiting its capacity to generate actionable insights for decision-making.45

Second, the core objective of the scenario construction stage is to identify key uncertainties that serve as the basis for generating clearly differentiated scenario alternatives.46 Some studies have referred to these uncertainties—factors that may exert significant influence on future developments—as “driving forces,” encompassing the social, technological, economic, political, and environmental dimensions.41 The number of key uncertainties used in scenario construction is recommended to be limited to two or three, few but precise.47 Scenario types are generally categorized into three chief classes—predictive, explorative, and normative—which are more frequently applied in planning-related studies.48 Explorative scenarios are designed to investigate multiple plausible future trajectories, whereas normative scenarios depict desirable or goal-oriented futures. Notably, the “trend continuation scenario” is typically regarded as a form of predictive scenario, serving as a baseline for comparison with alternative scenarios. Although there is no universally accepted standard for the number of scenarios, using two to four scenarios provides an optimal balance between comprehensiveness and interpretability.49

After the scenario schemes are constructed, they should be systematically tested, evaluated, and analyzed to verify the rationality of the scenario settings and their policy applicability.50 O’Brien and Meadows43 proposed comparing and optimizing different strategic options by using indicators such as vision consistency, robustness, and stakeholder response, thereby providing multidimensional scenario references to decision-makers.43

Methods

This study adhered strictly to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.51 The PRISMA guideline is a widely recognized and standardized protocol that provides a structured approach for the comprehensive exploration of specific topics, minimizes potential bias, and enhances the transparency and reproducibility of findings.52 According to the PRISMA procedure, the literature selection process comprises three main stages—identification, screening, and inclusion.53

Identification

In the identification stage, this study explicitly defined the search strategy, inclusion criteria, and database selection criteria. The Web of Science (WoS) and Scopus were chosen as the primary search databases due to their extensive coverage, multidisciplinary scope, inclusion of peer-reviewed publications, and publisher neutrality, which make them stand among the most authoritative and comprehensive academic databases worldwide.54 The search strategy was designed based on the research theme and included three groups of keywords combined using Boolean operators to maximize the retrieval of relevant studies.(1)Green space-related keywords: “green space” OR “greenspace” OR “ecological space” OR “green infrastructure”(2)Scenario analysis-related keywords: “scenario∗” OR “multiple scenarios”(3)Spatial planning-related keywords: “plan∗” OR “spatial plan∗” OR “urban plan∗” OR “management”

Screening and eligibility

The literature screening process adhered to the PRISMA framework (Figure 1) and comprised three main steps as follows: (1) preliminary screening: records were excluded based on database source, document type (restricted to WoS Core Collection), and language (English only); (2) title and abstract screening: studies irrelevant to the research topic were removed, such as those not addressing UGSs, scenario analysis, or spatial planning; and (3) full-text review: studies that did not involve rigorous scenario analysis or were unrelated to future-oriented uncertainty themes were further excluded. Data coding and analysis

To support both quantitative and qualitative analyses, this study developed a systematic literature coding framework.55 The coding framework consists of two main components (Table 1). The first part includes the basic information of each publication (A), such as publication year (A1), country (A2), and type of green space (A3). The second part is based on the three main steps of scenario analysis summarized in this study, including focal issue identification (B), scenario construction (C), and scenario evaluation and application (D). All reviewed studies were classified and organized according to this coding framework, with detailed explanations of the coding scheme provided in Table S4. The coding results were cross-validated by two researchers.

Results

Basic information review

This study conducted a statistical analysis of 157 selected publications to reveal the overall characteristics of scenario analysis in UGS studies (Figure 2). Regarding spatial distribution, the research cases were primarily concentrated in China, the United States, and Europe. Among them, China demonstrates a significantly higher number of studies, primarily focusing on UGS1, whereas studies in Europe and North America tend to emphasize UGS2. A few cases have also been found in Australia and South America, indicating the global relevance and cross-regional applicability of this research topic. Regarding publication years, the number of studies was relatively limited before 2020 but has been increasing markedly since 2021, with both UGS1 and UGS2 exhibiting a continuous upward trend. This indicates that scenario analysis application in UGS studies is expanding rapidly and has become a growing focus of academic attention. Regarding journal distribution, most publications were concentrated in leading international journals in the fields of ecology and urban planning, with Ecological Indicators, Urban Forestry & Urban Greening, and Land publishing the largest number of papers. Application status of scenario analysis in UGS studies

Focal issues in scenario analysis

Through a manual review of 157 publications, supplemented with high-frequency keyword extraction using the Bibliometrix package in R (Figure S1), seven categories of focal issues were identified (Figure 3A). These focal issues can be further categorized into two primary groups: single-objective oriented (n = 131) and multi-objective oriented (n = 26). Among the single-objective studies, climate-related topics constituted the largest proportion. This domain was among the earliest to adopt scenario analysis approaches.56 Most studies have directly adopted standardized climate scenario frameworks (e.g., Representative Concentration Pathways [RCPs] and Shared Socioeconomic Pathways [SSPs]), combined with climate and urban-scale simulation models (e.g., Weather Research and Forecasting model [WRF], InVEST, Future Land Use Simulation model [FLUS], and CA-Markov), to predict variations in the urban thermal environment under different scenarios.57,58 The second major topic concerns the ecological security patterns. Many studies have embedded scenario analysis within land-use simulations (e.g., FLUS and Patch-generating Land Use Simulation model [PLUS]) or ecological network modeling frameworks (e.g., Minimum Cumulative Resistance model [MCR], circuit theory, or graph-based networks) to assess the impacts of varying policy constraints, protection intensities, and external disturbances on ecological network structures.59,60Figure 3Focal issue and organizational structure(A) Types of focal issues.(B) Organizational structure. Regarding UGS types, studies on UGS1 have primarily focused on ecological security patterns (n = 31), followed by climate and air quality (n = 17). UGS2 studies were more concentrated on climate, air quality (n = 25), and stormwater management (n = 22), with relatively limited attention to ecological security patterns (n = 3). Figure 3B illustrates the organizational structure involved in defining focal issues among the reviewed studies. The results revealed that 63.69% of the studies (n = 100) were independently conducted by research teams, and 30.57% (n = 48) were conducted in collaboration with policy institutions. Scenario construction status and settings

Regarding scenario types (Figure 4A), explorative scenarios and their combinations with other scenario types were dominant, indicating that researchers commonly employ uncertainty variables to explore the future spatial patterns of UGSs. Moreover, predictive scenarios and their combinations account for a substantial proportion, primarily serving the purposes of result comparison and trend validation. Regarding the number of scenarios (Figure 4B), most studies developed 3–10 scenarios for comparative analysis. The median number of scenarios in UGS2 studies was slightly higher than that in UGS1 studies, indicating that studies focusing on urban built-up green spaces adopted more refined and diversified scenario settings. Both types of UGS studies contained a few outliers with higher scenario counts, primarily originating from studies employing multi-objective optimization algorithms (e.g., genetic algorithms) to generate multiple Pareto-frontier optimal solutions, typically for trade-off analysis among multi-objective schemes. Regarding temporal settings (Figures 4C and 4D), approximately 56.1% of the studies defined explicit future target years (e.g., 2030 or 2050), with prediction time spans primarily concentrated between 20 and 40 years. The temporal span was notably longer for UGS2 (20–40 years) than for UGS1 (10–20 years). In addition, a statistical analysis was conducted on the uncertainty factors extracted from all reviewed studies (Figure 5). The results revealed that uncertainty factors directly influencing the spatial patterns of UGS represent key determinants shaping scenario differences, including UGS quantity scale, UGS type, spatial allocation, and land-use conversion. Among them, land-use conversion regulates the quantity of UGS by adjusting the transition coefficients, and this factor primarily appears in UGS1-type studies. Policy constraints constitute another significant category of uncertainty factors, with UGS1 studies being more likely than UGS2 to use policy variables as the core basis for scenario differentiation. For example, Liu et al.37 developed scenarios with two different scales of UGS expansion based on France’s Plan Vert framework and regional master planning policies.37 Similarly, Li et al.59 established different future development scenarios by incorporating various spatial control policies in China, such as ecological red lines and basic farmland protection, as key variables.59 In addition, external factors such as rainfall, climate, and management regimes are critical sources of uncertainty that contribute to scenario differentiation, particularly in UGS2-type studies. Statistical analysis of the technical methods used in scenario construction (Figure 6) revealed clear differences between the two types of UGS studies. In UGS1 studies, future land-use simulation models, particularly FLUS and PLUS (both of which are essentially developed from the Cellular Automata [CA] framework), are the most widely used approaches; some studies have also directly applied CA models. Morphological Spatial Pattern Analysis (MSPA) is another frequently used method, both for ecological source identification and landscape pattern analysis. For ecosystem service assessment, InVEST is the most commonly applied model, while Carnegie–Ames Stanford Approach model (CASA) and Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation (RUSLE) are used less frequently and often in combination with InVEST. In addition, MCR, Least-Cost Path (LCP), and Circuitscape are commonly employed to identify ecological corridors. In contrast, UGS2 studies have relied more heavily on process-based urban environmental models, with Storm Water Management Model (SWMM) being the most widely used for simulating urban stormwater and runoff regulation, and ENVI-met being frequently applied to assess the effects of green spaces on urban thermal environments and microclimates. Regarding evaluation methods, only 8.3% of the studies have established evaluation indicator systems or employed systematic approaches (e.g., decision matrices) for the systematic comparison of scenario performance (Figure 7B).36,62 For instance, one study constructed a socio-ecological integrated analysis (SIA) framework comprising six dimensions and ten indicators to facilitate systematic scenario evaluations.63 This study categorized the evaluation indicators extracted from existing literature into three categories: structural evaluation, functional evaluation, and value evaluation. Structural evaluation primarily measures the spatial pattern characteristics, such as landscape metrics, of UGSs. Functional evaluation encompasses indicators such as landscape connectivity, heat island mitigation benefits, and carbon sequestration capacity. Value evaluation translates the ecosystem services and social benefits of UGSs into quantifiable economic values, using economic or monetization models. The results (Figure 7C) indicate that most studies have applied single-dimensional indicators for evaluation, whereas only five studies have simultaneously incorporated the structural, functional, and value dimensions.

In addition, statistical analysis revealed three primary approaches through which the information generated during the scenario evaluation process was applied to UGS planning decisions: spatial information (SI), quantitative indicators (QIs), and their combination (SI + QI). The results (Figure 7D) indicate that the combined SI + QI approach is predominant across the reviewed studies. Comparatively, UGS1 studies tend to rely on SI to support the optimization of macro-level UGS patterns, whereas UGS2 studies place greater emphasis on QI assessments to facilitate refined management and functional enhancement.

Discussion

Dissimilarities between the two types of UGS studies

The differences between the two types of UGS studies are reflected not only in research objects and methodologies but also in the divergent cognition of UGS among cities at different stages of urban development. According to the results, UGS studies exhibit significant disparities in knowledge output proportions and geographical distribution. Developing countries represented by China primarily focus on UGS1 studies, whereas developed countries in Europe and North America place greater emphasis on UGS2 studies. These differences were closely associated with the respective stages of urbanization. Most cities in developing countries are undergoing rapid urban expansion, and UGS planning focuses on mitigating the loss and fragmentation of green space, improving landscape connectivity, and enhancing ecological functions.64 Notably, China issued the Urban Green Space Classification Standard (CJJ/T85-2017)65 in 2017 and the Urban Green Space Planning Standard (GB/T 51346-2019) in 2019, broadening the spatial scope of UGS planning and management from the traditional “urban planning area” to the wider “municipal (county) domain.” This policy shift promoted the expansion of UGS studies from the intra-urban scale to a broader regional scale, leading to a substantial increase in studies represented by UGS1. In contrast, urbanization in developed countries has largely stabilized, and researchers in these studies adopted GI earlier as a strategic tool for advancing sustainable urban transformation,66 thereby forming an application-oriented research framework predominantly focused on UGS2.

These differences have led to a notable divergence between the two types of studies regarding the selection of focal issues. UGS1 studies have primarily focused on ecological and environmental topics, particularly regional ecological security and climate-related issues. For instance, Sun et al.67 provided scientific support for regional green space planning by evaluating the impacts of different green space protection scenarios on landscape connectivity.67 In contrast, UGS2 studies have placed greater emphasis on optimizing urban environmental quality and management strategies, focusing on climate regulation and stormwater management. This trend confirms that city managers in developed and highly urbanized regions are increasingly adopting nature-based solutions (e.g., green roofs, constructed wetlands, and urban green spaces) to mitigate stormwater risks and related environmental challenges.68,69 Moreover, the use of UGS in developed countries demonstrates pronounced social attributes70; consequently, related studies have placed greater emphasis on integrating social well-being with ecological benefits.

During scenario construction, UGS2 studies typically feature longer time spans and a greater number of scenarios than UGS1 studies. This is primarily because UGS2 studies focus on GI within urban built-up areas, where system performance is strongly influenced by long-term climate change and extreme rainfall events. Consequently, short-term scenarios are insufficient to accurately represent stormwater responses under future climatic variability.71 In addition, UGS2 studies often integrate model parameter sensitivity analysis,72 system combination comparisons,73 and multi-source climate input approaches.74 This necessitates the construction of multiple scenarios to test the performance stability and robustness of different GI configurations under diverse future environmental conditions. In contrast, UGS1 studies generally have shorter time spans (typically 10–20 years), and their design is constrained by policy orientation and established planning cycles.75 Accordingly, the temporal settings of UGS1 studies are often aligned with government planning cycles to ensure that planning outcomes remain operational and assessable during policy implementation.

Application review of scenario analysis

As a systematic approach for addressing future complexity and uncertainty, scenario analysis has been widely applied in UGS spatial planning. Most existing studies have focused on single-objective topics such as climate regulation and ecological security, whereas multi-objective research is emerging as a new frontier.76 The emergence of multi-objective agendas in UGS studies stems from the inherently multifunctional nature of UGS,77 which integrates ecological, social, and economic dimensions. The focus on a single objective may lead to functional conflicts and trade-offs78; thus, integrating multi-objective trade-off and optimization frameworks into spatial planning is essential.79 Moreover, the intrinsic complexity of urban systems and uncertainties associated with climatic and environmental conditions further highlight the need to address multi-objective issues in UGS planning. Increasing extreme climatic events, escalating land-use conflicts, and concurrent demands for ecological security and social equity collectively drive planning systems toward multi-objective and adaptive decision frameworks.78 Previous studies have shown that multi-objective frameworks yield more balanced and comprehensive outcomes than single-objective approaches.80

Scenario analysis explores multiple possible future development pathways by incorporating key uncertainties, whereas multi-objective optimization employs algorithmic approaches to identify Pareto-optimal solutions among competing objectives. For example, Hwang et al.34 constructed a spatial optimization model with carbon sequestration capacity, ecological connectivity, and cost as optimization objectives under land-use and budget constraints and applied a genetic algorithm to search for Pareto-optimal solutions.34 Each Pareto solution represents a specific green space configuration, reflecting the trade-offs among different objectives, and these solutions constitute a set of alternative planning scenarios for comparison. Similarly, Dong et al.76 first quantified multiple ecosystem service indicators by using the InVEST model and treated them as optimization objectives.76 The NSGA-II was then used to generate a Pareto-optimal solution set, from which representative scenarios—such as single-objective maximization and compromise solutions—were selected to compare the spatial outcomes of different GI configurations under multi-objective conditions. Overall, integrating scenario analysis with multi-objective optimization transforms UGS planning from a traditional “static optimum” paradigm into a “dynamic trade-off” process, enabling coordinated multi-objective decision-making under uncertainty.

In organizing focal issues, most studies have predominantly been led by academic research teams. Some studies incorporate local policy frameworks into scenario development; however, the thematic settings are often directly simplified into a few predefined scenarios (e.g., “disorderly expansion” and “ecological protection” scenarios), with such designs largely reflecting the subjective preferences of planners or researchers.17 Only a limited number of studies have introduced stakeholder participation mechanisms to strengthen the social representativeness and practical relevance of scenario formulation.81,82 Overall, the engagement of government agencies and stakeholders in the scenario construction process remains inadequate. This limited engagement partly leads to an absence of pluralistic cognitive foundations during scenario development.

The core of scenario construction is to identify and manage the key sources of uncertainty. Statistical results indicate that the uncertainties that shape scenario differences ultimately correspond to the control of UGS quantity, spatial distribution, and structural composition. Some studies directly define these three parameters to develop alternative scenario schemes; whereas others, depending on the focal issue, indirectly affect and constrain these parameters through external uncertainties such as policy restrictions, rainfall, climatic variation, or management models. Thus, the core of applying scenario analysis in UGS planning lies in translating future uncertainties into spatially actionable planning strategies with varying thematic objectives, ultimately achieving targeted functional optimization.83

All studies have provided spatial visualization or QI data during the scenario analysis. These findings not only facilitate the visual comparison of different scenarios but also strengthen the quantitative basis and practical applicability of planning decisions. This review further demonstrates that scenario analysis, as a systematic analytical framework, provides a scientifically rigorous and practically applicable approach for UGS optimization and planning management, offering a verifiable methodological foundation for addressing future uncertainties in urban environments.

UGS planning and management opportunities

This study adopts a widely used typology that classifies UGS planning and management opportunities into five dimensions: social, cognitive, financial, technological, and organizational/institutional.84 In the social dimension, the key opportunities for UGS planning and management involve integrating public participation into decision-making processes and enhancing social well-being. The literature review indicates that only a limited number of studies have introduced stakeholder participation mechanisms into scenario development, and most have emphasized the necessity of more substantial incorporation of public participation in future research.85,86 Several studies have emphasized that urban GI planning should address the needs of residents. Specifically, at the local scale, bottom-up participatory governance through community self-organization has been shown to enhance social cohesion and promote community health.87,88 Furthermore, UGS optimization should integrate socially oriented objectives, such as improving public health, social equity, and overall well-being.57 For example, planning processes should consider low-income groups to mitigate the risk of social inequities associated with green gentrification.89

Multiple studies have identified limited knowledge and awareness among governments and the public as a major challenge to current UGS planning. Several studies have suggested embedding UGS awareness enhancement into policy frameworks and public education systems to promote the transition of UGS planning and governance from “technical planning” to “cognition-driven governance.” For instance, Vahid Amini Parsa recommends integrating ecosystem service assessments into routine management decisions and strengthening the public and decision-makers’ understanding of GI values through education and information dissemination.90 Furthermore, applying spatial visualization tools is recognized as an effective approach to enhance cognitive understanding. For instance, high-resolution maps, radar charts, and other spatial visualization techniques91 enable complex information to be presented and compared intuitively and in a user-friendly manner, thereby enhancing planners’ and the public’s comprehension of planning outcomes.92 Moreover, several research findings have directly contributed to improvements in cognitive awareness. For instance, Dong et al. (2024) introduced the concept of a “safety boundary” to improve both planners’ and the public’s understanding of the cost-benefit dynamics of green space expansion, fostering a shift in planning approaches from “blind greening” toward “rational greening.”93

Numerous UGS2 studies have highlighted investment and maintenance costs as critical determinants of the long-term sustainability of GI. Fiscal mechanisms serve both as constraints on UGS expansion and essential enablers of its transformation. This necessitates renewed recognition of the economic value of UGS. Napoli et al.94 emphasized that UGS should be viewed not as a mere fiscal expense but as a long-term investment generating energy savings, health gains, and land value appreciation.94 Building on this, the exploration of innovative financing approaches, such as public-private partnerships (PPPs) and private investment incentives, is regarded as an effective strategy to alleviate financial constraints and foster the sustainable development of UGS.95

Several studies have highlighted the need for technological advancements to improve analytical precision and support long-term planning and decision-making. At the data level, researchers recommend using higher-resolution remote sensing datasets,96 incorporating real-time monitoring data to enhance predictive accuracy,97 and integrating long-term time-series datasets to support extended planning horizons.98 Some scholars have emphasized the need for more advanced and flexible modeling frameworks, such as open-source and user-friendly platforms that integrate urban tree inventory data to better support long-term UGS planning.99 Furthermore, numerous studies have emphasized the importance of enhancing spatial resolution—refining analyses to the block or community scale—to facilitate more detailed and context-specific planning and management of UGSs.100,101

Opportunities within organizational and institutional domains primarily lie in advancing cross-level, cross-sectoral, and cross-regional coordination mechanisms. Coordinating urban greening policies across administrative levels (districts and cities) can substantially enhance plan implementation efficiency and strengthen inter-policy coherence.102 Developing cross-sectoral coordination mechanisms facilitates the integration and optimization of UGS’s multifunctional roles. As UGS systems frequently transcend administrative boundaries, fostering cross-regional collaboration for the joint governance of ecological networks, particularly core ecological sources and corridors,103,104 is critical for maintaining the integrity and connectivity of ecological security patterns. Moreover, several studies advocate the integration of research findings into policy instruments for planning and management, such as embedding tree functional traits as management indicators within urban greening and forest management policies, to strengthen the scientific foundation of policy formulation.105

Building on the five opportunity dimensions identified above, this study outlines the key directions for advancing scenario analysis in UGS planning and management. These include strengthening cross-scale model integration to better link planning decisions across spatial scales; developing participatory scenario construction mechanisms involving the public and stakeholders to enhance the societal relevance of scenarios; advancing the integration of multi-source data and dynamic modeling approaches to better capture the complex interactions between ecological functions and human demands under future uncertainties; and facilitating the translation of scenario analysis outcomes into operational planning tools and spatial strategies. Specifically, scenario analysis outputs can be expressed as spatial configuration schemes under alternative development pathways, changes in key indicators, and trade-offs among multiple objectives, often communicated through maps or model-based simulations to support comparison among planning options and enhance decision support in UGS planning.

Limitations of the study

This study employs an systematic literature review approach, utilizing a rigorous and well-established analytical framework that offers a solid foundation for investigating the application of scenario analysis methods in UGS research. Nevertheless, certain limitations remain. First, due to resource and language constraints, this study considered only English language publications, which may have restricted the regional representativeness of the findings. Second, although diverse keyword combinations were employed in the search strategy, the broad and heterogeneous nature of the UGS concept may have resulted in the omission of some potentially relevant studies. Finally, although manual screening may have introduced minor subjective bias, the use of a relatively large sample size together with a dual-review verification process has effectively improved the robustness and reproducibility of the results.
