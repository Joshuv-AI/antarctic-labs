 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The goal of the proposed project was to improve the representation and prediction of aerosol particles that
“seed” clouds, thereby influencing cloud properties that impact warming and cooling of the atmosphere and
formation of precipitation. The study leveraged a broad range of land- and ocean-based observations, in
both the Northern and Southern Hemispheres, from DOE Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) and
other campaigns. Cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) serve as sites for formation of liquid water clouds and
control cloud droplet sizes and number concentrations. We used previously-developed DOE value-added
products to represent their concentration dependence on the range of supersaturations (percent relative
humidity exceeding 100%) observed in various cloud regimes. Existing models that are driven by proxy
variables, such as satellite-based optical properties, showed some skill in prediction of CCN. Observations
of particle size distributions, combined with simple assumptions for particle water uptake, also showed
some predictive skill. Complementing CCN, ice nucleating particle (INP) populations determine the initial
concentrations of ice crystals forming in clouds, the first step in forming precipitation over much of the
globe. While the database of observations of INPs is increasing, advances are needed in understanding INP
sources and how to model these to develop predictive capabilities. We leveraged our previous DOE-funded
Southern Ocean, Arctic, and North and South American INP data sets as well as existing collaborations
supported by the Atmospheric System Research (ASR) program and other agencies to address this question.
We found similarities in features of INP populations that are either presently misrepresented or lacking
representation in existing aerosol modeling and INP activation schemes. Arable soils were identified as a
key and ubiquitous INP type in terrestrial locations, and their concentrations could be related to aerosol
surface area. In contrast, biological INPs were highly variable in space and time in both terrestrial and
marine settings, and could not be predicted using existing standard aerosol measurements alone. We point
to the need for linking biological airborne particles to surface properties and emission mechanisms in order
to advance their prediction.

 PROJECT ACTIVITIES AND FINDINGS

1. Introduction

The goal of the proposed project was to improve the representation of aerosol-cloud interactions in global
climate models by leveraging a broad range of land- and ocean-based observations from DOE
Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) and other campaigns across the world. For cloud
condensation nuclei (CCN), we seek representation of the full CCN supersaturation spectrum for cloud
studies using previously-developed DOE value-added product methods. Most critically, for ice nucleating
particle (INP) representations, the motivation is based on observations of key similarities in features of
INP populations that are either presently misrepresented or lacking representation by the aerosol
modeling and INP parameterization schemes used in climate models. This work exploited our recent
DOE-funded Southern Ocean, Arctic, and North and South American data sets and leverages existing
collaborations funded by the Atmospheric System Research (ASR) program and other agencies. By
comparing observations from key land and ocean regions in both hemispheres, we sought to develop
improved model representations, including developing proxies for CCN and INP concentrations and
characteristics for regions that challenge parameterizations. In the text below, articles supported by this
project are shown in bold face.

An early activity under this grant was participation in preparation of a collaborative review that outlined
observational and modeling research needed to advance understanding of ice nucleating particles (INPs;
Burrows et al., 2022). The authors identified four advancements needed to improve representation of
INPs in models. (1) Linking INP activity to particle type, as identified by composition or other properties.
(2) Additional compilations of observed atmospheric concentrations of particle types that contribute to
INPs. (3) Testing whether INP efficiency can be well-parameterized by surface active site densities. (4)
Development of appropriate parameterizations for aerosol sources, concentrations, and atmospheric
evolution in models, that can then be used to drive estimates of INP concentrations. This project
contributed research toward addressing (1)-(3). Further, Burrows et al. (2022) noted the need for
accurate observations of aerosol size distributions across a full range of particle diameters, emphasizing
the current scarcity of accurate observations of supermicron particles. In addition to the utility of full size
distributions in estimating surface active site densities for INP parameterization, our work tested whether
concentrations of cloud condensation nuclei (CCN) could be adequately captured using aerosol size
distributions with simple assumptions for aerosol composition.

Below, we organize our other activities and findings around the two driving hypotheses for this project.

2. Hypothesis 1: Statement, Approach, and Findings

Hypothesis 1 postulated that CCN and INPs in marine regions are distinctly different in the Northern and
Southern Hemispheres, but can be harmonized by consideration of differences in transported continental
aerosols and differences in the sizes and compositions of regional marine CCN and INP emissions.
Hypothesis 1 was addressed using data from the Southern Hemisphere that included the DOE ARM-
sponsored campaigns MARCUS: Measurements of Aerosols, Radiation, and Clouds over the Southern
Ocean and MICRE: Macquarie Island Cloud and Radiation Experiment, both of which occurred over mid
to high latitude marine regions. For data from the Northern Hemisphere, we used data from DOE-ARM
field campaigns MOSAiC: Multidisciplinary Drifting Observatory for the Study of Arctic Climate, a
year-long series of observations in the High Arctic; and the COMBLE: Cold-Air Outbreaks in the Marine
Boundary Layer Experiment campaign, conducted on the island of Andøya in northern Norway, on the
Norwegian Sea. The Arctic datasets were complemented by collaborator data, especially those from long-
term measurements at Ny Alesund in Svalbard, also in the Norwegian Sea. The Southern Hemisphere

 3

datasets were supplemented by data from the CAPRICORN (ship-based; 2 phases) and SOCRATES
(aircraft-based) field studies. Together with MARCUS and MICRE, these Southern Ocean (SO) studies,
all conducted between 2016 and 2018, formed an unprecedented dataset of thermodynamic variables,
cloud and aerosol properties, and radiation observations over the pristine SO that is being used to advance
understanding of the processes controlling cloud phase and radiative budgets in this understudied region
(see overview of the combined datasets and emerging scientific discovery in McFarquhar et al., 2021).

Niu et al. (2024) explored seasonal and latitudinal variations in aerosol size distributions, CCN
concentrations, and INP characteristics in the SO. Seasonal variability was stronger than latitudinal, as
biology is a major driver of aerosol in this remote region along with sea spray. Nevertheless, a distinct
change in the aerosol was observed for samples obtain above and below 62˚S, which was also reflected in
the CCN concentrations (Figure 1). Poleward of this latitude, lower concentrations of INPs with higher
biological fractions were observed, while at the same time higher aerosol and CCN number
concentrations were recorded, presumably due to coastal Antarctic summertime biogenic emission and
subsequent gas to particle conversion processes. In the open ocean, where sea spray dominates the
aerosol, the aerosol hygroscopicity was consistent with organic composition (sea surface microlayer)
transitioning to higher hygroscopicity (sulfate or sea salt dominated) at larger sizes. Organics dominated
the IN activity although a minor but variable signal of dust contributions to INPs, from long range
transport, were also observed. Biological INPs (those active at temperatures above -20 C) were detected
episodically. To attempt to model emissions and simulate the concentrations of the two dominant
biogenic sources of marine INPs – biological and organic – Steinke et al. (2022) assumed their respective
sources to be heterotrophic bacteria and marine biopolymer aggregates. Their work and findings are
discussed further in Section 4 below.

Niu et al. (2024) noted that their analyses were hindered by the lack of aerosol size distributions
measurements across the full range of sizes of interest. This issue is a common one causing data gaps in
field campaigns, when either an instrument is not available or was not operational, motivating our
proposed task to explore proxy measurements and gap-filling methods. To this end, Moore et al. (2022)
used SO campaign data to develop a method to estimate dry aerosol surface area from bulk optical
methods, including lidar. This idea was first proposed in DeMott et al. (2016), with these new datasets
providing for the first time a means to directly confirm and refine the proposed relationships. Additional
marine aerosol studies reported in Bian et al. (in preparation) further demonstrated the utility of optical
measurements for estimating aerosol (dry) surface area. We have since applied the use of optical data to
infer aerosol properties for missing or incomplete observations in several of the studies reported here
(e.g., Testa et al., 2021; Williams et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024; DeMott et al., 2025).

We provided new analyses of the INP concentrations measured in the COMBLE study (Geerts et al.,
2022) to support our Northern Hemisphere marine CCN and INP investigations. COMBLE was a ground-
based study that had a particular focus on understanding the surface fluxes and cloud transitions during
cold-air outbreaks (CAOs),which occur frequently over high-latitude oceans. In a CAO, cold air flows
from ice-covered surfaces over warmer open water, with cloud streets formed at the start of the trajectory
transitioning to open-cellular convection. Figure 2 shows the location of the ARM infrastructure near
Andenes (ANX), along with the location of the longterm monitoring site at Zeppelin Station (ZEP), and
an example location of the ice-locked MOSAiC ship station (MOS). We leveraged the relative positions
of these sites to conduct “pseudo-Lagrangian” analyses over more than 1000 km (DeMott et al., 2025).
Specifically, we identified periods where CAO air mass trajectories approximated the dashed yellow line
in Figure 2, originating at MOS, traveling over ZEP, and then arriving at ANX, representing transition
from ice covered to open ocean and the corresponding sources of aerosols, including INPs.

In Figure 4, from Geerts et al. (2022), we compare the INP spectra observed at ANX during COMBLE
CAO events with the MARCUS Southern Ocean dataset. The mode between -17˚C and -23˚C for ANX
data are not evident in the MARCUS data, whereas both datasets have occasional contributions from
biological INPs active at temperatures warner than about -12˚C. Interestingly, the highest concentrations
of biological INPs were observed during polar night conditions at ANX (December to January). The
parameterization by McCluskey et al. (2018), shown as the yellow line, was developed for clean marine
airmasses arriving from the North Atlantic but has been found to generally describe SO INPs when
computed for appropriate sea spray aerosol surface area concentrations. We found, however, that it cannot
represent the COMBLE data even using the highest observed aerosol surface areas. This may be due to
enhanced INP emissions from INP-rich sea surface microlayers that have been reported to occur
episodically in the North Atlantic and Arctic.

Willims et al. (2024) reported size distributions of aerosol during COMBLE, including identification and
characterization of the sea spray mode and its dependence on wind speed. Mean concentrations of total
aerosol in the accumulation mode during non-CAO events were 98±80 cm-3, and during CAO events
109±75 cm-3. As seen in Figure 1, these concentrations are consistent with those seen across latitudes for
the SO, except for the springtime below 62˚S where biogenic emissions exert a strong influence.

To summarize our findings for Hypothesis 1:

 • CCN concentrations for open ocean regions in the absence of pollution plumes are similar in the
 NH and SH, except where biogenic emissions are active and elevate aerosol number
 concentrations.
 • A parameterization for marine INPs that has been shown to be applicable to some SH datasets
 underpredicts INP concentrations in the NH cases considered (Arctic springtime). We conclude
 that (organic) sea surface microlayers are episodic but important emissions sources of INPs in
 both the NH and SH.
 • Episodic contributions from biological INPs were observed in all marine field campaigns. Their
 concentrations are not predictable using simple correlations to other aerosol properties.

3. Hypothesis 2: Statement, Approach, and Findings

Hypothesis 2 postulated that CCN and INPs feeding convective cloud systems over central continental
regions in both hemispheres are dominated by similar biogenic sources that emanate from surrounding
arable regions; further, that these emissions are influenced by meteorology and season.

Hypothesis 2 was addressed using data from South America (the DOE-ARM sponsored CACTI: Cloud,
Aerosol, and Complex Terrain Interactions field campaign in Argentina; Varble et al., 2021), from two
shorter-term campaigns conducted at the ARM Southern Great Plains (SGP) site, and from the Puy de
Dôme ICe Nucleation Intercomparison Campaign (PICNIC) in central France. During the no-cost-
extension period of this project, we found that results from our work on the DOE-ARM sponsored SAIL
(Surface Atmosphere Integrated Field Laboratory) campaign in the Colorado Rockies were also consistent
with data from these terrestrial sites, and thus included some of those data in our synthesis.

We used observations of immersion-freezing INPs, including inferences on INP “type” through heating
and peroxide digestion of samples (cf. Hill et al., 2016): INPs that were inactivated after heating were
identified as likely biological in origin, whereas those removed after peroxide digestions were labeled as
non-heat-labile organic, and the remainder inorganic. The spectra for base and treated INPs observed
during CACTI are shown in Figure 5. Testa et al. (2021) demonstrated that the ratio of non-heat-labile

 7

organic and inorganic INPs was generally constant, especially at colder activation temperatures (-25 C),
and both were correlated with supermicron aerosol concentrations. These observations suggest that these
INP types likely originated from the regional arable topsoil, consistent also with air mass histories and
with Inp activity observed directly in a sample of Argentinian soil dust collected from the Pampas region.
Further, normalizing by aerosol surface area to obtain surface active site density (ns) spectra revealed that
these organic-containing soil particles have higher ice nucleating activity, as indicated by their ns spectra,
than mineral desert dusts.

samples. a) INP spectra of untreated samples superimposed over INP spectra following heat and H2O2
treatments. b) Spectra of computed biological, other organic and inorganic INP contributions derived from

Northern Hemisphere observations corroborate the importance of arable soils as sources of INPs. In
Figure 6 (from DeMott et al., in preparation), we show a compilation of ns spectra for non-heat-labile
organic INPs from all of the aforementioned studies. After normalization by supermicron surface area, we
find remarkable coherence among all sites, which span three continents and various seasons. Moderate
differences occur primarily at low T, with PICNIC and SAIL showing lower values than the other site for
activation temperatures below -25 C. Interestingly, both of these sites are at higher elevations (PICNIC,
1465m; SAIL, 2800m); differences may reflect different vertical profiles of supermicron aerosols,
although this hypothesis needs to be further explored. Prior organic-containing soil INP parameterizations
due to Tobo et al. (2014) and O’Sullivan et al. (2014) are in excellent agreement. In general, inorganic
(mineral) INPs were minor contributors to the overall INP activity. The findings support the
recommendations of Herbert et al. (2025), who found best agreement between their global model results
and observations when representing dust as a mixture of mineral and organic ice-nucleating components.
In a separate study at the SGP site, also supported by this grant, Cornwell et al. (2024) chemically
characterized individual INPs active at -32 C, again detecting organic-rich soil particles presumably of
agricultural origin. Finally, Knopf et al. (2026; in preparation) included soils from the SGP site in cloud
chamber ice activation experiments and subsequent microspectroscopic single-particle nanoscale analyses
of ice residuals. They identified fungi, cellulose, and polysaccharidic matter as the dominating sources of
soil particle ice nucleating activity. Taken together, the evidence points to the global importance of arable
(organic-containing) soil dusts in controlling immersion-freezing INP populations in terrestrial regions
and continental outflow, especially for activation temperatures below -20 C. However, arable soils are not

always represented as specific emissions sources nor carried as unique variables in regional and global
models. These findings therefore also suggest that further development of arable soil source maps and
emissions schemes is needed to improve predictions of aerosol-cloud interactions, especially for mixed-
phase clouds.

Figure 5b also shows biological (heat-labile) INPs (denatured by thermal treatment at 95°C) dominated
the total INPs active at -5 to -20 °C during the CACTI study. In contrast to the non-heat-labile organic
INPs, the contributions of biological INPs could not be parameterized with a single relationship for ns(T),
and concentrations of biological INPs active at a particular temperature did not correlate with organic
INPs. Concentrations of biological INPs active around -12 °C were found to peak during rain events and
under high relative humidity, but the emission mechanisms were independent of background aerosol
concentration. Rain impacts on these most efficient biological INPs, presumably lofted from plants and
soils, lasted over 24-hour periods. The importance of rain as a bioaerosol emission mechanism is well
known, and our observations confirmed that also biological INPs were elevated during and after
precipitation in the CACTI study.

All of the projects observed contributions from biological INPs (Figure 7). When present, biological INPs
were often the most important INP type at activation temperatures above -20 C, as also noted by Zhou et
al. (in review) for the SAIL study. However, to date, they remain the most challenging to parameterize.
Evidence points to contributions from multiple sub-populations that are likely site-specific (e.g., fungal
spores vs. pollens). Although correlations during CACTI suggested that biological INPs were mostly
supermicron in size, this was not always the case among the various studies. Although precipitation is
certainly an emission mechanism for biological INPs (Juergensen et al., in preparation), other
mechanisms are undoubtedly operative: ejection of spores triggered by changes in relative humidity,
emission via wildfires, attachment to windblown soil particles, and potentially others. Future effort is
needed to identify the specific components of biological INPs and thereby investigate their sources and
emission mechanisms.

As also noted for the marine field studies, the terrestrial field study datasets presented a challenge in
constructing complete aerosol size distributions, from which to derive key quantities needed for this work
such as surface area concentrations and optical properties. Observations over relevant size ranges were
sometimes not included, or did not cover the full time period; the ARM Value-Added Product (VAP)
merged size distribution was not yet available. To generate the required datasets, in Testa et al. (2021) we
demonstrated a method for merging data from 2 different sizing instruments when available that was then
applied in subsequent studies, and for using nephelometer scattering for PM1 and PM10 size cuts as a
proxy variable to estimate sub- and supermicron surface area concentrations.

Summer undergraduate researcher William Faletti used direct CCN observations and nephelometer
observations from the CACTI study to test the parameterization proposed by Shinozuka et al. (2015). He
found their equation underestimated the concentrations of CCN active at 0.4% supersaturation, but
obtained excellent agreement with a slight adjustment to one of the fit parameters. He also tested the use
of size distribution data to compute CCN(0.4%) assuming a contact hygroscopicity parameter, k, of 0.2.
These simple assumptions reproduced CCN(0.4%) remarkably well (Figure 8), except for occasional
overestimations. Further investigation revealed that these periods were associated with flow arriving at
the sampling site that had crossed the Córdoba urban corridor, resulting in a lower overall k associated
with urban pollution aerosol types. Nevertheless, the simple method reproduces changes in CCN(0.4%) of
at least a factor or 3, suggesting careful observations of aerosol size distributions could be adequate for
estimating CCN concentrations in the absence of more direct CCN observations.

To summarize our findings for Hypothesis 2:

 • INP concentrations in the NH and SH are often dominated, especially for activation temperatures
 below -20˚C, by a class of INPs that is organic-rich, but associated with mineral INPs. Their
 source is likely windblown arable soils.
 • As for marine INPs, episodic contributions from biological INPs were observed in all field
 campaigns. Their concentrations are not predictable using simple correlations to other aerosol
 properties. However, their concentrations were often correlated with the presence of precipitation.
 • Proxy methods for characterizing properties of the aerosol size distribution were developed and
 helped fill in data gaps. However, the importance and value of accurate size distributions for both
 sub- and supermicron particles is stressed. For example, variability in CCN number
 concentrations reflect variability in the size distribution and reasonable estimates of CCN active
 at different supersaturations can be made.

4. Using Findings to Advance Modeling of Aerosol-Cloud Interactions

Application of our findings toward improve of the presentation of aerosol-cloud interactions is the
ultimate goal of our work. Accordingly, we pursued collaborations with several modeling groups, which
were reported in the following journal articles.

Central Continental:

Waman et al. (2022) investigated the representation and impacts of secondary ice production (SIP) in
continental convective clouds. Representative compositions and spectra for immersion freezing ice nuclei,
accounting for primary ice production, were included based on terrestrial (here, continental U.S.) INP
observations from our projects.

Zhou et al. (in review) present a new surface active site density parameterization for continental
immersion-mode INPs (warm and cold season fits), dominated by coarse-mode soil dust aerosol sources.

Marine (here, all Southern Ocean):

Zhao et al. (2023) studied the simulation of SO aerosols and clouds in the CESM2 global model, with a
particular focus on cloud phase and SIP. Among their other findings, they concluded, based on

 11

simulations and comparisons with field observations, that sea spray organic aerosols are the most
important type of IN over the SO.
.
McCluskey et al. (2023) used CESM2 to simulate the SO atmosphere during MARCUS and found a
negative bias in simulated accumulation aerosol number concentrations, and a high bias in coarse mode
aerosol. These were then reflected in biases in INP types predicted. Most INPs in the marine boundary
layer are of marine origin, but they concluded that mineral dust aerosol sources and transport are
important contributor to the INP populations over the southern high latitudes

Raman et al. (2023) used E3SM to simulate immersion-mode INPs over an entire year, for comparison
to the MICRE dataset taken on Macquarie Island. The model underestimates dust and overestimates sea
spray aerosol concentrations by 1 to 2 orders of magnitude for most of the high-latitude sites in the
Southern Hemisphere. However, INP concentrations based on these species were within a factor of 10
more than half of the summer. This study suggested aras for improvement to the treatment of INPs in the
model.
