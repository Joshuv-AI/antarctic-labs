Immune Cell Responses to Fluid Shear: Overview and Design Considerations for In Vitro Modeling
Authors: Eric L Ginter, Laurel E Hind
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFluid flow is ubiquitous throughout the immune system. Immune cells exhibit sensitivity to fluid shear stress, altering their activation and behavior in response to the presence, magnitude, and timing of fluid flow. Therefore, understanding how flow regulates the immune response is crucial for understanding the immune system’s contribution to homeostasis, disease progression, and therapeutic interventions. Recent developments have indicated Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel protein, is a crucial mechanism for cell sensing and response to shear, but our understanding of how shear impacts immune function is far from complete. Open questions including: how cells respond to acute vs chronic shear stress exposure, how shear influences cell-cell communication, and direct molecular mechanisms require further investigation. In vitro modeling provides one promising approach to fill this gap, and many systems have been recently developed that incorporate flow to investigate the immune response in model organs and disease states. This review discusses recent advances in our understanding of fluid shear stress’s impact on immune cell behavior, and we provide a landscape of in vitro models integrating flow and immune cells. To guide future development, we evaluate five critical design considerations: flow directionality, channel cross-section, culture substrate, recirculation strategies, and TEER compatibility.Keywords: Shear stress, Immune response, Flow, Organ-on-chip, Piezo1, in vitro models

Introduction

The immune system is comprised of a finely balanced network of cells and organs critical for maintaining tissue homeostasis, connected through blood vessels and lymphatics. Fluid flow is ubiquitous in these environments, and most immune cell populations are mechanosensitive to fluid shear stress. While in vitro models provide simplified platforms to investigate cellular responses, traditional methods often operate under static conditions, failing to capture the effect of fluid shear stress. In the last decade, the importance of shear stress has gained increasing recognition, and the advent of biophysiological systems has made flow integration into in vitro models easier and more commonplace. Development of in vitro models that properly recapitulate in vivo results is particularly prescient with the FDA and NIH emphasis on New Approach Methodologies (NAMs) to reduce animal testing. This review synthesizes recent developments in understanding how flow impacts immune cell behavior and summarizes progress of incorporating fluid flow in in vitro models of the immune system. Finally, we offer an outlook on key design considerations and future directions for integrating flow into immune models.

Fluid Flow in the Body

Fluid flow in the body is responsible for transporting nutrients, metabolites, immune cells, and waste. Blood and lymph are the primary fluids in which immune cells experience fluid shear conditions. Blood is composed of ~50% red blood cells by volume, which confers a shear thinning property that results in distinct flow characteristics for large vessels (>300 um) compared to small vessels and capillaries. Reynolds numbers in small vessels and capillaries tends to be very small (~10−4−10−6), while in large vessels they are significantly higher (~102−103)[1]. Despite this broad range, blood flow is generally laminar across vessel types, though it can become turbulent around curves and bifurcations, under very high velocity, or as a result of diseases. The heart drives pulsatile blood flow, but as blood moves away from the heart the flow becomes less pulsatile and more unidirectional in nature. Therefore, capillaries experience only minor pulsatile effects and veins experience smooth unidirectional flow. Another critical component of immune cells in blood flow is margination of cell types; this is the phenomena of red blood cells tending to flow in the center of vessels while white blood cells are concentrated near vessel walls, where they experience local wall shear stress[2]. Wall shear stress for blood vessels ranges from ~1-100 dynes/cm2, inversely correlated with vessel size [3]. Lymph fluid is driven through lymphatic vessels by smooth and skeletal muscle contraction, and these vessels have valves that prevent backflow of fluid. The Reynolds number for lymph is very low, so laminar flow dominates and wall shear stress values are in the range of .5-5 dynes/cm2[4]. Flow direction can range from unidirectional to pulsatile during intense contraction[4].

Immune cells are carried by blood and lymph throughout the body and experience the broad range of changing shear conditions and physical forces as they circulate. Forces are transduced through the cytoskeleton and membrane bound proteins, which results in changes to transcription factors and distinct phenotypic changes to cell morphology and activation.

Impact of Flow on Immune Cells

Most immune cells exhibit sensitivity to fluid flow, and recent work has begun to define how shear stress shapes immune activation. Piezo1, a mechanosensitive membrane-bound ion channel protein, has emerged as a common transducer for shear sensitivity in many immune cell populations. However, shear’s impact on cell behavior and the underlying mechanisms remains poorly characterized, in part because many studies have been carried out in vivo where quantified flow rates and wall shear stresses are difficult to determine. Key findings and recent developments of immune cells and shear stress are summarized below.

Endothelial cells (ECs), which line the interior of blood and lymph vessels, are critical players in recruiting and activating immune cells from the bloodstream. EC expression of Piezo1 and its role in mechanosensing in ECs are well established[5], though the relationship between flow conditions and response is incomplete. For example, in the context of arterial stenosis, laminar high shear (~25 dynes/cm2) is pro-inflammatory[6], whereas in atherosclerosis, it is protective[7]. Further findings show ECs are pro-inflammatory under oscillatory shear stress and anti-inflammatory under laminar shear stress[8]. Oscillatory shear stress is seen in curved vessels such as the aortic arch, and the context dependent response of ECs to the same shear suggests vessel geometry and size as contributing factors. Mechanistic studies are required to determine how shear stress drives inflammatory cues in ECs and how these cells differentially recruit immune cells in response to varying flow rates. ECs in the body experience a wide range of physiological shear stresses, so characterizing EC shear responses will broaden our understanding of immune recruitment in healthy and diseased states.

Macrophages are tissue-resident phagocytes that play a central role in defending against pathogens and maintaining tissue homeostasis. Macrophages respond to shear stress in a time-dependent manner by upregulating expression of proinflammatory cytokines, decreasing anti-inflammatory cytokines[9], and upregulating inflammasome activation and Piezo1 expression[10]. Piezo1 is necessary for macrophage activation in response to infection[11] and mediates functional macrophage polarization. Deletion of Piezo1 in mice halts the progression of irritable bowel disease, with macrophages exhibiting more M1 polarization and reduced cytokine secretion[12]. In vivo, elevated shear stress following arterial-venous graft in mice caused macrophages to upregulate genes associated with inflammation and angiogenesis[13]. Collectively, these findings indicate increased shear stress is pro-inflammatory; however, this relationship is not straightforward. Wei et al. [14] found that low shear stress (5 vs 12 dynes/cm2) upregulates endothelial secretion of CCL7, causing subsequent pro-inflammatory macrophage responses, including increased TNF and IL-1β expression. This contrasting result may indicate that macrophages co-cultured with ECs change their shear-activation, demonstrating the need for sophisticated in vitro techniques to explore shear dynamics.

Neutrophils, the most abundant leukocytes in the body, are rapidly recruited to sites of infection following transendothelial migration and are mechanosensitive cells. Following shear exposure, neutrophils change their morphology by retracting pseudopods and demonstrating a round morphology [15]. One second of high shear stress exposure (>750 dynes/cm2), as may be seen with mechanical pump implantation following cardiac failure, caused significant changes to neutrophils: cell membrane integrity was compromised, adhesion receptor expression was decreased, and cell adhesion was diminished [16]. Low shear stress (5 vs 12 dynes/cm2) directly increases neutrophil NETosis, adhesion to ECs, and EC apoptosis[17]. Piezo1 has shown a direct role in driving NETosis, though conflicting findings indicate Piezo1 activation both increased[18] and decreased[17] NETosis in parallel plates and microfluidic channels, respectively. The microfluidic channels used by Baratchi et al did not include ECs, while the parallel plates in the Zhu et al study did. The differences in geometries and cell-cell interactions could be responsible for the varying results, though future work is necessary to clarify the impact of Piezo1 and shear stress on neutrophil effector function. In general, neutrophils show strong dependence on fluid shear stress for priming and activation and disruption of this relationship indicates a potential source of pathogenesis.

Dendritic cells (DCs) respond to host and pathogen-derived signals, migrating from tissues to lymph nodes and activating B and T cells via antigen presentation. Shear stress increases DC activation, including upregulating cytokine release (e.g. TNF, ICAM-1, CCL3) and prolonging costimulatory molecule expression[19]. DC Piezo1 has been linked to altered T-cell differentiation via substrate stiffness sensing, though no link to fluid shear stress has yet emerged[20]. Exploiting DC shear activation offers a promising therapeutic approach to engineering robust immune responses[21], though the downstream impact of shear-activated DCs on adaptive immunity remains an open question.

Self-tolerance, specificity, and antigen diversity of T and B cells are all imperative for an effective adaptive immune response, and dysfunction of these processes drives autoimmune disease (false activation) and cancer progression (false tolerance). Fluid shear stress is directly linked to T cell activation, NF-κB phosphorylation, and increased inflammatory cytokine expression (TNF, IFN-γ) [22]. Deleting T cell Piezo1 increases tumor aggression, reduces sensitivity to immunogenic therapies against cancer, lowers CD4+/CD8+ ratios, and impairs IL-9 secretion in CD4+ cells[23,24]. B cells also exhibit Piezo1-dependence, with Piezo1 inhibition causing decreased B cell activation to membrane-bound antigen[25] and reduced IgA class switching[26]. While these studies demonstrate the mechanosensitivity of B cell activation, the impact of fluid shear stress is not understood. More research to fully elucidate the impact of shear stress on adaptive immunity is necessary, but in vitro study of B and T cells is complicated by HLA-type match restrictions, making modeling intercellular interactions difficult.

While the link between fluid shear stress and immune modulation is established, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain poorly defined. The literature on this topic is often confounding, with context dependent differences in systems making acute synthesis of trends difficult. In vitro methods often lack critical cell-cell interactions and environmental factors, while in vivo techniques can be difficult to measure shear differences and quantify results. To move beyond observational findings, research must resolve the distinct impacts of acute versus chronic exposure and determine how shear stress dictates intercellular communication. Specifically, further study is needed to clarify the universal role of Piezo1 across all immune cell types. Advanced in vitro models are positioned to provide the controlled environments necessary to isolate these variables.

Studying Flow in Immune In Vitro Models

The impact of flow on immune cell behavior is established, yet a complete understanding of how shear stress impacts immune responses is missing; therefore, models of the immune system that integrate flow under physiologically relevant conditions are needed to answer open questions.

Transient Shear Exposure

One approach to characterize immune cell response to shear is to transiently expose cells to shear and study changes in behavior, protein expression, and morphology. Several methods including pipetting, cone and plate viscometers, and micropipette aspiration are common. Pipetting cells repeatedly offers a simple, low-cost method of shear exposure, but is not standardized or easily reproducible. Additionally, pipetting at higher shear rates can lead to turbulent flow, which is not indicative of flow conditions typically seen in the body. This method can be used to indicate initial shear sensitivity, but more controlled methods are preferred for quantifying differences. Cone and plate viscometers consist of a plate covered with a thin layer of fluid and a cone rotating over the top to apply a precise shear rate for a specified time interval. This method is helpful for bulk activation of a cell population under uniform, laminar conditions and is easily reproducible, though the shear is not as physiologically relevant as that in vessels due to overly simple geometries and uniform flow profiles that don’t match conditions in the body. Micropipette aspiration is a method of measuring cell mechanical properties; it consists of using negative pressure to partially suck in a cell and measure deformation. This method can also be used to apply controlled force to a cell, and cells can then be studied for mechanosensitivity. This technique provides single cell precision and reproducibility, but the deformation is not analogous to flow shear application. Regardless of method, cells exposed to transient shear exposure can be used in a wide array of assays to quantify changes in behavior. These methods are best used to demonstrate shear sensitivity of a cell population and quantify the scale of the response, but methods that allow prolonged exposure and relevant shear profiles are more indicative of interactions in the body.

Parallel Plate Flow Chambers

Parallel plate flow chambers offer a simple model for integrating flow in vitro. These chips use pumps to perfuse fluid between two plates to control shear stress. Foundational models utilize protein- or cell-coated glass slides to investigate the adhesion and migration of suspended immune cells under perfusion, for example, macrophages migrating on ICAM-1[27]. Ferrick et al. [28] designed a more advanced system that allows for controlling both shear rate and substrate while having enough protein for endpoint protein analysis. Compared to transient shear methods, parallel plate flow chambers are compatible with real-time imaging techniques and endpoint analysis. While these systems offer an affordable platform for studying flow environments in vitro, they are typically limited to only modeling short-scale flow exposure. Re-circulation pumps allow longer time scales and smaller media volumes, but lack complexity, including relevant vessel geometries and interactions between cell populations. The plate chamber geometry does not provide laminar flow profiles matching those seen in vivo, where tubular vessels induce parabolic laminar flow. For in vitro recapitulation of physiological vessel conditions, more refined models are necessary.

Organ on Chip

The advent of Organ-on-Chip (OOC) technology in the past 15 years has resulted in chips modeling virtually every organ of the body with varying degrees of complexity, aiming to capture the most essential elements of the target organ in the model. These devices lend themselves to perfusion, allowing tunable fluid shear stress integration. As a result, analyzing controlled flow in physiologically relevant contexts is more accessible than an in vivo study. OOC platforms have several advantages over traditional in vitro methods, such as allowing greater control and flexibility of culture conditions, and they allow multiple cell types to interact in physiologically relevant environments. Channel geometry and size can be tuned to control the resultant flow profile, allowing microenvironments modeling a wide range of physiological and pathological flow conditions. Immune-relevant models include chips recapitulating the circulatory system, lymphatic system, and inflammation-prone organs, including the lungs, intestines, and liver, with many notable immune-competent systems developed in recent years (See Table 1). While many immune-relevant OOCs have been created, these systems must be used to fully interrogate and contextualize flow-dependent mechanisms of immune cell activation. The flexibility of OOC comes at the expense of simplicity; designing and fabricating these systems is an involved, expensive process compared to simpler models. Commercial platforms are becoming available as this field develops, improving accessibility of OOC in research[29,30]. Recent prioritization of NAMs has provided additional funding for OOC platform development, as these models show potential to maximize physiological relevance of findings without the need for animal testing.

Design Considerations of Integrating Flow

With considerable progress in integrating flow with in vitro immune models, dissecting the impacts of flow on immune response is becoming increasingly possible. The physiological relevance of a model depends entirely on the design parameters chosen to mimic the in vivo environment and the system and conditions should be selected to maximize relevance for the question being studied. Here, we provide overviews and resources for design considerations we believe are critical in designing in vitro immune models.

Unidirectional vs Bidirectional vs Pulsatile flow

Gravity-driven bidirectional flow is currently the most common flow application for OOC devices due to its simplicity, but it is not physiologically relevant as flow in the body is unidirectional or pulsatile. Unidirectional or pulsatile flow requires integration of a pump, which increases costs, reduces widespread adoption, and decreases scalability. This is critical as the method of flow application impacts cell behavior, particularly in endothelial cells which show stark changes in morphology and gene expression in response to the type of flow[43,44]. For example, EC gene expression, including genes responsible for recruiting and activating immune cells, was altered in response to peristaltic pump-driven unidirectional flow compared to rocker-driven bidirectional flow[43]. This may in part be due to gravity driven flow not reaching physiologically relevant magnitudes, an inherent limitation of this method being that a 100-fold increase in flow rate requires a 100-fold increase in hydrostatic pressure. Conversely, pumps can be easily modulated to provide a full range of physiologically relevant shear stresses, with the additional benefit of choosing unidirectional or pulsatile flow to match the organ conditions being modeled.

Channel Geometry

Most OOC platforms utilize rectangular channel geometries due to the lithography techniques used to fabricate the chips. Channel geometry directly impacts wall shear stress[45,46], which can impact cell behavior. Rectangular cross sections result in flow profiles with non-uniform wall shear rates under laminar conditions, making circular cross-sections preferable for the study of shear-rate impact on cells. ECs exhibit morphological changes dependent on channel geometry and flow type, with cylindrical channels exhibiting distinct endothelial cell alignment and increased confluence compared to rectangular cross-sections[44]. Cylindrical channels for OOC can be fabricated by printing with sacrificial materials[44] or using a removable rod[47,48].

Membrane Permeability

Endothelial membrane integrity is crucial for modeling immune-relevant systems, including vasculature, spleen, and liver. Membrane permeability can vary widely in different tissues, from physiological values of 1000-6000 Ωcm2 for blood brain barrier to ~10 Ωcm2 for blood-testi barrier[49]. Transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) measurement provides a reliable metric for quantifying membrane permeability. Traditional TEER is performed by applying a small electrical current across a membrane with an electrode placed on either side, and impedance measurements correlate strongly with tight junction integrity[50]. This approach requires modification for compatibility with OOC devices: electrodes must be scaled down, and chip design modifications are required for accurate, repeatable measurement. Approaches, including integrating electrodes in PDMS layers, show potential to integrate TEER with OOC. Malik et al. [49] provide an in-depth look at how TEER can be combined with these models.

Endothelial cell selection is also critical for ensuring relevant membrane permeability and therefore must be considered when designing OOC models. Different ECs exhibit heterogeneous membrane integrity, with factors including vessel size, tissue of origination, cell line and vessel type impacting EC junction tightness and membrane permeability values [49]. Selecting a representative EC source for OOC applications is critical to ensure physiologically relevant results.

Culture Substrate

Culture substrates are typically either a synthetic polymer (e.g. PDMS) or a biological hydrogel (e.g. collagen, fibronectin). Just as immune cells show mechanosensitivity to fluid shear stress, these cells also respond to the stiffness of their substrates. Activation of macrophages cultured on stiff polyacrylamide gel is more pro-inflammatory than softer gels[51], and Calo et al. [52] determined that collagen gel concentration has a direct impact on gel pore size, stiffness, and neutrophil behavior. Culture substrates also provide important biochemical cues that can modulate cell activity; Kutluk et al. [53] provide an in-depth overview of the importance of extracellular matrix selection and incorporating biologically-relevant extracellular matrix constructs with OOC platforms.

Flow-Through vs Recirculation

Flow in in vitro devices requires an outlet for flowing fluid. Options include flowing through the device to a reservoir or recirculating in a closed system. Flow-through methods require few additional engineering considerations compared to their counterpart. This method is suitable for providing a physical stimulus to adherent cells, whereas recirculation is desirable when cells are suspended in the perfused media or when measuring cytokine release. Reducing dead volume from tubing has the benefit of a reduction in cell and media requirements and allows for more sensitive measurement of concentration-dependent analytes. Several novel approaches have recently emerged to allow tubing-free recirculation. Of note, Rajan et al. [54] developed a liver-on-chip platform with small volume requirements and re-circulation that can be used for pharmacokinetic analysis of drug clearance. Cook et al. [55] developed and released an open-source, low-cost 3D printed pump that utilizes a stir bar as an impeller to drive flow in a closed system. Smaller volume systems and re-circulation will allow increasingly relevant research questions to be answered.

Conclusion

Immune cells exhibit shear-induced activation in multiple contexts, and flow is an integral aspect of immune responses in vivo. Therefore, understanding immune interactions will require elucidating the contributions of flow shear stress. While shear is recognized as an important activation factor, more work is necessary to decipher the responses and mechanisms behind variable flow parameters such as magnitude and duration, as well as how cellular-level changes contribute to systemic responses. Tools including parallel-plate chambers and organ-on-chip models, integrate flow and show potential for investigations in this area, but further refinement is necessary to maximize the physiological relevance of findings. By designing in vitro systems integrated with relevant flow conditions, we can further unravel shear-mediated immune activation and develop new therapeutic tools to treat inflammatory dysfunction.
