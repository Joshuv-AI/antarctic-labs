Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) in haemoglobin SC disease: Moving from therapeutic hesitation to evidence‐guided care
Authors: Tahereh Setayesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Girard et al. report a retrospective, single‐centre, real‐world study evaluating hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) use in adults with haemoglobin SC disease in Quebec. Hydroxyurea was associated with reduced vaso‐occlusive crisis composite events, mostly mild adverse events and no observed hyperviscosity‐related complications, while highlighting adherence and prescription‐renewal barriers in routine care.Commentary on: Girard et al. Hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) use in adults with haemoglobin SC disease: A real‐world study in Quebec. Br J Haematol 2026; 209:1291‐1300.

In their paper, Girard et al. provide timely real‐world evidence supporting the use of hydroxyurea (hydroxycarbamide) in adults with haemoglobin SC (HbSC) disease. 1 Their retrospective, single‐centre, pre‐post cohort study from Quebec included 110 adults in the safety analysis and 75 in the effectiveness analysis. Hydroxyurea was associated with a 56% reduction in the annual vaso‐occlusive crisis composite (VOCC), from 0.57 to 0.25 events per year. Adverse events were mostly mild, and importantly, no hyperviscosity‐related complications were observed. 1

These findings are important because HbSC disease has long occupied an uncertain space in sickle cell disease care. Although HbSC is the second most common sickle genotype, it has often been described as ‘milder’ than homozygous sickle haemoglobin disease (HbSS), a label that has contributed to therapeutic hesitation and underrepresentation in clinical trials. 2 , 3 , 4 Contemporary cohorts demonstrate that HbSC disease is associated with substantial morbidity, including vaso‐occlusive pain, acute chest syndrome, avascular necrosis, proliferative retinopathy, splenomegaly, thromboembolic events and otological complications. 5 , 6 HbSC may be less haemolytic than HbSS, but it is not benign. Rather, it is biologically and clinically distinct. 2 , 3

The pathophysiology of HbSC disease provides a strong rationale for genotype‐specific evaluation of therapy. Haemoglobin C (HbC) promotes red‐cell dehydration, increasing intracellular haemoglobin concentration and thereby enhancing haemoglobin S (HbS) polymerisation. 2 This produces dense, poorly deformable erythrocytes in a circulatory environment often characterised by relatively preserved haemoglobin levels and increased viscosity. Thus, the central therapeutic concern in HbSC has not simply been whether hydroxyurea can induce fetal haemoglobin (HbF), but whether it can reduce sickling and vaso‐occlusion without worsening viscosity through excessive haemoglobin rise. For years, this concern was biologically plausible but clinically unresolved. 3

The evidence base has now changed. CHAMPS (Effects of Hydroxycarbamide and Magnesium on Haemoglobin SC Disease) prospectively evaluated hydroxycarbamide and magnesium in HbSC disease but was underpowered for definitive clinical conclusions. 7 A later multicentre retrospective cohort suggested that hydroxyurea increased mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and HbF and reduced pain events, with generally reversible cytopenias. 8 More recently, PIVOT (Prospective Identification of Variables as Outcomes for Treatment), a Ghana‐based randomised trial, represented a major advance by prospectively testing hydroxyurea in children and adults with HbSC disease; it showed substantial reductions in vaso‐occlusive pain events and hospitalisations, although dose‐limiting toxicities were more frequent than with placebo and were mostly mild and transient. 9 Girard et al. now extend this evidence into a North American adult real‐world setting, where comorbidities, adherence barriers and prescribing patterns may differ from trial conditions. 1

Several aspects of the Quebec study deserve emphasis. First, the magnitude of clinical benefit was consistent with PIVOT and prior retrospective observations, despite a relatively low baseline event rate. 1 , 8 , 9 Second, the absence of observed hyperviscosity‐related complications is reassuring, particularly given that this remains a major reason clinicians hesitate to prescribe hydroxyurea in HbSC disease. 1 , 3 Third, the study captures an important real‐world truth: treatment success depends not only on drug biology but also on prescription renewal, patient education, shared decision‐making and long‐term adherence support. 1

The magnitude of HbF induction in HbSC disease also deserves attention. In Girard et al., HbF increased from 2.53% to 5.45% after hydroxyurea, accompanied by a marked rise in MCV and a 56% reduction in VOCC. 1 This modest absolute HbF increase contrasts with the central role traditionally assigned to HbF induction in HbSS, where hydroxyurea efficacy has often been interpreted through sustained increases in HbF and F cells. In HbSC disease, however, the relationship between HbF induction and clinical benefit may be less linear. PIVOT and prior retrospective HbSC cohorts similarly support clinical benefit with hydroxyurea, but the extent to which this benefit is explained by HbF induction versus HbF‐independent effects remains uncertain. 8 , 9

Recent studies in humanised HbSC and HbSS mouse models provide mechanistic support for this interpretation. In these models, HbSC and HbSS erythrocytes are generated on a comparable humanised sickle background, with the critical difference being conversion of one human β‐globin allele from HbS to HbC at codon 6. 10 This comparison revealed that hydroxyurea can improve red‐cell pathology through HbF‐independent mechanisms. In adult mice, hydroxyurea improved red‐cell deformability and reduced oxidative haemoglobin and membrane injury, including red‐cell reactive oxygen species, ferryl haemoglobin, Heinz body formation and membrane‐associated damage, despite minimal or absent HbF induction. 10 Notably, these benefits occurred without correction of red‐cell dehydration, suggesting that hydroxyurea may improve membrane integrity and rheologic behaviour through pathways distinct from simply reversing the xerocytic phenotype.

Together, the clinical data from Girard et al. and PIVOT, and the mechanistic data from humanised mouse models, argue that HbF should not be treated as the sole pharmacodynamic marker of hydroxyurea response in HbSC disease. Future studies should report HbF and F cells alongside MCV, dense‐cell fraction, red‐cell hydration, viscosity, deformability, oxidative membrane injury and organ‐specific outcomes such as retinopathy. This HbSC‐specific framework may better capture why patients can derive clinical benefit even when HbF responses are modest.

At the same time, the limitations of the study should shape how the findings are interpreted. The pre‐post design allows each patient to serve as their own control, reducing interindividual variability, but it cannot fully account for secular trends, regression to the mean, changes in clinical behaviour or unmeasured time‐varying confounders. The study counted vaso‐occlusive events requiring day‐hospital, emergency or inpatient care; therefore, painful crises managed at home were not captured. Adherence was difficult to measure retrospectively, and the per‐protocol analysis was smaller and less statistically definitive. 1

The adherence findings are also actionable. Discontinuation was driven less by severe toxicity than by modifiable barriers, including prescription renewal misunderstandings and communication gaps. 1 Structured education, clear renewal plans and pharmacist‐supported monitoring may, therefore, be as important as further trials in improving long‐term hydroxyurea use in HbSC disease.

Taken together, the study by Girard et al. supports a practical shift in HbSC management. Hydroxyurea should no longer be dismissed because HbSC is perceived as mild nor should it be prescribed uncritically by extrapolating from HbSS. Instead, it should be considered through an HbSC‐specific framework: identify patients with clinically meaningful complications, discuss the existing evidence and uncertainties, monitor blood counts and haemoglobin carefully and incorporate rheological and organ‐specific end‐points where available. For a disease that has been understudied despite substantial morbidity, the emerging message is clear: HbSC disease requires evidence‐guided treatment, not therapeutic neglect.
