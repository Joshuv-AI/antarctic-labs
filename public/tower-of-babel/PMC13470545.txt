Paternal exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics induces inter- and transgenerational bronchopulmonary dysplasia-like damage in male offspring by FtMt hypermethylation-mediated ferroptosis
Authors: Yuehui Liang, Kangle Liu, Xin Wang, Chunsheng Lu, Ya Cheng, Yurui Yang, Yixian Wen, Xiao Jiang, Wenfeng Zhang, Lina Zhao, Jia Cao, Fei Han, Jinyi Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a major cause of chronic lung disease in both preterm infants and adults, but its etiology remains incompletely understood. In this study, F0 generation mice were exposed to polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs), and F1 to F3 generations were obtained by breeding. Multi-omics sequencing including whole genome methylation sequencing, single cell transcriptome sequencing and transcriptome sequencing was performed on the lungs of offspring. The levels of Fe2+, lipid peroxidation products and key gene expression were determined. Male mice exposed to PS-NPs at environmentally relevant doses produced offspring (F1 and F2) that exhibited a typical BPD-like phenotype. Meanwhile, the F0 males showed diminished sperm motility, demonstrating that paternal PS-NPs exposure constituted an etiological factor for BPD in descendants. Mechanistic studies showed that PS-NPs exposure upregulated the expression of DNA methyltransferase Dnmt3a, leading to global hypermethylation of the sperm genome. Importantly, the hypermethylated promoter signature of the mitochondrial ferritin (FtMt) gene partially resisted epigenetic reprogramming and was transmitted to the lungs of offspring, resulting in persistently low FtMt expression in F1 and F2 lungs. This led to increased intracellular Fe2+ levels, subsequently triggered ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells, and ultimately impaired alveolarization. Knockdown of FtMt confirmed that FtMt deficiency was sufficient to induce ferroptosis and BPD-like lung injury both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, using in vitro fertilization of F0 sperm combined with Dnmt3a siRNA microinjection, we directly demonstrated that Dnmt3a is a key driver for FtMt to escape reprogramming and maintain its hypermethylation. In summary, this study reveals for the first time that paternal PS-NPs exposure causes BPD through a Dnmt3a-FtMt hypermethylation intergenerational and transgenerational axis, providing an epigenetic basis for understanding paternal derived chronic lung disease and potential targets for early intervention.Keywords: Nanoplastics, Lung, Ferroptosis, Intergenerational effect, Methylation

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a chronic childhood lung disease characterized by alveolar arrest [1,2]. The pathogenesis of BPD has traditionally been linked to direct postnatal insults to the immature lung, including hyperoxia, inflammation, and mechanical injury [3]. However, clinical observations indicate that even among infants with comparable degrees of prematurity and similar postnatal care, substantial heterogeneity in BPD severity persists [4]. This heterogeneity strongly suggests that individual developmental damage plays a crucial role in determining the extent of lung injury. The classic “fetal programming” model of BPD emphasized the significant impact of the intrauterine environment on offspring lung development [5,6].

Notably, the parental origins of BPD, particularly paternal contributions, are gaining attention.

The Paternal Origins of Health and Disease (POHaD) hypothesis, which posits that paternal exposures to environmental pollutants can adversely affect offspring health, with immense threats for both biological research and public health [7]. In the context of paternal lineage, F1 offspring, derived from the paternal sperm, may be directly exposed to environmental factors. Therefore, the transmission of phenotypes from the directly exposed F0 to F1 offspring is classified as intergenerational inheritance. In contrast, effects observed in the F2 generation, the first generation not directly exposed, and beyond are defined as transgenerational effects [8]. Previous findings collectively indicated paternal exposure to environmental pollutants can adversely affect offspring development and long-term health, and may even have transgenerational effects. The male reproductive system is more sensitive to exposure to environmental pollutants, particularly during meiotic stages of germ cell development [9]. Notably, sperms can transmit information about parental environmental exposures to offspring, potentially altering offspring health phenotype.

Ordinarily, the observed associations between paternal exposure to environmental pollutants and offspring health outcomes are more likely mediated by sperm epigenetic alterations rather than direct mutagenic effects [10]. Most chemicals undergo rigorous mutagenicity screening during development, making it unlikely that such exposures directly cause DNA breaks or mutations [11]. Instead, epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, play a crucial role in mediating intergenerational or transgenerational effects. As the most extensively studied epigenetic modification, DNA methylation regulates critical biological processes, including cell differentiation and embryonic development. During embryogenesis, it governs gene expression patterns, transposon silencing, X chromosome inactivation, and genomic imprinting [12]. Emerging evidence implicates that altered DNA methylation reprogramming contributes to the transmission of paternal exposure effects, including those involving imprinted genes [13]. Notably, sperm-derived DNA methylation marks demonstrate greater stability during early embryonic development compared to oocyte-derived marks [14]. This preferential retention of sperm methylation patterns may help explain the disproportionate impact of paternal exposures on offspring phenotypes.

Microplastics (MPs), defined as plastic particles and fragments with diameters less than 5 mm, are generated through the environmental degradation of plastic waste. These particles can further degrade into smaller particles with a diameter between 1 and 100 nm, known as nanoplastics (NPs) [15]. Both MPs and NPs are identified as emerging environmental contaminants, and their potential health risks to various organ systems have attracted widespread attention [16,17]. Growing evidences have detected MPs/NPs in various human tissues and biological samples, including lung tissue [18], blood [19], breast milk [20], placenta [20], feces [20], testes [21], and semen [21]. Of particular concern is their accumulation in the testes, which has become a hot topic in human reproductive health. Growing studies indicate that MPs/NPs exert male reproductive toxicity, notably by reducing sperm count and impairing motility [22]. Given that sperm DNA damage may transmit paternal exposure information to offspring, resulting in health damage to the offspring [10]. Studying the transgenerational impacts and underlying mechanisms of parental MPs/NPs exposure holds significant scientific importance. To this end, we established a paternal PS-NPs exposure mouse model to evaluate multigenerational health effects. Our findings reveal that parental exposure to PS-NPs induced classic BPD-like injury across the F1–F2 male generations, demonstrating clearly transgenerational inheritance. To elucidate the mechanistic basis, we employed multi-omics methods, including Whole Genome Bisulfite Sequencing (WGBS), Reduced Representation Bisulfite Sequencing (RRBS), and transcriptome, and in vitro fertilization assay. These analyses identified key epigenetic and transcriptional alterations associated with the observed phenotypes. Furthermore, we validated the molecular mechanisms of PS-NPs-induced lung injury in vitro, providing mechanistic insights into the intergenerational and transgenerational effects.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Polystyrene Nanoplastics (PS-NPs, 80 nm in size) with a concentration of 2.5% (w/v) were purchased from Tianjin Baseline Technology Research Center (Tianjin, China). Particle size distribution and ζ-potential of PS-NPs suspensions were analyzed by dynamic light scattering by Zetasizer (Fig. S1A). PS-NPs size was morphologically characterized by scanning electron microscope (SEM) according to a previously described protocol (Fig. S1B). All the PS-NPs particle type was suspended in distilled water.

Deferoxamine was acquired from Med Chem Express (Brea, CA, USA). Mouse FtMt siRNA was obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Mouse Dnmt3a plasmid was obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The β-actin rabbit monoclonal antibody (AF5003) and glutathione detection kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The Gpx4 (30388-1-AP), Hopx (11419-1-AP) and Acsl4 (22401-1-AP) rabbit antibody were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). The detection kits for ferrous ions (Fe2+) and malondialdehyde (MDA) content were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). The 5-mC DNA quantification kit was obtained from EpigenTek (Farmingdale, NY, USA).

Mice

According to previously published epidemiological toxicological evidence, humans may ingest microplastics ranging from 0.014 g to 0.71 g per day via multiple environmental exposure routes including food, drinking water and air inhalation [23]. Combined with the well-recognized allometric Km conversion coefficient of 12.3 for extrapolating oral exposure dose from humans to mice, the equivalent exposure range for mice is calculated to be 2.11 mg/kg/d to 107.14 mg/kg/d with a standard human body weight of 60 kg, which covers real-world human environmental exposure scenarios [24]. Our previous publication systematically set PS-NP doses at 0 (control), 10, and 40 mg/kg/d for F0 male mice and fully assessed reproductive toxicity. The results indicated that the 10 mg/kg/d group exhibited the greatest reduction in sperm motility across all doses [25,26], leading us to choose this dose for subsequent exposures.

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) C57BL/6J mice were acquired from Pharmachem Biotechnology (Chengdu, China) and kept in a chamber with standard feeding environment following a 12-h light or dark cycle. Subsequently, the mice were randomly divided into the control group and the paternal PS-NPs exposure group. Mice in the paternal PS-NPs exposure group were administered 10 mg/kg/d of PS-NPs, while those in the control group received an equivalent volume of deionized water. The animal model was established through daily oral administration to the mice, conducted between 9 a.m. and 12 a.m. for 60 consecutive days. All animal procedures were meticulously carried out in strict accordance with the protocols approved by the ethics committee of Army Medical University.

At 24 h after the last treatment, some mice were sacrificed and their key organ, including epididymis, and testes were collected for further analysis. The remaining mice were then mated with unexposed female mice at a ratio of 1:2 and housed together to produce the F1 generation. Using the same method, the F2 and F3 generations were generated. Notably, the F1, F2, and F3 generations were not directly exposed to PS-NPs. On the 60th day post-birth, the offspring mice were sacrificed, and their above organs were harvested for further investigation.

Cell culture and treatment

MLE-12 cells, a mouse type II alveolar epithelial cell line obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), were cultured in DMEM/F-12 (1:1) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. The cell viability was determined by the CCK-8 assay.

To explore the function of FtMt gene, when the MLE-12 cells reached approximately 60% confluence, they were incubated with FtMt siRNA. Similarly, to investigate the role of Dnmt3a in methylation, the overexpression Dnmt3a plasmid was transfected into the MLE-12 cells. This transfection process was carried out using UltraFection 3.0 (4A Biotech, Beijing, China). Specifically, the plasmids and transfection reagents were mixed and incubated in DMEM/F-12 medium at room temperature for 10 min before being added to the MLE-12 cells. The transfected cells were then harvested 48 h later for subsequent analysis and evaluation.

In vitro fertilization (IVF)

Briefly, 4-week-old female mice were superovulated via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of Pregnant Mare's Serum Gonadotropin (PMSG, 5 IU). Forty-eight hours later, human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG) was administered via i.p. injection. Oocytes were collected 14-15 h after the hCG injection. The ampulla of the oviduct was punctured using a pre-warmed 1 ml syringe needle tip. The cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) were then released and gently drawn through two separate wash drops before being transferred to a fertilization drop for insemination. Male mice were euthanized and placed on ice. Both epididymides were promptly dissected out. Several incisions (2-3) were made in the cauda epididymis to allow the release of the dense, whitish semen. The tissue was then carefully transferred to an incubator to allow for sperm capacitation for 1 h. After the capacitation period, 2-3 μL of motile sperm, collected from the edge of the capacitation drop, were evenly introduced into the fertilization drop containing the COCs. The dish was promptly returned to the incubator for a 6-h fertilization period.

DNA extraction and methylation sequencing

Sperm and lung DNA were separately extracted using a DNA extraction kit (TIANGEN, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. DNA quality and concentration was assayed by NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA). We employed the EZ-DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research) to selectively convert non-methylated cytosine residues to uracil in 600 ng of genomic DNA that has been pre-treated with sodium bisulfite. Afterwards, we conducted a full-scale PCR using 1 μL of the bisulfite-converted DNA, and then carried out nested PCR on the resulting product. First, a total PCR was performed on 1 μL of the bisulfite-converted DNA. The PCR products were then diluted at a ratio of 1:20 for the nested PCR. Both the primary and nested PCR amplifications were executed with the following thermal cycling conditions: (1) Initial denaturation: 94°C for 2 min (2) Amplification cycles: 40 repetitions, including denaturation at 94°C for 25 s, annealing at 58°C for 1 min, and extension at 72°C for 2 min (3) Final extension: 70°C for 7 min. Next, 20 μL of the nested PCR products were combined with annealing buffer and streptavidin beads. After all the products were denatured and the pyrophosphate sequencing primers were successfully annealed, the final reaction was carried out. This reaction was also utilized for the analysis of CpG methylation.

The screening differentially methylated regions (DMRs) thresholds were set to meet these conditions as follows. The absolute value of the average methylation difference in the region was greater than or equal to 0.1. The number of CpG sites was greater than or equal to 3, and the length of the regional bases should be greater than or equal to 50 bp. The P-value of the difference in the methylation level was less than 0.05 after the statistical test and correction, and the P-value of the statistical test in the 2D Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) test method was less than 0.05.

Transcriptome sequencing

Lung and testis samples from male mice were lysed with Trizol reagent for further analysis. Sequencing libraries were generated using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina). The raw image data files obtained from high-throughput sequencing were further analyzed and converted into raw sequencing sequences after CASAVA base recognition. To quantify gene expression levels in each sample, the Htseq-count software was employed to calculate the Transcripts Per Million (TPM) values, which accurately reflected the expression levels of individual genes. Finally, bioinformatics analysis was conducted on the differentially expressed genes, and this analysis was based on the functions of the corresponding genes.

Histopathologic hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining

Tissue sections were initially immersed in a gradient solution. Subsequently, the tissues were immersed in xylene for permeabilization, and then the tissues after the completion of permeabilization were embedded in soft paraffin wax at 55°C. The embedded tissues were then sectioned, and the resulting sections were soaked in a cup filled with hematoxylin staining solution for 8 min. Following this, the sections were rinsed with distilled water for 5 min. Finally, the stained sections were sealed with xylene-neutral resin and scanned using a scanner for long-term preservation.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To measure cytokine levels, a mouse interleukin-6 (IL-6) ELISA kit and a mouse tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) ELISA kit (both from Byabscience, China) were employed. The assays were conducted strictly following the manufacturer's instructions. For cytokine detection, serum samples and supernatants of lung tissue homogenates were collected. The concentrations of cytokines were determined and expressed in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL).

Fe2+ and MDA detection

The determination of Fe2+ and MDA content was performed strictly following the product instructions of kits from Solarbio company. First, tissue or cell samples were disrupted. Subsequently, the supernatants obtained from the disrupted samples were used for the detection. The optical density (OD) values of each well were measured using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay at the corresponding wavelengths specified by the kit protocols.

Immunohistochemistry

Lung tissue sections underwent a series of pretreatment steps, including deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with the specific primary antibody overnight at 4°C. The following day, the sections were subjected to secondary antibody incubation, followed by 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) color development and hematoxylin counterstaining. Upon examination under a light microscope, the cell nuclei within the tissue appeared blue due to hematoxylin staining, while positive staining was manifested as a tan coloration of the cytoplasm.

Immunofluorescence

After deparaffinization and rehydration, 0.3% TritoX-100 was added dropwise to lung tissue sections and the tissue was permeabilized at room temperature for 15-20 min. Then, antigenic epitope repair was performed. To reduce non-specific binding, the tissue sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 60 min at room temperature. On the following day, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody in the dark at room temperature. After staining of nuclei with 4’,6 -diamidino-2- phenylindole (DAPI), the sample slides were subsequently blocked using glycerol gelatin blocking solution added dropwise to the sections. All the stained sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope, and the images were analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunoblotting

Protein samples were obtained by lysing tissue and cell samples with RIPA buffer and then subjected to immunoblotting experiments following the previously reported protocol [25]. Specifically, protein extracts (10-30 μg) were separated by gel electrophoresis through 12% SDS-PAGE gel and then transferred to a PVDF membrane. After blocked with 5% BSA for 1.5 h, the membrane was co-incubated with the specific primary antibody at 4°C overnight. Subsequently, the PVDF membrane was washed three times and then co-incubated with the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the immunoreactive protein signals were visualized by using a molecular imager. All results were analyzed by Image J software for protein intensities quantification.

Single-cell sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Single-cell libraries were constructed using the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform, followed by sequencing and bioinformatics data analysis using Seurat software. Cells were annotated based on known cell marker genes, and differentially expressed genes (DEGs, |log2FC|>1, adj.P < 0.05) for each cluster were identified using the FindMarkers function. For the same cell subpopulation across the three sample groups, the FindMarkers function in Seurat was used to screen for DEGs between groups. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the DEGs were performed using the clusterProfiler package, with a focus on the ferroptosis pathway, and P < 0.05 was set as the threshold for significant enrichment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0, and quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For data that conforms to normal distribution and exhibited homogeneity of variance, the student t-test was used to test the statistical differences between two sets of data, and one-way ANOVA followed by LSD is used to compare the differences between multiple sets of data. The qPCR results were analyzed using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Statistical significance was set at a P-value less than 0.05.

Result

Parental PS-NPs exposure caused lung injury in adult male offspring

In mice, spermatogonium cells require approximately 35 days to complete the differentiation and generate mature sperm [27]. To ensure that the effects of F1 offspring were derived from paternal germ cells exposed to PS-NPs, we established a sub-chronic PS-NPs exposure model in which male mice were treated with PS-NPs for 60 days. F1 offspring were originated from paternal germ cells exposed to PS-NPs, and F1 males were crossed with normal females to produce F2 offspring, and F2 male offspring were subsequently crossed with normal females to obtain F3 offspring (Fig. 1A).

Organ coefficients, as recommended by the American Society for Toxic Pathology (STP), were used to evaluate the general impact of treatment on organs [28]. While paternal PS-NPs exposure did not significantly alter the lung organ coefficient in female offspring (Fig. 1B), it led to a reduction in both lung organ coefficient and lung weight in F1 and F2 male offspring across two consecutive generations (Fig. 1C–E).

Histopathological analysis of lung tissue (H&E staining) further demonstrated abnormal lung morphology in F1 males from the exposed group, including alveolar collapse and septal thickening (Fig. 1F). Although alveolar collapse was also observed in F2 offspring, the severity was less pronounced (Fig. 1F). Notably, no obvious lung injury phenotype was transmitted to the F3 generation based on histological examination (Fig. 1F). Moreover, in PS-NPs-exposed F0 males, red-fluorescence-labeled PS-NPs were detected within the epididymis (Fig. 1G). Sperm quality analysis indeed revealed that PS-NP-treated mice exhibited a significant decrease in both sperm motility and the percentage of progressive motile sperm (Fig. 1H).

Paternal PS-NPs exposure altered offspring DNA methylation patterns and triggered ferroptosis signaling

To investigate the mechanism underlying paternal PS-NPs-induced lung injury in F1 and F2 male offspring, we performed integrated WGBS and transcriptome analyses on lung tissues from both F1 and F2 generations. WGBS results revealed that paternal PS-NPs exposure led to a significant increase in CG methylation, the primary form of DNA methylation, in lung tissues of F1 and F2 males compared with controls (Fig. 2A). In F1 males from the exposed group, the average CpG methylation was elevated across genomic regions, including gene bodies, the 2 kb upstream of transcription start sites, and the 2 kb downstream of transcription termination sites, indicating a global trend of DNA hypermethylation (Fig. 2B). A similar hypermethylation trend was observed in the lungs of F2 male offspring (Fig. 2C).

Subsequent KEGG enrichment analysis of common DMGs shared by the F1 and F2 generations revealed significant associations with ferroptosis signal (Fig. 2D and E).

To elucidate the association between DMRs in the lungs of the F1 generation and those of the F2 generation, a comparative localization and intersection diagram of DMRs between the two generations was constructed (Fig. 2F). The results further demonstrated that all chromosomes analyzed from F1 and F2 lungs showed consistent trends in DMR changes. To further validate the expression of the ferroptosis pathway in the lungs of F1 and F2 offspring, Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) based on transcriptomic data was performed, followed by partial correlation analysis of GSVA scores. The results revealed that the GSVA scores of the ferroptosis pathway were significantly higher in both F1 and F2 offspring of the PS-NPs-exposed group compared to the control group. These findings suggest that the ferroptosis pathway is likely activated in the lungs of F1 and F2 offspring following paternal PS-NPs exposure (Fig. 2G). Consistent with this, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of transcriptomic data also indicated activation of the ferroptosis pathway in the lungs of offspring (Fig. 2H). These results demonstrate that paternal PS-NPs exposure can alter DNA methylation patterns in F1 and F2 male offspring, with ferroptosis signaling potentially serving as one of the key regulatory mechanisms involved.

Alveolar epithelial cells were vulnerable to ferroptosis in offspring from paternal PS-NPs exposure

To validate the multi-omics findings, we measured the Fe2+ content in the lungs of F1 and F2 males from the parental PS-NPs exposure group. The results showed a significant increase of Fe2+ level in exposed group (Fig. 3A). Upon activation of ferroptosis-related signaling pathways, cells undergo a series of physiological changes that promote the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) into the surrounding tissue microenvironment, thereby influencing adjacent tissues [29]. Accordingly, TNF-α levels in lung homogenates from male offspring were elevated in the PS-NPs-exposed group compared with controls l (Fig. 3B). Similarly, serum IL-6 levels were also increased in male offspring following paternal PS-NPs exposure (Fig. 3C).

We next examined the expression of key ferroptosis regulators, Gpx4 and Acsl4, in mouse lung tissue. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the protein expression level of GPX4 was significantly lower in the lungs of F1 and F2 males from the exposed group, whereas ACSL4 protein expression level showed no significant change (Fig. 3D–G). These data provided compelling evidence that parental PS-NPs exposure can trigger ferroptosis in the lungs of F1 and F2 males, thereby contributing to lung injury.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, identifying the key effector cells was essential. We first analyzed the relative expression of classical ferroptosis biomarkers (Gpx4, Acsl4, Tfrc) across lung cell types using the single-cell database Tabula Muris [30]. The results showed that all three biomarkers were co-located in a group of cells annotated as epithelial cells (Fig. 3H). In the lung, alveolar and tracheal epithelial cells are the two major epithelial populations with significant numbers and functions. Single-cell analysis and transcriptomic analysis revealed a significant decrease in alveolar epithelial cell markers (Fig. 3I & Fig. S2A), indicating that alveolar epithelial cells may be the main type of damaged cells. Finally, paternal PS-NPs exposure significantly reduced both the number (identified by Spc marker) and proliferative capacity (identified by Ki-67 marker) of alveolar epithelial cells in the lungs of offspring (Fig. 3J–L). Collectively, these findings strongly suggest that alveolar epithelial cells are vulnerable to ferroptosis in offspring following paternal exposure to PS-NPs.

Paternal PS-NPs exposure induced BPD-like injury and ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells of offspring

The alveolar epithelial cells comprise type I (AT1) and type II (AT2) alveolar epithelial cells with AT1 cells predominantly differentiating from AT2 cells [31]. Damage to AT2 can not only impair the pulmonary surfactant secretion but also disrupt their differentiation function. Our single-cell RNA sequencing data revealed that epithelial cells are the cell type with the highest ferroptosis score (Fig. 4A) and paternal nanoparticle exposure leads to a reduction in AT2 numbers (Fig. 4B and C). Further KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes in epithelial cells also indicated a significant enrichment of the ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 4 D). As expected, immunofluorescence data confirmed that parental PS-NPs exposure can decrease the number of AT1 cells in F1 (Fig. 4E) and F2 (Fig. 4E), along with a significant reduction in the ratio of AT1 cells to the total cells (Fig. 4G). In male F1 and F2 offspring, major damage to AT2 cells was observed, as evidenced by decreased ratios of AT2 cells to the total cell population (Fig. 4F), and AT1/AT2 cell ratios (Fig. 4H). Pulmonary function tests on the offspring mice demonstrated that the main pulmonary function indices were significantly impaired in the offspring exposed to nanoparticles, including reduced lung capacity and diminished ventilatory function (Table 1). Based on the integrated analysis of pathological changes, pulmonary dysfunction, and the reduced number of AT2 cells, we proposed that paternal exposure to PS-NPs induces BPD-like injury in offspring.

Multi-omics indicated hypermethylation of FtMt as a potential mechanism for ferroptosis in offspring

Given the negative correlation between promoter methylation and gene transcription, we focused on chromosome 18, which exhibited the highest methylation rate (Fig. S2C), and identified potential ferroptosis driver genes based on high promoter methylation and low transcriptional expression in the F1 and F2 generations. The integrated analysis of WGBS and transcriptome data indicated that ferroptosis was a key pathway enriched with DMR-related genes. The Sankey diagram, which integrated comprehensive analysis of WGBS and transcriptome data, emphasized that FtMt was a key gene in the ferroptosis pathway of lung cells following paternal PS-NPs exposure (Fig. S2D). Analysis of the FtMt promoter using MethPrimer software revealed one CpG island, indicating that this region is amenable to methylation (Fig. S2E). FtMt is a crucial regulator of intracellular iron metabolism, with both ferrous and ferric iron-binding activities, and is involved in cellular iron-chelation processes [33]. Consistent with this, GSEA analysis of F1 and F2 lung transcriptomes showed a significant inhibition of the iron ion chelation process in GO enrichment (Fig. 5A and B).

Subsequently, whole-genome DNA methylation analysis of F1 and F2 male mouse lungs revealed that the FtMt promoter contained highly methylated sites (Fig. 5C and D). Correspondingly, FtMt mRNA levels in the lungs of F1 and F2 generations were significantly decreased compared to the control group (Fig. 5E). This transcriptional suppression was confirmed at the protein level, with Western blot analysis showing markedly reduced FtMt expression in F1 and F2 lung tissues (Fig. 5F and G).

To investigate the intergenerational and transgenerational persistence of epigenetic alterations, we examined whether DNA methylation changes in sperm of PS-NPs-exposed fathers were conserved in the lungs of their offspring. Reduced Representation Bisulfite Sequencing (RRBS) was performed on sperm from the F0 sperm, and Circos software was used to visualize chromosome methylation patterns in the sperm. The results revealed abnormal genome-wide DNA methylation in F0 sperm from paternal PS-NPs-exposed mice, characterized predominantly by hypermethylation, and FtMt promoter contained some highly methylated sites (Fig. 5H). KEGG enrichment analysis of hypermethylated genes in promoter regions showed significant involvement in ferroptosis and RNA degradation pathways (Fig. 5I). Compared with controls, sperm from exposed F0 males showed elevated methylation levels within gene bodies, as well as in regions 2 kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) and 2 kb downstream of the TTS (Fig. 5J). Further analysis of the RRBS data identified increased methylation at specific differential methylation regions within the promoter of the FtMt gene in F0 sperm from the exposed group (Fig. 5K). Notably, sites 4 and 5 of the CpG island in the FtMt promoter were also hypermethylated in F1 sperm from the exposed lineage (Fig. 5L). Consistent with these epigenetic changes, FtMt mRNA levels were significantly downregulated in testicular tissues of both F0 and F1 generations compared with controls (Fig. 5M).

Collectively, these results indicate that DNA hypermethylation is responsible for the persistent downregulation of FtMt across generations. The consistency between mRNA and protein levels across different generations provides strong evidence that DNA hypermethylation plays a key role in regulating FtMt expression.

Discussion

The intergenerational and transgenerational effects of NPs, particularly in mammalian models, remain poorly understood, with limited convincing evidence available to date. In this study, we demonstrated that paternal exposure to PS-NPs, spanning two spermatogenic cycles, led to BPD-like lung injury in F1 and F2 male mice. Further study revealed that this injury was closely associated with ferroptosis, driven by hypermethylation of the FtMt. The ferroptosis triggered by parental PS-NPs exposure directly reduced the number of AT1 and AT2 cells, resulting in disrupting the normal lung development and function. Mechanistically, Dnmt3a-mediated hypermethylation of the FtMt promoter suppressed FtMt expression and impaired cellular iron chelation, leading to intracellular iron overload. This iron overload subsequently promoted ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells (Fig. 9). Our findings provide an important mechanistic basis for future measures aimed at reducing NPs exposure and developing protective strategies during critical windows of spermatogenesis.

Nanoplastics exhibit significant reproductive toxicity, which can reduce sperm motility, decrease sperm count, and lower sex hormone levels [34]. Additionally, a few studies have found that they may also affect offspring development [35]. Even among nanoplastics, toxicity varies markedly with particle diameter. Ultra-small nanoplastics (≤50 nm) exhibit stronger cellular uptake efficiency, readily accumulate in mitochondria [36]. Larger nanoplastics (100-500 nm) are more likely to induce endoplasmic reticulum stress and inflammatory responses rather than predominant mitochondrial damage and epigenetic modification [36]. In our study, we selected typical polystyrene nanoplastics to explore germline-mediated transgenerational pulmonary toxicity. Future work will compare the epigenetic hazards of different-sized nanoplastics, which can help clarify the size-dependent threshold of intergenerational toxic risk.

Lung development commenced during the early embryonic stages [37], making it vulnerable to the long-term impact of adverse parental factors even after maturation. In this study, we found that paternal exposure to PS-NPs can induce BPD-like lung injury in F1 and F2 male mice. Although existing research has indicated that the potential lung hazards of PS-NPs, understanding the health effects of paternal PS-NPs exposure on offspring offers a critical foundation for evaluating the risks of PS-NPs pollution in key developmental windows. Our study demonstrated that male offspring mice showed consistent decreases in lung weight and organ coefficients, and lung injury exhibited intergenerational and transgenerational effects, manifesting from the F1 to the F2 generations. In contrast, female offspring mice did not have such phenotypic alterations, indicating a potential sex difference in the damage effects. Similar sex-dimorphic effects have been reported in other intergenerational toxicity studies. For instance, paternal exposure to inorganic arsenic has been shown to induce sex-specific metabolic alterations in F1 offspring [38], and prenatal PS-NPs exposure was found to cause sex-dependent intestinal toxicity in offspring [39]. Several plausible mechanisms may explain these sex-specific responses. Incomplete X-Chromosome Inactivation (XCI) in female consistent with recent studies on sex-biased respiratory developmental toxicity, mouse AT2 cells exhibit incomplete X chromosome inactivation, with approximately 68% of X-linked genes escaping transcriptional silencing in female lung tissue [40]. Moreover, females generally have lower total body iron content and smaller labile iron pools than males, due to estrogen-mediated regulation [41]. This means that even if FtMt is partially methylated, female lung tissue, equipped with a stronger “iron buffering system” can still maintain free Fe2+ levels below the ferroptosis threshold. These proposed mechanisms need further investigation to be validated.

Environmental pollutants are known to regulate gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms such as DNA methylation, a key process in mammalian transgenerational inheritance. In this study, paternal exposure to PS-NPs induced genome-wide hypermethylation in F1 and F2 offspring, suggesting that differentially methylated genes may be transmitted across generations and alter gene expression profiles. Transcriptomic analysis revealed activation of ferroptosis-related pathways in the lungs of both F1 and F2 males. Consistent with this, we observed decreased GPX4 expression and elevated Fe2+ levels, confirming typical ferroptosis features in the exposed offspring. These findings establish a novel connection between paternal PS-NPs exposure, lung injury, and ferroptosis. Previous research has reported that PS-NPs can initiate the production of ROS, increase iron ion levels, and promote ferroptosis via the Fenton reaction, a vicious cycle implicated in lung injury [42]. In bronchial epithelial cells, PS-NPs-induced ferroptosis primarily occurs through activation of the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway [42]. Notably, inhibiting ferroptosis can effectively reduce the damage of BPD [43], and ferroptosis also contributes to other lung diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [44]. In a cigarette smoke-induced mouse model of COPD, silencing the demethylase TET2 enhanced Gpx4 promoter methylation, leading to cigarette-smoke induced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis in airway epithelial cells [45]. Additionally, previous studies have shown that exposure to NPs can lead to lung injury through oxidative stress and inflammation [46]. In this study, serum IL-6 and lung Tnf-α were both elevated. Emerging evidence suggests that certain regulated cell death (RCD) pathways are interconnected through damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), forming a self-amplifying necroinflammatory circuit that exacerbates tissue injury [47]. The cytokine analysis in our study demonstrated that PS-NPs exposure promoted the release of Tnf-α in the lungs of offspring. As a typical necrotic trigger factor, the rise of Tnf-α suggested that ferroptosis may initiate other necrotic RCD pathways, potentially acting as part of a positive feedback loop in pathogenic injury. Future studies should explore the crosstalk between ferroptosis and other injury mechanisms to fully elucidate the pathophysiology of NP-induced lung injury.

Our multi-omics data indicate that lung epithelial cells are particularly vulnerable to ferroptosis. The findings were similar to previous reports that PS-NPs exposure can induce ferroptosis in bronchial epithelial cells, accompanied by lymphocyte inflammation around blood vessels in the bronchiolar region, as revealed by H&E staining [42]. Given that ferroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells may contribute to lung dysfunction in conditions such as pulmonary fibrosis [48], understanding its underlying mechanisms was crucial. Single cell sequencing studies have identified AT2 as key regulators of alveolar epithelial repair, owing to their capacity for self-renewal and differentiation into AT1 cells [49]. Our study found that parental exposure to PS-NPs significantly reduced the numbers of both AT2 and AT1 in the lungs of F1 and F2 offspring mice. The precise mechanisms linking intracellular iron homeostasis to lung function remain to be fully elucidated. Ferritin, an iron-storage protein, has been implicated in both lung and airway development. In animal models, iron chelation in embryonic mouse lung buds led to significant reductions in vascular branching and airway development [50]. Conversely, ferritin can also safeguard lung function through its antioxidant properties [51]. Our study identified that FtMt serves as a key molecule driver of lung ferroptosis. FtMt, a functional ferritin, stored ferrous ions within cellular mitochondria and precisely regulated mitochondrial free iron levels, thereby influencing iron metabolism [52]. Previous studies have reported its critical role in male reproduction, contributing to sperm epididymal maturation and male fertility [53]. These findings partially elucidated the sex-specific differences in the ferroptosis pathway regulated by the FtMt in male and female offspring with lung injury. On the other hand, upregulated inflammatory factors disrupted lung iron homeostasis via ferritin, promoting further free iron accumulation. Our findings revealed that the hypermethylation of FtMt led to decreased transcription and protein expression, resulting in impaired intracellular iron chelation. These findings highlight the intricate interplay between iron metabolism, ferroptosis, and alveolar maintenance, offering a mechanistic basis for understanding the long-term respiratory consequences of paternal PS-NPs exposure.

Ferroptosis, an iron-mediated cell death pathway, is known to be autophagy-dependent [54]. This process, termed ferritinophagy, involves autophagic degradation of ferritin, mediated by the selective cargo receptor nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (Ncoa4), leading to intracellular iron overload and subsequent Fenton reaction-driven oxidative damage [55]. Our study demonstrated that parental exposure to PS-NPs significantly increased Ncoa4 levels while decreasing ferritin heavy chain 1 (Fth1) expression in offspring, suggesting the involvement of ferritinophagy in the offspring lung epithelial cells. While ferritinophagy was widely recognized as an upstream regulatory mechanism of ferroptosis, the potential reverse regulation between ferroptosis and ferritinophagy remains unclear. Previous studies reported that knocking down Ncoa4 attenuated excessive ferroptosis induced by Gpx4 knockdown in bronchial epithelial cells (HBE) exposed to cigarette smoke extract (CSE), indicating a functional interplay between Gpx4 and Ncoa4 in regulating ferroptosis [44]. In our study, adding autophagy inhibitors to FtMt-knockdown cell lines partially restored ferroptosis levels. However, the precise regulatory relationship and underlying mechanisms connecting FtMt and Ncoa4 require further investigation.

Ferroptosis, as a novel form of cell death, exhibits highly selective destructive effects on alveolar epithelial cells. These cells are not only essential for the synthesis and secretion of pulmonary surfactant, but the abnormal activation of ferroptosis pathways can directly lead to dysfunction, apoptosis, and impaired regenerative capacity of alveolar epithelial cells. The direct consequences include alveolarization arrest and abnormal pulmonary vascular development, which constitute the typical pathological basis of chronic lung diseases such as BPD. More importantly, this pro-ferroptotic microenvironment “programmed” by early-life exposure and the resulting damaged alveolar structure may persist throughout the offspring's entire lifespan, thereby significantly amplifying their susceptibility to adult-onset diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Future study should clarify the specific pathways and accumulation patterns of transgenerational transmission for microplastics of different sizes, concentrations, and types, as well as their differential impacts on lung iron metabolism and the expression of key regulators of ferroptosis in offspring. Additionally, it is necessary to explore early intervention strategies, such as iron chelators, to evaluate their potential in reversing pulmonary damage induced by microplastics in offspring and to provide feasible solutions for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that paternal exposure to PS-NPs induces BPD-like lung injury in male F1 and F2 offspring. Mechanistically, we identify ferroptosis as a central pathway driving this intergenerational damage. The process involves a reduction in alveolar AT2 cell numbers, contributing to impaired lung development. Further analysis revealed that DNA methyltransferase Dnmt3a-mediated hypermethylation of the FtMt promoter is a critical upstream event. This epigenetic silencing suppresses FtMt expression, leading to mitochondrial iron overload and the cytosolic release of free iron. These disturbances establish a positive feedback loop that exacerbates ferroptosis. Our findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the intergenerational and transgenerational lung toxicity of paternal PS-NPs exposure and offer important evidence for potential strategies to prevent BP.
