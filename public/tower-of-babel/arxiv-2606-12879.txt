Diffusion-Network Alignment: An Efficient Algorithm and Explicit Probability Bounds
Ziao Wang, Lei Ying

Abstract
This paper studies a variation of the classic network alignment problem, named diffusion–network alignment. The goal is to align the vertices of a rooted diffusion tree to the vertices of a network, where the diffusion tree could be from a communication trace or contact tracing, and the network could be an online or offline social network. Different from the classic network alignment where both networks are fully observed, this model captures the information asymmetry of two networks. To solve this problem, this paper presents an efficient algorithm based on tree correlation tests to extract alignment information from local neighborhoods. We analyze the performance of the algorithm in the sparse graph regime and show that with high probability, all matched pairs are correct. Furthermore, for each vertex on the diffusion tree, this paper establishes an explicit lower bound on the probability that the vertex is correctly matched. These lower bounds are depth-dependent and increase as vertices get closer to the root.



I
Introduction

Network alignment.

The network alignment (or graph matching) problem refers to the problem of recovering the hidden correspondence between the vertices of two correlated graphs.
A primary motivation for this problem comes from social network deanonymization
[
37
,
28
]
, where one seeks to match the users of an anonymized social network (e.g. Twitter) to a correlated reference network in which user identities are known (e.g. Facebook).
Related alignment problems also arise in areas such as bioinformatics
[
43
]
and natural language processing
[
21
]
.

A classical mathematical model for network alignment is the correlated Erdős–Rényi (ER) graph pair model
[
40
]
. In this model, a base ER graph is first sampled on a latent vertex set, and two observed graphs are then generated by independently subsampling the edges of this base graph. As a result, each observed graph is an ER graph, while edges of the two observed graphs are correlated through the shared base graph and the latent vertex correspondence. This goal is to find the latent vertex correspondence (vertex alignment) based on the topology of the two observed graphs.
A sequence of works has characterized the information-theoretic limits of this problem across different graph density regimes
[
8
,
9
,
46
,
11
]
.
The other sequence of works studies the problem from an algorithmic perspective, and identifies a computational threshold for the sparse graph regime. In particular, when the edge correlation between the two graphs is above
\sqrt{\alpha}
, where
\alpha\approx 0.338
is the Otter’s constant
[
39
]
, polynomial-time algorithms are known to achieve reliable recovery
[
34
,
17
,
19
]
. Below this threshold,
[
31
]
provides evidence for the non-existence of such polynomial-time algorithms.

Diffusion-network alignment.

In the aforementioned line of works, a common assumption is that both graphs are
fully observable
. This paper studies a variation of the problem where one of the networks is a rooted diffusion tree. This rooted diffusion tree could be a communication trace, a trace from epidemic contact tracing, blockchain transition history.
This variation captures the information asymmetry of the network alignment problem. This paper aims at answering the following question: can one align the vertices of an observed diffusion tree with those of a correlated reference network?

Motivated by the above question, we consider the following
diffusion-network alignment
model. We consider two networks generated from the correlated Erdős–Rényi graph pair model. To model the information diffusion process on the first network, we employ the Independent Cascade (IC) Model, a classic model first proposed in
[
27
]
for information diffusion on networks. In this model, information propagates from a source node through repeated, probabilistic activations of neighboring nodes. The activated vertices, together with the edges through which they are activated, form a tree-structured diffusion cascade. We observe only this diffusion tree from the first network, while the second network is fully observed. The goal is to recover the correspondence between the vertices of the diffusion tree and those of the second network. In this work, we study diffusion–network alignment from an algorithmic perspective, and focus on studying efficient algorithms for this problem.

Relation to classic network alignment.
Diffusion–network alignment is closely related to classical network alignment, since the diffusion tree is sampled from the first network and inherits its structural information, which is correlated with the second network we observe. Therefore, it is natural to consider whether existing algorithmic approaches for network alignment can be adapted to diffusion-network alignment. In the following, we focus on discussing two algorithmic approaches that are known to achieve reliable recovery at the computational threshold
\sqrt{\alpha}
, while deferring a broader review of the literature to Appendix
A
.

The first approach is
subgraph counting

[
34
]
, which constructs vertex feature vectors by aggregating weighted counts of specific subgraph structures in the network and matches the vertices based on the similarity between their feature vectors. However, this approach assumes access to the entire network to perform the weighted counts and is therefore incompatible with diffusion network alignment, where the observation of the first network is restricted to a small subset of vertices.

The second approach is known as
tree correlation testing

[
17
,
19
,
32
]
, which reduces the task of matching two vertices to a hypothesis test of whether their tree-like local neighborhoods are generated independently or correlatedly. In diffusion–network alignment, the diffusion tree retains a tree-structured subgraph of the neighborhood of the diffusion source. This makes tree correlation testing a natural choice for the diffusion setting, as it operates directly on tree-like observations and does not require access to the entire graph.
In particular, the tree correlation testing approach developed in
[
32
]
can be adapted to match the root of the diffusion tree.
However, this direct application does not extend to non-root vertices in the diffusion tree.

This motivates our proposed algorithm, which exploits the fact that the diffusion cascade provides a natural structure for information propagation on the diffusion tree. The algorithm proceeds in two passes. In the upward pass, it applies tree correlation tests to collect local matching information starting from the bottom of the diffusion tree, and propagates this information upward toward the root. In the downward pass, the algorithm starts from the root and propagates the accumulated matching information toward the leaves. This two-pass procedure allows us to go beyond the direct application of existing tree correlation tests. It provides guarantees for matching non-root vertices in the diffusion tree, and also yields a stronger guarantee for matching the root.

Our results.

In this work, we introduce an efficient algorithm for the diffusion-network alignment problem, building on the approach motivated above. We analyze the proposed algorithm in the constant-degree graph regime and provide performance guarantees from two perspectives.

•

Depth-dependent probability bounds for correct matchings:

Under the assumption that the effective correlation between the diffusion tree and the second network is above
\sqrt{\alpha}
, we derive depth-dependent lower bounds on the probability of correct matching for each individual vertex in the diffusion tree. These depth-dependent lower bounds are constants bounded away from zero and become larger for vertices closer to the root.

•

Global correctness property:

We also show that, with high probability, the proposed algorithm is globally correct in the sense that for every vertex in the diffusion tree, it either outputs the true corresponding vertex or outputs no match at all.

Comparison to theoretical guarantees for constant-degree network alignment.

In the literature for network alignment in the constant-degree regime, algorithms are often evaluated in terms of achieving
partial recovery
, i.e., a non-vanishing fraction of vertices should be correctly aligned.
However, partial recovery may not always be possible for the diffusion-network alignment problem. For example, the diffusion process may produce a cascade containing only the root vertex, which occurs with a non-vanishing probability. Therefore, high-probability guarantees for recovering a positive fraction of the vertices are not possible. For this reason, this paper considers vertex-wise guarantees: we provide
explicit
constant lower bounds on the success probability for matching each individual vertex, conditioned on its depth in the diffusion tree, and we show that the algorithm produces no incorrect matchings with high probability. This notion provides a performance guarantee that is comparable in spirit to partial recovery, while being adapted to the diffusion–network alignment problem.

II
Model

In this section, we introduce the proposed diffusion-network alignment model.

Correlated Erdős–Rényi Graph Pair Model.

Let
n
be a positive integer,
\lambda
and
s
be two positive real numbers with
s\in(0,1]
. Throughout this paper, we assume that both
\lambda
and
s
are constant independent of
n
. The correlated Erdős–Rényi graph pair model
\mathrm{CER}(n,\lambda,s)
is defined as follows: We first sample a base graph
{\mathcal{G}}\sim\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{\lambda}{ns})
, where
{\mathcal{G}}
has
n
vertices with indices from
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and an edge is generated between each pair of vertices independently with probability
\frac{\lambda}{ns}
.
Given the base graph
{\mathcal{G}}
, the graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
is obtained by subsampling each edge in
{\mathcal{G}}
independently with probability
s
. The same subsampling process is then independently repeated to generate
{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
.
Then a permutation
\pi^{*}:[n]\rightarrow[n]
is chosen uniformly at random and used to permute the vertex labels in
{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
to obtain the graph
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
. The model finally outputs the two correlated graphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
both with marginal distribution
\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{\lambda}{n})
, and we denote
({\mathcal{G}}_{1},\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2})\sim\mathrm{CER}(n,\lambda,s)
.

Independent Cascade (IC) Model.

Given the graph pair
({\mathcal{G}}_{1},\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2})\sim\mathrm{CER}(n,\lambda,s)
,
we use the IC model to simulate a diffusion process that starts at the vertex
1
in the graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
.
At time step
0
, the vertex
1
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
becomes active (marked red in Figure
1
), indicating that it is the source of diffusion, while all other vertices are inactive (marked black in Figure
1
). The model proceeds in discrete time steps.
At each time step
k\geq 1
, for each edge
(u,v)
such that
u
is activated at time
k-1
and
v
is inactive, a single activation attempt is made along
(u,v)
, which succeeds independently with probability
q
. If at least one such attempt succeeds, vertex
v
becomes activated at time
k
. Here
q\in(0,1]
is a model parameter, and it is assumed to be a constant independent of
n
.
In Figure
1
, the edges with successful activation attempts are represented by blue arrows.
The process ends at the time step in which no new vertex is activated.

Diffusion Tree.

The IC model defined above is captured by the
diffusion tree
, denoted
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
. The diffusion tree
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
is rooted at vertex
1
, and for each
k\geq 1
, the set of vertices at depth
k
is exactly the set of vertices that are activated at the time step
k
of the IC process. For each non-root vertex in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
, its parent is set to be the vertex that activated it in the IC process. However, it is possible that a vertex has multiple active neighbors, and get activated by multiple of them in the same time step. For example, in Figure
1
, vertex
4
is activated by vertices
3
and
5
in time step
2
. To ensure the tree structure of
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
, we introduce a canonical order among the vertices at the same depth to select a unique parent for each vertex. The order is defined iteratively.
For each depth
k\geq 1
, a vertex at depth
k
has a higher order if its parent has a higher order among the vertices at depth
k-1
, and for multiple vertices with the same parent, the vertex with a smaller index has a higher order. In Figure
1
, vertices that appear further to the left in the diffusion tree correspond to higher canonical order. When a vertex is activated by multiple neighbors in the same time step, its unique parent is chosen to the one with the highest order among those vertices which activated it.

Diffusion-network Alignment.

In the diffusion-network alignment problem, we observe the diffusion tree
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
and the graph
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
, and have access to the model parameters
\lambda
,
s
and
q
. The goal is to find the corresponding vertex
\pi^{*}(u)
in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
for each vertex
u
in the observed diffusion tree
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
.

Notation.

Recall that the vertices of both graphs
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
are indexed by
[n]
. For each index
i\in[n]
, we use
i
to denote the vertex with index
i
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
(and also
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
), and use
\tilde{i}
to denote the vertex with index
i
in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
. Then following this notation, the vertex
i
in
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
corresponds to the vertex
\tilde{j}
in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}_{2}}
if
\pi^{*}(i)=j
. We use
V
(resp.
\tilde{V}
and

) to denote the set of vertices
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
(resp.
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
and
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
). For each

, we use

III
Preliminary: Tree Correlation Tests

Before presenting our algorithm for diffusion-network alignment, we first introduce the preliminaries of the tree correlation testing problem, which is known as a classic tool for network alignment in the sparse regime.

Poisson Galton–Watson trees.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The Galton–Watson branching tree with offspring distribution
{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda)
, denoted
{\mathrm{GW}}_{\lambda}
, is a distribution over unlabeled rooted trees. The tree has a distinguished vertex as its root.
Then iteratively for each vertex
v
in the tree, an independent random variable
X_{v}\sim{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda)
is sampled, representing the number of children attached to
v
.
For each
d\geq 0
, we use
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda}
to denote the depth-
d
truncation of
{\mathrm{GW}}_{\lambda}
, which is the distribution over the rooted trees obtained by generating a tree from
{\mathrm{GW}}_{\lambda}
and remove all the vertices with distance greater than
d
from the root. We define the extinction probability
p^{\mathrm{ext}}_{d,\lambda}
as the probability that there exist no vertices at distance
d
from the root under the law
{\mathrm{GW}}_{\lambda}
, and define
p^{\mathrm{ext}}_{\lambda}:=\lim_{d\rightarrow\infty}p^{\mathrm{ext}}_{d,\lambda}
.

Independent and correlated Galton–Watson tree distributions.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
s,\tilde{s}\in(0,1]
.
With the definition of
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda}
, we are now ready to define the independent tree pair distribution
\mathbb{Q}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
and the correlated tree pair distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
considered in the tree correlation test problem.

•

Independent tree pair distribution
\mathbb{Q}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
: Under this distribution, two unlabeled rooted trees
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
are sampled independently from distributions
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda s}
and
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda\tilde{s}}
respectively.

•

Correlated tree pair distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
:
Under this distribution, two correlated trees
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
with marginal distributions
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda s}
and
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda\tilde{s}}
are generated.
To generate the two correlated trees, an
intersection tree

{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
is first sampled from distribution
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda s\tilde{s}}
. Then for each
k\in\{0,\ldots,d-1\}
, we independently sample a random variable
X^{+}_{v}\sim{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda s(1-\tilde{s}))
for each vertex
v
at depth
k
in
{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
, and add
X_{v}
new children to
v
. For each newly added vertex
u
at depth
k
, a rooted tree
t_{u}
is independently sampled from the distribution
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d-k,\lambda s}
and attached to
u
as a subtree. After attaching
t_{u}
for each new vertex
u
, the resulting rooted tree is
{\mathcal{T}}
. To obtain tree
\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
, we again start with the intersection tree
{\mathcal{T}}^{*}
, and independently repeat the process of adding new children and attaching new subtrees, but instead with
X_{v}^{+}\sim{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda\tilde{s}(1-s))
and
t_{u}\sim{\mathrm{GW}}_{d-k,\lambda\tilde{s}}
. It can be readily seen from Poisson superposition that
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}
have marginal distributions
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda s}
and
{\mathrm{GW}}_{d,\lambda\tilde{s}}
respectively.

Likelihood ratio test.

In the tree correlation test problem, we are given two rooted trees
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
, and the goal is to distinguish the two hypotheses
H_{0}:({\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}})\sim\mathbb{Q}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
and
H_{1}:({\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}})\sim\mathbb{P}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}
.
The Neyman–Pearson Lemma
[
38
]
states that the
likelihood ratio test
is the most powerful test for hypothesis testing
[
30
]
. For the two trees
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
, their likelihood ratio is given by

L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}({\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}}):=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}({\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}})}{\mathbb{Q}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d}({\mathcal{T}},\tilde{\mathcal{T}})}.

(1)

The likelihood ratio for tree correlation tests has been studied in
[
32
]
, who derive a recursive formula that enables efficient computation of (
1
). Moreover, they show that, with an appropriately chosen threshold, the likelihood ratio test achieves a
vanishing Type I error
, while having a
non-vanishing power
. We state their recursive formula for the likelihood ratio in Appendix
J-A
and summarize the associated performance guarantees in Theorem
2
of Appendix
J-B
.

IV
Proposed Algorithm and Main Results

In this section, we present the proposed algorithm for diffusion-network alignment, and summarize the theoretical performance guarantees of the algorithm.

IV-A

Proposed algorithm for diffusion-network alignment

In sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs, the local neighborhood distribution converges to a Poisson Galton–Watson tree as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In the IC model, each vertex activates its neighbors independently with probability
q
, so the subtree rooted at a vertex
u
in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
can be regarded as a random subsample of its neighborhood in
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
. Consequently, this subtree retains the correlation with the local neighborhood of
\pi^{*}(u)
in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
.
Therefore, a natural approach to verify whether a vertex
u
should be matched to a vertex
\tilde{v}
is to apply the likelihood ratio test (
1
) to determine whether their subtree and neighborhood are correlated or independent. However, such local tests alone are insufficient for certifying the matchings, because neighborhoods of non-corresponding nodes are not necessarily independent, as they may partially overlap. In our approach, we therefore use likelihood ratio tests only as local evidence, and propagate this evidence upward and downward along the diffusion tree. Aggregating evidence through the propagation process allows the algorithm to better identify correct matches.

We now turn to formally describe the proposed algorithm. To this end, we first introduce a notation for the subtrees in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
and define the
breadth-first search (BFS) trees
in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}
. For a vertex
u
in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
and
d\geq 0
, we use

to denote the subtree rooted at
u
with depth up to
d
in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
. In other words,
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{IC}}_{u,d}
is the rooted tree obtained by removing all the vertices that has distance greater than
d
from
u
in the subtree rooted at
u
in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
. For example, in Figure
1
, the subtree
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathrm{IC}}_{2,1}
is the tree rooted at vertex
2
, with a single edge connecting to vertex

For two distinct vertices

and

in
\tilde{{\mathcal{G}}}_{2}

\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}^{\setminus\tilde{w}}_{\tilde{v},d}
is rooted at vertex
\tilde{v}
. For each
k\in[d]

k
in

in the graph

The proposed algorithm collects local evidence for matching by applying tree correlation tests to pairs of subtree

The proposed algorithm takes the model parameters

Recall that

For each vertex

The proposed algorithm is summarized in Algorithm

Upward pass:
The upward pass phase starts from the vertices at depth

of
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}
and moves towards the root, with iterations over empty depths in
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}

At the starting depth, the algorithm relies solely on Criterion 1 to match the vertices, since all matching candidate sets

are initialized as empty. A vertex
\tilde{v}\in\tilde{V}
is added to the matching candidate set

of

[
17

In this section, we sketch the proof of Theorem

The first ingredient is showing that the global correctness property holds with high probability. Importantly, this property remains valid even after conditioning on the event that a given vertex
v
appears at a specific depth
d
of
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}

1

The second ingredient is a per-vertex inclusion guarantee. Conditioning on the event that vertex
v
is at depth

of
{\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{IC}}}

we show that Algorithm
1
includes
\pi^{*}(v)
in the candidate seet

with probability at least
{p_{d^{\prime}}}-o(1)
, where
d^{\prime}=\min\{l^{+},{d_{\max}}-d\}
. This guarantee is formalized in Proposition
2

Together, these two ingredients yield the theorem as follows. The global correctness property ensures that, with high probability, each candidate set
{\mathcal{M}}_{u}
contains at most the true correspondence
\pi^{*}(u)
. On this event, the inclusion guarantee implies that
{\mathcal{M}}_{v}=\{\pi^{*}(v)\}
, meaning that vertex
v
is correctly matched. Applying this argument vertex-wise and combining the conditional and unconditional bounds gives the stated performance guarantees in Theorem

To prove the two propositions, it is important to introduce the notion of the

The live-edge graph fully captures the randomness in both

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we introduce the diffusion–network alignment problem, motivated by the information asymmetry in real-world networks. We formalize this problem under a probabilistic model that combines the correlated Erdős–Rényi graph pair model with an Independent Cascade diffusion process, capturing both structural correlation across networks and the randomness introduced by diffusion.

We propose a polynomial-time algorithm for diffusion–network alignment, and analyze its performance in the sparse regime. We provide performance guarantees for the algorithm from two perspectives. First, we established depth-dependent lower bounds on the probability of correctly matching each individual vertex in the diffusion tree. Second, we prove a global correctness guarantee, showing that with high probability all the vertex pairs matched by the algorithm are correct.

Several directions for future work on diffusion–network alignment remain open. One natural question is to characterize the optimal vertex-wise matching probabilities that can be achieved by polynomial-time algorithms under the global correctness requirement. Beyond efficient algorithms, it is also of interest to understand the information-theoretic limits of diffusion–network alignment.

5
,
1
]
. Recent studies go beyond cliques and consider planted subgraphs of more general structures
[
36
,
29
]
.

Despite the connections, these settings differ fundamentally from diffusion–network alignment in two respects. Firstly, although the diffusion tree is a subgraph of the underlying network
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
, the reference network
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}_{2}
we observe is only positively correlated with
{\mathcal{G}}_{1}
through a latent correspondence, rather than containing the diffusion tree as a subgraph. In contrast, both subgraph isomorphism and planted subgraph problems require the target subgraph structure to be strictly contained in the observed network. Second, the diffusion tree in diffusion-network alignment is generated by a random diffusion process on the latent network through the IC model. This is different from the setting where the queried or planted structure is deterministic or sampled as an induced subgraph.

,
{\mathcal{T}}_{k}
,
{\tilde{{\mathcal{T}}}}_{k}
are all trivial trees with only a root node and no edges. So
{\mathcal{E}}_{0}
is a trivial event that always holds. Then Lemma
9
implies that

{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=0}^{l^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{k})\geq{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=1}^{l^{\prime}}({\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\cap\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{F}}^{*}_{k})).

To bound this probability, we additionally introduce a sequence of events that controls the number of vertices at each level of the two graphs
{\mathcal{H}}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}
. For each
k\in[d]]
, let

{\mathcal{I}}_{k}=\{|\bar{H}_{k}|\leq(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{k}\log n\},

and we will instead bound
{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=1}^{l^{\prime}}{\mathcal{J}}_{k})
,
where
{\mathcal{J}}_{k}:={\mathcal{A}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{B}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\cap\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{F}}^{*}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
.
By the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=1}^{l^{\prime}}{\mathcal{J}}_{k})

\displaystyle\geq{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})-\sum_{k=2}^{l^{\prime}}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}).

(55)

So it suffices to bound
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})
and each term in the summation.

Bounding
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})
.
Notice that

{\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{c},{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{c},{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{c},{\mathcal{F}}_{1},\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}_{1}
are all trivial events, since
{\mathcal{H}}_{0}
and
\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}_{0}
are both trivial trees that only has a root.
The vertices in
\bar{H}_{1}
are the neighbors of
u
in the union graph
\bar{{\mathcal{G}}}\setminus\bar{N}^{\uparrow}
, and therefore
|\bar{H}_{1}|\sim{\mathrm{Binom}}(n-1-|N^{\uparrow}|,{\bar{\lambda}}/n)
, where
{\bar{\lambda}}=\lambda+\lambda s-\lambda sq
. By the Chernoff–Hoeffding bound (see Lemma
13
) we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Binom}}(n,{\bar{\lambda}}/n)\geq 2{\bar{\lambda}}\log n)\leq n^{-{\bar{\lambda}}}.

Given event
{\mathcal{I}}_{1}
, there are at most
4\bar{\lambda}\log^{2}n
vertex pairs within
\bar{H}_{1}
. The probability of having an edge between each pair is
O(n^{-1})
. Therefore,

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{1}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}{\mathcal{I}}_{1})\leq 4\bar{\lambda}\log^{2}n\cdot O(n^{-1})=\tilde{O}(n^{-1}).

(56)

To bound the probability of event
{\mathcal{F}}_{1}^{c}
, we need to construct the coupling between
(d_{u},\tilde{d}_{u},d^{*}_{u})
and
(c_{r},\tilde{c}_{r},c^{*}_{r})
.

From the definition of correlated Erdős–Rényi graph pair model, we have

(d_{u},\tilde{d}_{u},d^{*}_{u},n-1-|N^{\uparrow}|-d_{u}-\tilde{d}_{u}-d^{*}_{u})\sim\mathrm{Multi}(n-1-|N^{\uparrow}|,\lambda q(1-s)/n,\lambda(1-qs),\lambda qs/n,1-{\bar{\lambda}}/n).

Meanwhile, the number of children of the root in
{\mathcal{T}}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{T}}
has distribution

(c_{r},\tilde{c}_{r},c^{*}_{r})\sim{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda q(1-s))\otimes{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda(1-qs))\otimes{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda sq).

An upper bound for the total variation distance between multinomial distributions and independent Poisson distributions is provided in Lemma
16
in Appendix
I
.
By Lemma
16
, we can couple
(d_{u},\tilde{d}_{u},d^{*}_{u})
with
(c_{r},\tilde{c}_{r},c^{*}_{r})
such that

{\mathsf{P}}((d_{u},\tilde{d}_{u},d^{*}_{u})=(c_{r},\tilde{c}_{r},c^{*}_{r}))\geq 1-O\left(\frac{|N^{\uparrow}|+\log^{2}n}{n}\right)\geq 1-O(n^{-\gamma}).

Under this coupling, we have
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{1})\leq O(n^{-\gamma})
. Putting these together yields

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq 1-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{c})

\displaystyle\geq 1-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{c})-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq 1-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{1}|{\mathcal{I}}_{1})-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{I}}_{1}^{c})-{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{1})

\displaystyle\geq 1-O(n^{-\gamma}).

(57)

Bounding
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})
.
Notice that event
\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}
implies that
|\bar{H}_{h}|\leq(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{h}\log n
for each
h\in[k-1]
. Because
k\leq l^{\prime}=o(\log n)
, we have
\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}|H_{h}|\leq\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{h}\log n=O(n^{\gamma/4})
. This further implies
|H_{k-1}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
,
|\tilde{H}_{k-1}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
,
|H^{*}_{k-1}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
,
|\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}\tilde{H}_{h}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
and
|\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}H_{h}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
, since all these sets are subsets of
\cup_{h=1}^{k-1}H_{h}
. Then by union bound, we have

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{C}}_{k}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\leq\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}}{n}\cdot(|H_{k-1}|+|H_{k-1}^{*}|)\cdot|\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}\tilde{H}_{h}|=O(n^{\gamma/2-1}),

(58)

and

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{D}}_{k}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\leq\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}}{n}\cdot(|\tilde{H}_{k-1}|+|H_{k-1}^{*}|)\cdot|\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}H_{h}|=O(n^{\gamma/2-1}).

(59)

Events
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}
and
\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}
rule out the possible ability that any
j\in H^{*}_{k-1}
is connected to any vertices include in
\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}\bar{H}_{h}
when growing its neighborhood. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left(d_{j},\tilde{d}_{j},d^{*}_{j},n-|N^{\uparrow}|-\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}|H_{h}|-d_{j}-\tilde{d}_{j}-d^{*}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\sim\mathrm{Multi}\left(n-|N^{\uparrow}|-\sum_{h=0}^{k-1}|H_{h}|,\lambda q(1-s)/n,\lambda(1-qs),\lambda qs/n,1-{\bar{\lambda}}/n\right).

Then by Lemma
16
, we can couple
(d_{j},\tilde{d}_{j},d^{*}_{j})
with
(c_{\phi_{k-1}(j)},\tilde{c}_{\phi_{k-1}(j)},c^{*}_{\phi_{k-1}(j)})
such that

{\mathsf{P}}((d_{j},\tilde{d}_{j},d^{*}_{j})=(c_{\phi_{k-1}(j)},\tilde{c}_{\phi_{k-1}(j)},c^{*}_{\phi_{k-1}(j)}))\geq 1-O\left(\frac{|n-2n^{1-\gamma}|}{n}\right)\geq 1-O(n^{-\gamma}),

where the penultimate inequality follows by the assumption that
|N^{\uparrow}|\leq n^{1-\gamma}
. Because
|H^{*}_{k-1}|=O(n^{-\gamma/4})
, we can use union bound to get

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})=O(n^{-3\gamma/4}).

(60)

For each
j\in H_{k-1}
, we have

d_{j}\sim{\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n-1-|N^{\uparrow}|-\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}|H_{h}|,\lambda q(1-s)\right).

Recall that
c_{\phi_{k-1}(j)}\sim{\mathrm{Poi}}(\lambda q(1-s))
.
In Lemma
15
of Appendix
I
, we provide an upper bound of the total variation distance between binomial and Poisson random variables. By Lemma
15

and the assumption that
|N^{\uparrow}|\leq n^{1-\gamma}
and
\sum_{h=1}^{k-1}|H_{h}|=O(n^{\gamma/4})
, we can couple
d_{j}
and
c_{\phi_{k-1}(j)}
so that

{\mathsf{P}}(d_{j}=c_{\phi_{k-1}(j)})\geq 1-O\left(\frac{n^{1-\gamma}}{n}\right)\geq 1-O(n^{-\gamma}),

and it follows that

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})=O(n^{-3\gamma/4}).

(61)

Similarly, we can bound

{\mathsf{P}}(\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})=O(n^{-3\gamma/4}).

(62)

Given
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}
and
\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}
, we can apply union bound over pairs
j,j^{\prime}\in\bar{H}_{k-1}
and
j^{\prime\prime}\in[n]\setminus S\setminus(\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}\bar{H}_{h})
to get

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})\leq O(n\cdot n^{\gamma/2}\cdot n^{-2})=O(n^{-1+\gamma/2}).

(63)

Also, we know that

|\bar{H}_{k}|{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{sto.}}}{{\leq}}}{\mathrm{Binom}}(n-|N^{\uparrow}|-|\cup_{h=0}^{k-1}\bar{H}_{h}|,1-(1-{\bar{\lambda}}/n)^{(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{k-1}\log n}).

Therefore, by the Chernoff–Hoeffding bound,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{I}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}\left({\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n,\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{k-1}\log n}{n}\right)\geq(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{k}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-2{\bar{\lambda}}^{2}/3}.

(64)

Given events
{\mathcal{I}}_{k}
,
{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}
,
{\mathcal{D}}_{k}^{c}
and
\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h}
, we can use union bound to get

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{I}}_{k})\leq\frac{{\bar{\lambda}}}{n}\cdot(2{\bar{\lambda}})^{2k}\log^{2}n\leq n^{\gamma-1},

(65)

where the last inequality follows because
k=o(\log n)
. By equations (
58
)-(
65
), we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{J}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{D}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{F}}_{k}^{c}\cup\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}^{c}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{I}}^{c}_{k}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{C}}_{k}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{D}}_{k}|\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{*c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{F}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})

\displaystyle+{\mathsf{P}}(\tilde{{\mathcal{F}}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{I}}^{c}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k})

\displaystyle+{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}|(\cap_{h=1}^{k-1}{\mathcal{J}}_{h})\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{D}}^{c}_{k}\cap{\mathcal{I}}_{k})

\displaystyle=O(n^{-3\gamma/4}).

(66)

Finally, substituting (
57
) and (
66
) into (
55
) gives

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=0}^{l^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{k})

\displaystyle\geq 1-O(n^{-\gamma})-O(l^{\prime}\cdot n^{-3\gamma/4})\geq 1-\tilde{O}(n^{-3\gamma/4}),

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix H
Properties of sparse Erdős–Rényi Random Graphs

In this section, we prove a few properties in sparse Erdős–Rényi random graphs that are used in the proof of Propositions
1
and
2
.

H-A

Neighborhood size bounds

Lemma 10
.

Consider an Erdős–Rényi random graph
{\mathcal{G}}\sim\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{\mu}{n})
, where
\mu>1
is a constant independent of
n
.
Let
d=c\log n
for some
c\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{\log\mu}
, where
\gamma
is an arbitrarily small constant and
C
be a constant satisfying
C\geq 1
. For an arbitrary vertex
i
in
{\mathcal{G}}
, we have

{\mathsf{P}}\left(\exists k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}:|N_{{\mathcal{G}}}(i,k)|\geq K\mu^{k}\log n\right)\leq\tilde{O}(n^{-C/3}),

(67)

where constant
K:=\frac{C\mu}{\mu-1}\prod_{h=0}^{\infty}(1+\mu^{-h/2})
.

Proof.

In this proof, we omit the subscript
{\mathcal{G}}
in the notations
N_{{\mathcal{G}}}(i,k)
and
S_{\mathcal{G}}(i,k)
as is clear from the context.
For each
k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
, define event

{\mathcal{A}}_{k}=\left\{|S(i,k)|<C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\right\}.

Since

\displaystyle\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}C(\log n)\mu^{k^{\prime}}\prod_{h=0}^{k^{\prime}}(1+\mu^{-h/2})

\displaystyle\leq C(\log n)\prod_{h=0}^{\infty}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}\mu^{k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq K\mu^{k}\log n,

it suffices to show that
{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=0}^{d}{\mathcal{A}}_{k})\geq 1-\tilde{O}(n^{-C/3})
. We can rewrite this probability as

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=0}^{d}{\mathcal{A}}_{k})

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})-\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}^{c}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h}))

\displaystyle\geq{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})-\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h}).

(68)

Notice that
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
trivially holds so
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})=1
, and it suffices to bound each term in the summation in (
68
).

Fix some
k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
. Notice that given
|N(i,k-1)|=m
and
|S(i,k-1)|=m^{\prime}
, we have

{\mathcal{L}}(|S(i,k)|)={\mathrm{Binom}}(n-m,1-(1-\mu/n)^{m^{\prime}}).

Then conditioned on event
\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h}
, we have

{\mathcal{L}}(|S(i,k)|)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{sto.}}}{{\leq}}{\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n,\frac{C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})}{n}\right),

where
{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{sto.}}}{{\leq}}}
is the stochastic dominance operator. By Bennett’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}\left({\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n,\frac{C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})}{n}\right)\geq C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-C(\log n)\mu^{k}\left(\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\right)\cdot\left(1-\frac{C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})}{n}\right)\cdot\phi(\mu^{-k/2})\right),

where
\phi(x):=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
.
The function
\phi
satisfies
\phi(x)\geq\frac{x^{2}}{3}
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Because
\mu>1
is a constant, we have
\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\geq 2
and
\prod_{h=0}^{\infty}(1+\mu^{-h/2})<\infty
.
Because
k\leq d=c\log n
, we know that
C(\log n)\mu^{k}=o(n)
.
These further imply that

\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\cdot\left(1-\frac{C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})}{n}\right)>1

for all large enough
n
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{A}}_{k}^{c}|\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{A}}_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{C\log n}{3}\right)=n^{-C/3}.

Notice that the above inequality holds for every
k\in[d]
. Therefore, we have

{\mathsf{P}}(\cap_{k=0}^{d}{\mathcal{A}}_{k})\geq 1-dn^{-C/3}=1-\tilde{O}(n^{-C/3}),

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 11
.

Consider an Erdős–Rényi random graph
{\mathcal{G}}\sim\mathrm{ER}(n,\frac{\mu}{n})
, where
\mu>1
is a constant independent of
n
.
Let
d=c\log n
for some
c\leq\frac{1-\gamma}{\log\mu}
, where
\gamma
is an arbitrarily small constant and
C
be a constant satisfying
C\geq 1
. For two distinct vertices
i
and
j
in
{\mathcal{G}}
, and an integer
d^{\prime}\leq d
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}\left(\exists k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}:|N_{{\mathcal{G}}}(i,k)|\geq K\mu^{k}\log n\;\bigg|\;j\in S_{\mathcal{G}}(i,d^{\prime})\right)\leq O(n^{-\gamma/3}),

(69)

where constant
K:=\frac{C\mu}{\mu-1}\prod_{h=0}^{\infty}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})
.

Proof.

In this proof, we omit the subscript
{\mathcal{G}}
in the notations
N_{{\mathcal{G}}}(i,k)
and
S_{\mathcal{G}}(i,k)
as is clear from the context.
Define event

{\mathcal{B}}=\left\{\exists k\in\{0,\ldots,d\}:|N(i,k)|\geq\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}C(\log n)\mu^{k^{\prime}}\prod_{h=0}^{k^{\prime}}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})\right\}.

By the definition of constant
K
, we have

\displaystyle K\mu^{k}\log n\geq\sum_{k^{\prime}=0}^{k}C(\log n)\mu^{k^{\prime}}\prod_{h=0}^{k^{\prime}}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h}),

for any
k
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))\leq O(n^{-\gamma/3})
. Notice that the conditioned event
j\in S(i,d^{\prime})
is equivalent as there exists a path of length
d^{\prime}
between
i
and
j
in graph
{\mathcal{G}}

and
there exists no path of length less than
d^{\prime}
between
i
and
j
in
{\mathcal{G}}
. Let
\mathcal{P}^{d^{\prime}}_{i,j}
denote the collection of all possible path of length
d^{\prime}
between
i
and
j
in
{\mathcal{G}}
. We define a total order for the paths in
\mathcal{P}^{d^{\prime}}_{i,j}
as follows: For two distinct paths
p=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}})
and
p^{\prime}=(v_{0}^{\prime},v_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}}^{\prime})
with
v_{0}=v_{0}^{\prime}=i
and
v_{d^{\prime}}=v_{d^{\prime}}^{\prime}=j
. We say
p
has a higher order than
p^{\prime}
, denoted
p\prec p^{\prime}
, if there exists an index
d^{\prime\prime}\in[d^{\prime}-1]
such that
v_{k}=v_{k}^{\prime},\forall k<d^{\prime\prime}
and
v_{d^{\prime\prime}}<v_{d^{\prime\prime}}^{\prime}
. Then we can write

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}},\text{the highest order $d^{\prime}$-path between $i$ and $j$ in ${\mathcal{G}}$ is $p$}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathsf{P}}(\text{the highest order $d^{\prime}$-path between $i$ and $j$ in ${\mathcal{G}}$ is $p$}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\cdot{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\text{the highest order $d^{\prime}$-path between $i$ and $j$ in ${\mathcal{G}}$ is $p$},j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\text{the highest order $d^{\prime}$-path between $i$ and $j$ in ${\mathcal{G}}$ is $p$},j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap\{\text{no path in $\mathcal{P}$ with higher order than $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cap\{\text{no path with length less than $d^{\prime}$ between $i$ and $j$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}).

Notice that
{\mathcal{B}}
is an increasing graph property because if
{\mathcal{B}}
holds in
{\mathcal{G}}
, then the property still holds after adding an arbitrary edge to
{\mathcal{G}}
. On the other hand, both events

\{\text{no path in $\mathcal{P}$ with higher order than $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}

and

\{\text{no path with length less than $d^{\prime}$ between $i$ and $j$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}

are decreasing graph properties because if they hold in
{\mathcal{G}}
, removing any edge from
{\mathcal{G}}
does not break the properties. Then by Harris inequality
[
23
]
, we have

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}j\in S(i,d^{\prime}))\leq\max_{p\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}).

Fix an arbitrary
p\in\mathcal{P}
with
p=(v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}})
. For each
0\leq k\leq d
, define event

{\mathcal{B}}_{k}=\left\{|S(i,k)|\geq C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})\right\}

We also define

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}=\{S(i,k)\cap\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}}\}=\{v_{k}\}\}

for each
0\leq k\leq d^{\prime}
, and

{\mathcal{C}}_{k}=\{S(i,k)\cap\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}}\}={\emptyset}\}

for each
k>d^{\prime}
.
We then have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}(\cup_{k=0}^{d}{\mathcal{B}}_{k}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}(\cup_{k=0}^{d}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c})\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\})

\displaystyle\leq{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{0}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h})),

(70)

where the equality holds because
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{0}^{c}
are both trivial events that cannot happen.

Given event
\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h})
, we know that

|S(i,k)|{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{sto.}}}{{\leq}}}1+{\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n,\frac{\mu}{n}\cdot C(\log n)\mu^{k-1}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})\right),

where we add one to the right-hand side because when
k\leq d^{\prime}
, there is a known edge
(v_{k-1},v_{k})
on the given path
p
. By Bennett’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}\Bigg({\mathrm{Binom}}\left(n,\frac{\mu}{n}\cdot C(\log n)\mu^{k-1}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\geq C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\prod_{h^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h^{\prime}})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-C/3}.

Moreover, we have

1+C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})\prod_{h^{\prime}=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h^{\prime}})\leq C(\log n)\mu^{k}\prod_{h=0}^{k}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h}).

It then follows that

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h}))\leq n^{-C/3}.

(71)

On the other hand, given event
\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h})
, event
{\mathcal{C}}^{c}_{k}
essentially states that there exists an edge between
S(i,k-1)
and
\{v_{k+1},\ldots,v_{d^{\prime}}\}
. By the union bound over the vertex pair of these two sets, we can get

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mu}{n}\cdot d\cdot C(\log n)\mu^{k-1}\prod_{h=0}^{k-1}(1+\mu^{-h/2})(1+\mu^{-h})

\displaystyle\leq O(n^{-\gamma/2}),

(72)

Equations (
71
) and (
72
) together imply

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{k}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{k}^{c}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\}\cap(\cap_{h=0}^{k-1}{\mathcal{B}}_{h}^{c}\cap{\mathcal{C}}_{h}))=O(n^{-\gamma/2}).

(73)

By (

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathcal{B}}\mathchoice{\,|\,}{\mspace{2mu}|\mspace{2mu}}{|}{|}\{\text{path $p$ exists in ${\mathcal{G}}$}\})\leq O(n^{-\gamma/3}).

This holds for any path
p
, and completes the proof.
∎

I-A

Concentration inequalities

Lemma 12

(Bennett’s inequality
[
3
]
)
.

Suppose
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are independent random variables with finite second moments. Assume
X_{i}\leq B
a.s. for each
i\in[n]
. Let

. Then, for every

{\mathsf{P}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-{\mathsf{E}}[X_{i}])\geq x\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{V}{B^{2}}\cdot\phi(xB/V)\right),

where
\phi(t):=(1+t)\log(1+t)-t
for
t\geq 0
.

Lemma 13

(Chernoff–Hoeffding bound
[
14
]
)
.

Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
be a sum of independent Bernoulli random variables with
\mu={\mathsf{E}}[X]
. Then for any
\delta>0
,

{\mathsf{P}}(X\geq(1+\delta)\mu)\leq\left(\frac{e^{\delta}}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^{\mu}.

I-B

Technical lemmas for the Poisson approximation

Lemma 14
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
x<n
and
y\leq n-x
.
Then we have

\frac{{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Binom}}(n-x,\mu/n)=y)}{{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Poi}}(\mu)=y)}\leq\exp\left(\frac{\mu}{n}(x+y)\right).

Proof.

By the definition of binomial random variables, we have

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Binom}}(n-x,\mu/n)=y)=\binom{n-x}{y}(\mu/n)^{y}(1-\mu/n)^{n-x-y},

and by the definition of Poisson random variables, we have

{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Poi}}(\mu)=y)=\frac{e^{-\mu}\mu^{y}}{y!}.

It then follows that

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Binom}}(n-x,\mu/n)=y)}{{\mathsf{P}}({\mathrm{Poi}}(\mu)=y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-x)!}{(n-x-y)!}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{y}e^{\mu}\left(1-\frac{\mu}{n}\right)^{n-x-y}

\displaystyle\leq(n-x)^{y}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)^{y}e^{\mu}\left(1-\frac{\mu}{n}\right)^{n-x-y}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\mu}\left(1-\frac{\mu}{n}\right)^{n-x-y}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\mu-\frac{\mu}{n}(n-x-y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\mu}{n}(x+y)\right).

∎

Lemma 16

Appendix J
More Details on the Likelihood Ratio Tests

Recall the definition of distributions
\mathbb{Q}_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})},\mathbb{P}_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}
. The for two unlabeled trees
t
and
\tilde{t}
, the likelihood ratio is given by

L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})}{\mathbb{Q}_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})}.

J-A

Recursive formula of the likelihood ratio

The following recursive formula of
L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})
is stated in Proposition 4.3 of
[
32
]
.

First, for the case of
d=0
,

L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})=1.

Now suppose
d\geq 1
. For two unlabeled trees
t
and
\tilde{t}
, let
c
and
\tilde{c}
denote their respective root degrees. Denote by
t_{1},\ldots,t_{c}
(resp.
\tilde{t}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{t}_{\tilde{c}}
) the subtrees attached to the rooted of
t
(resp.
\tilde{t}
). Then the likelihood ratio
L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}
can be iteratively expressed as

L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{c,\tilde{c}\}}\Psi(k,c,\tilde{c})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma\in\mathrm{Inj}([k],[c])\\
\tilde{\sigma}\in\mathrm{Inj}([k],[\tilde{c}])\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}L^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}_{d-1}(t_{\sigma(i)},\tilde{t}_{\tilde{\sigma}(i)}),

(91)

where

\Psi(k,c,\tilde{c}):=e^{\lambda s\tilde{s}}\frac{(1-\tilde{s})^{c-k}(1-s)^{\tilde{c}-k}}{\lambda^{k}k!},

and we use
\mathrm{Inj}([m],[m^{\prime}])
to denote the set of all injective mappings from
[m]
to
[m^{\prime}]
for any
m\leq m^{\prime}
.

Remark 5

(Time complexity of computing the likelihood ratio)
.

Let
c_{\mathrm{max}}
denote the maximum vertex degree in the two trees
t
and
\tilde{t}
. Let
|t|
and
|\tilde{t}|
denote the total number of vertices in
t
and
\tilde{t}
respectively. Because the cardinality of the sets
\mathrm{Inj}([k],[c])
and
\mathrm{Inj}([k],[\tilde{c}])
can both be upper bounded by
c_{\mathrm{max}}!
, the time complexity of each recursive computation using (
91
) is at most
c_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{max}}!)^{2}
. Then the total complexity of computing
L_{d}^{(\lambda,s,\tilde{s})}(t,\tilde{t})
is at most
|t|\cdot|\tilde{t}|.c_{\mathrm{max}}^{2}\cdot(c_{\mathrm{max}}!)^{2}
.

Under the assumption that
