DOG-DPO:Dynamic Optimization in Geometry for Safety Alignment
Yi Nian 1 Tiankai Yang 1 Yudi Zhang 2 Qi Pan 1 Zelong Xu 3 Shenzhe Zhu 4 Qingqing Luan 5 Yue Huang 6 Xiangliang Zhang 6 Yue Zhao 1 1 University of Southern California 2 Iowa State University 3 University of Wisconsin–Madison 4 UT Austin 5 Independent Researcher 6 University of Notre Dame {yinian,tiankaiy,panq,yue.z}@usc.edu yudizhang628@gmail.com qingqingluan95@gmail.com zxu684@wisc.edu shenzhe@utexas.edu {yhuang37,xzhang33}@nd.edu

Abstract
Safety alignment for large language models relies on preference data, but current pipelines often train on large, redundant datasets. Existing data selection methods typically score each preference pair independently, collapsing directional preference information into scalar quality or diversity scores. This sample-centric view is especially limiting in multi-dataset settings, where shared safety directions coexist with dataset-specific residual risks. We propose DOG-DPO , a training-free data selection framework that treats preference pairs as structured geometric signals. DOG-DPO first represents each preference pair as a direction in model representation space. It then decomposes multi-dataset preference geometry into a global anchor subspace and dataset-specific residual subspaces. Finally, it selects subsets by maximizing diversity-based coverage, encouraging broad, non-redundant coverage of alignment directions before DPO training. Across six safety benchmarks and two model backbones, DOG-DPO achieves a strong utility–robustness trade-off using only 11% of the preference pairs. It recovers most of the safety gains of full-data training while remaining entirely teacher-free, training-free, and substantially faster than representative selection baselines. Introduction

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with safety preferences has become a central problem in modern AI systems
(
13
;
21
;
19
;
20
)
. Most alignment pipelines rely on large-scale human feedback, typically in the form of preference pairs used in reward modeling or Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
8
;
18
)
. While these approaches achieve strong empirical performance, they are inherently data-intensive
(
1
;
16
)
: collecting and training on large safety datasets is costly, noisy, and often inefficient. Recent studies suggest that only a small fraction of alignment data is truly necessary, indicating substantial redundancy in existing datasets
(
23
;
5
;
10
;
2
)
. This raises a fundamental question:
how should we select informative subsets of preference data for efficient alignment?
While prior work shows that careful data selection can match or even outperform full-dataset training, the underlying principles of selecting preference data remain poorly understood—especially in realistic settings where data comes from multiple heterogeneous sources
(
12
;
4
)
.

Existing data selection methods approach this problem from several perspectives,
including distributional diversity
(
23
;
2
)
,
training-signal analysis
(
5
;
24
)
, and
heuristic scoring
(
10
;
12
)
. While effective in their
respective settings, these approaches are primarily designed for generic data
selection and do not explicitly account for the structured nature of preference
data. In particular, they adopt a sample-centric view that scores or ranks each
preference pair independently, collapsing its information into a scalar. This view loses two structural properties that are intrinsic to
preference data.
First, preference signals are inherently directional
rather than pointwise
: a pair
(x,y^{+},y^{-})
does not just label two
candidates—it specifies the alignment direction along which the model should
shift. Sample-level scoring discards this directionality.
Second, in
multi-dataset settings, preference data carries cross-dataset structure
: some
alignment directions are well captured by a dominant anchor dataset, while
others remain dataset-specific and reflect heterogeneous risk domains

(
10
;
5
)
. Methods that pool preference
pairs into a single homogeneous candidate set cannot distinguish these two
regimes, leading to redundant signals on anchor-aligned directions and undercoverage of
dataset-specific ones. A third practical limitation compounds the conceptual
ones: training-signal-based methods require auxiliary forward/backward passes
or reference DPO runs to extract their selection signal, making them
computationally expensive and tightly coupled to the training process

(
5
;
24
)
.

In this work, we depart from the sample-centric view and treat preference data
as a
structured geometric object
in representation space. Each preference
pair is represented by the difference vector between preferred
and rejected response embeddings, yielding a directional alignment signal rather
than a scalar-scored sample. Across
V
preference datasets, these directions
contain both a dominant anchor geometry and dataset-specific residual variations.
This turns data selection into a
geometric coverage
problem: selecting
non-redundant directions that span both shared and residual preference structure.
We instantiate this view as
DOG-DPO
, a training-free framework that
builds anchor/residual coordinates and selects subsets using a determinant-based
diversity criterion. It requires no teacher LLM, no reference DPO run, and no
iterative validation.

We evaluate DOG-DPO on six safety alignment benchmarks across two backbones.
Using only 11.6% of the preference pairs, DOG-DPO remains competitive with
full-data training, recovers most safety gains while maintaining general LLM capabilities, reduces
inter-dataset selection bias, and expands feature-space coverage relative to
baselines. These results suggest that effective alignment depends not only on
data scale, but also on exploiting the structured geometry of heterogeneous
preference data. We summarize our contributions as follows:

•

A geometric reformulation of preference data.
Departing from the prevailing sample-centric view, we represent each preference pair as a directional signal in representation space and reveal that, across heterogeneous sources, these signals decompose into a shared anchor geometry and dataset-specific residual variations.

•

A geometry-driven selection framework.
Building on this view, we propose
DOG-DPO
, which selects subsets by maximizing a diversity-based coverage objective over the induced feature space, jointly capturing structural breadth and directional non-redundancy.

•

Training-free selection.
DOG-DPO requires no teacher LLM,
reference DPO training, or iterative validation, running as a single
pre-processing pass and achieving 15–35
\times
speedups over
representative baselines (Figure
3
).

•

Strong safety-alignment results.
Across six benchmarks and two
backbones, DOG-DPO uses only 11.6% of the preference pairs while remaining
competitive with full-data training and improving selection balance and
feature-space coverage over existing baselines.

2
Method

2.1
Problem Formulation

Preliminaries.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the policy LLM with parameters
\theta
, and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
a frozen reference policy. We write the policy in the standard last-layer form
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\propto\exp(Wh_{\theta}(x,y))
, where
h_{\theta}(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the final hidden representation of the response token at position
|y|
(or its mean over response tokens), and
W\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|\times d}
is the unembedding matrix mapping hidden states to vocabulary logits. Throughout, we treat
h
as a feature extractor; the geometry it induces on preference pairs is the object our selection operates on.

A preference dataset is a set of triples
(x,y^{+},y^{-})
where
y^{+}
is preferred over
y^{-}
. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)
(
8
)
aligns
\pi_{\theta}
with these preferences by minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{i}=-\log\sigma(m_{i})
, where the per-pair margin is
m_{i}=\beta[\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}^{+}\mid x_{i})-\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}^{-}\mid x_{i})]-\beta[\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{i}^{+}\mid x_{i})-\log\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_{i}^{-}\mid x_{i})]
and
\beta>0
is a temperature.

From DPO loss to a directional signal.

Substituting
\pi_{\theta}(y\mid x)\propto\exp(Wh_{\theta}(x,y))
into the DPO margin
m_{i}
, the
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
contribution is a
\theta
-independent constant that shifts the per-pair decision threshold but not the gradient with respect to
\theta
. The policy-dependent component therefore reduces to a linear functional of the unembedding
W
:

\displaystyle m_{i}^{\mathrm{policy}}

\displaystyle=\beta Wz_{i},

(1)

\displaystyle z_{i}

\displaystyle:=h(x_{i},y_{i}^{+})-h(x_{i},y_{i}^{-}).

That is, each preference pair contributes a training signal whose direction in representation space is fully captured by the hidden-state difference
z_{i}
, with
\beta
acting as a global scaling factor. Pairs with similar
z_{i}
contribute aligned signal to training; orthogonal
z_{i}
contribute independent signal.

Selection setup.

Given preference datasets
\{\mathcal{D}_{v}\}_{v=1}^{V}
with union
\mathcal{D}
, we select a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{D}
of size
K
before DPO training. Projecting
z_{i}
onto a low-rank preference basis
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
(Sec.
2.2
) gives
\phi_{i}=B^{\top}z_{i}
and quality
q_{i}=\|\phi_{i}\|_{2}
.

Quality-weighted kernel.

For a candidate subset
S
, we use a quality-weighted RBF kernel that combines signal magnitude and directional redundancy:

\displaystyle L_{S}

\displaystyle=[L_{ij}]_{i,j\in S},\qquad L_{ij}=q_{i}q_{j}K_{ij},

(2)

\displaystyle K_{ij}

\displaystyle=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|\phi_{i}-\phi_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

Here
K_{ij}
captures directional redundancy and
L_{ii}=q_{i}^{2}
injects per-sample signal strength into the same kernel.

Objective and greedy rule.

We use the log-determinant volume criterion from D-optimal experimental design
(
17
)
:

\displaystyle\hat{S}

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{|S|=K}\mathcal{J}(S),

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(S)

\displaystyle=\log\det\!\left(L_{S}+\varepsilon I\right).

which rewards large, non-redundant preference signals. Greedy selection uses

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(S,i)

\displaystyle=\theta q_{i}+(1-\theta)g_{i},

(4)

\displaystyle g_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{i}\log\det\!\left(L_{S}+\varepsilon I\right).

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}\log\det(L_{S}+\varepsilon I)=

\displaystyle\log\det(L_{S\cup\{i\}}+\varepsilon I)

(5)

\displaystyle-\log\det(L_{S}+\varepsilon I).

The parameter
\theta\in[0,1]
controls the extra quality bias:
\theta=0
uses only the quality-weighted kernel, while
\theta=1
becomes quality-only top-
K
. The following proposition establishes that our choice of
q_{i}=\|\phi_{i}\|_{2}
is
not arbitrary
: it makes the log-det objective simultaneously controlled by a quality-energy upper bound and a coverage lower bound.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{|S|}\geq 0
be the eigenvalues of
L_{S}\succeq 0
. Since
L_{ii}=q_{i}^{2}
,
\operatorname{tr}(L_{S})=\sum_{i\in S}q_{i}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle\det(L_{S})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i\in S}q_{i}^{2}\right)^{|S|},

(6)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(L_{S})

\displaystyle\geq\det(L_{S})\lambda_{\max}(L_{S})^{1-|S|},

(7)

\displaystyle\det(L_{S})

\displaystyle\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad K_{ij}\to 1.

(8)

Proof.

Eq. (
6
) follows from AM–GM on the eigenvalues and
\operatorname{tr}(L_{S})=\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}
. Eq. (
7
) follows from
\det(L_{S})=\prod_{j}\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{\min}(L_{S})\lambda_{\max}(L_{S})^{|S|-1}
. For (
8
), duplicate directions make rows of
K_{S}
identical, so
L_{S}=D_{q}K_{S}D_{q}
becomes rank-deficient by continuity.
∎

Spanning phase
\leftrightarrow
optimizing a conditioning bound.

The optimized quantity is the log-volume objective
\log\det(L_{S}+\varepsilon I)
. For fixed or slowly varying
\lambda_{\max}(L_{S})
, Eq. (
7
) shows that increasing
\det(L_{S})
raises a lower bound on
\lambda_{\min}(L_{S})
. Thus, early in selection, when redundant samples make
K_{S}
and hence
L_{S}
nearly rank-deficient, the greedy gain favors candidates dissimilar to the current subset: it improves the lower bound on the worst-covered direction.

Filling phase
\leftrightarrow
approaching a quality-energy bound.

After redundancy is reduced, diversity gains shrink and the determinant is mainly limited by the diagonal quality mass. Eq. (
6
) gives the relevant upper bound,

\det(L_{S})\leq(|S|^{-1}\sum_{i\in S}q_{i}^{2})^{|S|}
, which is tight when the eigenvalues are balanced. Therefore, once the conditioning/coverage bound has improved, further log-det gains are obtained by increasing the quality energy
\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}
, so high-magnitude samples become preferable. The greedy rule can be read as first improving the
\lambda_{\min}
lower bound through diversity, then filling covered regions to move toward the AM–GM quality-energy upper bound.

Why per-sample scoring falls short.

Scalar scoring methods select by top-
K
or additive scores
\sum_{i}s_{i}
. Such scores only optimize a separable quality proxy and do not control the spectral bound above: two subsets with comparable
\sum_{i}q_{i}^{2}
can have very different
\det(L_{S})
, and hence very different lower bounds on
\lambda_{\min}(L_{S})
, depending on whether their directions are diverse or duplicated. Our greedy rule keeps both channels: log-det for the coverage bound and
q_{i}
for signal strength.

Multi-dataset realization.

Eq. (
3
) leaves the basis
B
unspecified. We construct
B
from a largest-dataset anchor block and add per-dataset residual blocks to preserve dataset-specific safety directions. Sec.
2.2
–
2.4
detail the construction and variants.

2.2
Anchor and Residual Basis Construction

We instantiate the preference basis
B
from Sec.
2.1
by taking the PCA basis of
the largest preference dataset as an anchor, and complementing it with
per-dataset residual bases that capture structure not explained by that anchor.
The encoder
h
is a frozen pretrained backbone; representations

h(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are mean-pooled last-layer hidden states over
response tokens.

Per-dataset PCA and anchor selection.

For each
\mathcal{D}_{v}
, let
U_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
be the top-
k

PCA basis of the centered preference vectors
\{z_{i}^{(v)}-\mu_{v}\}
. We then
choose the anchor dataset

v_{*}=\arg\max_{v}|\mathcal{D}_{v}|,

(9)

and define the anchor basis as

B=U_{v_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r},\qquad r=k.

(10)

Default choice and robustness.

We default to the largest available dataset as the anchor primarily for two pragmatic reasons.
First,
statistical reliability
: principal-subspace estimation error
scales as
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
, meaning the largest dataset naturally yields the most
stable top-
k
basis under bootstrap resampling.
Second,
domain breadth
: in our data pool, the largest dataset spans the widest
range of harm categories, offering a well-distributed reference frame for global safety
boundaries.

Crucially, as we empirically demonstrate via our anchor-rotation ablation below,
the final data selection and downstream safety performance are highly insensitive
to this choice—alternating the anchor across different candidate datasets yields nearly
identical alignment outcomes. Therefore, anchoring on the largest dataset
is best conceptualized as a reliable and stable default choice to fix a coordinate system,
rather than a hypercritical design dependency. We provide further quantitative
metrics of candidate anchors in Appendix
C
.

Residual bases relative to the anchor.

For each dataset, we define the residual subspace by removing the anchor
component from its PCA basis:

R_{v}=(I-BB^{\top})U_{v},\quad T_{v}=\mathrm{orth}(R_{v})\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m_{v}},

(11)

where

m_{v}=\mathrm{rank}(R_{v})\leq k.

(12)

For the anchor dataset itself, this construction yields an empty residual block
up to numerical tolerance. More generally, the orthogonal decomposition

U_{v}=BB^{\top}U_{v}+(I-BB^{\top})U_{v}
separates the portion of dataset
v
that
is aligned with the anchor from the portion that remains dataset-specific.

2.3
Direction-wise Features

We now instantiate
\phi_{i}
from Sec.
2.1
as a concrete direction-wise feature
vector, with one coordinate per retained anchor/residual direction. For each
sample
z_{i}^{(v)}
, projection onto the two bases gives

z_{a,i}=B^{\top}z_{i}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},\qquad z_{r,i}^{(v)}=T_{v}^{\top}z_{i}^{(v)}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{v}}.

(13)

Since
B
and
T_{v}
are orthonormal, the squared coefficients

z_{a,i,j}^{2}
and
(z_{r,i,m}^{(v)})^{2}
provide raw per-direction energy
measures. We use them directly for the anchor block, and modulate the
residual block via a typicality weight described below.

Direction weighting.

Anchor and residual blocks play asymmetric roles. For the anchor block, we use
the raw squared coefficients
z_{a,i,j}^{2}
: because
B
is taken directly from
the largest dataset, these coordinates already measure how strongly sample
i

aligns with the reference geometry. Residual directions have no analogous
global reference score, so raw
(z_{r,i,m}^{(v)})^{2}
can over-weight atypical
or noisy dataset-specific outliers. We therefore modulate the residual block by a
Mahalanobis-based typicality weight in the dataset’s residual subspace,

\displaystyle w_{\mathrm{typ}}(z_{i}^{(v)})=\exp\!\Bigl(

\displaystyle-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(z_{r,i}^{(v)}-\mu_{r}^{(v)}\bigr)^{\top}

(14)

\displaystyle\bigl(\Sigma_{r}^{(v)}+\rho I\bigr)^{-1}\bigl(z_{r,i}^{(v)}-\mu_{r}^{(v)}\bigr)\Bigr),

where
\mu_{r}^{(v)}
,
\Sigma_{r}^{(v)}
are the per-dataset mean and covariance
in the residual subspace and
\rho I
is a numerical regularizer. This
favors residual signal that is
representative
of its source dataset
rather than anomalous.

Feature vector.

Setting

\psi_{i,j}^{\mathrm{anchor}}=z_{a,i,j}^{2}
and

\psi_{i,m}^{(v)}=(z_{r,i,m}^{(v)})^{2}w_{\mathrm{typ}}(z_{i}^{(v)})
,
we concatenate them:

\phi_{i}=\bigl[\psi^{\mathrm{anchor}}_{i,1},\dots,\psi^{\mathrm{anchor}}_{i,r},\,\psi^{(v)}_{i,1},\dots,,\psi^{(v)}_{i,m_{v}}\bigr]

(15)

This
\phi_{i}
is the concrete instantiation used in
\mathcal{J}(S)

(Eq.
3
); its magnitude
q_{i}=\|\phi_{i}\|_{2}
aggregates
sample-level signal across both blocks, while the direction
\hat{\phi}_{i}

encodes which anchor and residual modes the sample activates.

is the trace-only degeneration of DOG-D: dropping the

Ablation of the diversity term

DOG and DOG-D share the identical anchor/residual feature space and differ only in the selection rule: DOG ranks samples via Pareto frontier on the two scalar scores, while DOG-D adds the log-determinant diversity term. The gap between DOG and DOG-D therefore isolates the contribution of the diversity term within our framework. The substantial gap on LLaMA-3.2-3B at K=30k (HB-kw 0.235 vs. 0.090; AutoDAN 0.037 vs. 0.027) confirms that structured features alone are insufficient—directional redundancy must be explicitly suppressed, consistent with the spanning–filling analysis in Sec.

4
Conclusion

In this work, we study the problem of data-efficient alignment for large language models and identify a key limitation of existing data selection methods: they do not explicitly model the structured nature of preference data, especially in multi-dataset settings. We argue that preference signals admit a geometric interpretation, where alignment information is encoded as structured directions in representation space. Based on this view, we propose DOG-DPO, a dynamic and geometry-driven data selection framework that identifies anchor geometry and dataset-specific residual variations, and selects subsets that maximize geometric coverage via volume expansion. This formulation enables efficient selection without relying on external supervision or training. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our method achieves strong performance using significantly less data, while improving coverage and reducing redundancy. These results highlight that effective alignment depends not only on data scale, but on understanding the structured geometry of preference data.

5
Limitations

DOG-DPO is substantially more efficient than
training-based or judge-based selection, it still requires representation
extraction and subset optimization over the candidate pool, which can become
costly at very large scale. Third, our evaluation focuses primarily on safety
alignment benchmarks. Although the experiment
provides initial evidence that DOG-DPO transfers beyond the 1B–3B full-finetuning
setting, broader validation across more model families, larger backbones, and
non-safety preference domains remains future work.

6
Broader Impact

DOG-DPO can make safety alignment more efficient by reducing redundant
preference data, lowering training costs, and decreasing dependence on expensive
teacher or judge models. By selecting preference pairs through multi-dataset
geometric coverage, the method provides a practical way to retain complementary
alignment signals while keeping the selected training set compact. We see no
significant risk of direct negative societal impact when using it to improve the efficiency of existing alignment workflows from this data selection
method.

Appendix A
Related Work

A.1
Data Selection for Instruction Tuning and Alignment

Existing methods can be broadly categorized based on the type of signal used to guide selection.

Distribution- and representation-based methods.

A line of work models datasets as distributions in embedding space and selects samples to maximize diversity and coverage. Methods such as diversity metrics
23
, stratified sampling
15
, and QDIT
2
characterize global data distributions, while sparse autoencoder-based approaches
22
learn latent factors and prioritize underrepresented dimensions. These methods effectively improve coverage in representation space, but operate at a coarse level and do not explicitly capture alignment-relevant preference structure.

Training-signal-based methods.

Another line of work leverages training dynamics, such as gradients or loss signals, to identify informative samples. TAGCOS
24
clusters samples based on gradient embeddings to preserve diversity in parameter update directions, while principled data selection
5
analyzes sample difficulty to filter unstable examples. However, these methods typically require additional training or repeated model updates to obtain reliable signals, making them computationally expensive and tightly coupled with the training process.

Scalar scoring and heuristic filtering methods.

Some approaches rely on scalar scoring functions to rank and select samples. DEITA
10
combines quality, complexity, and diversity signals, often relying on external teacher models or LLM-based judges, while Less-Is-More
12
ranks samples using model-internal value signals such as difficulty or influence. These methods are simple and efficient, but treat samples independently and ignore structural redundancy, making them prone to biased selection and dataset collapse.

Summary.

Overall, existing methods rely on generic distributional, training, or scalar signals, without modeling the intrinsic structure of preference data. In particular, they do not account for multi-dataset settings where preference signals consist of both a dominant anchor geometry and dataset-specific residual variation. In contrast, our method explicitly models this structure by decomposing representations into anchor and residual components, enabling principled data selection that preserves global alignment consistency while maintaining heterogeneous coverage across datasets.

A.2
Our Position

In contrast to prior work, we explicitly model safety preference data as a structured geometric object in representation space. Our method decomposes preference signals into anchor and residual components. The base variant
DOG
operates only on simple scalar anchor/residual scores, while
DOG-D
further introduces our custom direction-wise feature representation and a diversity regularization term, enabling both structural coverage and redundancy reduction in a unified framework.

Appendix B
Connection to DPO signal coverage

This appendix expands on the geometric reading of
\mathcal{J}(S)=\log\det(M_{S}+\varepsilon I)

introduced in §
2.1
. The main text establishes (Proposition 1) that

\det(M_{S})
controls both the worst-covered eigendirection of
M_{S}
and its rank
deficiency. Here we make explicit
why
this quantity is a natural surrogate
for DPO training-signal coverage, and how the anchor/residual basis from
§
2.2
shapes what “coverage” means.

Why the log-determinant is the right surrogate.

We would ideally select
S
to maximize
\lambda_{\min}(M_{S})
, but this objective
is non-smooth and combinatorially hard. By Proposition 1(b),

\lambda_{\min}(M_{S})\;\geq\;\frac{\det(M_{S})}{\lambda_{\max}(M_{S})^{\,r-1}},

so for any subset on which
\lambda_{\max}(M_{S})
is controlled (which holds
automatically under our
\|\phi_{i}\|_{2}
-bounded features), maximizing
\det(M_{S})

lower-bounds
the worst-case directional signal. Equivalently, taking
logs,
\log\det(M_{S})
is a smooth, submodular, fully-additive-in-greedy-gain
surrogate for
\log\lambda_{\min}(M_{S})
. This is the formal statement behind
the spanning-phase intuition in §
2.1
: while
M_{S}
is rank-deficient
the right-hand side is zero, and the greedy log-det gain is the only term that
can lift it.

Coverage under the anchor/residual decomposition.

The basis
B
is built as an anchor block
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
plus a
per-dataset residual block
T_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times m_{v}}
(§
2.2
),
giving
\phi_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+m_{v}}
with two structurally different roles:

•

Anchor coordinates

\psi^{\mathrm{anchor}}_{i,\cdot}
live
in the shared subspace
B
. A reward direction
u
supported there
represents a globally-consistent safety concept, and
\lambda_{\min}
along

B
measures the worst-covered such concept.

•

Residual coordinates

\psi^{(v)}_{i,\cdot}
live in
dataset-specific orthogonal complements. A reward direction
u
with
residual support requires
at least one
sample from that dataset to
receive nonzero signal—anchor-only subsets cannot cover it.

Because
B
and
T_{v}
are orthogonal,
M_{S}
is block-structured and

\det(M_{S})
factorizes into anchor and per-dataset residual determinants
(up to cross terms that vanish when each block is independently spanned).
Maximizing
\log\det(M_{S})
therefore enforces directional coverage

within each block simultaneously
, which is precisely the failure mode
of pooled-PCA or trace-based selection: those collapse onto the dominant
anchor directions and leave residual blocks rank-deficient.

Appendix D
Dataset Descriptions

To ensure a comprehensive evaluation of safety alignment, we utilize five representative datasets covering various safety domains and preference modeling techniques. The detailed characteristics of these datasets are as follows:

•

PKU-SafeRLHF
: Developed by the Center for Human-Aligned AI (CHAAI) at Peking University, this is one of the most rigorous safety preference datasets. It contains 330k+ samples with fine-grained safety labels across 14 categories (e.g., unfairness, crime, sensitive topics). For our experiments, we utilize the
default/train
split and construct DPO pairs based on the
safer_response_id
, which represents the consensus of human annotators on which response better adheres to safety guidelines.

•

Anthropic HH-RLHF (Harmless Subset)
: This dataset is part of Anthropic’s “Helpful and Harmless” collection, specifically the
harmless-base
portion. It consists of human-model conversations where the model was tasked with being harmless. Each entry provides a “chosen” (more harmless) and “rejected” response, reflecting the model’s ability to refuse harmful requests or provide safe alternatives through red-teaming.

•

Safer-Instruct
: Safer-Instruct is an instruction-tuning dataset designed to improve the safety of LLMs through high-quality adversarial prompts. We specifically extract the
hate
and
self-harm
subsets. These categories are particularly valuable for testing the model’s refusal boundaries against explicit toxicity and high-risk personal safety scenarios, where the “chosen” responses are typically empathetic but firm refusals.

•

CValues-English
: This dataset (
david9dragon9/cvalues-english
) aligns LLMs with responsibility and sensitivity. It groups responses by safety and helpfulness level, and we map the
response_chosen
and
response_rejected
fields into our training pipeline.

•

Egida
: Egida is a specialized DPO dataset designed for safety alignment and defense against jailbreak attacks. We use the
Egida-DPO-Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
split, which provides high-quality preference pairs generated by strong teacher models (like Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). This dataset is instrumental in teaching the model to identify and resist sophisticated adversarial prompt techniques.
