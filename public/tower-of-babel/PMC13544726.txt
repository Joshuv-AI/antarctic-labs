Clinical evolution of primary hyperparathyroidism in China: A 40-year retrospective single-center study
Authors: An Song, Yingyu Chen, Rong Chen, Jiajia Wang, Shuzhong Liu, Surong Hua, Yan Jiang, Mei Li, Ya Hu, Weibo Xia, Ou Wang, Xiaoping Xing, Xunwu Meng
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT), the third most frequent endocrine disorder in Western countries, is a metabolic bone disease caused by autonomous excessive parathyroid hormone (PTH). Classic symptoms include bone resorption, nephrolithiasis, and symptoms associated with hypercalcemia.

The clinical spectrum of PHPT varies by region. In the United States and Europe, asymptomatic PHPT has been the predominant phenotype for the past 40 years. In China, PHPT is less common than in Western countries, and most clinical data come from a few tertiary centers. Recent reports from three such centers show a rising proportion (27%–55%) of asymptomatic PHPT.[1–3] However, the clinical spectrum of patients with PHPT in China over a longer period remains to be clarified.

The current study retrospectively analyzed the evolution in the clinical and biological features of patients with sporadic PHPT spanning four decades in a large tertiary referral center for metabolic bone diseases in China. The clinical characteristics of PHPT patients with different pathologies were further compared.

From 1980 to 2019, 2367 inpatients with PHPT were hospitalized at Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH). For data completeness, only cases with comprehensive medical records and initial admission to the Endocrinology Department were included. Patients with secondary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism and hereditary/familial PHPT were excluded. The definitions of hypercalcemic PHPT, normocalcemic PHPT, and asymptomatic PHPT were provided in the Supplementary Methods, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956. The clinical data were retrospectively collected, including general characteristics (e.g., gender, onset age, and duration of disease), clinical manifestations (e.g., bone involvement, urinary involvement, hypercalcemia-associated gastrointestinal symptoms, and hypercalcemic crisis), and pathological features. Follow-up data were obtained for patients who underwent parathyroidectomy. Biochemical indices included serum calcium (SCa), serum phosphorus (P), serum alkaline phosphatase, 24-hour urinary calcium, plasma ionized calcium, serum PTH, β-C-terminal telopeptide of collagen type 1, and 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The pathology of parathyroid lesions was determined by the pathologists at our center.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of PUMCH (No. I-23PJ1879) and conducted according to the principles in the Declaration of Helsinki. This data collection received ethics committee exemption from informed consent, hence consent was not obtained. The statistical analysis was provided in the Supplementary Methods, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956.

This study included 964 clinically sporadic PHPT patients (288 males and 676 females) hospitalized in the Endocrinology Department before surgery. These patients accounted for different proportions of all PHPT admissions at PUMCH over the study period: 100% (147/147) in 1980–1999, 50.95% (241/473) in 2000–2009, and 32.97% (576/1747) in 2010–2019. They came from 26 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities in China [Supplementary Figure 1A, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956]. The demographics and clinical characteristics of all patients are outlined in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956. Most were aged 40–70 years [Supplementary Figure 1B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956]. Among 964 patients, 298 (30.9%) had asymptomatic PHPT.

Asymptomatic PHPT cases increased sharply after 2000 [Supplementary Figure 1C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956]. During 2000–2019, the primary reasons for referral of the 293 asymptomatic patients were elevated SCa during health check-ups (34.5%, 101/293) and incidental parathyroid nodule(s) discovered by routine thyroid or neck ultrasounds (31.4%, 92/293). Other reasons included asymptomatic osteoporosis identified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (17.1%, 50/293), elevated PTH levels during health checks (12.6%, 37/293), and nonspecific symptoms like weakness and palpitations [Supplementary Figure 1D, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956].

The clinical and biochemical data of patients with PHPT over four decades are presented in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956. In the 2010s, both SCa (mean ± standard deviation: 2.85 ± 0.32 mmol/L vs. 2.96 ± 0.34 mmol/L, P <0.01) and PTH levels (median [first quartile, third quartile]: 170.0 [113.8, 336.9] pg/mL vs. 632.8 [252.1, 1354.6] pg/mL, P <0.01) significantly declined compared with the 2000s, while serum phosphate levels increased notably. The proportions of parathyroid hyperplasia (PH) and adenoma (PA) remained stable, but the incidence of parathyroid carcinoma (PC) significantly decreased in the 2010s (5.4% [28/522] vs. 11.2% [25/224] in the 2000s, P <0.01). Tumor size and weight have gradually and markedly decreased since the 2000s.

Overall, the PH and PA groups showed similar characteristics [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956]. Compared with those with benign diseases (PH and PA), the PC group had a significantly higher proportion of men (64.8% [46/71] vs. 27.2% [211/776], P <0.01), and more severe biochemical abnormalities. Although tumor size and weight in the patients with atypical parathyroid adenoma (APA) were comparable to the PC group, their clinical and biochemical features resembled those of benign cases [Supplementary Table 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956].

Among 893 patients who underwent pathological examinations after surgery, 882 (98.8%) achieved normal SCa and PTH levels within 3 days after surgery, among which 445 were followed for over 6 months at our center, with a median follow-up time of 50 (22, 78) months. Thirteen of the 445 followed patients (2.9%) failed to achieve remission (SCa ≥2.7 mmol/L within 6 months after surgery). Among the remaining 432 patients who attained postoperative remission, 53 experienced recurrence (12.3%), with a median time to recurrence of 108 (67, 157) months. Recurrence proportions differed significantly by pathological type, with each percentage calculated based on the corresponding pathological subgroup within the 432 remission patients: 2.8% (9/317) for PA, 10.8% (4/37) for PH, 5.7% (2/35) for APA, and 88.4% (38/43) for PC (P <0.01 for all pairwise comparisons with PC). The median nonrecurrence time for PC was 41.0 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 30.9–51.1 months), significantly shorter than for other types (P <0.001) [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C956]. Multivariate analysis indicated that hypercalcemic crisis (hazard ratio = 13.334, 95% CI: 3.157–56.327) and multiple lesions (hazard ratio = 5.357, 95% CI: 1.295–22.163) were independent risk factors for relapse in the 389 patients with nonmalignant PHPT (i.e., PH, PA, and APA).

This study presents data on the changes in the clinical spectrum of PHPT in this Chinese cohort over 40 years. Both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases increased significantly during this period. Compared with earlier reports from our center by Bilezikian and Meng[4], the proportion of asymptomatic PHPT increased from 3% before the 1990s to more than half in the last 5 years in the present study, suggesting asymptomatic PHPT now dominates the clinical landscape of PHPT in China.

This study described the proportion of APA cases within a large Chinese PHPT cohort, accounting for 8.4% (44/552) of all PHPT patients in the last decade, higher than the 0.5–7.7% reported in Western and Indian populations.[5,6] Due to its rarity and uncertain malignant potential, there is no consensus on APA management. In this and a previous study from our center, patients with APA have lower SCa and PTH levels than those with PC, and their clinical symptoms were similar to those of the benign groups.[7] The recurrence rate in APA was 5.7%, much lower than in PC. Cetani et al[5] also reported a similar recurrence rate of 3% after surgical resection of APA, with a median follow-up of 47.0 (0.3–252.0) months, whereas PC shows a recurrence rate of up to 40–60%. However, since APA shares many biochemical features with both PA and PC, preoperative diagnosis remains challenging. More large-scale research is required to clarify its clinical and genetic profiles and to guide diagnosis and postoperative monitoring.

This study has some limitations. First, as a tertiary referral center, our cohort might include more severe cases, potentially skewing the proportion of asymptomatic PHPT. Second, hereditary PHPT was only clinically excluded, so we could not completely rule out genetic factors. Last, as a retrospective study, some data—especially long-term follow-up—were missing. This retrospective, hospital-based study may be subject to selection bias, potentially overestimating the proportion of asymptomatic PHPT cases and limiting the generalizability of the findings. Future large-scale, population-based prospective studies are needed to validate our findings and better characterize PHPT in Chinese patients.

In conclusion, this study revealed a sharp increase in both symptomatic and asymptomatic PHPT diagnoses at our center since the 2000s, with a shift toward predominantly asymptomatic cases in recent years. Disease severity differed by pathological type among patients with clinically sporadic PHPT. With a recurrence rate of 12.3% over a median time of 9 years, long-term monitoring is essential. Further prospective and multicenter studies are needed to verify these findings.
