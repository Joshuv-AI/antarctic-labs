Abstract
It is known that every point on Lévy’s Dragon Curve admits a natural representation as a complex power series. We introduce a directed graph 𝒢 1 \mathcal{G}_{1} which characterizes this representation. In this paper, we study the translation of the curve by s = − 1 / 2 + i / 2 s=-1/2+i/2 . We identify another directed graph 𝒢 2 \mathcal{G}_{2} , that characterizes the translated curve and exhibits a revolving structure analogous to that of 𝒢 1 \mathcal{G}_{1} . This paper is based on an undergraduate honors thesis at University of North Texas .

Introduction

Self-similar fractals are structures that replicate their overall form at every scale, revealing intricate patterns found throughout nature, art, and mathematics. In this paper, we explore a particularly striking example: Lévy’s Dragon Curve—a self-similar curve notable for its ability to tile the complex plane.

Lévy’s Dragon Curve
L
(Figure
1
) was introduced and studied in 1938 by P. Lévy
[
6
]
. It is the unique attractor
L
in the complex plane satisfying the set equation:
L=\psi_{0}(L)\cup\psi_{1}(L)
,
where

\begin{cases}\psi_{0}(z)=(\frac{1-i}{2})z,&\\
\psi_{1}(z)=(\frac{1+i}{2})z+\frac{1-i}{2}.&\end{cases}

(1)

Figure
2
shows how
L
can be constructed by similar contractions
\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\}
from the isosceles triangle
A_{0}
with vertices
0,1
, and
\tfrac{1}{2}-\tfrac{1}{2}i
. It is easy to see from this recursive construction that the area of
L
is the same as that of
A_{0}
. In fact, Lévy proved that
L
is a space-filling curve.

A natural question that arises is whether there are more ways to express Lévy’s Dragon Curve
L
; more specifically, does an explicit representation exist? In fact, any point
z
of
L
can be represented by at least one infinite sequence
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbf{N}}
such that

z=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{x_{1}}\circ\psi_{x_{2}}\circ\dots\circ\psi_{x_{n}}(0).

In 2002, approaching the curve
L
thought the lens of functional equation, Kawamura
[
4
]
showed that any point
z
of
L
can be expressed as a complex power series. That is,

z=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\xi_{n}(1+i)^{-n},

where the infinite sequence
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\{0,1,i,-1,-i\}^{\mathbf{N}}
with the restriction that all non-zero values of
\xi_{n}
must follow the unit circle in a counter clockwise fashion, starting at 1. In other words the non-zero digits follow Figure
3
.

1
-1
i
-i

Figure 3:
The unit circle in the complex plane with rotation
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}

So we have shown that Lévy’s Dragon Curve can be expressed in a variety of different ways, each of which provide insight into the behavior of the curve. Then following question naturally arises.

Can any translation of Lev́y’s Dragon Curve be expressed in similar manner to the original Lev́y’s Dragon Curve?

In this paper, as the first step, we investigate the translation of Lévy’s Dragon Curve by
s=-1/2+i/2
.

2
Self-similar Sets and Functional Equations

The history of systematic mathematical research on self-similar sets dates back to 1981, when Hutchinson considered the non-empty compact set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the following set equation.

X=\varphi_{0}(X)\cup\varphi_{1}(X)\cup\dots\cup\varphi_{m-1}(X),

(2)

where
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{m-1}
are similarity contractions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(Recall that a map
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
similarity contraction
iff there exists a constant number
L(\varphi)\in(0,1)
so that the equality
\|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)\|=L(\varphi)\|x-y\|
holds for any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
).

Hutchinson
[
3
]
proved the following important theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Hutchinson (1981))
.

For any finite family of similarity contractions
\{\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{m-1}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, which is called an iterated function system (or IFS), there exists a unique non-empty compact solution
X
of (
2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We call
X
an attractor or a self-similar set for a given IFS.

It is well-known that any point of
X
can be represented by at least one coding sequence
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that

X=\left\{\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi_{x_{1}}\circ\varphi_{x_{2}}\circ\cdots\varphi_{x_{n}}(0):(x_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}\in\{0,1,2,\cdots(m-1)\}^{\mathbb{N}}\right\}

In this paper, we focus on a self-similar set
X
determined by IFS with 2 similarity contractions
\{\psi_{0},\psi_{1}\}
.

In 1957, G. de Rham studied the following general functional equation and showed the following result.

f(x)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{0}(f(2x)),&\qquad 0\leq x<1/2,\\
\varphi_{1}(f(2x-1)),&\qquad 1/2\leq x\leq 1,\\
\end{cases}

(3)

where
\varphi_{0},\varphi_{1}
are contractions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 2.2

(de Rham (1957))
.

There exists a unique continuous solution
f(x)
of (
3
) if and only if

\varphi_{1}(Fix(\varphi_{0}))=\varphi_{0}(Fix(\varphi_{1})),

where
Fix(\varphi_{0}),Fix(\varphi_{1})
are the unique fixed points of
\varphi_{0}
and
\varphi_{1}
, respectively. We call
x
a fixed point of
\varphi
if
\varphi(x)=x
, and we denote this as
x=Fix(\varphi)
.

Recall that Lévy’s Dragon Curve
L
is a self-similar set on
\mathbb{C}
, since
L
is a unique attractor generated by (
1
).

Consider the following functional equation:

f(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha f(2x),&\qquad 0\leq x<1/2,\\
(1-\alpha)f(2x-1)+\alpha,&\qquad 1/2\leq x\leq 1,\end{cases}

(4)

where
\alpha=(1-i)/2
.

Notice that (
4
) is a special case of de Rham’s functional equation (
3
) so then by applying de Rham’s theorem we have the following corollary.

Corollary 2.3
.

There exists a unique continuous solution
f(x)
of (
4
).

Proof.

Let
\psi_{0}=\alpha f(2x)
and
\psi_{1}=(1-\alpha)f(2x-1)+\alpha
, with
\alpha=(1-i)/2
. Observe that
Fix(\psi_{0})\text{ and }Fix(\psi_{1})
are 0 and 1 respectively. Then

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(Fix(\psi_{0}))=\psi_{1}(0)=\alpha

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(Fix(\psi_{1}))=\psi_{0}(1)=\alpha

Therefore, by Theorem 2.2 there exists an unique continuous solution of (
4
).
∎

Observe that the unique continuous solution of (
4
) is a complex-valued function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
and the image
f([0,1])
is Lévy’s dragon curve
L
. In other words,
\{f(x);0\leq x\leq 1\}
is a parametrization of Lévy’s dragon curve
L
.

Figure 4:
The graph of the complex-valued function
f(x)
. The color corresponds to the x-axis, which is the index set [0,1], with pink at 0, and red at 1. The y-axis and z-axis, are the real and imaginary components of
f(x)
respectively.

3
Revolving Representation of Lévy’s Dragon Curve

First, we define the binary expansion of
x\in[0,1]
as follows.

Definition 3.1
.

The binary expansion of
x\in[0,1]
is denoted by

x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}2^{-n}=(0.\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\cdots)_{2}\qquad\omega_{n}=\omega_{n}(x)\in\{0,1\}.

(5)

For
x\in[0,1]
with two binary expansions, choose the expansion with trailing zeros. However, if
x=1
, fix
\omega_{n}(x)=1
for every
n
.

Let
q(x,n)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\omega_{k}
. Equivalently,
q(x,n)
is the number of
1
’s occurring in the first
n
binary digits of
x
. By convention,
q(x,0)=0
.

In 2002, Kawamura studied the functional equation (
4
) where
\alpha
is a complex parameter satisfying
|\alpha|<1
, and gave the following explicit formula for the continuous solution of (
4
).

Theorem 3.2

(Kawamura, 2002
[
4
]
)
.

The unique continuous solution of (
4
) has the following expression.

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(x)\alpha^{n-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)},\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

(6)

Kawamura also showed that if
\alpha=(1-i)/2
,
f(x)
can be expressed as a complex power series restricted by a special
revolving condition
. That is, if
\alpha=(1-i)/2
, then
\alpha^{-1}=(1+i)
and

f(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(x)\alpha^{n}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}(x)(1+i)^{-n}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1.

(7)

Let
\xi_{n}:=\omega_{n}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}
. Observe that
\xi_{n}\in\{0,1,-1,i,-i\}
with the restriction that the non-zero values must follow the cyclic pattern from left to right:

1\to i\to(-1)\to(-i)\to 1\to\cdots.

The sequence
(\xi_{n})
varies depending on
x\in[0,1]
. Figure
5
shows the directed-graph
\mathcal{G}_{1}
of
(\xi_{n})
. In Figure
3
there is no characterization of the 0 digit, however it maintains a memory function in which the number preceding the 0 restricts the following digit. To describe this difference we create the following directed graph
\mathcal{G}_{1}
:

Figure 5:
The directed-graph
\mathcal{G}_{1}
, that determines the points of the Lévy’s Dragon Curve
L

In 2021, Kawamura and Allen
[
5
]
introduced the definition of
Generalized Revolving Condition
as follows.

Let
\theta
be an angle with
-\pi<\theta\leq\pi
and a rational multiple of
2\pi
. More precisely, there are
p\in\mathbf{N},q\in\mathbf{N}_{0}
such that
|\theta|=\frac{2\pi q}{p}
.

Define

\Delta_{\theta}:=\{0,1,e^{i\theta},e^{2i\theta},\cdots e^{(p-1)i\theta}\}.

Definition 3.3
.

A sequence
(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots)\in\Delta_{\theta}^{\mathbf{N}}
satisfies the Generalized Revolving Condition (GRC), if the subsequence obtained after the removal of its zero elements is a (finite or infinite) truncation of the sequence
(e^{i\theta})
. More precisely, let
(n_{i}):=\{n:\delta_{n}\neq 0\}
. Then,

\delta_{n_{i+1}}=e^{i\theta}\delta_{n_{i}}.

Notice that
\delta_{n}
moves on the unit circle counterclockwise if
\theta>0
, and clockwise if
\theta<0
.

Fix
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
so that
\Delta=\{0,1,i,-1,-i\}
.
Observe that any sequence
(\xi_{n})\in\Delta^{\mathbf{N}}_{\frac{\pi}{2}}
satisfies the GRC. Since
L=f([0,1])
, we have

L=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\xi_{n}\alpha^{n}:(\xi_{n})\mbox{ follows the directed-graph $\mathcal{G}_{1}$}\right\},

(8)

where
\alpha=(1-i)/2.
That is, any point
z
of L is characterized by a sequence
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
satisfying the GRC with
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

4
Main Results

We investigate the translation of
L
by
s=-1/2+i/2
and call this the s-shifted Lévy’s Dragon Curve:
L_{s}
.
L_{s}
inherits the self-similarity of
L
, so it is also the attractor of an IFS
\{\phi_{0},\phi_{1}\}
. Let
S(z)=z-\frac{1-i}{2}
on
\mathbb{C}
.
We obtain these functions by modifying
\psi_{i}
in the following way:

\phi_{i}=S\circ\psi_{i}\circ S^{-1}(z)

Thus,
L_{s}
is the unique attractor
L_{s}=\phi_{0}(L_{s})\cup\phi_{1}(L_{s})
, which is generalized by the following modified IFS:

\begin{cases}\phi_{0}(z)=(\frac{1-i}{2})(z-s)+s=(\frac{1-i}{2})z-\frac{1}{2},&\\
\phi_{1}(z)=(\frac{1+i}{2})(z-s)+(\frac{1-i}{2})+s=(\frac{1+i}{2})z+\frac{1}{2}&\end{cases}

(9)

Then a natural question arises: Can any point
z
of
L_{s}
be expressed as a complex power series with a similar sequence satisfying the GRC?

Consider the following functional equation

G(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha G(2x)-\frac{1}{2}&\qquad 0\leq x<\frac{1}{2},\\
(1-\alpha)G(2x-1)+\frac{1}{2}&\qquad\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq 1,\\
\end{cases}

(10)

where
\alpha=(1-i)/2
.
Notice that (
10
) is similar, but not equivalent to (
4
). It is also another special case of de Rham’s functional equation (
3
). Therefore, using Theorem 2.2, we have

Lemma 4.1
.

There exists a unique continuous solution
G(x)
of (
10
).

Proof.

Every contraction
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
has a unique fixed point
Fix(\phi_{i})
in
\mathbb{C}
. Since
Fix(\phi_{0})=\frac{1}{2(\alpha-1)}=\frac{-1+i}{2}
and
Fix(\phi_{1})=\frac{1}{2\alpha}=\frac{1+i}{2}
, observe that

\phi_{1}(Fix(\phi_{0}))=0=\phi_{0}(Fix(\phi_{1})).

∎

Now, the main interest is to find an explicit expression of
G(x)
. Observe that from (
10
), we have

•

If
x=0
,
G(0)=\alpha G(0)-1/2\implies G(0)=\frac{-1}{2(1-\alpha)}=\frac{-1+i}{2}
.

•

If
x=1
,
G(1)=(1-\alpha)G(1)+1/2\implies G(1)=\frac{1}{2\alpha}=\frac{1+i}{2}
.

•

If
x=1/2
,
G(1/2)=(1-\alpha)G(0)+1/2\implies G(1/2)=0
.

•

If
x=1/4
,
G(1/4)=\alpha G(1/2)-1/2\implies G(1/4)=-1/2
.

•

If
x=3/4
,
G(3/4)=(1-\alpha)G(1/2)+1/2\implies G(3/4)=1/2
.

Inductively, we can calculate
G(x)
for all dyadic points
x\in[0,1]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For any dyadic point
x\in(0,1)
; that is, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\omega_{k}=1
and
\omega_{n}(x)=0
for
\forall n>k
,
G(x)
has the following expression.

G(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

(11)

where
\alpha
=
(1-i)/2
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Let

F(x):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{k-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}.

Recall that
G(x)
is the unique continuous solution of (
10
).

Let
P(k)
be the statement:

F(x)=G(x)\mbox{ for all }x\mbox{ of the form }x=(0.\omega_{1}\cdots.\omega_{k-1}1)_{2}.

First, consider the base case
k=1
. Since
x=(0.1)_{2}=1/2
, it is clear that the statement
P(1)
holds as

\displaystyle F(1/2)=F((0.1)_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{0}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=0=G(1/2).

Second, consider the case
k=2
, that is
x=(0.01)_{2}=1/4
or
x=(0.11)_{2}=3/4

\displaystyle F(1/4)=F((0.01)_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{1-0}\cdot\alpha^{0}(1-\alpha)^{0}=-\frac{1}{2}=G(1/4).

\displaystyle F(3/4)=F((0.11)_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(-1)^{1-1}\cdot\alpha^{0}(1-\alpha)^{0}=\frac{1}{2}=G(3/4).

Therefore the statement P(2) holds.

We continue the proof by induction. Let
l\geq 2
and assume that the statement
P(l)
is true; that is,

F(x)=G(x)\mbox{ for all }x\mbox{ of the form }x=(0.\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\omega_{3}\dots\omega_{l-1}1)_{2}.

We want to prove
P(l+1)
.

Consider
x
having the form
x=(0.\omega_{1}\omega_{2}\omega_{3}\dots\omega_{l}1)_{2}
.
Notice that for
0\leq x<1/2
,
\omega_{1}=0
and
q(2x,n-1)=q(x,n)
and for
1/2\leq x\leq 1
,
\omega_{1}=1
and
q(2x-1,n-1)=q(x,n)-1
.

If
0\leq x<1/2
,

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=F(0.0\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{l}1)_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=2}^{l}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n+1}(x)}\alpha^{n-q(x,n)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(2x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(2x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(2x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha F(2x)

Then observe that
2x=(0.\omega 2\omega 3\omega 4\dots\omega_{l-1}1)
.
Therefore
P(l)
holds by assumption,

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}+\alpha G(2x)=G(x)

If
1/2\leq x\leq 1
,

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=F(0.1\omega_{2}\dots\omega_{l}1)_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=2}^{l}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n+1}(x)}\alpha^{n-q(x,n)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{1-{\omega_{n}(2x-1)}}\alpha^{n-q(2x-1,n-1)-1}(1-\alpha)^{q(2x-1,n-1)+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{(1-\alpha)}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(2x-1)}\alpha^{n-1-q(2x-1,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(2x-1,n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+(1-\alpha)F(2x-1)

Again, observe that
2x=(0.\omega 2\omega 3\omega 4\dots\omega_{l-1}1)
.
Therefore
P(l)
holds by assumption, we have

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}+(1-\alpha)G(2x-1)=G(x)

Therefore, we conclude that the statement
P(k)
is true for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Recall
\exists k\in\mathbb{N}\text{ such that }\omega_{k}=1
and
\omega_{n}=0
for
\forall n>k
, if
x
is a dyadic point. Therefore,
q(x,k-1)+1=q(x,k)=q(x,k+l)
for
\forall l\in\mathbf{N}
. Let

H(x):=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle H(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((-1)^{0}\alpha^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)}+\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}}\alpha^{n-1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,n-1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)}+(-1)^{1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)+1}\sum_{n=k+1}^{\infty}\alpha^{n-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha^{k-1}\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)}-\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)+1}\left(\frac{\alpha^{k}}{1-\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha^{k-1}\right)\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^{q(x,k-1)}\left(1-\left(\frac{1-\alpha}{1}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0

Therefore for a dyadic point
x\in[0,1]

G(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}.

Since the set of dyadic points is dense in [0,1] and
G(x)
is the unique continuous solution, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

The unique continuous solution of G(x) of (
10
) can be expressed as follows.

G(x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}\qquad x\in[0,1]

(12)

Where
\alpha=(1-i)/2
.

Finally, recall our main question, Is any point
z
of
L_{s}=G([0,1])
also characterized by a sequence satisfying the GRC?

Using (
7
) as a model, we have

\displaystyle G(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n-1-q(x,n-1)}(1-\alpha)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}\alpha^{n}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\alpha^{n}(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}.

Let
\gamma_{n}:=(-1)^{1-\omega_{n}(x)}(i)^{q(x,n-1)}
. Observe that
\gamma_{n}\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}
, which does not include
0
.
(\gamma_{n})
moves on the unit circle, but the movement varies depending on
x\in[0,1]
.

For example, consider the binary expansion of
x=(0.100110110)_{2}
. Then the corresponding digit sequence
(\gamma_{n})
is

(\gamma_{n})=(1,-i,-i,i,-1,i,-i,1,-i)

It is clear that any sequence
(\gamma_{n})\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}^{\mathbb{N}}
does not satisfy the GRC.

Loosely speaking, we can say that the sequence
(\gamma_{n})\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}^{\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence on the unit circle and with subsequent terms which must perform one of the following actions:

•

Stay in place

•

Move 1 step or 2 steps forward (counterclockwise) on the unit circle

•

move 1 step backward (clockwise) on the unit circle

Initially,
(\gamma_{n})
is seemingly much more complicated than the sequence
(\xi_{n})
. In fact, it is rather difficult to find the similarity between the revolving structures of the sequences
(\xi_{n})
and
(\gamma_{n})
.

However, from the viewpoint of the directed graph, the similarity is clear. We see that the sequence
(\gamma_{n})\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}^{\mathbb{N}}
follows the directed-graph
\mathcal{G}_{2}
shown in Figure (
6
).

The directed-graph
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, that determines the points of the Shifted Lévy’s Dragon Curve

Another use of this directed graph it to generate the digit sequence
(\gamma_{n})
directly from the binary sequence of

5
Future work

In this paper, we investigated a particular shifted Lévy’s dragon curve
L_{s}
and introduced the directed-graph
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, which characterizes
L_{s}
.

A natural next step is to examine the behavior under more general transformations. Specifically, let
\lambda
be an arbitrary scaling factor and
\tau
an arbitrary translation. Consider the set obtained by scaling the original Lévy’s dragon curve
L

by
\tau
and then scale by
\lambda
. Call the resulting set
L_{\lambda,\tau}
. It is straightforward to verify that
L_{\lambda,\tau}
is the unique attractor satisfying
L_{\lambda,\tau}=g_{0}(L_{\lambda,\tau})\cup g_{1}(L_{\lambda,\tau})
,
where the IFS is given by

\begin{cases}g_{0}(z)=(\frac{1-i}{2})z+(\frac{1+i}{2})\tau,\\
g_{1}(z)=(\frac{1+i}{2})z+(\frac{1-i}{2})(\tau+\lambda).\end{cases}

Furthermore, this naturally raises the question of whether the attractor
L_{\lambda,\tau}
admits an explicit representation analogous to (
13
). In particular, one may ask whether
L_{\lambda,\tau}
can be described via a modified sequence governed by a directed graph

\mathcal{G}_{3}
, whose structure is analogous to those of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}
.
