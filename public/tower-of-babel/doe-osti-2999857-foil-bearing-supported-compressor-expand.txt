Executive Summary
R&D Dynamics (RDD) was founded in 1990. The Principals, while working for Honeywell
and United Technologies Corp, pioneered foil bearing supported Air Cycle Machines, a
key component of aircraft Environmental Control Systems. The technology developed is
used today by 95% of the world’s aircraft, civil or military. R&D Dynamics was started
with the intention of bringing aerospace technology to the commercial world.

Every system has a key moving equipment. The foil bearing technology developed by
Principals of RDD improves the reliability and performance of the key moving equipment
by being completely oil free and allowing it to run at high speed. This results in equipment
that is efficient, smaller, lighter and more reliable than conventional Compressor-
Expander technologies. The goal of this program is to develop a fuel cell system
Compressor-Expander for heavy-duty vehicle applications. The Compressor-Expander
is the key component of the fuel cell balance of plant (BOP), which drives the
performance, reliability, service life and cost. The Compressor-Expander must meet
pressure and flow requirements efficiently over a wide operating range. It must be highly
reliable and have a long service life. It must also be versatile to allow broad usage across
many fuel cell platforms, and it must be able to be mass produced at lower cost.

Foil bearings are the enabling technology for this program. Foil bearings are inherently
oil-free, thus eliminating any possibility of oil contamination of the fuel cell. They also
enable very high operating speeds. High operating speed allows the compressor and
turbine to run at peak efficiently. High operating speed also results in smaller size and
weight.

The Compressor-Expander requires a high speed motor and motor drive. A DC
permanent magnet (DCPM) motor is used because it has a higher power density
 Page 2 of 46

compared with other motor types. The motor drive has silicon carbide switches (SiC
MOSFETs) that allow much higher switching frequencies compared with IGBTs. The
higher switching frequency is needed for the high operating speed. The motor drive
utilizes sensorless motor control technology, thus no rotor position feedback is required.

The result of the combination of these technologies is a unit that allows automotive and
heavy duty truck fuel cell manufacturers to deliver small, efficient and extremely reliable
fuel cell air management systems to their customers at a reasonable price.

Background
Existing products in the hydrogen fuel cell compressor space utilize an assortment of
technologies. Common among those products is the use of centrifugal compressors and
radial turbines. This technology is applied within the fuel cell compressor space because
of their small size which enables them to fit within tight engine bays of cars, trucks, and
heavy machinery. For decades, the same impeller technology has been used in
automotive turbochargers which boost the amount of air forced into an internal
combustion engine. They are reliable within their operating environment and produce
ample gains in power for the internal combustion engine with which they are integrated.
Other technologies like variable area turbine nozzles, wastegates, and compressor
casing treatments matured with turbochargers to extend their range and efficiency.

There are two critical limitations which prevent traditional turbocharger designs from being
directly implemented in a hydrogen fuel cell. The first limitation is the power source.
Common turbochargers are powered only by the exhaust gases from an internal
combustion engine. Hydrogen fuel cells do not produce a rise in pressure or volume
which would be enough to spin a turbocharger to the required pressure and flow for the
fuel cell. The solution is to include a high-speed electric motor to spin the turbomachine.
An expander could be optionally included to reduce the input power required. The
addition of an electric motor to a turbocharger does increase the complexity of the
turbomachine and requires a high-speed motor drive, but it does enable centrifugal
technology to be potentially applied to hydrogen fuel cells.

The second limitation is oil contamination. Traditional turbochargers utilize oil bearing
technology to support the high-speed machinery. While reliable for applications within an
internal combustion engine, oil from the bearings can enter the process flow of a
turbocharger. Internal combustion engines just burn this oil, but hydrogen fuel cells are
sensitive to oil contamination. Any amount of contamination has the potential to poison
the fuel cell stack, rendering it inoperable and stranding the end user of a hydrogen fuel
cell vehicle. Efforts to reduce the oil in the process gas can be undertaken, but if the
mitigation strategies like oil separators and better seals fail, the risk of damage to a
hydrogen fuel cell stack is high.

If turbocharger technology were to be applied to a hydrogen fuel cell balance of plant, oil
bearings would have to be eliminated from the turbomachine. Some companies presently
marketing hydrogen fuel cell compressors have recognized this limitation. Current
commercially available compressors utilize gas bearings or traditional ball bearings. The
gas bearing machines are supported using spiral groove bearings. While ball bearings
can support large loads, they are speed limited. A gearbox would be required between
the centrifugal compressor and ball bearing motor to fully capture the benefits of both
technologies, adding complexity and more spinning parts. Spiral groove bearings can
also support large loads at high speeds, but do not have the capability to support high
shock loads.

Current fuel cell compressor technology has drawn from other industries and applications
but has not coalesced around a particular formula for the turbomachine. A novel
 Page 5 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

application of an existing bearing technology could elevate the performance and reliability
of future hydrogen fuel cell compressors. Foil gas bearings have been utilized in Air Cycle
Machines found in 95% of the world's aircraft. This technology operates at the high
speeds demanded by centrifugal machinery without oil contamination. They are
compatible with electric motors. They also support high shock loads which are commonly
found on highways and roads traveled by vehicles today. Yet, the technology has not
been applied to marketed fuel cell compressors. An opportunity exists to improve fuel
cell compressors with world-proven foil gas bearing technology.

Project Objectives
The objective of this project is to design, develop, manufacture, and test an innovative
oil-free, foil gas bearing supported compressor-expander (CE) and motor drive for heavy
duty truck fuel cell systems. The successful completion of this project will yield a CE and
motor drive which are optimized for the specific DOE pressure and flow requirements
stipulated in the FOA. The same system will be as efficient as possible over a broad
operating range to minimize parasitic losses, small and lightweight to minimize space
usage, durable and reliable to maximize time in service, and inexpensive at high volume
to minimize costs once reaching production levels.

R&D Dynamics (RDD) will meet these goals by building and testing a CE and motor drive
of its own design and comparing their performance to the DOE’s air management system
targets and RDD’s predicted performance. The CE and motor drive will become available
for demonstration and further development testing after the conclusion of the project.

The CE is the key component of the fuel cell balance of plant (BOP), which means that it
drives a significant portion of the performance, reliability, service life, and cost of the fuel
cell. The BOP performance requirements for hydrogen fuel cells are increasing in terms
of pressure and flow, especially now that high-power applications like heavy-duty trucks
are being considered. The CE is critical in this capacity as it is the source of the pressure
and flow from the BOP. RDD must optimize the CE and motor drive to comfortably
produce the necessary pressure and flow for high power operation.

The efficiency of the BOP must be considered. While the BOP is necessary for operating
the fuel cell, any losses within the BOP are detrimental to the available power for an
application, in this case a heavy-duty truck, because it is a parasitic load. Maximizing the
efficiency of every component within the BOP will increase the available power for the
fuel cell or allow the fuel cell to be better optimized for the application. RDD must keep
efficiency in mind when developing the CE and motor drive not only at the maximum
pressure and flow conditions but over the broad range of operation required by a fuel cell
BOP.

Size and weight must be considered for the BOP in a heavy-duty truck fuel cell
application. While a heavy-duty truck is much larger than a passenger car and could
permit more space consumption, overuse of space by the BOP limits the amount of room
for other components like those for the fuel cell itself. A larger BOP may tend to be a
heavier one which would require the fuel cell to utilize more power. This wasted energy
 Page 6 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

reduces the efficiency of the fuel cell vehicle. RDD must reduce CE and motor drive size
and weight without affecting BOP performance.

The BOP application requirements of a heavy-duty truck environment demand high
reliability and durability to be competitive with incumbent technologies. Durable BOP
systems will last longer and travel further thus being more reliable. The ability to avoid
costly downtime on the road, especially when access to maintenance facilities is limited,
is very important. The technology composing the BOP, therefore, must be incredibly
durable and reliable to limit any scheduled or unexpected maintenance.

A strong advantage that incumbent technologies in the heavy-duty truck space possess
is low cost at high volumes. This advantage will allow those technologies to remain
relevant as new options like hydrogen fuel cells develop and try to penetrate those
markets. Finding a path towards achieving low cost at high volumes for the technology
composing the BOP is critical for the acceptance of hydrogen fuel cells in the heavy-duty
vehicle space.

RDD intends to utilize several technologies within its CE and motor drive to achieve
project objectives. Central to the success of RDD’s CE is the use of foil gas bearings.
Foil gas bearings utilize the process gas to lubricate the bearings and support the rotor,
in this case the air entering the cathode side of the fuel cell. Since process air is used
rather than oil or other fluids, there is zero risk of contaminating the fuel cell stack with
bearing lubricants. This is critical because even a small amount of oil will damage the
fuel cell. Foil gas bearings are a natural fit for this application.

The bearing technology RDD will use in its CE was pioneered decades ago in the air
cycle machine (ACM). ACMs are the refrigeration units within the environmental control
system of an aircraft that provide conditioned air to the crew and passengers. Many
aircraft in service today utilize ACMs supported by foil gas bearings because of the
extended reliability and durability of the technology. ACMs are usually overhauled after
10 years of continuous service without any other scheduled maintenance on the ACM.
Over the course of their service lives, ACMs must withstand start-stop cycles on the order
of 100,000 which are potentially abusive to the low friction coatings on the bearings. They
must also withstand dynamic loads like high external vibration or shock events during
takeoff, cruising, and landing of the aircraft.

RDD’s CE must withstand the same kinds of abuse as an ACM during its lifecycle. Heavy-
duty trucks can start and stop their vehicles multiple times a day depending on the needs
of the operator. Heavy-duty trucks operate over a wide variety of road conditions that can
create large external vibrations and shock loads. And the desired maintenance interval
for a fuel cell BOP CE is on the same order of magnitude as an ACM. ACMs therefore
share many of the same durability and reliability requirements as a turbomachine inside
a heavy-duty truck fuel cell. Foil gas bearings can be leveraged to extend the reliability
and durability of a heavy-duty fuel cell truck BOP like an ACM.

RDD has chosen to utilize high-speed centrifugal compressors and radial turbines to
produce the necessary pressure and flow for the fuel cell and to reduce the required input
power to the system. Centrifugal compressors can produce high pressure ratios and
efficiencies while being small and lightweight. However, those size benefits are only
realized while the compressor is operating at high speeds, otherwise multiple compressor
stages or a larger diameter compressor are necessary to achieve similar performance.
Foil gas bearings can easily support turbomachinery at high speeds with minimal bearing
losses due to the increase in speed. RDD has paired these technologies together to
leverage the advantages of both in one package. Radial turbines are compatible with foil
gas bearings for the same reasons.

RDD can utilize additional technologies to improve the operating region breadth of both
compressor and turbine with respect to fuel cell requirements. A surge bypass valve can
be integrated with the CE to broaden the operating region of the compressor into the
typical surge region of the compressor. The valve diverts excess flow back into the
process stream after the fuel cell but upstream of the turbine inlet. This allows the turbine
to recover more energy than it normally would and boosts the overall efficiency of the CE.
Casing treatments on the compressor inlet can also be used to further broaden the
compressor map into what would otherwise be a surge region for a given impeller
geometry.

RDD can also utilize variable compressor and turbine geometries to achieve greater
efficiencies over a broader operating range. Inlet guide vanes (IGVs) can be used to
adjust the efficiency of the compressor on off-design days, reducing the amount of power
consumed by the CE. A variable area turbine nozzle (VATN) can be used to increase the
amount of power recovered by the turbine in general but can also boost the efficiency of
the turbine. Applying both technologies can further increase the region where the CE
runs efficiently.

RDD can innovate within its motor drive and motor as well. Powering the CE will be a DC
permanent magnet (DCPM) motor. Because of the high-speed requirement for the
impellers and bearings and the use of a DC source voltage, the switches within the motor
drive must allow a high switching frequency. RDD has chosen to use silicon carbide (SiC)
MOSFETs for this task. SiC MOSFETs have the benefit of operating at high switching
frequencies with lower thermal losses. This enables SiC MOSFETs to tolerate higher
coolant temperatures. Additionally, the switches can be made compact, allowing them to
fit into smaller motor drives. RDD is leveraging this technology to create a small motor
drive which will spin the CE at the required speed for the fuel cell pressure and flow
requirements.

Project Results and Discussion
The first project milestone was to complete the preliminary design for the CE and motor
drive. The task entailed developing the product definition and performing an initial sizing
of the compressor, turbine, motor, bearings, and motor drive based on the product
definition and the system targets provided by DOE. This included identifying geometry,
speed, and power requirements. Preliminary calculations could then be made to iterate
and refine the design under the next milestone.

RDD worked with our industry partner, Loop Energy, to develop a product definition
pertaining to the fuel cell. The aerodynamic targets found in the FOA became the basis
for all the performance requirements for this project. The FOA was also the source for
the reliability specification within our standard. RDD drew from its prior experience
making automotive fuel cell compressors to define the operating environment,
classification, and other high-level standards regarding safety, vibration, and ingress
protection. The same experience defined the water cooling supply requirements, low
voltage power parameters, and the communication scheme to be used with the CE and
motor drive.

Loop Energy was able to provide feedback for the specific process gas entering the
turbine from the fuel cell. RDD supplied them with the aerodynamic targets from DOE
looking for feedback regarding how realistic they were. Loop Energy commented that the
fuel cell would benefit from operating at a greater pressure ratio but with the same mass
flow as compared to the DOE’s 100% flow operating point. Loop Energy suggested a
pressure ratio of 3.2 versus the original DOE target pressure ratio for the same mass flow
rate of 285 g/s. RDD incorporated this feedback into the performance requirements of
the system. Loop also suggested high voltage power parameters based on industry
norms for fuel cells of this size.

RDD’s prior experience with turbomachinery formed the other requirements within the
specification. CE health and safety specifications like volute burst pressure, maximum
thrust load, rotor balancing, and available instrumentation were defined. And the
connections for air, water, power, and data were defined. With all these details collected,
RDD with Loop Energy’s help finished the product definition for the CE and motor drive.

Prior to the start of this program and for a different customer, RDD developed a fuel cell
CE for an automotive application. RDD and this customer had great success within that
program, so to build upon that success, RDD chose to use that program as the basis for
the design of the CE for this project. This choice gave us initial designs and sizes for the
impellers, motor, and bearings. But the power rating of the base design was too low for
this project based on the product definition. Thus, the components would need to be
scaled and adjusted to make them compatible with the new application.

The compressor impeller diameter and blade geometry, initially taken from the existing
fuel cell CE library, was scaled up using proprietary aero design software. Using the
software, the impeller was analyzed until the performance of the scaled rotor was
compatible with RDD’s product definition for the compressor. From this analysis, RDD
obtained a maximum operating speed of 92 kRPM and chose to consider it an overspeed
condition for the CE. Additional refinement of the geometry of the compressor impeller
enabled RDD to place the Loop Energy operating point at 88 kRPM. The remainder of
the DOE’s target operating points were predicted to occur at lower speeds than that.

Using the overspeed condition and the aerodynamic requirements, RDD adjusted the size
of the turbine to be compatible with this fuel cell application. Similarly, appropriately sized
foil gas bearings were selected from our library of existing bearings based on the known
conditions of the impellers. With a rough idea of the shaft power required by the impellers,
the motor was scaled from the reference design. RDD determined that with the
preliminary compressor and turbine geometry and the overspeed condition defined, the
motor would need to be rated for approximately 35 kW and be larger than the reference
design.

The reference design utilized a two-pole motor which was sufficient for that application.
RDD decided to consider using a four-pole motor to understand if the size of the motor
could be reduced while maintaining the power requirement. The initial motor design used
in the mechanical layout was a four-pole motor. For both types, the rotor and stator were
scaled from the reference design to match the new speed and power requirements. Using
the design requirements, an initial length and diameter of the motor rotor were selected,
then an analysis was conducted to optimize the stress in the rotor assembly. Per the
results of the analysis, the stator stack was sized for the four-pole motor.

Knowing the power requirements of the motor, the motor drive was sized. RDD
completed software simulations to model the architecture of the motor drive. The
simulations allowed RDD to make choices regarding the size of those components and
their anticipated power losses. A subscale preliminary drive design rated to 10 kW was
designed for bench testing based partly on these simulations to verify the architecture of
the power electronics. RDD used this design to cost-effectively ensure that a higher-
power motor drive could operate within the same basic architecture as the lower-power
version.

Using the product definition, RDD identified the software requirements for the motor drive.
The motor drive had to communicate with the rest of the vehicle using the CAN 2.0B
protocol. This requirement influenced the design of the control board and mandated the
development of a GUI to interface with the motor drive within the lab space. At the same
time, RDD had to apply our proprietary sensorless motor control algorithms to the motor
drive. The control algorithms were tested in simulation first and then applied to the 10
kW drive ahead of testing using the high-power drive.

Each of these choices were used to create a preliminary layout of the CE and motor drive
based on their reference designs. The preliminary layout was used in the next phase of

The second project milestone was a detailed design and analysis of the CE and motor
drive. The task entailed finalizing the design of the compressor, turbine, bearings, motor,
and motor drive based on the initial designs from the preliminary design phase.
Additionally, the finalized layouts of the PCBs within the motor drive and finished solid
models with prints were a part of this task. Supporting the final design of the CE and
motor drive would be analyses for critical speed, thrust load, cooling, and fits and
clearances. Work towards completing this milestone began in Q3 2022.

RDD used an iterative approach for the detailed design of the CE and motor drive. This
approach allowed for each component of the design to be analyzed and improved in
parallel. As changes for each component became available, they were incorporated into
the main layout and reanalyzed. Many cases for each major component were considered
during this phase, especially as conflicts were resolved to harmonize the overall design.
A design for the CE and what became known as the V1 motor drive coalesced during this
period using this technique. The V2 motor drive was developed from the V1 motor drive
after the initial testing period of the CE and will be discussed later.

RDD finalized the design of the compressor, diffuser, and volute during this milestone.
Using the design specified during the preliminary design review, the geometries of the
                                    Page 13 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

hub and blades of the impeller were refined and optimized such that they met the DOE’s
targets and Loop’s point. A 3D solid model of the revised compressor impeller design
was created and incorporated into the system layout. RDD completed multiple FEAs to
investigate the stress and deflection in the impeller at the overspeed condition. Initially,
RDD found that the rotor stress was marginal at the overspeed condition. Many iterations
of this analysis were completed as material was added and subtracted to eliminate the
areas of high stresses. The impeller back geometry and seal diameter were altered after
considering the thrust load analysis of the CE. More FEA iterations were completed with
the choice for the seal diameter finalized and eventually RDD struck a balance between
the stresses in the impeller and the functional requirements of the system.

In parallel, the 3D models for the volute and diffuser were created. A fixed vane diffuser
was selected and scaled up from the reference design. The scaled diffuser was analyzed
with the final version of the compressor impeller and determined to be appropriate for the
application. The compressor volute was scaled up from the reference design. RDD
smoothed the profiles inside the volute within the solid models to eliminate trip points.
RDD refined the inlet and outlet sizes of the volute to accommodate the required mass
flow and pressure ratio at Loop’s point. And a casing treatment was sized and
incorporated into the compressor volute to broaden the compressor map. Both details

Upon further review of the compressor, RDD determined that the compressor would be
operating in surge at the DOE’s 50% flow and idle operating points. To accommodate
the existing compressor design, RDD implemented a surge bypass valve at the outlet of
the compressor attached directly to the volute. The bypass valve would divert excess
flow away from the fuel cell but would keep the compressor within its operating region.
RDD chose to divert the extra flow into the inlet of the turbine. Doing this would reduce
the required shaft power by allowing the turbine to recover extra energy than it would
have from the fuel cell exhaust alone. An opening was sized and iterated, and an off-the-
shelf valve was selected which could accommodate the required extra flow at the 50%
flow point.

RDD conducted a trade study during the detailed design of the motor. The goal of the
study was to compare the performance and economic advantages of two-pole and four-
pole motors. Within the same study, RDD also considered using Inconel or carbon fiber
for the magnet sleeve and silicon or amorphous steel for the stator stack. Each version
of the motor targeted the same outer diameter and length of the stator stack. Initially,
variations in the slot geometry and rotor construction were tolerated, however as the
design was refined, those details were fixed between cases.

RDD chose to incorporate significant margin when determining the rated power of the
motor. RDD did this based on experience within the automotive fuel cell space, which
has shown us that not all customers need an expander in their systems. Because an
expander reduces the amount of shaft power, the elimination of the expander would
significantly increase the required motor power for the same compressor operating point.
With the expander, the rated power of the motor would only need to be around 35 kW,
but to make this product a versatile platform RDD chose to rate the motor for this project
to 55 kW.

For the same torque requirement, two-pole and four-pole motors were designed and
simulated in the motor design software “SPEED.” Silicon steel was selected in both
instances. The slot geometry and rotor construction were optimized for the cases. For
the four-pole motor, concentric winding was selected whereas for the two-pole motor, lap
winding was chosen to raise the winding factor. The major takeaway from this
comparison was that the iron losses in the four-pole motor were much higher those for
the two-pole motor. This is due to the higher electrical frequency required from doubling
of the number of poles. The switching losses were reduced in the two-pole configuration

                                      Page 16 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

by 45% compared to the four-pole one. RDD also determined that manufacturing a four-
pole motor rotor would be much more difficult and expensive compared to a two-pole one.
As a result, RDD chose to utilize a two-pole configuration for the motor in this project.

During the two-pole and four-pole motor comparison, RDD also included the effects of
using Inconel or carbon fiber for the magnet sleeve. Carbon fiber had the benefit of
reducing losses by 39% compared to the original sleeve thickness for Inconel in both the
two-pole and four-pole cases. When sizing the Inconel sleeve, a balance between
magnetic losses and assembly stresses had to be maintained. A revised stress analysis
showed that the Inconel sleeve thickness could be reduced without exceeding the yield
stresses of the magnet or sleeve. Once that change was incorporated, the benefit of
using a carbon fiber magnet sleeve diminished. Due to manufacturing concerns for
carbon fiber sleeves, RDD decided to utilize an Inconel magnet sleeve.

With the number of poles, winding type, and the magnet sleeve material settled, RDD
worked to optimize the design of the motor and consider the material for the stator iron.
RDD considered traditional silicon steel and an amorphous alloy. For this study, the
geometry of the stator iron was fixed. Additionally, the geometry of the motor rotor was
optimized for stress and losses based on the first study and fixed. For these restraints,
RDD found that the amorphous alloy reduced iron losses by 87% when compared to the
silicon steel case. The same analysis predicted that the peak magnetic flux would be less
than the saturation flux for either material. RDD selected the amorphous alloy for the
stator iron material. RDD had finalized the design of the motor.

RDD conducted an iterative analysis process during the detailed design phase of the
project to optimize the CE. RDD conducted analyses for critical speed, thrust load,
cooling, transient response, and fits and clearances. Since each analysis affected other
areas of the machine design, RDD generally completed three iterations of each type of
analysis and incorporated the necessary changes to harmonize the analyses with the
overall design at each iteration.

RDD designs its turbomachinery to provide at least 40% margin between the maximum
operating speed of the machine and the analytical first bending mode of the rotor. RDD
also designs its turbomachinery to minimize the speed of the analytical rigid-body modes.
Foil gas bearing supported systems become unstable when operating at any rigid-body
or bending mode. By designing the rotor such that all the critical speeds are outside the
operating region of the machine, RDD mitigates any risks associated with operating at a
natural frequency. RDD performed the critical speed analysis multiple times. The
geometry of the rotor was updated in each case to match the latest configuration of the
motor rotor, impellers, and bearings. By the final iteration of the analysis, RDD achieved
a predicted first-bending mode of 149,000 RPM, well outside the operating range of the
CE. The rotating assembly design was frozen once the critical speed analysis was
passed

RDD completed many cases of thrust load and cooling flow analyses. As both analyses
are tightly coupled, they are completed and discussed together. RDD used a proprietary
internal software to estimate the thrust load generated by the CE. The same software
estimated the leakage flow through the seals from the impellers and any sources of
bearing air cooling. RDD minimized the net thrust load in its turbomachinery by adjusting
the internal geometry of the housings and seals. RDD utilizes a small amount of process
fluid as cooling flow for the bearings. This analysis ensured that enough flow is present
to prevent overheating the bearings. RDD had to balance these competing objectives
during this analysis. A sensitivity analysis was also performed on the seal geometry to
determine how sensitive the CE would be to manufacturing variations. Eventually, RDD
settled on a configuration which produced less than 5 lbf net thrust load at maximum
speed and pressure conditions. The changes produced here were integrated with the
rest of the model.

A stator cooling analysis was performed to ensure that the supply of cooling water was
sufficient to cool the stator. Using the material properties and geometry of the stator
stack, housing, and coolant, a thermal model was created in Solidworks. The flow and
temperature conditions were specified based on the product definition defined in the
preliminary design review. After each iteration of the stator design, this analysis was
repeated to confirm the temperature rise in the system. Each analysis, regardless of the
stator configuration, showed that the motor stator reached a steady state temperature
maximum less than 200 °F with the supplied coolant becoming 5 °F hotter. Some tweaks
to the cooling flow path were made to reduce the pressure drop through the water jacket.
RDD determined that this change in temperature was acceptable and froze the design of
the motor housing water jacket.

After scrapping the original lot of three motor rotors due to machine failures, RDD used a
motor rotor which was reworked after being damaged during parts manufacturing. This
motor rotor had a lower back EMF than the other three samples because the magnetizing
vendor did not completely saturate the magnet. During testing at 66 kRPM, the motor
shut itself down without warning. After disassembling the CE RDD found that this
particular motor rotor was not damaged like the other three, so the cause of this shutdown
was identified as a motor control issue. Testing was halted to perform a root cause
investigation into the machine failures.

RDD reviewed the assembly, analysis, and design of the rotating assembly, bearings,
and housings. Because of the high number of successful CE builds up until the failure
events, RDD did not expect the root cause to be a general assembly fault. This meant
that issues like housing misalignment, poor shaft runouts, and a large initial residual
unbalance in the rotating assembly were ruled out. Foreign object debris, tight shroud
and seal clearances, and bearing defects were eliminated for the same reason. With the
common assembly issues checked, RDD speculated that if all the parts were made per
the design specifications and assembled correctly, then there would be no issues running
this CE. The assumption that the parts were made to the design specifications had to be
challenged.

                                     Page 29 of 46

The physical evidence pointed to an issue with the magnet sleeve on the motor rotor.
The Inconel magnet sleeve is sized to balance the magnetic losses, assembly tolerances,
and stress in the sleeve. The analysis which determines if the state of stress is acceptable
uses the yield strength of heat-treated Inconel as the safety factor basis. Inconel can be
purchased in an annealed or heat-treated state. No yield strength is specified for
annealed Inconel per its specification, only a capability to meet a particular yield strength
once it is heat-treated. According to some material vendors, the approximate yield
strength of annealed Inconel at half the heat-treated value. So, the two versions of the
material are not functionally the same.

After reviewing the material certifications received with the magnet sleeves and other
shaft components, RDD discovered that its vendor purchased and utilized the annealed
version of Inconel without heat-treating it. This error stemmed from a misinterpretation of
a note on the original drawings for those components. Once the approximate annealed
yield strength of Inconel was substituted into the stress analysis, RDD discovered that the
shafts would yield within the operating range of the CE. Furthermore, this yielding would
begin to occur at the running speeds which RDD observed instability. The physical
evidence of this was the plastic deformation of the magnet sleeve. Hardness values are
listed in the material specifications for both varieties of Inconel, so RDD measured the
hardness of samples from scrapped motor rotors and spare pieces which were bought
but not used. The hardness of those samples matched the hardness of the annealed
material specification. RDD concluded that the material certifications were correct, and
that the wrong material was purchased and used.

RDD determined that the motor rotors contained this flaw late in Q2 2024. Any attempt
to rectify the flaw by heat treating the Inconel would scrap the magnets inside the rotor.
Any attempt to disassemble the shaft would scrap the Inconel and risk scrapping the
magnets. The only choice was to make new motor rotors. RDD purchased enough shaft
components for seven motor rotors but had only made five. The spare Inconel details
were immediately sent out for heat-treatment to correct the flaw. Quotes for the welding
and post-processing operations were sought at the same time. Quotes for more shaft
components to keep in reserve were also sought. No further testing was conducted
during the downtime due to a lack of usable hardware.

Manufacturing the replacement motor rotors took four months and was not completed
until the middle of Q4 2024. Unlike the first batch of motor rotors, these were held up at
magnetizing. Our magnetizing vendor did not fully saturate the magnets on the first
attempt. RDD refused to accept the hardware and insisted that the vendor try harder to
reach saturation. After some negotiations, RDD and the vendor agreed on the best path
forward and were able to achieve satisfactory surface strength. The delay cost RDD four
weeks and was the only reason that manufacturing the new rotors was extended into Q4.

The VATN backpressures the compressor causing it to flow less, consume less shaft
power, and move into a more efficient operating point. But the same action increases the
power captured by the turbine by increasing the inlet pressure and moving it to a more
efficient operating point for the same shaft speed. The net effect of this phenomenon is
a large reduction in input power, up to 6 kW, for the same speed and backpressure valve
position. This comparison was made with the backpressure valve in the fully open
position, minimizing its effect. Holding the nozzle fixed at the largest area, as the
backpressure valve closed, the COP remained flat at first before dropping to a minimum
value close to the surge line. The closing backpressure valve has the same effect on the
compressor as the VATN but reduces the inlet pressure of the turbine. This prevents the
turbine from moving into a more efficient operating point and ultimately reduces the COP.

The figure shows that a COP greater than 1.1 is attainable for several combinations of
pressure, flow, and speed greater than 50 kRPM by utilizing the VATN. Neither Loop

method to predict the exact COP of the CE and motor drive at the target operating points.
During actual system operation, the CE, VATN, and surge bypass valve would vary until

greater definition of the fuel cell, the exact COP cannot be predicted. Thus, RDD is

The development of the cost estimate and production plan started in Q1 2025. The FOA
set a cost goal of $12/kW for 100,000 units annually in the BOP of a heavy-duty fuel cell
truck. If the maximum net electrical power available in the fuel cell at the start of its life is

The CE and motor drive developed in this program were created using techniques
suitable for prototypes or low-volume production. Less focus was given to the most
effective methods for high-volume production during Budget Period 1 because it was
cheaper and faster to use processes like milling or turning instead of casting or forging.
The tooling costs would be reduced in the short term while the technology could be proven
ready. But high-volume production demands scalability with a reduction in unit costs.
RDD examined areas of improvement within its own manufacturing processes for this CE
and motor drive and has provided a rough cost estimate and suggestions for future efforts
regarding high-volume production.

The large housings were clear candidates for casting instead of milling and turning. RDD
consulted a reputable local vendor to understand costs for high-volume casting of the
main motor housings, compressor volute, and turbine volute. The vendor explained that
the molds and related tooling would be costly initially, but the tooling would last for about
100,000 parts. Machining would still be necessary on the cast blanks, but the costs would
be much lower compared to machining from a solid block.

The shaft assembly is another area for manufacturing improvement. The current design
uses hand-assembled hardware that requires multiple welding and finishing steps. The
shaft components are also machined before assembly. Instead, these components could
be made by creating forgings in near-net shape, which could then be turned to finished
size either before or after assembly. Investing in an assembly line for building the shaft
assemblies could reduce the number of operator-involved steps and cut down assembly
time. At higher production volumes, the cost and lead time to make shaft assemblies
could become more economical than they are today.

The motor stator is another area for process improvement. All the stators manufactured
in the CE used lap winding. Lap-wound stators are assembled by hand and are not
suitable for automated production. To enhance the scalability of stator manufacturing, a
different winding method such as concentric winding would need to be adopted. Stator

                                        Page 38 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

geometry would also have to be optimized to enable tooling to move between the teeth
in the stator iron. The stator would need to be redesigned for high-volume production
without sacrificing its small size and required output power. However, after redesigning
and applying these techniques, RDD expects stator manufacturing to scale effectively
with volume and become more cost-efficient.

High-volume production would also benefit electronic components. The power
electronics in the motor drive significantly contribute to the system's overall cost.
Electronic components, such as silicon carbide switches, gate drivers, and other parts,
must be purchased from third-party vendors on the open market. The quantities available
are very limited, with long lead times. Once a certain volume is reached, high-volume
orders of these components can be purchased directly from OEM suppliers at a
significantly lower negotiated price. These volume thresholds can range from twelve
thousand units per year to several hundred thousand annually. Reducing the cost of
power electronics remains a key challenge in making this type of motor drive cost-
effective for automotive use.

Following this investigation, RDD estimates that the CE and motor drive developed under
this program could cost approximately $3,800 per unit at a production scale of 100,000
units. This estimate includes the previously outlined assumptions aimed at improving
manufacturing processes. Further optimization is possible by exploring alternative
manufacturing methods. The cost exceeds the $3,000 target set in the FOA. However,
significant increases in the cost of materials and specialty components since the start of
this program in 2021 limit the ability to reduce the system's cost to DOE's target.
Nonetheless, opportunities exist to invest in manufacturing processes that could bring the
cost of a CE and motor drive closer to RDD’s estimated $3,000 per unit.

Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions
RDD successfully developed a foil gas bearing supported Compressor-Expander and
accompanying high speed motor drive intended for use in a heavy-duty fuel cell truck
application. The CE utilized a centrifugal compressor and radial turbine to capitalize on
the high-speed and small size advantages afforded by foil gas bearings. The CE
incorporated a compact motor to drive the compressor at high speeds. And a high speed
sensorless motor drive controlled the motor at the high-power operating points. The
variable area turbine nozzle broadened the effectiveness of the turbine. Silicon carbide
                                     Page 44 of 46

 R&D Dynamics Corporation

switches reduced the size of the motor drive while producing the high switching
frequencies needed for high-speed technology. The result is a compact system taking
up 40 L of volume and weighing 44 kg which can deliver the necessary flow and pressure
for a 250 kWe fuel cell specified by the DOE.

RDD achieved the 100% flow DOE target operating point at shaft speeds around 75
kRPM utilizing around 25-30 kW of input power depending on factors like ambient
conditions and turbine power recovered. Using the variable area turbine nozzle enabled
the CE and motor drive system to achieve a coefficient of performance greater than 1.1
for a variety of operating points at speeds greater than 50 kRPM. Further system
definition is required to determine the exact coefficients of performance of the CE and
motor drive at each of the target operating points. Isentropic efficiencies exceeding 70%
were achieved for both the compressor and turbine at many operating points including
the 100% flow target. The motor at the 100% flow target operating point was 88% efficient
while the motor drive was 97% efficient.

Motor manufacturing and motor design challenges impacted the schedule for this project.
The use of annealed Inconel in the motor rotors delayed progress by almost nine months
when considering the time spent troubleshooting the CE and making new hardware. This
delay significantly impacted the ability of RDD to complete every test demanded by the
SOPO. It also postponed the detection of magnetic saturation occurring within the motor
stator. This delay set the program back an additional 3 months. The effect was a loss of
one year while troubleshooting and remanufacturing components took place. RDD was
successful in resolving the root causes of both issues, but the scope of endurance testing,
surge bypass valve integration, and consideration of humidity effects were reduced or
eliminated entirely because of the time lost.

The CE and motor drive were durable and reliable through the abbreviated suite of
endurance testing once the motor design and manufacturing issues were resolved. No
degradation of performance was noted once correctly manufactured motor rotors and the
redesigned motor stator were utilized. Further endurance testing could be beneficial to
highlight the durability of the system as designed. RDD estimated that the CE and motor
drive would cost $3,800 per unit at 100,000 units per year. The cost of this system at
high volume production levels would be driven by the cost of major components like the
housings and power electronics even after these components are optimized for
manufacturability and investments are made in the assembly infrastructure. Despite the
reduced scope of testing due to motor issues, RDD successfully accomplished its goal of
developing a foil gas bearing CE and high-speed motor drive for a heavy-duty fuel cell
truck application.

Path Forward
The technology will be scaled to larger Compressor-Expanders based on commercial
needs. Future fuel cells are utilizing higher supply voltages. This will require SiC switches
rated for higher voltage and current to meet these needs. One of the most expensive
components is the SiC switches. The price of these components will need to come down
to meet the DOE price targets.
