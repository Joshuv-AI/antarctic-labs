Why do occupational hygienists and physicians fail to communicate and collaborate when faced with chemicals in the workplace? A qualitative study from the Netherlands
Authors: Julia M van Beekum, Dorine van Geuns, Floor Stuit, Gilbert W M Wijntjens, Jolanda Willems, Wendy Tijssen-Caan, Karin I Proper, Frederieke G Schaafsma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWorkers who handle hazardous substances are at risk of health impairments, occupational diseases and even premature death. Occupational diseases are preventable, but early detection, diagnosis and intervention remain inadequate. Therefore, we aim to gain insight into the determinants of timely identification of health effects caused by exposure to hazardous substances in the workplace as perceived by occupational health professionals.MethodsWe conducted seventeen online semi-structured interviews among Dutch occupational hygienists and occupational physicians on their perspectives and experiences in identifying chemical-related health effects in the workplace. The interviews were transcribed verbatim and the data were analysed using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model.ResultsOccupational hygienists and occupational physicians experience limited knowledge, skills, and self-efficacy for timely identification of chemical-related health effects. These modifiable determinants hinder effective collaboration between these occupational health professionals in translating exposure to hazardous substances into health effects and vice versa. Moreover, different system-level determinants in the Netherlands further impede this collaboration. Existing tools for occupational health professionals reinforce this gap by supporting either exposure assessment or health effect evaluation, rarely bridging the two. As a result, current tools are often tailored to a single professional group.ConclusionsThe results from our study suggest that gaps in knowledge, skills and self-efficacy hinder timely identification of chemical-related health effects in the Netherlands. The findings of this study can inform the development of multidisciplinary interventions and multifaceted strategies aimed at improving prevention of chemical-related occupational disease and timely implementation of appropriate (workplace) measures.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12995-026-00517-5.Keywords: Hazardous substances, Occupational disease, Occupational hygienist, Occupational physician, Prevention, Qualitative research

Introduction

Workers who handle hazardous substances, such as chemical products, are at risk of health impairments, occupational diseases and even premature death [1]. In the Netherlands, an annual estimated 3,000 deaths and 100,000 Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) are directly attributable to occupational exposure to hazardous substances, which corresponds with an estimated societal cost of between €400 million and €1.4 billion, including costs related to healthcare expenses, absenteeism, and presenteeism [1].

Prevention of occupational diseases is organised at three levels: primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention [2, 3]. At the secondary level, prevention focuses on the identification of initial indicators of adverse health effects caused by these hazardous substances, usually by workers’ health surveillance or consultations with occupational physicians. This allows for timely implementation of (workplace) interventions to prevent progression to irreversible health impairments or occupational diseases at the individual level as part of secondary prevention and at the organisational level as part of primary prevention [4].

Identification of health effects caused by hazardous substances requires close collaboration between occupational hygienists and occupational physicians [5–7]. More importantly, it underscores the need for translation of occupational exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, and vice versa. In practice, this implies that occupational physicians should be able to understand and rely on the information provided by occupational hygienists regarding potential chemical exposure risks in the workplace. This enables occupational physicians to timely identify health effects associated with the exposure level and contribute to informed policy advice. Similarly, occupational hygienists should be able to understand and rely on the information provided by the occupational physicians on the occurrence of adverse health effects among workers in order to advise on workplace control measures [8]. However, this reciprocal translation of information and the collaboration between these occupational health professionals is often ineffective or even non-existent in Dutch occupational healthcare settings [9, 10].

Although some possible explanations for insufficient prevention of chemical-related occupational diseases have been mentioned in the grey literature, an in-depth understanding of the underlying determinants is still missing [9]. As such, the present study aims to gain insight into the perceived determinants for timely identification of health effects caused by exposure to hazardous substances in the workplace.

Methods

Study design and setting

We performed an explorative qualitative study using semi-structured interviews among Dutch occupational health professionals (i.e. occupational hygienists and occupational physicians) to gain insight into the determinants for timely identification of health effects caused by hazardous substances in the workplace. A description of the Dutch occupational healthcare system can be found in Box 1. This study was structured using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [11].

Box 1Occupational healthcare in the NetherlandsIn the Netherlands, general provisions for occupational health and safety are regulated under the Dutch Working Conditions Act [12]. Introduced in 1980, this act established the employer’s formal responsibility for ensuring safe and healthy working conditions. This legislation also required certified occupational health services to employ certified experts in at least four distinct disciplines, namely occupational medicine, safety engineering, occupational hygiene, and organisational consultancy. A revision of this act in 1994 obliged employers to contract certified occupational health services to assist with the prevention of work-related health problems and with the management of sickness absence in general. The Dutch occupational healthcare system as of 1994 is privately funded and operates independently of the Health Insurance Act. This has resulted in a rapid expansion and commercialisation of occupational health services in the Netherlands. New legislation in 2002 (Gatekeeper Act) mandated that employers, employees, and occupational health professionals hold a shared responsibility in the management and guidance of sickness absence. This law imposes a duty on employers to take active steps to support the reintegration of sick employees into the workplace for a period of two years. Employers now hold a statutory obligation to continue paying wages during this period. Moreover, deregulation in the 2000s allowed employers to also hire independent or in-house certified experts to fulfil their legal obligations regarding preventive activities in the workplace. This has resulted in a significant outflow of occupational hygienists from occupational health services. The commercialisation and increased emphasis on sickness absence have led to contracts with occupational health services that prioritise individual sickness absence management over preventive activities at both the individual and workplace levels..

Participant recruitment

Participants for the interviews were selected through typical case sampling to ensure a diverse range of occupational hygienists and occupational physicians with varying professional backgrounds and levels of experience in assessing and managing the risks of hazardous substances. Individuals who did not speak Dutch or who were already involved in other research studies on health risks due to chemical exposure in the workplace were excluded from the study. A total of 30 occupational health professionals (14 occupational hygienists; 16 occupational physicians) were approached through a personalised recruitment email or directly at a professional conference. Those of them who expressed interest received an informed consent form. Out of the 30 invited occupational health professionals, 13 (5 occupational hygienists; 9 occupational physicians) decided not to participate in the study due to a lack of interest or limited availability or did not respond to the invitation.

Interview guide

A semi-structured interview guide was developed, which included introductory questions, main questions, and probes to guide the discussion. To ensure the questions were relevant to the study’s purpose and were appropriate in terms of language, the interview guide was reviewed by an advisory board of experts (6 occupational hygienists; 6 occupational physicians). Participants were purposively recruited from the researcher’s professional networks in the Netherlands and selected for their ample experience with prevention of chemical-related occupational diseases. Following this review of the interview guide, it was pilot tested with one occupational hygienist. Topics were centred around: (1) translating exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, and vice versa, (2) collaboration between the occupational hygienist and occupational physician, and (3) the use and need for tools that assist in this translation. The interview guides for occupational hygienists and occupational physicians can be found in Additional Files 1 and 2, respectively.

Data collection

The interviews were conducted and recorded via Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) between July and November 2024 by a female health scientist (DvG) with expertise in qualitative research. Interviews lasted approximately 60 minutes.

Data analysis

The interviews were transcribed verbatim. All personal data was anonymised to prevent identification. We used thematic analysis following the principles of Braun and Clarke to analyse the interviews [13]. In the first phase of analysis, an inductive approach was used to iteratively generate open codes, which were then grouped into themes. Four researchers (JvB, DvG, FS and FGS) were involved in the open coding of the data in this stage. A (verbatim) transcript was sent to one participant on request. Five coded transcripts were compared between the four researchers to establish the reliability of the coding procedure across observers, with any discrepancies discussed until consensus was reached. In the second phase, data analysis was mainly carried out by JvB. Themes were restructured deductively using the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation-Behaviour (COM-B) model for Behaviour Change [14]. The research group developed a coding scheme through discussions until consensus was reached. No new information or themes emerged at the end of the data collection process, as formally verified during content analysis. Therefore, it was concluded that data saturation had been reached [15]. In the final phase, the aforementioned advisory board reviewed and confirmed the main findings. Excerpts of the interviews were translated from Dutch into English by the principal researcher, a native Dutch speaker with advanced English proficiency, for inclusion in the results section.

Ethical considerations

This research was considered by the Medical Ethics Review Committee (METC 2024.0229) of the Amsterdam UMC as not being subject to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (WMO). Participants were asked to sign an informed consent form, and permission was sought to record and transcribe the meeting. Before recording, participants were informed on the background and aim of the study and had the opportunity to ask questions or express any concerns.

The results of this interview study were divided into the three major themes based on the COM-B model: (1) Capability, (2) Opportunity and (3) Motivation. The corresponding subthemes and illustrative quotes are described below.

Capability

Insufficient knowledge and skills of hazardous substances

Both occupational hygienists and occupational physicians reported a systematic lack of knowledge among Dutch occupational health professionals regarding hazardous substances and their associated adverse health effects, which was particularly pronounced among occupational physicians. Several participants also acknowledged a personal lack of toxicological knowledge. The systematic knowledge deficit was primarily attributed to shortcomings in post-graduate occupational health training programmes for occupational physicians, which were perceived as focusing predominantly on absenteeism management and return-to-work procedures rather than on the prevention of occupational diseases secondary to occupational exposure to hazardous substances. Moreover, most occupational physicians indicated that they seldom have contact with workers who are at risk of occupational exposure to hazardous substances. Consequently, they mentioned having fewer opportunities to develop expertise through experience.

There are many occupational physicians who know little about hazardous substances. That is not a judgment, but I think it is rather a reflection of their training. The subject is underemphasised. One of our occupational physicians is always complaining about the training program, saying: Well, 90% of the training for the current trainees is about absenteeism and only 5% is about hazardous substances. – P14 (Occupational hygienist)

Additionally, occupational hygienists cited their diverse educational backgrounds as another reason for the knowledge gap. For example, some occupational hygienists have technical backgrounds, while others have backgrounds in health sciences, resulting in different interests and areas of expertise within the field of occupational hygiene and a difference in expertise in hazardous substances.

Well, no offence intended, but if you are a safety engineer and you decide to do a post-graduate course in occupational hygiene, your background will be completely different from someone who enters the field after studying health sciences. Conversely, I did the Safety Engineering program, but I am not a technician. So please do not let me work with machines. […]. That is not my expertise. Likewise, I think there will be occupational hygienists whose expertise will not lie entirely in the chemical field.”– P1 (Occupational hygienist)

Lack of interprofessional knowledge

Occupational hygienists and occupational physicians both reported difficulties in determining when, how, or whether to consult each other. Additionally, participants expressed uncertainty about the responsibilities and knowledge of the other professional group, which can result in duplicated efforts or professional inaction, where tasks are being passed back and forth without clear ownership. Other reasons cited included missing contact information and a lack of awareness about their employment status at the same occupational health service or company.

Before going [to that company], I did not even know what an occupational hygienist was. I had never heard of it until my boss mentioned it. Because they had already been in contact with an occupational hygienist about the Risk Assessment and Evaluation, and suggested that perhaps I could give them a call. But at that moment, I really had no idea. It would not even have occurred to me to call them.- P2 (Occupational physician in training)

Opportunity

Structure of the Dutch occupational healthcare system

All participants agreed that the structure of the Dutch occupational healthcare system currently hinders effective prevention of occupational diseases. Due to private financing structures, employers determine funds allocation, leading to a primary focus on absenteeism management because of its immediate practical implications. Additionally, some participants noted that this structure results in a fragmented occupational healthcare practice, in which essential components such as absence management, workers’ health surveillance and risk assessments, are conducted separately, thereby contributing to a lack of system cohesion.

One problem is the current market forces and demands in a field with a real shortage of professionals. This makes it easy, lucrative, pleasant, and rewarding to primarily conduct sick leave consultations. It is easy to get lost in that comfortably so that is part of the problem. Employers are not raising the question [if the occupational physician should conduct other, preventive, tasks]. It is an overcrowded market. You quickly end up working reactively.- P11 (Occupational physician)

Moreover, both professional groups observed that an increasing number of occupational health professionals are being hired for temporary projects rather than being employed for a longer period of time by a company, resulting in a rapid turnover rate of occupational health professionals. Therefore, they have fewer opportunities to become familiar with each other, the company, and its processes. This lack of continuity can make it difficult to maintain consistent documentation systems, exposure assessments and medical examinations, which limits long term follow-ups and the quality of occupational healthcare.

Complex legislation and compliance

According to the participants, legislation is instrumental for employers lacking intrinsic motivation to engage in prevention. As a result, they mentioned that some companies often only invest in preventive measures after a visit from the labour inspectorate. However, non-compliance remains an issue, due to limited enforcement and therefore limited consequences.

Enforcement is very important in this regard, because you want to ensure that companies that are not voluntarily motivated to provide clear insight into their exposure do so. In that case, an inspection is necessary to convince those companies to comply. I think the labour inspectorate does that quite well, actually, but they also deal with limited capacity. - P7 (Occupational hygienist)

They also mentioned that privacy legislation in the Netherlands hinders the exchange of relevant information related to personal exposure data and medical data. As a result, monitoring exposure and addressing workers’ health complaints over time is becoming increasingly challenging.

Influence of company size

The size of a company influences the extend of efforts undertaken to prevent occupational diseases. Participants generally stated that larger companies have more resources and higher safety standards compared to small and medium enterprises. They adopt a more systematic approach to preventing accidents and occupational diseases, as well as managing risks in the workplace.

Type of occupational health service and work setting

Many participants indicated that the type of occupational health service can have an impact on the barrier for collaboration. Company-based occupational health services often operate on-site. Professionals working within the same company and occupational health service are more likely to encounter each other in daily practice. Therefore, collaboration occurs more naturally because both parties are readily accessible. In contrast, professionals working for larger occupational health services with contracts with many different companies, along with self-employed occupational health professionals, tend to operate independently, resulting in fewer collaboration opportunities.

Additionally, participants reported that although workplace assessments and associated visits are standard practice for occupational hygienists, Dutch occupational physicians often work remotely from the workplace of the employees they support and therefore visit the workplaces less frequent. Consequently, this often means that the Dutch occupational physicians are frequently not aware of the actual working conditions and the associated health risks. Furthermore, this situation once again reinforces the issue that the two professionals have fewer opportunities to interact naturally with each other.

Yes, [occupational physicians] have no idea. They see people during consultations, but they have never been on-site. For example, they do not know what the hall looks like or where people are working. I find that very reprehensible. - P14 (Occupational hygienist)

Lack of employee engagement

Participants noted that employees often lack a proactive attitude and do not adequately recognise when they are handling hazardous substances. As a result, they rarely consult an occupational physician for potential work-related health issues. Instead, they visit general practitioners, who usually do not consider work-related causes and treats a health issue based on its symptoms. Other reasons cited for not visiting an occupational physician might be unfamiliarity with the services or a lack of access to them.

Lack of integrated risk assessment and evaluation and worker health surveillance

The interviewees observed that companies, particularly small and medium enterprises, often fail to keep records of hazardous substances. Both occupational hygienists and occupational physicians observed that substance registers and risk assessments of exposure to hazardous substances are often outdated, incomplete or absent. Furthermore, some occupational physicians reported difficulty in accessing these risk assessments, indicating that employers do not always provide these documents upon request.

Although employers are legally required to offer worker health surveillance concerning workplace health risks, interviewees stated that these are rarely, if ever, carried out. Often, general health examinations are offered instead. These types of examinations focus more on lifestyle-related health effects and lack medical examinations concerning health risks at the workplace, such as exposure hazardous substances. Moreover, they indicated that these examinations are often outsourced to commercial companies, not related to the occupational health service, and are conducted by vitality coaches who often lack medical training. Occupational hygienists emphasise that the development of worker health surveillance should be a collaborative effort between the occupational hygienist and the occupational physician. However, they noted that this seldom occurs in practice. Consequently, these worker health surveillances mainly serve as legal checks rather than meaningful contributions to the management of occupational risks, because they are not based on the risks identified in risk assessments.

Inadequate information provision and assistance

While all participants recognised the value of tools in assisting with their daily tasks, they face challenges such as fragmentation and difficulty in finding them, which seem to be common obstacles. Occupational hygienists reported primarily using (software) tools that aid in assessing work-related risks concerning hazardous substances. Such tools often provide quantitative and/or qualitative estimates of exposure but offer little insight into dosage-related health effects. Other barriers in the use of the tools mentioned include being time-consuming, requiring expertise to use them, and occupational physicians often lack access to these toxicological databases. Occupational physicians, on the other hand, indicated that they mainly obtain their information on hazardous substances from guidelines and Safety Data Sheets (SDS). While these instruments provide guidance on assessing potential health effects, they are hazard-based and say little about the health effects at real exposure levels.

I often find [linking health effects to exposure] difficult. If you look at the Safety Data Sheets of hazardous substances, they often list health effects that can occur for so many different substances. Therefore, that information alone is not sufficient. You also need to know more about how people are exposed and to what degree. Simply knowing that someone works with hazardous substances is not enough to determine if health effects are the result of exposure. – P9 (Occupational physician)

Complexity of hazardous substances

Another common view among participants was that the field of occupational healthcare is broad and complex, particularly regarding hazardous substances. Assessing and evaluating potential health effects of exposure to hazardous substances is difficult, for example, due to multiple exposures to chemicals, difficulties in establishing causality, but also because symptoms of chronic effects often appear after many years or even decades, and affected individuals may no longer be in contact with occupational health services or their previous employers. All these factors make it challenging to stay informed about evolving risks of exposure to hazardous substances and respond to them effectively.

Motivation

Structural lack of interest in collaboration, prevention and hazardous substances

All participants emphasised the importance of collaboration in preventing occupational diseases. They noted that different areas of expertise can complement each other, and that collaborating can be enjoyable.

I once did [a workplace visit] with an occupational physician in training. Then you walk around together, and it is really fun, because it is like: “Oh, what are you looking at?” and “What am I looking at?” Then you learn from each other.– P5 (Occupational hygienist)

Although the participants reported a structural under-prioritisation in chemical-related disease prevention, nearly all of them expressed a personal interest in the topic. They cited broadening their expertise, gaining new knowledge, and diversifying their work as motivating factors.

I think it is great if you can take a proactive approach instead of having to clean up the mess afterwards. It is very satisfying to know you have given a company good advice, that those workers can handle [hazardous substances] safely and that we are going to prevent these occupational diseases from developing. It is nice to have contributed to that.- P2 (Occupational physician)

Lack of assertiveness

Both occupational hygienists and occupational physicians argued, however, that interest alone is not enough; a high level of assertiveness is also required. As the Dutch occupational healthcare system is strongly focused on absenteeism, it is essential that occupational physicians proactively incorporate preventive elements in their contracts with employers, such as advisory hours, workplace visits and health surveillance. However, some fear this could result in contract termination.

You would not need to be an occupational physician if you only dealt with absenteeism. Our added value lies in addressing other issues. Absenteeism management is a useful starting point because it is always there. The challenge lies in highlighting the other areas as well. Yes, I really enjoy that, but you have to dare it, right? Some of my colleagues do not dare to do so in this country. They ask: “How do you do that? What if they terminate the contract?” Well, whatever. There are ten others waiting for them. I dare to approach a company and say: You do not have this in order, you really need to do something about it. […]. I notice that people are really scared. There are many good occupational physicians, but there are also a lot of scared occupational physicians who try to please. - P6 (Occupational physician)

Low self-efficacy

Most occupational hygienists and occupational physicians reported high self-efficacy in their respective professional tasks, such as analysing workplace exposures or recognising health complaints, respectively. However, when it came to linking these tasks for the translation of exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, or vice versa, both professional groups reported low self-efficacy. This uncertainty sometimes caused them to delegate the translation related task to more experienced colleagues.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to gain insight into the perceived determinants for the timely identification of health effects caused by exposure to hazardous substances at the workplace according to occupational health professionals. The results suggest that poor capability and insufficient opportunity, despite high intrinsic motivation, hinder effective collaboration between occupational hygienists and occupational physicians in translating exposure to hazardous substances into health effects and vice versa. The results were mostly consistent across all participants, with some differences observed between the occupational health professionals, particularly regarding their knowledge of hazardous substances, work setting and used information sources.

Interpretation of the main findings

A main finding of this study was the reported lack of relevant knowledge about hazardous substances and their associated adverse health effects among occupational health professionals, especially among occupational physicians. For decades, the failure to recognise occupational diseases has primarily been attributed to the inadequate training of occupational physicians in various countries worldwide, including the Netherlands, as well as to a general lack of awareness and expertise among clinicians [16–23]. To illustrate, the four-year Dutch occupational medicine program dedicates very limited time for theoretical education in toxicology, with large differences in practical training in risk assessments related to exposure to hazardous substances [24]. Consequently, knowledge and skills often need to be acquired through practical experience after graduating and expertise in this area is predominantly found within a niche group [25]. Although all participants primarily expressed concern about the training of occupational physicians, there is also much to be said about the education of occupational hygienists. In the Netherlands, educational programs for occupational hygienists vary in their entry requirements, duration, and curriculum [26]. In addition, there is a global shortage of occupational hygienists, and many international university-based programmes are being discontinued due to financial constraints [27, 28]. Instead, practice-oriented programs at higher education level are offered, resulting in differences in theoretical depth and knowledge. These findings suggest that educational interventions aimed at enhancing knowledge of both occupational health professionals could improve timely identification of chemical-related occupational diseases.

Additionally, the privatisation and fragmentation of the Dutch occupational healthcare system since the 1990s have shifted focus towards managing absenteeism, which accounts for 75–80% of occupational physicians’ working time [4, 12]. Although a basic contract with an occupational health service or occupational physician is mandatory, employers often purchase the cheapest services only limited to sick leave guidance [4]. Such often only short-term, contracts offer little opportunity for the occupational physician to visit the workplace and engage in multidisciplinary collaboration, resulting in a lack of continuity and loss of acquired knowledge. In contrast, occupational preventive services in Germany are partially funded through the statutory accident insurance system, thereby incentivising employers to invest in prevention and reducing their reliance on market-based provision [29]. Similarly, the Finnish occupational healthcare system integrates occupational health services into the broader social security system. Employers are required to contract these services, with part of the costs reimbursed by the Social Insurance Institution, including 60% for preventive services and 50% for clinical services [30]. This collective financing provides employees with more structured access to preventive occupational healthcare, while also encouraging employers to invest in such measures.

International treaties and the Dutch Working Conditions Act mandate that occupational physicians should operate independently [31, 32]. However, despite legal independence, their professional autonomy may (unconsciously) be compromised in practice because employment contracts often create subordination to employers [4, 33, 34]. These findings are consistent with previous literature, stating that implementing preventive measures to reduce exposure to hazardous substances often conflicts with companies’ financial interests because these measures do not yield immediate benefits [35, 36]. A similar conflict between formal independence and contractual dependence has been observed in Turkey, where occupational physicians’ employment status undermines their mandated professional independence, leading to calls for stronger job security provisions to protect their preventive function [37].

Similarly, the commercialisation has led to an increase in the outsourcing of occupational health sources. Primarily larger companies operating in sectors with high exposure risks tend to have a dedicated safety and health department and company-based occupational health and safety service [38, 39]. These in-house services enable stronger collaboration between occupational hygienists and occupational physicians and facilitates greater allocation of time and resources to preventive measures [40]. However, despite the recognised benefits, most Dutch employers outsource their occupational health and safety services to external providers because of financial considerations and greater flexibility [12]. The observed decline in in-house occupational health services and the shift towards outsourcing align with trends reported in countries such as the United States and Canada, where reduced in-house capacity often positions occupational health professionals in a more reactive rather than proactive role in prevention and risk anticipation [29, 41].

Another finding confirmed by the participants was that Risk Assessment and Evaluation for hazardous substances as well as Worker Health Surveillance are often not properly implemented. According to the Dutch Labor Inspectorate, only 7% of companies that are required to conduct supplementary Risk Assessment and Evaluation for hazardous substances comply with the requirements set forth in the Dutch Working Conditions Act [42]. Furthermore, merely 21% of companies implement Worker Health Surveillance for their employees [39]. Previous studies have demonstrated that, across various international contexts, the content of Worker Health Surveillance is rarely informed by Risk Assessment and Evaluation and therefore does not represent real health risks in the workplace, which limits their overall effectiveness [9, 43, 44]. Thus, closer integration of these instruments is required to establish effective feedback loops for the prevention of chemical-related occupational diseases.

This study indicated that Dutch occupational hygienists and occupational physicians are largely confined to their respective domain, failing to integrate their perspectives and findings into each other’s discipline. Furthermore, differences of opinion were observed between the two professional groups regarding who was responsible for the translation of exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, and vice versa. This interdisciplinary gap partly stems from a lack of interprofessional knowledge. Evidence from different European countries, including Germany, France and Finland has shown that (occupational) healthcare professionals often have an insufficient understanding of the expertise and working methods of other (occupational) health care professionals, which hinders collaboration and information exchange across disciplines [45–47]. Existing tools for occupational health professionals reinforce this gap by supporting either exposure assessment or health effect evaluation, rarely bridging the two. As a result, current tools are often tailored to a single profession. A greater mutual understanding of professional capabilities, combined with new interdisciplinary practical tools, may lead to more sustainable collaborations.

On a positive note, all participants were intrinsically motivated to strengthen multidisciplinary collaboration and showed clear interest in the prevention of occupational diseases. At the same time, they acknowledged that translating exposure to hazardous substances at the workplace into health effects, and vice versa, can be challenging in practice, partly due to limited self-efficacy. Research has shown that Dutch occupational physicians with higher self-efficacy spend more time on preventive tasks [48]. Likewise, healthcare professionals with strong self-efficacy demonstrate greater assertiveness and more effective communication [49, 50]. This insight suggests that enhancing self-efficacy of occupational health professionals may encourage more proactive collaboration and support preventive efforts.

Methodological considerations

A strength of this study was the group of participants with different backgrounds and experiences with hazardous substances, resulting in a comprehensive understanding of the perspectives and experiences of Dutch occupational hygienists and occupational physicians. Additionally, the use of the COM-B model offered a validated theoretical framework to analyse behavioural determinants. In turn, the Behavioural Change Wheel, a framework that builds on the COM-B model, can translate these determinants into practical (evidence-based) interventions and implementation strategies [14]. Furthermore, the involvement of an advisory board of 12 experts ensured appropriateness of the interview guide and validated the final findings.

A methodological limitation of this study is that the interviews were conducted by a researcher other than the one who performed the thematic analysis. As a result, the researcher conducting the analysis was unable to provide input on the topics discussed during the interviews. This may have impacted the depth and interpretation of the interview data. However, to mitigate this limitation, four researchers were involved in the first phase of the analysis, including the interviewer, which enhanced the credibility. Additionally, selection bias may have occurred as occupational health professionals with a particular interest in this topic or critical views on the Dutch occupational healthcare system may have been more inclined to participate in this study. Hence, included participants may be more motivated to engage in prevention compared to other professionals working in this field. Furthermore, as the study was conducted in the Dutch occupational healthcare context, the findings may be limited in their applicability to other countries and or (occupational) healthcare systems.

Implications for future research and practice

An estimated one billion workers worldwide are annually at risk of adverse health outcomes annually due to occupational exposure to hazardous substances [1]. Therefore, timely identification of health effects resulting from such exposures is required. A multifaceted strategy is essential to optimise translation of occupational exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, and vice versa. Additionally, enhanced collaboration between occupational hygienists and occupational physicians is critical to addressing gaps in practice. From this study, limited knowledge, skills and self-efficacy emerged as key modifiable determinants within the COM-B model. In line with the Behavioural Change Wheel, interdisciplinary guidelines and joint training initiatives can facilitate better recognition of chemical-related occupational diseases and foster interdisciplinary collaboration between occupational professionals. Such intervention can particularly benefit occupational hygienists and occupational physicians working as independent professional or for those servicing small and medium-sized enterprises where specialised expertise and structured resources are often lacking. Consequently, building on the determinants of this study, future research should focus on which behavioural change techniques are most effective in supporting this process. The resulting insight could be relevant not only to the Netherlands but also to other countries facing similar challenges.

While this study also identified multiple sociopolitical determinants, these determinants lie beyond the behavioural scope of occupational health professionals in daily practice. Nevertheless, we recommend policymakers to critically review the policies and regulations within the Dutch occupational healthcare system and evaluate whether there are opportunities to improve prevention of chemical-related occupational diseases with more multidisciplinary collaboration.

Conclusion

This study provided insight into the determinants for the timely identification health effects caused by exposure to hazardous substances. Currently, occupational hygienists and occupational physicians often have limited knowledge, skills and self-efficacy in translating exposure to hazardous substances into health effects, and vice versa. Furthermore, the study indicated that current collaboration between occupational hygienists and occupational physicians is limited. However, both groups recognise the need for improvement and are intrinsically motivated to do so. The findings of this study can inform the development of practical and multidisciplinary interventions aimed at improving prevention of chemical-related occupational disease, the identification of health effects and the timely implementation of appropriate (workplace) measures.
