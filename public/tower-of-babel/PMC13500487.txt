Exploring pain sensitivity changes after acceptance and attention bias modification trainings: preliminary evidence on fear of pain and attention bias
Authors: Gozansky Einav, Weissman-Fogel Irit, Okon-Singer Hadas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cognitive trainings for pain are suggested as promising interventions targeting core mechanisms related to pain. However, their efficacy varies greatly, and the factors that moderate training efficacy are poorly understood. Thus, this study investigated how individual characteristics influence the effectiveness of two commonly used cognitive trainings, attention bias modification (ABM) and acceptance training, in modulating experimental pain. Eighty healthy females attended two sessions of either pain acceptance training or pain ABM training. Each participant underwent one active training session and one control session in a randomized order. Pre- and post-training assessments included emotional questionnaires, attention bias tasks, and quantitative sensory testing (QST). At the group level, both active trainings reduced sensitivity to the heat temperature participants were trained on, but were not more effective than the control conditions, and no other group-level improvements emerged in other pain sub-modalities. Nevertheless, greater attentional interference from pain-related images and higher fear of pain at baseline predicted greater reductions in heat pain sensitivity following the acceptance training. These preliminary findings emphasize the importance of baseline cognitive and emotional traits in shaping the efficacy of acceptance-based interventions. Tailoring interventions to individual profiles may enhance treatment outcomes, warranting further research into personalized approaches for pain management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56014-6.

Introduction

Pain is a complex perceptual phenomenon comprising interactions among sensory, psychological, and regulatory factors1–3. Contemporary models of pain, such as the fear-avoidance model, highlight the critical role of cognitive factors in the development and maintenance of chronic pain1,4,5. Specifically, heightened attentional focus on pain amplifies behavioral and physiological responses, while catastrophic interpretations and difficulties in regulating pain-related emotions further exacerbate the pain experience. These factors interact dynamically; catastrophizing and emotional dysregulation increase pain-related fears, leading to attentional and behavioral avoidance of activities perceived as possibly pain-inducing, further reinforcing maladaptive pain beliefs and sustaining a self-perpetuating cycle of pain4,6,7.

Building on this perspective, recent interventions have increasingly aimed to modify how individuals attend to and interpret pain-related information, and to promote adaptive emotional processing8,9. One such approach is cognitive trainings, an umbrella term for structured, computerized tasks designed to target specific cognitive mechanisms implicated in pain processing10–12. These interventions are often cost-effective, accessible, and adaptable for online delivery, and have been shown to induce both neural and behavioral changes13, highlighting their potential as effective therapeutic tools14,15. In research contexts, they also offer a valuable framework for testing the causal influence of specific pain-related cognitive mechanisms on pain and related cognitive processes16.

A widely used cognitive training approach aiming to alter cognitive evaluation of pain is pain acceptance training, an emotion regulation approach targeting catastrophic thinking and fears related to pain that are linked to heightened pain sensitivity17–20. This training teaches participants to simply notice the presence of thoughts, sensations, and emotions, without following, resisting, believing, or disbelieving them21,22. Through structured practice, participants learn to relate to pain with nonjudgmental awareness, aiming to reduce avoidance behaviors and emotional distress, thereby supporting goal-directed functioning despite ongoing pain23,24. Although other emotion regulation approaches, such as cognitive restructuring, distraction, and reappraisal, can reduce experimental pain intensity, acceptance training appears to yield stronger effects on pain tolerance and pain-related anxiety22,25,26.

Brief acceptance-based interventions have been shown to reduce pain ratings, increase tolerance and accelerate pain recovery, reduce pain-related anxiety and negative affect, and improve overall functioning despite ongoing pain22,24–30. Studies also indicate reductions in physiological markers of acute pain and pain anticipation (e.g., heart rate, skin conductance), suggesting enhanced autonomic regulation25,27,28. However, outcomes remain inconsistent, as training is often tested only on the trained pain modality without assessing generalization to other pain modalities or participants’ pain modulation abilities. Evidence also suggests that training effectiveness may depend on individual factors and the context in which pain is evaluated27,30,31.

Beyond emotion regulation–based approaches, other cognitive training strategies have been developed to target maladaptive processes linked to increased pain sensitivity and chronicity32–34, such as Attention Bias Modification [ABM]35. From the perspective of the fear-avoidance model, attention, and more specifically, attentional biases toward pain-related cues, play a central role in the early stages of threat detection, shaping downstream emotional and behavioral responses4,15. Building on this framework, ABM is one of the most commonly used and widely studied approaches aiming to teach individuals to redirect attention away from pain cues, potentially leading to reduced pain sensitivity, anxiety, and pain-related stress15,35,36. However, similarly to acceptance-based interventions, while some studies report that ABM increases pain thresholds and tolerance and reduces pain intensity in both healthy and clinical populations37–41, others fail to replicate these effects42–44. Furthermore, although initial evidence suggests that pain-related fear may moderate ABM effectiveness38, the predictive role of baseline attentional biases, as well as thier effects on attentional and emotional responses to pain, remain unclear35,36.

Thus, although evidence suggests that cognitive trainings interventions aimed at promoting adaptive emotional strategies or altering attentional biases can reduce pain and increase tolerance in both healthy and clinical populations22,37–39, findings remain inconsistent35,45, potentially due to individual differences in pain sensitivity and pain-related cognitions44,46. In parallel, sex-related differences in pain prevalence, sensitivity, and treatment response are well documented, with women typically reporting higher pain sensitivity and lower pain thresholds than men47–49. However, despite growing interest in sex differences in analgesic responses, considerably less is known about how women respond to psychological pain interventions50,51. Accordingly, the present study focuses on healthy women, allowing a more controlled investigation of these mechanisms, while addressing an important gap in the literature. Specifically, the present study examines how cognitive and pain-related emotional traits influence the efficacy of common cognitive trainings targeting distinct but related pain mechanisms, i.e., acceptance and attention training, in healthy women. This dual approach enables examination of the causal role of each mechanism in pain perception and identifying whether shared or distinct baseline traits predict responsiveness to each intervention.

To this end, we employed training protocols adapted from established paradigms, incorporating exposure to experimental pain during training to enhance ecological validity15,52. Each participant completed two sessions of a single training type (either acceptance or ABM), consisting of one active training session and one matched control (i.e., sham) session, in randomized order. Such active–control distinctions are commonly used in cognitive training research, including ABM (e.g.,42,53,54) and acceptance-based interventions (e.g.,23,28), where control conditions are designed to match the training format while excluding the specific therapeutic component.

Pain sensitivity to the trained stimulus and its generalization to other pain modalities, as well as to pain modulation abilities were assessed before and after the intervention using enhanced quantitative sensory testing (QST). Psychological and cognitive assessment were obtained via validated questionnaires and cognitive tasks. Pain sensitivity and task adherence were additionally monitored throughout the training sessions.

Building on previous findings, we hypothesized that individuals with higher emotional dysregulation, pain catastrophizing, fear of pain, and anxiety would benefit more from acceptance training26,55. In line with the fear-avoidance model, we further expected that acceptance training will reduce attentional biases toward pain cues and decrease fear of pain, catastrophizing, and anxiety, and that these changes will be associated with reductions in pain sensitivity55,56. Regarding the predictors of ABM training efficacy, since the training is designed to reduce attentional biases toward pain-related stimuli, we hypothesized that individuals with higher pre-intervention attentional bias and greater fear of pain will derive greater benefit from the training5,38. Moreover, we expected that training-related reductions in attentional bias would be correlate with decreases in pain sensitivity36,57. While our primary focus is on identifying trait-level predictors of intervention outcomes, we also report group-level effects, which were expected to be small to moderate, given the heterogeneity of responses26,31,44.

Results

To facilitate interpretation, results are presented separately for each intervention, beginning with the acceptance training.

Acceptance training

Training evaluation

Perceived difficulty of using the acceptance active and control sessions

At baseline, immediately after strategy learning, there was no difference in perceived difficulty of using both the active and control training strategies (t(76) = 0.82, p=.416). This remained consistent throughout the training, as participants reported a gradual decrease in the perceived difficulty of using both learned strategies (β=-1.94, p<.001), with no difference between them (p=.207), suggesting that as intended, the sessions were comparable in terms of ease of strategy implementation.

Changes in pain ratings during the acceptance training task

There was no difference between training sessions in the heat temperature (°C) administered during training (p=.56), which corresponded to the temperature required to elicit pain ratings of 40 on a 0–100 numerical analog scale (NAS), as assessed prior to training (see Table 1).

Average pain ratings decreased significantly from pre- to post-training in both training sessions (F(1,76) = 35.89, p<.001, ƞ²=0.32), with no differences between the sessions at baseline or at the last time point (both ps>0.10). However, a mixed linear model conducted across the five task blocks showed a main effect of session (β = 5.96, p<.001), indicating lower ratings throughout the task in the active compared to the control session.

Similarly, ratings of pain unpleasantness decreased from pre- to post-training in both sessions (F(1,76) = 35.75, p<.001, ƞ²=0.35), with no differences between sessions at baseline or at the final assessment (ps>0.08). However, across the five blocks, pain unpleasantness ratings were overall lower in the active acceptance strategy session compared to the control session (β = 8.25, p<.001).

Group-level training efficacy on QST experimental pain sensitivity

To evaluate the effects of training on pain sensitivity, a QST procedure was administered independently of the training, both before and after its completion. First, pain sensitivity was operationalized as the heat temperatures (°C) required to elicit pain ratings of 40 and 60 on a 0–100 NAS (pain-40 and pain-60, respectively). The QST procedure also included measures of tonic heat pain (THP) intensity, conditioned pain modulation (CPM), and cold pressor test (CPT) threshold and tolerance. Importently, at baseline before strategy implementation phase, there were no differences between active and control sessions in any of the QST measures (all ps > 0.1).

When evaluating the training effect on the QST measures, a significant main effect of phase emerged, with higher heat temperatures required to elicit a pain-40 rating at the post-training assessemt compared with the pre-training assessment (F(1,39) = 7.63, p=.009, ƞ²=0.164, see means in Table 1), indicating reduced pain sensitivity following the training. There was no main effect of session type or a phase × session interaction (both ps>0.46). There was also no order effect (F(1,38) = 0.2, p=.657), indicating comparable changes across experimental trials.

Similarly, pain-60 temperatures were higher post-training (F(1,39) = 5.50, p=.024, ƞ²=0.124), with no main effect of session and no phase × session interaction (both ps>0.092), and again with no order effect (F(1,38) = 0.11, p=.91).

No significant pre–post changes were observed in THP, CPM, or CPT measures (all ps>0.19; for the full reported ANOVA tables, see Sect. 4 in the supplementary materials), suggesting that reduced pain sensitivity was specific to heat sensitivity and did not generalize to other pain sub-modalities. For training effects on the secondary cognitive and psychological measures, as well as their associations with changes in pain, see Sect. 5 in the supplementary materials.

Predictors of training-related pain reduction

Predictors of suprathreshold pain-40 temperature change

To examine predictors of change in heat temperatures required to elicit a pain-40 rating following the acceptance training we first constructed a full model including all cognitive and psychological baseline predictors included in the pre-training baseline evaluation along with their interactions with the session condition (active vs. control session, see Sect. 6 in the supplementary materials). Following Montgomery et al. (2021), a simplified model retaining only significant predictors and interactions was then derived and compared to the full model. The simplified model was preferred based on parsimony58(χ²(14) = 16.56, p=.28; AIC full: 213.87, AIC simplified: 202.44), and explained 10.6% of the variance in pain-40 temperature changes (Marginal R²=0.106).

The prediction model revealed a significant interaction between session and interference from pain-related images in the low perceptual load condition of the perceptual load task (p=.021; Table 2). This result indicted that, in the active session only, greater interference from pain-related images predicted reduced pain sensitivity following the training, reflected by higher pain-40 temperatures post-training (b = 0.02, t(39) = 2.72, p=.03; Fig. 2B). No such effect was observed in the control session (t(39) = 0.02, p=.98).

Predicting suprathreshold pain-60 temperature change

Using the same model-building approach, a simplified prediction model was preferred over the full model for predicting changes in the heat temperatures corresponding to pain-60 ratings (χ²(12) = 10.18, p=.60; AIC full model: 216.35, AIC simplified model: 202.53), and explained 20.7% of the variance in pain-60 temperature changes (Marginal R²=0.207).

In this prediction model, lower attention bias in the dot-probe task significantly predicted greater reductions in pain-60 sensitivity (β=− 0.45, p=.024). In addition, significant interactions were observed between session effect and interference from pain-related images under the low perceptual load of the perceptual load task (p=.028) and between session effect and fear of pain (p=.029).

Follow-up analyses indicated that these three predictors contributed independently to pain outcomes. Specifically, neither two-way nor three-way interactions between pain-related interference, fear of pain, and session were significant (all ps>0.59), suggesting that these predictors influenced training outcomes independently from each other. To further validate these findings, separate models including each interaction term, while controlling for the other predictors and applying Bonferroni correction, confirmed that both interactions remained significant (fear of pain × training session: p=.048; pain-related interference × training session: p=.042), while attention bias did not remain a significant predictor (ps>0.12).

To better understand these prediction effects, post-hoc simple slope analyses with Bonferroni-corrected p-value were conducted, revealing that higher fear of pain predicted greater pain-60 reduction only in the active acceptance session (b = 0.09, t(39) = 2.33, p=.04; Fig. 2C), with no effect in the control session (t(39) = 0.56, p=.72). Similarly, greater interference from pain-related images in the perceptual load task predicted greater pain-60 sensativity reduction in the active acceptance session (b = 0.03, t(39) = 3.05, p<.001; Fig. 2D), but not in the control (t(39) = 0.71, p=.48).

ABM training

Training evaluation

Performance accuracy during the ABM training tasks

Accuracy in identifying the probe letter during the training tasks was high (84–100%) and similar across active and control ABM sessions (t(39) = 1.31, p=.19), indicating both sessions were equally easy to perform, as intended.

Attention bias to pain following ABM training

There was no significant change in attention bias from pre- to post-training (F(1,76) = 0.45, p=.50), with no interaction between time and session type (F(1,76) = 0.3, p=.72), indicating that attention bias to pain-related words in the dot-probe task did not change following either the active or control sessions. Accuracy rates also did not change before and after both sessions (active: t(39) = 0.095, p=.92; control: t(39) = 0.57, p=.57).

Pain ratings along the ABM training tasks

There was no difference between training sessions in the heat temperature (°C) administered during training (p=.72). Average pain intensity ratings decreased significantly from pre- to post-training in both sessions (F(1,79) = 47.1, p<.001, ƞ²=0.37), with no differences between sessions at baseline or at the last time point (both ps>0.10). In contrast to the acceptance training, pain ratings did not differ significantly across the five task blocks (p=.071), indicating comparable changes in pain sensativity over time.

Ratings of pain unpleasantness decreased from pre- to post-training in both sessions (F(1,77) = 31.48, p<.001, ƞ²=0.29), with no differences between sessions at baseline or at the final time point (both ps>0.3). However, during the five training task blocks, unpleasantness ratings were overall lower in the active compared to the control session (β = 4.75, p=.016).

ABM training effects on QST experimental pain sensitivity at the group level

At baseline, there were no differences between active and control ABM sessions in all QST measures (all ps>0.12).

When evaluating the ABM training effect on the QST measures, pain-40 temperatures were significantly higher post-training compared to pre-training (F(2,38) = 4.13, p=.049, ƞ²=0.098), indicating reduced pain sensitivity following the training. There was no main effect of session type or a phase × session interaction, and no order effect (all ps>0.17). No significant changes were found in all other QST measures, including Pain-60, THP, CPM, or CPT threshold and tolerance (ps>0.13, see Sect. 4 in the supplementary materials), indicating that the ABM training reduced pain sensitivity only to the pain stimuli participants were exposed to during the training session.

Predictors of ABM training-related pain reduction

As group-level changes in the ABM training emerged only for pain-40, a mixed-effects linear regression was conducted focusing exclusively on predictors of changes in pain-40 temperature. A comparison between the full and simplified models revealed no significant difference (χ²(17) = 22.34, p=.172; AIC full: 228.1, AIC simplified: 216.4), favoring the more parsimonious model (see Table S8, supplementary materials). In the simplified model, baseline pain-40 temperature was the only retained predictor, but neither it nor training session type reached significance (all ps>0.07), suggesting that no variable significantly predicted changes in pain-40 temperature following the ABM training.

Discussion

This study examined how individual cognitive and emotional characteristics influence the efficacy of two commonly used cognitive trainings (i.e., acceptance and ABM training) designed to target pain-related cognitive mechanisms. Our preliminary results suggest that at the group level, neither training produced pain relief beyond that observed in the matched control conditions, suggesting limited overall effectiveness. However, substantial individual variability emerged. Specifically, greater reductions in pain sensitivity following the active acceptance session were predicted by higher baseline levels of pain-related attentional interference and greater fear of pain. In contrast, no baseline characteristics predicted changes in pain sensitivity in the acceptance control session or either ABM session. These preliminary findings underscore the differential efficacy of the two interventions and highlight the importance of individual cognitive processes and emotional traits in shaping pain modulation mechanisms and determining treatment response.

To better understand the differential effects of each intervention, the following sections separately discuss findings from the acceptance and ABM trainings, focusing on group-level outcomes and individual differences in responses to the interventions.

Acceptance training

Our primary goal was to investigate individual differences in factors that modulate intervention efficiency. Indeed, while the overall group-level effects of the acceptance training were not statistically significant, a closer examination of individual differences revealed preliminary evidence for a meaningful variability in treatment response. Specifically, participants who showed greater attentional interference from pain-related cues experienced more pronounced reductions in pain sensitivity following the acceptance training. When individuals find their pain unacceptable, they are likely to prioritize avoiding it at all costs, often seeking immediate strategies to reduce or eliminate it. Paradoxically, this avoidance requires heightened sensitivity to pain signals, leading to increased attention toward potential pain cues59. Acceptance training may counteract this cycle by helping individuals attend to and acknowledge pain without judgment, lowering the hold of maladaptive cognitive patterns, such as rumination, exaggeration, and anger, and encouraging open awareness to all present-moment experiences60. Supporting that, previous research has shown that individuals with higher levels of pain acceptance report less attentional focus on pain and greater participation in daily activities, even in the presence of ongoing pain61,62.

In addition to attentional interference, we found that higher baseline levels of fear of pain predicted greater reductions in pain sensitivity following active acceptance training. This finding aligns with the fear-avoidance model as well as with the psychological flexibility model guiding psychological pain interventions59,63, highlighting the role of fears and catastrophizing in pain amplification and chronification4,64. Fear of pain elicits defensive mobilization, marked by heightened arousal, safety-seeking behaviors, and avoidance, factors that contribute to emotional distress and intensify the subjective experience of pain4,7,65. Empirical research further supports these links7, showing that individuals with a higher fear of pain report greater pain intensity18, increased disability17, and elevated pain-related anxiety7,66, which in turn is associated with heightened pain sensitivity67,68.

Consistent with this framework, greater reductions in state anxiety following the acceptance training were associated with lower post-training pain sensitivity. Together, these findings suggest that individuals with elevated pain-related fear and anxiety may benefit most from acceptance-based interventions, with emotional distress reduction serving as a key mechanism of change. This is in line with previous research showing that both baseline emotional functioning49,69, and reductions in pain-related anxiety55,56 predict improvements in pain severity and interference following psychological pain interventions.

Importently, our preliminary findings are particularly noteworthy in light of acceptance as an emotion regulation strategy. Unlike other emotion regulation strategies that aim to alter the experience of pain or emotion, acceptance focuses on perceiving the experience without trying to control or suppress it70,71. Neuroimaging evidence supports this distinction, showing that while strategies like reappraisal engage prefrontal control mechanisms to downregulate distress, acceptance reduces limbic and pain-related brain activity without increasing prefrontal engagement23,31. This may explain why individuals with heightened emotional reactivity, such as those experiencing strong fear of pain, are responsive to acceptance strategies, as these approaches target limbic overactivation directly, without requiring cognitively demanding control processes72. Future studies should examine whether a comprehensive assessment of both emotional traits and neurobiological pain processing can help predict who is most likely to benefit from acceptance-based training, ultimately enabling more personalized and effective interventions.

Despite these promising theoretical accounts, the group-level behavioral effects observed in the present study were modest. Reduced pain sensitivity emerged only for the trained suprathreshold heat stimulus following the active acceptance session. However, this reduction did not differ from changes in the control session nor extend to other pain sub-modalities, suggesting no clear advantage of the active training. These preliminary findings stand in contrast to prior evidence showing that acceptance-based strategies can reduce pain intensity and unpleasantness, increase pain tolerance, and enhance willingness to endure pain23–25,28,30,73.

One factor that may account for these discrepancies is our focus on generalization across thermal pain sub-modalities. Whereas most studies examined changes in a single modality targeted during training (e.g., heat, cold, or electrical pain), we assessed whether acceptance training for suprathreshold heat pain would generalize to untrained varied pain measures. While minor reductions were observed for trained heat stimuli, no generalization occurred to untrained sub-modalities. This suggests that a single training session may not suffice for cross-modality effects and that broader generalization may require extended practice and greater familiarity with the strategy52. Alternatively, these findings may indicate that effective symptom relief from acceptance interventions, particularly in clinical settings, requires targeting the specific pain modalities or symptoms most relevant to the individual’s experience, and that inter-individual differences in responsiveness may obscure effects at the group level.

Besides, unlike prior studies that instructed participants to actively apply acceptance regulation during pain assessments, we did not provide explicit instructions for its later use. This allowed us to examine spontaneous generalization and avoid demand characteristics previously shown to influence outcomes in acceptance studies31,74. We also did not directly assess participants’ actual strategy use or perceived effectiveness during the tasks, to prevent carryover effects that might shape expectations or behavior in the subsequent session. However, as our training effects were relatively smaller compared to previous findings on pain acceptance-based interventions, this suggests that intentional or guided use may be crucial for maximizing the effectiveness of acceptance strategies52,75. This idea further aligns with our findings showing that participants in both the active and control sessions reported a gradual decline in perceived regulation difficulty over time, consistent with evidence that regulatory skills improve with practice76,77. Together, these findings highlight the value of structured, multi-session training programs, particularly in clinical settings where adherence may be affected by intervention complexity78. Future work should investigate optimal training durations and differences between directed and undirected trainings, alongside sensitive assessments of adherence and strategy usage during pain, to promote broader and more durable effects of acceptance-based interventions.

ABM training

The present study provides no clear evidence for beneficial effects of ABM on pain sensitivity. Although pain sensitivity to the trained suprathreshold heat stimulus decreased following active training, this reduction did not differ from the control session and did not generalize to other pain sub-modalities. Furthermore, our preliminary results showed that neither attention bias toward pain cues nor any measured emotional variables predicted changes in pain sensitivity, indicating a limited impact of the intervention.

ABM training offers an experimental framework for exploring the role of attention in pain perception15. Specifically, attentional biases—such as facilitated attention to pain and difficulties in disengaging from pain-related cues—are believed to amplify pain both directly, through enhanced processing, and indirectly, by increasing fear and disability14. By modifying attentional focus, ABM aims to modify these biases and, in turn, influence pain perception. Despite this theoretical rationale, findings across studies are inconsistent, likely reflecting differences in methodology and sample characteristics35,44. In particular, differences in how ABM paradigms are implemented and measured may account for the variability in observed outcomes. For example, many studies compare training in directing attention away from pain to conditions in which attention is directed toward pain stimuli38,39,45, potentially amplifying between-group differences due to the negative impact of the latter condition.

Another methodological factor concerns the characteristics and sensitivity of the ABM paradigm used. The dot-probe task, used both during training and for measuring attentional bias, has been found to have relatively low reliability79, which may limit the ability to detect subtle attentional changes. In addition, although some studies suggest that using pain-related words as cues in the training task may produce more generalized effects than using or image-based stimuli38,80, the use of verbal pain-related cues in the current study may have reduced stimulus salience compared to more ecologically valid and emotionally engaging stimuli such as facial expressions or sensory-related images, thereby attenuating attentional capture and training effects81.

Previous research further indicates that variations in stimulus duration and attention bias assessment parameters , even within commonly used intervals such as 500 ms, can differentially influence attentional disengagement and training transfer effects, with shorter or longer exposures producing distinct outcomes in attentional control and related behavioral measures82,83. For example, Koster et al. (2010) found that an attention bias modification task with 500ms presentation time selectively influenced later attentional processes (e.g., 1500 ms), but not very early processes (100 ms)84. Similarly, Liang and Hsu (2016) reported that RT effects varied as a function of exposure duration (100ms vs 500 ms) duringtraining and assessment phases85. The effects of ABM may also depend on both the type and the timings of attentional bias being assessed. A recent meta-analysis evaluating studies assessing attentional biases towards pain using eye-tracking indicates that such biases are more evident during early attentional processes, such as first fixation and initial dwell time within the first 500 ms86. Thus, it is therefore plausible that changes in rapid, early-stage biases following ABM training may not be fully captured by conventional dot-probe assessments and my requirement more sensitive measurements (for a comprehensive review on this topic, see also87). Together, these findings highlight that both the temporal characteristics of stimulus presentation and the assessment method may constrain the ability of the paradigm to effectively induce or detect attentional bias changes .

Consistent with these claims, our training did not find any changes in attentional biases to pain, as measured by two distinct RT tasks nor changes in pain sensitivity. These outcomes align with the mixed findings in the literature, with some studies reporting reduced pain sensitivity following ABM in both healthy individuals37–39 and chronic pain populations40,41,54, while others report null effects42–44. Notably, recent reviews suggest that successful modification of attentional biases is a necessary condition for downstream symptom change36,57,88. Accordingly, studies that fail to induce attentional bias changes typically also fail to demonstrate symptom improvement, which may explain the lack of pain reduction in the present study . At the same time, emerging evidence suggests that more adaptive and individualized ABM protocols may enhance training efficacy. For example, tasks that dynamically adjust presentation times or difficulty levels based on individual performance have been associated with greater changes in attentional biases and, consequently, enhanced training gains and symptom reduction89. While such approaches focus on optimizing the training paradigm itself, the present study adopted a complementary approach, by examining individual factors that may influence responsiveness to a standardized training protocol90,91. Future research should therefore examine whether combining optimized stimulus saliency and task design with the identification of individual predictors of treatment response, alongside the use of more sensitive attention biases assessment (e.g., eye-tracking technology45,86) before and after the training, can improve both the detection and modification of attentional biases toward pain and ultimately enhance reductions in pain sensitivity.

Interestingly, although ABM training did not modify attentional biases or reduce pain perception in the current study, attentional interference predicted the benefit from acceptance training. This suggests that while attentional biases are relevant to pain processing, modifying them in isolation may be insufficient to produce robust changes in pain sensitivity. Instead, attentional biases may serve as markers of vulnerability, identifying individuals who are more engaged with pain-related threat and therefore more likely to exhibit heightened responses to pain or to benefit more or less from a specific intervention14,15,92. For instance, attentional biases assessed prior to medical procedures have been shown to predict acute post-surgical pain93, analgesic use one week later94, and even pain outcomes at 3- and 6-month follow-ups95. Morover, attentional biases have also been linked to reduced efficacy of distraction from pain among healthy individuals92.

According to contemporary cognitive models of attentional modulation of pain96,97, nociceptive stimuli can capture attention by interrupting goal-directed processing and prioritizing escape or survival responses. However, such attentional capture is less likely when other cognitive processes are actively engaged to maintain goal priorities and shield ongoing processing from distraction5,96,97. In this context, attentional biases toward pain—particularly when stimuli are task-irrelevant and do not signal immediate threat—may reflect difficulties in regulatory control, heightened emotional reactivity, and a tendency to prioritize potential threat over current goals14. Consistent with this account, attentional biases have been associated with higher pain catastrophizing86 and have been shown to moderate the relationship between daily pain severity and both disability and distractibility98. Hence, it is reasonable that our acceptance intervention was more effective for individuals with pre-existing attentional biases, as this training emphasizes accepting painful sensations while maintaining engagement with current, non-pain-related goals99,100. These findings highlight the potential value of using baseline attentional bias profiles to guide personalized intervention selection, whereby individuals with higher attentional engagement to pain may preferentially benefit from acceptance-based approaches, whereas those with different cognitive profiles might respond better to alternative strategies. Together, these findings further suggest that attentional biases may function less as a direct mechanism of change and more as moderators that shape the relationship between perceived pain and its cognitive and behavioral consequences.

In line with this interpretation, while our ABM training did not result in consistent changes in pain sensitivity or attentional biases, a notable reduction in state anxiety was observed across both the active and control sessions. This decrease appears to be independent of training direction and may reflect shared cognitive demands across both ABM sessions. One possible explanation relates to a novel feature of our task design: unlike earlier ABM studies, we incorporated heat pain stimuli after every 10 trials. This may have increased the salience of pain cues and required participants to rapidly redirect attention away from pain, potentially functioning as an implicit attentional control exercise.

This interpretation is consistent with the Attentional Control Theory which posits that deficits in attentional control contribute to heightened anxiety101, and reduced emotion regulation102–104. Strengthening attentional control has been proposed as one mechanism through which attentional training may reduce anxiety, particularly in individuals with high trait anxiety105–107. Although attentional control was not directly measured in the present study, the reduction in state anxiety observed specifically during the ABM sessions (but not during acceptance sessions), together with evidence that ABM can enhance attentional control108, suggests that the attentional demands of the task may have contributed to this effect. However, this interpretation should be taken with caution, as reductions in state anxiety could also reflect increased familiarity with the procedures and experimental environment, which can, in turn, modulate both attention and pain109–112. Future studies should assess attentional control directly to further clarify its causal associations with anxiety reduction in the context of ABM and acceptance-based interventions106,113.

Study limitations and future directions

This study examined the effects of two cognitive trainings in a sample of healthy young females. Males, older individuals, and people with chronic pain may differ in both pain processing and responsiveness to cognitive training47,50. For example, prior research shows that ABM effectiveness can vary by age114, and the impact of acceptance training may differ by sex69, with lower baseline anxiety predicting greater pain reduction in females, whereas the opposite was found in males49. While the homogeneous sample in our study helps control for potential confounds related to the influence of sex and age on both pain sensitivity and on the effect of the training, it also restricts the generalization of the results and their clinical applicability.

Moreover, because the study was conducted in a non-clinical population, baseline attentional biases were relatively small, raising the possibility of a floor effect. Participants, therefore, had limited room for improvement and may not have fully benefited from the training, which could have contributed to the observed null effects. Future research should examine whether stronger effects emerge in populations with chronic pain or attention deficits, where attentional biases are more pronounced and ABM interventions may yield greater benefits33,34,82,115.

The relatively small sample size further limits the strength of our conclusions. Reduced statistical power, particularly for detecting interaction effects or small training effects in our mixed-effects models with multiple predictors, may have hindered our ability to capture subtle effects. This limitation may have contributed to the null findings at the group level and constrains the robustness and interpretability of the results. Taken together, these methodological constraints suggest that our findings should be regarded as preliminary and primarily applicable to young adult females. Future studies should therefore examine how individual differences shape training efficacy in larger, more diverse, and clinically relevant populations.

Finally, beyond issues of sample composition and size, the study design itself presents limitations. The use of a single-session training protocol may restrict participants’ ability to fully benefit from the training or to generalize the learned strategies across different contexts57. However, while some studies suggest that increasing the number of training trials and sessions enhances the effects of cognitive training12,116,117, others report no significant improvements when increasing the number of training trials114. Besides, although the control sessions were designed to match the active training while excluding its core components, they were not entirely inert and may have involved non-specific effects (e.g., task engagement or repeated exposure to pain), which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Consequently, the observed effects should be interpreted as reflecting differences between two closely matched conditions rather than between an active intervention and a fully passive baseline.

Specific features of the training protocol and tasks might have also influenced the results, as reported in previous studies. For instance, stimulus characteristics (e.g., words versus faces38; sensory or affective words45, and presentation times80 can affect the efficacy of ABM trainings. Similarly, aspects of the QST assessments, including the CPM paradigm, may have impacted outcomes. Although a 5-minute rest was included before subsequent tasks to minimize carry-over effects, residual CPM-induced analgesia could still influence subsequent pain assessments, as its duration varies between individuals. Additionally, the relatively large number of experimental tasks may have increased cognitive load or fatigue in some participants, potentially affecting performance despite rest periods. Nevertheless, the inclusion of rest periods, the sequencing of tasks, and the measurement of cognitive performance both at the start of the session and after the interventions likely mitigated these effects. Future studies could include a passive control group assessed at two time points without intervention to better isolate training-specific effects, as well as optimize training protocols and task features to maximize efficacy.

Conclusions

Our preliminary findings show that, although ABM and acceptance training did not reduce pain sensitivity at the group level, individual baseline characteristics moderated the effectiveness of acceptance training. This highlights a potential for personalized pain management, especially among individuals with heightened pain-related attention or fear of pain. Acceptance training is easy to implement, adaptable to patients’ own pain experiences, and suitable for digital delivery, enhancing its clinical relevance. Future research should extend these preliminary findings and examine their generalization to other pain modalities and optimize tailoring and practice to maximize effectiveness across diverse pain populations.

Methods

Participants

Ninety-two healthy women participated in the study, which was conducted as part of a broader research project investigating how cognitive, psychological, and physiological factors shape pain perception and modulation, with a specific focus on women. Additional studies based on this dataset have been published118,119, focusing on associations among baseline variables, whereas the present study focuses on the training-related data. Participants were recruited over a one-year period, from December 2023 to December 2024. The experiment was conducted at the university, and all participants provided informed consent before participation. Due to technical issues, six participants could not complete the study; four missed the second session, and two were excluded for non-compliance. Thus, the final sample size included eighty participants (Mean age 23.24 ± 3; age range 18–33). Demographic information and psychological characteristics by training condition are presented in Table 3. The only significant difference between the training groups was the distribution of participants’ first language (χ²(1) = 11.43, p<.001); all other demographic variables and psychological factors did not differ between groups and between training sessions (all ps>0.1).

Participants were recruited via the university research participation system and social media advertisements. Inclusion criteria included: (i) reading fluency, (ii) age 18–40, (iii) no ADHD diagnosis, (iv) no acute or chronic pain in the last three months, (v) no cardiovascular, metabolic or neurological diseases, (vi) no current pregnancy, and (viii) no current use of analgesics or psychiatric medications. Participants were asked to avoid caffeine for 3 h and analgesics for 24 h before testing.

This study was approved by the University of Haifa Ethics Committee (approval number 438/21), and the research was performed in accordance with relevant regulations. Participants’ privacy rights were strictly observed following ethical guidelines, and they retain the autonomy to cease the experiment and the stimulations at any point. At the end of each experimental session, participants were debriefed and compensated with either course credit or monetary payment ($90), according to their preference, consistent with standard compensation rates ($15 per hour).

Experimental procedure

Figure 3 illustrates the experimental procedure. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two training approaches: ABM or acceptance training. Each participant completed two sessions, one month apart: one involving the active version of the assigned training approach, and one involving the control version of the same approach. That is, participants were only exposed to one training type (ABM or acceptance), but experienced both the active and control versions of that type. The order of active and control sessions was counterbalanced: half of the participants began with the active session, while the other half started with the control session. A relatively long interval of one month between sessions was implemented to minimize potential carryover effects, allowing any transient effects from the first session to dissipate and reducing the likelihood that performance or learning from the first session would influence responses in the subsequent session. The control (sham) sessions were selected based on commonly used procedures in the cognitive training literature (e.g.,42,53,54 for ABM; 23,28 for acceptance-based training). These control conditions were designed to closely match the active training in task structure, duration, and cognitive demands, while omitting the key mechanism of change specific to each intervention (i.e., attentional bias modification in ABM, and acceptance-based processes in the acceptance training).

Upon arrival to each session, participants completed demographic assessments (e.g., age, body mass index [BMI], first language) and self-report questionnaires, including the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), the Fear of Pain Questionnaire (FPQ-9), the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory – State (STAI-S), the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21), and the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS). To assess attentional biases, participants completed both the dot-probe task using pain-related words and a modified perceptual load task using pain-related images (see Sect. 4.4.4). Following these assessments, the QST protocol was used, which involved familiarization with the testing devices and procedures for tonic heat pain (THP), conditioned pain modulation (CPM) paradigm, and the cold pressor test (CPT) (see Sect. 4.4.3). The QST assessments were always conducted in the same fixed order to maintain consistency across participants and sessions.

Next, participants completed a 30-minute training session according to their assigned condition. Immediately after training, they completed a second dot-probe test block. After a short break, participants filled out the STAI-S questionnaire and repeated the perceptual load task and QST assessments to examine the effects of training on experimental pain. The STAI-S was the only psychological measure assessed after the training, as it captures state anxiety, whereas the other questionnaires (PCS, FPQ, DERS, and DASS-21) reflect trait-like or more stable aspects of psychological functioning, which were not expected to change after such a brief intervention.

To maintain a double-blind design, training and assessment procedures were conducted by separate experimenters, ensuring that the individuals administering the training were distinct from those conducting the measurements. Participants were blinded to the study aims, the implications of the strategy use, their allocated condition, and the randomized sequence. Additionally, randomization lists were generated by a collaborator using a computer program and were strictly adhered to throughout the study.

Training tasks

Each training session lasted approximately 30 min and contained 5 blocks with a self-timed break between each block (lasting on average 1 min). To minimize demand characteristics, the term “training” was deliberately avoided when explaining the procedure, as was done by Denny et al. (2020)71.

In both training types, the individually calibrated pain-40 temperature was applied to the non-dominant hand as the painful stimulus. At the start of each session, this temperature was revalidated using three consecutive ratings on a computerized visual analog scale (VAS; 0 = no pain, 100 = most intense pain imaginable). If the average rating fell below 30 or above 60, the temperature was adjusted by 1 °C, and pain was reassessed. Across participants, adjustments were infrequent and showed no systematic pattern between the two training sessions.

Acceptance training

We employed a task commonly used in emotion regulation and pain studies23,120, programmed using PsychoPy software121. The training task began with an explanation phase, followed by a brief practice phase in which participants applied the learned strategy during four short pain stimulations, verbalized their approach, and received feedback. To ensure understanding and adherence to the strategy, participants answered a multiple-choice question about their strategy implementation after each stimulus. Incorrect responses prompted further clarification of the strategy, both via on-screen instructions and additional explanation from the experimenter.

In the active acceptance strategy, participants were instructed to observe and accept their pain-related sensations, emotions, and thoughts without reacting or trying to avoid them. After familiarization, the “bus metaphor” was introduced, where emotions, thoughts, and sensations were compared to passengers shouting directions at the bus driver. Participants were encouraged to accept these “passengers” without reacting, recognizing their temporary nature.

In the control acceptance strategy, participants were instructed to focus on their pain sensations and respond naturally to their emotions without using any specific strategy. The “bus metaphor” here encouraged participants to let themself react naturally to the disruptive “passengers”, such as acknowledging feelings of unfairness or frustration while letting themselves immerse in the full range of emotions aroused by the pain. For a detailed script description of both strategy explanations and the full practice procedure, see Sect. 7 in the supplementary materials.

At the end of the explanation phase, participants completed 20 min of training in the learned strategy. Each block contained 8 trials, resulting in 48 trials in total. Each trial started with a 5-second presentation of a black fixation cross in the center of the screen (see Fig. 4A). Following this, a strategy cue appeared on the screen (either “Accept” or “React Naturally”, depending on the participant’s assigned session) and remained visible for 5 s. Participants were instructed to adopt the corresponding mindset for the remainder of the trial. After the strategy cue disappeared, an 8–10 s heat stimulus was applied, during which participants were asked to manage the pain using the cued strategy. A 10-second interstimulus interval (ISI) followed each stimulus, leading into the next trial. At the end of each block, they were asked to rate average pain intensity and unpleasantness felt during the block using two computerized VAS scales.

Attention bias modification for pain (ABM) training

For the ABM training, a modified version of the dot-probe task was used53,54 programmed in PsychoPy software121. The aim of the version used in the current study was to train attention away from the pain-related stimulus and toward the neutral one (i.e., ABM-avoidant training). The training was also adapted to include real painful stimulation, ensuring pain cues were more prominent and aligned with real-world experiences rather than relying solely on abstract pain-related stimuli.

Each block contained 80 trials, leading to 400 trials in total. Each trial began with a fixation screen for 500 ms. Then, one stimulus pair comprising a pain word and a neutral word appeared and remained visible for a short duration of 500 ms. After the word pair disappeared, one letter, either ‘p’ or ‘q’ (i.e., probe), appeared at the location of one of the words (see Fig. 4B). Participants were instructed to fixate at the center of the screen and to use the keyboard to indicate, as quickly and accurately as possible, which probe appeared. The probe disappeared immediately after the keypress or after one second, and a blank screen appeared for 200 ms before the next trial. Reaction times (RTs) and accuracy rates in indicating the probe identification were measured during all phases of the training session.

In the Active ABM training, the probe appeared 80% of the time at the location of the neutral word. Thus, in most training trials, participants were required to direct their attention away from pain words to detect the probe, though without being explicitly instructed to do so. In contrast, in the Control ABM training, the probe appeared equally often at both locations (i.e., at 50% of trials at the location of the neutral and 50% of trials at the location of the pain-related word), thus no training toward one kind of stimulus was expected.

In each trial, a pair of words was randomly selected from 30 pain–neutral pairs. The position of the pain-related word (top or bottom) and the probe (top or bottom) was counterbalanced, such that each of the four possible combinations occurred equally often (probe up/pain-related word up; probe up/ pain-related word down; probe down/pain-related word up; probe down/ pain-related word down). For details about the word selection and pairing procedure, see the supplementary materials, Sect. 1. Besides, to increase engagement and pain relevance, participants received a heat pain stimulus corresponding to their pain-40 temperature on their non-dominant hand for 8–10 s after every 10 trials. This resulted in 8 painful stimuli per block, and a total of 48 pain stimuli across the task, as was done in the acceptance training.

Measures

Measures assessed during training tasks

In both tasks, at the end of each of the five blocks, participants rated average pain intensity and unpleasantness felt during the block using two computerized 0-100 VAS scales. During the acceptance training, at the end of each block, participants also rated how easily they could apply the strategy’s instructions on a computerized VAS scale ranging from 0 (not easy at all) to 100 (extremely easy). In the ABM training, RTs and accuracy rates in indicating the probe identification were calculated for each block.

Quantitative sensory testing (QST)

Suprathreshold pain rating: determination of pain-40 and pain-60 temperatures

To determine individual temperatures corresponding to numerical pain scale (NPS) ratings of 40 and 60 (on a 0–100 scale), heat stimuli (43 °C, 45 °C, 47 °C; baseline 32 °C; ramp: 2 °C/sec; 8 s each) were delivered via a 30 × 30 mm Peltier thermode (TSA-2001, Medoc, Israel) applied to the dominant forearm, with 15-second intervals between stimuli. Participants rated each stimulus individually on the NPS (0 = no pain, 100 = maximum imaginable pain).

Based on initial ratings, additional heat stimuli, ranging between 39°C - 48°C, were presented in a randomized order, with the thermode repositioned after each heat stimuli application to prevent sensitization or habituation. This process continued until one temperature was consistently rated as 40 (Pain-40) and another as 60 (Pain-60) across three trials. Pain-40 was used in training sessions, and Pain-60 in the tonic heat pain and CPM procedures.

Tonic heat pain (THP)

In this procedure, the temperature of the thermode attached to the forearm of the dominant hand was increased at a rate of 2 °C/sec, from 32 °C to the participant’s pain-60 temperature, and remained fixed for 60 s. Participants verbally rated pain intensity on the NPS when the thermode reached the target temperature and every 10 s after that, resulting in 7 ratings overall. The THP pain value was calculated by averaging the 7 ratings given during the THP stimulation, and was also used as the test stimulus in the CPM paradigm.

Conditioned pain modulation (CPM)

CPM was assessed using a standardized protocol using hot water (45.5 °C) as the conditioning stimulus122–125, to avoid confusion and limit carryover effects to the subsequent cold pressor test (CPT). Participants were first familiarized with the water bath (Heto Cooling Bath CBN 8–30, Denmark) used as the conditioning stimulus by immersing their non-dominant hand for 20 s and rating their perceived pain. Following standard recommendations123, the water temperature was adjusted if the rating was below 20 or above 70 on the NPS, and was rated again by the participant [final temperatures: 44–47 °C; M = 45.24 °C (0.74)].

CPM was then assessed. Participants immersed their non-dominant hand in the hot water and verbally rated the pain on the NPS after 20 s. Immediately afterward, a pain-60 stimulus was applied to the dominant forearm, following the same procedure as in the THP assessment: the temperature increased from 32 °C at a rate of 2 °C/s until reaching the participant’s pain-60 threshold, which was maintained for 60 s. While keeping the hand immersed, participants were instructed to focus on the heat pain stimulus from the thermode and verbally rate the pain every 10 s. Before removing the immersed hand, they rated the water bath pain again. Participants were given a 5-minute rest after the CPM measurement before the next task.

CPM magnitude was calculated by subtracting the average THP rating (test-stimulus alone) from the average THP rating during the conditioning stimulus, with more negative values indicating better pain inhibition.

Cold pressure task (CPT)

The CPT involved a cold-water bath at 5 °C to induce moderate pain. Participants immersed their non-dominant hand, indicated when the cold sensation became painful (CPT pain threshold), and left their hand in as long as possible (CPT pain tolerance). A maximum limit of five minutes was set for safety and to prevent confounding results due to numbing, though participants were unaware of this time restriction126,127.

Psychological and cognitive predictors

Emotional predictors

Catastrophic thinking about pain was assessed by the Hebrew version of the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS128,129, which includes 13 items rated on a 5-point scale (0 = not at all, 4 = always) assessing pain rumination, magnification, and helplessness. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93 for both sessions. Participants also completed the Fear of Pain Questionnaire130, rating fear of nine painful situations from 1 (not afraid) to 5 (very afraid). The Hebrew version was validated using inter-judge reliability131. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85 for session 1 and 0.79 for session 2.

Participants’ psychological state was assessed using the Hebrew version of the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21132), where they rated 21 items on a 4-point scale (0 = Never, 3 = Almost always) based on their experiences over the past week. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.91 for session 1 and 0.89 for session 2. State anxiety before and after training was measured with the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory – State (STAI-S133), with participants rating their current anxiety using 20 items on a 4-point scale (1 = not at all, 4 = very much). The version used in the current experiment134 showed Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 for session 1 and 0.92 for session 2. Emotion regulation abilities were assessed using the Hebrew version of the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale135. This 36-item self-report questionnaire evaluates difficulties in regulating negative emotions, with items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = almost never, 5 = almost always). Higher scores indicate greater difficulties in emotion regulation. Cronbach’s alpha for the current sample was 0.92.

Cognitive tasks

Dot probe task

The same dot-probe task used in the ABM training was administered to assess attentional bias before and after each training session. In this task, participants completed one test block of 80 trials, where the probe randomly appeared in the location of either the neutral or pain word. RTs and accuracy were recorded, and the attention bias score was calculated by subtracting the RTs for correct trials where the probe appeared in the neutral word’s location from trials where the probe appeared in the pain word’s location.

Pain modification of the perceptual load task

To measure generalizable effects of the ABM task and to test far transfer to an additional attentional task assessing difficulties disengaging from pain-related cues, participants performed a novel modification of the perceptual load paradigm136,137 programmed using E-Prime 3.0 software138. In this task, participants were required to indicate whether a target letter (X or N) appeared on the screen by responding to the corresponding letter on the keyboard, while ignoring a pain-related or neutral distracting image presented simultaneously.

The task contained two perceptual load conditions, low and high, which were determined by the number of distracting letters appearing alongside the target letter. In the low perceptual load condition, only the target letter appeared on the screen, whereas in the high perceptual load condition, four distracting letters appeared on the screen together with the target letter (see Fig. 5).

Each trial began with a 500-ms fixation cross, followed by the letters for 1,350 ms or until response. An image (neutral or pain-related) appeared simultaneously will the appearance of the letters for 200 ms in the center of the screen. Participants were instructed to ignore the image and respond as quickly and accurately as possible, pressing X for the letter X, N for the letter N, or waiting if no target appeared (“Catch trials,” 10% of total trials). RTs and accuracy were measured, and interference from pain-related images was calculated in each load condition separately, by subtracting the RTs for correct trials where a neutral image appeared from trials in which a pain-related image appeared. The images were selected specifically for the current experiment, see the supplementary materials, Sect. 1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 29139 and R Statistical Software140, using the lmer141 and the interaction142 packages.

Because traditional power analyses are not directly applicable to mixed-effects models, we adopted a twofold approach to ensure sufficient statistical power. First, a regression-based sensitivity analysis for the planned moderator test analysis was conducted using G*Power 3.1.9.7143. The model included nine baseline predictors and a group moderator (for predictor × session interaction). For an incremental F-test (one tested moderator added beyond nine covariates), a total sample of N = 42 would provide 80% power to detect a small-to-medium incremental effect (f²=0.20, α = 0.05). Our study included a final sample of N = 40 participants for each training, which provides nearly sufficient sensitivity for small-to-medium effects; accordingly, the moderator analyses are treated as preliminary, and standardized regression coefficients (β) with 90% confidence intervals are reported alongside p-values to aid interpretation. Second, a post hoc power analysis based on the observed effect size in our mixed model analysis (f²=0.26) and sample size (N = 40) in our mixed linear models indicated actual power of 0.79, aligning with our planned estimation.

Data inspection

Normality was assessed via histograms and the Shapiro-Wilk test, followed by descriptive statistics. Pearson correlations showed intercorrelations among psychological questionnaires without multicollinearity, while cognitive variables were uncorrelated (Tables S2–S3, supplementary materials). Prior to the main analyses, group differences in demographic, psychological, and pain measures were examined to determine whether baseline factors could account for subsequent training effects.

Data cleaning

Data cleaning was performed according to standard procedure and recommendations in the literature144; See supplementary materials, Sect. 2.

Training task assessment

To evaluate whether the active and control sessions are comparable, in the acceptance training, perceived difficulty of using the emotional strategy as well as changes in precived difficulty along the training were assessed using a mixed-effects linear model with the factors session (active vs. control) and time (five time points: post-instruction and after each training block). In the ABM training, the task was assessed by comparing accuracy rates between sessions (active vs. control) and across time (first to last block) using paired t-tests. Training effects were further evaluated by examining changes in attention bias to pain (a common ABM outcome54,145), and task accuracy using a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA with the factors phase (pre- vs. post-training) and session (active vs. control).

Finally, to assess the trajectory of pain reduction along the trainings, changes in heat pain ratings during the training tasks were compared between active and control session separately for each training, using mixed-effects linear models, with the factors time (4 time points, after each block) x session (active vs. control).

Group-level training efficacy

Before assessing predictors of individual training efficacy, group-level changes in pain sensitivity were evaluated. For each training, a 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted for each pain outcome, comparing phase (pre- vs. post-training) and session (active vs. control). To control for the influence of pain-60 temperature on CPM magnitude123,146, post-training pain-60 values were included as a covariate in the CPM analysis. To account for the possibility of carry-over effects from the first session to the second, order effect was also assessed.

Similar analysis was conducted for examining training-related changes in cognitive and psychological measures, see supplementary materials Sect. 4.

Predictors of training-related pain reduction

To identify baseline predictors of pain reduction following each type of training (ABM and acceptance) separately, we constructed mixed linear models following Montgomery et al. (2021)147. To reduce multiple comparisons, predictive models were only constructed for pain outcomes that showed significant training-related changes.

For each training condition, a separate model included session (active vs. control) as a within-participants factor and examined cognitive (attention bias, interference from pain-related images under low/high load) and psychological variables (PCS, FPQ-9, DASS, STAI-S, DERS), along with their interactions with session. The outcome variable was the change in pain (Δ post–pre), and all predictors were centered. Random intercepts and baseline pain ratings were included to account for individual differences.

In a second step, models were refined by retaining only significant predictors and interactions, applying constraints to reduce overfitting and ensure parsimony58. Models were compared using ANOVA and AIC147. When significant interactions with the session emerged, simple slope analyses with Bonferroni correction were conducted. For models with multiple interactions, additional analyses tested higher-order (triplet) interactions and assessed whether each effect was independent by fitting separate models for each predictor.
