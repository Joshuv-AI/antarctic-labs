1. What are the major goals of the project?

The overall aim of the project is to develop quantitative predictive geochemical modelling of aqueous fluid-rock-
hydrocarbon interactions in the Earth by building on recent state-of-the-art experimental data and ab initio
calculations of extreme aqueous fluid chemistry. To achieve this aim, the project had three immediate major goals.
First, to use published experimental densities of salt solutions reported to 4.0 GPa and 400°C to test the accuracy of
geochemical equations of state for the standard and non-standard state properties of aqueous species. Non-ideality of
the partial molal volumes of the electrolytes and the complexes of the electrolyte ions are to be investigated. Second,
to investigate the behavior of CO2 in aqueous fluids under extreme conditions. The discovery by ab initio molecular
dynamics that aqueous H2CO3 predominates over CO2 at high pressures, the reverse of the situation at ambient
conditions, opens up new questions about the behavior of CO2-bearing fluids at high pressures. It was proposed to
investigate this topic to develop equation of state characterizations of aqueous CO2 and H2CO3. Third, it was
proposed to investigate the significance of the recent experimental discovery of immiscible hydrocarbon and
aqueous fluids at 3.0 GPa and 300°C through interactions with Professor Daniel (Lyon). As a complement to these
studies, ab initio molecular dynamics studies of CO2-bearing and organic fluids were being carried out by Professor
Ding Pan (Hong Kong).

2. What was accomplished under these goals?

The properties of ions and electrolytes were investigated by testing the aqueous speciation and non‐deality
models of deep Earth fluids during dimaond formation, and specifically to test the prediction of the chemical
compositions of the silicate mineral inclusions in diamonds worldwide. Excellent agreement was found.The results
indicate very surprisingly that traditional interpretations of the identity and compositions of silicate mineral
inclusions in diamonds is incorrect. Instead of telling us what kind of host‐rock the diamonds formed in at depth in
the Earth's mantle, our models indicate that the silicate inclusions tell us about the kind of fluids that formed the
diamonds, and the rock type from which the fluid originally came from.
On a second front, major progress was made in investigating the behavior of aqueous CO2‐H2O mixtures.
Traditional metamorphic and deep Earth C‐O‐H models of these fluids have been available for decades based on
increasingly complex equations of state fit to the limited amount of available experimental data and molecular
dynamic simulations. The fit equations end up with dozens of parameters and have very limited extrapolability.
Adding a new species requires a major research effort. In the present project, by taking a completely new
approach to the thermodynamics of CO‐H2O fluids, it has been possible to build a simple equation of state
approach to the properties of the aqueous CO2 component by developing a model that takes account of two
fundamental aqueous processes. First, accounting for the solvation of CO2 by water, using Born theory involving a
function of the inverse dielectric constant of the mixed CO2‐H2O system relative to the inverse dielectric constant
of CO2 (in a Raoultian approach) or H2O (in a Henry's Law approach). A single universal coefficient can be used for
the solvation contribution.Second, fitting a parameter for the short‐range interaction of CO2 and H2O molecules to
experimental data enables a predictive equation of state approach for this parameter valid over a very wide range
of temperatures and pressures.
On a third front, about a dozen new experiments were carried out, as planned, in Professor Daniel's laboratory in
Lyon to study the formation of immiscible hydrocarbon fluids as a function of pressure. Immiscible hydrocarbon
fluids were found at all pressures from 1.6 to 4.6 GPa at 300°C. These were made mainly of isobutane, propane,
and minor amounts of methane. As a next step, models of the formation of these fluids were developed.
Finally, a collaboration with Professor Ding Pan in Hong Kong resulted in the discovery by ab initio molecular
dynamics that aqueous formate (HCOO‐), an organic acid anion, is likely stable at upper mantle pressures and
temperatures. This is the first time an organic species (other than hydrocarbons) has been documented for deep
Earth fluids. The result is consistent with the abundant amounts of formate in our published classical
thermodynamic models of deep Earth aqueous fluid speciation.

3. What opportunities for training and professional development has the project provided?

The project has provided for the training of a graduate student, Jingyi Huang at Johns Hopkins University. In
addition, the PI taught a class in deep Earth modeling to an international group of graduate students, postdoctoral
associates, and early career faculty.

4. How have the results been disseminated to communities of interest?

The results have been reported at virtual meetings of the Geochemical Society, and the American Geophysical
Union, as well as in published manuscripts.

IMPACT
1. What is the impact on the development of the principal discipline(s) of the project?

The principal discipline of the project is physical chemistry of deep Earth fluids applied to understanding the role of
these fluids in the evolution of the Earth. The impact involves extending our modeling and experimental
capabilities for deep Earth fluids and the application to processes that are inaccesible in the deep earth.

2. What is the impact on other disciplines?

Development of a new physical chemistry involving thermodynamic and molecular understanding of aqueous
fluids and hydrocarbons under extreme conditions.

3. What is the impact on the development of human resources?

Through education and training, a new generation of researchers will be trained in the latest modeling techniques.

4. What is the impact on physical, institutional, and information resources that form infrastructure?

None.

5. What is the impact on technology transfer?

None.

6. What is the impact on society beyond science and technology?

The impact of society will be a new way to view the deep Earth, and possibly the evolution of other planets.
