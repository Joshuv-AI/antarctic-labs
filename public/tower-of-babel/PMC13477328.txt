Midterm Clinical and Echocardiographic Outcomes After Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Patients With Severe Bicuspid Aortic Valve Stenosis
Authors: Stephan Nienaber, Katharina Krabichler, David Fahlbusch, Victor Mauri, Jan Althoff, Samuel Lee, Jonathan Curio, Henning Guthoff, Karl Finke, Alexander Hof, Elmar Kuhn, Richard J. Nies, Merve Kural, Stephan Baldus, Hendrik Wienemann, Matti Adam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOutcomes >2 years after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) for bicuspid aortic valves (BAV) are scarce, and differences between balloon‐expandable transcatheter heart valves (THVs) and self‐expandable (SE‐THVs) are not yet well established.MethodsThis study aims to report midterm outcomes after TAVR in patients with BAV and includes 92 patients undergoing TAVR for BAV stenosis at the University Hospital Cologne between 2018 and 2023. The primary outcomes were all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, and stroke over a mean follow‐up of 2.9 years.ResultsAmong 92 consecutive patients undergoing TAVR for BAV stenosis (median age: 78.8 years), the incidence of all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, and stroke was 26.1%, 18.5%, and 14.1%, respectively, at 2.9 years, with no differences between balloon‐expandable THVs and SE‐THVs. Pacemaker implantation tended to be more frequent in the SE‐THV group. Periprocedural complications were rare, as reflected by a high Valve Academic Research Consortium‐3 technical success rate of 96.7%. Postprocedural transvalvular gradients were favorable, with SE‐THV showing lower values than balloon‐expandable THV (8.0 versus 10.5 mm Hg, P=0.007). Moderate or severe paravalvular regurgitation was uncommon, with no differences between groups, although a trend toward more relevant paravalvular regurgitation with SE‐THV was observed.ConclusionsTAVR for BAV is feasible with both SE‐THV and balloon‐expandable ‐THV, achieving high technical success and low complication rates. Hemodynamic differences were observed, but mortality and stroke did not differ significantly between groups. Overall outcomes remain suboptimal, highlighting the need for randomized trials comparing valve types and TAVR with surgery for patients with BAV.

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been established for patients aged ≥70 years across all risk levels for surgical aortic valve replacement. Promising results of pivotal TAVR trials in intermediate and low risk patients led to a growing use of TAVR over 2 decades. 1 , 2 , 3 However, patients with bicuspid aortic valves (BAV) were usually excluded from pivotal trials due to unfavorable anatomical characteristics and technical challenges, although BAV is the most common congenital valvular heart disease affecting 1% to 2% of the population and up to 9% of patients undergoing TAVR. 4 , 5

Recent propensity‐score matched substudies have already shown promising short‐term results regarding efficacy and safety of TAVR in the setting of BAV. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Conversely, first randomized data from NOTION‐2 (Nordic Aortic Valve Intervention 2) have shown a substantially higher risk of death, stroke, and rehospitalization for TAVR compared with surgical aortic valve replacement in patients with BAV. 10 In addition to conflicting short‐term data, midterm outcomes of TAVR patients with BAVs remain scarce or incomplete. One report on long‐term valve durability after TAVR was published recently but is markedly limited by selection bias as only patients with echocardiographic follow‐up were included, and information on mortality and stroke during follow‐up are lacking. 11 Studies comparing self‐expandable (SE‐THVs) and balloon‐expandable transcatheter heart valves (BE‐THVs) in tricuspid aortic valves indicate that SE‐THVs are more often associated with new permanent pacemaker implantation and paravalvular regurgitation ≥ moderate. 12 Conversely, SE‐THVs are frequently associated with lower mean transvalvular gradients. 13 , 14 Stroke was either less common with BE‐THVs or comparable between both valve types, and cardiovascular mortality was similar. 15 , 16 However, these findings are derived from tricuspid cohorts, and it remains unclear whether these findings also apply to bicuspid cohorts, where comparative data are scarce.

Furthermore, with the increasing use of TAVR in younger patients with longer life expectancy, the proportion of patients with BAV treated with TAVR is expected to rise and insights on midterm clinical and hemodynamic data are deemed crucial. 17 , 18 Therefore, this analysis aimed to both assess midterm outcomes following TAVR within patients with BAV from a German all‐comers cohort and compare outcomes between BE‐THVs and SE‐THVs.

METHODS

Study Population

All patients with severe aortic valve stenosis and bicuspid anatomy undergoing TAVR at the University Hospital Cologne between January 2018 and January 2023 were included. All TAVR procedures were reviewed and approved by a local multidisciplinary heart team. Before any TAVR procedure, patients underwent routine diagnostic evaluation, including echocardiography and contrast‐enhanced, ECG‐gated computed tomography (CT). The CT images were analyzed by 1 radiologist and at least 2 experienced cardiologists using 3mensio software (Pie Medical Imaging, Maastricht, The Netherlands).

TAVR was performed in case of suitable valve anatomy and with a mandate by the interdisciplinary heart team, or in cases when the patient explicitly opted for an interventional approach. All procedures were carried out in a hybrid theater. The choice of commercially available THVs was based on multidisciplinary heart team decisions, considering anatomical characteristics, comorbidity profile, and device‐specific features. The choice between SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs was primarily guided by annulus size, calcification pattern, valve morphology, and the vascular access suitability. Operator experience with specific devices may have also had an impact on the choice of THV type and cannot be fully excluded as a potential selection bias. The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants gave written informed consent for the procedure and the use of de‐identified procedural and outcome data for research purposes. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data Collection and Clinical End Points

Baseline, procedural, and follow‐up data were assessed in all patients included in the analysis. Echocardiographic outcomes were reported for patients who attended follow‐up visits at our institution. Follow‐up visits were routinely performed 30 days post TAVR. For midterm follow‐up patients or their referring physicians were contacted, and follow‐up of at least 2 years post procedure was again conducted at our center. A limited number of patients who could not attend the midterm follow‐up visit were subsequently assessed through structured telephone interviews. Transthoracic echocardiography was performed before TAVR, at discharge, at 30 days, and at midterm follow‐up of at least 2 years by experienced echocardiographers. Complications were defined according to the Valve Academic Research Consortium consensus document. 19 Death, cardiovascular death, and stroke were also adjudicated in accordance with the Valve Academic Research Consortium‐3 consensus document. Therefore, cardiovascular death was defined as death resulting from myocardial infarction, heart failure, sudden cardiac death, stroke, periprocedural death, death related to the TAVR prostheses, or death due to vascular causes. In cases where the cause of death was unknown, deaths were classified as cardiovascular as well. All deaths were adjudicated by members of the local heart team based on available medical records and follow‐up information from referring physicians. 19 Primary clinical end points were all‐cause mortality, cardiovascular death, and stroke at a mean follow‐up of 2.9 years (SD: ±1.8 years).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) or mean±SD, and categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. A composite end point, comprising stroke and all‐cause mortality, along with its individual components, is depicted through Kaplan–Meier survival analyses with 95% CIs. For a more detailed analysis of stroke incidence, Kaplan–Meier estimates were supplemented by Cox proportional hazards regression analyses to estimate hazard ratios (HRs), treating death as a censoring event. In addition, Fine–Gray competing risk models were applied to account for the competing risk of death.

Longitudinal changes in echocardiographic parameters, specifically aortic valve area and aortic valve mean gradient, were analyzed using linear mixed‐effects models to account for within‐subject correlations across repeated measurements at baseline, discharge, 30 days, and midterm follow‐up of 2.9 years. Models were adjusted for valve type (SE‐THV versus BE‐THV), access routes, and further relevant baseline variables including age, sex, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, peripheral artery disease, atrial fibrillation/flutter, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), and prior events such as stroke, coronary artery bypass grafting, heart valve surgery, percutaneous coronary intervention, and myocardial infarction. Post hoc paired comparisons between time points were performed with values of the midterm follow‐up (2.9 years) constituting the reference group. Lastly, an adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was conducted to account for relevant intergroup differences (GFR, access route, valve size) and to analyze key clinical outcomes at a median follow‐up of 2.9 years. No statistical analysis methods were used for missing data. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29) and the R Project for Statical Computing (Version 4.4.2).

RESULTS

Baseline Patient, Echocardiographic, and CT Characteristics

Over 5 years a total of 92 consecutive patients with severe BAV stenosis who underwent TAVR were included. The median age was 78.8 years, and 38 (41.3%) patients were female. The mean European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II was 3.12 (SD: ±2.45). Nine (9.8%) patients had a preexisting permanent pacemaker, 8 (8.7%) patients underwent previous cardiac surgery, and the same number of patients (8.7%) had experienced a previous stroke. The majority of patients had preserved left ventricular ejection fraction at baseline and the median aortic valve gradient was 44.5 mm Hg. According to preprocedural CT scans, the median aortic annulus perimeter was 79.4 mm, and the median Agatston score was 4131.5. Sievers type 1 was the predominant morphology, followed by Sievers type 0, observed in 8.7% of patients, whereas none of the patients exhibited Sievers type 2 morphology. Baseline characteristics were largely comparable between patients receiving SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs, except for the median aortic annulus area, the median minimal aortic annulus diameter, and preoperative GFR. The median GFR was 73 mL/min in the SE‐THV group compared with 61 mL/min in the BE‐THV group (P=0.039). Both the minimal aortic annulus diameter and the aortic annulus area were smaller in patients treated with SE‐THVs. Procedural Outcomes

Self‐expandable devices were used more frequently (63.0%) than BE‐THVs (37.0%) in our cohort (Figure 1). Transfemoral access was used in 81 (88.0%) of patients. When transfemoral access was deemed inadequate, transaxillary or transapical access routes were used in a total of 11 cases (Table 3). The transaxillary access was used only in the SE‐THV group (13.8% versus 0.0%, P=0.007), whereas the transapical access was solely performed in the BEV group (0.0% versus 8.8%, P=0.007). The median implanted valve size was 29 mm and numerically larger in the SE‐THV group (29 versus 26 mm, P<0.001), whereas annular size was in trend smaller in the SE‐THV group compared with BE‐THV (77.30 versus 82.45 mm, P=0.093). The standard anesthetic approach was conscious sedation, though general anesthesia was required in ~20% of the cases. Predilatation of the stenotic BAV was performed in 87% of patients, and postdilatation was employed in 23 cases (25.0%) and more frequently in the SE‐THV group (36.2% versus 5.9%). Technical success, as defined by the Valve Academic Research Consortium‐3 criteria, was achieved in 96.7%. Three cases were classified as technical failures due to conversion to surgical aortic valve replacement, requirement for implantation of a second THV, and coronary artery obstruction, respectively. Periprocedural stroke or transient ischemic attack occurred in 6 patients (6.5%), and 14 patients (15.2%) required new pacemaker implantation during hospitalization. There was no case of in‐hospital mortality.

Clinical Outcomes

At 30 days, 3 patients (3.2%) had died, with 2 cases (2.2%) attributed to cardiovascular causes. Stroke occurred in 7 patients (7.6%) of which 2 were disabling events (2.2%). Myocardial infarction was observed in 1 patient (1.1%), and 2 patients (2.2%) required aortic valve reintervention due to subacute valve migration toward the left ventricular outflow tract resulting in severe paravalvular regurgitation (PVR). Both cases were successfully managed with valve‐in‐ TAVR. Valve thrombosis with hypoattenuation affecting motion occurred in 1 patient with a SEV‐THV after discharge and was successfully resolved by oral anticoagulation with apixaban for 3 months. Acute conduction disturbances requiring permanent pacing were noted in 16 patients (19.5%), with 13 of these devices being implanted before discharge. The rate of new permanent pacemaker implantations was numerically higher in the cohort receiving SE‐THVs (20.8% versus 6.9%), although this finding did not reach statistical significance (P=0.10). There was no further difference observed between the 2 groups at 30 days (Figure 2; Table 4).

Midterm clinical follow‐up data were available for 85 patients. Information on mortality and other clinical outcomes were available for 92 and 85 patients, respectively. At a mean follow‐up of 2.9 years, 24 patients (26.1%) had died, with 17 deaths being attributed to cardiovascular causes. Five additional patients experienced a stroke, increasing the total number of strokes to 12 (14.1%). Four of the newly occurring strokes were classified as disabling. Additionally, 1 patient who had previously experienced a nondisabling stroke sustained a disabling stroke during midterm follow‐up, elevating the overall count of disabling strokes to 7. All‐cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, incidence of stroke, and the composite end point of all‐cause mortality and stroke are presented as time‐to‐event Kaplan–Meier curves over a 2‐year follow‐up period (Figure 2). Beyond the Kaplan–Meier analysis for stroke, Cox proportional hazards models were performed treating death as a censoring event. Furthermore, a Fine–Gray competing risk model was applied, considering death as a competing event for stroke. Cox proportional hazards analysis yielded an HR for stroke of 1.24 (95% CI, 0.277–5.530, P=0.78) with SE‐THV as the reference group, and the Fine–Gray competing risk analysis demonstrated highly comparable findings, with a subdistribution HR of 1.24 (95% CI, 0.283–5.450, P=0.77) (Figure S1). During follow‐up one more patient required permanent pacemaker implantation due to conduction disturbances, whereas no further cases of valve thrombosis were observed. At 2.9 years we did not find any significant differences between patients treated with SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs (Figure 2; Table 4). To account for relevant differences in GFR, diabetes status, access routes, and valve size between the 2 cohorts, a multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to assess key clinical outcomes at 2.9 years of follow‐up. The results of the unadjusted and adjusted analyses are displayed in Table 5. Importantly, no significant differences between the patients treated with BE‐THV and SE‐THV were observed regarding the composite of all‐cause death and stroke, all‐cause death, stroke, and cardiovascular death (Table 5).

Hemodynamic Outcomes

At baseline the aortic valve median gradient and mean aortic valve area were 44.5 mm Hg and 0.70 cm2, respectively. The majority of patients (85.9%) had preserved ejection fraction, 28.3% exhibited additional moderate or severe mitral regurgitation, and 19.6% had tricuspid regurgitation greater than moderate. At discharge, transvalvular gradients and aortic valve area improved to 9.0 mm Hg and 1.9 cm2, respectively. Moderate or severe PVR was observed in 7 patients (7.8%). Additionally, the prevalence of moderate or severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation decreased following TAVR.

At 30‐day follow‐up, echocardiographic data were available in 69 patients, demonstrating stable hemodynamics with a mean gradient of 9.0 mm Hg and an aortic valve area of 1.7 cm2. The incidence of ≥ moderate PVR was 8.7%, whereas moderate or severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation was observed in 23.2% of patients.

Midterm echocardiographic follow‐up, with a mean duration of 2.9 years, was available for 61 patients showing a stable mean gradient at 9.0 mm Hg, and an aortic valve area of 1.8 cm2 (Figure 3). The prevalence of moderate or severe PVR had declined to 4.9% (Figure 4). Similarly, the incidence of moderate or severe mitral and tricuspid regurgitation was significantly lower than at baseline, with rates of 4.9% and 8.2%, respectively. The aortic valve mean gradient was significantly higher in patients treated with BE‐THV than in patients with SE‐THV (Figure 3). The mean gradients in the BE‐THV group were already higher at discharge (11 versus 8 mm Hg, P<0.001) and this difference persisted at 30 days (12 versus 6 mm Hg, P<0.001) and at 2.9 years (10.5 versus 8 mm Hg, P=0.007). Regarding PVR, a tendency toward more relevant PVR in the SE‐THV group was observed (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

The key result of the analysis of TAVR outcomes in an allcomers cohort of patients with BAV anatomy showed that all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, stroke, and disabling stroke do not differ between patients who received an SE‐THV and a BE‐THV at short‐ and midterm follow‐up (2.9 years). The rates of all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality in the overall cohort were 26.1% and 18.5%, respectively, whereas the incidence of stroke was 14.1% at 2.9 years. Despite the more complex anatomy of BAVs, periprocedural complications were rare with both SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs, as reflected by a high rate of technical success (96.7%) in the overall cohort. Consistent with these findings, no in‐hospital deaths were observed; however, the incidence of periprocedural stroke or transient ischemic attack was 6.5%, with no significant difference between the 2 groups. Conversely, conduction disturbances and the need for pacemaker implantation trended higher with SE‐THV group than with BE‐THV. Postprocedural transvalvular gradients were favorable both at discharge and during the follow‐up period in the overall cohort, and SE‐THVs were associated with lower gradients compared with BE‐THVs. The overall rate of PVR ≥ moderate was relatively low and without clear differences between the 2 groups, even though there was a tendency for SE‐THVs to be associated with hemodynamically relevant PVR more frequently.

The BAV anatomy presents substantial challenges for TAVR operators. Adverse features, such as elliptically shaped, noncircular, or dilatation of aortic annuli and the ascending aorta, may complicate the procedure and ultimately compromise procedural outcomes. 20 In addition, severe and asymmetric calcifications of the cusps, often extending in the left ventricular outflow tract, as well as heavily calcified raphes, can further increase procedural complexity and may even preclude proper positioning of TAVR prostheses. 21 Previous registry data comparing TAVR in bicuspid and tricuspid aortic valve anatomy, reported an association between patients with BAV and higher rate of stroke, conversion to surgery, and annular rupture. 22 , 23 Moreover, hemodynamically relevant PVR occurred more frequently, and device success rates were lower in patients with BAV. 23 , 24 Recently, the first randomized data from a small cohort of bicuspid patients in the NOTION‐2 trial provided further evidence that TAVR in patients with BAV was associated with higher rates of stroke, hospitalization, and even all‐cause mortality, although these findings did not reach statistical significance due to limited sample size. 10 , 25 Due to limited and partly unsatisfactory evidence, the current guidelines still favor surgery as first‐line treatment for bicuspid anatomy, in contrast to the expanded TAVR indications for tricuspid valves. 26 With the ongoing expansion of TAVR indications to younger, lower‐risk patients, as reflected in the updated guidelines recommending TAVR from age 70 instead of 75, the proportion of patients with BAV undergoing TAVR is likely to rise. 26 In this context and in view of the technical specificities of the 2 established valve systems, a comparative evaluation of BE‐THVs versus SE‐THVs would be desirable and improve patient care. 27 , 28 The first multicenter study to assess different valve types in bicuspid anatomy with follow‐up beyond 1 year has recently emerged, but more than half of the patients were treated between 2007 and 2017 using earlier‐generation TAVR prostheses. 29 For contemporary TAVR, the diagnostic workup, procedural techniques, and postprocedural management have been substantially altered and improved, thereby limiting the relevance of those results for current TAVR practice. Different from the recent multicenter study with <50 patients per center, and supposedly much fewer patients per operator, all patients in our series have undergone consistent workup and sizing procedures ensuring homogenous planning and execution of the procedure. In a high‐volume center, we were able to recruit a substantial number of patients during a short recruitment period of 5 years, thereby strengthening the relevance and transferability of our findings to contemporary real‐world practice. The number of 92 cases using exclusively the most recent iterations of TAVR valve technology represents the largest single‐center series to date. Thus, our study provides a meaningful addition to the currently scarce evidence on TAVR in bicuspid anatomy. Notably, most published evidence is limited to follow‐up periods of only 30 days or, at most, 1 year. 30 , 31 In our cohort with follow‐up of almost 3 years, cardiovascular mortality, all‐cause mortality, and stroke rates did not differ significantly between SE‐THV and BE‐THV groups. Procedural complication rates were also comparable, with 1 case of annular rupture, 2 cases of cardiac tamponade, and 1 case of valve migration. In addition, a single case of valve migration was observed in the BE‐THV group, and 1 case of coronary obstruction occurred in the SE‐THV group. Interestingly, the observed coronary obstruction occurred in a patient with an anomalous circumflex artery and retroannular course showing external mechanical compression after THV deployment. This adverse event was explained by the patient‐specific anatomical feature rather than valve‐specific characteristics. Although periprocedural complications were not particularly high, TAVR in bicuspid anatomy remains associated with higher procedural risk compared with tricuspid aortic stenosis, where such complications have become rare exceptions. 1 , 32 Patients with heavily calcified raphes and pronounced leaflet calcifications more frequently experience aortic root injury, ≥ moderate PVR, and elevated 30‐day mortality. 21 In addition, several challenges may arise during the individual steps of TAVR implantation. Identifying the optimal implantation height may be difficult in case of only 2 cusps, and crossing the valve can be challenging with prominent raphes or large cusps. Lastly, valve deployment is more complex and can possibly result in suboptimal implantation depth, conduction disturbances, or PVR. 20

Although no differences were observed between SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs, the overall cohort rates of all‐cause mortality (26.1%), cardiovascular mortality (18.5%), and stroke (14.1%) at 2.9 years appear unsatisfactory. Interestingly, both the Evolut Low Risk TAVR bicuspid study and the PARTNER 3 (Placement of Aortic Transcatheter Valves 3) bicuspid registry have reported markedly lower event rates. In the Evolut Low Risk TAVR bicuspid study, 3‐year all‐cause mortality was 2.8%, cardiovascular mortality was 1.4%, and stroke was 6.9%, whereas the PARTNER 3 bicuspid registry demonstrated a 1‐year all‐cause mortality of 0.7% and a stroke rate of 2.0%. 7 , 33 , 34 On closer examination, however, these cohorts are not directly comparable to our population, which may explain the considerably different event rates. Patients in both trials were 8 to 9 years younger and had lower Society of Thoracic Surgeons Patient‐Reported Outcome Measures scores. More important, both studies applied extensive anatomical exclusion criteria, including severe raphe or left ventricular outflow tract calcification, risk of coronary obstruction, extreme leaflet calcification, annular irregularities, significant concomitant valvular disease, and ascending aortopathy whereas our data originate from a real‐world all‐comers series. 7 , 34 It appears self‐evident that outcomes improve when all patients with well‐known‐risk factors are excluded, which emphasizes the clinical relevance of our real‐world cohort, where all anatomical particularities were included and the heart team just recommended either surgical or transcatheter treatment. 3 , 35 , 36 , 37

A substantial proportion underwent TAVR because surgery was deemed too high risk, life expectancy was limited due to severe comorbidities, or patients explicitly preferred a transcatheter approach. Of note, recently published real‐world data have reported mortality rates of ~12% at 1 year and 21.2% at 3 years, which is consistent with our findings. 29 , 38

Transvalvular gradients improved after both SE‐THV and BE‐THV implantation (Figure 2). However, mean gradients were lower in the SE‐THV group, an effect attributable to the supra‐annular design of SE‐THVs and well described in larger tricuspid cohorts. 12 , 39 , 40 Interestingly, van den Dorpel et al. demonstrated that this echocardiographic discrepancy between platforms was not confirmed invasively and did not translate into differences regarding 2‐year mortality. 41 Nevertheless, the impact of postprocedural gradients on midterm outcomes in bicuspid anatomies remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Postprocedural, 7.8% of patients had ≥ moderate PVR, which is in line with other real‐world cohorts. 29 Overall, a nonsignificant trend toward higher PVR rates with SE‐THVs was observed. However, this finding remains clinically relevant, given the adverse prognostic impact of residual PVR. 42 , 43 Another important finding of the study is that the rate of new permanent pacemaker implantation was 15.9% at discharge and increased to 22.7% at 2.9 years. Not surprisingly, rates were consistently higher after SE‐THV compared with BE‐THV (26.5% versus 15.4% at 2.9 years). This observation aligns with data from tricuspid cohorts, which similarly demonstrate higher pacemaker rates following SE‐THV than BE‐THV implantation. 44

LIMITATIONS

With the observational nature, inherent study limitations such as unrecorded confounders may not be fully excluded. For instance, the choice between SE‐THV and BE‐THV may have been influenced by unmeasured clinical or anatomical factors, and operator‐related variations in implantation technique cannot be excluded. Data were collected in a single center and potentially not reflecting a TAVR cohort in different health care systems. Moreover, follow‐up data were not fully complete, particularly with regard to echocardiographic assessments at 2.9 years. Finally, the relatively small sample size is an important limitation of this study, potentially limiting statistical power and the generalizability of our findings. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted with caution and warrant confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

CONCLUSIONS

TAVR for BAV stenosis is feasible with both SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs, achieving high technical success and low complication rates. BE‐THVs were associated with higher transvalvular gradients, whereas SE‐THVs tended to be linked with more frequent PVR and the need for new permanent pacemaker implantation. Although mortality and stroke rates did not differ between SE‐THVs and BE‐THVs, overall event rates remain suboptimal, underscoring the need for further, ideally randomized controlled trials comparing valve types and evaluating TAVR against surgical aortic valve replacement in the setting of BAV anatomy.
