A feasibility study on inferring connectivity changes in frontal lobes of MDD patients via spectral DCM
Authors: Xiaojia Wang, Shuohui Li, Mingxin Zhang, Chunfeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany studies have shown that major depressive disorder (MDD) is highly relevant to the dysfunction of frontal lobes. Given the crucial role of brain network organization for MDD, this work focuses on inferring effective connectivity patterns and their changes in the frontal lobes of non-depressive and MDD subjects. We collected frontopolar left (Fp1), frontopolar right (Fp2), and frontal midline (Fz) channels of resting electroencephalogram (EEG) from 22 subjects with non-depressive states (non-depressive group), 23 subjects with mild depression (mild MDD group) and 12 subjects with moderate depression (moderate MDD group). Complete Physiology-Based Model was introduced to simulate the EEG signals of brain activities in the three targeted lobes. Then, spectral dynamic causal modelling was performed to investigate effective connectivity and its changes between the three targeted channels. The findings suggest that MDD groups exhibit a significant decline in the average strength of excitatory connectivity between pyramidal cells of the three lobes, compared to the non-depressed group. Additionally, the fluctuation of connectivity across trials among frontal lobes decreases in the mild and moderate MDD groups. The MDD groups also showed frontal asymmetry, specifically in the form of significant differences in bidirectional excitatory connections between Fp1 and Fp2. These effective connectivity changes of frontal lobes may indicate its clinical potential in future research on depression.Keywords: Depression, Complete physiology-based model, Dynamic causal modelling, Effective connectivity, EEG

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a highly prevalent neurological disorder characterized by long-term emotional, cognitive, and behavioral disturbances [1]. The prevalence of depression causes significant losses to society, yet the specific pathological mechanisms underlying MDD remain unclear. Studies have shown that the severity of MDD is positively correlated with organic dysfunctions of the frontal lobes, which may play a vital role in conscious activities and cognitive processes [2]. In particular, frontal impairments in MDD have been shown to impact patients’ cognitive flexibility, blunt reward-related decision-making, and result in persistent motivational and consummatory anhedonia [3, 4]. This work aims to build upon existing studies and further investigate the functional mechanisms of the frontal lobes in individuals with depression.

In neuroscience, brain function can be guided by two fundamental principles [5]: functional segregation (the independence of the function of a single brain region or neuronal population), and functional integration (coordination between different brain regions or neuronal populations). Functional segregation forms the foundation of brain organization research, and functional integration can be usually characterized by brain connectivity, e.g. structural connectivity, functional connectivity, and effective connectivity. Structural connectivity is based on anatomical information, functional connectivity focuses on statistical dependencies. These two types of connectivity are considered undirected. In contrast, effective connectivity is directed and can infer the causal relationship, referring to the influence of one brain region impact on another [6]. Previous studies have indicated that depressive patients exhibit hemispheric asymmetry [7], particularly in frontal alpha [8, 9], which suggests directionality in the relationships between brain regions. In the present work, the effective connectivity analysis is performed to explore the asymmetry of frontal brain regions in MDD patients.

There are two common techniques performed in effective connectivity analysis: data-driven and model-driven methods. According to [10], effective connectivity analysis leans towards hypothesis-driven (model-based) methods. Meanwhile, the generative model in model-driven methods can provide straightforward physiological explanations. The types of signals for such analysis include electroencephalogram (EEG), magnetoencephalogram, and functional magnetic resonance imaging. Among them, EEG has been widely used to assess the dynamics of brain regions due to its high temporal resolution, noninvasive, inexpensive, and portable nature. Studies suggest that resting-state EEG signals can reflect neural activity associated with behavioral performance and perceptual decisions [11]. Abnormal neural activities are often observed in depressed patients. Moreover, multiple studies based on different techniques, such as cross-sample entropy and asymmetry-sample entropy, have indicated differences in EEG spectral features between depressed patients and non-depressed individuals [12, 13]. Note that dynamic causal modeling (DCM) is one dominant model-driven technique used for effective connectivity analysis [14]. Recent advances have also been made in DCM extensions, dynamic directed transfer functions, and emotion regulation modeling [15, 16]. In our contribution, spectral DCM is employed to analyze effective connectivity on resting-state EEG signals obtained from the frontal lobes of depressed patients. Recent studies have advanced brain connectivity modeling and DCM-based applications in psychiatric disorders. DCM has also been increasingly applied to fMRI and EEG studies for objective evaluation and mechanism analysis of depression [17–19].

Spectral DCM usually consists of three main steps. Firstly, a generative model (e.g. neural mass model, NMM) is specified to describe how observed data (e.g. EEG signals) are generated. Secondly, a transformation is applied to convert the time-series output of the NMM into predicted power spectral densities (PSDs) in the frequency domain. The last step is model inversion and model comparison. Model inversion is conducted to estimate the parameters for all candidate model structures based on the maximization of the free energy concept using variational Laplace approach. Then, the model comparison finds out the optimal model structure with the highest maximized free energy. Currently, some exists NMMs (e.g. PBM [20] or Jansen’s model [21]) only consist of one excitatory connection from one brain region to another. Inspired by a recent work [22] in the context of epilepsy. This paper introduces the complete Physiology-Based Model (cPBM) into MDD studies as the NMM, where cPBM can generate multiple PSD peaks to simulate the activities of the targeted lobes [20, 23]. Notably, cPBM distinguishes two types of extrinsic connections: excitatory and inhibitory effective connections between brain regions. The brain function of depressed patients often exhibits decreased neural activities, cPBM incorporating inhibitory effective connections between brain regions may enable a more comprehensive analysis of effective connectivity patterns in the frontal lobes of depressed patients.

In summary, we collected the resting-state EEG signals from non-depressed subjects, mild and moderate MDD patients using three electrodes located at frontopolar left (Fp1), frontopolar right (Fp2), and frontal midline (Fz) in frontal lobes. Effective connectivity analysis was performed based on spectral DCM embedding cPBM to investigate the characteristics of model structure and model parameters. Specifically, we aimed to address the following questions:

Are the effective connectivity patterns among frontal lobes in MDD patients different from the non-depressive people? Are there any specific patterns of left-right frontal asymmetry in the effective connectivity of depressed patients?

Materials and methods

Data and pre-processing

In this study, we recruited a total of 14 non-depressed participants, and 43 subjects with suspected major depressive disorder from Shandong University and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical College, Shandong, China respectively. The severity of MDD was determined by 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [5]. Based on their scores, participants were divided into three groups: the non-depressed group (n = 22) included individuals with scores ranging from 0 to 7, the mild MDD group (n = 23) and the moderate MDD group (n = 12) included individuals with scores between 8 and 16 and over 17, respectively.

The EEG signals were recorded using a multichannel physiological acquisition system, RM6280C (Chengdu Instrument Factory, Sichuan, China). Figure 1 (a) illustrates the placement of the three electrodes of interest (named Fp1, Fp2, and Fz), according to the international 10–20 system with a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz. All subjects were instructed to keep calm with eyes closed for more than 5 min. Here, 5 min of EEG signals were selected from each subject and then divided into 60 trials. For each trial, a 6th-order Butterworth band-stop filter with stop band of 49∼51 Hz was used to eliminate the power line interference from the raw EEG data. Then, we applied the wavelet threshold filter with basis function of sym3 to remove eye movement artifacts [5]. Z-score standardization was employed to normalize the signals. Finally, the sample PSDs were computed, using the SPM spectral toolbox with a 12-pole AR process [24], as shown in Fig. 1 (b).

Model inversion and comparison

In this section, we conducted model inversion for each model structure to obtain the objective function free energy between three targeted brain regions. Then, model comparison is performed to find the optimal model structure for each trial.

In cPBM, there are 16 possible model structures () for the connectivity between two populations (see Fig. 2 (b)). Now, if we model the three populations simultaneously, the model space is increased to 4096 (). For simplicity, we performed pairwise DCM analyses across the three groups, with a total of 48 model structures constructed (). Figure 4 shows the two-step workflow for calculating the free energy objective function, to identify the optimal model and estimate the connectivity parameters. For each structure, by subtracting the predicted PSDs from the sampled PSDs, we get the model prediction error (see Eq. (4)), which is assumed to follow a Gaussian distribution. Additionally, according to the mean-field assumption we can define the log-model evidence for a given structure using the free energy and the Kullback-Leibler divergence between and the posterior (see Eq. (4)) [24]. The Kullback-Leibler divergence is a non-negative quantity and the free energy serves as a lower bound for the log-model evidence. Moreover, maximizing the free energy is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence. By estimating the free parameters of cPBM through the exponential terms via variational Laplace approach [17], we maximized the free energy of each model structure. The optimal model structure with the highest free energy is regarded as the one that best explains the sample PSD, based on model comparison. The optimal model structure together with its estimated parameters are selected for further analysis.

Discussion

Through our statistical analysis, the significant different connectivity patterns were found among non-depressed, mild MDD group and the moderate MDD group.

We conducted Kruskal-Wallis test to investigated statistical differences of the connections across three groups. Results show that the changes of excitatory connections originating from Fp1 and Fp2 are more consistent in mild and moderate MDD groups. Frontal asymmetry was also observed in MDD groups, characterized by a higher degree of statistical difference in the coupled excitatory connections between Fp1 and Fp2 comparing to the non-depressed group. The tendency of these connections is opposite between non-depressed and MDD groups, strength of excitatory connections originating from Fp2 was found to be higher in non-depressed group, whereas MDD groups exhibited higher strength of excitatory connections originating from Fp1.

The present findings suggest that the change of frontal connectivity mainly reflected in the excitatory connections (connections between pyramidal cells, ). The average strength of excitatory connections among frontal lobes, rather than inhibitory connections , decreased along with the deepening of MDD severity. This may indicate that reduction in excitatory connections rather than the enhancement of inhibitory connections may mainly active on the reduced frontal lobes activity in MDD subjects. In addition, when compared to the non-depressed group, the MDD groups showed a decreasing trend in the variance of connectivity over time among frontal lobes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we conducted a spectral DCM method on the EEG data of 57 subjects to analyze the effective connectivity among the three brain regions within frontal lobes where we summarized the connectivity patterns between different depressive groups. These results may have the potential to promote new insights on pathology of depression.

However, there are also several shortcomings in this study. The numbers of different types of subjects are unbalanced, especially the number patients with moderate MDD is relatively small. For future works, we plan to use parametric empirical Bayes and general linear model to implement a problem-oriented investigation of effective connectivity on a more comprehensive study.
