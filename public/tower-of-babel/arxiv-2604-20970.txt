E 6 E_{6} -local systems from cubic threefolds
Thomas Krämer, Daniel Litt, Marco Maculan

Abstract
. We produce infinitely many local systems on (level covers of) the moduli space of smooth cubic threefolds, with algebraic monodromy group equal to the exceptional group E 6 E_{6} . These local systems arise in the middle cohomology of abelian étale covers of the Fano scheme parametrizing lines in the universal cubic threefold.



1.
Introduction

The existence question for motives over number fields with exceptional Galois group was first raised for the groups
G_{2}
and
E_{8}
by Serre
[
25
, §8.8]
, who qualified it as
hasardeuse
.
His interest probably came from the fact that constructions based on curves and abelian varieties typically yield only classical groups. It is now known that all simple exceptional groups do occur as Galois groups of motives. In this paper, we consider the following version of the problem:

Question
.

Is every simple exceptional group realized as the algebraic monodromy group of a local system of geometric origin on a smooth complex variety?

This is not merely an analogue of Serre’s question: by a specialization argument, a positive answer also resolves the original question on motives if one interprets the motivic Galois group either as an
\ell
-adic monodromy group or as a Mumford–Tate group.
As we will recall in
section

1.2
, known constructions yield the sought-after local systems for all simple exceptional groups other than
E_{6}
. The main goal of this paper is to fill in this missing case: more precisely, we construct infinitely many pairwise distinct local systems of geometric origin whose algebraic monodromy group is the exceptional group
E_{6}
. By specializing these, we obtain many motives with Galois group
E_{6}
. The local systems that we construct arise in the middle cohomology of abelian étale covers of the family of Fano surfaces associated with a family of smooth cubic threefolds, as we now explain.

1.1.
Main result

Let
S
be a smooth complex variety and
\mathcal{Y}\to S
a family of smooth cubic threefolds, i.e. a subvariety

\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})

cut out by a nonzero global section of
\Sym^{3}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
for a vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
of rank five on
S
such that the projection
\mathcal{Y}\to S
is a smooth morphism. Let
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
be the fiber of this morphism at a point
s\in S
. The family is called
versal
if for general
s\in S
the Kodaira–Spencer map

T_{s}S\to\textup{H}^{1}(\mathcal{Y}_{s},T_{\mathcal{Y}_{s}})

is surjective, or equivalently if the classifying map
S\to\mathcal{M}
to the moduli stack
\mathcal{M}
of cubic threefolds is dominant. Passing from smooth cubic threefolds to their associated Fano surfaces, we get a smooth projective morphism

\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S

whose fiber
\mathcal{F}_{s}
at a point
s\in S
is the surface parametrizing lines on the cubic threefold
\mathcal{Y}_{s}
.

We are interested in the middle cohomology of this family of surfaces twisted by local systems of rank one. Let
\mathbb{L}
be a unitary complex local system of rank one on
\mathcal{F}
. By Ehresmann’s theorem, the higher direct image

\mathbb{V}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}

is a local system on
S
. For
s\in S
, the topological fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S,s)
acts on
\mathbb{V}_{s}=\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathbb{L})
via the monodromy representation

\rho_{s}\colon\pi_{1}(S,s)\to\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s})
.
By the
algebraic monodromy group
of
\mathbb{V}
we mean the Zariski closure

M_{s}\;:=\;\overline{\im\rho_{s}}\;\subset\;\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s})

of the image of
\rho_{s}
. Since
\mathbb{L}
is unitary, the connected component of the identity
M_{s}^{\circ}
is a reductive Lie group. We will consider the case where
\mathbb{L}
is of
finite order
in the sense that for some
n>0
the local system
\mathbb{L}^{\otimes n}
is trivial. The smallest such
n
is called the
order
of
\mathbb{L}
. To compare the monodromy representations arising as above for different choices of
\mathbb{L}
, recall that the
trace field

\mathbb{Q}(\rho_{s})\subset\mathbb{C}
is generated over
\mathbb{Q}
by the traces of
\rho_{s}(\gamma)
for all
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S,s)
. The trace field may change if one replaces
S
by a finite étale cover. To take this into account, define the
invariant trace field
as the intersection

\inv(\rho_{s})\;:=\;\bigcap_{\Gamma}\mathbb{Q}(\rho_{s}\rvert_{\Gamma})

over all finite index subgroups
\Gamma\subset\pi_{1}(S,s)
. We can then state our main result as follows.

Main theorem
.

There exists an integer
n_{0}>2
such that for any versal family
\mathcal{Y}\to S
of smooth cubic threefolds, the following holds:

(1)

For any local system
\mathbb{L}
of finite order whose restriction to a general fiber
F
of
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S
has order
n\geq n_{0}
, we have

(M^{\circ}_{s},\mathbb{V}_{s})\simeq(E_{6},V)\quad\text{and}\quad\inv(\rho_{s})=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})

where
V
is one of the two
27
-dimensional irreducible representations of
E_{6}
and
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
.

(2)

For
i=1,2
let
\mathbb{L}_{i}
be a local system of rank one with the above properties and put
\mathbb{V}_{i}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{i}
. If the two local systems
\mathbb{V}_{1}
and
\mathbb{V}_{2}
are isomorphic, then so are
\mathbb{L}_{1}\rvert_{F}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}\rvert_{F}
.

Note that the Fano surface
F=\mathcal{F}_{s}
of lines on a smooth cubic threefold has

\dim\textup{H}_{1}(F,\mathbb{Q})=10
, so for any
n\geq 1
there are many rank one local systems of order
n
on it. After replacing
S
by a finite level cover, they all extend to local systems
\mathbb{L}
on the total space of our family.

The local systems
\mathbb{V}
that we produce are distinct in the following strong sense. Let us say that a local system
\mathbb{W}_{1}
on a smooth variety
T_{1}
is
equivalent
to a local system
\mathbb{W}_{2}
on a smooth variety
T_{2}
if there is a smooth variety
T
with dominant morphisms
p_{i}\colon T\to T_{i}
such that
p_{1}^{\ast}\mathbb{W}_{1}\simeq p_{2}^{\ast}\mathbb{W}_{2}
. If this is the case, then the local systems
\mathbb{W}_{1}
and
\mathbb{W}_{2}
have the same invariant trace field. Hence the local systems
\mathbb{V}
arising from two distinct values
n_{1},n_{2}
of
n
in part (1) of the theorem are non-equivalent as long as
\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_{1}})\neq\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n_{2}})
, which holds for any pair of distinct integers
n_{1},n_{2}
unless one of them is an odd number and the other is twice that number.

Note that in the formulation of the main theorem we must have
n_{0}>2
. Indeed, if the restriction of
\mathbb{L}
to a general fiber of
\pi
has order
n=2
, then the local system
\mathbb{V}
of rank
27
on
S
is self-dual up to twist by a rank one local system. In this case
\mathbb{V}
cannot have connected monodromy group
E_{6}
since the
27
-dimensional irreducible representations of
E_{6}
are not self-dual. We haven’t computed the connected monodromy group for
n=2
but expect that it is the symplectic group
\Sp_{8}
and acts on
\mathbb{V}_{s}
via the nontrivial summand in the exterior square of the natural
8
-dimensional representation.

1.2.
Previous work

As we already mentioned at the beginning, all simple exceptional groups are known to occur as Galois groups of motives (operationalized, for example, as
\ell
-adic monodromy groups or Mumford-Tate groups):

G_{2}\colon

Dettweiler and Reiter
[
6
]
constructed a rigid rank
7
local system on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
with monodromy group
G_{2}
via Katz’s middle convolution operation. It is of geometric origin, and its specializations yield motives of type
G_{2}
.
Earlier, Gross and Savin
[
9
]
proposed a conjectural construction of such motives in the cohomology of a Shimura variety. Yun
[
27
]
later constructed exceptional motivic local systems whose
G_{2}
case is conjecturally equivalent to that of Dettweiler–Reiter.

E_{6}\colon

Patrikis
[
20
,
21
]
constructed

p
-adic Galois representations with Zariski dense image in
E_{6}

which are geometric in the sense of Fontaine–Mazur, i.e. unramified almost everywhere and de Rham at primes above
p
. Such Galois representations are conjectured to arise in the
p
-adic étale cohomology of an algebraic variety. Boxer, Calegari, Emerton, Levin, Madapusi Pera, and Patrikis
[
3
]
subsequently realized
E_{6}
as the Galois group of a motives occurring in the cohomology of a Shimura variety, though not a family of such.

E_{7},E_{8}\colon

Yun
[
27
]
, using ideas from the Langlands program, constructed motivic local systems on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
with monodromy groups
E_{7}
and
E_{8}
, whose specializations yield motives of these types.

F_{4}\colon

Patrikis
[
20
,
21
]
also found geometric
p
-adic Galois representations with Zariski dense image in
F_{4}
. Independently, Guralnick, Lübeck, and Yu
[
10
]
constructed rigid
F_{4}
-local systems on
\mathbb{P}^{1}\smallsetminus\{0,1,\infty\}
. Færgeman
[
8
]
recently showed that all rigid
G
-local systems are motivic.

For the groups
G=G_{2},E_{7},E_{8},F_{4}
, the first, third, and fourth constructions above also provide an answer to the geometric analogue of Serre’s question that we study here: that is, they provide local systems of geometric origin with algebraic monodromy group
G
. To the best of our knowledge, this geometric analogue has remained open for
E_{6}
prior to this work. Note that there are natural candidates for motivic local systems with monodromy
E_{6}
or
E_{7}
, namely the tautological local systems on Shimura varieties of type
E_{6}
and
E_{7}
, respectively. Although these are not known to be of geometric origin, Diao, Lan, Liu, and Zhu
[
7
]
show that they are geometric in the sense of Fontaine–Mazur, and are therefore conjectured to arise from geometry. In particular, by suitable specialization, one can construct Galois representations of the desired type that are conjecturally motivic. In contrast, the local systems constructed in this paper are not of Shimura type.

One of the goals of this paper is to explain how the remarkable geometry of cubic threefolds reflects the exceptional nature of the group
E_{6}
. It would be interesting to find similarly explicit geometric constructions for the other exceptional groups.

1.3.
Ideas of the proof

Before going into details, let us briefly explain the main ideas in the proof that the local system
\mathbb{V}
of the main theorem has connected monodromy group
M_{s}^{\circ}=E_{6}
. We proceed in two steps: first we observe that there is an inclusion
M_{s}^{\circ}\subset E_{6}
, and then we show that this inclusion cannot be strict.

The inclusion
M_{s}^{\circ}\subset E_{6}
follows directly from results in the literature. Indeed, Lawrence and Sawin
[
18
, lemma 2.9]
prove that the monodromy normalizes the Tannaka group
G
associated with the convolution of perverse sheaves on the abelian variety
\Alb(\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\eta}})
, where
\mathcal{F}_{\bar{\eta}}
is a geometric generic fiber of
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S
, see also
[
12
, th. 4.5]
. On the other hand, by
[
15
, th. 2]
the derived group of the connected component of the identity in
G
is
E_{6}
acting via one of its two irreducible
27
-dimensional representations (as explained in
[
16
]
, this is related to the fact that the monodromy of the family of lines in the universal cubic
surface
is the Weyl group
W(E_{6})
). Schur’s lemma then gives the desired inclusion
M_{s}^{\circ}\subset E_{6}
.

The main task of this paper is to prove that this inclusion cannot be strict. Note that the comparison results between algebraic monodromy groups and Tannaka groups in
[
18
, th. 4.7]
and
[
12
, th. 4.10]
do not apply here because the abelian scheme
\operatorname{Alb}(\mathscr{F}/S)
is not constant. In contrast, our proof is Hodge-theoretic in nature and builds on a higher-dimensional generalization of the theory developed in
[
17
]
to prove big monodromy results for curves.

Since
M_{s}^{\circ}
is the connected monodromy group of a local system of geometric origin, it is necessarily semisimple and it therefore suffices to show that
M_{s}^{\circ}
cannot be contained in any of the maximal semisimple subgroups of
E_{6}
. Our proof of this relies on an analysis of the derivative of the period map associated to
\mathbb{V}
at a general point of
S
, as we now explain.

Recall that the surface
F:=\mathcal{F}_{s}
parametrizes lines on the cubic threefold
Y:=\mathcal{Y}_{s}
. A line on
Y
is called of
second type
if its normal bundle splits as
\mathcal{O}(1)\oplus\mathcal{O}(-1)
. The locus of lines of second type is an effective bicanonical divisor
B\subset F
.
We first prove a reconstruction result for the restriction to
B
of the line bundle
\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{L}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{F}}
attached to our local system: if
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{F}
are globally generated (which is true for general
s
and local systems of high enough order), then we may functorially reconstruct
\mathcal{L}|_{B}
from the second derivative of the period map associated to the complex variation of Hodge structures associated to
\mathbb{V}
. Since
B\subset F
is an ample divisor, this is enough to functorially reconstruct the unitary local system
\mathbb{L}|_{F}
by the Narasimhan–Seshadri correspondence. We deduce from this functorial reconstruction that

(1)

\mathbb{V}
is not self-dual, and

(2)

\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{W}\oplus\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
where
\mathbb{W}
is irreducible of Hodge length
2
, rank
\geq 13
, and
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
is unitary.

It turns out that these two properties suffice to show that the connected monodromy group
M_{s}^{\circ}
cannot be contained in any of the maximal semisimple subgroups of
E_{6}
, using a case by case analysis based on the branching of the
27
-dimensional irreducible representation.

2.
The reconstruction technique

In this section we explain how to recover properties of an integrable connection on a smooth proper family from the local system underlying its relative de Rham cohomology, using a higher-dimensional version of the method developed in
[
17
]
for families of curves.

2.1.
The derivative of the period map

Let
S
be a smooth irreducible complex variety and
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
a smooth projective morphism of relative dimension
d
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a vector bundle on
\mathcal{X}
endowed with a flat unitary connection
\nabla\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{\mathcal{X}}
. For any point
s\in S
, we can consider the de Rham cohomology

\textup{H}^{\bullet}_{\operatorname{dR}}(X/\mathbb{C},(\mathcal{L},\nabla))\quad\text{of the fiber}\quad X\;:=\;\mathcal{X}_{s}.

It comes with a natural Hodge filtration. For all
p,q\geq 0
the coherent sheaves
\textup{R}^{q}\pi_{\ast}(\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{p}_{\mathcal{X}/S})
are locally free at
s
and, since
\nabla
is unitary, we have an isomorphism

\gr^{p}\textup{H}^{p+q}_{\operatorname{dR}}(X/\mathbb{C},(\mathcal{L},\nabla))\simeq\textup{H}^{q}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{p}_{X}).

We are interested in the cohomology in degree
d=\dim(X)
. Varying the point
s\in S
, we consider the relative de Rham cohomology

\mathcal{V}:=\mathcal{H}_{\textup{dR}}^{d}(\mathcal{X}/S,(\mathcal{L},\nabla))=\mathbb{R}^{d}\pi_{*}(\Omega^{\bullet}_{\mathcal{X}/S}\otimes\mathcal{L}),

where
\Omega_{\mathcal{X}/S}^{\bullet}\otimes\mathcal{L}
is the de Rham complex of the flat bundle
\mathcal{L}
. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\bullet}
be the Hodge filtration on
\mathcal{V}
and
\nabla\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S}
the Gauss–Manin connection. The Hodge filtration satisfies the Griffiths transversality condition:

\nabla(\mathcal{F}^{p})\;\subset\;\mathcal{F}^{p-1}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S}\quad\text{for all $p\geq 0$}.

On the graded pieces of the Hodge filtration, the Gauss–Manin connection gives rise to
\mathcal{O}_{S}
-linear maps

\gr^{p}\nabla\colon\gr^{p}\mathcal{V}\to\gr^{p-1}\mathcal{V}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S}

which make
\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\gr^{p}\mathcal{V}
a graded Higgs bundle in the following sense:

Definition 2.1
.

A Higgs bundle
\mathcal{H}
on
S
with Higgs field
\phi
is
graded
if the underlying vector bundle comes with a grading
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathcal{H}^{p}
such that

\phi(\mathcal{H}^{p})\subset\mathcal{H}^{p-1}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S}\qquad\text{for all $p\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

Fix now
s\in S
. The goal of this section is to recover information about the vector bundle
\mathcal{L}_{|X}
on the fiber
X:=\mathcal{X}_{s}
from the fiber of the Higgs bundle
\gr\mathcal{V}
at
s
. By taking the fiber at
s
of a graded Higgs bundle on
S
, one obtains the following linear datum:

Definition 2.2
.

An
infinitesimal Higgs bundle
at
s
is a finite-dimensional vector space
H
together with a linear map

\phi\colon H\to H\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S,s},

called the
Higgs field
, such that
\phi\wedge\phi=0
. We say that the infinitesimal Higgs bundle is
graded
if the underlying vector space is endowed with a grading

H=\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{p}

such that

\phi(H^{p})\subset H^{p-1}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S,s}\quad\text{for all }p\in\mathbb{Z}.

We will omit the Higgs field
\phi
from the notation if there is no risk of confusion.

Any Higgs bundle on
S
gives rise to an infinitesimal Higgs bundle by taking the fiber at
s
. Given an infinitesimal Higgs bundle
H
with Higgs field
\phi
, it will be often convenient to consider the adjoint

\theta\colon T=T_{S,s}\to\End(H)

to the Higgs field
\phi
, called
adjoint Higgs field
. For the adjoint Higgs field the condition
\phi\wedge\phi=0
reads

\theta(\delta_{1})\circ\theta(\delta_{2})=\theta(\delta_{2})\circ\theta(\delta_{1})

for all
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\in T
. Note that when
H
is graded, the adjoint Higgs field is a map

\theta\colon T\to\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\Hom(H^{p},H^{p-1})

In particular, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
-th iterate of
\theta
induces a linear map

\theta^{\circ n}\colon\quad\Sym^{n}T\to\bigoplus_{p\in\mathbb{Z}}\Hom(H^{p},H^{p-n}),\qquad\delta_{1}\cdots\delta_{n}\mapsto\theta(\delta_{1})\circ\cdots\circ\theta(\delta_{n}).

Morphisms and direct sums of infinitesimal Higgs bundles, and their graded version, are defined in the evident manner. The dual of an infinitesimal Higgs bundle
H
is also defined in the obvious way, while for the graded version we will describe our weight conventions later.

Example 2.3
.

When
H
is the fiber at
s
of the Higgs bundle
\gr\mathcal{V}
, we write

H:=H_{\mathcal{L}},

leaving the connection
\nabla
understood when no confusion can arise. With this notation, we have

H_{\mathcal{L}}^{p}\;\simeq\;\textup{H}^{d-p}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega_{X}^{p}).

Moreover, the adjoint Higgs field

\theta\colon T=T_{S,s}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{p\geq 0}\Hom(H_{\mathcal{L}}^{p},H_{\mathcal{L}}^{p-1})

can be interpreted as the differential at
s
of the period map of

\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{dR}}^{d}(\mathcal{X}/S,(\mathcal{L},\nabla)),

although we will not use this interpretation in what follows. Instead, we use that, via the Kodaira–Spencer map

\kappa\colon T\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{1}(X,T_{X}),

one can express
\theta
in terms of the cup product:

\langle\theta(t),\alpha\rangle\;=\;\alpha\cup\kappa(t)\quad\text{for all }t\in T\text{ and }\alpha\in H^{p}\simeq\textup{H}^{d-p}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega_{X}^{p}),

see
[
13

. We may therefore factor the
d

be an infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle which is compatible with

and let
H^{\vee}
be its dual as an infinitesimal Higgs bundle. We equip it with the grading

(H^{\vee})^{p}=(H^{d-p})^{\vee},

so that the resulting infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle is compatible with

. When
H=H_{\mathcal{L}}
is the fiber at
s
of the Higgs bundle
\gr\mathcal{V}
we have

(H_{\mathcal{L}})^{\vee}\simeq H_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}.

2.2.
The reconstruction result

From now on we assume that the family
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S
is
versal
in the sense that the Kodaira–Spencer map
\kappa
is surjective at
s
. Note that in this case the map
c
in (
2.2
) is determined uniquely by the
d
-th power of the adjoint Higgs field.

The goal of this section is to recover information about the vector bundle
\mathcal{L}_{|X}
on the fiber
X=\mathcal{X}_{s}
from the relative de Rham cohomology on a first order neighborhood of the point
s\in S
, or more precisely from the infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle
H_{\mathcal{L}}
in
example

2.3
. The idea is to recover information about
\mathcal{L}_{|X}
from the cup product map

c\colon\quad\textup{H}^{d}(X,\textstyle\bigwedge^{d}T_{X})\;\to\;\Hom(H_{\mathcal{L}}^{d},H_{\mathcal{L}}^{0})

and to reconstruct the latter from the
d
-th iterate of the Higgs field via the diagram (
2.1
). Note that the diagram only determines
c
on the image of the map

\mu:\Sym^{d}\textup{H}^{1}(X,T_{X})\to\textup{H}^{d}(X,\textstyle\bigwedge^{d}T_{X}).

To take care of this, let us rewrite the target of
\mu
as

\textup{H}^{d}(X,\textstyle\bigwedge^{d}T_{X})\;\simeq\;\textup{H}^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2})^{\vee}

via Serre duality. Then the kernel of the dual map
\mu^{\vee}
becomes a subspace

\ker(\mu^{\vee})\subset\textup{H}^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2})
, and we denote by

B\;\subset\;X

the base locus of the associated linear system. The evaluation map

\textup{H}^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}\to\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2}
factors through a map

(2.3)

\im(\mu^{\vee})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{B}\to\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2}\rvert_{B}

via the natural identification
\im(\mu^{\vee})\simeq\textup{H}^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2})/\ker(\mu^{\vee})
.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
H
be an infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle which is compatible with
\pi
. We
consider the graded coherent sheaf
\mathcal{E}(H):=\im\psi_{H}
where
\psi_{H}=\bigoplus_{p}\gr^{p}\psi_{H}
and
\gr^{p}\psi_{H}
is
the composite morphism

\gr^{p}\psi_{H}\colon H^{p}\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}\rvert_{B}\longrightarrow H^{p-d}\otimes\im(\mu^{\vee})\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}\rvert_{B}\longrightarrow H^{p-d}\otimes\omega_{X}\rvert_{B}.

The first arrow above is given by the map
H^{p}\to H^{p-d}\otimes\im(\mu^{\vee})
adjoint to
c
and the second is given by the evaluation map (
2.3
). Since all the maps in question depend functorially on
H
, this gives a functor

\mathcal{E}\colon\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{c}infinitesimal graded Higgs bundles\\
compatible with $\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to S$\end{tabular}\right\}\longrightarrow\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{c}coherent
sheaves on $B$\end{tabular}\right\},\quad H\longmapsto\mathcal{E}(H).

When
H=H_{\mathcal{L}}
is the fiber at
s
of the Higgs bundle
\gr\mathcal{V}
we have
\gr^{p}\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}=0
for
p\neq d
.
Hence in this case we will write

\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}=\gr^{d}\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}.

To state the reconstruction result, we need to consider for any vector bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
X
the evaluation map

\eta_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X})\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X}.

For a morphism
f\in\Hom(H_{1},H_{2})
of infinitesimal graded Higgs bundles, let
\gr^{i}f\in\Hom(H_{1}^{i},H_{2}^{i})
denote its component in degree
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. We then have:

Proposition 2.6
.

For every vector bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{X}
with an integrable unitary connection, we have a morphism

\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\im(\eta_{\mathcal{L}})\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}\rvert_{B}\to\mathcal{E}(H_{\mathcal{L}})
of
\mathcal{O}_{B}
-modules
which is functorial in the flat bundle
\mathcal{L}
. Moreover, we have:

(1)

If the evaluation maps
\eta_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
are surjective on
B
, then
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
is an isomorphism

\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}\colon\mathcal{L}\rvert_{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{E}(H_{\mathcal{L}}).

(2)

Suppose that
B
contains a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample. If
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
is surjective on
B
,
then for any morphism
f\colon H\to H_{\mathcal{L}}
of infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle compatible with
\pi
, we have

\mathcal{E}(f)=0\implies\gr^{d}f=0.

Proof.

By construction, the map
c
is the restriction of the cup product map

\textup{H}^{d}(X,\omega_{X}^{\vee})\to\Hom(\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X}),\textup{H}^{d}(X,\mathcal{L}))

to the image of
\mu
. Note that via Serre duality the adjoint to the previous map can be seen as the map

\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X})\to\Hom(\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X}),\textup{H}^{0}(X,\omega_{X}^{\otimes 2}))

given by multiplication of global sections. It follows that on
B
the following diagram is commutative:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X})\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X})^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{L}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cHom(\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X},\omega_{X})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\eta_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}})^{\vee}\otimes\id}

The above factorization of
\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}
induces the sought-for functorial morphism
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
.

(1) Since
\eta_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
are assumed to be surjective on
B
, the left vertical arrow in the above diagram is surjective on
B
and the right vertical arrow is injective on
B
. Hence the claim follows.

(2) The commutativity on
B
of the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{d}\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{0}\otimes\omega_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X})\otimes\omega_{X}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X})^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{L}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cHom(\mathcal{L}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{X},\omega_{X})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\gr^{d}f}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gr^{d}\psi_{H}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\gr^{0}f}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\eta_{\mathcal{L}}}\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}})^{\vee}\otimes\id}

together with the fact that
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
is surjective on
B
, gives that the vanishing of
\mathcal{E}(f)
implies that of the composite of the leftmost vertical arrows in the above diagram. It follows that
\gr^{d}f
takes its values in sections
s\in\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X})
vanishing identically on
B
hence on a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
as in the statement. But the assumptions imply that any such section vanishes identically on
X
: indeed,

\textup{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{X}(-B^{\prime}))=0,

because
\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{X}(-B^{\prime})
is anti-ample, since
\omega_{X}^{\vee}(B^{\prime})
is ample and
\mathcal{L}
is a flat bundle.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The existence of a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
contained in
B
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample is trivially verified if
\mu
is surjective, that is,
B=X
. It is also the case when
\mu
has corank
1
and
X
is canonically polarized: in this case,
B^{\prime}=B
is a bicanonical divisor and
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})\simeq\omega_{X}
is ample.

Note that a complex variation of Hodge structures is not determined uniquely by the underlying local system. But the above constructions essentially only depend on the local system
\mathbb{V}
:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(H_{1},\theta_{1})
and
(H_{2},\theta_{2})
be the infinitesimal graded Higgs bundles attached to two different choices of a complex variation of Hodge structures on a given underlying local system
\mathbb{V}
on
S
.

(1)

If
(H_{1},\theta_{1})
is compatible with
\pi
, then so is
(H_{2},\theta_{2})
.

(2)

In this case we have a canonical isomorphism

\mathcal{E}(H_{1})\simeq\mathcal{E}(H_{2})
.

Hence in this situation we will also denote the coherent sheaf in (2) by
\mathcal{E}(\mathbb{V}):=\mathcal{E}(H_{1})
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{V}
is semisimple, it admits an isotypic decomposition
\mathbb{V}=\bigoplus_{i}\mathbb{V}_{i}\otimes W_{i}
where the
\mathbb{V}_{i}
are pairwise non-isomorphic simple local systems
\mathbb{V}_{i}
and the
W_{i}
are vector spaces. By prop. 4.3.13 and rem. 4.3.14 in
[
23
]
, each
\mathbb{V}_{i}
underlies a complex variation of Hodge structure which is unique up to a shift of the Hodge bidegrees, and once we fix a choice of these shifts, any complex variation of Hodge structures on
\mathbb{V}
is obtained by choosing a bigrading on each
W_{i}
. The associated infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle is compatible with
\pi
iff the one associated with each
\mathbb{V}_{i}
is, hence claim (1) follows. Moreover, for
\alpha=1,2
the isotypic decomposition of the local system induces a canonical isomorphism

\mathcal{E}(H_{\alpha})\;\simeq\;\bigoplus_{i}\mathscr{E}(\mathbb{V}_{i})\otimes W_{i}

where the right hand side does not depend on the chosen bigrading on
W_{i}
, hence (2) follows.
∎

The vanishing statement in
proposition

2.6
(2) can be put in a more symmetric form, using that the functor
\mathcal{E}
is compatible with duality in the following sense. Given an infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle
H
compatible with
\pi
, we have a nondegenerate pairing

\beta_{H}\colon\mathcal{E}(H)\times\mathcal{E}(H^{\vee})\rightarrow\mathcal{O}_{B},

defined for all local sections
x
of

and

of

by

\beta_{H}(\psi_{H}(x),\psi_{H^{\vee}}(y))=\langle\psi_{H}(x),y\rangle_{H}=\langle x,\psi_{H^{\vee}}(y)\rangle_{H^{\vee}},

where
\langle-,-\rangle_{H}
is the duality pairing between the target of
\psi_{H}
and the source of
\psi_{H^{\vee}}
. For any morphism

of infinitesimal graded Higgs bundle compatible with

\beta_{H^{\prime}}(\mathcal{E}(f)(x),y)\;=\;\beta_{H}(x,\mathcal{E}(f^{\vee})(y)).

Corollary 2.9
.

Suppose that
B
contains a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a vector bundle on
\mathcal{X}
with an integrable unitary connection. Suppose that
\eta_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
are surjective on
B
.
Then,

(1)

for any endomorphism

we have

\mathcal{E}(f)=0\implies\gr^{0}f=\gr^{d}f=0.

(2)

if
\mathcal{L}
is a line bundle and
\textup{H}^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B})=\mathbb{C}
, for any direct sum decomposition
H_{\mathcal{L}}\simeq H_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus H_{r}
as infinitesimal graded Higgs bundles, there is
i\in\{1,\dots,r\}
such that both for

and for

, we have

H_{j}^{p}=\begin{cases}H^{p}_{\mathcal{L}}&\text{if $j=i$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

(1) By
proposition

2.6
(2), it suffices to prove that
\gr^{0}f=0
. From
eq.

2.4
we know that
\mathcal{E}(f^{\vee})
is adjoint to
\mathcal{E}(f)
and this latter vanishes, we have that
\mathcal{E}(f^{\vee})
also vanishes by nondegeneracy of
\beta_{H_{\mathcal{L}}}
. The result then follows from

2.6
(2) applied to

.

(2) Since
\mathcal{L}
is a line bundle and
\textup{H}^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B})=\mathbb{C}
, the only endomorphisms of
\mathcal{E}(H_{\mathcal{L}})\simeq\mathcal{L}\rvert_{B}
are homotheties with ratio in

. Identify
H_{i}
with a subobject of
H:=H_{\mathcal{L}}
and let

be the projection onto

followed by the inclusion of

in

. The morphism

is idempotent, hence so is

\gr^{0}(f_{i})=\gr^{d}(f_{i})=\lambda_{i}\id.

Since

for all but one

.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

For any ample divisor
D
in a smooth projective variety
Y
of dimension at least
2
, the natural map
\Pic^{0}(Y)\to\Pic(D)

is injective.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an algebraically trivial line bundle on
Y
and suppose that its restriction to
D
is trivial, i.e. it admits a nowhere vanishing section
\sigma\in\textup{H}^{0}(D,\mathcal{L})
. Taking global sections of the short exact sequence
0\to\mathcal{L}(-D)\to\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}\rvert_{D}\to 0
yields a short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L}(-D))\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(Y,\mathcal{L})\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(D,\mathcal{L})\longrightarrow 0

because
\textup{H}^{1}(Y,\mathcal{L}(-D))=0
as
\mathcal{L}(-D)
is antiample, by the Kodaira vanishing theorem. The section
\sigma
then lifts to a nonzero section of
\mathcal{L}
on
Y
, yielding an isomorphism
\mathcal{O}_{Y}\simeq\mathcal{L}
since
\mathcal{L}
is algebraically trivial.
∎

2.3.
Consequences for monodromy

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a line bundle on
\mathcal{X}
with an integrable connection
\nabla
and let
\mathbb{L}=\ker\nabla
be the local system on
\mathcal{X}(\mathbb{C})
of its parallel sections. We consider the local system

\mathbb{V}\;:=\;\textup{R}^{d}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}.

Suppose moreover that
\mathcal{X}\to S
is projective and that the local system
\mathbb{L}
has unitary monodromy. In this case
\mathbb{V}
underlies a natural polarizable complex variation of Hodge structures, and in particular admits a Hodge decomposition, for any
t\in S
,

\mathbb{V}_{t}\;=\;\bigoplus_{p+q=d}\mathbb{V}_{t}^{p,q}\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbb{V}_{t}^{p,q}\simeq\textup{H}^{q}(\mathcal{X}_{t},\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{p}_{\mathcal{X}/S}).

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose that
B
contains a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample, and
\textup{H}^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B})=\mathbb{C}
.
If
\eta_{\mathcal{L}}
and
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\vee}}
are
surjective on
B
,
then there is a unique simple complex local system
\mathbb{W}\subset\mathbb{V}=\textup{R}^{d}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}
such that

\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{t}\oplus\mathbb{V}^{0,d}_{t}\;\subset\;\mathbb{W}_{t}\qquad\text{for all $t\in S$}.

If
d\geq 1
and if
h^{0}(X,\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{X})
and
h^{d}(X,\mathscr{L})
are both non-zero, we have

\rk(\mathbb{W})\geq h^{0}(X,\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{X})+h^{d}(X,\mathscr{L})+d-1.

Proof.

By proposition 4.3.13 and remark 4.3.14 in
[
23
]
, the local system
\mathbb{V}
decomposes as a direct sum

\mathbb{V}\;=\;\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{V}_{i}

where each
\mathbb{V}_{i}\subset\mathbb{V}
is a simple local subsystem underlying a subvariation of complex Hodge structures.
By compatibility with the Hodge decomposition, it follows that there is an index
i_{0}
such that the local subsystem
\mathbb{W}:=\mathbb{V}_{i_{0}}\subset\mathbb{V}
satisfies
\mathbb{W}_{s}\cap\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{s}\neq 0
for some
i
. Thus
corollary

2.9
(2) implies

\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{s}\oplus\mathbb{V}^{0,d}_{s}\subset\mathbb{W}_{s}.

Since
\mathbb{W}\subset\mathbb{V}
underlies a subvariation of complex Hodge structures, this inclusion at
s
implies the same inclusion at every
t\in S
and gives

\displaystyle\dim(\mathbb{W}_{t})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\dim(\mathbb{V}^{0,d}_{t})+\dim(\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{t})+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\dim\mathbb{W}_{t}^{i,d-i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle h^{0}(X,\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{X})+h^{d}(X,\mathscr{L})+\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\dim\mathbb{W}_{t}^{i,d-i}.

It remains to show that if
h^{0}(X,\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{X})
and
h^{d}(X,\mathscr{L})
are both non-zero, then so are all the summands on the right hand side. This follows from Griffiths transversality: if
\mathbb{W}^{p,d-p}_{t}=0
for some
p\in\{1,\dots,d-1\}
, then

\mathcal{F}^{p}=\mathcal{F}^{p+1}\quad\text{for the Hodge filtration $\mathcal{F}^{\bullet}$ on $\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{S}$}.

Then Griffiths transversality implies
\nabla(\mathcal{F}^{p})\subset\mathcal{F}^{p}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{S}
, which means that
\mathcal{F}^{p}\subset\mathcal{W}
is a flat subbundle. Since
\mathbb{W}
is a simple local system, it follows that
\mathcal{F}^{p}=0
or
\mathcal{F}^{p}=\mathcal{W}
. But since by construction

\mathcal{F}^{p}_{s}=\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{s}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p-1}\mathbb{W}_{s}^{d-i,i},

we know that
\mathcal{F}^{p}\neq 0
(as
\mathbb{V}^{d,0}_{s}\neq 0
) and that
\mathcal{F}^{p}\neq\mathcal{W}
(as
\mathbb{V}^{0,d}_{s}\neq 0
). This yields the desired contradiction.
∎

Suppose now that the flat bundle
\mathcal{L}
is torsion. By its
order
we mean the least integer
n\geq 1
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes n}
is the trivial flat bundle. Let
K:=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n})\subset\mathbb{C}
be the
n
-th cyclotomic extension and let
R:=\mathcal{O}_{K}
be its ring of integers. Then
\mathbb{L}
comes by extension of scalars from a local system
\mathbb{L}_{R}
with coefficients in
R
. Hence
\mathbb{V}
is obtained from the local system
\mathbb{V}_{R}=\textup{R}^{d}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{R}
with coefficients in
R
by extension of scalars:

\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}_{R}\otimes_{R}\mathbb{C}.

In what follows we will be interested in local subsystems of
\mathbb{V}_{R}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
. We consider the tensor product

(2.5)

\mathbb{V}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}=\bigoplus_{i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\mathbb{V}_{i}\quad\textup{where}\quad\mathbb{V}_{i}:=\textup{R}^{d}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}^{\otimes i}.

Needless to say, with this notation we have
\mathbb{V}_{1}=\mathbb{V}
. If the hypotheses of
theorem

2.12
hold for
B
and the flat line bundle
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes i}
, we may consider the unique complex sublocal system

\mathbb{W}_{i}\subset\mathbb{V}_{i}

provided by
theorem

2.12
.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
d=2
. Suppose that
B
contains a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample, and
\textup{H}^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B})=\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a torsion line bundle of order
n
such that for all
i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
the maps
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\otimes i}}
are surjective on
B
. Then for any
R
-local subsystem

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}\subset\mathbb{V}_{R}

such that
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}\subset\mathbb{V}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
meets all
\mathbb{W}_{i}
trivially, the monodromy of
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}
is finite.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}
is an
R
-sublocal system, the complex local system
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
admits a decomposition compatible with the one in (

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}=\bigoplus_{i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}}\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{i}\quad\textup{where}\quad\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{i}=(\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C})\cap\mathbb{V}_{i}.

By assumption, the local systems
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{i}
and
\mathbb{W}_{i}
meet trivially, hence
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{i}
must be pure of Hodge type
(1,1)
because

. In particular,
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{i}
is unitary. Seeing
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{R}
just as an integral local system, the local system
\mathbb{V}_{R}^{\prime}
is then both integral and unitary, hence it has finite monodromy.
∎

Corollary 2.14
.

Let
d=2
. Suppose that
B
contains a divisor
B^{\prime}\subset X
such that
\omega^{\vee}_{X}(B^{\prime})
is ample, and
\textup{H}^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B})=\mathbb{C}
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a torsion line bundle of order
n
such that for all
i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
the maps
\eta_{\mathcal{L}^{\otimes i}}
are surjective on
B
. If every simple sublocal system of
\mathbb{V}_{i}
different from
\mathbb{W}_{i}
has rank
<\rk\mathbb{W}_{i}
, then

\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{W}\oplus\mathbb{W}^{\prime}

where the local system
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}

Under the assumptions on the rank of
\mathbb{W}
, the simple direct summand
\mathbb{W}
is an isotypic component of
\mathbb{V}
, hence it admits a unique complement
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
. The point is that in this case
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
comes from a
R

\mathbb{V}_{R}
because
\mathbb{W}
does. To argue for this last claim, notice that
\mathbb{L}_{R}^{\otimes i}
can also be seen as the Galois conjugate
\mathbb{L}_{R}^{\sigma}
for the unique
\sigma\in\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
such that

. As a consequence,

\mathbb{V}_{i,R}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{R}^{\otimes i}=\mathbb{V}_{R}^{\sigma}

is the Galois conjugate of
\mathbb{V}_{R}
under
\sigma
. Now, any local complex subsystem of
\mathbb{V}_{i}
is defined over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, thus it makes sense to consider its Galois conjugates under

. Since the simple sublocal systems of
\mathbb{V}_{i}
different from
\mathbb{W}_{i}
have smaller rank, the sublocal system
\mathbb{W}_{i}
coincides with its Galois conjugates under
\Gal(\overline{\mathbb{Q}}/K)
, hence it comes by extension of scalars from a unique saturated

-sublocal system

\mathbb{W}_{i,R}\subset\mathbb{V}_{i,R}.

The same uniqueness property shows

\mathbb{W}_{i,R}=\mathbb{W}_{R}^{\sigma}

where
\mathbb{W}_{R}:=\mathbb{W}_{1,R}
and
\sigma\in\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})

. It follows at once that the complement
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}
of
\mathbb{W}
comes from local subsystem
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}_{R}\subset\mathbb{V}_{R}
with coefficients in

. Moreover, seen as a
\mathbb{Z}
-local subsystem it is such that
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}_{R}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
meets trivially
\mathbb{W}_{i,R}
for any

.
Proposition

2.13
implies that
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
has finite monodromy, as desired.
∎

3.
The surface of lines on a cubic threefold

3.1.
Main results

To prove the main theorem of this paper, we are going to apply the results of the previous section to the family of Fano surfaces of lines deduced from a versal family of cubic threefolds. In this section we are going to prove that the hypotheses of
theorem

2.12
are fulfilled for these surfaces. More precisely, let
Y\subset\mathbb{P}(E)
be a smooth cubic threefold, where
\dim E=5
. Let
F:=F(Y)
be the surface of lines on
Y
. First of all, we show the following global generation result for algebraically trivial line bundles:

Theorem 3.1
.

For
\mathcal{L}\in\Pic^{0}(F)
generic, the line bundle
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
is globally generated. In particular, if
\Pic^{0}(F)
is simple,
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
is globally generated for all but finitely many torsion line bundles
\mathcal{L}
.

The second result concerns the rank of the linear map
\mu\colon\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})

induced by the cup product. The geometric meaning of this rank is better understood by considering the dual map

\mu^{\vee}\colon\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})\to\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})^{\vee}

where we use the identification
\textup{H}^{2}(F,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})^{\vee}\simeq\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})
given by Serre duality. By definition, the surface
F
embeds into the Grassmannian of projective lines in
\mathbb{P}(E)
,

F\hookrightarrow G:=\Gr_{2}(E).

The polarization
\mathcal{O}_{G}(1)
on
G
given by the Plücker embedding restricts to the canonical bundle of
F
, and the restriction map

\textup{H}^{0}(G,\mathcal{O}_{G}(2))\hookrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})

is injective. However it is not an isomorphism as cor. 1.8 and prop. 1.15 in
[
1
]
yield

h^{0}(G,\mathcal{O}_{G}(2))=50\qquad\text{and}\qquad h^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})=51.

Recall that a line
L\subset Y
is of
second type
if its normal bundle is

\mathcal{N}_{L/Y}\simeq\mathcal{O}_{L}(1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{L}(-1)
. Lines of second type form a divisor in
F
cut out by a bicanonical section

\sigma\in\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2}).

With this notation, we have:

Theorem 3.2
.

We have
\mu^{\vee}(\sigma)=0
and, for
Y
generic, the composite map

\textup{H}^{0}(G,\mathcal{O}_{G}(2))\hookrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu^{\vee}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})^{\vee}

is injective. In particular, for
Y
generic,
\rk\mu=50
, and the base locus
B
of
\ker(\mu^{\vee})\subset\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})
is the divisor of lines of second type on
Y
.

As usual, in the above statement, the generic quantifier means that the statement holds for
Y
in a suitable nonempty open subset of
\mathbb{P}(\Sym^{3}E^{\vee})
. Also,
B
is a bicanonical divisor, hence ample.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of these two results.

3.2.
Proof of
theorem

3.1

We begin with the following precise form of generic vanishing for
F
:

Lemma 3.3
.

For any nontrivial
\mathcal{L}\in\Pic^{0}(F)
we have

h^{0}(F,\mathcal{L})=h^{1}(F,\mathcal{L})=0\quad\text{and}\quad h^{2}(F,\mathcal{L})=6.

Proof.

Since
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\mathcal{L})=0
for any non-trivial line bundle
\mathcal{L}
with vanishing first Chern class and since

\chi(F,\mathcal{L})=\chi(F,\mathscr{O}_{F})=6
, we only need to see

\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{L})=0.

For this we will use Hodge theory. Since
\mathcal{L}
has vanishing first Chern class, it admits a (unique) unitary flat connection
\nabla\colon\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{F}
. Let
\mathbb{L}=\ker\nabla
be the local system of its flat sections. Because of the Hodge decomposition

\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathbb{L})=\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{L})\oplus\textup{H}^{0}(F,\mathcal{L}\otimes\Omega^{1}_{F})

it suffices to show
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathbb{L})=0
for every non-trivial rank one local system
\mathbb{L}
on
F
. In other words, we want to show

\textup{H}^{1}(\pi_{1}(F),\chi)=0

for the character
\chi\colon\pi_{1}(F)\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}
corresponding to
\mathbb{L}
. Collino
[
4
,
5
]
showed that the topological fundamental group
\pi_{1}(F)
is a nontrivial central extension of
\mathbb{Z}^{10}
by
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. By the Hochschild–Serre spectral sequence, this gives us an exact sequence

0\to\textup{H}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}^{10},\chi)\to\textup{H}^{1}(\pi_{1}(F),\chi)\to\textup{H}^{1}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{C}).

The left term in this sequence vanishes because
\chi
is non-trivial, and the right vanishes because
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
is torsion. Hence the same is true for the central term.
∎

We now prove the global generation result in
theorem

3.1
. Let
S\subset\Pic^{0}(F)
be a smooth curve passing through
0
with tangent direction

v\in T_{0}\Pic^{0}(F)\simeq\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}).

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the restriction of the Poincaré bundle to
F\times S
and let

\pr_{F}\colon F\times S\to F,\qquad\pr_{S}\colon F\times S\to S,

be the two projections. The coherent sheaf

\mathcal{E}:=\pr_{S\ast}(\mathcal{L}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F})

on
S
is torsion free, thus locally free because
S
is a smooth curve. Moreover, by the preceding lemma and the base change theorem for coherent cohomology, the vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
has rank
6
and, for any
s\in S\smallsetminus\{0\}
, it has fiber
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\mathcal{L}_{s}\otimes\omega_{F})
where
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is the restriction of
\mathcal{L}
to the fiber of
\pr_{S}
at
s
. The locus in
F\times S
where the evaluation morphism

\pr_{S}^{\ast}\mathcal{E}=\pr_{S}^{\ast}\pr_{S\ast}(\mathcal{L}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F})\to\mathcal{L}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F}

is surjective is open, and its complement has a closed image
T\subset S
via
\pr_{S}
by properness of
F
. Therefore it suffices to prove that
0\not\in T
. To do this, we are going to prove that the tangent vector
v
can be chosen so that the subspace

L_{v}:=\mathcal{E}_{0}\hookrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})

defines a base-point free linear system. We begin by giving an alternative description of
L_{v}
. To state it, recall that the cup product map
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\otimes\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})
is skew-symmetric and the induced linear map

(3.1)

\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})

is an isomorphism by
[
11
, chapter 5, lemma 2.5]
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Via the identification
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\simeq\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})^{\vee}
given by Serre duality and (
3.1
), we have

L_{v}=\{\textup{skew-symmetric bilinear forms $\phi$ on $\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})$ with $v\in\ker\phi$}\}.

Proof of
lemma

3.4
.

Standard arguments in deformation theory
[
24
, Proposition 3.3.4]
imply that
L_{v}
lies in the kernel of the linear map
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\to\textup{H}^{1}(F,\omega_{F})
given by cup product with
v
:

L_{v}\;\subset\;L^{\prime}_{v}:=\ker(\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\to\textup{H}^{1}(F,\omega_{F}):\alpha\mapsto\alpha\cup v).

On the other hand, applying Serre duality and taking the adjoint map of the cup product map

\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\otimes\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\to\textup{H}^{1}(F,\omega_{F})

one obtains the cup product map
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\otimes\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})
. Via the isomorphism (
3.1
) the kernel
L^{\prime}_{v}
can thus be seen as the subspace of skew-symmetric bilinear forms
\phi
on
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})
such that
\phi(v,-)
vanishes identically. These are in linear bijection with skew-symmetric bilinear forms on
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})/\mathbb{C}v
, thus

\dim L^{\prime}_{v}=\textstyle\binom{5-1}{2}=6.

On the other hand, the subspace
L_{v}
has dimension
6
because it is the fiber at
0
of the rank
6
vector bundle
\mathcal{E}
. Therefore
L_{v}=L^{\prime}_{v}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

To analyze the base locus of
L_{v}
, let
A:=\Pic^{0}(F)
.
The cubic hypersurface
Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
can be reconstructed from
F
as the image of the map

\mathbb{P}(T_{F})\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(T_{A})=\mathbb{P}(\Lie A)\times A\to\mathbb{P}(\Lie A)=\mathbb{P}(\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}))

(see
[
11
, Chapter 5, proof of theorem 4.3]
)
after taking an isomorphism
\mathbb{P}(\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}))\simeq\mathbb{P}(E)
as follows.
By
[
11
, Chapter 2, Corollary 4.20(ii)]
there is a canonical isomorphism

\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\simeq\textup{H}^{1,2}(Y)=\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y}).

On the other hand,
\textup{H}^{1,2}(Y)
is the degree
4
component of the Jacobian ring
R
of
Y
and the natural pairing
R_{1}\times R_{4}\to R_{5}
is perfect and gives an isomorphism
R_{4}\simeq R_{1}^{\vee}\otimes R_{5}
. Now
R_{1}=E^{\vee}
and
R_{5}
is of dimensional one by smoothness of
Y
, so the isomorphism

(3.2)

\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\simeq E\otimes R_{5}

gives the wanted isomorphism
\mathbb{P}(\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}))\simeq\mathbb{P}(E)
, through which we see
[v]\in\mathbb{P}(\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}))
as a point of
\mathbb{P}(E)
.

Lemma 3.5
.

A point
x\in F
is a base point of the linear system
L_{v}\subset\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})
if and only if the corresponding line
\ell_{x}\subset Y
contains
[v]\in\mathbb{P}(E)
.

Proof.

Pick a nonzero element of
R_{5}
and identify
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})
with
E
via (
3.2
). In the above description of
Y
, the line
\ell_{x}
is the subspace
\mathbb{P}(T_{x}F)\subset\mathbb{P}(\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}))
. Moreover, evaluating a global differential
2
-form at
x
corresponds to seeing it as skew-symmetric bilinear form on
\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})
via the isomorphism
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\simeq\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})^{\vee}
and then restricting such a form to
T_{x}F
. Since
L_{v}
is the subspace of skew-symmetric forms
\phi
with
v\in\ker\phi
, saying that any such form vanishes on
T_{x}F
means that
v
belongs to
T_{x}F
.
∎

In particular, the linear system
L_{v}
is base-point free if and only if
[v]\notin Y
. Therefore, the line bundle
\mathscr{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
is globally generated for general
\mathscr{L}\in\operatorname{Pic}^{0}(F)
.

To conclude the proof of
theorem

3.1
, it remains to justify the final claim.
By assumption the abelian variety
A=\Pic^{0}(F)
is simple. Let
\mathcal{P}
be the Poincaré bundle on
F\times A
and

\pr_{F}\colon F\times A\to F,\qquad\pr_{A}\colon F\times A\to A,

the two projections. By the
lemma

3.3
and the base change theorem for coherent cohomology, the coherent sheaf
\pr_{A\ast}(\mathcal{P}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F})

has fiber
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\mathcal{M}\otimes\omega_{F})
at any nontrivial
\mathcal{M}\in A
. Again, the locus in
F\times A
where the morphism

\pr_{A}^{\ast}\pr_{A\ast}(\mathcal{P}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F})\to\mathcal{P}\otimes\pr_{F}^{\ast}\omega_{F}

is surjective is open, and its complement has a closed image
Z\subset A
via
\pr_{A}
. Moreover, by construction, the intersection of
Z
with
A\smallsetminus\{0\}
is the locus of nontrivial
\mathcal{M}\in A
such that
\mathcal{M}\otimes\omega_{F}
is not globally generated. Let

\widetilde{Z}\subset A

be the Zariski closure of
Z\cap A_{\mathrm{tor}}
, where
A_{\mathrm{tor}}\subset A
denotes the torsion subgroup. By the main result of
[
22
]
, the subvariety
\widetilde{Z}
is a finite union of translates of abelian subvarieties of
A
. As we just showed, the subvariety
Z
is not the whole of
A
. Therefore, if
A
is simple, then
\widetilde{Z}
is finite. This completes the proof of
theorem

3.1
. ∎

3.3.
The Jacobian ring of the Fermat cubic threefold

We now begin preparations to prove
theorem

3.2
. To prove the generic lower bound for the rank of the map
\mu
in
theorem

3.2
we will reduce to the case of Fermat cubic threefold

Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}\quad\text{with equation}\quad F=x_{0}^{3}+x_{1}^{3}+x_{2}^{3}+x_{3}^{3}+x_{4}^{3}.

This case is down-to-earth enough to carry out an explicit computation in the Jacobian ring

R:=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{4}]/J\quad\textup{where}\quad J=(\partial F/\partial x_{i}:i=0,\dots,4)=(x_{0}^{2},\dots,x_{4}^{2}).

Let
R_{i}
be the
i
-th graded piece of
R
. In the proof of
theorem

3.2
we will be interested in computing the rank of the linear map

\nu\colon\Sym^{2}R_{3}\longrightarrow\Hom(\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}R_{1},\bigwedge^{2}R_{4}),\qquad f\cdot g\longmapsto[\phi\wedge\psi\mapsto f\phi\wedge g\psi+g\phi\wedge f\psi].

For
a,b\in R
here we wrote
ab\in R
for the multiplication of the ring
R
and
a\cdot b\in\Sym^{2}R
for the multiplication in the symmetric algebra over
R
.

Proposition 3.6
.

The linear map
\nu
has rank
50
.

Proof.

As we said, the proof is an honest computation. To begin with, for a subset
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}
of
\{0,\dots,4\}
write

x_{I}=x_{i_{1}\dots i_{k}}:=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{k}}\in R_{k}.

With this notation, we have
R_{k}=\langle x_{I}:I\subset\{0,\dots,4\},|I|=k\rangle
.
To express the map
\nu
with respect to this basis, notice that, for any
t,u\in\{0,\dots,4\}
and any subsets
I,J\subset\{0,\dots,4\}
of cardinality
3
, we have

x_{I}x_{t}\wedge x_{J}x_{u}\neq 0\quad\iff\quad t\not\in I,\;\;u\not\in J,\;\;I\cup\{t\}\neq J\cup\{u\}.

Moreover, if this is the case and
I\neq J
, then
x_{J}x_{t}\wedge x_{I}x_{u}=0
. The explicit expression of
\nu
then depends on the cardinality of
I\cap J
. If
|I\cap J|=3
, that is
I=J
, we have

\nu(x_{I}\cdot x_{I})(x_{t}\wedge x_{u})=\begin{cases}2x_{I}x_{t}\wedge x_{I}x_{u}&\textup{if $I\cup\{t,u\}=\{0,\dots,4\}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Suppose now
|I\cap J|=2
. In this case the sets
I\smallsetminus J
,
J\smallsetminus I
and
\{0,\dots,4\}\smallsetminus(I\cup J)
are singletons and we call respectively
i
,
j
and
k
their elements. Then,

\nu(x_{I}\cdot x_{J})(x_{t}\wedge x_{u})=\begin{cases}\pm\,x_{I}x_{j}\wedge x_{J}x_{k}&\textup{if $t=j$, $u=k$,}\\
\pm\,x_{I}x_{k}\wedge x_{J}x_{i}&\textup{if $t=k$, $u=i$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Finally, if
|I\cap J|=1
, we have

\nu(x_{I}\cdot x_{J})(x_{t}\wedge x_{u})=\begin{cases}\pm\,x_{I}x_{t}\wedge x_{J}x_{u}&\textup{if $t\not\in I$, $u\not\in J$,}\\
\pm\,x_{I}x_{u}\wedge x_{J}x_{t}&\textup{if $u\not\in I$, $t\not\in J$,}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The other computational advantage of the Fermat cubic threefold is that the map
\nu
is equivariant under the natural action on
R
of the subgroup
G\subset\GL_{5}(\mathbb{C})
fixing
F
. The group
G
is the semidirect product of the subgroup
S_{5}
permuting coordinates and the subgroup
T=\mu_{3}^{5}
of diagonal matrices whose nonzero entries are third roots of unity. For
k=1,2,3
the subspace

V_{k}:=\bigoplus_{|I\cap J|=k}\langle x_{I}\cdot x_{J}\rangle\subset\Sym^{2}R_{3}

is stable under the action of
G
, where the direct sums ranges over unordered pairs
I,J
of subsets with cardinality
3
. Moreover, if
|I\cap J|\geq 2
, the subspace
\langle x_{I}\cdot x_{J}\rangle
is an eigenspace for the action of
T
. Indeed,
x_{I}\cdot x_{J}
is an eigenvector for the character

T\ni t=(t_{0},\dots,t_{4})\longmapsto\prod_{i=0}^{4}t_{i}^{\delta_{I}(i)+\delta_{J}(i)}

where
\delta_{I}\colon\{0,\dots,4\}\to\{0,1\}
is the indicator function of
I
, and similarly for
J
. When
|I\cap J|\geq 2
, the above character determines the unordered pair
I,J
uniquely. Instead, when
|I\cap J|=1
, the eigenspace decomposition of
V_{1}
is

V_{1}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{4}W_{i}\quad\textup{with}\quad W_{i}=\bigoplus_{I\cap J=\{i\}}\langle x_{I}\cdot x_{J}\rangle.

Indeed, if
I\cap J=\{i\}
, the character of
x_{I}\cdot x_{J}
is
t_{i}^{2}\prod_{j\neq i}t_{j}
, hence depends only on
i
. Note that, for fixed
i
, there are exactly three unordered pairs
I,J
of subsets of cardinality
3
such that
I\cap J=\{i\}
.

To conclude the proof, since
\nu
is morphism of representations of
G
, it suffices to compute the rank of the restriction of
\nu
to each
T
-eigenspace of
\Sym^{2}R
. If
|I\cap J|\geq 2
, we saw that
\nu(x_{I}\cdot x_{J})
is nonzero, hence injective on the eigenspace
\langle x_{I}\cdot x_{J}\rangle
. Instead, we claim that the kernel of
\nu\rvert_{W_{i}}
has dimension one. This will conclude the proof, since we will then have

\rk\nu=\dim\Sym^{2}R_{3}-5=50,

because
R_{3}\simeq\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
has dimension
10
. To prove the claim, we may assume
i=0
, so that a basis of
W_{0}
is

f:=x_{012}\cdot x_{034},\qquad g:=x_{013}\cdot x_{024},\qquad h:=x_{014}\cdot x_{023}.

The explicit expression of
\nu
show that it has rank
\geq 2
on
W_{0}
, because for example we have:

\displaystyle\nu(f)(x_{2}\wedge x_{3})

\displaystyle=x_{0234}\wedge x_{0123},

\displaystyle\nu(g)(x_{2}\wedge x_{3})

\displaystyle=-x_{0234}\wedge x_{0123},

\displaystyle\nu(h)(x_{2}\wedge x_{3})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nu(f)(x_{2}\wedge x_{4})

\displaystyle=x_{0234}\wedge x_{0124},

\displaystyle\nu(g)(x_{2}\wedge x_{4})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\nu(h)(x_{2}\wedge x_{4})

\displaystyle=-x_{0234}\wedge x_{0124}.

The above also shows
\nu(f+g+h)(x_{2}\wedge x_{3})=0
. But the stabilizer of
0
in
S_{5}
acts transitively on unordered pairs in
\{1,\dots,4\}
and leaves
f+g+h
invariant, thus
\nu(f+g+h)=0
.
∎

3.4.
The upper bound on the rank in
theorem

3.2

Let
Y\subset\mathbb{P}(E)
be a smooth cubic threefold, where
E
is a vector space of dimension
5
, and
F:=F(Y)
the surface of lines on
Y
. With notation as in
theorem

3.2
, we are interested in the linear map

\mu\colon\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})

induced by the cup product. Via Serre duality, the dual of
\mu
can be seen as a map

\mu^{\vee}\colon\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})\to\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})^{\vee}.

We first show that
\mu^{\vee}
vanishes on the bicanonical sections cutting out the divisor of lines of second type. There is a distinguished such section, obtained as follows. Let
p\colon P\to F
be the universal line and
q\colon P\to Y
the projection. The proof of
[
11
, Chapter 5, Proposition 1.1]
shows that the determinant of the differential
\mathrm{d}q\colon T_{P}\to q^{\ast}T_{Y}
defines a section of

\mathcal{H}om(\det T_{P},q^{\ast}\det T_{Y})\cong p^{\ast}\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2},

and that via the above identification we have

\det(\textup{d}q)=p^{\ast}\sigma\quad\text{for a unique $\sigma\in\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})$}.

By
[
11
, Chapter 5, Proposition 1.1]
, the section
\sigma
cuts out the divisor of lines of second type.

Proposition 3.7
.

With the above notation, we have
\mu^{\vee}(\sigma)=0
. In particular, we have
\rk\mu\leq 50
.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is the map induced by the cup product, unwinding the identification given by Serre duality, it suffices to show that

\sigma(u\cup v)=0\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F}).

Note that
u\cup v\in\textup{H}^{2}(F,\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})
, hence evaluating
\sigma
on it yields an element of
\textup{H}^{2}(F,\omega_{F})
. The construction of the Fano surface works in families; in particular, it induces a map between the first-order deformations of
Y
and of
F
. Moreover, by
[
11
, chapter 5, prop. 2.14]
, this map is an isomorphism

\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F}),

whose inverse we denote by
u\mapsto u_{Y}
. Since
P
is the projective tangent bundle of
F
, we also have a natural linear map between first-order deformations
\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{1}(P,T_{P})
, written
u\mapsto u_{P}
. One checks that the following diagram commutes:

(3.3)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(P,q^{\ast}T_{Y})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(P,T_{P})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(P,p^{\ast}T_{F}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sim}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\ast}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{d}q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{d}p}

To prove the statement, it suffices to show that

(p^{\ast}\sigma)(p^{\ast}u\cup p^{\ast}v)=p^{\ast}\bigl(\sigma(u\cup v)\bigr)

vanishes. Indeed, the pullback map
p^{\ast}\colon\textup{H}^{2}(F,\omega_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(P,p^{\ast}\omega_{F})
is injective by the Leray spectral sequence, since
\textup{R}^{1}p_{\ast}\mathcal{O}_{P}=0
(as
P
is a
\mathbb{P}^{1}
-bundle over
F
). Now consider the commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(P,p^{\ast}T_{F})^{\otimes 2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{2}(P,p^{\ast}\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{2}(P,p^{\ast}\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2}\otimes p^{\ast}\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{1}(P,T_{P})^{\otimes 2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{2}(P,\bigwedge^{2}T_{P})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{H}^{2}(P,p^{\ast}\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2}\otimes\bigwedge^{2}T_{P}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cup}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\ast}\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cup}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textup{d}p^{\otimes 2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigwedge^{2}\textup{d}p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\ast}\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id\otimes\bigwedge^{2}\textup{d}p}

Via the isomorphism
p^{\ast}\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2}\otimes p^{\ast}\bigwedge^{2}T_{F}\simeq p^{\ast}\omega_{F}
, the composite map in the first row sends
p^{\ast}u\otimes p^{\ast}v
to
(p^{\ast}\sigma)(p^{\ast}u\cup p^{\ast}v)
. Therefore, since the rightmost square in (
3.3
) commutes, it suffices to show that the composition of the second row with
\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{1}(P,T_{P})
vanishes, i.e.

(p^{\ast}\sigma)(u_{P}\cup v_{P})=0\quad\text{for all }u,v\in\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F}).

By definition,
p^{\ast}\sigma=\det\textup{d}q
. Moreover, for any morphism
f\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{W}
of vector bundles of rank
3
, the following diagram commutes:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cHom(\det\mathcal{V},\det\mathcal{W})\otimes\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{V}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigwedge^{2}\mathcal{W}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\det\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{W}^{\vee}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\det\mathcal{W}\otimes\mathcal{V}^{\vee}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\bigwedge^{2}f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\det f\otimes\id}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\id\otimes f^{\vee}}

It follows that

(p^{\ast}\sigma)(u_{P}\cup v_{P})=\det\textup{d}q(u_{P}\cup v_{P})=(\textup{d}q)^{\vee}\bigl(\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\textup{d}q(u_{P}\cup v_{P})\bigr).

Since the left square in (
3.3
) commutes, we have

\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\textup{d}q(u_{P}\cup v_{P})=\textup{d}q(u_{P})\cup\textup{d}q(v_{P})=q^{\ast}u_{Y}\cup q^{\ast}v_{Y}=q^{\ast}(u_{Y}\cup v_{Y}).

The key point is that

\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{Y})=0,

hence
u_{Y}\cup v_{Y}=0
for all
u,v\in\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})
, which concludes the proof. Since
\bigwedge^{2}T_{Y}\simeq\Omega_{Y}^{1}(2)
, this cohomological vanishing follows from the exact sequence of differentials for
Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
, together with the standard exact sequences for the restrictions of
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}^{4}}^{1}(2)
and
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{4}}(-1)
to
Y
, and Bott vanishing on
\mathbb{P}^{4}
.
∎

3.5.
Conclusion of the proof of
theorem

3.2

We now give a lower bound on the rank of
\mu
. In fact, we are going to compute the rank of the composite map

\nu\colon\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{2}(F,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle c}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Hom(\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}),\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})),

where
c
is induced by the cup product. Via Serre duality, the map
c
can be viewed as the dual of the multiplication map

\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\otimes\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F})\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2}).

This map has image
\textup{H}^{0}(G,\mathcal{O}_{G}(2))\subset\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})
where
G=\Gr_{2}(E)
is the Grassmannian of lines in
\mathbb{P}(E)
and
\mathcal{O}_{G}(1)
denotes the Plücker polarization. Hence for the proof of
theorem

3.2
it only remains to show:

Proposition 3.8
.

For a generic cubic threefold
Y
we have
\rk\nu=50
. In particular
\rk\mu\geq 50
.

Proof.

We first express everything in terms of the threefold: The incidence
correspondence induces isomorphisms
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\Omega^{1}_{F})\simeq\textup{H}^{1}(Y,\Omega^{2}_{Y})
and

\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\simeq\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y})
. Moreover, for the Fano surface the wedge product maps

\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{0}(F,\Omega^{1}_{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}),\qquad\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathcal{O}_{F}),

are isomorphisms
[
11
, chapter 5, lemma 2.5]
.
Finally, by
[
11
, chapter 5, prop. 2.14]
we have a natural isomorphism

\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\longrightarrow}}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F}).

Via these isomorphisms
\nu
is identified with the cup product map

\nu\colon\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})\to\Hom\bigl(\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(Y,\Omega^{2}_{Y}),\bigwedge^{2}\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y})\bigr).

We now express this in terms of the Jacobian ring of the cubic threefold
Y
. To do this, pick coordinates on
E
and let
F\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{4}]_{3}
be a homogenous polynomial of degree three defining the smooth cubic
Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
. Consider the Jacobian ring

R(F):=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\dots,x_{4}]/(\partial F/\partial x_{i}:i=0,\dots,4)

with graded pieces
R(F)_{k}
for
k\geq 0
. By Griffiths’ description of the primitive cohomology of smooth hypersurfaces (see
[
26
, prop. 6.2]
for instance), there are
canonical identifications

\textup{H}^{1}(Y,\Omega^{2}_{Y})\simeq R(F)_{1},\qquad\textup{H}^{2}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y})\simeq R(F)_{4},\qquad\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})\simeq R(F)_{3}.

Moreover, via these identifications, the cup product becomes the product of the ring
R
. With this in mind, the map
\nu
is

\nu\colon\Sym^{2}R(F)_{3}\longrightarrow\Hom(\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}R(F)_{1},\bigwedge^{2}R(F)_{4}),\qquad f\cdot g\longmapsto[\phi\wedge\psi\mapsto f\phi\wedge g\psi+g\phi\wedge f\psi].

As in
section

3.3
, for
a,b\in R(F)
we wrote
ab\in R(F)
for the multiplication of the ring
R(F)
and
a\cdot b\in\Sym^{2}R(F)
for the multiplication in the symmetric algebra over
R(F)
. Now,
proposition

3.6
states that
\nu
has rank
50
for Fermat cubic threefold
Y
with equation
F=x_{0}^{3}+\cdots+x_{4}^{3}
. The statement then follows by semicontinuity.
∎

4.
Proof of the main theorem

4.1.
Statements

In this section we prove the main theorem of this paper. Let
S
be a smooth complex variety and
\mathcal{Y}\to S
a family of smooth cubic threefolds, meaning that

\mathcal{Y}\subset\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{E})

is a subvariety defined by a nonzero global section of
\Sym^{3}\mathcal{E}^{\vee}
such that the projection
\mathcal{Y}\to S
is smooth, where
\mathcal{E}
is a vector bundle of rank
5
on
S
. Let
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S
be the associated family of Fano surfaces of lines. Fix
s\in S
and let

Y:=\mathcal{Y}_{s}\quad\text{and}\quad F:=\mathcal{F}_{s}

be the fibers at
s
. Statement (2) in the main theorem will be deduced from the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

For
i=1,2
let
\mathbb{L}_{i}
be a rank one unitary local system over
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathbb{V}_{i}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{i}
. Assume the following:

(1)

the Kodaira–Spencer map
T_{s}S\to\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
is surjective,

(2)

the cup product map
\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})
has rank
50
,

(3)

the line bundles
\mathbb{L}_{i}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\omega_{F}
and
\mathbb{L}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\omega_{F}
on
F
are globally generated.

If the local systems
\mathbb{V}_{1}
and
\mathbb{V}_{2}
are isomorphic, then so are
\mathbb{L}_{1}\rvert_{F}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}\rvert_{F}
.

We now pass to statement (1) in the main theorem. Let
\mathbb{L}
be a complex rank one local system of order
n
on
\mathcal{F}
and consider the local system

\mathbb{V}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}

on
S
. We are interested in computing its algebraic monodromy group, that is, the Zariski closure

M:=\overline{\im\rho}\;\subset\;\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s})\quad\textup{of the monodromy representation}\quad\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S,s)\longrightarrow\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s}).

Since the local system
\mathbb{L}
is torsion, the connected component
M^{\circ}
is a semisimple Lie group.
We will deduce the main theorem of the paper from the following more precise statement:

Theorem 4.2
.

With the above notation, assume the following:

(1)

the Kodaira–Spencer map
T_{s}S\to\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
is surjective,

(2)

the cup product map
\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})
has rank
50
,

(3)

for all
i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
the line bundle
\mathbb{L}^{\otimes i}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\omega_{F}
on
F
is globally generated,

(4)

the restriction of
\mathbb{L}
to
F
has order
n>2
,

(5)

the group
M
is connected.

Then,

(M,\mathbb{V}_{s})\simeq(E_{6},V)

where
V
is one of the two irreducible representations of
E_{6}
with
\dim V=27
.

In the rest of this section we will prove
theorem

4.1
, then
theorem

4.2
, and then finally deduce the main theorem of this paper.

4.2.
Proof of
theorem

4.1

We apply the reconstruction results in
section

2
to
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S
. Let us recall the setup: The cup product induces a linear map

\mu\colon\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\longrightarrow\textup{H}^{2}(F,\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})

which by assumption has rank
50
. The target of this map is dual to
\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})
. As
h^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})=51
, it follows that the dual map

\mu^{\vee}\colon\textup{H}^{0}(F,\omega_{F}^{\otimes 2})\longrightarrow\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})^{\vee}

has a one-dimensional kernel. The base locus

B\subset F

of the linear system
\ker(\mu^{\vee})
is then a bicanonical divisor (in fact, by
proposition

3.7
, it is the divisor of lines of the second type)
and hence ample since
F
is canonically polarized; see
remark

2.7
. It follows from
proposition

2.6
and
lemma

2.8
that any isomorphism of local systems
\mathbb{V}_{1}\simeq\mathbb{V}_{2}
induces an isomorphism of coherent sheaves

\mathcal{L}_{1}|_{B}\simeq\mathscr{E}(\mathbb{V}_{1})\simeq\mathscr{E}(\mathbb{V}_{2})\simeq\mathcal{L}_{2}|_{B}

where
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is the holomorphic line bundle associated with
\mathbb{L}_{i}|_{F}
. Since
B\subset F
is an ample divisor, we thus have by
lemma

2.11
an isomorphism
\mathcal{L}_{1}\simeq\mathcal{L}_{2}
. Since the
\mathbb{L}_{i}
are unitary, it follows that
\mathbb{L}_{1}\rvert_{F}\simeq\mathbb{L}_{2}\rvert_{F}
. ∎

4.3.
Proof of
theorem

4.2

We begin by recalling the notation. Let
\mathbb{L}
be a complex rank one local system of finite order on
\mathcal{F}
and

\mathbb{V}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}.

The algebraic monodromy group of
\mathbb{V}
is the Zariski closure

M:=\overline{\im\rho}\;\subset\;\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s})\quad\textup{of the monodromy representation}\quad\rho\colon\pi_{1}(S,s)\longrightarrow\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s}).

Since the local system
\mathbb{L}
is of finite order, the neutral component
M^{\circ}
is a semisimple algebraic group. We will compare
M
with the Tannaka group of
F
for the convolution of perverse sheaves. To introduce it, recall that the Albanese morphism

\epsilon\colon F\longrightarrow A:=\Alb(F),

obtained by fixing some base point in
F
, is a closed embedding. We define the intersection complex

\delta_{F}\;:=\;\epsilon_{\ast}\mathbb{C}_{F}[2]

as the pushforward of the constant sheaf on
F
under the closed immersion
\epsilon
, shifted in cohomological degree
-2
so that it becomes an object of the abelian category
\textup{Perv}(A,\mathbb{C})
of perverse sheaves on
A
as in
[
2
]
. The group law on the abelian variety induces a convolution product on perverse sheaves so that the convolution powers of
\delta_{F}
generate a neutral Tannaka category
\langle\delta_{F}\rangle
, see for instance
[
12
, sect. 3]
. The explicit form of generic vanishing given by
lemma

3.3
, guarantees that

(4.1)

\textup{H}^{i}(F,\mathbb{L})=0\qquad\textup{for $i\neq 2$},

as soon as the restriction of
\mathbb{L}
to
F
is nontrivial. Under this assumption, the functor

\omega\colon\quad\langle\delta_{F}\rangle\;\longrightarrow\;\Vect(\mathbb{C}),\qquad Q\longmapsto\textup{H}^{0}(A,Q\otimes\mathbb{L})

is by construction a fiber functor; see
[
14
, proof of th. 13.2]
. The automorphisms of this fiber functor are represented by a reductive algebraic group

G_{F}\;\subset\;\GL(\mathbb{V}_{s})

which we call the
Tannaka group of
F
. Here we used that

\omega(\delta_{F})=\textup{H}^{0}(A,\delta_{F}\otimes\mathbb{L})=\textup{H}^{2}(F,\mathbb{L})=\mathbb{V}_{s}
.
We are interested in its derived group

G_{F}^{\ast}\;:=\;[G_{F}^{\circ},G_{F}^{\circ}],

which is a connected semisimple algebraic group. By
[
15
, th. 2]
we have

(4.2)

(G_{F}^{\ast},\mathbb{V}_{s})\;\simeq\;(E_{6},V)

where
V
is one of the two irreducible representations of
E_{6}
with
\dim V=27
. With this notation we have:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{L}
be a rank
1
local system on
\mathcal{F}
of finite order whose restriction to
F
is nontrivial. Then,

M^{\circ}\subset G_{F}^{\ast}\simeq E_{6}.

Proof.

Because of
eq.

4.2
, the representation
\mathbb{V}_{s}
of
G_{F}^{\ast}
is irreducible. By Schur’s lemma, since the group
M^{\circ}
is semisimple, it suffices to show that
M^{\circ}
normalizes
G_{F}
. This is a special case of
[
12
, th. 4.5]
. Strictly speaking, the cited result is for
\ell
-adic cohomology. However, we can apply this here because the local system
\mathbb{L}
is torsion, hence of geometric nature.
∎

The interesting part is proving that the inclusion
M^{\circ}\subset G_{F}^{\ast}
is indeed an equality. To prove this, from now on, we will assume the additional hypotheses (1) – (5) in
theorem

4.2
. We will argue by contradiction and assume that
M
is strictly contained in
G_{F}^{\ast}
. Then
M
will be contained in a maximal semisimple subgroup

H\subset G_{F}^{\ast}.

The maximal semisimple subgroups of
E_{6}
up to conjugacy and the corresponding branching of the representation
V
can be found for instance in
[
19
, p. 298]
. We recall them in
table

1
together with the dimensions of the occurring irreducible summands and with the data of whether or not
V
is self-dual as a representation of

. Note that if in our geometric situation
\mathbb{V}_{s}
were self-dual as a representation of
H
, then
\mathbb{V}_{s}
would also be self-dual as a representation of the monodromy group

. But then the local system
\mathbb{V}
would be self-dual, which is ruled out by the following result:

Lemma 4.4
.

With assumptions as in
theorem

4.2
, the local system
\mathbb{V}
is not self-dual.

Proof.

If the local system
\mathbb{V}
were self-dual, then
theorem

4.1
would yield an isomorphism of local systems
\mathbb{L}\rvert_{F}\simeq\mathbb{L}^{\vee}\rvert_{F}
, which contradicts our assumption that
\mathbb{L}\rvert_{F}
has order

.
∎

, the base locus

are ample; see

. As

the line bundle
\mathbb{L}^{\otimes i}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}\omega_{F}
is globally generated. It follows that we can consider the sub local system

\mathbb{W}_{i}\subset\mathbb{V}_{i}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}^{\otimes i}

\mathbb{V}\simeq\mathbb{W}\oplus\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
where
\mathbb{W}
is simple of rank at least

and
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}

is connected, this implies
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
is trivial as desired.
∎

, we rule out the non-self-dual cases one by one. From now on we identify
(G_{F}^{\ast},\mathbb{V}_{s})

is contained in the factor

. On the other hand, the

have dimension

. ∎

4.4.
Proof of the main theorem

We are now in position to prove the main theorem in
section

1.1
. First note that by
theorem

3.1
, there is some
n_{0}>2
with the following property: given a very general smooth cubic threefold
Y\subset\mathbb{P}^{4}
, we have that any line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
F
of finite order
n\geq n_{0}
is such that
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
is globally generated. Indeed, let
\eta
be the generic point of the moduli space of smooth cubic threefolds,
Y_{\eta}
the generic cubic
3
-fold, and
F_{\eta}
its Fano surface of lines. By
[
11
, chapter 1, 2.13(i)]
, the intermediate Jacobian of a very general cubic
3
-fold, and hence the (isomorphic, by
[
11
, chapter 5, corollary 3.3]
) Albanese of the corresponding Fano surface of lines, is simple; thus the Albanese of
F_{\eta}
is geometrically simple. Hence
theorem

3.1
implies that there exists
n_{0}
such that any line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
F
of finite order
n\geq n_{0}
satisfies that
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F_{\eta}}
is globally generated. For each
n\geq n_{0}
the set of cubic threefolds whose Fano surface of lines
F
admits an
n
-torsion line bundle
\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
fails to be globally generated is hence contained in a proper closed subset of moduli. Thus the same
n_{0}
suffices for a very general
Y
as claimed.

To prove statement (1) of the main theorem the key point is that, the base
S
being smooth, for any étale morphism
S^{\prime}\to S
(not necessarily finite) the induced map between topological fundamental groups has image a subgroup of finite index in
\pi_{1}(S)
. In particular, the connected component of the algebraic monodromy group is insensitive to passing such an
S^{\prime}
.

Choosing very general
s\in S
and setting
Y=\mathcal{Y}_{s},F=\mathcal{F}_{s}
, we may assume that

(1)

the Kodaira–Spencer map
T_{s}S\to\textup{H}^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
is surjective,

(2)

the cup product map
\Sym^{2}\textup{H}^{1}(F,T_{F})\to\textup{H}^{2}(F,\bigwedge^{2}T_{F})
has rank
50
,

(4)

the restriction of
\mathcal{L}
to
F
has order
n\geq n_{0}
,

where
\mathcal{L}
is the flat line bundle associated with the rank one local system
\mathbb{L}
. Indeed, under the assumption that the family
\mathcal{Y}\to S
is versal, the first condition holds for generic
s
by definition of a versal family; the second holds for generic
s
by
theorem

3.2
; and the last holds for generic
s
by assumption in the statement of the main theorem.
Moreover, as
s
was very general we may assume by the first paragraph of this proof that all line bundles
\mathcal{L}
on
F
of order
n
satisfy that
\mathcal{L}\otimes\omega_{F}
is globally generated, and in particular that

(3)

for all
i\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
the line bundles
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes i}\otimes\omega_{F}
on
F
are globally generated.

Passing to a finite étale cover of
S
, we may further assume that the rank one local system
\mathbb{L}
on
\mathcal{F}
has order
n
and that

(5)

the algebraic monodromy group of
\mathbb{V}:=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}
is connected.

The assumptions of
theorem

4.2
are thus satisfied, and the monodromy statement in (1) follows.

We now prove statement (2) of the main theorem. For
i=1,2
we may assume as above that
\mathbb{L}_{i}
is a local system of finite order
n_{i}\geq n_{0}
on
\mathcal{F}
whose restriction to the fibers of
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}\to S
has order
n_{i}
. By the choice of
n_{0}
, the associated line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{i}
is then such that both
\mathcal{L}_{i}\otimes\omega_{F}
and
\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\vee}\otimes\omega_{F}
are globally generated. If the local systems
\mathbb{V}_{1}=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{1}
and
\mathbb{V}_{2}=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{2}
are isomorphic, then
theorem

4.1
implies that the restrictions
\mathbb{L}_{1}\rvert_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}
and
\mathbb{L}_{2}\rvert_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}
are isomorphic.

It remains to prove the statement about the invariant trace field
\inv(\rho_{s})
of
\rho_{s}
. First, note that

\mathbb{Q}(\rho)\subset K:=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{n}),

where
\zeta_{n}=e^{2\pi i/n}
. Indeed, the local system
\mathbb{L}
is obtained by extension of scalars from a local system
\mathbb{L}_{K}
defined over
K
. It follows that
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}_{K}\otimes_{K}\mathbb{C}
with
\mathbb{V}_{K}=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{K}
so the traces of
\rho_{s}
lie in
K
, giving the inclusion. For the converse inclusion, after replacing
S
by a finite étale cover, we may assume that

\inv(\rho_{s})=\mathbb{Q}(\rho_{s}).

For
\sigma\in\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
, consider the conjugated local systems
\mathbb{L}_{K}^{\sigma}:=\mathbb{L}_{K}\otimes_{\sigma}K
and the monodromy representation
\rho_{s}^{\sigma}
associated with
\mathbb{V}_{K}^{\sigma}:=\mathbb{V}_{K}\otimes_{\sigma}K=\textup{R}^{2}\pi_{\ast}\mathbb{L}_{K}^{\sigma}
.
Suppose by contradiction that there exists a nontrivial
\sigma\in\Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})
acting trivially on
\mathbb{Q}(\rho_{s})
. Then
\rho_{s}
and its Galois conjugate
\rho_{s}^{\sigma}
have the same traces, hence are isomorphic (since
\rho_{s}
is semisimple). It follows that the corresponding local systems
\mathbb{V}_{K}
and
\mathbb{V}_{K}^{\sigma}
are isomorphic. By (the already-proven) statement (2) of the main theorem, this implies that, for a fiber
\mathcal{F}_{s}
, the restrictions of the underlying rank-one local systems satisfy

\mathbb{L}|_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\cong\left(\mathbb{L}|_{\mathcal{F}_{s}}\right)^{\otimes r},

where
r\in(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is determined by
\sigma(\zeta_{n})=\zeta_{n}^{r}
, since
\mathbb{L}_{K}^{\sigma}\cong\mathbb{L}_{K}^{\otimes r}
. This contradicts the assumption that
\mathbb{L}
has order
n
, and thus concludes the proof of the main theorem. ∎
