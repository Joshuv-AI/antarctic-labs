Abstract
. This paper studies one-dimensional non-Hausdorff manifolds that are similar to ‘‘graphs with split vertices’’. It is shown that if M M is a connected one-dimensional non-Hausdorff manifold such that the set of its ‘‘non-Hausdorff’’ points is locally finite, and each component of its complement has a countable base, then there exists a quotient map π : M → Γ \pi\colon M\to\Gamma onto an open one-dimensional CW complex, which maps the non-Hausdorff points of M M to the vertices of Γ \Gamma . Moreover, Γ \Gamma is the minimal Hausdorff quotient of M M , that is, for every continuous map f : M → N f\colon M\to N into a Hausdorff space N N , there exists a unique continuous map f ^ : Γ → N \hat{f}\colon\Gamma\to N such that f = f ^ ∘ π f=\hat{f}\circ\pi .

1.
Introduction

A
manifold
is usually defined as a locally Euclidean space
{M}
that is also Hausdorff and has a countable base.
The latter two properties are equivalent to the metrizability of
{M}
and to the possibility of embedding it into Euclidean spaces.

Although non-metrizable, and in particular non-Hausdorff, manifolds have been studied relatively little, they often appear implicitly as quotient spaces of certain decompositions of spaces, in particular of foliations on manifolds.
In most cases they are rather “pathological” and do not carry substantial information.
However, under certain assumptions on the “regularity” of the behavior of leaves of foliations, their leaf spaces sometimes allow one to “encode” certain combinatorial properties of the decompositions themselves.

Let us briefly list known publications on non-metrizable manifolds, interest in which has grown significantly in recent years.
In
[
7

non-Hausdorff letter
\mathbb{Y}
was proved.
The so-called
long line

\mathbb{X}
and the

were studied in
[
21

.
In
[

In

In particular, it was proved that for every group

A systematic study of non-metrizable manifolds was carried out in the monograph

1.1.
Main result of the paper

Let
{M}
be a ‘‘traditional’’ one-dimensional
manifold
, that is, a topological space locally homeomorphic to
[0,1)
.
It is well known and easy to prove that if
{M}
is connected, Hausdorff, and has a countable base, then it is homeomorphic to one of the following manifolds:

[0,1]
,
[0,1)
,
(0,1)
, or
S^{1}
.

If
{M}
is a non-Hausdorff manifold, then one can distinguish points at which it “fails to be Hausdorff.”
Namely, we say that points
x,y\in{M}
are
inseparable (by open neighborhoods)
if every neighborhood of
x
intersects every neighborhood of
y
.

A point
x\in{M}
that is inseparable from some other point is called a
branch point
of
{M}
(see Subsection
2.3
).
If
x
is separable from all points
y\in{M}\setminus\{x\}
, then
x
is called a
Hausdorff point
of
{M}
.

Clearly, the relation of being inseparable on
{M}
is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive.
Therefore, consider its transitive closure: points
x,y\in{M}
are called
chain inseparable
if there exists a sequence

x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}=y\in{M}

such that
x_{i}
is inseparable from
x_{i+1}
for all
i=0,1,\ldots,k-1
.

This is an equivalence relation on
{M}
, which we denote by
\approx
.

Let
{\Gamma}={M}/\!\approx
be the corresponding quotient space endowed with the quotient topology with respect to the quotient map
{\pi}\colon{M}\to{\Gamma}
.

Denote by
{M_{br}}
the set of all branch points of
{M}
, and let
{M_{hs}}={M}\setminus{M_{br}}
be its complement—the set of all Hausdorff points of
{M}
.
Set also

\displaystyle{{M}^{0}}

\displaystyle:={M_{br}}\cup\partial{M},

\displaystyle{{M}^{1}}

\displaystyle:={M}\setminus{{M}^{0}}={M_{hs}}\setminus\partial{M}.

Let
K
be a one-dimensional CW complex (see Subsection
2.6
), and let
P\subset K
be a subset of its vertices (0-cells).
Then the complement
K\setminus P
is called an
open one-dimensional CW complex
or an
open topological graph
.

Definition 1.1.1
.

Let
{M}
be a one-dimensional
manifold
.

{M}
is called
graph-like
if

(1)

the set

{{M}^{0}}={M_{br}}\cup\partial{M}
of its branch points is locally finite;

(2)

every connected component of

{{M}^{1}}
has a countable base.

The following theorem is the main result of the paper.

Theorem 1.1.2
.

Let
{M}
be a connected graph-like manifold such that

is not homeomorphic to the circle

and

be the factor map into the space of chain inseparable point classes of

Then

is an open one-dimensional CW complex such that its set of vertices is
{\pi}({{M}^{0}})
.

Moreover,

is the

in a sense that

is a Hausdorff space and for every continuous map

to a Hausdorff space

there exists unique continuous map

, which makes the following diagram commutative:

\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{{\pi_{{M}}}}
\textstyle{N}
\textstyle{{\Gamma}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

Note that in Theorem

might not have a countable base.

Also, in the exceptional case

Even more, all points of

are Hausdorff and the map

However, the circle

1.3.
Examples of non-graph-like manifolds

Example 1.3.1
.

Let
A=\{(0,0)\}\cup\{(0,1/n)\mid n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be the origin together with a sequence on the

In this case, each point

It follows that the set

.
Thus

is an open topological graph.
On the other hand, when we identify

with

, the image

is the convergent sequence

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Quotient mappings

Recall that a mapping
f\colon X\to Y
between topological spaces is called a
quotient mapping
if it has the following property:
a subset
B\subset Y
is open if and only if its preimage
f^{-1}(B)
is open.
It follows from the definition that every quotient mapping is continuous.

The following well-known and elementary lemma contains several properties of quotient mappings that will be used in this paper.

Lemma 2.1.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to Y
be a quotient mapping between topological spaces.
Then the following statements hold.

(a)

Let
\xi\colon Y\to Q
be a mapping into an arbitrary topological space.
Then
\xi
is continuous if and only if the composition
\xi\circ f\colon X\to Q
is continuous.

(b)

Let
B\subset Y
be a subset of
Y
such that its preimage
is non-empty.
Then the restriction
f|_{f^{-1}(B)}\colon f^{-1}(B)\to B

is also a quotient mapping.
In particular, if
f|_{f^{-1}(B)}
is a bijection then
it is a homeomorphism.

2.2.
Locally finite families of sets

Let
{M}
be a topological space,

a subset, and

An open neighbourhood

.
Clearly,

Recall that a subset

•

.

Also recall that a family of subsets

of a topological space

is called

if every point
x\in{M}
has a neighbourhood that intersects only finitely many elements of
\beta
;

•

if each
B_{i}
is clopen in the induced topology

, i.e. each

has an open neighbourhood that does not intersect the other elements of

;

•

if for each

of

such that

Let

An open neighbourhood

does not intersect the other elements of

.
Clearly, the family

2.4.
One-dimensional manifolds

A topological space
({M},\tau)
is called a
one-dimensional manifold
if for every point
x\in{M}
one of the following equivalent conditions hold:

•

there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
homeomorphic either to
(-1,1)
or to
[0;1)
; such neighbourhoods will be called
standard
;

•

there exists an open neighbourhood
U
of
x
and an open embedding
{\phi}\colon U\to[0;1)
; such embeddings are called
(coordinate) charts
.

The following elementary lemma is the one-dimensional case of the Brouwer invariance of domain theorem.
This statement has a ‘‘homological’’ nature, but in the one-dimensional case it reduces to connectedness.
For the reader’s convenience, we give a short proof.

Lemma 2.4.1
.

Let
{M}
be a one-dimensional manifold,
U\subset{M}
an open subset,
x\in U
, and
{\phi},{\psi}\colon U\to[0;1)
two open embeddings.
Then

•

either
{\phi}(x)={\psi}(x)=0
, in which case
x
is called a
boundary point
,

•

or
{\phi}(x),{\psi}(x)\in(0;1)
, in which case
x
is called an
interior point
.

Proof.

Note that the point
0\in[0;1)
is characterised by having a basis of open connected neighbourhoods
V
such that
V\setminus\{0\}
is connected.
On the other hand, every point
t\in(0;1)
has a basis of open connected neighbourhoods
V
such that
V\setminus\{0\}
is disconnected.
The statement of the lemma now follows because the above properties are preserved under the homeomorphisms
{\phi}
and
{\psi}
.
∎

The set of all boundary points of a one-dimensional manifold
{M}
is denoted by
\partial{M}
and called the
boundary
of
{M}
, and its complement
\mathrm{Int}({M}):={M}\setminus\partial{M}
consists of all interior points and is called the
interior
of
{M}
.
It follows easily from the definition that
\mathrm{Int}({M})
is open, while
\partial{M}
is closed and discrete.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
{M}
and
{N}
be one-dimensional manifolds and
{\phi}\colon{M}\to{N}
an open embedding.
Then
{\phi}(\mathrm{Int}({M}))\subset\mathrm{Int}({N})
and
{\phi}(\partial{M})\subset\partial{N}
.

The following simple lemma gives a characterisation of atlases.

Lemma 2.6.2
.

(2)

.

Fix an arbitrary homeomorphism

\displaystyle{\psi}(0)=x,

\displaystyle{\psi}\bigl((-1;0)\bigr)=P,

\displaystyle{\psi}\bigl((0;1)\bigr)=Q.

\displaystyle{\phi_{\lambda}}\bigl((i;s)\bigr)

\displaystyle=P,

\displaystyle\subset\mathrm{hcl}({\phi_{\lambda}}(i)),

\displaystyle j

\displaystyle\in\partial K_{\mu},

\displaystyle{\phi_{\mu}}\bigl((j;t)\bigr)

\displaystyle=Q,

\displaystyle{\delta_{j}}({\phi_{\mu}})

\displaystyle\subset\mathrm{hcl}({\phi_{\mu}}(j)).

Finally, let

\displaystyle P_{i,\lambda}

\displaystyle={\phi_{\lambda}}\bigl((i;\bar{s})\bigr),

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle={\phi_{\lambda}}\bigl([s;\bar{s}]\bigr),

\displaystyle Q_{j,\mu}

\displaystyle={\phi_{\mu}}\bigl((j;\bar{t})\bigr),

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle={\phi_{\mu}}\bigl([t;\bar{t}]\bigr).

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=P\cup\{x\}\cup Q,
