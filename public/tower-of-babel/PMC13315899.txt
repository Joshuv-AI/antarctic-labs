Phenotype tailored lifestyle intervention for weight management in individuals with emotional eating: A secondary analysis of the PHENO-Diet trial
Authors: Diego Anazco, Afton Koball, Lizeth Cifuentes, Jose Villamarin, Jamie Friend, Sima Fansa, Maria A. Espinosa, Thomas M. Rieck, Karen Grothe, Michael Camilleri, Maria D. Hurtado, Andres Acosta, Matthew M. Clark, Donald D. Hensrud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn a proof-of-concept trial, a phenotype tailored lifestyle intervention (PLI) led to greater weight loss than a standardized lifestyle intervention (SLI). This secondary analysis evaluated the effect of the PLI for individuals with an emotional eating phenotype (EE) on weight loss outcomes, eating behaviors and mood.MethodsThis original clinical research article is a secondary analysis of a 12-week, non-randomized, date-of-treatment trial in which adults with obesity underwent phenotyping and were assigned sequentially to either a PLI (N = 84) or a SLI (N = 81). The EE phenotype was defined as a HADS-A (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale for Anxiety) score ≥7. Weight loss outcome and changes in eating behaviors and mood were evaluated among participants with EE assigned to an SLI (n = 19) or a PLI (n = 26).ResultsParticipants with EE assigned to the PLI achieved greater weight loss at 12-weeks compared with those assigned to the SLI (−5.9% ± 4.4 vs. −2.6% ± 4.8; p = 0.02). Participants with EE assigned to the PLI demonstrated reduced HADS-A and HADS-D scores, and the Three-Factor Eating Questionnaire Emotional Eating subscale (TFEQ-EE), while those assigned to the SLI had minimal or no improvements after 12-weeks. Among participants with EE assigned to the PLI, Higher HADS-A scores and lower scores in the Weight Efficacy Lifestyle questionnaire (WEL) negative emotions subscale and TFEQ-EE at baseline were associated with greater weight loss at 12-weeks. (NCT04073394).ConclusionThese findings support tailoring behavioral weight-loss interventions for adults with obesity and EE. Larger randomized studies with longer follow-up are needed to further refine phenotype-based approaches.

Introduction

Lifestyle interventions are at the core of weight management interventions, however, weight loss outcomes are variable and often not sustained in the long-term [1]. Greater initial weight loss and higher adherence to lifestyle interventions have been consistently associated with superior weight loss outcomes across lifestyle interventions [2]. Additionally, multi-component lifestyle interventions (e.g., diet, activity, behavior therapy), long duration (e.g., one year), and/or high intensity interventions (weekly contact) have proven to be the most effective [3].

Given the heterogeneity in responses to current standard-of-care lifestyle interventions to treat obesity, individualized strategies that target the disease's underlying pathophysiological traits and associated eating behaviors have provided an important framework to enhance weight loss outcomes [4]. To date, our multidisciplinary team have provided initial support for four obesity phenotypes based on pathophysiologic and behavioral traits: abnormal satiation (“hungry brain”), abnormal postprandial satiety (“hungry gut”), emotional eating (EE) and abnormal resting energy expenditure (“slow burn”) [5,6].

In a recent non-randomized, proof-of-concept clinical trial by our team, adults with obesity were assigned to receive either a standard lifestyle intervention (SLI) or a phenotype-tailored lifestyle intervention (PLI) based on phenotypic testing at baseline [7]. In this 12-week study, the group assigned to the PLI had superior weight loss (7.4 kg) when compared to the SLI group (3.1 kg, p < 0.01) [7]. The purpose of this secondary analysis is to examine outcomes in those with the EE phenotype. The EE phenotype, specifically, represents an important area of further examination given the strong association between negative mood and eating behaviors in many individuals living with obesity [8]. In prior studies, we have identified that the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale – Anxiety (HADS-A) questionnaire correlated with elevated scores on EE questionnaires [8,9]. As such, our group has historically used the HADS-A questionnaire to classify individuals with the EE phenotype [7,10]. This phenotype is characterized by negative mood states, high anxiety, and reward-seeking behaviors in relation to both negative (e.g., distress) and positive (e.g., excitement) emotions [7], which can contribute to obesity and hinder the outcomes of weight loss interventions [[11], [12], [13]].

The current study, a secondary analysis, sought to examine the effects of a phenotype-specific lifestyle intervention for adults with obesity and EE [7]. It was hypothesized that participation in a short term 12-weeks EE phenotype-specific lifestyle intervention would result in superior weight loss outcomes at week 12 compared with EE participating in a standard lifestyle intervention.

Methods

Study design

This is a secondary analysis of a previously completed 12-week, single-center, non-randomized, proof-of-concept clinical trial. The complete description of the study procedures and interventions have been previously published [7]. In summary, this study recruited men and women aged 18–65 years with a BMI greater than 30 kg/m2 without a history of bariatric surgery procedures or use of medications known to affect weight. The study was approved by the institutional research review board at the Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04073394). All participants provided written informed consent for study participation.

All study participants underwent validated obesity phenotype testing [10] at baseline and after 12 weeks, which consisted of overnight fasting, resting energy expenditure measurement by indirect calorimetry, body composition analysis by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry scans, standardized 1338.9 kJ (320 kcal) radiolabeled breakfast followed by solid gastric emptying via scintigraphy, ad libitum buffet meal, and multiple behavioral and appetite questionnaires.

The study consisted of two distinct, non-parallel phases (see Fig. 1). Participants were assigned sequentially to either phase based on their period of enrollment. During the first phase, 81 participants were assigned to the SLI consisting of a structured two-day program with a 12-week follow up by an experienced, multi-disciplinary team of obesity experts [14]. The recommendations included a calorie deficit of 2092 kJ (500 kcal) per day based on measured resting energy expenditure at baseline, 150 min of moderate physical activity per week, and achieving 10,000 steps daily. In addition, participants assigned to the SLI received 12 individual sessions with a health and wellness coach and had access to self-monitoring and stimulus control tools with cognitive-behavioral approaches.Fig. 1Study flowchart.

Study flowchart.

For the second phase, involving a separate cohort of 84 participants, a multi-disciplinary team of obesity experts were trained to apply a PLI based on the results from the baseline phenotyping tests. Four distinct phenotypes were identified based on key parameters of energy balance regulation and previously defined cut-offs [7,10]. The abnormal satiation phenotype was characterized by a high calorie intake to achieve satiation during an ad libitum meal (>894 kcal in females, >1376 kcal in males). The abnormal postprandial satiety was defined by an accelerated gastric emptying half-life (<101 min in females, <86 min in males). The abnormal energy expenditure was defined as a reduced measured to expected REE ratio (<96% in females, <94% in males). EE was characterized by a HADS-A ≥ 7 for both males and females (75th percentile, used in original phenotype definition by our group) [7,8]. A set of interventions was derived for each specific phenotype based on previous nutritional studies [7]. The present study expands on the rationale for the PLI targeting the EE phenotype.

Lifestyle intervention for the emotional eating phenotype

The PLI for the EE phenotype was structured to target emotional regulation, resiliency strategies, mindfulness, acceptance and commitment, cognitive reframing, goal setting strategies, self-monitoring, and stimulus control [15]. Support for the design of this intervention is from prior research that has shown that a mindfulness-based framework can lead to increased self-awareness and a decrease in EE [16]. The dietary recommendations for the intervention included a low-calorie diet with 3 meals a day and avoidance of snacks to discourage the use of food as a coping mechanism. In addition, the intervention consisted of a high-intensity cognitive behavioral therapy program focused on the identification and awareness of distinct EE patterns, while fostering the simultaneous development of a support network and positive coping skills. Health and wellness coaching strategies were also incorporated into the intervention to improve and build confidence towards the adoption and implementation of positive health behaviors [17,18].

The PLI for EE included 12 weekly interactive video telehealth group sessions incorporating different topics relevant to EE: 1) Cognitive behavioral therapy strategies to manage thoughts, feelings, and behaviors (e.g., challenging automatic thinking patterns, practicing positive self-talk), 2) Resiliency and positive coping strategies (e.g., cultivating gratitude, exploring wellness actions and their alignment with one's values and desires, identifying resiliency strategies), 3) Emotion regulation skill development (e.g., practicing opposite action in response to distress), 4) Mindfulness (e.g., mindful eating and meditation practices), 5) Self-Compassion (e.g., self-strength identification), 6) Strengthening one's support system (e.g., finding practical and emotional support outlets, boundary setting), and 7) Harm reduction strategies to manage cravings in high risk food environments (e.g., urge surfing skills to manage unwanted behaviors). These group sessions were co-led by a clinical health psychologist (KG or MMC) and a health and wellness coach (JF). These sessions were designed to include mindful moment activities such as expression of gratitude, reflective questioning, and identification of personal strengths to increase social connection and motivate healthier lifestyle behaviors.

Outcomes

For this secondary analysis, the primary outcome was the total body weight loss percentage (TBWL%) at 12-weeks across intervention arms (SLI vs PLI) for participants with the EE phenotype. The TBWL% was calculated as [(baseline weight – post-intervention weight)/baseline weight] ∗100. Secondary o included the TBWL% at 4-weeks, and the post-intervention changes (post-intervention - baseline scores) in behavioral questionnaires at 12-weeks in participants with or without EE phenotype. In addition, we explored if baseline or post-intervention changes in these questionnaires were associated with weight loss among participants with EE assigned to the PLI. Statistical analysis

For this secondary analysis, we compared the TBWL% at 4- and 12-weeks between study participants with EE that were assigned to either the SLI or PLI with independent samples T tests. Multivariate regression models were conducted to compare the change in mood and behavioral questionnaire scores (end of study - baseline) including the baseline variable for each item as a covariate. For individuals with EE, we adjusted for the presence of concomitant abnormal postprandial satiety, since there was a difference in this parameter across groups assigned to the SLI and the PLI. For these comparisons we reported Least Square (LS) means and standard error, and we used LS means differences Student's t tests to compare between intervention groups. In addition, we also conducted linear regressions to evaluate if baseline values or post-intervention changes in behavioral questionnaires were associated with weight loss outcomes at 12-weeks between different intervention groups. No data imputation was used for missing values. Continuous variables were analyzed by independent sample t tests and categorical variables with Pearson's chi-squared tests as appropriate. A two-sided p value of 0.05 was set for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed in JMP Pro Version 16.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Study participants

Between July 2020 and August 2021, 165 study participants were assigned sequentially to one of the two treatment phases based upon their date of enrolment: 81 were assigned to the SLI and the next 84 were assigned to the PLI. Among these cohorts, 19 study participants had the EE phenotype in the SLI (23.5%) and there were 26 participants with EE phenotype assigned to the PLI (31.0%) (see Fig. 1).

When comparing participants with or without the EE phenotype assigned to either intervention, there were similar baseline anthropometric or demographic characteristics, except for age, with participants having the EE phenotype being younger than non-emotional eaters (NEE) (see Table 1). Not surprisingly, EE had higher HADS-A, HADS-D and HADS-total scores, as well as higher scores in the TFEQ-EE and TFEQ-UE domains when compared to NEE. Moreover, EE had lower WEL scores across all situational factors and in the total score at baseline compared to NEE. The proportion of participants that completed the trial was similar for EE (84.4%) and NEE (90.0%).

Among participants with EE, there were similar baseline anthropometric, demographic, and clinical characteristics when comparing participants assigned to the SLI (EE-SLI) and the PLI (EE-PLI; see Table 2). No patients assigned to the SLI had concomitant abnormal postprandial satiety, while 7 participants in the PLI had concomitant abnormal postprandial satiety (26.9%). There were no differences in behavioral questionnaires or in the proportion of participants that completed the trial.

Weight loss outcomes for participants with the emotional eating phenotype

Among participants with the EE phenotype, those in the EE-PLI achieved numerically superior weight loss outcomes at 4 weeks when compared to those in the EE-SLI (TBWL%: 3.6 ± 3.3 vs. −2.2 ± 1.6; p = 0.09). At 12-weeks, EE-PLI achieved statistically significantly superior weight loss outcomes when compared to participants with the EE-SLI (TBWL%: 5.9 ± 4.4 vs. −2.6 ± 4.8; p = 0.02; Fig. 2A and B). At 12-weeks, the proportion of participants achieving at least 5% of their body weight was statistically significantly higher for the participants with EE that were assigned to the PLI (n = 12/21, 57.1%) than for those assigned to the SLI (n = 4/17, 23.5%; p = 0.04; Fig. 2C).Fig. 2A) Weight loss outcomes for participants with emotional eating (EE) assigned to either a standard lifestyle intervention (EE-SLI) or a phenotype-tailored lifestyle intervention (EE-PLI). Post-intervention changes in behavioral and psychological variables

EE-PLI participants experienced a reduction in the post-intervention HADS-A score (−3.2 ± 0.7), while participants with EE-SLI had similar post-intervention scores to their baseline scores (0.01 ± 0.7; p = 0.006; see Table 3). Moreover, participants with EE assigned to the PLI had a decrease in their HADS-D and HADS total score. In contrast, those with EE assigned to the SLI had an increase in both parameters after the intervention (p = 0.007 and p = 0.002, respectively). In addition, EE-PLI had a larger increase in the WEL negative emotions score (9.5 ± 2.5) when compared to the EE-SLI (0.5 ± 3.4; p = 0.02). The PLI also led to a larger reduction in the TFEQ-EE subscale than the SLI in participants with EE (p = 0.04). Finally, there were no significant differences in the post-intervention change in any behavioral parameter when comparing participants without EE assigned to either the SLI or PLI.

Behavioral predictors of weight loss response to lifestyle interventions

When assessing study participants assigned to either the SLI or PLI, higher scores in the TFEQ-EE domain at baseline were associated with inferior weight loss outcomes for participants with EE (R2 = 0.19, p = 0.001) but not for participants without EE (R2 = 0.003, p = 0.62). There was a correlation between the post-intervention change in HADS-A and TBWL% at 12 weeks for participants with EE (higher decrease in HADS-A associated with better weight loss outcomes, R2 = 0.17, p = 0.03), however, there was no correlation between these parameters in participants without EE (R2 = 0.04, p = 0.07). Furthermore, in univariate regression analyses including patients with EE assigned to the PLI, there was a moderate correlation between TBWL% at 12 weeks in both the WEL negative emotions score (lower baseline scores were associated with superior weight loss outcomes, R2 = 0.47, p = 0.002) and the TFEQ-EE score (higher scores were associated with inferior weight loss outcomes, R2 = 0.31, p = 0.02).

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of a non-randomized, proof-of-concept clinical trial, participants with the EE phenotype assigned to the PLI experienced greater short-term weight loss (12-weeks) and improvement in eating behaviors and mood symptoms compared to those assigned to the SLI. Participants with EE who received the PLI achieved significantly greater weight loss at 12- weeks and demonstrated greater improvements in EE symptoms, mood (self-reported symptoms of anxiety and depression), and self-efficacy to manage overeating during negative emotions. The reduction in TFEQ-Emotional Eating scores reflect a decreased tendency to eat in response to negative emotions and the improvement in the WEL Negative Emotions domain as indicating greater confidence in resisting overeating during emotionally challenging situations. Similarly, changes in HADS scores are described in terms of improvements in anxiety and depressive symptoms. We believe these revisions improve the clinical relevance and interpretability of our findings while remaining consistent with the validated constructs assessed by these instruments. These findings provide further initial evidence of the benefits of precision medicine obesity interventions [23].

Despite similar short-term weight-loss outcomes to other lifestyle intervention trials [24], our findings indicate that addressing EE-specific patterns might enhance response within this specific phenotype. The superior effectiveness of the PLI on weight loss for individuals with EE is likely attributable to its intentional focus on mechanisms underlying EE and obesity [25,26]. This offers a plausible explanation for the observed greater weight loss effects compared to SLI which focused primarily on standard behavioral weight-management strategies such as self-monitoring and stimulus control without additional tailored material aimed at salient EE factors. For example, EE intervention elements were designed to enhance emotion regulation [27], reduce maladaptive cognitive patterns and promote positive self-talk [28], incorporate harm-reduction and self-regulation principles to support sustainable behavior change, and foster body acceptance and self-compassion [29,30].

Beyond weight loss benefits, the PLI was associated with reductions in anxiety and depressive symptoms (measured by HADS-A, HADS-D, and HADS-total), which contrasts with prior long-term behavioral studies reporting minimal changes in anxiety symptoms despite weight loss [31]. A recent observational study assessed the effects of an 18-month combined lifestyle intervention consisting of 18 cognitive behavioral therapy-based psychoeducation group sessions delivered by psychologists, nutritional counseling guided by dietitians, and exercise sessions guided by a physical therapist [32]. The intervention led to a significant reduction in combined depression and anxiety symptoms and primary depression symptoms (HADS-total and HADS-D) scores at 18- months, however, there was only a minimal, non-significant, reduction in anxiety symptoms (HADS-A score) [32]. Moreover, the change in the mood symptoms was not correlated to weight loss outcomes. In the current study, we showed that by selecting individuals with an EE phenotype at baseline and assigning them to a PLI consisting of intensive behavioral sessions led to a significant reduction in anxiety and depression symptoms (HADS-A, HADS-D and HADS-total scores). In addition, for participants with EE assigned to the PLI, there was a correlation between the post-intervention change in anxiety (HADS-A) and weight loss outcomes. This suggests that selecting participants based on the EE phenotype and addressing their emotional and cognitive vulnerabilities directly via targeted intervention techniques may yield broader psychological and wellness benefits.

A secondary analysis of the Weight loss Referrals for Adults in Primary care (WRAP) trial assessed the long-term effect of either a brief intervention for obesity (educational booklet with weight management strategies) or a commercial behavioral management program (for a duration of 12- or 52-weeks) in symptoms of depression and anxiety [31]. The investigators reported there were no significant differences among treatment groups in the 5-year change of depression and anxiety symptoms, assessed by the HADS questionnaire. Nonetheless, these results should be taken cautiously, as less than half of participants in each treatment group completed the follow-up visit at 5-years. Moreover, it should be noted that a different secondary analysis of this trial identified that higher anxiety and depression symptoms (HADS-A and HADS-D) scores at baseline were associated with a lower engagement with the intervention and a lower attendance for follow-up visits [33]. These results could suggest that individuals with an EE phenotype might need a more intensive behavioral weight loss intervention targeting specific traits, such as anxiety and decreased self-efficacy, which may improve adherence and the probability of successful weight loss and maintenance. Nonetheless, it is important to recognized that the present study evaluated changes in mood over a 12-week intervention period, without extended follow-up as in the WRAP study, and therefore differences in patterns of HADS scores across studies are expected. The observed correlation between reductions in anxiety (HADS-A scores) and weight loss highlights the potential bidirectional relationship between emotional regulation and obesity outcomes [13,25,34]. Moreover, it was surprising to find that individuals with EE in the SLI had worse anxiety and depression symptoms by the end of treatment. While broadly effective, the SLI may have been insufficient to address the complex mechanisms underlying EE, which explains the current study's results.

The PLI also resulted in improvements in EE symptoms and eating self-efficacy compared to SLI. In this study, EE-PLI had numerically higher improvements in several domains of the WEL questionnaire when compared to those assigned EE-SLI, however, this difference was only statistically significant for the negative emotion domain. Importantly, a secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial of a lifestyle intervention for adults at high risk for diabetes assessed the effects of self-efficacy in weight-loss outcomes at 12-months [35]. In this study, improvements in the WEL negative emotion subscale were associated with superior weight loss outcomes at 12- months. Moreover, that study identified that a 35-point increase in total eating self-efficacy (WEL scores) was associated with a 5% reduction in total body weight [35]. In the current study, for individuals with EE, the PLI led to an improvement of 34.9 points in eating self-efficacy (WEL-total score), while those assigned to the SLI had an improvement of 25.9 points. Furthermore, among individuals with EE, lower eating self-efficacy during negative emotions (WEL-negative emotions subscale scores) at baseline were associated with superior weight loss outcomes at 12-weeks. In contrast, it should be noted that individuals without EE at baseline had similar improvements in overall eating self-efficacy (WEL-total scores) regardless of the intervention (SLI: 23.4 vs. PLI: 24.2). By directly addressing the affective and cognitive processes that precipitate overeating, the PLI exemplifies a precision-behavioral approach to obesity treatment. The PLI targeted a distinct phenotype with tailored, EE mechanism-based strategies that extend beyond generic lifestyle modification [27,36] and which aid in enhancing self-efficacy and improving mood. Additionally, consistent with prior evidence that group-based interventions can enhance engagement and outcomes in emotional eating and binge-eating disorder [37], the interactive group component of the PLI may have contributed to improved adherence and behavioral change.

Understanding baseline characteristics which impact weight loss outcomes, and which could be intervened upon in targeted intervention aligns with a precision medicine approach for weight management [5]. Results from this study found that at baseline, individuals with EE exhibited higher loss of control eating (TFEQ-UE) and emotional eating (TFEQ-EE scores) and lower eating self-efficacy (WEL)—patterns that mirror previous reports linking anxiety and emotion-driven eating to suboptimal weight loss and higher attrition in weight-management programs [38]. This further underscores the value of tailoring interventions to behavioral phenotypes.

Collectively, these findings reinforce the clinical relevance of precision behavioral interventions, particularly for individuals with obesity and EE, who may otherwise experience limited success with standard behavioral programs. Strengths of this study include its novel application of a phenotype-based precision lifestyle framework, the use of validated behavioral and psychological measures, and direct comparison of tailored versus standard weight management approaches.

Limitations

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a post-hoc analysis of a proof-of-concept trial that was not randomized or powered to detect all between-group differences. The sequential two-phase trial approach with a non-randomized allocation based on enrollment time was selected for feasibility and logistical reasons during the COVID-19 pandemic. Moreover, the sequential design also enabled standardization of training, as the team was trained on the PLI protocol only after the SLI participants completed the intervention. However, this approach may have introduced temporal confounding, including potential learning effects related to increased provider experience and pandemic-related changes in daily routines, stress levels, healthcare utilization, and participant engagement over time. The two-phase, non-parallel design may therefore have introduced unmeasured confounding, although baseline characteristics were generally similar across groups.Second, some participants in the PLI arm received additional phenotype-based components, complicating the isolation of EE-specific effects. Third, the short 12-week duration limits conclusions about long-term efficacy and maintenance, however, early weight loss has been widely recognized as a clinically meaningful predictor of longer-term weight maintenance [39]. This study was designed as a short-term, proof-of-concept study to assess whether phenotype-guided lifestyle interventions could impact weight management and phenotypic traits. As such, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary evidence suggesting the feasibility and short-term efficacy of phenotype-tailored lifestyle interventions. Finally, the modest sample size and single-center design may limit generalizability to broader populations or clinical settings.

Conclusion

Future randomized controlled trials with larger, more diverse samples and longer follow-up are warranted to confirm these findings and evaluate the durability of weight and behavioral outcomes. Research should also explore interactions among multiple obesity phenotypes and the integration of digital or community-based delivery platforms to enhance scalability. Evaluating mechanisms of change—such as improvements in emotion regulation, eating self-efficacy, and social connectedness—will be essential to refining phenotype-tailored weight management interventions for clinical implementation.•Individuals with emotional eating demonstrate greater baseline psychological vulnerability, including higher anxiety and depression symptoms, greater uncontrolled and emotional eating, and poorer eating self-efficacy; as such, identifying behavioral phenotypes prior to obesity treatment planning is warranted.•A precision lifestyle intervention tailored to emotional eating produced clinically superior outcomes compared to a standard lifestyle intervention, including greater 12-week weight loss, higher rates of achieving ≥5% total body weight loss, reduced anxiety and depression symptoms, and improved confidence in managing eating during negative emotions.•Emotional and behavioral factors are closely tied to weight-loss response in patients with emotional eating. Greater reductions in anxiety symptoms and improvements in eating self-efficacy were associated with better weight outcomes, indicating that these are important therapeutic targets for future obesity interventions.
