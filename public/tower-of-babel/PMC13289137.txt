Automated monitoring in clinical and operational workflows
Authors: Ju-Chuan Yen, Chia-An Hsu, Min-Huei Hsu
Published: 2026

Automation in healthcare is often framed in terms of efficiency. These two Editors’ Choice papers suggest a more important role: improving the consistency, fidelity and clinical usefulness of data. In BMJ Health & Care Informatics, Slattery et al1 and Loo et al2 examine automated monitoring in two very different settings—the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) and the operating room—yet arrive at a similar conclusion. Continuous, automated measurement may offer a more reliable alternative to intermittent, subjective and labour-intensive assessment.

Slattery et al address one of the most difficult problems in neonatal care: assessing pain in infants who cannot communicate verbally. Conventional approaches rely on intermittent bedside observation and specialist judgement, both of which are vulnerable to inconsistency and missed changes in condition. Their study evaluated a wireless biosensor system used with the Clinical Sensor Pain Scale and the Automated Sensor Pain Scale to capture vocalisation, movement and physiological changes during routine procedures.3,5 The result is a model of pain assessment that shifts practice away from episodic human interpretation and towards continuous, sensor-based monitoring.

The implications are clinically important. In a population at risk of both undertreatment and overtreatment, more objective recognition of acute procedural pain could improve both safety and quality of care. The reported validity and inter-rater reliability, including among non-clinicians, further suggest that such tools may support more standardised assessment in settings where specialist expertise is not consistently available.

Loo et al, by contrast, focus on inefficiency in the operating room. Using an ambient surgical platform that combines wall-mounted cameras with computer vision-based artificial intelligence, they automatically timestamped and segmented perioperative events during total knee arthroplasty. Rather than relying on narrow indicators such as turnover time, their approach captures the broader procedural workflow and identifies where variation occurs.

That matters because operating room inefficiency is not simply a financial problem. It also reflects how well teams, time and resources are coordinated. By replacing manual electronic health record timestamping with passive workflow capture, the system generates a more granular account of perioperative practice while reducing documentation burden on staff. The finding that variation was greatest during room setup and the active procedure points to clear opportunities for benchmarking and targeted improvement.6,8

Taken together, the two studies support a broader proposition: automation matters not only because it reduces workload but because it can reveal clinical and operational patterns that manual processes capture poorly. In the NICU, automation addresses the subjectivity and intermittency of bedside pain scoring. In the operating room, it replaces incomplete manual documentation with continuous workflow data. In both settings, automated monitoring makes variation more visible and therefore more actionable.9 10

The promise, however, should not be overstated. Slattery et al acknowledge that their algorithm requires further refinement, particularly in relation to vocalisations, and wider validation across neonatal populations, including infants with airway differences. Loo et al similarly note the vulnerability of computer vision models to environmental complexity, such as equipment occlusion, and the possibility that unmeasured patient factors contributed to the observed variation. These are not peripheral concerns. They reflect a familiar challenge in applied healthcare artificial intelligence (AI): performance under study conditions does not guarantee robustness in routine care.

Even so, both papers indicate where health informatics is heading. One uses biosensors to infer a physiological state; the other uses ambient vision systems to characterise organisational flow. Neither merely digitises an existing task. Both redefine what can be observed, measured and improved. That is the stronger editorial point: automated monitoring will matter less for making healthcare faster than for making it more standardised, more accountable and ultimately safer.
