Enhancing chlorpyrifos degradation and plant growth through biochar-assisted microbial remediation
Authors: M Kiran, R Sindhu, M Supreeth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe extensive use of chlorpyrifos (CPF) in agriculture has led to persistent soil contamination, posing serious risks to environmental and human health. Developing sustainable, in situ remediation strategies is therefore a priority. This study evaluated an integrated plant-microbe-biochar system for the remediation of CPF-contaminated soils. Indigenous CPF-degrading bacteria were isolated from agricultural soils and screened for degradation efficiency in mineral salts medium (MSM) and soil microcosms. The most efficient isolate, designated C78, was identified by 16S rRNA gene sequencing as Klebsiella pneumoniae. In MSM supplemented with 200 mg/L CPF, K. pneumoniae C78 degraded > 80% of the pesticide within 72 h. The strain also exhibited multiple plant growth-promoting traits, including indole-3-acetic acid production, phosphate solubilization, and ammonia production, and retained these functions in the presence of CPF. Soil microcosm and pot experiments were conducted using Ricinus communis L. grown in soils spiked with CPF (100 and 200 mg/kg), with and without biochar amendment and bacterial inoculation. Inoculation with K. pneumoniae C78 significantly accelerated CPF dissipation compared with non-inoculated controls, and the combined treatment with biochar further enhanced degradation, leading to near-complete CPF removal within 21 days. GC–MS/MS analysis showed no accumulation of common toxic intermediates such as 3,5,6–trichloro–2–pyridinol (TCP) or DETP/TMP. Biochar amendment improved soil conditions and supported higher microbial activity, while plant–microbe interactions in the rhizosphere promoted seed germination, seedling vigor, biomass production, chlorophyll content, and protein levels under CPF stress. Overall, the results demonstrate that integrating CPF-degrading K. pneumoniae C78 with R. communis L. and biochar amendment constitutes a promising, eco-compatible strategy for enhancing CPF degradation and restoring the productivity of contaminated agricultural soils.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10532-026-10328-4.Keywords: Chlorpyrifos, Bioremediation, Plant-microbe interactions, Biochar amendment, Soil contamination

Introduction

Organophosphorus pesticides, particularly chlorpyrifos (CPF; O,O–diethyl O–3,5,6–trichloro–2–pyridyl phosphorothioate), have been extensively applied in agriculture for decades to control a broad spectrum of insect pests (Bose et al., 2021; Lian et al. 2022). Although effective, their intensive and often indiscriminate use has led to widespread environmental contamination and ecological risks (El-Sesy et al., 2026). In soils, CPF typically exhibits a half-life of 60–120 days, depending on soil properties and environmental conditions (Wang et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2022), while residues have been detected in soils, sediments, surface and groundwater, crops, and human biological samples, including breast milk (Lara-Moreno et al., 2022; Rayu et al., 2017; Supreeth and Raju 2017). Its main degradation product, 3,5,6–trichloro–2–pyridinol (TCP), is often more persistent than the parent compound, with reported half-lives in soil of 65–350 days (Lian et al. 2022).

Concentrations of CPF in agricultural soils vary widely across regions and land-use types, with reported levels ranging from tens to several hundred µg/kg (Bhandari et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2020; Aioub et al. 2024). Such contamination levels are of concern given CPF’s well-documented toxicity to non-target organisms and humans. CPF inhibits acetylcholinesterase, a key enzyme in the nervous system, leading to neurotoxicity in both target pests and non-target species (Kiran et al. 2025). Epidemiological and experimental studies have associated CPF exposure with endocrine disruption, metabolic disorders, obesity, and certain cancers, including breast cancer (Pathak et al. 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Wołejko et al. 2022). In aquatic organisms, CPF and its metabolites induce oxidative stress, impair antioxidant defenses, and alter neurotransmitter metabolism, resulting in increased disease susceptibility, neurotoxicity, behavioural changes, and genotoxic effects such as DNA damage and nuclear abnormalities (Nguyen et al., 2023; Subaramaniyam et al. 2023; Sud et al. 2020; Dutta et al. 2024). These findings underscore the urgent need for effective and sustainable strategies to remove CPF from contaminated environments.

Several physicochemical methods, including ultrasonication, photocatalytic degradation, and adsorption onto engineered materials or biochar, have shown promise for CPF removal (Jahani et al. 2023; Nguyen et al., 2023). However, these approaches can be costly, energy-intensive, and technically demanding for large-scale field applications. In contrast, bioremediation using indigenous CPF-degrading microorganisms offers a cost-effective and environmentally friendly alternative (Yadav et al. 2025). Soil bacteria capable of degrading CPF can transform it into less toxic products, contributing to the detoxification of contaminated soils and the restoration of soil biological activity (Krishna Raj et al. 2025; Nayak et al., 2021). Plant–microbe interactions in the rhizosphere further enhance this process by stimulating microbial activity, increasing pollutant bioavailability, and improving plant growth under contaminated conditions (Wang et al., 2021).

Biochar, a carbon-rich material produced by the pyrolysis of biomass, has recently gained attention as a soil amendment for pesticide-contaminated soils (Lodariya et al. 2025). Biochar can sorb organic contaminants such as CPF, modify soil physicochemical properties, and provide microhabitats that promote microbial colonization and enzyme activity, while simultaneously reducing pesticide uptake by plants (Singh et al. 2024). Nonetheless, strong sorption may also reduce contaminant bioavailability for microbial degradation, and the net effect of biochar on CPF dissipation remains context-dependent. Furthermore, comprehensive studies examining the combined effects of plant–microbe systems and biochar amendment on CPF degradation, soil biochemical recovery, and plant performance in soil microcosms are still limited (Singh et al. 2024; Wang et al., 2021).

Therefore, this study aimed to (i) isolate and characterize indigenous CPF-degrading bacteria from agricultural soils, and (ii) evaluate the synergistic effects of microbial inoculation and biochar amendment on CPF degradation, soil biological properties, and plant growth under controlled and soil microcosm conditions. By integrating plant–microbe interactions with biochar-assisted remediation, we seek to develop an efficient, environmentally sustainable strategy for mitigating CPF contamination in agricultural soils.

Materials and methods

Soil sampling, chemicals and fortification of CPF

For isolation of Potential CPF-degrading bacterial strains, the soil samples were randomly collected from CPF-contaminated soil at a depth of 3–15 cm using sterile tools. Samples were collected from different sites of sugarcane, paddy fields, and from an area of different vegetable growing agricultural fields suspected of previous CPF application in the Mysuru region, Karnataka. The collected soil samples were transported to the laboratory in a sterile polythene bag, air dried, sieved through a 2 mm mesh, and stored at 4 °C until further use. Analytical grade CPF was procured from an authorized supplier. For experimental contamination, CPF was dissolved in 50 mL of deionized water and added to 1.5 kg of soil to obtain two treatment concentrations: 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg.

Experimental design and layout

The seed of R.communis.L (Castor seeds) was selected for pot experiments. For each treatment, 1.5 kg of soil spiked with CPF at concentrations of 100 mg and 200 mg/kg, were transferred into plastic pots (Jabeen et al., (Jabeen 2016)). Seeds were surface sterilized using 1% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for five minutes to prevent microbial contamination and improve germination. Twenty sterilized seeds of R.communis.L were sown per pot.

The pots were maintained under greenhouse conditions with a controlled temperature of 25 ± 1 °C, under a photoperiod of 16 h light and 8 h dark. Following seed germination, healthy seedlings were allowed to grow for 4 weeks (28 days). Plants were watered regularly using deionized water, and growth conditions were maintained throughout the study. After the experimental duration, the plants were carefully uprooted, with shoots cut above the soil surface and roots separated from the bulk soil. Control groups included uninoculated pots containing both CPF-amended and unamended soil.

The following morphological and physiological parameters were recorded: (a) seed germination percentage (%), (b) shoot length (cm), (c) root length (cm), (d) leaf length (cm), (e) shoot fresh weight (g), (f) root fresh weight (g), (g) shoot dry weight (g), (h) root dry weight (g) and (d)The vigor index. Experimental conditions and measurement variables are detailed in (Supplementary Table S1).

Isolation and screening of CPF-degrading bacteria

CPF-degrading bacteria were isolated from soil samples using an enrichment culture technique adapted from Akbar et al. (2014). Briefly, 10 g of soil was added to 90 mL of mineral salts medium (MSM; g/L: K₂HPO4 1.8, NH₄Cl 4.0, MgSO4·7H2O 0.5, FeSO4·7H2O 0.1, trace elements 10 mL/L) in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks. Chlorpyrifos (CPF) was added to a final concentration of 200 mg/L as the sole carbon source, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0. Flasks were incubated at 37 °C on a rotary shaker at 120 rpm. The enrichment was continued for four weeks, with weekly transfers (10% v/v) into fresh MSM containing 200 mg/L CPF. After the final enrichment step, serial dilutions were plated onto MSM agar supplemented with 200 mg/L CPF. Colonies able to grow on CPF-containing plates were picked and repeatedly streaked on fresh MSM–CPF plates to obtain purified isolates for further characterization.

Morphological and biochemical characterization of isolated CPF-degrading bacteria

Preliminary characterization of the CPF-degrading isolates was based on their cultural, morphological, and biochemical traits. Colony characteristics (colour, shape, elevation, margin, and size) were recorded for each isolate. Cell morphology and Gram reaction were examined by light microscopy following Gram staining (Rayu et al., 2017). Biochemical characterization was performed using standard procedures (Cao et al. 2018), including tests for indole and methyl red, catalase and oxidase activities, urease and citrate utilization, lactose utilization, and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) production.

Molecular identification of CPF-degrading bacterial isolates

The genomic DNA extraction was carried out according to the protocol described (Maleki et al., 2013) with slight modifications. A single colony of each isolate was cultured in 50 ml of LB broth for 18–24 h at 30 °C. Then the culture was centrifuged for 5 min at 5000 g, and the supernatant was discarded. Eventually, the genomic DNA of lysed bacterial cells was precipitated with 0.6 volume of isopropanol and purified using 70% ethanol. To amplify the fragment of the 16S rDNA gene, a set of primers (i.e., forward (F): 5' AAGCCCTGGAAACGGGGT-3' and reverse (R): 5'-CGTGTGCAGCCCAAGACA 3') was used. PCR amplification was performed using 0.4 µM primer, 40 ng chromosomal DNA, and the final volume was brought to 25 µl. The PCR amplification was performed using the thermal cycler (Cyber Lab Smart PCR, Bangalore) program as follows: 94 °C for 5 min as a primary denaturation step, 35 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 57 °C for 1 min, 72 °C for 105 s and final extension was 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were visualized using gel electrophoresis on 1% agarose (Qiagen, Germany) and compared with a 1 kb DNA ladder (Merck, Bangalore). PCR products of high-yield isolates were purified using a PCR purification kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The amplicon was sequenced at Bangalore Genei (Bangalore, India).

GC–MS/MS analysis of soil for detection of CPF-degradation metabolites

The QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) extraction protocol was employed for the analysis of soil samples. The soil samples were processed with the addition of acetonitrile following homogenization in distilled water. The resulting extract was subsequently added with MgSO4, NaCl, Na3C6H5O7.2H2O, and Na2HC6H5O7, vortexed and centrifuged at 5,000 rpm at 10 °C for 5 min. The supernatant was subjected to MgSO4 and PSA treatments and spinned at 5000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was reconstituted in ethyl acetate, filtered through a 0.2 µm PTFE membrane, and analyzed by GC–MS/MS (kiran. et al., (Kiran 2026)).

GC–MS/MS instrumental conditions

Gas chromatography parameters

The GC–MS/MS analysis was conducted using a Thermo Scientific TSQ 9610 Triple Quadrupole system equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injector and auxiliary heater were maintained at 250 °C and 230 °C, respectively. The GC oven was programmed as follows: initial temperature 60 °C for 2 min, ramped to 180 °C at 20 °C/min, then to 280 °C at 10 °C/min, and held for 10 min. Total run time was 28 min with a splitless injection volume of 1 µL (ABO EL-Maali & Yehia Wahman, 2015; El-Gawad 2016).

Mass spectrometry parameters

Mass spectrometric detection employed electron impact ionization at 70 eV. The ion source and quadrupole temperatures were set at 230 °C and 150 °C, respectively. Full scan mode was used over a mass range of 40–400 amu, along with selected ion monitoring (SIM) for quantitative detection of Chlorpyrifos at m/z = 197 and 314 (Abioye et al. 2023).

Plant growth-promoting (auxiliary) characteristics of K. pneumoniae C78

Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production

The ability of K. pneumoniae C78 to produce IAA was quantified colorimetrically using Salkowski’s reagent (Tang and Bonner, 1948; Singh et al. 2020). The strain was grown in LB broth supplemented with 50, 100, and 150 µg/mL tryptophan and incubated at 37 °C, 120 rpm. At 48, 72, and 96 h, culture supernatants were mixed with Salkowski’s reagent, incubated in the dark for 30 min, and absorbance was recorded at 530 nm. IAA concentrations were determined from a standard curve (Fig. 2).

Production of IAA by bacterial sp. C-78 at different time intervals (48, 72, and 96 h). a Tryptophan supplemented with CPF(100 mg) and b Tryptophan supplemented with CPF(200 mg)

To evaluate the effect of CPF on IAA production, LB broth was supplemented with 100 or 200 mg/L CPF in the presence of 100 µg/mL tryptophan (Supplementary Table. S2). The results indicated that C78 retained IAA production capability in the presence of CPF, although the magnitude of production varied with CPF concentration and incubation time.

Ammonia production

Ammonia production was evaluated by inoculating McCartney bottles containing 10 mL peptone water and incubating at 35 °C for 96 h (Akbar et al. 2014). The addition of Nessler’s reagent resulted in yellow colour development, confirming NH₃ production (Fig. 3), with colour intensity reflecting relative ammonia levels. The effect of CPF (100 and 200 mg/L) on ammonia production was assessed by supplementing peptone water with CPF. C78 continued to produce ammonia in the presence of CPF, indicating tolerance and retention of nitrogen-related plant growth-promoting activity (Figs. 4 and 5).

Phosphate solubilization

Phosphate solubilization by C78 was assessed on Pikovskaya agar (Chakraborty et al. 2009; Akbar and Sultan 2016). After one week of incubation at 35 °C, the formation of clear halos around colonies (Fig. 6) indicated phosphate solubilization. The solubilization index (SI) was calculated as the ratio of total diameter (colony + clearing zone) to colony diameter.

To study the influence of CPF, Pikovskaya agar plates were amended with 100 and 200 mg/L CPF. C78 maintained its phosphate-solubilizing activity under CPF stress, although halo size and SI were slightly affected at higher CPF concentrations.

Chlorophyll and protein content of Ricinus communis L.

Total chlorophyll content was determined in leaves of treated plants following the method of Khan et al. (2023). Fresh leaf tissue was extracted in 100% acetone, filtered, and absorbance was measured at 645 and 663 nm. Chl a = 12.7 A₆₆₃ – 2.7 A₆₄₅

Chl a = 12.7 A₆₆₃ – 2.7 A₆₄₅

Chl b = 22.9 A₆₄₅ – 4.7 A₆₆₃

Chl b = 22.9 A₆₄₅ – 4.7 A₆₆₃

Total chlorophyll = Chl a + Chl b

Total chlorophyll = Chl a + Chl b

Total soluble protein in roots, stems, and leaves was estimated by the Bradford method (Bradford 1976). Samples were mixed with Coomassie Brilliant Blue G-250 reagent, incubated at room temperature (5–10 min), and absorbance was recorded at 595 nm. Protein concentrations were calculated from a BSA standard curve and expressed as mg/mL. Evaluation of CPF degradation ability in soil under in-vitro conditions by selected isolated strains

The degradation ability of selected microbial isolates toward Chlorpyrifos (CPF) in soil under in-vitro conditions was evaluated using a controlled experimental setup. Initially, the isolates were cultured in Mineral Salt Medium (MSM) supplemented with 100 mg/L CPF as the sole carbon source until they reached an optical density (OD620) of approximately 0.6, indicating active growth in the exponential phase. For the soil degradation study, sterilized soil substrates were prepared to eliminate interference from native microorganisms. The soils were then artificially contaminated with CPF at concentrations of 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg to simulate different levels of pesticide pollution. Subsequently, the prepared soil samples were inoculated with the selected isolates using MSM culture (OD620 ≈ 0.6) at a rate of 10 mL per kilogram of soil. The inoculated samples were incubated at 30 °C for a period of 28 days under controlled laboratory conditions, as shown in (Supplementary Fig. S1c). At regular intervals of 7, 14, 21, and 28 days, soil samples were collected to determine the residual CPF concentration.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay was performed using the agar well diffusion method to assess the antibacterial effect of CPF. MSM agar plate were uniformly inoculated with standardized bacterial cultures, and wells were carefully punched into the agar. Varying concentrations of CPF were introduced into the wells under aseptic conditions. The plate were then incubated at 37 °C for 72 h (Fig. 5). Following incubation, the zones of inhibition around each well were measured in millimeters. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of CPF that produced a clear zone of inhibition, indicating effective suppression of bacterial growth. This method allows for preliminary evaluation of antimicrobial potential.

Plant–microbe interactions and the effects of biochar amendment on chlorpyrifos degradation

The enhanced remediation of CPF is driven by synergistic plant–microbe-biochar interactions in pot experiments with Ricinus communis L. (castor). Biochar (10–15 g/kg) improves soil physicochemical properties (porosity, aeration, water retention, nutrient availability), provides protective microhabitats for microbial colonization and biofilm development, and adsorbs CPF to reduce acute phytotoxicity while enabling gradual release for biodegradation (Zou et al. 2024). R. communis L. roots stimulate rhizosphere activity by releasing exudates (sugars, amino acids, organic acids) that serve as carbon and energy sources for microorganisms, promoting microbial growth, diversity, and enzymatic activity essential for CPF degradation. Klebsiella pneumoniae C78 (1.6 × 107 cells g−1), an indigenous CPF-degrading bacterium inoculated into CPF-spiked soil (100 and 200 mg/kg), utilizes CPF as a carbon source. The combined effects of plant–microbe interaction with biochar amendment improved microbial habitats, increased substrate availability, enhanced root-microbe interactions, and favorable soil conditions, creating a highly active rhizosphere environment that accelerates CPF degradation without phytotoxic symptoms, even under high pesticide loads.

Data management and statistical analysis

The raw data were entered and organized using Microsoft Excel. Statistical analysis were performed using the statistical package for the social sciences (SPSS) software. Differences among treatments were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test to determine significant differences between treatment means at a significance level of p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. DNA sequences were analyzed using the NCBI BLAST database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). Sequence similarity matrices and phylogenetic relationships were generated and visualized using the Tree Explorer module of MEGA X software. The obtained sequences were deposited in the NCBI GenBank database.

Results

Enrichment and isolation of CPF-degrading bacteria

From CPF-contaminated agricultural soils, a total of 56 bacterial isolates were obtained using enrichment culture in MSM supplemented with CPF as the sole carbon source. Potential CPF-degrading strains were initially screened based on their tolerance to CPF using inhibition-zone/MIC-type assays (Supplementary Fig. S1A) and subsequently evaluated for degradation in MSM. Among these, isolate C-78 consistently showed the highest CPF degradation and was selected for detailed characterization.

On solid media, C-78 formed circular, smooth, convex colonies with entire margins. Microscopic observations of Gram-stained preparations revealed Gram-negative, non-motile, rod-shaped cells occurring singly, with an average cell size of approximately 1–2 µm in length and 0.5–0.8 µm in width. Similar rod-shaped, pigmented CPF-degrading bacteria have been reported previously from contaminated soils (Asamba et al., 2022). The C-78 isolate was routinely maintained on LB medium and in glycerol stocks and could grow in LB supplemented with 100 mg/L CPF.

Morphological and biochemical characterization

Morphological characterization by Gram staining revealed that isolate C-78, subsequently identified as Klebsiella pneumoniae C-78, was a Gram-negative rod. Representative observations, including colony morphology on different agar media, Gram staining of K. pneumoniae C-78, and phosphate-solubilization activity indicated by the formation of a clear halo zone, results are presented in (Fig. 6). Biochemical characterization (Supplementary Fig. S3) demonstrated that the isolate was positive for catalase, urease, methyl red (MR), lactose fermentation, and citrate utilization, but negative for oxidase activity, indole production, and H₂S production (Table 2). This biochemical profile is consistent with the characteristics commonly reported for members of the genus Klebsiella. Following morphological and biochemical characterization, the chlorpyrifos (CPF)-degrading capability of strain C-78 was further evaluated and confirmed in both mineral salt medium (MSM) and soil microcosm systems using established protocols (El-Zayat et al. 2025).

Molecular identification of the selected strain

Molecular identification of strain C-78 was performed by amplifying and sequencing a fragment of the 16S rRNA gene using primers F: 5′-AAGCCCTGGAAACGGGGT-3′ and R: 5′-CGTGTGCAGCCCAAGACA-3′. PCR yielded a single amplicon of approximately 1 kb (Fig. 7). BLAST analysis of the 16S rRNA gene sequence against the NCBI GenBank database revealed a high level of similarity with Klebsiella pneumoniae sequences, allowing the isolate to be assigned as Klebsiella pneumoniae C78.

a 16S rDNA amplification showing a 1 kb DNA ladder. b The Constructed Phylogenetic tree of bacterial isolate C-78

Several genera have previously been reported to degrade CPF, including Acinetobacter sp. MemCl4 (Pailan et al., 2016), Hortaea sp. B15 (Hadibarata et al., 2023), Enterobacter sp. SWLC2 (Jha et al., 2022), and Massilia populi YC-XJ1 (Li et al. 2020). The identification of K. pneumoniae C78 as an efficient CPF degrader adds to the diversity of known CPF-degrading bacteria and supports the role of proteobacterial taxa in organophosphate biotransformation.

Mechanistic role of biochar in the plant–microbe system

Biochar addition to CPF-contaminated soil enhanced remediation by promoting synergistic interactions among Ricinus communis L., K. pneumoniae C78, and the soil matrix. Owing to its high surface area and functional groups, biochar adsorbed CPF, reducing its immediate bioavailability and phytotoxicity while simultaneously improving soil structure and providing microhabitats that supported microbial colonization and activity.

In pot experiments, soils spiked with CPF (100 and 200 mg/kg) and planted with R. communis were either inoculated with C78, amended with biochar, or subjected to combined inoculation and biochar amendment. The combined treatment (plant + biochar + C78) showed the greatest enhancement in plant growth and CPF dissipation. CPF degradation in soil by K. pneumoniae C78 with and without biochar

CPF dissipation in soil microcosms inoculated with K. pneumoniae C78, with and without biochar amendment, was monitored over 7, 14, 21, and 28 days by GC–MS/MS analysis (TIC overlays shown in Fig. 9; detailed chromatograms in supplementary Figs. S3–S29).

Detection of chlorpyrifos in soil samples at 0-28 days with the TIC overlaying: A Day-0 and 7, 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF-amended soil, B Day-14 and 21, 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF-amended soil, C Day-28 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF-amended soil D Day-7 and 14 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF along with C-78 amended soil E Day-21 and 28 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF along with C-78 amended soil (F) Day-7 and 14 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF along with C-78 + biochar amended soil (G) Day-21 and 28 100 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg CPF along with C-78 + biochar amended soil

In soils inoculated with C78, CPF concentrations decreased progressively over time, whereas control soils (without inoculum) showed much slower dissipation. Biochar amendment further accelerated CPF removal. A slight decrease in CPF peak area was observed after 7 days in C78-inoculated soil, with a more marked reduction in the biochar-amended treatment. By 14 days, CPF levels were substantially reduced in both treatments, with the greatest decline in the biochar + C78 system. A pronounced decrease was observed by 21 days in biochar-amended soils, indicating strong synergistic effects between biochar and the bacterial inoculum.

Compared with previously reported CPF degradation efficiencies e.g., 27.3% reduction over 3 days by Methylobacterium populi YC-XJ1 (Li et al. 2020) and 40–60% degradation over 7–10 days by Achromobacter xylosoxidans JCp4 and Ochrobactrum sp. FCp1 at 200 mg/kg CPF (Akbar and Sultan 2016) the combined application of K. pneumoniae C78 and biochar in this study resulted in near-complete CPF removal within 21 days under the tested conditions. Notably, GC–MS/MS analysis did not detect the accumulation of common CPF metabolites such as 3,5,6-trichloro-2-pyridinol (TCP) or DETP/TMP at the end of the incubation, suggesting further transformation and mineralization of these intermediates in the biochar–microbe–plant system.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that indigenous bacteria from CPF-contaminated agricultural soils can be effectively exploited for pesticide bioremediation. From 56 isolates obtained by enrichment culture, Klebsiella pneumoniae C78 was identified as a highly efficient CPF degrader in both liquid MSM and soil microcosms. The ability of C78 to utilize CPF as a carbon source, together with its tolerance to relatively high CPF concentrations, underscores the adaptive capacity of native soil microbiota under pesticide-stressed conditions.

The identification of C78 as K. pneumoniae is consistent with earlier reports of CPF-degrading strains within Proteobacteria, including Acinetobacter sp., Enterobacter sp., Massilia populi, and others (Pailan et al., 2016; Jha et al., 2022; Elshikh et al. 2022). The biochemical profile of C78 (Gram-negative, catalase-positive, oxidase-negative, urease- and citrate-positive, lactose-fermenting) aligns with typical characteristics of Klebsiella species, supporting its taxonomic assignment. The absence of detectable accumulation of TCP and other common CPF metabolites suggests that C78, especially in the presence of biochar and plant roots, may mediate further degradation steps beyond the initial hydrolysis of CPF.

The plant–microbe–biochar system evaluated with Ricinus communis further enhanced CPF dissipation. Biochar addition likely contributed via multiple mechanisms: (i) adsorption of CPF, which reduced acute phytotoxicity and allowed better plant establishment; (ii) improvement of soil structure, moisture retention, and habitat heterogeneity, supporting microbial survival and activity; and (iii) moderated release of CPF from sorption sites, making it gradually available to degraders rather than causing toxic spikes(Kamyab et al. 2025; Lü et al. 2024). Under these conditions, K. pneumoniae C78 showed higher degradation efficiency in biochar-amended soils than in soils without biochar, confirming a synergistic interaction.

The enhanced remediation performance observed in this study can be attributed to the synergistic interactions among biochar, plant roots, and microbial communities. Biochar improves soil physicochemical properties, including porosity, aeration, water retention, and nutrient availability, while its porous structure provides protective microhabitats for microbial colonization and biofilm development (Zou et al. 2024). Simultaneously, plant roots stimulate rhizosphere activity through the release of root exudates, such as sugars, amino acids, and organic acids, which serve as readily available carbon and energy sources for microorganisms (Chen & Liu 2024). These root-mediated processes promote microbial growth, diversity, and enzymatic activity, thereby enhancing the degradation of contaminants. In addition, biochar can adsorb pollutants, reducing their toxicity to microorganisms while facilitating their gradual release and subsequent biodegradation (Zhu et al. 2026). The combined effects of improved microbial habitats, increased substrate availability, enhanced root–microbe interactions, and favorable soil conditions create a highly active rhizosphere environment that accelerates microbial degradation processes and ultimately improves overall remediation efficiency (Zou et al. 2024).

In parallel, K. pneumoniae C78 exhibited several plant growth-promoting (PGP) traits, including IAA production, phosphate solubilization, and ammonia production, which were maintained under CPF exposure (Tejashwini et al. 2026). These PGP activities, together with reduced pesticide stress, translated into improved seed germination, seedling vigor, biomass accumulation, chlorophyll content, and protein levels in R. communis L. grown in CPF-contaminated soils. Thus, C78 functions not only as a CPF degrader but also as a rhizobacterium that supports plant growth under contaminant stress(Mansee et al. 2024).

Compared with earlier CPF bioremediation studies, the integration of an indigenous CPF-degrading bacterium, a tolerant plant species (R. communis L.), and biochar amendment in the present work yields higher degradation efficiencies and improved plant performance. This tripartite system offers a promising, environmentally friendly approach for the restoration of CPF-contaminated agricultural soils (Kurnia et al. 2024).

Nevertheless, further work is required to (i) elucidate the complete metabolic pathway and key enzymes involved in CPF and TCP degradation by K. pneumoniae C78, (ii) assess the stability and effectiveness of inoculation under field conditions with variable environmental factors, and (iii) evaluate potential ecological and biosafety considerations associated with large-scale application of K. pneumoniae strains. Despite these considerations, the findings provide a strong experimental basis for developing biochar-assisted, plant–microbe-based strategies for mitigating organophosphate pesticide contamination in agroecosystems.

Although the isolate demonstrated promising functional characteristics, its identification as Klebsiella pneumoniae necessitates careful biosafety consideration. K. pneumoniae is an opportunistic pathogen associated with a range of human infections and is known to harbor virulence and antibiotic resistance determinants. Therefore, any potential environmental or biotechnological application of this strain should be approached with caution. Comprehensive characterization of its pathogenicity, virulence genes, and antimicrobial resistance profile is essential before considering field-scale applications. Furthermore, the possible ecological impacts on indigenous microbial communities and the risk of dissemination in the environment should be thoroughly evaluated. Consequently, the present study is limited to laboratory-scale assessment, and further biosafety and risk assessment studies are required before practical implementation can be considered.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that indigenous soil bacteria, particularly Klebsiella pneumoniae C78, can effectively biodegrade chlorpyrifos (CPF) in contaminated agricultural soils. Among the isolates obtained, K. pneumoniae C78 showed the highest degradation efficiency in both liquid culture and soil microcosms. When combined with biochar amendment and Ricinus communis L. (castor bean), CPF dissipation was markedly accelerated, leading to near-complete removal of CPF (100–200 mg/kg) within 21 days and improved plant growth, as confirmed by GC–MS/MS analysis. The absence of detectable levels of common toxic intermediates such as TCP under the tested conditions suggests efficient transformation and mineralization of CPF in the biochar-plant-microbe system. Overall, integrating native CPF-degrading bacteria with biochar amendments and phytoremediation using R. communis L. constitutes a promising strategy for restoring pesticide-contaminated soils. Future work should focus on field-scale validation, elucidation of degradation pathways and key enzymes, and assessment of long-term ecological safety to support practical implementation in agricultural settings.
