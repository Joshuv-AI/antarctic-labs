Meteorological trend analysis and atmospheric coupling using multi model assessment of urban climate dynamics for weather forecasting
Authors: Abdulla Al Kafy, Hamad Ahmed Altuwaijri, Tekalign Ketema Bahiru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding urban meteorological patterns and their coupling with larger atmospheric systems is crucial for improving weather forecasting and climate modeling. This study presents a comprehensive meteorological trend analysis of Kuwait City from 1982 to 2021, employing multiple analytical models to examine atmospheric parameters and their interconnections. Our analysis reveals significant trends in key meteorological variables: annual rainfall (-1.869 mm/year, Z = -2.55), relative humidity (-0.09%/year, Z = -2.06), maximum temperature (+ 0.047 °C/year, Z = + 2.23), and minimum temperature (+ 0.068 °C/year, Z = + 2.37). Seasonal analysis shows that rainfall decline is most pronounced during winter (-0.897 mm/year) and spring (-0.504 mm/year), which represent the main precipitation seasons in Kuwait. Temperature increases observed across most months and seasons, indicating a persistent warming trend. The Innovative Trend Analysis method revealed additional distributional changes in several meteorological variables that complement the results of the Mann-Kendall test and linear regression, providing insights into long term variability of key meteorological parameters that traditional methods missed. These findings indicate a shift in local atmospheric dynamics, with potential implications for urban heat island effects and regional weather patterns. Our multi-model approach offers an advanced methodology for assessing complex meteorological trends in urban environments, contributing to improved weather forecasting capabilities and understanding of urban-atmosphere interactions in arid regions.

Introduction

Climate change is influencing the intensity, frequency, and spatial distribution of weather and climate extremes worldwide. Rising temperatures, changing precipitation patterns, and increasing climate variability have been widely reported across many regions worldwide1,2. These changes have important consequences for human societies and natural systems. Climate-related hazards such as heatwaves, droughts, floods, and severe storms can damage infrastructure, disrupt economic activities, threaten water and food security, and cause substantial social and environmental losses3,4. In addition to direct socioeconomic impacts, climate change can also affect ecological processes by altering species distribution, migration patterns, and ecosystem stability. However, the magnitude and nature of climate change impacts vary significantly across regions due to differences in geography, climate regimes and local environmental conditions5,6. Understanding regional and local climate trends is therefore critical for assessing environmental risks and supporting effective resource management and sustainable development strategies.

Arid and semi-arid regions are particularly sensitive to climate variability and change. The Arab region, including the Arabian Peninsula, is characterized by extremely low and highly variable precipitation, high evaporation rates, and frequent temperature extremes. These climatic conditions create a fragile environmental balance in which even small changes in meteorological variables can have significant implications for water resources, agriculture, and ecosystem stability. Recent studies indicate that several parts of the Middle East and North Africa have experienced noticeable warming and changes in precipitation patterns over the past decades5,7. Increasing temperatures have intensified heat stress and water demand, while reduced rainfall and prolonged dry periods have increased the risk of drought and desertification. These climatic changes may exacerbate environmental pressures in the region, including land degradation, water scarcity, and reduced agricultural productivity8. Moreover, extreme weather events can damage infrastructure, disrupt urban systems, and negatively affect public health and livelihoods.

Urban areas in arid environments are particularly vulnerable to climatic variability because of their high population densities, expanding infrastructure, and limited water resources. Many cities in the Arab region have experienced rapid urbanization during the past few decades, often accompanied by increased environmental stress and resource demand. Urban development can also influence local climatic conditions through changes in land cover, surface properties, andenergy balance. These processes may amplify temperature extremes, alter local humidity conditions, and modify rainfall patterns9. In addition, rapid economic development and land-use change have been identified as factors that may intensify environmental pressures and increase vulnerability to climate change impacts in several Arab countries10. As a result, monitoring and understanding long-term climatic variability in urban environments has become increasingly important for sustainable urban planning, infrastructure development, and environmental management.

Assessing long-term trends in meteorological variables is a key step in understanding regional climate variability and detecting signals of climate change. Trend analysis of climatic variables such as precipitation, temperature, and humidity provides valuable information about long-term environmental changes and their potential impacts on water resources, agriculture, ecosystems, and urban systems. Numerous statistical techniques have been developed to analyze climatic trends in time series data. These include Spearman’s rho test, the Mann–Kendall test (MKT), the seasonal Kendall test, linear regression models, and the Theil–Sen estimator11–15. Among these methods, the MKT has become one of the most widely used techniques for detecting monotonic trends in hydrological and meteorological data due to its nonparametric nature and robustness to nonnormal distributions16–19.

Despite their widespread use, traditional statistical approaches have certain limitations when applied to complex environmental datasets. Conventional trend detection methods typically identify only the overall direction of change in a time series and may fail to reveal hidden patterns within different value ranges of the data3. These methods may also have limited ability to detect non-linear trends, structural changes, or variations occurring in different portions of the dataset20,21. In recent years, Innovative Trend Analysis (ITA) has been proposed as an alternative approach that provides additional insights into data behavior by examining trends within different data clusters and visualizing them graphically22. This technique allows researchers to identify trends in low, medium, and high data values and to detect non-linear behavior that may not be captured by conventional statistical methods3,15,23. Combining ITA with traditional approaches, such as MKT and linear regression, may therefore provide a more comprehensive assessment of climatic trends.

Several studies have investigated climatic variability and trends in different parts of the Arab region. For example, Tarawneh and Chowdhury24 analyzed temperature and rainfall trends in Saudi Arabia using the MKT and linear regression techniques. Merabtene et al.25 examined rainfall variability in Sharjah, United Arab Emirates, while Chowdhury et al.26 assessed precipitation trends in the eastern region of the UAE. Climate change impacts have also been investigated in other parts of the Middle East, including Syria27 and Iraq9. Additional studies have focused on different regions of Saudi Arabia, highlighting significant climatic variability and warming trends across the Arabian Peninsula5,7,28–32. While these studies provide important insights into regional climate variability, most of them rely primarily on traditional statistical methods and often focus on specific climatic variables or limited spatial regions.

Despite growing research on climate trends across the Arabian Peninsula, relatively few studies have examined the long-term variability of multiple meteorological variables in Kuwait. In particular, comprehensive analyses of rainfall, dry days, humidity, and temperature trends using integrative statistical approaches remain limited. Moreover, previous studies in the region have often relied on a single analytical method, which may not fully capture the complex variations in climatic time series. Addressing these gaps is important for improving the understanding of climate variability in Kuwait and for supporting environmental management and climate adaptation strategies in the region.

To address these research gaps, this study investigates the long-term variability and trends in key meteorological variables in Kuwait City from 1982 to 2021. Specifically, the study analyzes rainfall, dry days, relative humidity, and both maximum and minimum air temperatures across daily, monthly, seasonal, and annual temporal scales. The analysis integrates three complementary trend detection approaches, including ITA, MKT, and Linear Regression Method (LRM) methods. By combining these techniques, the study aims to provide a robust assessment of climatic variability and to identify potential changes in hydroclimatic conditions over the past four decades. The novelty of this study is that it presents one of the first long-term multi-parameter meteorological trend analyses for Kuwait City. The results contribute to a better understanding of long-term climate variability in Kuwait City and provide updated evidence of regional climate change trends in the Arabian Gulf region. These findings may support future environmental planning, water resource management, and climate adaptation strategies in arid urban environments.

Materials and methods

Study area

Kuwait City is the capital and largest city of Kuwait, located on the southern coast of Kuwait Bay. The population of Kuwait City is estimated at around 2.5 million, making it one of the most populous cities in the country (Fig. 1). Kuwait City is a global city, known for its significant role in the oil and finance industries33,34. The city serves as the headquarters of many international companies and organizations, and it is also a major center for trade and commerce in the Middle East. The city is situated in a desert region, and the geography is characterized by flat, sandy terrain. The city is located on the coast of the Persian Gulf, and it is surrounded by the Kuwaiti desert. There are many modern skyscrapers and high-rise buildings, as well as traditional souks and markets. The city also has many green spaces and parks, and it is surrounded by desert landscapes. However, due to the hot and dry weather, the city does not have many green spaces and the environment is not very conducive for outdoor activities. The climate in Kuwait City is arid and hot, with high temperatures and low rainfall. Summer temperatures can reach up to 50 °C, and the city experiences very minimal rainfall (approximately 100 mm) throughout the year. The city is also affected by strong winds and dust storms, which can make the weather even more uncomfortable35.

Data collection

To fulfill the objective of this study, we collected the daily rainfall, Tmax, Tmin, and humidity data sets during 1982–2021 from the NASA Langley Research Center (LaRC) for Kuwait City. LaRC provides meteorological data from 1982 and ensures data quality. Kuwait City’s four seasons were considered in the seasonality analysis: summer (June-August), autumn (September-November), winter (December–February), and spring (March–May). Before analyzing the data, homogenization and quality control of the data were checked.

The daily meteorological dataset for Kuwait City contains 14,610 observations with no missing values across all analyzed variables, including Tmax, Tmin, Rainfall, and Humidity, indicating a complete and continuous record for the study period. Descriptive statistics show that the mean daily Tmax averaged 30.63 °C (range 9.69–48.49 °C) and Tmin averaged 21.58 °C (range 2.92–38.17 °C), which are physically consistent with the climatic conditions of Kuwait’s arid environment. Rainfall exhibited a mean daily value of 0.18 mm, with a maximum of 45.32 mm, reflecting the highly intermittent precipitation regime typical of desert climates. Relative humidity averaged 45.87%, varying between 18.38% and 95.81%, indicating substantial seasonal variability while remaining within physically realistic limits. Outlier detection using the interquartile range (IQR) method identified 2,308 rainfall outliers, while the Z-score method detected 180 rainfall outliers and two humidity outliers. However, these rainfall outliers were not removed because precipitation in arid environmentsoften occurs as rare but intense storm events, which naturally appear as statistical outliers due to the predominance of zero-rainfall days. Eliminating such observations would remove physically meaningful extreme precipitation events and potentially bias estimates of rainfall variability and long-term climatic trends. No statistical outliers were detected for Tmax and Tmin using either the IQR or Z-score methods, indicating stable and physically consistent temperature observations throughout the dataset. For humidity, only two outliers were detected by the Z-score method. However, these values remained within the valid physical range of 0–100% and were therefore retained. The absence of significant outliers in temperature and humidity variables reflects the relatively continuous nature of these atmospheric parameters compared with precipitation, which is highly intermittent in arid environments. Logical consistency checks further confirmed that Tmax was always greater than or equal to Tmin, rainfall values were non-negative, and humidity remained within the valid range of 0–100%, providing strong evidence of the overall reliability and internal consistency of the dataset. Therefore, all observations were retained for subsequent analysis to preserve the integrity of the climatic signal.

Innovative trend analysis

Innovative trend analysis (ITA) is a statistical method that is used to identify and analyze trends in time series data, specifically in climatic parameters such as precipitation, temperature, or wind speed. This method was introduced by Sen36. It is based on the decomposition of the time series data into several components, such as trend, seasonal, and residual components and it is used to identify both linear and non-linear trends, dominant frequency, and abrupt changes in the data37.

The collected meteorological data were split into two halves (1982–2001 and 2002–2021) from the beginning time series through to the finish time series, and then each sub-series was individually sorted in ascending order. Based on the two-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, the first sub-series (1982–2001) was plotted along the horizontal X-axis, and the second sub-series (2002–2021) was drawn along the vertical Y-axis (Fig. 2). If the data points in the scatter plot were collected on the 1:1 (45o) line, it indicates data did not exhibit any discernible trend (data with no trend). Data above the 1:1 line indicates an increasing trend and below the line indicates decreasing trend. Moreover, if the trend line doesn’t seem to be parallel with the 1:1 line then data are classified into three groups ‘low’, ‘medium’, and ‘high’. This study divided the values into three groups following Alashan38 and Das et al.39. Mann–Kendall test analysis

The Mann–Kendall test (MKT) is a non-parametric statistical method used to detect trends in time series data, such as precipitation, and temperature data40,41. The MKT is widely used in hydrology, meteorology, and other fields to analyze trends in climate variables over time. The MKT is based on the Kendall rank correlation coefficient, which measures the association between two variables. It compares the rank of the data values at different time points to determine whether there is a consistent upward or downward trend. To perform the MKT, the data is first ranked according to their magnitude. The MKT statistic, S, is calculated as the difference between the number of positive rank differences and the number of negative rank differences. If S is positive, it indicates an upward trend, if S is negative, it indicates a downward trend, and if S is zero, it indicates no trend. The MKT also provides a p–value, which is used to determine the significance of the trend. A low p–value (typically less than 0.05) indicates that the trend is statistically significant, while a high p–value indicates that the trend is not statistically significant. The MKT assumes that the data is independent and does not account for the potential effects of autocorrelation, seasonality, or other temporal dependencies. Therefore, the MKT is commonly used in conjunction with other statistical methods, such as time series analysis, to assess the robustness of the trend and to identify potential underlying causes or patterns15,39,42.

Linear regression model

Linear regression is a statistical method that is used to model the relationship between a dependent variable and one or more independent variables. In the context of climate research, linear regression can be used to assess the linear trends of climatic parameters such as precipitation, temperature, or wind speed over time. The linear regression model assumes that there is a linear relationship between the independent variable (time) and the dependent variable (climatic parameter). The model is represented by a straight line, which is described by an equation of the form:

Results

Variability of meteorological factors

Rainfall and dry days

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of monthly rainfall in Kuwait City for the periods 1982–2001, 2002–2021, and the overall period 1982–2021. The results show that precipitation in Kuwait City is highly seasonal and concentrated in the winter months. During the entire study period (1982–2021), mean monthly rainfall during the main rainy season (November to March) ranged from 9.84 mm in March to 17.00 mm in January. In contrast, rainfall during the summer months was extremely low, with mean values ranging from 0.06 mm in September to 0.40 mm in August, indicating nearly dry conditions during this period.

A comparison of the two subperiods reveals a noticeable decline in rainfall during the more recent decades. The average monthly rainfall during 1982–2001 ranged between 0.04 mm and 21.79 mm, whereas during 2002–2021 it ranged between0.01 mm and 10.25 mm. This decline is also evident in the annual totals, which decreased from 89.55 mm during 1982–2001 to 41.79 mm during 2002–2021. Seasonal totals show a similar pattern. Winter rainfall decreased from 49.87 mm in the earlier period to 23.49 mm in the later period, while spring and autumn rainfall also declined. These results indicate a substantial reduction in precipitation in Kuwait City during the last two decades. The maximum rainfall values further highlight the high variability of precipitation events. For example, the maximum monthly rainfall reached 55.97 mm in January during 1982–2001 and 96.72 mm in February during 2002–2021. Such large values compared with the monthly means indicate the occurrence of occasional intense rainfall events. The minimum rainfall values were close to zero in most months, especially during the summer season, confirming the arid climate of the region.

The skewness statistics in Table 1 indicate that rainfall distributions are positively skewed for all months. Positive skewness suggests that most observations correspond to low amounts of rainfall, while a few extreme events produce high precipitation values. For the entire period (1982–2021), skewness ranged from 0.80 in January to 6.07 in September. The highest skewness values occurred during the summer and early autumn months, reflecting the rarity of rainfall and the presence of occasional extreme events. In general, skewness values increased during the later period (2002–2021) for several winter and spring months, indicating greater variability in rainfall distribution during recent decades. Table 2 summarizes the variability of monthly dry days in Kuwait City for the same periods. On average, the city experienced approximately 307 dry days per year during 1982–2021. The number of dry days increased from an average of 301 days during 1982–2001 to 313 days during 2002–2021, suggesting a decline in the frequency of rainfall events in the more recent period. Correspondingly, the average number of rainy days decreased from about 64 days per year during 1982–2001 to 52 days during 2002–2021, with an overall average of 58 rainy days for the entire period.

Maximum temperature

Table 3 represents the descriptive statistics of Tmax in Kuwait City during 1982–2001, 2002–2021, and 1982–2021. The results show a clear seasonal variation in Tmax. During 1982–2001, mean monthly Tmax ranged from 17.30 °C in January to 41.39 °C in July. In the later period (2002–2021), mean monthly Tmax ranged from 17.84 °C in January to 42.24 °C in August, indicating generally higher temperatures in recent decades. A comparison of the two periods reveals a consistent increase in Tmax across most months. For example, the mean Tmax in August increased from 41.25 °C during 1982–2001 to 42.24 °C during 2002–2021, while July Tmax increased from 41.39 °C to 42.11 °C. The annual mean Tmax also increased from 30.20 °C to 31.05 °C between the two periods. For the entire study period (1982–2021), the mean daily Tmax was 30.63 °C, with an observed maximum of 48.49 °C. Seasonally, the highest temperatures occurred during summer, with a mean seasonal Tmax of 41.18 °C, whereas winter temperatures were considerably lower, with mean Tmax values ranging between 16.63 °C and 21.34 °C. This pattern reflects the strong seasonal temperature contrast typical of the arid climate of Kuwait. The skewness values of Tmax were generally small, indicating that Tmax distributions were approximately symmetric for most months. Slight positive skewness observed in some months suggests occasional extreme high temperature events, while small negative skewness in other months indicates minor deviations toward lower temperature extremes. This decline is consistent with the negative ITA slope of −2.388 and the negative linear slope of −1.7491 mm/year. Therefore, the annual rainfall series shows a clear downward tendency. Seasonal rainfall also showed mostly negative trends. Autumn, winter, and spring all had negative Mann Kendall Z values of −1.32, −2.89, and − 2.79, respectively. Among these, winter and spring showed the strongest declines. Sen’s slope values further indicate decreasing seasonal rainfall of −0.136 mm/year in autumn, −0.897 mm/year in winter, and − 0.504 mm/year in spring. In contrast, summer rainfall showed a very weak increasing tendency with Z = 0.43, but Sen’s slope was 0.000 mm/year, indicating that the increase was negligible in practical terms. This is expected because summer rainfall in Kuwait City is extremely small.

At the monthly scale, the clearest decreasing trends were observed in January, February, and March, with Mann Kendall Z values of −2.29, −2.12, and − 3.60, respectively. Sen’s slope values for these months were − 0.456 mm/year in January, −0.191 mm/year in February, and − 0.365 mm/year in March. March showed the strongest monthly decline. Negative but weaker trends were also observed in April, May, September, October, and December. In November, the MKT indicated no monotonic trend with Z = 0.00, and Sen’s slope was also 0.000 mm/year, although the ITA and linear slope were negative. Slight positive Mann Kendall Z values were found in June and July, but Sen’s slope remained 0.000 mm/year in both months, which means that rainfall in these months did not show any meaningful long-term increase. August also had Sen’s slope equal to 0.000 mm/year, and its ITA and linear slopes were negative. These outcomes suggest that rainfall decline was concentrated mainly in the wetter months of the year, especially from January to March.

The ITA results in Table 8; Fig. 4 support the overall pattern of declining rainfall. Annual rainfall points lie below the 1:1 line, confirming a negative annual trend. Seasonal rainfall in autumn, winter, and spring also plots mainly below the 1:1 line, indicating a decline in rainfall in these seasons. At the monthly level, January, February, March, and December show clear negative ITA slopes, with March having the steepest monthly decline at −0.542. June and July show very small positive ITA slopes of 0.007 and 0.005, respectively, which indicates only a weak upward tendency. These increases are very small and should not be over interpreted because rainfall in these months is minimal. The linear regression slopes are broadly consistent with the MK test and ITA for the annual series and for most seasons. However, the agreement is not complete at the monthly scale. For example, September has a slightly positive Mann Kendall Z value but a negative ITA slope and a small positive linear slope. October and November also show some differences among methods. Therefore, it is more accurate to state that the three methods show broad agreement in the overall pattern, especially for the annual and major seasonal trends, rather than complete agreement in every month.

Trends in Tmax

The long-term behavior of Tmax in Kuwait City shows a clear warming signal. As indicated in Table 3, the mean daily Tmax increased from 30.20 °C during 1982 to 2001 to 31.05 °C during 2002 to 2021. Table 9 shows that this warming is evident at the monthly, seasonal, and annual scales, with most Sen’s slopes being positive. On the monthly scale, the strongest rise in Tmax was observed in March, where MKT gave Z = 3.79 and Sen’s slope was 0.085 °C/year. This was the steepest monthly increase in the series. Other pronounced positive trends were found in October (0.062 °C/year), February (0.060 °C/year), August (0.052 °C/year), May (0.051 °C/year), and June (0.047 °C/year). Positive but weaker changes were observed in July (0.032 °C/year) and in September (0.043 °C/year). In contrast, the trends in January, April, and December were positive but weak, while November showed almost no meaningful change. The table shows Z = −0.22 for November, but both Sen’s slope and the linear slope are slightly positive. This indicates that November is essentially stable rather than clearly warming or cooling.

The seasonal trend is also strong. Summer, autumn, winter, and spring all show positive Sen’s slopes of 0.042 °C/year, 0.032 °C/year, 0.058 °C/year, and 0.045 °C/year, respectively. Winter exhibits the largest seasonal rate, which indicates that the cooler part of the year has warmed substantially. At the annual scale, Sen’s slope is 0.047 °C/year, and the linear slope is 0.0470 °C/year, showing very close agreement between the two methods. This corresponds well with the increase in mean daily Tmax reported in Table 3. The ITA plots in Fig. 5 reveal an important detail not visible in the MKT alone. In almost all months and seasons, most data points lie above the 1:1 line, confirming an overall upward trend in Tmax. The rise is especially clear in March, summer, spring, and the annual series, where the separation from the 1:1 line is larger. The March panel is especially important because both the table and the plot identify March as the month with the strongest warming. In contrast, April and November show weaker displacement from the 1:1 line, which agrees with their modest slopes. December is more mixed in the ITA plot, with weaker change in the higher values, which explains why the warming signal in December is not strong despite a small positive Sen’s slope.

Trends in Tmin

The Tmin series reveals a broad warming trend, but its seasonal and monthly patterns are not identical to those of Tmax. This is important because rising Tmin reflects stronger nighttime warming, which is often associated with increasing heat stress and reduced nocturnal cooling. Table 4 already shows that the mean daily Tmin increased from 21.23 °C during 1982 to 2001 to 21.94 °C during 2002 to 2021. Table 10 confirms that this increase is present in most months and seasons. The strongest monthly increase in Tmin occurred in August, where Sen’s slope reached 0.066 °C/year and the linear slope was 0.0699 °C/year. Strong warming was also evident in June with 0.065 °C/year, March with 0.063 °C/year, September with 0.059 °C/year, July with 0.058 °C/year, and February with 0.046 °C/year. These results show that the rise in Tmin is particularly notable from late winter through the warm season. The monthly trends are weaker in January, where Sen’s slope is only 0.005 °C/year, and in November and December, where Sen’s slopes are − 0.007 °C/year and − 0.006 °C/year, respectively. Thus, late autumn and early winter do not show the same warming signal as the rest of the year. At the seasonal scale, the highest increase was observed in winter, with Sen’s slope of 0.050 °C/year, followed by spring with 0.041 °C/year, summer with 0.035 °C/year, and autumn with only 0.014 °C/year. Annual Tmin also increased, with Sen’s slope of 0.068 °C/year and a linear slope of 0.0504 °C/year. The annual Sen’s slope is higher than the seasonal values, which suggests that the cumulative warming in nighttime temperatures is substantial when the full year is considered.

Humidity trends

Relative humidity shows a different climatic behavior from temperature. While Tmax and Tmin generally increased, humidity tended to decline over the same period. This contrast is important in an arid environment because lower humidity, combined with higher temperature, changes surface dryness and atmospheric demand. Table 5 already indicates that the mean daily humidity decreased from 46.87% during 1982 to 2001 to 44.88% during 2002 to 2021. Table 11 shows that this decline is visible in most months, seasons, and the annual series. The annual humidity trend is negative, with Z = −2.06, Sen’s slope of −0.090%/year, and a linear slope of −0.0909%/year. This close agreement between Sen’s slope and the linear regression supports the conclusion that Kuwait City has become gradually drier in relative humidity from 1982 to 2021. On the monthly scale, the highest decline was observed in March, with a rate of −0.237%/year (linear slope was − 0.2353%/year). Strong decreases were also found in October (−0.204%/year), spring (−0.131%/year), autumn (−0.130%/year), February (−0.119%/year), April (−0.114%/year), January (−0.099%/year), and September (−0.103%/year). In contrast, June shows only a weak declining rate of −0.019%/year. July is the only month with a positive Mann-Kendall Z value and a low positive Sen’s slope of 0.027%/year, but the ITA slope is −0.001, and the linear slope is 0.0316%/year. This means that July is not a clear increasing month. It is better interpreted as nearly stable or weakly mixed, depending on the method used. The seasonal pattern is also consistent with drying. Winter, spring, summer, and autumn all have negative Sen’s slopes of −0.079, −0.131, −0.029, and − 0.130%/year, respectively. The strongest seasonal decreases occur in spring and autumn, while the weakest occurs in summer. This seasonal pattern agrees with Table 5, which showed that humidity was already lowest in the summer months and higher in winter.

ITA plots in Fig. 7 show that most monthly and seasonal humidity points lie below the 1:1 line, which supports the overall drying tendency. November is more mixed, with some values close to the 1:1 line, which explains why the Mann Kendall result for November is weak. However, the overall direction in November is still slightly negative in Table 11 because both Sen’s slope and the linear slope are negative. Therefore, November should not be described as showing an opposite result. It is better described as a weak and less coherent decline compared with other months. From a meteorological perspective, the declining humidity trend, together with increasing Tmax and Tmin, suggests a warmer and generally drier atmospheric environment in Kuwait City over the study period. This does not mean improved comfort. Lower relative humidity may reduce moist discomfort in some situations, but in an arid climate it also reflects stronger dryness of the air and greater evaporative demand. In practical terms, the combined pattern of higher temperature and lower humidity points to increasing thermal and hydroclimatic stress rather than a simple improvement in environmental conditions.

Discussion

This study evaluated long term variability and trends in rainfall, dry days, humidity, and air temperature in Kuwait City during 1982 to 2021 using three complementary trend detection approaches. The results consistently indicate a shift toward warmer and drier atmospheric conditions. Annual rainfall declined significantly, dry days increased, both maximum and minimum temperatures rose, and relative humidity generally decreased. These trends are consistent with broader climatic changes reported across the Arabian Peninsula and surrounding regions.

The rainfall analysis showed a statistically significant decline at the annual scale, with Sen’s slope indicating a decrease of about 1.87 mm/year. The strongest monthly decline occurred in March, while the largest seasonal decrease was observed during winter, which represents the primary rainfall season in Kuwait. Because winter precipitation accounts for most of the region’s annual rainfall, reductions during this season strongly influence the regional water balance. The decrease in rainfall is also reflected in the increasing number of dry days and longer consecutive dry periods identified in the results. The declining rainfall trend observed in Kuwait City is consistent with findings reported in several parts of the Middle East. Patlakas et al.7 reported decreasing precipitation across parts of the eastern Mediterranean and Middle East, while Tarawneh and Chowdhury24 found declining rainfall trends in Jordan. Similar reductions were documented in Iran by Some’e et al42. and in Sharjah by Merabtene25. Alsubih et al43. also reported increasing drought conditions in parts of Saudi Arabia associated with declining rainfall. These studies collectively indicate that the rainfall reduction observed in Kuwait City reflects a regional hydroclimatic trend rather than an isolated local anomaly. The physical drivers of declining rainfall in the region are likely related to large scale atmospheric circulation changes. Climate studies have shown that warming temperatures can alter the position and intensity of subtropical high-pressure systems and Mediterranean storm tracks that influence precipitation across the Middle East. For example, Seager et al44. and Zappa et al45. reported that poleward expansion of the Hadley circulation and shifts in midlatitude storm tracks can reduce precipitation in subtropical dry zones. Similarly, Almazroui45 found that precipitation variability across the Arabian Peninsula is strongly influenced by changes in atmospheric circulation patterns associated with global warming.

The increase in dry days and consecutive dry days observed in Kuwait City further supports the rainfall findings. The results indicate that rainfall events are becoming less frequent even when the total precipitation change is moderate. In arid climates such as Kuwait, small reductions in rainfall can significantly increase the number of dry days because precipitation events are already rare. This pattern has also been observed in other arid regions of the Middle East. Hasanean and Almazroui29 reported an increase in drought frequency across the Arabian Peninsula due to combined reductions in rainfall and increases in temperature. Similar trends in dry days and drought indices have been reported across North Africa and West Asia47,48. These findings suggest that the increase in dry conditions observed in Kuwait City is consistent with broader hydroclimatic trends across the region. Temperature trends in Kuwait City show a clear signal of warming. Both Tmax and Tmin increased during the study period, with annual rates of approximately 0.047 °C/year for Tmax and 0.068 °C/year for Tmin. The stronger increase in Tmin suggests that nighttime temperatures are rising more rapidly than daytime temperatures. This pattern of asymmetric warming has been widely reported in global climate studies and is often linked to greenhouse forcing and changes in atmospheric moisture and cloud cover49,50. The warming rates estimated in this study are comparable to those observed in other cities across the Arabian Gulf region. Studies have reported temperature increases of approximately 0.03–0.06 °C/year in cities such as Doha, Abu Dhabi, and Riyadh, indicating that Kuwait City is experiencing warming consistent with regional climate trends46,49,51. AlDousari et al.10 reported that land surface temperature in Kuwait increased by about 5 °C between 1991 and 2021, highlighting the rapid warming observed in the region. The seasonal distribution of warming observed in this study is also consistent with regional climate behavior. The strongest warming occurred during late winter and spring, particularly in March, which corresponds with transitional atmospheric circulation patterns in the region. Transitional seasons often exhibit stronger warming signals because they are sensitive to shifts in atmospheric circulation and land surface energy balance.

Relative humidity showed a decreasing trend across most months and seasons, with an annual decline of about 0.09%/year. The strongest reductions occurred during spring and autumn months. Lower humidity levels in arid climates are commonly associated with rising air temperature and decreasing precipitation, both of which reduce the availability of atmospheric moisture. Similar declines in humidity have been documented in neighboring regions. Bleej52 reported decreasing humidity trends in northern Iraq, while regional climate analyses suggest that increasing temperature across the Middle East contributes to greater atmospheric dryness. When temperatures rise and precipitation decreases, the atmosphere tends to exhibit higher evaporative demand and lower relative humidity. The combined pattern of increasing temperature and decreasing humidity suggests that Kuwait City is experiencing a progressive intensification of arid conditions. Previous studies have shown that such climatic changes can exacerbate drought conditions in arid environments47. DeNicola et al.53 and Chowdhury and Al-Zahrani54 also emphasized that increasing heat and water scarcity are major environmental challenges in the Arabian Peninsula. Although declining humidity may reduce moist heat stress in some cases, higher temperatures and increased evaporative demand may intensify thermal stress and water scarcity in desert environments. Therefore, the combined trend of warming and drying observed in this study likely reflects increasing atmospheric dryness rather than improved human comfort.

Methodologically, this study used three complementary approaches to identify climatic trends. MKT provided a nonparametric statistical assessment of monotonic trends, Sen’s slope estimated the magnitude of change, and ITA provided graphical insight into the distributional behavior of the time series. The general agreement among these approaches strengthens confidence in the identified climatic trends, although minor differences between methods were observed in some months.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. The analysis is based on temporal observations from a single meteorological station, which limits the ability to assess spatial variability across Kuwait or the wider Gulf region. In addition, the study focuses solely on historical meteorological observations and does not examine atmospheric circulation patterns, land-use change, or urban heat-island effects that may influence local climate variability. Future research should integrate spatial climate datasets, regional climate models, and circulation analysis to better understand the mechanisms driving the observed trends.

Conclusion

This comprehensive meteorological analysis has revealed significant changes in Kuwait City’s climate trends, with important implications for weather forecasting and urban climate modeling. The integration of multiple analytical methods, including MKT, ITA, and Linear Regression Modeling, has provided robust insights into the complex interactions between urban environments and atmospheric processes. Annual rainfall exhibited a significant declining trend of − 1.869 mm/year, while relative humidity decreased by − 0.09%/year. In contrast, both maximum and minimum air temperatures showed clear increasing trends, with annual warming rates of 0.047 °C/year for Tmax and 0.068 °C/year for Tmin. Seasonal analysis further indicated that rainfall decline was most pronounced in winter (− 0.897 mm/year), the primary precipitation period in Kuwait. These findings indicate a gradual shift toward warmer and drier climatic conditions in Kuwait City. The simultaneous increases in temperature and decreases in rainfall and humidity suggest increasing hydroclimatic stress in this arid urban environment. Such changes may have important implications for water resource management, environmental sustainability, and climate adaptation strategies in the Arabian Gulf region. The observed trends are also consistent with broader warming patterns reported across several parts of the Middle East and North Africa.

The application of multiple trend-detection techniques yielded consistent results and improved the reliability of the trend assessment. In particular, ITA complemented traditional statistical approaches by revealing distributional variations within the meteorological time series. Despite these contributions, the present study is limited to temporal analysis using a single-station dataset. Future research should incorporate spatial datasets, high-resolution climate observations, and regional climate modeling to better understand the spatial variability and driving mechanisms of climate change in Kuwait and the surrounding Arabian Gulf region.
