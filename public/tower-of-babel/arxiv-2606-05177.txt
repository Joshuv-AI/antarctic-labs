MCBench: A Multicontext Safety Assessment Benchmark for Omni Large Language Models
Manh, Tamas, Junae, Amar, Rollin, Gholamreza, Trang, Lizhen, Dinh

Abstract
Existing multimodal safety benchmarks focus solely on visual inputs and cannot assess Omni Large Language Models (LLMs) that process vision, audio, and text. We introduce MCBench, a benchmark with 1196 1196 scenarios spanning four safety categories that require integrating multiple modalities for accurate safety assessment. Each unsafe scenario is paired with a minimally different safe counterpart to assess model sensitivity. Our evaluations of state-of-the-art models reveal significant challenges. Omni LLMs struggle with subtle or non-physical risks but perform better when salient visual or acoustic cues are present. Analysis of reasoning traces shows that, although models can extract modality-specific information, they often fail to integrate these cues effectively for safety judgments. Our findings reveal that current Omni LLMs lack robust cross-modal reasoning in safety-critical settings, underscoring the need for improved architectures and training strategies for multimodal safety.

To address these gaps, we introduce MCBench, the first multimodal, multicontext safety benchmark tailored for evaluating the safety-awareness of Omni LLMs in the multicontext setting. Table
1
illustrates the comparison between our benchmark and existing multimodal safety benchmarks. MCBench consists of
1196
multicontext safety scenarios across four coarse-grained taxonomies: physical harm, social harm, illegal harm, and property damage. To study the oversensitivity and insensitivity of Omni LLMs, we collect unsafe scenarios paired with corresponding safe scenarios that differ only in minimal contextual elements. Thus, the benchmark dataset consists of unsafe-safe scenario pairs. We first explore fine-grained categories and scenario pairs by collaborating with the Claude-Sonnet-4.5 model to better cover real-world safety situations. Then, we leverage existing generative models to generate corresponding multimodal inputs (audio and images) for each scenario. To ensure the quality of the benchmark, we include human experts in both the scenario design and data generation phases to filter out unrealistic scenarios and low-quality multimodal inputs.

We conduct a safety-awareness analysis of widely used open-source and proprietary Omni LLMs using MCBench. The results reveal three key findings regarding the safety-awareness of the evaluated models. First, current Omni LLMs struggle to assess safety in social and legal liability scenarios, while they perform well on physical harm and property damage categories. Second, visual context is challenging but provides informative cues that help models make accurate safety assessments. Third, failures in safety assessment arise primarily from limitations in Omni LLMs’ reasoning. More specifically, although these models can successfully extract ground-truth predicates from multimodal inputs, which are necessary for making safety assessments, they often fail to combine these cues to make accurate predictions. Furthermore,
our ablation studies reveal that current Omni LLMs lack robust mechanisms for balanced multicontext integration. When encountering ambiguous cues, these models exhibit oversensitivity, focusing on a single potentially concerning signal while ignoring contradictory evidence from other modalities. This leads to systematic false positives on safe scenarios, where isolated cues might appear concerning without proper contextual integration.

To summarise, our contributions are threefold:

•

We introduce MCBench, which focuses on evaluating the safety-awareness of Omni LLMs in the multicontext setting.

•

We analyze state-of-the-art open-source and proprietary models on our benchmark. The analysis reveals that existing Omni LLMs struggle with scenarios involving subtle or non-physical safety risks (Social Harm and Illegal Harm), while performing substantially better on categories with salient visual and acoustic cues (Physical Harm and Property Damage).

•

By analyzing reasoning traces, we find that models can extract relevant information but lack effective cross-modal integration capabilities. Models also exhibit oversensitivity, making safety judgments based on single concerning cues rather than holistically evaluating all available evidence.

Multimodal Multicontext Safety Benchmark

2.1
Overview

Problem definition.
We define the problem of multimodal multicontext safety as follows: Given a triplet of omnimodal situational data
T=\{I,A,S\}
, where I, A, and S denote an image, an audio clip, and a speech utterance, respectively, a model must classify the safety label
R(I,A,S)\in\{\text{safe},\text{unsafe}\}
based on the input modalities. Since safety assessment is determined by the combination of visual, acoustic, and linguistic contexts, multimodal language models must effectively aggregate information cues across all modalities to make accurate safety assessments.

Dataset description.
We curate the Multimodal Multicontext Safety Benchmark (MCBench), which consists of unsafe-safe scenario pairs, to evaluate how effectively multimodal language models assess safety levels in complex scenarios where context is derived from three modalities: vision, audio, and speech. As shown in Figure
1
, each data instance consists of a natural language query and a multimodal context representing the current situation. The multimodal context comprises an image, an audio clip, and a speech utterance. Furthermore, the ground-truth label is provided along with the corresponding predicate to determine the safety assessment of the input situation. The predicate is a critical component of our benchmark, as it enables a deeper analysis of Omni LLMs’ ability to aggregate multimodal inputs for decision-making.

Benchmark taxonomy.
Figure
2
demonstrates the safety taxonomy evaluated in our benchmark for multimodal language models. We adopt three existing coarse-grained categories: physical harm, property damage, and illegal harm, from prior work
[
37
]
and propose social harm as a new category to provide a more comprehensive coverage of multicontext safety scenarios. The fine-grained categories in
[
37
]
focus primarily on visual context for safety assessment and thus do not adequately cover multicontext safety scenarios. To address this gap, we define new fine-grained categories for each coarse-grained category to better align with the multicontext safety problem. The complete taxonomy is detailed below:

•

Physical harm consists of three fine-grained categories, Violence & Weaponized Threats, Environmental Hazards, and Public Safety & Medical Emergencies, covering safety concerns in general domains, such as child and public safety.

•

Social harm covers safety concerns related to social aspects and is subcategorized into Identity-based Exclusion (e.g. hate speech and discrimination), Information-related Violations (e.g. misinformation and social manipulation), and Exploitation & Interpersonal Harm (e.g. harassment and bullying).

•

Illegal harm includes safety concerns involving activities that lead to illegal consequences, subcategorized into Crimes Against People (e.g. child exploitation and weapon offenses) and Crimes Against Systems & Property (e.g. financial and cyber crimes)

•

Property damage covers safety scenarios related to harmful behaviors that cause critical damage to either person or property, divided into Energy & Utility System Hazards (e.g. gas leak and fires), Environmental & Structural Threats (e.g. weather-related damage), and Chemical, Industrial & Vehicular Damage (e.g. hazardous materials and industrial failures)

2.2
Data Collection

To construct MCBench, we design a two-phase data collection framework. In the first phase, we leverage large language models to generate diverse safety scenarios for each coarse-grained category in our taxonomy. In the second phase, we use image and audio generation models to create synthetic multimodal content for each scenario. Figure
3
demonstrates the data generation framework for MCBench.

Figure 3:
The overall pipeline of benchmark construction which consists of two phases: the multicontext safety scenario generation and multicontext data generation.

Phase 1: Multimodal Safety Scenario Generation.
We leverage Claude-Sonnet-4.5
[
3
]
to systematically develop fine-grained subcategories for each of our four coarse-grained safety categories. We provide the model with detailed definitions and examples for each coarse-grained category, and then iteratively prompt it to propose granular subcategories, which are refined by human experts. Through this process, we obtain two to three fine-grained subcategories per coarse-grained category.

In the next step, we prompt Claude-Sonnet-4.5 to generate unsafe scenarios for each fine-grained subcategory. To ensure that scenarios require reasoning across multiple modalities, we first instruct the model to formulate unsafe scenarios using an If-Then logical structure:
L=\text{IF }<\text{condition 1}>\text{ AND }<\text{condition 2}>\text{ AND }\ldots\text{ THEN UNSAFE}
, where multiple contextual conditions collectively lead to an unsafe situation. We then generate corresponding safe scenarios by modifying one or two conditions in each unsafe scenario, creating paired instances that differ only in minimal contextual elements. For each unsafe-safe scenario pair, we prompt the model to generate detailed textual descriptions that serve as prompts for image and audio generative models in Phase 2. Specifically, we obtain a tuple
(T_{I},T_{A},S,R,L)
, where
T_{I}
and
T_{A}
are textual descriptions for the image and audio context,
S
is a speech utterance,
R\in\{\text{safe},\text{unsafe}\}
is the ground-truth safety label, and
L
is the If-Then predicate for the safety assessment.

In the final step, we manually verify and refine the generated scenarios. We eliminate scenarios that are unrealistic or contain logical flaws inconsistent with common-sense knowledge. We also review and correct the If-Then predicates, ensuring that each predicate provides sound reasoning for the safety assessment and aligns with real-world understanding.

Phase 2: Multimodal Data Generation.
Using the textual descriptions from Phase 1, we synthesize contextual images with Gemini-Flash-2.5
[
8
]
and contextual audio with Stable Audio 1.0
[
11
]
. We directly use image descriptions
T_{I}
as prompts for the image generation model to generate the corresponding image
I
. However, some scenarios involving sensitive content (e.g., child safety) cannot be generated due to the safety policies of Gemini-Flash-2.5. We exclude these scenarios from the benchmark because they lack a complete multimodal context. For audio generation, we observe that current audio generation models excel at synthesizing single sound events but struggle with complex multi-event soundscapes. Therefore, we decompose complex audio descriptions
T_{A}
into individual sound events and generate them separately. For instance, the contextual audio description ”
A pill bottle opens, followed by the writing of a farewell note
” can be decomposed into the template
\langle
sound event 1
,
relationship
,
sound event 2
\rangle
. We prompt Claude-Sonnet-4.5 to extract this structured representation from each
T_{A}
and then generate the corresponding audio
A
, where the temporal relationship is either ”
followed by
” or ”
simultaneous
”. Each sound event is then synthesized as a 6-second audio clip using Stable Audio 1.0. Finally, we combine the clips based on their relationship: sequential clips are concatenated end-to-end, while simultaneous clips are overlaid with a 3-second overlap to create a natural blend.

As a final quality control step, we manually verify the alignment between the generated multimodal content (images and audio) and the textual scenario descriptions from Phase 1. Instances where the synthesized content does not accurately reflect the intended scenario are removed from the dataset.
Through this curation process, we obtain the final benchmark dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(I_{i},A_{i},S_{i},R_{i},L_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each instance consists of: (1) a multimodal context
(I_{i},A_{i},S_{i})
containing an image, an audio clip, and a speech utterance; (2) a ground-truth safety label
R_{i}\in\{\text{safe},\text{unsafe}\}
;
and (3) a predicate
L_{i}
explaining the safety assessment. Table

3.2
Main Results

We evaluate both open-source models and proprietary models. All models are evaluated using a “General Prompt” that elicits chain-of-thought reasoning
[
31
]
. Specifically, the prompt instructs models to follow a three-step process: first, analyze the given image; second, extract contextual information from the audio and speech utterance; and finally, assess the safety level by aggregating information from all modalities.

Table
3
demonstrates the performance of state-of-the-art Omni LLMs on multimodal safety assessment across the four coarse-grained categories in MCBench. This table reveals several key findings. First, all Omni LLMs perform above random chance, demonstrating basic multimodal safety reasoning capability.
Second, even the best-performing models achieve modest accuracy: Gemini-Flash-2.5 and Qwen-Omni-2.5-3B both reach approximately
64.5\%
average accuracy, indicating that MCBench poses significant challenges for both proprietary and open-source models. Third, we observe category-specific challenges, particularly for the Social Harm and Illegal Harm categories. Some open-source models (InternOmni, OmniVinci, Baichuan-Omni-1.5) exhibit oversensitivity in the Social Harm category,
classifying safe scenarios as unsafe at a rate below that of a random classifier.
Conversely, other open-source models, Qwen-Omni-2.5 family and AnyGPT, show insensitivity to unsafe Social Harm scenarios. Even proprietary models struggle with this category: Gemini-Flash-2.5 achieves
44\%
accuracy on unsafe Social Harm cases. Similar patterns emerge for Illegal Harm. This finding indicates that state-of-the-art Omni LLMs struggle to combine information cues across multimodal context inputs to make safety judgments for intangible and hidden effects, such as social and legal liability scenarios. In contrast, models perform substantially better on Physical Harm and Property Damage categories, suggesting these domains involve more recognizable visual and acoustic cues.

3.4
Failure Diagnosis

Figure 8:
An oversensitive safety assessment example of Omni LLMs for the Illegal Harm category in MCBench. Although all three models are capable of generating ground-truth premises, they failed to combine the extracted information cues from multimodal inputs. To understand why models make incorrect safety assessments, we analyze their reasoning traces through the lens of two key capabilities: perception and reasoning. Perception refers to extracting relevant information from multimodal inputs, while reasoning refers to integrating perceived information to make safety judgments.
To evaluate these capabilities separately, we conduct two complementary analyses. First, to assess perception quality, we compare models’ reasoning traces against ground-truth predicates to identify whether errors stem from misperception of contextual cues or from faulty reasoning despite correct perception. Second, to isolate reasoning ability, we provide models with ground-truth predicates as input and evaluate whether they can make accurate safety assessments when given correct contextual information. This approach reveals whether models fail due to perception errors or reasoning failures.
We focus our analysis on Qwen-Omni-2.5 3B, Qwen-Omni-2.5 7B, and Gemini-Flash-2.5. The Qwen-Omni-2.5 variants enable us to examine how model scale affects perception and reasoning capabilities, while Gemini-Flash-2.5 provides insight into state-of-the-art proprietary model performance.

Evaluation metric for perception-alignment.
We leverage ground-truth safety judgment predicates to evaluate perception alignment between reasoning traces and the ground-truth multicontext information. We formulate the alignment evaluation between them as an entailment prediction task. Let
L=\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
denote a predicate consisting of
N
premises
p_{i}
. For each premise, we first construct an entailment query

\text{\small query}=\textit{\small Premise}:\langle\text{\small reasoning trace }\rangle\backslash\text{\small n }\textit{\small Hypothesis:}\langle p_{i}\rangle

Next, we prompt the Claude-Sonnet-4.5 model to predict the entailment outcome
\textbf{o}_{i}\in\{\text{entail, likely entail, not entail}\}
between the given reasoning trace and the predicate premise
p_{i}
. The entailment outcome is then discretized as follows:

s_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }o_{i}=\text{entail}\\
0.5,&\text{if }o_{i}=\text{likely entail}\\
0,&\text{if }o_{i}=\text{not entail}\\
\end{cases}

The perception alignment score is computed as follows:

\text{p\_align}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{|L|}s_{i}}{|L|}

The perception alignment score is in the range of 0 to 1, where 1 indicates complete alignment and 0 indicates complete misalignment.

3.4.1
Perception Diagnosis

We conduct an ablation study measuring the perception alignment score in the reasoning traces of the evaluated Omni LLMs. Figure
7
presents the perception alignment scores for three models: Gemini-Flash-2.5 and the Qwen2-Omni-2.5 family (3B and 7B), in the four coarse-grained categories of MCBench. The Qwen-Omni-2.5 7B model demonstrates higher perception alignment than its smaller 3B counterpart, suggesting that model scale improves the ability to extract relevant contextual information from multimodal inputs.
However, when comparing perception alignment to task accuracy, despite lower perception scores, Qwen2-Omni-2.5 3B achieves significantly higher accuracy (
64.5\%
) than the 7B model (
55.2\%
). This inverse relationship suggests that the 3B model may rely on shallow heuristics or spurious correlations rather than genuine multimodal reasoning. In other words, the smaller model exploits shortcuts that yield correct predictions without requiring accurate perception of all relevant contextual cues. The Gemini model shows the highest perception alignment score between its responses and the ground-truth premises, which also corresponds to the highest accuracy in safety judgment. This pattern reveals that the Gemini model is able to extract relevant information cues from complex multimodal inputs to make accurate safety judgments.

Although the Gemini model demonstrates an excellent multimodal perception capability for the illegal harm category, achieving the highest perception alignment score (
0.698
), it exhibits oversensitivity in the unsafe subset of this category. As demonstrated in Figure
8
, the Qwen-Omni-2.5 family and Gemini models successfully extract relevant information cues for making a safety assessment; however, they fail to make an accurate assessment. The main cause is that they overlook the extracted information cues and focus solely on the speech utterance to make an appropriate safety judgment. The prior work
[
18
]
observes the same issue with visual LLMs. These results highlight a fundamental challenge: current Omni LLMs struggle to effectively integrate information across modalities for safety assessment, suggesting the need for architectures or training strategies that enforce balanced multimodal reasoning.

Conclusion

We introduce a new multicontext multimodal safety benchmark, called MCBench, to examine the safety-awareness of Omni LLMs. Unlike existing benchmarks that rely on single-modality cues, MCBench scenarios require integrating information across vision, audio, and speech to determine a safety assessment, reflecting the complexity of real-world safety situations. This design makes MCBench a rigorous testbed for advancing the development of safe Omni LLMs. Through analysis of reasoning traces for popular open-source and proprietary models, we find that existing models fail to aggregate extracted cues from multicontext to make accurate predictions, often overlooking critical contextual cues when making safety judgments. In addition, models are oversensitive in safe scenarios, making safety judgments on single concerning cues rather than considering all available evidence. This finding suggests that future research should focus on enhancing information aggregation across modalities to enhance the safety-awareness of Omni LLMs.
