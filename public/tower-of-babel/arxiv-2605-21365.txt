L 2 L^{2} over Wasserstein: Statistical Analysis for Optimal Transport
Riccardo Passeggeri, Rohan M. Shenoy, Pengcheng Ye

Abstract
. Optimal transport provides an inherently geometric and highly structured framework for studying spaces of probability measures, supplying a rich theoretical toolkit for contemporary statistics, machine learning, and generative modelling. In applications, however, the measures of interest are almost never known precisely, calling for a theory of optimal transport that accounts for statistical uncertainty. We construct such a framework, lifting the classical theory to the setting of random probability measures. We introduce the L 2 L^{2} over Wasserstein space L W 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) , establishing that it inherits the formal Riemannian structure of the Wasserstein space by characterising distances and geodesic geometry. The structure induces random flows with Wasserstein gradient flow sample paths, making it the natural extension of the Wasserstein space which allows for random gradient flow dynamics. We ensemble statistical convergence results of the optimal transport machinery using the empirical measure within the L W 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) framework. Moreover, in the setting of Bayesian non-parametrics, we refine Schwartz’s consistency theorem to the Wasserstein topology and deduce posterior convergence of the same machinery in L W 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) . We demonstrate that the growing theory of random token sampling for transformer models using self-attention flow paths can be embedded into the L W 2 ​ ( ℝ d ) L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d}) framework. The results provide a unified treatment of random optimal transport and its consequences for principled inference and generative modelling under the statistical uncertainty of random sampling. Keywords: Optimal transport, random measures, Wasserstein gradient flows, empirical measures, Bayesian inference, propagation of chaos, transformers, self-attention.



1.
Introduction

This work introduces the
L^{2}
over Wasserstein space, lifting the classical theory of optimal transport to random probability measures. We demonstrate the utility of the space for applications in statistical analysis, Bayesian analysis, and generative modelling, consolidating both new and existing results under one unified framework for optimal transport under uncertainty.

Classical Optimal Transport

The study of optimal transport was introduced by Monge in a Memoir from 1781
[
Mon81
]
, considering the problem of finding a volume-preserving mapping to transport mass from a starting distribution
\mu
to a terminal distribution
\nu
whilst minimising the transport cost. The modern mathematical formulation of the optimal transport problem between probability measures, due to Kantorovich in 1942
[
Kan42
]
, replaces the search for a deterministic transport map
T:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
from the Monge formulation with the more flexible notion of a coupling, a joint distribution over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. The resulting Wasserstein distance
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)
, obtained by optimising the expected squared displacement over all such couplings, endows the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with a rich geometric structure, respecting that of the base space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The topological and metric properties of the Wasserstein space are treated comprehensively by Villani in
[
Vil03
]
and
[
Vil09
]
.

By Brenier’s fundamental theorem (1991)
[
Bre91
]
, in the setting of absolutely continuous measures one obtains an optimal transport map, realised as the gradient of a convex potential. This, in turn, underpins a formal Riemannian structure on
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where geodesics are characterised by the Benamou–Brenier theorem, and gradient flows governed by the continuity equation—a framework extensively developed by Ambrosio, Gigli, and Savaré
[
AGS08
]
.

In section
2
we characterise the classical theory, broadly following chapters 1 and 5 from
[
CNWR25
]
as primary reference. We formulate the

p
-Wasserstein space over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and present Brenier’s theorem in the absolutely continuous case. We then explore Otto’s formal Riemannian structure on
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
from
[
Ott01
]
. The Benamou–Brenier theorem from
[
BB00
]
identifies the alternative dynamic formulation of the Wasserstein
distance as the minimised kinetic energy over all curves of measures subject to the continuity equation, inducing a geodesic geometry characterised via displacement interpolations. The section ends by introducing Wasserstein gradient flows, which serve as a widely used foundation for optimization algorithms that operate directly on the space of probability measures and are applied to many problems in statistics and machine learning.

Optimal Transport on the realisations of random probability measures

The central contribution of this paper is to lift the classical optimal transport framework to the setting of
random probability measures
with appropriate structure developed in section
3
. The framework we develop allows one to discuss the optimal transport problem between distributions subject to statistical uncertainty.

In the language of Kallenberg
[
Kal17
]
, a random measure
\xi
is a measure-valued random variable;
we focus on random probability measures with

\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
realisations for which the classical optimal transport theory applies. We carefully define the

L^{2}
over Wasserstein space
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq L^{2}(\Omega;(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2}))
as the
L^{2}
space of probability measures endowed with the Wasserstein topology, naturally equipping the space with the metric

(1.1)

d(\xi,\eta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[W_{2}^{2}(\xi(\omega),\eta(\omega))\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}.

The structure induced by this metric topology is rich. Within our framework, we are able to lift the geodesic geometry of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Constant-speed geodesics in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are obtained by applying the Benamou-Brenier displacement interpolation to the sample paths, characterising
d^{2}(M_{0},M_{1})
as the infimum of
expected
kinetic energy over sample paths
M_{t\in[0,1]}(\omega)
subject to the continuity equation.

We demonstrate that the
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
framework gives rise to a formal Riemannian structure over the space of random probability measures, where the tangent space at a random measure
M
is identified as the
L^{2}
random vectors whose realisations are tangent vectors to the Wasserstein space, endowed with the Wasserstein Dirichlet form inner product from
[
vRS09
]
- the energy of the random tangent vector with respect to the law of
M
,
\mathbb{P}_{M}
, over the Wasserstein space. In the context of Wasserstein gradient flows with respect to a functional over
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, this definition induces a random gradient flow with Wasserstein gradient flow sample paths. The space
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
can thus be considered the natural extension of the Wasserstein space which allows for random gradient flow dynamics.

Statistical analysis of empirical measures in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Section
4
demonstrates the utility of the new definitions by consolidating a statistical theory for the optimal transport machinery constructed from empirical measures. Given sample draws from a population measure
\mu
, we view the empirical measure
\hat{\mu}_{n}
as a random probability measure in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We characterise an
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
law of large numbers, a well-known result in the i.i.d. case, now in the language from our framework, with convergence both
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely and in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
at rates provided by a theorem from Fournier and Guillin
[
FG15
]
. We also show convergence in the non-i.i.d. case under strong mixing conditions, providing adjusted Fournier-Guillin rates.

Via the stability results of the preceding section, we demonstrate the uniform convergence of the gradient flow paths initialised at the empirical measure to the gradient flow initialised at the population measure
\mu
in both i.i.d. and non-i.i.d. cases, providing explicit propagation of chaos rates using a Grönwall bound.

We unify both new and existing results within
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
space to provide a single, canonical framework to study the optimal transport of randomly sampled empirical measures in Wasserstein space.

Bayesian Analysis in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Section
5
explores the applicability of the
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
framework for Bayesian non-parametrics. Many examples of existing research combining variational inference with optimal transport focus on Bayesian robustness (
[
MBTL25
]
) and approximate Bayesian computation as in
[
BJGR19
]
,
[
D+19
]
,
[
LHWY26
]
,
[
MHK20
]
among other works. However, many of these assume the data are known a priori, thus treating the posterior as a deterministic measure. To target uncertainty quantification, one assumes the posterior is itself a random measure, where the data are not known and are modelled as random processes.

More concretely, the data-generating distribution
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is unknown, hence modelled by a prior
\Pi
: a random probability measure which, under our framework, is an element of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The posterior
\Pi_{n}(\cdot,\omega)=\Pi(\cdot|X_{1}(\omega),\ldots,X_{n}(\omega))
, updated via Bayes’ theorem after observing

n
random i.i.d. draws from
\pi
, is again a random probability measure in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Bayesian consistency, the concentration of posterior mass around the truth
\pi
in the Wasserstein metric, follows from the classical theorem of Schwartz
[
Sch65
]
, which we refine from the weak topology to Wasserstein topology.

Under moment conditions on the prior and posterior, we establish uniform posterior consistency in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We study the properties of the optimal transport machinery under Bayesian uncertainty, performing a similar analysis section to
4
, now for the Bayesian posteriors in place of empirical measures. We are able to discuss the Bayesian gradient flow as a propagation of uncertainty which can be controlled and stabilised appropriately under the sufficient conditions we show for posterior concentration in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Transformers with
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
token samples

The introduction of the transformer architecture in 2017 by Vaswani et al.
[
VSP+17
]
marks a profound milestone in generative modelling. Central to the architecture is the self-attention mechanism which, as remarked by Geshkovski et al.
[
GLPR25
]
, invites pairwise interaction between tokens. A recent line of work, explored by
[
GLPR25
]
,
[
Rig26
]
,
[
BPA26
]
and
[
ABG+26
]
among others, has focused on the mean-field dynamics of the transformer model - these works demonstrate a token clustering phenomenon over the depth of the transformer, both empirically and theoretically in the neural ODE setting. The growing theory highlights connections between attention dynamics and areas of mathematics such as interacting particle systems, optimal transport,
synchronization models, and gradient flows, sitting nicely within the framework we develop. A key feature is the use of the empirical measure of the token particles to encapsulate mean-field dynamics between the tokens induced by a vector field acting on the empirical measure.

The initialised empirical measure at time
0
represents a token sample. Assuming tokens are sampled from an underlying population distribution, in the language of section
4
, the empirical measure is a random measure in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Agazzi et al.
[
ABG+26

explore multiple propagation of chaos results after injecting noise at different stages in the transformer model, including at the initialisation of the empirical measure. We embed their results within the
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
framework (marginally generalising their i.i.d. initialisation of the empirical measure to non-i.i.d. strong mixing). Moreover, expanding this viewpoint to the Bayesian paradigm, we consider sampling tokens from a Bayesian posterior, inviting the theory developed in section
5

Related works and literature

The key feature of our approach is that it operates directly on the random measures themselves, rather than on their laws, distinguishing our framework from several recent works in the literature. In effect, this difference in approach is equivalent to the difference between exploring properties of real-valued random variables, and properties of their laws (probability distributions), only now at the level of
measure-valued
random variables.

Pinzi and Savaré
[
PS25b
]
, consider the
Wasserstein-over-Wasserstein
(WoW) space of random measure laws, the ground cost is the Wasserstein distance, rather than the Euclidean. The 2-WoW distance between random measure laws
\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
can be expressed using our definitions

W_{W_{2}}^{2}(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{Q})=\inf_{\xi\sim\mathbb{P},\eta\sim\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[W^{2}(\xi(\omega),\eta(\omega))\right]\qquad\text{s.t. }\xi,\eta\in L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

which asserts that
\xi
and
\eta
are optimally coupled. Here one defines an optimal coupling (at the lifted level of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
) for
\xi,\eta
in the infimum. Contrasting with our setting (
1.1
) we assume that
\xi,\eta
are defined on the same probability space a priori, hence already coupled.

The authors additionally develop gradient flows in the WoW space in
[
PS25a
]
, demonstrating a nested superposition result, first superposing the dynamics of particles in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(subject to the continuity equation) to the dynamics of measures on
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then further superposing to the dynamics of random measure laws on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. As analogy, our framework defines a single (stochastic) superposition from
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, though we do not discuss this in detail.

The inner product that we impose on the tangent space to
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
was originally introduced as the
Wasserstein Dirichlet form
by von Renesse and Sturm in
[
vRS09
]
. The authors establish the existence of a unique, continuous, symmetric Markov process on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
by proving that the Dirichlet form (
3.20
) is closed, applying standard Dirichlet form theory as in
[
FOT10
]
. Subsequent contributions, including
[
Sch18
]
and
[
Sch24
]
, have constructed further diffusion processes on various Wasserstein spaces in a similar fashion. Our structure, arising naturally from the definition of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the resulting dynamic equivalence of Theorem
3.9
, coincides with the one studied in these works, placing our results within the general setting of random Wasserstein dynamics. However, while the aforementioned works focus on randomness introduced by diffusion processes on Wasserstein spaces, primarily motivated by statistical modelling, we are interested instead in random gradient flows driven by a prescribed functional on the Wasserstein space
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The adapted Wasserstein distances, introduced by Bartl, Beiglböck and Pammer in
[
BBP24
]
, are explored by Acciaio et al. in
[
AKPR25
]
where the authors address optimal transport between stochastic processes under bi-causal constraints. While the paper explores a different structural concern from ours, the developments are similarly extend definitions of classical optimal transport to random processes.

Catalano and Lavenant
[
CL23
]
, targeting Bayesian inference as we do in section
5
, similarly work with random measure laws rather than directly at the level of random measures. The authors develop a natural framework to investigate posterior merging rates using Dirichlet process priors, as the sample size increases, according to their Wasserstein over bounded Lipschitz distance, treating posterior updates as a stochastic process.

2.
Classical Optimal Transport

2.1.
The Wasserstein Space

The utility of optimal transport lies in its endowment of the space of probability measures with a geometric structure which canonically respects that of the underlying space. The most common optimal transport distance is the Wasserstein distance which lifts the metric on the ground space
\mathcal{X}
to the space of measures on
\mathcal{X}
,
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. When the ground space is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the common choice of ground metric is the Euclidean norm.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two probability measures with finite
p^{\text{th}}
moment. Then
p
-Wasserstein distance between
\mu
and
\nu
is given by

(2.2)

W_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}}\left(\int||x-y||^{p}\gamma(dx,dy)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},

where
\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}
is the set of couplings between
\mu
and
\nu
- the set of joint distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
respectively. One verifies
W_{p}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})=||x-y||
so that
(\mathbb{R}^{d},||\cdot||)
is isometrically embedded in
(\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{p})
via
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
; it is in this manner the Wasserstein distance provides its naturally geometric distance between probability measures. The metric topology induced by this distance on
\mathcal{P}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is explored extensively in Villani’s monographs
[
Vil03
]
and
[
Vil09
]
. The topology induced by the Wasserstein metric is characterised by the following equivalence.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
Vil03
]
Theorem 7.12)
.

A sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfies
W_{p}(\mu_{n},\mu)\to 0
if and only if it converges weakly to
\mu
, (denoted
\mu_{n}\overset{w}{\to}\mu
), and the
p
-th moment, denoted
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\cdot)
, converges:
\int\|x\|^{p}\mu_{n}(dx)\to\int\|x\|^{p}\mu(dx)
.

We show the equivalence in Appendix
A.2
, while further equivalences are documented in Theorem 7.12 of
[
Vil03
]
. A useful result towards proving Theorem
2.1
is that the functional
W_{2}(\cdot,\mu)
is lower semi-continuous with respect to the weak topology, as shown in the preceding Appendix
A.1
. The equivalence of Theorem
2.1
proves powerful in sections
4
and
5
where we establish convergence results by proving weak convergence and moment convergence, rather than computing
W_{p}
directly, which is intractable in general.

The case
p=2
presents additional interesting structure for the Wasserstein space, in close connection with convex analysis. The following theorem of Brenier
[
Bre91
]
provides the existence of a transport map when the source measure is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.

Theorem 2.2

(
[
Bre91
]
)
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two probability measures such that
\mu
has a density and let
X\sim\mu
. If
\bar{\gamma}
is an optimal coupling for the Kantorovich problem with squared Euclidean cost i.e.

\int\|x-y\|^{2}\bar{\gamma}(dx,dy)=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}}\int\|x-y\|^{2}\gamma(dx,dy)=W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu),

then there exists a convex function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
(X,\nabla\varphi(X))\sim\bar{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}
.
\nabla\varphi
is referred to as the Brenier map.

A full proof relies on results concerning gradients of convex functions such as cyclical monotonicity and Rockafellar’s Theorem.
As is remarked in 1.4 of
[
CNWR25
]
,
\mu
admitting a density is the crucial assumption of Brenier’s Theorem as it guarantees that
\nabla\varphi(X)
is well-defined (with convex functions being differentiable Lebesgue-almost everywhere). We use
\mathcal{P}_{2,\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to denote the class of absolutely continuous measures with finite second moment. Restricting to this space, Brenier’s Theorem shows that an optimal coupling is always deterministic, provided as the gradient of a convex function
\varphi
.

2.2.
Curves of probability measures

Given the map
x\mapsto\delta_{x}
which isometrically embeds
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\|\cdot\|)
into
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),W_{2})
, one can understand dynamics on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by superposing the corresponding dynamics of particles on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying an appropriate evolution equation.

The standard prescription of dynamics on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
using differential calculus is via an ordinary differential equation (ODE). Given a time-dependent family of vector fields
{(v_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
, consider the ODE

(2.3)

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{t}(X_{t}).

Under suitable assumptions on
{(v_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
(e.g. Lipschitz growth), (
2.3
) admits a unique solution for any given initial condition
X_{0}
.
Suppose now that
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and similarly let
\mu_{t}
denote the law of
X_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Informally, the curve of measures
{(\mu_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
describe the evolution of a
collection
of particles with (potentially infinitesimal) mass prescribed by the measure at each time
t
.
The dynamics of
{(\mu_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
are subject to the
continuity equation
.

Proposition 2.3

(Continuity equation
[
Vil03
]
)
.

Suppose that
X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}
, and that
{(X_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
evolves according to the dynamics (
2.3
), assumed to be well-posed.
Let
\mu_{t}
denote the law of
X_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Then,
{(\mu_{t})}_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the following equation in the weak sense,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{t}+\divergence(\mu_{t}v_{t})=0,

i.e. for all compactly supported and smooth test functions
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int\varphi d\mu_{t}=\int\langle\nabla\varphi,v_{t}\rangle d\mu_{t}.

The equation expresses a conservation of (probability) mass: the change in density at any point is equal to the divergence of the probability mass escaping at that point.
When
\mu_{t}
admits a smooth density then an integration by parts yields the strong form of (
2.4
), identifying
\mu_{t}
with its density.

Consider a single particle of unit mass evolving according to the ODE (
2.3
) with trajectory
x\mapsto X_{t}
. The instantaneous kinetic energy is given by
\|\dot{X}_{t}\|^{2}=\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}
and the total kinetic energy of the trajectory over the time interval
[0,1]
is given by the integral

\int_{0}^{1}\|v_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}dt
. One verifies that the energy minimising trajectory over curves with fixed endpoints
X_{0},X_{1}
is given by the straight line interpolating
X_{0}
to
X_{1}
,
t\mapsto(1-t)X_{0}+tX_{1}
.

The picture is superposed to
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Viewing all particles in aggregate, weighted by the probability mass assigned the measure
\mu_{t}
at time
t
one measures the instantaneous kinetic energy of the system with

(2.6)

\displaystyle\|v_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}^{2}\coloneqq\int\|v_{t}\|^{2}d\mu_{t},

where the total kinetic energy over the time interval
[0,1]
is given by

(2.7)

\int_{0}^{1}\|v_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|v_{t}(x)\|^{2}\mu_{t}(dx)dt.

As Chewi et al.
[
CNWR25
]
remark in 5.1, the velocity field generating the path
\mu_{t}
can be highly non-unique. One would like to choose the velocity field minimising the kinetic energy in (
2.7
) while describing the dynamics of the curve and conclude that this choice is unique.

The action of the velocity field
v_{t}(\cdot)
on a single particle
x_{t}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
describes a tangent vector to its trajectory
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
at time
t
. To fully prescribe the dynamics of a curve in Wasserstein space, one would like to identify tangent vectors. Theorem
2.4
of Otto
[
Ott01
]
below informs us that these should be gradients of convex functions, recalling Brenier’s Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 2.4

(
[
Ott01
]
)
.

There is a unique family
{(v_{t})}_{t\geq 0}

satisfying (
2.4
) minimising the kinetic energy (
2.6
) for each
t\geq 0
. This family is given by the limit

(2.8)

\displaystyle v_{t}=\lim_{h\searrow 0}\frac{\nabla\varphi_{\mu_{t}\to\mu_{t+h}}-{\mathrm{id}}}{h}\qquad\text{in}~L^{2}(\mu_{t}),

where
\mathrm{id}
is the identity function.

A full proof is provided in
[
Ott01
]

2.4

\|v_{t}\|_{\mu_{t}}
equal to the metric derivative
|\dot{\mu}|(t)
, as in section 1.1 of
[
AGS08
]
.

\mu_{t}
is absolutely continuous allows Brenier’s Theorem to provide the existence of an optimal transport map, letting one write (

3.4.
Gradient flow paths of random probability measures

In definition
2.5
we defined the tangent space to
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for which the metric induced by the given Riemannian structure coincides with that of the Wasserstein distance. Given the formulation of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

for which the metric induced by the lifted Riemannian structure will coincide with our
L^{2}
over Wasserstein metric. We will show that the resulting structure gives rise to a canonical random Wasserstein gradient flow, in sense that it induces a random gradient flow where the sample paths are each deterministic Wasserstein gradient flows.

In light of the (random) dynamic formulation of the
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
distance

L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is now apparent. The tangent space we define consists of
L^{2}
random vectors whose realisations are tangent vectors to
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the
L^{2}
closure of gradients of functions in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
was given by the corresponding kinetic energy
\int_{0}^{1}\|v_{t}\|^{2}dt

\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Definition 3.10

(Tangent space to
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

Let

where we recall

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{M(\omega)}\mathcal{P}_{2,\rm ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\overline{\bigg\{\nabla\psi\,\Big|\,\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}~\text{compactly supported, smooth}\bigg\}}^{L^{2}(M(\omega))},

Remark 3.11
.

The inner product (

[
vRS09
]
who use standard Dirichlet form theory (as in
[
FOT10
]

after proving closure of the Dirichlet form (
3.20

[

and
[

\mathbb{P}_{M}
of the random measure

The fact that our structure, emerging naturally from the definition of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and the dynamic equivalence established in Theorem

However, the type of random dynamics we focus on is different. While these works investigate randomness via diffusion processes on Wasserstein space, aiming at statistical modelling, our interest lies instead in random gradient flows, generated by a given functional.

The formal Riemannian structure enables us to make the identification of random Wasserstein gradient flows for random probability measures, thereby showing that
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the appropriate space in which to study random gradient flows. Recall from Proposition
2.10
that the Wasserstein gradient of a functional evaluated at a measure in
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is given by the Euclidean gradient of the first variation of the functional evaluated at
\mu
i.e.
\nabla\mkern-10.0mu\nabla\mathcal{F}(\mu)=\nabla\delta\mathcal{F}(\mu)
.

We would like to determine first what functionals to consider. Given our definition of
L^{2}
over Wasserstein as a space for random optimal transport, we would like to consider functionals of random probability measures as random elements too.

Definition 3.12

(Random functional)
.

4.3.
Distances for non i.i.d. sampled empirical measures under strong mixing

We demonstrate that weakening the i.i.d. assumption by allowing for controllable correlation between nearby samples still permits convergence for the empirical measure as in the i.i.d. case. 5.
Bayesian Analysis in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

5.1.
The Bayesian paradigm

The Bayesian framework, as in Gelman et al.’s Bayesian Data
Analysis
[
GCS+13
]
, offers a principled framework for reasoning under uncertainty: one begins with a prior belief about an unknown quantity, and updates this belief in light of observed data to form a posterior. In classical parametric statistics, the unknown quantity is a finite-dimensional parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, and the prior is a probability distribution over the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. In the non-parametric setting, the unknown quantity is itself a probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and the prior must accordingly be a distribution over the space of probability measures, i.e., a random probability measure in the language of definition
3.1
.

Suppose we assume that the unknown data-generating distribution
\pi
has finite second moments, and is hence in
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, or even nicer is absolutely continuous in
\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then the structure and theory developed in section
3
applies. Before observing any data, the uncertainty about
\mu
is encoded by a prior
\Pi
, a random measure in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
or
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. For each sample
\omega\in\Omega
,
\Pi(\omega)
is a candidate probability measure representing one’s belief about the true data-generating process. As data
X_{i}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\pi
accumulates, Bayes’ Theorem prescribes the update from the prior to the posterior via

(5.44)

\Pi_{n}(\cdot)=\Pi(\cdot|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}p_{\mu}(X_{i})\Pi(d\theta),

where
p_{\mu}
denotes the density of
\mu
. The posterior
\Pi_{n}(A,\omega)=\Pi(A|X_{1}(\omega),\dots,X_{n}(\omega))
for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is again a random probability measure in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The Bayesian learning process is encompassed by the sequence of random probability measures
(\Pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}\subset L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
indexed by the amount of data observed,
\Pi
encoding prior uncertainty, and
\Pi(\cdot|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
encoding posterior uncertainty after
n
observations. As
n\to\infty
, one expects the posterior to concentrate i.e. the random probability measure
\Pi_{n}
should become less ‘dispersed’ around the truth
\mu
under appropriate measures of dispersion. Following our preceding exposition, the natural choice is given by the
W_{2}
distance on the sample paths, and the distance
d
at the lifted level of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The analysis we develop for the Bayesian paradigm generalises section
4
: assuming some underlying truth
\pi
as the data generating process, we discuss convergence (
consistency
in the Bayesian setting) of optimal transport distances, geodesics and gradient flows. As was the case for the empirical measures in section
4
, the key property is pathwise and
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
convergence of the distances, unlocking the subsequent results. This will follow from Schwartz’s Theorem for Bayesian consistency, specifically in the case of the topology of weak convergence of probability measures.

5.2.
Wasserstein consistency

Bayesian consistency is the phenomenon of a Bayesian posterior distribution concentrating around the true parameter value as the sample size
n\to\infty
. Formally, a sequence of posterior distributions is consistent at
\pi
if for any neighbourhood
U
of
\pi
,

(5.45)

\Pi_{n}(U)=\Pi(U|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\overset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

Evidently within our framework, the neighbourhoods
U
are open sets of the Wasserstein topology, as in the preceding sections. Posterior consistency is fundamental to Bayesian analysis, and as such is well-studied; crucially, Schwartz’s Theorem of 1965
[
Sch65
]
below provides sufficient conditions for consistency of the Bayesian posterior. Preparing the assumptions, we provide the following two definitions.

Definition 5.1
.

Given a (random measure) prior
\Pi
over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and a probability measure
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\pi
is said to be in the KL support of
\Pi
if for all
\varepsilon>0
,

(5.46)

\mathbb{P}_{\Pi}\left(\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\KL(\mu,\pi)\leq\varepsilon\}\right)>0.

Informally, the KL condition ensures that the prior distribution places positive mass on models arbitrarily close to
\pi
, where close is understood in entropic sense. The other condition asks for a uniformly consistent sequence of test functions.

Definition 5.2
.

A set
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to satisfy testing conditions for
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a neighbourhood of
\pi
such that there are test functions
(\varPhi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
satisfying

(5.47)

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\varPhi_{n}(X_{n})]\leq Ce^{-cn},\qquad\sup_{\mu\in U^{c}}\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[1-\varPhi_{n}(X_{n})]\leq Ce^{-cn}

where
X_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\pi
and a pair of positive constants
c,C>0
.

The testing conditions informally assert that the hypothesis
H_{0}:\mu=\pi
should be testable against complements of neighbourhoods of
\pi
, i.e.
H_{1}:\mu\in U^{c}
. The test
\varPhi_{n}
is interpreted as follows: the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\mu=\pi
is rejected with probability
\varPhi_{n}
, the probability of a type I error. The complement test
1-\varPhi_{n}
is the probability of rejecting
H_{0}
when the data are sampled from
\pi
, the probability of a type II error. Together, the testing conditions ensure that the probability of either error vanishing in large sample. With the conditions in hand, we may now formulate Schwartz’s Theorem as in
[
Sch65
]
.

Theorem 5.3

(
[
Sch65
]
)

Let
\pi

\Pi

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\pi

\KL

\Pi

5.1

\pi
, i.e. for every neighbourhood
U

\pi

(5.48)

\mathbb{P}_{\Pi}(U^{c}\cdot|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\to 0,\qquad\text{$\mathbb{P}$-a.s.}

where

is the joint law of
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}

The testing conditions for Schwartz’s Theorem are dependent on the topology one endows the parameter space with. In our non-parametric setting, the parameter space is
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

Transformers with
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
tokens

6.1.
Self-attention as a gradient flow

We predominantly follow Rigollet’s analysis from
[
Rig26
]
sections 2 and 3 (and the preceding work of collaborators
[
GLPR25
]
) lifting the convergence result to
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The reader is naturally directed to Vaswani et al.
[
VSP+17
]
for a more comprehensive review of transformer architecture.

A vanilla transformer concatenates attention blocks, consisting of an attention layer and a feed-forward (MLP) layer, both components typically followed by normalization and residual connections. Letting
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{n}
denote the matrix of
n
tokens of dimension
d
, a single layer performs the composition of the attention and MLP for each token.

The attention block computes a soft-max weighted average of all other tokens. Given (learnable) matrices
Q,K,V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and a temperature parameter
\beta>0
, the attention operator takes the form

(6.55)

\mathrm{Att}(X)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\exp\big(\beta\langle QX_{i},KX_{j}\rangle\big)}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp\big(\beta\langle QX_{i},KX_{k}\rangle\big)}\,VX_{j}.

(
6.55
) can be interpreted as a non-linear interaction rule; each token updates as a weighted average of all others, with weights depending on their (cosine) similarity in feature space.

A transformer processes data sequentially through layers via updates of the form
X_{k+1}=X_{k}+F(X_{k})

where
X_{k}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{n}
denotes the matrix of token embeddings at layer
k
in the network, and
F
encodes a composition of attention, feed-forward and normalizing operations. Interpreting the layer index as a time discretisation, passing to the continuous-time limit
\dot{X}_{t}=F(X_{t})
, the resulting system is regarded as a non-linear flow on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n}
. The attention mechanism defines a non-local velocity field, coupling each token particle to all others through a kernel dependent on the pairwise similarities.

This perspective places transformers within the theory of interacting particle systems and mean-field dynamics as in
[
Rig26
]
where a simplified continuous-time self-attention model is introduced, retaining self-attention and layer normalization while dropping the MLP layer. Let
x_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
denote the position of the
i
-th token at time
t
, and let
\beta>0
be an inverse-temperature parameter.
The self-attention (SA) dynamics are given by

(6.56)

\dot{x}_{i}(t)={\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x_{i}(t)}\left(\frac{1}{Z_{\beta,i}(t)}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\beta\langle x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t)\rangle}\,x_{j}(t)\right),\qquad Z_{\beta,i}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{\beta\langle x_{i}(t),x_{k}(t)\rangle},

where
{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}y=y-\langle x,y\rangle x
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, enforcing a layer normalization effect, restricting all tokens to the unit sphere.
The exponential weights represent attention scores, while the normalization ensures that each row of the attention matrix forms a probability vector.

(
6.56
) describes
n
particles on the sphere interacting through the kernel
K(x,y)=e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}
.
The interplay between this non-local interaction and the spherical geometry produces rich collective dynamics such as clustering and synchronization studied in great depth by Rigollet
[
Rig26
]
and collaborators Geshkovski et al.
[
GLPR25
]
, as well as Bruno et al.
[
BPA26
]
.

As Rigollet remarks, to leverage the machinery of optimal transport, it would be valuable if one could identify the dynamics in (
6.56
) with a Wasserstein gradient flow. However, it is shown in
[
SABP21
]
that (
6.56
) cannot in fact describe the Wasserstein gradient flow of a functional due to a symmetry issue. Circumventing the issue,
[
Rig26
]
provides the surrogate, un-normalised self-attention (USA) model

(6.57)

\dot{x}_{i}(t)={\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x_{i}(t)}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{\beta\langle x_{i}(t),x_{j}(t)\rangle}\,x_{j}(t)\right),

demonstrating that it retains many of the features of the standard (SA) model, while enjoying a more tractable analysis. One reformulates (
6.57
) using the empirical distribution of the tokens
\mu_{t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}(t)}
, evolving according to the continuity equation (
2.4
) with the family of vector fields given by

(6.58)

v_{t}(x)={\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{(\cdot)}\int e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}y\,\mu_{t}(dy).

For (
6.57
), the continuity equation (
2.4
) is the Wasserstein gradient ascent of the interaction energy

(6.59)

\mathcal{E}^{\beta}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\beta}\iint e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}\,d\mu(x)d\mu(y),

pointing in the direction of energy increase.

Recall from the discussion following the Wasserstein
C^{1}
definition
3.15
that one can extend the gradient flow to discrete measures. Indeed, one verifies the interaction energy is Wasserstein
C^{1}
in the sense of that definition, and the gradient flow theory applies to the discrete empirical measure without further modifications (e.g. mollification).

We have explored a theory where the base space is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whereas the normalisation restricts the particles to lie on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. The Wasserstein gradient thus needs to lie on the tangent space of the sphere - one identifies the Wasserstein gradient of a functional on the sphere as the projection of the Wasserstein gradient back onto the sphere
{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{(\cdot)}\nabla\delta\mathcal{F}(\mu)(\cdot)
.

6.2.
Sampling
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

The fast-developing theory for the mean-field (USA) dynamics applies once we fix the token initialisation, and then let the token distribution evolve through the random (USA) gradient flow (
6.57

7.
Discussion

This work provides the natural framework to treat the problem of optimal transport under uncertainty. We lift the classical theory of section
2
to the paths of random probability measures in section
3
and consider the optimal transport problems induced by their realisations, hence discussing a random optimal transport. In particular, we introduce the
L^{2}
over Wasserstein space
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which, pathwise, inherits all of the theory of deterministic transport, allowing us to define a natural random theory in section
3
via
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
optimal transport with that same pathwise theory extended to the
L^{2}
level
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Crucially, we demonstrate that the formal Riemannian structure
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
induces over the space of random probability measures, gives rise to random gradient flow with Wasserstein gradient flow sample paths, thus
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be considered the right setting within which to explore random dynamics in Wasserstein space.

The framework developed in section

A.1.

Proof.

Let

\nu_{n}\overset{w}{\to}\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\liminf_{n\to\infty}W_{2}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\geq W_{2}(\mu,\nu).

Let
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma_{\mu_{n},\nu_{n}}
be an optimal coupling for each

, and let

. Let

\displaystyle\sup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{n_{k}}(K^{c})

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{n_{k}}((K_{\mu}\times K_{\nu})^{c})\leq\sup_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{n_{k}}(K_{\mu}^{c}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})+\gamma_{n_{k}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times K_{\nu}^{c})

\displaystyle=\sup_{k\geq 1}\mu_{n_{k}}(K_{\mu}^{c})+\nu_{n_{k}}(K_{\nu}^{c})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.

Bounding each term and matching rates will deliver the result.

(Gap error). We can rewrite

As

Towards proving the convergence, we first collect a more general lemma for any
\mu_{k},\nu_{k}
converging to

Lemma C.4
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
If
W_{2}(\mu_{k},\mu)\to 0
and
W_{2}(\nu_{k},\nu)\to 0
then
W_{2}(\eta_{t}^{k},\eta_{t})\to 0
for each
t\in[0,1]
where
\eta_{t}^{k}
,
\eta_{t}
are the geodesic interpolations from
\mu_{k}
to
\nu_{k}
and
\mu
to
\nu
respectively.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.1
, the convergence in Wasserstein metric immediately implies weak convergence of the measures
\mu_{k}\overset{w}{\to}\mu
,
\nu_{k}\overset{w}{\to}\nu
and convergence of the second moments,
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mu_{k})\to\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mu)
,
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\nu_{k})\to\mathcal{M}_{2}(\nu)
.

We first claim that the sequence of couplings
(\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma_{\mu_{k},\nu_{k}})
is tight, as the both sequences of corresponding marginals are tight. Indeed, computing for
\mu_{k}
, from the convergence of the second moment we know that
C_{\mu}\coloneqq\sup_{k}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\mu_{k})<\infty
and that by Markov’s inequality

(C.9)

\gamma_{k}\left(\{\|x\|>R\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)=\mu_{k}(\{\|x\|>R\})\leq\frac{1}{R^{2}}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\mu_{k})\leq\frac{C_{\mu}}{R^{2}},

with an identical bound on the second marginal with
C_{\nu}\coloneqq\sup_{k}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\nu_{k})
. Then

\displaystyle\gamma\left((B_{R}\times B_{R})^{c}\right)\leq\gamma_{k}(\{\|x\|>R\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})+\gamma_{k}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{\|y\|>R\}\right)\leq\frac{C_{\mu}+C_{\nu}}{R^{2}},

which can be made arbitrarily small. As
B_{R}\times B_{R}
is compact in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the bound above is uniform in
k
, the sequence
(\gamma_{k})
is tight.

With this now in hand, by Prokhorov’s Theorem there is a subsequence
(\gamma_{k_{j}})
converging weakly to some
\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We first verify that this is indeed a valid coupling with marginals
\mu,\nu
. Let
f\in C_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a bounded continuous function from
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Then
(x,y)\mapsto f(x)
is bounded continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From the weak convergence of the couplings
\gamma_{k_{j}}\to\gamma^{\prime}
,

(C.10)

\int f(x)\gamma^{\prime}(dx,dy)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int f(x)\gamma_{k_{j}}(dx,dy)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int f(x)\mu_{k_{j}}(dx)=\int fd\mu

where the last equality uses the weak convergence of
\mu_{k_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}\mu
. As
f
was arbitrary, we conclude that the first marginal of
\gamma^{\prime}
is
\mu
, with an identical argument showing the second marginal is
\nu
. Hence
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}
.

We now demonstrate optimality of the coupling. Denote for
R>0
the truncated cost
f_{R}(x,y)=\min(|x-y|^{2})
which is bounded continuous, allowing us to use Portmanteau Theorem applied to the weakly convergent sequence of marginals
\gamma_{k_{j}}

(C.11)

\int f_{R}d\gamma^{\prime}=\lim f_{R}d\gamma_{k_{j}}\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int|x-y|^{2}\gamma_{k_{j}}(dx,dy)=\liminf_{j\to\infty}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{k_{j}},\nu_{k_{j}}).

By assumption we have weak convergence of the marginals, thus from the
W_{2}
triangle inequality

\left|W_{2}(\mu_{k_{j}},\nu_{k_{j}})-W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\right|\leq W_{2}(\mu_{k_{j}},\mu)+W_{2}(\nu_{k_{j}},\nu)\to 0

so that the liminf on the RHS of (
C.11
) is exactly
W_{2}(\mu,\nu)
. Then taking via monotone convergence theorem,
\mathbb{R}\to\infty
in (
C.11
) delivers

\int|x-y|^{2}\gamma^{\prime}(dx,dy)\leq W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu),

thus
\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}
is indeed optimal.

This immediately gives weak convergence of the corresponding interpolants. Indeed the map
\varPhi_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(x,y)\mapsto(1-t)x+ty
is continuous, and so with the weak convergence of
\gamma_{k_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}\gamma^{\prime}
, the continuous mapping theorem gives

\eta_{t}^{k_{j}}=(\varPhi_{t})_{\#}\gamma_{k_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}(\varPhi_{t})\#\gamma^{\prime}\eqqcolon\eta_{t}^{\prime}

as desired. Thus we have shown weak convergence of
\eta_{t}^{k_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}\eta_{t}
along the subsequence
(k_{j})

We now show the convergence of the second moments. Having assumed convergence of the second moments for the marginals,

(C.12)

\int\left(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\right)\gamma_{k_{j}}(dx,dy)=\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mu_{k_{j}})+\mathcal{M}_{2}(\nu_{k_{j}})\to\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mu)+\mathcal{M}_{2}(\nu)=\int\left(\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}\right)\gamma^{\prime}(dx,dy).

The modulus is convex, thus
g(x,y)=\|(1-t)x+ty\|^{2}
satisfies
g(x,y)\leq\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}
. (
C.12
) shows that
\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}
is an integrable dominating function for
g(x,y)
, establishing uniform integrability of
g
under
(\gamma_{k_{j}})
. For
R>0
we split

(C.13)

\int gd\gamma_{k_{j}}=\int_{B_{R}\times B_{R}}gd\gamma_{k_{j}}+\int_{(B_{R}\times B_{R})^{c}}gd\gamma_{k_{j}}.

The second term vanishes uniformly in
j
as
R\to\infty
by the domination
g(x,y)\leq\|x\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}
paired with the tightness of the sequence
(\gamma_{k_{j}})
and the fact that the weak limit
\gamma^{\prime}
has finite second moment. For the first term,
g
is bounded continuous on the domain, thus by Portmanteau Theorem the weak convergence
\gamma_{k_{j}}\overset{w}{\to}\gamma^{\prime}
delivers

\int_{B_{R}\times B_{R}}g\gamma_{k_{j}}\to\int_{B_{R}\times B_{R}}\gamma^{\prime},

for each
R>0
, thus together we indeed find

\int gd\gamma_{k_{j}}\to\int gd\gamma^{\prime}

i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{2}(\eta_{t}^{k_{j}})\to\mathcal{M}_{2}(\eta_{t})
and we have shown convergence of the second moment along the subsequence
(k_{j})
,

Putting the weak convergence together with the convergence of the second moment, we conclude by Theorem
2.1
that along the subsequence
(k_{j})_{j\geq 1}
,
W_{2}(\eta_{t}^{k_{j}},\eta_{t})\to 0
.

To conclude convergence of the full sequence
\eta_{t}^{k}

\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the optimal coupling is unique (induced by a Brenier map), hence any limit point of couplings

must be equal to
\gamma^{\prime}
. By a standard result, if every subsequence has a further subsequence converging to the same limit, then the whole sequence converges. Thus we may conclude
W_{2}(\eta_{t}^{k},\eta_{t})\to 0
as

.
∎

C.6.
Mollified empirical measures

The empirical measures
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\omega),\hat{\nu}_{n}(\omega)
are always discrete and as such lie in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\setminus L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. While we’ve thus far been able to establish
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
convergence results (for both the true measures and the geodesics), the discrete empirical measures are incompatible with the gradient theory proposed in section
3.4
which takes place in
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(unless we have a Wasserstein
C^{1}
functional which allows us to extend the results to all of
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
). In particular, there is no Brenier map from
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\omega)
to
\hat{\nu}_{n}(\omega)
, so we cannot sensibly identify tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\hat{\mu}_{n}(\omega)\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
in the same manner as we did for absolutely continuous measures and so we have no immediately compatible notion to lift the gradient structure from
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
without enforcing constraints on the types of functionals we consider.

A natural alternate direction, is to consider a
mollification
of
\hat{\mu}_{n}(\omega)
via a convolution with a Gaussian kernel with some bandwidth
\sigma>0
, producing smooth, absolutely continuous approximations to
\hat{\mu}_{n}
(and hence
\mu
itself) in
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Such a direction has proven popular of late (
[
NGK21
]
,
[
Wan23
]
,
[
FN24
]
,
[
ZM24
]
,
[
CEF20
]
). We predominantly follow the analysis of
[
GGPNW19
]
. Taking
n\to\infty,\sigma(n)\to 0
(at some appropriate rate) recover convergence to
\mu
in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, while retaining absolute continuity and hence the Riemannian structure of
L^{2}_{W,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition C.9

(Mollified empirical measure)
.

Let
Z_{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
be an isotropic Gaussian on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of scale
\sigma>0
. The (Gaussian) mollified empirical measure at bandwidth
\sigma
is given by

(C.28)

\hat{\mu}_{n}^{\sigma}(\omega)\coloneqq\hat{\mu}_{n}(\omega)*Z_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}(X_{i}(\omega),\sigma^{2}I_{d})\in\mathcal{P}_{2,ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

which admits a smooth density

(C.29)

\hat{p}_{n}^{\sigma}(x,\omega)=\frac{1}{n(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x-X_{i}(\omega)\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right).

We also define the corresponding smoothed population measure,
\mu^{\sigma}\coloneqq\mu*Z_{\sigma}
.

In proposition
C.12
we will verify the convergence of
\hat{\mu}_{n}^{\sigma(n)}
to
\mu
both pathwise and in
L^{2}_{W}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as
n\to\infty
,
\sigma(n)\to 0
via a triangle inequality decomposition

W_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{n}^{\sigma(n)}(\omega),\mu)\leq W_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{n}^{\sigma(n)}(\omega),\mu^{\sigma(n)})+W_{2}(\mu^{\sigma(n)},\mu).

In preparation, we collect the following lemma.

Lemma C.10
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\sigma>0
. Then

(C.30)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\sigma},\mu)\leq d\sigma^{2}.

Proof.

Let
X\sim\mu
and
Z\sim Z_{\sigma}
. Define the coupling
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\mu,\mu^{\sigma}}
as the joint law of
(X,X+Z)
. The first marginal is
\mu
, and second marginal is given by
\mu^{\sigma}
(as an independent convolution of the two), hence indeed
\widetilde{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{\mu,\mu^{\sigma}}
. By the sub-optimality of this coupling we can bound the Wasserstein distance

(C.31)

W_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\sigma},\mu)\leq\int\|x-y\|^{2}\widetilde{\gamma}(dx,dy)=\mathbb{E}\left[\|X-(X+Z)\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}[\|Z_{\sigma}\|^{2}]=d\sigma^{2},

where the last equality uses the fact that
Z_{\sigma}
is a d-dimensional isotropic Gaussian.
∎

A result of Goldfeld et al.

.

D.1.
Proof of corollary

: Corollary to Schwartz’s Theorem under the weak topology

Proof.

A
\mu

\pi

\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which can be represented by arbitrary unions of basis elements, given by subsets of the form

\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\forall i\in\{1,\dots,k\},\left|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[g_{i}]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[g_{i}]\right|<\varepsilon_{i}\right\}

is bounded continuous,

and

U\coloneqq\left\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[g]<\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[g]+\varepsilon\right\}

and instead show that the probability of the complements of such sets vanish almost surely, i.e.
\mathbb{P}_{\Pi}(U^{c}|X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\to 0

\pi^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}
-a.s. and we only have to verify the testing conditions on these sets. Define

(D.1)

\varPhi_{n}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})={\rm 1}\kern-2.40005pt{\rm I}\left\{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g(X_{i})>\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[g]+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right\}.

which satisfies the testing conditions. Thus, to guarantee weak posterior consistency, we need only to verify that the condition that the prior has positive KL support for the true density.

E.1.
Lipschitz constant for the interaction energy on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}

For ease of notation, let
h_{\mu}(x)=\int e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}\beta y\,\mu(dy)
with
v_{\mu}(x)={\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}(h_{\mu}(x))
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\mu,\nu}
be an optimal coupling so that

h_{\mu}(x)-h_{\nu}(x)=\beta\iint(e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}y-e^{\beta\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle}y^{\prime})\gamma(dy,dy^{\prime}).

Considering the integrand, the triangle inequality provides

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}y-e^{\beta\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle}y^{\prime}\right\|\leq\left|e^{\beta\langle x,y\rangle}-e^{\beta\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle}\right|\|y\|+e^{\beta\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle}\|y-y^{\prime}\|\leq(\beta+1)e^{\beta}\|y-y^{\prime}\|

where we have used the mean value theorem to bound the first difference. Using the bounds on the flow,

\|v_{\mu}(x)-v_{\nu}(x)\|\leq\|h_{\mu}(x)-h_{\nu}(x)\|\leq(\beta+1)e^{\beta}\int\|y-y^{\prime}\|\gamma(dy,dy^{\prime})\leq(\beta+1)e^{\beta}W_{2}(\mu,\nu),

where the last inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwartz. Thus the first Lipschitz constant
L_{1}=(\beta+1)e^{\beta}
.

Working towards the other Lipschitz constant, we first decompose

v_{\mu}(x)-v_{\nu}(x^{\prime})={\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}(h_{\mu}(x)-h_{\nu}(x^{\prime}))+({\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}-{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x^{\prime}})h_{\mu}(x^{\prime}).

Again from the mean-value theorem, the first term
h_{\mu}(x)-h_{\nu}(x^{\prime})\leq\beta e^{\beta}\|x-x^{\prime}\|
, and as the projection retains the inequality here,

\|{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}(h_{\mu}(x)-h_{\nu}(x^{\prime}))\|\leq\beta e^{\beta}\|x-x^{\prime}\|.

For the second term, as
\|h_{\mu}(x^{\prime})\|\leq e^{\beta}
, using the inequality on the projection

\|({\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x}-{\mathbf{P}}^{\perp}_{x^{\prime}})h_{\mu}(x^{\prime})\|\leq 2e^{\beta}\|x-x^{\prime}\|.

Thus, combining the two, we find
L_{2}=(\beta+2)e^{\beta}
. Combining the constants as in the proof of proposition
4.12
, we find
L=L_{1}^{2}+1+2L_{2}
, so that
