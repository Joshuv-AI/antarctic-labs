The relationship between psychological resilience and well-being among older adults
Authors: E Sarac, E Yildiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychological resilience, the capacity to adapt positively in the face of adversity, has emerged as a key internal resource that may buffer older adults against stressors such as chronic illness, functional decline, bereavement, and social isolation. As populations age globally, understanding the role of resilience in sustaining quality of life becomes increasingly important for individuals, families, and healthcare systems.AimTo examine the association between psychological resilience and well-being among older adults.MethodsUsing a cross-sectional design, a convenience sample of 196 volunteer older adults (≥ 65 years) was recruited from a university hospital setting, with data collected via face-to-face interviews to ensure clinical and cognitive suitability. Data were collected using the demographic characteristics form, the “Resilience Evaluation Scale,” and “World Health Organization Well-Being Index”.ResultsThe mean age of older adults was 68.07 ± 2.71. The average scores for “psychological resilience” and “well-being” were 22.47 ± 7.45 and 10.35 ± 6.39. The findings indicated a significant positive correlation between psychological resilience and overall well-being. While a significant difference was observed in resilience and well-being scores according to variables such as gender, educational status, having a chronic disease and having a relative who is a healthcare worker (P < 0.001), no significant difference was found in terms of other variables (P > 0.05).ConclusionAdults aged 65 and above in this study demonstrated a moderate level of resilience and lower well-being scores. Higher resilience scores were significantly correlated with increased subjective well-being. These results suggest that resilience acts as a vital internal resource that relates to mental health outcomes in the aging population, highlighting the potential for interventions focused on enhancing psychological strengths.Keywords: Psychological resilience, Well-being, Older adults, Self-efficacy, Self-confidence, Geriatric mental health

Introduction

The global demographic shift toward an aging population represents more than just a statistical change; it represents a profound psychological challenge for aging individuals due to their increased risk of chronic illness, bereavement, and social isolation [1]. In Turkey the elderly (aged ≥ 65 years) account for ≈9.7% of the total population, yet the national healthcare framework remains primarily focused on the “deficit model”, which prioritizes physical pathology over psychological strengths [2, 3]. This descriptive focus on multi-systemic physical and cognitive decline fails to account for the dynamic buffering mechanisms that allow older adults to maintain a high level of well-being with individuals reporting a mean self-rated successful aging score of 8.2 out of 10 despite functional losses [1].

Analytical research suggests that psychological resilience serves as a critical internal resource, enabling individuals to adapt positively to adversity [4], however, resilience is not a static trait; it is a socio-ecological process shaped by cultural context [3]. In the Turkish context, in which elder care is traditionally viewed as a family duty, the intersection of individual self-efficacy and collective social support creates a unique environment for resilience [5]. When family support is unavailable or families become overwhelmed by providing such support, the lack of tailored psychological assistance services in Turkey becomes a significant risk factor for the well-being of the elderly [6].

Despite the theoretical importance of resilience, there remains a significant gap in the Turkish geriatric literature. Whereas the Turkish version of the Resilience Evaluation Scale [7] was recently reported to be reliable and valid, its psychometric utility was initially analyzed only among university students There remains the necessity to analytically evaluate whether the scale’s dimensions of self-efficacy and self-confidence similarly function to buffer the impact of chronic disease on subjective well-being in an elderly sample (≥ 65 years). By examining these relationships within a university hospital setting, the present study aimed to move beyond a descriptive account of aging to analyze how internal resources can be leveraged to protect mental health in a rapidly aging society.

Whereas the relationship between resilience and well-being has been studied internationally, the expression of these constructs is deeply embedded in cultural and socio-economic contexts. The present study provides critical data on a specific elderly population from a Turkish hospital in which such sociodemographic factors as level of education and family structure significantly affect psychological outcomes.

Conceptual clarification: resilience versus subjective well-being

So as to ensure theoretical precision and address the potential overlap between psychological constructs, the present study differentiated between resilience as a “process/capacity” and well-being as a “state/outcome”. Resilience for the elderly is defined as the internal capacity to adapt positively to the stressors and challenges associated with aging. It was conceptualized not as a fixed state, but as a dynamic resource consisting of self-efficacy defined as the individual’s judgment of their capabilities to organize and execute the courses of action required to manage age-related stressors, such as health declines, shifts in social roles, and the maintenance of daily living activities and self-confidence (the belief in one’s value and capabilities) [7]. Resilience is the mechanism via which an individual processes adversity [1]. In contrast, well-being is defined as a current subjective state of mental health, encompassing feelings of vitality, interest, and positive mood [8]. The World Health Organization-Five Well-Being Index (WHO-5) [9, 10] is used to measure an individual’s psychological adaptation to their environment at a specific point in time.

Theoretical framework of the study

The relationship between these two constructs is predictive rather than synonymous. In our analytical model, resilience functions as a buffering mechanism; it is the psychological toolset that predicts the level of subjective well-being an older adult experience [1]. This is evidenced by our finding that as the internal capacity (resilience) increases, the reported mental health state (well-being) improves significantly. By maintaining this distinction, the study avoids treating resilience and well-being as identical, instead focusing on how specific internal strengths relate to current mental health status in the geriatric population [11].

Furthermore, the concept of psychological well-being should be considered not only in terms of measurable individual indicators, but also in the context of the meaning of life, sense of value, and the fulfillment of emotional needs [12]. Psychological resilience is considered one of the important elements for not only maintaining life but also living a meaningful and high-quality life in old age [7]. Studies on older adults in Turkey have increased since the 2000s. Before this period, the elderly in the country mostly lived with their children. Therefore, older individuals did not have difficulties in managing their daily lives independently and consequently did not experience psychological and physical problems [13]. However, the changing social structure in Turkey, especially since the 2000 s, has forced the elderly to live alone. They have had to struggle alone with many problems such as economic difficulties and physical limitations.

In this context, this study, which will be conducted on well-being and resilience variables, aims to obtain unique data and produce effective results in improving the quality of life of Turkish elderly individuals. This study fills a critical gap by shifting from the traditional deficit-focused research in Turkey to a strength-based perspective. It specifically addresses the lack of resilience data in the rapidly growing Turkish elderly population. Furthermore, it provides the first application of the recently validated RES scale in a Turkish geriatric sample, moving beyond its initial validation in younger populations. Based on the theoretical framework of positive aging and the existing literature regarding internal resources in later life, the following hypotheses were formulated for this study;

Hypothesis 1 (H1): There is a significant positive correlation between psychological resilience (specifically self-efficacy and self-confidence) and subjective well-being among older adults.

Materials and methods

Study design, population, and sample

This study employed a cross-sectional research design. The sample consisted of 196 volunteer older adults aged 65 years and above who attended Ataturk University Hospital for treatment. The respondents provided written informed consent in the current study and the privacy rights of the participants have been observed. Participants were selected using a convenience sampling method. The inclusion criteria were (a) being 65 years or older, (b) being cognitively healthy, and (c) providing voluntary consent to participate in the study.

Participants were recruited between April 1 and June 25, 2025, from various outpatient clinics at a university hospital. A convenience sampling method was used to identify potential participants. Older adults attending outpatient services were approached by the researcher in hospital waiting areas and were informed about the purpose of the study and its procedures. Information was also provided regarding the study instruments, including the demographic information form, the Resilience Evaluation Scale, and the WHO-5 Well-Being Index. Individuals who expressed interest in participating were screened for eligibility based on the inclusion criteria. Cognitive health was assessed informally through the researcher’s evaluation of the participant’s ability to engage in a coherent face-to-face conversation and to comprehend the study procedures and questionnaire items. Eligible participants were provided with detailed information about the study and signed a written informed consent form before participation. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Ataturk University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No. 02). Data were collected through face-to-face interviews conducted by the researcher. Each interview lasted approximately 15 min. For participants who experienced difficulties completing the questionnaire independently, the researcher recorded their verbal responses directly on the survey form to ensure accurate data collection. A limitation of this study is that cognitive health was determined through informal assessment rather than through a standardized cognitive screening instrument, such as the Mini-Mental State Examination. Although face-to-face interviews allowed the researcher to evaluate participants’ general comprehension and responsiveness, the absence of an objective cognitive assessment increases the possibility that some individuals with mild cognitive impairment were included in the sample. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution,, as cognitive functioning may influence self-reported measures of resilience and well-being.

Data collection

The data collection process was carried out between April 1 and June 25, 2025. Participants were informed about the study and the survey before data collection. Data were collected with a questionnaire divided into three sections as follows: “the demographic characteristics form” (created by the authors, including demographic features of participants), “Resilience Evaluation Scale (RES)”, and “World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5).

Demographic characteristics form

The form included information on age, gender, marital status, education, income status, presence of chronic diseases, living alone or not, and having a relative who is a healthcare worker. This form also included an open-ended question as follows: “What do you do to feel good in your daily life?”.

Resilience Evaluation Scale (RES)

The Resilience Evaluation Scale (RES) is a 9-item scale that assesses psychological resilience factors of individuals. It was developed by Meer et al. [14] and validated in the Turkish context by Turkkan et al. [7]. The RES has a two-factor structure. One of the sub-dimensions is self-confidence (trust in oneself), reflecting three items, and the other one is self-efficacy (positive beliefs about adaptive coping with stressful events or situations), clustering six items. The scale has a 5-point response range from 0 to 4 as follows: strongly disagree (0), disagree (1), undecided (2), agree (3), and strongly agree (4). A total score can be calculated by summing the individual item scores and ranges from 0 to 36. Higher scores indicate greater resilience. While the internal consistency of the RES in the original scale was 0.89, in the Turkish context, it was 0.90. It was found as 0.94 in our study, indicating high internal consistency.

World Health Organization Well-Being Index (WHO-5)

WHO-5 is a 5-item scale that is a short and effective measurement for the detection of the mental health of individuals [9, 10]. It has one-factor structure. The overall raw score ranges from 0 (absence of well-being) to 25 (highest level of well-being). A 100-point scale score can also be calculated by multiplying the raw score by four. Cronbach’s alpha value was 0.83 for the Turkish context. In our paper, it was found as 0.94 [15].

Data analyses

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.00. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test. Since the data were found to be normally distributed, parametric statistical tests were employed. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for all scales and subscales. Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships between age and the study variables. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare mean scores between two-group categorical variables (e.g., gender and marital status), whereas one-way ANOVA was used to compare mean scores across variables with more than two categories (e.g., education level and income status). When significant differences were identified, Games–Howell post hoc tests were conducted to determine pairwise group differences. Total and subscale scores were calculated according to the scoring procedures of the respective instruments.

To identify factors independently associated with psychological resilience and subjective well-being, separate multiple linear regression analyses were conducted for each outcome variable. Age, gender, marital status, education level, income level, presence of chronic disease, living arrangement (living alone versus living with others), and having a healthcare professional relative were included as independent variables. All predictors were entered simultaneously into the regression models to evaluate their unique contributions while controlling for the effects of the remaining variables.

The demographic information form included an open-ended question asking participants, “What do you do to feel good in your daily life?” Responses were analyzed using a descriptive thematic approach. Similar activities and coping strategies were grouped into thematic categories, and the frequency of each category was calculated as a percentage of the total sample. This analysis was conducted to identify commonly reported factors contributing to subjective well-being. For example, “conversation with my friends” emerged as the most frequently reported strategy, reported by 39.3% of participants.

To reduce the risk of Type I error (false positives) associated with multiple statistical comparisons, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to analyses involving sociodemographic and clinical variables. Eight independent variables—age, gender, marital status, education level, income level, presence of chronic disease, living alone, and having a healthcare professional relative—were examined in relation to four primary outcomes: total resilience, self-competence, self-confidence, and WHO-5 well-being scores. By applying a rigorous threshold, we ensured that findings regarding the impact of gender, chronic disease, and education on resilience represent robust associations rather than random statistical noise. This is particularly relevant for our clinical sample, in which 59.7% of participants had chronic conditions, necessitating a high degree of statistical confidence when identifying true predictors of well-being.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

The average age was 68.07 ± 2.71 years, while the minimum was 65, and the maximum was 79 in our research. The study population comprised 40,3. % women and 65,8% married. While 10,2% were literate, 59.7 had chronic diseases. 49.5% of the participants reported their income as being equal to their expenses. In regard to cohabitation status, 69.9% were living alone, while 61.7% had a relative who was a healthcare worker. In response to the open-ended question “What do you do to feel good?” 39.3% of the participants responded “conversation with my friends” at the highest level. Analyses of resilience evaluation scale and WHO-5 index

The total score for RES was 22.47 ± 7.45, while the scores for self-efficacy and self-confidence were 14.51 ± 5.15 and 7.96 ± 2.59. Additionally, the total score for WHO-5 was found as 10.35 ± 6.39. Overall, participants demonstrated a moderate level of resilience and a lower level of well-being. The World Health Organization-5 Well-Being Index is a raw scoring system ranging from 0 (absence of well-being) to 25 (highest level of well-being). To standardize these results, a 100-point scale score can be calculated by multiplying the raw score by four. When converted to the 100-point scale mentioned in the methodology, this mean of 10.35 corresponds to a score of 41.4, which falls well below the midpoint of the scale.

Comparative analysis of RES and well-being scores across demographic characteristics

Gender, education level, and the presence of a chronic disease were significantly associated with psychological resilience and well-being (p < 0.05). In contrast, age, marital status, and income level were not significantly associated with either outcome (p > 0.05). Furthermore, living alone was significantly associated with self-confidence scores, whereas having a healthcare worker relative was significantly associated with self-efficacy scores (p < 0.05). Male participants reported higher mean scores on both psychological resilience (24.87 ± 6.34) and well-being (11.21 ± 6.72) than female participants. Because the assumption of homogeneity of variance was violated, Games–Howell post hoc analyses were conducted following significant ANOVA results. Participants with a university education demonstrated higher levels of resilience (25.19 ± 6.85) and well-being (13.65 ± 6.39) than those with lower educational attainment. Similarly, participants without chronic diseases reported higher resilience (24.98 ± 6.95) and well-being (17.70 ± 6.00) scores than those with chronic diseases.

A significant difference in self-confidence was observed according to living arrangements (p = 0.019), with participants living alone reporting lower self-confidence scores (7.22 ± 3.08). In addition, self-efficacy scores differed significantly according to the presence of a healthcare professional relative (p < 0.05), with higher scores observed among participants who reported having a healthcare worker relative (15.50 ± 5.07). Discussion

The findings of the present study indicate that older adults demonstrated moderate levels of psychological resilience and relatively low levels of well-being. Existing evidence suggests that higher resilience is associated with a reduced risk of depression, greater life satisfaction, and increased perceived control, all of which contribute to a more active and fulfilling life in later years. Our findings are consistent with a growing body of literature reporting moderate to moderately high levels of resilience among community-dwelling older adults, reflecting their capacity to adapt to age-related challenges while still being vulnerable to stressors such as chronic illness, bereavement, and social isolation [16, 17]. Previous studies have documented similar resilience profiles in older populations, with resilience influenced by factors including social support, functional status, cognitive functioning, coping strategies, and perceived control [17, 18]. The consistency of these findings supports the conceptualization of resilience as a dynamic process rather than a fixed trait in later life.

In the present study, older adults also reported relatively low levels of well-being. This finding is consistent with previous research examining well-being among similar populations [19]. Notably, a significant proportion of participants (59.7%) reported having at least one chronic disease, and the presence of a chronic condition was significantly associated with lower well-being. Because the data were collected in a hospital setting, the sample likely included individuals experiencing ongoing health-related challenges, which are known to adversely affect well-being. This finding is consistent with previous research on well-being among older adults in clinical settings [15]. These findings should be interpreted within the context of the study’s cross-sectional design, which permits the identification of associations but does not allow conclusions regarding causality. For example, although resilience and well-being were positively associated, the present findings do not support the conclusion that resilience causes improvements in well-being. Rather, the results suggest that resilience and well-being coexist as related psychological resources in this population. Furthermore, the mean well-being score of 10.35 was below the standardized raw-score threshold of 13, a cutoff often utilized in clinical settings to identify individuals who may benefit from further assessment of emotional well-being. However, well-being in older age is highly heterogeneous and may be influenced by factors such as social support, meaningful engagement in activities, coping resources, and perceived purpose in life. Taken together, our findings align with the broader literature and highlight the need for targeted interventions to sustain or enhance well-being in later life.

Our analyses revealed significant associations between psychological resilience, well-being, and several sociodemographic factors, including gender, education level, and presence of chronic disease. These findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that psychological outcomes in later life are may vary according to demographic and social characteristics [19]. However, the present findings should be interpreted as associations observed within this hospital-based sample as associations observed within this hospital-based sample, and further research is required to explore the mechanisms underlying these relationships. In the present study, women reported lower levels of psychological resilience than men. One possible explanation is that older men may have had greater access to social and economic resources throughout their lives, which may contribute to higher resilience scores. Conversely, women in this age cohort may have experienced cumulative caregiving responsibilities, financial constraints, or other life-course stressors that could be associated with lower levels of resilience. While these interpretations align with the broader literature on sociodemographic influences on health and well-being in later life (e.g., [19]), longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the pathways through which gender-related experiences influence psychological resilience among older adults. Educational attainment was also positively associated with both psychological resilience and well-being. Resilience scores increased progressively across education levels. These findings suggest that higher educational attainment may be associated with greater health literacy, enhanced cognitive resources, and broader social support networks, all of which may contribute to better psychological functioning in later life [20–22]. Notably, university-educated older adults reported substantially higher well-being scores than participants with lower levels of education. This finding may reflect educational inequalities that are particularly relevant within the local context and highlights the importance of considering educational attainment in the development of policies and interventions aimed at promoting healthy aging.

Our results indicate that participants without chronic diseases reported higher psychological resilience and well-being than those with chronic conditions. This finding aligns with the broader literature, as chronic illnesses can impose ongoing stress, functional limitations, and symptom burdens that undermine coping resources and life satisfaction. Consistent with Liu et al. [19], health status interacts with demographic and psychosocial factors to shape aging outcomes, underscoring the importance of effective chronic disease management and preventive care in promoting resilience and well-being. The observed association may reflect several underlying mechanisms, including lower physical symptom burden, greater functional independence, and enhanced perceived control and self-efficacy.

Our findings indicate that older adults living alone are more likely to report higher self-confidence, which may reflect a resilience mechanism involving the maintenance of routines, perceived control, and social ties. As a component of resilience, self-confidence helps distinguish resilience as a dynamic adaptive process rather than a fixed trait, although it is also shaped by prior experiences, personality, and social support [23]. However, living alone may also pose risks, such as social isolation, safety concerns, and reduced access to care. Therefore, it is important to contextualize whether higher self-confidence reflects successful aging in place or masks unmet needs [24]. The present findings are consistent with previous research.

In the present study, having a healthcare worker in the family was associated with significantly higher self-efficacy compared to not having such family members. Additionally, these participants reported higher total resilience scores and greater subjective well-being. While we previously implied that this exposure reinforces personal beliefs, we now frame this as a hypothesis: access to a relative with medical expertise may serve as a psychological resource that facilitates healthcare navigation and enhances perceived control. Future research is needed to determine whether this relationship is indeed driven by “insider knowledge” or by other social support mechanisms. This suggests that access to a healthcare worker in the family may represent a unique psychological resource that buffers the impact of aging, offering a novel perspective for future social support research.

This lack of association diverges from some literature suggesting that marital status and marital satisfaction are typically related to well-being and quality of life in later years. These findings may be associated with cohort- or life-stage characteristics unique to this population. For instance, in certain social contexts, the psychological state of older adults may be more closely linked to strong community ties or established social safety nets than to marital status alone [25, 26]. Furthermore, in our sample, the high prevalence of chronic illness may be a more prominent correlate of psychological health than whether an individual is married or single. Consistent with our cross-sectional design, these results represent the absence of an observed association at a single point in time and highlight that the psychological outcomes of older adults in this setting appear to be independent of marital status.

Contrary to some existing literature, our findings indicate that age and income status were not significantly associated with psychological resilience or well-being. Specifically, age showed no significant correlation with total resilience scores or well-being. Similarly, no significant differences in these psychological resources were observed across different income levels. These results diverge from studies documenting a negative association between ageism and mental health or a strong link between socioeconomic status and well-being [27, 28]. This lack of association in our study may stem from the narrow age distribution of the sample, which primarily consisted of individuals in the 65–74-year age bracket and may have lacked the variability needed to detect age-related effects observed in older cohorts, such as those aged 80 years and above. Furthermore, the divergence regarding income status may reflect the specific cultural and clinical context of this hospital-based sample, where factors such as the high prevalence of chronic illness, as well as geographic and health-status-related characteristics, may exert a greater influence on psychological health than purely economic factors.

In our study, 40% of participants identified conversation and communication with friends when asked, “What do you do to feel good in your daily life?” This finding highlights the central role of social bonds in the aging process and suggests that, for many older adults, social interaction is a preferred resource for maintaining emotional well-being. Frequent interaction with friends may serve as an important resource associated with higher life satisfaction, providing validation and empathy that can buffer against the loneliness frequently reported in geriatric populations [29, 30].

Limitations of the research

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences between resilience and well-being. Second, the reliance on self-reported instruments may introduce social desirability bias and recall bias. Older adults, in particular, may exhibit a positivity effect, potentially leading to inflated scores on well-being and resilience scales. In our study conducted at the university hospital, the limited availability of resources for the elderly can be cited as a limitation of our research. Future longitudinal studies utilizing objective psychological assessments are recommended to validate these findings.

A significant limitation of this study is the use of a convenience sampling method within a single university hospital setting. This approach introduces potential selection bias, as the participants were recruited from individuals already seeking or present in a clinical environment, which may not be representative of the broader community-dwelling older population in Turkey. For instance, individuals who volunteer for research in a hospital setting may possess higher health literacy or different levels of psychological distress compared to those in the general population. Consequently, the representativeness of the sample is limited, and the findings, specifically the moderate resilience and lower well-being scores, may not be fully generalizable to older adults in different geographic regions or socioeconomic contexts. Future research should utilize stratified random sampling across multiple centers and community settings to enhance external validity and provide a more comprehensive understanding of resilience dynamics in the geriatric population.

Conclusion

The psychological resilience levels of older adults were moderate, while well-being levels were low in our study. Demographic features, gender, education, and having a chronic disease were effective factors on resilience and well-being. Male participants and those who did not have chronic diseases were more prone to have higher resilience, self-efficacy and self-confidence, and well-being in comparison to females. Moreover, older adults with a bachelor’s degree and with a healthcare worker relative had higher resilience, self-efficacy and self-confidence, and well-being. Participants who were living alone had lower self-confidence. Older individuals who participated in our study preferred to have more conversations with their friends to feel better. For future papers, this study needs to be conducted on a large sample size, including older thresholds as 80 +.
