Clinicopathological features, immunophenotyping, and immunohistochemical biomarkers associated with germline BRCA1/2 variants in breast cancer: a retrospective cohort of 265 patients
Authors: Feifei Huang, Zhongyi Tong, Yi Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) progression and treatment response are influenced by germline BRCA1/2 (gBRCA) variant status. Carrying BRCA1/2 gene variants increases an individual’s risk of developing breast cancer. With the advent of poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, these patients may achieve disease control and effective treatment. However, the clinicopathological characteristics and immunophenotypes associated with this gene in breast cancer remain incompletely understood. A total of 265 patients with invasive breast cancer were retrospectively enrolled and tested for gBRCA variants. Germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants are associated with distinct clinicopathological features, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) enrichment, and adverse prognosis. Integrating BRCA1/2 testing with immunohistochemical (IHC) biomarkers (including programmed death-ligand 1(PD-L1) in TNBC) may help refine risk stratification and inform individualized therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57652-6.Keywords: Breast cancer, Germline BRCA1/2, Triple-negative breast cancer, PD-L1, p53, PrognosisSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Genetics, Oncology

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide and remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality1,2. Immunophenotyping profiling based on estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and the proliferation marker Ki-67 enables clinical subtype classification that guides therapeutic decision-making and prognostic assessment3. Beyond these core markers, additional biomarkers and genomic alterations—including germline BRCA1/2 (gBRCA) variants, tumor protein P53 (TP53) alterations, programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) —have gained increasing importance for risk assessment and treatment selection4–7.

Among the breast cancer population, 5%–10% of patients have clear hereditary genetic variants and are classified as having hereditary breast cancer (HBC). Susceptibility genes associated with this type of cancer include BRCA1/2, ATM, CDH1, LKB1, PTEN, PALB2, TP53, and CHEK28,9. Different gene variants exhibit specific associations with breast cancer. For instance, after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for triple-negative breast cancer, patients with CDH1 variants show a higher rate of pathological complete response (pCR). Variants in PALB2/MLH1 increase the risk of developing bilateral breast cancer, while ATM variants are more strongly associated with a family history of cancer8–10. Under the influence of these susceptibility genes, this breast cancer population is more likely to develop ovarian cancer, with a comorbidity rate of 7.3%10,11. Among all genetic variants, BRCA1/2 variants account for 15% and exhibit stronger mutational effects than variants in other genes12. Individuals carrying such variants have a 40%–80% probability of developing breast cancer13,14. Studies have shown that breast cancer patients with BRCA variants tend to have a poorer prognosis. This underscores the urgent need for the further implementation of precision diagnosis and treatment strategies in clinical practice.

Pathogenic germline variants in BRCA1/2 may influence tumor biology and treatment sensitivity15–17. As key tumor suppressor genes, BRCA1 and BRCA2 are involved in the homologous recombination repair of DNA double-strand breaks. When cells cannot properly perform homologous recombination repair—for example, due to BRCA gene variants—they rely on alternative repair pathways, such as non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which is less accurate and error-prone. NHEJ leads to the accumulation of additional variants and chromosomal instability, thereby increasing the risk of malignant transformation18,19. The gBRCA variant rate is higher in HER2⁻ breast cancer than in HER2⁺ breast cancer20, and 60%–80% of gBRCA-mutated breast cancers are triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) 21. Patients with this type of breast cancer have a poorer prognosis, underscoring the urgent need for the further implementation of precision diagnosis and treatment strategies. BRCA1/2-deficient tumors may benefit from platinum-based chemotherapy and poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors through synthetic lethality22–25.

PD-L1 testing on tumors is recommended because the expression level of this protein may indicate potential benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors. The predictive value of PD-L1 has not been definitively established; however, studies have shown that PD-L1 inhibitors, when used in combination with chemotherapy in advanced and metastatic breast cancer, can improve the pCR rate26,27. The presence of TILs in tumors has been shown to have prognostic and predictive value for treatment response in TNBC and HER2-positive breast cancer7,28. In several clinical trials, TILs have been identified as a predictor of pCR following chemotherapy28. We are curious as to whether there is an association between these tumor-intrinsic markers and the BRCA gene.

Notably, differences exist in the clinicopathological features of breast cancers associated with BRCA1 versus BRCA2 variants. Breast cancers with BRCA1 variants predominantly exhibit a triple-negative phenotype (accounting for approximately 60–80%), high histological grade, elevated Ki-67 proliferation index, earlier age of onset (median around 43 years), and are often accompanied by abundant tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes. In contrast, breast cancers associated with BRCA2 variants more frequently present as the Luminal subtype (hormone receptor-positive), with a lower proportion of TNBC (approximately 16%), relatively later age of onset (median around 47 years), and a higher propensity for axillary lymph node metastasis29–31. These differences suggest that BRCA1 and BRCA2 may influence breast cancer tumorigenesis and progression through distinct biological mechanisms. However, the associations of gBRCA variants with clinicopathological parameters, immunophenotype distribution, and prognostic outcomes in real-world breast cancer cohorts remain to be further elucidated.

Based on this, in this retrospective cohort study, we evaluated associations between gBRCA status and clinicopathological parameters, immunohistochemical (IHC) biomarkers, immunophenotyping distribution, and progression outcomes in 265 patients with invasive breast cancer. We further explored PD-L1 and p53 patterns in TNBC versus non-TNBC across BRCA1/2 status.

Materials and methods

Study population and data collection

Clinical data were retrieved from the Laboratory Information System (LIS). This study consecutively enrolled patients with histologically confirmed invasive breast cancer who underwent gBRCA testing in the Department of Pathology, The Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, between September 2020 and June 2024. Patients with other malignancies or those who did not undergo IHC testing were excluded. After applying the above criteria, a total of 265 eligible patients were included. Follow-up data were censored on August 31, 2025. Nineteen patients had incomplete time data and were treated as censored in the statistical analysis. Follow-up times ranged from 1 to 170 months, with a median of 84.5 months. Tumor specimens were obtained by core needle biopsy or surgical resection and processed as formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded sections for histopathology and IHC. Three pathologists reviewed the cases based on retrospective reports and reached a consensus. Peripheral blood was collected for gBRCA testing. Extracted variables included age, sex, tumor size and location, histological grade, lymph node metastasis, lymphocytic infiltration, vascular invasion, family history of breast/ovarian cancer, treatment information, and follow-up outcomes. All cases met World Health Organization criteria for invasive breast carcinoma. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol meets the ethical demand of The Second Xiangya Hospital and was approved by the institutional ethics committee. The ethical approval number for this research project is (2021) Ethics Review (Clinical Research) No. (KO55). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Immunohistochemistry and FISH

Tissue samples were prepared into paraffin sections, were dewaxed in turpentine and graded ethanol. All prepared sections were subjected to IHC using the EliVision two-step method. The selected primary antibodies were all monoclonal antibodies purchased from China Fuzhou Maixin Biotech Co., Ltd., including: ER (Catalog No.: KIT-0012), PR (Catalog No.: KIT-0013), HER2 (Catalog No.: KIT-0043), Ki-67 (Catalog No.: RMA-0731), PD-L1 (Catalog No.: RMA-0854), and P53 (Catalog No.: RMA-0674). Other reagents were also purchased from Fuzhou Maixin Biotech Co., Ltd., including the secondary antibody kit [Elivision™ plus Polymer HP (Mouse/Rabbit) IHC Kit (Catalog No.: KIT-9901)], EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (Catalog No.: MVS-0098), DAB Chromogen (Catalog No.: DAB-0031), and PBS Buffer (Catalog No.: PBS-0060). All experiments were performed on the Lumatas BioSystems fully automated immunohistochemistry stainer (manufactured by Lumatas, USA, model: Titan S), strictly following the reagent instructions and the instrument’s standard operating procedures (SOP). ER/PR positivity was defined as ≥ 1% tumor nuclear staining32. HER2 was scored by IHC (0/1 + /2 + /3 +); IHC 2 + cases were reflexed to fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) for HER2 amplification. Ki-67 was dichotomized as high (≥ = 20%) versus low (< 20%) in line33. PD-L1 positivity was defined as tumor proportion score (TPS) ≥ 1%34. p53 was categorized as NULL (0%), LOW (1–60%), and HIGH (> 60%) to reflect recommended staining patterns. All slides were independently evaluated by experienced pathologists.

The preparation of sections for FISH experiments and the deparaffinization steps were consistent with IHC. After deparaffinization, the sections were boiled in distilled water at 100 °C for 20 min. The sections were then digested in pepsin solution at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by sequential immersion in two room-temperature 2 × SSC washing solutions for 5 min each. Subsequently, the sections were dehydrated in 70%, 80%, and 100% ethanol for 2 min each and air-dried at room temperature. The HER2/CEP17 probe purchased from Guangzhou LBP Medicine Science and Technology Co., Ltd.( Batch No.: 2407002, 2,412,001, 2,507,002, 2,501,001, 2,502,001, 2,505,001) was applied to the tissue sections, coverslipped, sealed with mounting medium, and placed in a hybridization instrument (Abbott, Model: ThermoBrite StatSpin) programmed for denaturation at 85 °C for 5 min and hybridization at 37 °C for 12–16 h. After hybridization, the coverslips were removed, and the sections were immersed in pre-warmed washing solution (2 × SSC/0.1% NP-40) at 47 °C for 10 min, followed by immersion in room-temperature 2 × SSC washing solution for 5 min. The sections were then dehydrated in 70%, 85%, and 100% ethanol for 2 min each and air-dried at room temperature. DAPI stain (provided with the probe kit) was applied, and after 5 min of incubation, the sections were observed. All steps from probe application to slide reading were performed in a dark room. The sections were interpreted by two senior pathologists, with results classified as positive (+) or negative (−).

Germline BRCA1/2 testing and variant classification

Genomic DNA was extracted from ethylene diamine tetraacetic Acid (EDTA)-anticoagulated peripheral blood. The BRCA1/2 gene variant testing was performed using the Human BRCA1/2 Gene Variant Detection Kit (Batch No.: 250811C04Z, 250317C01Z, 241113C04Z, 240703C01Z, 240228C02Z, 230920C02Z, 230520C01Z, 221122C10Z) manufactured by Xiamen AmoyDx Co., Ltd. in China. The concentration and quality of the extracted blood nucleic acid met the operational requirements of the detection kit: the DNA concentration of peripheral blood samples should be greater than 1.25 ng/μL, and the total amount should exceed 10 ng. This kit is based on an improved molecular inversion probe technology to construct DNA sequencing libraries and achieve variant detection in target regions through high-throughput sequencing. The experimental process involves hybridization with specially designed probes to specifically capture target regions, followed by extension, ligation, enzyme digestion, and amplification to enrich the target regions. Finally, after magnetic bead purification, a sequenceable library is obtained. Subsequently, sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq CN500 platform, and the sequencing data were analyzed for variant calling using the "Sequencing Data Analysis Software" analysis pipeline provided by AmoyDx Co., Ltd. The population databases used for data screening were 1000G, ExAC, and gnomAD, and the variant databases used were BRCAExchange and ClinVar. Variants were interpreted according to International Agency of Re-search on Cancer/American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (IARC/ACMG) criteria and categorized into five classes: class 5 (pathogenic), class 4 (likely pathogenic), class 3 (VUS), class 2 (likely benign), and class 1 (benign). For the primary analysis, classes 4–5 were grouped as pathogenic (P), and classes 1–3 were grouped as non-pathogenic (NP)20. A prespecified sensitivity analysis excluded VUS (class 3) and compared P (classes 4–5) against benign/likely benign (classes 1–2).

Definitions of immunophenotyping and outcomes

Patients were classified as having triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC; ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-negative) or non-TNBC based on IHC and FISH results.﻿ The primary outcome was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as time from diagnosis to first documented recurrence or metastasis, whichever occurred first. Second primary malignancy was analyzed as a secondary outcome and excluded in sensitivity analyses for PFS.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. Continuous variables were summarized using mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) depending on distribution. Kaplan–Meier curves were compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed (e.g., Schoenfeld residuals). Analyses were conducted on available (non-missing) data; the effective sample size (n) for each variable is indicated in table footnotes. Two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Where multiple biomarker comparisons were performed, results were interpreted as exploratory; false discovery rate control may be applied in supplementary analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics and association with BRCA1/2 status

Among 265 patients, 264 (99.6%) were female and 1 (0.4%) was male. Forty-three patients (16.2%) carried pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants (P group), and 222 (83.8%) were classified as NP. BRCA1/2 pathogenic status was associated with younger age, more advanced TNM stage, higher vascular invasion, higher lymphocytic infiltration, positive family history, and increased risk of progression outcomes (Table 1). No significant differences were observed for sex, lymph node metastasis, histologic type, histologic grade, tumor diameter, tumor location, or Miller–Payne grade after neoadjuvant therapy (where available). Additionally, we performed a prespecified analysis excluding cases with variants of uncertain significance (VUS). The distribution of VUS variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 was similar (13 for BRCA1 and 16 for BRCA2), and no substantial changes were observed. The statistical significance of these associations, along with the effect sizes and confidence intervals, remained similar to those of the primary analysis.

BRCA1/2 status and immunophenotyping distribution

TNBC was significantly enriched in the P group compared with the NP group (Fig. 2A). Among TNBC patients, the distribution of BRCA1 versus BRCA2 variants differed within the P group. In the P group, 84% (26/31) of TNBC patients harbored BRCA1 variants, whereas only 16% (5/31) carried BRCA2 variants (Fig. 2B). This pattern is consistent with previous reports that TNBC is more tightly linked to BRCA1 than to BRCA2 germline variants21. When stratified by immunophenotyping, PD-L1 positivity within TNBC was higher in the P group than in the NP group (Fig. 2C), whereas p53 high expression did not show a significant difference (Fig. 2D).

BRCA1/2 status and progression outcomes

Among the 265 patients with follow-up data, final outcomes were obtained for 246 patients, including 42 (17 with progression) in the P group and 204 (49 with disease progression) in the NP group. This included 31 (12 with progression) patients in the P group and 107 (22 with progression) in the NP group within the TNBC-enriched subset. Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated worse PFS in the P group compared with the NP group (log-rank P value shown in Fig. 2E). The median PFS was 985 days in the P group and 2071 days in the NP group. We further analyzed the TNBC-enriched subset and found that PFS remained significantly different between the P and NP groups (Fig. 2F). Within the TNBC-enriched subset, the median PFS was 967 days in the P group and 2110 days in the NP group. In Cox regression, BRCA1/2 pathogenic status was associated with an increased risk of progression outcomes in both unadjusted and adjusted models (Table 2). Given the wide confidence intervals, these findings should be interpreted considering event counts, model degrees of freedom, and potential collinearity among covariates.

Molecular genetic characteristics of germline BRCA1/2 variants

In total, 2840 BRCA1/2 variant records were collected across 265 reports (multiple variants per individual). Among these, there were 1191 BRCA1 variants (41.2%) and 1649 BRCA2 variants (58.8%), with a BRCA1-to-BRCA2 variant ratio of approximately 0.7:1 (Fig. 3). Figure 3 also shows the distribution of BRCA1 and BRCA2 variants across different classes. Class 1 variants accounted for the vast majority; due to their high frequency, only the ten most common variants are shown in Fig. 3. Variants of classes 2 to 5 are listed individually to illustrate their different variant types. BRCA2 variants accounted for a larger proportion of total records than BRCA1 in the overall dataset. Most variants were benign (class 1). Pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants (classes 4–5) were less frequent but more diverse in variant type and distribution, including missense, frameshift insertions/deletions, splice-site variants, and exon-level deletions (Fig. 3 AND Table S1). Pathogenic variants were more concentrated in BRCA1 than BRCA2 in this cohort.

Discussion

This study links gBRCA pathogenicity with a distinct clinicopathological profile characterized by younger age, TNBC enrichment, higher proliferation, and poorer progression outcomes. The observed lower ER/PR positivity and higher Ki-67 in the P group are consistent with a more aggressive phenotype and may partially explain the worse prognosis. Importantly, PD-L1 positivity was higher among TNBC cases with pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants, suggesting potential implications for immunotherapy-oriented biomarker assessment in this subgroup.

Early studies on BRCA gene in breast cancer primarily focused on their role as key components of the homologous recombination repair (HRR) pathway and the consequent therapeutic implications for PARP inhibitors35–37. In recent years, accumulating evidence has revealed a strong association between pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants and core breast cancer biomarkers, including ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-6738. Although the large population of patients with ER/PR-positive breast cancer accounts for a substantial number of gBRCA variants carriers, previous studies have clearly demonstrated that gBRCA variants are more highly enriched in TNBC and low hormone receptor-expressing subtypes, with frequencies reaching up to 12%21. High Ki-67 expression may provide additional useful information beyond standard diagnostic assessment in BRCA1/2-associated tumors, potentially indicating more aggressive tumor behavior and poorer prognosis in these patients The findings of the present study are generally consistent with this literature, further supporting the preferential distribution of gBRCA variants in aggressive biological subtypes. Clinically, this subgroup of patients warrants special attention and should be recommended for BRCA gene testing to enable targeted therapy directed at these variants.

During the initial data analysis phase, we performed exploratory comparative analyses of clinicopathological characteristics and immunophenotypes between patients carrying pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants in BRCA1 and those carrying such variants in BRCA2. However, due to the small sample size, the statistical results from the subgroup analyses had wide confidence intervals and lacked sufficient statistical power to generate meaningful or generalizable conclusions. Given that non-pathogenic variants have no definitive clinical value for prognostic assessment or treatment guidance, our primary analysis focused solely on the P group. In the P group, the immunophenotyping was predominantly enriched in TNBC (31/43), while the remaining 9 patients included 5 with BRCA1 variants and 4 with BRCA2 variants, all classified as Luminal B subtype. This finding is inconsistent with previous studies reporting that more than 75% of breast cancers with germline BRCA2 variants belong to the Luminal subtype21. We speculate that the limited sample size of the P group in this study prevented a comprehensive analysis of immune subtyping, resulting in no statistically significant scientific implication. Nevertheless, the enrichment of TNBC in the P group was predominantly driven by BRCA1 variants, which is consistent with previous research findings. This further supports the biological interpretation that BRCA1 dysfunction may underlie the phenotypic and therapeutic differences between the P group and the NP group. This is consistent with previous studies, as prior data have shown that 60–80% of breast cancers with gBRCA1 variants are triple-negative21. We can conclude that BRCA1-associated TNBC exhibits distinct genomic instability profiles and treatment responses.

Notably, this study provides novel findings beyond existing knowledge. Although we did not observe a significant advantage of gBRCA variants in relation to histological type or grade of breast cancer, gBRCA variants demonstrated potential value in early clinical subtyping and assessment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, which may play a key role in future treatment decisions. More importantly, we identified an independent association between gBRCA variants and PD-L1 expression, particularly within the TNBC subgroup. The relatively low proportion of Luminal B patients in our cohort objectively reduced the confounding effect of hormonal signals on the immune microenvironment, thereby allowing a clearer depiction of the intrinsic association between BRCA variants and PD-L1. These findings provide important clinical clues for future in-depth exploration of the biological mechanisms through which BRCA variants upregulate PD-L1 expression. Further validation of our findings is needed in larger, multicenter cohorts. Additionally, mechanistic studies should be conducted to elucidate the molecular links between the DNA damage response pathway and PD-L1 regulation, and to prospectively evaluate whether gBRCA variant status can serve as an independent predictive biomarker for the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Because the clinical adoption of PARP inhibitors and the promotion of BRCA1/2 testing have only begun in recent years, the tumor onset time of the included cases in this study was concentrated within five years. Given that early-stage breast cancer patients have a favorable prognosis and high overall survival, we focused on progression outcomes including recurrence, metastasis, and second primary malignancies. Follow-up analysis showed that tumor progression outcomes was significantly higher in group P than in group NP, suggesting that pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants are an unfavorable prognostic factor. Meanwhile, consistent results were observed in the TNBC-enriched subtype, which may reflect the inherently more aggressive biological characteristics of this subtype. The observed difference in PFS in the overall population may mainly stem from the discrepancy within the TNBC subtype, rather than a universal effect of BRCA variants across all immunophenotyping. Although significant differences were observed in the TNBC subtype, the limited sample size of other immunophenotyping precluded us from ruling out the possibility that BRCA variant status may exert distinct effects in non-TNBC subtypes. Larger sample sizes are required in future studies to conduct more comprehensive stratified analyses. In Cox regression analysis, clinical stage was associated with progression risk in univariate analysis, but this association disappeared after multivariate adjustment. In contrast, BRCA1/2 status was significantly associated with a higher risk of progression outcomes in both univariate and multivariate analyses, indicating that it may serve as a predictive biomarker for breast cancer prognosis.

Several methodological considerations warrant attention. First, grouping VUS with non-pathogenic variants can bias associations; therefore, we prespecified sensitivity analyses excluding VUS. The results showed no substantial changes compared with the primary analysis, with statistical significance, effect sizes and confidence intervals remaining comparable. This indicates that the inclusion or exclusion of VUS did not materially affect the study conclusions. Second, progression endpoints should ideally distinguish breast-cancer recurrence/metastasis from second primary malignancies; we therefore treated second primaries as secondary outcomes. Third, the adjusted Cox model yielded wide confidence intervals for BRCA1/2 status, indicating limited precision potentially due to a small number of events and/or model complexity. Future studies with larger cohorts, standardized treatment data, and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and clarify mechanisms.

Among the 265 germline testing reports, a total of 2840 BRCA1/2 variants were identified, of which BRCA2 accounted for 58.4% and BRCA1 for 41.2%. Benign variants were predominantly found in BRCA2, whereas pathogenic variants were more common in BRCA1. According to the ACMG classification, grade 1 variants had the highest proportion, with multiple high-frequency missense variants (e.g., BRCA2 c.4563A > G, BRCA1 c.2612C > T, etc.), which may represent relatively common variants in the Chinese population. Among grade 4 and grade 5 variants, grade 5 variants accounted for a higher proportion; all grade 4 variants were detected as single cases, mostly located in splice sites or early exons of BRCA1, suggesting potential sensitivity to PARP inhibitors. Grade 5 variants exhibited diverse types, with greater diversity observed in BRCA1. The ratio of BRCA1 to BRCA2 in pathogenic variants was approximately 1:0.38. Overall, the majority of variants were missense (approximately 99%), whereas pathogenic variants showed greater complexity and lacked clear hotspot variants, consistent with previous findings in Chinese populations39.

Conclusions

Germline pathogenic/likely pathogenic BRCA1/2 variants are associated with younger onset, TNBC enrichment, higher proliferative activity, and inferior progression outcomes in this real-world cohort. Routine integration of germline BRCA1/2 testing with IHC profiling—particularly PD-L1 assessment in TNBC—may improve individualized risk stratification and guide therapeutic decision-making.
