Robot-assisted versus laparoscopic partial nephrectomy for multiple ipsilateral renal tumours: surgical, functional and oncological outcomes
Authors: Changwei Shi, Cheng Peng, Jialong Song, Li Wang, Deqiang Lv, Yibo Chen, Senming Cao, Xinran Chen, Xin Ma, Qingbo Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the outcomes of laparoscopic partial nephrectomy (LPN) and robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) for the treatment of multiple ipsilateral renal tumours (MIRT). The primary outcomes were perioperative results, including the rate of postoperative complications and ‘Trifecta’ achievement. The secondary outcomes included renal functional preservation and oncological survival.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed the data of patients with MIRT who underwent LPN or RAPN between 2010 and 2024. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed to adjust for potential baseline confounders, resulting in a final matched cohort of 19 patients per group. Perioperative data and functional, oncological, and survival outcomes were reviewed.ResultsIn the matched cohort, the RAPN group was associated with significantly fewer postoperative complications (10.5% vs. 47.4%, P = 0.029). Notably, 88.9% (8/9) of the complications in the LPN group were Clavien–Dindo grade I. The RAPN group also showed a higher ‘Trifecta’ rate (63.2% vs. 21.1%, P = 0.020) and shorter postoperative hospital stay (4 vs. 6 days; P = 0.044). At 12 months postoperatively, the RAPN group demonstrated significantly lower absolute (9.1 vs. 18.4 µmol/L, P = 0.041) and percentage changes (12.0% vs. 25.9%, P = 0.024) in serum creatinine (Scr). No significant differences were identified in disease-free survival (P = 0.386), cancer-specific survival (P = 0.678), or overall survival (P = 0.116).ConclusionsFor patients with MIRT, RAPN demonstrates superior perioperative results and better preservation of renal function compared to LPN. These findings support the use of robotics as the preferred minimally invasive strategy for complex cases with two ipsilateral renal lesions, potentially enhancing surgical safety and functional outcomes.

Background

Multifocality of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is defined as the presence of two or more tumour foci within the same kidney, separated by intervening normal tissue [1]. Previous studies have reported an incidence ranging between 5% and 25% for multiple ipsilateral renal tumours (MIRT) [2, 3]. In patients with MIRT, prior studies have demonstrated comparable cancer-specific survival (CSS) and metastasis-free survival (MFS) between nephron-sparing surgery (NSS) and radical nephrectomy (RN) [4]. Given the high risk of multiple renal surgeries in patients with MIRT, the balance between renal function preservation and oncologic safety becomes critical and can be achieved through partial nephrectomy (PN) [5]. However, multifocal tumours pose significant technical challenges, including prolonged warm ischaemia time (WIT), increased estimated blood loss (EBL), and elevated incidence of postoperative complications [6]. With advancements in surgical techniques, robot-assisted surgery is increasingly being used in challenging urological cases [7, 8]. Robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) for MIRT was first reported by Rogers et al. in 2008 [9]. Recent studies have shown that RAPN is a feasible and safe treatment for MIRT [10, 11]. Although RAPN has gained prominence in complex nephron-sparing surgeries, particularly because of its technical advantages of precision and reduced ischaemia time, no direct comparative evidence for laparoscopic techniques in MIRT currently exists. This study aimed to evaluate the perioperative outcomes, postoperative renal function, and oncological outcomes of RAPN and laparoscopic partial nephrectomy (LPN) using a propensity score matching analysis.

Methods

Patients population

We conducted a retrospective data collection and analysis by accessing electronic medical records, laboratory reports, and imaging data from patients with RCC who sought consultation at the Department of Urology at People’s Liberation Army General Hospital between January 2010 and August 2024. Tumour number was assessed based on preoperative CT or MRI and pathology reports. Patients with recurrent or metastatic RCC, postoperative pathology-proven benign tumours, and those who did not undergo partial nephrectomy were excluded. Patients with three or more ipsilateral tumours were excluded to better compare the complexity of MIRT between the LPN and RAPN groups. Ultimately, 70 patients were included in the analysis. The patient inclusion flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The patients were divided into two groups based on the type of surgery: LPN (n = 20) and RAPN (n = 50). This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of our hospital (S2025-060-01).

Surgical technique

All LPN and RAPN procedures were performed by a dedicated team of surgeons with extensive experience at our centre, rather than a single surgeon. RAPN procedures were performed using the Da Vinci Si surgical system. The technical aspects of these procedures have been described in detail in prior publications [12, 13]. For each patient with MIRT, the surgical approach utilised either a trans- or retroperitoneal access route, determined by the tumour location, prior surgical history, and patient comorbidities [14]. When feasible, tumours in close proximity were resected en bloc during a single dissection; alternatively, sequential resections were performed. Total main renal artery clamping was used for all procedures in this study; neither selective branch clamping nor zero ischemia techniques were performed. In some cases, tumours were resected individually under sequential total main renal artery clamping, allowing for brief reperfusion intervals between resections, resulting in multiple warm ischaemia sequences (WIT). Consequently, the WIT was designated as the sum of these individual ischaemia durations. In all cases, renorrhaphy closure was performed using either a single or double-layer technique according to the tumour location and the surgeon’s preference.

Measurements and outcomes

The primary outcomes of this study were perioperative results, including the rate of postoperative complications and ‘Trifecta’ achievement. The secondary outcomes included the preservation of postoperative renal function and oncological survival outcomes. Each tumour was scored using the R.E.N.A.L. and PADUA scores [15, 16]. Radiological characteristics of the renal tumours were evaluated and quantified by specialized practitioners. Intra- and postoperative complications were documented based on the modified Clavien–Dindo classification [17]. The ‘Trifecta’ outcome was defined as WIT under 25 min, negative surgical margins, and no perioperative complications, in accordance with the criteria established by Khalifeh et al. [18]. Pathological staging adhered to the 2019 TNM system, histologic subtype was categorized following the 2016 World Health Organization guidelines, and tumour grading was determined using the Fuhrman system. A positive surgical margin was identified by the presence of tumour cells on the inked resection surface of the kidney [19]. Renal function was assessed pre- and postoperatively via the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), calculated with the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula [20]. The preoperative, first-day postoperative, and 12-month postoperative eGFR were calculated.

Due to the complexity of multiple resection beds, a retroperitoneal closed-suction drain was routinely placed at the end of all procedures to actively monitor for potential postoperative bleeding or urine leak. The strict institutional criteria for surgical drain removal mandated a total drainage volume of less than 50 mL per 24-hour period, ensuring the fluid was serous (non-haemorrhagic and non-urinous).

Postoperatively, each patient was regularly followed up through medical consultations or telephone interviews to assess renal function, tumour recurrence, and survival status. Postoperative renal function was assessed using serum creatinine (Scr) and eGFR measurements obtained 12 months after surgery. Survival outcomes included disease-free survival (DFS), CSS, and overall survival (OS).

Propensity score matching

To estimate the propensity scores, we utilised a multivariate logistic regression model incorporating baseline variables that might influence surgical and survival outcomes. We executed a 1:1 propensity-score matching without replacement using the nearest-neighbour method to balance the baseline characteristics between the groups and minimise selection bias. Matching was performed using the R matching package (version 4.5.1; The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are expressed as median values with interquartile ranges, while categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Statistical comparisons involved the use of t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to assess the survival outcomes. Log-rank tests were used to compare the DFS, CSS, and OS between the two groups. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0; GraphPad, Boston, MA, USA). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value of < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Twenty patients underwent LPN and 50 underwent RAPN. Baseline demographic and tumour imaging characteristics are presented in Table 1. Before propensity score matching, compared to the LPN group, the smaller tumour in the RAPN group had a significantly larger maximum diameter (1.8 vs. 1.4 cm; P = 0.048). Additionally, the RAPN group had significantly higher maximum R.E.N.A.L score (8 vs. 7; P = 0.029), sum R.E.N.A.L score (13 vs. 11; P = 0.045), maximum PADUA score (8 vs. 8; P = 0.032), and sum PADUA score (15 vs. 16; P = 0.008). The RAPN group had a higher mean preoperative creatinine concentration (74.5 vs. 85.6 µmol/L; P = 0.020). The other baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, with no significant differences observed.

Assessment of covariate balance after 1:1 propensity score matching confirmed that all key characteristics were adequately balanced between the groups, yielding a final matched cohort of 19 patients in the LPN group and 19 patients in the RAPN group. There were no significant differences between the groups in terms of age, sex, body mass index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, medical history of diabetes or hypertension, contralateral kidney surgery, solitary kidney, tumour side, tumour size, R.E.N.A.L. score, PADUA score, preoperative Scr or preoperative eGFR (Table 1).

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative data are shown in Table 2. After propensity score matching, the RAPN and LPN groups exhibited no significant differences in operative time, EBL, WIT, need for blood transfusion, or positive surgical margins. Compared to the LPN group, the RAPN group had a significantly shorter median postoperative drainage time (3 vs. 4 days; P = 0.033), shorter median time to full ambulation (2 vs. 3 days; P = 0.023), and shorter median postoperative hospital stay (4 vs. 6 days; P = 0.044). The rate of postoperative complications was significantly lower (10.5% vs. 47.4%; P = 0.034). In the LPN group, eight of the nine complications were Clavien–Dindo grade I, including five cases of acute kidney injury treated with diuretics, two cases of mild haematuria, and one case of postoperative ileus requiring medical management. The only grade II complication was anaemia requiring blood transfusion. In the RAPN group, both complications were Clavien–Dindo grade I, including one case of mild haematuria and one case of postoperative ileus. The rate of ‘Trifecta’ achievement was significantly higher (63.2% vs. 21.1%; P = 0.020) in the RAPN group.

The renal functional outcomes are shown in Table 4. In the matched cohort, at 24 h postoperatively, the RAPN group exhibited a significantly lower percentage increase in Scr (54.2% vs. 35.3%, P = 0.021) and a significantly smaller absolute decline in eGFR (22.2 vs. 30.8 mL/min/1.73 m², P = 0.047), while the absolute increase in Scr showed a favourable trend in the RAPN group (40.6 vs. 28.2 µmol/L, P = 0.069). Similarly, at pre-discharge (the last measurement before hospital discharge), the RAPN group consistently demonstrated significantly better renal function preservation, with a lower percentage increase in Scr (39.8% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.022) and a smaller absolute decline in eGFR (23.3 vs. 15.5 mL/min/1.73 m², P = 0.034), with the absolute increase in Scr again showing a trend favouring RAPN (29.6 vs. 18.4 µmol/L, P = 0.074). At 12 months postoperatively, the RAPN group demonstrated significantly lower absolute (9.1 vs. 18.4 µmol/L, P = 0.041) and percentage changes (12.0% vs. 25.9%, P = 0.024) in Scr, along with significantly lower absolute in eGFR (8.0 vs. 16.7 mL/min/1.73 m², P = 0.028).

Prognostic outcomes

Following propensity score matching, the median follow-up duration was 83 months for the LPN group and 54 months for the RAPN group. Throughout the follow-up period, disease recurrence was observed in three patients in each group, while cancer-specific mortality was recorded in one patient per group. All-cause mortality affected one patient in the LPN group and three in the RAPN group. According to Kaplan–Meier analysis, no statistically significant differences were identified in DFS (P = 0.386), CSS (P = 0.678), or OS (P = 0.116; Fig. 2a–c).

Discussion

Since the introduction of robotic surgery systems, the comparison between LPN and RAPN has become a topic of considerable debate. Numerous studies have performed systematic reviews and meta-analyses comparing LPN and RAPN and have shown that RAPN offers superior perioperative outcomes in certain aspects [21–23]. However, these studies primarily focused on cohorts of patients with single renal tumours. Few studies have explored the feasibility and safety of LPN and RAPN in MIRT [8, 9, 24]. The relative advantages of RAPN over LPN in the management of MIRT require further validation and elucidation. This study evaluated the perioperative, functional, and oncological outcomes between LPN and RAPN in patients with MIRT. To minimize selection bias and address potential confounders, propensity score matching was applied to balance key baseline variables across both groups. Prior to matching, patients in the LPN group displayed smaller tumour sizes, lower tumour complexity scores, and reduced preoperative Scr levels. As summarized in Table 1, all matched characteristics were well balanced, confirming the effectiveness of the matching procedure in creating comparable cohorts.

Our experience demonstrates the multifaceted advantages of robotic technology in PN, including reduced EBL, shorter WIT, and decreased length of postoperative hospital stay [19]. According to our results, RAPN seems to have no significant advantages over LPN in terms of perioperative results. Detailed outcomes included operating time, blood loss, ischaemia time, and positive surgical margins. Biebel et al. performed a matched-pair analysis of 50 ipsilateral robot-assisted partial nephrectomies of multiple tumours (iRAPN-MTs) and 146 RAPNs of single tumours, excluding completely off-clamp procedures, and found that iRAPN-MT was associated with increased operative time and WIT [25]. For MIRT, the considerable complexity of the surgery itself (requiring excision and reconstruction of multiple lesions) is the underlying cause of the significantly prolonged operative time and WIT. Consequently, the differences in the operative time and WIT between the two minimally invasive approaches may become less pronounced. Although intraoperative parameters were comparable, RAPN demonstrated significant advantages in postoperative recovery, showing shorter median times to full ambulation, drain removal, and hospital discharge. We acknowledge, however, that these recovery metrics in the RAPN group, particularly the 3 days until drain removal and 4 days of hospital stay, are slightly higher than the global averages allowed by modern Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) pathways. This reflects our conservative institutional protocol, which mandates routine drain placement for complex MIRT cases. Because multiple resection beds typically generate more initial serosanguinous oozing than standard single-tumour partial nephrectomies, the drainage output naturally took longer to fall below our removal threshold of < 50 mL per 24 h. Furthermore, the greater surgical trauma associated with multiple resections slightly delayed patients in achieving full, unassisted ambulation. Nonetheless, the enhanced dexterity and precision of the robotic platform still significantly minimized surgical trauma, accelerating this recovery timeline compared to conventional laparoscopy.

‘Trifecta’ achievement in partial nephrectomy was first introduced by Hung et al., encompassing three key outcomes: a negative surgical margin, preserved renal function, and the absence of urological complications [26]. Notably, there is no consensus in the literature regarding the definition of these criteria. In the present study, ‘Trifecta’ was defined with reference to the work of Khalifeh et al. as negative surgical margins, absence of complications, and WIT < 25 min. The ‘Trifecta’ achievement rate in the RAPN group was 63.2%, comparable to rates previously reported (61.7%–84.1%) [26–28]. In our cohort, although the majority of complications in the LPN group were minor (Clavien–Dindo grade I), their occurrence precluded ‘Trifecta’ achievement in those patients. Therefore, the superior precision and reduced surgical trauma of the robotic platform, which resulted in fewer minor complications, were the main drivers for the higher Trifecta success rate observed in the RAPN group. Importantly, potential confounding factors that could influence ‘Trifecta’ achievement, including diabetes, hypertension, and pre-existing chronic kidney disease as indicated by baseline creatinine and eGFR, were effectively balanced between the two groups following propensity score matching. Compared to the LPN group, the RAPN group showed a significantly higher trifecta success rate even after adjusting for these confounders, suggesting that robot-assisted surgery may improve renal function preservation.

This conclusion is further supported by the renal function outcomes. In the present study, the RAPN group showed a significant difference in first-day and long-term postoperative eGFR. Because hypoperfusion parameters, such as warm ischaemia time and estimated blood loss, were comparable between the two groups, the mechanism behind this functional advantage warrants further exploration. We hypothesise that this functional advantage stems from resection precision. Although tumour enucleation was the standard surgical intent for all cases to maximize nephron sparing, the technical constraints of conventional laparoscopy, such as rigid instrumentation and two-dimensional vision, make it difficult to precisely follow the tumour pseudocapsule in complex MIRT. This often leads to the inadvertent excision of a wider rim of healthy parenchyma. Conversely, the robotic platform’s 3D visualization and superior dexterity enable surgeons to perform enucleation much more reliably along the capsule edge. This inherently reduced loss of healthy parenchyma likely translates into the superior long-term renal function preservation observed in the RAPN group. Kobayashi et al. also found in a propensity score-based study that RAPN was significantly associated with a better preservation of both total and segmental renal function [21]. Ohba et al. retrospectively analysed data from 174 patients who underwent either LPN or RAPN and evaluated both global and split renal function before and 6 months after each procedure. They found that RAPN was significantly associated with superior postoperative preservation of split renal function [29]. However, additional comparative studies are required to confirm our conclusions.

Histopathological analysis revealed that most patients had tumours with consistent pathological types. There were no significant differences in the pathological characteristics between the two groups. When comparing oncological outcomes, no significant differences in DFS, CSS, or OS were observed between the two groups. A long-term outcome study cohort that included 140 patients treated with MIRT demonstrated favourable CSS after treatment with RN or NSS [30]. As there are no comparative studies on the survival outcomes of LPN and RAPN, our results demonstrate, to some extent, that there is no significant difference in tumour outcomes between the RAPN and LPN groups. However, multicentre prospective studies are required to verify this conclusion.

Current guidelines strongly recommend PN in patients with solitary kidneys, with the primary goal of maintaining sufficient renal function to prevent dialysis and achieve a durable cancer-free status [31]. On the basis of our findings, RAPN is recommended for patients with solitary kidneys undergoing MIRT. Some studies have recommended preoperative neoadjuvant therapy to reduce the difficulty of surgery [32]. Further prospective studies are required to validate these findings.

The present study had some limitations. First, it was a retrospective, non-randomised study. Although there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of key variables, potential selection bias or confounding factors may have influenced the results. Additionally, most RAPN procedures were performed in the latter half of the study period owing to the developmental characteristics of the surgical techniques. This accounts for the significantly shorter mean follow-up duration in the RAPN group (54 months) than that in the LPN group (83 months). Nevertheless, all surgeries were performed by experienced surgeons, thereby minimising the impact of this factor. Despite these limitations, this study is among the few to compare LPN and RAPN in the treatment of MIRT, offering a comprehensive analysis of the surgical and oncological outcomes of RAPN in this context.

Conclusions

In patients with MIRT, RAPN appears to be more favourable than LPN in terms of perioperative outcomes (i.e., postoperative complications, postoperative hospital stay, and ‘Trifecta’ achievement), and postoperative and long-term renal function preservation. These findings support the use of robotics as the preferred minimally invasive strategy for complex bilateral renal tumours, potentially enhancing both surgical safety and functional outcomes. Prospective randomised studies with large sample sizes and long-term survival follow-ups are needed to validate our findings.
