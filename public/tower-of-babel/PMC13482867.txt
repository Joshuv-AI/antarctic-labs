CAF-derived PTGDS drives pancreatic cancer neuroendocrine differentiation and chemoresistance via PAQR9-MAPK
Authors: Jingwen Liang, Wang Peng, Zhou Luan, Luyao Liu, Hai Huang, Haochen Cui, Mengdie Cao, Shiru Chen, Si Xiong, Shuya Bai, Wei Chen, Bin Cheng, Yuchong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tumor microenvironment, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), critically influences pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) progression. Neuroendocrine differentiation (NED) is associated with aggressive tumor behavior and therapy resistance in multiple malignancies, yet its regulation within the PDAC environment is poorly characterized. Through integrated analysis of clinical specimens and transcriptomic datasets, we identified significant enrichment of NED markers correlating with poor patient outcomes. Single-cell RNA sequencing further identified Glycoprotein prostaglandin D2 synthase (PTGDS) as a CAF-derived factor potentially driving NED. Functional studies demonstrated that PTGDS secreted by CAFs robustly induced NED while enhancing proliferation, invasion, migration, and gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic cancer cells. Mechanistically, we established that PTGDS activates MAPK signaling through its membrane receptor PAQR9, and the pharmacological inhibition of MAPK signaling or knockdown of PAQR9 effectively reversed PTGDS-induced malignant phenotypes. These findings reveal a novel CAFs/PTGDS-PAQR9-MAPK signaling axis that promotes NED and drives PDAC progression, offering new therapeutic opportunities to overcome stromal-mediated chemoresistance.

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most lethal malignancies, characterized by its complex anatomical location, aggressive progression, and poor prognosis [1–4]. Due to the lack of specific early symptoms and effective screening methods, the majority of PDAC patients are diagnosed in advanced stages, with only 15-20% eligible for potentially curative surgical resection [5]. Moreover, even after surgery, patients face a high risk of recurrence and metastasis. Current chemotherapy regimens further suffer from limited efficacy due to the frequent development of chemotherapy resistance [6].

Neuroendocrine differentiation (NED) is defined by the expression of neuroendocrine markers (e.g., SYP, NSE, CHGA, CD56) in tumor cells or the presence of neuroendocrine granules detectable via electron microscopy [7]. Notably, 10% ~ 20% of squamous carcinoma and adenocarcinoma exhibit NED, with the highest prevalence observed in adenocarcinoma. In recent years, NED has been identified in malignancies of the lung, prostate, and gastrointestinal tract [7–10]. Neuroendocrine differentiation is increasingly recognized as a manifestation of lineage plasticity, a dynamic process in which adenocarcinoma cells acquire a neuroendocrine-like phenotype in response to therapeutic or environmental pressure [11]. While advanced NED can result in overt histological transformation, focal NED typically involves expression of neuroendocrine markers in tumor cells that retain their original epithelial or ductal morphology [12]. Furthermore, tumor cells with neuroendocrine features contribute significantly to disease aggressiveness and treatment resistance [13]. Emerging evidence suggests that NED in epithelial tumor cells may drive drug resistance and recurrence in prostate adenocarcinoma (PCA) and NSCLC [8, 14–18].

Neuroendocrine differentiation has emerged as a critical factor in pancreatic cancer progression. Growing evidence indicates that NED contributes significantly to early metastasis and poor prognosis in pancreatic cancer, with neuroendocrine marker expression being strongly associated with chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer cells [12, 19–22]. However, the regulatory mechanisms through which NED drives malignant processes in pancreatic cancer remain poorly understood and require further investigation.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) plays a pivotal role in cancer progression, particularly in pancreatic cancer [23, 24]. Comprising a complex network of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and diverse stromal cells - including macrophages, inflammatory cells, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and mesenchymal stem cells - the TME creates a dynamic niche that supports tumor growth. In pancreatic cancer, CAFs are especially prominent and exert unique tumor-promoting functions. These cells drive the formation of a dense, fibrotic stroma that not only acts as a physical barrier to drug delivery but also actively contributes to chemoresistance. Notably, CAFs facilitate ECM remodeling through the secretion of various regulatory factors, further reinforcing treatment resistance in pancreatic cancer. CAFs actively remodel the pancreatic cancer ECM through secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and collagen, thereby reducing drug permeability and contributing to drug resistance [25]. Mechanistically, CAF-derived SDF-1 has been shown to upregulate SATB-1 expression in pancreatic cancer cells, promoting both malignant progression and gemcitabine resistance [26]. Additionally, CAFs activate the TGF-β1/SMAD2/3 pathway to elevate ATF4 expression, which similarly confers resistance to gemcitabine [27]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests CAFs may drive neuroendocrine differentiation and chemoresistance in other malignancies, as demonstrated by CD105-dependent SFRP1 secretion in prostate cancer [10]. However, the role of CAFs in promoting neuroendocrine differentiation in pancreatic cancer remains to be explored.

In this study, we aim to elucidate the role of CAF-derived PTGDS, a secreted protein involved in prostaglandin metabolism, in promoting NED in pancreatic cancer cells and its subsequent impact on tumor progression and chemoresistance. We demonstrate how PTGDS-receptor interactions activate oncogenic pathways driving neuroendocrine differentiation in PDAC, revealing novel targets to overcome gemcitabine resistance. This mechanistic insight provides a foundation for developing combinatorial therapies to enhance treatment efficacy.

Identification of molecular neuroendocrine lineage features in PDAC and their prognostic implications

To investigate the presence of neuroendocrine differentiation in pancreatic cancer, we examined the expression of Pan-cytokeratin (Pan-CK) and the NED markers SYP and CHGA in tumor tissues from clinical pancreatic cancer patients. (Fig. 1A, B). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated that, among Pan-CK-positive tumor cells, a subset exhibited simultaneous fluorescence signals for SYP or CHGA. These findings indicate that Pan-CK-SYP or Pan-CK-CHGA double-positive tumor cells have undergone neuroendocrine differentiation, providing direct evidence for the presence of NED in pancreatic cancer tissues. Notably, while these cells acquired neuroendocrine molecular features, they retained characteristic glandular or ductal architecture of adenocarcinoma on H&E-stained sections (Fig. 1B). This observation aligns with the established models of focal NED or lineage plasticity, which are often observed in aggressive PDAC subtypes [12]. To evaluate the clinical relevance of NED in PDAC, we quantified SYP and CHGA expression via qPCR in tumor tissues from 71 clinically diagnosed PDAC patients who had not received any prior oncological therapy. CAFs promote NED in PDAC, enhancing the proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer cells

To investigate whether CAFs promote NED in PDAC, CAFs and normal fibroblasts (NFs) were isolated from human PDAC and normal pancreatic tissues, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining and western blot revealed that CAFs expressed higher levels of α-SMA and FAP compared to NFs (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Pancreatic cancer cells (SW1990 and PANC-1) were treated with conditioned media from CAFs (CAF CM) and NFs (NF CM). Pancreatic cancer cells treated with CAF CM showed significantly increased expression of neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin (SYP) and chromogranin A (CHGA) compared to those treated with NF CM or DMEM (Fig. 2B and D). Transwell migration and invasion assays demonstrated that CAF CM markedly enhanced the migratory and invasive abilities of pancreatic cancer cells compared to NF CM and DMEM (Fig. 2C). Consistently, wound healing assay showed a significantly higher migratory rate in the CAF CM group after 48 h (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, colony formation assay revealed that CAF CM promoted the clonogenic capacity of pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 2F). These findings are consistent with observations in prostate cancer, where tumor cells undergoing neuroendocrine differentiation exhibit increased invasive, migratory, and proliferative capacities. Notably, to evaluate the effect of CAF CM on the chemosensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells, we performed the CCK8 assay to assess cell viability following gemcitabine treatment. The results demonstrated that CAF CM significantly reduced the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to gemcitabine compared to the control group (Fig. 2G). In vivo studies further evaluated the effects of CAFs on PDAC progression. An orthotopic PDAC model was established in C57BL/6 mice by co-injecting Panc02 cells and CAFs derived from KPC mice (KPC-CAFs) (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B). The CAF-injected group exhibited significantly higher bioluminescence intensity compared to the non-CAF group, indicating accelerated tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 1D and 1E). Tumors from the CAF-injected group showed significantly accelerated growth compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Tumor tissues were collected at the endpoint for qPCR and immunohistochemistry analysis, which confirmed increased expression of neuroendocrine markers in tumors from the CAF-injected group (Supplementary Fig. 1F–H). These results indicate that CAFs promote NED in pancreatic cancer cells and enhance their proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance.

To further explore the role of CAFs in pancreatic cancer chemotherapy resistance, we also conducted a drug resistance experiment to evaluate the effect of CAFs on tumor growth and drug resistance in pancreatic cancer mice when the chemotherapy drug gemcitabine was used. The mice were divided into four groups: 1) Control (PBS); 2) CAFs (KPC-CAFs co-injection); 3) GEM (gemcitabine treatment); 4) CAFs + GEM group. All mice were treated with the same pancreatic orthotopic tumor model as described above. After the start of the experiment, the mice were treated according to the grouping scheme. Specifically, the CAFs + GEM treatment group and the GEM group were injected with gemcitabine (50 mg/kg) on days 5, 8, 11, 14, 17, and 20 after cell injection. The experimental results showed that compared with the GEM group alone, the tumor growth of mice in the CAFs + GEM group was significantly increased (Supplementary Fig. 1I–K). This result indicates that CAFs significantly enhance the tumor’s resistance to gemcitabine chemotherapy in the pancreatic cancer mouse model and promote the chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer.

PTGDS secreted by CAFs promotes NED in PDAC and enhances the proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer cells

To identify key drivers of CAF-induced NED in PDAC, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were analyzed using GEO datasets. After quality control, gene expression normalization, and batch effect correction, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on variably expressed genes from 41177 cells. Cell populations were classified based on their marker gene expression, including fibroblasts, immune cells, and epithelial cells (Fig. 3A–D). Patients were categorized into high-NED and low-NED groups based on neuroendocrine marker expression in tumor cells. Differential gene expression analysis between CAFs from these two groups revealed significant upregulation of several secreted proteins in the high-NED group. Among the upregulated secreted proteins identified, PTGDS, APOE, SERPINE1, and CXCL8 demonstrated the most significant differential expression. To further identify these candidates, we performed a systematic screening approach. First, we analyzed RNA expression profiles from pancreatic cancer patients in the TCGA database, which revealed significantly elevated levels of all four molecules in tumor tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 3E). Importantly, high expressions of PTGDS, APOE, and SERPINE1 correlated with worse patient prognosis (Fig. 3G). Subsequent qPCR validation in our cell models showed that PTGDS, APOE, and SERPINE1 were markedly upregulated in CAFs versus NFs, while CXCL8 did not exhibit a significant difference (Fig. 3F). To investigate potential roles in neuroendocrine differentiation, we overexpressed plasmids for all four candidates (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and transfected them into HEK 293 T cells. Conditioned media containing the respective overexpressed protein were then used to treat pancreatic cancer cell lines. qPCR analysis identified PTGDS as having the most potent effect in promoting neuroendocrine differentiation of pancreatic cancer cells among the four proteins tested (Supplementary Fig. 2B–E). Among these candidates, PTGDS demonstrated the most potent and consistent induction of NED markers across multiple PDAC cell lines, while APOE, CXCL8, and SERPINE1 produced only minimal or negligible effects. To further elucidate the effects of PTGDS-induced NED on pancreatic cancer cells, we treated pancreatic cancer cells with species-specific PTGDS recombinant proteins or PTGDS-enriched conditioned media (PTGDS CM) from 293 T cells overexpressing PTGDS (Supplementary Fig. 2A). The expression levels of SYP and CHGA in pancreatic cancer cells were significantly upregulated after treatment with PTGDS CM or species-specific PTGDS recombinant proteins compared to control media (Fig. 4B, C, and 4E). These results further confirmed that PTGDS actively promotes neuroendocrine differentiation in pancreatic cancer cells. PTGDS CM significantly enhanced the malignant potential of pancreatic cancer cells, as evidenced by increased migration, invasion, and colony formation capacity compared to control treatment (Fig. 4F–H). To investigate the effect of PTGDS on the chemoresistance of pancreatic cancer cells, we used the CCK8 assay to detect the sensitivity of cells to gemcitabine. PTGDS CM treatment substantially decreased pancreatic cancer cell sensitivity to gemcitabine (Fig. 4I), suggesting that PTGDS may contribute to chemotherapy resistance through its effects on neuroendocrine differentiation.

In vivo, KPC-CAFs with downregulated PTGDS expression were co-injected with pancreatic cancer cells into mice. Tumors from the PTGDS-downregulated group showed significantly slowed growth compared to controls and exhibited significantly lower bioluminescence intensity compared to the control group (Supplementary Fig. 3A, 3B, and 3F). At the endpoint, tumor tissues were collected for qPCR and immunohistochemistry analyses, revealing decreased expression of neuroendocrine markers in PTGDS-downregulated tumors (Supplementary Fig. 3C–E). These findings suggest that CAF-derived PTGDS promotes NED in pancreatic cancer cells and is associated with enhanced tumor progression and poor prognosis. We also explored the role of PTGDS in pancreatic cancer chemoresistance. Luciferase-labeled Panc02 cells were mixed with KPC-CAFs treated with different methods and injected into the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice to construct a pancreatic carcinoma in situ model. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: 1) Control + PBS group; 2) PTGDS sh + PBS group; 3) Control + GEM treatment group; 4) PTGDS sh + GEM treatment group. Through in vivo imaging (Supplementary Fig. 3G and H) and tumor tissue macroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3J), it was found that PTGDS knockdown significantly attenuated PTGDS-mediated tumor growth and increased the sensitivity of mice to gemcitabine. These results indicate that PTGDS is a key molecule in tumor chemoresistance and may serve as a potential target for the treatment of pancreatic cancer.

PTGDS activates PAQR9 to promote proliferation, migration, invasion, and chemoresistance in pancreatic cancer

To explore the molecular mechanism by which PTGDS promotes NED in pancreatic cancer cells, immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) was used to identify receptors targeted by PTGDS. Pancreatic cancer cells treated with PTGDS-enriched media underwent IP-MS analysis, which identified 77 unique proteins, with PAQR9 emerging as the most prominent receptor (Fig. 5A; Supplementary Fig. 4A). PAQR9, a G protein-coupled receptor belonging to the progesterone and lipocalin receptor (PAQR) family [28], activates intracellular signaling cascades including MAPKs, PI3K, Akt, and c-Src pathways [29–32]. Unlike receptors that directly scaffold MAPK components, PAQR9 primarily signals through Gs-cAMP to initiate the MAPK pathway [30], indicating that a cAMP-dependent regulatory mechanism for MAPK signaling. PAQR9 regulates fasting-induced ketogenesis and fatty acid oxidation in the liver by modulating the protein stability of PPARα [33]. Co-IP confirmed the interaction between PTGDS and PAQR9 (Fig. 5B). Molecular docking analysis revealed a high potential for direct binding between PTGDS and PAQR9 (free binding energy: −20.8 kcal/mol) (Fig. 5C). Through in vivo experiments, we explored the role of PTGDS and PAQR9 in pancreatic cancer chemotherapy resistance. The pancreatic carcinoma in situ model was constructed by mixing PAQR9 knockdown and control Panc02 cells with KPC-CAFs treated with different treatments and injecting them into the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice. The mice were randomly divided into 4 groups: 1) Ctrl/PBS group; 2) PTGDS⁺/PAQR9⁻/PBS group; 3) Ctrl/GEM group; 4) PTGDS⁺/PAQR9⁻/GEM group. The results of in vivo experiments showed that the knockdown of PAQR9 could hinder the function of PTGDS in promoting tumor growth and chemoresistance. This result further indicates that the function of PTGDS is mediated by the pancreatic cancer cell receptor PAQR9.

CAF-secreted PTGDS activated the MAPK signaling pathway through PAQR9

To explore the specific mechanisms underlying the PTGDS-PAQR9 axis in pancreatic cancer, we performed GSEA analysis using single-cell sequencing data from the GEO public database. This analysis compared high and low PAQR9-expressing pancreatic cancer cells, revealing a significant enrichment of the MAPK signaling pathway in PAQR9-high-expressing cells (Supplementary Fig. 4B)

The MAPK signaling pathway plays an important role in the malignant transformation of cells, associated with the development and progression of tumors such as breast, ovarian, esophageal, colon, gastric, and hepatocellular carcinomas [34–36]. Recent studies have shown that the MAPK signaling pathway plays an important role in the regulation of tumor NED [15, 16, 37, 38].

Western blot analysis demonstrated that the MAPK signaling pathway was further upregulated in human pancreatic cancer cells after treatment with CAF CM or PTGDS CM (Supplementary Fig. 4C and D). To investigate whether PTGDS promotes NED in a PAQR9-dependent manner, we sequentially modulated the expression of PTGDS and PAQR9. Similarly, the changing levels in these groups of p-MEK and p-ERK suggested that PTGDS activates the MAPK pathway through PAQR9 (Supplementary Fig. 4E).

To further assess whether PTGDS functions through PAQR9 regulation of the MAPK signaling pathway, we incubated human pancreatic cancer cells with PD98059, an MEK1/2 inhibitor. We found that PD98059 reversed the changes in the p-MEK, p-ERK, SYP, and CHGA proteins activated by PTGDS CM and PAQR9 overexpressing in pancreatic cancer cells (Fig. 6C). Additionally, PD98059 also increased pancreatic cancer cells’ sensitivity to gemcitabine (Supplementary Fig. 4H). Discussion

PDAC is an aggressive malignancy characterized by profound intratumoral heterogeneity, which drives therapeutic resistance and poor clinical outcomes. This study demonstrates that CAF-derived PTGDS promotes NED in pancreatic cancer cells through engagement of the PAQR9 receptor. Activation of the PTGDS–PAQR9 axis triggers MAPK/ERK signaling, establishing a novel pathway that enhances tumor progression, invasion, and chemoresistance. These findings underscore how stromal-tumor metabolic crosstalk within the tumor microenvironment contributes to PDAC aggressiveness and treatment failure.

A mechanistic insight from our study reveals the PTGDS-PAQR9-MAPK signaling axis as a critical driver of NED in PDAC. This finding extends the established association between NED and aggressive tumor behavior observed in multiple malignancies, including prostate and lung cancers, where neuroendocrine features correlate with therapeutic resistance and poor clinical outcomes [7–10, 13–15, 18, 38, 39]. Importantly, we demonstrate that pancreatic cancer cells expressing canonical NED markers (SYP/CHGA) exhibit marked resistance to standard chemotherapy, particularly gemcitabine. This is consistent with previous reports indicating that neuroendocrine-like cells possess a survival advantage in harsh environments, such as chemotherapy. Although classical neuroendocrine morphology of NED was not uniformly observed, increased expression of neuroendocrine-associated markers was strongly associated with drug resistance and poor clinical outcomes in PDAC. These findings suggest that molecular-level ductal–neuroendocrine lineage plasticity, rather than overt neuroendocrine differentiation, may serve as an adaptive mechanism driving therapeutic resistance. This aligns with prior reports in pancreatic cancer, where cytokeratin-positive ductal tumor cells were found to express synaptophysin while retaining ductal morphology—a phenomenon termed ductal–neuroendocrine lineage plasticity that is linked to poor prognosis and chemoresistance [12].

The role of CAFs in modulating the tumor microenvironment and influencing cancer cell behavior is well-established [23, 24, 40]. In our study, we confirm that CAFs, through the secretion of PTGDS, significantly enhance the neuroendocrine phenotype of pancreatic cancer cells. PTGDS, a prostaglandin D2 synthase, has been implicated in various cancers, although its role remains controversial depending on the cancer type. Here, we provide evidence that in PDAC, PTGDS promotes NED through activation of the PAQR9 receptor, which activates the MAPK pathway, thereby promoting tumor progression. This is consistent with the previous findings in SCLC that the MAPK pathway promotes NED [8].

Notably, MAPK pathway activation has been widely implicated in chemoresistance across multiple cancer, including PDAC. Emerging evidence suggests that MAPK signaling enables tumor cells to evade chemotherapy-induced cell death. For example, FGF19-mediated MAPK activation has been shown to drive cisplatin resistance in ovarian cancer through autophagy induction [41]. In pancreatic cancer, a malignancy characterized by pronounced chemoresistance, the role of MAPK is particularly significant. Studies have indicated that blocking the IQGAP1-MAPK interaction can inhibit ERK signaling activation and suppress gemcitabine resistance in pancreatic cancer [42]. Intrinsic p38 MAPK signaling in pancreatic cancer cells promotes IL-1α-mediated inflammatory responses in the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing chemoresistance [43]. The natural compound Oblongifolin C confers gemcitabine resistance to pancreatic cancer cells via the Src/MAPK/ERK pathway [44].

The role of the MAPK pathway in pancreatic cancer extends beyond cell proliferation and survival. Evidence suggests that MAPK signaling significantly promotes tumor cell migration and invasion by regulating cytoskeletal composition, cell adhesion molecules, and the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, thereby enhancing the invasive and migratory capabilities of tumor cells. Under the regulation of the MAPK pathway, pancreatic cancer cells not only accelerate the growth of primary tumors but also markedly increase their ability to invade both local and distant tissues [45, 46]. Moreover, activation of the MAPK pathway is involved in shaping the tumor microenvironment, including enhancing the function of cytokines and growth factors secreted by CAFs, which further promotes tumor progression and chemoresistance [47]. Given the multifaceted role of the MAPK pathway in pancreatic cancer, targeting this signaling cascade has become an important strategy in cancer therapy. In our study, we found that the use of MAPK pathway inhibitors, such as the MEK inhibitor PD98059, effectively suppressed PTGDS-induced activation of the MAPK pathway via PAQR9, thereby inhibiting pancreatic cancer cell migration, proliferation, and chemoresistance, and significantly increasing the sensitivity of pancreatic cancer cells to gemcitabine. This finding provides a theoretical basis for the development of combination therapeutic strategies, particularly the use of MAPK inhibitors in conjunction with conventional chemotherapeutic agents, which holds promise for improving the therapeutic efficacy and survival outcomes of patients with pancreatic cancer.

Our study highlights the implications of PTGDS-PAQR9-driven NED in chemoresistance. PDAC patients typically exhibit poor responses to gemcitabine, the first-line chemotherapeutic agent, and our data suggest that NED is a key factor in this resistance. Tumor cells undergoing NED may enable them to evade chemotherapy and contribute to disease recurrence. Increased expression of NED markers suggests that ductal-neuroendocrine profile plasticity may be a mechanism for PDA treatment resistance.

Importantly, our findings demonstrate that inhibiting PTGDS-PAQR9-MAPK signaling can partially reverse the neuroendocrine phenotype and restore sensitivity to gemcitabine in pancreatic cancer cells. This suggests that targeting PTGDS or the PAQR9 pathway could offer a novel therapeutic strategy to overcome chemoresistance in PDAC. Given the complex interplay between CAFs, tumor cells, and the microenvironment, future studies are needed to explore the potential of combining PTGDS inhibitors with conventional chemotherapies to improve patient outcomes.

In conclusion, our study provides novel insights into the role of CAF-secreted PTGDS in promoting NED and chemoresistance in PDAC via the PAQR9 receptor. This research underscores the importance of the tumor microenvironment in shaping cancer progression and highlights PTGDS-PAQR9-MAPK signaling as a potential therapeutic target. Further investigation into the molecular mechanisms governing NED and its contribution to chemoresistance in PDAC will be critical for developing more effective treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Single‑cell RNA‑sequencing data analysis

The single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data for pancreatic cancer were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession numbers GSE214295 and GSE212966). The Harmony algorithm was utilized for batch integration and correction (nclust = 50, max_iter = 10). Only cells with a minimum of 400 detected genes and a maximum of 15% mitochondrial gene expression were retained for analysis. Normalization was conducted using the log-normalization method. Clustering was performed based on 10 principal components with a resolution parameter set to 0.04. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within each cluster were identified using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat, and a heatmap was generated to illustrate the distinct differences among the groups. The cell types for each cluster were annotated using the CellMarker 2.0 database (http://yikedaxue.slwshop.cn/). Two-dimensional visualization of the clusters was achieved through the use of t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in cancer cells were analyzed with the DESingle algorithm and subsequently cross-referenced with secreted protein genes obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database.

Tissue specimens

After obtaining informed consent and complying with ethical and institutional guidelines, all 7 tumorous and matched adjacent nontumorous pancreas tissue specimens were collected from patients with PDAC underwent curative resection due to pancreas cancer and 64 ultrasound-guided biopsy samples of pancreatic cancer tissue were collected at the Tongji Hospital, Huazhong University of Science and Technology TJ-IRB202410053. These samples were used in CAFs/NFs isolation and subsequent experiments.

Cell lines and culture

The human PDAC cell line SW1990 was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). PANC-1 and HEK 293 T cells were purchased from the Cell Resource Center, Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology at the Chinese Academy of Science (Shanghai, China). Mouse pancreatic cancer cell line Panc02 was acquired from Boster Biological Technology (Wuhan, China). All cell lines were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Cegrogen, Germany) in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37 °C. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma using the Bimake mycoplasma detection kit.

Isolation and culture of fibroblasts

Mouse CAFs were isolated from palpable tumors (>150 days old) of KPC mice. Human CAFs and NFs were obtained from PDAC and adjacent normal pancreatic tissues, respectively, and processed within 30 minutes of collection. The isolation protocol was modified from previous methods [48, 49]. Tissues were minced and dissociated in DMEM containing 1 mg/mL Collagenase l for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were cultured in DMEM with 15% FBS. Fibroblasts were allowed to outgrow for 1–2 weeks and subsequently purified by selective adhesion (20 min in serum-free DMEM). Purity was confirmed by cell morphology and validation of a-SMA and FAP expression via immunofluorescence and western blot. CAFs were used at passage numbers less than 10.

Mice and tumor models

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed in a pathogen-free environment. All animal experiments were conducted following the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Hospital T1-2024-05-011. To minimize bias and ensure that the studied variables did not influence the data within each group, the mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups (n = 5 per group). Blinding was not used in the animal studies.

Before any surgical procedure or imaging, mice were induced with 4% isoflurane in oxygen (flow rate: 0.8 L/min) in an induction chamber, and then maintained on 1–2% isoflurane via a nose cone. The loss of pedal withdrawal and palpebral reflexes confirmed adequate depth of anesthesia. Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad throughout surgery and recovery.

In the mouse orthotopic model of pancreatic cancer, luciferase-labeled Panc02 cells (4 × 10⁵) were combined with CAFs (1×10⁵), derived from pancreatic cancer tissues of KPC mice, and injected into the tail of the pancreas. Bioluminescence was assessed three weeks post-injection, five minutes after the administration of 100 μl of potassium D-luciferin (30 mg/ml in PBS) via intraperitoneal injection.

Four weeks following the surgical procedure, mice were under deep isoflurane anesthesia (5% induction, 2–3% maintenance) until loss of reflexes was confirmed. While fully unconscious, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, ensuring rapid and humane death without recovery of consciousness. Tumor growth was monitored through caliper measurements, and the volume was calculated according to the formula: volume = XY²/2, where X represents the longest diameter and Y represents the shortest of the two perpendicular diameters.

Plasmid construction and lentiviral transfection

The target gene sequences were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and primers were designed (see Supplementary Table S2) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. The target gene sequences were amplified using 2× Phanta Flash Master Mix (P510-01, Vazyme), with cDNA derived from pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tissues and cell lines serving as the PCR templates. Subsequently, the cleaved fragment and vector were ligated using Ligation High (LGK-100, TOYOBO), following the digestion of the former with restriction enzymes (Thermo Fisher). The authenticity of the plasmid and its inserted sequences was confirmed by Sanger sequencing conducted by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Plasmids containing the target genes or sequences, along with a lentiviral vector packaging system, were co-transfected into HEK 293 T cells using Polyethylenimine Linear (PEI) MW 40000 (40816ES02, YEASEN).

ELISA

The cells of each group were uniformly and equally seeded in the culture dish and treated for the designated duration. The supernatant was collected and combined spun down, and assayed as the manufacturer’s protocol. ELISA assay used was human PTGDS (CUSABIO).

Immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) and Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

The N-terminus of the PTGDS sequence in the overexpression plasmid was tagged with Flag. The PTGDS-Flag supernatant was collected from HEK 293 T cells transfected with the plasmid for 3 days and subsequently concentrated using an ultrafiltration tube (UFC901096, Millipore). Human cancer-associated fibroblasts were treated with the enriched PTGDS-Flag supernatant for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were harvested and immunoprecipitated using either an anti-Flag antibody (66008-4-Ig, Proteintech) or an IgG control antibody (30000-0-AP, Proteintech). The precipitated proteins were then sent to Mouhou Bio for mass spectrometry analysis.

For the Co-IP assay, human pancreatic cancer cell lines transfected with the PAQR9-HA overexpression plasmid were treated with the enriched PTGDS-Flag supernatant for 24 h. The cells were subsequently collected, and PTGDS along with its bound proteins were pulled down using the anti-Flag antibody (66008-4-Ig, Proteintech). Western blot analysis was then performed using anti-Flag (66008-4-Ig, Proteintech) and anti-HA antibodies (81290-1-RR, Proteintech).

Molecular docking

To understand the interaction of PTGDS with the extracellular domain of PAQR9, a molecular docking simulation was conducted using the GRAMM server (https://gramm.compbio.ku.edu/). The tertiary structures of PTGDS (AlphaFold: AF-P41222-F1) and PAQR9 (AlphaFold: AF-Q6ZVX9-F1) were obtained from Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org/). The best model was selected based on docking scores from the top ten models, and the resulting docked complexes were visualized using PyMOL. Subsequently, PDBePISA(https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/pistart.html) was used to analyze the interaction between the receptor and ligand along with its two-dimensional representation.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (q-RT PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from the cell lines in question using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA with HiScript® III RT SuperMix (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). The primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. and are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blotting

Western blot analysis was performed as described previously [50]. The primary antibodies targeted SYP (MCE: YA664), CHGA (Proteintech: 60135-2-ig), PTGDS (Zenbio: R25461), PAQR9 (Immunoway: YT6470), p-MEK (ABclonal: AP0209), MEK (ABclonal: A4868), p-ERK (ABclonal: AP0485), ERK (ABclonal: A4782), and GAPDH (Proteintech: 60004-1-Ig). Protein bands were visualized using Beyo ECL Plus and quantified with ImageJ.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining of CAFs and NFs was performed as previously described [50]. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies against α-SMA and FAP, followed by incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies and DAPI. Images were captured using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss). The specific antibodies targeted α-SMA (Servicebio: GB13044-50) and FAP (Beyotime: AF6858).

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of tissues. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, heat-induced antigen retrieval was carried out using EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) in a pressure cooker for 90 s. Endogenous peroxidases were blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with 10% normal goat serum to block nonspecific binding.

Subsequently, sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C. overnight. The primary antibodies used were: rabbit anti-CHGA (Proteintech:10529-1-AP) and rabbit anti-SYP (Proteintech:17785-1-AP). After washing, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, ab205718) at 37°C. for 45 min. Antigen detection was achieved using a 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (Maxim, DAB-4033). Finally, the sections were counterstained with Harris hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with an eco-friendly mounting medium.

Colony formation assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates containing 2000 cells per well for 7 days. The cells were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, followed by staining using 0.1% crystal violet. Subsequently, photographs were captured and subjected to analysis.

CCK8 toxic assay

The Cell Counting Kit (CCK-8) (40203ES92, Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the sensitivity of the cells to gemcitabine. 1000 cells per well were seeded in 96-well plates, and after cell adhesion, treated with different concentrations (0, 10 nM, 100 nM, 1 μM, 10 μM, 100 μM, 1 mM) of gemcitabine (MCE-HY-17026). After incubating for 48 h at 37 °C, CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. 2 h later, an absorption value at 450 nm was detected at the microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China).

Scratch test assay

Cells were cultured in 6-well plates until 90% confluence. The cell layer was gently scraped using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip, and the cells that became detached were rinsed away with a medium that did not contain serum. The remaining cells were then cultured in a serum-free medium, and the migration progress was observed and documented in 0 and 48 h.

Migration and invasion assay

Cell suspensions in DMEM were placed on the upper side of the Transwell chamber (Miliband, USA), while 10% FBS medium was added to the lower chamber. To conduct invasion experiments, Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA) was applied to the upper part of the chamber. After 24 h, the cells located at the bottom of the chamber were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by staining with crystal violet and subsequent photography.

Statistical analysis

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but our sample sizes are similar to those reported in previous studies. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (Version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (Version 8). The student’s t-test and the Wilcoxon test were employed to compare differences between the two groups. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess correlations. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with comparisons made using the Log-rank test. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
