Combined impact of smoking, alcohol and social inequalities on premature mortality in urban India
Authors: Vendhan Gajalakshmi, Fiona Bragg, Ben Lacey, Vendhan Kanimozhi, Manigandan Chandrasekaran, Ramu Ashok Kumar, Paul Sherliker, Richard Peto, Sarah Lewington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssociations with mortality of social inequalities, smoking and drinking differ widely between populations. Here we recruited 500,810 adults aged ≥35 years from Chennai city during 1998–2001 in South India and followed to 2020 for verbal-autopsy-assessed cause-specific mortality. Among those initially without chronic disease, multivariable-adjusted Cox regression (censored at age 70 years) related initial characteristics to mortality rates. Even among non-smoking non-drinkers, those with no schooling had almost three times the mortality rate ratio (RR) of those with >11 years education (men, RR of 2.76, 95% confidence interval (CI) 2.58–2.95; women, RR of 2.93, 95% CI 2.69–3.19), with substantial mortality excesses from each major disease category. These social inequalities were further exacerbated by much higher smoking and drinking prevalences among less educated men. Adjusted for education, male smoking (versus not, RR of 1.26, 95% CI 1.22–1.29) and drinking (versus not, RR of 1.52, 95% CI 1.48–1.56) were independently associated with risk and together almost doubled male mortality (both versus neither, RR of 1.89, 95% CI 1.84–1.94). Few women (<0.1%) smoked or drank.Subject terms: Risk factors, Epidemiology, Diseases

Main

In many populations, substantial health inequalities persist from childhood into adult life, with lower education or socioeconomic status (SES) associated with a higher burden of alcohol drinking and smoking1–4 and with higher adult mortality rates5 over and above any effects of smoking or drinking. Most studies of the separate and combined associations of social inequalities, smoking and drinking with mortality have been in Western populations6–12; the findings in other populations could well be very different.

We report, to our knowledge, the largest prospective study in India—involving 500,000 urban men and women aged ≥35 years in Chennai city—of the independent and joint associations of education, adult SES, smoking and alcohol drinking with mortality before the age of 70 years.

Results

The main analyses excluded 33,323 recruits aged ≥70 years plus 8,878 with chronic disease, leaving 458,609 (Supplementary Table 2). The baseline characteristics varied with years of education (Table 1) and age (Supplementary Table 3). The mean age was 46 years (s.d. 9), indicating mid-century birth. Particularly for women, those born more recently had more education than those born earlier. Few women (<0.1%) ever smoked or drank. Among men, the lack of education was strongly associated with low SES and with the prevalence of smoking or drinking (65% (15,145 out of 23,385) with no schooling ever smoked or drank versus 21% (8,828 out of 42,688) with >11 years of education).

The 7,127 men and 3,006 women resurveyed in 2013–2014, about 14 years after baseline, had, at baseline, had fairly typical patterns of education, SES, smoking and drinking (Table 1 and Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). Self-reported educational level (four categories) was highly reproducible and unchanged in 91% (9,192 out of 10,133) of those resurveyed, but the study-assessed SES score was poorly reproducible, partly owing to increasing household assets (Supplementary Table 4). Among men, at resurvey, 7.5% (182 out of 2,410) of those who reported current smoking at baseline had quit, and 7.8% (161 out of 2,066) of those who reported current drinking at baseline had quit. Hence, 97% (6,900 out of 7,127) reported the same smoking status at baseline and resurvey and 96% (6,870 out of 7,127) reported the same drinking status at baseline and resurvey; smoking (ever/never) and drinking (ever/never) were both unchanged in >99%, suggesting high reliability (Supplementary Table 5). Although the amounts reportedly smoked and drunk by individuals fluctuated between baseline and resurvey, the average number of cigarettes/bidi smoked among men who smoked reduced only slightly between baseline and resurvey (Supplementary Table 6), whereas the mean consumption of alcohol among men who drank increased slightly (Supplementary Table 7). The usual consumption among all study participants in the baseline-assigned categories of smoking and drinking is estimated as the mean consumption at resurvey in those baseline-assigned categories.

During 20-year follow-up (16.4 ± 5.4 (mean ± s.d.) person-years at risk), there were 52,127 deaths at ages 35–69 years (31,337 men and 20,790 women), of which 47,436 (91%) were identified by active follow-up and 4,691 (9%) were identified through passive follow-up alone. Among men and women who never smoked or drank, education was strongly inversely associated with mortality (Fig. 1a). On average, those with no schooling had about three times the overall mortality rate at ages 35–69 years of those with tertiary education (men, mortality RR of 2.76, 95% CI 2.58–2.95; women, RR of 2.93, 95% CI 2.69–3.19) and about twice the overall mortality rate of those with 8–11 years of education. As SES was strongly correlated with education it was also inversely associated with mortality, although not as strongly as was education. In multivariate analyses relating all-cause mortality jointly to baseline SES score (quartiles) and years of education (four categories) SES was of little independent relevance, but the relevance of education remained unchanged (Extended Data Figs. 1 and 2).

Within each educational level, among those who never smoked or drank, the mortality rate in men was about 1.5 times that in women. Few women smoked or drank, but at each educational level, men who smoked and drank had substantially higher mortality than men who did neither (Fig. 1b). Smoking and drinking were both strongly inversely associated with education, and subsequent analyses of associations between educational level and mortality omit (unless stated otherwise) participants who ever smoked or drank.

For each of the nine underlying-cause-of-death categories (stroke, heart disease, renal disease, cancer, tuberculosis (TB), other respiratory disease, other medical causes, all medical causes and non-medical causes), there was a strong inverse association between educational level and mortality (Fig. 2), which was similar among men and women (Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4). Even after excluding the few with >11 years education (who had the lowest mortality), strong inverse associations remained. The mortality RRs comparing no schooling versus 8–11 years of education (the reference group in Fig. 2) were most extreme for respiratory TB (RR of 4.17, 95% CI 3.58–4.86) and other respiratory disease (RR of 3.35, 95% CI 2.87–3.92) but were about 2 for each of the other cause-of-death categories. These RRs, adjusted for age and sex, involved only participants who never smoked or drank.

In the analyses of all-cause mortality adjusted for age, education and SES, men who both smoked and drank had almost twice the mortality rate of those who did neither (RR of 1.89, 95% CI 1.84–1.94); men who smoked had 1.26 (95% CI 1.22–1.29) times the mortality rate of those who never smoked in analyses adjusted for age, education, SES and drinking, and men who drank had 1.52 (95% CI 1.48–1.56) times the mortality rate of men who did not drink in analyses adjusted for age, education, SES and smoking (Supplementary Table 8). These RRs are less extreme than the RRs adjusted only for age but still accounted for 22% of all male mortality in the study population (Supplementary Table 9).

Most cigarette smokers used only cigarettes, most bidi smokers used only bidis and the mean daily consumption of cigarettes plus bidis at baseline was 10 (s.d. 5) among cigarette-only smokers, 14 (s.d. 8) among bidi-only smokers and 11 (s.d. 7) among all men who smoked (Table 1). The mean age men started smoking was 21.9 years (s.d. 4.2). The earlier education had ended, the earlier smoking started, the greater the mean daily consumption and the greater the use of bidis.

Analyses adjusted for alcohol and education found positive associations of cigarette smoking, bidi smoking and any smoking with each of vascular, neoplastic and respiratory mortality, which accounted for the association of smoking with overall mortality (Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6). This smoking-associated excess mortality appeared to be approximately proportional to the usual daily consumption of cigarettes or of bidis (Fig. 4), despite the narrow range of usual consumption (Supplementary Table 7). Bidi smokers had higher RRs than cigarette smokers for all-cause mortality (Extended Data Fig. 5) and, particularly, respiratory mortality (Extended Data Figs. 6–8). Comparing bidi smokers directly with cigarette smokers, the all-cause mortality RR adjusted only for smoking frequency was 1.44 (95% CI 1.39–1.50; χ21 = 368). If further adjusted for alcohol and education, however, this fell to 1.28 (95% CI 1.23–1.33; χ21 = 149).

Discussion

In this urban Indian population, born around the mid-century, one in ten men and one in three women had had no schooling. Even among those who did not smoke or drink, those with no schooling had about three times the death rate in middle age (35–69 years) of those with tertiary education. Few women smoked or drank, but men who both smoked and drank had about double the death rate in middle age of those with neither habit. As men with no schooling were three times as likely to smoke or drink as men with tertiary education, smoking and drinking exacerbated the social inequalities in premature mortality. Although previous prospective studies in India have demonstrated associations of mortality with smoking, drinking and lower educational level or SES, they have not had sufficient power to quantify reliably the independent effects of these risk factors13–19.

The all-cause mortality RR of 1.26 associated with smoking among men is less extreme than in high-income settings20–22. However, mean consumption per smoker was only about 11 cigarettes or bidis per day, less than is now typical in high-income countries, and smoking began at mean age 22.4 years, which is somewhat later than is now typical21,23. This mortality RR is also less extreme than the mortality RR of 1.7 (with narrow confidence limits) reported in a large unmatched case–control study of a nationally representative sample of Indian deaths15. The present mortality RR of 1.26 (also with narrow limits) is, however, consistent with two smaller prospective studies in India, one in Mumbai14 and one south Asian but largely in India (PURE16). However, these comparisons are dependent upon the quantity smoked (which differed between studies) and duration (which was not consistently reported).

Risks associated with bidis (inexpensive, handmade; a small amount of tobacco wrapped in temburni leaf) are less well documented prospectively than risks from cigarettes. In this and other Indian studies13,17, bidis were associated with drinking and lack of education, and men smoking only bidis consumed higher numbers daily than those smoking only cigarettes. Therefore, although bidis were associated with higher risks than cigarettes (as in Mumbai14), this difference was so attenuated after standardizing for the daily number smoked, drinking and education as to suggest that residual confounding could largely or wholly account for whatever remained of it. Residual confounding cannot plausibly account for most of the difference between smokers and non-smokers but could well account for some of it.

For alcohol, the mortality RR comparing men who did and did not drink was 1.52 in analyses adjusted for smoking, SES and education, an excess of 52% (with narrow confidence limits) and a twofold difference in the usual amount drunk (mean 14 versus 28 10-g units per week) corresponded to a twofold difference in excess mortality (39% versus 78%). This RR of 1.52 among men who drank was greater than in three other largely or wholly Indian prospective studies (RR of 1.1 in Kerala, 1.2 in Mumbai and 1.3 in PURE16,18,19), although among heavier drinkers, the excess mortality was comparable in PURE and this study. The generally lower RRs in other Indian prospective studies of drinkers may reflect differences in usual alcohol intake, which are difficult to compare between studies or unexplained oddities in the small group who drank but did not smoke, as this small group strongly affects the alcohol RR.

Smoking and drinking are closely correlated; therefore, it is difficult to assess reliably their separate effects, especially because smoking or drinking patterns in men doing both may differ from those in men doing only one. Nevertheless, their joint effects are easier to assess reliably because the number doing both is large, as is the twofold mortality RR comparing them with men doing neither. If this excess mortality is mainly causal, about half the premature mortality in men who both smoke and drink is due to the combined effects of those habits. The attributable proportion may well, however, be somewhat less than half, as the confounding of smoking and drinking with lack of education is so strong that some residual confounding is likely to remain in education-adjusted analyses.

The absolute differences in mortality associated with smoking and drinking were similar in men with no schooling and with tertiary education; therefore, the proportional differences were greater in those with tertiary education. In Mumbai18 there were no strong interactions with years of education (≤8 versus >8 years) for drinking or cigarette smoking, but for bidi smoking, the RR was greater with more education. Most other studies were in high-income countries and did not report much interaction6,7,9,11,12,18.

Although our asset-based household SES quartiles were inversely associated with mortality, they were poorly reproducible at resurvey (partly reflecting the rapid pace of economic development in India) and of little independent relevance in analyses adjusted for educational level, smoking and drinking. By contrast, years of schooling were, for unclear reasons, remarkably strongly and independently predictive of mortality from each of the nine groups of causes, even among men and women who never smoked or drank. Inverse associations with many causes might well reflect major disparities in access to effective healthcare, but we cannot quantify these. The inverse association of educational level with mortality confirms5 that there are strong social determinants of health but does not identify their proximal causes or indicate that substantially more education would substantially reduce the disparities. It was stronger in this study than in high-income settings24,25, perhaps reflecting greater social disparities in Chennai26 or greater disparities in healthcare27.

It may, however, not be necessary to understand the exact mechanisms of social inequalities in health to reduce them, partly by widespread access to effective healthcare and partly by addressing the wider determinants of health (for example, education, housing and lifestyle factors). As India developed over the past half-century, the probability that a 50-year-old would die before age 70 years decreased from about half at 1970 death rates (relating to people born in the first quarter of the century) to only about one third at 2010 rates28 (relating to those born around the mid-century, as in our study); therefore, further substantial progress can reasonably be anticipated, especially if access to effective healthcare improves29.

The chief strengths of this study are the large sample size, quality control of the baseline survey, availability of a partial resurvey (allowing assessment of the long-term relevance of the information collected at baseline), active follow-up of deaths (ensuring near-completeness, except in demolished areas), verbal autopsy methods (allowing better assessment of causes of death than from official records and reducing the number of unspecific and unknown causes of death), the experience of the Chennai-based researchers in conducting large-scale epidemiological studies (which also accounts for the study’s location) and exclusion of individuals with chronic disease at recruitment (helping avoid biases owing to reverse causality). Although the main mortality analyses used ever-smoking and ever-drinking only at baseline, the sample resurvey showed these rarely varied, limiting any adverse consequences of our inability to account for time-varying exposures and confounding in the absence of repeat resurveys of the full study population. Most men who smoked also drank, most men who drank also smoked and the number doing both was substantially larger than the numbers doing only one. The risk associated with doing both can therefore be assessed reliably. By contrast, the subdivision of this risk into two parts, one associated with smoking and one with drinking, is less reliable, as it depends on the risks in the smaller groups with only one habit (and on assumptions about the independence of their effects). As smoking, drinking and lack of education are correlated, some of the adjusted risk associated with smoking or drinking may well reflect residual or unmeasured confounding.

Major limitations are that no blood or other biological material was stored; therefore, no analyses of genetic or biochemical factors are possible, and little information was collected on physical activity or dietary factors. Verbal autopsy methods did not distinguish between several causes of death (such as ischaemic and haemorrhagic stroke), and analyses describe only mortality not incidence. Moreover, apart from smoking and drinking, the correlates of education that are directly or indirectly responsible for its inverse association with mortality are largely unknown; in particular, no information was collected on caste, income or access to healthcare.

In this population, very low body mass index (BMI <20 kg m−2) was associated with lack of education and increased all-cause mortality30. Blood pressure was not strongly associated with educational level, although it was strongly related to mortality; each 20-mm Hg difference in usual systolic blood pressure (SBP) was associated with a twofold difference in vascular mortality30, as in Western populations31.

Perhaps less importantly, the population is not representative of Chennai (as it omits homeless or transient people and may somewhat underrepresent the wealthy), of all Indian cities (as smoking and drinking prevalences vary, although Chennai is not exceptional in the context of India’s major cities with respect to educational opportunities or the prevalence of major risk factors) or of rural India. The qualitative findings should, however, be relevant throughout India and in many other middle-income settings2,4: social inequalities in adult mortality are remarkably steep, for unclear reasons, and smoking, drinking and, particularly, their combination exacerbate these differences.

Realistic strategies for limiting mortality from smoking and drinking should recognize that, at least for this generation in this population, virtually none of the men who were smoking or drinking in about the year 2000 actually stopped over the next decade or more, despite the strong global and national pressures to change behaviour. Increased taxation could help32 but could also exacerbate inequalities. How, realistically, can men best be protected from continuing and women from starting? Even more importantly, how can realistic strategies be found for sustainably improving the delivery of effective healthcare, particularly to those most in need of it? At present, the main reasons for the extraordinarily strong relationships in this study between educational status and adult mortality remain unclear and need to be better understood.

Methods

Ethics statement

This is a historical study involving questionnaires, physical measurements and active follow-up (with no biological material collected from the study participants). The study research proposal was approved in 1997 by the Cancer Institute (WIA) in Chennai, ensuring that the study fulfilled the criteria required by the Indian Council of Medical Research regarding the treatment of human subjects in medical research. The Chennai Epidemiological Research Center’s Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) now has ethical oversight for the study. Secondary analyses of anonymized data have been approved by the IEC and Oxford’s Tropical Research Ethics Committee.

Study design, participants, and procedures

The study design, population and methods have been described previously30,33. The study took place in the south Indian city of Chennai (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the baseline survey, from 1 January 1998 to 31 December 2001, fieldworkers sought to visit all households in two of the city’s ten administrative zones and all in either 30% or 50% of the streets (randomly selected) in five of the remaining eight zones. All household members aged ≥35 years were invited to participate; 5% (25,184 out of 525,994) of those present declined, and 500,810 were recruited after providing informed consent (which was verbal, to be fully inclusive of those with minimal schooling) to the in-person interview and to passive (through public municipal records) and active follow-up. Trained fieldworkers administered a questionnaire (Supplementary Information pages 2–4) that included occupation (but not income), household indicators of SES and, for each participant in the household, demographic characteristics, educational level, tobacco smoking habits, alcohol drinking habits, selected dietary factors, medical history and the participant’s children’s names (for verification during any follow-up of fieldwork reliability and participant identity). Physical measurements included height, weight, peak expiratory flow rate and blood pressure but no invasive tests. Weekly throughout the baseline survey, 5% of recent interviewees were randomly selected for prompt re-interview and remeasurement by a fieldwork supervisor for quality control and fieldworker training and discipline. To help assess reproducibility and usual intake levels, a resurvey was undertaken in 2013–2014, about 14 years after the baseline survey, of 10,161 participants in randomly selected streets in the study areas, using the same procedures and precautions.

Educational level was self-reported as years of formal education, later categorized as none, 1–7, 8–11 or >11 (tertiary). A composite household-asset-based SES score was obtained by summing scores on the basis of several self-reported indicators—housing type and number of rooms; housing ownership; type of cooking fuel; and household ownership of a bicycle, motorcycle, car, refrigerator, television or electric grain grinder (but not individual characteristics, for example, caste, religion, occupation or income)—and the score was grouped into quartiles. Data were self-reported on smoking (each of cigarettes, bidis or other) and chewing (various products) and included status (never, ex or current), frequency (times per day) and ages at starting and stopping. Data were self-reported on drinking each of various alcoholic products including status, frequency (days per week), quantity on days when drinking and ages at starting and stopping, from which weekly intake was estimated in standard 10-g units of alcohol. For smoking and for drinking, the main analyses compared ever- versus never-users, as cessation (before or after baseline) was uncommon (Results).

Mortality was monitored by active and passive follow-up to 2020, when coronavirus disease 2019 intervened. Active follow-up involved visits to all houses (annually to 2005 and then biennially) by trained non-medical graduate fieldworkers. Participant identity and fieldwork integrity was verified by fieldworkers recording again participants’ children’s names, blinded to the baseline information. If participants had moved, information was sought from neighbours about when and where to. For those moving elsewhere in Chennai, follow-up was attempted at the new address. Those who could not be contacted or traced (generally because of area demolition for redevelopment) were monitored by passive follow-up. This used manual and computerized probabilistic linkage to city mortality records, considering name, age, sex, address and name of father or spouse, seeking confirmation of probable matches by fieldworker visits.

Causes of death were sought by verbal autopsy, using fieldworker interviews with families or other informants34,35. An open narrative, supplemented by a short checklist, yielded a one-page chronological description, written in the field, of any signs or symptoms and any treatment received before death. Again for training, quality control and discipline, a randomly selected 5% sample of narratives were repeated by a supervisor visiting informants a week after the fieldworker35. Each written narrative was assessed by two study doctors independently assigning a three-character ICD-10 code for the probable underlying cause (Supplementary Table 1), then discussing and resolving any discrepancies.

Statistical analyses

The main analyses excluded participants with chronic disease (myocardial infarction, stroke, cancer, asthma or TB) to avoid or limit reverse causality and are of mortality at ages 35–69 years, because verbal autopsy arrives at the probable underlying cause of death reliably in middle age (7% of deaths before 70 years were from unspecified causes in this study; Supplementary Table 1) but less so at older ages (42% of deaths at 70 years or older were from unspecified causes)34,35. Follow-up (from recruitment) was censored at 1 January 2020, emigration from Chennai or death.

Cox regression analyses were used to estimate RRs by educational level (four categories), SES score (quartiles), smoking (ever or never) and drinking (ever or never), stratified by 5-year groups of age at risk from 35–69 years (censoring at age 70 years), sex and, where appropriate, adjusted for each other. Although the ratio of the mortality rates in two categories may vary during follow-up, Cox regression can still be used to estimate an overall mortality RR, that is, effectively averaged over the whole follow-up period (without requiring an exactly constant ‘proportional hazard’). Ages were recorded in completed years (thus, 0.5 was added to mean ages).

Where there are four categories, Cox regression arbitrarily designates one the reference group and yields the variances and co-variances of the log RR values for the three others. From them, the variance of the log mortality rate in each of the four groups (one being the reference group) was estimated36. These do not depend on which group was arbitrarily designated the reference group, and each approximates the inverse of the number of deaths in its group. If the variance of the log mortality rate in one group is V, the 95% CI plotted for that group’s RR runs from RR × exp(−1.96√V) to RR × exp(1.96√V). For the reference group (with RR of 1) as for other groups, its CI then describes how chance could have affected the log mortality rate in that one group.

Participants resurveyed after about 14 years were categorized by smoking habits reported at baseline (past or current <10, 10 or >10 cigarettes or bidis per day), and for each baseline-assigned category mean consumption per day at resurvey yielded the estimated usual cigarette or bidi consumption during follow-up among all study participants in that category (not just those resurveyed). Similarly for alcohol, resurveyed participants were categorized by drinking habits reported at baseline (past or current ≤1, 2–6 or 7 days week−1), and for each baseline-assigned category, mean intake per week at resurvey yielded the estimated usual alcohol intake during follow-up in all participants. All-cause mortality RRs (estimated from all participants and adjusted for age, education and alcohol or smoking) in these baseline-assigned smoking or drinking categories were plotted against usual consumption, thereby allowing fully for the ‘regression dilution bias’ that can result from random errors or within-person temporal variability in baseline measurements when assessing associations between an exposure and disease37.

The relevance of education, smoking and drinking to the probability of a 35-year-old dying before age 70 years was assessed by combining the risk factor prevalences and age-adjusted RRs in this study with age- and sex-specific all-cause mortality rates from Chennai official statistics38. The probability of a 35-year-old dying before age 70 years was calculated by multiplying the RRs by a common constant to make their inverse-variance-weighted average match the uniformly age-standardized death rate per 1,000 person-years at ages 35–69 years (that is, the simple mean of the seven age-specific rates at ages 35–39 years to 65–69 years) in urban Tamil Nadu in 2010 (the median year of death in the study)39.

The analyses used SAS (v9.4) and R (v4.1.2).
