Not All Subjectivity Is the Same! Defining Desiderata for the Evaluation of Subjectivity in NLP
Urja Khurana, Michiel van der Meer, Enrico Liscio, Antske Fokkens Pradeep K. Murukannaiah Department of Intelligent Systems, Delft University of Technology Leiden Institute of Advanced Computer Science (LIACS), Leiden University Computational Linguistics and Text Mining Lab, Vrije Universiteit Amsterdam {u.khurana, e.liscio, p.k.murukannaiah}@tudelft.nl,m.t.van.der.meer@liacs.leidenuniv.nl, antske.fokkens@vu.nl

Abstract
Subjective judgments are part of several NLP datasets and recent work is increasingly prioritizing models whose outputs reflect this diversity of perspectives. Such responses allow us to shed light on minority voices, which are frequently marginalized or obscured by dominant perspectives. It remains a question whether our evaluation practices align with these models’ objectives. This position paper proposes seven evaluation desiderata for subjectivity-sensitive models, rooted in how subjectivity is represented in NLP data and models. The desiderata are constructed in a top-down approach, keeping in mind the user-centric impact of such models. We scan the experimental setup of 60 60 papers and show that various aspects of subjectivity are still understudied: the distinction between ambiguous and polyphonic input, whether subjectivity is effectively expressed to the user, and a lack of interplay between different desiderata, amongst other gaps.

2
Subjectivity in Data

To establish a fitting evaluation paradigm, we start by describing how subjectivity and related concepts are interpreted in the NLP field. Next, we discuss what the
sources
of subjectivity are. Finally, we reflect on the types of information to incorporate in a subjectivity-sensitive dataset.

2.1
Subjectivity and Related Concepts

Subjectivity pertains to the existence of multiple valid ground-truths
(
101
;
79
;
70
)
. NLP literature also discusses other concepts related to subjectivity.
Perspectivism

(
10
;
25
)
recognizes that the ground-truth depends on individual perspectives. This has taken shape in the form of a
perspectivist
framework that enables representing different perspectives in data;
weak
when taking multiple perspectives into account only during the annotation stage and
strong
when extending this to incorporating multiple perspectives in the model output as well. Subjective interpretations can lead to
disagreement

(
98
)
when different individuals have opposing views on a specific input.
70
uses the term
human label variation
to refer to instances with different plausible perspectives, since
disagreement
could imply that at least one judgment is incorrect.
Disagreement
can also arise in factual matters. This captures annotator
errors
, either from misunderstanding (genuine misinformation or misinterpreting explicit annotation guidelines) or attention drifts during annotation.

2.2
Sources of Subjectivity

Where do multiple viewpoints or beliefs that lead to disagreement stem from?
5
suggest three potential sources of subjectivity in their Triangle of Reference framework that maps out the process of interpreting an input:
task ambiguity
,
input ambiguity
, and
annotator attitude
.
1
1

1

We adopt the terminology for these three sources from
42
and
54
.

Task Ambiguity.

Imprecise task definitions and annotation guidelines may (un)intentionally leave enough space for an annotator to fill the gaps through their own interpretation
(
79
)
. For instance, in “
Berlin
decided …
”, the entity
Berlin
can be annotated as location or organization. Without explicitly guiding annotators in such scenarios (in this case, where a city name is used to refer to a government), unintended disagreement can arise. Precise guidelines can still leave some room for personal interpretation, e.g., in Natural Language Inference where implications are debatable or in hate speech where severity is a personal judgment.

Input Ambiguity.

Even when the task is clearly defined, an input can still warrant multiple interpretations, e.g., when more context is needed to make an accurate judgment. Consider an input that contains a reclaimed slur; determining whether it is hate speech or not requires the knowledge of
who
is behind the piece of text.

Annotator Attitude.

Factors such as an annotator’s demographic, emotional state, and experience can yield different valid judgments
87
. This is particularly the case when there is no universally agreed upon ground-truth.

2.3
Incorporating Subjectivity in Data

To train and evaluate models that can handle subjectivity, adequate datasets should be available. The conventional approach to building datasets in NLP is to collect
n
annotations per sample and aggregate them into a single “gold” label, e.g., through majority voting. However, when there is variation in annotator response, this results in a loss of information on human disagreement. This has led to calls for the release of raw annotations
(
73
;
10
;
65
)
, with recent work showing the importance of having a large number of annotations per sample
(
44
;
30
)
.

Annotators.

Beyond the raw annotations, other dimensions are required to model subjectivity effectively. To model the opinion of a specific individual, for each annotator we would need demographic information
(
8
;
73
)
and a sufficient amount of annotated samples. Moreover, disagreement can also stem from annotation mistakes (e.g., due to attention drift), requiring methods
(
27
;
105
;
43
;
37
)
and metrics
(
1
)
that can distinguish such
noise
from actual signal.
18
introduce metrics that capture inter-annotator disagreement in annotation, taking into account the interdependency of the sources of subjectivity.

Annotator Population.

Raw annotations and annotator information are especially valuable when we are strategic about
who
is annotating. When subjectivity needs to be captured, carrying out the annotation procedure and choosing the appropriate annotator(s) is an essential task. Smarter sample annotation techniques are needed to get a wider picture of how an input is perceived. This could be accomplished either by ensuring that a specific sample is annotated by a variety of demographics
(
99
)
or by selecting annotators of a specific target group
(
28
)
.
68

17
advocate for a more thoughtful process by providing a framework for annotation collection that takes into account subjectivity throughout the multiple annotation stages.

3
Dimensions of Subjective Input

What do the sources of subjectivity mean for a subjectivity-sensitive model? Where
task ambiguity
plays an overarching role when collecting ground-truth labels across multiple samples,
input ambiguity
and
annotator attitude
can primarily occur at a sample level. These two sources require the model to handle subjectivity differently, an aspect that has not yet been addressed in previous research. We characterize model behavior when dealing with different sources of subjectivity on two dimensions:
ambiguity
and
polyphony
(Figure
2
).

Figure 2:
Dimensions of sources of subjectivity at a
sample-level
:
ambiguity
on the x-axis and
polyphony
on the y-axis. For each quadrant, we show the desired model output (as icons) with an example situated in hate speech detection.

When an input is non-ambiguous and monophonic, a model can directly incorporate the objective ground-truth. However, modeling scenarios involving subjective factors is more challenging as the following subsections illustrate.

3.1
Ambiguous
\leftrightarrow
Non-Ambiguous

An input is
ambiguous
when it can warrant different interpretations because the information (e.g., context) needed to make a judgment is incomplete. Inputs that can be interpreted without filling information gaps are
non-ambiguous
.

Ambiguous.

When faced with an ambiguous input, a model should identify the spectrum of different possible interpretations and disambiguations. For instance, in
“I will take this b
{
*/i
}
tch out
” the ambiguity lies in who the
b-word
refers to; the slur or the dog. In a multi-turn conversation, the model can engage with the user to increase the information needed to resolve ambiguity. When a response is directly needed, the model can provide the user with the different possibilities accordingly.

Non-ambiguous.

A non-ambiguous input removes the requirement for a model to understand the different interpretations. In such a situation, the model can directly give a suitable response based on the exact information outlined in the input.

3.2
Polyphonic
\leftrightarrow
Monophonic

Independent of ambiguity, certain inputs may not have an agreed-upon ground truth, allowing for judgments based on an individual’s interpretation. Personal taste, demographics, background, experience, and other factors influence this interpretation. We consider such cases to be
polyphonic
: different perspectives co-existing. Where there is unanimity, we consider that to be
monophonic
.

Polyphonic.

A polyphonic input is open to multiple valid perspectives, which the model should capture. Take for example the statement
“I hate boomers”
; the meaning is clear but the polyphony lies in whether boomers are considered targets of hate speech. A model that can capture polyphony for the right reasons will be able to generalize well in the real world. Thus, the model should recognize
what
aspect(s) of the input enable different perspectives and
why
, e.g., is it the gender or culture that potentially influences the opinion or are there other factors? Not every different perspective has to be attributed to specific demographics, certain deviations can also be due to individual differences.

Monophonic.

When an input has a single valid belief, often connected to factuality, the model can directly predict the ground-truth without the need to consider a variety of potential perspectives. For instance, the phrase
“Exclude those *** from society”
should not warrant any subjective interpretation due to its overt form of hate speech.

3.3
Ambiguous and Polyphonic

Ambiguity and polyphony can be intertwined. Consider an input that lacks context
and
warrants multiple perspectives, e.g.,
“Wow, they are such a great community”
. Here, the ambiguity lies in the sentiment of the sentence and the polyphony in the gravity of the (potential) negative sentiment. In such a scenario, a model is expected to capture the appropriate disambiguation and clarification questions, and the different possible perspectives.

4
Subjectivity in Models

How a model expresses subjectivity influences its evaluation. Thus, we discuss ways of expressing and instilling subjectivity in models, considering both classification and free-text generation objectives.

4.1
Expressing Subjectivity

To move away from rigid responses in line with the majority preference, the model’s output should reflect the spectrum of different perspectives or be aligned to an individual’s perspective.

Spectrum of Perspectives.

A model that reflects diverse perspectives and values is
pluralistically aligned
.
91
map out two operationalizations of pluralistic alignment that reflect a multitude of viewpoints.
(1) In the
distributional
approach, the model outputs the distribution of perspectives in the generated response, allowing the user to be informed of the presence of a variety of other opinions. This can either be expressed in the logits or softmax distribution of the model, or directly in the generated response
(
58
)
.
(2) In the
overton
approach, the generated response summarizes different
reasonable
perspectives. Determining what is reasonable depends on the individual; thus, it requires (some degree of) personalization. While an autoregressive model would generate a free-text response, an autoencoder can express this through a multi-label setup.

Individual Preferences.

A model that can tailor its response according to the preferences of an individual or a group (where these preferences are shared) is
steerable

(
91
)
. The model considers a variety of perspectives and values and steers its response to be closest to the perspective of the user. This is closely related to
personalization
, where preferences go beyond values and include matters such as
textual style
or
lifestyle
(e.g., food or films)
(
109
)
.

4.2
Instilling Subjectivity

We distinguish between approaches that introduce the notion of subjectivity at inference, when training or fine-tuning, and when aligning the models.

Inference-time Strategies.

Integrating subjectivity at test time is a more computationally efficient solution than training/fine-tuning, particularly in the case of LLMs. A popular approach is persona prompting
(
86
;
16
)
,
where the model is instructed in a prompt to impersonate a specific
persona
. This can span different demographics. The persona could either steer the subjectivity toward a specific perspective or provide a spectrum of multiple perspectives.
Instead of a persona, we can also retrieve user history (examples relevant to the task of choice) and include it in the prompt using Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG;
51
,
51
). The model then uses this history to adapt its response. Used for a variety of personalization tasks
(
52
;
82
;
83
;
61
;
75
)
, this approach, to our knowledge, has not been applied to
pluralistic steering
.

Training/Fine-tuning.

Different approaches have been devised to enable subjective modeling during the fine-tuning stage. The conventional approach is to use
soft
labels, i.e., the distribution of class annotations for a single sample
(
39
;
96
;
23
;
107
)
. Instead of optimizing for a single (aggregated)
hard
label, the goal is to minimize the distance between the estimated distribution and the actual distribution. This is typically done using distance-based loss functions such as
cross-entropy
,
Kullback Leibler-
, or
Jensen-Shannon divergence
.
Another avenue is learning from individual annotators’ labels to make distributional or single predictions
(
77
)
. Strategies for this include fine-tuning on annotator-specific labels
(
15
;
33
)
, ensembling over annotator-specific models
(
44
)
, multi-task settings
(
59
)
, and PEFT-based strategies that provide a cheaper solution than fully fine-tuning autoregressive models
(
95
;
36
)
.

Alignment Methods.

Preference tuning methods for large language models are designed primarily to optimize according to the majority preference
(
11
)
. As such, strategies are being developed to create
pluralistic
alignment methods, i.e., methods that can reflect a multitude of preferences and tailor their responses accordingly
(
111
;
72
;
40
)

5
Desiderata for Subjective Models

After outlining the desired model output when facing a subjective input, the natural next step is to evaluate its performance.
But what does it mean for a model to perform well on subjective input?

To answer this question, we propose desiderata for a subjectivity-sensitive model. These desiderata are motivated by three overarching ideals rooted in our preceding discussion of how subjectivity is represented in NLP data and models: (1) representation of all, including minority voices, (2) based on the correct sources of subjectivity in the input, and (3) the representation of subjectivity (in data and models) generalizing to the real world.

D1.

Recognize when subjective modeling is necessary

As we highlight in Section
2.1
, certain inputs have multiple valid perspectives. For a model to make predictions that reflect this diversity, the first step is to
recognize when
an input needs to be treated subjectively versus objectively. Even when a task is generally considered to be subjective, this step is vital—there will be cases where raters will have consensus, e.g., when dealing with a severe case of hate speech; predicting an aggregate estimate would be sufficient in that case.

D2.

Recognize which type of subjective modeling is needed

As we describe in Section
3
, model behavior depends on the type of subjective input. Thus, a model should
recognize which
type of subjectivity the input contains: ambiguity, polyphony, or both.

D3.

Recognize why there is subjectivity in the input

Building on the desired model behavior we outline in Section
3
, a model should
recognize why
there is ambiguous or polyphonic subjectivity in the input. Using this information, the model can delineate the different disambiguations, clarifications, or perspectives to resolve. This facilitates better predictions and more reliable generalization to unseen scenarios.

D4.

Predict subjectivity accurately

After recognizing the source of the subjectivity, a model should predict the subjectivity correctly.
A model can
predict
subjectivity in different ways, as we discuss in Section
4.1
.

D5.

Calibrate the output with respect to the frequency of opinions

As we illustrate in Section
4.2
, certain models are trained to estimate the distribution of human judgment for an input. Depending on how subjectivity is expressed (distributional or overton), the predicted distribution should be
calibrated
to the actual human judgment distribution. This applies both when a probability distribution is obtained directly from the model and when an overton response is generated. For an overton response, the linguistic quantifiers should be well-calibrated. This also extends to when a model relies on uncertainty metrics to identify whether it has encountered a subjective input.

D6.

Represent all perspectives

A subjectivity-sensitive model should account for the minority voices that are usually lost in aggregation. The model should
represent
a range of diverse viewpoints, minimizing bias toward dominant perspectives. Relevant perspectives should be decoupled from dominant perspectives. For example, it should not only echo a Western perspective but also other cultures when the input warrants it.

D7.

Express subjectivity without hampering user experience

A subjectivity-sensitive model carries with it the responsibility of
informing
a user of different perspectives. This mirrors the responsibility of showing users explanations of decision-making in the field of explainable AI (XAI). What counts as a “good” explanation depends on the user
(
20
)
.
In a similar vein, we argue that subjectivity should be
expressed
in a way that does not hamper user experience. Consider a model giving a summary of all the different perspectives. It is of limited use when a response enumerates all potential perspectives,
2
2

2

Note that this excludes the setting where the output of the model is
distributional
.
making it rather infeasible to grasp the overall view. Here, it should strike a balance between being concise and detailed according to user preference.

Practical considerations.

Our desiderata can be evaluated sequentially. Note that the model does not have to be explicitly designed to address all of the desiderata, but can capture them implicitly as well (e.g., a model does not have to be explicitly designed to output binary labels for
D1
, it can cover this desideratum implicitly when directly predicting the human judgment distribution). To what extent all the desiderata should be covered by the model also depends on the application, e.g., for an NLI model doing requirement analysis,
D6.

Represent all perspectives
is not of utmost importance.

6
Subjectivity Evaluation Practices

In Section
5
, we formulate the desiderata for a subjectivity-sensitive model in a top-down fashion. We now look bottom-up, exploring how these desiderata translate to concrete evaluation practices. We discuss existing metrics and analyze how current evaluation practices relate to our desiderata in both classification and free-text generation settings.

8
Conclusion

The impactful nature of NLP models in society demands that these models be sensitive to subjectivity, representing multiple perspectives in their output.
We analyze the current evaluation paradigms of subjectivity-sensitive models.
We begin by understanding subjectivity and how it is represented in NLP data and models, which leads us to define the desiderata for a subjectivity-sensitive model. We take a top-down approach to design
seven
desiderata, driven by our ideals for a model to (1) represent all, based on (2) the correct source of subjectivity to (3) generalize to the real world.
We then analyze
60
papers to map evaluation metrics to our desiderata. This mapping allows us to identify gaps, which can be fruitful directions for future work. Among these gaps, we primarily find that existing work has overlooked nuances in the types of subjectivity: polyphony versus ambiguity, and we argue that this distinction is crucial for effective evaluation. Additionally, current research efforts do not touch on our seventh desideratum:
Express
, nor the interplay between different desiderata. We finally highlight the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration to facilitate datasets and annotation strategies that can better capture subjectivity, and in turn, improve subjectivity-sensitive model development and evaluation.
