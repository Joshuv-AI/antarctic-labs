Executive Summary
Recent research, including the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Grid Modernization Lab
Consortium (GMLC) Designing Resilient Communities project, has investigated the relationship
between utility investment planning and community goals and identified a central challenge:
Utilities and their regulators have not adjusted the investment planning process to robustly
calculate resilience metrics and weigh them against broader community goals (Jeffers et al.
2023). To evaluate resilience investments with confidence, the metrics that best reflect the
resilience goals of communities must be scientifically validated. The Planning for Seattle at the
Convergence Of Resilience and Energy (SCORE) project demonstrates how this challenge can
be addressed through the development and application of a novel resilience metric in a municipal
utility planning context. SCORE iterates and applies the social burden metric originally
introduced by Jeffers et al. (2018), extended by Wachtel et al. (2022), and tied to human
development theory by Clark et al. (2023). SCORE’s hypothesis is that resilience investments,
when sited strategically using a robust resilience metric, offer measurable benefits for most
customers in a neighborhood—regardless of their direct electrical connection to an investment—
by supporting their access to critical services during potential disruptions.

Common grid reliability metrics do not capture important temporal, spatial, and categorical
priorities for increasing energy resilience. Systemwide or feeder-level outage metrics do not
identify:

 • Which kinds of services are affected by outages, including critical household needs like
 nutrition and health.
 • Where services are without power, including specific locations that may lack proximate
 alternatives that households can access during disruptions.
 • How long specific services are disrupted, recognizing that longer disruptions of certain
 services impact households more profoundly than relatively short disruptions.

Outage metrics can indicate the impacts of outages but cannot measure the full scope of
consequences to customers that could result from those outages. The consequences of power
outages for surrounding residents are caused by disruptions to electricity-dependent critical
services, in addition to electricity itself. Power outages can decrease a community’s access to
healthcare, fuel, safe indoor temperatures, and provisions, such as food and water. This project
developed a consequence-focused resilience metric, critical service access, which quantifies the
relative access to critical services provided to households by distribution infrastructure during
normal conditions and major disruptions. Critical service access is derived from previously
published social burden methods, operationalized to produce household-level results that
consider transportation networks, a simplified service framework, and the role individual
households play in resilience. This work also extends published social burden methods to

visualize the spatial relationship between electrical infrastructure, critical services, and hourly
outage risk.

Critical service access is calculated by analyzing the spatial relationship between households,
critical services, and distribution system components to understand the types of critical services

 v

that share common energy infrastructure and the value those services have for surrounding
households. This metric allows utilities and their stakeholders to move beyond a binary
identification of certain locations as “critical,” toward a continuous measurement of the
criticality of each distribution system component, which varies by household and by outage
duration. The metric also demonstrates the resilience benefits that grid investments can have for
customers outside a specific feeder or section where an upgrade is installed. Critical service
access has useful attributes that can support grid investment prioritization and system planning.
The metric can be calculated for a single household during normal conditions or aggregated
across households, distribution feeders, and outages to understand how electricity supports
resilience in a specific area over time.

Decreases in critical service access during outages—or “access interruptions”—can be used to
measure, categorize, and compare the potential consequences of long duration outage scenarios
and investment opportunities. Utilities can also use an analysis of access interruption to quantify
the benefits of potential resilience upgrades that reduce access interruption risk. Most utilities
plan reliability and resilience investments at a feeder level. Feeders are the individual distribution
circuits served by substations. While some feeders may only serve a few large customers, most
feeders have hundreds or thousands of customers. This project used a spatially granular grid
analysis that facilitates targeted resilience interventions at a feeder- or feeder-segment level.
Dividing feeders into isolatable sections and combining those sections with the critical service
access metric allows planners to identify where energy improvements, such as diesel generators,
solar-plus-storage, distribution automation and distributed generation microgrids, could best
protect access to food, fuel, health, shelter, and other public safety services.

This project did not differentiate between types of resilience investments, because the
community resilience benefits generated by an energy investment depend more on the services
supported by the investment and the people who accesses those services during outages – i.e.,
where the investment is installed in relation to households, services, and outages – than on the
type of technology installed. In this analysis, hypothetical investments in solar-plus-storage,
distribution automation, diesel generators, or microgrids are considered identical; what varies is
the feeder or feeder segment on which a hypothetical investment is installed. However, different
resilience investments create different costs and benefits for local customers. Future work should
analyze the cost, savings, and emissions impacts associated with each investment option to
understand where these factors may align with or contradict investment locations identified using
only the resilience metric produced by this project.

Locations were identified in a South Seattle, Washington study area that could be high priorities
for resilience investment and summarize their potential neighborhood-scale benefits, both for
residents and for the distribution system. This analysis was complemented by direct feedback
from study area residents collected with survey and focus group methodologies. Key takeaways
from this analysis include the following observations:

 • Critical service access is highly distributed. Across Seattle, most feeders provide less
 than 1% of the city’s critical service access (Figure ES-1). The feeder with the highest
 contribution to citywide critical service access (2.5%) is downtown. Feeders with the
 greatest contributions to critical service access are those that power many nonresidential

 vi

 service locations in areas that are easily accessed by many households. The top 10
 feeders shown in Figure ES-1 together power 346 nonresidential service locations and
 more than 9,000 residential locations and provide 14% of Seattle’s critical service access.
 Although these results indicate an outage on any single feeder will not cause widespread
 or severe disruptions to critical services in aggregate, long duration outages involving
 many feeders have the potential to significantly reduce critical service access for certain
 households.

  boundaries. The inclusion of locations on underground feeders in aggregated critical service
 access and the exclusion of underground feeders from the joint analysis leads to a slight
 overestimation of the total critical service access on downtown feeders.

  • Proximity to powered services mitigates household impacts. A long duration outage at
 an individual household will reduce that household’s ability to access electricity-
 dependent services typically accessed at home: cooking and storing food, storing
 medication, using electrical medical devices, thermal comfort, and communicating with
 people outside the home. However, the status of power at nonresidential service locations
 around that household will either exacerbate or mitigate its loss of critical service access.

 vii

 A household that loses power but is relatively close to powered nonresidential critical
 services such as grocery stores or pharmacies will experience relatively fewer access-
 hours interrupted. A household that maintains power or is restored quickly but lives
 farther from powered critical service locations still experiences nonzero access-hours
 interrupted because its ability to meet its needs outside the home is reduced.
  • Grid upgrades benefit everyone. Upgrades such as automatic switching equipment and
 ties to nearby feeders installed on one feeder section can still benefit households located
 on another section, as demonstrated by a simulated outage. The simulation suggests that
 upgrade benefits spill over: customers proximate to upgrades experience a 20%-40%
 reduction in critical service access interruptions (Figure ES-2). These results underscore
 the importance of measuring the benefits of utility investments for all customers by
 considering the value and proximity of critical service locations supported by those
 investments rather than considering only the customers directly powered by upgraded
 equipment.

  service access-hours interrupted at each household because of the investments implemented.
 All households experience benefits through increased access to nonresidential critical
 services, even households not powered by sections where investments are implemented.

  • Major outages can be identified using service access loss. Current methodologies
 identify “major event days” for the purpose of differentiating statistically aberrant outage
 causes like rare storms from more common performance issues. Our critical service

 viii

 access analysis suggests another way to define event severity, based on the duration and
 magnitude of access loss in each category of critical service. This process, customizable
 by utilities and their partners, can help decision makers plan energy and resilience
 improvements around the concrete outcomes that electricity supports rather than the
 abstract availability of electricity.
 • Frequent visits to critical service locations indicate widespread benefits. Responses
 from study area participants suggests households that rely on daily or weekly visits to
 dialysis centers, doctor’s offices, pharmacies, and food banks—an indication of
 heightened vulnerability to outages at these locations—also visit all locations more
 frequently. This observation suggests there are many opportunities to provide resilience
 benefits to all customers by investing in resilient energy at clusters of different types of
 critical service locations.
 • Home-based services drive potential outage consequences. Feedback from study area
 residents suggests maintaining access to water, food refrigeration, internet, and safe
 indoor temperatures during power disruptions—especially those lasting multiple days—
 could be key priorities for energy resilience projects in the study area. Access to services
 typically accessed in residential spaces is difficult to replicate in shared spaces without
 significant coordination with emergency management organizations and site operators.
 • Resilience hubs offer a strategic alternative. Although energy resilience investments
 for residential locations offer clear benefits, some of which cannot be replicated in a
 public service, maintaining power at all homes during major disruptions might not be
 affordable. Resilience hubs offer one possible alternative by locating key services within
 an accessible public facility with resilience power. Investment in resilience hubs presents
 an opportunity for Seattle City Light to coordinate with the Seattle Office of Emergency
 management and specific facility operators to implement energy resilience projects where
 services can be quickly, comfortably, and safely accessed by community members.
 Because the critical service access metric can be calculated separately for normal days
 and for outage days, this metric provides a baseline against which utilities can evaluate
 trade-offs between the provision of power at residential and nonresidential locations. The
 metric also provides an opportunity for utilities to evaluate a wider set of cost-effective
 strategies to mitigate outage consequences. By doing so, utilities can expand resilience
 planning beyond the provision of electricity to the improvement of outcomes during
 major outages, using feedback from residents that can be directly applied to investment
 decisions and benefit quantification.
 • Locations where multiple services are accessed in a single trip are high priority.
 Community engagement results suggest participants prefer service locations that offer
 multiple services. For example, participants who indicated Costco and the Safeway at
 9262 Rainier Avenue as top High Frequency locations also visit the on-site fuel stations
 and pharmacies at each of those chain grocery stores.

The critical service access metric provides additional, valuable information beyond the outage
statistics typically used by utilities to prioritize investments. The methods used to calculate
critical service access have the potential to help utilities map real customer consequences onto
operational infrastructure. By setting strategic targets for critical service access performance
during outage events, utilities can begin to manage outage impacts that are not currently

discernible to system planners and decision makers, and to thereby alleviate the consequences of
adverse system events for their customers.

1 Introduction
Energy system metrics often focus on the performance of physical infrastructure, failing to
consider the relationships between infrastructure systems and the economic and social networks
that rely on them. Moreover, many of these metrics are both spatially and temporally aggregated
at scales that do not support strategic, targeted resilience decisions. Recent research highlights
the importance of understanding interdependencies between critical services and households at
multiple geographics scales for effective disaster planning (Strengthening Post-Hurricane Supply
Chain Resilience 2020; Mitsova et al. 2021). Meanwhile, longstanding emergency management
guidance has acknowledged the definition of a critical facility can extend beyond hospitals, fire
stations, police stations, and emergency operations centers to include locations with a range of
criticality determined by local considerations (FEMA P-1019 Emergency Power Systems for
Critical Facilities: A Best Practices Approach to Improving Reliability 2014). Electric utilities
provide a networked resource on which most critical services depend. Evaluating the
performance of this networked, interdependent system during major disruptions and strategically
increasing its resilience requires metrics that consider impacts beyond the direct loss of
electricity and that can operate at multiple geographic scales.

In power system regulatory and planning communities, established reliability metrics such as the
System Average Interruption Duration Index (SAIDI) and the System Average Interruption
Frequency Index (SAIFI) have been supplemented by customer-centric metrics such as
Customers Experiencing Multiple Interruptions (CEMI) and Customers Experiencing Long
Interruption Durations (CELID; IEEE Draft Guide for Collecting, Categorizing, and Utilizing
Information Related to Electric Power Distribution Interruption Events 2022). These new metrics
highlight customer experiences that are typically masked by system-level performance metrics
reported by utilities, tracking customers that experience particularly frequent (CEMI) or
particularly long duration (CELID) outages. Although these metrics help shift focus from
systemwide averages toward individual impacts, they do not measure the potential consequences
of those outages. Reliability metrics capture electricity loss but not the implications of that loss
for households or communities.

The Planning for Seattle at the Convergence Of Resilience and Energy (SCORE) project
was designed to develop and apply a resilience metric focused on the consequences of power
outages for households in the Duwamish Valley of Seattle, Washington. The resulting
performance metric—critical service access—measures household access to critical services
provided at thousands of locations across the study area. This metric is combined with
distribution system information to quantify disruptions in service access and to identify
infrastructure that could be particularly important to the community during long duration
outages. This approach can be used alongside community engagement processes, cost-benefit
analyses, and other distribution system metrics to help utilities make informed resilience
investments by minimizing the most severe consequences of long duration power outages for
households.

 1

This work builds on foundational efforts to measure the connection between human needs and
energy system resilience. Day et al. apply the capabilities framework 7 to understand why energy
is used, and Clark et al. outline how that understanding can inform the prioritization of critical
infrastructure for mitigation (Day et al. 2016; Clark et al. 2018). Following long duration power
outages in Puerto Rico and Texas, Clark et al. (2022) and Peterson et al. (Peterson 2023;
Peterson et al. 2024) inventory and categorize how those outages burdened residents by
disrupting their ability to meet specific critical needs. Jeffers et al. demonstrate how those
burdens could be optimally reduced and systematically considered in a theoretical planning
process involving communities, utilities, city governments, and regulators (Jeffers et al. 2017;
2018; 2023). Clark et al. and Wachtel et al. refine the calculation of social burden toward a
standard that can measure the variation in power outage impacts among various customers
(Wachtel et al. 2022; Clark et al. 2023). Hart et al. (2024) use the social burden metric to show
spatial and demographic variation in potential outage consequences to support planning efforts in
the Southern California Edison utility territory. The SCORE project demonstrates how critical
service access—derived through modifications to the social burden metric—can support more
granular analysis of resilience investment trade-offs and be integrated more directly into utility
planning processes, such as siting feeder upgrades, gathering community priorities, and
establishing resilience performance targets. This work helps build confidence in the
measurement of human needs to improve both utility performance and community resilience.

The project team includes researchers from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL),
the Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI), the University of Washington (UW), and the
University at Buffalo (UB). NREL led project design and execution, including the development
of critical service access methods. EPRI led efforts to model distribution system outages and the
feeder 8 upgrades that decrease those outages, ensuring the project leveraged best-available
electric utility data and methods. UW and UB led the development, dissemination, and analysis
of a community survey and focus group discussions designed to explore resident priorities
related to power disruptions and critical services and deepen the connection between energy
system metrics and reported household needs. Seattle’s municipal electric utility—Seattle City
Light (SCL)—served as the project’s primary stakeholder, providing insight to the research team
about how the project’s methods could be integrated into the utility’s processes and priorities and
collaboratively shaping the project’s outputs for applicability to decision making.

Georgetown, South Park, Highland Park, South Beacon Hill, Dunlap, and Rainier Beach (Figure
1). Boundaries for these neighborhoods were sourced from the Seattle Department of
Neighborhoods; these neighborhoods are based on the Seattle City Clerk’s Office Geographic
Indexing Atlas and align with census geometries (City of Seattle, n.d.). Georgetown and South
Park are home to nearly 6,000 people and represent the core neighborhoods of the Duwamish
Valley. Additional adjacent neighborhoods were included in the project’s community
engagement and critical service access analysis to broaden the number of areas considered for

The Duwamish Valley is named for the native Duwamish Tribe, which has inhabited the area for
millennia. The valley is part of the watershed of the Duwamish River, which flows to the west
and north to the southern reaches of the City of Seattle. In 2001, 5.5 miles of the Duwamish
River were declared a Superfund site by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency after a long-
documented history of pollution from industrial activities along the Duwamish River. This
history of pollution has combined with socioeconomic factors and insufficient investment to
contribute to significant infrastructure vulnerabilities to major power disruptions. These
vulnerabilities can be partially addressed through energy resilience projects that target critical
service access—especially during emergencies (Duwamish Valley Action Plan 2022; Lovell et al.
2024; Ryan 2018).

Resilience investments such as infrastructure hardening, grid flexibility and control technologies,
and distributed energy resources produce benefits that are difficult to quantify because these
investments are designed to avoid rare, unpredictable, and unevenly distributed consequences. In

 3

many cases, grid resilience benefits are treated as a spillover benefit of investments that are
targeted to improve reliability and system capacity, a practice that fails to fully appreciate the
benefits of key investments in rare but highly consequential outages. To keep energy affordable,
utilities and other stakeholders seek to deploy resilience solutions where they can make the most
difference. The critical service access metric demonstrated in this report provides new
information to utilities about the potential benefits of energy resilience upgrades by exposing the
relationship between energy infrastructure and the economic, social, and transportation networks
that might depend on that infrastructure. Because the metric is computed at the household scale,
it measures the relative benefits that energy infrastructure—including both public and private
locations—provides to various customers based on their residential locations. These metrics pave
the way for decision makers to quantify customer benefits and to develop more spatially targeted
resilience strategies.

Seattle City Light provided distribution system data, including spatial, connectivity, load,
outages, and other characteristics of feeders across the municipal territory. The NREL team used
public data to develop the critical service access metric, and the metric was joined to the Seattle
feeder data to analyze the consequences associated with various outages (Sections 2, 3, and 4).
The EPRI team used distribution system data to model estimated outage durations at subfeeder
scales for more granular analysis (Sections 3 and 4). The UW and UB teams led community
engagement efforts, through which they gathered extensive information about community energy
resilience priorities to facilitate comparison with the critical service access metric and integration
into utility planning processes at SCL (Section 5). The project team integrated feedback from
decision makers at SCL to ensure results can be used to support strategic resilience investments.
High-priority investments identified by the project are summarized according to their
neighborhood-scale resilience benefits; this information can be included in utility presentations
to the Seattle City Council for suggested implementation of the investments.

This report first describes how the critical service access metric is calculated (Section 2). Section
3 presents an analysis of how critical service access changes in the study area during both a
simulated outage and historic outages. In Section 4, the extent to which potential investment
locations reduce outage durations and interruptions in critical service access for study area
households is evaluated. Finally, in Section 5, feedback provided by study area residents about
their priorities and concerns related to long duration power outages is summarized in light of the
critical service access metric.

2 Critical Service Access
The consequences of power outages for residents are caused by disruptions to electricity-
dependent critical services rather than to electricity itself. Some electrical circuits serve lighting
in empty rooms and golf courses during hurricanes, others pump basement sumps, maintain
medical equipment, and refrigerate food at grocery stores. Long duration power outages can
decrease a community’s access to healthcare, fuel, safe indoor temperatures, and provisions such
as food and water—especially when people lose power at their homes. Although power sector
organizations emphasize the importance of critical services in resilience planning, they also
acknowledge the multijurisdictional coordination required to measure and mitigate the
interdependencies between energy systems and critical services is a core challenge for the
industry (State Governance, Planning, and Financing to Enhance Energy Resilience 2021;
McCurry and Nethercutt 2023). This section introduces critical service access, a consequence-
focused resilience metric that quantifies the relative access to critical services provided to
households through supporting distribution infrastructure. This metric joins information typically
siloed in either electric utility planning or emergency management and can be evaluated across
any combination of geographic jurisdictions, supporting the multijurisdictional coordination
required for effective energy resilience planning. This metric was applied across all of Seattle
and present a neighborhood-scale analysis for South Seattle, using downscaled distribution
system data to support more targeted resilience planning.

Critical service access also addresses a core challenge utilities face as they prioritize distribution
system resources. In principle, and sometimes in law, all customers are created equal. But during
a system outage, utilities know that some customer outages cause secondary effects, limiting the
availability of shared services that businesses may provide. The consequences of an outage at a
specific location depend both on the kinds of services offered at that location and the number of
people that may rely on that location for those services. It follows that customer outages are not
equally consequential to residents and communities and that the number of customers out on a
feeder cannot provide full insight into the potential consequences caused by an outage on that
feeder. Critical service access addresses this challenge by analyzing the spatial relationship
between households, critical services, and distribution system components to understand where
energy resilience investments might provide the most benefits.

Public data, primarily from national sources, was used to identify locations providing critical
services to households across Seattle (see Appendix A.1 for data sources). Locations providing
critical services include residential buildings, which are assumed to provide services only to
people living on-site, and nonresidential buildings in 23 categories, which are assumed to
provide services to households within a 60-minute drive radius (Figure 2).

Critical service access measures a household’s access to all critical service locations it can reach
within a given travel time—in this case, an hour. This travel time threshold was selected to
consider a wide range of services surrounding the study area while limiting the computational
resources required to calculate travel routes between locations. As a result, most service
locations considered—especially churches, banks, pharmacies, child care centers, schools, and
urgent care locations—are located outside the SCL service territory. Table 1 shows the number
of nonresidential service locations powered within a 60-minute drive of Seattle households,
grouped by category of services provided. Note that this approach uses average traffic
conditions; future work can examine the affect of traffic-related disruptions on access to critical
services.

The distribution feeders included in the analysis are all radial feeders. In distribution grid design,
radial feeders are differentiated from networked feeders. Radials are fed by a single power
source rather than by a network of routes through which power can flow to reach an endpoint.
The multiple paths of a networked feeder allows added levels of resilience to outages. Although
Seattle City Light operates both radial and networked distribution feeders, the networked feeders

straight-line distance, an approach that oversimplifies the complex and shifting relationship

A scoring matrix assigns a value reflecting the relative quality or quantity of service provided by
each location type across five categories of electricity-dependent critical services: provisions,
health, public safety, shelter, and workforce (Table 3). These categories, and their corresponding
subcategories, were developed using U.S. Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA)
Community Lifeline guidance, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security critical infrastructure
sectors, human capabilities research, and community engagement conducted as part of this
project and others in the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Renewables Advancing Community
Energy Resilience portfolio (Whitehouse.Gov 2013; Clark et al. 2018; Federal Emergency
Management Agency 2023; Strengthening Post-Hurricane Supply Chain Resilience 2020; Power
Outage Incident Annex to the Response and Recovery Federal Interagency Operational Plans
2017; Clark et al. 2023). The scoring matrix was informed by community engagement, detailed
in Section 5.

The assignment of critical service scores depends on information about service location types
included in public datasets. For example, hospital location data from the U.S. Department of
Homeland Security include information about the level of trauma care available at each location.
Hospitals providing “Level 1” trauma care are assigned a higher score in the “Health –
Healthcare Procedures” category than other types of hospitals. Grocery store location data from
the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) include information about the size of each store.
“Small” grocery stores are assigned a lower score in the “Provisions – Food & Water” category,
whereas “Large” grocery stores are assigned a higher score in this category. “Small” and “large”
are designated in the USDA grocery store data—but not defined—and these subcategories were
interpreted as relating to the capacity of each store. Residential locations provide the following
services: shelter, store food, cook food, store medication, and healthcare device. The analysis
considers only permanent service locations included in publicly available databases; services
provided at temporary or mobile locations are not considered. However, future work can
leverage this model to analyze the optimal spatio-temporal distribution of ad-hoc emergency
services. See Appendix A.2 for detailed service scores assigned to locations.

In addition to accessing services outside the home, each household is assumed to access a subset
of critical services inside their home: heating and cooling, hygiene, communication, medication
and food storage, food preparation, and the use of electrical medical devices. These in-home

travel time and 2) services provided inside residential locations have high value for residents but

value for each individual resident—especially those that live relatively far from nonresidential
locations—but higher value for the community in aggregate. This difference in value is because
the value of a single nonresidential service location is calculated by summing the access-
weighted service scores it provides to each household within a 60-minute drive radius.

Joining residential and nonresidential service locations to utility feeder data reveals the spatial
distribution of critical service access across electrical infrastructure. Electric utility planners can
often access information about the location of distribution infrastructure components, their age,
voltage ratings, and the number of customers served by each, but utility information systems do
not often integrate information about the detailed types of customers and services associated with
system components or the proximity of those customers and services to one another.
Summarizing the spatial relationship between the distribution system and the broader social,
economic, and transportation systems it serves can add critical context to utility decision making.
Indeed, utilities can use this additional information to evaluate the performance of their system
and prioritize actions 1) more collaboratively with external stakeholders, who might be more
familiar with social, economic, and transportation systems than with electrical systems, and 2)
with more nuance than current binary definitions of “critical” and “not critical” allow.

Service locations are joined to their nearest feeder line, and the critical service access provided
by each location is aggregated by feeder, then divided by the total critical service access
available in an area. This spatial joining and aggregation provides a relative measurement of
each feeder’s contribution to an area’s total critical service access. SCL operates 162 overhead

distribution feeders. Households within the Seattle municipal boundary have a total critical
service access of 28,497,470, which includes access provided by nonresidential service locations
within a 60-minute drive radius outside the Seattle municipal boundary. This value reflects the
total services, discounted by distance, available to households in Seattle. The value would
increase if the number of service locations increased, the score assigned to service locations for
the services they provide increased, or service locations were moved closer to areas with dense
housing.

Because households can access a wide range of services across large areas, including areas
outside the SCL utility territory, critical service access is highly distributed across grid
infrastructure. If the critical service access provided by locations on SCL feeders were divided
evenly across all feeders, each would provide 0.6% of the city’s access. However, access is
clustered according to the location of residential and nonresidential services, the length of
feeders, and the proximity of the transportation infrastructure that allows residents to access
nonresidential services. Across Seattle, most feeders provide less than 1% of the city’s total
critical service access (Figure 5). The feeder with the highest contribution to citywide critical
service access (2.5%) is downtown. Feeders with the greatest contributions to critical service
access during normal conditions are those that power many nonresidential service locations in
areas that are easily accessed by many households (see Sections 3 and 4 for analysis of critical
service access during outage conditions.) Taken together, the top 10 feeders shown in Figure 6
power 361 nonresidential service locations and more than 11,000 residential locations and
provide 14.7% of Seattle’s critical service access. These results are reported for total critical
service access—access in individual service categories, such as Health or Provisions, has
different spatial distributions.

Note nonresidential service locations in areas with networked feeders (downtown and the
university district) are considered outside the SCL distribution system in this analysis because
the distribution system data used for the project include only radial feeders, not smaller
networked distribution systems operated by SCL. This omission leads to locations in the
downtown and university district areas not being associated with any feeder because they are too
distant from radial feeders in the project database to be spatially joined. A future analysis that
includes the undergrounded, networked distribution systems will require a more complex
examination of power flow between networked and radial systems operated by Seattle City Light
during outages, and this analysis would provide a more accurate estimate of the relative
contribution of all overhead feeders, especially those close to downtown.

The spatial relationship between residential locations, nonresidential locations, population, and
feeders is a core driver of the relative critical service access provided by each feeder. The critical
service access provided by a feeder is controlled by the number, density, and value of both
residential and nonresidential locations powered by that feeder. Nonresidential locations near
densely populated residential areas and transportation infrastructure that allows people to access
them relatively quickly will contribute highly to critical service access on a feeder. The value of
a nonresidential location for each household decreases exponentially with increasing travel time.
While distant nonresidential locations contribute minimally to Seattle’s access to critical
services, their non-zero contributions reflect the real opportunity for households to travel to these
locations to access services, and the importance of infrastructure outside the utility’s control for
community resilience.

Analyzing critical service access on sections of each feeder provides a more granular
prioritization of locations where resilience investments could provide the highest benefits for
Duwamish Valley residents. Feeder sections are portions of connected distribution lines within
each feeder that are separated by protective devices such as fuses, reclosers, or switches and
therefore operate as single components during outages and restorations. Locations on the same
feeder section lose and regain power together during outages. Although the average length of
feeders serving the Duwamish Valley study area is 20 miles, the average length of feeder
sections serving the area is 0.3 miles. Analysis of feeder sections can support decisions targeting
resilience investments with greater precision. See Section 3 for details about the methods used to
divide feeders into sections. Figure 8 shows feeder sections in the Duwamish Valley that provide
more than 1% of the study area’s critical service access as well as the residential and
nonresidential service locations powered by those feeder sections.

certain locations as “critical,” toward a continuous measurement of how critical each distribution
system component is for different customers. The metric also demonstrates the resilience
benefits grid investments can have for customers beyond those powered by a specific feeder or
section. However, a more strategic resilience investment strategy must consider the spatial and
temporal distribution of long duration outages to identify locations where high-value resilience
services coincide with high outage vulnerability, because in these areas, investments can
minimize the potential consequences experienced by households during long outages.

3 Critical Service Access Interruptions
Critical service access changes during outages based on which sections of a distribution system
are experiencing outages at a given time. Households with relatively high access to critical
services during normal conditions can experience significant decreases in access to critical
services for relatively long periods if their critical service network intersects with distribution
infrastructure that is particularly susceptible to long duration outages. In this section, the extent
to which critical service access has been, or could be, interrupted by long duration power outages
is analyzed in two neighborhoods in the Duwamish Valley: Georgetown and South Park. This
analysis characterizes the potential consequences of long duration outages at the household level
to understand the potential benefits of grid investments that avoid those consequences. First,
outages are downscaled from feeders to smaller feeder sections to more precisely measure the
spatiotemporal pattern of hourly outages and critical service access. Next, a long duration outage
impacting the study area is simulated to measure the potential consequences of a prolonged lack
of access to critical services. Finally, historic outage data and critical service thresholds are
applied to identify past outages that could have been especially consequential for the study area
because of the decrease in access to critical services across each service category.

3.1 Downscaling Outages
SCL provided outages recorded at the feeder level from 2019 to 2023. A feeder-level outage
analysis could obscure sections within a feeder that are more likely to experience longer-than-
average outage durations. A fault on a portion of the feeder may not cause every customer on the
feeder to lose power because of sectionalizing protection equipment, and the fault may cause
some customers to lose power for longer because of restorations impeded by geographic
considerations. To increase the spatial granularity of outage duration estimates and the
estimation of critical service access interruptions and resilience benefits, a downscaling analysis
is conducted for outages on the top 10 feeders identified as contributing the highest critical
service access for households in Georgetown and South Park (Figure 9).

The downscaling analysis estimates the annual duration of outages each feeder section is likely
to experience based on the historically recorded duration and frequency of outages on each
feeder, modified using assumptions about the vulnerability of each section based on distribution
system attributes. This approach is useful when distribution system topology data are available;
and outage data are aggregated by feeder rather than by customer meter. The analysis uses a
distribution grid model from Seattle City Light that includes the failure rates and repair times of
connected devices, the location of switches and ties to adjacent feeders, and feeder-level outage
durations for 2019–2023. First, feeder-level outage durations and customer counts are used to
generate a SAIDI value for each of the top 10 feeders (Figure 10). Two feeders in the southwest
of the study area have the longest average historical outage durations at 3.9 and 3.3 hours per
year.

Figure 10. System Average Interruption Duration Index for the top 10 feeders for Georgetown and

Feeder-level SAIDI is used as an estimate of feeder reliability against which the failure rates and
restoration times of components are calibrated. This calibration considers the nature of the
component (e.g., overhead versus underground), topology (e.g., failure in one section would
interrupt power in a downstream section if no protection and redundancy were available), and
protection scheme (e.g., automated switches to connect to energized portions of the same or
alternative feeder) of the grid. In this way, even though the feeder has one SAIDI value, different
sections are assigned longer or shorter expected outage durations. This facilitates analysis of
resilience upgrades at sub-feeder scale, which helps identify where upgrades could support the
highest critical service access for lowest cost. Figure 11 demonstrates this process for a single
feeder. Section-level SAIDI estimates are calibrated to ensure they aggregate to the same feeder-
level SAIDI estimates derived from historic outage data. Results predict the section-level
distribution of expected annual outage durations to increase the granularity of outage
consequence measurements and identify where more granular resilience investments might
provide relatively higher benefits.

3.2 Critical Service Access During a Major Outage
Section-level outage durations disaggregated from feeder outage data are used to simulate the
potential consequences of a long duration outage in Georgetown and South Park. This analysis
helps us understand how critical service access might be interrupted for certain households and
for the study area during major power disruptions. Avoiding critical service access interruptions
can be measured as a meaningful resilience benefit of grid investments, and this benefit can be
compared across investments and households to support informed prioritization at SCL.

In the absence of customer-level outage data for a major outage in the SCL records, a
hypothetical long duration outage is simulated using the downscaled outage results described in
Section 3.1. This outage does not reflect a real event or consider the statistical likelihood of
coincident outages on feeder sections serving the study area. Rather, the simulated outage
assumes a widespread outage for the neighborhood and estimates a restoration timeline for each
feeder section based on that section’s relative vulnerability to outages, measured by its expected
outage hours presented in Section 3.1. Results cannot be interpreted as actual or likely outage
consequences but rather as an example of how critical service access can be combined with
outage data and grid attributes to examine the potential spatiotemporal consequences of a long
duration outage in a specific neighborhood.

 26

In the simulated long duration outage, the top 10 feeders providing critical service access for
these two neighborhoods are assumed to lose power at h = 0 (12:00 a.m.). Feeder sections are
then “restored” at the hour that corresponds to their predicted annual outage duration value until
the final feeder section is “restored” at h = 14 (4:00 p.m.). The simulated outage is 14 hours long
at worst-performing locations because, according to the downscaled outage analysis, the feeder
section with the highest expected annual outage hours has 14 hours of annual expected outage
time. These expected outage hours are unlikely to occur consecutively, instead occurring
throughout the year in several shorter outages. However, the simulated long duration outage is
designed to explore the potential consequences of low-probability, high-impact events. Critical
service access is computed as a fraction of baseline for each household in each hour of the
simulated outage, according to the sections (and locations) that are not yet restored.

Figure 14 shows the decrease in critical service access, from a baseline of 100%, experienced by
the study area during the simulated outage. In the first 2 hours of the outage, access to critical
services in the Provisions and Workforce categories drops to approximately 60% of baseline and
is restored relatively quickly, whereas access to critical services in the Health and Shelter
categories drops to approximately 35% of baseline and is restored slightly slower. This larger
decline is caused by widespread loss of power at residential locations, which contribute highly to
services in the Health and Shelter categories, so access in these categories will remain low until
power at a plurality of households is restored. Access to critical services in the Public Emergency
category is restored soonest. Across all categories, 30%–60% of neighborhood access to critical
services is maintained during the outage because locations outside the top feeders are assumed to
remain powered, which demonstrates some customers can avoid outage consequences by
traveling outside the study area for critical services if they have the means to do so.

prescription medication at pharmacies, and electrical medical devices and refrigerated
medication at homes. Critical health services typically accessed at home—powering devices and

procedures and prescription medication decreases to below 60% and 70% of baseline,

without power measures the length of time they lose access to critical services inside their home,

Households that experienced high access-hours interrupted did not necessarily experience the
longest outage duration (Figure 16). This observation underscores the additional information
provided by the critical service access metric. A long duration outage at an individual household
will reduce that household’s ability to access electricity-dependent services typically accessed at
home: cooking and storing food, storing medication, using electrical medical devices, thermal
comfort, and communicating with people outside the home. However, the status of power at
nonresidential service locations around that household will either exacerbate or mitigate that
household’s loss of critical service access. A household that loses power but is relatively close to
powered nonresidential critical services such as grocery stores or pharmacies will experience
relatively lower access-hours interrupted. A household that maintains power or is restored
quickly but lives farther from powered critical service locations still experiences nonzero access-
hours interrupted because its ability to meet its needs outside the home is reduced.

3.3 Identifying Major Critical Service Access Interruptions
Although the simulated outage helps identify the potential consequences of a long duration
outage in Georgetown and South Park, determining whether those consequences justify the
energy investments required to avoid them is challenging. Investments in grid resilience are
costly relative to how frequently they are necessary to avoid the worst outcomes of long duration
outages. To keep energy affordable and justify larger investments targeted to mitigate high-

 30

impact, low-probability outages, utilities and other stakeholders seek to deploy resilience
solutions where they will reduce the worst consequences of power outages. Because critical
service access establishes a numeric relationship between discrete locations and unique
categories of electricity-dependent critical services, outages can be classified as “major” critical
service access interruptions according to the duration and magnitude of access loss in each
category of critical service. This process—customizable by utilities and their partners—can help
decision makers plan energy and resilience improvements to improve the concrete outcomes that
electricity supports rather than to improve the abstract availability of electricity.

Based on a wide set of sources, each critical service subcategory is assigned a duration threshold,
beyond which a lack of access could cause potentially harmful consequences (Figure 18). For
example, a disruption to food storage lasting longer than 4 hours can cause food spoilage and a
potentially high associated financial loss for households (United States Department of
Agriculture 2013). A disruption to medical procedures or devices lasting longer than 2 hours
could result in increased mortality (Bawaneh et al. 2019). Gaps exist in research or guidance
about the relationship between critical services, disruption durations, and consequences. Where
guidance about disruptions to specific service categories was unavailable, a default duration
threshold of 12 hours is assigned, which is the duration exceeded by 50% of major power outage
events recorded by the U.S. Energy Information Administration (Ericson et al. 2022). See
Appendix A.3 for more details about the justification behind these thresholds.

Light’s full 6-year outage database and identify major critical service interruptions at a higher
resolution. In this analysis, locations inherit the outage status of their assigned feeder. This

 32

assumption significantly overestimates outages and consequences because all locations on a
feeder are assumed to be without power if that feeder is listed as being de-energized at a given
time in Seattle City Light’s feeder-level outage records. Furthermore, the thresholds established
for this analysis should be refined by local decision makers to ensure measurements are aimed at
reasonable and widely agreed-upon resilience goals.

This approach provides a benchmark performance metric with which resilience investments can
be strategically planned. Investments that reduce both the number of critical locations that lose
power and the duration of major disruptions at those locations are likely to create meaningful
resilience benefits for communities. This metric prioritizes locations because of the services they
provide and the households they are near, and benefits can be connected to specific critical
services that households need. In this way, critical service access helps utilities understand where
and how long duration power outages create real consequences for customers, set strategic
targets based on that understanding, and measure the potential benefits of energy resilience
upgrades for more informed decisions.

4 Measuring the Resilience Benefits of Investments
The top 10 feeders contributing to critical service access in Georgetown and South Park are
analyzed to identify where targeted energy resilience investments could offer maximum benefits
for neighborhood residents. This analysis considers two types of resilience investments:
distribution automation switches and ties to adjacent feeders. These investments are often cost
effective because they benefit many customers; entire feeder sections can benefit from
accelerated restorations and experience reduced outages. However, automated switches that shift
electrical load from one feeder to another rely on some feeders remaining energized during
outage events. When a long duration outage affects many feeders, or where adjacent feeders
frequently experience simultaneous outages, these investments do not necessarily improve
resilience. In these cases, strategically sited backup generation may be more beneficial relative to
its cost because it can provide reliable electricity independent of distribution system conditions.
The benefits of potential investments are evaluated according to two metrics: a reduction in
customer hours interrupted, a metric commonly used by utilities to assess system performance,
and a reduction in critical service access-hours interrupted, the metric introduced in this study.
Results identify feeder sections (and associated locations and customers) that offer maximum
benefits if protected from long duration outages.

Investment solutions are evaluated by iterating through candidate locations on each feeder and
assigning a hypothetical switch or tie, depending on the characteristics of each location.
Candidate tie locations are limited to existing open three-phase switches, which ensures they
could function as ties to adjacent feeders. Each potential switch-and-tie combination reconfigures
sections in each feeder, creating shorter sections with fewer customers. The benefits created by
each solution are estimated by first recomputing the expected annual outage hours for each
section—assuming the new configuration—using component types, feeder topology, and
protection schemes, consistent with the methodology described in Section 3.1. Then, for each
section, the new annual outage hours estimate is multiplied by the number of customers on the
section to derive the reduction in customer hours interrupted and the critical service access
provided by the section to derive the reduction in critical service access-hours interrupted.

Although this analysis focuses on the resilience value of distribution automation equipment
(switches and ties), it serves as a proof-of-concept for evaluating the resilience benefits of other
distribution system upgrades. For example, microgrids provide energy services when the
distribution system is operational and can protect critical services during long and widespread
outages. Future work can leverage the model and method outlined here to optimally site and size
a portfolio of microgrids that strategically protects critical service access for Seattle City Light
customers.

The benefits created by each solution are aggregated by feeder. The benefits provided by
different solutions are compared, and the tie-and-switch combination that maximizes benefits on
each feeder is selected as the priority investment. Figure 20 shows the location of proposed
distribution automation switches and feeder ties identified as minimizing critical service access-
hours interrupted (a) or minimizing customer hours interrupted (b). The location of two switches
and one tie changes depending on which benefit metric is used (Figure 20b). In the access-hours
interruption case, the highest benefits appear to be concentrated along fewer sections, whereas in

 34

the customer hours interrupted case, benefits appear to be spread across a greater number of
sections.

maximally beneficial investments. Investments selected by minimizing customer hours
interrupted and investments selected by minimizing access-hours interrupted lead to equal
reductions in access-hours interrupted. The neighborhood-scale benefits created by these two sets
of prioritized investments are identical in part because the analysis was designed to select an
investment on every feeder. In this sense, each scenario sought to deploy the best resilience
solution on each feeder regardless of cost constraints. A more realistic assessment would either
set minimum benefit targets for investments or maximum cost thresholds. Without constraining
the analysis with a specific cost or benefit target, investments minimize expected annual outage
hours on each feeder. Future research can incorporate cost constraints and targets for critical
service access and customer minutes interrupted to better understand the relationship between
these two metrics.

These results indicate switches and ties can be installed at whichever set of locations is most
cost-effective and the neighborhood-scale benefits created by these investments will remain the
same. However, the benefits resulting from these investments will vary at each household
according to the metric used and each household’s specific needs. In its current form, the critical
service access metric treats each household’s need for critical services as equal. This assumption
is not realistic. For example, households with aging residents may be associated with critical
service needs that differ from other households. Future research can map different household
needs and modify the household-level critical service access metric using those different needs.

The benefits visualized in Figure 20 consider each feeder’s annual expected outage hours
independently. Major power outages cause multiple feeder sections or whole feeders to lose
power simultaneously, and the spatiotemporal pattern of long duration outages affects the
potential consequences experienced by the neighborhood. A joint probability analysis shows two
of the ties selected by this analysis (one selected in both cases and one selected in the customer
hours interrupted case) connect feeders that experience more than 30% of the same major outage
hours from 2019 to 2025 (Figure 20, “high outage risk” ties). Because these feeders are likely to
experience major outages at the same time, ties allowing these feeders to share power are less
likely to increase neighborhood resilience. These outages could have resulted from common-
cause events like physical damage to a substation. If these events cannot be mitigated upstream
at the station, the feeder sections associated with these ties may be good candidates for
distributed generation microgrids or undergrounding, which—although more costly—are more
likely to achieve the benefits available at these investment locations.

To measure how these investments change critical service access during a long, widespread
outage in Georgetown and South Park, the simulated outage described in Section 3 was
recomputed using decreased expected outage hours for each section where distribution
automation switches and ties shown in Figure 20 are hypothetically installed. Sections with
reduced expected outage hours are restored sooner in the simulated outage, proportional to their
decrease in outage hours. Although this outage is unlikely to occur as simulated, results
demonstrate how investments can hypothetically reduce critical service access interruptions by
reducing the number and duration of simultaneous outages across the neighborhood.

Figure 21 demonstrates the reduction in potential outage consequences associated with different
resilience investments. This outage causes a critical service access interruption of more than
984,000 access-hours across the study area. Implementing the distribution automation switches
and ties identified in Figure 20 on all 10 feeders could reduce this interruption by 23%, to
760,000 access-hours. More than 80% of this reduction can be achieved by implementing
investments on only the top 5 feeders. These results suggest the extent to which critical service
access prioritization may yield “low-hanging fruit” for utilities: high value investments that can
lead to significant, measurable critical service access benefits at a reasonable cost.

5 Community Engagement
The project team engaged directly with Duwamish Valley residents via a survey and focus
groups to gather information about their needs, preferences, and sensitivities in the event of a
power outage. This information was gathered for comparison with the critical service access
metric discussed previously and for integration into utility planning processes at Seattle City
Light. This section presents results from a digital community survey, 2024 Duwamish Valley
Critical Community Needs Research, administered from June to October 2024 (n = 117
households), and three focus groups conducted with a subset of survey respondents (n = 12).
Survey respondents include 117 residents living within 1 mile of the Duwamish Valley study
area boundary, based on the assumption that critical infrastructure investments in one location
are likely to benefit the surrounding area (Figure 23). Survey results and focus group discussions
highlight community concerns about power disruptions as well as the specific locations and the
types of services residents consider most important. This information can inform utility priorities
for energy resilience investments, including diesel generators, solar and battery storage,
distribution automation and redundancy, restoration strategies, and coordination with emergency
management agencies.

Households in the SCORE survey sample are larger and more educated, earn lower incomes, and
are more likely to have children at home than the average household in study area census tracts.

Respondents were asked to describe their concerns about power outages, identify the locations
(e.g., grocery stores, pharmacies) they use during nonemergency circumstances, and identify the
locations and services they would hypothetically use during a prolonged power outage.
Participants who took the SCORE survey and indicated they were interested in future

 40

engagement were invited to attend a focus group to discuss their power outage priorities. Unlike
the survey, which captured individual behaviors and preferences, the focus groups emphasized
collective prioritization and reasoning. Focus group participants were asked to collaboratively
identify which locations would be most beneficial for the community, first by choosing one top-
priority location in each of five categories (grocery store, gas station, convenience store, school,
place of worship), then by choosing two high-priority locations across all categories. Participants
were asked to discuss the logic behind their choices, and these discussions offered insight into
community-level values, deliberation processes, and the social dynamics of risk and risk
mitigation.

A key difference between past survey efforts and the Duwamish Valley survey was the use of a
hypothetical power outage scenario rather than real past events. A hypothetical scenario was
necessary because Seattle had not experienced a major outage in recent years that could inform
all participant responses. The scenario was designed with the following assumptions to simplify
analysis and meet project objectives:

 • The electrical grid is the only infrastructure system that is not working (i.e., loss of water
 and disruptions to transportation were expressly excluded from the hypothetical
 scenario).
 • A large proportion of the population would remain in the area and seek services locally
 rather than evacuate.
 • Locations that residents use in typical situations (“blue-sky” circumstances) to meet their
 basic needs are also likely to be the locations preferred by residents to meet needs during
 a power outage (“black-sky” circumstances).
 • Preferable locations and basic household needs do not depend on season.

questions, and focus group design are available by contacting the research team.

5.1 Concerns During Power Outages
Although most respondents (82%) reported prior experience with power outages lasting longer
than an hour, fewer (18%) reported experience with power outages lasting longer than a day.

Despite this lack of experience with long duration power outages, a reduced likelihood of power
outages was frequently ranked as the first or second most important potential benefit of local
solar projects. This preference was exceeded only by affordability, which almost half of question
respondents ranked as the single most important potential benefit of potential solar projects. The
priority placed on affordability was consistent with the respondents’ concerns about ongoing
electrical costs—nearly 40% of respondents indicated their typical electricity bill is not
affordable.

Respondents indicated they were concerned about outages placing further financial strain on
their households: The majority of respondents (51%) expressed anxiety about their ability to
afford their basic needs during a long power outage. Specific financial concerns cited included
the inability to work (n = 4) and having to replace spoiled food (n = 13), underscoring the ways
in which outages can cause economic strain by both reducing income and increasing
expenditures.

5.2 Critical Service Locations
To identify specific opportunities for resilience investment, respondents were asked to identify
specific locations near their neighborhood where they access services to meet their needs during
normal conditions and indicate the frequency with which they visit those locations. Respondents
selected as many locations as they wanted from a list of five locations in each of several
categories, populated using location data from Seattle. Respondents were also given the option to
write in the names of locations not shown in the list of five prepopulated locations. Respondents
were asked only about locations in categories they indicated visiting.

Locations were categorized into two groups based on responses. High-frequency locations are
those selected by more than 20% of respondents (a natural break observed in the data) as places
they visit daily or weekly (e.g., grocery stores, convenience stores, gas stations, schools, and
places of worship). Low-frequency, high-impact locations are those selected by less than 20% of
respondents but are nevertheless critical because of the potentially serious consequences for the
health and well-being of vulnerable households if unavailable during a long power outage (e.g.,

 43

 food banks, pharmacies, dialysis centers, and doctor’s offices). Locations with low response rates
 and no assumed critical impact were excluded from further analysis (e.g., hardware stores,
 banks).

 survey respondents because respondents could select multiple grocery stores) with Costco (n =

5.3 Vulnerability to Power Outages
Social vulnerability refers to the susceptibility of individuals or groups to the adverse impacts of
stressors and shocks, including disasters and power outages that often accompany them, because
of factors such as socioeconomic status and limited access to resources. Social vulnerability is
typically measured at aggregated scales such as census tracts, where data related to social
vulnerability factors are routinely collected by the census and combined into statistical indicators
such as the popular Social Vulnerability Index (Flanagan et al. 2011). Assessing vulnerability
using aggregated demographics does not consider the specific mechanisms through which
households may experience relatively more severe consequences from a long duration power
disruption.

For example, census data report 12% of individuals living in the survey area as having a
disability. However, the U.S Census Bureau American Community Survey defines disability as a
self-reported difficulty with any of six basic areas of functioning: hearing, vision, cognition,
ambulation, self-care, and independent living (United States Census Bureau n.d.). In both the
SCORE and SASPER surveys, “disability” is made more specific by asking participants whether
they or anyone in their household has a condition that could be worsened by a power outage.
Conditions identified by respondents as potentially worsened by a power outage include sleep
apnea and respiratory illnesses that require electrical medical devices; diabetes, which requires
refrigerated insulin; and a more general reliance on access to medications. Approximately 25%
of SCORE respondents and 46% of SASPER respondents reported having a condition that could
be worsened by a power outage. These findings indicate 2–3 times more people could be
relatively more vulnerable to power outages than census disability data suggest. Approximately
15% of respondents report depending on an electrical medical device, and 59% report depending
on prescription medication. Eliciting categories like these helps to specify household needs that
directly contribute to household vulnerability to critical infrastructure service disruptions.

Although the survey included many questions about sociodemographic indicators used in
vulnerability indices, the survey specifically focused on how power outages might impact the
ability of individual households to meet their basic needs. Therefore, vulnerability is defined
based on the behaviors, rather than the characteristics, of households. According to this
classification, vulnerable households are those that make daily or weekly visits to pharmacies,
food banks, doctor’s offices, or dialysis centers. These respondents are classified as a high-
impact service-dependent population (HISDP) based on their more frequent visits (relative to
other survey respondents) to locations that, if unavailable, could cause significant adverse
consequences for those dependent on them (Figure 29). Approximately 30% of respondents are
classified as HISDP.

these investments are designed to avoid (medicine, healthcare, or affordable food being
unavailable for households who need those services more frequently).

6 Conclusions
Grid reliability metrics remain the standard for evaluating utility performance because they are
well-established and embedded in regulatory frameworks. However, although metrics such as
SAIDI and SAIFI track average outage duration or frequency at the system or feeder level, they
are not designed to measure resilience specifically. Customer-centric metrics such as CELID and
CEMI provide additional insight by highlighting customers who experience especially long
duration or frequent outages, but they still focus only on the extent of electricity loss. These
metrics do not capture the broader consequences of power outages, nor do they identify which
critical services are disrupted, where those disruptions occur, or how long communities are left
without access to essential services. More specific information about the relationship between
the distribution system, outages, and critical services is required for utility planners and grid
operators to make strategic decisions that increase resilience for customers.

Critical service access is a consequence-focused metric that addresses these gaps by evaluating
the performance of a community’s critical service network across both space and time. This
project developed a model that calculates critical service access for any electric distribution
system using distribution component locations, historic outage records, and publicly available
national datasets. This capability allows utility planners to map the contribution of distribution
infrastructure to critical service access at different geographic scales across their territory;
measure the potential consequences of long duration outages in a given area for specific
households; identify major events in their historic outage data, based on the interruption to
critical service access those events caused; and measure the potential reduction in outage
consequences achieved through resilience investments, such as distribution automation, feeder
ties, backup generation, undergrounding, overhead line hardening, or distributed generation
microgrids. Although measuring the benefits of distribution automation and feeder ties involves
considerable uncertainty about the granular spatial distribution of outage durations before and
after investments, measuring the benefits of backup generation or distributed generation
microgrids is more certain. These solutions are designed to support the exact critical services and
outage duration deemed most important to mitigate.

In Seattle, this metric was applied at both neighborhood and citywide scales. Results represent a
static measurement of critical services in Seattle (i.e., at the time the study was conducted),
because service locations change as neighborhoods develop and utility conditions evolve.
Community feedback and simulated outage consequences highlight the challenge of increasing
resilience for critical services typically provided at home—including food storage, thermal
comfort, and communication—which have widespread impacts when disrupted. Survey
responses also suggested uncertainty among participants about alternative locations where these
services could be reliably accessed during a widespread power outage affecting residential
locations. These findings contributed to the decision to incorporate residential services into the
service scoring matrix discussed in Section 2 for a more comprehensive and spatially granular
representation of the accessibility of specific services across the study area. The resulting critical
service access metric allows utility planners to more systematically measure the potential
consequences of long duration outages for specific areas of their service territory and evaluate
trade-offs between resilience investments at locations with different densities of residential and
commercial customers.

 57

Critical service access incorporates assets beyond the utility’s service territory, reflecting the
reality that resilience depends on factors outside the utility’s control, because households rarely,
if ever, consider distribution system boundaries when making decisions about where to access
critical goods and services. The complexity of the critical service landscape underscores the
importance of coordinating resilience planning efforts across geographic boundaries and
governmental jurisdictions. Using critical service access, a utility’s distribution system decisions
can be communicated to residents and external organizations as avoided disruptions to critical
services they recognize and depend on. This approach provides an opportunity to streamline
collaboration between utility decision makers and external stakeholders for more productive and
holistic resilience strategies.

7 Future Work
Additional applied research is necessary both to ensure service scores and categorizations reflect
community needs and to align the scale of metric aggregation (i.e., single neighborhood) with the
scale of decision making at various organizations responsible for managing emergencies and
building resilience. Results indicate the feeder contributing the highest relative amount of critical
service access for Seattle residents is a downtown feeder powering densely populated residential
structures, a pharmacy, and dozens of EV charging stations. The high number of EV charging
stations proximate to a densely populated area creates a high aggregated critical service score for
this feeder in the “Fuel” category. Although the services on this feeder might be useful for many
residents during emergency conditions, the utility of these services is limited by vehicle
electrification rates across the city and the capacity of the single pharmacy on this feeder to serve
such a dense area. Ranking feeders using static, baseline critical service access does not support
a robust examination of investment trade-offs by utilities.

Future work should explore dynamic interdependencies between locations on different feeders,
their capacity, and their relative value for surrounding residents. During major disruptions,
service locations that retain power or are restored quickly will experience increased visits from
households that typically access services at locations currently without power. A significant
limitation of the current critical service access model is the lack of data or assumptions about the
capacity of each service location to support surrounding households during major disruptions
based on the hourly availability of services in an area. If the model is adjusted to dynamically
consider the increase in reliance on a relatively smaller number of service locations, decision
makers can measure the risk of communities not having access to sufficient local resources and
find opportunities to protect this access with investments and emergency coordination. The
method outlined in Section 3 to classify outages as major interruptions to critical service access
using category-specific duration thresholds is a first step toward this more dynamic model.

The model used in this study does not consider potential disruptions to traffic patterns, which
could significantly change the critical service access of specific locations or for specific
households when key transportation infrastructure is damaged or congested. Future work can
leverage more traffic data beyond average travel speeds and experiment with the hypothetical
closure of certain routes to help decision makers understand the sensitivities between critical
services, electrical infrastructure, and transportation infrastructure. The model does not consider
services provided at temporary or mobile locations, although these services are commonly

 58

provided during major emergencies. Future work can leverage this study’s model to analyze the
optimal spatio-temporal distribution of ad-hoc emergency services.

Future work should explore the relationship between critical service access and the costs of
energy resilience investments to understand the circumstances in which distributed generation
microgrids are the best option to achieve resilience goals. Microgrids provide energy services
when the distribution system is operational and can protect critical services during long and
widespread outages. Although this analysis focuses on the resilience value of distribution
automation equipment, it serves as a proof-of-concept for evaluating the resilience benefits of
other distribution system upgrades. A future analysis can leverage the model and method
outlined here to optimally site and size a portfolio of microgrids that strategically protects critical
service access for Seattle City Light (or other utility) customers. However, more work is needed
to effectively combine the monetary value provided by distributed generation during normal
conditions with the non-monetary value provided during major disruptions for a comprehensive
benefit-cost analysis.

Results indicate switches and ties can be installed at whichever set of locations is most cost-
effective and the neighborhood-scale benefits created by these investments will remain the same.
This result might change if the analysis is constrained by cost or benefit parameters, and future
work should perform these sensitivity tests. For example, a cost can be calculated for each
potential upgrade and the analysis can identify the combination of upgrades that maximizes
critical service access for a target cost. Similarly, the analysis can identify a combination of
upgrades that achieves a target critical service access number (during a single outage or using
annual historic outage data) with minimal cost. Microgrids powered by distributed generation
introduce additional complexity, since the monetary costs and benefits of these microgrids
depend on the size and temporality of the electrical loads they serve. Future work should
integrate location-specific electrical load data with location-specific critical service access to
more effectively compare the benefits of different resilience upgrades with their costs. These
additional analyses will determine how the model and method outlined in this report can be used
to strategically and meaningfully invest in distribution system resilience.

The benefits resulting from the investments analyzed in this study will vary in each household
according to the metric used and each household’s specific needs. In its current form, the critical
service access metric treats each household’s need for critical services as equal, when some
households have greater need than others for certain services. Future research can work to map
different household needs and modify the household-level critical service access metric using
those different needs. Future research can also incorporate cost constraints and targets for critical
service access and customer minutes interrupted to test whether investment prioritization is
sensitive to the use of either benefit metric. Finally, physical and operational constraints must be
considered by SCL engineers to verify the proposed tie switch locations are feasible in practice.

The approach used to identify major interruptions to critical service access provides a benchmark
performance metric with which resilience investments can be strategically planned. The
frequency and spatial distribution of major critical service interruptions defined in Section 4
should be compared with major event day data collected by the U.S. Energy Information
Administration and utility partners to determine how this approach could be used as a standard
utility performance measurement. Customer-level outage information is critical for accurate

 59

calculation of major critical service interruptions, and future work can help utilities improve their
outage data management systems to facilitate this kind of performance metric calculation.

Investments that reduce both the number of important locations that lose power and the duration
those locations lose power during major disruptions will create meaningful resilience benefits for
communities. However, the relationship between outage duration and resilience benefits is likely
exponential, not linear; losing access to communication services likely becomes increasingly
consequential as a major outage persists. Furthermore, using historical outages to calibrate
outage likelihood underestimates the risk of the types of events that have occurred or could occur
beyond the period of study. Future work should explore the exponential weighting of service
access loss over the duration of outage scenarios and compare results with feedback from
residents about their experiences during long outages, while also exploring probabilistic
approaches to rare events.

8 Glossary
Social Burden

The burden to a population for obtaining services needed from infrastructure, which increases
during disruptions. Increased burden can be characterized as an increase in the time or cost
associated with obtaining services or an increase in the adverse consequences associated with an
inability to obtain services.

Critical Service Access

The travel-time-weighted value of available critical services aggregated for an individual
household, a nonresidential location, or a distribution feeder, neighborhood, city, or other study
area.

System Average Interruption Duration Index

The minutes of nonmomentary (>5 minutes) electric interruptions the average customer
experiences, typically measured annually.

System Average Interruption Frequency Index

The number of nonmomentary (>5 minutes) electric interruptions the average customer
experiences, typically measured annually.

Customers Experiencing Multiple Interruptions

The number of customers who experience multiple nonmomentary (>5 minutes) electric
interruptions, typically measured annually with a threshold given in number of interruptions. For
example, CEMI-12 reports the total number of customers who have experienced 12 or more
outages each year.

Customers Experiencing Long Interruption Durations

 60

The number of customers who experience electric interruptions that last longer than a given
threshold, typically measured annually with a threshold given in hours. For example, CELID-8
reports the total number of customers who have experienced a cumulative total of more than 8
hours of outages each year.

Feeder Sections

Portions of connected distribution line within each feeder that are separated by protective devices
such as fuses, reclosers, or switches that can operate together during outages to isolate customers
on a given section.

Access-Hours Interrupted

An interrupted access-hour is a cumulative loss in access to critical services equivalent to being
completely without access to critical services for a period of 1 hour. Access-hours combine the
duration of an interruption to critical services with the magnitude of the interruption, measured
as a percent decrease from a baseline critical service access of 100%. A 20% decrease in access
to critical services for 4 hours is equivalent to a 10% decrease for 8 hours. Access-hours
interrupted can be measured for individual critical service categories or for all critical service
categories in aggregate.

Customer Hours Interrupted

The outage duration associated with a distribution system component multiplied by the number
of customers associated with that component. Customer hours interrupted can be calculated for a
single distribution system node or for an entire utility territory.

A.4 Duration Thresholds for Major Critical Service Interruptions
Thresholds were assigned based on when people would likely experience an adverse
consequence if a service category was not available somewhere nearby at a given hour during an
ongoing power disruption.

 (Hours)

 1. Cook Food, Obtain Food/Water, Store Medications, Heating/Cooling, Hygiene
 • Duration threshold: 48 hours
 • Sources: Guzman (2024), The Seattle Times (2024), Red Cross (n.d.)
 • Justification: These services are typically accessed in private, residential locations. If
 these locations are without power for an extended period, residents would need to access
 these services at locations where emergency management organizations have coordinated
 their provision, such as resilience hubs. The Seattle Times and a personal interview with
 Issaquah, Washington emergency management after the November 2024 bomb cyclone
 reveal resilience hubs and shelters open approximately 48 hours after widespread power
 outage begin. Red Cross indicates post-storm shelters open within 72 hours of an event.
 The threshold was set at 48 hours to reflect the more conservative estimate between these
 two examples. There is a lack of guidance for resilience hubs on the duration of a
 disruption that justifies opening, which is an area for future research.
 2. Store Food
 • Duration threshold: 4 hours
 • Source: United States Department of Agriculture (2013)
 • Justification: USDA emergency guidance explains food will last in a refrigerator for 4
 hours.
 3. Healthcare Procedure, Operate Medical Device
 • Duration threshold: 2 hours
 • Sources: Bawaneh et al. (2019); DeSalvo et al. (2014)
 • Justification: Research suggests outages 2 hours or longer at healthcare facilities are
 associated with a 43% increase in mortality. Research underpinning the creation of the
 Health and Human Services emPOWER Program cites the use of hospitals and shelters
 by people needing to operate electrical medical devices, which overwhelms medical staff

 72

 and endangers patients. This latter research does not cite a timeline in which people leave
 their home to find a location to operate a medical device but underscores another
 mechanism through which these conditions could cause increased mortality.
  4. Public Safety
  • Duration threshold: 1 hour
  • Source: FEMA P-1019 Emergency Power Systems for Critical Facilities: A Best
 Practices Approach to Improving Reliability (2014)
  • Justification: Police stations, fire stations, emergency medical service dispatch, and
 emergency operations centers provide services identified by FEMA and the National Fire
 Protection Code 70 (National Electric Code) as those that might “require continuous
 operation for reasons of public safety.” Hospitals, schools, and emergency shelters are
 also discussed as potentially belonging in this category, but those locations provide
 services addressed specifically in categories separate from “public safety.”
  5. Workforce
  • Duration threshold: 72 hours
  • Sources: Power Outage Incident Annex to the Response and Recovery Federal
 Interagency Operational Plans (2017); Urban Sustainability Directors Network (2019)
  • Justification: A “long-term interruption” is the subject of FEMA’s Power Outage Incident
 Annex planning document, defined as 72 hours or longer based on a list of previous
 events referenced in the introduction. The Urban Sustainability Director’s Network
 Resilience Hub Guide cites 72 hours as a common duration through which resilience
 hubs should be prepared to provide resources supported by backup power. This duration,
 however, does not indicate how long after a disruption begins that hubs should plan to
 provide “Workforce” services such as childcare or banking (internet communications).
 However, given that services in the “Workforce” categories focus on the recovery phase
 of an emergency, after services addressing more acute needs have been stabilized, a
 duration longer than that of other categories was selected. Additional research should
 refine this duration.
  6. Obtain Over-the-Counter Medications, Obtain Fuel, Obtain Prescription Medications
  • Duration threshold: 12 hours
  • Source: Ericson et al. (2022)
  • Justification: No published guidance about the relationship between disruption length and
 adverse consequences in these critical service categories. Used the duration exceeded by
 50% of major power outages reported by the U.S. Energy Information Administration.
