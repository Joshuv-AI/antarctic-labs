Analysis of implementation status and influencing factors of standard infection control methods for dental environmental surfaces by dental hygienists in South Korea: a questionnaire-based study
Authors: Hye-Young Yoon, Sun-Jung Shin, Bo-Mi Shin, Hyo-Jin Lee, Jin-Sun Choi, Kyoung-Nam Kim, Soo-Myoung Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGiven reports that the Coronavirus Disease 2019 can survive on environmental surfaces for a period, public attention to surface disinfection has increased. However, only a few studies have investigated the current status of standard infection control practices for environmental surfaces. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the implementation status of standard infection control methods for dental environmental surfaces among South Korean dental hygienists and to analyze the factors influencing these practices.MethodsA survey was conducted to investigate surface infection control methods in dental office environments. A questionnaire, consisting of general characteristics and infection control methods stratified by environmental surfaces, was distributed to dental hygienists. Overall, 190 dental hygienists responded to the survey; responses from eight participants with incomplete data were excluded, and the remaining 182 responses were analyzed using SPSS software.ResultsThe surfaces with the highest implementation rate of standard infection control methods were the bracket Table (23.1%), followed by the light-curing unit head. In contrast, drawer handles (0.5%) had the lowest implementation rates. The odds of implementing standard infection control methods were higher for dental hygienists treating 40 or more patients daily compared with those treating 19 or fewer patients. In addition, the odds of implementing standard infection control methods were higher when an infection control personnel was present than when absent.ConclusionBased on a thorough understanding of dental institutions and accumulated evidence, our findings highlight the need to improve standard infection control methods for efficient implementation, and offer strategies to increase their implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08810-9.Keywords: Environment, Dental equipment, Dental hygienists, Infection control

Introduction

Notably, aerosols and droplets are generated during several dental procedures, particularly during treatments using rotary instruments, ultrasonic scalers, air-water syringes, and air polishing [1, 2]. These aerosols and droplets contain oral microorganisms and various opportunistic pathogens [1–5]. Compared to aerosols, droplets are relatively larger particles and can contaminate nearby areas, whereas aerosols can remain suspended in the air even after treatment ends and easily spread throughout the environment via air currents [6]. From this perspective, aerosols and droplets can contaminate dental staff and patients, as well as all exposed surfaces and the environment around the dental unit chair [7, 8].

Surfaces contaminated with microorganisms can be a direct cause of cross-infection and act as reservoirs for microbial contamination [9, 10]. Surfaces frequently exposed to aerosols during dental procedures include light handles and chair control switches. Previous studies have shown that a large number of bacteria exist on these surfaces and various others [11–14]. Therefore, to prevent cross-infection, the Korean Ministry of Health and Welfare(MOHW) [10] and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention(CDC) [9] have recommended that environmental surfaces potentially contaminated with microorganisms after patient treatment should be appropriately managed using surface barriers, cleaning agents, and disinfection methods based on the treatment administered and the characteristics of the equipment used. The details are as follows: dental surfaces are categorized as clinical contact and housekeeping surfaces. Clinical contact surfaces are directly contaminated by contact with the hands or gloves of dental healthcare personnel (DHCP), including light handles, chair control switches, and drawer handles, and should be managed by replacing barriers or disinfecting them after each patient. Barriers should be used on surfaces where disinfectants cannot be applied. Housekeeping surfaces include the floors, walls, and windows of the dental clinic, which do not come into direct contact with patients and are infrequently touched by DHCP. Housekeeping surfaces need to be disinfected or cleaned once per daily cycle; if body fluid contamination is suspected, the surfaces should be cleaned and disinfected.

Standard methods for infection control in dental offices are similar in many countries, and each time a new infectious disease emerges, these methods are updated to prevent transmission within healthcare facilities [15, 16]. Recently, due to the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, infection control guidelines for COVID-19 in dental offices were updated and made more stringent; however, monitoring for compliance with standard infection control practices in dental offices has not been well evaluated. A study examining awareness and practice levels of infection control among DHCP reported high awareness of infection control but comparatively low practice levels [17, 18]. This indicates that additional strategies are needed to enhance implementation in addition to supplementing the guidelines. The updated dental infection control guidelines to prevent the spread of COVID-19 include certain requirements that make implementation more difficult, such as demanding isolation rooms with ventilation systems or unit chair arrangements spaced 2 m apart [16, 19]. Moreover, measures for items reported as lacking in practice in previous studies have not been established, and the guidelines have yet to be supplemented [16].

Guidelines for standard dental infection control methods must be improved and supplemented to be more practicable in dental healthcare institutions. To achieve this, understanding the conditions within dental institutions must be prioritized, and infection control status survey data can serve as the basis for this. Without an understanding of the conditions in dental healthcare institutions, an increase in practice rates will be difficult, even if infection control guidelines are supplemented, and the insufficient improvement in infection control practice rates surveyed after the COVID-19 pandemic supports this [20, 21].

To date, studies have examined the current status of practices and the analysis of influencing factors regarding instrument reprocessing and personal protective equipment use. However, research investigating the current status of standard infection control practices for environmental surfaces remains insufficient, and studies on specific infection control practices for various environmental surfaces are lacking. Furthermore, no study has analyzed the factors influencing environmental surface infection control practices.

The COVID-19 pandemic has heightened the level of attention towards infection control among patients and increased concerns regarding the risk of infection in dental offices. The number of patients visiting dentistry has decreased by more than 25% during the COVID-19 pandemic, and it was found that the greatest reason for this was concern about the risk of infection [22]. Furthermore, in a study conducted in South Korea, 34.3% of participants reported that, following the COVID-19 pandemic, they pay closer attention to the cleanliness and hygiene of instruments and equipment during dental visits than they did before the pandemic [23]. The heightened public interest in infection control within dental offices, caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, demonstrates that now is the time for even more rigorous implementation of infection controls. Correspondingly, the dental infection control standard guidelines in South Korea have been established at the national level. Hence, it is time to inspect the implementation of standard infection control methods for surface management [10, 24]. To establish the evidence base for this, more data on the actual practices of surface control methods in dental institutions is needed.

Therefore, we aimed to investigate the implementation status of standard infection control measures for dental environmental surfaces among South Korean dental hygienists, identify factors influencing standard infection control practices, and provide foundational data for developing practicable environmental surface control measures in dental healthcare institutions.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The questionnaire survey for this study was conducted among dental hygienists in South Korea. Convenience sampling was used to recruit participants. After receiving IRB approval, participants were recruited by distributing a Google Form link along with a recruitment notice on a dental hygienist community website with approximately 80,000 members. The Google Form link was posted from January 8 to February 19, 2025.

In a previous study that did not focus on surface management but instead conducted a regression analysis on factors influencing infection control practices among dental hygienists in South Korea, responses from a total of 124 participants were ultimately analyzed [25]. Similarly, we recruited 190 participants to account for dropout rates and conducted the survey. To prevent duplicate responses, the personal information (name and phone number) provided by respondents to receive compensation for participating in the study was verified before being used in the analysis, and no duplicate responses were found. The final analysis included responses from 182 participants, excluding eight with insufficient responses.

Ethical approval

The Institutional Review Board of Gangneung-Wonju National University approved this study (GWNUIRB-2024-7-3). Research participants were informed of the purpose and extent of the study and were given the option to voluntarily participate in the survey. They were also informed that they could withdraw from the study at any time during its course.

Questionnaire

A structured questionnaire was used to assess whether dental hygienists implemented standard infection control methods for surfaces in their dental offices. The questionnaires used in the study were developed for this study and have not previously been published elsewhere (see Supplementary Text S1).

The draft questionnaire was developed by two experts based on the standard dental infection control guidelines of MOHW and CDC [9, 10]. The draft was reviewed by five internal experts, and subsequently pilot-tested with two dental hygienists. Following the pilot test, minor modifications were made to the composition of the items and the format, and the clarity of the questions as well as the required completion time were verified.

The final questionnaire comprised nine items on the general characteristics of the dental hygienists and 12 items on infection control practices for specific surfaces in dental offices.

General characteristics

General characteristics included three sub-items related to personal background (gender, age, and years of experience) and four sub-items related to work environment (working area, type of dental institution, number of patients seen per day, number of employees, and presence of infection control personnel). The definitions of each work environment sub-item are as follows:

Working area: The region where the institution is located. For the analysis, it was reclassified into the metropolitan area (Seoul and Gyeonggi-do) and non-metropolitan areas (Gangwon-do, Chungcheong-do, Gyeongsang-do, Jeolla-do and Jeju-do).

Infection control practices for surfaces

The standard guidelines by the MOHW and CDC categorize environmental surfaces into clinical contact surfaces and housekeeping surfaces, and suggest three main infection control methods for these surfaces: surface barriers, surface disinfection, and surface cleaning [9, 10]. Based on this, the survey on infection control practices for surfaces in dental offices was designed for nine clinical contact surfaces (bracket table, unit chair control switch, light handle, unit chair headrest, drawer handle, light-curing unit head, scaler body, 3-way syringe handle, and computer mouse) and 3 housekeeping surfaces (walls, floors, and windows). Also, the questionnaire consisted of multiple responses for infection control methods (surface barriers, disinfection, cleaning) applied to each surface. Then, through a dropdown selection format, it was made possible to select answers for questions regarding the timing, approaches, ingredients and reasons of the selected method. The general definitions and detailed guidelines for each method are as follows [9, 10].

Surface barriers: This refers to protecting surfaces that are frequently touched by DHCP but are difficult to disinfect, by covering them with barriers such as clear plastic wrap or plastic-backed paper. If surface barriers are used, they should be replaced for each patient. The application of surface barriers is recommended for most clinical contact surfaces.

Surface barriers: This refers to protecting surfaces that are frequently touched by DHCP but are difficult to disinfect, by covering them with barriers such as clear plastic wrap or plastic-backed paper. If surface barriers are used, they should be replaced for each patient. The application of surface barriers is recommended for most clinical contact surfaces.

Surface disinfection: This refers to managing surfaces with agents that have disinfectant effects. If surface barriers are not used, it is recommended that surfaces be disinfected after each patient. Even when barriers are applied, surface disinfection is advised if visible contamination is present. Conversely, the use of high-level disinfectants or spray application methods is discouraged.

Even when barriers are applied, surface disinfection is advised if visible contamination is present. Conversely, the use of high-level disinfectants or spray application methods is discouraged.

Surface cleaning: This refers to the physical removal of contaminants such as soil, dust, and organic matter from surfaces, which reduces the amount of organic material without killing microorganisms. For surface cleaning, it is suggested that, in the absence of visible contamination, the procedure be applied once daily or weekly depending on the surface.

Operational definition of standard infection control practices implementation

In accordance with standard guidelines, for clinical contact surfaces it was considered compliance with standard guidelines if surface barriers were routinely replaced for each patient and surface disinfection or surface cleaning was performed concurrently. Additionally, even in cases where surface barriers are not used but surface disinfection is routinely performed for each patient and surface cleaning is carried out concurrently, this was considered to be in compliance with standard guidelines. Therefore, respondents who selected both “surface disinfection” or “surface cleaning” and “surface barriers,” and chose “for each patient” as the timing for performing surface barriers, or those who selected both surface disinfection and surface cleaning and chose “for each patient” as the timing for performing surface disinfection, were coded as having “implemented” standard infection control; the remaining respondents were coded as “non-implemented.

For housekeeping surfaces, it was considered compliance with standard guidelines if surface disinfection or cleaning was performed once daily routinely, and additionally performed when contamination was visibly present. Therefore, respondents who selected “surface disinfection” or “surface cleaning” and, regarding the timing of surface disinfection or cleaning, selected “When contamination is visible” and “Before the start of all treatments” or “After the end of all treatments” were coded as having “implemented” standard infection control; the remaining respondents were coded as “non-implemented.”

That is, the implementation rate refers to the percentage of respondents that “implemented” the standard infection control methods for each surface.

Statistical analysis

The frequency and percentage of dental hygienists’ general characteristics and their methods for performing environmental surface infection control in dental offices were calculated. Differences in the implementation of standard infection control methods for environmental surfaces according to the general characteristics of dental hygienists were analyzed using the Chi-square test. When cells with an expected frequency of less than 5 constituted 20% or more of the total, Fisher’s exact test was used for analysis.

To identify factors influencing the implementation of standard environmental surface infection control methods in dental offices, a simple logistic regression analysis was performed using the significant variables identified in the Chi-square test as independent variables. In both the chi-square test and regression analysis, gender was excluded among the general characteristics because the differences between women and men were too large. In logistic regression analysis, because of the diverse surfaces under analysis and the large number of comparison items, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure [26]. The adjusted p-values calculated using the Benjamin–Hochberg algorithm were expressed as q-values. The FDR threshold was set to q = 0.10, which means that up to 10% of the results deemed significant could be false positives [26]. Therefore, for the q-value, statistical significance was accepted when q < 0.10.

To assess the reliability of the infection control practice items, Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) coefficient was calculated. All subscales demonstrated reliability at the acceptable level or higher: surface barriers (KR-20 = 0.850), surface disinfection (KR-20 = 0.915), surface cleaning (KR-20 = 0.865).

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 28.0.; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY), and the significance level (α) was set at 0.05.

Results

General characteristics of study participants

Overall, 182 dental hygienists (176 females, 6 males) completed the questionnaire. The participants’ ages ranged from 21 to 50 years, with a mean age of 29.19 ± 4.41. Table 1 presents the general characteristics of the participants. Notably, over 50% of the participants (n = 135, 74.2%) had less than 5 years of work experience. The highest number of patients seen per day was over 40 (n = 59, 32.4%). In addition, 61 (33.5%) participants had infection control personnel, while 121 (66.5%) did not (Table 1).

Comparison of infection control methods by clinical contact surface

The proportion performing ‘disinfection or cleaning with surface barriers’ was highest for bracket Table (35.7%), followed by light-curing unit heads and scaler body. Reasons for performing infection control methods

When allowing multiple selections to confirm the reasons for performing infection control methods as described above, the percentage responding ‘Based on information obtained from external organizations’ was highest for bracket Table (23.1%), followed by light-curing units, and 3-way syringe handles. For all environmental surfaces, the highest response rate was ‘according to the institution’s internal guidelines’, with the highest rate being 70.3% for bracket tables and light-curing heads. The percentage that responded ‘to reduce costs’ was high at 9.9% for unit chair headrests and 9.3% for drawer handles, light-curing units, and scaler bodies. The percentage that responded ‘to save time’ was highest for computer mouse (27.5%), followed by unit chair headrests, drawer handles, and walls (Table 6).

Discussion

The Standard Guidelines for Infection Control in Dental Practice provide standard infection control methods for instrument reprocessing, use of personal protective equipment (PPE), dental unit water line (DUWL) control, and environmental surface management. However, the implementation status has been investigated primarily focusing on instrument reprocessing, PPE, and DUWL [20, 21, 27]. Notably, microorganisms can survive on surfaces for some time, and this issue resurfaced during the COVID-19 pandemic, thereby increasing interest in surface control [24, 28, 29]. Dental procedures frequently generate droplets and aerosols, leading to common surface contamination within dental offices. Previous studies confirmed that when using air-polishing devices and ultrasonic scalers, aerosols contaminated the practitioner’s clothing and surfaces around the patient, such as chair lights, chair tray, and x-ray board [30]. In addition, surfaces up to 2 m away from the patient’s mouth were contaminated by aerosols [30]. Furthermore, opportunistic pathogens, such as Staphylococcus, Micrococcus, and Pseudomonas, along with oral bacteria, were detected in media exposed to the dental offices’ environment for 20–30 min [4, 5]. Similarly, various opportunistic pathogens, including oral bacteria, were confirmed in samples swabbed from dental unit chair surfaces [13]. Infection control for surfaces within the dental offices’ environment has become increasingly important to prevent the spread of infectious microorganisms. Previous studies investigating the implementation of infection control practices on environmental surfaces have merely confirmed whether such practices were performed or not, without examining specific surfaces. In addition, investigation of the specific infection control methods used, including whether standard infection control methods were implemented, have been insufficient. Therefore, we aimed to identify infection control practices by environmental surface type and verify the implementation of standard infection control methods for specific surfaces, providing foundational data to establish improvement strategies for enhancing implementation rates.

For the nine types of clinical contact surfaces, only surface disinfection was performed frequently. The Dental Infection Control Standard Guidelines recommend using low-level or intermediate-level disinfectants; however, it was found that alcohol is almost exclusively used as a surface disinfectant in South Korea [9, 10]. Alcohol is advantageous because it is cheap, fast-acting, and less irritating. However, its disadvantages include reduced effectiveness in the presence of organic residues, as well as high volatility and flammability [31, 32]. Furthermore, it is ineffective against spores and only partially disinfects fungi and viruses [31, 32]. Due to these disadvantages, the Korean Dental Infection Control Standard Policy and Procedure recommends against using alcohol for disinfecting broad environmental surfaces [10]. In addition, alcohol can adversely affect certain surface materials (metal, rubber, and plastic), leading to usage restrictions based on the surface material [31, 32]. Factors limiting alcohol use exist; however, considering its frequent use in dental institutions, developing infection control methods that enhance the efficacy of alcohol may be necessary. Therefore, more specific guidelines for alcohol use on environmental surfaces should be supplemented.

Since most surfaces in dental environments are made of metal or plastic, the use of disinfectants like alcohol is inevitably limited, making surface barriers unavoidable. Williams et al. [11] confirmed a reduction in bacterial contamination on surfaces covered with barriers. Dental infection control standard guidelines also emphasize the use of surface barriers, noting that it is difficult to completely disinfect equipment surfaces, and disinfectants are not safe for equipment or dental staff [9, 10]. However, our results show that surface barriers were not applied to most clinical contact surfaces. Furthermore, it is important to replace surface barriers after each patient. However, this practice was found to be inadequately implemented, except on some surfaces. Stronger guidelines to increase surface barriers usage are needed; however, consideration for medical waste issues must also be addressed.

Most dental institutions were found to implement infection control methods based on their guidelines, suggesting that the content of these guidelines varies between institutions and differs from standard guidelines. Standard guidelines provide the basic direction for infection control, allowing each dental institution to establish its own infection control policies and implementation methods according to its specific circumstances [10]. This highlights the need for specific, clear standard guidelines regarding surfaces and disinfectants to address the content variation in dental institutions’ guidelines. Among clinical contact surfaces, sterilization, a higher-level infection control method than surface barriers or disinfection, was sometimes performed on scaler body and 3-way syringe handles, which are used in close proximity to the patient’s oral cavity and are frequently exposed to blood or saliva. Notably, some dental unit chairs are designed to allow sterilization of the separable scaling body and 3-way syringe handle for infection control. Therefore, sterilization could be considered the recommended standard infection control method for clinical contact surfaces, depending on their distance from the patient’s oral cavity and sterilizability.

For housekeeping surfaces, surface cleaning was performed frequently, while a relatively high proportion reported not cleaning walls and windows. Given that the distance from the dental unit chair where treatment occurs can influence the necessity and implementation of surface management, specific standard guidelines that account for this distance are needed. Therefore, dental institutions should establish their guidelines based on these standards to increase implementation rates.

Although it is pre-FDR correction data, factors affecting the implementation of standard infection control methods were the number of patients per day and the presence of infection control personnel. This finding is consistent with those of previous studies that identified factors influencing practices related to PPE use and instrument reprocessing [20, 33]. The higher the number of patients per day, the greater the likelihood that the dental institution operates on a hospital-level scale, which can indicate a high implementation rate [17, 33, 34]. Joo et al. [20] reported that the lower the number of patients assigned to dental hygienists per day, the higher the infection control implementation rate. We observed that the implementation rate increased with a larger number of staff, although this was not statistically significant in our study. Therefore, securing a sufficient number of dental hygienists relative to the number of patients visiting the dental office per day is necessary to implement standard infection control methods.

The South Korean dental hospital accreditation standards mandate the placement of infection control personnel within the institution. Previous studies investigating the factors that influence infection control practices regarding PPE use and instrument reprocessing revealed higher implementation rates in hospitals than in clinics, attributing this to the impact of institutional oversight, such as accreditation assessments conducted in hospitals [33, 35]. Similarly, although not statistically significant, our findings suggest that implementation rates of standard infection control methods may have been higher in hospitals than in clinics. Previous studies indicated that years of experience is a statistically significant factor that influences infection control practices, and that higher years of experience correlate with greater experience in infection control practices and education, leading to higher practice rates [17, 36]. However, these studies did not target environmental surfaces. In our study, years of experience did not show a statistically significant difference, and the implementation rate was similar regardless of the years of experience. Similar to our study, Joo et al. [20], conducted a study after the COVID-19 pandemic, and found that years of experience was not a significant factor affecting dental infection control practice rates. This suggests that after COVID-19, creating conditions that enable infection control implementation, such as the number of patients visiting daily and the presence of infection control personnel, is more important than years of experience in ensuring the implementation of standard infection control practices. Regarding infection control personnel, implementation rates may increase when supported by institutional regulations. Infection control-related regulations targeting private dental clinics should be established to facilitate the implementation of these control methods in private dental clinics.

When the working area was reclassified into the metropolitan area and non-metropolitan areas for a cross-analysis, the implementation rate of standard infection control practices was higher in the non-metropolitan areas for most surfaces, and a significant difference was observed in the bracket table.

Similarly, a previous study comparing the use of protective equipment and the reprocessing of instruments between metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas found that implementation rates were higher in non-metropolitan areas [20, 25]. A previous study cited research indicating that increased work fatigue and job stress among nurses had a negative impact on infection control; based on this, it can be inferred that high levels of work fatigue among dental hygienists at dental institutions located in the metropolitan area—where daily patient numbers and COVID-19 infected individuals are relatively concentrated—may lead to a decline in overall infection control implementation [20].

Under South Korean law, dental hygienists are authorized to perform the following duties related to the prevention and hygiene management of dental and oral diseases (including temporary fillings, placement of temporary restorations, removal of restorations, taking dental impressions, placement and removal of orthodontic wires, and other related tasks) [37, 38]. However, because the legal scope of duties is ambiguous and dental hygienists are the only specialized personnel in dental institutions in South Korea, it was reported that they perform more duties than those specified by law; in particular, the frequency and time spent on clinical assistance tasks were found to be high [38]. Compared to other countries where dental institutions have diverse specialized staff, dental hygienists in South Korea are unable to focus on tasks that require specialized expertise, and this situation may have affected their ability to perform infection control practices.

This study has some limitations. First, it only assessed the implementation of certain items among standard infection control practices. Second, participants were recruited from an online dental hygienist community via a Google Forms link, which constitutes convenience sampling. As a result, the sample may not be representative of all dental hygienists, and the findings should be interpreted with caution regarding generalizability. Furthermore, since the scope of duties performed by dental hygienists varies by country, differences in workload and responsibilities may further limit the applicability of this study’s findings. Third, because the data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, responses may have been subject to social desirability bias and recall bias. Finally, a limitation of this study is that the small sample size of the preliminary test did not allow for sufficient validation of the questionnaire.

The Standard Guidelines for Infection Control in Dental Practice emphasize that, in addition to implementing surface disinfection, the use of PPE by dental personnel is crucial during infection control practices [9, 10]. We did not investigate the use of PPE during surface infection control. Furthermore, a broader assessment and accumulation of information regarding the implementation of standard infection control methods to understand the reality of dental care in South Korea is required to facilitate the establishment of the South Korean standard manual for dental infection control to enhance implementation based on this foundation.

Conclusion

Our findings showed that the implementation rate of standard infection control methods for environmental surfaces in South Korean dental clinics highest at the bracket table, at 23.1%. This implementation rate increased with a higher number of patients visiting per day and when infection control personnel are present. These findings highlight the need to improve standard methods for efficient infection control implementation based on a thorough understanding of current domestic dental institutions and accumulated evidence. It also highlights the requirement for specific guidelines focused on influencing factors to enhance the implementation of standard infection control methods.
