Caregiver beliefs and practices about communication disorders in early childhood development centres from Durban: A qualitative study
Authors: Vuyelwa Zandile Peter, Mershen Pillay, Yougan Saman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildren with communication difficulties from impoverished families may face barriers to accessing rehabilitation services, shaped by caregiver health-seeking behaviour as one of the factors informing poor follow-up for intervention. These factors warrant closer examination, particularly for children aged birth to 5 years, to enable early intervention and improved prognosis. This study aimed to explore the beliefs of a group of caregivers, namely parents or legal guardians, early childhood development centre practitioners (teachers and support staff) and parents of adults who are Deaf, regarding the causes and practices of communication disorders.Materials and methodsThe study employed a qualitative, exploratory, cross-sectional phenomenological design, with data collected through focus groups and semi-structured interviews. The 30 participants included 19 early childhood development practitioners and 11 caregivers. Seven of whom were parents of children who underwent developmental communication screening and four were parents of adults who are Deaf. The parents and early childhood development practitioners were randomly selected from early childhood development centres that had screening conducted. Additionally, purposive sampling was used to recruit parents of people who are Deaf. All participants were from the eThekwini Municipality (Durban) in KwaZulu-Natal Province, South Africa.ResultsTwo key themes emerged: the intersectionality of beliefs about the causes of communication disorders and complex practices for their management. Participants attributed communication disorders to social and spiritual factors, and these beliefs influenced their complex decision-making about treatment and help-seeking.ConclusionHealth professionals seeking to establish community-based rehabilitation services for communication disorders must first understand caregivers’ beliefs and practices, as these shape behaviours regarding service use and influence intervention programme acceptance and follow-up.Keywords: Public Health, Health Services Accessibility, Qualitative Research, Communication

Introduction

In a diverse, multicultural context like South Africa, understanding religious and cultural beliefs about developmental disabilities is crucial for successful intervention implementation when resources are scarce, to ensure acceptance and minimise waste.1 However, the nature and choice of healthcare-seeking by caregivers (CGs) of children under 5 years are not well documented, despite the fact that these decisions significantly impact treatment outcomes, as they depend on CGs’ decision-making to seek intervention.2

In South Africa, Penn et al3 highlighted the importance of understanding how traditional cultural beliefs about the causes of genetic disorders influence healthcare practices. The study foregrounded the significant role extended family members had in influencing choices and decision-making about health-seeking pathways. Furthermore, their study emphasised the importance of religious factors in shaping health-seeking behaviours (HSB) and advocated that health professionals acknowledge the public’s beliefs in a multicultural context. Building on the significance of embracing traditional cultural beliefs among health professionals, Moshabela et al4 further introduced the concept of plural HSB, describing how individuals often integrate cultural beliefs with medical interventions. To further elaborate on the interplay between traditional and conventional practices in HSB, shaped by cultural concepts, Kpanake5 argued that failure to consider a person’s cultural concepts may limit the effectiveness of interventions in a multicultural context.5 Recognising individual(s) as both social and spiritual beings provided valuable insights for developing interventions and fostering collaboration with patients’ families and communities to influence HSB positively.5 Such behaviours underscore the need for health professionals to gain deeper insights into the patterns of practice shaped by underlying cultural beliefs, particularly in the context of disabilities, as it remains under-researched in South Africa, as noted in earlier studies.6 To ensure that effective rehabilitation services are delivered, a deeper exploration of clients’ cultural systems, values and beliefs is needed to align services with their expectations, as Taye suggests.7 This cultural sensitivity by health professionals significantly impacts healthcare access and service acceptance.8,9 This cultural understanding is especially critical during early intervention, where adult decision-making significantly influences the child’s outcomes.

Recent studies have expanded the understanding of how cultural influences shape different types of disability-related HSB. For example, Mbanjwa and Harvey10 highlighted how CGs of children with disabilities hold diverse, at times contradictory, beliefs about the causes of their children’s conditions. Similarly, Asha et al11 reported on mothers who received speech-language therapy and found that information provided regarding the condition and satisfaction with the intervention was diverse. Earlier, Sadiki12 reported that cultural beliefs influence the understanding of health conditions and disability. The authors attribute insufficient informational counselling on the condition, resulting in parental self-blame and viewing having a child with a disability as a possible ‘punishment’ from God. Also, Mkabile et al13 focused their findings about disability causes on CGs of children with intellectual disabilities (IDs), echoing similar sentiments. However, literature focusing on communication disorders (CDs) in younger children remains scant, particularly with a broader definition of CGs that includes legal guardians, early childhood practitioners and parents of people with disabilities in the South African context, which warrants further research to address this paucity.

From a conceptual perspective, we define CDs as a range of conditions that can affect a person’s ability to speak, understand or communicate through other means. These can occur during the critical early developmental phase (0–5 years) of a child’s life and significantly disrupt normal brain and nervous system development, affecting behaviour, cognition and motor skills, caused by several factors.14 CDs create substantial barriers to academic progression and future vocational opportunities.15

In South Africa, children with disabilities, particularly CDs, face compounded marginalisation through intersecting barriers of cultural beliefs, socioeconomic constraints and limited healthcare access.16 These multiple barriers impede access to intervention services, including logistical challenges, limited appropriate schooling and perceived inadequacies in health professionals’ knowledge.17 Naidoo and Khan18 identified additional challenges, including inadequate funding, resource limitations and insufficient numbers of trained professionals, particularly in rural and peri-urban areas. From these identified barriers, there is a scarcity of reports on how beliefs shape CGs’ HSB for CDs, particularly from a qualitative perspective.

In response to the literature gap, Ehlert and Coetzer19 reported contrasting patterns of practice among HSB mothers of children aged 0–5 years regarding hearing detection through quantitative research methods. The authors reported that the majority (95.3%) of their participants opted to consult health professionals, while a minority (2.7%) would consult a traditional healer following their preferences. Similarly, sociocultural beliefs attributing CDs to supernatural forces or punishment can result in stigma and social isolation, as reported by Mabetha et al.2 Porter et al20 reported on the impact of cultural and religious beliefs on parental decision-making among parents of Deaf children. Porter et al20 stated the need to investigate further the extent of cognitive bias among health professionals and parents to promote rational decision-making regarding HSB. Of interest is that Mabetha et al’s2 study, which focused on child survival, is critical to understanding how HSB influence outcomes in SA; this remains unknown, especially for CDs. Though Mabetha et al2 focused on participants who experienced child death rather than CDs, their assertions that beliefs regarding health and sickness are critical in the formulation of health promotion material are shared by the current study. One concludes, then, that there is a need to further investigate the applicability within early childhood development centres (ECDC). This is because there is a need for more literature examining the extent to which CGs’ beliefs influence HSB, especially among young children.

While ECDC aims to foster holistic child development, research reveals that there are significant knowledge gaps about CDs by educators.21 Yousuf Hussein et al22 found that while ECD practitioners (ECDP) had some knowledge regarding the causes of hearing loss and associated risk factors, they needed to demonstrate a better understanding of identification and management methods. This study is the first time in the South African context that ECDPs have been engaged, and the findings highlight a critical gap in CGs’ comprehensive understanding of CDs in ECD settings. Furthermore, in the absence of parents, ECDPs become CGs who foster early development, making them highly regarded.23 Moreover, there is a scarcity of studies that have engaged with and reported on the combined views of caregivers such as parents, ECDPs and parents of adult people who are Deaf (PPWD) regarding their beliefs about the causes and practices of CDs. The latter has had the lived experience of raising a child with CDs, thus providing a unique perspective on their journey.1,11

However, the opposing view by Sekhon et al24 suggests that culturally tailored knowledge can be produced only with deeper insight into religious and cultural beliefs. It is crucial to understand these beliefs when aiming for culturally sensitive interventions that are also clinically valuable. Others further echo that excessive emphasis on cultural accommodation may compromise the clinical effectiveness of intervention.25 Furthermore, some research shows that adherence to traditional practices can interfere with critical interventions. Therefore, these studies suggest that improved children’s health outcomes can be achieved when medical standards are consistently applied, a finding that has not been well documented for children with CDs.

In light of differing views on the significance of beliefs in informing HSB, the question is: What beliefs do CGs in ECDC hold about the causes of CDs and the practices used to address them? This study explored CGs’ beliefs about the causes of CDs affecting ECD and the practices to address these challenges in ECDs. The study identified two objectives:

To explore CGs’ beliefs about the potential causes of CDs in early childhood.

The Ecological Systems Theory (EST) developed by Bronfenbrenner26 was considered an appropriate theoretical model for situating the current study, as it aligns well with the sociocultural context. This framework considers the microsystem beliefs of the immediate family and the macrosystem culture to which the child belongs.26

Materials and methods

Study context description

In KwaZulu-Natal (KZN) Province, where the study was conducted, the indigenous isiZulu-speaking clan accounts for a large percentage of the population (70%), with isiZulu (77%) being the most commonly spoken language in South Africa. The isiZulu traditional belief system (TBS) is based on paternal authority, patriarchy and polygamy, with the acknowledgement of a single deity and communication with ancestors as intermediaries accessed directly or through traditional healers.27 People are believed to be spiritual beings, with the dead remaining as real as the living and being consulted on various matters, including health-related issues.28 Some isiZulu speakers subscribe to Christianity, with two distinct religious philosophies having developed over time: one is orthodox in various forms (eg, Catholics, Baptists), and reliance on the Bible leads to rejection of TBS and the use of traditional medicines to address health conditions.29 The other is a combination of TBS and Christian elements, herein referred to as a combined belief system,28 in which devotees incorporate both aspects, including addressing health-related issues. This may result in their seeking guidance from a traditional healer first to address health issues and then attending a clinic if additional remedies are needed.3

The study was conducted in the high-density residential areas of Phoenix, Inanda, Ntuzuma and KwaMashu (PINK) in the eThekwini Municipality, with approximately 1.15 million people living in 70 km2. This is one of the largest low-income household areas in eThekwini (Durban), with almost 41% of households earning less than the internationally recognised poverty line of US$110.60 per month and with an 18.7% unemployment rate.30 The primary residents are of African and Indian descent, with isiZulu (77%) and English being the main spoken languages.31 Most occupants use public sector health services, accessed through primary healthcare clinics, where patients are referred upwards to more specialised services,32 such as screening and intervention for CDs. The study was conducted post COVID-19 lockdown with strict gathering restrictions in 2021.

Design

A qualitative, descriptive, cross-sectional phenomenological design was adopted by the researchers to explore participants’ lived experiences related to their understanding of the causes and practices regarding CDs for children attending ECDs in a resource-limited setting. The study used semi-structured interview methods to collect data from participants.33

Data collection methods

Data generation included focus groups (FGs) and semistructured individual interviews (IDIs).34 The FG method generated data and facilitated deeper participant contributions at familiar ECD sites.35 Conversely, IDI participants were selected due to the anticipated lower numbers.36 Conducting these interviews in participants’ homes created a comfortable, private environment conducive to sharing personal narratives and detailed accounts of their caregiving journey without the burden of transport.33 This methodological approach ensured comprehensive data collection while respecting participants’ unique circumstances and needs.

Data collection tools

The researcher used a similar structure outline for both the interview schedules and discussion guides that were used for the IDI and FGs, which consisted of three sections with open-ended questions about CDs see (online supplemental file 1).37 The FG and IDI guides and schedule were informed by literature and adapted for the study (online supplemental file 2).37 The tool consisted of four sections, which included (a) demographic details, (b) knowledge, beliefs and (c) practices related to CDs. The schedules were translated into isiZulu and then back-translated into English to ensure translation accuracy and content validity.38

The study used an inclusive sampling strategy to capture diverse perspectives on beliefs about CDs. The researcher intentionally included three participant groups with different caregiving roles and life experiences to reflect complementary and contrasting views across early communication development (see table 1). Stratifying participants into these heterogeneous groups helped reduce bias.39

First, ECDPs were included because they act as secondary CGs in collaboration with parents, possess expertise in ECD and are well positioned to identify children at risk of developmental delays, as they routinely observe children and participate in early identification and referral processes.

Second, there are CGs, including biological parents or legal guardians and relatives of all assessed children; both those who passed and those who failed were included to capture CG perspectives that shape follow-up, HSB and intervention uptake. Their input provided valuable insights into existing knowledge, beliefs and practices in this area, which remains significantly underexplored in the literature. These insights are essential for designing interventions that are more responsive to the target populations.

Third, PPWD were recruited as they have lived experience raising children with CDs. They offer retrospective, lived-experience insights into long-term outcomes and the implications of early childhood practices and beliefs. Combining these CG perspectives enabled triangulation across caregiving and experiential viewpoints, thereby strengthening the contextual understanding of the beliefs that shape CD identification and management within the local ECD ecosystem.40

Sampling methods and sample size

The study used two sampling methods: simple random sampling to recruit ECDP and CG and purposive sampling for PPWD.41 Simple random sampling (also known as the fishbowl method) is a common qualitative research method 40 that ensures a fair, transparent selection process while countering selection bias.42 The randomisation entailed writing the names of the 30 visited sites on separate slips, placing them in a container, then shuffling and selecting one at a time40. From the selected sites, four FG comprising the ECDP were held with those who agreed to participate, each consisting of three to five participants and conducted face-to-face. The same procedure was followed to select and invite 20 CGs of children screened for CDs, but only seven attended the face-to-face discussions at an agreed ECD site. However, concerns regarding conducting research at sites where screening took place were rationalised as best positioned to provide informed reflections on identification, referral and follow-up practices. The researchers acknowledge the potential for selection bias arising from recruiting participants from ECD centres that already conduct developmental screening. Hence, the researcher used simple random and purposive sampling to address participant selection bias.43 Finally, four IDI interviews were conducted with the hearing PPWD at their homes, and the researcher was alone.

Data collection process

This study was part of a multistage data collection process, where phase one entailed conducting CD screening for children aged birth to 5 years at 30 ECDs sites. Phase two consisted of conducting interviews with relevant stakeholders. The first step entailed obtaining relevant gatekeeper permission from the Department of Social Development (DSD), the Department of Health and the KZN Blind and Deaf Society (KZNBDS). Followed by accessing the eThekwini North District Social Development database of all ECDs in the PINK area to map their locations and select sites that would form the study’s population of focus. The contact information for the PPWD was obtained from the KZNBDS database, in accordance with ethical guidelines.

A pilot was conducted at two sites before data collection commenced, to trial the data collection tool(s) and assess feasibility. The findings informed changes for the main study; however, the results were not included in the analysis.44,45

Phase one commenced with CD screening, and subsequently a database of 30 sites and all the children who were assessed was compiled and organised by residential area to enable further selection of participants. Thereafter, phase two began, during which simple random (fishbowl) sampling for the selection of CG and ECDP, and purposive sampling of PPWD as eligible participants, were conducted telephonically, and consenting participants were interviewed. All sessions were conducted in the participants’ preferred language(s), were face-to-face and audio-recorded, and lasted 45–90 min. In their interviews, all participants were assisted in responding to a question to ensure diverse points of view were presented.37 Both verbal and non-verbal behaviours were observed and documented in field notes, including how their perspectives were expressed.36

Member checking was conducted, where participants were offered the opportunity to read and revise the verbatim transcripts to improve information accuracy and reduce misinterpretation.46 This was carried out by delivering the transcripts using diverse methods, such as email and printed hard copies, based on the participants’ preferences.

Data analysis

Initial verbatim transcription of all FG discussions and IDI was conducted, with necessary translations into English.47 The primary researcher (VZP) conducted the analysis independently, and an independent researcher conducted a secondary analysis of 20% of the transcripts, coding the data and performing thematic analysis to enhance transparency and reduce interpretive bias.48 Subsequently, the researcher uploaded the transcriptions to NVivo (V.12) and conducted further analysis following the steps recommended by Braun and Clarke49. The analysis employed a pragmatic approach within an Ecological System Framework,35 focusing on beliefs about the causes and practices of CDs.

Data triangulation was achieved through multiple data sources (interviews and field notes) and methods (FG and IDI). The analysis was achieved through an initial, separate analysis of IDI and FG, supported by field notes, followed by composite integration to identify convergent and divergent perspectives across participant groups.50 This methodological triangulation enabled comparison of shared, group-level beliefs emerging from FGDs with more personal, reflective accounts elicited during IDIs, thereby enhancing the credibility of the findings. Differences between FGDs and IDIs were examined through methodological triangulation, and reflexive team analysis was retained as analytically meaningful to capture contextual variation in beliefs. This approach was appropriate given the heterogeneous sample, which yielded rich, diverse data from FG and IDI and was further strengthened through triangulation with field notes.50

Trustworthiness

The researcher adhered to the FG and IDI guidelines, which had been piloted before data collection, to ensure the study’s tools’ credibility. Dependability was ensured through detailed descriptions of the study protocol, field notes taken during the sessions and data collection analysis using methods such as a coding book (online supplemental file 3)38 . The data collection tool development was informed by the literature, translated into the participants’ home language and back-translated. Furthermore, the tools were piloted and changes were incorporated before the main study, ensuring reliability and validity by the researcher.34,35 The audit trail demonstrates confirmability, dependability and rigour, which are essential in qualitative research.51

A concern noted is that the ECD used in the study may be more sensitised to CDs and to early identification practices than ECD settings that do not engage in screening, offering context-specific reasons. However, the current findings are not transferable to all ECD centres in the region.

Member checking was conducted to ensure responses were accurately captured and that the data were reliable and authentic, as part of strengthening trustworthiness.37 Code and thematic saturation were not formally quantified using statistical or model-based approaches.48 Instead, the final sample size was determined pragmatically, with data collection curtailed due to logistical constraints associated with the resurgence of COVID-19. While saturation is widely regarded in research as the guiding principle for determining adequacy in qualitative sample sizes,50 it remains inherently difficult to operationalise and lacks universally accepted quantitative thresholds.48 Furthermore, qualitative sample size decisions are often influenced by practical considerations such as time, resources and contextual constraints.50,51

The process of using an independent coder and comparing analyses enhanced the credibility and reliability of the results.52 Findings from the independent coder were compared with those of the primary researcher, with areas of convergence and divergence systematically examined, then discussed to strengthen analytical rigour and credibility.51 Furthermore, the study employed multiple methods, such as thick descriptions, inter-rater reliability, member checking and triangulation, as strategies to ensure credibility.53 Given the well-documented disruptions imposed by COVID-19, including restricted access to participants, physical distancing requirements and time constraints on data collection, early cessation of interviews represents a methodologically defensible adaptation rather than a limitation that compromises study rigour.

Researcher positionality

The researcher only had a relationship with the participants after the study commenced. The interviews were conducted by VZP, a female, qualified Speech Language Therapist and Audiologist with a Master’s degree and fluent in English and isiZulu. During the study, she was employed in the higher education sector. The participants in this research are fellow isiZulu-speaking Africans, and the researcher defines this understanding in terms of cultural, linguistic and religious similarities.54 Establishing rapport and familiarity was critical when collecting qualitative, authentic information.55 However, to obtain objective participant responses about their experiences, the researcher remained impartial for the whole research process, and participants were not coerced into agreeing or disagreeing with the researcher.35 Being aware of the interviewing process’s influence on neutrality and the possibility of offending the participants, the researcher carefully selected and sensitively posed questions as humanly possible. The primary purpose of this phase was to understand the participants’ thoughts and actions regarding CDs in children. However, the researcher understood herself as an African educated and research trained in the Western philosophy. This dual perspective of researching from the provider and user perspectives is unique and offers diverse experiences.

Subtheme 1: Social causes

The social factors attributed to cause CDs are identified as two subthemes: an unconducive family environment and poor language models.

C1: Unconducive family environment

Participants in this sample attributed CDs to be caused by abuse and neglect, complicated by the unfavourable domestic circumstances in which some children live. The abuse included physical and verbal, such as shouting, which the CGs suggested caused interactional impairments that resulted in fear of interacting with others, particularly adults. This fear can result in social withdrawal associated with CDs in children, with those who are abused and neglected suffering emotionally and mentally, resulting in underlying problems. As a result, CDs do not qualify as a biomedical impairment alone but can be seen as a symptom of relational falling-out, in which the verbal manifestations of social harm or hearing impairment in a child are suppressed, thereby capturing the history of conflict, neglect and verbal aggression. In this way, silence is reconceptualised as an adaptive protective strategy rather than a deficit. The ideology is placed in the context of abusive relationships between CGs and children, which is heightened by the lack of supportive relationships between schools and communities in the mesosystem. This is further framed by the cultural beliefs and values that stigmatise disclosure and traditional mediation in place of clinical intervention. This intersectional belief, in turn, results from a multifaceted exchange across ecological levels, as the child’s silence is a response to a non-beneficial relationship space.

A participant who upheld the TSB contended that incest causes CDs, and inappropriate consensual intercourse between family members is believed to result in a child being conceived and born with disabilities, including CDs. The disability of the child is viewed as an apparent and moral outcome of a serious cultural offence. Instead of being a biological occurrence, this disability serves as a noticeable symbol of social penalty, thereby leading to the realisation of an ethical violation. The incestuous, intentional behaviour occurs, whereas the mesosystem supports its ethical evaluation through local gossip. Finally, the behaviour is upheld through established cultural values and taboos. As a result, there are the alleged causes of the disorder, which are ecologically built, and in which personal health is introduced into the larger framework of moral and social order.

In my neighbourhood, it once happened that siblings were in a relationship, and they had a disabled child. I told her that sometimes a family member is attracted to another family member, which is not right. Others, you will find that they did not know they were related, and when it is discovered, it is too late, and they decide to continue, and then they end up having a problem. (CG EK1) FG.

Combined, the narratives reveal disability and CDs as socially meaningful impressions of moral breach and relational toxicity, not only biomedical events. Furthermore, this disrupted relation and how symptoms become understandable, for example, incest or chronic aggression, is how disorders manifest. Silence and withdrawal appear as protective adjustments necessitating the restoration of relationship safety and dignity through trauma-informed healing. Thus, family systems that support culturally resonant messaging are needed. The FG and IDI identified abuse, neglect and adverse domestic conditions as key drivers causing CDs. Not only does such abuse prevent interaction, but the FG insights also show how fear of being abused further discourages communication by children. Such insights necessitate the promotion of the child’s social and emotional well-being amidst abusive family dynamics in supportive environments.

C2: Poor language models

Adults model to children how language is structured and used, transferring this knowledge between generations through social interactions. Furthermore, the sampled CGs and the ECDP reported that children learn language through imitation and by observing adults’ verbal and nonverbal communication. For example, oversimplification and baby talk, as children learn by observation, are poor examples that can prevent children from acquiring language skills that enable them to engage with others to express feelings and opinions successfully, as well as to facilitate information processing to enable knowledge acquisition. A cultural model in which language acquisition uses imitation as a process of social learning is reported. Thus, having a disorder signifies a breakdown in this intergenerational transmission of language’s symbolic codes, where ‘baby talk’ constitutes a deficient cultural transmission method. This belief system within nested ecologies, that views the adult-child verbal interaction as an impoverished model, is deemed to be insufficient. Further compounded by pressures of being from a low socioeconomic status, which limit parental resources for other priorities. Finally, shaped by cultural norms that may not prioritise enriched linguistic exchange, the child’s language is not sufficiently stimulated. The perceived cause is thus an ecological transaction across systemic levels.

I would say that a child takes words that he/she hears from adults, so the more he/she hears, the more the child learns to speak. (CG- EK5) FG.

The FGs reveal a strong consensus that children’s language development is deeply shaped by the communication models available to them, with poor adult interaction emerging as a key social driver of CDs. Participants consistently linked imitation-based learning with structural disadvantages where power hierarchies, gender and age inequities, and socioeconomic hardship all constrain children’s opportunities for stimulation. These vulnerabilities intersect with cultural norms, including taboos and beliefs surrounding disability. These harmful practices, such as abuse, compound emotional and communicative harm. Addressing these socially rooted barriers requires systemic, equity-focused interventions that transform family environments, reduce structural barriers and expand developmental opportunities for all children.

Subtheme 2: Spiritual causes

The current participants held diverse spiritual beliefs, viewing people as spiritual beings, and they linked CDs to supernatural causes influenced by religious and cultural beliefs. The three subthemes that emerged in this section were Orthodox Christian (OCB), TBS and a combination of the two.

C1: Orthodox Christian beliefs

Those participants who subscribed to OCB believed in a supreme being, God, with supernatural powers. They contended that a child with disabilities was ‘a gift from God’, which is influenced by their understanding that such children, including those with CDs, should be cherished despite their challenges. This Christian-based view stems from the belief that God permitted the child to have disabilities, based on the theology of accessibility.

I told myself that many children could not hear, and God created them that way. (PPWD NZ) IDI.

We just think that maybe God created them like this (CG Q2) FG.

Some Christian participants who also upheld traditional beliefs contended that supernatural beings and ancestors are involved in the activities of the living, including their health.

We believe in different things as human beings, as you can see that some are Christians and do not believe in ancestors, while others do. (ECDP BB2) FG

I believe that God controls our lives, so we cannot blame the ancestors or any other person (ECDP EK3) FG.

It was really sad, and there is nothing we can do, but we had to accept what God has blessed us with (PPWD SN) IDI

Therefore, the current study participants described disability as divinely authored, framing CDs as sacred identities rather than biomedical deficits. These views, suggesting congruency between FG and IDI regarding a supernatural entity’s involvement. This belief, grounded in the macrosystem of Christian doctrine and ancestral entities, appears to directly shape family opinions by promoting acceptance while simultaneously justifying non-intervention. The interaction between structures such as schools and health services can create tension or challenge the integration of spiritual meaning with clinical interpretation. While this worldview can nurture compassion, it may also appear to deter timely therapeutic engagement when conditions are viewed as predestined. These divergent insights highlight the need for culturally sensitive health strategies that honour spiritual understandings while gently integrating evidence-based guidance to support more holistic and responsive care pathways. Integrating spiritual beliefs into educational efforts may help bridge gaps and encourage a more holistic approach to care and support.

C2: Traditional belief systems

Those participants who subscribe to TBS have a practice that permits married and unmarried men to have multiple female partners, with one CG attributing the cause of CDs to polygamy. This is believed not to be a simple sexual act but a spiritual relationship with psychological and physical effects. Polygamy is not understood merely as a social structure by these participants. Still, as a spiritually charged institution where intercourse is a cosmological act, it is a spiritual repercussion intrinsically inscribed on the unborn child. A CD thus becomes a symbolic manifestation of spiritual disharmony or transgression within this complex relational web. This belief is anchored in the concept of traditional cultural and spiritual norms that sanctify marriage practices. These norms directly shape the family dynamics and prenatal care. Their influence permeates, shaping how community networks interpret health outcomes and thereby constructing an ecological cause for developmental conditions.

Going back to what my sister was asking, I think polygamy plays a big role because having multiple partners can affect the pregnancy if one of the wives is pregnant. If we are doing sex, we are not only involved physically, but we become spiritually involved as well. Therefore, if you are having sex with one of your girlfriends, you may pass on whatever the other girl has that you’ve been involved with before you become involved with the one you’re involved with at that moment. It’s not that some children are just born with disabilities, some of it’s because of what I’ve just explained. In polygamy, it happens that you’d find that a child looks just like the other wife, who’s not her mother. The father can transfer genes from one woman to the other, and then those genes are transferred to the child. (CG EK3) FG.

In many isiZulu TSB families, males are regarded as leaders of the household, with fathers needing to acknowledge the paternity of a child to enable family acceptance and their denial being perceived as causing emotional distress, resulting in children having CDs. This belief stems from the TBS practice where the family of an unwed pregnant woman must inform the paternal family of the alleged father of the child. Then the father must acknowledge the child and take responsibility by paying ‘damages’ (fines) and conducting cleansing rituals as stated by the current participants. This reveals a cultural logic in which a CD is a morally understandable symbol of the embodied anger of wronged parents and ancestors. The child’s disability is a meaningful social construct. Thus, a supernatural sanction for a father’s social transgression (abandonment) is highly frowned on. Furthermore, the transgression occurs within family relations; its judgement is enforced by ancestral/spiritual oversight. This is also an entire causal narrative authored by the traditional cosmology that inseparably links the moral, social and biological realms.

The parents and ancestors will become angry because the guy gets a girl pregnant and has run away from his responsibilities, for example, paying for damages. The anger falls on the child, who is born with speech and hearing problems (ECDP S1) FG.

An ECDP from the cohort acknowledged the role of TBS and reported that a CD would manifest in a child if certain rituals were not performed to appease the ancestors. Such rituals include ‘imbeleko’, a ceremony to introduce and welcome the child to the living relatives and their ancestral spirits. To faciliate this ceremony the traditional practitioner (TP) can be consulted to facilitate the process. Some participants who believed in TSB were vocal about the integral role that rituals and ceremonies play in ensuring the health and safety of a new family member.

The findings from both the FG and IDI show divergent views on how CDs are interpreted through overlapping spiritual, cultural and gendered belief systems. OCB and TSB practices coexist to shape meaning and responses. CDs may be viewed as divinely bestowed identities or ancestral signs, promoting acceptance yet delaying intervention due to ritual expectations or fatalistic interpretations. Gendered power structures such as polygamy and paternity-related authority further influence how children’s needs are prioritised. FGs emphasised culturally grounded causal beliefs (eg, polygamy-related unpaid damages causing disability), largely absent in IDI. In contrast, individual parents foregrounded personal emotional experiences, characterising their journeys as ‘painful’ and ‘heartbreaking’, reflecting a shift from collective explanatory models to individualised narratives of distress. This multilayered cosmology underscores the necessity of culturally sensitive health strategies that respect spiritual frameworks, address misconceptions, enable families to integrate faith with evidence-based guidance and ensure holistic, equitable developmental support for children.

Theme 1: Identifying CD

The identifying CD subtheme was divided into two categories: observation and consultation.

C1: Observation

The current participants reported observing the children in their natural environment to determine whether they had reached their communication development milestones. They compared them with family members’ children, siblings and neighbours. The sampled ECDP used play observations while children interacted with their friends to identify those who were having difficulties and they reported their findings to the parents. Furthermore, a child’s failure to follow classroom instructions, social isolation, the use of gestures instead of speech and delayed developmental milestones (eg, not speaking by age three) are possible primary red flags as reported by the current participants.

I observed the child playing (ECDP S1) FG

The problem is that they are unable to say when they want to go to the toilet; sometimes they end up pooping on themselves. In some cases, they would go straight to the toilet without saying a word. Even when they are hungry, it is a problem because they cannot speak for themselves. This is especially problematic if the parent did not inform us of the child’s health condition. It becomes a problem to interact with the child since he is unable to speak. (ECDP BB1) FG.

A sampled parent of a person who is Deaf noted what they knew about developmental milestones, noting that their child did not attain speech-language milestones. Also, parents often first notice a child’s inability to respond to their name or to loud noises, such as music. Another parent in this study reported that, while observing the child, they would use their knowledge of developmental milestones and compare them with expectations to detect CDs.

If you called out his name, he wouldn’t respond until you touch or tap him. (PPWD SN) IDI.

I noticed when she was young that she could not talk. I would see that when I spoke to her, she would not respond. (PPWD SS) IDI.

It is sometimes said that you can only tell when the child is above six months that you notice that the child is supposed to be at this stage, but is not. One CG reported that, despite a lack of formal knowledge about CD, maternal instincts were used to observe and identify a problem. This practice is informed by a cultural belief that women are perceptive and rely on their intuition to detect problems, including those unrelated to CDs. A CG reported that they understood that not meeting developmental milestones could be related to traditional rituals not being performed, which could manifest in the child as physical activity difficulties and CDs.

…. because I have noticed that a woman can sense something is wrong… (CG EKJ1).FG.

I agree with what my sister was saying because I’ve noticed that it’s like a woman can sense when something is wrong. Once you’ve had sex with another person, you can feel when something is wrong. I once sensed that my partner was cheating on me, but then I’m a good person, so I just moved on. (CG EKJ3) FG.

There is congruence between FG and IDI regarding observations that constitute a form of local, embodied detection. Identifying a CD involves reading the child’s social text, their non-response to a name, inability to signal need or deviation from developmental milestones as signs of a disrupted cultural script for interaction. The practice within the child’s immediate daily interactions provides the primary evidence. The observational knowledge is then validated or challenged, for example, between home and the early childhood programme. Fundamentally, it is further shaped by the cultural templates for typical development. Not forgetting that gendered intuition assigned to mothers also forms an ecologically embedded assessment process.

C2: Consultation

The CG who upheld Christian beliefs reported consulting the ECDP to determine whether the child required intervention to confirm the suspicion of a CD or seeking advice from health professionals. In contrast, the ECDPs consult social workers as referral sources or as informants about where to seek intervention. The practice of consulting the ECDP or health professionals serves to confirm the initial suspicions of the presence of CDs.

I would ask mam [referring to ECDP supervisor] and seek therapy maybe’ (CG K1) FG.

You go to a social worker and inform them of your story; then, they will refer you to a speech therapist, where they will teach you. (ECDP NK1) FG.

One participant reported conducting an internet search to acquire knowledge to understand the problem better, while others have sought assistance from school educators for learners with disabilities. The parents of a person who is Deaf reported that the specific school placement was challenging and delayed due to late identification. Still, they did not specify how the parents identified the problem.

If there is anything that I do not understand, I Google it. (CG EKJ1) FG.

They usually get help when they get into school for that particular disability. There are different types of disabilities at different levels as well (ECDP EK4) FG.

It was very late when he got into (special needs) school because he only got in after the doctors assisted him in X hospital. (PPWD SN2) IDI.

From the FG and IDI, divergent methods are reported, with no clear agreement. These statements reveal a modern yet fragmented set of cultural methods for navigating disorders. A digital search (Google), formal referral networks and delayed institutional gateways constitute a complex, often inefficient help-seeking system. The late diagnosis underscores a disagreement between lived observation and clinical systems. Moreover, the CG’s actions are mediated by the weak links between home, health services and school. The consultation pathway is thus a transactional journey across poorly aligned ecological layers (figure 1). The fragmentation can be viewed as a contemporary form or an uneven landscape for interpreting disorder, underscoring the need for clearer, better-aligned consultation pathways that enable timely identification and coordinated support for children with CDs.

Subtheme 2: Acknowledging CDs

Once CGs identified a CD, they used various mechanisms to acknowledge its presence, which influenced their care-seeking behaviour. There are four subthemes being 1) wait-and-see, 2) fear of disclosure, 3) CDs not being a problem and family and community support.

C1: Wait and see

Despite being aware that her child could not speak for 4 years, the parent of a person who is Deaf thought that they had not fully developed and required additional time before intervention was sought. This is possibly informed by child-rearing practices influenced by family and community beliefs about child development rather than by knowledge. The participant’s hope that a child will ‘get better with age’ is a culturally patterned narrative of developmental delay. This pattern of reframing a potential disorder not as a fixed deficit but as a temporary lag within a natural growth process. The belief constitutes a possible form of optimism, rather than labelling. Furthermore, some were influenced by limited awareness, assuming the child was not responding as ‘ignoring’ or ‘naughty’ rather than presenting with a CD. This acknowledgement of family expectations and developmental observations, influenced by potential gaps in corrective feedback from health or educational services, ultimately shaped cultural models of childhood that normalise a wide range of developmental pacing.

Yes, but even in that case, we had hope that maybe it’s because he hasn’t fully developed, he’s going to get better with age. (PPWD, SS1) IDI.

Divergent perspectives emerged between FG participants and IDI respondents regarding the ‘wait-and-see’ approach. Participants’ accounts suggested that PPWD predominantly hoped their child would improve over time, despite evident developmental delays. In contrast, ECDPs reported that children with CDs often experience persistent challenges, with underlying causes typically becoming apparent only after professional assessment and intervention. These findings suggest that, at the microsystem level, parental ‘wait-and-see’ practice, characterised by hope for spontaneous improvement despite observable delays, contrasts with perspectives at the professional level of ECDPs. The professionals appear to be operating within broader mesosystem and exosystem influences. Identifying communication difficulties as persistent conditions whose underlying causes typically emerge through formal assessment and intervention.

C2: Fear of disclosure

Some ECDPs reported that parents ‘do not disclose’ the child’s condition, as they have not accepted that there is a problem or they fear that their child will be treated differently. After observing the presence of CD, ECDP also engages parents to determine whether they know the child has a challenge and to ask why they did not disclose the condition. After the confrontation, an explanation related to fear of disclosure is provided, as reflected by the ECDP. There is a tendency for CGs not to disclose existing medical conditions due to fear of discrimination or the child being prevented from attending the ECD. The sources indicate that a fear of disclosing disabilities is a significant barrier to early identification and intervention for children with CDs. Concerns about discrimination, social stigma, and parental denial primarily drive this fear. The responses can be part of the second and third sphere (figure 1).

Sometimes you will find that a parent will bring a child to the crèche and not say anything about his condition. It is only when you ask the parent about the condition of the child that they start explaining that the child has been in and out of hospitals for this condition. Sometimes, the kids will first see a problem with that child. (ECDP B1) FG.

It was very painful, but I had to accept it, especially when she was in school. She is well and healthy, just like the other kids; it’s just that she can't speak(PPWD SS) IDI.

There is a divergence of views between FG and IDI, suggesting a cultural nuance of stigma and selective disclosure. The child’s condition is hidden, only disclosed on direct inquiry. The FG highlighted educators’ concerns about non-disclosure, framing parents as ‘in denial’ or ‘negligence.’ Conversely, IDI reframed silence as a structural constraint, with parents citing ‘powerlessness,’ financial barriers, and fear of bullying, shifting interpretation from perceived neglect to context-driven protective decisions. Thus, early detection underscores the disorder as a socially understandable reality before it is formally acknowledged. The non-disclosure can be seen as a lack of proactive communication from the family to the early childhood programme, creating an ecological gap. Therefore, while peer interactions within the classroom provide immediate detection, unofficial diagnosis is a reality. The lack of disclosure gap is sustained by norms that may associate disability with shame or failure.

C3: CDs are not a problem

Some ECDP reported that CGs deny that CDs are a problem and justify the disorder by attributing it to heredity. The family would defend against CDs by stating that it was not unexpected, as other family members had a similar condition. The family guinely believed the child would outgrow the condition, for example, a lisp or stutter. This understanding stems from the belief that some conditions ‘run in the family’, making them acceptable in certain families. Two CGs reported that acceptance of the hereditary presence of CDs within families prevented professional identification because they were not seeking medical advice and intervention. This reveals a cultural logic of normalisation. The presence of a CD is not interpreted as a clinical problem but is narratively assimilated into family lineage, a hereditary trait or a benign developmental pattern. This constitutes a causation that defuses pathology by embedding it within kinship history. Moreover, this belief in family stories and genetic attributions, reinforced by extended family comparisons, is fundamentally shaped by cultural norms that may prioritise familial acceptance and continuity over medical intervention. The disorder is thus ecologically framed as a familial condition rather than a clinical one. This can be situated

One of the things that you would find as a teacher is that sometimes a child does not develop according to their age, as I have had the experience earlier, and then when you talk to the parent, they would tell you that it runs in the family. In some instances, a child would start to show some developments at the age of four or five. (ECDP B3) FG.

FG and IDI converged in framing CDs as ‘not a problem’, underpinned by spiritual, familial and developmental narratives. While FG reflected community-level endorsement of these beliefs, IDI revealed parents’ lived reliance on them. Across both, biomedical interpretations were deprioritised, with social functioning and happiness outweighing clinical labelling, reinforcing a shared cultural logic that situates CDs within acceptable variations of normality.

Subtheme 3: Help-seeking

The cultural practices associated with seeking care for children with CD led CG egivers to first access services from health professionals at clinics or TPs, and then from health professionals if the condition did not improve.

C1: Seek medical care first

Consulting health professionals was first influenced by the participant’s belief that the condition was of medical rather than spiritual origin. The first health professional consulted was at their local primary healthcare clinic, where nurses and doctors referred them to higher levels of care to access the necessary services. The practice of consulting health professionals first was influenced by their religious belief that all children are a gift from God, irrespective of their disabilities. These statements convey a biomedical undertone, in which the clinic and hospital are spaces of institutional authority. The hearing aid becomes an endpoint of a diagnostic quest. The decision originates in the family’s successful navigation of the link between home and clinical services. In addition, it is enabled by the available public health infrastructure. This represents a transactional process where ecological access facilitates a clinical rather than traditional or spiritual interpretation and treatment of the disorder. CGs and ECDPs further reported fear of long clinic queues associated with unpleasant experiences at public facilities, and the high cost of private care or transport often leads parents to postpone seeking help until a crisis occurs. The participants responses demonstrate a bidirectional move between spheres (figure 1)

I go to the clinic and see the nurse (EK3, CG) FG.

They can find it [help] in the clinics and at the hospital (ECDP FG 2) FG.

Her dad suggested we take her to Y Hospital. At the Hospital, they kept examining her until they discovered that one ear could hear but not too much, and the other could not hear at all. We were up and down until they gave her a machine [hearing aid]. (PPWD SS) IDI

The service at the clinic is bad now, and this thing of waking up very early at 4 am. There was this other time when I got to the clinic and an old lady was standing alone holding her umbrella, and it was raining. (CG K2) FG

FG and ID converged on practical barriers to medical care, including a tiered clinic-to-hospital pathway, delays driven by ‘wait and see’ attitudes and systemic deterrents such as long queues, poor service and financial strain. Furthermore, both datasets situated care-seeking within pluralistic belief systems: while medical intervention addresses physical symptoms, spiritual frameworks that view the child as a ‘gift from God’ shape meaning, acceptance and resilience. Thus, medical engagement is not rejected but integrated with religious and cultural logics, balancing clinical action with spiritual stewardship.

C2: First, consult traditional care

Those who subscribed to TSB and some with combined beliefs consulted the supernatural through TPs or spiritual leader first. Participants who ascribed to TSB believed that they performed rituals to appease the ancestors through animal offerings or cleansing, including for CD. A few participants first consulted traditional spiritual leaders, such as pastors, diviners, prophets and healers, who would conduct services and prayers for the child or provide formulations with healing powers to be prayed over. Their justification was that CD’s existence in the child’s life, as God’s will, may result in CGs hesitating to seek medical intervention, the family first consulting religious leaders and waiting for God to intervene before health professionals are sought.

Other CGs believed that demonstrating their acceptance of the condition and their faith in the Divine by adhering to the TP remedies can remediate the CD. However, should this not address the condition, they augmented its management by seeking medical intervention through the local clinic’s services (figure 1). Consulting a TP can be understood as a culturally prescribed ritual to decode a disorder’s hidden spiritual or ancestral meaning, positioning it within a traditional cosmology rather than a biomedical framework. This help-seeking pathway is a performance of deep cultural logic. Moreover, this practice is anchored in the indigenous belief systems. This worldview directly shapes the family’s initial response, guiding them toward traditional healers within their community before, or instead of, engaging with formal clinical services. The choice reflects an ecologically coherent navigation of available and culturally sanctioned support systems.

Some go see the sangoma or inyanga to get help with traditional stuff… (ECDP S4) FG.

We took her to church to be prayed for because we believe in prayer (CG EKJ3) FG

The findings show that these selected CGs interpretations of CDs emerge at the intersection of gendered expectations, cultural beliefs, and socioeconomic realities, producing both shared and divergent pathways to detection and care. Strong agreement across datasets highlights maternal and culturally grounded observational practices in early identification. However, help-seeking diverges due to religious, economic, and epistemic differences. Systemic barriers such as costs, queues and distance favour traditional and religious pathways, with FGs emphasising ritual prerequisites and positioning ‘spiritual clearance’ as essential before biomedical care within a pluralistic healing framework.

Discussion

This study explored the beliefs of a group of CGs about the causes and practices related to CDs in ECDs in Durban, KZN, SA. Through the lens of Urie Bronfenbrenner’s EST, the analysis reveals that CDs are not perceived merely as intrinsic biomedical deficits but as culturally and religiously meaningful symbols inscribed by social, spiritual and moral realities across nested environmental systems. Data on factors influencing service uptake are scarce in poor socioeconomic settings, where people face complex challenges in accessing specialised healthcare. This necessitated understanding CDs using the EST as a lens.

The EST provides a comprehensive framework for understanding CDs shaped by dynamic interactions across multiple environmental layers. This analysis integrates qualitative research and literature findings to explore the role of family, community and cultural systems in CD identification, development and management, emphasising the importance of intersectionality. This model explains how personal characteristics intersect with systems and structures to shape people’s experiences.56 The five spheres represent the various levels of influence, potential sources of marginalisation and their interlinkages (figure 1). The inner sphere is a child with a disability, such as a CD, who belongs to a family (second sphere) that forms part of a community (third sphere) with different structures, beliefs and practices. Each child has individual factors, such as race and gender, that are associated with the family’s social status.40,55 These parents’ education level and belief system align with specific behaviour in the arena of family and community. The fourth kind of sphere comprises organisations such as churches, ECDCs and healthcare services that operate within the social, economic and political spheres; the fifth sphere and legal provisions define, for example, which services exist.56 The model contends that an individual’s health help-seeking behaviour is influenced by these factors, which also interact with one another.

Family environments as determinants

Family environments (Microsystem) are important determinants of communication development, as CGs model early language interactions. At the microsystemic level, relational disruptions, due to abuse and neglect, are perceived to be actualised as protective silence or unorganised communication, placing the symptoms of the child as adaptive responses to a hostile environment. Furthermore, results from this research reveal that factors such as poor language models and unconducive settings hinder effective interaction and lead to CDs.57 The result supports Sylvestre’s et al and Mothupi et al 56,58 suggestion that language development is susceptible to socioeconomic factors, exacerbated by parental practices that do not foster language stimulation (see figure 1). These factors inhibit emotional security and foster social withdrawal, reinforcing communication difficulties, as reported by participants. Social withdrawal, a product of abuse and neglect, exacerbates the isolation of children with CDs, further diminishing their educational and social opportunities.57 The current findings on poor social determinants are further supported by Mkabile and Swartz’s 59 findings, though their study focused on IDs; the poor home environment sentiments can be generalised to cause CDs. The intersectionality of beliefs about CDs, shaped by abuse, neglect and family power dynamics, perpetuates marginalisation worsened by disrupted family interactions, which hinder communication. At the same time, societal stigma and systemic inequalities at broader levels exacerbate isolation, necessitating culturally sensitive, multi-level interventions.

In the SA context, social factors have been consistently identified to contribute to causing disabilities and should be affirmed. Therefore, there are similarities in how CDs relate to social factors. This was supported by Sylvestre et al 56 who found that speech-language development in neglected children tends to be less developed than in their non-neglected counterparts, which the current participants allude to. Intersectional factors, such as gender and age within family power dynamics, further exacerbate barriers, as evidenced by data revealing how fear of abuse prevents children from speaking out.

Family and support system interactions

The family support interaction (mesosystem) represents connections to external systems, such as schools, healthcare providers and spiritual leaders. Based on spiritual etiologies such as OCB, assuming disability to be a gift from God and TBS, which ascribe the occurrence of disorders to the anger of ancestors or transgressions such as incest, polygamy, reshape the concept of CDs as spiritual events. This layer illustrates the intersection of traditional and modern healthcare systems, where cultural norms and systemic accessibility shape help-seeking behaviours (see figure 1). Similar findings were reported by Mabetha et al,2 who noted that CGs’ HSB adversely impact child survival and their assertions can thus be generalised to CDs. The critical link between home, school and health services is often fragmented, characterised by weak ties that hinder the flow of information and timely referral. Consequently, help-seeking manifests as a fragmented cultural anchor that shapes navigating between digital searches (eg, Google), traditional healers and clinical gateways within a resource-constrained system. Sadiki12 highlights the importance of coordinated networks among CGs and the healthcare system in promoting timely identification and intervention for children with disabilities, which is crucial for successful outcomes. Complex practices related to identifying, acknowledging and seeking help for CDs further illustrate this ecological embeddedness. The complex practice underscores that disability experiences are shaped by interacting factors at individual, interpersonal, community and systemic levels, reinforcing the idea that identification and help-seeking are ecologically embedded rather than isolated acts.55,60

However, fear, discrimination and the lack of medical facilities hinder timely assistance. Mbatha and Mokwena60 observed that due to such barriers, the stress levels of CGs of children with disability increase, as confirmed in the current study. Likewise, Cooke et al 16 found an increase in CG stress, which impacted HSB, though the study sample was mainly white and middle-class. One possible explanation of the stress10 is financial constraints, high transport costs, a higher risk of poverty, rejection, a lack of support from extended family and inaccessible healthcare resources. The stress causes demonstrate the cross-cutting effect of discrimination as a cause of disability60. Such a comparison indicates that social class interacts with limited healthcare access, aggravating problems and affecting the effectiveness of interventions 58. This micro+meso+macrosystemic transaction of ecological communication guides the help-seeking behaviours, establishing lines that can be friendly to either traditional mediation or clinical care, and thus may lead to delayed intervention.

Community and structural barriers

At the community level (exosystem level) and at the structural barrier level, community resources and broader societal structures significantly impact CD outcomes (see figure 1). Structural inequities, such as long wait times, inadequate service delivery at health facilities, and limited community awareness, hinder effective CD management. These challenges, also noted by Naidoo and Khan18 and Mbanjwa and Harvey,10 who identified barriers to the uptake of interventions in their studies on hearing loss in newborns and disability. The current study participants echoed similar assertions.

This research highlights the influential role of TPs and spiritual leaders in shaping family decisions, reflecting the intersection of cultural beliefs with healthcare systems. Spiritual beliefs, such as viewing CDs as ‘gifts from God’, illustrate how community norms influence and shape responses regarding CDs, as faith sustained them through difficult situations, as echoed by Torres and Ohanjunwa.17 Furthermore, spirituality was central to improving the quality of life and CG health.17 This practice is similar to Mkabile et al’s13 assertion that disabilities are ‘God’s will’.13 To cope with the diagnosis, the practice of consulting religious leaders and organisations, such as churches, for deliverance prayers is similar to the current study.13 In line with authors such as Mabetha2, this study also found that supernatural factors were cited as causes of CDs.

However, a unique finding of this study is that participants maintain OCB and TBS in depicting pluralistic and diverse belief systems for understanding CDs, as reported by Moshabela et al4 regarding changes in healing practices, which they advocate for tolerance for plurality in health-seeking practices. Duma61 explained that, based on parents’ belief systems, they use available healthcare avenues to seek intervention. However, the unique aspects introduced by the current study participants, is polygamy and incest, voiced by CGs who believe in TBS, are not comparable with other studies to the knowledge of the researchers. The literature corroborates these findings, emphasising the importance of culturally sensitive approaches for effective intervention.12 The present study found that denial of paternity was associated with childhood disability.

In contrast, Duma61 linked paternal denial to blame, rejection and separation from the paternal family. Similarly, Torres and Ohajunwa17 associated these dynamics with abandonment and loneliness among single mothers caring for children with disabilities. Mbanjwa and Harvey10 further attributed parental separation to fathers withdrawing on learning of the child’s disability, often leaving mothers experiencing anger and resentment. However, in the current study, parental separation was more strongly associated with perceptions of familial or ancestral anger, suggesting a distinct sociocultural explanatory framework.

Cultural beliefs and practices

The cultural beliefs and practices (macrosystem) explore the impact of overarching cultural and societal beliefs on understanding CDs. Data reveal a nuanced interplay between OCB and TBS, where ancestral influences and divine interpretations coexist. This duality reflects broader cultural attitudes towards disability, shaping family responses and intervention strategies. This is not unique to CDs but has been reported in other health domains.62

Intersectionality is particularly evident here, as cultural, spiritual and socioeconomic factors collectively influence help-seeking behaviour and resource access (figure 1). Unlike previous studies, which primarily focused on hearing impairment and emphasised hearing loss, causation and intervention beliefs,63 this study highlights the complex interaction between belief systems and intervention practices for CDs. These results lend support to Duma61 participants who reported that culture and religion are important in proactive seeking of intervention, as supported by the current study’s code ‘active parent’.58 Furthermore, Mkabile and Swartz 59 emphasised that religion and beliefs are deeply rooted in cultural contexts, urging health professionals to remain sensitive to these perspectives as they influence parenting practices.59 Building on these insights, this study’s results support the idea that the intersection of beliefs informs health-seeking practices, offering a more comprehensive understanding of their influence on CD interventions.

Temporal dimensions and change

The temporal dimensions and change (chronosystem) deal with the issues practised across generations and cultural changes. The current study practices were divided into three: identifying CDs, then acknowledging them and seeking help. These actions were seen within the context of causes that were both spiritual and physical (medical), similar to Mkabile 13 Identifying and acknowledging that a child has CD is essential for help-seeking, with people’s understanding of what causes a health condition inevitably affecting whom they consult. These results lend support to Mbanjwa and Harvey,10 who found that eight of their participants resolved to accept the child’s disability over time, as motherhood changed them. In some families where other members also had similar CD (eg, stutter) or other disabilities, it was not viewed as problematic but rather usual due to its existence in the family, impacting intervention patterns. The current study findings contradict Duma,61 where some families struggle to accept the child with a disability diagnosis as they speculate that beliefs do not bring relief but rather are additional stress, such as mocking and taunting of children.

Consequently, the experience of introducing modern healthcare into traditional environments demonstrates not only flexibility but also cultural relevance. This highlights the interconnectedness of the many factors that affect and complicate access to treatment; the spiritual beliefs around the causes often influence the methods sought.9 This intersects with their socioeconomic circumstances, culture and treatment options, influencing their choices.64 It also demonstrates the complexity and interconnection between the many factors that influence health practice behaviours.

Study limitations

Several limitations may have affected this study, including the small CGs sample and the fact that the results represent only a minority population in KZN Province34. While SA is home to several ethnic groups and subcultures, only Africans from the urban PINK area who belong to the Zulu clan were included; thus, its generalisability is limited. This means that CGs opinions from other ethnic groups and geographic settings must be investigated, including issues related to access to ECD and healthcare services, given availability and proximity38. Furthermore, some details about the biographical (eg, siblings and other family members with disabilities) could not be collected at the time of data collection, which is acknowledged to impact the awareness, beliefs, and practices that would be shared by participants.

Recommendations

This study offers several key recommendations. First, as part of their training, health professionals must recognise that families can seek their preferred services, including traditional healers. While such preferences may lead to perceptions of delays in seeking intervention, they should not be viewed as a lack of adherence to follow-up appointments. Instead, healthcare providers should focus on providing accurate information, emphasising timely services and offering non-judgemental support to empower families to make informed decisions that enhance the child’s well-being. Understanding the diverse factors influencing perceived reluctance to seek intervention is essential.

Extensive health education on the causes of disabilities, including CDs and the services available, is necessary. This education should incorporate biomedical and culturally sensitive nonmedical explanations, emphasising that while families are entitled to their interpretations and preferred practitioners, testing for physical causes is also necessary. Health professionals should reassure families that acknowledging medical causes does not dismiss their cultural or spiritual understandings of disability. Clear communication is essential to convey that diagnosis is the first step toward choosing interventions with long-term benefits supported by accessible, judgement-free services. Health messaging must align with the WHO’s (2016) definition of health, which includes physical, mental and social well-being rather than merely the absence of disease.

Individual, family and community belief systems must be considered when designing intervention programmes, as they influence service acceptance. Collaboration between healthcare professionals and cultural or religious leaders is vital to providing information that fosters cooperative care pathways. Training for healthcare professionals should address the interconnectedness of cultural attitudes and religious beliefs shaping health conceptualisations. Promoting cultural tolerance during training can enhance service uptake and adherence, improving outcomes.

Finally, ensuring that all children, regardless of disability status, have opportunities to meet their developmental milestones is crucial. This approach enables meaningful engagement with their environment and prepares them for a fulfilling future. Acknowledging and respecting diverse religious and cultural beliefs in this process ensures equitable and inclusive care for all children.

Conclusions

This EST perspective highlights that CDs emerge and persist within interconnected systems influenced by family dynamics, community resources and cultural beliefs. Demonstrated points of marginalisation based on beliefs, effective management of CDs requires a holistic approach that addresses systemic inequities while respecting cultural differences. This study’s integrative analysis underscores that beliefs and practices are not isolated but are produced through continuous interaction across ecological layers. Effective intervention, therefore, requires a holistic, multisystemic approach that addresses relational safety in the microsystem, strengthens collaborative networks in the mesosystem, and respectfully engages with the spiritual and cultural norms of the macrosystem to promote equitable, timely and culturally consonant support for children with CDs and their families.

Future interventions should focus on leveraging community-based networks, prioritising CG education and incorporating culturally sensitive practices to remove barriers across all ecological levels. Such an approach can promote equitable outcomes for children with CDs and their families, fostering meaningful, sustainable change across diverse communities.
