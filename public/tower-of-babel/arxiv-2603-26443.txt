Abstract
. In analogy with the spectral theory of geometrically finite hyperbolic manifolds, we initiate the study of resonances on geometrically finite ( q + 1 ) (q+1) -regular graphs of groups. We prove the meromorphic continuation of the resolvent of the adjacency operator on such spaces and give a geometric characterization of the resonant states. In contrast to the hyperbolic surfaces setting, geometrically finite graphs have only finitely many resonances and may be computed explicitly, yet exhibit many of the same qualitative phenomena as in the hyperbolic manifolds setting. Particularly interesting examples arise from algebraic curves over finite fields.



1.
Introduction

Resonances
of the Laplacian, also called quantum resonances, are a well established spectral invariant on certain classes of noncompact Riemannian manifolds. On closed manifolds, the Laplacian has discrete
L^{2}
-spectrum; resonances, which are also discrete, serve as a suitable replacement for the failure of this fact to remain true in the setting of infinite volume. A prototypical and yet extremely fruitful setting is that of
geometrically finite
hyperbolic surfaces, where the study of resonances dates back to Patterson
[
40
,
38
,
39
]
. We refer to
[
24
,
48
,
8
,
10
,
32
]
for a small selection of papers studying resonances on geometrically finite, or more particularly, convex cocompact hyperbolic surfaces.

Over the past decades, the study of the spectral geometry of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on hyperbolic surfaces has been paralleled by the spectral theory of the adjacency operator on
(q+1)
-regular graphs. From an algebraic perspective, there is already an evident parallel by viewing hyperbolic surfaces as the locally symmetric space of the group
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
, and viewing
(p+1)
-regular graphs as the “locally symmetric spaces” of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Q}_{p})
(via the Bruhat-Tits tree). Additionally, there is by now a long tradition of developing ideas in one setting and subsequently adapting them to the other setting. A notable example of great interest is optimal spectral gaps on random regular graphs and hyperbolic surfaces. Friedman
[
20
]
proved that a random
(q+1)
-regular graph asymptotically almost surely has adjacency operator spectral gap below
2\sqrt{q}+\varepsilon
for every
\varepsilon>0
as the number of vertices goes to infinity; this had been previously conjectured by Alon
[
1
]
. A new proof was later given by Bordenave-Collins
[
7
]
using the notion of strong convergence. These papers laid a template for tackling the analogous question of optimal Laplacian spectral gap
\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon
for random hyperbolic surfaces, which was subsequently proven by Hide-Magee
[
26
]
and Magee-Puder-van Handel
[
33
]
in the setting of random covers of hyperbolic surfaces, and Anantharaman-Monk
[
4
,
2
,
3
]
(inspired by Friedman’s approach) and Hide-Macera-Thomas
[
25
]
(inspired by the approach of Bordenave-Collins) in the Weil-Petersson model of random hyperbolic surfaces. See also the recent survey article by Monk-Naud
[
36
]
.

The aim of this article is to establish a notion of resonances for the adjacency operator on a certain class of infinite graphs which are the “right” analogues of geometrically finite manifolds in the setting of graphs. In fact, the objects we study must be properly understood not as graphs in the usual sense, but as
graphs of groups
. We defer a detailed discussion of the precise definition of geometrically finite graphs (and the justification for why they deserve to be named as such) to Sections
2
and
3
. However, for now we state that, analogously to the setting of hyperbolic surfaces, geometrically finite graphs are those which can be decomposed into finitely many pieces, one of which is a finite graph called the
compact core
, which we denote by
\mathcal{L}
, and the rest of which are either
(orbifold) funnels
or
cusps
. We say that such a graph of groups is
asymptotically
(q+1)
-regular
if it is
(q+1)
-regular outside of a compact set.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}

-regular graph of groups with vertex set

. Then the resolvent

.

Let

\displaystyle\textnormal{grst}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{0})=\{u\textnormal{ purely outgoing for which }\exists\ J\in\mathbb{N}\textnormal{ s.t. }\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\Big)^{J}u=0\}.

If instead

\displaystyle\textnormal{grst}(\mathcal{G},\mu_{0})=\{u\textnormal{ purely outgoing of constant type s.t. }\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\Big)u=0\}.

In contrast to the setting of hyperbolic manifolds, geometrically finite
(q+1)
-regular graphs of groups have only finitely many resonances.

Theorem 1.3
.

The number of resonances in finite. More specifically:

(1)

The number of resonances, counted without multiplicity, is bounded by
2|\mathcal{L}|
, where
|\mathcal{L}|
is the number of vertices in the compact core.

(2)

The multiplicity of a given resonance is bounded by
3(|\mathcal{L}|+n_{c}+(q+1)n_{f})^{2}
, where
n_{c}
and
n_{f}
are the number of cusps and funnels, respectively.

The bound on the number of resonances without multiplicity is essentially sharp. On the other hand, the bound on the multiplicity is likely far from sharp; in this work we simply sought to get an explicit bound. Generically we should expect resonances to have multiplicity one. Theorem
1.3
is proven by combining Proposition
5.2
(where a slightly sharper bound is given than in (2) above) and Corollary

.

It is a consequence of our results that resonances can be computed explicitly via reduction to a finite dimensional linear algebra problem. In Section
7
we compute resonances explicitly for several example. A particularly interesting class of examples are constructed via algebraic curves over finite fields; we summarize this construction in Section
3.4
. In Section
7.7
, we compute the resonances explicitly for two examples constructed from elliptic curves. In particular, we observe that the resonances correspond to the “trivial”
\ell^{2}
-eigenvalues (
\pm\sqrt{q}
), the remaining
\ell^{2}
-eigenvalues (which are known to all lie on the unit circle as a result of Drinfeld’s proof of the Langlands correspondence and Petersson conjecture for
\textnormal{GL}(2)
over function fields
[
16
,
17
]
), the square root of the zeros of the Hasse-Weil zeta function (which are known to all lie on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
as a result of Weil’s proof of the Riemann hypothesis in this setting
[
47
]
), and additional “topological” resonances at the special values
\pm 1
. We discuss this further in Section
7.8
.

In the setting of geometrically finite hyperbolic manifolds, the theory of resonances is very closely connected with the Lax-Phillips scattering theory
[
29
]
. Roughly speaking, the resonances correspond to the poles of the scattering operator. The Lax-Phillips scattering theory is itself defined in terms of the (automorphic) wave equation, and in general the resonances control the long term behavior of waves on the manifold. In follow-up work, we plan to connect the theory of resonances on geometrically finite graphs developed in this paper with the wave equation on regular trees and graphs
[
13
,
12
,
5
]
, and in particular with the Lax-Phillips scattering theory in this setting studied by the second author in
[
42
]
. This should in turn be very closely related to the analogue of Eisenstein series in this setting which we plan to also develop; this should also allow us to rigorously describe in representation theoretic terms the structure of the resonances for arithmetic examples mentioned in the previous paragraph.
More generally, there is a plethora of natural follow-up questions by analogy with the manifolds setting, such as the connections to Ruelle resonances of the geodesic flow (see Hilgert’s recent review article, where the missing notion of resonances for the averaging operator on infinite graphs was marked as an important open question
[
27
, Problem 6.31]
).
Such a quantum-classical correspondence should lead to a connection between resonances and zeros of zeta functions (in analogy to the correspondence with zeros of the Selberg zeta function obtained in
[
8
]
for geometrically finite hyperbolic surfaces).
Furthermore, the distribution of resonances on large random geometrically finite graphs can be expected to have interesting properties (see Section
7.9
for some numerical experiments in this direction which show interesting phenomena). In addition to its intrinsic interest, we hope that this setting may act as a testing ground for existing conjectures and new approaches to the study of resonances on geometrically finite hyperbolic manifolds.

Let us briefly outline the article. In Section
2
we discuss graphs of groups and give the precise definition of geometric finiteness in this setting. In Section
3
we give further justification for why the class of objects we study is the natural analogue of geometric finiteness in this setting; this section is primarily expository and not strictly necessary for the later sections. In this section we also discuss a particularly interesting class of examples of geometrically finite graphs which arise from algebraic curves over finite fields. In Section
4
we study the resolvent on our model geometries, i.e. on funnels and cusps. In Section
5
we use these results to prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
6
, we study generalized resonant states and prove Theorem
1.2
. Finally, in Section
7
, we discuss the explicit computation of resonances and provide many examples, including several arithmetic examples whose resonance structure has deep connections to arithmetic.

2.
Geometrically finite graphs of groups: definitions

2.1.
Graphs of groups

In order to properly discuss our results, we must utilize the language of
graph of groups
, which lies in the domain of
Bass-Serre theory
. The standard reference for this theory is Serre
[
43
]
. When a group
\Gamma
acts on a tree
\mathcal{T}
, we want to construct a quotient “graph”
\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{T}
which will allow us to in turn recover
\Gamma
. In case
\Gamma
acts freely on
\mathcal{T}
, then we can make sense of
\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{T}
as a graph in the usual sense, and we can recover
\Gamma
from the quotient via the usual covering space theory. We shall however be particularly interested in the case that
\Gamma
does not act freely.

Arguably the most important example of a discrete subgroup of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is
\Gamma=\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. However, seeing as this group has torsion elements, the quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
has orbifold points. We naturally would want to view
\Gamma
as an example of a geometrically finite group, but this would require in general working with orbifolds rather than manifolds. On the other hand, the Selberg lemma tells us that
\Gamma
(and more generally any finitely generated subgroup of a linear group over a field of characteristic zero) has a finite index torsionfree subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}
. Consequently
\Gamma^{\prime}\backslash\mathbb{H}
is a genuine manifold. In light of the Selberg lemma, we see that in the manifold setting, there is essentially no need to work with orbifolds since, up to finite (orbifold) covering (which from a spectral theoretic perspective is easy to handle), we can always assume we are working with a genuine manifold.

In the setting of discrete subgroups of the isometry group of the tree there is no analogue of the Selberg lemma. In fact, torsion is an essential part of the theory and, in particular, the function field theoretic analogue of the modular curve, which is the discrete subgroup
\Gamma=\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}[T^{-1}])
inside of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}((T)))
, contains no finite index subgroup which is torsionfree; see Section
3.4
for further discussion of this example. Note that
\Gamma
naturally acts on the Bruhat-Tits tree of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}((T)))
, which is a
(q+1)
-regular tree
\mathcal{T}
, but we cannot (and should not) interpret the quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathcal{T}
as a graph in the usual sense because we are then losing crucial torsion information about
\Gamma
.

We now give the definition of a graph of groups. Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a graph consisting of a set of vertices
\mathcal{V}
and edges
\mathcal{E}
. We shall allow for the possibility of loops and multiple edges. Let
\vec{\mathcal{E}}
denote the set of oriented edges; note that we treat each loop as having two distinct orientations. An oriented edge
\overrightarrow{e}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}
gives rise to a tuple
(v_{1},v_{2})
of vertices consisting of an
origin

o(\overrightarrow{e})=v_{1}
and a
terminus

t(\overrightarrow{e})=v_{2}
of the oriented edge. Let
\{G_{v}\}_{v\in\mathcal{V}}
and
\{G_{e}\}_{e\in\mathcal{E}}
be collections of groups; these are called the
vertex groups
and
edge groups
, respectively. Suppose for each oriented edge
\overrightarrow{e}
with terminus
t(\overrightarrow{e})
we have a monomorphism
\phi_{\overrightarrow{e}}:G_{e}\hookrightarrow G_{t(\overrightarrow{e})}
. The ensemble of data
(\mathcal{G},\{G_{v}\},\{G_{e}\},\{\phi_{\overrightarrow{e}}\})
is called a
graph of groups
; we shall often abuse notation and simply refer to
\mathcal{G}
as a graph of groups.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a group acting on a tree
\mathcal{T}
without edge inversions. From this we may then naturally form a corresponding
quotient graph of groups

\Gamma\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
. Vertices
\mathcal{V}
and edges
\mathcal{E}
correspond to orbits of vertices and edges, respectively, of
\mathcal{T}
under
\Gamma
. All vertices (resp. edges) in the same orbit have conjugate (and thus isomorphic) stabilizer subgroups. Thus for each vertex
v\in\mathcal{V}
, we set
G_{v}
as the stabilizer of any lift of
v\in\mathcal{T}
, and similarly for the edge subgroups (viewed as abstract groups). Finally, given an oriented edge
\overrightarrow{f}
in
\mathcal{T}
with terminus
w
, we naturally have the stabilizer of
f
, denoted
\Gamma_{f}
, as a subgroup of the stabilizer of
w
, denoted
\Gamma_{w}
. This provides a monomorphism
\phi_{\overrightarrow{f}}:\Gamma_{f}\hookrightarrow\Gamma_{w}
abstractly as groups; any other choice of
\overrightarrow{f}
in the same orbit would give rise to the same monomorphism. Thus for the directed edge
\overrightarrow{e}
in
\mathcal{E}
which
\overrightarrow{f}
projects to, we define
\phi_{\overrightarrow{e}}\coloneqq\phi_{\overrightarrow{f}}
.

The fundamental theorem of Bass-Serre theory says that there is a natural one-to-one correspondence between groups acting on trees without inversion (and up to conjugacy by the automorphism group of the tree), and graphs of groups. The construction in the previous paragraph provides a map from the former set to the latter. The map going the other way involves the construction of the
fundamental group of a graph of groups
. However, we will not have need for this construction, so we do not describe it further here. We remark that the quotient graph of groups
\Gamma\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
contains complete information about the
\Gamma
action on
\mathcal{T}
. Under this correspondence, given a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
, we refer to the corresponding tree
\mathcal{T}
as the
Bass-Serre tree
of
\mathcal{G}
, and corresponding group
\Gamma
as the
fundamental group of
\mathcal{G}
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph of groups, and let
\mathcal{T}
and
\Gamma<\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
be the corresponding Bass-Serre tree and fundamental group. In case
\mathcal{G}
is such that
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{E}
are countable, and all vertex and edge groups are finite, then
\mathcal{T}
is locally finite, and
\Gamma
is discrete (in fact, the converse is also true). From now on we shall only be interested in this case, and these conditions shall be assumed without further mention. We define the
degree
of
v\in\mathcal{V}
as the degree of any its lifts in
\mathcal{T}
. This can be readily read off from the data of
\mathcal{G}
:

\displaystyle\textnormal{deg}(v):=\sum_{\overrightarrow{e}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}:t(\overrightarrow{e})=v}\frac{|G_{v}|}{|G_{e}|}.

(2.1)

We shall be particularly interested in
(q+1)
-regular graphs of groups in the sense that every vertex has degree
q+1
; this is equivalent to
\mathcal{T}
being an infinite
(q+1)
-regular tree.

The vertices of
\mathcal{T}
contain a natural measure on the vertices just assigning mass one to each vertex. The natural quotient measure
\nu
on
\mathcal{V}
is defined as
\nu(v)=\frac{1}{|G_{v}|}
. This can be explained as follows. If
G_{v}=1
, then elements in the orbit of
\Gamma
of any lift of
v
in
\mathcal{T}
are in bijection with elements in
\Gamma
. However, more generally elements in the orbit of any lift
\tilde{w}
of a vertex
w\in\mathcal{V}
are in bijection with cosets of
G_{w}=\Gamma_{\tilde{w}}
inside of
\Gamma
which is of “size”
\frac{1}{|G_{w}|}
relative to
\Gamma
. Thus we should have that
\nu(w)=\frac{1}{|G_{w}|}
.

2.2.
Geometrically finite graphs of groups

We are now ready to define the class of graphs of groups which we will study, namely
geometrically finite graphs of groups
. These are graphs of groups which can be “cut” into finitely many pieces, each of which is of a specified form. To make precise the notion of “cutting” a graph of groups, we slightly extend the notion of graphs of groups to that of
graphs of groups with boundary
. This means that we allow some vertices to have “half-edges”
e
which are “edges” with specified terminus vertex
v
, but not origin vertex, and with
G_{e}
and
\phi_{\overrightarrow{e}}:G_{e}\hookrightarrow G_{v}
specified. We call such half-edges
prongs emanating from
v
. Heuristically these are the objects one obtains when one “cuts” a graph of groups along a collection of edges, and one just considers some subcollection of the resulting connected components. Clearly two graphs of groups with boundary can be “glued together” along a prong from each assuming the edge groups are the same (and upon choosing an isomorphism between these edge groups).

We can now define the main constituents of geometrically finite graphs of groups, namely orbifold funnels and cusps.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
be a locally finite rooted tree with root
o
. An
orbifold funnel
is a graph of groups with boundary whose underlying vertex and edge set are the same as
\mathcal{G}
, but with a prong
h
emanating from
o
. The vertex and edge groups are such that
G_{o}=G_{h}
and more generally
G_{v}=G_{\{v,w\}}\leq G_{w}
for every vertex
v
such that there is an edge
\{v,w\}
with vertex
w
closer to
o
(and
h
) than
v
. In case
G_{o}=1
, and thus all other
G_{v}=1
, we simply call it a
funnel
. See Figure
2.1
.

3.1.
Geometric finiteness in negative curvature

Originally, a hyperbolic 2- or 3-manifold was called geometrically finite if it admitted a finite-sided convex polyhedral fundamental domain in the corresponding hyperbolic space. However, this definition no longer makes sense if one works more generally with negatively curved manifolds; in fact this definition is no longer even suitable if one restricts to higher dimensional hyperbolic manifolds.

Let
X
be a Hadamard manifold with pinched negative curvature. Let
\Gamma
be a group acting discretely and isometrically on

[
11

discussed several competing definitions of geometric finiteness in this setting (and which were all known to be equivalent to the classical definition of geometric finiteness for hyperbolic 2- and 3-manifolds), and he proves that these definitions are all equivalent. We shall focus on one particular definition which ultimately also makes sense more generally in the setting of groups acting on negatively curved metric spaces (such as trees).

The space
X
has a natural
boundary at
\infty
denoted

X\sqcup\partial X
naturally compactifies

. The

\Lambda_{\Gamma}

of

(the choice of

A point

is said to be

if for some (any)

and some (any) geodesic ray
\beta
tending towards
\lambda

such that
\Gamma.x\cap N_{r}(\beta)
accumulates at
y
, where
N_{r}(\beta)
is a neighborhood of radius

around
\beta
. This definition is essentially saying that there exists
\gamma_{n}\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma_{n}.x
follows along the geodesic
\beta
to within a bounded distance.

A subgroup
G<\Gamma
is called

if there is a unique point

which is fixed by all of

(see, e.g., Bowditch
[
11

for the definition of horocycles; see also Section

if the subgroup

Bowditch proves that one (equivalent) definition of the geometric finiteness of

is that every element of
\Lambda_{\Gamma}
is either a conical fixed point or a bounded parabolic fixed point.

The analogue of a Hadamard manifold in the setting of graphs is a locally finite tree
\mathcal{T}
such that every vertex has degree at least 2. The tree also has a natural boundary
\partial X
which can also be defined in terms of classes of asymptotically equivalent geodesic rays. Suppose
\Gamma
is a group acting discretely on

Theorem 3.1

(Paulin

Suppose
\Gamma<\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
is a discrete subgroup. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

is either a conical limit point or a bounded parabolic fixed point.

(2)

The quotient graph of groups

We remark to the reader that, in case one were to consult Théorème 1.1 of Paulin, one would need to also read the comment on the top of page 6 of the same article to make the direct connection with Definition

3.2.
Funnels and convex cocompact groups

Next we want to give an independent motivation for the above introduced notion of funnels, based on analogies with hyperbolic geometry. Suppose
\gamma\in\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is a hyperbolic element in the sense that it fixes exactly two points
\alpha,\beta\in\partial\mathbb{H}\simeq S^{1}
. Then
\gamma
acts as a translation by some length
\ell
along the unique geodesic connecting
\alpha
and
\beta

\langle\gamma\rangle\backslash\mathbb{H}
. This is called a hyperbolic cylinder. The geodesic connecting
\alpha
and
\beta
projects to a closed geodesic of length
\ell
. If we remove this geodesic, we obtain two disconnected “funnels”. In general in the context of hyperbolic geometry, a funnel is any hyperbolic surface with boundary which arises in this way; see, e.g.,
[
9
]

We now describe the analogous situation for trees. Suppose
\mathcal{T}
is a locally finite tree. Let
\gamma\in\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
be an element which fixes exactly two points
\alpha,\beta
of the boundary
\partial\mathcal{T}
. Then
\gamma
translates by some length
\ell
along the unique geodesic connecting
\alpha
and
\beta
, and
\langle\gamma\rangle\backslash\mathcal{T}
is isomorphic to a cycle graph of length
\ell
(the image of the geodesic under the projection) with each vertex on the cycle having several attached rooted trees. See Figure
3.1

v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell}
, with edges
\{v_{1},v_{2}\},\{v_{2},v_{3}\},\dots,\{v_{\ell},v_{1}\}

but which are not part of the cycle, then in addition to the cycle graph (which now is a graph (of groups) with boundary), we obtain finitely many funnels in the sense of Definition
2.1

More generally suppose
\Gamma
is a discrete subgroup of
\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
which setwise preserves some geodesic. Then

is either finite or contains a finite index subgroup isomorphic to

. Let

3.3.
Cusps

We again start in the setting of hyperbolic surfaces. Suppose
\gamma\in\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
is parabolic, i.e. fixes exactly one point at infinity. Thus
\gamma
is conjugate to
\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
. The hyperbolic manifold
\langle\gamma\rangle\backslash\mathbb{H}
is called a parabolic cylinder. This surface is foliated by closed horocycles. One end of this surface is “cusped” and the other end is “flared”. If we cut this surface along some closed horocycle and take the “cusped” end, we call the resulting surface a cusp. Notice that a cusp is non-compact but has finite volume.

It is well known that for geometrically finite
\Gamma
, we have that
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}
is compact if and only if it has no cusps and no funnels, and it has finite volume if and only if it has (necessarily finitely many) cusps and no funnels.

On trees, in contrast to the setting of hyperbolic geometry, any element
\gamma\in\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
which fixes only one point on the boundary necessarily also fixes some vertex (in fact infinitely many vertices).
In particular, the group generated by such an element cannot possibly act freely on
\mathcal{T}
, so the quotient cannot possibly be a
(q+1)
-regular graph in the usual sense. Furthermore, and relatedly, we know that a cusp has finite volume but is not compact. However, a finite volume graph can only have finitely many vertices, at least if all vertices have the same mass. However, using the volume form described in Section
2
, we can indeed have finite volume non-compact quotient graphs of groups.

In order to most clearly highlight the similarities between the setting of hyperbolic surfaces and regular graphs, and in particular to motivate the definition of cusp in the latter setting, we make use of the Bruhat-Tits tree. We shall briefly recall the basic facts that we need, but we encourage the reader to consult Serre
[
43

[
15

F

\mathbb{Z}
is a discrete cocompact subgroup of
\mathbb{R}

Proof.

For points

Furthermore, the

For

The quotient graph of groups

By the previous lemma, we can take as our fundamental domain in the Bruhat–Tits tree the points

Analogously to the setting of the hyperbolic plane, we can cut our parabolic cylinder along the edge connecting vertices at levels

3.4.
Arithmetic lattices

A lattice in a topological group is a discrete subgroup with co-finite volume. If we take our group to be
\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
for some locally finite tree
\mathcal{T}
, then a discrete subgroup
\Gamma<\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
is a lattice if and only if
\Gamma\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
has finite volume. However, as discussed in Bass-Lubotzky
[
6
]
, lattices in
\textnormal{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
are not necessarily geometrically finite, even if one assumes that
\mathcal{T}
is regular. The corresponding quotient graphs of groups may have infinitely many ends, and the underlying graph may have fundamental group which is a free group on infinitely many generators. This is in stark contrast to the setting of lattices in
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
which are always geometrically finite.

On the other hand, if one restricts to the setting of lattices in
\textnormal{PGL}(2,F)
(or more generally a semisimple algebraic group over
F
with
F
-rank one, which in turn has an associated Bruhat-Tits tree which is either a regular or biregular tree), then any lattice
\Gamma
in such a setting is necessarily geometrically finite, and the quotient has finitely many cusps (possibly none) and no funnels. See Theorem 6.1 of Lubotzky
[
31
]
. As remarked before, in characteristic zero, all lattices are in fact cocompact, and thus in particular the corresponding quotients have no cusps. Cusps are genuinely a positive characteristic phenomenon, as we saw in the previous section.

A very illustrative and in many ways prototypical example of a non-cocompact lattice in
\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}((T)))
is given by
\Gamma=\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}[T^{-1}])
. Nagao’s theorem
[
37
]
tells us the structure of
\Gamma\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
. The underlying graph is a path graph, with nodes labelled by
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We have
G_{0}=\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q})
and for
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle G_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}&a_{0}+a_{1}T^{-1}+\dots+a_{j}T^{-j}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

The edge groups are

\displaystyle G_{\{0,1\}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}&\mathbb{F}_{q}\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},

with the obvious inclusions into
G_{0}
and
G_{1}
, and for
j\geq 1
,
G_{\{j,j+1\}}=G_{j}
with the obvious inclusions into
G_{j}
and
G_{j+1}
. See Serre
[
43
]
Chapter II Section 1.6. In particular, we remark that, if we cut along the edge
\{0,1\}
, the graph of groups with boundary consisting of vertices
j\geq 1
is a cusp. This is very similar to
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
which also consists of a single cusp. See Figure
3.3
.

Figure 3.3
.
Illustration of Nagao’s theorem. This is the graph of groups corresponding to the quotient of the Bruhat-Tits tree of
\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}((T)))
by the lattice
\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}[T^{-1}])
.

We now briefly discuss the general construction of arithmetic lattices in
\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}((T)))
. This discussion is based on Serre
[
43
]
Chapter II Section 2. Let
C
be a smooth projective algebraic curve over a finite field
k_{0}
. Let
K
be the function field of
C
, and let
P
denote a closed point of
C
. Let
K_{P}
be the completion of
K
with respect to the valuation associated to
P
and
\mathcal{O}_{P}
the corresponding ring of integers; abstractly,
K_{P}
is isomorphic to
k((T))
where
k
is the residue field at
P
(a finite extension of
k_{0}
). Let
C^{\textnormal{aff}}=C\setminus\{P\}
, which is necessarily an affine curve having
P
as its unique point at infinity. The ring of regular functions
R
on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
is a Dedekind domain.

The subgroup
\Gamma=\textnormal{PGL}(2,R)
is a lattice in
G=\textnormal{PGL}(2,K_{P})
. If we take
C=\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
P=\{0\}
, then
\Gamma=\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}[T^{-1}])
which was discussed above. The vertices of the quotient of the Bruhat-Tits tree by
\Gamma
, i.e. elements in

\displaystyle\textnormal{PGL}(2,R)\backslash\textnormal{PGL}(2,K_{P})/\textnormal{PGL}(2,\mathcal{O}_{P}),

(3.1)

have a geometric interpretation as rank-2 vector bundles on
C
which trivialize on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
(modulo tensoring with a line bundle
L
corresponding to a divisor of the form
\ell[P]
with
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
). We wish to explain this, at least on a heuristic level. Roughly speaking, rank-2 vector bundles on
C
which trivialize on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
are constructed as follows. We start with the trivial bundle on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
and the trivial bundle on some arbitrarily small neighborhood
U
of
P
. To construct a global vector bundle, we must specify the transition data on the intersection of
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
and
U
, which is an arbitrarily small punctured disk centered at
P
. Such data is given by an element in
\textnormal{GL}(2,K_{P})
(note that
K_{P}
is exactly those functions defined on an arbitrarily small punctured disk). However, if we pre-compose with an automorphism of the trivial bundle on
U
(i.e. an element in
\textnormal{GL}(2,R)
;
R
consists of regular functions on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
), or post-compose with an automorphism of the trivial bundle on
U
(i.e. an element of
\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathcal{O}_{P})
;
\mathcal{O}_{P}
is regular functions on an arbitrarily small neighborhood of
P
), then we get the same bundle. Therefore, such bundles are parametrized by elements in the double coset
\textnormal{GL}(2,R)\backslash\textnormal{GL}(2,K_{P})/\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathcal{O}_{P})
. The passage from
\textnormal{GL}(2)
to
\textnormal{PGL}(2)
amounts to quotienting by the aforementioned equivalence relation, i.e. tensoring with a line bundle
L
corresponding to a divisor of the form
\ell[P]
. At the level of transition maps, tensoring with such a line bundle corresponds to multiplying the transition map by a meromorphic section of
L
which is trivial on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
, i.e. a diagonal matrix in
\textnormal{GL}(2,K_{P})
.

The structure of the cusps carries geometric information in the language of vector bundles. Let
\textnormal{Pic}(R)
denote the Picard group of
R
, i.e. the group of isomorphism classes of line bundles on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
, with group operation given by tensor product (or equivalently the class group of the Dedekind domain
R
). In the case at hand, it turns out that
\textnormal{Pic}(R)
is finite. Let
c\in\textnormal{Pic}(R)
. We can then form an associated line bundle
L_{c}
on
C
. Let
L_{P}
be the line bundle corresponding to the divisor
[P]
. Let
L_{P}^{n}
be the
n
th tensor power of
L_{P}
. Any rank-2 vector bundle of the form
L_{c}\oplus(L_{P}^{n}\otimes L_{c}^{-1})
is trivial on
C^{\textnormal{aff}}
. Furthermore, such vector bundles all define distinct elements in (
3.1
) for
n\geq 1
. Additionally, the corresponding subgraph of groups for
n\geq m_{c}
sufficiently large defines a cusp. As we vary over
c\in\textnormal{Pic}(R)
, we obtain distinct cusps, and all cusps arise in this way.

The spectral theory of the adjacency operator on such arithmetic quotients is closely related to the study of automorphic forms for
\textnormal{PGL}(2,K)
. In the setting of arithmetic hyperbolic surfaces (or more generally arithmetic locally symmetric spaces associated to
\textnormal{SL}(2,F)
, where
F
is a number field, such as Hilbert modular varieties or Bianchi manifolds), there is a close relationship between resonances and the zeta function of
F
. This link is expressed in the language of Eisenstein series where it essentially corresponds to the Langlands-Shahidi method. In Section
7.8
, we compute explicitly the resonances for several arithmetic examples as described in this section, and we observe the same phenomenon. We discuss this connection further in that section.

4.
Resolvent on model spaces

In order to prove Theorem
1.1
we follow the approach of
[
23
]
(see also
[
9
]
) which consists of first proving exact meromorphic resolvents on the model geometries, i.e. on cusps and funnels. In a next step we then construct a global parametrix in Section
5
.

We begin by recalling what it means for a family of operators to be meromorphic.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces. A family of operators

\mathbb{C}\supseteq\Omega\ni z\mapsto A(z)\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

is called
meromorphic
if for every
z_{0}\in\Omega
there exist
J\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ \varepsilon>0
and operators
A_{j}\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
such that

A(z)=\sum_{j=-J}^{\infty}A_{j}(z-z_{0})^{j}\quad\text{for }z\in B(z_{0},\varepsilon)\setminus\{z_{0}\},

where the series converges in the operator norm. Moreover, we call
A

finitely meromorphic
if
A_{-J},\ldots,A_{-1}
are finite-rank operators and
holomorphic
if
A_{-J}=\ldots=A_{-1}=0
.

4.1.
Resolvent on a funnel

Funnels are not actually graphs of groups, but rather graphs of groups with boundary. Because of this, it will be most convenient for us to study the resolvent first on the
(q+1)
-regular tree where the analysis is straightforward, and then deduce from this properties for the funnel (which naturally arises as a subgraph of the tree). Handling orbifold funnels is also straightforward because these arise as subgraphs of groups of a quotient of the tree by a finite group.

4.1.1.
Resolvent on the
(q+1)
-regular tree

Let
\mathcal{T}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
denote a
(q+1)
-regular tree. Then the
\ell^{2}
-spectrum of
A
is given by
[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
. Thus, the resolvent

R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\coloneqq\left(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\right)^{-1}\colon\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\rightarrow\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V}),\quad z(\mu)\coloneqq\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2},

exists for
\lvert z(\mu)\rvert>1
. In that region we denote by

K_{\mu}\colon\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\quad K_{\mu}(x,y)\coloneqq(R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\delta_{y})(x),\qquad\delta_{y}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\colon x=y,\\
0&\colon x\neq y,\end{cases},

the kernel of
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)
such that
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)f(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}}K_{\mu}(x,y)f(y)
for all
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
. Since
A
is invariant under the action of the automorphism group
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
of the tree we have
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\circ g=g\circ R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)
for all
g\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
. In terms of the kernel this means that
K_{\mu}(gx,gy)=K_{\mu}(x,y)
and since
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{T})
acts transitively on paths of the same length,
K_{\mu}(x,y)\eqqcolon k_{\mu}(d(x,y))
only depends on the distance
d(x,y)
between
x
and
y
. We can now compute the kernel explicitly. Note that

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\delta_{y}](x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z\in\mathcal{V}}k_{\mu}(d(x,z))\delta_{y}(z)=k_{\mu}(d(x,y)),

\displaystyle\Big[\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\delta_{y}\Big](x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}\begin{cases}k_{\mu}(d(x,y)-1)+qk_{\mu}(d(x,y)+1)&x\neq y\\
(q+1)k_{\mu}(1)&x=y.\end{cases}

Therefore the identity

\displaystyle\Big(z(\mu)R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)+I\Big)\delta_{y}=\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\Big)\delta_{y},

results in the following equations:

(i)

z(\mu)k_{\mu}(0)+1=\frac{q+1}{2\sqrt{q}}k_{\mu}(1)
,

(ii)

z(\mu)k_{\mu}(d)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}(k_{\mu}(d-1)+qk_{\mu}(d+1))
for
d>0
.

This is an order 2 linear recurrence relation with characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle q\lambda^{2}-(\mu+\mu^{-1})\sqrt{q}\lambda+1=(\sqrt{q}\lambda-\mu)(\sqrt{q}\lambda-\mu^{-1}),

so the general solution (for
d>0
) is given by
C_{1}(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{q}})^{d}+C_{2}(\frac{\mu^{-1}}{\sqrt{q}})^{d}
. However, our choice of parametrization implies that the resolvent defines a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
for
|z(\mu)|>1
, which in turn forces that
C_{1}=0
. From (
i
) we then infer that

\displaystyle C_{2}=-\frac{2}{\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1}}.

Therefore,

[R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)f](x):=-\frac{2}{\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{V}}\left(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\right)^{d(x,y)}f(y)

(4.1)

provides a meromorphic extension of the resolvent to
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus\{\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
as a family of operators from
C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
to
C(\mathcal{V})
.

4.1.2.
Convergence of the resolvent on weighted
\ell^{2}
-spaces

In this section we investigate the convergence of the resolvent on the weighted
\ell^{2}
-spaces. For this we fix a base point
o\in\mathcal{V}
and define, for
N\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the functions

q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\colon\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}(v)\coloneqq q^{Nd(o,v)}.

We will prove the following

Theorem 4.2
.

For every
N\geq 0
the resolvent
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)
is finitely meromorphic as a family of operators

R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)\colon q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\rightarrow q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})

for
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{-N}
.

We first define, for
d>0
and
f\in C(\mathcal{V})
, the spherical averaging operators

L_{d}f(x)\coloneqq(q+1)^{-1}q^{-d+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathcal{V}\\
d(x,y)=d\end{subarray}}f(y).

Thus, (
4.1
) can be rewritten as

R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)f(x)=-\frac{2}{\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1}}\left(f(x)+\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\right)^{d}(q+1)q^{d-1}L_{d}f(x)\right)

(4.2)

which converges for
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\backslash\{\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
and
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
by the previous section. We now investigate the convergence of this series for functions in weighted
\ell^{2}
-spaces.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
f\in q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
we have

\lVert\chi_{m}(L_{d}f)\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{q^{1-d}}{q+1}q^{-2N(m+d)}\frac{q^{4N(\min(m,d)+1)}-1}{q^{4N}-1}\lVert f\rVert_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}^{2},

where
\chi_{m}
denotes the characteristic function of
S(o,m)\coloneqq\left\{x\in\mathcal{V}\mid d(o,x)=m\right\}
.

Proof.

We can write
f=\sum_{k}f_{k}
with
f_{k}:=\chi_{k}f
. Lemma 6.1 of
[
19
]
tells us that we can only have
\chi_{m}(L_{d}f_{k})\neq 0
if
d+k-m
is even and
|d-k|\leq m\leq d+k
, i.e., if
k=m+d-2j
for some
0\leq j\leq\min(m,d)
, and that, moreover,

\lVert\chi_{m}(L_{d}f_{k})\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{q^{1-d}}{q+1}\lVert f_{k}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Thus, we calculate

\displaystyle\lVert

\displaystyle\chi_{m}(L_{d}f)\rVert_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}\lVert\chi_{m}(L_{d}f_{m+d-2j})\rVert_{\ell^{2}}\leq q^{\frac{1-d}{2}}(q+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}\lVert f_{m+d-2j}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq q^{\frac{1-d}{2}}(q+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}q^{-(m+d-2j)N}\lVert(q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}f)_{m+d-2j}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq q^{\frac{1-d}{2}}(q+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}q^{-2(m+d-2j)N}}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}\lVert(q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}f)_{m+d-2j}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq q^{\frac{1-d}{2}}(q+1)^{-\frac{1}{2}}q^{-N(m+d)}\sqrt{\frac{q^{4N(\min(m,d)+1)}-1}{q^{4N}-1}}\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}\lVert(q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}f)_{m+d-2j}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}.

Since
\sum_{j=0}^{\min(m,d)}\lVert(q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}f)_{m+d-2j}\rVert_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq\lVert f\rVert_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}^{2}
we obtain the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Suppose
f\in q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}
and
\mu\neq\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. We wish to show that
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)f\in q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}
if
|\mu|>q^{-N}
. Using Lemma
4.3
, we begin by observing that

\displaystyle\|L_{d}f\|^{2}_{q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}L_{d}f\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{-2Nm}\|\chi_{m}(L_{d}f)\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N}q^{-d}q^{-2Nd}\|f\|^{2}_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}\Big(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q^{-4Nm}q^{4N\textnormal{min}(m,d)}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N}q^{-d}q^{-2Nd}\|f\|^{2}_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}\Big(\sum_{m=0}^{d}q^{-4Nm}q^{4Nm}+\sum_{m=d+1}^{\infty}q^{-4Nm}q^{4Nd}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim_{N}q^{-d}q^{-2Nd}d\|f\|^{2}_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}

Therefore, using (
4.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)f\|_{q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mu}\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{d}q^{d}\|L_{d}f\|_{q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\lesssim_{\mu,N}\|f\|_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}}\Big(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{\mu q^{N}}\Big)^{d}\sqrt{d}\Big).

This converges as long as
|\mu|>q^{-N}
.

Finally, we prove that it is finitely meromorphic. This follows immediately from the explicit formula (
4.1
) because at each pole,
\mu=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
, the residue operator simply involves projecting onto the space of constants (in case
\mu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
), or the space of functions which take value
(-1)^{d(o,v)}c
at vertex
v
for some constant
c
and choice of
o
(in case
\mu=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
). Thus in both cases the residue operator is rank one.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

We can also obtain the same result by using the Schur test for the radial kernel
k_{\mu}
of the resolvent
R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)
. This approach will be used in the discussion of the resolvent on a cusp in Section

.

Remark 4.5
.

Suppose
H
is a finite group which fixes some point
o
of
\mathcal{T}
. Let
\mathcal{P}:=H\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
, and let
R_{\mathcal{P}}(\mu)
denote the resolvent on
\mathcal{P}
. The formula for the kernel function of

is simply given by

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{P}}(\mu)(x,y)=\sum_{h\in H}R_{\mathcal{T}}(\mu)(x,h.y).

From this it is clear that Theorem

also applies to

. In particular, given any orbifold funnel, we can find some such

4.2.
Resolvent on a cusp

We now turn our attention to the resolvent on a cusp. We shall start by considering the resolvent of a certain operator which we can in some sense interpret as the adjacency operator on a graph, but we must yet again slightly extend the definition of graph. We start by considering the infinite path graph with vertices labeled by the integers. We then define a not-necessarily-integer-valued stabilizer function where vertex
k
has
\mathcal{S}(k)=q^{k}
, and the edge
(i,i+1)
has
\mathcal{S}(i,i+1)=q^{i}
. See Figure
4.1
. If we define the operator
A_{\mathcal{Z}}
by the same formula as in (
2.2
), we see that
A_{\mathcal{Z}}
simply acts by

\displaystyle[Af](k)=qf(k-1)+f(k+1).

We also note that, using the same definition for degree as given in (
2.1
) (replacing
|G_{v}|
and
|G_{e}|
by
\mathcal{S}(v)
and
\mathcal{S}(e)
, respectively), the object
\mathcal{Z}
can be thought of as a
(q+1)
-regular graph, and the proof of Proposition
2.4
implies that
A_{\mathcal{Z}}
is in fact bounded in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
(where vertex
k\in\mathbb{Z}
has mass
q^{k}
). We thus know that the resolvent must define a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
for large
\mu
. We call
\mathcal{Z}
the
idealized parabolic cylinder
.

The object
\mathcal{Z}
is a useful tool to study the resolvent on cusps. The notion of graph of groups with boundary naturally also leads to the notion of graph with stabilizer function with boundary by just remembering the data of the size of each vertex and edge group. If we cut
\mathcal{Z}
along the edge connecting vertices 0 and 1, then the graph of groups with stabilizer function with boundary corresponding to the positive integers, which we denote
\mathcal{C}
, is a cusp in the sense that its stabilizer function matches that of a cusp. Any other
(q+1)
-regular cusp has the same stabilizer function up to scaling the stabilizer function by some positive integer.

Figure 4.1
.
The idealized parabolic cylinder
\mathcal{Z}
. As a graph with stabilizer function, the green part is a cusp.

We wish to explicitly find the kernel function
K_{\mu}(\cdot,\cdot)
for
R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)
. It must satisfy the identity

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)f](k_{1})=\sum_{k_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}}K_{\mathcal{Z}}(k_{1},k_{2})f(k_{2})q^{-k_{2}}.

We have that

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\delta_{k_{2}}](k_{1})

\displaystyle=K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})q^{-k_{2}}

\displaystyle[A\delta_{k_{2}}](k_{1})

\displaystyle=q\delta_{k_{2}+1}(k_{1})+\delta_{k_{2}-1}(k_{1})

\displaystyle[AR_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\delta_{k_{2}}](k_{1})

\displaystyle=q^{-k_{2}}\Big(qK_{\mu}(k_{1}-1,k_{2})+K_{\mu}(k_{1}+1,k_{2})\Big)

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)A\delta_{k_{2}}](k_{1})

\displaystyle=q^{-k_{2}}\Big(qK_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2}-1)+K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2}+1)\Big).

Therefore, the defining identities for the resolvent (as a left and right inverse to
\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)
) give the relations:

\displaystyle q^{k_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}(k_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{2}K_{\mu}(k_{1}-1,k_{2})+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}K_{\mu}(k_{1}+1,k_{2})-z(\mu)K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle q^{k_{2}}\delta_{k_{2}}(k_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{2}K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2}-1)+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2}+1)-z(\mu)K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2}).

If we were to fix
k_{1}
and consider those
k_{2}\geq k_{1}
, we see that we would get a linear recurrence relation with characteristic polynomial

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}-2z(\mu)\sqrt{q}\lambda+q=(\lambda-\sqrt{q}\mu)\left(\lambda-\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right).

The general solution would then be
C_{1}(k_{1})(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{k_{2}}+C_{2}(k_{1})\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k_{2}}
. However, we know that for
\mu
large,
R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)
must define a bounded operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
, which in turn implies that we must have
C_{1}(k_{1})=0
. For example, if we were to take the function
f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{k}
, which is clearly in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
, then

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)f](\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}}K_{\mu}(\ell,m)q^{-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\ell}K_{\mu}(\ell,m)q^{-m}+\sum_{m=\ell+1}^{\infty}\Big(C_{1}(\ell)(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{m}+C_{2}(\ell)\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{m}\Big)q^{-m}

which must be finite for
|\mu|
large.

We can therefore write
K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})=C(k_{1})\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k_{2}}
. On the other hand, if we fix
k_{2}
, and consider all
k_{1}\leq k_{2}
, the corresponding sequence must satisfy the same recurrence relation, which is to say that
C(k_{1})=C_{1}(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{k_{1}}+C_{2}(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{k_{1}}
. We claim that the boundedness condition on
R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)
forces
C_{2}=0
. For this we consider the sequence of elements
q^{n/2}\delta_{n}
with
n\to-\infty
. These elements all have norm
1
. We have

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)q^{n/2}\delta_{n}](n-1)=K_{\mu}(n-1,n)q^{n/2}q^{-n}.

Therefore
\|R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)q^{n/2}\delta_{n}\|\geq|K_{\mu}(n-1,n)\sqrt{q}|
. When
|\mu|
is large, we know that
\|R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)q^{n/2}\delta_{n}\|
must remain bounded as
n\to-\infty
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left(C_{1}(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{n-1}+C_{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)^{n-1}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)^{n}\sqrt{q}

must be uniformly bounded in
n
. This is only possible if
C_{2}=0
.

We can apply a similar analysis to the case that
k_{1}\geq k_{2}
. We conclude that

\displaystyle K_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})=\begin{cases}C(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{k_{1}}(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{k_{2}}&k_{1}\geq k_{2}\\
C(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{k_{2}}(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{k_{1}}&k_{2}\geq k_{1}\end{cases}=C\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{|k_{1}-k_{2}|}q^{\textnormal{min}(k_{1},k_{2})}.

Finally, we can compute what
C
is by setting
k_{1}=k_{2}=k
, which results in

\displaystyle q^{k}=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{2}C\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}q^{k-1}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}C\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}q^{k}-z(\mu)Cq^{k},

and thus
C=-\frac{2}{\mu-\mu^{-1}}
. In summary we have proven

Proposition 4.6
.

The formula

\displaystyle[R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)f](k)=-\frac{2}{\mu-\mu^{-1}}\Bigg(\sum_{\ell<k}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k-\ell}f(\ell)+\sum_{\ell\geq k}(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{k-\ell}f(\ell)\Bigg)

(4.3)

provides a finitely meromorphic continuation of the resolvent of
\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}
to
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
as a family of operators from
C_{c}(\mathcal{Z})
to
C(\mathcal{Z})
.

4.2.1.
Convergence of the cusp resolvent on weighted
\ell^{2}
-spaces

We shall ultimately be interested in the resolvent on the cusp
\mathcal{C}
, rather than on
\mathcal{Z}
. In Section
5
, we shall construct an approximation to the resolvent on geometrically finite graphs by “gluing together” our model resolvents on funnels and cusps. The model resolvent on the cusps shall take the form of
R_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)
, defined below and which we now focus on.

Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
denote the subgraph with stabilizer function corresponding to
n\geq 2
. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
denote the indicator function of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

We define

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu):=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}.

For every
N\geq 0
,
R_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)
defines a finitely meromorphic family of operators

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu):q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{C})\to q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{C}),

on
|\mu|>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
with poles at
\pm 1
.

Proof.

We wish to prove that

\displaystyle\tilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu):=q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}R_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}

defines an operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{C})
for
|\mu|>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
for
\mu\neq\pm 1
. The kernel function for
\tilde{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)
is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{\mu}(k_{1},k_{2})=-\frac{2}{\mu-\mu^{-1}}q^{-N(k_{1}+k_{2})}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{|k_{1}-k_{2}|}q^{\textnormal{min}(k_{1},k_{2})}

if
k_{1}\neq 0
and
k_{2}\neq 0
, and is zero when
k_{1}=0
or
k_{2}=0
.

We can bound the operator norm by the Schur test:

\displaystyle\|\tilde{K}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)\|^{2}\leq\Big(\sup_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}|\tilde{K}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)(k_{1},k_{2})|q^{-k_{2}}\Big)\Big(\sup_{k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}|\tilde{K}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)(k_{1},k_{2})|q^{-k_{1}}\Big).

We have,

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}}|\tilde{K}_{\mathcal{C}}(\mu)(k_{1},k_{2})|q^{-k_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{|\mu-\mu^{-1}|}\sup_{k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}}\Big(\sum_{k_{2}=1}^{k_{1}-1}\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{|\mu|}\Big)^{k_{1}-k_{2}}q^{-N(k_{1}+k_{2})}

\displaystyle+\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mu|\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{k_{2}-k_{1}}q^{-N(k_{1}+k_{2})}\Big)

The first sum evaluates to

\left(\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert q^{-\frac{1}{2}+N}}\right)^{k_{1}}\left(\frac{\left(\frac{\lvert\mu\rvert}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}\right)^{k_{1}}-1}{\frac{\lvert\mu\rvert}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}-1}-1\right)\leq\frac{q^{-2Nk_{1}}-\left(\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert q^{-\frac{1}{2}+N}}\right)^{k_{1}}}{\frac{\lvert\mu\rvert}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}-1}

if
\lvert\mu\rvert\neq q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}
and to
q^{-2Nk_{1}}k_{1}
if
\lvert\mu\rvert=q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}
, which are both uniformly bounded in
k_{1}
for
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
. Moreover, for
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}
, it is bounded by
\frac{1}{\frac{\lvert\mu\rvert}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}-1}
. The second sum evaluates to

q^{-2Nk_{1}}\sum_{k_{2}=k_{1}}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}\right)^{k_{2}-k_{1}}=\frac{q^{-2Nk_{1}}}{1-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{\lvert\mu\rvert q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}}

if
\lvert\mu\rvert>\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+N}}
. Thus, the supremum is finite for
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
and the same argument applies to the second supremum.

∎

5.
Resolvent on geometrically finite graphs of groups

The point of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
. We begin by recalling a version of the analytic Fredholm theorem which we shall use.

Theorem 5.1

(Analytic Fredholm theorem; see Theorem 6.7 of
[
9
]
)
.

Suppose
E(s)
is a finitely meromorphic family of compact operators on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. If
I+E(s)
is invertible for at least one point in the domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
, then
(I+E(s))^{-1}
exists as a finitely meromorphic family of operators on all of
\Omega
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We first discuss the proof strategy. We begin by constructing a finitely meromorphic family of operators
P(\mu):q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
for
|\mu|>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
, and which act as a parametrix to
\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)
in the sense that

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)P(\mu)=I+K(\mu),

where
K(\mu):q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}\to q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}
is a meromorphic family of compact (in fact, finite rank) operators on this same domain. After showing that
I+K(\mu):q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}\to q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}
is invertible for some
\mu
sufficiently large, we conclude by the analytic Fredholm theorem that

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=P(\mu)(I+K(\mu))^{-1}

has the claimed properties.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{L}\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{n_{f}}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{n_{c}}
be the decomposition of
\mathcal{V}
into a (not necessarily
(q+1)
-regular) compact core
\mathcal{L}
, a finite number
n_{f}
of
(q+1)
-regular orbifold funnels
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, and a finite number
n_{c}
of
(q+1)
-regular cusps
\mathcal{C}_{i}
. Let
H_{j}
be the finite group which we can quotient the standard funnel by to obtain
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, and let
\mathcal{P}_{j}:=H_{j}\backslash\backslash\mathcal{T}
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
denote all vertices at distance at most 1 from
\mathcal{L}
. Let
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
denote all vertices at distance at most 2 from
\mathcal{L}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{k}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{\prime}
, respectively, denote all vertices of distance at least one from the root of
\mathcal{F}_{k}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
, respectively. We thus have

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{n_{f}}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{n_{c}}^{\prime}.

For each orbifold funnel
\mathcal{F}_{j}
, we have a natural inclusion
\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}:\mathcal{F}_{j}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{j}
; we use the same notation for the natural map
C(\mathcal{F}_{j})\hookrightarrow C(\mathcal{P}_{j})
. We also have the adjoint map
\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}:C(\mathcal{P}_{j})\to C(\mathcal{F}_{j})
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}:=\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}(\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime})
. We likewise have for each
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
inclusions
\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}:\mathcal{C}_{\ell}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{Z}
; again we use the same notation for the map
C(\mathcal{C}_{\ell})\hookrightarrow C(\mathcal{Z})
. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}:=\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}(\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{\prime})
(the definition does not depend on the choice of
\ell
). We have that

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{G}}\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}f

\displaystyle=\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}A_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}f

(5.1)

\displaystyle A_{\mathcal{G}}\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}g

\displaystyle=\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}A_{\mathcal{Z}}g

(5.2)

whenever
f\in C(\mathcal{P}_{j})
is supported on
\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}
, and
g\in C(\mathcal{Z})
is supported on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

Let
R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu)
denote the resolvent of the induced subgraph associated to the vertices in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
(i.e. we keep all edges between any two vertices in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
). We remark for future use that if we write
A_{\mathcal{G}}=A_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}+B
, then the image of
B
is supported on
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
because every edge from a vertex in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is also an edge in the induced graph of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
.

We define

\displaystyle P(\mu)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{\prime}},

where
\mu_{0}
is some fixed value which we later set to be sufficiently large. It is immediate that if
f\in q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
, then
\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}f\in q^{-Nd(o_{\mathcal{P}_{j}},\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{P}_{j})
, and
\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}^{\prime}}f\in q^{-Nd(o_{\mathcal{Z}},\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
. Similarly if
g_{j}\in q^{Nd(o_{\mathcal{P}_{j}},\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{P}_{j})
, then
\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}g_{j}\in q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
, and if
g_{\ell}\in q^{Nd(o_{\mathcal{Z}},\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
, then
\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{\ell}}^{*}g_{\ell}\in q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
. By Theorems
4.2
(with Remark
4.5
) and
4.7
, we conclude that
P(\mu)
is a finitely meromorphic family of operators from
q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
for
|\mu|>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
, and it is holomorphic for
\mu\notin\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
.

We now consider
\big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\big)P(\mu)
. We obtain

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}

(5.3)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{Z}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime}}

(5.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}

(5.5)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\iota^{*}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{Z}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime}}+\frac{[A_{\mathcal{G}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}

(5.6)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}\frac{[A_{\mathcal{P}_{j}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}^{*}\frac{[A_{\mathcal{Z}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime}}.

(5.7)

Note that in the second term in (
5.3
) and the term in (
5.4
) we have used (
5.1
) and (
5.2
). Going from the first two lines to the last three lines simply involves repeatedly using the identity
AB=BA+[A,B]
. We immediately recognize that the second term in (
5.5
) is equal to
\sum\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}
, and the first term in (
5.6
) is equal to
\sum\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\prime}}
. To analyze the first term in (
5.5
), we write
A_{\mathcal{G}}=A_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}+B
, and use the resolvent identity:

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})=R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu)+(z(\mu_{0})-z(\mu))R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu)R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0}).

We then get

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\Big(\frac{B}{2\sqrt{q}}+\frac{A_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}\frac{B}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+(z(\mu_{0})-z(\mu))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}

As previously mentioned, the image of
B
is supported on
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. Therefore the first term above is simply zero. Because
\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\sum\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}+\sum\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime}}=I
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)P(\mu)=I+K(\mu),

where

\displaystyle K(\mu)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(z(\mu_{0})-z(\mu))\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}+\frac{[A_{\mathcal{G}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}}

(5.8)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}^{*}\frac{[A_{\mathcal{P}_{j}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{P}_{j}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}^{*}\frac{[A_{\mathcal{Z}},\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}]}{2\sqrt{q}}R_{\mathcal{Z}}(\mu)\iota_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}^{\prime}}.

We now claim that the image of
K(\mu)
always lies in
C(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})
, which is a finite-dimensional space. The first term in (
5.8
) clearly has this property. In general if
A
is the adjacency operator of some graph, and
X
is some subgraph, then
[A,\mathbbm{1}_{X}]
has image supported on
\partial X\cup(X^{\prime}\setminus X)
where

X^{\prime}
is vertices at distance at most one from
X
. To see this, we simply record the image of
\delta_{x}
for every vertex
x
. If
x\in(X\setminus\partial X)
, then
[A,\mathbbm{1}_{X}]\delta_{x}=0
. Similarly if
x\notin X^{\prime}
, then
[A,\mathbbm{1}_{X}]\delta_{x}=0
. Suppose
x\in(X^{\prime}\setminus X)
. Then
A\mathbbm{1}_{X}\delta_{x}=0
, whereas
\mathbbm{1}_{X}A\delta_{x}
is supported on
\partial X
. Similarly, if
x\in\partial X
, then
A\mathbbm{1}_{X}\delta_{x}=A\delta_{x}
which is supported on
X^{\prime}
. However for
y\in X
, we have
[A\delta_{x}](y)=[\mathbbm{1}_{X}A\delta_{x}](y)
, so
[A,\mathbbm{1}_{X}]\delta_{x}
is supported on
X^{\prime}\setminus X
. This observation immediately implies that the image of
K(\mu)
always lies in
C(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})
. Furthermore it is clear from the formula for
K(\mu)
that it defines a finitely meromorphic family of compact operators:

K(\mu):q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to C(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})\subset q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})

for
|\mu|>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
.

Now, we can clearly choose
\mu_{0}
and
\mu
sufficiently large so that
\|R_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}(\mu_{0})\|
,
\|R_{\mathcal{P}_{j}}(\mu)\|
and
\|R_{\mathcal{Z}}\|
are all as small as desired, and thus we can find a
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
such that
\|K(\mu_{1})\|<\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
. In particular, taking any
\varepsilon<1
, we obtain that
(I+K(\mu_{1}))^{-1}
exists as a convergent Neumann series. The meromorphic Fredholm theorem implies then that

\displaystyle(I+K(\mu))^{-1}:q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})

is finitely meromorphic on the specified domain. We therefore obtain that
R(\mu)=P(\mu)(I+K(\mu))^{-1}
is finitely meromorphic on this domain as a family of operators
q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})\to q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
.

∎

One consequence of the proof is the following bound on the multiplicity of a resonance. It’s likely that this bound can be greatly improved, but for now we content ourselves with simply deriving a finite explicit bound.

Proposition 5.2
.

The multiplicity of a resonance is at most
(2(|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f})+1)(|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f})
.

Proof.

This follows from analyzing the explicit nature of
(1+K(\mu))^{-1}
in the proof. We know that
K(\mu)
maps onto
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
. We could thus write
K(\mu)
with respect to
C(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})\oplus C(\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})
:

\displaystyle K(\mu)=\begin{bmatrix}A(\mu)&B(\mu)\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}.

We can then explicitly write

\displaystyle(I+K(\mu))^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(I_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}+A(\mu))^{-1}&-(I_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}+A(\mu))^{-1}B(\mu)\\
0&I_{\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}\end{bmatrix}.

We thus see that if we were to Laurent expand at a pole, any coefficient of a negative power in the expansion would be of the form

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}C&D\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

and thus in particular the image of all such coefficients lies in
C(\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})
, which has dimension
|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f}
. However, we also see that
(I_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}}+A(\mu))^{-1}
has entries which have poles of degree at most
2(|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f})
(since each entry is a polynomial of degree at most 2), so the pole is of order at most this. To obtain the resolvent, we must multiply
(I+K(\mu))^{-1})
by
P(\mu)
, which itself has poles of order at most 1. Negative Laurent coefficients of
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)
are thus of the form
\sum P_{i}B_{j}
where
B_{j}
is a negative Laurent coefficient of
(I+K(\mu))^{-1}
, and there are most
2(|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f})+1
many terms in the sum, each of which lands in a space of dimension at most
|\mathcal{L}|+2n_{c}+(1+q)n_{f}
. We thus obtain the claimed bound on the multiplicity.
∎

6.
Characterization of resonant states

6.1.
Purely outgoing functions

Let
\mathcal{G}
be geometrically finite. Let
\mathcal{H}
be any finite connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
containing the compact core
\mathcal{L}
. Then
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{n}\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{m}
consists of finitely many funnels
\mathcal{F}_{j}
and cusps
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
. Note, however, that it is possible that several of these funnels are subfunnels of the same single funnel appearing as a connected component of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}
. See Figure
6.1
.

Figure 6.1
.
Here
\mathcal{G}
has two cusps and two funnels. The pink region represents a possible exceptional locus
\mathcal{H}
of an outgoing function. When we remove
\mathcal{H}
, we are left with 11 funnels and 2 cusps.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, and let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite connected subgraph containing
\mathcal{L}
with
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{C}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{m}\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{1}\sqcup\dots\sqcup\mathcal{F}_{n}
. A function
f\in C(\mathcal{V})
is called
purely outgoing of parameter
\mu
and exceptional locus
\mathcal{H}
if

(i)

on each
\mathcal{C}_{\ell}
there exists a polynomial
p_{C_{\ell}}
in one variable such that
f(v)=p_{C_{\ell}}(d(o,v))(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{d(o,v)}
for all
v\in C_{\ell}
and any choice of
o\in\mathcal{L}
,

(ii)

on each
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, there exists a polynomial
p_{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}}
in one variable such that
f(v)=p_{\mathcal{F}_{\ell}}(d(o,v))(\mu\sqrt{q})^{-d(o,v)}
for all
v\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
and any choice of
o\in\mathcal{L}
.

We say that a purely outgoing solution is
of constant type
if all of the above polynomials are in fact constants.

This definition will be crucial in giving a geometric interpretation to the resonances. We can already immediately observe that if
u
is a purely outgoing solution of constant type with parameter
\mu_{0}
and exceptional locus
\mathcal{H}
, then
(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0}))u
is compactly supported (it is supported on
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
).

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
and define the meromorphic family of vectors

\tilde{f}_{\mu}\coloneqq(I+K(\mu))^{-1}f

in
q^{Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
for
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
with
K
as in (
5.8
). Then
\tilde{f}_{\mu}|_{\mathcal{V}\setminus(\mathrm{supp}f\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})}\equiv 0
.

Proof.

By the proof of Theorem
1.1
we have
(I+K(\mu))\tilde{f}_{\mu}=\tilde{f}_{\mu}
on
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
. But for
\lvert\mu\rvert
large enough we have
f=(I+K(\mu))\tilde{f}_{\mu}
so that
\tilde{f}_{\mu}
has to vanish identically on
\mathcal{V}\setminus(\mathrm{supp}f\cup\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime})
for these
\mu
. By meromorphicity, this holds for all
\lvert\mu\rvert>q^{\frac{1}{2}-N}
.
∎

Proposition 6.3
.

Suppose
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
and
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Suppose near
\mu_{0}
we have

\displaystyle P(\mu)=\frac{P_{-1}}{\mu-\mu_{0}}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}P_{j}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}.

(1)

Suppose
\mu_{0}\notin\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
(and thus
P_{-1}=0
). Then
P_{j}f
is purely outgoing of degree at most
j
.

(2)

Suppose
\mu_{0}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
. Then
P_{j}f
is purely outgoing of degree at most
j+1
.

Proof.

Because
P(\mu)
has a simple pole at
\mu_{0}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
, we know that
S(\mu):=\frac{1}{2}(\mu-\mu^{-1})(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})P(\mu)
is holomorphic on all of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Suppose near
\mu_{0}
we have

\displaystyle S(\mu)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}S_{j}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}.

Because
S(\mu)
is holomorphic, we can compute the coefficients of its power series expansions by simply taking higher and higher derivatives.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be the largest connected subgraph of
\mathcal{G}
containing
\mathcal{L}
and the support of
f
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{i}
be one of the cusps of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{H}
. Then by (
4.3
) (labelling vertices of
\mathcal{C}_{i}
by
\mathbb{N}
) we have

\displaystyle[S(\mu)f](k)

\displaystyle=-(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})\sum_{\ell\leq k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)^{k-\ell}f(\ell)

\displaystyle=-(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})\left(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)^{k}\sum_{\ell\leq k}\left(\frac{\mu}{\sqrt{q}}\right)^{\ell}f(\ell)=A_{i}(f,\mu)\left(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)^{k},

where
A_{i}(f,\mu)
is a constant that depends on
f
and
\mu
(but not on
k
), proving that
S(\mu)f
is purely outgoing of constant type on the cusps, and thus in particular
S(\mu_{0})f=S_{0}f
is purely outgoing of constant type.

Now we handle the funnels. Let
\mathcal{F}_{j}
be one of the funnels of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{H}
with root
o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}
. Then
\mathcal{F}_{j}
is a subfunnel of a bigger funnel
\mathcal{Y}_{j}
of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{L}
. Using (
4.1
), we have

\displaystyle[P(\mu)f](v)

\displaystyle=-(\mu-\mu^{-1})\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{j}}\Big(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{d(v,y)}f(y)

\displaystyle=-(\mu-\mu^{-1})\Big(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{d(v,o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}})}\left(\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{j}\cap\mathcal{H}}\Big(\frac{1}{\mu\sqrt{q}}\Big)^{d(o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}},y)}f(y)\right)

\displaystyle=B_{j}(f,\mu)(\mu\sqrt{q})^{-d(v,o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}})}.

Here we have used the fact that
d(v,y)=d(v,o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}})+d(o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}},y)
. We conclude that
S(\mu_{0})f=S_{0}f
is purely outgoing of constant type.

We now consider derivatives in
\mu
of
P(\mu)f
. This follows directly from the product rule. On each cusp
\mathcal{C}_{i}
we get

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\Big)^{m}\Big(A_{i}(f,\mu)\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k}\Big)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{m}\binom{m}{\ell}\Big(\big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\big)^{m-\ell}A_{i}(f,\mu)\Big)\prod_{j=0}^{\ell-1}\Big(\frac{-k-j}{\mu}\Big)\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k}.

The argument on each funnel is virtually identical. Seeing as
S_{j}f=\frac{1}{j!}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\mu^{j}}\big|_{\mu=\mu_{0}}S(\mu)f
, we conclude that indeed
S_{j}f
is purely outgoing of degree at most
j
.

We now wish to relate the power series expansion of
S(\mu)
at
\mu_{0}
with the Laurent series expansion of
P(\mu)
at
\mu_{0}
. In case
\mu_{0}\notin\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
, then
\frac{1}{(\mu-\mu^{-1})(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})}
is holomorphic at
\mu=\mu_{0}
, and we conclude that
P_{j}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
S_{\ell}
with
\ell\leq j
, from which the result follows. In case
\mu_{0}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
, then
\frac{1}{(\mu-\mu^{-1})(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})}=\frac{1}{\mu-\mu_{0}}Z(\mu)
where
Z(\mu)
is holomorphic at
\mu=\mu_{0}
, in which case we see that
P_{j}
is a linear combination of
S_{\ell}
with
\ell\leq j+1
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

If
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})
is defined, then
u=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})f
is the unique purely outgoing solution of constant type of

\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\right)u=f

(6.1)

for
f\in C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
.

Proof.

First off note that since
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})=P(\mu_{0})(I+K(\mu_{0}))^{-1}
, by Lemma
6.2
and Proposition
6.3
we immediately get that
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})f
is purely outgoing of constant type.

Now suppose
u
is purely outgoing of constant type. We then clearly have that
f=(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0}))u
is compactly supported. We may therefore apply
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})
to it, i.e. we get that

u=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\Big)u=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu_{0})f.\qed

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
. Suppose
u
is a purely outgoing solution with exceptional locus
\mathcal{H}
, parameter
\mu
, and degree
d
. Then

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)^{J}u=0\textnormal{ on $\mathcal{H}^{\prime}$}\iff d<J.

In particular, if
u
is a purely outgoing solution of

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)u=0,

then
u
is purely outgoing of constant type. In case
\mu=\pm 1
, then
u
has degree at most 1.

Proof.

On each cusp
\mathcal{C}_{i}
and each funnel
\mathcal{F}_{j}
of
\mathcal{G}\setminus\mathcal{H}
, with polynomials
p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}
and
p_{\mathcal{F}_{j}}
, one immediately calculates that for vertex
k\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
and
v\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
with
d(o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}},v)=d
, we have

\displaystyle\Big[\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)u\Big](k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(k-1)\mu+p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(k+1)\mu^{-1}-p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(k)(\mu+\mu^{-1}))\Big(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\Big)^{k}

\displaystyle\Big[\Big(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\Big)u\Big](v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(d-1)\mu+p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(d+1)\mu^{-1}-p_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(d)(\mu+\mu^{-1}))\Big(\mu\sqrt{q}\Big)^{-d}.

In general, if
f(x)
is a polynomial whose highest order term is of the form
c_{d}x^{d}
, then
g(x)=f(x-1)\mu+f(x+1)\mu-f(x)(\mu+\mu^{-1})
is degree at most
d-1
, and the coefficient of
x^{d-1}
in
g(x)
is
c_{d}d(\mu-\mu^{-1})
which is non-zero if
\mu\neq\pm 1
. Therefore, in this case,
g(x)
is a polynomial of degree exactly
d-1
. This shows inductively that if
\mu\neq\pm 1
and if
u
is purely outgoing of degree
d
, then
(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu))^{J}u
is purely outgoing of degree
d-J
(with the identically zero function viewed as the unique polynomial of degree
-k
for every
k\geq 1
). As the only purely outgoing solution which is identically zero on
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
is the function for which all of the associated polynomials are identically zero, the result follows.

The case of
\mu=\pm 1
easily follows from direct analysis.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

If
0\neq u
is purely outgoing with parameter
\mu\neq\pm 1
and

\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\right)u=0,

then
\mu
is a resonance.

Proof.

Since
0
is a purely outgoing solution of constant type by Lemma
6.5
we found two purely outgoing solutions so that Proposition
6.4
implies that
\mu
is a resonance.
∎

Definition 6.7
.

If
\mu_{0}
is a resonance we write, for
\mu
in a small neighborhood of
\mu_{0}
,

R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{A_{j}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}+R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu),

where
A_{j}
are finite rank operators and
R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
is holomorphic. Recall that we define

\mathrm{grst}(A_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{0})\coloneqq\textnormal{span}(\mathrm{im}A_{1}\cup\dots\cup\mathrm{im}A_{J})

the space of
generalized resonant states at
\mu_{0}
. We also define

\mathrm{rst}(A_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{0})\coloneqq\textnormal{grst}(A_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{0})\cap\ker\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\right)

the space of
resonant states at
\mu_{0}
.
Note that, as each
A_{j}
has finite rank,
\mathrm{im}_{C_{c}(\mathcal{V})}A_{j}=\mathrm{im}_{q^{-Nd(o,\cdot)}\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})}A_{j}
.

Before discussing the next proposition, we wish to motivate it by discussing the structure of the resolvent of an operator
B
on a finite-dimensional space near an eigenvalue. Suppose
\lambda_{0}
is an eigenvalue. Then near
\lambda_{0}
we have an expansion of the form

\displaystyle(\lambda-B)^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{(B-\lambda_{0})^{j-1}\Pi_{\lambda_{0}}}{(\lambda-\lambda_{0})^{j}}+R(\lambda),

where
R(\lambda)
is a holomorphic family of operators near
\lambda_{0}
,
\Pi_{\lambda_{0}}
is the projection onto the
\lambda_{0}
generalized eigenspace (which necessarily commutes with
B
and thus with
B-\lambda_{0}
), and
J
is such that
(B-\lambda_{0})^{J}\Pi_{\lambda_{0}}=0=((B-\lambda_{0})\Pi_{\lambda_{0}})^{J}
. Note that in particular we have that
(B-\lambda_{0})\Pi_{\lambda_{0}}
is nilpotent. This result can be proved easily using the Jordan normal form. See e.g.
[
14
]
. In light of the below proposition, the above expansion motivates generalized resonant states as the analogues of generalized eigenfunctions, and resonant states as the analogues of eigenfunctions.

6.2.
Generalized resonant states:
\mu_{0}\neq\pm 1

Proposition 6.8
.

Let
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
be a resonance. Then there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mu_{0}
such that

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{A_{-j}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}+R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{B_{-j}}{(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{j}}+R_{\textnormal{hol}}^{\prime}(\mu)

where
R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
and
R_{\textnormal{hol}}^{\prime}(\mu)
are holomorphic and

(1)

We have
B_{-j}=(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0}))^{j-1}B_{-1}
.

(2)

-B_{-1}
is a (finite rank) projection operator.

(3)

\textnormal{span}(\textnormal{im}(B_{-1})\cup\dots\cup\textnormal{im}(B_{-J}))=\textnormal{span}(\textnormal{im}(A_{-1})\cup\dots\cup\textnormal{im}(A_{-J}))
.

Proof.

Since
z^{\prime}(\mu_{0})\neq 0
as long as
\mu_{0}\neq\pm 1
, the inverse function theorem for holomorphic functions implies that there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\mu_{0}
such that
z(\mu):U\to V
is biholomorphic with inverse
\mu(z):V\to U
.

Property (1) now follows immediately from the identity
(\frac{A}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})-(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))R(\mu)=I
by comparing coefficients in the Laurent series on both sides. Property (2) follows from standard resolvent formalism arguments; see e.g. the proof of Theorem C.9 in
[
18
]
.

Property (3) essentially just follows by analyzing how Laurent series expansions change under holomorphic change of coordinates. We can write
\mu(z)-\mu_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}(z-z(\mu_{0}))^{k}
with
a_{1}\neq 0
. We have that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}=\frac{1}{(z-z(\mu_{0}))^{j}}\frac{1}{(a_{1}+a_{2}(z-z(\mu_{0}))+\dots)^{j}},

and
\frac{1}{(a_{1}+a_{2}(z-z(\mu_{0}))+\dots)^{j}}
is holomorphic near
z(\mu_{0})
. We thus see that
A_{-j}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
B_{-k}
with
k\geq j
and, by reversing the roles of
z
and
\mu
, we see that
B_{-j}
can be expressed as a linear combination of
A_{-k}
with
k\geq j
. This shows Property (3).
∎

We can characterize the generalized resonant states as follows:

Theorem 6.9
.

If
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
is a resonance, then

\mathrm{grst}(A_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{0})=\left\{u\in C(\mathcal{V})\mid u\text{ purely outgoing},\,\exists\ J\in\mathbb{N}\colon\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\right)^{J}u=0\right\}.

Proof.

Let us first prove that the left hand side is contained in the right hand side. Let
u\in\mathrm{grst}(A_{\mathcal{G}},\mu_{0})
and recall that
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=P(\mu)(I+K(\mu))^{-1}
. We can write

R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=P(\mu)(I+K(\mu))^{-1}=\left(\sum_{k=-1}^{\infty}P_{k}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{k}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{B_{-j}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}+B_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)\right)

for some finite range operators
B_{j}\colon C_{c}(\mathcal{V})\rightarrow C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
and a holomorphic operator
B_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
; let
B_{0}
be the constant term in the power series expansion of
B_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
at
\mu_{0}
. Since
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)
is meromorphic we may write

R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{A_{-j}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}+R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu).

We get that

A_{-j}=\sum_{k=-1}^{j}P_{k}B_{-j-k}.

In case
\mu_{0}\notin\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
, we have that
P_{-1}=0
, and we immediately get that every element in the image of
A_{-j}
is purely outgoing by Proposition
6.3
.

Suppose
\mu_{0}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\}
. It is clear that the image of every
A_{-k}
with
k\geq 2
is purely outgoing. However, for
k=-1
, we must consider the term
P_{-1}B_{0}
, and
B_{0}
does not necessarily map compactly supported functions to compactly supported functions. In case
\mu_{0}=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
, then
P_{-1}
only arises from the part of
P(\mu)
arising from the funnels. From the explicit formula (
4.1
), it is clear that the image of
P_{-1}
consists of functions which are constant on the funnels in case
\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
, or else are constant times
(-1)^{d(o,x)}
on the funnels in case
\mu_{0}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. These are exactly the outgoing conditions for such values of
\mu_{0}
. We can perform a similar analysis in case
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
. We thus see that in all cases we have that
\textnormal{im}(A_{-j})
is purely outgoing.

We now consider the other direction. For this we will use in a crucial way that
\mu_{0}\neq\pm 1
. Let
u
be purely outgoing with parameter
\mu_{0}
and annihilated by
(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0}))^{M}
for some
M
. Suppose the exceptional locus of
u
is
H
. Let
u_{\mu}
denote the function which agrees with
u
on
\mathcal{H}
, and is purely outgoing with parameter
\mu
and with the same polynomials on each funnel and cusp as
u
.

Then

f(\mu)\coloneqq\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\right)^{M}u_{\mu}

is finitely supported by Lemma
6.5
(which crucially uses the fact that
\mu_{0}\neq\pm 1
) and
f(\mu_{0})=0
so that we may write
f(\mu)=(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{k}\tilde{f}(\mu)
for some function
\tilde{f}
with
\tilde{f}(\mu_{0})\neq 0
and some
k
.

Let
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)^{M}
be defined via meromorphic continuation as the
M
th power of
R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)
on the locus of
\mu
on which it makes sense to take an
M
th power (i.e.
|\mu|\gg 1
). Suppose near
\mu_{0}
we have that

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\sum_{j=-J}^{\infty}B_{j}(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{j}.

We claim then that near
\mu_{0}
,

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)^{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=-J}^{\infty}\binom{j}{M-1}B_{j}(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{j-(M-1)}

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=-J-(M-1)}^{-1-(M-1)}\binom{\ell+(M-1)}{M-1}B_{\ell+(M-1)}(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{\ell}+R^{\prime}_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu),

where
R^{\prime}_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
is holomorphic at
\mu_{0}
. This essentially follows from the identity

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(1-z)^{M}}=\frac{1}{(M-1)!}\frac{d^{M-1}}{dz^{M-1}}\frac{1}{1-z}.

For
z(\mu)\gg 1
, we have the identity

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)^{M}=\frac{1}{(M-1)!}\frac{d^{M-1}}{dz^{M-1}}R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu(z)).

By meromorphic continuation, we see that this identity is always true. The identity in (
6.2
) then follows from simply termwise differentiating the Laurent series for
R(\mu)
.

But then, for
\mu
close to
\mu_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle u_{\mu}

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)^{M}(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{k}\tilde{f}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{\ell=-J-(M-1)}^{-1-(M-1)}\binom{\ell+(M-1)}{M-1}B_{\ell+(M-1)}(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{\ell}+R^{\prime}_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)\right)(z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0}))^{k}\tilde{f}(\mu).

Let
L
be the most negative number such that
B_{L+(M-1)}\tilde{f}(\mu_{0})\neq 0
. We must have that
k\geq-L
since the LHS is defined at
\mu=\mu_{0}
. However, if
k>-L
, then the RHS would vanish at
\mu=\mu_{0}
. Thus in fact
k=-L
, and we get
u_{\mu}=B_{L+(M-1)}\tilde{f}(\mu)+S_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)\tilde{f}(\mu)
, where
S_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)
is a holomorphic family of operators which vanishes at
\mu=\mu_{0}
. Thus
u=u_{\mu_{0}}=B_{L+(M-1)}\tilde{f}(\mu_{0})
, so
u
is a generalized resonant state.
∎

6.3.
Generalized resonant states:
\mu_{0}=\pm 1

We now examine that case of
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
. These points are tricky for a number of interrelated reasons which all center around the fact that
\pm 1
are the branch points of our parametrization of
\mu\mapsto z(\mu)
.

Proposition 6.10
.

At
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
, we have that the resolvent has a pole of order at most 2. Let
w(\mu):=\frac{\mu-\mu_{0}}{\sqrt{2}\mu^{\frac{1}{2}}}
. Suppose for
\mu
close to
\mu_{0}
we write:

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\frac{A_{-2}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{2}}+\frac{A_{-1}}{\mu-\mu_{0}}+R_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu)=\frac{B_{-2}}{w(\mu)^{2}}+\frac{B_{-1}}{w(\mu)}+R^{\prime}_{\textnormal{hol}}(\mu).

Then

(1)

w(\mu)^{2}=z(\mu)-z(\mu_{0})
.

(2)

All elements in
\textnormal{im}(B_{-2})
and
\textnormal{im}(B_{-1})
are eigenfunctions of
\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}
with eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
.

(3)

The image of
B_{-2}
is exactly the
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunctions of
\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}
with eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
.

(4)

\textnormal{span}(\textnormal{im}(A_{-2})\cup\textnormal{im}(A_{-1}))=\textnormal{span}(\textnormal{im}(B_{-2})\cup\textnormal{im}(B_{-1}))
.

Proof.

First off we remark that it is clear that
w(\mu)
is a holomorphic change of variables for
\mu
close to
\mu_{0}
. Furthermore, claim (1) is obvious. Granting for now that at
\mu_{0}
we have a pole of order at most 2, then Claim (2) follows immediately from the identity
\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})-w(\mu)^{2}\Big)R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=I
. Claim (4) follows in the same way that Claim (3) was proved in Proposition
6.8
.

We now follow the strategy of Lemma 8.8 of Borthwick
[
9
]
. The strategy is to use
\ell^{2}
estimates for the resolvent in order to show that it can maximally diverge to second order at
\mu_{0}
. Note that for this argument to work it is essential that
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
lie in the
\ell^{2}
spectrum of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
and can be approached from the
\ell^{2}
resolvent set, so the
\ell^{2}
resolvent and our meromorphically continued resolvent coincide.

As a first step to get the
\ell^{2}
bounds we note that, because
A_{\mathcal{G}}
is self-adjoint, we get that, for
\phi\in C_{c}(X)
,

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{X}\bar{\phi}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}\Big)\phi dx\Big|\geq\Big|\textnormal{Im}\int_{X}\bar{\phi}\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}\Big)\phi dx\Big|=\Big|\textnormal{Im}\big(\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}\big)\Big|\|\phi\|^{2}.

By (1) we have
\textnormal{Im}(\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2})=\textnormal{Im}(w(\mu)^{2})
. Applying Cauchy-Schwarz, we get

\displaystyle\Big|\left(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu)\right)\phi\Big|\geq|\textnormal{Im}(w(\mu)^{2})|\|\phi\|.

This in turn implies the bound

\displaystyle\|R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)\|\leq\frac{1}{|\textnormal{Im}(w(\mu)^{2}|},

for
\mu
close to
\mu_{0}
. This in turn implies that
R(\mu)
has a pole of order at most 2 at
\mu_{0}
.

We thus see that
w(\mu)^{2}R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)
is a well-defined operator near
\mu=\mu_{0}
acting from
C_{c}(\mathcal{V})
to
C(\mathcal{V})
. Furthermore, from the
\ell^{2}
bound, anything in its image is in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
. Therefore anything in the image of
B_{-2}
is in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
. On the other hand, we already know by Claim (2) that everything in the image must be an eigenfunction with eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
.

On the other hand, let
f\in\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
be an eigenfunction
\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{q}}-z(\mu_{0})\Big)f=0
. Then we consider (again by approaching
\mu_{0}
outside of the
\ell^{2}
resolvent set)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{2}}-z(\mu)\Big)f=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)\Big(\frac{A_{\mathcal{G}}}{2\sqrt{2}}-z(\mu_{0})+w(\mu)^{2}\Big)f

\displaystyle=R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)w(\mu)^{2}f=B_{-2}f+O(w(\mu))

We thus conclude, by taking the limit
\mu\to\mu_{0}
, that
f\in\textnormal{im}(B_{-2})
.

∎

Proposition 6.11
.

Suppose
\phi
is an outgoing eigenfunction of constant type. Then it satisfies the outgoing condition on the entirety of all of the funnels and cusps.

Proof.

On the cusps this is immediate since if we know the value on an infinite subray of the cusp, the fact that we have an eigenfunction and that each vertex of the cusp has only two neighbors uniquely determines the value of the eigenfunction on the rest of the cusp, and it is immediate that it is indeed outgoing with the same parameters.

On the funnels, the proof is also straightforward. We argue by contradiction. Suppose
x
is a vertex of maximal height where the outgoing condition begins to hold, i.e. for every vertex of strictly higher height, the outgoing condition already holds. Let
y
be a descendant one step higher than
x
with value
c
. Then the
q
descendants of
y
must have value
c\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}\mu}
. We get the equation

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}\left(\frac{cq}{\sqrt{q}\mu}+\phi(x)\right)=c\cdot\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}

and when we solve we get
\phi(x)=c\mu\sqrt{q}
. This implies that all other descendant neighbors of
x
also have value
c
, and that
\phi(x)
together with its descendants satisfies the outgoing condition, contradicting the fact that we assumed that
x
didn’t satisfy it.
∎

Proposition 6.12
.

Suppose
\phi
is an eigenfunction which is outgoing on all the funnels and cusps and is in
\ell^{2}(\mathcal{V})
and has eigenvalue
z(\mu)
with
\mu
on the unit circle. Then
\phi
is identically zero on all of the funnels and cusps.

Proof.

On the cusps for example, the value at height
k
is
c(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{k}
for some
c
. If we compute the contribution to the
\ell^{2}
-norm from the cusp, we obtain

\displaystyle c\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{k}\cdot q^{-k}

which is
\infty
unless
c=0
.

Similarly the outgoing condition on the funnels implies that the value is
c(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}\mu})^{k}
for vertices at distance
k
from the root of the funnel. The contribution to the
\ell^{2}
-norm from the funnels is thus

\displaystyle c\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}q^{-k}\cdot q^{k}

which again is infinite unless
c=0
.
∎

Theorem 6.13
.

The generalized resonant states for
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
are the outgoing eigenfunctions of constant type with eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{L}
is the compact core,
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
is all vertices of distance at most one from the compact core, and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
is all vertices of distance at most two from the compact core. Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
denote all vertices on any funnel and at distance at least one from the root of the funnel (and recall that the root of every funnel lies in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}
). Similarly, let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
denote all vertices on any cusp and at distance at least one from the root of the cusps (and, similarly, recall that the root of every cusp lies in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\setminus\mathcal{L}
). In particular we have
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{L}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\sqcup\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

The proof makes use of the explicit formula for the resolvent as
P(\mu)(I+K(\mu))^{-1}
. Recall that
P(\mu)
can be decomposed into three parts; see (
5.8
). One part is constant, call it
Q
, for which the image of any element only depends on its restriction to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
, and its image is supported on
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
. Additionally we have a part
F(\mu)
coming from the funnels for which the image of any element only depends on its restriction to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
and has image supported on
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
, and a part
C(\mu)
coming from the cusps for which the image of any element only depends on its restriction to
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and has image supported on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

The components
F(\mu)
and
C(\mu)
are given by a very explicit formula. We first discuss
F(\mu)
. Suppose
f
is a function supported on
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}
be all vertices on the funnel
\mathcal{F}_{j}
at distance at least one from the root. Then
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
consists of
q
vertices
v^{(j)}_{1},\dots,v^{(j)}_{q}
which are all at distance one from the root. Note that
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}
consists itself of
q
funnels with roots
v^{(j)}_{1},\dots,v^{(j)}_{q}
. Since
F(\mu)
is holomorphic at
\mu=\pm 1
, we can express the power series expansion of
F(\mu)
via differentiation. Suppose
w
is a vertex on the funnel of
\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}
with root
v^{(j)}_{i}
. We see that

\displaystyle[F(\mu)f](w)=-\frac{2}{\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1}}\Big(\frac{f(v_{i}^{(j)})}{(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{d(o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}},w)-1}}+\frac{\sum_{k\neq i}f(v_{k}^{(j)})}{(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{d(o_{\mathcal{F}_{j}},w)+1}}\Big).

The key observation is the following. If we know that
[F(\mu_{0})f](w)=0
for all
w\in\mathcal{F}_{j}^{\prime}
, then we must have that
f(v_{i}^{(j)})=0
for all
i
. The former condition is equivalent to the vector
[f(v_{1}^{(j)}),\dots,f(v_{q}^{(j)}]^{T}
being in the kernel of the matrix
(1-(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2})I+(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2}M
where
M
is the matrix of all ones. Note that the eigenvalues of
M
are
q
and
0
. In order for
(1-(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2})I+(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2}M
to have non-trivial kernel, we must have that either
(1-(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2})=0
or
(1-(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2})=-q(\sqrt{q}\mu)^{-2}
. We can check that
\mu=\pm 1
does not satisfy either of these equations, which implies that
[f(v_{1}^{(j)}),\dots,f(v_{q}^{(j)}]^{T}
is identically zero. This would then imply that not only is
[F(\mu_{0})f](w)=0
, but
[F(\mu)f](w)\equiv 0
as a function of
\mu
. The desired conclusion which we shall use later in the proof is the following: if
E
is an operator whose image lies in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
, and
F(\mu_{0})E=0
with
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
, then
F(\mu)E=0
. Phrased another way, if we write

\displaystyle F(\mu)=F_{0}+F_{1}(\mu-\mu_{0})+F_{2}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{2}+\dots

with
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
, then if
F_{0}E=0
with
E
as before, then
F_{j}E=0
for all
j
.

We can perform a nearly identical analysis on
C(\mu)
. As before, suppose
f
is supported on
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\prime}
denote all vertices on the cusp
\mathcal{C}_{j}
at distance at least one from the root. Then
\mathcal{C}_{j}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
consists of a single vertex,
v^{(j)}
, which is distance one from the root. Suppose
w
is some vertex on
\mathcal{C}_{j}
. Then

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu-\mu^{-1}}{2}[C(\mu)f](w)=f(v^{(j)})(\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu})^{d(w,v^{(j)})}.

We thus see that if
-\frac{\mu-\mu^{-1}}{2}[C(\mu_{0})f]=0
, then in fact
f(v^{(j)})=0
for all
j
, and thus
-\frac{\mu-\mu^{-1}}{2}[C(\mu)f]\equiv 0
. We can write

\displaystyle C(\mu)=\frac{C_{-1}}{\mu-\mu_{0}}+C_{0}+C_{1}(\mu-\mu_{0})+\dots

with
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
. We thus see that if
E
is an operator whose image lies in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
and such that
C_{-1}E=0
, then
C_{j}E=0
for every
j
.

We next recall from the proof of Proposition
5.2
that if we write

\displaystyle(I+K(\mu))^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\frac{B_{-j}}{(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}}+R_{H}(\mu),

we must have that each
B_{-j}
has image lying in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime\prime}
.

We now explicitly examine the resolvent

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}(\mu)=\left(Q+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}F_{j}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}+\sum_{j=-1}^{\infty}C_{j}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}\right)\left(\sum_{j=-J}^{\infty}B_{j}(\mu-\mu_{0})^{j}\right)

with
\mu_{0}=\pm 1
. We know from Proposition
6.10
that we have a pole of order at most 2 at
\mu_{0}
. We must therefore have that
C_{-1}B_{k}=0
for any
k\leq-2
, and hence
C_{j}B_{k}=0
for any
k\geq-2
and any
j
. Similarly we must have
F_{0}B_{k}=0
for any
k\leq-3
, and hence
F_{j}B_{k}=0
for any
k\leq-3
and any
j
. Similarly,
QB_{k}=0
for any
k\leq-3
.

We now consider the
(\mu-\mu_{0})^{-2}
term in the expansion, which would be

\displaystyle QB_{-2}+F_{0}B_{-2}+F_{1}B_{-3}+\dots+F_{J-2}B_{-J}+C_{-1}B_{-1}+C_{0}B_{-2}+\dots+C_{J-2}B_{-J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle QB_{-2}+F_{0}B_{-2}+C_{-1}B_{-1}.

We know that any non-zero element in the image must be an
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunction, and hence must be identically zero on each funnel and cusp by Proposition
6.12
. Therefore, we must have that
F_{0}B_{-2}=C_{-1}B_{-1}=0
, and thus
F_{j}B_{-2}=C_{\ell}B_{-1}=0
for all
j
and
\ell
. Finally we examine the
(\mu-\mu_{0})^{-1}
term in the expansion, which is

\displaystyle QB_{-1}+F_{0}B{-1}+F_{1}B_{-2}+\dots+F_{J-1}B_{-J}+C_{-1}B_{0}+C_{0}B_{-1}+\dots+C_{J-1}B_{-J}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle QB_{-1}+F_{0}B_{-1}+C_{-1}B_{0}.

It is clear from this formula that anything in the image is purely outgoing of constant type. We thus see that all generalized resonant states must be eigenfunctions which are purely outgoing of constant type and eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
. To show the converse direction, we can use the exact same argument that we used in the second half of the proof of Theorem
6.9
.

∎

7.
Examples and computations

7.1.
The resonance matrix

We now discuss how we may use the results of the previous section to explicitly compute the resonances and generalized resonant states. Let
A_{\mathcal{L}}
be the adjacency matrix of the induced subgraph corresponding to the compact core
\mathcal{L}
. Let
B(\mu)
be the diagonal matrix with entry
(v,v)
equal to
c_{v}\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}+f_{v}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}\mu}
where
c_{v}
is the number of cusps attached to
v
, and
f_{v}
is the number of funnels attached to
v
. We define the
resonance matrix
as

\displaystyle H(\mu)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}(A_{\mathcal{L}}+B(\mu))-z(\mu)I.

The following is immediate:

Proposition 7.1
.

The resonances are exactly those
\mu_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
such that
\textnormal{det}(H(\mu_{0}))=0
. If
\mu_{0}
is a resonance, then the associated resonant states correspond to elements in the kernel of
H(\mu_{0})
.

Proof.

First off we remark that Property (1) of Proposition
6.8
and Proposition
6.10
imply that any time
\mu_{0}
is a resonance, the set of resonant states associated to
\mu_{0}
is non-trivial. By Theorems
6.9
and
6.13
(along with Lemma
6.5
), we know that resonant states are necessarily outgoing of constant type. However, Proposition
6.11
implies that outgoing eigenfunctions of constant type satisfy the outgoing condition on the entirety of every funnel and cusp. This means that for such outgoing eigenfunctions of constant type, the value on a given funnel or cusp is completely determined by the value at the vertex of
\mathcal{L}
that the funnel/cusp is attached to. More specifically, if
f
is an outgoing eigenfunction of constant type with eigenvalue
z(\mu_{0})
, then given a vertex
v\in\mathcal{L}
, on every vertex
w
on a cusp adjacent to
v
we must have that
f(w)=\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu_{0}}f(v)
, and on every vertex
u
on a funnel adjacent to
v
we must have
f(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}\mu_{0}}f(v)
. Therefore,
f|_{\mathcal{L}}
would lie in the kernel of
H(\mu_{0})
. Conversely, suppose a non-zero function
f
were in the kernel of
H(\mu_{0})
. We can extend
f
to a function on all of
\mathcal{G}
by simply requiring that it satisfy the outgoing condition on the entirety of every funnel and cusp, and it’s clear that such a function would be an outgoing eigenfunction of constant type.
∎

Corollary 7.2
.

The number of resonances, not counted with multiplicity, is at most
2|\mathcal{L}|
.

Proof.

The determinant of
H(\mu)
is a polynomial in
\mu
and
\mu^{-1}
, and thus can be written in the form
\mu^{k}f(\mu)
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and some polynomial
f
. Since each entry in
H(\mu)
can be written as
\mu^{\ell}g(\mu)
where
g
is a polynomial of degree at most 2, we conclude that
f
has degree at most
2|\mathcal{L}|
.
∎

7.2.
The tree

We already computed in Section
4.1.1
, that the resonances of the tree correspond to
\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. Furthermore, the explicit formula for the resolvent in (
4.1
) tells us that the generalized resonant states for
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
is constant functions, and for
-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
it is the functions of the form
(-1)^{d(o,v)}c
for some
c
and choice of origin
o
. We now quickly recompute this using the results of the previous section. We can think of the tree as a compact core consisting of a single vertex
o
together with
(q+1)
attached funnels. Therefore

\displaystyle H(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}\left(\frac{q+1}{\sqrt{q}\mu}\right)-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}=-\frac{(\mu-q^{-1}\mu^{-1})}{2}.

This clearly has zeros at
\mu=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. When
\mu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
, assuming the value at
o
is 1, then the value on each funnel is
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}})^{k}=1
, and we can do the same analysis for
\mu=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
and rederive the corresponding resonant state.

7.3.
Parabolic cylinder

This consists of a single vertex
o
with one attached cusp and
q
attached funnels. The resonance matrix is thus

\displaystyle H(\mu)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}\left(\frac{q}{\sqrt{q}\mu}+\frac{\sqrt{q}}{\mu}\right)-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}=\frac{\mu-\mu^{-1}}{2}.

Thus the resonances are
\pm 1
. The resonant state for
\mu_{0}=1
is 1 at
o
,
q^{k/2}
at height
k
on the cusp, and
q^{-k/2}
at height
k
on the funnels. Similarly, the resonant states for
\mu_{0}=-1
is 1 at
o
,
(-1)^{k}q^{k/2}
at height
k
on the cusp, and
(-1)^{k}q^{-k/2}
at height
k
on the funnels.

7.4.
Hyperbolic cylinder

Consider the
(q+1)
-regular hyperbolic cylinder with central geodesic of length
N
. Then the resonance matrix is the
N\times N
circulant matrix whose first row is

\displaystyle\left[\frac{q-1}{2q\mu}-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}},0,\dots,0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{q}}\right].

Standard facts about circulant matrices imply that the determinant of this matrix is

\displaystyle\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{q-1}{2q\mu}-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)\right).

Thus resonances occur whenever one can find
\mu
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{q-1}{2q\mu}-\frac{\mu+\mu^{-1}}{2}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi j}{N}\right)

One can directly solve that solutions correspond to
\mu=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}e^{2\pi ij/N}
. As
N\to\infty
these equidistribute on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
.

\displaystyle(\mu^{2}+1)^{2}(\mu^{2}-2)(2\mu^{4}-2\mu^{2}+1).

The resonance at
\mu=\sqrt{q}
corresponds to the constant function, and the resonance at
\mu=-\sqrt{q}
corresponds to the function of the form
(-1)^{d}
where
d=0
or
1
based on the bipartite coloring of the graph. These are clearly
\ell^{2}
-eigenvalues.

We see that we also have resonances at
\mu=\pm i
. In computing the corresponding resonant states, we find that they are in fact
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunctions (both
\mu=i
and
\mu=-i
have two associated
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunctions).

Finally, we are left with the polynomial
2\mu^{4}-2\mu^{2}+1
. This in fact corresponds to the numerator of the Hasse-Weil zeta function associated to the elliptic curve (or equivalently, the Dedekind zeta function attached to the function field of the elliptic curve). The Hasse-Weil zeta function of a (projective) curve over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is defined as

\displaystyle Z_{X}(T):=\exp\Big(\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{N_{r}}{r}T^{r}\Big),

where
N_{r}
is the number of points on
X
over the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{r}}
. Weil
[
47
]
proved several remarkable facts about such zeta functions. One is that

\displaystyle Z_{X}(T)=\frac{P(T)}{(1-T)(1-qT)},

where
P(T)
is a polynomial of degree
2g
where
g
is the genus of
X
. Second is that all zeros of the polynomial
P(T)
lie on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
; this is the analogue of the Riemann hypothesis in this setting (but is indeed known to be true!).

We can compute the Hasse-Weil zeta function of the projective closure of our elliptic curve, and we get

\displaystyle Z_{X}(T)=\frac{2T^{2}-2T+1}{(1-T)(1-2T)}.

Letting
P(T)
, be the denominator, we find that
P(\mu^{2})
is exactly equal to the unaccounted for polynomial in the determinant of the resonance matrix. The Riemann hypothesis (Weil’s theorem) implies that all the associated resonances lie on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{2^{\frac{1}{4}}}
.

We now compute another example. This second example is based on the example accompanying Figure 5 in
[
45
]
. Consider the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}+x+1
over
\mathbb{F}_{3}
. The corresponding graph with stabilizer functions is given in Figure
7.3
.

Figure 7.3
.
Stabilizer size data for the quotient graph of groups corresponding to (
7.2
).

We find that the resonances correspond to zeros of the polynomial

\displaystyle(\mu^{2}+1)^{2}(\mu-1)^{2}(\mu+1)^{2}(\mu^{2}-3)(3\mu^{4}+1).

(7.2)

We again get the “trivial” resonances at
\mu=\pm\sqrt{3}
. We also compute that we get
\ell^{2}
-eigenvalues at
\mu=\pm i
(each of multiplicity 2). We also have resonances at
\mu=\pm 1
(each of multiplicity 2). However, we compute that they are in fact not
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunctions.

On the other hand, the Hasse-Weil zeta function of the projective closure of this curve is

\displaystyle\frac{3T^{2}+1}{(1-T)(1-3T)}.

Again, setting
T=\mu^{2}
, the numerator of the Hasse-weil zeta function shows up in the polynomial governing the resonances, and all the associated resonances lie on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{3^{\frac{1}{4}}}
.

7.8.
Arithmetic lattices

The resonances of the modular curve
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})\backslash\mathbb{H}
come in three types (see, e.g.,
[
28
]
):

(1)

A trivial resonance at
s=1
(corresponding to the constant function).

(2)

Resonances corresponding to the other
L^{2}
-eigenvalues. By the Selberg conjecture, these are expected to all lie on the line
\textnormal{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{2}
.

(3)

Resonances corresponding to poles of the Eisenstein series/scattering matrix, which is given explicitly by

\displaystyle\phi(s)=\frac{\xi(2s-1)}{\xi(2s)},

where
\xi
is the completed Riemann zeta function. By the Riemann hypothesis, this is expected to have all of its poles lying on
\textnormal{Re}(s)=\frac{1}{4}
.

More generally, let
F
be a number field, and let
\mathcal{O}
be its ring of integers. Let
M_{F}^{\infty}
denote the set of infinite places of
F
, and for each
v\in M_{F}^{\infty}
let
F_{v}
denote the associated local field (i.e.
F_{v}\simeq\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
). Then the group
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathcal{O})
is a non-uniform lattice in
\prod\limits_{v\in M^{\infty}_{F}}\textnormal{SL}(2,F_{v})
. We thus obtain the locally symmetric space

\displaystyle\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathcal{O})\backslash\prod\limits_{v\in M^{\infty}_{F}}\textnormal{SL}(2,F_{v})/\prod\limits_{v\in M^{\infty}_{F}}K_{v},

(7.3)

where
K_{v}
is the maximal compact subgroup of
\textnormal{SL}(2,F_{v})
. This is a quotient of the symmetric space
(\mathbb{H}^{2})^{r}\times(\mathbb{H}^{3})^{c}
where
r
is the number of real embeddings of
F
, and
2c
is the number of complex embeddings (which come in
c
complex conjugate pairs). The cusps of this locally symmetric space correspond to elements in the class group of
F
. Spectral theory in this setting is more complicated in part due to the fact that one must consider the full algebra generated by the Laplacians in each factor. It is better to pass to the representation theoretic perspective; see e.g.
[
21
]
. Without going into too much detail, resonances in this context, properly defined and parametrized, correspond to joint eigenvalues of joint
L^{2}
-eigenfunctions of all these partial Laplacians. By the generalized Ramanujan conjecture such eigenvalues are expected to be either trivial or tempered. Additionally we have resonances corresponding to poles of the meromorphic continuation of the constant term of the Eisenstein series/scattering determinant. The Langlands-Shahidi method
[
44
]
relates the Eisenstein series to the Dedekind zeta function of the number field, and the generalized Riemann hypothesis predicts that its zeros (which give poles of the scattering determinant) all lie on the critical line. Finally there are “topological” resonances at the special value (
s=\frac{1}{2}
for hyperbolic surfaces) whose multiplicity is expressed in terms of the class number and the trace of the scattering matrix; for the modular curve this value happens to be zero.

We now wish to describe how we are observing the same phenomenon in the examples from Section
7.7
. In the set-up described in Section
3.4
, we start with a smooth projective algebraic curve
C
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a point
P
on
C
(which we think of as a “point at
\infty
”). The analogue of (
7.3
) is (
3.1
). Thus in analogy with the number field setting, we should expect the resonances to be of the following form:

(1)

Trivial resonances at
\pm\sqrt{q}
corresponding to trivial
\ell^{2}
-eigenfunctions.

(2)

Resonances corresponding to the other
\ell^{2}
-eigenvalues which all lie on the unit circle (the analogue of the Selberg conjecture) as a consequence of Drinfeld’s proof of the Langlands correspondence for
\textnormal{GL}(2,\mathbb{F}_{q}(C))

[
16
,
17
]
.

(3)

Resonances on the circle of radius
\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{4}}}
corresponding to square roots of zeros of the Hasse-Weil zeta function of
C
. The connection between resonances and the Hasse-Weil zeta function (the function field analogue of the Dedekind zeta function) should come from the connection of them both to Eisenstein series (via the Lax-Phillips scattering theory and the Langlands-Shahidi method, respectively). The fact that they all lie on this circle is a consequence of Weil’s proof of the Riemann hypothesis in this setting
[
47
]
.

(4)

Resonances at
\mu=\pm 1
whose multiplicity is related to the number of cusps and the functional equation of the zeta function/trace of the scattering matrix.

In follow-up work we plan to make this correspondence precise as part of our planned follow-up work connecting resonances with the Lax-Phillips scattering theory in this setting.

Large random geometrically finite graphs

We now show numerics of large random geometrically finite graphs. To generate the below graphs, which were generated using Maple
[
34
]
, we started with a random
7
-regular graph on 500 vertices (thus
q=6
). We then randomly chose
c
edges which we removed and replaced with a cusp at each endpoint of the removed edge, and randomly chose
f
of the remaining edges of the original graph which we also removed and replaced with a funnel at each endpoint of the removed edge. We then computed those
\mu
such that the associated resonance matrix becomes non-invertible. On the graph we show the unit circle (in blue), the circle of radius
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
(in green), the points
\pm\sqrt{q}
(in black) and the points
\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
(in green). We remark that, by the Kesten-Mckay law, a large random finite regular graph will have nearly all of its resonances (which correspond to eigenvalues) on the unit circle (which, with respect to Laplacian eigenvalue, corresponds to the Ramanujan range
[-2\sqrt{q},2\sqrt{q}]
).

We begin by showing what happens if we add more and more cusps (and no funnels); see Figure
7.4
. Visually, we observe that the resonances appear to either get pushed onto the real axis, or close to the imaginary axis (but away from 0). However if keep adding cusps, then all of the resonances are on the real line. As we add more and more, the resonances are pushed closer and closer to
\pm\sqrt{q}
. This makes sense because if we replace all edges by cusps, then we get a bunch of disconnected graphs consisting of one vertex attached to
q+1
cusps. From the perspective of spectral theory, this is essentially the same thing as the modular curve (Section
7.6
) which we computed to have resonances only at
\pm\sqrt{q}
.

We now consider the case of adding more and more funnels (but no cusps); see Figure
7.5
. This has the effect of pushing the resonances towards the circle of radius
\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
. This makes sense when we consider the case of the hyperbolic cylinder from Section
7.4
with longer and longer central geodesic (in which case the resonances were equidistributing on this circle). However, as we keep adding more and more funnels, they begin to either be close to zero or be exactly
\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}

\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}
