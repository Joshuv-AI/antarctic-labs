Persistent sleep disturbances in disaster-exposed children: the longitudinal impact of maternal psychopathology
Authors: Elif Kocak, Serkan Gunes, Nazmiye Ince, Dilek Altun Varmis, Emine Merve Kalinli, Gupse Inal Ulutas, Kubra Sahin, Tugce Hilda Demirci, Emine Cilogulları, Elif Gozde Yuce Antepuzumu, Cumali Yuksekkaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNatural disasters significantly impact children’s physiological and psychological health, with sleep disturbances being among the most prevalent consequences. This study aims to investigate the longitudinal relationship between maternal psychopathology and children’s sleep disturbances after the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes.MethodsA total of 132 mother-child dyads (children aged 8–12 years) who directly experienced the earthquakes were assessed at 3 months (T1) and 12 months (T2) post-disaster. Children’s sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), while maternal depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress symptoms were evaluated using the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and PTSD Checklist for DSM-5 (PCL-5), respectively.ResultsChildren’s overall sleep quality improved from T1 to T2, although 65.9% continued to report poor sleep quality at T2. Maternal PTSD symptoms decreased significantly over time, whereas reductions in depression and anxiety were non-significant. Maternal psychopathology variables (BDI, STAI, PCL-5) were significantly correlated with children’s PSQI scores at T2. Hierarchical regression analysis revealed that baseline child sleep disturbances, maternal psychopathology, earthquake exposure severity, and maternal education level together explained 38% of the variance in children’s sleep quality at T2.ConclusionsMaternal mental health is significantly associated with the persistence of children’s sleep disturbances following the earthquakes. Interventions targeting maternal psychopathology and early sleep problems may help improve long-term sleep outcomes in disaster-affected children.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, Earthquake, Family impact, Natural disaster, Post-disaster psychology, PTSD, Sleep disorders

Background

Natural disasters, such as earthquakes, are among the most severe and unpredictable traumatic events affecting large populations worldwide. On February 6, 2023, southeastern Türkiye experienced two major earthquakes with magnitudes of 7.8 and 7.5. These quakes caused massive damage, numerous fatalities, and serious upheaval for both individuals and communities [1]. Children are particularly vulnerable in the aftermath of such disasters, as they can face sudden loss of safety, displacement, and potential bereavement, all of which can have long-lasting physiological and psychological impacts [2].

Among the various sequelae of trauma, sleep disturbances have emerged as a prominent and persistent problem in children exposed to disasters. Sleep problems may arise both as immediate stress reactions and as chronic conditions that interfere with emotional regulation, cognitive development, and physical health [3, 4]. Indeed, poor sleep quality has been shown to exacerbate symptoms of anxiety and depression and to increase the risk of developing posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in children and adolescents [5].

Mothers frequently serve as primary caregivers and emotional anchors in post-disaster contexts, and their psychological functioning can significantly influence children’s recovery processes [6, 7]. The intergenerational transmission of trauma suggests that maternal psychopathology may not only reflect shared exposure but also actively mediate child outcomes through both behavioral and biological mechanisms. Maternal psychiatric problems may disrupt caregiving behaviors, undermine emotional availability, and impair the establishment of consistent routines, which are essential for healthy sleep development in children [6, 8]. Maternal stress may also affect the family’s emotional atmosphere through mechanisms such as altered cortisol secretion. This physiological dysregulation in the mother can contribute to a state of hyperarousal in the child, disrupting the relaxation processes necessary for sleep [6]. Moreover, family systems theory posits that the family functions as an emotional unit, where distinct patterns of emotional reactivity in one member can destabilize the entire system. In this framework, maternal psychopathology does not exist in isolation but serves as a regulatory context that directly impacts the child’s physiological and emotional equilibrium, including sleep architecture [9].

Although disaster-related sleep disturbances in children have received increasing attention, the role of familial and environmental factors in shaping these outcomes remains underexplored. In particular, maternal mental health is a critical yet often overlooked determinant. This focus is particularly pertinent within the cultural context of Türkiye, where families often exhibit a collectivist structure with mothers serving as the primary emotional anchor and caregiver [10]. This intense maternal involvement may influence both the management of the child’s condition and the family’s help-seeking behaviors, creating a specific backdrop for interpreting the study’s findings.

The present study aims to investigate the longitudinal relationship between maternal psychopathology and children’s sleep disturbances following the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes. Specifically, it examines changes in maternal mental health and child sleep quality over the first post-disaster year and explores how maternal depressive symptoms, anxiety, and PTSD predict sleep outcomes in children. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for developing integrated, family-centered intervention strategies to promote resilience and long-term well-being in disaster-affected populations. Based on the existing literature and theoretical framework, we hypothesized that higher levels of maternal psychopathology would significantly predict greater sleep disturbances in children at 12 months post-disaster, even after controlling for baseline sleep problems, earthquake exposure severity, and sociodemographic factors. This longitudinal timeframe was specifically chosen to distinguish between the acute stress period and long-term psychopathological outcomes.

Methods

Participants

This study involved children and their mothers who were in the affected region during the Türkiye earthquakes and directly experienced events such as structural collapse, emergency evacuation, injury, or bereavement. They were all referred to the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Clinic at Adana City Research and Training Hospital (ACRTH). ACRTH is a major tertiary hospital situated in the southern part of Türkiye and serves as a key medical center for patient care after the earthquakes. Participants were recruited from individuals referred to the clinic for post-disaster evaluation, rather than through a general population screening program.

Children were included in the study based on the following criteria: (1) ages 8–12 years, (2) IQ score above 80 (normal cognitive functioning), (3) not having a chronic neurological or medical disease, and (4) no diagnosis of any psychiatric disorders based on DSM-5 before the earthquakes. The inclusion criteria for mothers were as follows: (1) IQ score above 80 (normal cognitive functioning), (2) not having a chronic neurological or medical disease, and (3) no diagnosis of any psychiatric disorders based on DSM-5 before the earthquakes. Individuals with a post-earthquake diagnosis of psychotic or bipolar disorder were excluded from the study.

Assessment timeline

The assessment intervals were selected based on established trajectories of post-disaster psychopathology. Time 1 (T1, May 2023): Conducted at three months post-disaster, this assessment was timed to capture the clinical picture as the window for spontaneous recovery typically begins to close. Literature suggests that while acute stress reactions are common immediately after trauma, symptoms persisting beyond three months are indicative of an established disorder rather than transient distress [11].

Time 2 (T2, February 2024): The 12-month follow-up was designed to evaluate the chronicity of symptoms and identify persistent psychopathology when natural remission is no longer expected. Additionally, this time point coincides with the one-year anniversary of the earthquakes, allowing for the assessment of anniversary reactions, which can trigger symptom exacerbation and provide a robust measure of long-term impact [12].

At T1, a total of 165 children and their mothers were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 23 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria: eight were outside the target age range, five had IQ scores below 80, four had chronic neurological or medical conditions, and six had a pre-earthquake psychiatric diagnosis in either the child or the mother. Thus, the sample comprised 142 children and their mothers at T1.

Participants were re-approached before T2; however, five were unreachable. During the follow-up period, one mother received a diagnosis of psychotic disorder, another was diagnosed with bipolar disorder, and three participants experienced major life stressors (e.g., serious accidents or the loss of a relative). The final sample consisted of 132 child-mother dyads who met study requirements (Fig. 1).

Diagnostic procedures

All participants underwent thorough psychiatric assessments conducted by psychiatry experts based on DSM-5 criteria, both at T1 and T2. The assessment process included detailed clinical interviews with children and mothers, behavioral observations, reviews of official medical records, and administrations of standardized rating scales. The absence of pre-earthquake psychiatric disorders was rigorously verified through a two-step process: detailed retrospective clinical interviews conducted at T1 and a comprehensive review of the participants’ electronic medical records to confirm no prior history of psychiatric diagnosis or treatment. Additionally, a qualified psychologist conducted intellectual assessments on all participants.

Following the psychiatric evaluations, participants were instructed to complete the questionnaires at T1 and T2. Instruments were administered in a standardized, quiet clinical setting with supervision to ensure comprehension. The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was completed by children, and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), and PTSD Checklist for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (PCL-5) were completed by mothers. The Exposure Severity Scale (ESS) was completed by children and mothers only at T1.

Instruments

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a 24-item self-report instrument developed by Buysse et al. [13] and adapted into Turkish by Agargun et al. [14]. The fifth and nineteenth items, which are completed by the participant’s spouse or roommate, are excluded from the total score. Eighteen items included in the total PSQI score are analyzed across the following seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleep medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each component is scored on a scale from 0 to 3, yielding a total PSQI score ranging from 0 to 21. A global score of 5 or above indicates poor sleep quality.

Although originally designed for adults, the PSQI has been widely used in pediatric populations [15, 16]. To ensure validity and comprehension in the 8–12 age group, the scale was administered under the supervision of a researcher, who provided clarifications as needed. In the present study, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.82 at T1 and 0.84 at T2.

Beck depression inventory

The Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) is a self-report measure developed by Beck to assess the risk and severity of depressive symptoms [17]. BDI includes 21 items rated on a 0–3 Likert scale, with total scores ranging from 0 to 63. The validity and reliability of the Turkish version of the scale were established by Hisli [18]. In the current study, a cutoff score of 17 was used to indicate clinical depression, in line with prior research employing the scale in Türkiye [19]. In this study sample, the BDI showed high internal reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.88 at T1 and 0.89 at T2.

State–trait anxiety inventory

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) is a widely used, standardized self-report measure designed to assess anxiety. The instrument consists of two 20-item subscales: the State Anxiety Scale (S-STAI), which evaluates the individual’s current emotional state, and the Trait Anxiety Scale (T-STAI), which measures a general predisposition toward anxiety. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, resulting in subscale scores ranging from 20 to 80. A cutoff score of 39–40 has been commonly recommended to identify clinically significant levels of anxiety [20]. In the present study, the Turkish version of the STAI was employed, and a threshold score of 40 was used to indicate elevated anxiety symptoms [21].

PTSD checklist for diagnostic and statistical manual of mental disorders, fifth edition

The PTSD Checklist for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (PCL-5) is a 20-item self-report instrument designed to assess the presence and severity of PTSD symptoms. Each item is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 80. The PCL-5 includes four symptom clusters corresponding to the DSM-5 criteria for PTSD: re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations, and hyperarousal [22]. The Turkish adaptation and psychometric validation of the scale were conducted by Boysan et al., who identified a cutoff score of 47 or higher as indicative of probable PTSD [23].

Exposure severity scale

The extent of earthquake exposure was evaluated using the 2-point Exposure Severity Scale (ESS), where each of nine specific events was scored as 1 (“yes”) or 0 (“no”). These events included sustaining injuries, loss of close individuals, damage to family or property, witnessing traumatic events, experiencing perceived threat to life, receiving institutional support, and displacement. The total exposure score ranged from 0 to 9, with a score of 3 or greater indicating moderate exposure. This scale has been reliably used in previous research [24].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic characteristics of participants and baseline clinical variables. Continuous variables were presented as means (M) and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots.

To examine longitudinal changes in child sleep outcomes and maternal psychopathology, paired-sample t-tests were conducted for continuous variables. Changes in the proportion of participants meeting clinical cutoff scores across time points were analyzed using McNemar’s test. To investigate the associations between maternal psychopathology and children’s sleep outcomes, Pearson correlation analyses were conducted. A hierarchical linear regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of children’s sleep disturbances. The significance level was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

Sample size and power analysis

A priori power analysis was conducted to determine the required sample size for hierarchical regression analysis. Assuming a medium effect size (f² = 0.15), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 118 participants. The final sample of 132 child-mother dyads in this study exceeded this requirement, suggesting adequate power to detect significant relationships among variables.

Ethical consideration

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Adana City Research and Training Hospital and was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to data collection, all procedures and aims of the study were thoroughly explained to participants. Written informed consent was obtained from adult participants. For child participants (aged 8–12 years), written informed consent to participate was obtained from their parents or legal guardians, and assent was obtained from the children.

Results

In the attrition analysis comparing participants who completed both assessments (T1 and T2) with those who dropped out (n = 10), no significant differences were found in terms of children’s age, maternal age, PSQI total score, BDI, T-STAI, PCL-5 scores, or exposure severity at baseline (all p-values > 0.05).

Characteristics of participants

The final sample consisted of 132 mother-child dyads. The mean ages for children and mothers were 10.18 years (SD = 1.27) and 42.73 years (SD = 4.53), respectively. The child sample was 54.5% female (n = 72). Regarding maternal sociodemographics, the majority of mothers had less than a high school education (63.6%) and were unemployed (75.0%). Most children (84.1%) lived in two-parent households. In terms of earthquake exposure, a substantial proportion of both children (78.0%) and mothers (74.2%) reported moderate to high levels of trauma exposure (ESS ≥ 3). Changes in sleep quality and maternal psychopathology from T1 to T2

Children’s overall sleep quality significantly improved over the one-year period (t(131) = 2.07, p = 0.041, Cohen’s d = 0.18). Specifically, significant reductions were observed in the subcomponents of subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction. Despite the improvement in mean scores, a notably high proportion of children (74.2%, n = 98) exhibited poor sleep quality (PSQI ≥ 5) at T1. This high prevalence remained persistent over time, showing only a non-significant decrease to 65.9% (n = 87) at T2 (p > 0.05).

Regarding maternal mental health, posttraumatic stress symptoms demonstrated a significant decline from T1 to T2 (p = 0.009, Cohen’s d = 0.23). This improvement was reflected in clinical rates, where the proportion of mothers meeting the PTSD threshold dropped significantly from 68.9% to 59.1% (p = 0.037, Odds Ratio = 2.45). In contrast, reductions in maternal depression and anxiety scores were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), and the prevalence rates for these conditions remained stable over time (Table 2).

Correlation between maternal psychopathology and children’s sleep outcomes

Longitudinally, maternal psychopathologies at baseline (T1), depression, anxiety, and PTSD, were significantly correlated with children’s total sleep disturbances at T2 (p < 0.001, p = 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively), indicating that higher levels of maternal distress at three months post-disaster were associated with poorer child sleep quality at one year.

At the 12-month follow-up (T2), all maternal psychopathology indicators were significantly and positively correlated with children’s total sleep disturbance scores (all p < 0.001). Across specific sleep domains, maternal symptoms were consistently associated with poorer subjective sleep quality, increased sleep disturbances, and greater daytime dysfunction. Notably, maternal PTSD symptoms demonstrated the strongest associations with child sleep outcomes compared to depression and anxiety (Table 3).

Hierarchical regression analysis predicting sleep quality at T2

A four-step hierarchical linear regression was conducted to identify predictors of children’s sleep disturbances at 12 months post-disaster. Baseline sleep disturbances (Step 1) emerged as the strongest predictor, accounting for 20% of the variance. The inclusion of maternal psychopathology variables (Step 2) significantly improved the model (ΔR² = 0.09, p < 0.01), with depression, anxiety, and PTSD each independently predicting poorer sleep quality. Earthquake exposure severity (Step 3) explained an additional 5% of the variance. In the final step involving sociodemographic factors, only a lower maternal education level was significantly associated with greater sleep disturbances. The final model explained 38% of the total variance in children’s sleep quality at T2 (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This longitudinal cohort study may provide valuable insights into the role of maternal psychopathology in the evolution of sleep disturbances among children affected by the 2023 Türkiye earthquakes. To our knowledge, this is the first study examining how maternal mental health parameters, specifically depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress, affect children’s sleep outcomes in the aftermath of a large-scale natural disaster. The findings underscore the importance of maternal psychological functioning as a crucial correlate of post-disaster sleep disturbances in children.

Children’s sleep trajectory

The reduction in PSQI total scores between T1 and T2 may suggest a partial improvement in children’s sleep patterns over time. Specifically, improvements were noted in subjective sleep quality, sleep disturbances, and daytime dysfunction subcomponents, indicating that some aspects of sleep regulation had begun to normalize one year after the traumatic event. These findings are consistent with prior longitudinal research reporting that while sleep disturbances are highly prevalent in children exposed to traumatic events, there is often a gradual trend toward improvement as external stressors diminish and daily routines are reestablished [3, 24–26]. The observed improvement in subjective sleep quality may hint that children have experienced an increased sense of safety and predictability in their environment over time, which is critical for healthy sleep regulation. Similarly, reductions in sleep disturbances and daytime dysfunction may imply better nighttime rest and improved daytime functioning, potentially reflecting an adaptive coping process and resilience.

However, despite these statistically significant improvements, it is noteworthy that a considerable proportion of children (65.9%) continued to report poor sleep quality at T2. This persistent prevalence may highlight the chronic nature of sleep problems following large-scale disasters and suggest that the partial improvement observed does not necessarily translate into full remission at the individual level. Previous studies have emphasized that sleep disturbances can become entrenched symptoms of posttraumatic stress and may act as mediators or predictors of future emotional and behavioral problems [25, 27, 28]. The persistence of sleep problems may also reflect ongoing environmental stressors, such as unstable housing, loss of community support networks, or continued parental distress [6]. Given that maternal psychopathology has been shown to significantly predict child sleep outcomes in this cohort, the relatively high rates of maternal depression, anxiety, and PTSD may have continued to influence children’s sleep patterns.

Our findings regarding persistent sleep disturbances are consistent with research across various disaster contexts, suggesting that sleep disruption can be a common post-traumatic sequela regardless of the disaster type. For instance, studies following Hurricane Harvey have similarly reported that sleep disturbances in children persist long after the acute event and are closely linked to parental distress [27]. Similarly, research on war-affected and refugee children has identified sleep problems as a core symptom of trauma, often exacerbated by parental mental health struggles and displacement [29]. However, a key distinction in earthquake trauma, as observed in our study, may be the unpredictability of aftershocks, which maintains a state of chronic hyperarousal unlike the relatively predictable course of weather-related disasters. Despite these contextual differences, the strong association between maternal psychopathology and child sleep in our study aligns with findings from war and flood literature, supporting the hypothesis that the maternal emotional buffer is a universal mechanism protecting or risking child sleep in the aftermath of trauma.

These results may emphasize the importance of targeted, sustained interventions to address sleep disturbances in children following disasters. Interventions focusing on sleep hygiene and establishing consistent bedtime routines may be beneficial. Moreover, sleep disturbances should be recognized not merely as secondary symptoms but as important clinical targets in their own right, given their potential to perpetuate or exacerbate other psychological difficulties.

Maternal symptom patterns over time

In the present study, maternal psychopathological symptoms demonstrated differential trajectories over the one-year period after the earthquakes. While depressive symptoms and trait anxiety showed slight but non-significant reductions, posttraumatic stress symptoms exhibited a statistically significant decline, both in mean scores and in the proportion of mothers surpassing the clinical cutoff.

The divergence between the declining PTSD trajectory and the stable depression/anxiety scores likely reflects the changing nature of post-disaster stressors. PTSD symptoms are often driven by immediate threat cues and fear conditioning related to the event itself [30]. The reduction in maternal PTSD symptoms may imply that certain trauma-related symptoms gradually ameliorate over time, particularly as acute external stressors are resolved and families begin to re-establish safety and routine. This aligns with prior literature indicating that PTSD symptoms in disaster-exposed adults often show a natural decline within the first year post-trauma, especially when individuals are able to access social support and maintain functional daily roles [30, 31].

In contrast, depression and anxiety are frequently maintained by secondary stressors, the chronic, daily hardships that follow a disaster, such as financial instability, housing uncertainty, and the loss of social support networks [31]. Bokszczanin reported that depressive and anxiety symptoms, unlike acute posttraumatic stress responses, may persist longer due to ongoing caregiving burdens, cumulative daily stressors, and limited coping resources [32]. In disaster contexts, mothers may often assume heightened caregiving roles while also coping with personal losses and environmental instability, which may exacerbate or sustain internalizing psychopathology. In our sample, high rates of maternal unemployment and lower education levels may have perpetuated a sense of hopelessness and worry, preventing the remission of internalizing symptoms even after the immediate danger had passed.

The discrepancy between PTSD symptom improvements and the relative stability of depressive and anxiety symptoms may underscore the importance of adopting a multidimensional approach when evaluating and treating maternal mental health post-disaster. While trauma-focused interventions may effectively reduce PTSD symptoms, adjunctive strategies targeting chronic mood and anxiety disorders should also be integrated to comprehensively address maternal psychopathology.

Predictors of children’s sleep disturbances

The strongest predictor in the regression model was the baseline sleep disturbances assessed at three months post-disaster. This finding may highlight the persistent and chronic nature of sleep problems in children after trauma exposure [24, 27]. Early sleep disturbances may reflect maladaptive sleep patterns, heightened arousal, and dysregulated emotional processes that can become entrenched over time, thereby contributing to long-term difficulties.

The addition of maternal psychopathology variables significantly improved the predictive power of the model, underscoring the central role of maternal mental health in shaping children’s sleep trajectories. Each psychopathology domain contributed independently, suggesting that different maternal symptom dimensions may influence child sleep via distinct mechanisms. Maternal depression, characterized by withdrawal and low energy, may primarily impact sleep through behavioral pathways, such as the failure to enforce consistent bedtime routines or provide emotional scaffolding during the night. Depressed mothers may struggle to establish and maintain structured sleep environments, thereby exacerbating sleep difficulties in children [8, 33]. Maternal anxiety and PTSD likely operate through emotional contagion and threat modeling. An anxious or traumatized mother may exhibit continued hypervigilance and verbalize fears about safety. Children, looking to their mothers for cues about the environment, internalize this fear, resulting in a state of alertness that directly opposes the relaxation required for sleep onset [34, 35]. The presence of maternal anxiety may also contribute to increased bedtime resistance and nighttime awakenings in children, as anxious caregivers may unintentionally reinforce sleep-disruptive behaviors through over-involvement or excessive reassurance. A mother’s PTSD symptoms may act as a potent reminder of the trauma, keeping the earthquake memory active in the family system and preventing the child from developing a sense of post-disaster security. Moreover, maternal PTSD can reflect the unique and pervasive influence of trauma-related symptomatology on child sleep, potentially mediated by altered parenting behaviors and disruptions to household stability and routines [6, 36]. Mothers experiencing intrusive memories, hyperarousal, or emotional numbing may be less available emotionally and practically to support healthy sleep practices in their children, thereby perpetuating sleep difficulties.

Alongside behavioral dynamics, physiological and genetic mechanisms likely contribute to the strong association observed between maternal psychopathology and children’s sleep disturbances. The concept of stress contagion suggests that maternal physiological dysregulation, such as altered cortisol secretion stemming from chronic anxiety or PTSD, can directly influence the child’s physiological state through bio-behavioral synchrony [37, 38]. A mother’s heightened state of arousal may create a tense emotional atmosphere, leading to physiological hyperarousal in the child that is incompatible with sleep initiation and maintenance [25]. Furthermore, it is critical to acknowledge the role of gene-environment interactions. Mothers and children share genetic vulnerabilities that may predispose them both to anxiety disorders and sleep dysregulation [6]. Therefore, the correlations found in this study likely reflect a complex interplay where maternal psychopathology acts as both an environmental stressor (through compromised parenting and emotional climate) and a marker of shared biological susceptibility to post-disaster distress.

Beyond these unidirectional mechanisms, it is crucial to recognize the likely bidirectional nature of this relationship. Persistent sleep disturbances in children can serve as a chronic stressor for mothers, disrupting their own sleep and increasing the caregiving burden. This, in turn, may sustain or exacerbate maternal anxiety and depression, creating a “vicious cycle” within the family system. Thus, the strong correlations observed in our study may reflect not just the impact of the mother on the child, but a reciprocal maintenance of distress.

In addition to maternal psychopathology, earthquake exposure severity scores for both children and mothers further enhanced the model, with children’s exposure emerging as a slightly stronger predictor. This may imply that direct trauma experiences contribute uniquely to long-term sleep problems beyond parental influences. Previous research has shown that the degree of trauma exposure, including life-threatening experiences and loss, is closely associated with sleep disruptions in children [3, 39]. Interestingly, maternal exposure severity also played a significant role, likely reflecting the indirect impact of caregiver distress and environmental instability on child sleep.

Among sociodemographic factors, only maternal education level was significantly associated with children’s sleep outcomes, indicating that lower maternal education might be linked to greater sleep disturbances. This finding may reflect broader socioeconomic and contextual vulnerabilities, such as reduced access to resources, lower health literacy regarding sleep hygiene, and increased cumulative stress burden [28]. The lack of significant effects for other demographic variables (e.g., child age, maternal age, employment status, family structure) may hint that psychological and exposure-related factors can be more proximal determinants of sleep problems in this context.

Based on these findings, we propose feasible, evidence-based intervention strategies for post-disaster settings. Clinical priority should be given to screening and treating maternal PTSD symptoms. Since maternal posttraumatic stress emerged as a stronger correlate of child sleep disturbances than depression or anxiety in our study, trauma-focused interventions for mothers may yield the dual benefit of alleviating maternal distress and improving children’s sleep regulation. Also, the significant association between lower maternal education and child sleep disturbances may highlight a critical target for public health initiatives. Mothers with lower educational attainment may face challenges in accessing health information or maintaining structural routines during crises. Therefore, simplified, culturally adapted sleep hygiene education, focusing on re-establishing bedtime routines and reducing environmental chaos, should be proactively provided to these families to mitigate the long-term impact of the disaster.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its methodological strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings and guide future research.

Primarily, regarding the assessment of sleep, the study relied solely on self-reported measures in children. The absence of multi-informant evaluations, such as teacher reports or parental observations, may have introduced common method bias. Although the PSQI is a widely validated tool, it remains susceptible to reporting biases in young populations. Future studies integrating objective measures, such as actigraphy, alongside multi-informant data would provide a more robust validation of these subjective reports.

Closely related to this measurement issue is the lack of control for the children’s own internalizing symptoms, such as depression and anxiety. Since post-disaster emotional dysregulation in children could serve as a confounder, it is possible that the observed sleep disturbances were partly driven by the child’s own psychological distress rather than solely by maternal psychopathology. Disentangling these effects in future research will require concurrent assessments of child psychopathology.

Beyond individual factors, the study’s scope regarding family dynamics was limited. We focused exclusively on maternal psychopathology, leaving paternal mental health and broader parenting practices unassessed. Given that fathers and other caregivers also play significant roles in post-trauma recovery, their exclusion limits our understanding of the complete family environment. Similarly, the influence of broader contextual factors, such as ongoing environmental stressors, community support systems, and cultural beliefs, was not extensively examined. These elements are critical in shaping recovery, highlighting the need for contextually tailored research.

Regarding the study sample, participants were recruited from a single tertiary referral center, which likely introduces a selection bias toward more severe cases compared to the general population. Our findings should be generalized with caution to broader community samples.

Finally, from a methodological perspective, while we identified maternal psychopathology as a significant predictor, we utilized a hierarchical regression framework rather than a mediation analysis due to sample size constraints. Future research with larger cohorts should employ mediation models to explicitly test the mechanistic pathways linking trauma exposure, maternal symptoms, and child sleep disturbances.

In conclusion, addressing these limitations through objective assessments, comprehensive family evaluations, and larger, culturally diverse cohorts will further refine our understanding of the interplay between maternal mental health and child sleep in disaster contexts.
