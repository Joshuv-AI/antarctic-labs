Injury Patterns in Soccer-Induced Maxillofacial Fractures and Ocular Trauma: A Narrative Review
Authors: Anaas M Mergoum, Jacob Lentner, Brooke Echter, Jacob Roskam, Grant S Hamilton, James D Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaxillofacial fractures and ocular trauma are significant injuries in soccer, with potential functional, aesthetic, and visual consequences if unrecognized. Few reviews have specifically examined both injury patterns in this sport. The purpose of this narrative review is to synthesize the current literature to identify injury patterns associated with maxillofacial fractures and ocular trauma in soccer, thereby aiding clinicians in timely diagnosis and management. Given the heterogeneity and limited quality of the available evidence, this review also highlights areas where further research is needed.Main BodyThe zygoma was the most commonly fractured maxillofacial bone in soccer (32.8%), followed by the nasal (28.4%), mandibular (25.8%), orbital (6.5%), maxillary (3.6%), and frontal sinus (2.8%) regions. Among mandibular fractures, the angle was the most vulnerable site, accounting for 39.2% of cases. Head-to-head contact was the most frequent mechanism resulting in injury; however, although less common, arm or elbow impacts were more likely to result in fracture when they occurred.Ocular injuries most often present as orbital or eyelid contusions and lacerations (37.1%) and hyphemas (19.5%), with direct ball impact as the primary cause. Boys (60.4%–69.5%) were at greater risk than girls for ball sport-related ocular trauma, particularly those aged 2–9 years, with soccer being the leading sport implicated. Overall, these trends are based largely on small case series, reflecting the low quality of available evidence.ConclusionsThe zygoma is the most frequently fractured maxillofacial bone, typically resulting from head-to-head collisions. Direct soccer ball impact accounts for most ocular injuries in this sport, with orbital or eyelid contusions and lacerations most common, followed by hyphemas. Pediatric players may be particularly susceptible to soccer-related ocular trauma, possibly due to an underdeveloped orbital rim. Fundoscopic examination is essential after ball impact to detect vitreoretinal injury, even when visual acuity is normal. Further well-designed studies are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40798-026-01049-2.Keywords: Maxillofacial fractures, Ocular injury, Soccer, Facial trauma, Footballers

Introduction

Ocular and maxillofacial injuries frequently co-occur in sports. Notably, up to six percent of athletes sustaining facial trauma may develop visual loss [1]. Soccer is no exception. An increased incidence of maxillofacial fractures has been reported, particularly among players occupying the central forward (striker) position [2]. Given soccer’s global popularity, the overall burden of these injuries is substantial [3]. These injuries tend to be more severe than the more prevalent facial soft-tissue injuries, such as lacerations [4], which have been reviewed elsewhere [5, 6].

Because soccer is the most popular sport worldwide, sports medicine clinicians need to have a thorough understanding of these injury patterns to help prevent potentially permanent sequelae. The aim of this review is to analyze the current literature to identify patterns of facial injuries, specifically those involving the maxillofacial bones and the eye globe, that are characteristic of soccer players. We also discuss clinical presentation, management strategies and directions for future research.

Methods

We conducted a comprehensive literature search using the Ovid platform without restrictions on language or publication date. The search was completed in February 2026. To identify relevant publications, the following search terms were used: "facial injury" and "ocular injury" in combination with "soccer", "futbol", "footballers", "facial contusions", "facial laceration", and "facial fracture". Reference lists of articles identified through the primary search strategy were screened for relevance, with additional seminal studies integrated from existing literature repositories. A total of 139 studies were identified, of which 68 were included in this review (Fig. 1). Levels of evidence used for assessing studies in this review are provided in the supplementary appendix (Table S1).

Maxillofacial Fractures

Epidemiology

Sports-related injuries account for approximately 20% of all maxillofacial fractures reported annually [7]. Although the overall frequency of these injuries in elite competitions may appear low—approximately 5.69% during the 2022 FIFA World Cup—soccer accounts for a substantial proportion of maxillofacial trauma globally due to its widespread popularity [8]. For example, a study conducted in Italy reported a 6.4% increase in maxillofacial fractures between 2011 and 2019, with soccer identified as the most common cause (54.2%) [9]. Similarly, in a study evaluating six different sports, soccer was the primary etiology, accounting for 73.9% of recorded maxillofacial fractures [10]. In the United States, soccer-related craniomaxillofacial injuries increased between 2003 and 2012, followed by a decline from 2016 and 2020, reaching a nadir in 2020 with an estimated incidence of < 20,000 [11]. The authors attributed this recent decrease to improved injury awareness and prevention strategies, as well as reduced participation during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Maxillofacial injuries in soccer most commonly occur in men in their third decade of life (mean age: 25–33 years) [12–15]. Men, particularly those aged ≥ 30 years, are significantly more likely to require hospitalization compared to women [11]. A recent retrospective study conducted in England reported that midface fractures (including zygoma, maxilla, nasal, isolated orbital floor fractures) were the most common fractures in soccer players, compared with mandibular, panfacial (e.g., concurrent mandibular and midface fractures), or frontal sinus fractures [12]. Patterns and Mechanism of Injury

Head-to-head collisions were the most common injury mechanism for maxillofacial fractures. However, patients who were punched or elbowed in the face had a higher risk of facial fracture compared to those involved in head clashes or other mechanisms of injury. Of note, elbow impacts were more likely to result in temporomandibular joint contusions [12, 14]. In one study, approximately 62% of soccer-related maxillofacial injuries were attributed to elbow-to-face impacts [10]. These mechanisms of injury can be high-impact and frequently require surgical intervention [16].

Concurrent head trauma such as basilar skull fractures or intracranial injury are seen at lower rates in soccer compared with other mechanisms of trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents (8% vs. 67%, respectively) [12, 17]. This difference is likely due to lower velocity impacts sustained in soccer. Nevertheless, a retrospective study performed in Germany showed that concurrent maxillofacial and basilar skull fractures do occur in soccer, with concussion being the most common associated injury (19%) [18].

Zygoma

A high index of suspicion for zygomatic fractures is warranted, as the zygoma represents the most frequently fractured maxillofacial bone in soccer, accounting for 32.8% of cases in our analysis (Table 1) [14]. One reason the zygoma is highly prone to fractures in soccer players might be explained by the aerial mechanism of contact between players while attempting to head the soccer ball (Fig. 2).

On physical examination, findings may include flattening of the cheek (or loss of the zygomatic arch projection), a palpable step in the infraorbital rim, or trismus [19]. Trismus occurs because the zygoma forms the malar eminence and the superior portion of the masseter muscle attaches to the zygoma (Fig. 3A) [5]. The zygoma is an important part of facial structure because it protects vital structures such as the orbit, and has an impact on esthetics (e.g., width of the face), which may explain why these fractures are commonly treated surgically (∼83.6% in one retrospective study) [14, 20]. Indications for surgery include displaced or unstable fractures, comminuted bony fragments, or loss of function such as restriction of movement of the mandible (Fig. 3A) [20].

Nasal

Along with zygomatic fractures, nasal fractures are among the most common maxillofacial injuries in soccer. In previous case series [21, 22], they were identified as the most frequent facial fractures in soccer across all age groups. However, in the current analysis, nasal fractures were the second most commonly fractured bone, accounting for 28.4% of cases (Table 1). In the United States, soccer-related nasal fractures appear to be less frequent than in some other countries, likely reflecting the popularity of other sports. For example, basketball-related nasal fractures present to emergency departments approximately three times more often than soccer-related nasal fractures [23]. Nevertheless, a recent study of professional soccer players reported that nasal fractures were more than twice as common as zygomatic fractures (46% vs 21%), with elbow-to-face being the main mechanism of injury [24]. This suggests that professional players are more prone to nasal fractures, whereas zygomatic fractures may be more prevalent among amateur players.

Clinical presentation includes epistaxis, perceived new-onset of nasal obstruction (the patient might complain of difficulty breathing), nasal asymmetry, swelling, and bruising. Tenderness on palpation with crepitus or bony step-off suggests a possible nasal fracture [25]. These fractures should be reduced immediately if possible or within 3 to 10 days to allow for the edema to subside. Septal hematomas need to be drained to prevent pressure-induced avascular necrosis of the nasal cartilage, which can occur within 3 days if untreated [26]. Moreover, complex fractures, such as comminuted or open fractures, often necessitate operative intervention [27, 28].

Mandible

Our analysis revealed that the mandible is another frequently fractured bone in soccer players, accounting for 25.8% of maxillofacial fractures (Table 1). The angle of the mandible was the most common fracture site (Table 2). A study conducted in Japan showed that soccer caused more mandibular fractures than midface fractures [29]. Although most of these fractures were attributed to player-to-player collisions, the precise mechanism of impact was not always specified. However, Gallagher et al. identified elbow impact as the most common mechanism associated with mandibular fractures in soccer players [12]. The coronoid process may be uniquely vulnerable in soccer, as fractures at this site were observed only in soccer players and not in other sports in a Dutch study published in 2016 [30].

Clinical presentation depends on the fracture pattern (e.g., single vs multiple fractures) but may include pain with jaw movement, loose teeth, lower lip numbness due to inferior alveolar nerve injury (Fig. 3B), and dental malocclusion [28]. Mandibular fractures are frequently open injuries accompanied by intraoral or extraoral lacerations and active bleeding [19]. Airway assessment is critical, as multiple mandibular fractures may allow posterior displacement of the mandible and compromise the airway [19]. Nondisplaced or minimally displaced mandibular fractures with a normal bite may be treated nonoperatively (e.g., strict soft diet for 4–6 weeks) [19, 31, 32]. In one study, 57% of mandibular fractures acquired during soccer required surgery [12]. Indications for operative management include significant displacement, malocclusion, comminution, or condylar process fracture [32].

Orbit

A study conducted in England identified soccer as the most frequent cause of orbital fractures (approximately 30%) [33]. In contrast, orbital fractures accounted for only 6.5% of injuries in our review (Table 1). Furthermore, ocular impacts from soccer balls are more likely to result in globe injuries than orbital fractures, likely due to the distensibility of the ball.

The inferior orbital floor, being structurally weaker, is particularly prone to fracture. Entrapment of the inferior rectus muscle within a floor fracture may result in ocular motility dysfunction and diplopia, particularly with upward gaze. Additional clinical findings may include pain and crepitus on palpation of the orbital rim with infraorbital numbness (Fig. 3A), enophthalmos (orbital contents displaced inward causing a sunken eye appearance), and hypoglobus (posterior displacement of the eyeball). Orbital fractures with tissue entrapment may trigger the oculocardiac reflex, leading to nausea, vomiting, sinus bradycardia, and even syncope [34]. Emergent surgical intervention is required in such cases to release incarcerated tissue, terminate the reflex stimulus, and relieve symptoms. Early treatment may also prevent fibrosis secondary to prolonged muscle ischemia, which can result in permanent extraocular movement restriction [35]. However, soccer players commonly present with isolated orbital floor fractures, which may explain why most do not require surgery [6]. Other indications for operative intervention are ocular motility dysfunction, persistent diplopia, posterior globe displacement due to increased risk of hypoglobus, or complications such as infraorbital nerve damage or retrobulbar hematoma [34–38].

Maxilla and Frontal Sinus/Bone

Isolated maxillary and frontal sinus fractures were the least common injuries observed in the current analysis (Table 1). This may reflect the greater force required to fracture these bones compared to the nasal and zygomatic bones. For example, the force required to fracture the maxilla is twice that required to fracture the zygoma [19]. In soccer, the maxilla is often fractured as part of the maxillo-zygomatic-orbital complex, which was the most frequently reported fracture by Roccia et al. [15] This complex involves the articulation of the maxilla, zygoma, and orbital floor.

Complex midface fractures such as Le Fort fractures—characterized by separation between the pterygoid plates and the posterior maxilla—are rare in soccer due to the substantial force required to produce them [39, 40]. A violent kick to the face may represent an exception, with at least one case reported in the literature [14, 41].

Patients with maxillary fractures may present with epistaxis, malocclusion, loose teeth, and elongation or asymmetry of the face [19]. Frontal sinus fractures present with edema with tenderness on palpation of the frontal bone, frontal headaches, and forehead numbness (due to supraorbital nerve injury). Clinicians should ask about rhinorrhea or salty-tasting postnasal drip, which could suggest cerebrospinal leak due to posterior table fracture (i.e., fracture of the posterior wall of the frontal sinus) [28, 42, 43].

Indications for operative interventions for maxillary fractures include significant displacement, comminution, functional impairment (e.g., restricted mandibular or ocular movement), malocclusion, nerve injury, or Le Fort fractures [19]. Surgical indications for frontal sinus fractures include displaced posterior table fracture greater than one cortex thickness, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, or nasofrontal duct injury [44]. Nondisplaced fractures limited to the anterior table of the frontal sinus are often treated conservatively [28].

Ocular Injuries

Epidemiology

In a recent epidemiological study on ocular trauma, contusions and abrasions were identified as the most common types of eye injuries (57.3%) across five sports, including soccer, baseball, basketball, American Football, and tennis [51]. These findings align with an earlier study reporting that contusions and abrasions were also the most frequent soccer-related ocular injuries (36.1%) [52]. Similar to patterns observed in maxillofacial fractures, young men in their third decade of life account for the majority of soccer-related eye injuries [53].

Although young adult males represent the highest-risk group, children are also particularly vulnerable. Boys are more frequently affected than girls (60.4%–69.5%), with soccer consistently identified as the sport most commonly associated with pediatric ocular injuries [54–57]. Furthermore, an epidemiological study from South Korea found that eye injuries among adolescents most often occurred at school during sports activities, whereas in younger children and infants, injuries were more likely to occur at home during unstructured play [55].

Patterns and Mechanism of Injury

Research has shown that soccer is a leading cause of eye injuries among athletes, with the ball being the primary source of these injuries [58, 59]. The impact from the soccer ball was responsible for the majority of cases (77.5%-80.9%), while upper extremity impact to the eye constituted the second leading cause (10.1%) [51, 52]. A cross-sectional study[60] conducted in the United States showed that basketball was responsible for more ocular injuries than soccer, which is consistent with a previous study conducted in college athletes [61]. However, soccer was linked to a significantly higher rate of major ocular injuries involving both the anterior segment (e.g., hyphema, lens dislocation, corneal or scleral laceration) and the posterior segment (e.g., retinal detachment, globe rupture). Notably, impacts from the soccer ball showed a particular tendency to cause posterior segment injuries. When the anterior segment was affected, hyphema and iritis or iridocyclitis occurred more frequently compared with other sports [51, 58, 60, 62]. In particular, amateur soccer players often presented with posterior segment ocular trauma almost always due to impact from a soccer ball [63]. The current analysis demonstrated that hyphema was the second most common soccer-related ocular injury (19.5%), following orbital or eyelid contusions and lacerations, which accounted for 37.1% of cases (Table 3).

Compared with balls used in other sports, the soccer ball demonstrates less orbital penetration, but remains in contact with the orbital space for a substantially longer duration—2.5 to 10 times longer—allowing greater transmission of injurious forces to the posterior segment of the eye. It is postulated that this mechanism of injury could significantly damage the iris or ciliary body blood vessels leading to a higher risk of hyphema [64]. The soccer ball appears to have a “suction effect” on the orbital contents as the ball rebounds off the orbit, which may further damage the eye globe. This blunt impact from a soccer ball compresses the anterior chamber and stretches the posterior wall of the eye, potentially causing retinal tears, choroidal rupture, macular holes, vitreous hemorrhage, and scleral rupture [65]. Ball size and inflation pressure (e.g., underinflated vs overinflated) do not affect the risk of orbital penetration [66]. Evaluation of Vision

Although it is reasonable to expect that patients with acute retinal injuries would present with visual disturbances, evidence suggests this is not always the case. In a prospective observational study of patients with suspected soccer-related ocular trauma, 42.2% of those with visual acuity of 20/40 or better were found to have vitreoretinal lesions [67]. Thus, normal visual acuity and the absence of external signs of injury (e.g., hyphema) do not exclude posterior segment involvement in soccer players. These lesions were most commonly located in the superotemporal quadrant of the retina (Fig. 3A), with commotio retinae representing the most frequent ophthalmoscopic finding, particularly among pediatric patients [64, 67, 68]. In fact, one case series of soccer ball-induced ocular injuries reported commotio retinae in 50% of affected patients [69].

Relative afferent pupillary defect with sudden vision loss indicates optic nerve or retinal damage [5]. Optic nerve damage, vitreous hemorrhage, and globe rupture often portend poor visual outcomes [65]. Urgent ophthalmology referral is warranted in cases of suspected globe rupture (e.g., globe asymmetry), with or without extrusion of orbital contents, the presence of a penetrating foreign body, symptoms such as flashing lights, floaters, visual field defects, or diplopia, abnormal pupillary reactions, or if retinal detachment is identified on the initial examination [28, 70]. Optical Coherence Tomography

Optical coherence tomography (OCT) angiography may aid in the work-up and management of soccer ball-related posterior segment injuries [71]. Choroidal ruptures from blunt trauma may subsequently lead to choroidal neovascularization, which is prone to leakage of fluid and blood into the retina, damaging photoreceptors and resulting in vision loss. OCT is valuable for detecting choroidal ruptures, identifying signs of central retinal artery occlusion (e.g., increased retinal hyperreflectivity or loss of normal retinal architecture), and diagnosing traumatic macular holes in affected athletes, thereby facilitating timely intervention and reducing the risk of complications (Table 4) [72–74]. Notably, one case series reported that 30% of patients referred for soccer ball-related retinal injuries were found to have traumatic macular holes [75].

Pediatric Patients

Pediatric patients appear to be more susceptible to severe ocular injuries than adults. A recent retrospective cohort study that evaluated ocular trauma in pediatric sports revealed that baseball (38.5%), basketball (16.9%), and soccer (14.9%) were the leading causes, with contusions (82.4%) and hyphema (8.1%) most frequently reported [76]. Similarly, a retrospective case series examining the initial presentation of sports-related ocular injuries in youth (mean age 16.2 years) identified hyphema as the most common injury, with soccer accounting for the highest proportion of cases, followed by baseball and basketball [77]. Pediatric athletes (≤ 18 years of age) accounted for more than half of sports-related ocular injuries [78]. A potential explanation is an underdeveloped orbital rim (Fig. 3B). Unlike the globe and orbit (which attain adult size by the age of 7), the orbital rim experiences significant growth and maturation during puberty [69, 75]. In the United States, soccer-related ocular injuries result in more emergency department visits among patients ≤ 18 years than among adults (68.2% vs. 31.8%) [52]. These findings support the use of protective eyewear in prepubertal children, particularly polycarbonate lenses, which have been shown to reduce the retinal impact of eye injuries [79].

Discussion

Maxillofacial Fractures

The current review suggests that the zygomatic bone is the most common maxillofacial fracture seen in soccer. This is consistent with findings of previous studies where the zygoma was the most common fracture site, accounting for approximately 32.7–50% of fractures in soccer players [7, 13, 14, 16, 30, 46]. Similarly, in a retrospective study including both amateur and professional athletes, soccer players were found to have a higher incidence of midface fractures than rugby players, with the zygoma being the most common site of fracture, followed by isolated orbital fractures [47].

We speculate that mid-air collisions—particularly an opponent’s head or elbow descending from above—may predispose the zygoma to serve as the initial point of contact. Earlier studies by Sane et al. [48] and Frenguelli et al. [22] identified nasal fractures as the most common maxillofacial injury, whereas more recent reports by Papakosta et al. [14] and Gallagher et al. [12] found the zygoma and mandible to be more frequently affected. A 13-year study (2007–2019) of professional soccer players by Kozlowski et al. [24] reported nasal fractures as still predominant, with zygomatic fractures second. These discrepancies may reflect differences in injury definitions, playing style, officiating, rule changes, or variations between amateur and professional competition, as well as more standardized reporting at the professional level. Greater force is required to fracture the zygoma than the nasal bone, which may also suggest more forceful or less regulated play in amateur settings [19].

Across studies summarized in Table 2—particularly those with larger sample sizes—the mandibular angle was the most common fracture site, accounting for approximately 39.2% of mandibular fractures in soccer. Structural vulnerability at this site may relate to the proximity of impacted third molars [94]. Notably, Ruslin et al. [30] observed coronoid process fractures exclusively in soccer players, with the zygoma being the most frequently fractured bone in that cohort. The anatomic proximity of the coronoid process to the zygomatic arch may explain this association, although the mechanism of injury was not specified.

Multiple studies reported player-to-player collisions as the leading cause of facial fractures, but did not distinguish between ground-level and aerial impacts [22, 29, 45, 48]. Studies by Maladière et al. and Papakosta et al. found that head-to-face contact was the most common mechanism in amateur players (37.1% and 47.3%, respectively) [14, 40], whereas Kozlowski et al. [24] reported elbow-to-face contact (36%) as most common at the professional level. Gallagher et al. [12] further demonstrated that elbow impacts were more often associated with mandibular fractures, while head impacts more frequently resulted in zygomatic fractures; only one mandibular fracture was linked to head impact. This pattern is biomechanically plausible, as the mandible at ground level is more vulnerable to elbow strikes. Although head-to-face contact was the most frequent mechanism overall, elbow impacts were more likely to result in fractures, possibly due to the higher velocity and concentrated force of the elbow. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for emergency department referral in concussed patients with maxillofacial fractures, particularly those involving the midface from high-impact mechanisms such as arm or elbow-to-head strikes [95, 96].

Ocular Trauma

The current analysis indicates that orbital or eyelid contusions and lacerations and hyphemas are the most common soccer-related ocular injuries, whereas retinal tears or detachments are rare (1.4%). In one case series, hyphemas accounted for 50% of soccer ball-induced injuries, exceeding corneal abrasions (21%) [69]. This is clinically important as hyphema is associated with a higher risk of vitreoretinal lesions (53.3% vs. 33.3%), and prior data suggest that soccer players have at least a twofold increased risk of developing hyphema compared with non-soccer players [53].

Although ocular injuries are less frequent in soccer compared to other sports, they tend to be severe when they occur, often involving the posterior segment (e.g., retinal tears or vitreous hemorrhage, particularly in the superotemporal retina) or presenting as hyphema with anterior chamber trauma. Most studies in Table 3 included both adult and pediatric populations; however, Leshno et al.[64] focused exclusively on children and found commotio retinae (16 cases) and eyelid contusions (9 cases) to be more common than hyphema (6 cases). Whether this reflects a lower incidence of hyphema in pediatric patients remains uncertain given the small sample size.

Most reviewed studies identified mechanisms of injury without correlating them to specific ocular diagnoses [60, 67]. An exception was a study by Zhang and colleagues [58], who reported that orbital contusions were far more often caused by soccer ball impacts (169 cases) than by player collisions (4 cases). Overall, the soccer ball appears to account for up to 80% of globe injuries [51, 52].

Given the high rate of ball-related facial trauma, all players with suspected ocular injury (particularly from ball impact) should undergo fundoscopic examination within 24–48 h, as posterior segment injuries may be present despite normal visual acuity [67]. Additional indications include visual changes, persistent pain, photophobia, flashes, pupillary abnormalities, or signs of orbital trauma (e.g., ecchymosis, edema and proptosis) [97].

Protective Facial Equipment

Soccer players may be at greater risk of ocular injury than athletes in sports such as hockey or American Football, in part because protective eyewear is not required, despite evidence that it reduces retinal stress associated with impact [78, 79]. Protective eyewear is strongly recommended for pediatric athletes, especially those who are prepubertal or functionally one-eyed [67].

Facial mask use has also increased in soccer, including at the highest level; notably, five players at the 2022 FIFA World Cup wore protective masks for orbital, nasal, or midface injuries [8]. Advances in technology have led to lighter, customized masks that improve comfort and may enable both professional and amateur players to protect healing injuries, continue participation [98, 99] or potentially shorten return-to-play after maxillofacial surgery [8, 100–102].

Evidence supporting mouthguard use to prevent facial fractures in soccer remains limited. One study of professional athletes in Switzerland reported a higher rate of mandibular fractures among mouthguard users; however, these injuries occurred in ice hockey players rather than soccer players, limiting the applicability of those findings to soccer [103].

Limitations

While this review provides a comprehensive synthesis of injury patterns associated with maxillofacial fractures and ocular trauma in soccer, several limitations regarding both the methodology of the included studies and the scope of the review process must be acknowledged. For example, some of the included studies categorized injuries as “multiple fractures” without specifying fracture types [22, 30], while others reported fractures as a combined maxilla–zygomatic–orbital complex, thereby preventing identification of the individual bones involved [15]. Cerulli et al. [10] included only operatively managed fractures, likely biasing results toward more severe injuries (e.g., zygomatic fractures). In addition, only six studies informed trends in soccer-related ocular injuries and several ocular trauma studies pooled data from multiple sports. For example, Patel et al. [60] reported only the three most common soccer-related ocular injuries and grouped the remainder as “all others”, unlike Burke et al. [69], who provided more detailed sport-specific data. Consequently, some relevant data could not be incorporated, potentially limiting the precision of our analysis. Moreover, our conclusions are constrained by the small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and heterogeneity of the included studies, including differences in injury definitions, competition levels, and outcome measures. Variability in injury surveillance across countries, organizations, and levels of play introduces additional confounding factors. Finally, despite a comprehensive search, it is possible that relevant studies were not identified.

Conclusions

The overall quality of available evidence concerning injury patterns in soccer-induced maxillofacial fractures and ocular trauma is low, highlighting the need for larger, well-designed studies to validate these findings. Future research should compare injury patterns across key demographic groups—male versus female athletes, professional versus amateur players, and pediatric versus adult populations—to better delineate potential differences. Many of the included studies are dated and reflect periods when soccer participation was predominantly male, thereby limiting generalizability to today’s more diverse playing population. Updated epidemiological data that better represent contemporary participation are therefore needed. Additionally, further investigation is warranted to determine whether aerial collisions produce different injury patterns than ground-level impacts in soccer.
