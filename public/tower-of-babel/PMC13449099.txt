Differing effects of Nefecon and Sparsentan in a patient with recurrent IgA nephropathy post-kidney transplantation
Authors: Anja S Mühlfeld, Jürgen Floege, Saskia von Stillfried, Peter Boor, Eleni Stamellou, Rafael Kramann, Claudia Seikrit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIgA nephropathy (IgAN) is a major cause of late graft loss after kidney transplantation, yet evidence for newly approved IgAN-specific therapies in this setting is scarce. We report a young male transplant recipient with biopsy-proven recurrent IgAN 7 years after living-donor transplantation. Treatment with targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon) was associated with a delayed and incomplete antiproteinuric response, clinically relevant systemic glucocorticoid-related adverse effects and progressive graft function decline. After discontinuation of Nefecon, combined therapy with Sparsentan and empagliflozin resulted in sustained albuminuria reduction, stabilization of graft function, improved blood pressure control, and resolution of metabolic complications. This case highlights challenges and opportunities in integrating novel IgAN therapies into post-transplant care.

INTRODUCTION

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) represents one of the most common causes of kidney failure worldwide. With a reported incidence of 18 cases per 1000 kidney transplants, IgAN demonstrates a high frequency of recurrence [1]. While asymptomatic IgA deposits—without clinically relevant hematuria or proteinuria—may appear early after transplantation, symptomatic recurrence characterized by progressive proteinuria, hematuria, and declining graft function typically develops over several years. Acute, fulminant recurrences are rare [2–5]. The cumulative incidence of clinically manifest recurrence reaches ~23% at 15 years post-transplantation, with a median time to recurrence of 3.4 years. These findings emerge from a multicenter European study, which also identified an association between IgAN recurrence and the development of de novo donor-specific antibodies [4]. Further risk factors are rapid progression to kidney failure in native kidneys prior to transplantation, young recipient age (<36 years) and an early increase in proteinuria within the first 2 years post-transplantation as well as transplant-associated factors such as steroid-free protocols, treatment with sirolimus, or lack of induction with antithymocyte globulin [6–8].

Once established, IgAN recurrence in the transplant doubles the risk of graft failure [5]. In recent years, multiple innovative therapeutic approaches for IgAN have been approved or are in advanced clinical testing [9–13]. Despite these promising advancements, there are few reports on the use of these newly approved drugs—such as Nefecon and Sparsentan—for IgAN recurrence in transplant recipients [14, 15]. Here we present a case of a young patient with IgAN recurrence following living-donor kidney transplantation, who was sequentially treated with Nefecon and Sparsentan.

Case report: Several years ago a 26-year-old patient presented to our center with newly established arterial hypertension, microhematuria, and proteinuria and was diagnosed with primary IgAN. At the time of diagnosis, he had already established chronic kidney disease with an eGFR of 32 ml/min/1.73 m2 and albumin/creatinine ratio (ACR) of 0.8 g/g. Despite optimal CKD treatment and a course of systemic glucocorticoids the kidney function deteriorated, and he developed kidney failure only three years later. Four years after IgAN diagnosis, he underwent a living donor kidney transplantation from a family member. After induction therapy with basiliximab, maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus (1.5 mg twice daily, trough level 5–7 µ/l), mycophenolate mofetil (1000 mg twice daily), and low-dose prednisolone (5 mg per day). Protocol biopsy three months after transplantation showed only one of 17 glomeruli with segmental sclerosis and little tubulointerstitial fibrosis and atrophy (less than 15%). During the first six post-transplant years, his baseline eGFR remained stable at approximately 44 ml/min without proteinuria. Over the years he had developed marked obesity (BMI 36 kg/m²) and a prediabetic metabolic situation (HbA1c 6.1%). Blood pressure was well controlled around 125/80 mmHg.

Seven years after transplantation—the patient developed microhematuria and albuminuria increased up to an ACR of 1200 mg/g within six months despite the use of maximally allowed doses of RAS inhibition, prompting a diagnostic kidney biopsy. The biopsy excluded both cellular and antibody-mediated rejection as well as thrombotic microangiopathy and confirmed recurrent IgAN, with mesangial hypercellularity in ≤50% of glomeruli, no endocapillary hypercellularity, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, 5% cortical interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy and no crescents (Fig. 1), corresponding to MEST-C Score: M0, E0, S1, T0, C0. Four out of 14 glomeruli were globally sclerotic (33%). There were no signs of arteriosclerosis.

Nefecon was initiated at a dose of 16 mg once daily. Baseline immunosuppression was continued with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and prednisolone 5 mg/day (Fig. 2). At treatment initiation of Nefecon, blood pressure was ~135/90 mmHg with mild peripheral edema. His antihypertensive therapy included ramipril 5 mg twice daily, metoprolol 95 mg once daily, amlodipine 5 mg twice daily, and torasemide 5 mg once daily. Hypercholesterolemia was treated with pravastatin and he received vitamin D supplementation. Due to marked obesity (BMI 36 kg/m²) accompanied by prediabetes, semaglutide therapy (0.25 mg/week) was started.

On his next presentation after two months of treatment, the patient had developed new peripheral edema and increased blood pressure. eGFR remained at 44 ml/min; however, ACR had increased to 2480 mg/g. One month later, eGFR had declined to 37 ml/min, and ACR had risen further (for details see Fig. 1). Blood pressure had increased as well as edema and body weight despite semaglutide therapy. Subsequently, torasemide was increased to 10 mg. Hypercholesterolemia worsened, prompting the addition of ezetimibe. Subsequently blood pressure normalized, and edema resolved.

Five months after Nefecon treatment initiation, eGFR had further declined with modest improvement in ACR to 1518 mg/g. The patient continued to experience significant treatment-associated adverse effects: despite nearly one year of semaglutide therapy, his diabetes had worsened substantially, with HbA1c rising from 6.1% to 8.3%. He had gained 11 kg and reported severe dyspnea. Diagnostic echocardiography and chest radiography were unremarkable. After 9 months of treatment Nefecon was tapered to zero over four weeks. Microhematuria had resolved at this time.

Two months after the discontinuation of Nefecon, proteinuria persisted at 1 g/d with an ACR of 583 mg/g while kidney function had substantially declined from an eGFR of 44 ml/min at the start of treatment to 23 ml/min. Despite the significant modifications in medication, tacrolimus trough levels varied only moderately between 4.9 and 7.4 ug/l (target range of 5–7 µg/l) with mild adjustments of ±0.5 mg. Changes in treatment did not coincide with treatment changes of Nefecon or Sparsentan.

Given the above developments, with a persisting proteinuria of 1 g/d despite prior treatment with Nefecon, treatment with empagliflozin and the newly approved dual angiotensin and endothelin A receptor antagonist Sparsentan was initiated. The prednisolone dose of 5 mg remained stable, Semaglutide was discontinued. Over the subsequent year, eGFR improved and stabilized at ~25 ml/min. ACR decreased to 250 mg/g. Glycemic control improved substantially, with normalization of HbA1c to 6.0%. Dyspnea resolved, and the patient lost 6 kg of body weight. Potassium serum levels and liver enzymes were unremarkable. Blood pressure control also improved, allowing reduction of torasemide and discontinuation of amlodipine.

Before publication, written informed consent was obtained in compliance with the declaration of Helsinki.

DISCUSSION

We report a young patient with recurrence of primary IgAN after living donor kidney transplantation who was sequentially treated with Nefecon and Sparsentan. During the first year after recurrence and while being treated with Nefecon his graft function declined markedly. Proteinuria transiently increased with Nefecon, probably due to worsened hypertension, and then finally decreased from a maximum ACR of ~3500 mg/g to ~600 mg/g. At the same time the patient suffered from substantial treatment-associated side effects, in particular dyspnea and increases in blood pressure, progression of prediabetes to overt diabetes, weight gain, and edema. After discontinuation of Nefecon these adverse events disappeared suggesting that Nefecon indeed contributed to the systemic corticosteroid side effects despite its targeted-release formulation. As proteinuria persisted, Sparsentan and Empagliflozin were initiated. During the following year proteinuria improved markedly, and kidney function stabilized.

The revised 2025 KDIGO guideline on the management of IgAN suggests simultaneous treatment of the CKD-aspect and immune-aspect of IgAN [16]. In the present case we did not initiate Nefecon and Sparsentan in parallel, as Sparsentan had not yet been approved in Europe at the time recurrent IgAN was diagnosed in our patient. Our experience described in this article raises several important questions. How can these novel agents be safely integrated into existing immunosuppressive regimens in the setting of a transplant patient? Were the observed adverse effects under Nefecon attributable to cumulative steroid exposure, or were they mediated through CYP-dependent interactions with tacrolimus? Finally, can these therapies meaningfully improve long-term allograft outcomes?

There are only a few reports on the use of Nefecon for recurrence of primary IgAN in transplant patients [17]. Lopez-Martinez et al. described the successful treatment of five patients with recurrent IgAN as evidenced by a reduction in proteinuria following enteric budesonide treatment [15]. In this brief letter to the editor, data on adverse events or on concurrent medication are not available. More recently another case report described the clinical course of a young patient with IgAN recurrence post-transplantation [14] which shows several analogies to our case: Both patients were young and experienced a rapid deterioration of kidney function following the diagnosis of IgAN recurrence. Both were treated with Nefecon while maintaining standard immunosuppression and both developed considerable side effects. Consistent with these previous reports and with findings from native kidney IgAN patients in the NefIgArd trial [9] our patient ultimately had an improvement in proteinuria by 53% over nine months. More importantly, during the Nefecon phase kidney function deteriorated further and stabilized only after the start of Sparsentan and Empagliflozin, which is remarkable since both agents are reported to be associated with a dip in eGFR after initiation. Whether the reduction in proteinuria over time was a delayed response to Nefecon or an effect of Sparsentan and Empagliflozin cannot be discerned. However, in the Nefigard phase III trial, after tapering Nefecon, proteinuria decreased for only three months and then increased again, which is inconsistent with the course observed in our patient (Fig. 2). In addition, our patient experienced multiple side effects during Nefecon treatment such as severe dyspnea, worsening of edema, blood pressure, glucose control, hypercholesterolemia as well as weight gain. This might have additionally triggered rapid kidney function loss. Thus, in our patient, Nefecon therapy was not associated with a timely stabilization of graft function in the context of recurrent IgAN, although microhematuria resolved.

Nefecon, like other budesonide formulations, undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism in the liver, which limits systemic bioavailability and thereby reduces corticosteroid-related adverse effects. This metabolism is mediated by CYP3A4, which can be influenced by concomitant medications such as calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs), that may alter CYP3A4 activity, changing Nefecon bioavailability [18, 19]. The critical question arises, if Nefecon side effects may be accentuated in transplant patients due to altered CYP3A4 metabolism secondary to standard immunosuppression. In addition, CNI exposure and trough levels should be closely monitored and adjusted if necessary. Our patient had well-controlled tacrolimus levels during the Nefecon treatment, thus deterioration of eGFR and glycemic control were not related to CNI overdosing.

Alternatively, the addition of Nefecon treatment to maintenance prednisolone therapy after transplantation might have caused additive corticosteroid side effects. Nefecon treatment leads to systemic steroid exposure, even though first pass metabolism limits systemic bioavailability and both might add up in a systemic exposure of 13–15 mg prednisolone equivalent. Because of that, one can discuss temporarily reducing or halting the oral prednisolone maintenance dose during treatment with Nefecon in immunological low-risk kidney transplant patients. However, whether the systemic effect of Nefecon is effective enough to suppress rejection in immunological high-risk patients remains to be shown. Therefore, the use of Nefecon as a disease-modifying drug for the treatment of IgAN after kidney transplantation may be challenging.

This is one of the first reported cases of a patient successfully treated with Sparsentan in the context of recurrent IgAN post-transplantation. The treatment was well tolerated, and no unexpected adverse events occurred. Proteinuria decreased rapidly, consistent with findings from trials in native kidney IgAN patients treated with Sparsentan [10, 20]. In our case, treatment with Empagliflozin was started simultaneously with Sparsentan, so it is conceivable that Empagliflozin also contributed to the antiproteinuric effect [21]. However, a recent meta-analysis of 9 studies, encompassing 882 patients, showed that with Sodium Glucose Cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) urinary protein to creatinin ratio (UPCR) levels remained unchanged [22]. Vice versa, there is evidence from the SPARTACUS phase 2 trial as well as from real-world data from Germany that Sparsentan administered after initiation of an SGLT2 inhibitor can exert pronounced antiproteinuric effects in IgAN affecting native kidneys (UACR/UPCR changes of −56% to 62% after 5–6 months) [20, 23].

Following treatment initiation of Sparsentan and empagliflozin, we also observed a prompt improvement of blood pressure allowing for a reduction of antihypertensive medication. This effect is noteworthy, as standard immunosuppression with tacrolimus, a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor, could theoretically also impair Sparsentan metabolism by lowering clearance resulting in elevated plasma levels and thereby might have led to an enhanced antihypertensive effect of Sparsentan. However, in our case, no hyperkalemia or worsening edema was observed, suggesting that systemic Sparsentan levels did not increase markedly even in the context of concomitant tacrolimus therapy [18, 19].

Our report has several limitations. First, our patient was not treated simultaneously with Sparsentan and Nefecon, as Sparsentan was not yet approved at the time of diagnosis. Thus, we could not assess the effects of concurrent treatment with both agents. Second, Sparsentan was initiated only 2 months after the discontinuation of Nefecon, making it difficult to exclude potential carryover effects from the prior treatment. However, the rapid reduction in proteinuria—consistent with the known effects of Sparsentan—suggests a similar efficacy in the transplant setting. Third, causality of effects remains uncertain and also recurrent IgAN alone could explain kidney function deterioration.

Our case illustrates both the opportunities and challenges of integrating novel disease-specific therapies into the management of recurrent IgAN after kidney transplantation. Treatment with targeted-release budesonide (Nefecon) was associated with clinically relevant systemic adverse effects potentially contributing to worsening of proteinuria and graft function decline. In contrast, subsequent therapy with Sparsentan combined with optimized supportive care resulted in a pronounced and sustained reduction in proteinuria, stabilization of kidney function, and overall clinical improvement. This suggests that dual endothelin–angiotensin receptor blockade can be effective and well tolerated in the transplant setting, whereas Nefecon in combination with standard immunosuppression including prednisolone, may increase the risk of systemic corticosteroid side effects. This case underscores the need for prospective studies in the post-transplant setting to define optimal sequencing, combination strategies, and the long-term impact of these emerging therapies on allograft outcomes.
