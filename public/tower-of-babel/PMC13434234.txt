Emission dipole orientation reveals dynamic single-molecule interactions with 2D crystals at solvent interfaces
Authors: Wei Guo, Tzu-Heng Chen, Nathan Ronceray, Eveline Mayner, Kenji Watanabe, Takashi Taniguchi, Aleksandra Radenovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Direct observation of individual fluorescent emitters is essential for studying quantum materials, chemical reactions, and biological systems. However, current single-molecule tracking methods only focuses on the localizations of molecules, overlooking molecular configuration and orientation. In this work, we introduce a high-throughput polarized single-molecule localization microscopy that simultaneously resolves the locations and emission dipole orientations of single fluorescent emitters with nanometer precision. Using the interface between pristine hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) and an organic solvent as a challenging platform, we capture over 10⁵ fluorescent events and reveal distinct molecular interaction dynamics at room temperature. The measured dipole orientations align with the three-fold (C₃) rotational symmetry of the h-BN lattice, and molecular dynamics in the liquid environment can be modulated electrochemically, suggesting a route for on-demand control of quantum emitters. We also find that lateral diffusion at the solid–liquid interface is far more dynamic than that of solid-state emitters. This simultaneous tracking of molecular conformation and photophysics advances the understanding of single-molecule interactions and enables real-time sensing through two-dimensional materials.

Introduction

Large-scale characterization of fluorescent emitters and the observation of single-molecule conformational dynamics are essential for quantum materials1,2, chemical reactions3,4 and biological systems5,6. Solid-state quantum emitters have been widely used in computing and sensing7–9; and these color centers are usually characterized and observed using optical methods1,9–11. However, for other fluorescent emitters that are located at solid-liquid interfaces and are extremely dynamic12–15, then traditional optical techniques are troubled by bulk measurements and optical diffraction limits (200–300 nm resolution). For example, recent studies have increasingly focused on the hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) with single molecules14,16–20, because of its high mechanical strength, atomically flat surface and the ability to slow down the diffusion movement. The wide band gap of h-BN also makes it compatible with fluorescence imaging on the surface compared to graphene and MoS2 substrates21,22. Surface defects on h-BN play a critical role not only in solid-state quantum emitters but also in molecular sensing in nanofluidics13,14, where the quantum emission could also be induced in the solvent environment.

To understand the photophysics of these emitters in complex environments with high throughput, interactions between molecules and pristine h-BN can be studied by single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM). However, conventional SMLM only provides information about the intensity and localization of the emitters, comprehensive profiling demands additional properties such as spectrum23–25 and polarization2,14,26. Among these properties, the three-dimensional (3D) orientation of the emission dipole is of particular interest, since quantum emitters, which formed by irradiation27,28, doping29–31, and etching32,33, usually result in dipolar emission that are modulated by the external environment. This interest also stems from the compatibility with SMLM without sacrificing temporal and spatial resolution. Although a variety of techniques have been developed for 3D-orientation SMLM (e.g., pupil splitting34–36, back-focal-plane modulation37–39, excitation modulation40,41) to study the heterogeneity of biological systems, their potential in materials science remains to be fully explored. Dipole orientation provides a window into intrinsic molecular conformations and their interactions with materials42–44. Therefore, high-throughput molecular orientation and localization detection enables the transition from “case studies” to quantitative statistical analysis of molecular dynamics.

In this work, we utilize a customized four-pupil polarized single-molecule localization microscopy (pSMLM) to map the nanoscale localization and track the 3D-orientation of fluorescent emitters. This approach correlates molecular orientation and conformation with the crystal lattice in a solvent environment. By optimizing azimuthal accuracy, our four-pupil design specifically adapts to the challenges of the 2D material interface, enabling simultaneous 6D imaging. Our findings reveal that molecular interactions at the liquid-solid interface are regulated by both the h-BN crystal structure and electrochemical conditions, providing insights into the dynamic behavior of emitters in complex surface environments.

Results

Experimental concept

To observe non-invasively the molecular dynamics of the h-BN surface in response to the nanoscale environment14,45, thick h-BN flakes (tens to hundreds of layers) are exfoliated from the high-quality crystals to the coverslip and immersed in organic solvent. We chose acetonitrile as the experimental solvent due to its linear geometry, without tautomer, and highest purity (>99.9%) among commercial organic solvents. The whole sample is then placed in a custom-built chamber and fixed on the inverted pSMLM microscope (Fig. 1a). The 561 nm laser is chosen as an excitation beam, and fluorescent emitters are generated at the liquid-solid interface of h-BN flakes. The averaged spectrum of these emitters is 600–700 nm14,45, but it is still challenging to interpret the behavior of each emitter based on this averaged characteristic and spectrum. Moving beyond the standard 2D Gaussian of PSFs, polarization measurement extracts 3D dipole orientations by analyzing the fluorescence of transition dipole moments. This capability is realized through a custom four-pupil-splitting pSMLM setup (Fig. 1b), designed to facilitate high-throughput characterization of fluorescent emitters at solid-liquid interface. We selected this strategy because it balances acquisition time (number of photons) with localization accuracy more effectively than alternative designs46–48. Due to its compact PSF shape, this strategy achieves superior signal-to-noise ratios and the highest azimuthal accuracy compared to other PSF-engineering methods. To meet the needs of molecular dynamics studies at the millisecond scale, in this work, we also extend it to orientation measurements of the azimuth (φ), polar (θ), and wobble angles (α) of emission dipoles (the insert of Fig. 1b) based on the rotational dipole model49,50 (Supplementary Note 1). The azimuth and polar angles represent the average orientations of the dipole in the plane and out of the plane, respectively, while the wobble angle is the half-cone angle when the molecule is assumed to wobble uniformly inside a hard cone. The fluorescence from the dipoles (red path) is divided by three beam splitters into four channels with different linear polarization states: 0°, 90°, 45°, and 135° separately. Subsequently, four polarized PSFs from the same emission dipole are recorded simultaneously by a single camera and the simulated annealing algorithm is employed to estimate the 3D-orientation by iterating a loss function involving the PSFs and degree of linear polarization (DoLP) map (Supplementary Note 2).

Figure 1c shows the super-resolved reaction sites of the liquid-activated emitters on the h-BN surface in high throughput and the nanoscale resolution of SMLM compared to widefield in the zoomed-in diagram. The imaging strategy is similar to point accumulation in nanoscale topography (PAINT), where the transient landing and leaving of fluorescent emitters on h-BN generate a series of single-molecule fluorescence signals. After individual fluorescent emitters are localized by fitting their PSFs, the brightness of a reconstructed spot in the SMLM image represents the frequency in which the emitter was detected at that location (Fig. 1c). Furthermore, these discrete but bright spots are spread throughout the entire flake, so the size of these clusters is related to the photon numbers and dynamics of generated emitters, and it determines the resolution of the SMLM image (≈30 nm in this work, as estimated by the decorrelation algorithm51 in Supplementary Note 3). Further results on dipole orientation and rotational mobility are shown in Fig. 1d and each parameter can be rendered as a pseudo-color map, showing the ability of the method to reveal wobble, polar, and azimuthal angles. Moreover, Fig. 1e shows two typical experimental polarized-PSFs and their fitting results, for which an empirical interpretation can be stated as follows considering a stationary dipole: The distribution of the light intensity on the four polarization channels will depend on the azimuthal angle, while the presence of the polar angle will lead to shifts and deformations of polarized PSFs. In the case of wobbling dipoles, both features will be degraded. In the following sections we will present a step-by-step description of the molecule-crystal interaction through dipole orientation.

The breaking of axial symmetry

Directly measuring the structure and dynamics of single-molecule interactions is highly challenging. We propose using dipole orientation as a fast and sensitive method to track changes between different molecular structural states, based on their distinct dipole configurations. Here, the h-BN in the acetonitrile solvent creates fluorescent emission in the visible range through a reversible interaction. At a constant power of 2 × 103 W cm2 with circularly polarized laser (ellipticity > 97%) as the excitation light, the ability to extract polar and wobble angles allows us to identify three distinct populations instead of two (Fig. 2a) on a flat crystal where no edges were included. The bin width of the 2D heat map (Fig. 2a) is chosen based on numerical simulations of polar-PSFs at different SNRs (Supplementary Note 2.2), which takes care of overestimating orientational accuracy compared to the Cramer-Rao lower bound. Figure 2b illustrates the orientation of three states in the spherical coordinate and rotational diffusion in a cone. The first state has the smallest polar angle and allows the dipole to wobble in a large conical region due to thermal fluctuations, etc. The second state is almost parallel and firmly locked to the h-BN surface. The third state is slightly different in that it has a larger wobble angle compared to the first, but the polar angle is nearly 90°. Since the C3 symmetry and 2D confinement effect of h-BN, this state suggests that it originates from the binding of fluorescent emitters with rotational mobility, instead of the unrestricted wobbling, at symmetric contact sites on the lattice. Moreover, the angular fluorescence intensity of a fixed emission dipole at the far-field depends on <sin(θ)2 > . The emitter in state-3 has the best overlap with the excited circularly polarized light in the X-Y plane, so the excitation and photon collection efficiency are the highest. However, the emission in state-2 and state-1 gradually weakens as the polar angle decreases. Both solid-state quantum emitters and fluorescent dyes are regulated by external conditions2,45,52–54, and our situation is no exception. To further understand the dynamics of the emitters, we employed an electrochemical system45 while also measuring dynamically the polarization states. Here we use a two-electrode system with a working electrode (indium tin oxide, ITO) and a counter/reference electrode (platinum, Pt). All potentials reported herein are measured against a Pt reference. The whole sample was scanned with cyclic voltammetry from 0-2 V for 15 cycles to ensure the stability of electrodes and solvent in this working range (Supplementary Note 3). Figure 2d shows the change in the number of localizations during the three consecutive cycles. Under the application of an increasingly positive electrochemical potential (orange curve), the localization (blue curve) decreases and vice versa, which can be observed by all SMLM techniques but cannot be correlated to the molecular structure45,52. But when analyzing each set of emitter states separately, we find that the proportion (abbreviated as P, violet curve) of wobble angles α > 15° (i.e., state-1 and −3, P1 + P3) is consistent with the trend of the overall number of localizations. In contrast, the proportion of state-2 (α < 15°) increases with increasing voltage, suggesting that emitters with large wobble angles are more easily quenched and vice versa.

Under the condition of no voltage applied (Fig. 2a), the value of P1 + P3 is dominates the variation of the overall numbers. However, under the influence of the potential gradient, the share of this fraction is significantly suppressed to less than 50% (Fig. 2d), indicating that the rotational diffusion correlates with more reactivity and is more sensitive to the environmental conditions than that of the state-2. In terms of temporal dynamics of these emitters, although macroscopic changes in the number of localizations respond to the changing electrochemical environment with a phase delay of a few seconds, the gradient (green curve, dN_emitters/dt) shows that the emitter does respond immediately when the voltage is reversed. Besides, after stacking the histograms of polar angle for state-2 into a 2D heat map (Fig. 2e), the pixel colors in each row indicate the number density at the current potential. Although the structure of state-2 is more stable, it can be noticed that these dipole orientations still shift slightly in the polar angle. The emitter density during the voltage rise phase produces a significant displacement at 0.7 V, with a peak 40° ± 1° (dashed line); when the voltage falls back to 0.4 V, the emitter density returns to its original distribution, and the total number of emitters is still lagging behind the potential. On the right, we show five typical polar-angle distribution curves and fit them with a double Gaussian model, which clearly shows the shift of the two peaks under electrochemical modulation.

In-plane orientations of emitters

In this section, we turn our attention to the azimuth angle of each emitter to study how fluorescent emitters interact with the crystal, taking advantage of the well-defined crystal structure of h-BN. All localized emitters are rendered as pseudo-color maps and stacked over time based on the azimuth angle (Fig. 3a). The multiple orientations observed at specific locations on the crystal demonstrate the heterogeneity of emitters, suggesting that multiple closely spaced binding sites cannot be fully resolved because of the resolution limit of SMLM ( ≈ 30 nm). The azimuth angles of emitters in the four regions of interests (ROIs) can be plotted as short sticks. Three of the ROIs are sharp crystal edges and one is a line channel. The orientation of these dipoles is not strictly aligned with the crystal edges, where ROIs 1, 3, and 4 are at angles to the boundary, and ROI 2 is oriented coinciding with the direction of the crystal edge. However, when we plot the histogram of the Δφ = |φedge-φemitters | , these angles are well-separated in three groups and are spaced radially apart from each other, indicating a structured arrangement rather than a random distribution. When we compare the box plots in Fig. 3d, the interquartile range of ROIs 4 is significantly smaller than that of ROIs 3 even though the directions of the two regions are almost parallel, which can be explained by reasoning that the line channel confines the emitters more than the open edge. The larger width of the box plot for ROIs 2 is understood in that two peaks exist in Fig. 3c. The respective proportions of the three states in each ROI are P1 > P2 > P3, while the fraction of the state-3 is almost negligible in these areas (Fig. 3e), compared to the flat area (dash line). We further examine the cluster of state-2 in the flat crystal area for which the azimuthal angle is most accurate, because the rotation-limited model considers the average orientation of the dipole, and thus dipole wobble will weaken the azimuthal accuracy significantly. The angular distribution in the flat center of the flake exhibits six distinct peaks and can be grouped into two clusters (Fig. 3f), where they are marked in red and orange separately. And when the sample was rotated about 21° (Supplementary Note 5), the position of the azimuthal distribution moved the same as well. Then according to the C3 symmetry of the h-BN structure, the azimuthal orientation of dipoles initially measured in the 0°-180° range can be reconstructed to span the entire 0°−360° range (Fig. 3g) and overlapped with the crystal lattice in Fig. 3h. Remarkably, we now readily find that the azimuthal angle does not strictly follow the h-BN structure, where the averaged orientation is 47° ± 5° (median ± standard deviation) with respect to the lattice and the angle of the two symmetric clusters is 26° ± 7°. Here, we show the symmetry of the emitter orientation with respect to the material lattice with the help of h-BN crystals.

Molecular dynamics at solid-liquid interface

Interfacial fluorescent emitters are more dynamic than solid-state emitters and 2D h-BN is an ideal platform for single-molecule tracking due to their specific adsorption capacity for molecules and appropriate kinetic temporal scales14,18,55. We selected around 2 × 105 localizations within a 12 × 12 μm2 flat region at the center of the crystal, and more than 3 × 104 trajectories to obtain reliable statistical results. Figure 4a shows a trajectory map for events longer than four consecutive frames, which are connected based on the localization of random active emitters with nanometric precision (see Methods section on trajectory linking and analysis). These connected events show two main types: random walking on the crystal surface and trapping into bright spots by a fixed location, which is discriminated according to the probability density function (PDF) of the one-dimensional displacements shown in Fig. 4b. These PDFs can always be represented by a superposition of two Gaussian models with increasing delay times (τ). The narrowed Gaussian with a constant standard deviation of 30 nm represents the trapped event, while the broader part with an increased standard deviation represents the trajectories of random walk. The diffusion coefficient of these emitters in trajectory is 7.02 × 10-14 m2 s-1. The dwell times of the trapped event and the trajectory follow the double exponential decay as Fig. 4c, and the time constants that correspond to chemisorption are 35 and 68 ms, respectively. The inset shows that the traveling distance of trajectory events is significantly longer than trapped events. The emitter dynamics so far have been limited to the analysis of localizations. But here we can extend the dimension of molecular diffusion by including orientational information, allowing us to further stratify the trapped events into anchored, jumping, and switching behaviors. Their respective percentages approximately follow the exponential distribution as shown in Fig. 4e and Fig. 4f shows typical examples of these events. The anchored denotation means that the orientation and mobility of the dipole remain constant when the emitter is continuously activated at a fixed location. The jumping event indicates that the orientation of a dipole changes significantly from one stable state to another during successive activations. Finally, the event of switching means that the emitter, although trapped in a fixed position within the resolution of SMLM, switches back and forth between two equilibrium states. Figure 4e shows that the dwell times of the three subgroups are close to each other and their dwell times are on the same order of magnitude. In addition, because the orientation of these emitters is changed from one state to another, then we interpret this as the alternate capture of individual molecules by neighboring localizations rather than the reorientation in identical positions. When the position of each state is marked (stars in the trajectory illustration in Fig. 4f) in the event, it enables the grouping of emitters with similar states, thereby enhancing the SMLM resolution for samples without prior knowledge. This would not be possible without manually altering the environmental conditions, suggesting that this behavior may come from at least two sources. The double localization56 results of these two demonstration events show that the distances between the two neighboring point sources are 8 ± 3 nm and 11 ± 4 nm, respectively. The 2D heat map of the trapped events (Fig. 4g) shows that trapped emitters mostly belong to state-2, which means the freely moving emitters are more active as states-1 and -3.

Finally, we turn our attention back to the trajectory events, which also include three basic types, as shown in Fig. 4h: (1) the molecule is captured after random walking; (2) captured before leaving and (3) wandering freely on the surface. The azimuth angle is represented by an arrow in the figure, the color of the arrow codes the polar angle, and the arrow length is the wobble angle. The three basic behaviors described above can be further combined into complex behaviors (4) and (5). The trajectory(4) indicates that the same molecule is captured by three different locations consecutively, while trajectory(5) is that a molecule is captured by the same location multiple times. This can be validated by the squared displacement (SD) of Fig. 4i, where the trapped positions are presented as platforms on the curve. In addition, the angular difference between the orientation of the dipole and the direction of movement is uniformly distributed in Fig. 4j, indicating that the movement of the dipole is mainly governed by Brownian motion instead of the crystal lattice and that the transition dipole moment does not affect the macroscopic motion of the molecule. When we review the trajectory shown in Fig. 4h, we find that the trapped emitter seems to have a relatively smaller wobble angle (short arrow) or a larger polar angle (green color of arrow). This further validates the observation in Fig. 4g that the degree of angular freedom is inversely correlated to capture events.

Discussion

This work applies single-molecule orientation-localization microscopy to investigate the polarization properties of fluorescent emitters at the solid-liquid interface, where molecular dynamics are more complex than in solid-state systems. To address this, we present an end-to-end pipeline, ranging from calibration to data analysis, for tracking orientational and localization dynamics. High-throughput characterization reveals that the dipole orientation reflects molecular conformation and exhibits C3 symmetry relative to the crystal lattice. Our experiments show that the rotational mobility of emitters is highly sensitive to electrochemical conditions, and their 3D-orientation has a significant preference for the h-BN structure. Additionally, the solid-liquid interface offers extensive possibilities for the tracking of single-molecule dynamics: emitters can remain anchored, jump, and move laterally on the h-BN surface. This combination of molecular conformation and dynamics allows us to achieve real-time and large-scale readouts of excited states at the molecular level, which are unmatched by bulk measurements. While this orientation analysis is most effective for linearly polarized emitters (e.g., carbon/oxygen doped defects) rather than high-symmetry centers (e.g., D3h boron vacancies), it remains a powerful tool for detecting symmetry breaking under electric fields or external stress. Ultimately, we aim to advance the understanding of molecular interaction, enhance trace sensing in nanofluidics, to open up possibilities for advanced optical characterization by enabling the simultaneous tracking of molecular conformations and photophysical activity.

Methods

Sample preparation

The precision coverslips (CG15XH, Thorlabs) were sonicated in 2% Hellmanex III and rinsed with deionized water for residual impurities before being exposed to oxygen plasma (90 s, 100 W). Pristine h-BN flakes were exfoliated from high-quality crystals by using low-adhesion Nitto tape and then transferred to clean coverslips without pressing them. Samples were left to stand for 24 h to improve transfer efficiency. The sample was mounted on the custom microscope, and its surface was completely covered with acetonitrile solvent (99.9% purity). The sample chamber was rinsed twice with the same acetonitrile solvent to clean residues before imaging. The chamber is machined from the polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and it is resistant to organic solvents. We used coverslips coated with an ITO layer (70-100 Ω per square, Diamond Coatings Ltd) and kept electrodes fully immersed in the organic solvent during electrochemistry experiments.

Imaging setup

The 561 nm laser is modulated into circularly polarized light (97% ellipticity) by a combination of a polarizer (LPVISE100-A, Thorlabs) and a quarter-wave plate (WPQ05M-561, Thorlabs), and then illuminates the sample with normal incidence. The experimental sample is fixed on the nano-positioning system (Mad City Labs) by a customized sample stage. Fluorescence is collected by a 60×, 1.42 NA oil-immersion objective lens (PLAPON60XO, Olympus), which performs well for polarization imaging. Excitation light around 561 nm wavelength is reflected by the dichroic mirror (Di03-R561-t1, Semrock), and the fluorescence from the sample is further filtered out by a combination of low-pass (750 nm, to remove the fluorescence from boron vacancy) and notch filters (561 nm, to clean the reflected light from excitation beam). The variable waveplate (LCC1223-A, Thorlabs) is used for compensating for the phase distortion caused by the birefringence and transmission coefficient difference of the first dichroic mirror coating. After the tube lens, a 50:50 non-polarizing beam splitter (BS016, Thorlabs) splits the fluorescence into two channels, and the polarization state of one path is rotated 45° by the half-wave plate (AHWP10M-580, Thorlabs), and then two polarizing beam splitters (PBS251, Thorlabs) split them into four channels. To introduce z-axis information and to take full advantage of the polarization properties, the PSF image of one redundant channel is defocus about 150 nm compared to the other three channels. Finally, all beams are reflected by mirrors to sCMOS (Prime-95B, Photometrics) for imaging and the maximum FOV is about 40×40 μm2. In summary, the pSMLM employed in this study achieves precise calibration of polarization distortion through two steps: variable waveplate compensation in the emission path and numerical compensation in post-processing. As a comparison, Rimoli35 used a four-channel setup with the same focal plane to perform 2D-localization and relied solely on light intensity to calculate the in-plane angle and the in-plane projection of the wobble angle. The effective NA of the objective lens is limited to 1.2 to maintain the accuracy of pure light intensity calculation. However, in our work, axial information coupling and three-dimensional localization are realized by defocusing one redundant channel among the four polarization channels (I0 + I90 = I45 + I135). Finally, the azimuth angle, polar angle, and wobble angle of the dipole were obtained through PSF fitting, and the localization bias caused by the dipole angles was corrected at the same time. Therefore, we can achieve six-dimensional (x, y, z, φ, θ, α) simultaneously imaging.

Imaging detection and estimation algorithms

The imaging strategy was performed in an out-of-focus mode at about 100 nm in the axial axis, and the images of the four channels were registered by affine transformation and localized. Subsequently, after the PSFs of the same dipole in each channel are taken out from the raw data and assigned together, dipoles with empty channels of more than two are deleted, avoiding mislocalization by utilizing intrinsic physical constraints. The azimuthal angle of the dipole is calculated directly from the difference in light intensity of each channel, and the polar and wobble angles are fitted simultaneously by the simulated annealing algorithm.

Trajectory linking and analysis

When the localization of the emitter in two consecutive frames is less than a given distance threshold, these two emitters will be connected on the temporal sequence. For trapped events, this threshold is 32 nm, and the threshold for randomly walking trajectories is 125 nm. The distance threshold is set according to the full width at half maximum (FWHM ≈ 2.355σ for a Gaussian distribution) of the probability distribution of one-dimensional displacements, so the confidence that the event originated from the same emitter is larger than 95.44% (2σ). The pipeline used to identify the three subcategories of capture events is as follows: The first step is to identify if a reorientation has occurred using outliers and thresholds to determine if it belongs to an anchored event. Subsequently, jumping and switching events are further determined by comparing the difference between the dipole orientations at the beginning and the end of the event sequence.
