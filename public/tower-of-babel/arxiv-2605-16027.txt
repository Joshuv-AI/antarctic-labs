Nearest-neighbour matching on unbounded supports and covariate shift transfer
Simon VIEL

Abstract
Expectations of multivariate functions with missing labels occur in various fields such as transfer learning and average treatment effects. Although non-parametric estimators based on nearest-neighbour matching are frequently used in this context, the existing literature assumes that the covariates live in some well-shaped compact subset of ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} , with densities that are bounded away from zero. In this paper, we show that the usual rates of convergence can be achieved with minimal assumptions on the covariate supports. These assumptions are replaced with conditions on the source and target distributions, among which a measure of the tranferability between the two probability measures. We show that these conditions are general, can be applied to distributions supported on manifolds, and allow the target distribution to have a heavier tail than the source distribution. We also show that this control of the transferability is needed for any estimator to achieve good rates of convergence. Finally, applying our results to the estimation of treatment effects, we could relax the assumption that the assignment probabilities had to be bounded away from zero and one.



I
Introduction

We study non-parametric estimation of an expectation of a function with missing observations. In many practical situations, we can find
expectations involving a pair of random variables, a covariate vector and a label, for which the labels are partially observed. In the
context called
covariate shift

(
22
)
, such expectations can be encountered in various fields, such as
econometrics
(
10
)
, natural language processing
(
6
)
;
(
13
)
, and
brain-computer interfaces
(
24
)
;
(
16
)
. A common problem in these situations is to estimate
an expectation of the form
\mathbb{E}[h(X^{*},Y^{*})]
, observing a sample of the covariate vector
X^{*}
without the labels
Y^{*}
and a sample of
the labelled pair
(X,Y)
. In the covariate shift scenario, we assume that the conditional distributions of
Y^{*}
given
X^{*}
and
Y

given
X
are the same while the covariate distributions
Q:=P_{X^{*}}
and
P:=P_{X}
of
X^{*}
and
X
may differ. In supervised learning,
setting
h(x,y):=l(F(x),y)
for some loss function
l
and some hypothesis function
F
, our problem becomes risk estimation, which
is a key step for empirical risk minimisation.

Another important situation is the estimation of treatment effects in econometrics. This context features a triplet of random variables

(X,Y,W)
, where
W\in\{0;1\}
designates whether an individual received an active or a passive treatment. Of the two potential
outcomes
Y(0)
and
Y(1)
, only
Y_{i}=Y_{i}(W_{i})
is observed for the individual
i
. The objective of estimating the average treatment effect

\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]
, using the observations
(X_{i},Y_{i},W_{i})_{i=1,\dots,N}
, then relates to our problem.

In the case where the two covariate distributions
Q
and
P
have respective densities
q
and
p
with respect to a reference measure, the
quantity of interest can be rewritten as an expectation weighted by a density ratio:
\mathbb{E}[h(X^{*},Y^{*})]=\mathbb{E}[h(X,Y)q(X)/p(X)]
. In that
regard, many sources have introduced weights for the source observations that estimate the density ratio
q/p
, also called the

importance
, see
(
25
)
,
(
14
)
,
(
18
)
, and
(
31
)
.

Another natural approach to covariate shift adaptation is to consider, as an estimator of the expectation
\mathbb{E}[h(X^{*},Y^{*})]
, an empirical
average over the target sample
(X_{j}^{*})_{j=1,\dots,m}
such as
\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{h}(X_{j}^{*})
, where
\hat{h}(X_{j}^{*})
replaces
h(X_{j}^{*},Y_{j}^{*})
due
to the missing label
Y_{j}^{*}
. Therefore, such an estimator only depends on the pointwise estimator
\hat{h}(z)
. However, because we are
aiming to estimate an expectation and take the average over the target sample of the pointwise estimator
\hat{h}(z)
, the latter need
not be consistent for the final estimator to be consistent. Instead, it turns out to be more beneficial to use a very localised pointwise
estimator for
\hat{h}(z)
. Such local estimators can be obtained, for example, using
k
-nearest neighbours (
k
-NN) algorithms with a
small parameter
k
. It is known that while the variance of a
k
-NN pointwise predictor scales in
1/k
, the variance of the final
estimator of the expectation scales in
1/n
for all choices of
k
, where
n
is the sample size of the source sample. Because the bias
of
k
-NN estimators increases with
k
, taking
k=1
is then a common choice. In a series of studies on the estimation of average
treatment effects (ATE), a problem related to ours, Abadie and Imbens (
1
,
3
,

2
,
4
) established several asymptotic results for
k
-NN matching estimators with
fixed
k
. The link between matching estimators and importance estimation was shown in
17
. In the context of
covariate shift adaptation,
1
-NN algorithms can be found in
18
and
21
. Recently,
k
-NN
matching estimators were extended by
11
to
k
-NN local polynomial estimators to reduce the bias in higher
dimensions. Other estimation problems with expectations or integrals have also used
1
-NN algorithms, for instance to estimate entropy

(
23
)
or residual variance
(
8
)
. Algorithms based on
k
-NN matching have fast computation
times and require no hyper-parameter tuning. As we shall see, they also perfectly take into account the decay of the source probability
mass.

However, the study of
k
-NN type estimators in the literature is frequently connected to the
strong density assumption

(
5
)
, assuming that the covariates have a density that is bounded away from zero and infinity on some compact
well-shaped subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This assumption can be found in
1
,
17
,

21
,
11
, and
27
. Although this assumption can be a standard
approximation in practice, the way rates of convergence for the estimators depend on the approximated support diameter or density bounds,
for instance, was often not made explicit in previous studies. In this paper, we show that the same rates of convergence can be obtained
under milder assumptions on the covariate supports and distributions. We extend previous results to unbounded domains, and we also allow
probability densities to go to zero near the boundary of the support. In contrast, we introduce conditions that measure the transferability
between the two probability measures
P
and
Q
, based on the integrability of functions linked to the importance
\,\mathrm{d}Q/\,\mathrm{d}P
.
Studying the moments of the density ratio is a natural way to measure transferability between the source and target distributions

(
19
)
. Similar measures were obtained on bounded supports using ball-mass ratios, where the singularity between the
source and target distributions is quantified via the
transfer exponent

(
15
)
;
(
26
)
;

(
7
)
, or a variant using
\alpha
-families of distributions
(
20
)
. Eventually, the transfer function

(
30
)
measures the transferability on unbounded domains using local mass assumptions on the two probability
distributions. In this paper, we use a related transferability measure that can be applied to wider classes of multivariate distributions,
including Gaussian distributions. For our specific problem, we even allow the target distribution to have a heavier tail than the source
distribution.

In a second direction, we show that if the covariate observations
X
and
X^{*}
are given as vectors in some Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
associated distributions
P
and
Q
need not have density with respect to the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Instead, the relevant
information lies in the
intrinsic dimensions
of
P
and
Q
, characterised by the volumes of balls under these measures. It means
that the observations may be located on a
d_{0}
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, up to some small noise. We stress that no knowledge
about the supports or the intrinsic dimensions is required in order to compute the estimators. Nonetheless, the size-dependent rates of
convergence that we obtain depend only on the intrinsic dimensions of
P
and
Q
and not on the dimension
d
of the overlying space.

Let
d
be a positive integer,
\mathcal{Y}
a measurable space,
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
a given measurable function, and let
P_{X,Y}
and
P_{X^{*},Y^{*}}
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}
. We intend to estimate the expectation

e(h):=\mathbb{E}[h(X^{*},Y^{*})]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{Y}}h(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}P_{X^{*},Y^{*}},

using only an unlabelled sample
(X_{j}^{*})_{j=1,\dots,m}
from
P_{X^{*}}
and an
independent labelled sample
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
from
P_{X,Y}
. This formulation enables us to tackle regression (
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
)
and classification (
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1;1\}
) problems at the same time. The
source
distribution
P:=P_{X}
and the
target
distribution

Q:=P_{X^{*}}
are assumed to have support in some (possibly unknown) Borel set
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can then equivalently write

e(h)=\mathbb{E}[g_{h}(X^{*})]=\int_{\mathbb{X}}g_{h}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z),

where
g_{h}:z\in\mathbb{X}\mapsto\mathbb{E}[h(X^{*},Y^{*})\mid X^{*}=z]\in\mathbb{R}
is the regression function. Note that by the
covariate shift assumption, the conditional distributions of
Y
given
X
and of
Y^{*}
given
X^{*}
are the same, so we also have

g_{h}(z)=\mathbb{E}[h(X,Y)\mid X=z]
. When there is no ambiguity, we shall simply write the function
g
instead of
g_{h}
. The final estimators
we consider have the form

\hat{e}(h):=\hat{e}_{m,n}\big(\hat{h}\big):=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\hat{h}(X_{j}^{*}),

where
\hat{h}
is a random function independent
from the target sample
(X_{j}^{*})_{j=1,\dots,m}
. When the pointwise estimator is linear in the observed labels, that is when
\hat{h}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(z)h(X_{i},Y_{i})
for some random
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
-measurable weights
w_{1}(z),\dots,w_{n}(z)\in\mathbb{R}
, then the final
estimator can be written as the weighted average

\hat{e}(h)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{i}(X_{j}^{*})\right)h(X_{i},Y_{i}).

We consider non-parametric pointwise estimators
\hat{h}(z)
that are based on nearest-neighbour algorithms. More precisely, for a fixed
nonnegative integer
L
, the pointwise estimator
\hat{h}_{L}(z)
approximates the regression function
g_{h}
by a polynomial of order
L

inside the
k
th-order Voronoï cell created around the point
z
with the sample
(X_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n}
. The coefficients of the polynomial
are then fitted by a least-square minimisation using the source observations
(X_{i},Y_{i})
such that
X_{i}
belongs to this Voronoï cell. The
case where
L=0
is of special interest, where we approximate the function
g_{h}
in the Voronoï cell by a constant.

To summarise, the key contributions of this paper are the following.

1.

We show that the usual rates of convergence for
k
-NN matching and local polynomial estimators extend to situations with general
unbounded supports, where the covariate densities are allowed to approach zero and infinity.

2.

We relate these rates to the intrinsic dimensions of the source and target distributions, in the case the covariate vectors are
supported on lower dimensional subspaces, up to some small noise.

3.

We discuss the general conditions we introduce on the relative behaviour of the source and target distributions.

4.

We apply our results to the estimation of treatment effects under the same general assumptions for the covariate vector, and we also
allow the probability to assign a given treatment to approach zero.

This paper is organised as follows. Section
II
contains the results for the rates of the matching and the local polynomial
estimators on an unbounded domain, under simple design assumptions that show the effect of the covariate shift transferability, followed by a
discussion of minimax lower bounds. Section
III
contains extensions of these results for both estimators under general design
assumptions. An application of these results to the estimation of average treatment effects can be found in Section
IV
. In Section

V
, the conditions on the source and target distributions are shown to be general and easy to interpret, even when the
distributions are supported on manifolds. Possible extensions of the results are discussed in Section
VI
. Numerical
experiments are given in Section
VII
. A conclusion is given in Section
VIII
and proofs of our results can be
found in Section
IX
. Technical lemmas are gathered in an Appendix Section.

on the set

. The indicator function

is equal to

on

and

on the complement of

. The set

in

. For

,

and

denote, respectively, the minimum and the maximum between

and

.
Finally, for

, while

.

For

, we denote by

. For all

II-B

Rates for the matching estimator on an unbounded domain

In this section, we provide a direct extension of the results on the convergence rates for the matching estimator when the covariate vector
has a density that is allowed to approach zero. Let
k\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket
and
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
be fixed. We consider the final estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)=\hat{e}_{m,n}\big(\hat{h}_{0}\big)
, with the choice of the classical pointwise matching estimator, defined for all
z\in\mathbb{X}
by

\hat{h}_{0}(z):=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}h(X_{i},Y_{i})\text{ if }\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0},\text{ else }\hat{h}_{0}(z):=0.

Censoring when the NN-radius is too large is in general necessary with unbounded supports to avoid high-distance integrability
difficulties. It is also justified by the fact that, for a general smooth function
g
, no relevant information about the quantity
g(z)

can be deduced from the observations when they are all far from the point
z
. The exact value of the censor radius
r_{0}
only affects
the constants in the rates of convergence of the estimators, and its tuning is beyond the scope of this paper. We can therefore consider

r_{0}=1
, which generally has little impact on the conditions below where the radius
r_{0}
is used. It is also possible to omit censoring under
additional assumptions on the regression function
g
, for example, if
g
is bounded
Q
-almost surely on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case, any unknown
radius
r_{0}
can be used in the conditions below.

We now state conditions on the distributions of the random variables
(X,Y)
and
X^{*}
. In this section, we consider a simple design
that makes the conditions on the relative behaviour of the source and target distributions appear clearly. A more general setup is
described in Section
III
.

(X1)

The conditional variance
\operatorname{Var}(h(X,Y)\mid X)
is bounded by some constant
\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. There exists
\ell\in(0,1]

such that the regression function
g
is bounded and
\ell
-Hölder continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(X2)

The distributions
P
and
Q
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
respective densities
f_{P}
and
f_{Q}
. Besides, there exist constants
c_{P},C_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all

r\in[0,2r_{0}]
,
P(B(z,r))\geq c_{P}f_{P}(z)r^{d}
, and
Q(B(z,r))\leq C_{Q}f_{Q}(z)r^{d}
.

(X3)

The following integrals are finite:

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)^{2}}{f_{P}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty,\text{ and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)}{f_{P}(z)^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty.

Condition
(X1)
is standard in the non-parametric regression literature. Condition
(X2)
allows us to control the
volume of a ball under
P
and
Q
by the volume of the ball for the Lebesgue measure times the density of
P
or
Q
taken at the
center of the ball. This condition, which will be relaxed in the next section, is satisfied for several classes of distributions
on unbounded domains, such as exponential distributions or Pareto distributions, and it has been used in previous studies on covariate shift
learning
(
29
)
;
(
30
)
. Finally, Condition
(X3)
features the

covariate shift transferability
condition. The first integral condition is equivalent to asking the importance function

\mathrm{d}Q/\mathrm{d}P=f_{Q}/f_{P}
to be integrable with respect to the measure
Q
, or equivalently square-integrable with respect
to the measure
P
. This condition was already present in
19
or in studies on importance estimation; see

14
. Meanwhile, the second integral condition is very similar to the first one, and the two will be equivalent in some
cases. Following the definitions of the transfer function and the integrability index
\gamma^{*}
from
30
,
our condition means that
\gamma^{*}(P,Q)\geq 1/2
. In many situations, we can interpret this condition by saying that the target distribution

Q
is allowed to have a heavier tail than the source distribution
P
, as long as it is not twice as heavy.
We are now in a position to state the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Mean-squared error)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(X1)
to
(X3)
. Then there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the mean-squared
error of the estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{e}_{0}(h)-e(h))^{2}]\leq C\left(\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}+\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1\wedge 2\ell/d}\right).

In Theorem
1
, because the upper bound of the variance term
C(m^{-1}+n^{-1})
does not depend on the parameter
k
,
taking
k=1
can be optimal for the mean-squared error as it minimises the upper bound of the bias term
C(k/n)^{1\wedge 2\ell/d}
.
The estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
then achieves the parametric rate of convergence
m^{-1}+n^{-1}
as soon as
d\leq 2\ell
, where
\ell\in(0,1]

is the regularity of the regression function. Therefore, it requires
d\leq 2
for a Lipschitz function
g
. In higher dimensions and
with more regularity on the function
g
, we can give an extension to the estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
that has a lower bias than in the
upper bound of Theorem
1
. This extension is the object of the next section.

II-C

Rates for the local polynomial estimator on an unbounded domain

In this subsection, we analyse the rates of convergence of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)=\hat{e}_{m,n}\big(\hat{h}_{L}\big)
, with the choice of
a more general pointwise estimator
\hat{h}_{L}
, introduced by
11
to reduce the bias when the regression
function
g
has more regularity. They use local polynomials of order
L\in\mathbb{N}
to approximate the regression function inside the Voronoï
cells, where the choice
L=0
leads to the classical matching estimator from the previous section.

We set
N_{L}
the set of
d
-tuples of non-negative integers whose sum does not exceed
L
, with cardinality
K^{*}:=K^{*}(d,L)
. For
\alpha\in N_{L}
, we write
\zeta_{\alpha}(z,T):=(T-z)^{\alpha}:=\prod_{j=1}^{d}(T_{j}-z_{j})^{\alpha_{j}}
, which is a local monomial in
T
in
d

coordinates, of degree at most
L
, and centered at the point
z
. Finally,
\zeta(z,T)=(\zeta_{\alpha}(z,T))_{\alpha\in N_{L}}
will denote
the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}
made up of all such local monomials. The estimator of the regression function and its derivatives is the least
square estimator

\hat{G}_{L}(z):=\underset{b\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}\left(h(X_{i},Y_{i})-b^{\top}\zeta(z,X_{i})\right)^{2}.

Therefore, the pointwise estimator of the regression function is

\hat{h}_{L}(z):=e_{0}^{\top}\hat{G}_{L}(z)=e_{0}^{\top}M(z)^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}h(X_{i},Y_{i})\zeta(z,X_{i})\text{ if }\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0},\text{ else }\hat{h}_{L}(z):=0,

where
e_{0}
is the vector in the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}
, corresponding to the tuple
(0,\dots,0)\in N_{L}
, and

M(z)
is the square matrix

M(z):=\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}\zeta(z,X_{i})\zeta(z,X_{i})^{\top}=\left(\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}(X_{i}-z)^{\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}}\right)_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in N_{L}}.

Note that when
L=0,\,\zeta(z,T)
is the constant polynomial
1\in\mathbb{R},\,M(z)=k
, and
\hat{h}_{L}(x)
coincides with the classical estimator

\hat{h}_{0}(x)
defined in Section
II-B
. In what follows, we will need the constant
D:=D(d,L):=\sum_{i=1}^{L}i\binom{d+i-1}{i}
.
In addition to the list of conditions from Section
II-B
, we consider the following conditions.

(X1’)

The conditional variance
\operatorname{Var}(h(X,Y)\mid X)
is bounded by some constant
\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. The regression function
g

is bounded and
L
-times differentiable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and its derivative of order
L
is a bounded and Lipschitz continuous function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(
\mathcal{L}
)

There exists a constant
V_{P}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all
x\in B(z,3r_{0}),\,f_{P}(x)\leq V_{P}f_{P}(z)
.

Condition
(X1’)
asks for more regularity on the regression function
g
than in the previous section, while Condition

(

L

)
complements Condition
(X2)
with a local upper bound on the source density. As we discuss in Section
V
, it
is satisfied for the same types of distributions as the ones satisfying Condition
(X2)
.
Adapting the nice proof technique of
11
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 2

(Mean-squared error)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(X1’)
,
(X2)
,
(X3)
, and
(

L

)
. Then, taking
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
, there
exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the mean-squared error of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{e}_{L}(h)-e(h))^{2}]\leq C\left(\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}+\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{1\wedge 2(L+1)/d}\right).

Theorem
2
shows similar rates of convergence than in Theorem
1
, that is,
m^{-1}+n^{-1}+(k/n)^{1\wedge 2\ell/d}
, where
\ell
denotes the regularity of the regression function, but this time allowing
\ell>1
. As a consequence, the local
polynomial estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
, with a constant parameter
k
, can achieve the parametric rate of convergence
m^{-1}+n^{-1}
in
dimension
d>2
, as long as we choose
L+1\geq d/2
. In Section
III
, we show that the same result holds under milder assumptions on
the regression function
g
and the covariate distributions
P
and
Q
. But first, we prove minimax lower bounds for the estimation of

e(h)
that show that the integrability of the importance function
f_{Q}/f_{P}
, as in Condition
(X3)
, affects the best achievable rate
of convergence.

II-D

Minimax lower bounds

In this section, we provide minimax lower bounds for the estimation of the expectation
e(h)
depending on the transferability between the
source and the target probability measures. We start by fixing the dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and a regularity parameter
\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, which
can be arbitrarily large. In this study, we consider the regression setup
Y=g(X)+\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon
is a Gaussian random variable
independent from
X
, and we consider the function
h(x,y):=y
. To state minimax lower bounds, we introduce a class for the distributions
of
X,Y,X^{*}
that satisfy a list of conditions that focus on the integrability of the function
f_{Q}^{\alpha}/f_{P}^{\gamma}
, as in Condition

(X3)
. Note that in our regression setup, the couple of distributions
(P_{X,Y},Q)
depends only on the covariate distributions

P
and
Q
, the regression function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the distribution of the noise
\varepsilon
. For
\gamma\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, we say
that the pair
(P_{X,Y},Q)
belongs to the class
\Sigma(\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}})
when:

•

the noise
\varepsilon
follows the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\Lambda^{2})
for some
\Lambda^{2}\leq 1
,

•

the regression function
g
has derivatives up to order
\ell
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for all
s\in\llbracket 0;\ell\rrbracket,\,\frac{\lVert D^{s}g\rVert_{\infty,\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{s!}\leq\Lambda
,

•

the distributions
P
and
Q
have density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with respective densities
f_{P}
and

f_{Q}
that have infinitely many derivatives, all bounded by
1
, on their convex support, and

•

we have the transferability control

\Lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)^{2}}{f_{P}(z)^{\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}z\leq\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}},\text{ and}\quad\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)}{f_{P}(z)^{\gamma/2}}\,\mathrm{d}z\leq\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}}.

In the last point above, we relax Condition
(X3)
, which corresponds to the case where
\gamma=1
, allowing the target distribution
to have a heavier tail than in the previous sections. However, the following theorem shows that we can then lose the parametric rate of
convergence.

Theorem 3
.

There exists a universal constant
\mathcal{K}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that for all
\gamma\in[0,1],\,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
large enough,

\underset{\hat{E}_{n}}{\inf}\;\underset{(P_{X,Y},Q)\in\Sigma(\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}})}{\sup}\mathbb{E}[(\hat{E}_{n}-e(h))^{2}]\geq\mathcal{K}\;\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}}{n^{\gamma}\ln(n)^{2}}.

The above theorem shows that the control of the covariate shift transferability is a necessary ingredient to obtain good rates of
convergence, and an arbitrary number of bounded derivatives for the regression function
g
cannot compensate for a poor transfer
exponent
\gamma<1
. In addition, we shall see in Section
III-A
that the
k
-NN local polynomial estimator achieves
the rate of convergence
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\,n^{-\gamma}
as soon as
d\gamma\leq 2\ell
, which shows that in this case the minimax lower bound is
tight up to a logarithmic factor.

III
Rates for the estimators under general conditions

In this section, we extend the results from Section
II-B
under milder assumptions on the distributions of
(X,Y)
and

X^{*}
. Because we do not assume that the covariate support
\mathbb{X}
is compact, the regression function and its derivatives need not be
bounded on the whole domain
\mathbb{X}
, but only locally. We also show that the covariate distributions
P
and
Q
need not be absolutely
continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This latter assumption was only used to control the volumes of balls under
the measures
P
and
Q
, as in the second part of Condition
(X2)
. We now therefore work directly with conditions on the
volumes of balls under
P
and
Q
, which offers several benefits. First, they give definitions for the intrinsic dimensions of
the measures
P
and
Q
, which limit the rates of convergence of the
k
-NN estimators instead of the overlying dimension
d
. This
allows the study of covariate distributions supported on known or unknown submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, without any modification to the
estimators. Second, even when the covariate distributions have density with respect to the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, studying
the volumes of balls instead of the densities works better in several cases, including Gaussian distributions, distributions with a
density that goes to infinity near a finite point, or distributions supported on a non-regular support. These examples are addressed
in Section
V
.

III-A

Rates for the local polynomial estimator

In this section, we study the rates of convergence of the local polynomial estimator under milder assumptions than in Section

II
. Keeping the notation from Section
II-C
, we recall the definition of the pointwise local polynomial
estimator, defined for
Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
by

\hat{h}_{L}(z):=e_{0}^{\top}M(z)^{-1}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}h(X_{i},Y_{i})\zeta(z,X_{i})\text{ if }\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0},\text{ else }\hat{h}_{L}(z):=0.

Recall that if we set
L=0
, then
\hat{h}_{L}
coincides with the classical matching estimator, defined as
\hat{h}_{0}(z)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in I_{k}(z)}h(X_{i},Y_{i})

when
\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0}
. This means that we discuss the properties of the two estimators at the same time,
L=0
being a particular case.
We introduce the following conditions, generalising the list from Section
II
.

(A1)

There exist
\ell\in(0,L+1]
and measurable functions
\mathcal{G}_{\ell},\sigma^{2}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for
Q
-almost
all
z\in\mathbb{X}
, the conditional variance
x\mapsto\operatorname{Var}(h(X,Y)\mid X=x)
is bounded by
\sigma^{2}(z)
on
B(z,r_{0})\cap\mathbb{X}
, and
the regression function
g
is the restriction of a function having derivatives of order
\lceil\ell\rceil-1
that are all

(\ell+1-\lceil\ell\rceil)
-Hölder continuous on
B(z,3r_{0})\cap\operatorname{Conv}(\mathbb{X})
, with constant
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)\lceil\ell-1\rceil!
.

(A2)

There exist
d_{P},d_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and measurable functions
p,\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},v:\mathbb{X}\times[0,r_{0}]\to[1,\infty[
, such that for

Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
, all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
, and all Borel sets
\mathcal{A}\subset B(0,3)^{K^{*}}
, we have

\displaystyle P(B(z,r))

\displaystyle\geq p(z)r^{d_{P}},

\displaystyle Q(B(z,2r))

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,r^{d_{Q}-d_{P}}P(B(z,r)),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\frac{X_{1}-z}{r},\dots,\frac{X_{K^{*}}-z}{r}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(v(z,r)p(z)r^{d_{P}})^{K^{*}}\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert,

where
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert
designates the Lebesgue measure of the Borel set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{dK^{*}}
.

(A3)

There exist
\gamma_{\textup{res}}\in[0,d_{Q}/d_{P}],\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\in[0,(d_{Q}+2\ell)/d_{P}]
, and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in[0,\ell/d_{P}]
, such that the following
integrals are finite:

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{var}}:=\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(z)^{2DK^{*}}(\lVert g\rVert_{\infty,B(z,r_{0})\cap\mathbb{X}}^{2}+\sigma^{2}(z))\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{res}}:=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[v(z)^{2DK^{*}+1-\gamma_{\textup{res}}}\sigma^{2}(z)\right]p(z)^{1-\gamma_{\textup{res}}}\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}:=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[g(z)^{2}+v(z)^{2DK^{*}+1-\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)^{2}\right]p(z)^{1-\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,\text{ and}

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}:=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\left\lvert g(z)\right\rvert+v(z)^{DK^{*}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)\right]p(z)^{-\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,

where we have set, for
Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
,
v(z):=\underset{r\in[0,r_{0}]}{\sup}\frac{p(z)r^{d}}{P(B(z,r))}v(z,r)\leq\underset{r\in[0,r_{0}]}{\sup}v(z,r)
.

Condition
(A1)
extends Conditions
(X1)
and
(X1’)
by assuming local bounds on the conditional variance, the
regression function, and its derivatives. Asking
g
to be the restriction of a regular function on the convex hull of
\mathbb{X}
is a technical
condition ensuring the validity of Taylor expansions around points of
\mathbb{X}
.

Conditions
(A2)
and
(A3)
are the main subjects of Section
V
. Defining the intrinsic dimension of a measured metric
space by the volume of balls is classical in the literature
(
15
)
;
(
20
)
. Note that we assume that the
support
\mathbb{X}
is a subset of an Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
instead of a general metric space only to have access to higher order Holder regularity
and therefore Taylor expansions for the local polynomial estimators with
L\geq 1
.
Intuitively, the numbers
d_{P}
and
d_{Q}
in Condition
(A2)
represent the intrinsic dimensions of the distributions
P
and
Q
,
respectively, and they limit the convergence rates of the estimators. When
P
and
Q
are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue
measure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can take
d_{P}=d_{Q}=d
. In general, because Condition
(A2)
holds for
Q
-almost all
z
, we must have
d_{Q}\leq d_{P}
,
which can be expected in view of the nature of the covariate shift problem, where the measure
Q
must be absolutely continuous with respect to

P
. However, we will see that having
d_{Q}=d_{P}
is needed to achieve parametric rates of convergence. We stress that these dimensions
need not be known to compute the estimators.

Then the function
p
could be up to some constant the density function of
P
, as in Condition
(X2)
, while the function
v

represents the local multiplicative amplitude of
P
. We will see that the function
v
often grows slowly compared to the vanishing of
p
,
and therefore will not play a major role in the integrability conditions. Similarly, when
d_{P}=d_{Q}
, the function
\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}
could be up to
some constant the importance
\,\mathrm{d}Q/\,\mathrm{d}P
. Note that bounding directly the volume of balls
B(z,2r)
under
Q
by the volume of

B(z,r)
under
P
is more general, given the first part of Condition
(A2)
, than bounding
Q(B(z,2r))
by
q(z)(2r)^{d_{Q}}

for some measurable function
q
, as we did in Condition
(X2)
, and it allows for more situations as we shall discuss in Section

V
. This formulation was also used in the context of transfer exponents; see
15
, Example
4
.

Finally, Condition
(A3)
features integrability conditions that control the transferability between the distributions
P
and
Q
.
The constant
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{var}}
simply controls the growths of the functions
g,\,\sigma^{2}
and
v
. The next two integrability conditions differ only
through the functions
\sigma^{2}
,
g
, and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
, and they are therefore equivalent when these functions are bounded away from
zero and infinity. Since the function
p
is bounded above by
r_{0}^{-d_{P}}
, the last three integrability conditions in
(A3)

become stronger as
\gamma_{\textup{res}},\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}
and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}
increase, which also improves the convergence rates; see Theorems
4
and

5
. This can be related to the comments on the range of the transfer function in
30
.
The minimum exponents required to achieve parametric rates are
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=\gamma_{\textup{cov}}=1
and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=1/2
. With these parameters, setting the functions

\Delta_{1}(z):=g(z)^{2}+v(z)^{2DK^{*}}(\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)^{2}+\sigma^{2}(z))
and
\Delta_{2}(z):=\left\lvert g(z)\right\rvert+v(z)^{DK^{*}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)
, the integrability
conditions require that

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Delta_{1}(z)\,\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{X}}\Delta_{2}(z)\,\frac{\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)}{p(z)^{1/2}}<\infty.

They are similar to Condition
(X3)
, but they also incorporate the potential growths of the regression function
g
and the
conditional variance
\sigma^{2}
, in the case where they are not globally bounded, or when they both approach zero.

Note that in the case where the two distributions have the same intrinsic dimension, that is,
d_{Q}=d_{P}
, when the integrability conditions
are satisfied for
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
and some
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\geq 1
, and when we choose the classical pointwise matching estimator with
L=0
, for
which
D=0
, then the multiplicative amplitude
v
does not appear in the integrability conditions, so that the related inequality
in Condition
(A3)
can be omitted.

We now analyse the rates of convergence of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
, starting with its conditional variance.

Theorem 4

(Conditional Variance)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1)
to
(A3)
, and that we set the parameter
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
. Then there exists a constant

C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, depending only on
d
and
L
, such that the conditional variance
V_{m,n}:=\hat{e}_{L}(h)-\mathbb{E}[\hat{e}_{L}(h)\mid(X_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket}]
of
the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[V_{m,n}^{2}]\leq C\left[\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{var}}}{m}+\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\textup{res}}}{k^{1-\gamma_{\textup{res}}}\,(n+1)^{\gamma_{\textup{res}}}}\right],

with the constants
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{var}},\mathcal{C}_{\textup{res}}
, and the exponent
\gamma_{\textup{res}}
from Condition
(A3)
.

Theorem
4
shows that the conditional variance of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
achieves the parametric rate of convergence

m^{-1}+n^{-1}
as soon as we can take
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
in Condition
(A3)
. It first requires
d_{Q}\geq d_{P}
, and therefore
d_{Q}=d_{P}
, since
the inequality
d_{Q}\leq d_{P}
is always implicit, as mentioned above. Second, taking
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
requires the integrability condition

\int_{\mathbb{X}}v(z)^{2DK^{*}}\sigma^{2}(z)\,\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,

which is similar to the condition
\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)^{2}}{f_{P}(z)}\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty

from Section
II
, when the multiplicative amplitude and the conditional variance
\sigma^{2}
are globally bounded.
We now analyse the conditional bias of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
.

Theorem 5

(Conditional Bias)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1)
to
(A3)
, and that we set the parameter
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
. Then there exists a constant

C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, depending only on
d
and
L
, such that the conditional bias
B_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\hat{e}_{L}(h)\mid(X_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket}]-e(h)
of the estimator

\hat{e}_{L}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[B_{n}^{2}]\leq C\left[\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\left(\frac{k+1}{n+1}\right)^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}+\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}^{2}\left(\frac{k+1}{n+1}\right)^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\right],

with the constants
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}},\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}
, and the exponents
\gamma_{\textup{cov}},\gamma_{\textup{bias}}
from Condition
(A3)
.

Theorem
5
shows that taking
k=1
can be optimal to minimise the upper bound on the rate of the conditional bias. The inequality

\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\leq\ell/d_{P}
in Condition
(A3)
translates the effects of the regularity of the regression function and the intrinsic
dimension of
P
on the bias of the
k
-NN estimator. It means that the rate for the second-order conditional bias is always controlled
by
n^{-2\ell/d_{P}}
, which is a standard non-parametric rate, no matter the singularity between
P
and
Q
. However, the bias term can
be larger than
n^{-2\ell/d_{P}}
when the fourth part of Condition
(A3)
is not satisfied for
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=\ell/d_{P}
. Setting
k=1
,
the conditional bias of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
is controlled by the parametric rate of convergence
n^{-1}
as soon as we can take

\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\geq 1/2
and
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\geq 1
. First, it requires
d_{P}\leq 2\ell
and
d_{P}\leq d_{Q}+2\ell
, where the second inequality is automatic when

d_{P}=d_{Q}
. Second, it requires the two integrability conditions

\int_{\mathbb{X}}(g(z)^{2}+v(z)^{2DK^{*}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z)^{2})\,\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty,\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{X}}(\left\lvert g(z)\right\rvert+v(z)^{DK^{*}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell}(z))\,\frac{\mathrm{d}Q(z)}{p(z)^{1/2}}<\infty.

They are similar to Condition
(X3)
, but they also incorporate the growths of
g
and its local Hölder constant.

Combining the two previous results together, we can conclude when the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
for the expectation
e(h)
achieves
the parametric rate of convergence.

Corollary 1

(Mean-squared error)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1)
to
(A3)
, with the parameters
d_{Q}=d_{P}\leq 2\ell
,
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1,\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\geq 1
, and

\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\geq 1/2
. Suppose further that
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
,
k^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}\leq\alpha n^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}-1}
, and
k^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\leq\alpha n^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}-1}
,
for some constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. Then there exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the mean-squared error of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)

satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{e}_{L}(h)-e(h))^{2}]\leq C(m^{-1}+n^{-1}).

Regarding the classical matching estimator, taking a small parameter
k
is optimal for the mean-squared error. Note that the parameter
L

need not be chosen according to the regularity or the sample sizes, since to achieve parametric rates of convergence, we simply
need
L+1\geq d_{P}/2
, and choosing a higher
L
than necessary only impacts the constants in the convergence rates. Therefore, it is possible
to always set
L:=\lfloor d/2\rfloor
, and the conditional bias of the estimator
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
will automatically be negligible compared to its
conditional variance with enough regularity on the regression function and enough strong integrability conditions.

Although the theoretical results require
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
, which grows rapidly with the dimension
d
, practice shows that a
smaller parameter
k
seems to suffice, as
11
mentioned. However, we must have
k\geq K^{*}
for the local
polynomial estimator to be well-defined, otherwise the matrix
M(z)
cannot be invertible. Because the quantity
K^{*}(d,\lfloor d/2\rfloor)
grows
exponentially with the dimension
d
, the rates of convergence still deteriorate through constants as the dimension
d
increases.
If the set
\mathbb{X}
is a known
d_{0}
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we may restrict the least-square minimisation for
\hat{G}_{L}(z)
to the
tangent space of
\mathbb{X}
at the point
z
. Doing this reduces the constants
K^{*}(d,L)
and
D(d,L)
to
K^{*}(d_{0},L)
and
D(d_{0},L)
,
respectively, without changing the rates of convergence. Adaptive estimators that eliminate the impact of the overlying dimension
d
on
the constants in the convergence rates of the estimators are left to future research.

III-B

Faster rates for the matching estimator

In the previous section, we showed that the locally linear estimator
\hat{e}_{1}(h)
was well suited when the regularity parameter

\ell
of the regression function was between
1
and
2
. In this section, we prove that in this case, the same rates of convergence
can also be achieved by the simpler matching estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
, under some additional conditions. This improvement in the rates of the
conditional bias of the matching estimator was the main subject of
27
. As previously, their results can be naturally
extended to this paper’s framework, without assuming that the source distribution has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure that is
bounded away from zero on its support.

We assume in Condition
(A1)
that the function
g
has first-order derivatives
that are locally
(\ell-1)
-Hölder continuous, where
\ell\in(1,2]
. We consider two additional conditions.

(A4)

There exist measurable functions
\delta:\mathbb{X}\to[0,r_{0}]
and
\Lambda_{P}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for
Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all
r\in[0,\delta(z)]
, setting the signed measure
P_{z}:=P(z+\cdot)-P(z-\cdot)
, we have

\left\lvert P_{z}\right\rvert(B(0,r))\leq 2\Lambda_{P}(z)\,P(B(z,r))\,r^{\ell-1}.

Besides, the following integral is finite:

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}^{\prime}:=\int_{\mathbb{X}}\lVert\nabla g(z)\rVert\Lambda_{P}(z)\,p(z)^{-\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty.

(A5)

The following supremum is finite:

\mathcal{C}_{\textup{boundary}}:=\underset{L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}}{\sup}L^{\gamma_{\textup{bias}}-1/d_{P}}\int_{\delta<r_{0}}\lVert\nabla g(z)\rVert\,p(z)^{-1/d_{P}}\exp(-Lp(z)\delta(z)^{d_{P}})\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty.

Condition
(A4)
translates into a local log-Hölder regularity on the source distribution
P
. Let
\mathbb{X}^{c}
denote the complement of the
set
\mathbb{X}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
\delta(z,\mathbb{X}^{c}):=\inf\{\lVert z-y\rVert\mid y\in\mathbb{X}^{c}\}
denote the distance between the point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the set
\mathbb{X}^{c}
.
If
P
has density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then we can set
\delta(z):=\delta(z,\mathbb{X}^{c})
and

\Lambda_{P}(z):=\underset{y\in B(z,r_{0})\cap\mathbb{X}}{\sup}\,\frac{\left\lvert f_{P}(y)-f_{P}(z)\right\rvert}{f_{P}(y)\lVert y-z\rVert^{\ell-1}}.

Meanwhile, Condition
(A5)
is another integrability condition that incorporates boundary effects. These two conditions are also discussed
in Section
V
.
These two conditions extend the framework of
27
, where the regression function
g
was supposed to have continuous
second-order derivatives on a compact set, which implies that its gradient was Lipschitz continuous, corresponding to the case where
\ell=2

here. In addition, the source density function
f_{P}
was supposed to be Lipschitz continuous on its support, and because it was also supposed to
be bounded away from zero, it was automatically log-Lipschitz continuous. Finally, when the function
\lVert\nabla g\rVert
is bounded and
p
is
bounded away from zero, the finiteness of the quantity
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{boundary}}
in Condition
(A5)
, with the maximal exponent
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=2/d_{P}
, implies
the geometric condition
(A)
in
27
.

Theorem 6

(Conditional Bias)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1)
to
(A5)
with the parameter
\ell\in(1,2]
, and with the exponents
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\in[0,(d_{Q}+2)/d_{P}]

and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in[1/d_{P},\ell/d_{P}]
. Then there exists a universal constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the conditional bias
B_{n}:=\mathbb{E}[\hat{e}_{0}(h)\mid(X_{i})_{i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket}]-e(h)
of the estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[B_{n}^{2}]\leq\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\left(\frac{k+1}{n+1}\right)^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}+(\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}^{2}+\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}^{{}^{\prime}2}+\mathcal{C}_{\textup{boundary}}^{2})\left(\frac{k+1}{n+1}\right)^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}},

with the constants
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}
from Condition
(A3)
,
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{bias}}^{\prime}
from Condition
(A4)
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{boundary}}
from Condition

(A5)
.

Theorem
6
recovers similar rates of convergence for the conditional bias of the matching estimator than in Theorem
5
,
if we consider the way in which they depend on the transferability exponent
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}
. The difference lies in the fact that we now
allow
1/d_{P}<\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\leq 2/d_{P}
instead of
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\leq 1/d_{P}
, as in Theorem
5
. This is particularly relevant when the intrinsic
dimension
d_{P}
is equal to
3
or
4
, if we want to reach the critical exponent
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=1/2
.

Corollary 2

(Mean-squared error)
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1)
to
(A5)
with
\ell=2
,
d_{Q}=d_{P}\leq 4
,
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1,\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\geq 1
, and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\geq 1/2
.
Suppose further that
k^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}\leq\alpha n^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}-1}
and
k^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\leq\alpha n^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}-1}
for some constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. Then there
exists a constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the mean-squared error of the estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{e}_{0}(h)-e(h))^{2}]\leq C(m^{-1}+n^{-1}).

We can deduce from this theorem that the final matching estimator
\hat{e}_{0}(h)
can achieve parametric rates of convergence when
the intrinsic dimensions are equal and no more than
4
, and under transferability conditions similar to those of
section
III-A
.

IV
Application to the estimation of average treatment effects

We now discuss how the results from the previous sections can be applied to the estimation of average treatment effects. We consider a
random vector
(X,W,Y(1),Y(0))
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0;1\}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. In this random vector,
X
is a vector of
covariates for an individual,
W
corresponds to whether the individual received an active (
W=1
) or a passive treatment (
W=0
), and

Y(1)
and
Y(0)
are the two potential outcomes for the active and passive treatment, respectively. One objective is then to
estimate the average treatment effect
\mu:=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]
, when for each individual
i\in\llbracket 1;N\rrbracket
, only the quantities

(X_{i},W_{i},Y_{i}(W_{i}))
are observed, but not the potential outcome
Y_{i}(1-W_{i})
for the treatment the individual did not receive. The
missing outputs
Y_{i}(1-W_{i})
are then replaced by an average of the observed outputs
Y_{j}(1-W_{i})
for the
k
units
j
that have the
covariates closest to
X_{i}
between the individuals given the treatment
W_{j}=1-W_{i}
, as proposed by
1
. The
average treatment effect context then relates to our problem, the source and target distributions being the conditional
distributions of
X
given
W=1
and
X
given
W=0
, or vice versa. For the covariate shift setup, we assume that the treatment

W
is independent from the outcomes
(Y(1),Y(0))
given the covariates
X
, a situation called
unconfoundedness
.

In this scenario, it is commonly assumed that the covariate vector
X
lives in a bounded domain
\mathbb{X}
and has a density with respect to
the Lebesgue measure that is bounded away from zero; see, for example,
1
and
11
. However, we
can show that the same rates of convergence for the same estimators hold under milder assumptions on the covariate support and
distribution. Although our main focus is the average treatment effect
\mu
, the same results are valid for the average treatment effect
on the treated
\mu^{\textup{treat}}:=\mathbb{E}[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid W=1]
.

We assume that we have i.i.d. replications
(X_{i},W_{i},Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0))_{i\in\llbracket 1;N\rrbracket}
of the vector
(X,W,Y(1),Y(0))
. Write
S
for the marginal
distribution of
X
, supported on some possibly unknown Borel set
\mathbb{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Fix
k\in\llbracket 1;N\rrbracket
, for
S
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
all
w\in\{0;1\}
, set
\hat{\tau}_{k+1,w}(x)
the distance between the point
x
and its
(k+1)
th-nearest neighbour among the observations

X_{i}
such that
W_{i}=w
, with the convention
\hat{\tau}_{k+1,w}(x):=\infty
if there are not enough observations. Keeping the notation from
Section
III-A
, in particular the set of multi-indices
N_{L}
and the vector of monomials
\zeta(z,T)
, we define the final
estimator

\hat{\mu}_{L}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(2W_{i}-1)(Y_{i}-\hat{g}_{L,1-W_{i}}(X_{i})),\text{ where}

\hat{g}_{L,w}(x):=\mathbf{1}\left(\hat{\tau}_{k+1,w}(x)\leq r_{0}\right)e_{0}^{\top}\underset{b\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{L}}}{\operatorname{argmin}}\sum_{\underset{W_{i}=w}{i=1}}^{N}(Y_{i}-b^{\top}\zeta(x,X_{i}))^{2}\mathbf{1}\left(\lVert X_{i}-x\rVert<\hat{\tau}_{k+1,w}(x)\right),

for
S
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and all
w\in\{0;1\}
.
Apart from censoring when
\hat{\tau}_{k+1,W_{i}}(X_{j})>r_{0}
, which is not necessary in most cases and could be omitted in practice, the
estimator
\hat{\mu}_{L}
coincides with the local polynomial estimator from
11
. In particular, when
L=0
, we
recover the pointwise matching estimator, introduced in
1
,

\hat{g}_{0,w}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{\underset{W_{i}=w}{i=1}}^{N}Y_{i}\;\mathbf{1}\left(\lVert X_{i}-x\rVert<\hat{\tau}_{k+1,w}(x)\right).

We set, for
S
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and all
w\in\{0;1\}
, the regression function
g_{w}(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y(w)\mid X=x]
.
We consider the following conditions.

(A1’)

There exist
\ell\in(0,L+1]
and measurable functions
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,0},\mathcal{G}_{\ell,1},\sigma_{0}^{2},\sigma_{1}^{2}:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for
S
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{X}
and all
w\in\{0;1\}
, the conditional variance
z\mapsto\operatorname{Var}(Y(w)\mid X=z)

is bounded by
\sigma_{w}^{2}(x)
on
B(x,r_{0})\cap\mathbb{X}
, and the regression function
g_{w}
is the restriction of a function having
derivatives of order
\lceil\ell\rceil-1
that are all
(\ell+1-\lceil\ell\rceil)
-Hölder continuous on
B(x,3r_{0})\cap\operatorname{Conv}(\mathbb{X})
with constant

\mathcal{G}_{\ell,w}(x)\lceil\ell-1\rceil!
.

(A2’)

There exist
d_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and measurable functions
p:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},v:\mathbb{X}\times[0,r_{0}]\to[1,\infty[
such that for
S
-almost all

x\in\mathbb{X}
, all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
, and all Borel sets
\mathcal{A}\subset B(0,3)^{K^{*}}
, we have

\displaystyle S(B(x,r))

\displaystyle\geq p(x)r^{d_{0}},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\frac{X_{1}-x}{r},\dots,\frac{X_{K^{*}}-x}{r}\right)\in\mathcal{A}\right)

\displaystyle\leq(v(x,r)p(x)r^{d_{0}})^{K^{*}}\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert,

where
\left\lvert\mathcal{A}\right\rvert
designates the Lebesgue measure of the Borel set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{dK^{*}}
.

Besides, there exist measurable functions
\eta_{0},\eta_{1}:\mathbb{X}\to\,(0,1)
such that for
S
-almost all
x,z\in\mathbb{X}
such that
\lVert z-x\rVert\leq 2r_{0}
, and all
w\in\{0;1\}
,

\eta_{w}(x)\leq\mathbb{P}(W=w\mid X=z)\leq 1-\eta_{1-w}(x).

(A3’)

There exist
\gamma_{\textup{res}}\in[0,1],\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\in[0,1+2\ell/d_{0}]
, and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in[0,\ell/d_{0}]
, such that for all
w\in\{0;1\}
,
the following integrals are finite:

\int_{\mathbb{X}}(\lVert g_{w}\rVert_{\infty,B(x,r_{0})\cap\mathbb{X}}^{2}+\sigma_{w}^{2}(x))v(x)^{2DK^{*}}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty,

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[\sigma_{w}^{2}(x)(1-\eta_{w}(x))^{2}v(x)^{2DK^{*}+1}\right]\frac{p(x)^{1-\gamma_{\textup{res}}}}{\eta_{w}(x)^{\gamma_{\textup{res}}}}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty,

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[(g_{w}(x)^{2}+\mathcal{G}_{\ell,w}(x)^{2}v(x)^{2DK^{*}})(1-\eta_{w}(x))^{2}v(x)\right]\,\frac{p(x)^{1-\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}}{\eta_{w}(x)^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty,\text{ and}

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left[(\left\lvert g_{w}(x)\right\rvert+\mathcal{G}_{\ell,w}(x)v(x)^{DK^{*}})(1-\eta_{w}(x))\right]\,\frac{1}{(\eta_{w}(x)p(x))^{\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty,

where we have set, for
S
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{X}
,
v(x):=\underset{r\in[0,r_{0}]}{\sup}\frac{p(x)r^{d}}{S(B(x,r))}v(x,r)\leq\underset{r\in[0,r_{0}]}{\sup}v(x,r)
.

This list of conditions is similar to that in Section
III
, with three major differences.

•

We have two regression functions and conditional variances due to the presence of the two potential outcomes
Y(1)
and
Y(0)
.

•

We only have one unconditional distribution
S
for the covariate vector
X
. As a result, the source and target distributions,
which are the conditional distributions of
X
given
W=1
and of
X
given
W=0
, or vice versa, naturally share the same intrinsic
dimension
d_{0}
, which is the intrinsic dimension of
S
as given in Condition
(A2’)
.

•

The transferability between the source and target probability measures is controlled via the functions
\eta_{0}
and
\eta_{1}
, which give
local bounds to the conditional probability to assign a treatment
w
given the covariate vector
X
, translating the selection bias.
Then the last three parts of Condition
(A3’)
measure the integrability of the function
x\mapsto 1/(\eta_{w}(x)p(x))
with respect
to
\mathrm{d}S(x)
or
p(x)\mathrm{d}S(x)
.

In the literature, the following global assumptions are common.

•

The conditional variances
\operatorname{Var}(Y(0)\mid X)
and
\operatorname{Var}(Y(1)\mid X)
, the regression functions
g_{0}
and
g_{1}
, and all their
derivatives, are bounded on
\mathbb{X}
.

•

The distribution
S
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is bounded away from zero and infinity on the
compact set
\mathbb{X}
.

•

The conditional probability of assigning a given treatment
\mathbb{P}(W=w\mid X)
is bounded away from zero and one on
\mathbb{X}
.

In this case, the integrability conditions in
(A3’)
are automatically satisfied. Our list of conditions is therefore more
general and enables the study of treatment effect estimation when the covariate vector is supported on an unbounded domain, on a known or
unknown submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and when the conditional probability to assign a given treatment may approach zero or one.

We are now in a position to state the following result.

Theorem 7
.

Suppose that we have Conditions
(A1’)
to
(A3’)
, and that we have set the parameter
k\geq(2D+1)K^{*}
. Then there exists a
constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that the mean-squared error of the estimator
\hat{\mu}_{L}
satisfies

\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\mu}_{L}-\mu)^{2}]\leq C\left(\frac{1}{N}+\frac{1}{k^{1-\gamma_{\textup{res}}}N^{\gamma_{\textup{res}}}}+\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)^{\gamma_{\textup{cov}}}+\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)^{2\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\right).

In particular, if we have
L+1\geq d_{0}/2
, which can be ensured by setting
L\geq\lceil d/2\rceil-1
, and if the number
k
of nearest neighbours
grows slowly, if at all, with the sample size
N
, then the estimator
\hat{\mu}_{L}
for the average treatment effect
\mu
can achieve the
parametric rate of convergence
N^{-1}
in all dimensions. Achieving this rate requires two main elements. First, the regression functions

g_{0}
and
g_{1}
must be sufficiently regular. If
\ell\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
denotes the minimum between the Hölder regularity parameters of
g_{0}
and

g_{1}
, then we ask
d_{0}\leq 2\ell
, where
d_{0}
is the intrinsic dimension of the covariate distribution
S
. Second, we must control
the selection bias, which is measured by the integrability of the functions
1/\eta_{0}
and
1/\eta_{1}
. More precisely, Condition

(A3’)
must be satisfied for
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1,\,\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\geq 1
, and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\geq 1/2
.
In the case where, for
w\in\{0;1\}
, the functions
\sigma^{2}_{w},g_{w},\mathcal{G}_{\ell,w}
, and
v
are bounded away from zero and infinity on

\mathbb{X}
, then the integrability conditions become

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{1}{\eta_{w}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty,\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{1}{(\eta_{w}(x)p(x))^{1/2}}\,\mathrm{d}S(x)<\infty.

If, in addition, the distribution
S
has density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with density equal to
p
up to a
constant, then the integrability conditions become

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{p(x)}{\eta_{w}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty,\text{ and }\int_{\mathbb{X}}\left(\frac{p(x)}{\eta_{w}(x)}\right)^{1/2}\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

As a result, it implies that the conditional selection probability function
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}(W=w\mid X=x)
is allowed to approach zero, but not faster than the covariate probability density
p
.

V
Conditions on the relative behaviour of the source and target distributions

V-A

Usual distributions on the Euclidean space

In this section, we study the conditions
(A2)
to
(A5)
for distributions
P
and
Q
that have density with respect to the
Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, denoted respectively by
f_{P}
and
f_{Q}
. To show that these conditions are the most general, we first give intuitive
interpretations before investigating what they become for common distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. These conditions can be put into two categories:
the volume bounding conditions and the integrability conditions. We start by discussing volume bounding.

In the first part of Condition
(A2)
, writing
P(B(z,r))\geq p(z)r^{d_{P}}
, here
d=d_{P}
is the dimension of the overlying space

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
p
can be up to some constant the minimum of the density
f_{P}
over a subset of
B(z,r_{0})
that always intersect
B(z,r),r\in[0,r_{0}]
with a volume that is comparable to that of
B(z,r)
. Boundary effects are discussed at the end of this section. When
there are no boundary effects,
p
can often be set to the density
f_{P}
multiplied by a positive constant. The last part of Condition

(A2)
, writing
Q(B(z,2r))\leq\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z)P(B(z,r))
when
d_{Q}=d_{P}
, works well with the previous lower bound. Indeed, it is then
sufficient to show that there exists a function
q:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that for
Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all
r\in[0,2r_{0}]
,
Q(B(z,r))\leq q(z)r^{d}
, and then set
\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}(z):=2^{d}q(z)/p(z)
. For example,
q
can be the maximum of
f_{Q}
over
B(z,2r_{0})
multiplied by the volume
of the unit ball. However, the general formulation works better with boundary effects; see the proof of Proposition
4
.
Similarly, the last part of Condition
(A2)
is satisfied if, for instance,
v(z,r)p(z)
is the maximum of
f_{P}
over
B(z,r)
.
Although it is classical in the literature to assume that
v
is a bounded function
30
, our results are
still valid in the case where any power of
v
is negligible compared to
f_{Q}
; see the proof of Proposition
1
.

Finally, in Condition
(A4)
, writing
\left\lvert P_{z}\right\rvert(B(0,r))\leq 2\Lambda_{P}(z)P(B(z,r))\,r^{\ell-1}
for all
r\in[0,\delta(z)]
,
where
P_{z}:=P(z+\cdot)-P(z-\cdot)
, since
P
has density
f_{P}
with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can write

\left\lvert P_{z}\right\rvert(B(0,r))=\int_{B(0,r)}\left\lvert f_{P}(z+x)-f_{P}(z-x)\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}x.

If the density
f_{P}
is Hölder-continuous on
B(z,r)
, with exponent
\ell-1\in(0,1]
and constant
L(z)
, then

\left\lvert P_{z}\right\rvert(B(0,r))\leq 2r^{\ell-1}L(z)\left\lvert B(0,r)\right\rvert\leq 2r^{\ell-1}\left\lvert B(0,1)\right\rvert\frac{L(z)}{p(z)}P(B(z,r)),

using Condition
(A2)
. Condition
(A4)
is then satisfied for
\Lambda_{P}(z):=\left\lvert B(0,1)\right\rvert L(z)/p(z)
, which, with the
intuition behind Condition
(A2)
, can be, up to a negligible function, the log-Lipschitz norm
\lVert\nabla f_{P}(z)\rVert/f_{P}(z)
of a
differentiable density
f_{P}
, when
\ell=2
. This reasoning works if the density
f_{P}
is Hölder-continuous on
B(z,\delta(z))
, and we
can take into account boundary effects by setting
\delta(z)
the minimum between
r_{0}
and the distance
\delta(z,\mathbb{X}^{c})
between
z
and
the complement of
\mathbb{X}
.

We now discuss the integrability conditions. Although they are easier to state when the functions
\sigma^{2},\,g
and all its derivatives are
bounded, they do not change if these functions have growths that are negligible compared to the decay of
Q
, for instance polynomial
growths against a sub-exponential tail for
Q
. The same applies to the functions
v
and
\Lambda_{P}
from Conditions
(A2)
and

(A4)
, which are often negligible compared to the decay of
Q
. Following the intuition behind the functions
p
and
\mathcal{R}_{P,Q}
, the
integrability conditions are often equivalent to conditions of the form

\int_{\mathbb{X}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)^{\alpha}}{f_{P}(z)^{\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty,

with
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. With a finite parameter
k
, we saw that the conditional bias of the final estimator

\hat{e}_{L}(h),\,L\in\mathbb{N}
was comparable to the conditional variance when the integrability conditions were satisfied for the couples of
exponents
(\alpha,\gamma)=(1,1/2)
and
(\alpha,\gamma)=(2,1)
. In both cases, the exponent on
f_{Q}
is twice the exponent on
f_{P}
,
which, as stated above, allows
Q
to have a heavier tail than
P
as long as it is not twice as heavy.

The different integrability condition is Condition
(A5)
. It features an integral over the extended boundary

\{z\in\mathbb{X}\mid\delta(z)<r_{0}\}
, that is, points whose distance to the boundary of
\mathbb{X}
is less than
r_{0}
. This condition is then
trivially satisfied when
\mathbb{X}
has no boundary, in other words when the density
f_{P}
is regular on the whole space
\mathbb{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It is
also not present in section
III-A
since the local polynomial fitting takes into account boundary effects. See the
end of this section for a further discussion of this condition.

We now go through a series of examples to see what all these conditions become for common distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, starting with
multivariate Gaussian distributions.

Proposition 1

(Gaussian distributions)
.

Let
P
and
Q
follow multivariate Gaussian distributions
P\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(\mu_{P},\Sigma_{P})
and
Q\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(\mu_{Q},\Sigma_{Q})
.
Assume that the norms of
\sigma^{2},\,g
and all its derivatives are controlled above by some function
z\mapsto C\exp(A\lVert z\rVert)
and below by

z\mapsto c\exp(-a\lVert z\rVert)
, where
a,c,A,C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. Then the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies the conditions
(A2)
to
(A5)
, for

\gamma_{\textup{res}},\gamma_{\textup{cov}},\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
if and only if
2\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}>\max\{\gamma_{\textup{res}};\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\}\Sigma_{P}^{-1}
and
\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}>\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\Sigma_{P}^{-1}
, in the
sense of positive semi-definite matrix inequalities. In particular, with the critical choices
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}=\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=1/2
, the couple

(P,Q)
satisfies the lists of conditions if and only if
2\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}>\Sigma_{P}^{-1}
.

Note

Gaussian vectors give an example of multivariate distributions with unbounded support and no boundary effects. They are
often excluded in the context of regression on unbounded domains because they do not satisfy the local mass property

30
. With our notation, it is translated by the fact that the multiplicative amplitude
v(z)
is not bounded
as
z
approaches infinity. However, the critical transferability condition
2\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}>\Sigma_{P}^{-1}
is a good illustration of the
paradigm ”the target distribution is allowed to have a heavier tail than the source distribution, as long as it is not twice as heavy”
as stated in Section
II
.

Next, we consider the example of vectors with independent coordinates, with one coordinate following a Gamma distribution and the others
following uniform distributions. This example enables the study of univariate Gamma distributions while not restricting to the case
d=1
.

Proposition 2

(Gamma distributions)
.

Let
P\sim\Gamma_{1}(\mu_{P},s_{P})
and
Q\sim\Gamma_{1}(\mu_{Q},s_{Q})
, where the distribution
\Gamma_{1}(\mu,s),\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},s\in[1,\infty[
is defined
by the density
z\mapsto\frac{\mu^{s}}{\Gamma(s)}z_{1}^{s-1}e^{-\mu z_{1}}\mathbf{1}\left(z_{1}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*},z_{i}\in[0,1],i\in\llbracket 2;d\rrbracket\right)
, representing univariate
Gamma distributions in an overlying space of dimension
d
. Assume that
\sigma^{2},\,g
and all its derivatives have polynomial growths on

\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]^{d-1}
. Then the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies the conditions
(A2)
and
(A3)
, for
\gamma_{\textup{res}},\gamma_{\textup{cov}},\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

if and only if
2\mu_{Q}>\max\{\gamma_{\textup{res}};\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\}\mu_{P}
,
\mu_{Q}>\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\mu_{P}
,
2s_{Q}>\max\{\gamma_{\textup{res}};\gamma_{\textup{cov}}\}s_{P}
, and
s_{Q}>\gamma_{\textup{bias}}s_{P}
. In particular, with
the critical choices
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}=\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=1/2
, the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies the the conditions
(A2)
and
(A3)
if and
only if
2\mu_{Q}>\mu_{P}
and
2s_{Q}>s_{P}
.

Note

This example features a mix of several regimes. First, we have the Gamma tail regime
z_{1}\to\infty
which gives the
intuitive critical transferability condition
2\mu_{Q}>\mu_{P}
. Second, we have the power regime
z_{1}\to 0
which gives the similar
transferability condition
2s_{Q}>s_{P}
. It shows that we can also handle densities that go to zero near the boundary of the support.
Finally, we also have boundary effects due to the uniform coordinates
z_{i},i\in\llbracket 2;d\rrbracket
and the regime
z_{1}\to 0
. However, due to
the simple geometry of the support, the boundary effects are easily dealt with and do not add any additional conditions.
See Proposition
4
for a discussion of more complex boundary effects.

Note

If we want to apply the results from Section
III-B
and therefore need Conditions
(A4)
and
(A5)
,
then we need the slightly stronger condition
2s_{Q}>s_{P}+1
. This is due to the fact that power densities of the form
z_{1}^{s}
lack log-regularity
when
z_{1}\to 0
, which is needed in Section
III-B
.

We now address the case where the regression function has a growth towards infinity that is comparable to the decay of the distributions.

Proposition 3

(Pareto distributions with a polynomial regression function)
.

Let
P
and
Q
follow Pareto distributions in overlying space of dimension
d,\,P\sim\operatorname{Par}_{1}(\mu_{P})
and
Q\sim\operatorname{Par}_{1}(\mu_{Q})
, where the distribution
\operatorname{Par}_{1}(\mu),\mu\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is defined by the density
z\mapsto z_{1}^{-\mu-1}/\mu\,\mathbf{1}\left(z_{1}\in[1,\infty),z_{i}\in[0,1],i\in\llbracket 2;d\rrbracket\right)
. Assume that
\sigma^{2}
is bounded away from zero and infinity and that for all

z\in[1,\infty)\times[0,1]^{d-1}
and all
s\in\llbracket 1;\lceil\ell\rceil\rrbracket
,
cz_{1}^{M}\leq\left\lvert g(z)\right\rvert\leq Cz_{1}^{M}
and
\lVert D^{s}g(z)\rVert\leq Cz_{1}^{M}
, where

M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
c,C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. Then the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies the conditions
(A2)
to
(A5)
, for
\gamma_{\textup{res}},\gamma_{\textup{cov}},\gamma_{\textup{bias}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

if and only if
\mu_{Q}>2M
,
2\mu_{Q}+1>\gamma_{\textup{cov}}(\mu_{P}+1)
,
2\mu_{Q}+1>2M+\gamma_{\textup{res}}(\mu_{P}+1)
, and
\mu_{Q}>M+\gamma_{\textup{bias}}(\mu_{P}+1)
. In particular, with the
critical choices
\gamma_{\textup{cov}}=\gamma_{\textup{res}}=1
and
\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=1/2
, the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies the lists of conditions if and only if
\mu_{Q}>2M
and

2\mu_{Q}>2M+\mu_{P}+1
.

Note

The previous example shows that the growth of the regression function can be incorporated in the transferability conditions.

Note

In the particular case where the function
g
is exactly a polynomial in
z_{1}
of degree
M>d/2
, we believe that a closer
look to the estimator without censoring when
\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)>r_{0}
could lead to the critical transferability conditions
\mu_{Q}>2M
and
2\mu_{Q}+d>2M+\mu_{P}+1
, provided that we have the additional assumption
\mu_{P}>d/2
. It would allow the target distribution to have a heavier tail than
the source distribution even when
\mu_{Q}\approx 2M
. We do not investigate this case further here.

The examples above show that our integrability conditions can be easily interpreted for common distributions. Besides, these conditions
are local and allow the mixing of several regimes for the densities (decaying towards infinity, non-continuity, going to zero near a
finite point, etc.).

We now discuss the effects of the geometry of the support
\mathbb{X}
on the distribution conditions, considering the region near the boundary
of
\mathbb{X}
. Because the conditions are local, to study boundary effects, we may assume without loss of generality that
\mathbb{X}
is compact.
We also assume that the functions
\sigma^{2},\,g
, and all its derivatives, are bounded away from zero and infinity.

The two conditions that suffer the most from boundary effects are the first part of Condition
(A2)
, and Condition
(A5)
,
here writing, respectively,

P(B(z,r))\geq p(z)r^{d},\text{ and }\underset{L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}}{\sup}L^{\gamma_{\textup{bias}}-1/d}\int_{B(\partial\mathbb{X},r_{0})}p(z)^{-1/d}\exp(-Lp(z)\delta(z)^{d})\,\mathrm{d}Q(z)<\infty.

Condition
(A2)
is satisfied when
P
has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{X}
that is bounded away from zero,
and when there exists a constant
c\in(0,1]
such that for almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
,

\left\lvert B(z,r)\cap\mathbb{X}\right\rvert\geq c\left\lvert B(z,r)\right\rvert,

leading to the
strong density assumption

(
5
)
. The conjunction of this assumption with the condition

\underset{L\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}}{\sup}L^{1/d}\int_{\mathbb{X}}\exp(-L\delta(z)^{d})\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty
was studied in
27
, who showed it holds on
general sufficiently regular subsets
\mathbb{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can also consider densities that are not bounded away from zero near the boundary,
as we saw in Proposition
2
with Gamma distributions. Finally, it is possible to relax the condition
\left\lvert B(z,r)\cap\mathbb{X}\right\rvert\geq c\left\lvert B(z,r)\right\rvert
, as well as the upper bound conditions on the densities, as can be seen in the following example.

Proposition 4
.

Consider the set
\mathbb{X}:=\{z\in[0,1]^{d}\mid\forall i\in\llbracket 2;d\rrbracket,z_{i}\leq z_{1}^{s}\}
, where
s\geq 1
. Let
\mu_{P},\mu_{Q}>-s(d-1)
and let
P,Q

follow the distributions with respective densities
f_{P}:z\mapsto(s(d-1)+\mu_{P}+1)z_{1}^{\mu_{P}}
and
f_{Q}:z\mapsto(s(d-1)+\mu_{Q}+1)z_{1}^{\mu_{Q}}

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{X}
. Suppose that the functions
\sigma^{2}
and
g
are bounded away from zero and infinity on
\mathbb{X}
,
as well as
\mathcal{G}_{\ell}
on
\operatorname{Conv}(\mathbb{X})
. Let
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then the couple
(P,Q)
satisfies Conditions
(A2)
to
(A5)
,
for
\gamma_{\textup{res}}=\gamma_{\textup{cov}}=\gamma,\gamma_{\textup{bias}}=\gamma/2
if and only if
2\mu_{Q}+((2-\gamma)s-1+\gamma)(d-1)>\gamma\mu_{P}-1
.

With the critical choice
\gamma=1
, the list of conditions is satisfied if and only if
2\mu_{Q}>\mu_{P}-s(d-1)-1
, while the strong density
assumption
\exists c\in(0,1],\forall z\in\mathbb{X},\forall r\in[0,1],\left\lvert B(z,r)\cap\mathbb{X}\right\rvert\geq c\left\lvert B(z,r)\right\rvert
is not satisfied when
s>1
, and the
target density is not bounded when
\mu_{Q}<0
.

This last example is particular since the function
p
in Condition
(A2)
will not be the density function
f_{P}
, up to a positive
constant, as in the previous examples, but it also depends on the geometry of the support through the exponent
s
. See the proof of Proposition

4
for the details.

V-B

Distributions on a manifold support

In this section, we study the conditions
(A2)
to
(A5)
when the covariate distributions
P
and
Q
on the Euclidean
space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
have intrinsic dimensions smaller than
d
. This can happen, for example, when the support
\mathbb{X}
of the distributions is a
submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
d_{0}
. Note that the estimators we consider are adaptive in the sense that no knowledge of the
support
\mathbb{X}
or its dimension
d_{0}
is required for the computations.

We first consider the example of the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\sum_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}^{2}=1\}
. It is a smooth compact

(d-1)
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with no boundary. Assume that the distributions
P
and
Q
are absolutely continuous with
respect to the Haar measure on
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
, with densities that are bounded away from infinity, and a density for
P
that is bounded away from
zero. Then there exist constants
c,C,C_{P,Q}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
such that for all
z\in\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
and all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
,

cr^{d-1}\leq P(B(z,r))\leq Cr^{d-1},\text{ and}\quad Q(B(z,2r))\leq C_{P,Q}P(B(z,r)),

ensuring the validity of Condition
(A2)
with the intrinsic dimensions

d_{Q}=d_{P}=d-1
. If the density of
P
is Lipschitz continuous on the sphere, then Condition
(A4)
is satisfied, setting

\delta(z):=r_{0}
. Finally, Condition
(A5)
is trivially satisfied, since the submanifold
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
has no boundary, and Condition

(A3)
is satisfied, as the source density is bounded away from zero on the compact submanifold
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
.

We can also consider non smooth manifolds, or manifolds with boundaries. Assume that the distributions
P
and
Q
have density with
respect to the natural surface measure on the unit sphere for the infinity norm, defined as
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}:=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\max\{\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert,i\in\llbracket 1;d\rrbracket\}=1\}
. Assume further that the two densities are bounded away from infinity and that the density of
P
is bounded
away from zero and Lipschitz continuous on
\mathbb{X}_{\infty}
. Then the intrinsic dimensions of
P
and
Q
are
d_{Q}=d_{P}=d-1
. The only major
difference compared to the previous situation on the Euclidean unit sphere
\mathbb{S}_{d-1}
is the presence of a boundary. Here, Condition

(A4)
is satisfied when
\delta(z):=\delta(z,\partial\mathbb{X}_{\infty})
is the distance between the point
z
and the boundary

\partial\mathbb{X}_{\infty}:=\{z\in\mathbb{X}_{\infty}\mid\exists i\neq j,\,\left\lvert z_{i}\right\rvert=\left\lvert z_{j}\right\rvert=1\}
. However, due to the simple geometry of the boundary,
we can show that Condition
(A5)
is satisfied.

Next, we consider the case of non compact manifolds. We use the description of submanifolds of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
through graphs. Let
\mathbb{X}
be the
global submanifold
\mathbb{X}:=\{(a,\varphi(z))\mid a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\}
, where
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-d_{0}}
is a function with continuous
first-order derivatives on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
. Then the regression function
g
on
\mathbb{X}
depends only on the function
g_{0}:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}
,
defined as
g_{0}(a):=g(a,\varphi(a))
. Similarly, we replace the study of the distributions
P
and
Q
, supported on the submanifold

\mathbb{X}
, by that of the distributions
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
, supported on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, where for a Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, we have set

P_{0}(A):=P(\{(a,\varphi(a))\mid a\in A\})
, and similarly for
Q_{0}
. We can then write, for almost all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
and all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
, setting
L(a):=\lVert\nabla\varphi\rVert_{\infty,B_{d_{0}}(a,r_{0})}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
z:=(a,\varphi(a))\in\mathbb{X}
,

P_{0}\left(B_{d_{0}}\left(a,\frac{r}{1+L(a)}\right)\right)\leq P(B(z,r))\leq P_{0}(B_{d_{0}}(a,r)),

where the norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is assumed to be any Minkowski norm.
Assuming that
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
have density with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, we can obtain bounds of the form

p_{0}(a)r^{d_{0}}\leq P_{0}(B_{d_{0}}(a,r))
, as in Section
V-A
, which implies that the distributions
P
and
Q
have intrinsic
dimensions
d_{Q}=d_{P}=d_{0}
. In addition, the integrability conditions can be fully expressed on
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, using the function
g_{0}
and the
distributions
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
. For example, the last part of Condition
(A3)
becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}}(\left\lvert g_{0}(a)\right\rvert+\lVert D^{\ell}g_{0}(a)\rVert)\left(\frac{1+L(a)^{d_{0}}}{p_{0}(a)}\right)^{\gamma_{\textup{bias}}}\,\mathrm{d}Q_{0}(a)<\infty.

In general, because the volume bounding and the integrability conditions are local, they are preserved by local maps that transport
the source and target probability measures. For a general submanifold
\mathbb{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we fix
z\in\mathbb{X}
and we assume that there exist an
orthogonal transform
\Omega_{z}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, an open subset
A_{z}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
, and an
L_{z}
-Lipschitz function
\varphi_{z}:A_{z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d-d_{0}},\,L_{z}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, such that
\mathbb{X}\cap B(z,2r_{0})=\{\Omega_{z}(a,\varphi_{z}(a))\mid a\in A_{z}\}
. We can then replace the study of the
regression function
g
and the distributions
P
and
Q
, locally around
z
, by that of the function
g_{0}:A_{z}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined by

g_{0}(a)=g(\Omega_{z}(a,\varphi_{z}(a)))
, and the measures
P_{0}
and
Q_{0}
on
A_{z}
, defined by
P_{0}(C):=P(\{\Omega_{z}(a,\varphi_{z}(a))\mid a\in C\})
, and similarly for
Q_{0}
. For example, setting
z=\Omega_{z}(a_{z},\varphi_{z}(a_{z}))
, if the measure
P_{0}
satisfies, for all

r\in[0,r_{0}],\,p_{0}(a_{z})r^{d_{0}}\leq P_{0}(B_{d_{0}}(a_{z},r))\leq p_{0}(a_{z})v_{0}(a_{z})r^{d_{0}}
, which can be shown as in Section
V-A
,
then we have, for all
r\in[0,r_{0}]
,

\frac{p_{0}(a_{z})}{(1+L_{z})^{d_{0}}}r^{d_{0}}\leq P(B(z,r))\leq p_{0}(a_{z})v_{0}(a_{z})\,r^{d_{0}}.

The above derivations show that when the covariate vectors
X
and
X^{*}
have support on a
d_{0}
-dimensional submanifold
\mathbb{X}_{0}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we can take
d_{Q}=d_{P}=d_{0}
in Condition
(A2)
, reducing the conditional bias of the
k
-NN matching and local polynomial
estimators from
n^{-\ell/d}
to
n^{-\ell/d_{0}}
. In fact, we can obtain similar results if
X
and
X^{*}
are not exactly supported on

\mathbb{X}_{0}
, but only up to some small noise. This means that the results for the rates of the final estimators extend to the case where the
distance from the covariate vector
X
to the submanifold
\mathbb{X}_{0}
is no larger, in distribution, than
n^{-1/d_{0}}
. In other words, we
assume that we can write
X=X^{(0)}+n^{-1/d_{0}}\eta
, where
X^{(0)}
is supported on
\mathbb{X}_{0}
, and
\eta
is a random variable on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

having a finite moment of order
d_{0}
. In that case, it is possible that the distribution of
X^{(0)}
has intrinsic dimension
d_{0}
,
yet the distribution of
X
does not satisfy the first part of Condition
(A2)
with
d_{P}=d_{0}
. However, the main results of this
paper are still valid with the intrinsic dimension
d_{0}
, because we again have, for
Q
-almost all
z\in\mathbb{X}
and all
\lambda\in[0,d_{0}]
,

\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)^{\lambda}]\leq C\left(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\tau}_{k+1}^{(0)}(z)^{\lambda}]+n^{-\lambda/d_{0}}\right),

for some constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
, where
\hat{\tau}_{k+1}^{(0)}(z)
is the
(k+1)
th order statistic of the sample
(\lVert X_{i}^{(0)}-z\rVert)_{i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket}
. It implies that the conditional bias of the
final estimators has the rate
n^{-\ell/d_{0}}
.
In contrast, when the distance from
X
to the submanifold
\mathbb{X}_{0}
is greater than
n^{-1/d_{0}}
in distribution, it means that the
noise is too large and therefore adds more dimensions to the covariate vector
X
.

VI
Possible extensions

Labels:
In the estimator
\hat{h}_{0}(z)
, the final labels
h(X_{i},Y_{i})
can be replaced by
h(z,Y_{i})
. This approach was considered
in
21
. In this case, the assumptions on the regression function
g
are replaced by assumptions on the function

x\mapsto\mathbb{E}[h(z,Y)\mid X=x]
with a fixed
z
, where
x\in B(z,3r_{0})
. The two approaches will have the exact same rates of convergence
and will only slightly differ in the constants. The same applies to the local polynomial estimator
\hat{h}_{L}(z)
in section

III-A
, where the final labels
h(X_{i},Y_{i})
can be replaced by
h(z,Y_{i})
with only minor changes. Of course, when
h(x,y)
is
a function of
y
, as in the context of average treatment effects, the two approaches are strictly equivalent.

Mixed distributions:
The results of this paper naturally extend to the case where the covariate vectors
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
are
independent but not identically distributed, and similarly for the target vectors
X_{1}^{*},\dots,X_{m}^{*}
. In this case, setting
P_{i},\,i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket
the distribution of
X_{i}
, and
Q_{j},\,j\in\llbracket 1;m\rrbracket
the distribution of
X_{j}^{*}
, the distributions
P
and
Q
in Conditions

(A2)
to
(A5)
now represent the mixed distributions
P:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}
and
Q:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}Q_{j}
, respectively.
In particular, the first part of Condition
(A2)
, writing
P(B(z,r))\geq p(z)r^{d}
, translates the idea that sufficiently many
vectors
X_{i},\,i\in\llbracket 1;n\rrbracket
, have some probability to fall inside the ball
B(z,r)
.

Optimal bandwidth:
In the low smoothness regime, that is when the regularity parameter
\ell
is smaller than
d_{P}/2
, then
the
k
-NN matching and local polynomial estimators
\hat{e}_{L}(h)
are not rate-optimal. Their rates of convergence are limited by
their squared conditional bias term, which behaves like
n^{-2\ell/d_{P}}
, as soon as we have a good control of the transferability between
the source and target distributions. The pointwise estimators
\hat{h}(z)
we gave are kernel estimators with the local bandwidth

\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)
, which behaves like
n^{-1/d_{P}}
when
k
is fixed. Alternatively, we can use for
\hat{h}(z)
a kernel estimator with the
non-adaptive local bandwidth
\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)^{2d_{P}/(2\ell+d_{P})}
, which behaves like
n^{-2/(2\ell+d_{P})}
, creating a very localised pointwise
estimator. In this case, the mean-squared error of the final estimator
\hat{e}(h)
would have the rate
n^{-4\ell/(2\ell+d_{P})}
, which
is an improvement over the rate
n^{-2\ell/d_{P}}
when
\ell<d_{P}/2
.

VIII
Conclusion

We showed that the
k
-NN matching and local polynomial estimators for covariate shift adaptation can be applied to covariate vectors
with densities that can approach zero and infinity, and whose support can be an unbounded non-regular set. We provided optimal rates of
convergence that depend on the intrinsic dimensions of the covariates and on the transferability between the source and target
probability measures. For future research, one could investigate how to generalise the results outside the framework of
k
-NN algorithms
or study the optimisation of the censor radius.

IX-A

Proof of Theorem
3

We derive minimax lower bounds using Le Cam’s method. Let
\Phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the function defined by
\Phi(x):=C\exp(-x^{2}/2)
, where
the constant
C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is chosen small enough so that for all
s\in\llbracket 0;\ell\rrbracket,\,\lVert D^{s}\Phi\rVert_{\infty}\leq s!
.
Now fix
\gamma\in[0,1]
and
\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. We consider the function
g_{1}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined by
g_{1}(z):=\Lambda\Phi(z_{1}-r)
, for a
constant
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
and a location parameter
r\in\mathbb{R}
to be chosen later. This regression function is compared with the regression function

g_{0}:z\mapsto 0
. We set the source density at
f_{P}:z\mapsto e^{-z_{1}}
, and the target density at
f_{Q}:z\mapsto\alpha e^{-\alpha z_{1}}
, where

\alpha:=\frac{\gamma}{2}+\frac{1}{\ln(n)}\in(0,1)
, both on the convex support
\mathbb{X}:=\mathbb{R}_{+}\times[0,1]^{d-1}
. The second and third points in the
definition of
\Sigma(\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}})
are satisfied.
The transferability condition writes

\Lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)^{2}}{f_{P}(z)^{\gamma}}\,\mathrm{d}z=\Lambda^{2}\alpha^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-2z_{1}/\ln(n)}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}=(\Lambda\alpha)^{2}\frac{\ln(n)}{2}\leq\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}},\text{ and}

\left(\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{f_{Q}(z)}{f_{P}(z)^{\gamma/2}}\,\mathrm{d}z\right)^{2}=\Lambda^{2}\alpha^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z_{1}/\ln(n)}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\right)^{2}=(\Lambda\alpha\ln(n))^{2}=\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}},

setting
\Lambda^{2}:=(\alpha\ln(n))^{-2}\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}\leq 1
for
n
large enough.
Next, we control the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the distributions
\mathbb{P}_{1}:=(P_{Y,1}\cdot P)\otimes Q
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}:=(P_{Y,0}\cdot P)\otimes Q
, corresponding to the regression functions
g_{1}
and
g_{0}
, respectively. The only difference between
\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\mathbb{P}_{0}
lies
in the conditional distributions
P_{Y,1}
and
P_{Y,0}
given
X=z
, and these conditional distributions are Gaussian distributions with
the same variance
\Lambda^{2}
and with respective locations
g_{1}(z)
and
g_{0}(z)
. Therefore, we have

\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{(g_{1}(z)-g_{0}(z))^{2}}{2\Lambda^{2}}f_{P}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(z_{1}-r)^{2}e^{-z_{1}}\,\mathrm{d}z_{1}\leq\frac{e^{-r}}{2}P_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi^{2}),

where
P_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi^{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Phi(x)^{2}e^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x=C^{2}e^{1/4}\sqrt{2\pi}
.
Setting the parameter
r
such that
P_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi^{2})e^{-r}:=\frac{2\ln(2)}{n}
, we therefore have
\operatorname{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq\ln(2)/n
,
and applying Le Cam’s theorem
(
28
)
yields

\underset{\hat{E}_{n}}{\inf}\;\underset{(P_{X,Y},Q)\in\Sigma(\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}})}{\sup}\mathbb{E}[(\hat{E}_{n}-e(g))^{2}]\geq\frac{(e(g_{1})-e(g_{0}))^{2}}{32}.

However,

e(g_{1})-e(g_{0})=\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{X}}g_{1}(z)f_{Q}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z=\Lambda\alpha e^{-\alpha r}\int_{-r}^{\infty}\Phi(x)e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Since the function
\Phi
is non-negative and non-increasing on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we have

\alpha\int_{-r}^{\infty}\Phi(x)e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\Phi(x)\,\alpha e^{-\alpha x}\,\mathrm{d}x\geq P(\Phi),

where
P(\Phi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\Phi(x)e^{-x}\,\mathrm{d}x>0
. As a consequence, we find

\underset{\hat{E}_{n}}{\inf}\;\underset{(P_{X,Y},Q)\in\Sigma(\gamma,\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}})}{\sup}\mathbb{E}[(\hat{E}_{n}-e(g))^{2}]\geq\frac{P(\Phi)^{2}\Lambda^{2}}{32}\,e^{-2\alpha r}=\frac{P(\Phi)^{2}}{32}\mathcal{C}_{\textup{cov}}(\alpha\ln(n))^{-2}\,e^{-2\alpha r},

hence the result, setting
\mathcal{K}:=\frac{e^{-2}P(\Phi)^{2}}{32P_{\mathbb{R}}(\Phi^{2})^{2}}
.

We also have the conditional negative correlations

\displaystyle A_{\ell}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\geq aA_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}+(1-a)A_{\ell}^{(n-1)},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\geq bB_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}+(1-b)B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}.

Therefore,

A_{\ell}^{(n)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}\geq abA_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}+a(1-b)A_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}+b(1-a)A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}\\
+(1-a)(1-b)A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}.

We recognise the terms
aA_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)},\,bA_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}
, and
(1-a-b)A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}
, so that applying the induction hypothesis, we find

\displaystyle A_{\ell}^{(n)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}-I_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\geq ab\,(A_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}-A_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}-A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)}+A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)})

\displaystyle=ab(A_{\ell}^{(n-1)}-A_{\ell-1}^{(n-1)})(B_{\ell^{\prime}}^{(n-1)}-B_{\ell^{\prime}-1}^{(n-1)})

\displaystyle\geq 0,

which concludes the proof by induction.

For the second point, we can write the expectation of non-negative random variables thanks to tail distribution functions as follows. Let

\Phi^{+}
denote the right-continuous generalised inverse of a non-decreasing function
\Phi
, then

1-1

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{y}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y))\,\Phi_{z}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z))\,\mathbf{1}\left(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)+\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)<\lVert y-x\rVert,\max\{\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y);\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\}\leq r_{0}\right)]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\mathbb{P}(\Phi_{y}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y))>s,\,\Phi_{z}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z))>t,\,\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)+\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)<\lVert y-x\rVert,\max\{\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y);\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\}\leq r_{0})\,\mathrm{d}(t,s)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\mathbf{1}\left(\max\{\Phi_{y}^{+}(t);\Phi_{z}^{+}(s)\}\leq r_{0},\Phi_{y}^{+}(t)+\Phi_{z}^{+}(s)<\lVert y-x\rVert\right)\mathbb{P}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)>\Phi_{y}^{+}(s),\,\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)>\Phi_{z}^{+}(t))\,\mathrm{d}(t,s)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\mathbb{P}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)>\Phi_{z}^{+}(t)\mid\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0})\,\mathbb{P}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)>\Phi_{y}^{+}(s)\mid\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)\leq r_{0})\,\mathrm{d}(t,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{z}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z))\mid\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(z)\leq r_{0}]\,\mathbb{E}[\Phi_{y}(\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y))\mid\hat{\tau}_{k+1}(y)\leq r_{0}].

∎
