A plug-and-play approach with fast uncertainty quantification for weak lensing mass mapping
H. Leterme, A. Tersenov, J. Fadili, J.-L. Starck

Abstract
Context. Upcoming stage-IV surveys such as Euclid and Rubin will deliver vast amounts of high-precision data, opening new opportunities to constrain cosmological models with unprecedented accuracy. A key step in this process is the reconstruction of the dark matter distribution from noisy weak lensing shear measurements. Aims. Current deep learning-based mass mapping methods achieve high reconstruction accuracy, but either require retraining a model for each new observed sky region—limiting practicality—or rely on slow MCMC sampling. Efficient exploitation of future survey data therefore calls for a new method that is accurate, flexible, and fast at inference. In addition, uncertainty quantification with coverage guarantees is essential for reliable cosmological parameter estimation. Methods. We introduce PnPMass, a plug-and-play approach for weak lensing mass mapping. The algorithm produces point estimates by alternating between a gradient descent step with a carefully chosen data fidelity term, and a denoising step implemented with a single deep learning model trained on simulated data corrupted by Gaussian white noise. We also propose a fast, sampling-free uncertainty quantification scheme based on moment networks, with calibrated error bars obtained through conformal prediction to ensure coverage guarantees. Finally, we benchmark PnPMass against both model-driven and data-driven mass mapping techniques. Results. PnPMass achieves performance close to that of state-of-the-art deep-learning methods while offering fast inference—converging in just a few iterations—and requiring only a single training phase, independently of the noise covariance of the observations. It therefore combines flexibility, efficiency, and reconstruction accuracy, while delivering tighter error bars than existing approaches, making it well suited for upcoming weak lensing surveys.



1
Introduction

In the coming years, next-generation observational instruments such as the Euclid space telescope and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory will deliver galaxy catalogs with unprecedented precision and sky coverage. These surveys are expected to enhance our understanding of large-scale structures in the Universe and refine existing models of its formation.

A central task in analyzing data from such surveys is mass mapping through weak gravitational lensing. The aim is to reconstruct the distribution of dark matter from noisy shear measurements, which capture the distortion of galaxy shapes caused by inhomonegeous mass distribution along the line of sight
(
13
)
.
The resulting mass maps are then used to constrain the plausible ranges of cosmological parameters.
Accurate mass mapping is crucial for optimizing cosmological inference—recent work by
37
showed that replacing the classical Kaiser-Squires method
(
13
)
with a more advanced algorithm such as MCALens
(
32
)
can significantly improve the figure of merit (FoM) in realistic weak lensing pipelines.
Moreover, it is essential to rigorously quantify uncertainty, as errors in mass reconstruction can propagate to the estimation of cosmological parameters, potentially amplifying discrepancies.

In recent years, deep learning-based approaches have achieved state-of-the-art reconstruction accuracy in weak lensing mass mapping. Two notable examples are DeepMass
(
11
)
, which directly predicts a point estimate from a noisy shear map, and DeepPosterior
(
23
)
, a Bayesian method that samples from the posterior distribution of the mass map.
Despite their performances, both methods face limitations that are incompatible with upcoming stage-IV surveys such as Euclid and Rubin, where vast amounts of data are set to be delivered.
DeepMass offers fast inference, as a single forward pass through the network suffices to produce a point estimate from an initial, computationally inexpensive estimate derived from a noisy observation. However, as an end-to-end method, the network is sensitive to factors like varying noise levels and masks, and must therefore be retrained for each new observation. DeepPosterior, by contrast, is more flexible and does not require retraining, but inference is computationally expensive since multiple samples must be drawn to obtain a single point estimate.

In this paper, we introduce PnPMass, a novel deep learning-based method for weak lensing mass mapping that, like DeepMass, generates accurate point estimates without posterior sampling and, like DeepPosterior, does not require retraining for each new observation.
Our approach builds on the plug-and-play (PnP) framework
(
38
)
, which enables efficient reconstruction by integrating deep priors into physical models. PnP methods have become widespread in computational imaging and physical modeling
(
14
)
, and have seen growing theoretical support in recent years
(
25
;
35
;
10
;
5
)
, including convergence guarantees to a fixed point under appropriate conditions.
Our key insight is to show that, with appropriate algorithmic design—particularly regarding the data-fidelity term—it is possible to train a denoiser on white Gaussian noise with standard deviation randomly drawn from a given interval, independently of the noise characteristics of the actual observation. This allows for high flexibility across datasets and survey conditions while maintaining fast and accurate reconstructions.

Additionally, we propose an uncertainty quantification (UQ) method for PnPMass that does not rely on sampling. Specifically, we adapt the moment network approach by
12
, originally developed for end-to-end methods like DeepMass, to our PnP framework.
To obtain statistically calibrated uncertainty estimates, we further apply Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR)
(
24
)
to derive per-pixel error bars with provable coverage guarantees.

Our experiments, conducted on the
\kappa
TNG
simulated dataset
(
20
)
, demonstrate that PnPMass achieves competitive reconstruction accuracy while remaining flexible—the model is trained once for all, unlike DeepMass—and requiring only a few iterations at inference—significantly fewer than those needed by DeepPosterior for sampling. Furthermore, our UQ method, which requires only one additional iteration to compute per-pixel intervals, produces smaller error bars than DeepMass. Background on weak lensing mass mapping

In this paper, deterministic vectors are denoted with bold lower-case Greek or Latin letters (
e.g
.

\boldsymbol{\kappa}
), while random vectors are represented with bold sans-serif capital letters (
e.g
.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}
). Deterministic matrices are indicated by standard bold capital letters (
e.g
.

\mathbf{A}
). Furthermore, indexing is done using brackets (
\boldsymbol{\kappa}[k]
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}[k]
, or
\mathbf{A}[k,\,l]
).

2.1
The mass mapping problem

This section provides a brief overview of the weak lensing mass mapping problem, for which a comprehensive review has been proposed by
15
. The objective is to recover a convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}
, which causes isotropic dilations of background galaxies, from a shear map
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathbb{C}^{K^{2}}
, which induces anisotropic stretching. Both fields are discretized over a square grid of size
K\times K
, with
K\in\mathbb{N}
, and represented as flattened one-dimensional vectors. In the weak lensing regime, the convergence is proportional to the projected mass along the line of sight between the background galaxies and the observer
(
13
)
. Reconstructing the convergence field is thus equivalent to estimating the projected mass distribution.

In practice, only variations in
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
around its mean value can be recovered, due to mass-sheet degeneracy. Similarly, only the variations in
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
around its mean value contribute to the reconstruction. We therefore assume, without loss of generality, that both
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
are zero-centered. Under this assumption, a first-order relationship between shear and convergence can be expressed as:

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa},

(1)

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
is a linear operator defined as:

\mathbf{A}:=\mathbf{F}^{\herm}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F},

(2)

where
\mathbf{F}
and its adjoint (Hermitian / conjugate transpose)
\mathbf{F}^{\herm}\in\mathbb{C}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
represent the two-dimensional discrete Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms, respectively. Moreover,
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{C}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
is a diagonal matrix satisfying
\mathbf{P}[0,\,0]=0
, and for all
(k_{1},\,k_{2})\in\left\{0,\ldots,K-1\right\}^{2}\setminus\{(0,\,0)\}
,

\mathbf{P}\left[Kk_{1}+k_{2},\,Kk_{1}+k_{2}\right]:=\frac{(k_{1}+ik_{2})^{2}}{k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}}.

(3)

Due to
\mathbf{P}
being zero at the zero frequency, the operator
\mathbf{A}
is not invertible. Its kernel is the subspace of constant vectors, whereas its image is the subspace of zero-mean vectors. The pseudo-inverse of
\mathbf{A}
is equal to its adjoint
\mathbf{A}^{\!\herm}
, which shares the same kernel and image. Therefore, the convergence map can be recovered via:

\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\mathbf{A}^{\!\herm}\boldsymbol{\gamma},

(4)

yielding an exact reconstruction when both
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
are restricted to the subspace of zero-mean vectors.

A direct observation of the shear, however, is impossible in practice. Instead, a noisy estimator can be obtained by binning a galaxy catalog at the desired resolution and averaging the corresponding galaxy ellipticities within each pixel. Then, (
1
) becomes:

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{n},

(5)

where
\mathbf{M}\in\{0,\,1\}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
is a diagonal matrix encoding the observational mask (
e.g
.
, survey boundaries or contamination from bright stars in the foreground), and
\boldsymbol{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{K^{2}}
is a realization of proper complex Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}
with zero-mean and diagonal covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}\times K^{2}}
. For any pixel
k\in\{1,\ldots,K^{2}\}
where
\mathbf{M}[k,\,k]=1
, the variance is given by:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}[k,\,k]=\sigma_{\ellip}^{2}/N_{k},

(6)

where
\sigma_{\ellip}^{2}>0
is the variance of intrinsic ellipticities, and
N_{k}
is the number of galaxies observed in pixel
k
.

For pixels
k\in\{1,\ldots,K^{2}\}
such that
\mathbf{M}[k,\,k]=0
(
i.e
.
, within masked regions), we adopt a strategy similar to that proposed by
32
;
23
: the noise variance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}[k,\,k]
is artificially set to a very high value in order to reduce the signal-to-noise ratio (S/R) to near zero.
Then, (
5
) can be approximated by:

\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{n}.

(7)

In the remaining of the paper, pixels outside and within the mask are referred to as active pixels and masked pixels, respectively.

2.2
Overview of mass mapping methods

2.2.1
Classical approaches

The most simple method to the mass mapping problem has been proposed by
13
, and is later referred to as the Kaiser-Squires (KS) solution. It consists in applying (
4
) to the noisy shear map
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, followed by Gaussian smoothing to reduce the noise.
More recent approaches have proposed to adopt a variational framework by solving minimization problems of the form

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}}f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})+g(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}),

(8)

where
f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})
represents a data fidelity term measuring the resemblance between
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}
, while
g
denotes a handcrafted regularization function penalizing unexpected solutions.
Notable examples include iterative Wiener filtering
(
4
)
, a forward-backward splitting algorithm with
\ell^{2}
regularization corresponding to a Gaussian prior with predefined power spectrum; the GLIMPSE2D algorithm
(
16
)
, which enforces sparsity in a wavelet dictionary; and the MCALens algorithm
(
32
)
, which models the solution as a combination of a Gaussian and a sparse component in a wavelet dictionary.

2.2.2
Data-driven approaches

Deep learning models have demonstrated significantly improved reconstruction accuracy over classical approaches by learning the distribution of convergence maps from large datasets of simulations. From a Bayesian perspective, where the convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is viewed as the realization of a random vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}
with an unknown distribution, the inverse problem (
7
) becomes:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}=\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}},

(9)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}
denotes the random vector from which
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is drawn.

Among deep learning-based approaches, DeepMass
(
11
)
takes the noisy shear map
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as input, computes a naive estimate such as the KS or iterative Wiener solution, passes it through a deep neural network (typically, a UNet), and outputs an enhanced reconstruction
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\deepmass}
. The model is trained on simulated pairs of convergence maps and their corresponding noisy shear maps,
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, following the forward model (
7
).

While DeepMass achieves high reconstruction accuracy, it suffers from a major limitation: it requires retraining for each new observation
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, as the noise covariance
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
depends on the galaxy number density in each pixel. As a result, the training data must be adapted accordingly, reducing the method’s flexibility.

Alternatively, DeepPosterior
(
23
)
generates samples from the posterior
p_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\!\left(\cdot\right)
by using a Markov Chain Monte-Carlo (MCMC) algorithm. In this approach, the prior
p_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}\!\left(\cdot\right)
is implicitly learned via neural score matching. Then, averaging over the samples yields a point estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{\deepposterior}
. This method directly allows for UQ by computing sample statistics. In contrast to the previous approach, DeepPosterior only needs to be trained once, independently of the noise properties. However, inference is slow, as it requires many samples to produce a single estimate.

Other methods have been proposed, with similar limitations.
30
;
29
;
3

introduced mass mapping techniques based on conditional generative adversarial networks (GANs) or diffusion models.
Recently,
39
developed a fast sampling method based on conditional Wasserstein GANs, significantly reducing generation time per sample compared to DeepPosterior.
Despite their state-of-the-art performances, the above methods suffer from the same limitations as DeepMass: the training phase is specific to a given observation and therefore lack flexibility. More precisely, given a newly-observed sky area, or another galaxy survey, these models must be re-trained relatively to the new configuration—with a specific mask
\mathbf{M}
and per-pixel noise level
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
. Our approach specifically addresses this issue.

3
Proposed approach based on plug-and-play

In this section, we introduce PnPMass, a PnP-based algorithm specifically tailored for the mass mapping problem.
We cast the problem into a real-valued formulation, where we define:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}:=\pmatrix{\Real}\boldsymbol{\gamma}\\
\Imag\boldsymbol{\gamma}\lx@close@alignment\in\mathbb{R}^{2K^{2}},\qquad\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}:=\pmatrix{\Real}\boldsymbol{n}\\
\Imag\boldsymbol{n}\lx@close@alignment\in\mathbb{R}^{2K^{2}},

(10)

\tilde{\mathbf{A}}:=\pmatrix{\Real}\mathbf{A}\\
\Imag\mathbf{A}\lx@close@alignment\in\mathbb{R}^{2K^{2}\times K^{2}},\quad\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}:=\frac{1}{2}\pmatrix{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\lx@close@alignment\in\mathbb{R}^{2K^{2}\times 2K^{2}}.

(11)

Then, (
7
) becomes

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}},

(12)

where the noise
\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}
is a realization of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}})
.

In Sect.
3.1
, we introduce a fixed-point iteration composed of a “forward” step, involving a linear operator
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
and a step-size
\tau>0
, followed by a “backward” step using a deep denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
.
Sect.
3.2
establishes properties on
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
that enable accurate recovery of the convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
from the noisy shear input
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
In Sect.
3.3
, we show that a suitable choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
allows the denoiser to be trained on white Gaussian noise, independently of the noise covariance matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. Sect.
3.4
then derives bounds on
\tau
to guarantee convergence to a fixed point.
Additionally, Sect.
3.5
details properties required for the denoiser, including fixed-point conditions, and noise-level awareness.
Finally, in Sect.
3.6
, we present a variant of PnPMass that incorporates prior knowledge of the Gaussian structure of convergence maps, outside peak regions dominated by large matter aggregates.

3.1
Fixed-point iteration

Given an observation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2K^{2}}
and a step-size
\tau>0
, we introduce a “forward” operator
\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau):\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}
, defined, for any input
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}
, by:

\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau):=\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}),

(13)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}\times 2K^{2}}
denotes a linear operator to be defined. We also consider a deep learning-based denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}\to\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}
with trained parameters
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
.
The PnP method, inspired by standard operator splitting methods in optimization theory for solving inverse problems—see
(
33
)
and Appendix
F
—consists in iterating the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
taken as the composition of the forward operator and the trained denoiser:

\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau):=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\circ\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau).

(14)

Towards studying the properties of this operator, we state our main assumptions:

(H.1)

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}:(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}\to(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
;

(H.2)

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is non-expansive on
(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
,
i.e
.
,
\beta
-Lipschitz continuous on
(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
with
\beta\leq 1
;

(H.3)

\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is symmetric positive semidefinite;

(H.4)

\image\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\subset(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
;

(H.5)

Let
\lambda_{\min}
and
\lambda_{\max}=\|\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\|
denote, respectively, the minimum and maximum
nonzero
eigenvalues of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
. We also introduce:

\rho^{\star}:=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}}.

(15)

For fixed
\rho\in\left[\rho^{\star},\,1\right[
, the step-size
\tau
satisfies

\frac{1-\rho}{\lambda_{\min}}\leq\tau\leq\frac{1+\rho}{\lambda_{\max}}.

(16)

Under the above assumptions, the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
enjoys the following properties.

Proposition
\thetheorem

Under assumptions
(H.1)
–
(H.5)
, the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
admits a unique fixed point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
.
Moreover, the sequence of iterates
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(k)})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by

\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(k+1)}=\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(k)},\,\tau),

(17)

starting at
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(0)}\in(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
, converges linearly to
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
with convergence rate
\rho
.

Proof

See Appendix
A
.

3.2
Which noise to train the denoiser ?

Under the above assumptions,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
obeys the fixed-point equation

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}})\Bigr).

(18)

Assessing whether the fixed point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
lies sufficiently close to the true convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is a challenging question that falls beyond the scope of this paper. A mathematical framework is nevertheless sketched in Appendix
B
, thereby constraining the noise level at which the denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
must be trained.

Our intuition can be summarized as follows. Assuming the residual
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-\boldsymbol{\kappa}
is small enough, by applying the forward step to the fixed point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
, we get the following approximation:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}:=\forward_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\ \tau)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\bigl(-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\bigr)

(19)

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}.

(20)

Therefore, applying the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
introduced in (
14
) to the fixed point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
yields

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}\bigr)\approx\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\bigr).

(21)

Thus,
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
acts as a denoiser on the true convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
corrupted by a Gaussian noise
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}:=\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}
, which is an outcome of

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}:=\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}),\quad\mbox{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}:=\tau^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}\tilde{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}.

(22)

In practice, we train the denoiser by minimizing the empirical quadratic risk,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}\Bigr)-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}\right\|^{2},

(23)

using a dataset of
n
training samples, where the ground truth convergence maps
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}
are generated from simulations based on the
\Lambda
CDM model, and the noisy inputs are computed from
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}
by adding noise
\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}
drawn according to (
22
).

3.3
Choice of operator
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}

One of the main objectives of this paper is to implement a mass mapping method whose training phase is
independent
of the training noise covariance matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. Clearly, given our discussion in Sect.
3.2
, the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
in (
22
) must be independent of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
.
To this end, we use the degree of freedom offered by the choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
. More precisely, we propose to make the following choice:

\tilde{\mathbf{B}}:={\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2},

(24)

in which case (
22
) and (
13
) become:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=\tau^{2}{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau):=\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}+\tau{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}).

(25)

Beware here that strictly speaking,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
is not a covariance matrix as it is only semidefinite positive with
\ker\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}=\ker\mathbf{A}
, the subspace of constant vectors. But this is fine as we are only interested in random perturbations along
(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}=(\ker\mathbf{A})^{\perp}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}
is symmetric positive definite.

In Appendix
C
, we show that with the choice (
24
),
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}={\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
verifies assumptions
(H.3)
and
(H.4)
. In fact, we even have the equality
\image\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
.

By closely inspecting (
25
), one sees that the residual
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}
is multiplied by
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}
, which corresponds to a noise-whitening step. This is in a stark contrast with the choice
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\!:=\!{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}
, which yields
\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau):=\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}+\tau{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})
. This alternative choice is the one dictated by the standard variational (or Bayesian MAP) perspective with a data fidelity

\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})\bigr\|^{2}

as negative log-likelihood, whose gradient is
{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})
.
This clearly shows that the widely-adopted optimization or Bayesian perspective on PnP is not necessarily the most appropriate one and may even be misleading in the PnP context—see also the discussion in Appendix
F
. The fixed-point perspective, on the other hand, offers more insight and flexibility.

In Appendix
D
, we show that

{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{I}-\frac{1}{K^{2}},

(26)

(the constant
1/K^{2}
is subtracted from all matrix elements). In practice, the image size
K
is sufficiently large to neglect the non-diagonal elements. Therefore, from (
25
) and (
26
), it is valid to take the approximation

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{0}\approx\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}.

(27)

In turn, the denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is trained on zero-mean white Gaussian noise with a standard deviation equal to the step-size
\tau
. In Sect.
3.4
, we discuss how to select an appropriate range for
\tau
.

3.4
Step-size selection

As discussed in Sect.
3.1
, existence and uniqueness of a fixed point as well as convergence of the PnP iteration (
17
) to this fixed point require
\tau
to satisfy
(H.5)
. Substituting the expression for
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
from (
24
), condition (
16
) specializes to

\frac{1-\rho}{\lambda_{\min}}\leq\tau\leq\frac{1+\rho}{\lambda_{\max}},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\lambda_{\max}=\bigl\|{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigr\|.

(28)

It is well known that
\lambda_{\max}
can be accurately computed via the power iteration method. In Appendix
E
, we will also discuss how the power iteration can be used to efficiently compute
\lambda_{\min}
.

One can also get a simple estimate of the upper-bound for
\tau
via an upper bound of
\lambda_{\max}
. Indeed, observe that

\displaystyle\bigl\|{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigr\|\bigl\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq\bigl\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\bigr\|=1/\min_{k}\tilde{\sigma}_{k},

where we have used (
26
) and
\tilde{\sigma}_{k}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}[k,\,k]^{1/2}
denotes the noise standard deviation at pixel
k
. Therefore,

\tau\leq(1+\rho)\min_{k}\tilde{\sigma}_{k},

(29)

is a sufficient condition for the right hand side of (
28
) to hold. Thus, the choice of the largest step-size is essentially governed by the smallest noise level in the input shear map—that is, the pixel with the largest number of observed galaxies.

3.5
Denoiser training

3.5.1
Ensuring convergence to a fixed point

The denoiser ought to comply with
(H.1)
and
(H.2)
for Proposition 1 to hold.
As discussed in Sect.
3.3
(see also Appendix
C
), with the choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}
given in (
24
), one has

(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}:=\ker(\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp},

(30)

that is, the subspace of zero-mean convergence maps.
In turn,
(H.1)
tells us that the denoiser must not introduce any bias,
i.e
.
the denoised version of a zero-mean map must also be zero-mean. This is a natural and expected property. It is consistent with the mass-sheet degeneracy property discussed in Sect.
2.1
and can be enforced by explicitly appending a mean-centering layer at the output of the denoiser.

Regarding the non-expansiveness assumption
(H.2)
, it can be encouraged during training by introducing a regularization term that controls the spectral norm of the denoiser’s Jacobian
(
21
)
. However, this strategy is computationally very expensive, and we simply chose to ignore this constraint in practice. A theoretical understanding of the training dynamics, including a potential implicit bias towards non-expansive denoisers, would be valuable. This avenue is beyond the scope of this work and is left for future research.

3.5.2
Noise-level-aware denoiser

According to the discussions in Sects.
3.2
,
3.3
and
3.4
, the denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is trained by solving (
23
), where
\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}
are sampled from a zero-mean white Gaussian distribution with variance
\tau^{2}
. The bound (
29
) shows that the noise level
\tau
used for training depends on the noise covariance matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
of the mass mapping problem. Therefore, at this stage, the training phase remains dependent on
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
. However, this dependence now reduces to a single scalar
\tau
, whereas the full (diagonal) covariance matrix was required for training DeepMass.

We aim to push this one step forward by making the training phase completely independent of the covariance matrix. One simple strategy would be to select a small value for
\tau
, to cover a broad range of survey conditions. However, as our experiments confirm, the choice of
\tau
strongly affects reconstruction quality.

We therefore adopt a more flexible approach, in which the model is enabled to adapt to different noise conditions during inference. To this end, the network is trained on a dataset
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, where each input
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i}
is obtained by corrupting the ground truth
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}
by noise
\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}
drawn from
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\,\sigma_{i}^{2})
, with
\sigma_{i}
being randomly picked among a wide range of noise levels.
For higher reconstruction accuracy, we use a noise-level-aware model, following an approach initially proposed by
40
. In this context, the denoiser network takes the noise standard deviation
\sigma
as an additional entry,
i.e
.

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\sigma)
, where
\sigma>0
denotes the noise standard deviation at which the model is expected to operate.

During inference with PnPMass, according to (
27
), the intermediate outputs are propagated though the network with noise standard deviation
\tau
. Then, the fixed-point iteration introduced in (
14
) now reads

\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau):=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)\circ\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau),

(31)

where now, both the forward and denoising steps depend on the step-size
\tau
. This is again reminiscent of the forward-backward splitting (FBS) algorithm as discussed in Appendix
F
.

3.5.3
Training on multiple cosmologies

Generating training data via simulation requires selecting a set of cosmological parameters, which implicitly shapes the prior distribution from which convergence map samples are drawn. If the chosen parameters diverge significantly from the true, unknown cosmology that one wants to infer, the trained denoiser may struggle to generalize and thus fail to produce accurate reconstructions. One way to overcome this is to generate training data across a distribution of plausible cosmologies, enhancing the network’s robustness to distributional shifts.

3.6
PnPMass on non-Gaussian residuals

In the spirit of DeepMass
(
11
)
, the PnPMass algorithm can be enhanced by considering a priori knowledge about the underlying physics and data structure. For this, we use a hypothesis made by
32
for MCALens: convergence maps can be seen as the superposition of a Gaussian field and a non-Gaussian one (hereafter referred to as a residual field):

\boldsymbol{\kappa}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}+\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\nongauss},

(32)

where
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}
, the Gaussian component, can be estimated from the noisy shear map
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
using an iterative Wiener filtering method (see Sect.
2.2.1
), assuming the power spectrum
\mathbf{P}_{\!\gauss}
is known, coined
\mathcal{W}_{\!\mathbf{P}_{\!\gauss}}
,

\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}:=\mathcal{W}_{\!\mathbf{P}_{\!\gauss}}\!(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}).

(33)

Then, the inverse problem (
12
) can be re-written as

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\nongauss}=\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\nongauss}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}},

(34)

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\nongauss}
denotes the residual shear map

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\nongauss}:=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}.

(35)

Then, PnPMass can be used to estimate
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\nongauss}
.

In order this approach to remain consistent with the previous theoretical framework, the denoiser must be trained on a suitable dataset. Specifically, we consider the training set
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
introduced in Sect.
3.2
. Then, for each
i\in\left\{0,\ldots,n\right\}
, we compute the Gaussian component
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}_{i}
from the noisy input
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i}
, using a Wiener filter with power spectrum
\mathbf{P}_{\!\gauss}
. Finally, we get the residual training set
\bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\nongauss}_{0,\,i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\nongauss}_{i}\bigr)_{i=1}^{n}
by subtracting
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\gauss}_{i}
to both the noisy input and the ground truth.

4
Uncertainty quantification

In Sect.
4.1
, we introduce a fast UQ method for PnPMass. This approach is based on moment networks, originally proposed by
12
to quantify uncertainty in end-to-end deep learning models such as DeepMass.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first UQ method within the PnP framework that does not rely on ensembles or posterior sampling—both of which can be computationally demanding.
Ensemble-based approaches estimate uncertainty by training and evaluating multiple networks, capturing variability across model parameters
(
28
;
36
)
. Posterior sampling methods, in contrast, are Bayesian UQ methods that seek to approximate the posterior distribution of the reconstructed images, given some implicit or explicit priors. For example,
17
combined implicit PnP priors with Langevin diffusion to draw posterior samples. Alternatively,
6
proposed a Bayesian extension of the PnP framework by placing a prior over the denoiser’s parameters and propagating uncertainty through the reconstruction pipeline.

A sampling-free method was introduced by
22
to approximate uncertainty in Monte-Carlo dropout networks. This approach could in principle be adapted to the Bayesian PnP framework proposed by
6
as a way to reduce computational costs. This line of research targets uncertainties in the model design and parameters—commonly referred to as epistemic uncertainty—and is left for future work. In contrast, the present study assumes perfect model design and training, and instead quantifies irreducible uncertainty arising from data noise—known as aleatoric uncertainty. We refer readers to
1
for a broader discussion of the distinction between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty.

Next, in Sect.
4.2
, we describe the CQR algorithm
(
24
)
, which was adapted for mass mapping in a previous work
(
18
)
. This method serves as a postprocessing step that can be applied to any reconstruction method with an initial uncertainty estimate, providing per-pixel non-asymptotic marginal coverage guarantees without relying on prior assumptions about the data distribution.

4.1
Variance estimation with moment networks

In Sect.
4.1.1
, we adapt the main principles of posterior variance estimation using moment networks, as described by
12
, to DeepMass specifically. Then, in Sect.
4.1.2
, we extend this approach to the PnP framework and provide theoretical insights supporting its use. Finally, in Sect.
4.1.3
, we explain how to get error bars from the estimated posterior variance.

4.1.1
Moment networks for DeepMass

Given a simulated dataset
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, where the pairs
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})
are i.i.d.
samples of the ground truth convergence maps and observed noisy shear maps, DeepMass attempts to solve the mass mapping problem by learning a deep neural network
\mathcal{M}_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}
on
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
according to

\hat{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\in\argmin_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathcal{M}_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i})-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}\right\|^{2}.

(36)

As
n\to\infty
, (
36
) is expected to approach a minimizer of the population risk,
i.e
.

\hat{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\in\argmin_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left\|\mathcal{M}_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\right\|^{2}\Bigr],

(37)

where the expectation is to be understood with respect to to the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})
.

DeepMass adopts a Bayesian perspective and attempts to interpret (
37
) as the solution to

\min_{\mathcal{D}\,\text{is $\mu_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}$-measurable}}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\left\|\mathcal{D}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\right\|^{2}\Bigr].

(38)

where
\mu_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}
is the conditional distribution of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}
given
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}
. The closed-form solution to (
38
) is known as the posterior conditional mean estimator

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\,|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\bigr].

(39)

The key assumption in DeepMass is that the following approximation holds:

\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\approx\mathcal{M}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}).

(40)

Intuitively, this can make sense if the architecture of the neural network
\mathcal{M}_{{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}
, seen as a parametrized class of functions, is expressive enough, in such a way that the two optimization problems (
37
) and (
38
) are close.

This reasoning can be extended to estimate higher-order moments, including the posterior conditional variance per pixel:

\mathbb{V}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\,|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\bigr]:=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\,|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\bigr]\Bigr)^{2}\bigm|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\Bigr],

(41)

which is known to be the solution to

\min_{\mathcal{V}\,\text{is $\mu_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}\,|\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}$-measurable}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{V}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})-\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}-\widehat{\mathcal{D}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}\bigr)^{2}\right\|^{2}\right].

(42)

To this end, we consider a new neural network model
\mathcal{G}_{\!{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
trained on a dataset
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
by solving

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\!{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i})-\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\right\|^{2},

(43)

where we have defined the target
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}:=\left(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}-\mathcal{M}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i})\right)^{2}
. Therefore, similarly to what we have argued for (
37
), (
43
) is an empirical version of

\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{G}_{\!{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})-\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}-\mathcal{M}_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{w}}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}})\right)^{2}\right\|^{2}\right],

(44)

Comparing (
42
) and (
44
), combining (
40
) and (
41
), and arguing as above, we can reasonably assume that

\mathbb{V}\bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\,|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\bigr]\approx\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}).

(45)

The corresponding model is referred to as an order-2 moment network.

4.1.2
Adaptation to PnPMass

The moment network approach is not directly applicable to the PnP framework for several reasons. First, PnP, which is iterative, does not necessarily have a Bayesian interpretation, let alone a posterior conditional mean (see Sect.
3.3
and Appendix
F
). Recall also that we do not want to train any network on a dataset that depends on the noise covariance matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}
, for flexibility reasons. However, we will now describe how, within the setting presented in Sect.
3
, the posterior conditional variance can be estimated with an order-2 moment network trained on zero-mean white Gaussian noise.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
be the denoiser network that has been trained by solving (
23
) on a training dataset
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, where we have denoted
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i}:=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}
, with
\boldsymbol{n}_{0,\,i}
being sampled from
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\tau_{i}^{2}\mathbf{I})
conditionally on
\tau_{i}
—see (
22
) and (
27
).
The noise standard deviations
\tau_{i}
are samples from the uniform distribution on an interval
\left[\tau_{\min},\,\tau_{\max}\right]
containing the bounds in (
28
). As discussed in Sect.
3.5.2
,
\tau
is also an input of the denoiser network.

We now consider the random vector

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}:=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0},

(46)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}
has been introduced in (
22
). In the context where we consider the step-size
\tau
to be an outcome of
\mathsf{T}\sim\mathcal{U}(\left[\tau_{\min},\,\tau_{\max}\right])
,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}
is now a mixture of zero-mean Gaussians with uniform mixing over
\mathsf{T}
. The noisy inputs
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i}
from the training set are then assumed to be drawn i.i.d.
from
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}
.
With a similar reasoning as in Sect.
4.1.1
, we can infer that, given
\tau
and a noisy input
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0}
,

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0},\,\tau)\approx\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\bigm|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0},\,\mathsf{T}=\tau\Bigr].

(47)

For the posterior conditional variance, we consider a model
\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
trained on
(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i},\,\boldsymbol{v}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, with

\boldsymbol{v}_{i}:=\left(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}-\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0,\,i},\,\tau_{i})\right)^{2},

(48)

and we get, following again Sect.
4.1.1
, that

\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0},\,\tau)=\mathbb{V}\Bigl[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\bigm|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0},\,\mathsf{T}=\tau\Bigr].

(49)

Now, we will argue that, after reaching a sufficient number of iterations, the outputs of
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
respectively approximate the posterior conditional mean and variance given a noisy observation
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
To this end, let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}:=\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\ \tau)

denote the output of the forward step applied to the fixed point, satisfying (
19
).
In Sect.
3.1
, we have informally established conditions on the training noise to favor small residuals
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Therefore, we consider (
20
) as a reasonable approximation:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}\approx\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0},\,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\boldsymbol{n}_{0}:=\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}.

(50)

For a number of iterations
N_{\iterations}\geq 1
, denote
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{N_{\iterations}}(\cdot,\tau)
the
N_{\iterations}
-th composition of the PnPMass fixed point operator
\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{N_{\iterations}}(\cdot,\tau)
. Clearly, the PnPMass iteration (
17
) also reads

\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(N_{\iterations})}=\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}^{N_{\iterations}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(0)},\tau).

(51)

Denote
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\,\tau):=\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(N_{\iterations})},\ \tau)
the output of the
(N_{\iterations}+1)
-th forward step.
For
N_{\iterations}
large enough, we have from Proposition 1 that
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{(N_{\iterations})}\approx\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
which, combined with (
19
) and (
20
) yields

\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\,\tau)\approx\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}},\ \tau)=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}\approx\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0},

(52)

and therefore,

\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}},\,\mathsf{T})\approx\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0},

(53)

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}
has been introduced in (
46
).
Plugging this into (
47
) and (
49
), replacing
\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0}
by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0}
, we get, respectively:

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0},\,\tau)\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}},\,\mathsf{T})=\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\,\tau),\,\mathsf{T}=\tau\right]

(54)

and

\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0},\,\tau)\approx\mathbb{V}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}},\,\mathsf{T})=\mathcal{P}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\,\tau),\,\mathsf{T}=\tau\right].

(55)

It is then sufficient that
\mathcal{P}(\cdot,\,\tau)
is injective to deduce that

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0},\,\tau)\approx\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\;\middle|\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]

(56)

and

\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{0},\,\tau)\approx\mathbb{V}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\;\middle|\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right].

(57)

4.1.3
Uncalibrated pre-estimation of per-pixel error bars

Our goal is to provide per-pixel error bars with marginal and non-asymptotic coverage guarantees. More precisely, given a desired miscoverage rate
\alpha\in\left]0,\,1\right[
, we want to estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}
such that, for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,K^{2}\}
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}[k]\notin\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}[k],\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}[k]\right]\,\middle|\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}\leq\alpha.

(58)

To this end, we will start with a rough estimate based on a Gaussian assumption. This will then be calibrated (see Sect.
4.2
). More precisely, we start with the following quantiles
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}
from a Gaussian distribution of mean
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
and variance
\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}
, which are provided by Algorithm
1
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\pm}[k]:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}[k]\pm\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[k],

(59)

where
\Phi
is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\,1)
, and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\sqrt{\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}}
is the standard deviation estimate. If the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}[k]
, given
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, were
\mathcal{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}[k],\,\hat{\boldsymbol{v}}[k])
, then (
58
) would be satisfied by construction. This is however not the case, hence the need of a calibration step that we are about to describe.

4.2
Calibration with conformal predictions

In Sect.
4.1
, the pre-estimates of the per-pixel error bars were based on several assumptions entailing several approximations:

\bullet

(
47
) and (
49
) state that the networks
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\!\hat{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}
are only approximations to the posterior mean and variance given
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{0}
, respectively. As discussed there, the approximation quality depends on factors such as the complexity of the neural networks used and the quality and diversity of the training data. As such, the method primarily captures aleatoric uncertainty—while epistemic uncertainty, associated with model expressiveness or limited knowledge, remains unaddressed.

\bullet

(
52
) is yet another approximation of the point estimate which, as we argued there, relies on the assumption that the fixed point
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
of PnPMass lies close enough to
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
. In practice, this is not necessarily true and thus bias—non-zero residuals in (
52
)—may still be present. This is especially true near masked regions where the S/R is very low.

\bullet

The per-pixel error bars have been derived under a Gaussian assumption for the posterior distribution, making them only rough uncertainty estimates.

For all these reasons, the miscoverage inequality as stated in (
58
) is not guaranteed to hold. In this section, we present a calibration method directly inspired by CQR
(
24
)
, for which we can obtain (marginal) coverage guarantees that are valid for finite samples (non-asymptotic) and do not depend on any assumption on the models or the data distribution (distribution-free). The choice of this method over other calibration approaches such as risk-controlling prediction sets
(
2
)
, based on concentration inequalities, is motivated by the fact that it explicitly avoids overconservative error bands, making it suitable in a context where one want to get error bars as tight as possible. We refer readers to
18
for a detailed discussion on this topic.

In Sect.
4.2.1
, we review the general principles of CQR for mass mapping, and we propose in Sect.
4.2.2
a method to minimize the size of the error bars without the need for model selection on a validation set.

4.2.1
General principles

We consider a calibration set
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})_{i=n+1}^{n+m}
of size
m\in\mathbb{N}
. For each pair
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i})
, we compute the lower and upper bounds
(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}_{i},\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}_{i})
from (
59
). Then, we compute a vector of conformity scores, denoted by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{2}}
, defined, for each pixel
k\in\{1,\ldots,K^{2}\}
, by:

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}[k]=\max\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}_{i}[k]-\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}[k],\,\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{i}[k]-\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}_{i}[k]\right).

(60)

Then, the calibration vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
is obtained by computing, for each pixel, the
(1-\alpha)(1+1/m)
-th empirical quantile of
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{i}[k])_{i=n+1}^{n+m}
. The term
(1+1/m)
, which accounts for finite-sample correction, imposes a lower bound to the target error rate:
\alpha\geq 1/(m+1)
.
Finally, the calibrated bounds are defined as follows:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}_{\cqr}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}_{\cqr}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}+\boldsymbol{\lambda}.

(61)

We denote by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}^{-}_{\cqr}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}^{+}_{\cqr}
the random vectors from which
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-}_{\cqr}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{+}_{\cqr}
are drawn. Their random nature comes from that of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}
, from which the noisy observation is drawn, as well as
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{i})_{i=n+1}^{n+m}
, from which the calibration set is drawn. If
(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}_{i},\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}_{i})_{i=n+1}^{n+m}\cup\{(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}},\,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}})\}
are exchangeable (
e.g
.
, i.i.d.
), and the conformity scores are almost surely distinct, then we have the following (marginal) miscoverage guarantee at pixel
k
:

\alpha-\frac{1}{m+1}\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}[k]\notin\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}^{-}_{\cqr}[k],\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}}^{+}_{\cqr}[k]\right]\right\}\leq\alpha.

(62)

Observe that averaging over pixels, we deduce that the expected proportion of pixels whose mass estimates fall outside the corresponding predicted error interval also verifies the bounds in (
62
).

The bound in (
62
) presents a major difference compared to (
58
). Whereas the latter is conditional given
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, the former is marginalized over all possible outcomes of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\Gamma}}}
. Consequently, some inputs may lead to errors more likely than others. Conformal prediction with conditional guarantees is a very challenging problem and has only been solved in specific cases
(
9
)
. We leave this for future work. In addition, the coverage guarantee provided in (
62
) is also marginalized over the distribution of all calibration sets. Therefore, accidentally picking an out-of-distribution calibration set may lead to a higher error rate than expected. This effect can be mitigated by increasing the size of the calibration set.

Note that the calibration procedure is not independent of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, unlike the training phase. However, we point out that the former is several orders of magnitude cheaper than the latter, computationally speaking, as detailed in Sect.
6.4
.

4.2.2
Minimizing the size of the error bars

We can easily show that the miscoverage guarantee from (
62
) still holds if the pre-calibration bounds
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\pm}
, introduced in (
59
), are replaced by

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{\pm}_{\mu}[k]:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}[k]\pm\mu\,\Phi^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}[k],

(63)

for any multiplicative constant
\mu>0
. The calibration vector is then recomputed for each
\mu
, and is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mu}
. This allows us to minimize the mean error bar size over
\mu
:

\mu^{\ast}\in\argmin_{\mu>0}\mu\epsilon+\lambda_{\mu}

(64)

where
\epsilon>0
denotes the mean half-size of the pre-calibration error bars (averaged over all test images and all pixels), and
\lambda_{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the mean value of the calibration vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mu}
, which is a non-increasing function of
\mu
.
This procedure
is computationally inexpensive, since
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}_{i}
and
\hat{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{i}
are computed only once. For each candidate
\mu
, only the conformity scores
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mu,\,i}
and their corresponding quantiles must be recomputed in order to get
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{\mu}
.

6.2.1
Accuracy vs error bar size

After calibration, all methods achieve the same miscoverage rate, as guaranteed by (
62
), but the mean error bar size differs across methods.

First, let us compare our approach with Wiener and MCALens. PnPMass, in both standard and residual versions, produces smaller error bars than the two model-driven methods—see y-axis in Fig.
3
.
We hypothesize that the combination of higher reconstruction accuracy and adaptive pre-calibrated error bars obtained with order-2 moment networks helps discriminate between smooth regions and peak areas. This, in turn, may result in more refined uncertainty quantification by correctly assigning higher uncertainties near peaks.
In contrast, Wiener and MCALens produce error bars that are independent of the input images: they are initialized at
\boldsymbol{0}
and subsequently calibrated using a parameter that depends only on pixel location.

More surprisingly, PnPMass also produces smaller error bars than DeepMass, despite being slightly less accurate. This result is statistically significant, as it is observed for all
512
test images. To explain this phenomenon, we propose the following conjecture: DeepMass detects more peaks than PnPMass, which improves reconstruction accuracy, but it also tends to hallucinate more peaks, which negatively affects the confidence level. In other words, the optimal precision-recall balance for peak detection depends on the metric one aims to prioritize. Consequently, a trade-off arises between reconstruction accuracy and error bar size. The same reasoning applies when comparing the standard and residual versions of PnPMass.

It is worth noting that error bars could, in principle, have been initialized at
\boldsymbol{0}
for all mass mapping methods, avoiding the need to train an order-2 moment network for DeepMass and PnPMass. However, this would ultimately result in larger calibrated error bars to compensate for the poorer initialization. A plot similar to Fig.
3
, with zero-initialization for all methods, is shown in Appendix
G
, Fig.
6
.

6.2.2
Impact of calibration on the confidence intervals

For all methods based on moment networks, the pre-calibration error bars yield a miscoverage rate slightly above the target
\alpha
. Interestingly, calibration
reduced
the error bar size for DeepMass, which may seem counter-intuitive. This effect arises from the optimization strategy described in Sect.
4.2.2
. Our interpretation is that, while error bars were under-estimated for most pixels, some regions—particularly around peaks—were overly conservative, leading to an overall overestimation that calibration was able to correct.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced PnPMass, a PnP-based mass mapping method that combines the strengths of DeepMass and DeepPosterior—namely, flexibility (no need to retrain for each new observation) and fast inference—while maintaining competitive reconstruction accuracy. We further proposed a fast UQ strategy for PnPMass based on moment networks, followed by conformal prediction to mitigate several sources of bias, including model uncertainty. Our results show that PnPMass yields the smallest calibrated error bars among all evaluated mass mapping methods. Together, these contributions make our approach well suited to handle the large data volumes expected from upcoming stage-IV surveys such as Euclid and Rubin.

Several directions remain open to fully integrate our framework into weak lensing pipelines.
First, further investigations are needed to assess the robustness of our approach across different cosmologies. Our experiments focused on
\kappa
TNG, a dataset simulated from a single set of cosmological parameters. A more realistic setting would involve datasets spanning a range of cosmological models.
Second, although conformal prediction provides coverage guarantees on average, it may fail to control miscoverage around peak structures, which are particularly important for cosmological parameter inference
(
37
)
. A promising avenue to address this limitation is to employ conditional conformal prediction
(
9
)
, rather than the standard version used here.
Finally, it will be crucial to assess how uncertainties estimated at the mass-mapping stage propagate to cosmological parameter inference and affect the resulting FoM. In particular, this analysis could clarify whether the residual variant should be preferred over the standard version of PnPMass, as optimal cosmological parameter estimation may depend on a trade-off between reconstruction accuracy and size of the confidence intervals.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition 1

We start with consider this preparatory lemma.

Lemma
\thetheorem

Suppose that
(H.3)
and
(H.4)
hold. Then, for
\rho\in\left[\rho^{\star},\,1\right[
,
the forward operator
\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
is
\rho
-contractive on
(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
if, and only if, the step-size
\tau
satisfies (
16
).
The smallest contraction factor
\rho=\rho^{\star}
is obtained for
\tau=\tau^{\star}
, with

\tau^{\star}:=\frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}}.

(65)

Proof

Denote for short the subspace
\mathbb{V}:=(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
. By construction, and since
(H.4)
holds, we have:

\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau):\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{V}.

(66)

Let
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{V}
. The forward operator is an affine map, and we thus have

\displaystyle\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau)-\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime},\,\tau)=(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\,(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}).

(67)

Since
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
is symmetric positive definite on the orthogonal to its kernel, the Lipschitz constant
L_{\tau}
of
\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
on
\mathbb{V}
is exactly given by

L_{\tau}=\max(|1-\tau\lambda_{\min}|,|1-\tau\lambda_{\max}|).

(68)

Consequently,
\forward_{\!\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
is
\rho
-contractive on
\mathbb{V}
if, and only if,
L_{\tau}\leq\rho
. Expanding this inequality, and considering that
\lambda_{\min}\leq\lambda_{\max}
, this is equivalent to (
16
). The lower bound
\rho^{\star}
introduced in (
15
) ensures the non-emptiness of the interval. Moreover, setting
\rho=\rho^{\star}
reduces the range of possible step-size values to
\tau=\tau^{\star}
such as introduced in (
65
). Finally, having
\rho<1
provides the contractive property of

Appendix B
Insights into convergence properties

In this section, we sketch a mathematical framework for studying the convergence properties of our algorithm, which leads to constrain the noise level at which the denoiser must be trained, as explained in Sect.
3.2
.

Inserting (
12
) into the fixed-point equation (
18
), we infer that

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}(-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}})\Bigr)=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0}\Bigr),

(71)

where we denoted the residual
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}:=\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}-\boldsymbol{\kappa}
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}=\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}
. Observe that
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}
is a sample of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}
as defined in (
22
).

Clearly,
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
acts as a denoiser on the true convergence map
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
corrupted by the “noise”
(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0}
. Intuitively, one would like
(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
to be small enough so that the dominating part of the noise would be that from
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}
. This intuition is exact when
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}
(
i.e
.
denoising), in which case, with the natural choice
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}=\mathbf{I}
and
\tau=1
, (
71
) reads
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0}\bigr)
with
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{n}}
. One should then train the denoiser with noise
\boldsymbol{n}_{0}
.

To get some insight into this intuition in the general case, let us now bound the residual
\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
be a sample of the zero-mean random noise
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}
used during training, and assume that the support of the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}
is contained in
(\ker\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})^{\perp}
.
Subtracting
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
to both sides of (
71
), we have:

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0}\Bigr)\!-\!\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\Bigr)\right\|+\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\Bigr)\!-\!\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|(\mathbf{I}-\tau\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}})\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}+\boldsymbol{n}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\|+\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\Bigr)-\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right\|

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\rho\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{r}}\right\|+\left\|\boldsymbol{n}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\|+\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\Bigl(\boldsymbol{\kappa}+\boldsymbol{\zeta}\Bigr)-\boldsymbol{\kappa}\right\|,

(73)

where we used
(H.2)
in (
72
), and the triangle inequality and Lemma 1 (Appendix
A
) in (
73
).
We then deduce, after taking the expectation with respect to the joint distribution of
(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}})
, that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}}\right\|\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho)^{-1}\left(\,\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\bigr\|\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\bigr)-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\right\|\right]\,\right)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\rho)^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\bigr\|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\!+\!\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\bigr)-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{K}}\right\|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\right),

(74)

where we used Jensen’s inequality.

We aim to minimize the expected norm of the residual such as introduced above. Ideally, the denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
would be trained to minimize the population quadratic risk, which is nothing but the second term in the upper bound (
74
). In practice, however, training is performed using the empirical quadratic risk, which introduces an additional generalization error term that must be accounted for.
Regarding the first term in (
74
), it captures the discrepancy between the noise in training and that in the PnP iteration. We have

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\right\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}\right\|^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\right\|^{2}\right]-2\nu\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}\right\|^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\right\|^{2}\right]^{1/2},

where
\nu\in[-1,1]
is the correlation coefficient between
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}
. Minimizing the above expression wrt
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}\right\|^{2}\right]
and
\nu
gives that the optimal values are, respectively,
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}\right\|^{2}\right]
and
1
. Clearly, the noise
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}
ought to have the same variance as
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{N}}_{0}
and has correlation
1
with it.

\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}

Recall that
\tilde{\mathbf{B}}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}:={\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
with the choice made in (

Appendix D
Proof of (
26
)

We embark from (
11
) to see that

\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}=\Real(\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}),

where
\mathbf{A}^{*}
is the adjoint matrix of
\mathbf{A}
. Using now (
2
), we get

\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{F}^{\herm}|\mathbf{P}|^{2}\mathbf{F}.

From (
3
), it follows that
|\mathbf{P}|^{2}
is a diagonal matrix with

|\mathbf{P}[0,0]|=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad|\mathbf{P}[i,i]|=1,\forall i\geq 1.

This entails that

\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{F}^{\herm}\mathbf{F}-\mathbf{F}[1,\,:]^{\top}\mathbf{F}[1,\,:],

where
\mathbf{F}[1,\,:]=
is the first row of
\mathbf{F}
. Let
\mathds{1}
be the column vector of ones of size
K^{2}
. Since
\mathbf{F}[1,\,:]:=\mathds{1}^{\top}/K
(the zero frequency component), and
\mathbf{F}
is unitary, we get that

\mathbf{A}^{*}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}-\mathds{1}\mathds{1}^{\top}/K^{2}=\mathbf{I}-1/K^{2}.

Appendix E
Computing
\lambda_{\max}
and
\lambda_{\min}

\lambda_{\max}
can be easily computed from the power iteration applied to
{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
starting from a random uniform vector. We now turn to computing the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of
{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
. From Appendix
C
, we have
{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}=\ker\mathbf{A}=\image\mathds{1}
, whose orthogonal is the subspace of zero-mean vectors. Let
\lambda_{1}=0<\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{\min}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{K^{{}^{2}}}=\lambda_{\max}
, be the eigenvalues of
{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
sorted in ascending order. Denote
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
the corresponding unit norm eigenvectors, where
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\mathds{1}^{\top}/K
. Define the matrix
\mathbf{C}:=\lambda_{\max}\mathbf{I}-{\tilde{\mathbf{A}}}^{\!\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}
, whose eigenvalues are
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{i}
, and eigenvectors are
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}
. The key observation is that
\mathbf{C}
is again symmetric positive semidefinite and, orthogonally to
\image\mathds{1}
, the largest eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}
is
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}
.

The idea now is to apply the power iteration to
\mathbf{C}
with an appropriate initial vector. For instance, we draw a vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
form the uniform distribution (or any distribution which has a density with respect to the Lebesgue measure), in which case we can write

\boldsymbol{x}_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{K^{2}}c_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i},

where, for all
i
,
c_{i}\neq 0
with probability one. We then project
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
on
(\ker\mathbf{A})^{\perp}=(\image\mathds{1})^{\perp}
,
i.e
.
,

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\frac{1}{K^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K^{2}}\boldsymbol{x}_{0}[i].

Since
(\ker\mathbf{A})^{\perp}
is the span of
(\boldsymbol{u}_{i})_{i\geq 2}
, we have

\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\sum_{i=2}^{K^{2}}c_{i}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}.

Hence

\mathbf{C}^{k}\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=c_{2}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})^{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}+\sum_{i=3}^{K^{2}-1}c_{i}(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{i})^{k}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}.

Applying the power iteration to
\mathbf{C}
with initial vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
allows to compute
\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}
. Plugging the value of
\lambda_{\max}
(computed using,
e.g
.
, power iteration) gives an estimate of
\lambda_{\min}
.

Appendix F
PnPMass and the FBS algorithm

We assume the existence of a proper convex, lower semicontinuous function
g
such that

\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau)=\prox_{\tau g}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}),

(77)

where the proximal operator is defined as

\prox_{\tau g}:\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}\mapsto\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}-\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tau g(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime\prime}).

(78)

Under this assumption, the fixed-point iteration (
31
) can be written as

\mathcal{H}_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime},\,\tau):=\prox_{\tau g}\left[\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}-\tau\nabla\!f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\,\cdot)(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})\right],

(79)

where we have considered the following data fidelity term

f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}\right\|_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}}^{2}.

(80)

The operator (
79
) is nothing but the fixed-point operator of the well-known FBS algorithm.
It consists of a forward step—gradient descent with respect to the data fidelity term
f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\,\cdot)
—and a backward step—proximal mapping with respect to the regularization term
g
. This is the reason why PnP-type algorithms with an FBS structure are usually referred to as PnP-FBS in the literature
(
25
;
5
)
.

It is well-known that if the step-size satisfies
0<\tau<2/\bigl\|\tilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\bigr\|
, then the sequence of iterates of FBS with operator (
79
) converges to a minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
of the following minimization problem—similar in form to (
8
):

\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}}f_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\tilde{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime})+g(\boldsymbol{\kappa}^{\prime}),

(81)

with the proviso that the set of minimizers is non-empty.

The FBS has been widely used for astronomical data analysis; see the comprehensive treatment in
(
33
)
. The iterative Wiener filtering algorithm
(
4
)
for mass mapping (see Sect.
2.2.1
), is also an instance of the FBS algorithm.

While some proximal operators can be seen as denoisers, the converse is clearly not true. Thus, there is no reason that a deep-learning based denoiser
\mathcal{D}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\cdot,\,\tau)
to be interpreted as a proximal operator. At best, the denoiser network can be constrained during training to be firmly non-expansive,
i.e
.
to be the resolvent of a maximally monotone set-valued operator as proposed in
(
35
)
. While this point of view brings interpretability, it is however not clear it leads to better denoising results. Consequently, PnP algorithms such as PnPMass are not expected to solve any optimization problem in general, hence the fixed-point perspective that we advocated in this paper.

It is worth recalling again (see Sect.
3.3
) that the data fidelity term defined in (
80
) does not correspond to the negative log-likelihood typically used in Bayesian frameworks: the Mahalanobis norm would be defined with respect to
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1}
rather than
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{-1/2}
. Furthermore, the regularization function
g
does not necessarily correspond to a negative log-prior; see
31
for a thorough discussion. As a result,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
should not be interpreted as a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, even if (
77
) is satisfied.
