Technical Report – A Context-Sensitive Multi-Level Similarity Framework for First-Order Logic Arguments: An Axiomatic Study
Victor David, Jérôme Delobelle, Jean-Guy Mailly

Abstract
Similarity in formal argumentation has recently gained attention due to its significance in problems such as argument aggregation in semantics and enthymeme decoding. While existing approaches focus on propositional logic, we address the richer setting of First-Order Logic (FOL), where similarity must account for structured content. We introduce a comprehensive framework for FOL argument similarity, built upon: (1) an extended axiomatic foundation; (2) a four-level parametric model covering predicates, literals, clauses, and formulae similarity; (3) two model families, one syntax-sensitive via language models, both integrating contextual weights for nuanced and explainable similarity; and (4) formal constraints enforcing desirable properties.



Introduction

Formal argumentation typically involves two components: a representation module structuring arguments and their relations in a graph (support or attack), and a reasoning module determining the acceptability of arguments via a semantics. This paradigm is now widely explored in knowledge representation and reasoning, with applications in decision-making
(
37
;
28
)
, XAI
(
13
;
20
)
, judgmental forecasting
(
23
;
19
)
, and enthymeme-based argumentation
(
9
;
16
)
.

Across these diverse applications, a common concern has emerged: the need to assess not only whether arguments exist and interact, but also how similar they are. This notion of similarity, first formalized in the propositional setting
(
2
)
, has been explored through an axiomatic lens to ensure rational behavior
(
3
;
4
)
. One key motivation is to improve
argument aggregation
in gradual semantics, where each argument receives a degree of acceptability.
For example, consider three arguments:
A_{1}=\langle\{p_{1},p_{1}\Rightarrow q\},q\rangle
,
A_{2}=\langle\{p_{2},p_{2}\Rightarrow q\},q\rangle
, and
A_{3}=\langle\{p_{3},p_{3}\Rightarrow r\},r\rangle
. While
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
support the same claim
q
,
A_{3}
supports an unrelated claim
r
. Suppose all three arguments attack a fourth argument
B=\langle\{\neg q\land\neg r\},\neg q\land\neg r\rangle
. In gradual semantics, the degree of acceptability of
B
is influenced by its attackers. If similarity is not taken into account,
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
may overly lower the acceptability of
B
due to overlapping information.
In contrast, a similarity-aware approach would recognize the overlap between
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
, down-weighting their combined effect, while treating the impact of
A_{3}
independently. This prevents redundancy, leading to more accurate evaluations.

More recently, similarity has also been used to evaluate enthymeme decoding
(
8
)
, where the goal is to reconstruct incomplete arguments. In that setting, similarity helps quantify how well the decoding preserves the original information.

While most existing approaches focus on propositional logic, only few works have considered First-Order Logic (FOL)
(
14
;
15
)
.
However, these approaches struggle with syntactic proximity, and face scalability issues due to variable instantiation. To overcome these limitations, we propose a new framework for FOL argument similarity that (i) captures syntactic sensitivity via language-model-based measures, (ii) avoids instantiation by approximating variables as constants, and (iii) supports context-sensitive weighting on symbols and clauses.

Our framework defines a four-level similarity model, from predicates to full formulae, with theoretical guarantees and high potential for NLP applications such as text similarity, or argument clustering.
With recent progress in text-to-logic translation
(
21
;
29
;
36
;
26
;
32
;
27
)
, applying our hybrid similarity models, combining logical structure with multi-level semantic evaluation, appears increasingly promising for natural language arguments, though their full potential remains an open research direction.

Logic and Arguments

First-Order Logic and Arguments

We consider a standard first-order logic (FOL) language
{\mathcal{L}}
, built from a set of predicate symbols
P,Q,\dots
and terms constructed from constants, variables, and function symbols. A formula
\phi
is built using the standard logical connectives (
\neg,\wedge,\vee,\Rightarrow,\Leftrightarrow
) and quantifiers (
\forall,\exists
).

We write
\Phi\vdash\alpha
for deduction, and denote the deductive closure of
\Phi
by
\mathtt{CN}(\Phi)=\{\alpha\mid\Phi\vdash\alpha\}
.
A set of formulae
\Phi
is inconsistent if
\Phi\vdash\bot
.
Following
(
10
)
, a FOL argument is a minimal set of formulae sufficient to deduce a claim.

Definition 1
.

A FOL
argument
is a pair
A=\langle\Phi,\alpha\rangle
s.t.
\Phi\subset{\mathcal{L}}
,
\alpha\in{\mathcal{L}}
and: (1: consistency)
\Phi\not\vdash\bot
, (2: inference)
\Phi\vdash\alpha
, and (3: minimality) no
\Phi^{\prime}\subset\Phi
satisfies
\Phi^{\prime}\vdash\alpha
.
We write
\mathtt{S}(A)=\Phi
(support),
\mathtt{C}(A)=\{\alpha\}
(claim), and denote the set of all FOL arguments by
{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
. An argument is trivial if
\mathtt{S}(A)=\emptyset
and
\mathtt{C}(A)=\top
.

As discussed in
(
2
)
,
two finite sets of formulae
\Phi,\Psi\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
are
equivalent
, i.e.,
\Phi\cong\Psi
, iff there is a bijection
{f:\Phi\rightarrow\Psi}
s.t.
\forall\phi\in\Phi
,
\phi\equiv f(\phi)
.
We use this restricted equivalence instead of
\mathtt{CN}(\Phi)=\mathtt{CN}(\Psi)
to prevent false matches from incorrect information.
For example the sets
\{\texttt{Square(a)},\linebreak\texttt{Square(a)}\Rightarrow\texttt{Rectangle(a)}\}
and
\{\texttt{Rectangle(a)},\linebreak\texttt{Rectangle(a)}\Rightarrow\texttt{Square(a)}\}
should not be equivalent.

We use pairwise equivalence between formula sets to define a bijective equivalence between FOL arguments.

Definition 2
.

Two FOL arguments
A,B\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
are
bijectively equivalent
, denoted
A\approx B
, iff there exist bijections:
(1)
f:\mathtt{S}(A)\rightarrow\mathtt{S}(B)
with
\forall\phi\in\mathtt{S}(A),~\phi\equiv f(\phi)
; (2)
f^{\prime}:\mathtt{C}(A)\rightarrow\mathtt{C}(B)
with
\forall\psi\in\mathtt{C}(A),~\psi\equiv f^{\prime}(\psi)
.

CNF Transformation

Throughout the paper, we assume that
{\mathcal{L}}
is a first-order language over a finite signature, i.e., with a finite set of constants, functions, and predicates. This restriction ensures that the space of CNF formulae remains bounded and comparable, which is reasonable in our context, as formulae originate from natural language with a limited vocabulary.

A formula
\phi
is in
conjunctive normal form
(CNF) if it is a conjunction of clauses
\phi=\bigwedge_{i}C_{i}
, each
C_{i}
being a disjunction of literals.
Following
(
5
)
, we map each formula
\phi\in{\mathcal{L}}
to a compiled CNF formula
{\mathtt{CNF}}(\phi)\in{\mathcal{F}}\subset{\mathcal{L}}
, where
{\mathcal{F}}
is a fixed finite sublanguage containing only quantifier-free formulae in conjunctive normal form (CNF), possibly obtained after Skolemization. The CNF transformation involves standard steps: eliminating implications and biconditionals, pushing negations inward (De Morgan), standardizing variables, applying Skolemization to eliminate existential quantifiers with Skolem functions or constants, dropping universal quantifiers, distributing disjunctions over conjunctions, and applying deterministic syntactic normalization (e.g., ordering literals and clauses).

Example 1
.

Consider the sentence:

“Every dog loves some bone.”

Its FOL form is:

\forall x.~\texttt{Dog}(x)\Rightarrow\exists y.~(\texttt{Bone}(y)\wedge\texttt{Loves}(x,y))
.
CNF compilation yields:

\{\neg\texttt{Dog}(x)\vee\texttt{Bone}(f(x)),\ \neg\texttt{Dog}(x)\vee\texttt{Loves}(x,f(x))\}
.

This transformation is not unique, as CNF representations may vary with normalization or Skolem naming.
We do not fix a strategy, but assume that one method
{\mathtt{CNF}}
is chosen.

Important note:

Although
{\mathtt{CNF}}(\phi)
is not always logically equivalent to
\phi
, since Skolemization preserves only satisfiability, it provides a canonical approximation, well-suited for structural comparison in our similarity framework.

This approximation is a deliberate and practical choice. Skolemization handles quantifier-heavy formulae by replacing existential quantifiers with terms. This is useful in natural language settings, where grounding is infeasible due to vast or undefined domains (e.g., all people, all locations), avoiding the combinatorial explosion of full instantiation.

By contrast, the Order-Sorted (OS) FOL framework proposed in
(
14
)
preserves equivalence more precisely, but becomes impractical in large or open domains. Our approximation thus favors scalability and operational feasibility, while preserving sufficient structure for fine-grained similarity comparison.

Definition 3
.

For a finite set of formulae
\Phi\subseteq{\mathcal{L}}
, the
CNF compiled set
is:

{\sqcup}(\Phi)=\bigcup_{\phi\in\Phi}{\mathtt{CNF}}(\phi)
,
with variable renaming to avoid clashes.

We extend the classical FOL argument definition to a CNF-based form, where all components are in CNF and the claim is a set of clauses. While semantically equivalent, this structure enables finer analysis, such as minimality.

Definition 4
.

A
CNF argument
is a pair
A=\langle\Phi,\Psi\rangle
such that
\Phi\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
,
\Psi\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
and:
(1)
\Phi\not\vdash\bot
, (2)
\mathtt{CN}(\Psi)\subseteq\mathtt{CN}(\Phi)
, and (3) no
\Phi^{\prime}\subset\Phi
satisfies
\Phi^{\prime}\vdash\Psi
.
We denote the universe of all CNF arguments by
{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
(included in
{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
).

We now define the compiled version of an argument.

Definition 5
.

A
compiled argument

A^{c}=\langle{\sqcup}(\Phi),{\sqcup}(\alpha)\rangle
is obtained by compiling both the support and the claim of
A=\langle\Phi,\alpha\rangle\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
.

Some arguments in
{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
may compile into forms that do not satisfy the constraint (3) of a CNF argument. For example,
A=\langle\{P(a)\wedge P(b)\},P(a)\rangle\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
, but
A^{\mathtt{c}}=\langle\{P(a),P(b)\},\{P(a)\}\rangle\notin{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
. As shown in
(
5
)
, clausal argument ensures minimality.

Multi-Level Arguments Similarity

A similarity measure indicates to what extent two objects (e.g., predicates, clauses, or arguments) share common features, such as semantic meaning, or syntactic structure.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathbb{X}
be a set of objects. A
similarity measure
on
\mathbb{X}
is a function
\mathtt{simX}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
, with
1
for maximum similarity and
0
for none.

We propose a multi-level similarity model for CNF formulae in FOL, structured around four levels of abstraction:
Predicate and term similarity (
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}
), forming the foundation;
Literal similarity (
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}
), which depends on
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}
;
Clause similarity (
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}
), which depends on
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}
;
Set-of-clauses similarity (
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}
), which depends on
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}
.
All these levels are instantiated in the following sections.
Each level builds on the one below, enabling structured and explainable computations.
We call any tuple
{\mathbf{M}}=\langle\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}\rangle
a
similarity model
.

Similarity between two CNF arguments is computed separately on premises and claims using our multi-level method, then aggregated with weights reflecting their importance, as in
(
2
)
.

Definition 7
.

Let a factor
\eta\in(0,1)
, and a similarity model
{\mathbf{M}}=\langle\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}\rangle
.
Let
A,B\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
,
we define the
similarity measure between FOL arguments
from
{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})\times{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
to
[0,1]
by
\mathtt{sim}\mathtt{Arg}^{{\mathbf{M}}}_{\eta}(A,B)=

\eta\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}}))~+

(1-\eta)\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}})).

Axiomatic Foundations

Principles that argument similarity measures should satisfy have been discussed in
(
2
;
1
)
.
Some of the principles
can be stated exactly as in
(
5
)
, since they do not concern the internal structure of the arguments.

Principle 1
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Maximality
iff

\forall A\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
,
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,A)=1
.

Principle 2
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Substitution
iff

\forall A,B,C\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
, if
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,B)=1
then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)=\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
.

Principle 3
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Symmetry
iff

\forall A,B\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
,
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,B)=\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,A)
.

This list includes desirable, but not always required, principles. For instance, some argue that symmetry is not essential for similarity
(
35
;
25
)
, and
(
14
)
explores non-symmetric measures for OS-FOL arguments.

For content-related principles, we propose the following adaptations in the FOL setting using a similarity model.

The principle Syntax Independence states that similarity should ignore syntactic variation and rely only on structure.

Definition 8
.

Let
A=\langle\Phi,\alpha\rangle,B=\langle\Phi^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}\rangle\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
.
Let
\pi
be a bijective renaming function over predicate names and term symbols (constants and variables), extended homomorphically to formulae and sets of formulae.
We say that
A
and
B
are
isomorphic
, if:

\pi(\Phi)=\Phi^{\prime}\text{ and }\pi(\alpha)=\alpha^{\prime}
.

Principle 4
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Syntax Independence
iff for any bijective renaming function
\pi
,

\forall A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
, s.t.
A
(resp.
B
) and
A^{\prime}
(resp.
B^{\prime}
) are isomorphic wrt
\pi
, it holds that
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,B)=\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
.

Unlike prior work in propositional logic
(
2
)
, where principles were defined over syntactic structures, we reconsider the need for Syntax Independence. In our framework, predicate and constant names carry importance, making similarity intentionally syntax-dependent.
This choice is motivated by practical needs: in real applications, semantically related symbols (e.g.,
house
and
apartment
) should be closer than unrelated ones (e.g.,
house
and
banana
), despite differing syntax.

In the following content-sensitive principles, we focus on compiled arguments which have no irrelevant information, ensuring reliable similarity evaluation. We now reformulate the Minimality and Non-Zero principles, which ensure that overall argument similarity arises directly from predicate and term-level similarity.
Minimality states that no argument similarity should arise without such base-level similarity.

The first condition excludes the case where both arguments have an empty support and so no intersection to compare, while the second and third conditions ensure that no predicate and term similarity appears in the supports or claims of the arguments, respectively.

Principle 5
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Minimality
iff

\forall A,B\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
s.t.
A^{\mathtt{c}},B^{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
, if:

1.

A^{\mathtt{c}}
and
B^{\mathtt{c}}
are not trivial,

2.

\forall C_{1}\in\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\forall(P_{1},\vec{a_{1}})\in C_{1},\forall a_{1}^{i}\in\vec{a_{1}},\forall C_{2}\in\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\forall(P_{2},\vec{a_{2}})\in C_{2},\forall a_{2}^{j}\in\vec{a_{2}}
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P_{1},P_{2})=0
and
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a^{i}_{1},a^{j}_{2})=0
,

3.

\forall C_{1}\in\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\forall(P_{1},\vec{a_{1}})\in C_{1},\forall a_{1}^{i}\in\vec{a_{1}},\forall C_{2}\in\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\forall(P_{2},\vec{a_{2}})\in C_{2},\forall a_{2}^{j}\in\vec{a_{2}}
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P_{1},P_{2})=0
and
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a^{i}_{1},a^{j}_{2})=0
,

then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,B)=0
.

Conversely, Non-Zero
(
17
)
ensures that if similarity exists in the supports (here at the predicate and term level), it must propagate and result in some degree of similarity between arguments.

Principle 6
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Non-Zero
iff

\forall A,B\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
s.t.
A^{\mathtt{c}},B^{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
, if:

1.

A^{\mathtt{c}}
and
B^{\mathtt{c}}
are not trivial,

2.

\exists C_{1}\in\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}})
and
\exists C_{2}\in\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}})
s.t.
\exists(P_{1},\vec{a_{1}})\in C_{1}
and
\exists(P_{2},\vec{a_{2}})\in C_{2}
s.t. either
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P_{1},P_{2})>0
or there exists a position
i
, where
a^{i}_{1}\in\vec{a_{1}}
and
a^{i}_{2}\in\vec{a_{2}}
s.t.
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a^{i}_{1},a^{i}_{2})>0
,

then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,B)>0
.

In the following principles, we aim to study the impact of similarity between arguments at the intermediate level between clauses. More specifically, we focus on how the aggregation of clause-level similarity behaves.

We define next Monotony principles to capture how adding new information to one argument affects its similarity to a third. S-Monotony (S for support) generalizes classic Monotony, while C-Monotony (C for claim) extends the Dominance principle
(
2
)
.

The first condition, defines that argument
B
has the same support (resp. claim) as argument
A
, with one additional clause. Condition 2 ensures that the similarity over the remaining component (claim or support) is equal, allowing us to isolate the effect of similarity on a single component of the argument.
We then examine extreme cases: adding a clause with no similarity (score 0) must not increase the similarity between arguments, while adding a clause with full similarity (score 1) must not decrease it.

Principle 7
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Monotony
iff
\forall A,B,C\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
such that
A^{\mathtt{c}},B^{\mathtt{c}},C^{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
, if:

1.

\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}})=\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}})\cup\{\beta\}
(where
\beta
is a new clause),

2.

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))=\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))
; and either

\bullet
(
S-Monotony
0
) if
\forall\alpha\in\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\beta)=0
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\geq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
; or

\bullet
(
S-Monotony
1
) if
\exists\alpha\in\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
s.t.
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\beta)=1
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\leq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
.

1.

\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}})=\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}})\cup\{\beta\}
(where
\beta
is a new clause),

2.

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))=\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))
; and either

\bullet
(
C-Monotony
0
) if
\forall\alpha\in\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\beta)=0
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\geq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
; or

\bullet
(
C-Monotony
1
) if
\exists\alpha\in\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
s.t.
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\beta)=1
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\leq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
.

We propose also a new family of principles, Reinforcement, that formalize and constrain the impact of distinct pieces of information between two arguments that are otherwise similar.
Conditions 1 and 2 ensure that
A
and
B
differ by exactly one clause, and Condition 3 focuses on a single component (support or claim). We define two versions of the Reinforcement principle. In the first (Reinforcement
≥
), if, for every clause in a component of
C
, the differing clause of
A
is at least as similar to it as that of
B
, then
A
must be at least as similar to
C
as
B
is. In the second (Reinforcement
>
), if the differing clause of
A
is strictly more similar to every clause in
C
than that of
B
, then
A
must be strictly more similar to
C
than
B
is.

Principle 8
.

A similarity measure
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}
satisfies
Reinforcement
iff
\forall A,B,C\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{L}})
such that
A^{\mathtt{c}},B^{\mathtt{c}},C^{\mathtt{c}}\in{\mathtt{Arg}}({\mathcal{F}})
, if:

1.

\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}})\setminus\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}})=\{\alpha\}
,

2.

\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}})\setminus\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}})=\{\beta\}
,

3.

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))=\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))
; and either

\bullet
(
S-Reinforcement
≥
) if
\forall\phi\in\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\phi)\geq\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\beta,\phi)
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\geq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
; or

\bullet
(
S-Reinforcement
>
) if
\forall\alpha\in\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\phi)>\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\beta,\phi)
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)>\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
.

1.

\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}})\setminus\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}})=\{\alpha\}
,

2.

\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}})\setminus\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}})=\{\beta\}
,

3.

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))=\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(C^{\mathtt{c}}))
; and either

\bullet
(
C-Reinforcement
≥
) if
\forall\phi\in\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\phi)\geq\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\beta,\phi)
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)\geq\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
; or

\bullet
(
C-Reinforcement
>
) if
\forall\alpha\in\mathtt{C}(C^{\mathtt{c}})
,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\alpha,\phi)>\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(\beta,\phi)
, then
\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(A,C)>\mathtt{sim}{\mathtt{Arg}}(B,C)
.

Context-Sensitive Multi-Level Similarity

Motivated by practical applications involving natural language argumentation, we propose a three-stage pipeline that bridges symbolic representation, contextual weighting, and structured similarity computation:

(I) Logical Transformation.
This step maps input texts to CNF-FOL representations, making argument structure explicit and enabling comparison over predicates, terms, and clauses.
We use ChatGPT-4o for CNF generation, but automating and evaluating this step is left for future work.

(II) Weight Assignment.

This stage assigns contextual weights to logical components, allowing the model to prioritize informative elements for similarity.
For instance, action predicates (e.g.,
Tease
) may matter more than descriptive ones (e.g.,
HasColor
), and agent constants (e.g.,
dog
) more than peripheral details (e.g.,
brown
).
Weights are manually set in our examples; automating their learning is left for future work.

(III) Similarity Computation.
The similarity between two CNF arguments is computed in four steps:

1. Unweighted Clause Alignment:

Each clause from a premise or claim is matched to its best semantic match in the other argument, using a flat literal similarity measure.

2. Weighted Clause Similarity:
Clause-pair scores are refined using the importance weights assigned to predicates, terms, and clauses, producing a context-aware similarity.

3. Formulae Similarity:
Clause similarities are aggregated into formulae-level scores using an aggregation.

4. Argument Similarity:
Computed from the aggregated formulae scores (see Definition
7
).

This pipeline combines scalable learning-based models with symbolic representations to compute explainable, context- and syntax-aware similarity scores, offering theoretical guarantees and adaptability to natural language.

Like argument equivalence (Definition
2
), based on local matchings between formulae, our similarity model compares argument components by identifying best-matching clause pairs. To apply this idea, we first use flat clause similarity to select matches based on intrinsic similarity. Then, importance weights refine these matchings at two levels: predicate and term weights adjust literal-level similarity, while clause weights influence formulae-level similarity, integrating contextual relevance into the overall similarity.

Step 1: Unweighted Best Clause Matching

To better capture the semantic contribution of polarity, we incorporate it directly into the predicate symbol. For instance, the literal
\neg\texttt{P}(a,b)
is rewritten as
\texttt{notP}(a,b)
. This transformation allows the similarity function to distinguish between positive and negative predicate forms, rather than treating polarity as a separate feature. It also enables the identification of logical correspondences between concepts with opposite polarity, such as
\texttt{notEven}(x)
and
\texttt{Odd}(x)
, which may convey equivalent meanings.

In the examples, we will use SBERT
(
31
)
(
all-MiniLM-L6-v2
) to compute semantic similarity between predicates (with polarity) and parameters, denoted by
\mathtt{simP}^{\text{SBRT}}
. While newer models (e.g.,
(
22
)
) offer slight improvements, SBERT provides strong performance with simpler experimentation.
If syntax independence is desired, we could use
\mathtt{simP}^{eq}(t_{1},t_{2})
, which returns
1
if
t_{1}=t_{2}
, and
0
otherwise.

Definition 9
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the
flat literal similarity measure
between two literals
(P,\vec{a})
,
(Q,\vec{b})
is defined as:

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(Q,\vec{b}))=\frac{1}{2}(\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,Q)+\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b}))

The parameters similarity
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b})
combines two complementary strategies:

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\lambda\times\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})+(1-\lambda)\times\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})

where
\lambda
is a weighting parameter that controls the trade-off between order-sensitive and order-invariant elements.

•

Ordered similarity
:
Compares terms positionally, assuming meaningful arity and order:

———

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathtt{min}(|\vec{a}|,|\vec{b}|)}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a_{i},b_{i})}{\mathtt{min}(|\vec{a}|,|\vec{b}|)}

•

Best-match (unordered) similarity
: Matches each term to its most similar counterpart in the other vector:

—

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\frac{\sum\limits_{a_{i}\in\vec{a}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a_{i},\text{B}_{a_{i}}^{\vec{b}})+\sum\limits_{b_{j}\in\vec{b}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(b_{j},\text{B}_{b_{j}}^{\vec{a}})}{|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|}

where
\text{B}_{x}^{\vec{y}}
is the best-matching element of the vector
\vec{y}
for a given term
x
, based on a similarity measure
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}
:

—————

\text{B}_{x}^{\vec{y}}=\mathtt{argmax}_{y\in\vec{y}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(x,y)

Definition 10
.

Let
\mathbb{X}

\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim}\mathbb{X}}(x,Y)=g_{y\in Y}(\mathtt{sim}\mathbb{X}(x,y))

Definition 11
.

Let two clauses

and
C_{2}
,
\alpha,\beta\in[0,+\infty)
and
\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}
a membership function on literals. The

similarity measure is defined as follows:

—–

\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\beta,\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\dfrac{A}{A+\alpha B+\beta C},~\text{where:}

—–

A=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{x\in C_{1}}\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2})+\sum_{y\in C_{2}}\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(y,C_{1})\right)

—–

B=\sum_{x\in C_{1}}\left(1-\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2})\right)

—–

C=\sum_{y\in C_{2}}\left(1-\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(y,C_{1})\right)

(

Step 2: Weighted Clause-Level Similarity

We now introduce weights to reflect the contextual importance of each predicate and term.
We assume they are normalized to sum to 1, enabling similarity to be computed as a relative, interpretable aggregation, analogous to an attention distribution.
In the following, we use
w_{p}
to denote both predicate weights and term (parameter) weights.

Definition 13
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the
weighted literal similarity measure
between two literals
(P,\vec{a})
,
(Q,\vec{b})
is defined as:

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{weight},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(Q,\vec{b}))=

\frac{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(Q)\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,Q)~+~\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b})\times(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(Q)~+~(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}

where

w_{\text{ord}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathtt{min}(|\vec{a}|,|\vec{b}|)}w_{p}(a_{i})w_{p}(b_{i})
and
w_{\text{unord}}=\sum_{a_{i}\in\vec{a}}w_{p}(a_{i})\,w_{p}(\text{B}_{a_{i}}^{\vec{b}})+\!\sum_{b_{j}\in\vec{b}}w_{p}(b_{j})\,w_{p}(\text{B}_{b_{j}}^{\vec{a}})
.

This formulation emphasizes salient elements by using the contextual importance weights of the predicates, i.e.,
w_{p}(P)w_{p}(Q)
, and those of the parameters,
w_{\text{ord}}
and
w_{\text{unord}}
, which reflect the computation of
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}
and
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}
.

Step 3: Formulae-Level Similarity

Let
w_{c}(C)
be the contextual weight of clause
C
, with weights normalized over a set of formulae
\Phi
(e.g., premises or claim), such that
\sum_{C\in\Phi}w_{c}(C)=1
.

Definition 14
.

The
weighted average best match similarity measure
between two sets of clauses
\Phi
,
\Psi
is defined as:

—

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{\sum\limits_{(C_{1},C_{2})\in\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{2})}{\sum\limits_{(C_{1},C_{2})\in\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})}

where
\mathtt{bm}
relies on the flat clause similarity
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}
to define the best matching clause pairs, i.e.,
\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)=

\left\{\begin{aligned} (C_{1},C_{2})\mid\,&\big(C_{1}\in\Phi,\,C_{2}=\mathtt{argmax}_{C^{\prime}\in\Psi}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})\big)\\
\text{or }&\big(C_{1}\in\Psi,\,C_{2}=\mathtt{argmax}_{C^{\prime}\in\Phi}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})\big)\end{aligned}\right\}

and where
w_{g}
denotes the
contribution weight
of a clause pair, computed using an aggregation function
g
:

————–

w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})=g(w_{c}(C_{1}),w_{c}(C_{2})).

In practice,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}
is instantiated as the weighted clause similarity defined by a model, using a specific
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}
and literal similarity
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{weight},\lambda}
.
The flat version
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}
uses the same
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}
and
\lambda
with
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}
to ensure consistency.

To compute

The

Hence:

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}_{\mathtt{dic}}(\text{CNF\_T1},\text{CNF\_T2})=\frac{1.056+0.2}{2\times 0.9+4\times 0.05}\simeq 0.628.

The contribution weight of a clause pair reflects its importance to the overall formulae similarity, independently of its similarity score. In Figure

Conclusion and Future Work

We introduce a new framework for measuring similarity between FOL arguments, based on a model with four levels: predicates/terms, literals, clauses, and formulae.
Our main contributions are:
1. An extended axiomatic foundation providing theoretical guarantees for similarity computations;
2. A structured, parametric model that leverages the internal FOL structure to enhance the understanding of argument similarity;
3. Two model families, one syntax-sensitive (via language models) and one syntax-independent, both integrating contextual weights that reflect the relative importance perceived by humans; and
4. Formal constraints that guide the design of similarity measures, ensuring they satisfy desirable properties.
This work presents the first hybrid framework for argument similarity, combining FOL-CNF structure, language model, and contextual weighting to jointly capture both structural and semantic aspects.
The resulting model is theoretically grounded and shows strong potential for applications in natural language argumentation.

Our framework opens several research directions. On the theoretical side, we plan to explore non-symmetric similarity measures, where the extended fuzzy Tversky measure offers a natural foundation for this. We also aim to define layer-specific principles to guide the definition of similarity functions at each layer, extending our axiomatic work and enhancing modular interpretability.
On the empirical side, we will evaluate the framework on textual similarity benchmarks and analyze the impact of different parameter choices. Finally, we will investigate how contextual weight calibration affects score sensitivity and interpretability.

, formally for any literals

\pi(l_{b})=l_{b}^{\prime}

To prove that the principle is satisfied at the level of arguments, we simply need to apply Definition
7

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(P,\vec{a}))=1

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(Q,\vec{b}))=\frac{1}{2}(\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,Q)+\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b}))

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\lambda\times\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})+(1-\lambda)\times\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})

with
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^{\mathtt{min}(|\vec{a}|,|\vec{b}|)}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a_{i},b_{i})}{\mathtt{min}(|\vec{a}|,|\vec{b}|)}

and

\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}(\vec{a},\vec{b})=\frac{\sum\limits_{a_{i}\in\vec{a}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(a_{i},\text{B}_{a_{i}}^{\vec{b}})+\sum\limits_{b_{j}\in\vec{b}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(b_{j},\text{B}_{b_{j}}^{\vec{a}})}{|\vec{a}|+|\vec{b}|}

where
\text{B}_{x}^{\vec{y}}=\mathtt{argmax}_{y\in\vec{y}}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(x,y)
.

For our purpose, we focus on

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(P,\vec{a}))=\frac{1}{2}(\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,P)+\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{a}))
. We know that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(t,t)=1
for any
t
, so
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,P)=1
, and for the same reason, we can simply deduce that
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{a})=1
(because both fractions involved in the computation of
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{ord}}^{\text{}}
and
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{unord}}^{\text{}}
reduce to
1
). We obtain
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{flat},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(P,\vec{a}))=1
.
∎

Lemma 2
.

Let
(P,\vec{a})
be a literal.
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{weight},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(P,\vec{a})=1
.

Proof.

Let us recall how to compute the weighted similarity between literals:

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{weight},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(Q,\vec{b}))=

\frac{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(Q)\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,Q)~+~\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{b})\times(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(Q)~+~(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}

We have already established (proof of Lemma
1
) that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,P)=1
and
\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{a})=1
, so it is easy to see that

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}^{\text{weight},\lambda}((P,\vec{a}),(P,\vec{a}))\\
\\
=\frac{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(P)\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}(P,P)+\mathtt{sim}_{\text{para}}^{\text{}\lambda}(\vec{a},\vec{a})\times(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(P)+(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}\\
\\
=\frac{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(P)+(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}{w_{p}(P)w_{p}(P)+(\lambda w_{\text{ord}}+(1-\lambda)w_{\text{unord}})}=1\end{array}

which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3
.

For any
\alpha\in[0,+\infty)
,
\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{2})=1
if and only if
C_{1}=C_{2}
.

Proof.

We recall that

\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{2})=\dfrac{A}{A+\alpha B+\alpha C},~\text{where:}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{x\in C_{1}}\oplus^{g}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2})+\sum_{y\in C_{2}}\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(y,C_{1})\right)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in C_{1}}\left(1-\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2})\right)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in C_{2}}\left(1-\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(y,C_{1})\right)

Now, let us first assume that
C_{1}=C_{2}
. We need to consider two cases: either
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}
is a flat literal similarity measure or it is a weighted literal similarity measure. We start with the former.

If
l\in C
, then obviously
\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(l,C)=1
because the flat literal similarity between a literal and itself is equal to
1
(Lemma
1
). So both
B
and
C
simplify to
0
, and thus
\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{1})=\frac{A}{A}=1
.

Now, consider the case where
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}
is a weighted similarity measure. Reasoning is similar to the case of flat literal similarity, and from Lemma
2
we can deduce that
\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(l,C)=1
when
l\in C
, which allows to simplify the computation of
B
and
C
to obtain
0
, and finally
\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{1})=\frac{A}{A}=1
.

Now, let us assume that
\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{2})=1
. Reasoning towards a contradiction, assume that
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
. Without loss of generality, we consider that there is a literal
l\in C_{1}
such that
l\not\in C_{2}
. This means that
B=\sum_{x\in C_{1}}(1-\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2}))>0
(because
\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}(x,C_{2}))<1
). This implies that
A+\alpha B+\alpha C>A
(even if
C=0
), and thus
\text{Tve}^{\alpha,\alpha,\oplus^{\mathtt{max}}_{\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}}}}(C_{1},C_{2})<1
, which is a contradiction, so we deduce that
C_{1}=C_{2}
. This reasoning holds for both the flat and weighted literal similarities, so this concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}\in\{{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{sb,\lambda},{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}\}
be a similarity model, and
\Phi
be a set of clauses.
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\Phi,\Phi)=1
holds.

Proof.

Recall how similarity is computed. For the similarity models considered,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}
is used to compare sets of clauses:

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Psi)=\frac{\sum\limits_{(C_{1},C_{2})\in\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{2})}{\sum\limits_{(C_{1},C_{2})\in\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})}

where
\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)=

\left\{\begin{aligned} (C_{1},C_{2})\mid\,&\big(C_{1}\in\Phi,\,C_{2}=\mathtt{argmax}_{C^{\prime}\in\Phi}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})\big)\\
\text{or }&\big(C_{1}\in\Psi,\,C_{2}=\mathtt{argmax}_{C^{\prime}\in\Phi}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})\big)\end{aligned}\right\}

w_{g}(C_{1},C_{2})=g(w_{c}(C_{1}),w_{c}(C_{2}))
with
g
an aggregation function. Let us study what happens when
\Phi=\Psi
. First of all,
\mathtt{bm}
simplifies into
\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Phi)=\{(C_{1},C_{2})\mid C_{1}\in\Phi,\,C_{2}=\mathtt{argmax}_{C^{\prime}\in\Phi}\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})\}
. Since
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}
is a symmetric fuzzy Tversky similarity measure (Definition
12
),
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}^{\text{flat}}(C_{1},C^{\prime})=1
if and only if
C_{1}=C^{\prime}
(Lemma
3
). So, the simplification of
\mathtt{bm}
continues:
\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Phi)=\{(C_{1},C_{1})\mid C_{1}\in\Phi\}
, and then

\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Phi)=\frac{\sum\limits_{C_{1}\in\Phi}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{1})\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{1})}{\sum\limits_{C_{1}\in\Phi}w_{g}(C_{1},C_{1})}

and again,
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}
is a fuzzy Tversky similarity measure (Definition
12
) so
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{1})=1
, thus
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Phi)=1
.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}
be a similarity model, and
\Phi,\Psi
be two sets of clauses. If
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\Phi,\Psi)=1
then
\Phi=\Psi
.

Proof.

We assume that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\Psi,\Psi)=\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Psi)=1
. Observing the fraction that defines
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}
, this implies that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{2})=1
for all
(C_{1},C_{2})\in\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)
.

Reasoning towards a contradiction, we assume that
\Phi\neq\Psi
,
i.e.
(without loss of generality) there is a clause
C_{1}\in\Phi
such that
C_{1}\not\in\Psi
. In the computation of
\mathtt{bm}(\Phi,\Psi)
, there is thus a pair of clauses
(C_{1},C_{2})
such that
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
, which implies (from Lemma
3
) that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}}(C_{1},C_{2})<1
. This leads to
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}^{\mathtt{bm}}(\Phi,\Psi)\neq 1
, hence a contradiction, and this concludes the proof.
∎

Proof (Proposition
1
)
.

We know that
{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}=\langle\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{P}}}^{eq},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{L}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{C}}},\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}\rangle
satisfies all principles except Non-Zero, since, having no similarity elements can lead to a global similarity of
0
, even if there is some partial local similarity, due to importance weight focusing on the dissimilarity.
However, consider a slightly modified model
{\mathbf{M}}_{NZ}
, identical to
{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}
in all respects, but with the constraint that all local similarities must be strictly positive: i.e., for all object pairs considered at any level, if
\mathtt{sim}(x_{i},y_{j})>0
, then they are assigned non-zero weights, and all weights over the domain of comparison are strictly positive.
Under this construction, any local similarity value greater than
0
will necessarily propagate a strictly positive contribution through the weighted aggregation steps. Thus, the global similarity will also be greater than
0
, satisfying the Non-Zero principle.
Consequently, the modified model
{\mathbf{M}}_{NZ}
satisfies all the axioms simultaneously, proving their compatibility.
∎

Proof (Theorem
10
)
.

We start by considering both families of similarity models
{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{\rho,\lambda}
with
\rho\in\{sb,eq\}
. Let
A,B
be two arguments such that
A\approx B
. Recall that this means there are two bijections
f,f^{\prime}
(respectively between the supports of
A
and
B
, and between the claims of
A
and
B
) such that
\forall\phi\in\mathtt{S}(A)
,
\phi\equiv f(\phi)
, and
\forall\psi\in\mathtt{C}(A)
,
\psi\equiv f^{\prime}(\psi)
. This means that, when the supports (respectively claims) of
A
and
B
are compiled into CNF, we obtain exactly the same set of clauses. This means that
\mathtt{sim}\mathtt{Arg}^{{\mathbf{M}}}_{\eta}(A,B)=

\eta\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}))+(1-\eta)\times\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}})).

From Lemma
4
, we know that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}))=1
and
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}))=1
, so
\mathtt{sim}\mathtt{Arg}^{{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{\rho,\lambda}}_{\eta}(A,B)=1
for
\rho\in\{sb,eq\}
.

Now, we assume that
\mathtt{sim}\mathtt{Arg}^{{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}}_{\eta}(A,B)=1
. From Definition
7
, we deduce that
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}}))=1
and
\mathtt{sim{\mathtt{S}}}(\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}}),\mathtt{C}(V^{\mathtt{c}}))=1
. With the similarity model
{\mathbf{M}}^{x,w_{g}}_{eq,\lambda}
, the only way to obtain a similarity of
1
between two sets of clauses is if these sets of identical (Lemma
5
), which implies that
\mathtt{S}(A^{\mathtt{c}})=\mathtt{S}(B^{\mathtt{c}})
and
\mathtt{C}(A^{\mathtt{c}})=\mathtt{C}(B^{\mathtt{c}})
, and thus
A\approx B
, which concludes the proof.
∎
