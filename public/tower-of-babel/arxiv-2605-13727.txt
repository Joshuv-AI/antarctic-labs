Stochastic evolution equations driven by arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes
Gergely Bodó, Sonja Cox, Adam Jakubowski, Markus Riedle

Abstract
We establish the first existence and uniqueness result for mild solutions of abstract stochastic evolution equations driven by arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes in Hilbert spaces. The coefficients are assumed to satisfy global Lipschitz conditions, and no moment assumptions are imposed on the driving noise. The principal difficulty arises from the fact that cylindrical Lévy processes exist solely in a generalised sense and typically admit no semimartingale or Lévy–Itô decomposition, which precludes the use of classical existence methods. To overcome these obstacles, we develop a pathwise adaptive Euler–Peano approximation scheme based on noise-dependent stopping times and a fixed-point formulation of the mild solution operator. The resulting approach avoids stochastic calculus techniques relying on semimartingale decompositions and provides a robust and flexible framework for treating multiplicative cylindrical Lévy noise in infinite-dimensional systems.

60H15, 60G20, 47D06, 60G51

cylindrical Lévy processes, stochastic partial differential equations, mild solutions, adaptive Euler–Peano approximation

Introduction

Stochastic evolution equations in infinite-dimensional spaces arise naturally in the modelling of complex systems, such as fluid dynamics, quantum fields, and spatially extended phenomena. Random perturbations in these settings are often highly irregular and cannot be represented by genuine processes taking values in the state space. Cylindrical Lévy processes provide a natural framework for such noises: they generalise cylindrical Brownian motion to incorporate jumps and heavy tails, offering a rich class of driving signals for infinite-dimensional dynamics.

However, two intrinsic features of cylindrical Lévy processes make the analysis of stochastic evolution equations particularly challenging. First, these processes exist only in a generalised sense and do not take values in the underlying space. Second, they typically lack a semimartingale or Lévy–Itô decomposition, which rules out classical stochastic calculus techniques. As a result, standard approaches—such as fixed-point arguments, Galerkin approximations, and variational methods—cannot be applied directly. In the multiplicative-noise setting, even embedding the equation into a larger space to recover a genuine Lévy process fails, a strategy that works for additive noise.

In this paper, we establish the first existence and uniqueness result for an abstract stochastic evolution equation driven by an arbitrary cylindrical Lévy process, under global Lipschitz assumptions on the coefficients and without imposing moment conditions on the noise. This result goes beyond previous work, which has been restricted to additive noise, such as Brzeźniak and Zabczyk
[
6
]
, and Priola and Zabczyk
[
19
]
, or to specific classes of the driving noise such as symmetric
\alpha
-stable cylindrical processes in Bodó, Týbl, and Riedle
[
5
]
, and Kosmala and Riedle
[
15
]
.

A first systematic study of cylindrical Lévy processes in Hilbert and Banach spaces was carried out by Applebaum and Riedle in
[
2
]
. A general integration theory with respect to arbitrary cylindrical Lévy processes was subsequently developed by Jakubowski and Riedle
[
12
]
, and later refined in joint work of Bodó and Riedle
[
4
]
. Both contributions are based on the decoupling method introduced by Kwapień and Woyczyński
[
16
]
. Our approach in the current work makes essential use of this integration theory and requires several additional technical refinements, which are developed in the paper.

Our existence and uniqueness result is obtained via an adaptive Euler–Peano approximation scheme specifically designed for equations driven by cylindrical Lévy noise. The method is formulated in terms of the mild solution operator associated with the equation and can be viewed as a pathwise fixed-point approximation. Starting from the initial condition, the approximation is updated only at noise-dependent stopping times determined by the first exit of the current iterate from a prescribed neighbourhood of its image under the mild solution map. Between successive update times the approximation is frozen, while at each update it is reset according to the evolution prescribed by the equation. This construction, which goes back in spirit to the Euler–Peano approach described by Bichteler
[
3
, Chapter 5.4]
and is inspired by the pathwise solution theory for classical stochastic differential equations developed by Lowther
[
17
]
, avoids any reliance on semimartingale or Lévy–Itô decompositions and is therefore well suited to the cylindrical setting.
From a deterministic viewpoint, the resulting scheme can be seen as an infinite-dimensional analogue of the Peano–Carathéodory construction, or equivalently of Amann’s notion of
\epsilon
-approximate solutions for ordinary differential equations; see, for example,
[
1
, Chapter II.7]
.

A key ingredient of our analysis is a stochastic Grönwall-type inequality due to Lowther
[
17
]
. For completeness, we include a proof of this result in Proposition
4.4
. While several variants of stochastic Grönwall lemmas are available in the literature—see, for instance, the recent work
[
9
]
and the references therein—their application typically relies on an explicit identification of the martingale and finite-variation components of the underlying stochastic integrals. This step is particularly delicate in the present setting, since cylindrical integrators generally do not admit a tractable semimartingale decomposition. In particular, a careful analysis of their jump structure is required, as carried out in
[
5
]
.

The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 introduces the functional-analytic and probabilistic framework, including the space of adapted càdlàg processes, the metrics used in the analysis, and preparatory continuity results in an Émery-type topology. In Section 3, we recall the construction of stochastic integrals with respect to cylindrical Lévy processes and establish auxiliary results, such as dominated convergence statements and quadratic variation identities in the cylindrical setting. Section 4 is devoted to abstract stochastic evolution equations driven by cylindrical Lévy noise: we introduce the notion of a mild solution, develop a pathwise adaptive Euler–Peano approximation scheme based on noise-dependent stopping times, and, using a stochastic Grönwall-type inequality, prove existence and uniqueness under standard Lipschitz assumptions.

Semimartingales in Hilbert spaces

Let
U,V
and
H
be separable Hilbert spaces equipped with inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and corresponding norms
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. We identify each Hilbert space with its dual. The Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
H
is denoted by
\Borel(H)
.

We denote by
L(U,H)
the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
U
to
H
, equipped with the operator norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{U\rightarrow H}
. The subspace of Hilbert-Schmidt operators is denoted by
L_{2}(U,H)
, with the standard Hilbert-Schmidt norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\rm HS}
. For each
F\in L(U,H)
, we write
F^{\dagger}
for the adjoint operator of
F
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},P)
be a complete filtered probability space. We denote by
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;H)
the space of equivalence classes of
H
-valued measurable functions on
\Omega
, equipped with the topology of convergence in probability.

An
H
-valued stochastic process
(X(t):\,t\geq 0)
is said to have càdlàg paths if its sample paths are right-continuous on
[0,\infty)
and have left limits on
(0,\infty)
. The definition of càglàd paths is analogous. For such a process
X
, we define the running supremum process by

X^{*}(t):=\sup_{s\leq t}\left\lVert X(s)\right\rVert.

Given a stopping time
\tau
, the corresponding stopped process
X^{\tau}
is defined by

X^{\tau}(t)=X(\tau\wedge t)
for all
t\geq 0
.

Let
T>0
be fixed. For
H
-valued càdlàg processes
X
and
Y
define

d^{\rm ucp}_{T}(X,Y):=E\left[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert X(t)-Y(t)\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]

By identifying processes which are almost surely indistinguishable,
d^{\rm ucp}_{T}
becomes a metric on the space
of
H
-valued càdlàg processes on
[0,T]
.

Let
D_{H}
denote the space of
H
-valued adapted càdlàg processes, which is equipped with the topology of uniform convergence on compacts in probability, which is induced by the pseudo-metric

d^{\rm ucp}(X,Y):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}d^{\rm ucp}_{k}(X,Y).

By identifying processes which are almost surely indistinguishable,
d^{\rm ucp}
becomes a metric on
D_{H}
, turning
(D_{H},d^{\rm ucp})
into a complete metrisable topological vector space.

We provide three simple observations regarding convergence in
d^{\rm ucp}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of processes in
D_{H}
converging to
0
in
d^{\rm ucp}
. Then there exists a sequence
(\epsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of strictly positive numbers converging to
0
such that, for each
T>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(X_{n}^{*}(T)>\epsilon_{n}\right)=0.

Proof.

Let
(a_{n,m})_{n,m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a doubly indexed
\R
-valued sequence with
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n,m}=0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We claim that there exists a sequence
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
tending to infinity such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}a_{n,m_{n}}=0
. Indeed, by rescaling we can assume
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|a_{n,1}|\leq 1
.
By assumption, for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
there exists an
N_{m}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N_{m}=\inf\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sup_{k\geq n}|a_{k,m}|\leq\frac{1}{m}\}
. As
N_{1}=1
, we can define
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
by
m_{n}=\sup\{\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}:N_{\ell}\leq n\}
. Note that
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing. Moreover,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{n}=\infty
, since if we had
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}m_{n}=M
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}
, then
N_{M+1}=\infty
, which leads to a contradiction. Finally,
|a_{n,m_{n}}|\leq\frac{1}{m_{n}}
by definition of
m_{n}
, which confirms our claim. The lemma now follows from applying this observation to
a_{n,m}:=P(X_{n}^{*}({m})>m^{-1})
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
H
-valued random variables such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lambda_{n}\xi_{n}=0\qquad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;H)

for every real sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Then the collection
\{\xi_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is bounded in probability, i.e., for all
\epsilon>0
there exists an
m>0
such that
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P(\|\xi_{n}\|>m)<\epsilon
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not bounded in probability. Then there exists an
\epsilon>0
and a sequence
(n_{m})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
of integers tending to infinity such that
P(\|\xi_{n_{m}}\|>m)>\epsilon
. In particular,
(m^{-1}\xi_{n_{m}})_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
fails to converge to
0
in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;H)
, which contradicts the assumption.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of adapted
H
-valued càdlàg processes on
[0,T]
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm ucp}_{T}(X_{n},0)=0
. Then there exists a subsequence
(X_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|X_{n_{k}}\|
is an adapted real-valued càdlàg process.

Proof.

By ucp convergence, there exists a subsequence
(X_{n_{k}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

P(X_{n_{k}}^{*}(T)>2^{-k})<2^{-k},

so that the Borel-Cantelli lemma implies that
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}X_{n_{k}}^{*}(T)<\infty
almost surely.

For
\omega
in this almost sure event, define

S_{m}(t,\omega):=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\|X_{n_{k}}(t,\omega)\|,\qquad t\in[0,T].

Each
S_{m}(\cdot,\omega)
is càdlàg, being a finite sum of càdlàg functions. Moreover, for
n\geq m
,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|S_{n}(t,\omega)-S_{m}(t,\omega)|\leq\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}X_{n_{k}}^{*}(T,\omega).

Since
\sum_{k}X_{n_{k}}^{*}(T,\omega)<\infty
, the sequence
(S_{m}(\cdot,\omega))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
is uniformly Cauchy on
[0,T]
. Therefore it converges uniformly to

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|X_{n_{k}}(t,\omega)\|
for all
t\in[0,T]
, which completes the proof.
∎

We introduce another metric on
D_{H}
. To this end, fix
T>0
and let
\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}:=\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}(H)
denote the space of all processes
(\Gamma(t):\ t\in[0,T])
of the form

\displaystyle\Gamma(\omega,t)=\Gamma_{0}(\omega)\mathbb{1}_{\{0\}}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}O_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(2.1)

where
\Gamma_{0}
is an
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable
L(H)
-valued random variable,
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
A_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
and
O_{i,k}\in L(H)
for all
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
, such that

\displaystyle\sup_{(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]}\left\lVert\Gamma(\omega,t)\right\rVert_{H\rightarrow H}\leq 1.

The stochastic integral process of a simple process
\Gamma
of the form (
2.1
) with respect to any
H
-valued, càdlàg process
(X(t):\,t\in[0,T])
is defined by

\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}X(s):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}O_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\big(X(t_{i+1}\wedge t)-X(t_{i}\wedge t)\big).

We introduce for
H
-valued càdlàg processes
X
and
Y
on
[0,T]
the mapping

d^{\rm em}_{T}(X,Y):=\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\Gamma(0)(X-Y)(0)+\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}(X-Y)(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg].

By identifying processes which are almost surely indistinguishable,
d^{\rm em}_{T}
becomes a metric
on the space of
H
-valued càdlàg processes on
[0,T]
. Hence, we obtain a metric on
D_{H}
by

d^{\rm em}(X,Y):=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k}}d^{\rm em}_{k}(X,Y).

The metric

is defined analogously to the metric associated with the semimartingale topology, also known as the Émery topology, on the space of real-valued semimartingales, originally introduced in
[
8
]
. In contrast to
[
8
]

\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}

, the space of bounded linear operators on

For similar reasons, we restrict attention to deterministic time intervals when defining simple processes, rather than allowing random intervals. In the real-valued case, both choices lead to equivalent metrics; see, for example,
[
13
]
.

Obviously, by taking
\Gamma(\omega,t)=\Id_{H}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,T]}(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\omega\in\Omega
, where
\Id_{H}
is the identity on

d_{T}^{em}(X,Y)\to 0
implies
d_{T}^{ucp}(X,Y)\to 0
. Consequently,
d^{\rm em}
induces a topology on
D_{H}
stronger than

.

The metric

, defined for càdlàg processes on
[0,T]
, generates the same topology on

even when formulated without taking a supremum over time. This observation, established in the following lemma, enables us to relate the present results to those in our earlier work

H

[0,T]

Let
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of partitions of

\displaystyle d_{T}^{\rm em}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\Gamma(0)(X-Y)(0)+\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}(X-Y)(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in\pi_{n}}\left\lVert\Gamma(0)(X-Y)(0)+\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}(X-Y)(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg],

\displaystyle\quad\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in\pi_{n}}\left\lVert\Gamma(0)(X-Y)(0)+\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}(X-Y)(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg],

whereas the reverse inequality follows trivially. It thus suffices to prove that for every partition
\pi
, the quasi-metric defined for each

in the space of
H
-valued, adapted, càdlàg processes on
[0,T]
, given by

\displaystyle\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\Bigg[\sup_{t\in\pi}\left\lVert\Gamma(0)(X-Y)(0)+\int_{(0,t]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}(X-Y)(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\Bigg],

generates the same topology as

on the space of
H
-valued, adapted, càdlàg processes on the interval

.

Let

\mathcal{F}_{0}

Letting

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
H

Then, for every

As
B\in L(H,V)
, we can assume, by taking

, that
\operatorname{dim}(V)\leq\operatorname{dim}(H)
.
Let

. For each

\displaystyle d_{T}^{em}\big(\mathbbm{1}_{S}\mathbbm{1}_{(p,q]}BX,0\big)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}(V)}E\Bigg[\left\lVert\int_{(0,T]}\Gamma(s)\,{\mathrm{d}}\big(\mathbbm{1}_{S}\mathbbm{1}_{(p,q]}BX\big)(s)\right\rVert_{V}\wedge 1\Bigg]

Y
, we may introduce the notion of covariation via the polarisation identity by setting

[X,Y]:=\frac{1}{4}\left([X+Y]-[X-Y]\right).

Similarly to the real-valued case, it can be shown that the covariation is bilinear.

The following result on the continuity of the quadratic variation (and covariation) in the metric
d^{\rm em}
in the real-valued case can be found in
[
14
, Prop. 2.10]
. A similar argument yields the following extension to the Hilbert space-valued setting:

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
H
-valued semimartingales, and let
X
be an
H
-valued semimartingale. Then, for every
T>0
, the following implication holds:

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm em}_{T}(X_{n},X)=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm em}_{T}([X_{n}],[X])=0.

Proof.

Let
{\rm TV}(f)(t)
denote the total variation of the function
f\colon[0,\infty)\to\R
on
[0,t]
.
Since
d^{\rm em}_{T}([X_{n}],[X])=d^{\rm em}_{T}([X_{n}]-[X],0)
, and the sequence
([X_{n}]-[X])_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of real-valued semimartingales of finite variation, it follows from
[
14
, Pr. 2.7]
that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E\left[\big({\rm TV}\left([X_{n}]-[X]\right)(T)\big)\wedge 1\right]=0\quad\text{implies}\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm em}_{T}([X_{n}],[X])=0.

(2.3)

Moreover, as in the real-valued case, it follows from the characterisation of covariation as a limit over partitions (see
[
18
, Th. 26.5]
) and Hölder’s inequality that the real-valued covariation process of two
H
-valued semimartingales
Y
and
Z
satisfies
\big([Y,Z](t)\big)^{2}\leq[Y](t)[Z](t)
for all
t\geq 0
. From this, we deduce, that

{\rm TV}[Y,Z](T)\leq\sqrt{[Y](T)}\sqrt{[Z](T)}
.
Using this inequality and bilinearity of the covariation, we get for each
t\in[0,T]
that

\displaystyle{\rm TV}\left([X_{n}]-[X]\right)(t)

\displaystyle={\rm TV}\left([X_{n}-X]+2[X,X_{n}-X])\right)(t)

\displaystyle\leq[X_{n}-X](T)+2\sqrt{[X](T)}\sqrt{[X_{n}-X](T)}\,\quad a.s.

Consequently, it is sufficient by (
2.3
) to show that
[X_{n}-X](T)
converges to
0
in probability.
For this, we may assume
X=0
and it remains to show that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E[[X_{n}](T)\wedge 1]=0.

As
d^{\rm em}_{T}(X_{n},0)\to 0
implies
d^{\rm ucp}_{T}(X_{n},0)\to 0
, it follows that
X_{n}(0)
and
X_{n}(T)
both converge to
0
in probability. Hence, by Equation (
2.2
), it remains only to show that
(I_{n}(T))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
in probability, where

I_{n}(T):=\int_{(0,T]}\langle X_{n}(s-),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}X_{n}(s).

By the subsequence principle, it suffices to show that any subsequence has a further subsequence that converges to
0
in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
. To this end, let
(I_{n_{m}}(T))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
be a subsequence of
(I_{n}(T))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Since our assumptions imply that
d^{\rm em}_{T}(X_{n_{m}},0)\to 0
as
m\rightarrow\infty
, it follows that
d^{\rm ucp}_{T}(X_{n_{m}},0)\to 0
as
m\rightarrow\infty
. By Lemma
2.3
there exists a subsequence
(X_{n_{m_{k}}})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that the sum
\xi(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\lVert X_{n_{m_{k}}}(t)\right\rVert
defines a real-valued càdlàg process.

Using
\xi
, we define a sequence
(\tau_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
of stopping times by

\tau_{\ell}:=\inf\left\{t\in[0,T]:{\xi(t)}>\ell\right\}\quad\text{for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N},

where we note that since
\xi
is càdlàg, we have
P(\cup_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\})=1
.
Fix
e\in H
with
\left\lVert e\right\rVert=1
, and define for each
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
a process

Y_{k,\ell}(t):=\langle\ell^{-1}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(t-)\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau_{\ell}\wedge T]}(t),\cdot\rangle\,e\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

Clearly, for each
k,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
we have that
Y_{k,\ell}(t)\in L(H)
for all
t\in[0,T]
, moreover, it follows from the very definition of
\tau_{\ell}
that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert Y_{k,\ell}(t)\right\rVert_{H\rightarrow H}\leq 1
. Approximating
Y_{k,\ell}
by a sequence
(S_{k,\ell,r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}
and using
[
18
, Th. 26.3]
, we get

\displaystyle E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(\tau_{\ell}\wedge T)|\wedge 1]

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\ell,1\}\,E\left[\left|\int_{(0,T]}\langle\ell^{-1}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s-)\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau_{\ell}]}(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s)\right|\wedge 1\right]

\displaystyle=\max\{\ell,1\}\,E\left[\left(\left|\int_{(0,T]}\langle\ell^{-1}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s-)\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau_{\ell}]}(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s)\right|\left\lVert e\right\rVert\right)\wedge 1\right]

\displaystyle=\max\{\ell,1\}\,E\left[\left\lVert\int_{(0,T]}Y_{k,\ell}(s)\,{\rm d}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]

\displaystyle=\max\{\ell,1\}\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}E\left[\left\lVert\int_{(0,T]}S_{k,\ell,r}(s)\,{\rm d}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\right]

\displaystyle\leq\max\{\ell,1\}\sup_{\Gamma\in\mathcal{S}_{{\rm prd}}^{1,{\rm op}}}E\left[\left\lVert\int_{(0,T]}\Gamma(s)\,{\rm d}X_{n_{m_{k}}}(s)\right\rVert\wedge 1\right],

which, since
d^{\rm em}_{T}(X_{n_{m_{k}}},0)\to 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
, implies that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(\tau_{\ell}\wedge T)|\wedge 1]=0\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

From this, we can deduce that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(T)\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}}|\wedge 1]

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(\tau_{\ell}\wedge T)\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}}|\wedge 1]

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(\tau_{\ell}\wedge T)|\wedge 1]=0\qquad\text{for all }\ell\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
P(\cup_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\})=1
, or equivalently
P(\cap_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}^{c})=0
, we have

\lim_{\ell\rightarrow\infty}P(\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}^{c})=0.

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed. By using the simple estimate

E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(T)|\wedge 1]\leq E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(T)\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}}|\wedge 1]+P(\{\tau_{\ell}=\infty\}^{c}),

we first choose
\ell^{*}
large enough so that
P(\{\tau_{\ell^{*}}=\infty\}^{c})<\epsilon/2
. Then, having fixed
\ell^{*}
, we now take
k
to be large enough so that
E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(T)\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell^{*}}=\infty\}}|\wedge 1]<\epsilon/2
. Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we may conclude that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n_{m_{k}}}(T)|\wedge 1]=0.

Since the argument holds for an arbitrary subsequence
(I_{n_{m}}(T))_{m\in\mathbb{N}}
, the subsequence principle allows us to conclude that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E[|I_{n}(T)|\wedge 1]=0
, which finishes the proof of our claim.
∎

3
Cylindrical Lévy processes and their stochastic integrals

A cylindrical random variable
Z
in a Hilbert space
U
is a linear and continuous mapping
Z\colon U\rightarrow L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
.
Its characteristic function is defined as

\varphi_{Z}\colon U\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\varphi_{Z}(u)=E\big[e^{iZu}\big].

Let
{\mathcal{F}}=({\mathcal{F}}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a filtration on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},P)
satisfying the usual conditions.
A family
(L(t):t\geq 0)
of cylindrical random variables
L(t)\colon U\rightarrow L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
is called a cylindrical Lévy process if, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and every
u_{1},...,u_{n}\in U
, the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued stochastic process

\big(\big(L(t)u_{1},...,L(t)u_{n}\big):t\geq 0\big)

is a Lévy process with respect to
{\mathcal{F}}
.

The characteristic function of
L(t)
is, for each
t\geq 0
, of the form

\varphi_{L(t)}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C},\qquad\varphi_{L(t)}(u)=\exp\left(tS(u)\right),

where the mapping
S:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is given by

S(u)=ia(u)-\frac{1}{2}\langle Qu,u\rangle+\int_{U}\left(e^{i\langle u,h\rangle}-1-i\langle u,h\rangle\mathbb{1}_{B_{\mathbb{R}}}(\langle u,h\rangle)\right)\,\lambda({\rm d}h).

Here,
a:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous mapping with
a(0)=0
, and
Q:U\rightarrow U
is a positive and symmetric operator. The set function
\lambda\colon\mathcal{Z}_{\ast}(U)\to[0,\infty]
is defined on the algebra

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{\ast}(U)\!:=\big\{\{u\in U:\!(\langle u,u_{1}^{\ast}\rangle,\dots,\langle u,u_{n}^{\ast}\rangle)\in A\}:u_{1}^{\ast},\dots,u_{n}^{\ast}\in U,A\in\Borel(\R^{n}\setminus\{0\}),n\in\mathbb{N}\big\},

and satisfies for each
C:=\{u\in U:\!(\langle u,u_{1}^{\ast}\rangle,\dots,\langle u,u_{n}^{\ast}\rangle)\in A\}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\ast}(U)
that

\lambda(C)=\lambda_{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}}(A),

where
\lambda_{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}}
is the Lévy measure of
\big(\big(L(t)u_{1},...,L(t)u_{n}\big):t\geq 0\big)
. The triplet
[a,Q,\lambda]
is called the characteristics of
L
.
See
[
2
]
and
[
21
]
for this and related results.

Stochastic integration with respect to cylindrical Lévy processes was introduced in the recent work
[
4
]
by two of us. In the present paper, however, we require additional properties of the stochastic integral that, while well established in the case of genuine Lévy processes, must be carefully verified in the cylindrical setting. In many instances, the cylindrical nature of the integrator introduces significant analytical challenges.

We begin with a brief review of the construction of the stochastic integral, referring to
[
4
]
for full details. Fix
T>0
and let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
U
with characteristics
[a,Q,\lambda]
. An
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued predictable step process
\Psi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(U,H)
is of the form

\displaystyle\Psi(\omega,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}F_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(3.1)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=T
,
A_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
and
F_{i,k}\in L_{2}(U,H)
for all
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
. The space of all
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued predictable step processes is denoted by
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}(U,H)
.

Let
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
be of the form (
3.1
). It follows from
[
23
, Th. VI.5.2 and Prop. VI.5.3]
that there exists an
H
-valued random variable
F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i}))\colon\Omega\to H
for each
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
, satisfying

\big(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})\big)(F_{i,k}^{\dagger}h)=\langle F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1})-L(t_{i})),h\rangle\quad\text{$P$-a.s.\ for all}\;h\in H.

Using these
H
-valued random variables, the stochastic integral of
\Psi
is defined, for all
t\in[0,T]
, by

I(\Psi)(t):=\int_{(0,t]}\Psi(t)\,{\rm d}L(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}\mathbb{1}_{A_{i,k}}F_{i,k}(L(t_{i+1}\wedge t)-L(t_{i}\wedge t)).

Thus, the integral
I(\Psi)(t):\Omega\rightarrow H
is a genuine
H
-valued random variable.

We consider the measure space
\big(\Omega\times[0,T],\mathcal{P},P_{T}\big)
, where
\mathcal{P}
denotes the predictable
\sigma
-algebra, generated by the left-continuous,
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
-adapted processes, and the measure
P_{T}
is defined by
P_{T}:=P\otimes{\rm Leb}|_{[0,T]}
.

Definition 3.1
.

A predictable process
\Psi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(U,H)
is
L
-integrable if there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of processes in
\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
such that

(1)

(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges
P_{T}
-a.e. to
\Psi
;

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{m,n\rightarrow\infty}d_{T}^{em}(I(\Psi_{m}),I(\Psi_{n}))=0.

In this case, the stochastic integral process
(I(\Psi)(t):\,t\in[0,T])
of
\Psi
is defined as the limit
of
(I(\Psi_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
d^{\rm em}_{T}
. The class of all
L
-integrable
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued predictable processes will be denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}:=\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}(U,H)
.

It follows immediately from the fact that convergence in
d^{\rm em}_{T}
implies convergence in
d^{\rm ucp}_{T}
, that the integral process has càdlàg paths. Furthermore, as shown in
[
4
]
, the integral process is an
H
-valued semimartingale.

To characterise the space of
L
-integrable predictable processes, fix a cylindrical Lévy process
L
in
U
with characteristics
[a,Q,\lambda]
.
If
F\colon U\to H
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator in
L_{2}(U,H)
, then there exists a genuine Lévy process
FL
in
H
satisfying

\langle FL(t),h\rangle=L(t)(F^{\ast}h)\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,h\in H.

The Lévy process
FL
has genuine characteristics
(a_{F},Q_{F},\lambda_{F})
satisfying

\displaystyle\langle a_{F},h\rangle=a(F^{\dagger}h)+\int_{H}\big(\langle\theta(u),h\rangle-\langle u,h\rangle\mathbbm{1}_{[-1,1]}(\langle u,h\rangle)\big)\,\lambda_{F}({\mathrm{d}}u),

\displaystyle Q_{F}=FQF^{\dagger},\qquad\lambda_{F}=\lambda\circ F^{-1}\quad\text{on }{\mathcal{Z}}_{\ast}(H).

Here, the characteristics
(a_{F},Q_{F},\lambda_{F})
is given in terms of the truncation function

\theta\colon H\to H,\qquad\theta(u)=\begin{cases}u,&\text{if }\left\lVert u\right\rVert\leq 1,\\
\tfrac{u}{\left\lVert u\right\rVert},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Using this characteristics, we define functions

\displaystyle\zeta_{L}:L_{2}(U,H)\rightarrow\R,\qquad\zeta_{L}(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{H}\Big(\left\lVert h\right\rVert^{2}\wedge 1\Big)\,\lambda_{F}({\mathrm{d}}h)+{\rm Tr}(FQF^{\dagger}),

\displaystyle\eta_{L}:L_{2}(U,H)\rightarrow\R,\qquad\eta_{L}(F)

\displaystyle=\sup_{O\in E_{L(H)}}\left\lVert a_{OF}\right\rVert,

where
E_{L(H)}
denotes the closed unit ball in
L(H,H)
.

For a measurable function
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(U,H)
define

\displaystyle m_{L}(\psi):=\int_{(0,T]}\Big(\zeta_{L}(\psi(t))+\eta_{L}(\psi(t))+\big(\left\lVert\psi(t)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\wedge 1\big)\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,

and denote by
\mathcal{M}_{L}
the space of Lebesgue a.e. equivalence classes of measurable functions
\psi:[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(U,H)
for which
m_{L}(\psi)<\infty
.

The next result provides a characterisation of the space of predictable processes that are integrable with respect to a given cylindrical Lévy process. The proof, presented in
[
4
]
, is based on a decoupling method introduced in
[
16
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

A predictable Hilbert-Schmidt operator-valued process
\Psi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow L_{2}(U,H)
is integrable with respect to
L
if and only if

m_{L}(\Psi)<\infty
a.s.

Proof.

See Theorem 4.4 in
[
4
]
.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

It follows from Theorem
3.2
that every adapted càglàd process
\Psi
is
L
-integrable. Indeed, since every càglàd function on a compact interval is bounded, it follows from
[
4
, Re. 3.17]
, that, for each
\omega\in\Omega
, there exists a finite constant
C_{\omega}
such that
m_{L}(\Psi(\omega))\leq C_{\omega}
. Hence,
\Psi
is
L
-integrable by Theorem
3.2
.

The following stochastic dominated convergence result will be of great importance throughout this paper.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
L
-integrable, predictable processes
converging to an
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued predictable process
\Psi

P_{T}
-a.e. If there exists an
L
-integrable predictable process
\Upsilon
satisfying for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
that

(\zeta_{L}+\eta_{L})(\Psi_{n}(\omega,t))\leq(\zeta_{L}+\eta_{L})(\Upsilon(\omega,t))\quad\text{for $P_{T}$-a.a.\ $(\omega,t)\in\Omega\times[0,T]$},

then
\Psi
is
L
-integrable and satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{T}^{em}(I(\Psi_{n}),I(\Psi))=0.

Proof.

See Theorem 7.5 in
[
4
]
.
∎

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
L
-integrable, predictable processes
converging to an
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued predictable process
\Psi

P_{T}
-a.e. If we have

\esssup_{\omega\in\Omega}\sup_{s\in(0,T]}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert\Psi_{n}(\omega,s)\right\rVert<\infty,

then
\Psi
is
L
-integrable and satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{T}^{em}(I(\Psi_{n}),I(\Psi))=0.

Proof.

The proof is completely analogous to that of
[
4
, Th. 7.5]
, with the exception that we can now use
[
4
, Re. 3.17]
to obtain an upper bound on
m_{L}
by a constant.
∎

The following lemma provides a tool to obtain upper bounds for
\zeta_{L}
and
\eta_{L}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
U,H
and
V
be separable Hilbert spaces, and
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
U
. For each
C\in L_{2}(U,H)
and
D\in L(H,V)
with
\left\lVert D\right\rVert\leq r
for some
r>0
, the following assertions hold:

(1)

\zeta_{L}(DC)\leq\max\{r,1\}\,\zeta_{L}(C)
;

(2)

\eta_{L}(DC)\leq d_{r}\left(\eta_{L}(C)+\zeta_{L}(C)\right)
for some constant
d_{r}>0
depending only on
r
.

Proof.

The proof of (1) is completely analogous to the second part of
[
4
, Le. 3.14]
, while (2) follows from
[
4
, Le. 3.9]
,
[
4
, Re. 3.10]
, and an argument similar to
[
4
, Le. 3.16]
.
∎

Throughout this paper, we will use the following properties of stochastic integrals. Although these results are standard in classical stochastic analysis, see e.g.
[
7
, Th. 2.7]
, we provide detailed proofs due to the cylindrical nature of our integrator.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
U
, and let
\Psi
be a predictable,
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued process that is integrable with respect to
L
. Then the integral process
(Y(t):\,t\in[0,T])
, defined by
Y(t):=\int_{(0,t]}\Psi\,{\rm d}L
, satisfies the following properties:

(a)

Let
V
be a separable Hilbert space and
B\in L(H,V)
. Then, for all
0\leq p<q\leq T
and
S\in\mathcal{F}_{p}
, we have that
\mathbb{1}_{S}B\Psi
is
L
-integrable and

\mathbb{1}_{S}B\left(\int_{(p,q]}\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)=\int_{(p,q]}\mathbb{1}_{S}B\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s).

(3.2)

(b)

Let
\Phi:\Omega\times[0,T]\rightarrow H
be a uniformly bounded, adapted, càglàd process. Then, for each fixed
t>0
, it follows that
\langle\Psi^{\dagger}\Phi,\cdot\rangle
is
L
-integrable and

\int_{(0,t]}\langle\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}Y(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\big\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(s)\Phi(s),\cdot\big\rangle\,{\rm d}L(s).

(c)

For every stopping time
\tau
, we have that
\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\tau]}\Psi
is
L
-integrable and

Y(\tau\wedge T)=\int_{(0,T]}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\tau]}(s)\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s).

Proof.

(a): First, observe that identity (
3.2
) holds for all
\Psi\in\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
by direct computation using the definition of the stochastic integral.

Now consider a general integrand
\Psi\in\mathcal{I}_{{\rm prd},L}^{\rm HS}(U,H)
. By definition, there exists a sequence
(\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{\rm prd}^{\rm HS}
converging
P_{T}
-a.e. to
\Psi
and
such that
d_{T}^{em}(I(\Psi_{m}),I(\Psi_{n}))\to 0
as
m,n\to\infty
. Since identity (
3.2
) applies to each
\Psi_{n}
, Lemma
2.5
implies that
d_{T}^{em}(I(\mathbbm{1}_{S}B\Psi_{m}),I(\mathbbm{1}_{S}B\Psi_{n}))\to 0
. Since
(\mathbbm{1}_{S}B\Psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
\mathbbm{1}_{S}B\Psi

P_{T}
-a.e.,
it follows that
{\mathbb{1}_{S}B\Psi}
is
L
-integrable, and

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(p,q]}\mathbb{1}_{S}B\Psi_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)=\int_{(p,q]}\mathbb{1}_{S}B\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\quad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,V),

On the other hand, by the continuity of
B
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{1}_{S}B\left(\int_{(p,q]}\Psi_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)=\mathbb{1}_{S}B\left(\int_{(p,q]}\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)\quad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega,V).

Since equality (
3.2
) holds for each
\Psi_{n}
, the proof is completed.

(b): We first establish that the process
\langle\Psi^{\dagger}\Phi,\cdot\rangle
is
L
-integrable. Since
\Phi
is uniformly bounded and the space of
L
-integrable predictable processes is a vector space, dividing by the uniform bound of
\Phi
if necessary, we may assume that
\Phi
is uniformly bounded by
1
. By noting that
\langle\Psi(s)^{\dagger}\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle=\langle\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle\circ\Psi(s)
for all
s\in[0,T]
, we may use Lemma
3.6
to establish that there exists a constant
c>0
such that
m_{L}(\langle\Psi^{\dagger}\Phi,\cdot\rangle)\leq c\,m_{L}(\Psi)<\infty
almost surely. The fact that
\langle\Psi^{\dagger}\Phi,\cdot\rangle
is
L
-integrable now follows from Theorem
3.2
.

We first show the identity under the assumption that
\Phi
is of the form

\Phi(\omega,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}h_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}}(\omega)\right)\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(t),

(3.3)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=t
,
A_{i,k}\in\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
and
h_{i,k}\in H
for all
i=1,...,n-1
and
k=1,...,N(i)
. By Part (a) of this lemma, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}\langle\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}Y(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}h_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}},Y(t_{i+1})-Y(t_{i})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\int_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}\left\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(s)\Bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{N(i)}h_{i,k}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{i,k}}\Bigg),\cdot\right\rangle\,{\rm d}L(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]}\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(s)\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}L(s).

Now consider an arbitrary uniformly bounded, adapted, càglàd process
\Phi\colon\Omega\times[0,T]\to H
. By
[
11
, Lem. 1.2.19 and Rem. 1.2.20]
, there exists a sequence
(\Phi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of processes of the form (
3.3
) such that
\Phi_{n}\to\Phi

P_{T}
-a.e. and satisfying

c:=\esssup_{\omega\in\Omega}\sup_{s\in(0,T]}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert\Phi_{n}(\omega,s)\right\rVert<\infty.

(3.4)

By Theorem 26.3 in
[
18
]
, for each fixed
t>0
, we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(0,t]}\langle\Phi_{n}(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}Y(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\langle\Phi(s),\cdot\rangle\,{\rm d}Y(s)\qquad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\R).

(3.5)

In order to complete the proof, it remains to establish that, for each
t>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{(0,t]}\left\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(s)\Phi_{n}(s),\cdot\right\rangle\,{\rm d}L(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\left\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(s)\Phi(s),\cdot\right\rangle\,{\rm d}L(s)\qquad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\R).

(3.6)

According to Corollary 7.3 in
[
4
]
, this is the case if and only if

\lim_{n\to\infty}E\!\left[\,m_{L}\!\left(\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\Phi_{n}-\Phi),\cdot\rangle\right)\wedge 1\right]=0.

(3.7)

As a first step, we observe that, since
\Psi
is
L
-integrable, there exists a set
\Omega_{\Psi}\subseteq\Omega
with
P(\Omega_{\Psi})=1
such that, for every
\omega\in\Omega_{\Psi}
,

m_{L}(\Psi(\omega))=\int_{(0,T]}\Big(\zeta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))+\eta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))+\big(\left\lVert\Psi(\omega,t)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\wedge 1\big)\Big)\,{\mathrm{d}}t<\infty.

By Lemma
3.6
, it follows that, for all
\omega\in\Omega_{\Psi}
and
t\in(0,T]
,

\displaystyle\zeta_{L}\left(\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\omega,t)(\Phi_{n}-\Phi)(\omega,t),\cdot\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\max\{2c,1\}\,\zeta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t));

\displaystyle\eta_{L}\left(\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\omega,t)(\Phi_{n}-\Phi)(\omega,t),\cdot\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq d_{2c}\left(\eta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))+\zeta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))\right).

Moreover, a straightforward calculation yields, for all
\omega\in\Omega_{\Psi}
and
t\in(0,T]
,

\|\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\omega,t)(\Phi_{n}-\Phi)(\omega,t),\cdot\rangle\|_{2}^{2}=\|\langle(\Phi_{n}-\Phi)(\omega,t),\cdot\rangle\circ\Psi(\omega,t)\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\,\|\Psi(\omega,t)\|_{2}^{2}.

Using the above bounds, we may apply Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem for each fixed
\omega\in\Omega_{\Psi}
, with the dominating function

t\mapsto 3\,\zeta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))+\eta_{L}(\Psi(\omega,t))+2\big(\|\Psi(\omega,t)\|_{2}^{2}\wedge 1\big),

to obtain

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{L}\big(\langle\Psi^{\dagger}(\omega)(\Phi_{n}-\Phi)(\omega),\cdot\rangle\big)=0\qquad\text{for all }\omega\in\Omega_{\Psi}.

Since
P(\Omega_{\Psi})=1
, this proves (
3.7
) and thus completes the proof of (b).

(c): Let
\sigma:=\tau\wedge T
and define
\sigma_{n}=2^{-n}[2^{n}\sigma+1]
and
N_{n}=[2^{n}\,T]
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
[x]
denotes the integer part of any real
x
. Define the following subset of
\Omega\times[0,\infty)
:

J_{n}:=\bigcup_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\{\sigma\geq k2^{-n}\}\times(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}].

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle Y(\sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\left\{{k2^{-n}\leq\sigma<(k+1)2^{-n}}\right\}}Y((k+1)2^{-n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\left(\mathbb{1}_{\left\{{k2^{-n}\leq\sigma}\right\}}-\mathbb{1}_{\left\{{(k+1)2^{-n}}\leq\sigma\right\}}\right)Y((k+1)2^{-n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\{k2^{-n}\leq\sigma\}}\Big(Y((k+1)2^{-n})-Y(k2^{-n})\Big).

Since the filtration
{\mathcal{F}}
is right-continuous, the set
\{k2^{-n}\leq\sigma\}
is
\mathcal{F}_{k2^{-n}}
-measurable. Hence, by Part (a) of this lemma and linearity of the integral, we obtain

\displaystyle Y(\sigma_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\mathbb{1}_{\{\sigma\geq k2^{-n}\}}\int_{(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]}\Psi\,{\rm d}L

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{n}}\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(k2^{-n},(k+1)2^{-n}]\times\{\sigma\geq k2^{-n}\}}\Psi\,{\rm d}L=\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{J_{n}}\Psi\,{\rm d}L.

Since
Y
has right continuous paths and
\sigma_{n}\downarrow\sigma=\tau\wedge T
, we have

Y(\tau\wedge T)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}Y(\sigma_{n}).

On the other hand, applying Theorem
3.4
with
\Psi
as an integrable dominating function shows

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{J_{n}}\Psi\,{\rm d}L=\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau\wedge T]}\Psi\,{\rm d}L,

which completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
L
be a cylindrical Lévy process in
U
, and let
\Psi
be a predictable,
L_{2}(U,H)
-valued process that is integrable with respect to
L
. Define the integral process
(Y(t):\,t\in[0,T])
by
Y(t):=\int_{(0,t]}\Psi\,{\rm d}L
. Then, for every uniformly bounded, real-valued, non-negative, adapted càglàd process
Z
, the following identity holds:

\int_{(0,t]}Z(s)^{2}\;{\rm d}[Y](s)=\left[\int_{(0,{\cdot}]}Z(s)\Psi(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right](t)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

(3.8)

Proof.

Fix
t\geq 0
, and assume first that
Z
is of the form

\displaystyle Z(s)=\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}Z_{i}\mathbbm{1}_{(t_{i},t_{i+1}]}(s)\qquad\text{for }s\in[0,t],

(3.9)

where
0=t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}=t
, and each
Z_{i}
is a real-valued,
\mathcal{F}_{t_{i}}
-measurable random variable. Let
(\pi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of nested partitions of
[0,t]
with mesh tending to
0
and such that
\{t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\}\subseteq\pi_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Corollary 26.7.2 in
[
18
]
yields

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}Z(s)^{2}{\rm d}[Y](s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k\in\pi_{n}}Z(t_{k,n})^{2}\left\lVert Y(t_{k+1,n})-Y(t_{k,n})\right\rVert^{2}\qquad\text{in }L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\R).

Since
Z
is non-negative and constant on each interval
(t_{k,n},t_{k+1,n}]
, it follows that

Z(t_{k,n})^{2}\left\lVert Y(t_{k+1,n})-Y(t_{k,n})\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert Z(t_{k,n})\int_{(t_{k,n},t_{k+1,n}]}\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert^{2}=\left\lVert\int_{(t_{k,n},t_{k+1,n}]}Z\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert^{2}.

Hence, by Theorem 26.5 in
[
18
]
, we obtain in
L_{P}^{0}(\Omega;\R)
that

\displaystyle\int_{(0,t]}Z(s)^{2}{\rm d}[Y](s)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{k\in\pi_{n}}\left\lVert\int_{(t_{k,n},t_{k+1,n}]}Z\Psi\,{\rm d}L\right\rVert^{2}=\left[\int_{(0,\cdot]}Z(s)\Psi(s){\rm d}L(s)\right](t),

which proves Equation (
3.8
) for
Z
of the form (
3.9
).

Now let
Z
be an arbitrary uniformly bounded, real-valued, non-negative, adapted càglàd process.
Since both sides of (
3.8
) are quadratically homogeneous, we may, without loss of generality, assume that
\lvert Z\rvert\leq 1

P
-a.s.

By
[
4
, Rem. 3.6]
, we have
m_{L}(Z\Psi)\leq m_{L}(\Psi)<\infty
, which guarantees that
Z\Psi
is
L
-integrable. Let
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of processes of the form (
3.9
) converging
P_{T}
-a.e. to
Z
. Since
Z\leq 1
, we may choose the sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
so that
\lvert Z_{n}\rvert\leq 1

P
-a.s. for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then, again by
[
4
, Rem. 3.6]
, it follows that
m_{L}(Z_{n}\Psi)\leq m_{L}(\Psi)

P
-a.s., implying that each
Z_{n}\Psi
is
L
-integrable.
Applying Theorem
3.4
, we obtain

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm em}_{T}\left(\int_{(0,\cdot]}Z_{n}(s)\Psi(s){\rm d}L(s),\int_{(0,\cdot]}Z(s)\Psi(s){\rm d}L(s)\right)=0.

By Lemma
2.6
, this implies

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm em}_{T}\left(\left[\int_{(0,\cdot]}Z_{n}(s)\Psi(s){\rm d}L(s)\right],\left[\int_{(0,\cdot]}Z(s)\Psi(s){\rm d}L(s)\right]\right)=0.

(3.10)

On the other hand, since
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lvert Z_{n}\right\rvert\leq 1
, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem yields, for each

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{(0,t]}Z_{n}(s)^{2}{\rm d}[Y](s)=\int_{(0,t]}Z(s)^{2}{\rm d}[Y](s)\quad\text{for $P$-a.a.\ }\omega\in\Omega.

(3.11)

Combining Equations (

4
Existence and uniqueness of solution

We consider an abstract evolution equation of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathrm{d}}X(t)&=\big(AX(t)+F(X(t))\big){\mathrm{d}}t+G(X(t-))\,{\mathrm{d}}L(t),\qquad t\geq 0,\\
X(0)&=X_{0},\end{split}

(4.1)

where
A
is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup
(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
H
, and

F\colon H\to H
,
G\colon H\to L_{2}(U,H)
are given maps. The initial condition is specified by an
H
-valued,
{\mathcal{F}}_{0}
-measurable random variable
X_{0}
, and the noise is modelled by an arbitrary cylindrical Lévy process
L
on
U
.

Definition 4.1
.

A process
X
is called a
mild solution
to (
4.1
) if it is an
H
-valued, adapted, càdlàg process such that, for every
t\geq 0
,

X(t)=S(t)X_{0}+\int_{(0,t]}S(t-s)F(X(s))\,{\mathrm{d}}s+\int_{(0,t]}S(t-s)G(X(s-))\,{\mathrm{d}}L(s)\quad P\text{-a.s.}

A mild solution is called unique if any two mild solutions are indistinguishable.

The main finding of this work is the following existence and uniqueness result:

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that the following assumptions are satisfied:

(A1)

(S(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a contraction semigroup on
H
;

(A2)

there exists a constant
c_{F}
such that

\left\lVert F(h_{1})-F(h_{2})\right\rVert\leq c_{F}\left\lVert h_{1}-h_{2}\right\rVert\qquad\text{for all }h_{1},h_{2}\in H;

(A3)

there exists a constant
c_{G}
such that

\left\lVert G(h_{1})-G(h_{2})\right\rVert_{L_{2}(U,H)}\leq c_{G}\left\lVert h_{1}-h_{2}\right\rVert\qquad\text{for all }h_{1},h_{2}\in H.

Then there exists a unique mild solution to Equation (
4.1
).

The proof of this theorem is provided at the end of this section. One of the main ingredients of the proof is a stochastic Grönwall result, which we will prove first. To that end, we establish the following inequality:

Lemma 4.3
.

If
X
is a real-valued, strictly positive semimartingale then

\ln\left(\frac{X^{*}(t)}{X(0)}\right)\leq\left(\int_{(0,\cdot]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\;{\rm d}X(s)\right)^{*}(t)\quad\text{a.s.\ for all $t\geq 0$.}

Proof.

(The proof originates from
[
17
]
).
Applying Itô’s formula,
[
20
, Th. II.32]
, to the non-decreasing semimartingale
X^{\ast}
and the function
\beta\mapsto\ln\beta
, we obtain, for each
t>0
, that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{X^{*}(t)}{X(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{\ast}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{\ast}(s)+\sum_{s\in(0,t]}\left(\ln X^{\ast}(s)-\ln X^{\ast}(s-)-\frac{1}{X^{\ast}(s-)}\Delta X^{\ast}(s)\right).

Since it follows from the inequality
\ln(1+\beta)\leq\beta
for all
\beta>-1
that the summation is non-positive, we conclude, for each
t>0
, that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{X^{*}(t)}{X(0)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\;{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

(4.2)

Since
X^{*}
is nondecreasing, the process
1/X^{*}
is nonincreasing, and hence also of finite variation. Define

Y(t):=\frac{X(t)}{X^{*}(t)},\qquad t\geq 0,

so that
X=Y\,X^{*}
. Applying the integration-by-parts formula for products of semimartingales
(see, e.g.,
[
20
, Cor. 2 to Th. II.22]
) yields

{\mathrm{d}}X(s)={\mathrm{d}}\bigl(Y(s)X^{*}(s)\bigr)=Y(s-)\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{*}(s)+X^{*}(s-)\,{\mathrm{d}}Y(s)+{\mathrm{d}}[Y,X^{*}](s).

Dividing by
X^{*}(s-)
and integrating over
(0,t]
gives

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X(s)\\
&\qquad=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{*}(s)+\bigl(Y(t)-Y(0)\bigr)+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}[Y,X^{*}](s).\end{split}

(4.3)

Since
X^{*}
has finite variation, its quadratic covariation with any semimartingale
is purely discontinuous. Hence, by
[
20
, Th. II.28]
,

[Y,X^{*}](t)=Y(0)X^{*}(0)+\sum_{0<s\leq t}\Delta Y(s)\,\Delta X^{*}(s),

and therefore, for
0<s\leq t
,

\Delta[Y,X^{*}](s)=\Delta Y(s)\,\Delta X^{*}(s).

Consequently, the stochastic integral with respect to the finite-variation process

[Y,X^{*}]
reduces to a sum over jumps:

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}[Y,X^{*}](s)=\sum_{0<s\leq t}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,\Delta Y(s)\,\Delta X^{*}(s).

(4.4)

Observe that
\Delta X^{*}(s)\neq 0
only if
X
attains a new maximum by a jump at time
s
.
In this case,

X^{*}(s)=X(s),\qquad X^{*}(s-)=\sup_{r<s}X(r)\geq X(s-),

so that

Y(s)=1,\qquad Y(s-)=\frac{X(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\leq 1,\qquad\Delta Y(s)=1-Y(s-).

If
\Delta X^{*}(s)=0
, the corresponding summand in (
4.4
) vanishes.
Hence,

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}[Y,X^{*}](s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0<s\leq t\\
\Delta X^{*}(s)>0\end{subarray}}\frac{1-Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,\Delta X^{*}(s).

(4.5)

Decompose
X^{*}
into its continuous and jump parts,

X^{*}=(X^{*})^{c}+\sum_{0<s\leq\cdot}\Delta X^{*}(s).

On the support of
{\mathrm{d}}(X^{*})^{c}
, the running maximum increases continuously, and hence

X(s)=X^{*}(s)
at those times, implying
Y(s-)=1
. Therefore,

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}(X^{*})^{c}(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,d(X^{*})^{c}(s).

For the jump part we have

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,\ d\Big(\sum_{0<u\leq s}\Delta X^{*}(u)\Big)=\sum_{0<s\leq t}\frac{Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,\Delta X^{*}(s).

Combining these identities with (
4.5
) yields

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{Y(s-)}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{*}(s)+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}[Y,X^{*}](s)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{*}(s).

Substituting this equality into (
4.3
) gives

\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X(s)=\frac{X(t)}{X^{*}(t)}-1+\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\mathrm{d}}X^{*}(s),\qquad\text{$P$-a.s.\ for all }t\geq 0.

(4.6)

Fix
t\geq 0
, and work on a sample path outside a
P
-null set on which (
4.6
) holds for all times. Define

\tau:=\inf\{s\in[0,t]:X^{*}(s)=X^{*}(t)\}.

Since
X^{*}
is nondecreasing and càdlàg, we have
\tau\leq t
, and
X^{*}
is constant on
[\tau,t]
.
By definition of
\tau
, either
X(\tau)=X^{*}(\tau)
or
X(\tau-)=X^{*}(\tau-)=X^{*}(t)
.

If
X(\tau)=X^{*}(\tau)
, then applying (
4.6
) at time
\tau
yields

\int_{(0,\tau]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X(s)=\int_{(0,\tau]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

Since
X^{*}
is constant on
[\tau,t]
, it follows that

\int_{(0,\tau]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

If
X(\tau-)=X^{*}(\tau-)=X^{*}(t)
, then, letting
t\uparrow\tau
in (
4.6
) gives

\int_{(0,\tau)}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X(s)=\int_{(0,\tau)}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

Since
X^{*}
is constant on
[\tau,t]
and
\Delta X^{*}(\tau)=0
, we obtain

\int_{(0,\tau)}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s)=\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

Therefore, in either case,

\left(\int_{(0,\cdot]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X(s)\right)^{*}(t)\geq\int_{(0,t]}\frac{1}{X^{*}(s-)}\,{\rm d}X^{*}(s).

Combining this with (
4.2
) completes the proof.
∎

Although the next result does not provide a quantitative bound in terms of an exponential function, we refer to it as a stochastic Grönwall result, as it captures the typical application of the Grönwall inequality.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
H,U
and
V
be separable Hilbert spaces, and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of
H
-valued processes of the form

Y_{n}(t)={S_{n}(t)}\left(\int_{(0,t]}F_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}G_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)+C_{n}(t)\qquad\text{for all }t\geq 0,

where, for some constants
K_{1},K_{2}>0
, the following conditions hold:

(1)

the sequence
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of
\mathcal{L}(V,H)
-valued, strongly continuous, deterministic functions satisfying
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert S_{n}(t)\right\rVert_{V\rightarrow H}\leq 1
;

(2)

the sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of
V
-valued,
P
-a.s. Bochner integrable, predictable processes satisfying, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
, that
F_{n}^{*}(t)\leq K_{1}Y_{n}^{*}(t-)
;

(3)

the sequence
(G_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of
L_{2}(U,V)
-valued,
L
-integrable, predictable processes satisfying, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\geq 0
, that
G_{n}^{*}(t)\leq K_{2}Y_{n}^{*}(t-)
;

(4)

the sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
consists of
H
-valued, càdlàg, adapted processes converging to
0
uniformly on compacts in probability.

Then
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
uniformly on compacts in probability.

Proof.

(The proof originates from
[
17
]
).
We first prove the claim under the additional assumption that there exists a sequence
(\epsilon_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of strictly positive reals converging to
0
and satisfying

\displaystyle\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert C_{n}(t)\right\rVert\leq\epsilon_{n}\qquad\text{a.s.\ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.7)

To this end, we define a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
V
-valued processes, and another sequence
(D_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
-valued processes for all
t\geq 0
by

\displaystyle Z_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=\int_{(0,t]}F_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}G_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}L(s);

\displaystyle D_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=Y_{n}(t)-C_{n}(t)=S_{n}(t)Z_{n}(t).

Since
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of
V
-valued semimartingales, it follows from
[
18
, Co. 26.6]
that the expression
\left\lVert Z_{n}\right\rVert^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}
defines a positive semimartingale for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using the elementary fact that the function
\ln
is increasing, and applying Lemma
4.3
to the strictly positive semimartingale
\left\lVert Z_{n}\right\rVert^{2}+\epsilon_{n}^{2}
demonstrates, for each
t>0
, that

\displaystyle{\ln\left(\epsilon_{n}^{-2}\left(D_{n}^{*}(t)\right)^{2}+1\right)}

for all

the adjoint of

Combining Equations (

Lemma 4.5
.

Equation (

Proof.

To prove that

has càdlàg trajectories for each

[
22
, Th. I.8.1]
to conclude that there exists a Hilbert space
\hat{H}
such that

with an isometric embedding

for all

\hat{H}
to

gives that, for every

\displaystyle\Lambda(X)(t)

\displaystyle\;=\pi\hat{S}(t)iX_{0}+\int_{(0,t]}\pi\hat{S}(t-s)iF(X(s))\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}\pi\hat{S}(t-s)iG(X(s-))\,{\rm d}L(s)

\displaystyle\;=\pi\hat{S}(t)iX_{0}+\pi\hat{S}(t)\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iF(X(s))\,{\rm d}s+\pi\hat{S}(t)\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iG(X(s-))\,{\rm d}L(s)

\displaystyle\;=\pi\hat{S}(t)\left(iX_{0}+\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iF(X(s))\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iG(X(s-))\,{\rm d}L(s)\right).

(4.14)

Since

\hat{H}
-valued Lebesgue integral process

By Theorem 7.4 in
[

\Lambda:(D_{H},d^{\rm ucp})\rightarrow(D_{H},d^{\rm ucp})

Lemma 4.7

\lambda_{n}
results in the identity

\lambda_{n}X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(t)=\pi\hat{S}(t)\left(\int_{(0,t]}A_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}B_{n}(s)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)+C_{n}(t),

(4.19)

where

\displaystyle A_{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{n}\hat{S}(-s)i\Big(F\left(X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(s-)\right)-F(0)\Big);

\displaystyle B_{n}(s)

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{n}\hat{S}(-s)i\Big(G\left(X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(s-)\right)-G(0)\Big);

\displaystyle C_{n}(t)

\displaystyle:=\lambda_{n}\pi\hat{S}(t)\left(ix_{0}+\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iF(0)\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}\hat{S}(-s)iG(0)\,{\rm d}L(s)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\lambda_{n}\Big(X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(t)-\Lambda\left(X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}\right)(t)\Big).

Letting
Y_{n}(t):=\lambda_{n}X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(t)
, Lipschitz continuity implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{n}(s)\right\rVert\leq\lambda_{n}c_{F}\left\lVert X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(s-)\right\rVert=c_{F}\left\lVert Y_{n}(s-)\right\rVert,

where
c_{F}
is the Lipschitz constant of
F
. By a completely analogous computation for
B_{n}
, and using that the semigroup
\hat{S}
is strongly continuous, we arrive at the estimates

A_{n}^{*}(t)\leq c_{F}Y_{n}^{*}(t-)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad B_{n}^{*}(t)\leq c_{G}Y_{n}^{*}(t-).

(4.20)

Since by the definition of
X
we have

\left\lVert X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}(t)-\Lambda\left(X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T}\right)(t)\right\rVert<\epsilon\qquad\text{for all }\,t\geq 0,n\in\mathbb{N},

we obtain that the sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
uniformly on compacts in probability. By Proposition
4.4
, the sequence
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
uniformly on compacts in probability, which further implies that the sequence
(\lambda_{n}X^{\tau_{n}\wedge T})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
H
-valued processes also converges to
0
uniformly on compacts in probability. Lemma
2.2
shows that the collection
(X^{*}(\tau_{n}\wedge T))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables is bounded in probability, which means

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P(X^{*}(\tau_{n}\wedge T)>m)=0.

Standard arguments show

\displaystyle P\left(X^{*}(T\wedge\tau)=\infty\right)

\displaystyle=P\left(\bigcap_{m=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\{X^{*}\left(T\wedge\tau_{n}\right)>m\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}P\Big(X^{*}(T\wedge\tau_{n})>m\Big)=0.

Step 2:
We complete the proof by establishing

P\left(\tau=\infty\right)=1
.
Fix
T>0
. Since
X(t)=X_{n}(t)
for all
t<\tau_{n+1}
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Lemma
4.7
yields

\displaystyle X(\omega,\tau_{n})=X_{n}(\omega,\tau_{n})=\Lambda(X_{n-1})(\omega,\tau_{n})=\Lambda(X)(\omega,\tau_{n})\qquad{\text{for all }\omega\in\{\tau\leq T\}}.

Applying Remark
4.6
together with part (c) of Lemma
3.7
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}}\,X(\tau_{n})&=\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}\pi\hat{S}(\tau_{n})\bigg(ix_{0}+\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau_{n}]}(s)\hat{S}(-s)iF(X(s))\,{\rm d}s\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau_{n}]}(s)\hat{S}(-s)iG(X(s-))\,{\rm d}L(s)\bigg).\end{split}

(4.21)

By Step 1 and Lipschitz continuity, the processes

\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau\wedge T]}(t)\,\hat{S}(-t)i\,F(X(t)):\,t\geq 0\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\bigl(\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau\wedge T]}(t)\,\hat{S}(-t)i\,G(X(t-)):\,t\geq 0\bigr)

are integrable for every
T\geq 0
; the former in the Lebesgue sense, the latter stochastically with respect to
L
by Remark
3.3
. Moreover, the first of these processes dominates the integrand of the first integral in (
4.21
), while the
m_{L}
-functional of the second process dominates the
m_{L}
-functional of the integrand in the second integral in (
4.21
). Hence, by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and Theorem
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}}\,X(\tau_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad={\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}}\,\pi\hat{S}(\tau)\Bigg(ix_{0}+\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau\wedge T]}(s)\,\hat{S}(-s)i\,F(X(s))\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{(0,T]}\mathbb{1}_{(0,\tau\wedge T]}(s)\,\hat{S}(-s)i\,G(X(s-))\,\mathrm{d}L(s)\Bigg).

In particular, the sequence
\bigl({\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}}\,X(\tau_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability.

Since
\tau_{n}<\tau_{n+1}
and
X_{n}
is constant on
[\tau_{n},\infty)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it follows
from the definition of
X
and
X_{n}
that

\|X(\tau_{n+1})-X(\tau_{n})\|\ =\|X_{n+1}(\tau_{n+1})-X_{n}(\tau_{n})\|=\|\Lambda(X_{n})(\tau_{n+1})-X_{n}(\tau_{n+1})\|\geq\varepsilon

P
-a.s. on
\{\tau\leq T\}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle(1\wedge\epsilon)\,P(\tau\leq T)

\displaystyle\leq E\!\left[\bigl\|\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}\bigl(X(\tau_{n+1})-X(\tau_{n})\bigr)\bigr\|\wedge 1\right].

Since
\bigl({\mathbb{1}_{\{\tau\leq T\}}}\,X(\tau_{n})\bigr)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is Cauchy in
L^{0}_{P}(\Omega;H)
, the right side converges to zero as
n\to\infty
, and hence
P(\tau\leq T)=0
.

Since
P(\tau\leq T)=0
for all
T>0
, we conclude
P(\tau=\infty)=1
.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of processes in
D_{H}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm ucp}(X_{n},\Lambda(X_{n}))=0.

Then, there exists a process
X\in D_{H}
so that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}d^{\rm ucp}(X_{n},X)=0

and
X=\Lambda(X)
.

Proof.

It suffices to establish that there exists a process
X\in D_{H}
such that the following convergences hold:

\Lambda(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(i)}}}{{=}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda(X_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(ii)}}}{{=}}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm(iii)}}}{{=}}X,

where the limits are in the topology of uniform convergence in probability on compact intervals. The fact that
{\rm(ii)}
holds is a direct consequence of our assumptions, provided at least one of the two limits exists. Hence, it remains only to establish that there exists a process
X\in D_{H}
such that
{\rm(i)}
and
{\rm(iii)}
both hold.

For each
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the processes

\displaystyle D_{m,n}

\displaystyle:=X_{m}-X_{n}-\Lambda(X_{m})+\Lambda(X_{n})

\displaystyle A_{m,n}

\displaystyle:=F(X_{m})-F(X_{n})

\displaystyle B_{m,n}

\displaystyle:=G(X_{m})-G(X_{n}).

It follows that

\displaystyle X_{m}(t)-X_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\Lambda(X_{m})(t)-\Lambda(X_{n})(t)+D_{m,n}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,t]}S(t-s)A_{m,n}(s-)\,{\rm d}s+\int_{(0,t]}S(t-s)B_{m,n}(s-)\,{\rm d}L(s)+D_{m,n}(t),

where
d^{\rm ucp}(D_{m,n},0)\rightarrow 0
as
m,n\rightarrow\infty
and

\displaystyle A_{m,n}^{*}(s-)

\displaystyle\leq c_{F}(X_{m}-X_{n})^{*}(s-)

\displaystyle B_{m,n}^{*}(s-)

\displaystyle\leq c_{G}(X_{m}-X_{n})^{*}(s-).

Using an argument analogous to that in Proposition
4.9
, the dilation theorem
[
22
, Th. I.8.1]
enables us to apply Proposition
4.4
and conclude that
X_{m}-X_{n}\rightarrow 0
in probability uniformly on compacts as
m,n\rightarrow\infty
. Since
D_{H}
is complete with respect to convergence in probability uniformly on compact intervals, there exists a process
X\in D_{H}
such that
X_{n}\rightarrow X
in probability uniformly on compacts as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This establishes the existence of a process
X\in D_{H}
for which
{\rm(iii)}
holds. Finally, combining
{\rm(iii)}
with Lemma
4.5
yields
\Lambda(X_{n})\rightarrow\Lambda(X)
in probability uniformly on compacts as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which establishes
{\rm(i)}
.
∎

Lemma
4.10
allows us to approximate the solution of the evolution equation (
4.1
). Indeed, the proof of Proposition
4.9
explicitly constructs the processes
X_{n}
pathwise. Lemma
4.10
then establishes that these processes converge to the solution of (
4.1
). This reasoning forms the basis of the following proof of our main result, Theorem
4.2
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

By Proposition
4.9
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a process
X_{n}\in D_{H}
such that
\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lVert X_{n}(t)-\Lambda(X_{n})(t)\right\rVert<1/n
. Hence, Lemma
4.10
implies that there exists a process
X\in D_{H}
such that
X=\Lambda(X)
and
X_{n}\rightarrow X
in probability uniformly on compact intervals as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,
X
is a fixed point of
\Lambda
and hence a mild solution of Equation (
4.1
).

To establish uniqueness, assume that
X\in D_{H}
and
Y\in D_{H}
are both mild solutions of Equation (
4.1
), that is,
\Lambda(X)=X
and
\Lambda(Y)=Y
. Define a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
D_{H}
by

\displaystyle Z_{n}=\begin{cases}X\quad\text{if }\,n\,\text{ is odd};\\
Y\quad\text{if }\,n\,\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

It follows from the definition of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that
Z_{n}-\Lambda(Z_{n})\rightarrow 0
in probability uniformly on compact intervals as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Thus, by Lemma
4.10
,
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges in probability uniformly on compact intervals, hence subsequences
(Z_{2n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Z_{2n-1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converge in probability uniformly on compact intervals to the same limit. Consequently, we must have that
X=Y
, that is
X
and
Y
are indistinguishable.
∎
