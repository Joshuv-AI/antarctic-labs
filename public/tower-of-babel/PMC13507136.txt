Digital technology adoption and clean energy transition among rural households in China
Authors: Wen Wang, Yi Zhang, Risheng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Digital technology has changed how rural households allocate factors and the environment they make decisions in. Yet, we do not fully understand how digital adoption can lead to a household energy transition. The micro survey data utilized in this study are from the Hundred Villages and Thousand Households project, Jiangxi Province, China. It utilizes ordered probit models and propensity score matching to study the link between digital technology adoption and the intensity of rural households’ clean energy consumption. The estimates show a positive and statistically significant association, and the matching results remain consistent after adjusting for observable differences between adopters and non-adopters. Mechanism analyses suggest two pathways. Digital adoption is associated with a higher non-farm employment share, consistent with higher time opportunity costs of collecting traditional fuels and improved budget capacity, and with stronger social capital, consistent with stronger peer influence and lower transaction frictions in adopting clean energy. The association is strongest for cooking energy, followed by space heating, and weakest for water heating. In addition, information acquisition use is more strongly associated with clean energy consumption than social entertainment use. These findings suggest that narrowing gaps in digital access and effective use may complement efforts to accelerate rural household clean energy transitions in China.

Introduction

Under the global agenda of climate governance and energy transition, progress toward the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals remains uneven. However, the uptake of clean fuels and technologies in rural areas of developing economies remains incomplete1. According to Tracking SDG7: The Energy Progress Report 2024, about 2.1 billion people worldwide lacked access to clean cooking fuels and technologies in 2022. Large urban–rural gaps persist, with rural access to clean cooking at about 54%2. China is the world’s foremost energy consumer. This development prioritizes that rural households utilize energy efficiently. Moreover, the initiative falls within the ambit to achieve carbon neutrality3,4. Although the rural electrification and infrastructure has improved the household energy use in rural China still reflects the long-term coexistence of modern commercial energy and traditional biomass5. Households have constraints which result in limited optimization and a trade-off between health-affordability, convenience and fuel-acquisition costs. Hence the persistence is not just a supply-side phenomenon. Continuous use of solid fuels is linked to the indoor air pollution and health effects6,7. The external costs are unable to be fully internalized and they are one of the most crucial barriers for improving rural environmental sustainability8,9. Identifying the exogenous factors which relax the household constraints and change their opportunity sets would help improve the transition10,11 in the rural energy.

Mainly the current literature base on household endowments and external environments. Energy ladder hypothesis-driven work finds income growth to be an important driver of energy upgrading11,12. Furthermore, educational attainment, household size, and access to infrastructure are also important explanatory variables13–16. Nonetheless, much of this literature focuses on traditional socioeconomic correlates and pays little attention to how adoption of digital technologies reshapes rural livelihood choices and constraints. The systematic implementation of the “Internet Plus” strategy and the improvement of the basic digital infrastructure have reduced the information friction and transaction cost in rural China. The rural Internet users reached to 322mn with a rural Internet penetration rate of 69.2%, as per CNNIC17. As digital technology proliferate, they are increasingly incorporated into rural livelihood strategies, social networks and consumption preferences18,19. Recent studies also show that digital development is closely linked to energy and environmental outcomes in China. For example, digital infrastructure construction improves energy efficiency20, and the digital economy develops in coordination with ecological resilience21.

Prior studies have established the correlation between using the internet and the household welfare as well as pro-environmental behaviour, however, the micro-level mechanism through which digital technology affects rural household energy transition remains under-explained22–24. Previous evidence has stressed the informational function of digital access. Nonetheless, systematic empirical research investigating the deeper mechanisms of factor reallocations remains scarce. In practice, digital technology use may influence household energy choices through a livelihood pathway by facilitating non-farm employment and reshaping intra-household labor allocation, which can relax liquidity constraints and increase the time opportunity cost of collecting traditional fuels25–27. It may also operate through a social capital pathway by strengthening social networks and peer effects, thereby lowering perceived uncertainty and transaction frictions in adopting clean energy9,14,28–32. In addition, much of the literature focuses on cooking energy and often relies on binary indicators, which may obscure heterogeneity across end uses and the prevalence of energy stacking behaviors5. Finally, digital technology is used for heterogeneous purposes, and evidence remains scarce on whether information-oriented use and social interaction-oriented use differentially shape household energy decisions. This study addresses these gaps. It examines the micro-level mechanisms of non-farm employment and social capital through which digital technology use shapes rural household energy choices, rather than the informational function alone. It also moves beyond a binary cooking indicator by using a composite index across cooking, water heating, and space heating, and it distinguishes information-oriented use from social interaction-oriented use. In doing so, the study clarifies not only whether digital technology use matters for the rural energy transition, but also how and through which pathways it works.

Research design

Data

This study uses data from the 2021 ‘Hundred Villages and Thousand Households’ survey on rural revitalization in Jiangxi Province, China. Fieldwork was conducted between December 2021 and January 2022. The survey was administered through face-to-face interviews by trained enumerators under faculty supervision following standardized training and a pilot assessment. The questionnaire collected household- and village-level information, including demographics, land endowments, production activities, and consumption patterns. The first-round survey covered 1,080 households from 12 counties across 108 villages. After dropping observations with missing values on the outcome and key covariates, the final analytic sample includes 853 rural households.

Variable definitions

Dependent variable: clean energy consumption

The main variable studied here is clean energy consumption. In rural China, the energy sources used by households include firewood, straw, coal, electricity, solar energy, biogas, liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), and piped natural gas. This study divides these sources into two groups. Firewood, straw, and coal are treated as non-clean energy. Electricity, solar energy, biogas, LPG, and piped natural gas are treated as clean energy.

The classification is based on the pollutants released when a fuel is burned in the home, not on whether the fuel is fossil or renewable. Firewood, straw, and coal are solid fuels. When they are burned indoors, they release high levels of fine particulate matter and carbon monoxide, which harm household air quality and health. LPG and piped natural gas are fossil fuels, but they burn more completely and release far fewer indoor pollutants. For this reason, the World Health Organization39 and the International Energy Agency40 treat LPG and natural gas as clean household fuels, and this study follows the same standard. Town gas from coal gasification was not recorded as a separate fuel type in the survey, so it does not affect the classification used here. Clean energy consumption is measured in three areas, namely cooking, water heating, and space heating. The variable takes the value 0 if a household uses clean energy in none of the three areas, 1 if in one area, 2 if in two areas, and 3 if in all three areas. It is therefore an ordered categorical variable with values 0, 1, 2, and 3.

Independent variable: digital technology use

The primary explanatory variable is digital technology use. Following Zhang and Zhang41, household use of internet-enabled digital devices is used as a proxy. It is measured by a binary indicator based on the survey question ‘Do you use a smartphone or computer?’ The indicator equals 1 for ‘yes’ and 0 otherwise. To further distinguish usage purposes, two additional binary indicators are constructed for social interaction and information acquisition based on the corresponding survey items. Each indicator equals 1 if the household reports the relevant use and 0 otherwise.

Mediator variables: non-farm employment and social capital

Non-farm employment is measured as the proportion of household labor engaged in non-agricultural activities. Social capital is proxied by perceived access to emergency financial support. It is measured using the survey item ‘How many neighbors can lend money if someone in the family is sick and needs 5000 yuan urgently’. Responses are coded from 1 to 5, with higher values indicating stronger social networks and greater access to informal support. The survey did not provide consistent information on organizational membership or frequency of interaction, so this item was used instead. It was answered by all households and reflects the part of social capital that matters most for energy adoption, namely whether a household can actually draw on its network to act.

Control variables

Control variables encompass individual, household, and village characteristics. Individual controls include the household head’s gender, age, education level, and experience in village cadre positions. Household controls comprise family size, cultivated land area, forestland area, credit access, poverty status, and consumption levels. Cultivated land area and forestland area are included because they reflect a household’s access to traditional biomass at low or zero cost. Forestland area is related to the availability of firewood, and cultivated land area is related to the availability of crop residue such as straw. Both reduce the relative cost of traditional fuels and may therefore slow the shift to clean energy. Village controls include economic development level, distance to the township government, and electricity pricing mechanisms.

Descriptive statistical results

With regards to the objective of using digital technologies, a majority of 57.8% of households use it mainly for socialising, followed by 25.2 per cent for accessing information.

that do not.

Propensity score matching

Households consciously choose to use digital technology based on their own skills, needs, economic conditions, and other factors. There may be self-selection bias if Logit or Probit models are used directly to estimate the influence of digital technology use on clean energy consumption. To address this issue, Propensity Score Matching (PSM), developed by Rosenbaum and Rubin in 198343, is employed in this paper. PSM is a common method for addressing self-selection bias and is suitable for causal inference with sampled data. In this paper, four mainstream matching methods of nearest neighbor matching (“one-to-one matching” with n = 1), nearest neighbor matching within calipers(r = 0.04), radius matching, and kernel matching are utilized to ensure the robustness of the findings. Empirical results

Impacts of digital technology use on clean energy consumption

Table 3 presents the estimation results regarding the influence of digital technology on household clean energy consumption. Model 1 examines the univariate relationship. Models 2 through 4 progressively incorporate controls for individual, household, and village-level characteristics. Across all specifications, the coefficients for digital technology remain positive and statistically significant at the 1% level. This robustly confirms Hypothesis 1. In Model 4, the coefficient on digital technology use is positive and statistically significant, indicating a higher propensity to be in a higher clean-energy consumption-intensity category among users.

Regarding positive determinants, educational attainment, village cadre experience, daily consumption levels, and tiered electricity pricing significantly promote clean energy adoption. Higher education is associated with greater awareness of the health and environmental benefits. This cognitive advantage fosters a preference for clean fuels. Village cadre experience implies greater familiarity with national energy transition policies and a stronger sense of social responsibility. Consequently, cadres act as community role models for energy upgrading. Higher daily consumption levels prompt households to optimize energy structures to manage expenditures. Tiered electricity pricing mechanisms incentivize energy conservation and emission reduction, thereby encouraging the shift toward clean energy.

Conversely, age, land endowments (cultivated and forest), and poverty status exert significant negative effects. Aging inhibits the abandonment of traditional solid fuels due to habit inertia and path dependence. Extensive landholdings provide abundant, zero-marginal-cost biomass resources such as straw and firewood. This resource availability reduces the economic incentive to switch fuels. A history of poverty reflects persistent budget constraints. These financial limitations hinder the ability to invest in or afford clean energy alternatives.

Gender, family size, credit access, village economic development, and distance do not exhibit statistically significant impacts. This suggests these factors are not primary determinants of energy consumption variation within this specific sample context.

Mechanisms

Tables 6 and 7 report the results of the mechanism analysis. Because the outcome variable is ordinal, Eq. (16) is estimated by ordered probit, in line with the baseline model. The total effect of digital technology use on clean energy consumption is then divided into a direct effect and an indirect effect with the KHB method, and the indirect effects are tested with bootstrap based on 1,000 replications.

Table 6 reports the effect of digital technology use on the two mediators. Digital technology use has a significant positive effect on both non-farm employment and social capital. The effect is significant at the 5% level for non-farm employment and at the 10% level for social capital. This means that digital technology use raises the non-farm employment of rural households and strengthens their social capital. Table 7 reports the decomposition of the total effect. The total effect is 0.401 and is significant at the 1% level. The direct effect is 0.363 and accounts for 90.5% of the total effect. The indirect effect through non-farm employment is 0.020, and the indirect effect through social capital is 0.018. The bootstrap 95% confidence intervals are [0.003, 0.039] and [0.001, 0.037], and both intervals exclude zero. The total indirect effect of the two channels is 0.038, which accounts for 9.5% of the total effect and is significant at the 5% level. These results support Hypothesis 2. Non-farm employment and social capital both work as channels between digital technology use and clean energy consumption. It should be noted that the indirect effect through social capital is significant only at the 10% level, so the evidence for this channel is weaker than the evidence for non-farm employment, but its direction is consistent with the theoretical expectation.

These results can be explained as follows. Digital technology helps households obtain information on non-farm jobs, and this raises their non-farm employment. Higher non-farm employment eases budget constraints through an income effect. It also raises the time cost of household labor, so households tend to replace self-collected traditional fuels with purchased commercial energy. At the same time, digital technology helps households maintain and expand their social networks. Households with more social capital are more sensitive to peer effects, so they are more likely to adopt clean energy.

Heterogeneity analysis

How can the use of digital technology increase the clean energy consumption of households? As Internet penetration has increased, households’ access to the Internet has grown dramatically. It is already difficult to analyze the influence of digital technology use on clean energy consumption based solely on whether they use it. Therefore, this paper continues with a more detailed portrayal of the purpose of digital technology use to reveal how households can leverage it to enhance clean energy consumption. Table 7 presents the results of examining the impact of two representative Internet activities, socializing and accessing information, on the clean energy consumption of households. The use of digital technology is found to significantly increase clean energy consumption. Model 16 specifically highlights the effect of using digital technology for socializing on household clean energy consumption. The result is significant, passing the 1%significance test. The possible reason for this is that using digital technologies to socialize can enhance households’ social capital and help spread clean energy information. Households with superior social capital are more likely to understand and observe others’ clean energy consumption practices. This, in turn, can influence their own energy consumption behavior, leading to increased consumption of clean energy. The estimation results from Model 17 indicate that using digital technology to access information positively affects clean energy consumption. One possible reason is that digital technology enables households to access valuable information on various aspects of clean energy consumption. By utilizing digital platforms, households can learn about the health benefits, environmental impacts, associated costs, and the availability of clean energy options. Accessing this information can play a crucial role in shaping households’ attitudes and behaviors towards clean energy consumption (Table 8).

Discussion

Using the 2021 “Hundred Villages and Thousand Households” survey data in Jiangxi Province this study empirically examines the impact of using digital technology on the clean energy consumption of rural households. Through the use of ordered Probit models and Propensity Score Matching (PSM), the study emphasizes the multifaceted contribution of digital technology to the rural energy transition. Subsequent sections discuss the internal logic and research contributions in the decision-making of existing literature.The digital technology helps to significantly enhance the rural household clean energy consumption. Controlling for endogeneity, this conclusion is robust.

According to an analysis, households that use digital technologies consume far more clean energy than other ones. The finding of this study is consistent with Ren et al., (2021) and Twumasi et al.,24 by improving access to information through internet technology it will help in energy transition. This research develops a composite index of cooking, space heating and water heating unlike other studies that concentrate mainly on cooking energy. The findings show that digital technology positively affects several household energy scenarios. The household energy system is not the only thing being upgraded as a result of digital technology, but rather the whole system.

In the second place, it recognizes and verifies that the non-farm employment and social capital have a two-fold mediating role. This makes current scholarly understanding of transmission pathways more complex. A narrow through income or social networks in isolation has been the approach of previous literature to the non-farm employment.Regarding the non-farm employment mechanism, digital technology is an instrument for reducing labour-market information asymmetry and promoting non-farm participation. In addition to easing budget constraints through income effects, non-farm work raises opportunity cost of time. According to Jin et al.,27, the implicit costs of non-farm employment; like collecting biomass, firewood increases. This leads households to replace self-collected fuels with purchased energy. This build up encourages the adoption of clean energy through social norms. This aligns with Wen et al.,14 on switching to clean fuels due to peers. As the digital technology is being employed adeptly, it helps in enhancement and maintaining social capital. Conclusions and policy implications

Conclusions

The “Hundred Villages and Thousand Households” project micro-survey data was used in this study. Using ordered Probit and mediation effect models, it systematically analyses the impact of digital technology adoption on rural household clean energy consumption and clarifies the underlying mechanisms. The following is the primary research finding.

First, the adoption of digital technology is associated with higher clean energy use among rural households. The conclusion remains unchanged after control for self-selection bias via Propensity Score Matching(PSM). Households who use digital technology have a clean energy consumption intensity that is 33.3% higher than non-users. This numerical data shows that the digital gap is a limiting factor in the existing rural energy transition.

Mediating mechanisms may take the form of non-farm employment and social capital. For economic mechanisms, digital technology helps reduce information asymmetry in the labour market and allows participation in non-farm employment. This change makes labor more costly and allows budget constraints to ease. This is associated with a shift from traditional biomass to commercial energy. In terms of social mechanism, digital technology helps in the accumulation of social capital by creating and enlarging the networks. Through demonstration mechanisms and peer effects clean energy technologies can get their adoption threshold and transaction costs reduced.

Third, the effects of digital technology show marked heterogeneity in terms of dimensions and motivations. The promoting effect is strongest for cooking energy, followed by space heating, and worst for water-heating in terms of energy types. The health necessities drive cooking energy. The cooking energy is amenable to substitution through digital information channels. By contrast, space heating as well as water heating are subject to much stronger physical constraints imposed by the housing fabric. The lock-in state created by such hardware constraints cannot be fully broken by income or information effect alone. In terms of usage motivation, the effectiveness of energy use transition acceleration is optimal in active information acquisition rather than social entertainment. By their yardstick, it is more important to eliminate information asymmetry and cognitive bias than to create social distance.

Policy Implications

This study makes the following policy recommendations to facilitate the rural energy transition in the context of the digital economy based on.

Develop digital infrastructure in rural areas to remove barriers. The government must further the “Digital Countryside” programme. Efforts to improve network coverage and penetration of terminals in the hinterland should be invested in. The focus of policies now has to be shifted from hardware deployment to reducing data costs and device acquisition costs. These measures aim to make sure that low-income groups benefit from digital dividends. It also prohibits the evolution of the digital divide as one more energy poverty. Utilize digital technologies to stimulate non-farm job growth and upgrade structure. To benefit rural farmers, policymakers must create digital platforms that enable precise matching of labor supply and demand. At the same time, we need to expand digital skills training programs, such as those on e-commerce remote delivery.

Improving rural households’ employability in the digital economy raises the time opportunity cost of collection of traditional fuels. This provides economic reasoning for replacing biomass with clean energy. That will build targeted service system of Internet+energy popularization In light of the effective information acquisition efficiency driving transition, authorities should establish clean energy promotion platforms compatible with rural usage habits. Clean energy’s health and economic benefits should be intuitively explained through short videos and social media campaigns. Digital storytelling interrupts existing energy use patterns path dependence by steering people away from cognitive biases. Utilize the Exhibit Function of Digital Social Capital Promotion of household photovoltaic systems or clean heating technologies should prioritise the training of village cadres and digital elites as “model households”. Showing the effect of their use in Wechat groups and village affairs platforms utilises the trust mechanism of acquaintances society. This strategy helps to allay adoption concerns among ordinary rural households through neighborhood effects.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the data are cross-sectional. Propensity score matching addresses selection on observable characteristics, but it cannot fully rule out reverse causality or unobservable confounders, so the estimates are best read as robust associations rather than causal effects. Second, the proxy for social capital captures the resource mobilization aspect of social networks but does not fully reflect its trust and norm dimensions. Third, the health status of household members is not included as an explanatory variable, because health is more likely an outcome of energy choice than an exogenous determinant and the survey did not collect a suitable measure. Fourth, the sample covers one province in a single year, which limits external validity. Future research could use panel data and instrumental variable methods, richer measures of social capital and health, and broader geographic coverage.
