ABSTRACT
The exposure of electric distribution systems to environmental risks, like wild fire, and population
related vulnerabilities can benefit from a spatial risk analysis framework that can inform utility
actions such as increased vegetation management or other maintenance actions as well as provide
data for resilience-oriented investments such as storage enabled microgirds.
This work develops and applies a data-driven GIS-based methodology to a medium-voltage service
area (an electric distribution system) in north-central New Mexico. Publicly available datasets and
proprietary utility data are connected to spatially quantized zones representing single feeder areas.
For each zone, single metrics related to outage information, critical infrastructure, wild fire danger
and other risk indicators are computed and normalized. Then, composite metrics are formed by
weighted combinations of single metrics to form a more complete risk profile. Composite metrics
are then also weighted and combined to form derived metrics to highlight zones where multiple
risks intersect.
High level results indicated the North-West of the area study as having the highest fire risk, while
the South-East was the most impacted by outages during 2025. Additionally, the central part of the
system was identified as having the most critical infrastructure and at-risk population demographics.
A single micro-grid case study showed how the calculated rankings can help prioritize storage
deployment to meet various resilience goals.
The presented framework is transparent, reproducible, customizable, and transferable to other
locations that would like to better understand spatial risks present to their area of operation.

1. INTRODUCTION

Electric grids are exposed to various risks inherent to surrounding area characteristics. As electric
systems often span vast geographic regions, specific local risks to sections of the system may not
be fully understood, understood in a way related to the rest of the system, or understood at a spatial
resolution that can help direct community or utility action. This is simply because each feeder,
neighborhood, or community receiving electric service has its own set of interconnected physical
assets, environmental features, and population demographics that all contribute to risk exposure.
For example, one electric circuit may be completely or partially enclosed in a national forest area
and require a different vegetation management approach than a system that exists entirely outside
of a national forest. These differing management approaches may impact the risk of fire and
thus, increase the risk of public safety power shutoff events. Further, depending on what critical
infrastructure may be receiving service from a circuit at high risk of disconnection, there may be
additional cascading impacts to available community services or even a reduction in the ability of
a location to respond to certain events. However, knowing about such risks may allow planners to
mitigate possible issues by investigating energy storage solutions for critical infrastructure in high
risk locations or pursuing other preventative actions.
This work aims to identify various high risk locations and increase the general understanding
of spatial risks by utilizing a data-driven framework to calculate and rank multiple risk metrics
associated with unique service zones of a medium voltage distribution system. Known issues in
the area of study involve wildfire risk, vulnerable and tribal populations, and ensuring continued
service to critical infrastructure during outage events. Publicly available data from sources like the
US Census and US Geological Survey and proprietary data provided by the regional electric utility
will be the initial inputs for this work. Resulting risk metrics will be presented as maps, however,
the underlying data can also be numerically analyzed to facilitate comparisons between locations
within the area of study.
The ability to quantify spatial risks in this way will allow stake holders to make informed and
defensible decisions regarding infrastructure planning and maintenance which ultimately impacts
the security and reliability of electric service provided to the community.

2. METHODOLOGY

To accomplish the goals of this work, an area of interest was defined, and then individual zones
within that area were also defined to later rank and compare among each other. In this case, an entire
distribution system was chosen as the area of interest, and individual electrical circuits, or feeders,
were chosen as the specific zones to compare. Further details about this process are presented in
Chapter 3.
To more accurately direct the collection of data, initial risks and goals from this work were collected
from the utility. These risks included wildfire danger leading to a public safety power shut off and
handling vegetation management near lines that run through national forest land. The main goal of
the utility is to provide consistent and reliable power to their customers.
This input led to data collection about national forest boundaries, fire risk metrics, utility outage
data, critical infrastructure (CI) locations, and various Census statistics. After the raw data was
collected, it was then associated to a single feeder zone and normalized. Chapter 4 describes the
required processing for each data source and presents these initial metrics. These initial single
metrics were then used to produce multiple weighted combinations of risk intended to provide a
customized metrics that could more clearly inform actions. A generalized flow chart of the entire
process is shown in Figure 2-1 which highlights main actions taken and how area rankings are
available from all computed metrics.

3. AREA OF STUDY

The area of study for this work was the electrical distribution system of Kit Carson Electric
Cooperative (KCEC) which serves over 29,000 customers in north central New Mexico [1]. Figure
3-1 shows over the 5,500 miles of system lines covering roughly 5,000 square miles. The different
colors in the image represent groupings of the utility provided GIS (geographic information system)
data by electrical circuit identifier, or feeder.

 Figure 3-1. Line topology of service area feeders.

 19

Service areas for each feeder were created by buffering grouped lines by 1 mile. This was done to
capture characteristics surrounding each line in the following analysis. The buffered lines are shown
in Figure 3-2. At this resolution, overlapping service areas of distribution feeders is common. Even
in this relatively rural setting, there are situations that result with multiple feeders running along
side each other, but are serving different areas. Due to this spatial overlap, and without specific load
connection information, it can be difficult to distinguish which circuit may be providing service to
a specific area where multiple feeders exist.

 Figure 3-2. Line topology with 1 mile buffer.

 20

To handle service area overlap, a 1 km grid was created over the entire area of interest, and number
of feeders present in each grid square were counted. The result of this spatial ’quantizing’ is shown
in Figure 3-3. Groups of grid cells were created based on cells that served the same single feeder.
After grouping, there were 34 single feeder zones identified, as shown in Figure 3-4. While the
presented single feeder zones may be appear small or disjointed, the actual service area of each
feeder is continuous and typically larger, but may overlap, or be near to another feeder, which
disqualifies that location as being fed by a single feeder.

4. SINGLE METRICS

This chapter focuses on data collection and processing required to form single metrics, or a metric
that is based on a single data point. Single metrics created for this work involve utility outage and
customer data, publicly available infrastructure data, Census created demographic data, and USGS
provided wild fire data.

4.1. Reported Outage Data

Reported outage data from 2025 was provided by KCEC. The data was processed so that feeder
names could be used to group and analyze outage events. Reports identified at the substation
or higher level were not included in this analysis as it wasn’t clear how to properly de-aggregate
outages to the feeder level.
The collected data of interest were maximum duration outage, total number of reported outages,
and average duration of outage. Figures 4-1, 4-2, and 4-3 present these metrics, respectively. Each
result was normalized to the available data set to more clearly identify areas that were impacted
more by outage events in 2025. From a high level, the figures show that the south-east experienced
more outage impact than the rest of the system.

4.2. Critical Infrastructure

Most critical infrastructure (CI) data used in this work was collected using GeoCricket [2] before
August 2025, when the Homeland Infrastructure Foundation-Level Data (HIFLD) Open [3] was
discontinued. Collected infrastructure types included: banks, cellular towers, microwave towers,
child care centers, dialysis centers, hospitals, law enforcement locations, fire stations, nursing
homes, power plants, veterans health administration buildings, public schools, colleges, and others.
Most data points came from the HIFLD, while the next most common source was the US geological
survey (USGS). Figure 4-4 provides the identified CI location, source, and type. Also visible in
the figure is various clusters of CI not associated to any single feeder zone represented by the light
gray area. This is due to the spatial resolution of the zone creation process and lack of detailed load
connection data. While knowing what CI is serviced by what feeder could help better distinguish
feeder rankings, this amount of data loss was deemed acceptable for this work.

 Figure 4-4. Service area critical infrastructure and single feeder zones (shown as grey).

each respective zone and then normalized. As shown in Figure 4-5, the north eastern area had the
highest count of CI. This was due to the ≈ 30 microwave service towers in this area, which are often
placed in concentrated locations at higher altitudes. Figure 4-6 shows the normalized CI variety,
which is slightly less concentrated, with maximums in the south-west and center of the area.

 Figure 4-5. Normalized count of CI per per single feeder zone.

 Figure 4-6. Normalized types of CI per per single feeder zone.

4.3. Fire Related

Fire risk related data is presented in this section which includes maximum routed distance from
fire assistance assets, national forest boundaries, and a USGS calculated wildfire risk index.

4.3.1. Road Routed Distance

A timely response to wildfire situations is often vital to minimize spread and damage. As such, the
further distance a fire responder may have to travel to address the situation could be associated to
more damage a fire could cause. To that end, a routed road distance from points along power lines
to fire response assets was chosen as the first fire risk metric.
Instead of straight line distance, it has been shown that routed road distance may be a more accurate
distance indicator in rural areas [4]. To accomplish such routing, a road network was collected
using the Python package OSMnx [5]. The area of interest for road routing was a bounding box
surrounding all system assets as well as some surrounding roads and nearby fire assistance assets.
The resulting road network model, fire response assets considered, and power line locations is
shown in Figure 4-8.

 Figure 4-8. Routing work input data.

 28

Points along all electric lines were created every 528 feet (1/10th of a mile) to act as destinations
for fire responders and the routed distances to the nearest three straight line distance fire assets
were calculated. The shortest calculated route was associated to each power line location, and then
the furthest distance per single feeder zone was selected to represent the worst-case-scenario for
that zone. After all zones had a maximum distance result, all distances were normalized. The
normalized maximum distance to a fire response asset is shown in Figure 4-9 where the North-West
has the maximum distance while most other areas report much lower normalized distances. This is
likely due to the lack of fire response assets in the North-West, which is visible in 4-8, though not
as pronounced when normalized rank is used to color single feeder zones. It should be noted that
at the time of data collection, sources did not indicate any fire assistance assets in the North-West
of the area of study.

 Figure 4-9. Normalized maximum distance from fire response assets.

 29

4.3.2. National Forest Areas

Vegetation management of national forests is performed by the US Forest Service, but utility lines
must pass through national forest to serve customers. While a thru-way is often provided for lines
to pass though such areas, the larger surrounding area is out of utility control, and as such, may be
at higher risk of fire. Defined national forest boundaries were collected from [6] and are presented
in Figure 4-10. As shown, total overlap of single feeder zones and national forest is over 41%.

 Figure 4-10. National forest service boundaries and single feeder zones.

 30

For each single feeder zone, the amount of overlap with national forest land was identified, and then
a percent of national forest area per zone to zone area was calculated. The resulting percentages
are presented in Figure 4-11, where multiple feeders report over 90% national forest coverage.

 Figure 4-11. Percent of single feeder zones overlapping national forest.

4.3.3. Wildland Fire Potential Index

The Wildland Fire Potential Index (WFPI) is daily updated description of available fuel and other
measurements related to fire potential created by the US Geological Survey [7]. The values
associated with WFPI range from 0-150 and are provided in raster form at a 1 km resolution. An
example of WFPI data for a single day is shown in Figure 4-12, with the area of interest boundaries
projected on top of the WFPI values. It is worth noting that not all 1 km squares have a reported
WFPI value or report a null value. Additionally, some data values above 150, which correspond to
terrain type descriptions, were filtered out of the raw WFPI data. This led to some locations in the
figure being shown as white.

 Figure 4-12. Single day WPFI example with service area outline.

 32

Data for 1 day forecast of WFPI was collected for a full year from [8] and then loaded into a
multi-dimensional array using the Python Xarray [9] package. This allowed for easier spatial
and temporal filtering to single feeder zones of interest. The full year average of WFPI for each
single feeder zone is shown in Figure 4-13. While the average WFPI is presented below, in future
calculations, the normalized version of this metric is used. An observations from these results is
that the Western part of the system tends to have higher WFPI than the Eastern.

 Figure 4-13. Full year WFPI average fore each single feeder zone.

4.4. Census Statistics and Designations

Statistics and other demographic information for this work was collected from the 2024 ACS5
(American Community Survey 5-year Estimate) dataset [10] and then associated to the correspond-
ing census geometry acquired from [11]. Two geometry types, census tract and census block
group, were used for this work as some statistics are only available at larger aggreated areas, while
others exist at a finer resolution. Tribal designated areas were also collected from [11]. All used
geometries and their relation to the earlier defined single feeder zones is shown in Figure 4-14

 Figure 4-14. Geometries from Census provided data.

Specific demographic information collected from the census was percent population over 65, percent
of population in poverty, and percent of population with a disability. While percent of population
over 65 is self explanatory, and represented 18% of the US population in 2024[12]; Population in
poverty or with a disability are slightly more complicated.
The Census calculates poverty according to a defined summation of household income [13] which
is then compared to tables [14] for the corresponding year of interest. For example, in 2024, any
single adult in the United States would be considered to be living in poverty if their annual income
was less than $16,320, while a household of 2 adults and two children would be considered to be
living in poverty if the total household income was less than $31,812. In 2024, the percent of US

5. COMPOSITE METRICS

While single metrics are informative on their own, a weighted combination may provide a more
complete picture of certain risks. For this work, system criticality, outage impact, fire risk, and the
identification of vulnerable pollutions is calculated.

5.1. System Criticality

The composite system criticality metric is composed of three single metrics: number of customer
meters, count of CI, and type of CI. The number of customer meters was scaled by 0.5, while
the other two metrics were weighted by 0.25, and the resulting metric was then itself normalized.
This choice of weightings used here is to illustrate how one may prioritize certain metrics over
others during the combination phase, In this case, the goal was to provide balance between the
infrastructure and customer criticality. The resulting composite criticality metic is shown in Figure
5-1. The highest rank area could be thought of having the most CI and customers in relation to the
rest of the system.

 Figure 5-1. Normalized system criticality.

5.2. Outage History

The single metrics involved in calculating the composite outage impact were: number of outages,
maximum duration of outage, and average outage duration. In this case, all single metrics were
weighted evenly, or, the sum of the three metrics was divided by 3, and then normalized. The
resulting composite metric is shown in Figure 5-2. The most outage impacted area is identified
in the South-East, which was also observed from the individual metrics. Of note is the difference
between the impact scores across the area, which could be used to more clearly identify problem
areas in the region.

8. CONCLUSION

This work demonstrated a data-driven GIS-based methodology that identified and ranked various
risks present on a medium-voltage distribution system. Calculated metrics presented included single
metrics (based on only one data source), composite metrics (defined as a weighted combination
of related single metrics), and derived metrics (weighted combinations of composite metrics).
All computed metrics could be ranked and visualized to inform stake holders of possible risks
present in the area of study or the data analyzed to inform resilience-oriented screening of utility
investments.
In this particular case, it was shown that a certain part of the system (North-West) was unusually
far away from any fire response asset which made it rank higher than other locations for fire risk.
Numerous feeders were identified to be entirely inside of national forest land, which may have
implications regarding vegetation management and the associated fire risk. A simple comparison
of computed metrics from two zones demonstrated how results could help identify possible benefits
of a storage-enabled microgrid compared to other areas in the system which may provide higher
value for continuity of service and critical infrastructure support.
The presented methodology could be modified, refined, and adapted to other locations that would
like to better understand spatial risks in their area, as well as identify possible indicators to better
place critical infrastructure.
