Extracorporeal photopheresis versus systemic immunosuppression for treatment of immune-related adverse events: clinical outcomes from the prospective two-arm PRIA study
Authors: Lisa Wein, Carolin Ertl, Theresa Ruf, Monika Morak, Ying Wang, Christina Schmitt, Xiomara Garza Vazquez, Valerie Glatzel, Richard David-Rus, Mohammed Mitwalli, Jerome Srour, Pia Schöpf, Dirk Tomsitz, Lars E French, Lucie Heinzerling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor-induced immune-related adverse events (irAEs) can be steroid-refractory (sr) or steroid-dependent (sd), requiring second-line therapy. Evidence guiding optimal management of sr/sd-irAEs while preserving antitumor efficacy is scarce. This study compared extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP) with systemic immunosuppressants (IS) for treatment of sr/sd-irAEs.MethodsThis prospective two-arm study included 46 patients (23 ECP, 23 IS) with 12 distinct types of irAEs across 6 tumor entities, all classified as steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent. Toxicities affected the gastrointestinal tract, skin, lung, musculoskeletal system, and serosal membranes, with up to seven prior irAE treatment lines. Patients received six cycles of ECP or investigator’s choice IS over 12 weeks. Longitudinal assessments included clinical irAE outcomes, quality of life (QoL), and tumor response. ECP was more frequently used in patients with multi-toxicity (39% vs 9%; p=0.02) and multi-resistance to prior second-line immunosuppression (39% vs 17%; p=0.19).ResultsAt week 12, ECP showed a lower cumulative corticosteroid exposure (395 mg vs 1,260 mg prednisolone equivalent; p=0.03), significantly improved QoL (p=0.01), and a numerically higher irAE response rate without statistical significance compared with IS (94% vs 81%; p=0.85). In advanced cutaneous melanoma, ECP was associated with superior overall survival (15 vs 10 months; p=0.02), and longer progression-free survival (9 vs 3 months; p=0.01). No significant safety concerns were observed with ECP; one fatality in the IS group was infection-related.ConclusionsECP demonstrated clinical outcomes comparable to IS in sr/sd-irAEs, with significantly lower cumulative corticosteroid exposure and improved QoL. Furthermore, ECP was associated with a favorable safety profile and showed no evidence of compromised antitumor activity. A multicenter trial is planned for further investigation.Trial registration numberNCT05700565.

Introduction

The efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) across various tumor entities has prompted their increased use, including patients with early tumor stages in the adjuvant and neoadjuvant setting.14 About 2–10% of ICI-treated patients develop steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent immune-related adverse events (sr/sd-irAEs), exhibiting resistance to steroids or recurrence on steroid taper, respectively, and can be associated with irreversible organ damage or death.58 Current oncology guidelines from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) recommend escalation to systemic immunosuppressive (IS) therapies such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α inhibitor infliximab or mycophenolate mofetil for sr/sd-irAEs.911 Similar to corticosteroids, IS may induce adverse effects and can compromise antitumor immunity.1215 Despite irAE treatment, cancer patients experience irAE-associated morbidity, long-term complications, and impaired quality of life (QoL) even after cessation of ICI therapy.16 Moreover, the occurrence of irAEs and more specifically sr/sd-irAEs frequently necessitates treatment interruption or permanent discontinuation of ICI therapy.16 17 Regardless of numerous guidelines,911 there is little comparative evidence regarding optimal second-line therapy for sr/sd-irAEs with the aim to maintain tumor control, minimize long-term sequelae, and preserve the QoL. This gap also extends to alternative therapy approaches such as extracorporeal photopheresis (ECP). While prospective clinical studies investigating irAE therapy are scarce, evidence to improve irAE management is obtained through dedicated registries, such as the web-based Side Effect Registry Immuno-Oncology (SERIO; www.serio-registry.org).18 This prospective two-armed study investigated optimal treatment of sr/sd-irAEs comparing IS with ECP. ECP uses leukapheresis, followed by treatment of the collected leukocytes with the photosensitizing agent 8-methoxypsoralen and subsequent irradiation of leukocytes with ultraviolet-A irradiation, inducing DNA crosslinking and apoptosis.19 Immunomodulatory effects of ECP include the modulation of macrophage polarization and shifting T-cell subpopulations towards more tolerogenic phenotypes potentially carrying the risk of reduced antitumor effect, and altering adiponectin signaling.20 21 ECP is an established treatment for cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, autoimmune diseases like lichen ruber, graft-versus-host disease, and solid organ transplant rejection. It demonstrates a favorable safety profile.22 23 The interim analysis of this prospective cohort (n=21) suggested improved clinical outcomes with ECP compared with IS, including a higher irAE response rate (93% vs 80%; p=0.54), improved QoL scores, and numerically longer overall survival (23 vs 12 months; p=0.27), although differences were not statistically significant due to the limited sample size.24 Building on this, the present manuscript reports the final analysis of a larger cohort with extended follow-up, enabling a more comprehensive evaluation of clinical outcomes, including corticosteroid exposure, irAE worsening events, and detailed analyses of organ-specific irAE patterns, multi-toxicity, and multi-resistant irAEs.

Methods

Study design and treatment

This prospective, single-center, open-label study allocated patients 1:1 to two groups using travel distance to the study center as an instrumental variable to minimize treatment selection bias. Patients living within a 2-hour radius of the clinic were assigned to the ECP group, whereas those with longer travel distances were allocated to the second-line systemic immunosuppressant (IS) group. The study was conducted at the Ludwig Maximilian University (LMU) Skin Cancer Center from January 2022 to May 2025. Participants received either ECP using the Therakos Cellex system (Mallinckrodt Pharmaceuticals, Bedminster, New Jersey, USA) biweekly per-protocol or an investigator-chosen IS regimen according to routine prescribing practice and guidelines.9 10 25 The specific agent was selected by the treating physician based on the affected organ system, severity of toxicity, comorbidities and prior treatment history. The distribution of systemic IS agents used in the comparator arm is summarized in table 1. A patient flow diagram illustrating screening, allocation, and follow-up is provided in figure 1A. Continuing prior immunosuppression was allowed at the physician’s discretion, if previously initiated IS therapy was insufficient, but discontinuation was not feasible due to toxicity severity. Patients who experienced clinical benefit were allowed to continue study treatment beyond the 12-week study treatment period. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. ECP was compared with investigator-chosen IS for the treatment of sr/sd-irAEs, based on the hypothesis that ECP would demonstrate superior clinical efficacy and induce distinct changes in the immunophenotype.

Patients

Eligible patients had been treated with at least one ICI (anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), anti-programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), or anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4)) for a histologically confirmed malignancy in the neoadjuvant, adjuvant or metastatic setting alone or in combination with another tumor therapy. Patients needed to exhibit an irAE of any grade (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE V.5.0))26 refractory to corticosteroids (sr)—defined as no symptomatic improvement after at least 72 hours on a prednisolone-equivalent ≥1 mg/kg/day, or an sd-irAE defined as the inability to taper to ≤5 mg prednisolone equivalent/day without symptom recurrence. Resolution of irAEs was defined as CTCAE grade 0. Key exclusion criteria included contraindications to ECP, pregnancy, and body weight less than 40 kg. Multi-toxicity was defined as irAEs affecting more than one organ system simultaneously.

Endpoints and assessments

The primary endpoint comprised changes in immunophenotype; the secondary endpoint was the clinical response of irAEs as defined by CTCAE V.5.0. Exploratory outcomes included cumulative corticosteroid requirement, overall and progression-free survival (OS and PFS), QoL, as well as tumor response according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria V.1.1 at the first staging after study treatment initiation.27 Tumor assessments were performed according to national and European guideline recommendations every 3–6 months depending on tumor type and stage, with a minimum interval of 3 weeks after initiation of study treatment.28 29 At baseline and weeks 1, 4, 8, and 12, detailed monitoring of the adverse event(s), concomitant medications and health-related QoL using the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer QoL Questionnaire Core-30 (EORTC QLQ-C30) as well as comprehensive medical and laboratory evaluations were performed (figure 1B).30 In order to create a homogenous group, tumor outcomes were evaluated for the subgroup of patients with advanced cutaneous melanoma. PFS and OS were defined from the start of irAE to documented tumor progression, until death from any cause or last follow-up. This time point was chosen to specifically evaluate oncologic outcomes following the development of clinically relevant irAE requiring second-line treatment. The minimum follow-up time was 5 months, and the median follow-up time was 13 months. Treatment was discontinued before week 12 in two ECP and four IS patients (figure 1A). The predefined primary endpoint of the prospective study was longitudinal changes in immunophenotype assessed by serial peripheral blood sampling and immune profiling analyses. Interim analysis had shown that these translational analyses require in-depth immune profiling. Thus, these data will be reported separately in a dedicated translational publication. The present manuscript therefore focuses on the clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R V.4.4.3, Microsoft Office Suite, and SPSS (V.20). Continuous data are presented as median (IQR) or mean (SD), and categorical data as percentages. For testing, we used Fisher’s exact test, Mann-Whitney U test with Hodges-Lehmann estimate (HLE) for shift in median location, Wilcoxon test and Student’s t-test; for continuous data we used the non-parametric test in case of failure of normality; statistical significance was defined as a p-value<0.05. We also reported odds ratio (OR), difference in proportions, and the HLE on median location shift together with the corresponding 95% CIs. For the difference in proportion, we reported Newcombe-Wilson 95% CI. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test (LRT); hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% CIs were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. Sample size was based on feasibility considerations, as no published comparative data were available at study initiation to support a formal a priori power calculation in this patient population. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the primary and key exploratory endpoints using the observed effect sizes. For irAE-level analyses, statistical power was additionally adjusted for clustering using the design effect method (DEFF=1 + (k − 1) × intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)), where the ICC was estimated from the observed data via one-way analysis of variance. All power calculations assumed a two-sided α of 0.05 (online supplemental table S1). The trial was designed to screen 55 patients and to enroll 50; due to slower-than-anticipated accrual, 50 patients were screened, resulting in 46 enrollments. Because some patients experienced more than one irAE, irAE-related analyses were conducted at the irAE event level. In total, 59 irAE events occurring in 46 patients were included. To account for the clustering of multiple irAEs within individual patients, irAE-level binary outcomes (response rate, worsening events) were additionally analyzed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure and robust (sandwich) SEs (R package geepack). For the multi-toxicity and multi-resistant irAE subgroups, GEE was not applicable due to the small number of patient-level clusters in the IS arm. Given the exploratory nature of secondary analyses, no adjustment for multiple testing was applied. Results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. An interim analysis was undertaken without prespecified stopping criteria.

Results

Patients and treatment

In this prospective study, outcomes of ICI-induced sr/sd-irAEs were compared between patients treated with ECP versus those receiving IS. Baseline characteristics, including age and tumor stage, were balanced between groups (table 1) and considered representative of the target population, although a higher proportion of patients in the IS group received dual checkpoint inhibition compared with the ECP group (74% vs 39%). Within the IS cohort, seven distinct immunosuppressants were administered, with infliximab being the most frequently employed agent (17/23 patients (74%), table 1). A total of 46 patients with cancer, comprising six distinct tumor entities, were evaluated. Patients exhibited overall 59 sr/sd-irAES with 12 different irAE types affecting the gastrointestinal tract, skin, lungs, musculoskeletal system, and serosal membranes (table 1). The simultaneous occurrence of multiple irAE (multi-toxicity) was more frequent in the ECP group (9/23 (39%) of patients; overall 33 irAE) compared with the IS group (2/23 (9%) of patients; overall 26 irAE; p=0.02; OR, 6.96 (95% CI 1.17 to 75.9; table 1). Furthermore, 39% of patients treated in the ECP group (9/23) versus 17% of patients treated in the IS group (4/23) showed resistance to second-line irAE IS therapies before study start, that is, so-called multi-resistant irAEs (table 1). Consequently, the ECP group had a cumulative total of 16 previously failed IS therapies prior to study commencement, whereas the IS group had nine (p=0.19; OR, 2.98 (95% CI 0.66 to 16.09); table 1). Adequate statistical power (>80%) was achieved for cumulative corticosteroid exposure and irAE worsening, whereas other endpoints showed limited power (online supplemental table S1).

Therapy of steroid-refractory and steroid-dependent irAE

By conclusion of the 12-week study period, 94% (31/33) of irAEs were resolved or improved in the ECP group compared with 81% (21/26) in the IS group (p (GEE analysis)=0.85; OR, 1.21 (95% CI 0.170 to 8.700); figure 2A). While at baseline, the median severity of irAEs in both groups was CTCAE grade 3 (IQR 2–3); in week 12, the ECP group demonstrated a median CTCAE grade of 0 (IQR 0–1) (p=9.191e−07), and the IS group 0.25 (IQR 0–1) (p=6.052e−05; figure 2E). At last follow-up (median follow-up 13 months, minimum 5 months), the ECP group showed complete resolution in 70% of irAEs (23/33), resolution with sequelae in 15% (5/33), and improvement in 9% (3/33) compared with the IS group with 62% (16/26) complete resolution, 11% (3/26) resolution with sequelae, and 11% (3/26) improvement (figure 2A). In the IS cohort, ongoing symptoms at last follow-up were noted in 12% (3/26) of cases, and irAE-related mortality remained 4% (1/26). Time to initial symptom improvement was rapid in both groups, with a median of 1-week (ECP: (IQR 1–4); IS: (IQR 1–4)). Complete symptom resolution and normalization of laboratory parameters were observed between weeks 1 and 52, with a median duration of 12 weeks in both groups (ECP (IQR 8–14.5), IS (IQR 8–13)).

Within this study, sr/sd-irAE of five different organ systems (gastrointestinal 38, cutaneous 11, serosal 6, pulmonary 2, musculoskeletal 2) were treated. In both groups, irColitis was the most prevalent condition, with ECP achieving a complete resolution rate of 91% (11/12) compared with 81% (13/16) in the IS group (p=0.61; OR, 2.46 (95% CI 0.17 to 145.04)) (table 2). Across both cohorts, only 2 of the 11 treated cutaneous irAEs achieved complete remission, both occurring in the ECP group (p=0.48; difference in proportion, 0.28 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.48)). All six ECP-treated cases of irSerositis/irCapillary Leak syndrome achieved complete remission, although in three cases persistent sequelae remained. Both patients with irPneumonitis, treated solely with ECP, achieved complete remission. In the ECP group, two patients with irArthritis, including one arthritis–myositis overlap, showed either resolution with sequelae at 32 weeks or no remission (figure 2D).

During the study, irAE worsening occurred significantly less frequently in ECP patients compared with IS patients in 6 versus 15 cases, respectively (p=0.0028 (GEE-analysis); OR, 0.04 (95% CI 0.004 to 0.318); table 2). Specifically, 11 out of 17 patients treated with infliximab experienced rapid initial improvement followed by subsequent deterioration. However, eight cases achieved sustained improvement after a second administration of the respective medication. One patient in the ECP group and one in the IS group developed new irAEs during the treatment period (ECP group: irPneumonitis; IS group: irGastritis). The newly developed irAEs were successfully managed with continuation of the study treatment, both achieving complete remission. Rechallenge of ICI was performed in 12 patients, constituting 30% of the ECP group (7/23) and 22% of the IS group (5/23). Recurrence of the same irAE was experienced in 57% (2/7) of the ECP group and 60% (3/5) in the IS cohort, which were treated by continuation of the study treatment. One newly developed irColitis on ICI rechallenge in the ECP group led to discontinuation of both ICI and ECP.

ECP therapy exhibited a favorable safety profile, with fatigue being the exclusive adverse event documented in 13% (3/23) of treated individuals, comparable to 17% (4/23) of patients in the IS cohort. Significant adverse events in the IS group included a fatal opportunistic bacterial pneumonitis, occurring 16 days after the start of the study treatment in a patient diagnosed with an immune-related Lyell syndrome. Additionally, arthritis linked to the integrin antagonist vedolizumab (online supplemental figure S1, patient #4), recurrent bacterial parotitis and persistent hiccups in temporal association with the administration of the TNF-α inhibitor infliximab were reported. Treatment-related adverse events were recorded and graded according to CTCAE criteria. Safety outcomes are summarized in online supplemental table S2.

Steroid tapering and additional immunosuppressive therapy

In the intention-to-treat cohort, cumulative steroid requirements from baseline to week 12 were lower in the ECP group compared with IS (ECP 395 mg vs IS 1,260 mg prednisolone equivalent; p=0.03; figure 2C). Findings were consistent in the per-protocol population restricted to patients completing week 12, excluding those with incomplete steroid exposure data due to irAE resolution or death (p=0.02; table 2). At week 12, in 91% (21/23) of ECP patients versus 61% (14/23) of IS patients (p=0.03; OR, 6.5 (95% CI 1.1 to 70.5)), steroids could be tapered to less than 7.5 mg prednisolone equivalent per day (table 2). Steroid doses had to be re-escalated eight times after tapering attempts in the IS cohort, compared with four times in the ECP cohort (p=0.08; HLE shift in median location −2.67×10⁻⁵ (95% CI −2.06×10⁻⁵ to 4.18×10⁻⁵); table 2). Previously initiated IS therapy was continued in eight of nine multi-resistant patients in the ECP group and in one of four in the IS group (table 2). During the study, the ECP patients required no additional immunosuppressants to be initiated, whereas in the IS cohort five patients were started on one new IS and one patient required three additional IS due to worsening of the irAE symptoms (p=0.005; −0.23 (95% CI −0.42 to −0.10); table 2).

Tumor response and survival outcomes

Patients with unresectable cutaneous melanoma stage III and stage IV American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 2017; ECP, 12; IS, 15) were analyzed for tumor outcome. Since the study population had different tumor entities and different treatment settings which are not comparable, analysis of tumor outcome was focused on tumor-bearing melanoma patients. Although this represents a small subset of patients, the first tumor staging following study initiation demonstrated a complete response in 17% of ECP patients (2/12) versus 0% (0/15) in IS patients (p=0.18; difference in proportion, 0.17; (95% CI −0.06 to 0.45)), partial response in 17% of ECP patients (2/12) versus 20% in IS group (3/15) (p>0.99; OR, 0.81 (95% CI 0.05 to 8.5)), and stable disease in 33% in ECP group (4/12) versus 13% in IS group (2/15) (p=0.35; OR, 3.11 (95% CI 0.35 to 41.9)). Progressive disease according to RECIST V.1.1 occurred in 33% (4/12) versus 67% (10/15) of patients receiving ECP versus IS (p=0.13; OR, 0.26 (95% CI 0.04 to 1.6)), respectively (figure 3A, online supplemental table S3).

A survival advantage was observed in the ECP cohort, with a median OS since the start of irAE until death or last follow-up of 15 months (IQR 13–21) compared with 10 months (IQR 7–16) in the IS cohort (p=0.02; HR, 0.20 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.94)) (figure 3C, online supplemental table S4). Similarly, the ECP cohort demonstrated superior PFS, with a median of 9 months (IQR 4–15) versus 3 months (IQR 2–6) in the IS cohort (LRT p=0.01, HR, 0.27 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.84); figure 3C, online supplemental table S4).

Quality of life in patients with sr/sd-irAE

QoL improved in all patients during second-line therapy. Patients treated with ECP consistently demonstrated higher QoL scores at all time points compared with patients treated with IS, in week 12 in detail 70.7 (ECP) versus 49.3 (IS) (p=0.01; mean difference 21.4 (95% CI 5.9 to 36.6), figure 3B). From baseline to week 12, the mean improvement of QoL scale was 28 points in the ECP group compared with 13 in the IS group (p=0.19; difference in mean, 14.78 (95% CI −7.82 to 37.39), including only patients with available scores at baseline and week 12; ECP, 19; IS, 14). The improvement in QoL in both groups started on average 1 week after the start of second-line therapy (range 1–12 weeks).

Special patient populations: multi-toxicity and multi-resistant irAEs

Multi-toxicity with simultaneously more than one organ system affected by an irAE was present more frequently in the ECP group. Patient #2, suffering from irColitis, irHepatitis and irDermatitis, illustrates a successful multi-toxicity treatment with ECP (online supplemental figure S1). At last follow-up, the overall response rate (ORR) of multi-toxicity irAEs in the ECP group was 95% (18/19) and in the IS group 80% (4/5) (p=0.38; OR, 0.15 (95% CI −0.11 to 0.29); table 2). Among patients with advanced cutaneous melanoma and multi-toxicity, tumor response at first staging after study therapy showed progressive disease in both patients in the IS group, whereas in the ECP group two of five patients (40%) had progressive disease, with one complete response, one partial response, and one stable disease (p=0.42; difference in proportion −0.60 (95% CI −0.89 to 0.25); table 2).

IrAEs that were refractory to at least one second-line IS therapy, such as infliximab or vedolizumab, before study start were classified as multi-resistant irAEs. This included nine patients in the ECP group experiencing 13 irAEs, and four patients in the IS group with 4 irAEs. At last follow-up, the irAE ORR of these multi-resistant irAEs was 85% (11/13) in the ECP group compared with 50% (2/4) in the IS group (p=0.22; OR, 4.8 (95% CI 0.22 to 113.3); table 2). ECP patient #1 experienced prompt resolution of irColitis following the first ECP cycle after having failed to respond to multiple IS (online supplemental figure S1).

Discussion

Management of severe irAEs remains one of the major challenges of modern immuno-oncology. In this prospective, two-arm study, ECP was compared with investigator-chosen immunosuppression in sr/sd-irAEs and showed comparable irAE remission rates, reduced corticosteroid exposure and improved QoL. Although underpowered to report oncology outcomes, patients treated with ECP showed longer PFS and OS in the advanced cutaneous melanoma subgroup. Given the small sample size and non-randomized design, however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Current treatment strategies of sr/sd-irAEs rely on second-line immunosuppression, often accompanied by prolonged corticosteroid therapy, which has been associated with diminished tumor response,31 32 inferior survival,12 33 34 and increased risk of infections. As evidence-based non-IS alternatives for these patients remain limited, ECP, a modality already employed in oncologic settings,35 may offer an approach that controls irAEs while potentially preserving antitumor activity.

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective comparative study investigating the treatment of therapy-refractory irAE. Although prospective trials are starting to emerge, that is, employing interleukin-6 receptor blockade to treat immune-related colitis and arthritis (NCT03601611),36 37 and Janus kinase inhibitors for life-threatening irAEs,38 comparative data are lacking.39 Furthermore, ongoing prospective trials do not specifically address treatment of sr/sd-irAEs (NCT05335928, NCT04038619, NCT06470971, NCT06841705, NCT04407247, NCT04797325), a critical unmet medical need. This study investigates the treatment of sr/sd-irAE with ECP in comparison to systemic immunosuppressants. ECP has shown a favorable side effect profile,24 preserves antitumor effects,40 and was effective in treating irAE in case studies, likely through induction of leukocyte apoptosis leading to immune modulation towards an anti-inflammatory, regulatory phenotype.20 24 41 However, ECP requires high patient adherence and is restricted to specialized centers. While the predefined primary endpoint of the study was immunophenotyping, these analyses will be reported separately in a translational-focused publication. The present report focuses on the clinical outcomes of this prospective study.

Due to the complexity of the patients with several prior IS therapies that could be continued, the attribution of irAE resolution to the specific intervention remains challenging. In the ECP arm, 9/23 patients had received prior second-line IS therapy, indicating a more treatment-refractory population (multi-resistant patients); of these, 8 continued IS treatment during ECP, compared with 4/23 patients in the IS arm, with continuation in 1 patient. However, after inclusion in the study, patients in the IS group received an additional eight IS agents beyond the investigator-chosen therapy, while the ECP group received none. This may have influenced clinical outcomes and may limit direct comparability between both cohorts.

Furthermore, the cohorts were not balanced with multiorgan toxicity being more frequent in the ECP group. Multi-toxicity has been reported in approximately 9–40% of ICI-treated patients.4244 Since high irAE-response rates (18/19 responders; ORR 95%) were observed in ECP-treated patients with multi-toxicity, ECP may be particularly suitable in this context. Another challenging aspect in irAE management remains ICI rechallenge. It is known that 29% of irAEs re-occur when patients are re-exposed to the same ICI regimen.45 46 When re-exposing 80 patients after previous combination immunotherapy with an anti-PD-(L)1-antibody alone, an irAE occurred in 31 (39%). Only 14 patients (18%) exhibited the same irAE with 6 being grade 1–2 CTCAE, 7 severe (grade 3–4) and 1 patient died due to toxic epidermal necrolysis.4750 However, there is little knowledge on the reoccurrence of therapy-refractory irAE after ICI re-exposure. In this study, ICI rechallenge was performed in 12 patients with sr/sd-irAEs and was tolerated in five cases (42%; 3/7 in the ECP group and 2/5 in the IS cohort).

This study has several limitations. The heterogeneous study population, including diverse organ toxicities, pretreatments, and tumor entities, limits comparability of treatment effects; however, this heterogeneity reflects real-world clinical practice in the management of irAEs. The relatively small sample size reduces statistical power and precludes definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy. Even though some of the very hard to treat irAEs, like polyserositis, showed response to ECP, future prospective studies could focus on the more frequent irAEs like hepatitis and colitis to create larger and more homogenous cohorts. Among all endpoints, adequate statistical power (>80%) was achieved for cumulative corticosteroid exposure (Cohen’s d=1.07; power 95.1%) and irAE worsening events (Cohen’s h=0.84; ICC-adjusted power 87.3%), lending greater confidence to these specific findings and supporting the steroid-sparing effect of ECP. Conversely, irAE response rate, QoL, and tumor outcome analyses were substantially underpowered. Importantly, however, observed effect sizes were predominantly medium to large across all endpoints (Cohen’s h=0.41–0.84; Cohen’s d=0.68–1.07; HR, 0.20–0.27), providing a robust empirical foundation for the sample size calculation of a future confirmatory trial. Multiple secondary and exploratory outcomes were evaluated without adjustment for multiple testing; these findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. While irAE-related analyses were conducted at the event level, potential intrapatient correlation due to multiple events within the same patient was addressed by applying GEE with an exchangeable correlation structure. Although allocation based on travel distance was used as an instrumental variable, residual imbalances between cohorts were present, and the non-randomized design limits causal inference. Imbalance was observed with respect to, for example, gender and organs involved. Importantly, the ECP group had a vastly longer steroid pretreatment duration and a higher number of previous failed irAE therapies, as well as a higher frequency of multi-toxicity and multi-resistance. This indicates that the ECP group had more complex and chronic irAE and the IS group more acute cases, affecting the internal validity of the steroid-sparing claim. It could also lead to an underestimation of ECP treatment effects due to a more challenging baseline risk profile. In addition, the open-label design may have introduced bias, particularly in subjective outcomes such as QoL and clinical grading of irAEs, potentially influencing their assessment and reporting. Although tumor outcomes were favorable in the ECP group, the majority of patients in the IS group received combination ICI therapy, which may have distorted tumor outcomes. Survival analyses were calculated from the time of irAE onset rather than ICI treatment initiation. While this approach allows evaluation of oncologic outcomes following clinically significant irAEs, it differs from conventional survival analyses and may introduce bias. A prospective, randomized multicenter study is currently being planned to further investigate the therapeutic potential of ECP as a second-line treatment for sr/sd-irAEs. ECP therapy broadens the treatment options for irAEs, providing a safe and effective approach that improves patients’ QoL while not abrogating tumor response.

Conclusion

ECP therapy was associated with remission rates comparable to those of IS in sr/sd-irAEs while significantly reducing cumulative corticosteroid exposure, improving QoL, and exhibiting a favorable safety profile. In the subgroup of patients with advanced melanoma, longer PFS and OS were observed. ECP may represent a promising therapeutic alternative for sr/sd-irAEs and warrants further investigation.
