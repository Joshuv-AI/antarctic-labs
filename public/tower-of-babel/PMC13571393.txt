IMAC Technique in CTO Lesions With Heavy Calcification

IMAC has emerged as a valuable strategy for plaque modification in the treatment of CTOs with heavy calcification before guidewire in the true lumen of distal segment. It serves as another option after low-profile balloons or microcatheters fail to cross the lesion, which facilitates following antegrade or retrograde wire crossing. CTO = chronic total occlusion; IMAC = intravascular plaque modification with rotational atherectomy.

With the advent of dedicated chronic total occlusion (CTO) devices and widespread CTO crossing algorithms, the success rate of CTO percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has improved to 85% to 94% in experienced operators. However, heavy calcification in CTO lesions is still an independent predictor for PCI failure.1 Antegrade wiring by guidewire manipulation, escalation, and the parallel wire technique (PWT) is usually the first step to overcome CTO lesions with unfavorable anatomy.2 However, dense calcium or fibrocalcific plaques sometimes disturb high-penetration wire crossing. Sometimes, even if the wire is in the CTO body, low-profile balloons and/or microcatheters are unable to cross the lesion because of a heavily calcified CTO body.Take-Home Message•The IMAC technique serves as an effective strategy for plaque modification to facilitate wire crossing in severely calcified CTO lesions.

According to algorithms from the European CTO Club, when antegrade wire escalation fails, antegrade dissection and re-entry (ADR) is an option for bypassing the heavily calcified plaque of the CTO segment by tracking the extraplaque space.3 The “move-the-cap” and “knuckle wire” techniques are used to create subintimal dissection, and re-entry is completed by the Stingray system (Boston Scientific) or the simultaneous 3-dimensional live intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) (AnteOwl IVUS, Terumo) tip-detection ADR method.4 However, subintimal tracking has some disadvantages, including formation of hematoma compressing the distal lumen, high restenosis rate after stenting, and side branch loss. In addition, the Stingray and simultaneous 3-dimensional live IVUS system have long learning curves and may not be available. It should be noted that the “move-the-cap” or “knuckle wire” techniques will not succeed during whole vasculature calcification even after wire penetration into the CTO body. The retrograde approach is another option after antegrade failure, however calcification is also a major issue in retrograde approaches, particularly when it obstructs retrograde wire or microcatheter crossing.

We present 3 cases describing a novel technique for treating heavily calcified CTOs with impenetrable bodies; we have named it the “intravascular plaque modification with rotational atherectomy” (IMAC) technique. This technique is used before wire penetration of the distal cap or wire not in the true lumen of the distal segment. The tip of the antegrade guidewire may be in the extraplaque or intraplaque space within the vessel architecture. After rotational atherectomy (RA) at an impenetrable lesion, antegrade wire escalation (illustrated in case 3), parallel wire crossing (illustrated in case 2), retrograde dissection re-entry crossing (illustrated in case 1), or retrograde wire escalation can be performed. The IMAC technique facilitates antegrade wire crossing after intravascular plaque modification and/or creates a localized subintimal dissection inside the CTO segment, which enables following re-entry and limited extraplaque space as compared with ADR before the proximal cap.

Case Summaries

Case 1

The patient was a 42-year-old man who presented with exertional angina for 3 months. He was diagnosed with right coronary artery (RCA) CTO 1 month before.

Case 2

The patient was a 74-year-old woman who presented with exertional angina for 1 year and severe dyspnea on mild exertion (NYHA functional class III status) during the previous 2 weeks. The electrocardiogram showed atrial fibrillation with rapid ventricular response on admission, and blood tests revealed elevation of plasma N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide levels (24,643 pg/mL) and troponin T levels (0.59 ng/mL). Echocardiogram showed left ventricular systolic dysfunction and low left ventricular ejection fraction (25%). Coronary angiography from 1 year before revealed left anterior descending artery (LAD) CTO, left circumflex artery CTO, and 80% stenosis in the mid-RCA. Because of potential improvements in the left ventricular ejection fraction after LAD CTO PCI, revascularization of the LAD CTO was initiated.

Case 3

The patient was a 74-year-old man who presented with exertional angina for 5 years. Coronary angiography revealed RCA CTO, and revascularization was attempted but failed.

Procedural Steps

Case 1

Coronary angiography revealed CTO with heavy calcification in the mid-RCA (Figure 1A). An antegrade approach with guidewires including the Fielder XTR (Asahi Intecc), Fielder XTA (Asahi Intecc), Gaia Third (Asahi Intecc), Pilot 200 (Abbott Vascular), and Conquest Pro (Asahi Intecc) was attempted. The Conquest Pro wire entered the subintimal space (Figure 1B). The procedure then shifted to the retrograde approach, but both the proximal and distal caps were heavily calcified, preventing the advancement of antegrade (Corsair Pro, Asahi Intecc) and retrograde (Truecross, Insight Lifetech) microcatheters (Figures 1C and 1D). To cross the lesion, reverse controlled anterograde and retrograde subintimal tracking (CART) with the active greeting technique was attempted, however neither a microcatheter nor the smallest balloon available (0.6 × 10 mm) could be advanced antegradely into the heavily calcified lesion. Therefore, we decided to use the IMAC technique (Figure 1E). A RotaWire Floppy (Boston Scientific) was advanced as far as possible through the antegrade route created by the Conquest Pro wire. The tip of the RotaWire was in the subintimal space as shown by IVUS after RA recanalization (Figure 1F). We started RA with a 1.25-mm burr (Boston Scientific) at 190,000 to 200,000 rpm, and it crossed the lesion successfully (Figure 1G). Then, a 2.5 × 15 mm balloon was used to dilate the proximal CTO lesion (Figure 1H), and reverse CART with the active greeting technique was completed by advancing the Pilot 200 wire retrogradely into an antegrade guiding catheter (Figure 1I). IVUS (OptiCross, Boston Scientific) demonstrated severe coronary calcification in the proximal CTO cap, false lumen in the mid-RCA, and coronary hematoma created by antegrade guidewire and RA in the distal RCA (Figure 1F). Revascularization was performed using sequential 3.0 × 38 mm, 3.0 × 38 mm, and 3.0 × 13 mm drug-eluting stents (DESs), and final angiographic imaging demonstrated an excellent result (Figure 1J).Figure 1Case 1(A) The RCA is occluded from the proximal segment. Case 2

Coronary angiography showed LAD occlusion with heavy calcification and left circumflex artery occlusion (Figure 2A). Antegrade approach and PWT with guidewires including the Fielder XTR, Fielder XTA, Judo3 (Boston Scientific), Judo6 (Boston Scientific), Gaia Third, Pilot 200, and Conquest Pro 8 to 20 (Asahi Intecc) was attempted, and the Pilot 200 wire entered the subintimal space (Figures 2B and 2C). The Corsair Pro microcatheter failed to be advanced to the mid-LAD given heavy calcification, and the tip was broken. As a result, we performed the IMAC technique. A RotaWire Floppy was advanced as far as possible through the antegrade route created by the Pilot 200 wire. We started RA with a 1.25-mm burr (Boston Scientific) at 190,000 to 200,000 rpm in the mid-LAD (Figures 2D and 2G). After that, another Pilot 200 wire was successfully advanced into the true lumen of the distal LAD using PWT (Figures 2E and 2H). Three overlapping-sequential DESs (2.5 × 38 mm, 2.75 × 38 mm, and 3.5 × 28 mm) were deployed from the distal LAD to the mid left main artery. Case 3

Coronary angiography revealed RCA CTO with heavy calcification (Figure 3A). An antegrade approach with guidewires including the Fielder XTA, Gaia Third, Pilot 200, and Conquest Pro was attempted, and the Conquest Pro wire entered the subintimal space (Figure 3B). Retrograde wire escalation was attempted with the Gaia Third, Pilot 200, and Conquest Pro wires, all of which entered the subintimal space. The retrograde knuckle wire technique was attempted but failed given heavy calcification. Then, the IMAC technique was performed. The calcification in the mid-RCA prevented the advancement of an antegrade TrueCross microcatheter. A RotaWire Floppy was advanced as far as possible through the antegrade route created by the Conquest Pro wire. The RotaWire was confirmed to be intravascular via orthogonal views, using vessel calcification and the retrograde wire as landmarks (Figures 3C and 3D). RA with a 1.25-mm burr (Boston Scientific) at 190,000 to 200,000 rpm was performed in the mid-RCA (Figure 3E). Upon retraction of the RotaWire into the microcatheter, a wire fracture was noted (Figure 3F). This may have been due to “wire fatigue” during multiple RAs in heavy calcification. In this case, the proximal part of the RotaWire was firmly embedded in the mid-RCA was relatively small, and the patient was clinically stable. Therefore, the wire fracture was managed conservatively, and the procedure was continued via an antegrade approach. After that, a Gaia Third wire was successfully advanced into the true lumen of the distal RCA under retrograde wire guidance (Figure 3G). After exchanging for a RotaWire Floppy through a TrueCross microcatheter, the mid-RCA was treated with RA (1.5-mm burr) at 190,000 to 200,000 rpm (Figure 3H). Potential Pitfalls

First, the precise manipulation of the guidewire within the intravascular space is crucial, and accurate 3-dimensional vascular wall assessment from multiple angles based on calcification or retrograde wire as landmarks prevents the RotaWire exiting the vessel architecture. The procedure demands advanced wire-handling skills and a precise spatial awareness of the guidewire's position within the vasculature.

Second, in specific instances using IMAC where the guidewire approximated the vessel's outer layer, there is risk of vessel perforation, and RA should be performed with special caution in the subadventitial space. RA using small-sized burr (the 1.25-mm burr) with a pecking motion at high speed (180,000 rpm) is recommended to reduce the risk of perforation. However, subintimal hematoma may form slowly after manipulation of antegrade guidewires and microcatheters.

Third, advancement of a RotaWire in the CTO lesion is the greatest challenge. As the microcatheter is unable to cross the CTO lesion, it should be inserted as deeply as possible into the impenetrable lesion. To avoid knuckling and fracture in the CTO body, the RotaWire should be advanced gently and slowly with patience.

Conclusions

The IMAC technique has emerged as a valuable strategy for plaque modification in the treatment of CTO with heavy calcification before guidewire in the true lumen of the distal segment. It can serve as another option after low-profile balloons or microcatheters fail to cross the lesion, which facilitates following antegrade or retrograde wire crossing.
