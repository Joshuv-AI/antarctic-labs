Cumulative Antigen Suppression Reduces Clonal Plasma Cell Evolution in Gaucher Disease
Authors: Noor Ul Ain, Noffar Bar, Lilu Guo, Katherine Klinger, Punita Gupta, Atta Ur Rahman, Ruhua Yang, Yanhong Deng, Natalia Neparidze, Shiny Nair, Pramod K. Mistry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChronic antigenic stimulation is implicated in the pathogenesis of monoclonal gammopathy and multiple myeloma, yet longitudinal human evidence linking sustained antigen exposure to modifiable clonal plasma cell evolution remains limited. Gaucher disease (GD), caused by biallelic GBA1 pathogenic variants, is characterized by accumulation of glucosylsphingosine (LysoGL1), the direct antigenic target of GD‐associated clonal immunoglobulins and a pharmacologically suppressible stimulus. We conducted a longitudinal retrospective cohort study of 235 adults with GD (3628 person‐years; 2662 clinical visits), with incident‐only ascertainment from a confirmed gammopathy‐free baseline. Thirty‐one patients (13.2%) developed incident monoclonal gammopathy. GD conferred a ninefold excess risk relative to population expectations (SIR 9.04; 95% CI 5.79–13.45). Male sex independently increased hazard 3.3‐fold (HR 3.30; 95% CI 1.55–7.03; p < 0.01), mirroring the established male predominance of plasma cell dyscrasias. GD‐specific therapy, initiated solely for systemic disease indications, was associated with a dose‐dependent reduction in incident risk. In univariate Cox regression with age as the time scale, each additional therapy year conferred a 9% lower hazard (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.88–0.94; p < 0.001), extending mean MGUS‐free time by 8.4 years between untreated and most‐treated quartiles. Cumulative therapy progressively suppressed circulating LysoGL1, with eliglustat achieving substantially deeper reduction than enzyme replacement therapy alone. Propensity score‐weighted analyses were directionally concordant (IPTW HR 0.19; 95% CI 0.11–0.30; p < 0.001). These findings establish GD as a tractable model of antigen‐driven oncogenesis—and provide a precedent for investigating whether therapeutic reduction of a defined antigenic stimulus can modify oncogenic trajectory in other chronic inflammatory states.

Introduction

Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) represents the near‐universal precursor to multiple myeloma and reflects clonal plasma cell expansion under selective pressures that remain incompletely defined [1, 2]. Molecular analyses of immunoglobulin genes in plasma cell dyscrasias demonstrate somatic hypermutation patterns consistent with antigen‐driven selection. These observations support a role for chronic antigenic stimulation in clonal evolution [3, 4]. However, direct longitudinal human evidence linking sustained antigen exposure to a therapeutically modifiable risk of plasma cell dyscrasia remains limited.

Gaucher disease (GD) is a lysosomal storage disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in GBA1, resulting in deficiency of lysosomal glucocerebrosidase and progressive accumulation of glucosylsphingosine (GlcSph; LysoGL1). LysoGL1 is the most sensitive biomarker of GD disease activity [5], a potent immunostimulatory lipid [4, 6], and the direct antigenic target of GD‐associated clonal immunoglobulins [7]. Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that type II NKT‐TFH cells specific for Gaucher lipids promote germinal center B‐cell activation and inflammation [6]. In parallel, GD‐specific therapy—either ERT or substrate reduction therapy (SRT)—, substantially reduces circulating LysoGL1 [5], decreasing clonal immunoglobulin levels in murine models [8, 9].

These observations suggest a continuum in GD in which sustained antigenic stimulation may drive progression from polyclonal activation to MGUS and, in some patients, to overt plasma cell malignancy [10]. Consistent with this, AID/APOBEC‐associated mutagenic processes have been implicated in plasma cell evolution [11, 12, 13], and elevated APOBEC expression has been reported in murine GD models [14, 15]. Notably, APOBEC expression appears only partially attenuated by SRT, suggesting persistence of mutagenic programs despite biochemical correction [15]. This partial treatment resistance is consistent with the pattern of delay rather than complete prevention observed clinically. Importantly, these inferences regarding APOBEC‐mediated mutagenesis derive exclusively from preclinical murine GD models; direct genomic evidence for APOBEC mutational signatures in human GD‐associated MGUS or myeloma clones is currently lacking and will require prospective whole‐genome sequencing studies.

GD, therefore, provides a uniquely tractable human model for investigating antigen‐driven oncogenesis: the initiating stimulus is genetically determined, biochemically quantifiable, and pharmacologically suppressible in a dose‐dependent manner. Here, we leverage a deeply phenotyped longitudinal GD cohort with incident‐only ascertainment from a confirmed gammopathy‐free baseline to quantify excess clonal plasma cell risk and test the hypothesis that cumulative GD‐specific therapy, initiated for standard systemic disease indications independent of any clonal outcome, is associated with reduced incident monoclonal gammopathy.

Methods

Study Design and Cohort

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal cohort study of adult patients with GD followed at a single tertiary referral center. Inclusion required confirmed GD based on demonstration of reduced leukocyte acid β‐glucosidase activity and biallelic pathogenic variants in GBA1 identified by gene sequencing [16]. To ensure evaluation of incident clonal plasma cell evolution, individuals with pre‐existing monoclonal gammopathy at cohort entry were excluded, thereby restricting analyses to de novo events arising during longitudinal follow‐up. This incident‐only design eliminates prevalent case bias and reverse causation and enables direct assessment of whether cumulative therapy modifies clonal initiation. Additional exclusions were applied for patients aged < 20 years at cohort entry (given the adult focus of the analysis) and those with fewer than three clinic visits (insufficient longitudinal data) (Figure S1). The study was conducted under a Yale Human Investigation Committee–approved Natural History protocol (ongoing since 2004) and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki; all patients provided informed consent for participation.

Clinical Follow‐Up and Laboratory Evaluation

Patients were followed according to the standard of care at our center, with clinical evaluations approximately every 6–12 months [10]. Serum protein studies (SPEP/IFE) and free light chain (FLC) assays were performed in CLIA‐certified clinical laboratories at Yale. Plasma LysoGL1 levels were measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) as previously described [5]. Screening was applied uniformly regardless of treatment status, minimizing differential detection bias. When monoclonal gammopathy was identified, patients were evaluated through the Yale Myeloma Program with bone marrow biopsy when clinically indicated. Diagnostic classification required a detectable monoclonal protein on SPEP with confirmatory immunofixation; criteria are detailed in the Outcome Definition subsection below [17, 18].

Treatment Exposure

GD‐specific therapy included enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) and/or pharmacogenetically guided eliglustat SRT. Treatment was initiated strictly for approved clinical indications related to systemic GD manifestations including cytopenias, hepatosplenomegaly, skeletal disease, or other disease‐related complications and was not influenced by the presence, suspicion, or risk of monoclonal gammopathy. Monoclonal gammopathy was not an approved indication for GD‐specific therapy at any point during the study period, and no treatment decisions were made based on clonal plasma cell findings. This structural independence between treatment assignment and the measured outcome is a key design feature of the study. Treatment exposure was evaluated using three complementary approaches: binary exposure (ever vs. never treated); treatment‐timing analysis stratifying by delay from GD diagnosis (early initiation ≤ 5 years, late initiation > 5 years, or untreated), which eliminates immortal time bias by defining groups prospectively from diagnosis; and time‐varying exposure modeling, described in the Section 2.5. Cumulative therapy duration was modeled both continuously (years of exposure) and categorically (quartiles).

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome was incident MGUS, smoldering myeloma, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, or multiple myeloma, confirmed by SPEP with immunofixation. Only events with detectable monoclonal protein on SPEP were included; isolated FLC ratio abnormality without intact immunoglobulin was excluded given the ~82% false‐positive rate of standard FLC reference intervals demonstrated in the iStopMM population‐based cohort [17, 18]. Only postentry events were included, restricting analyses to incident clonal disorders (Figure S1).

Statistical Analysis

Cox proportional hazards regression was used for all time‐to‐event analyses with age as the underlying time scale and left truncation at cohort entry [19, 20]. Univariate analyses applied Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction [21]. The primary multivariable clinical model included sex, splenectomy, and diabetes (maximum three predictors; EPV ~10). LASSO‐penalized Cox regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation was used for penalized variable selection from 16 candidates; full selection rationale is presented in Supporting Information Methods S1 [22]. Firth‐penalized regression confirmed robustness [23]. Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed by Schoenfeld residuals; no violations were detected in the primary model (global p = 0.474); a borderline violation for cumulative therapy years (p = 0.041) supported use of time‐varying exposure as the primary framework (Table S1; Methods S2). Time‐varying Cox models updated treatment exposure at each of the 2662 clinical encounters. RMST analyses used a 25‐year horizon (the latest time point retaining risk‐set ≥ 4 in all groups; 20‐ and 30‐year sensitivity analyses in Table S2A). LysoGL1 was analyzed as a time‐varying covariate; longitudinal associations with therapy duration were assessed by linear mixed‐effects regression (Tables S6 and S7). Ten prespecified multiplicative interaction terms were tested using LRT p‐values (Table S3). Competing risks were evaluated with Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models treating nonplasma cell malignancies as competing events [24]. Prespecified sensitivity analyses included restriction to nonsplenectomized patients, IPTW with propensity scores from seven baseline covariates (Supporting Information Methods S3), and E‐value estimation (Supporting Information Methods S4). SIRs for MGUS were calculated against age‐ and sex‐stratified reference rates from Therneau et al. [25] using exact Poisson methods. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2). Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

GD Confers a Marked Excess Risk of Monoclonal Gammopathy

The analytic cohort comprised 235 adults with GD contributing 3628 person‐years of follow‐up across 2662 clinical encounters. Median age at cohort entry was 35 years (IQR 22–47); 46.4% were male (Table 1). Genotype distribution was predominantly mild: 53.2% were homozygous N370S/N370S, 38.7% were N370S/other, and 8.1% carried severe compound heterozygous variants (Table 1). Ninety‐two percent of patients received GD‐specific therapy during follow‐up, with a median cumulative treatment duration of 17 years (IQR 6–26). The cohort was predominantly nonobese (Table 1).

Incident monoclonal gammopathy was diagnosed in 31 patients (13.2%), comprising 24 MGUS, 4 smoldering multiple myeloma, 1 Waldenström macroglobulinemia, and 2 cases of multiple myeloma. Compared with patients who remained event free, those who developed gammopathy were significantly older at GD diagnosis (median 37 vs. 21 years; p < 0.001), more frequently male (67.7% vs. 43.1%; p = 0.012), and had higher prevalence of diabetes (22.6% vs. 7.8%; p = 0.019). Patients who developed gammopathy also had substantially shorter cumulative treatment duration (median 6 vs. 18 years; p = 0.002), and a smaller proportion received any GD‐specific therapy (71.0% vs. 95.1%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). SRT exposure was markedly less common in the gammopathy group (12.9% vs. 42.2%; p < 0.001), consistent with the substantially deeper LysoGL1 suppression achieved by eliglustat relative to ERT [26, 27, 28].

GD conferred a ninefold excess risk of incident MGUS relative to the age‐ and sex‐matched general population [25] (SIR 9.04; 95% CI 5.79–13.45). The risk of gammopathy was greater in males (SIR 11.28; 95% CI 6.31–18.61) than in females (SIR 6.79; 95% CI 3.11–12.89), mirroring the established male predominance of plasma cell dyscrasias in the general population; the confidence intervals overlap and a formal statistical comparison of the two SIRs was not performed This pattern was sustained across all age strata examined (Figure 1A, Table 2).

Male Sex Is an Independent Risk Factor for Incident Gammopathy

Male sex conferred a 3.3‐fold higher hazard of incident monoclonal gammopathy on univariate analysis (HR 3.30; 95% CI 1.55–7.03; p < 0.01; Table 4), preserved across the multivariable clinical model (HR 3.26; 95% CI 1.48–7.17; p < 0.01; Table 5, Model A) and Cox models adjusting for time varying treatment status and splenectomy (HR 2.61; 95% CI 1.29–5.28; p < 0.01) (Table S4). GD itself exhibits no sex‐related phenotypic gradient in disease severity, treatment response, or LysoGL1 levels; the male excess therefore mirrors the established male predominance of MGUS and multiple myeloma in the general population rather than a GD‐specific mechanism [25]. Kaplan–Meier curves showed males accumulating risk 10–15 years earlier than females (Figure 2A); Fine–Gray competing‐risks analysis confirmed the sex difference after accounting for non–plasma‐cell malignancy as a competing event (subdistribution HR 2.41; 95% CI 1.15–5.05; p = 0.020; Figure 2B).

GD‐Specific Therapy Is Associated With Substantially Reduced Incident Gammopathy Risk

GD‐specific therapy initiated for systemic disease indications without reference to clonal plasma cell findings (see Section 2) was associated with substantially lower incident risk across all analytical frameworks. In binary time‐fixed models, treated patients had an 88% lower hazard than untreated patients (HR 0.12; 95% CI 0.05–0.28; p < 0.001; Figure 3A). Given the susceptibility of this estimate to immortal time bias, the primary inference is derived from time varying models. Stratification by treatment modality showed the lowest event rates in the patients transitioning from ERT to SRT (Figure 3B) [26, 27, 28]. Treatment‐timing analysis confirmed that this association was independent of immortal time bias. Both early initiators (HR 0.16; 95% CI 0.06–0.45; p < 0.001) and late initiators (HR 0.12; 95% CI 0.05–0.29; p < 0.001) had substantially lower risk than untreated patients (Figure 3C). Early and late treatment initiators exhibited similar risk profiles relative to untreated patients (Figure 3C). This observation aligns with the nonsignificant treatment‐timing interaction (LRT p = 0.69), though statistical power was limited by the low event count (31 events). Because this stratified analysis categorizes patients strictly by initiation delay, it does not capture cumulative treatment years. Instead, dose–response data from our time‐varying cumulative‐years model demonstrate that the primary driver of protection is the total duration of sustained antigen suppression, rather than the historical timing of treatment initiation itself.

In time‐varying Cox models, the unadjusted binary on/off model yielded HR 0.41 (95% CI 0.19–0.87; p = 0.020); the adjusted binary model was directionally consistent (HR 0.47; 95% CI 0.21–1.04; p = 0.064) (Figure 4). The continuous cumulative‐years model provides the primary dose–response characterization and is reported in the following subsection. Separate ERT and SRT models showed directionally protective trends for both (ERT: HR 0.42, p = 0.026; SRT: HR 0.38, p = 0.138) (Table S4). The SRT estimate was imprecise owing to limited cumulative SRT‐exposed person‐time (FDA approval 2014 vs. ERT 1991). Male sex remained an independent predictor across all specifications (HR 2.61; 95% CI 1.29–5.28; p < 0.01) (Table S4). IPTW analysis yielded a concordant protective estimate (HR 0.19; 95% CI 0.11–0.30; p < 0.001; Figure S2); residual covariate imbalance (maximum SMD = 0.449) biases this estimate conservatively toward the null, rendering it a lower bound rather than a primary adjusted estimate. A Treatment × Sex interaction was observed by LRT (p = 0.047) but did not survive Bonferroni correction across 10 pre‐specified tests; all interaction results are hypothesis‐generating (Table S3).

Cumulative Therapy Duration Demonstrates a Dose‐Dependent Association With Incident Risk

Each additional year of GD‐specific therapy was associated with a 9% reduction in incident hazard (HR 0.91; 95% CI 0.88–0.94; p < 0.001), constituting the primary exposure‐response characterization. A comparable per‐year effect was observed for cumulative ERT exposure (HR 0.92 per year; 95% CI 0.89–0.95; p < 0.001; Table S5). Transition to SRT, modeled as a binary variable, was associated with lower risk (HR 0.29; 95% CI 0.10–0.84; p = 0.02) (Table 4). Stratification of the cohort into quartiles of cumulative treatment duration showed progressive separation of Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence curves across higher‐exposure groups (Q2–Q4); Q1 (0–9 years) did not separate clearly from untreated across early age bands, consistent with limited cumulative exposure in this group (Figure 5A, log‐rank p < 0.001). Quartile assignment reflects end‐of‐follow‐up exposure and is descriptive; causal inference rests on the time‐varying models. The apparent age reversal between Q2 (9–18 years) and Q3 (18–27 years) in the Kaplan–Meier risk set is an artifact of survivorship and selection biases introduced by grouping patients by final follow‐up duration. Patients who accumulated 18–27 years of therapy (Q3) typically initiated treatment in early adulthood, meaning they contribute person‐time primarily at younger ages. In contrast, Q2 patients generally started therapy later in life, accruing fewer total treatment years and entering the risk set at older ages. This same survivorship selection, compounded by stochastic variation from a small event count (n = 55), explains why the cumulative incidence in Q3 (~20%) falls below both Q2 and Q4. Ultimately, these demographic distortions demonstrate why stratified quartile cohorts remain strictly descriptive, and why valid causal inference must rely on the time‐varying Cox models. Restricted mean MGUS‐free time at 25 years increased monotonically from 16.35 years in untreated patients (n = 19; events = 9) to 24.7 years in Q4 (n = 50; events = 5), an absolute difference of 8.4 MGUS‐free years (Figure 5B; pairwise differences in Table S2B).

GD‐Specific Therapy Is Associated With a Duration‐Dependent Delay in Clonal Emergence

To assess whether cumulative treatment influences the timing of clonal evolution, rather than merely its probability, the age at MGUS diagnosis or the last MGUS‐free follow‐up was compared across ordered therapy groups using the Jonckheere–Terpstra (JT) trend test.

In the complete cohort (n = 235), the median age at the time of event or censoring exhibited a monotonic increase: 42 years in the untreated group (n = 19, 9 events), 43 years in the 1–15‐year treatment group (n = 90; 10 events), and 58 years in those with more than 15 years of therapy (n = 126; 12 events; JT statistic = 10040.5; permutation p < 0.001; Figure 7A). An analysis confined to treated patients (n = 216) corroborated a consistent delay (JT statistic = 7623.5; permutation p < 0.001; Figure 7B). Among the 31 incident cases, the age at gammopathy diagnosis demonstrated a positive directional correlation with cumulative exposure (Spearman's r = +0.30; p = 0.105; Figure 7C). Given that the JT analysis integrates both event and censored observations, the time‐varying Cox models furnish the primary causal estimates; Figure 7A–7C serve as complementary descriptive evidence. A sensitivity analysis focusing solely on cases is presented in Figure S4.

Discussion

In this longitudinal cohort study of 235 adults with GD followed across 3628 person‐years, GD conferred a ninefold excess risk of incident monoclonal gammopathy relative to the age‐ and sex‐matched general population (SIR 9.04; 95% CI 5.79–13.45). Cumulative GD‐specific therapy delayed clonal emergence, extending mean MGUS‐free time by 8.4 years in a manner that scaled with the duration of antigenic suppression rather than treatment initiation alone. Male sex independently increased risk in a pattern mirroring the established male predominance of plasma cell dyscrasias in the general population, a disparity not attributable to GD‐specific factors, suggesting that GD amplifies intrinsic plasma cell biology rather than introducing a disease‐specific oncogenic mechanism. To our knowledge, this is the first longitudinal human study to demonstrate a dose‐dependent reduction in incident clonal plasma cell risk associated with cumulative suppression of a genetically defined antigenic stimulus, establishing GD as a tractable model of antigen‐driven oncogenesis in which cumulative burden, not treatment initiation alone or any single biomarker measurement, is the operative determinant of clonal risk. Importantly, MGUS in GD, as in the general population, does not inevitably progress to symptomatic myeloma or Waldenström macroglobulinemia; a substantial proportion of patients may remain at the MGUS precursor stage indefinitely. With only three patients progressing to overt plasma cell malignancy in this cohort, the current dataset is insufficient to characterize genomic or clinical predictors of progression risk or to determine whether GD‐associated MGUS carries a higher transformation rate than sporadic MGUS. This constitutes an important knowledge gap warranting longitudinal follow‐up.

Therapy Modifies the Immunometabolic Milieu

Our findings extend the established mechanistic framework into longitudinal human context. Cumulative therapy duration, rather than treatment type, treatment initiation alone, or any single LysoGL1 measurement, serves as the primary predictor of reduced clonal risk. This establishes that the oncogenic stimulus is chronically renewed and necessitates sustained suppression over several years to effectively alter the clonal trajectory. Because therapy was assigned strictly on systemic disease criteria without reference to clonal findings, and untreated patients consisted predominantly of mild N370S/N370S homozygotes who did not meet treatment thresholds, conventional confounding by indication is an unlikely explanation for the observed association. This suggests that the observed protective effects are not merely a result of traditional confounding by indication. This is further substantiated by an E‐value of 6.6, indicating a robust association that is highly resistant to nullification by unmeasured covariates [29]. The most parsimonious explanation for this delay‐not‐prevention pattern is incomplete substrate suppression. Treated patients exhibit persistent, residual LysoGL1 elevation (Figure 6B), and the 10–15 year trajectory delta between outcome groups (Figure 6C) temporally parallels the clinical delay in clonal onset. While single‐timepoint LysoGL1 measurements lacked independent risk association, clonal evolution depends on cumulative, decades‐long area‐under‐the‐curve (AUC) exposure rather than snapshot ambulatory values.

In a hierarchical framework, this persistent extracellular antigenic pressure acts as the upstream driver of downstream genomic evolution. Chronic, low‐level LysoGL1 exposure maintains an immunometabolic stress that triggers cell‐intrinsic mutagenic programs. This is supported by murine GD models, where Apobec1 expression remained elevated 4.94‐fold despite near‐complete biochemical correction, suggesting that cell‐intrinsic mutagenic programs, once initiated by chronic ancestral stimulation, become partially decoupled from immediate metabolic correction. Thus, combining a human data‐driven model of cumulative metabolic exposure with downstream preclinical mutagenic mechanisms offers a comprehensive framework for why early, aggressive, and sustained antigen suppression yields optimal clonal protection.

In univariate time‐varying Cox regression, LysoGL1 was not independently associated with incident risk (HR 1.01; p = 0.965) (Table S4). Two nonmutually exclusive interpretations are plausible: circulating LysoGL1 at a single time point may inadequately reflect cumulative antigenic exposure or therapy may confer protection through mechanisms partially independent of circulating levels, including the suppression of intracellular substrate pools not fully represented by plasma LysoGL1. Formal mediation analysis integrating cumulative LysoGL1 AUC was not feasible given sample size constraints and represents an important objective for prospective study.

SRT Achieves Deeper Substrate Suppression Than ERT

The pharmacodynamic basis for the SRT advantage is relevant here. ERT achieves visceral clearance but exhibits residual LysoGL1 elevation (~13‐fold above normal) due to limited tissue penetration [5, 30]. In contrast, eliglustat achieves ~60% deeper suppression with near‐normalization in metabolically eligible patients [26, 28, 30]. These pharmacodynamic differences are recapitulated in the cumulative incidence curves (Figure 3B), where the ERT to SRT group demonstrates the lowest clonal event rates across the duration of follow‐up. These observations raise the hypothesis that earlier transition to eliglustat SRT in CYP2D6‐eligible patients may represent a clinically actionable strategy for reducing gammopathy risk, warranting prospective evaluation.

Incident Versus Prevalent Disease

Two recent studies offer significant context while addressing distinct research questions. Istaiti et al. reported a 30.6% prevalence of monoclonal gammopathy among 411 systematically screened adults with GD, with no association to treatment [31]. Additionally, the ICGG Gaucher Registry analysis of 2123 patients with GD1 found no variation in the cumulative incidence of MGUS across different treatment‐timing categories [32]. These apparent discrepancies are fully explained by fundamental differences in study design. Cross‐sectional prevalence measures the accumulated clonal disease regardless of its onset and is unable to detect a 9%‐per‐year hazard reduction, which necessitates years of cumulative exposure to become evident. A binary treatment classification at a single time point fails to capture this cumulative gradient. The ICGG analysis categorized treatment based on initiation delay at a single time point, rendering patients treated for 5 years indistinguishable from those treated for 30 years within the same category, and it combined prevalent with incident events without a confirmed gammopathy‐free baseline.

Furthermore, 65.9% of positive cases in the Istaiti study met criteria for LC‐MGUS only using the standard FLC reference interval (0.26–1.65), which has since been demonstrated in the iStopMM cohort (n = 75 422) to produce ~82% false positives, with none of the misclassified individuals progressing to lymphoproliferative disorder over 4648 patient‐years [17, 31]. Application of revised age stratified reference intervals would reduce the prevalence of light chain MGUS by approximately 82%. This adjustment would bring the combined gammopathy prevalence closer to the intact MGUS rates observed in the present study. Exclusion of isolated light chain MGUS from the primary endpoint was therefore a principled methodological decision with direct implications for interpretive comparability. One case of light chain smoldering myeloma confirmed by bone marrow assessment was included. Age band comparison supports this interpretation: intact MGUS prevalence was 13.2 percentage points lower in the Yale ≥ 60 cohort—where cumulative treatment exposure is greatest—while higher prevalence in the 40–59 band likely reflects ascertainment enrichment within a long‐term tertiary referral population (Figure S5).

Clinical Implications

Clonal risk in GD does not align with conventional indicators of disease severity. Variables such as GBA1 genotype, splenectomy status, bone density, and avascular necrosis were not independently associated with incident gammopathy risk. In contrast, cumulative therapy exposure and male sex emerged as the primary predictors [10, 33]. These findings give rise to four clinically relevant considerations.

First, surveillance for monoclonal gammopathy should be incorporated into routine care for all adults with GD, independent of systemic disease burden. This includes periodic serum protein electrophoresis, immunofixation, and FLC assessment. This approach is supported by converging evidence from longitudinal, registry based, and cross‐sectional studies [31, 33, 34]. GD represents a molecularly defined at risk state in which the antigenic driver is known, quantifiable, and therapeutically modifiable. This positions the disease as a suitable model for prospective surveillance and early intervention strategies, analogous to emerging frameworks in other high‐risk populations [34].

Second, time varying analyses indicate a progressive reduction in clonal risk with increasing duration of therapy. This pattern is consistent with a model in which earlier and sustained treatment reduces cumulative antigenic stimulation prior to the establishment of clonal selection. Although the interaction between treatment timing and risk did not reach statistical significance (LRT p = 0.69), the direction and consistency of the effect across models support further evaluation in larger prospective cohorts.

Third, the depth of substrate suppression achieved—with eliglustat SRT achieving substantially greater LysoGL1 reduction than ERT alone—supports prospective evaluation of whether CYP2D6‐eligible patients represent a population in whom the risk–benefit calculation for transitioning to SRT should incorporate clonal risk reduction alongside systemic disease control.

Finally, the oncologic implications of GD extend beyond plasma cell disorders. Increased risks of hepatocellular carcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and selected B‐cell malignancies have been reported suggesting that the combined effects of chronic antigenic stimulation and mutagenic stress may contribute to a broader spectrum of malignancy risk beyond the plasma cell lineage [32].

Limitations

The untreated comparator group comprised predominantly mild‐phenotype N370S/N370S homozygotes who never met treatment thresholds—meaning conventional severity‐driven confounding biases toward lower, not higher, gammopathy risk in untreated patients, making the observed protective association likely conservative rather than inflated. To address potential unmeasured confounding, we calculated an E‐value of 6.6. Furthermore, IPTW analysis achieved partial covariate balance (3 of 7 covariates reaching SMD < 0.10). Residual imbalance was concentrated in genotype severity and is likely to bias the weighted estimate toward the null. Accordingly, the IPTW result should be interpreted as a conservative lower bound rather than the primary adjusted estimate. Thirty‐one events constrain power for subgroup and interaction analyses. The continuous treatment duration model provides the most stable estimate, and the direction of effect was consistent across all analytical approaches. The single center design may limit generalizability to more heterogeneous GD populations. However, the use of a uniform surveillance protocol within a continuous IRB approved natural history study spanning 22 years reduces ascertainment variability, which is a common limitation of registry‐based analyses. SIR estimates were derived using Therneau et al. [25] reference rates. As this cohort was enriched for Mayo Clinic referrals with higher surveillance intensity than a general‐population sample, expected rates may be inflated, making the SIR of 9.04 a conservative lower bound while a general‐population reference such as Kyle et al. would yield higher estimates [1, 25]. Finally, the absence of cumulative LysoGL1 AUC data and formal mediation analysis are important limitations that future prospective studies should address. Patients presenting with isolated FLC ratio abnormalities, but no detectable intact monoclonal protein were excluded from the primary endpoint per our prespecified protocol. This approach is strongly supported by data from the iStopMM cohort; applying their revised, age‐stratified FLC reference intervals would further minimize misclassification. Whether FLC‐only clonal pathways in GD exhibit similar treatment‐modifiable dynamics remains an important objective for prospective tracking. Furthermore, this study did not pre‐specify a pretreatment censoring sensitivity analysis to evaluate whether patients who eventually initiated therapy had higher baseline MGUS rates during their untreated observation window than those who remained permanently untreated. If executed in larger, expanded cohorts, this specific analytical framework will be highly valuable to isolate the within‐person effects of metabolic clearance and further solidify causal inference.
