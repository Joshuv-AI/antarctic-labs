Analysis of the ERG25 gene family based on the whole-genome sequence in Trametes versicolor and its response to light stress
Authors: Xihong Cheng, Qin Huang, Yushan Li, Qiuyun Wu, Wenbing Gong, Ling Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ergosterol, a precursor of vitamin D₂ and a substrate for steroid drug synthesis, is a key metabolite in macrofungi. In this study, Trametes versicolor PS genome (58.86 Mb) was assembled using HiFi sequencing and 79 genes involved in ergosterol biosynthetic pathways were annotated. Six ERG25 family members (TvERG25-1 ~ 6) were identified, encoding proteins of 327 ~ 372 amino acids with 37,617 ~ 42,809 Da MW. TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4 displayed highly similar gene architectures, and together with TvERG25-3, shared conserved motif and clustered within subfamily II, whereas TvERG25-2 and TvERG25-6 contained fewer conserved motifs. Light-responsive elements were abundant in six genes. HPLC analysis revealed ergosterol level in T. versicolor mycelia increased under white, blue, and green light stress (P < 0.001), peaking under blue light (2076.0 ± 1.1 μg/g). Ergosterol levels correlated positively with TvERG25-2, TvERG25-4, and TvERG25-6 expression. This study establishes a basis for elucidating TvERG25 function and for developing T. versicolor strains with enhanced ergosterol content.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-56305-y.

Introduction

Trametes versicolor (Basidiomycota, Polyporaceae) is a wood-decaying fungus with diverse pharmacological activities, including antitumor, antioxidant and hepatoprotective effects1–3. Its fruiting bodies and mycelia are rich in bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, triterpenoids and ergosterol4,5.Ergosterol (C₂₈H₄₄O; MW 396.65) is widely applied in industry and pharmaceuticals, particularly for anti-inflammatory, antitumor, antioxidant and Alzheimer’s disease drug development6–9 and serves as a precursor of vitamin D₂ as well as an essential substrate for steroid drug synthesis10. As a fundamental component of fungal cell membranes, ergosterol also plays a crucial role in maintaining membrane integrity, fluidity, permeability and substance transport11,12 . Although ergosterol can be derived from natural sources, microbial fermentation or chemical synthesis13–15, the latter two are often constrained by complexity, high cost and undesired by-products, whereas natural sources are advantageous for their simplicity, environmental friendliness and retention bioactivity16.At present, natural ergosterol is primarily sourced from macrofungi such as Agaricus bisporus (352 mg/100 g dw), Boletus edulis (234 mg/100 g dw) and Lentinus edodes (217 mg/100 g dw)17 . Notably, a wild T. versicolor PS strain collected by our team in Changsha exhibited 310 mg/100 g dw ergosterol in its fruiting bodies18, highlighting its potential as a novel natural source of ergosterol.

The biosynthesis of ergosterol involves a series of enzymatic reactions in which mevalonic acid (MVA) and farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) are converted into ergosterol19. Research on fungal ergosterol biosynthesis and metabolic pathways had mainly focused on S. cerevisiae. and C. albicans, where ergosterol synthesis involved a complex sequence of approximately 25 enzymatic steps, with ERG11, ERG24, ERG25 and ERG26 serving as key genes in the final stages20–23. ERG11 encoded lanosterol 14α-demethylase, catalyzing C-14 demethylation of lanosterol, and its over-expression decreased the lanosterol precursor while increasing downstream sterol24. ERG24 encoded C-14 sterol reductase, catalyzing the reduction of 4,4-dimethylcholesta-8,14,14-trien-3β-ol to 4,4-dimethylzymosterol25, and it was essential for ergosterol biosynthesis and ion homeostasis in Aspergillus niger26. ERG26 (encoding sterol-4alpha-carboxylate 3-dehydrogenase) and ERG27 (encoding 3-keto steroid reductase) were oxidoreductases that were localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and are likely to form a demethylation complex to catalyze the final steps of zymosterol synthesis. In C. albicans and A. fumigatus, ERG25 has been identified as an essential rate-limiting enzyme in the pathway, whose inactivation led to the accumulation of sterol intermediates and cell death27. In Candida, ERG25 encoded C-4 methyl sterol oxidase, which catalyzed C-4 demethylation reaction28. Moreover, deletion of ERG25 reduced ergosterol content in the cell membrane, thereby attenuating fungal virulence and decreasing the mortality of infected mice29. Although the architecture and functions of ERG25 homologs have been partially characterized in yeasts, systematic investigations of ERG25 families in macro-fungi remain limited. Recent whole-genome analyses of representative macro-fungi such as Ganoderma lucidum, L. edodes, Stropharia rugosoannulata and Morchella sp.30–33 have facilitated the identification of key genes involved in secondary metabolic pathways and elucidated structural and functional characteristics of several gene families, including ER-associated factors. These advances established a foundation for preliminary explorations of the ERG25 gene family in T. versicolor and its roles in ergosterol biosynthesis and metabolic regulation.

Light, a pivotal environmental regulatory factor, profoundly affects morphogenesis and metabolic processes in macro-fungi. Experimental evidence demonstrated that blue light exposure in L. edodes increased pileus diameter, optimized mycelial biomass accumulation and elevated ergothioneine level in fruiting bodies34. Blue and green light further accelerated primordium development, whereas red light enhanced the synthesis of some secondary metabolites, including phenol, benzoic acid and gallic acid, thereby improving mycelial antioxidant activity35. These findings revealed light could modulate both macrofungal development and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Ergosterol biosynthesis was influenced by multiple environmental factors, including chemical stimuli, pH and ultraviolet (UV) radiation. In Geotrichum candidum, exposure to boric acid disrupted mitochondrial membrane integrity, thereby decreasing ergosterol content36. Low pH promoted iron uptake and ergosterol biosynthesis in Cryptococcus neoformans37. Moreover, UV radiation accelerated ergosterol conversion to vitamin D₂ in dried mushrooms, leading to a reduction in ergosterol level38. However, the relationship between light treatment and expression of genes involved in ergosterol biosynthesis in macro-fungi remains unclear.

In this study, a wild T. versicolor strain (PS) which exhibited a relatively high ergosterol level, isolated and taxonomically identified by our team, was selected as the research subject. High-Fidelity (HiFi) sequencing was employed to perform whole-genome sequencing of its monokaryotic mycelia, facilitating comprehensive identification, structural annotation, and phylogenetic characterization of the ERG25 gene family- key catalyst in ergosterol biosynthesis pathway. Moreover, the effects of five distinct light stress on ergosterol content and ERG25 gene family expression were systematically investigated. These results will provide a foundation for elucidating the light-responsive regulatory mechanisms of the ERG25 gene family in T. versicolor and offering evidence supporting its potential for T. versicolor as an important natural source of ergosterol.

Materials and methods

T. versicolor PS strain

The T. versicolor PS strain, originally collected and tissue-isolated by Xie Ling et al., has been deposited in the China Center for Type Culture Collection (CCTCC M 2,021,010) at Wuhan University, Wuhan, China. The sample was collected at Orange Island, Changsha, Hunan Province, China (GPS: N 28.187206° E 112.96084°).

Preparation of sequencing samples

The PS strain stored at − 4 °C was reactivated by transfer onto PDA solid medium. The activated strain was incubated in a light-protected incubator at 24 °C for 7–9 days and then cultivated onto substrate for fruiting body production, as previously described18, Upon maturation of the fruiting bodies, small tissue pieces were excised and suspended in conical flasks for 3–4 days to allow spore release. The dispersed spores were collected using an inoculation loop and transferred onto PDA . After incubation for 3–4 days, the cultures were examined under a microscope to confirm mycelial growth and to check for monokaryotic mycelia formation.

DNA extraction, library construction and HiFi sequencing

Three biological replicates of mycelium were established. Upon full colonization of the petri dish, mycelium was harvested and DNA was extracted using the CTAB methods39. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using 0.7% agarose gel electrophoresis (KS-600ZT, Kunshan Jielimei Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., China), a Nanodrop micro-spectrophotometer (Nanodrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) and a Qubit fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Only samples exhibiting an A260 / A280 ratio > 2 were retained for downstream analysis. HiFi whole-genome sequencing was performed on the PacBio technology. Sequencing libraries were generated on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) via the VAHTS® Universal Plus DNA Library Prep Kit (Vazyyme Biotech, Nanjing, China), with PE-150 strategy achieving an average coverage depth of 85.37 × . Prior to de novo assembly, raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using Fastp (version 0.21.0) to remove low-quality reads, including reads containing > 5% N base, adapter contamination sequences, and PCR amplified repetitive sequences. All of the above sequencing was performed at Wuhan Benagen Technology Co.,Ltd (Wuhan, China).

Genome assembly and annotation

K-mer number and frequency were first analyzed using Genome Scope (v2.0) and Jellyfish (v2.2.10). Survey analyses were performed to estimate genome size, heterozygosity and the proportion of repetitive sequences. Genome assembly was then conducted using NECAT (https://github.com/xiaochuanle/NECAT), followed by two rounds of error correction using second- and third-generation sequencing data. The assembled genome was subsequently evaluated for continuity, consistency and completeness, with completeness assessed using BUSCO (v4.1.2). The encoded protein sequences were aligned against the non-redundant (NR) protein database and the KEGG metabolic pathway database by Diamond BLASTP (v2.0.11.149) to obtain functional annotation and potential metabolic pathways. Transporter Classification Database (TCDB; http://www.tcdb.org/) was used to classify and annotate transport proteins, while Gene Ontology (GO) terms were predicted based on the UniProt database.

Analysis of the ERG25 gene family in T. versicolor (TvERG25)

Gene identification and structural analysis

TvERG25 protein sequences were retrieved from the whole-genome data of T. versicolor by HMM Search function in TBtools-II. These sequences were compared against the HMM profile of the ERG25 protein domain (No. PF04116) obtained from InterPro (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/). Based on this comparison, six TvERG25 gene family members were identified with their gene structures and conserved domains subsequently analyzed using TBtools-II.

Physicochemical properties and structural prediction of encoded proteins

The amino acids (AAs) composition, molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI) and instability index (II) of TvERG25 encoded proteins were analyzed through TBtools-II, while subcellular localization (SL) was predicted with Cell-PLoc (www.csbio.sjtu.edu.cn/bioinf/CellPLoc-2). Conserved motif identified and visualized by the MEME database (MEME-Submission form) integrated into TBtools-II. Secondary and tertiary structure predictions were conducted primarily through the SOPMA database (NPS@: SOPMA secondary structure prediction) and SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/). All structural visualizations were generated by Adobe Illustrator 2020.

Phylogenetic analysis of encoded proteins

Protein sequences of ERG25 orthologs from 10 macro- fungi (including Rhodotorula, Phellinus pini, and Trichoderma sulphureum) were retrieved from the NCBI Protein database. Based on the protein domains obtained from InterPro (2.5), the ERG25 protein sequences were extracted and aligned using TBtools-II. Subsequently, a phylogenetic tree was inferred using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method in MEGA 6.0 software, with branch support assessed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates.

Cis-regulatory elements (CREs) analysis of gene promoters

The CDS and 2000 bp upstream regions of start codons in the TvERG25 gene were extracted by TBtools-II. CREs within the promoter regions were predicted based on the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/), and the processed data were then visualized with TBtools.

Culture of T. versicolor mycelia under different light stress

6mm Mycelial blocks of the T. versicolor PS strain were inoculated onto PDA medium and incubated at 24 °C, under LED light (light-emitting diode, Light of Wonder Lighting Co, China) treatments, with light colors including white (550 nm ± 2.2 cm), red (600 nm ± 2.2 cm), blue (450 nm ± 2.2 cm), and green ( 550 nm ± 2.2 cm). Cultures maintained in darkness served as the control. After 7 days, some fresh mycelia were collected for RT-PCR analysis, while the others were dried for HPLC analysis.

Ergosterol content analysis by HPLC

2.870 mg of ergosterol standard (purity ≥ 98%, Augsburg, Germany) was accurately weighed and dissolved in methanol (AR grade) to a final volume of 10 mL in a volumetric flask. Working standards were serially diluted to concentrations of 25, 50, 100 and 200 μg/mL. Mycelia were harvested, dried at 60 °C to constant weight, ground and sieved through a 20-mesh screen. A precisely weighed 0.200 g portion of the resulting powder was subjected to ultrasonic extraction with 2 mL of methanol for 30 min in a centrifuge tube. Then the extract was brought to volume with methanol and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane prior to HPLC analysis, following Gessner’s method40 with minor modifications. HPLC analysis was performed using an LC-30AD system (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) under the following conditions: chromatographic column-Kromasil C18 (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm), flow rate-1.0 mL/min, column temperature-25 °C, injection volume-10 μL, mobile phase- methanol, detection wavelength-282 nm, elution mode- isocratic. Each treatment was performed in triplicate.

Analysis of TvERG25 gene expression

Fresh mycelia obtained were rapidly ground in liquid nitrogen, and 0.05–0.1 g of sample was used to extract total RNA using the Trizol method. Reverse transcription was performed using a kit (YUNBIO, Kunming YoungGen Biotechnology Co., Ltd), and quantification was performed using a KAIAO ultramicro spectrophotometer (K5800, Beijing Kaiao Technology Development Co., Ltd., China). The specific primers of TvERG25 family were designed using the predicted CDS sequences and Primer3 Plus software (Table 1), with the internal control gene designed by our group in previous study41. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche Diagnostics, Germany) according to the protocol of Su et al42, employing the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., China). The total reaction volume was 20 μL, consisting of 2 μL template cDNA, 10 μL SYBR, and 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers, with deionized water added to a final volume of 20 μL. Each gene was tested in triplicate, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. Data processing

Statistical analyses were performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS version 27, while graphical representations were generated with GraphPad Prism 10.1.2, Origin 2018 and OriginPro 2025b (10.2.5.212). Statistical significance was defined at P ≤ 0.05, high significance at P ≤ 0.01 and extremely high significance at P ≤ 0.001.

Results

Whole-genome evaluation

K-mer analysis estimated the genome size of T. versicolor PS at 58.86 Mb, with a sequencing depth of 85.37 × , a heterozygosity ratio of 0% and a repeat content of 21.01%. The assembled genome exhibited a Contig N50 of 484.44 kb, a Contig N90 of 338.15 kb, a GC content of 57.22%, a mapping rate of 88.02% for second-generation reads and a coverage of 99.84%, reflecting high continuity and consistency. BUSCO assessment revealed 98.9% complete BUSCOs, confirming excellent assembly integrity. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the genome data were reliable and suitable for downstream analyses.

Functional annotation of coding genes

Gene structure prediction identified 14,035 coding genes in T. versicolor PS, of which 10,874 (77.5%) were assigned GO terms and categorized into biological processes (BP, 1,498 genes), cellular components (CC, 5,412), and molecular functions (MF, 3,864) (Figure S1). GO annotation indicated that genes were primarily involved in phosphorylation (213), proteolysis (188) and methylation (117) in BP processes, associated with the membrane (1,566), nucleus (954) and cytoplasm (754) in CC, and mainly related to ATP binding (686) and metal ion binding (577) in MF. KEGG annotation further classified 6,143 genes into metabolism (3,926), genetic information processing (1,137), cellular processes (720), environmental information processing (312), and organismal systems (48), collectively indicating a marked enrichment of genes associated with metabolism and genetic information processing (Figure S2).

Based on whole-genome data, a total of 96 genes associated with ergosterol biosynthesis or metabolism were further identified and subjected to GO, KEGG, NR and TCDB functional annotation (Fig. 1 and Table S1). GO annotation assigned 26 genes, the majority of which (23 genes) were classified under BP related to “ergosterol reactions and pathways” while 3 genes were assigned to MF associated with “catalysis of ergosterol-related reactions”. KEGG annotation revealed 37 relevant genes, of which 35 were implicated in metabolism processes and 2 in cellular processes. NR annotation further identified 16 genes encoding enzymes or proteins related to sterol biosynthesis, including lanosterol synthase and sterol O-acyltransferase 2. Transport proteins primarily mediated the exchange of chemicals and signaling molecules across biological membranes, playing essential roles in fundamental cellular processes such as nutrient uptake, metabolite secretion and signal transduction. To investigate the potential functional linkage between ergosterol biosynthesis and transport proteins in T. versicolor PS, 17 ergosterol-related genes were annotated by using TCDB database (Fig. 1 and Table S1). These included 9 genes classified as lectrochemical potential-driven transporters, 6 as accessory factors involved in transport(six ERG2) and 2 as incompletely characterized transport systems (7-dehydrocholesterol reductase and ERG4). Notably, electrochemical potential–driven transporters comprised four oxysterol-binding proteins (OSBPs) and five OSBP homologs (OSHs), which were hypothesized to mediate ergosterol transport in T. versicolor. OSHs were also proposed to facilitate the predominant non-vesicular transfer of ergosterol from ER to the plasma membrane (PM).Fig. 1GO, KEGG, TCDB and NR annotation of genes associated with ergosterol biosynthesis or metabolism.

Identification and structural analysis of TvERG25 family

Six TvERG25 family members (TvERG25-1 to TvERG25-6) were identified from the T. versicolor PS whole genome via HMM search. Gene structure analysis in Fig. 2A revealed that 5 genes except TvERG25-3, which contained two exons and one intron, possessed multiple exons and introns (2 ~ 6), with TvERG25-2 containing six exons and five introns, TvERG25-5 and TvERG25-6 four exons and three introns each, and TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4 three exons and two introns. Conserved domains analysis in Fig. 2B showed that TvERG25-1 (175 ~ 300 AAs), TvERG25-3 (200 ~ 325 AAs), TvERG25-4 (175 ~ 300 AAs), and TvERG25-5 (150 ~ 275 AAs) all harbored a FA_hydroxylase domain of comparable length, with TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4 displaying similar positions of FA_hydroxylase domain and the similarity of exon–intron numbers, structure and length, suggesting the similarities in structure and function. TvERG25-2 contained a longer FA_hydroxylase domain (125 ~ 375 AAs) and a short CYB5 domain (10 ~ 75 AAs), with the highest number of exons and introns, indicating structural complexity. TvERG25-6 presented a unique architecture, possessing an ERG3 domain (175 ~ 300 AAs) and a first exon exceeding 1,200 AAs. These structural and domain variations likely reflected functional divergence or adaptation to distinct environmental conditions.Fig. 2Structures and conserved domains of TvERG25 family members. Analysis of CREs in the promoters of TvERG25 members

CREs prediction was performed using the PlantCare database because this database has been empirically validated and widely adopted for CREs annotation in macrofungal promoter43. CREs are specific DNA regions that interact with transcription factors to regulate gene expression. As shown in Fig. 3, CREs in the promoters of ERG25 family from T. versicolor broadly classified into three functional categories: hormone-responsive, stress-responsive, and growth- or metabolism-related elements. Hormone-responsive elements included those associated with the plant-specific methyl jasmonate (MeJA) and auxin and salicylic acid. Stress-responsive elements comprised light, low-temperature and defense-related responses. Growth- and metabolism-related elements primarily involved zein metabolism motifs. The distribution of major CREs was largely conserved among TvERG25 family, with light-responsive elements located at the 5′ end, central region and 3′ end in five genes, while in TvERG25-2 they were predominantly confined to the central region and 3′ end. The recurrent, multi-positional distribution of light-responsive elements indicated that these genes might play critical roles under different light or light intensities stress, providing a foundation for exploring the relationship between light response and ergosterol regulation in T. versicolor. Furthermore, MeJA -responsive elements were also conserved in all six genes.Fig. 3Distribution of CREs in the promoter regions of TvERG25 members.

Structural prediction of encoded proteins by TvERG25 members

A total of nine conserved motifs were identified among the encoded proteins by TvERG25 family members, designated as Motif 1 ~ 9. As shown in Fig. 4, Motif 2 and Motif 8 were simultaneously present in TvERG25-1, TvERG25-3, TvERG25-4 and TvERG25-5, corresponding to the FA_hydroxylase domain illustrated in Fig. 2. In addition to Motif 2 and Motif 8, Motif 1, Motif 3, Motif 4, Motif 5 and Motif 6 were also present in TvERG25-1, TvERG25-3 and TvERG25-4. Notably, the positions of all conserved motifs in TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4 were nearly identical, suggesting that the encoded proteins by the two genes share highly similar motif architectures. However, the positions of Motif 1, Motif 2 and Motif 4 in TvERG25-3 were shifted downstream relative to those in TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4. By contrast, encoded proteins by TvERG25-2 and TvERG25-6 contained fewer and distinct conserved motifs, with the former possessing Motif 6 and the latter containing Motif 7 and Motif 9, indicating that they might function as “non-housekeeping genes”.Fig. 4Conserved motifs of encoded proteins by TvERG25 members. Phylogenetic analysis of encoded proteins by TvERG25 members

The ERG25 family protein sequences from representative basidiomycete species were compared and a phylogenetic tree was constructed (Fig. 6 and Table S2). The tree showed that all encoded proteins by ERG25 from these species cluster into four subfamilies (I ~ IV), subfamily I contains 12, subfamily II contains 16, subfamily III containis 11, and subfamily IV contains 13 members. Notably, TvERG25-1 - TvERG25-6 exhibited high sequence similarity to L. betulinus proteins LbERG25-3, LbERG25-6, LbERG25-5, LbERG25-2, LbERG25-4 and LbERG25-1, respectively. Discussion

The ergosterol biosynthetic pathway can be divided into three major modules: MVA biosynthesis, FPP biosynthesis and the terminal stage of ergosterol formation, involving more than 20 enzymatic reactions44. Research on fungal ergosterol biosynthesis and metabolic pathways had mainly focused on S. cerevisiae. and C. albicans, where ergosterol synthesis involved a complex sequence of approximately 25 enzymatic steps, with ERG11, ERG24, ERG25 and ERG26 serving as key genes in the final stages45. In T. versicolor, ERG27 additionally played a critical role in the conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol (Fig. 10). Some non-essential genes, including ERG6(encoding sterol 24-C-methyltransferase), ERG2(encoding C-8 sterol isomerase), ERG3(encoding Delta7-sterol 5-desaturase), ERG5(encoding sterol 22-desaturase) and ERG4(encoding Delta24-sterol reductase), also might participate in regulating ergosterol biosynthesis in T. versicolor.Fig. 10A series of enzymatic reactions of lanosterol transformed into ergosterol in T.versicolor. In yeast, ergosterol was synthesized primarily in ER, yet its concentration was markedly higher in PM. This distribution might maintain through non-vesicular transport mediated by specific transport proteins46 The principal proteins mediating this process, OSBPs and their homologs (OSHs), were localized at membrane contact sites, where their absence disrupted intracellular ergosterol homeostasis30,47. OSBPs possessed the conserved structural domains—the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain, two phenylalanine in an acidic tract (FFAT) motif and the OSBP related domain (ORD)—which together facilitated sterol transfer between the ER and the trans-Golgi network (TGN)48,49. Similarly, OSH proteins shared the C-terminal ORD domain with OSBPs, enabling sterol binding and mediating sterol transport between the ER and PM46. In T. versicolor, transporters, comprising four OSBPs and five OSH, were hypothesized to mediate ergosterol transport, with OSH facilitating the predominant non-vesicular transfer of ergosterol from ER to PM(Fig. 10). In contrast to yeast, which required seven OSHs for ergosterol transport, T. versicolor contained five OSHs, with a limited number of OSBPs additionally contributing to ergosterol transport and homeostatic regulation. This provides a theoretical basis for further elucidation of the ergosterol regulatory mechanism.

ERG25 encoded methylsterol monooxygenase, which catalyzed the sequential oxidation of two methyl groups at the C-4 position of the sterol nucleus, representing an essential step in the conversion of sterol intermediates into mature ergosterol. ERG25 was not only widely conserved in fungi but was also present in plants. In Arabidopsis thaliana, sterol methyl oxidases (SMOs) as ERG25 homologs primarily regulated the levels of 4,4-dimethylsterol and 4α-methylsterol precursors50. Similarly, in A. fumigatus, mutations in the ERG25 homologous genes SMO (ERG25A and ERG25B) resulted in the accumulation of C4-demethylase intermediates, thereby disrupting ergosterol biosynthesis and enhancing the fungal response to environmental stress51. Nevertheless, SMO-encoding genes were predominantly found in plants and relatively rare in fungi. Current research on fungal ERG25 genes has largely focused on identifying potential anti-fungal drug targets. ERG25 knockdown from C. glabrata was found to decrease lethality of infected mice, and it suggested that ERG25 constituted a promising candidate target for anti-fungal therapy29. Among the six ERG25 genes in T. versicolor identified through whole-genome analysis, TvERG25-1, TvERG25-3 and TvERG25-4 possessed the conserved motifs 1 ~ 6 and motif 8 (Fig. 4). Moreover, the encoded proteins by the three genes clustered within subgroup II in phylogenetic tree analysis (Fig. 6) and displayed further relatively conserved features, and responsible for maintaining classical ergosterol biosynthesis, analogous to the SMO genes in Aspergillus fumigatus52. In contrast, we speculated TvERG25-2 and TvERG25-6 may be associated with an increase in ergosterol content under blue light or other light-induced stress conditions (Fig. 9). These results provided a solid theoretical basis for considering ERG25 family genes as targets for prioritizing T. versicolor strains to enhance ergosterol production.

Light influenced not only the growth and development of macro-fungi but also the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites. It has been confirmed for UV radiation to be able to reduce ergosterol content in dried Cordyceps militarsi38. Kim et al. reported that ergosterol contents in Lyophyllum ulmarium exposure to green, blue and yellow LED lights reached at 155.66, 160.21 and 171.55 mg/100 g, respectively, all exceeding those observed in the control group53. Despite increasing evidence, the relationship between light and the regulation of ergosterol-related genes in macro-fungi remains insufficiently understood. In this study, blue light exerted a strongly regulatory effect on ergosterol accumulation in T. versicolor, with an extremely significant difference observed between blue and green light (Fig. 7). Moreover, previous studies demonstrated ergosterol levels of 4.4% before and 83.7% after photoactivation, indicating that light treatment may modulate ergosterol levels via gene expression54. These findings provide important evidence supporting further investigation into the regulatory interplay between light quality and ergosterol biosynthesis. Whole-genome analysis of T. versicolor mycelia also revealed that harbored light-responsive CREs distributed at multiple positions with high frequency in six TvERG25 members (Fig. 3), indicating their potential roles in ergosterol biosynthesis under varying light conditions. HPLC (Fig. 7c) and RT-PCR analyses (Fig. 8) supported this hypothesis, although the magnitude of gene regulation differed across light treatments. Positive associations between ergosterol content and the expression of TvERG25-2, TvERG25-4 and TvERG25-6 showed that these genes may potentially regulate ergosterol levels under light-induced conditions. Furthermore, based on RT-PCR results and correlation analysis, we found that within the ERG25 family, the expression of TvERG25-2 was significantly negatively correlated with TvERG25-5, whereas TvERG25-4 showed significant positive correlations with TvERG25-1, TvERG25-3 and TvERG25-6, and TvERG25-6 was positively correlated with TvERG25-1 and TvERG25-4. These findings suggested that the TvERG25 family members might potentially regulate ergosterol content via two distinct pathways under different light stress: one pathway involving TvERG25-2 and TvERG25-5, and another pathway comprising TvERG25-1, TvERG25-3, TvERG25-4 and TvERG25-6, although the relationships among these six genes lacking of experimental validation. Although TvERG25-2, TvERG25-4, and TvERG25-6 were significantly upregulated, no obvious correlation in terms of their gene structure or conserved motifs have been found based on bioinformatics analysis above. It was speculated that more complex regulatory mechanisms involved in ergosterol synthesis and will be verified through more further researchs.
