Engineering bilayer SEI via ultrasonic fields for long-life lithium batteries☆
Authors: Yu Liu, Hongcheng Liang, Peng Wang, Jingni Li, Xinyi Hu, Huanhuan Yang, Chunlei Li, Shiyou Li, Dongni Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractExternal ultrasound fields regulate SEI growth kinetics to construct a robust bilayer architecture featuring a compact inorganic inner layer, effectively alleviating the “range anxiety” of lithium batteries.Keywords: Lithium battery, Solid electrolyte interphase, Spatial structure, Ultrasound, Cycle-stable

Introduction

Redox reactions occurring in the electrolyte lead to the formation of inorganic particles or low-molecular-weight organic compounds, which together form the mixed interface (solid electrolyte interphase, SEI) between the electrode and the electrolyte. This interface effectively enhances the properties of the interfacial film and facilitates lithium-ion transport [1], [2], [3], [4]. The mixed SEI formed by organic and inorganic substances exhibits different ionic conductivity at different locations, resulting in the uneven lithium deposition. However, the organic component in the heterogeneous SEI offer excellent structural flexibility, tunable electronic properties, and low density, while inorganic components provide superior ionic conductivity, high thermal stability, and good mechanical strength and chemical stability [5], [6], [7]. Studies have shown that lithium ions preferentially migrate along material interfaces rather than through individual components, due to the smaller gap energy between LiF and Li2CO3 [8], [9], [10]. Additionally, increasing the content of fluorides and oxalate compounds in the SEI can further enhance its mechanical properties. Compared with the ingredient types of inorganics and organics, recent research also indicates that the morphology and spatial structure of the substances within the SEI play a significant role in determining its overall performance [11], [12], [13], [14]. Various structural models have been proposed for the SEI, among which the most widely accepted are the mosaic structure and the bilayer structure. Studies have shown that the bilayer structure, composed of an inorganic inner layer and organic outer layer, offers superior battery electrochemical performance compared to the mosaic structure. This improvement mainly focused on the cycling stability stems from its ability to simultaneously enhance mechanical stability and lithium-ion transport efficiency, whose final apparent result shows a low swelling ratio SEI film [15], [16], [17].

The introduction of functional film-forming additives (such as fluoroethylene carbonate, FEC) is an effective approach to regulate the structure of the SEI. Currently, this regulation is primarily achieved by increasing the amount of additive. However, increasing the dosage not only leads to the formation of an excessively thick SEI, which can further cause adverse effects such as increased battery impedance, but also results in waste due to unreacted excess additives. Compared with increasing the additive dosage, enhancing utilization efficiency may be a more effective strategy [18], [19], [20]. The operating environment of lithium-ion batteries is relatively complex, and external factors such as temperature and vibration can negatively affect the cycling performance and safety of the battery. Since batteries operate under varying environmental conditions, their electrochemical performance under factors such as temperature and mechanical vibration often exhibits significant variability and is difficult to predict. Currently, a preliminary understanding has been developed regarding the effects of factors such as temperature and mechanical vibration on batteries, with substantial progress achieved in the study of temperature effects [21], [22], [23]. In contrast, the impact of vibration on battery performance is more pronounced and represents a critical factor that warrants focused attention.

Studies have shown that prolonged vibrational exposure of batteries can lead to a decline in battery consistency [24]. Therefore, understanding how vibration affects the evolution of the SEI is essential for enhancing battery performance and advancing the development of electric vehicles. In recent years, extensive research has been conducted on the structural damage caused by mechanical vibration at different frequencies; however, the effects of both mechanical vibration and ultrasonic vibration on the properties of the SEI have not yet been systematically investigated or verified [25]. Previous studies have indicated that continuous exposure to vibrational environments can result in structural defect in the SEI, increased internal resistance, and capacity degradation [26], [27], [28]. In contrast, Lee et al. used ultrasound to remove the organic components of the SEI, which altered its composition and improved charge transfer. Moreover, the application of ultrasound enhances the cycling stability of the battery, enabling it to maintain a favorable discharge capacity even under high-rate conditions [29]. In addition, the agitation and cleaning effects of ultrasound can induce the change in the surface composition of the electrode during the initial reaction stage, thereby increasing the likelihood of additives contacting active sites and optimizing the SEI performance. Cui et al. applied ultrasound to promote the diffusion kinetics of soluble products formed on the electrode surface, which facilitated the decomposition of lithium difluoro bis(oxalate) phosphate (LiDFBOP) into fluorides and oxalate compounds. This process reduced interfacial impedance and enhanced the battery cycling performance. They also provided a detailed explanation of the decomposition process of LiDFBOP on the graphite anode surface [30]. During this process, micro-jets, turbulence, and micro-disturbances generated by the collapse of cavitation bubbles on the electrode surface contribute to improved mass transfer. The ultrasonic cavitation effect increases the disorder of the electrolyte near the surface of the electrode, facilitates the exposure of surface active sites, enhances interfacial kinetics, and thereby promotes the formation of interfacial inorganic compounds, effectively regulating the interfacial composition and structure. Film-forming additives can improve the electrochemical performance of batteries, primarily due to the formation of a high-performance SEI [31], [32]. Therefore, the application of ultrasound to regulate the SEI and suppress interfacial side reactions is particularly important for enhancing battery performance.

Although preliminary studies have confirmed that ultrasound can alter SEI components or promote the diffusion of reaction products, a systematic mechanistic understanding of how ultrasound directionally regulates the spatial hierarchical configuration of the SEI, particularly in achieving the desired “inorganic inner layer/organic outer layer” bilayer structure to synergistically optimize mechanical properties and ionic conduction, is still lacking. Meanwhile, current interfacial optimization strategies generally rely on increasing the concentration of film‑forming additives, which, however, results in issues such as excessively thick films and increased impedance due to low utilization efficiency, while the regulatory approach of leveraging external physical fields to improve additive utilization remains unexplored. To fill the above gaps, this work innovatively introduces ultrasound into the electrolyte system, breaking away from the conventional regulation approach that relies solely on chemical formulations. Instead, we harness the microjets and turbulent disturbances generated by ultrasonic cavitation to actively intervene in the enrichment and reaction pathways of the additive FEC at the electrode interface during the initial stage, aiming to drive the in‑situ construction of a bilayer SEI with compositional gradient distribution using extremely low additive amounts. The core novelty of this study lies in elucidating the physico‑chemical coupling mechanism by which ultrasound regulates interfacial chemical evolution and the advantageous spatial structure of the SEI film, thereby offering a novel non‑invasive physical‑field‑assisted design paradigm to resolve the long‑standing bottleneck in high-energy batteries where additive efficiency and interfacial stability are difficult to achieve simultaneously.

Experimental section

Materials preparation

LiPF6 was dissolved in ethylene carbonate/diethyl carbonate (EC/DEC), with all electrolyte chemicals purchased from Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co., Ltd., to prepare a 1.0 M base electrolyte (FEC-Control). FEC was added at 7.0 wt.% to prepare the FEC-containing electrolyte. The graphite electrode was prepared by coating active graphite (Ningbo New Energy Technology Co., Ltd.), acetylene black (AB), and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) at a mass ratio of 8:1:1 onto Cu foil, with an active material loading of 1.82 mg/cm2. CR2025 Li||Gr coin cells were assembled in an Ar-filled glove box (LS-750S, Chengdu Delis Co., Ltd.) with H2O and O2 levels below 0.1 ppm. Cells containing FEC were divided into FEC-Us and FEC-Non. In FEC-Us, ultrasound was applied only during the 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ FEC-associated reduction region of the first formation discharge. The subsequent formation cycles and long-term cycling were conducted without ultrasound. In FEC-Non, the same electrochemical protocol was used without ultrasound. FEC-Non Initial denotes the FEC-Non electrode collected at the 1.0 V formation state.

Electrochemical Measurements

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed using CR2025 Li||Gr coin cells (graphite-electrode area: 1.54 cm2) at 0.1 mV/s from the open-circuit potential to 0.01 V. EIS was measured from 105 to 10-2 Hz using a 5 mV sinusoidal perturbation. Potential-resolved in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PRIs-EIS) was collected by applying staircase potentials from 2.0 to 0 V with a 5 mV potential increment between adjacent EIS measurements. LSV, EIS, and PRIs-EIS were performed on a DH7000 electrochemical workstation (Jiangsu Donghua Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd.), whereas galvanostatic cycling was conducted on a LAND CT2001A system (Wuhan, China) over 0.01 to 2.50 V. The cells underwent five formation cycles at 0.05 C before cycling at 1.0 C. Interfacial stability was evaluated by chronoamperometry at 0.01 V for 6 h. Ultrasound was provided by a bath-type ultrasonic cleaner (KQ-50DB, Kunshan Ultrasonic Instruments Co., Ltd.; 45 kHz, 40 W), and the assembled coin cell was placed in the ultrasonic bath. For FEC-Us, ultrasound was applied only during the 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ FEC-associated reduction region of the first formation discharge. The subsequent formation cycles and long-term cycling were conducted without ultrasound. A schematic of the cell-in-bath configuration for this first-cycle ultrasound step is provided in Fig. S6. Long-term cycling tests used the same programmed protocol under room-temperature test conditions.

Material characterization

The graphite anode for characterization was disassembled in the Ar-filled glove box, followed by cleaning with dimethyl carbonate (DMC), and then was dried under vacuum conditions. An atomic force microscope (AFM, Park NX10, Korea) in non-contact mode was used to observe the products of SEI on the graphite surface. After calibrating the probe, topology and elastic modulus images were collected over 5 × 5 μm2 area at resolution of 256 × 256 pixels. The line scanning rate was set to 0.5 Hz. Scanning electron microscope (SEM, Hitachi Regulus8100, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200, Japan) were employed to identify the morphology and micro-structure on the surface of the graphite. The surface composition was analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi, USA) with monochromatic Al Ka radiation (Mono Al Ka, 1486.6 eV). The structure information was detected by an X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance type X-ray diffractometer) with a scanning 2θ range of 10°-80° and a scanning rate of 2°/min (Rigaku D/Max-2000), which was analyzed by the Rietveld refinement program (General Structure Analysis System 2 (GASA2) software). The surface element information was collected using a time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometer (TOF-SIMS, IONTOF GmbH) with Cs ion gun of sputter phase was utilized to measure the mass fragment of the reaction product in the depth of 1 μm.

Results and discussion

FEC decomposition by ultrasound reinforcement

To investigate the effect of ultrasound on interfacial dynamic, LSV was used to identify the FEC-associated reduction region and to examine how ultrasound affects the interfacial reduction response during the initial graphite/electrolyte reaction. As the potential decreases, the FEC-containing electrolyte exhibits an additional reduction response within 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ relative to the FEC-free control (Fig. 1a), indicating that this region is associated with FEC-involved reduction and early SEI formation rather than with the base electrolyte alone. Therefore, the 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ interval was selected as the FEC-associated reduction region for applying ultrasound during the first formation discharge. In Fig. 1b, FEC-Us shows a more pronounced reduction feature near 1.1 V than FEC-Non under 45 kHz ultrasound. This enhanced response is attributed to ultrasound-assisted interfacial renewal, including improved near-surface electrolyte exchange, reduced local concentration gradients, and more frequent access of FEC molecules to active graphite sites. These transport effects can increase the local FEC-involved reduction response within the selected potential region and promote earlier formation of FEC-derived interphase species. Importantly, this interpretation does not imply direct ultrasonic molecular scission of FEC or nonspecific decomposition of all electrolyte components. Instead, the LSV results indicate that ultrasound modifies the local interfacial reaction environment during the FEC-associated reduction region, providing the electrochemical basis for applying ultrasound only during the 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ segment of the first formation discharge.

Evaluation of electrochemical performance of Li||Gr with ultrasound

To evaluate the effect of ultrasound on the electrochemical performance of graphite-based half-cells, galvanostatic cycling was used to compare Li||Gr cells containing FEC-Us and FEC-Non at 1.0 C (Fig. 2a). For a graphite half-cell, higher reversible capacity reflects more usable charge storage, while higher capacity retention reflects a more durable electrode/electrolyte interphase during repeated lithiation and delithiation. FEC-Us retains 80.77% capacity after 500 cycles, whereas FEC-Non begins a rapid capacity decline after approximately 250 cycles. Such late-cycle abrupt decay is more consistent with accelerated interfacial failure, electrolyte consumption, and continuous parasitic reactions than with a reversible environmental fluctuation [33]. Rate testing further shows higher discharge capacities for FEC-Us than for FEC-Non at each tested rate (Fig. 2b). At 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 C, FEC-Us delivers 280.0, 269.3, and 194.5 mAh/g, respectively, compared with 274.3, 236.1, and 149.7 mAh/g for FEC-Non. FEC-Us also recovers a larger fraction of its initial capacity when the rate returns to 0.1 C, indicating better tolerance to high-current operation. The cycling trend is considered together with the higher rate capability, larger exchange current density (Fig. 2c), lower symmetric-cell polarization (Fig. 2d). These convergent results support improved interfacial stability. As an additional device-level check, 1 Ah NCM811/graphite pouch cells prepared using the same first-formation-discharge ultrasound protocol retained 94.47% capacity after 350 cycles at 1 C at room temperature, whereas the corresponding FEC-Non pouch cells retained 89.76% (Fig. S1). This pouch-cell cycling-performance comparison is used as supporting evidence for the interfacial-stability trend rather than as a replacement for the mechanistic characterization in Li||Gr cells.

Electrochemical performance and kinetic analysis of the electrodes in FEC-Us and FEC-Non electrolytes. (a) Cycle performance at 1.0 C. (b) Rate capability. (c) Tafel plots showing the exchange current densities. (d) Voltage profiles of Gr||Gr symmetrical cells employing FEC-Us and FEC-Non at 0.5 mA·cm−2.

To further understand the performance differences between FEC-Us and FEC-Non, Tafel tests were conducted to evaluate the electrochemical behavior related to Li+ intercalation/deintercalation kinetics and charge transfer across the SEI film (Fig. 2c). Graphite||graphite symmetric cells were tested with initial and final potentials set at -0.25 V and 0.25 V, respectively, using a step potential of 0.1 V. The results show that the FEC-Us exhibits a higher exchange current density than the FEC-Non system (1.698 mA cm−2 for FEC-Us and 0.741 mA cm−2 for FEC-Non, respectively), suggesting improved interfacial stability and faster lithium-ion transport kinetics in the FEC-Us system [34], [35], [36]. The same symmetric cells were also used to assess the effect of ultrasound on interfacial stability over time (Fig. 2d). The results indicate that FEC-Us exhibits a stable potential profile during the 400-hour cycling process, whereas FEC-Non displays a higher overpotential, indicating that the formation of an unstable SEI film that may lead to localized short circuits. After 400 hours, the overpotential of the FEC-Us system is only 0.0679 V, significantly lower than that of the FEC-Non system (0.6324 V). This demonstrates that the ultrasound-modified SEI structure effectively facilitates Li+ transport and enhances interfacial stability.

Effect of Ultrasound on the Property of SEI Film

To gain deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying the differences in electrochemical performance and SEI properties induced by ultrasound, the SEI formed after the formation cycles was analyzed. First, XRD was employed as a qualitative check to examine whether the applied ultrasound caused obvious bulk structural damage to the graphite material (Fig. 3a,b). The patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 10-80° at 2° min-1. The graphite (002), (101), and (004) reflections remain identifiable for both FEC-Us and FEC-Non after formation (Fig. 3c-f), and no obvious new crystalline bulk phase is detected. Therefore, the XRD results support only the boundary that the applied 45 kHz ultrasound does not visibly destroy the bulk graphite structure under the present condition. The small peak-position and intensity differences are not used to infer lattice contraction, interlayer-spacing regulation, quantitative strain, or SEI architecture, because such subtle differences are not sufficient by themselves to support a quantitative structural interpretation. In addition, SEM analysis shows no significant cracks for FEC-Us after pre-cycling, whereas pronounced cracks are observed for FEC-Non. These cracks suggest weaker interfacial protection and greater accumulated mechanical/electrochemical degradation in FEC-Non, which may contribute to its subsequent inferior cycling performance. More broadly, previous studies have shown that amorphous or polymeric interfacial components can contribute to passivation, mechanical buffering, and Li+ transport within the SEI [37], [38], [39], [40], [41]. Such amorphous or polymeric interfacial components can act as a protective buffer layer, enhancing interfacial stability and facilitating Li+ transpor. This interfacial “trade-off” mechanism aligns well with the superior cycling stability and rate performance of the FEC-Us system, supporting that the ultrasound-assisted process favors the formation of a robust and uniform SEI film..

(g, h) TEM images of the SEI layers formed in (g) FEC-Us and (h) FEC-Non. (i, j) Contact angle measurements indicating the swelling behavior of SEI films in (i) FEC-Us and (j) FEC-Non.

To further determine the state of inorganic components in FEC-Us and FEC-Non, TEM was employed to characterize their microstructures. The results reveal that the inorganic components in FEC-Non are present in a crystalline state (Fig. 3g), whereas in FEC-Us, crystalline inorganic particles are not observed and are replaced by an amorphous SEI film (Fig. 3h). This indicates that ultrasound promotes the transformation of the SEI structure from a mosaic to a layered arrangement. As indicated in previous studies, FEC-Us and FEC-Non are expected to exhibit different electrochemical properties (Fig. 1a) [9]. Studies have demonstrated that a layered structure helps enhance the electrochemical performance of the battery and suppresses the swelling behavior of the SEI film [42], [43], implying that FEC-Us is likely to deliver superior performance compared to FEC-Non. Contact angle measurements further show that FEC-Us has a larger contact angle than FEC-Non, indicating weaker swelling behavior and thereby supporting the above hypothesis (Fig. 3i,j). In addition, the reduced swelling behavior of the SEI film suggests poorer wettability of the electrolyte toward the SEI. This is attributed to the generally lower affinity of inorganic components for the electrolyte compared to organic ones. These results imply that, after pre-cycling process, a denser amorphous inorganic inner layer may have formed in the FEC-Us system, leading to the decreased wettability of SEI film and consequently a lower swelling ratio.

Furthermore, XPS was employed to characterize the composition of the SEI film after pre-cycling, in order to clarify the specific causes of the observed differences. The results show that FEC-Us contains lower amounts of inorganic species (LiF: ∼686 eV; Li2CO3: 532.8 eV) and organic species (C=O/H-O: 531.8 eV; R-OLi: 530.9 eV) compared to FEC-Non (Fig. 4a-c). This is likely due to the participation of EC and FEC in the Li+ solvation structure, which suppresses electrolyte decomposition reactions [44], [45]. In addition, ultrasound promotes the formation of a compact and dense inner SEI layer, which further hinders subsequent reactions, leading to an overall reduction in the content of these species in FEC-Us. This further confirms that the SEI film formed after ultrasonic treatment can effectively passivate the electrode interface, with the decomposition of substances being suppressed to varying degrees (Fig. 4b,f). Post-cycling SEM images show that surface particulates disappear after ultrasonic treatment, while granular crystal accumulation is observed in FEC-Non. These findings further confirm that ultrasound influences the nucleation and growth of inorganic components in the SEI layer, thereby altering its structure (Fig. 4d,h).

KPFM was also employed to characterize the SEI films after the pre-cycling process (Fig. 4i–l). As illustrated in the Supporting Information (Fig. S1), KPFM operates by detecting the electrostatic force between the conductive probe and the sample surface, allowing quantitative mapping of local surface work functions and electronic potential distributions. The work function values decreased slightly from 3.61 to 3.51 eV for FEC-Us and from 3.23 to 3.03 eV for FEC-Non. Compared with the results obtained during the first lithiation process discharged to 1.0 V (Fig. 9c,f), these changes correspond to reductions of approximately 2.7% and 6.2%, respectively. This indicates that the SEI films continue to experience partial dissolution or reconstruction during subsequent electrochemical processes, while the SEI formed in FEC-Us exhibits higher stability and stronger resistance to dissolution.

Using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF-SIMS) depth profiling combined with ion three-dimensional imaging, the spatial architecture and compositional distribution of the SEI films formed on graphite electrodes after pre-cycling in FEC-Us and FEC-Non electrolytes were further elucidated (Fig. 5). The characteristic ions PO3-, LiF2-, and C2HO- were selected to represent the phosphorus-containing species derived from LiPF6 decomposition, inorganic fluorides, and organic decomposition products of the electrolyte, respectively. Comparative analysis revealed that in the FEC-Us system, the LiF2- signal dominates throughout the entire depth profile, while PO3- and C2HO- signals are mainly enriched in the outer layer and rapidly attenuate with increasing sputtering depth. This indicates the formation of a compact LiF-rich inorganic inner layer and an organic/phosphorus-rich outer layer, exhibiting a well-defined stratified structure. In contrast, for the FEC-Non sample, the intensities of PO3- and LiF2- signals are comparable and uniformly distributed across the entire depth, suggesting a compositionally mixed SEI with an indistinct interface and a heterogeneous “mosaic-like” structure. Furthermore, in FEC-Us, the signal of carbonate-derived organic fragments within the inner layer nearly vanishes (Fig. 5c,g), while the LiF2- signal remains constant (Fig. 5b,f). This observation suggests that, compared with FEC-Non, the cavitation effect induced by ultrasonication facilitates the formation of an amorphous LiF-rich inorganic inner layer with superior passivation capability at the FEC decomposition potential. Such a compact structure effectively prevents the penetration of electrolyte into the initial SEI and blocks electron transport, thereby suppressing further electrolyte decomposition. Conversely, in FEC-Non, the C2HO− signal remains high throughout the SEI depth, implying that organic species with high swelling ratios persist within the inner layer. These organic domains allow the electrolyte to permeate through the SEI and continuously react at the electrode interface, ultimately resulting in significantly elevated signal intensities for both organic and inorganic components (Fig. 5d,h).

The negative-ion mode depth-resolved ion distribution further corroborates the aforementioned trends. As highlighted by the red dashed boxes in Fig. 5i,j, the enclosed regions correspond to the crossover zones where the signal intensities of PO3- and LiF2- vary inversely, delineating the transition from the organic/phosphorus-rich outer layer to the LiF-enriched inorganic inner layer of the SEI film. The results indicate that, in the FEC-Us system, this transition occurs more rapidly and within a narrower range. Afterward, the LiF2- signal remains predominant, while the PO3- intensity is substantially lower, implying that the SEI film formed under ultrasonic treatment effectively suppresses the penetration of LiPF6-containing electrolyte and thus prevents further side reactions. In contrast, the corresponding transition zone in FEC-Non is broader, and the final intensities of PO3- and LiF2- become comparable (Fig. 5a,e). This suggests that, without ultrasonic assistance, the electrolyte can infiltrate the inner SEI region and continue to react, producing a large amount of phosphorus-containing species.

After normalization of the signal intensities of the three ion fragments, the relative contents of substances in FEC-Us and FEC-Non can be further compared (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). The ion fragment contents in FEC-US are an order of magnitude higher than those in FEC-Non, indicating a higher concentration in FEC-US. Specifically, in FEC-Non, the content of PO3− in the outermost layer is higher than that of LiF2− and eventually reaches a similar intensity, suggesting that the SEI film formed in the FEC-Non system does not effectively inhibit reactions at the electrode surface. It is noteworthy that although LiPF6, FEC, and the solvent decompose at different thermodynamic potentials, their reactions may overlap in the complex interfacial environment of the electrode surface. This overlap leads to comparable signal intensities of their decomposition products detected at the outer surface, suggesting competitive interfacial reactions among these species. In the inner region, however, the C2HO− signal remains higher than those of LiF2-and PO3-, indicating that solvent-derived organic species dominate both the inner and outer layers of the SEI in the FEC-Non system. Interestingly, in FEC-Us, the PO3- signal intensity consistently remains lower than that of LiF2−, and the intensity of organic species decreases sharply with increasing sputtering depth. This indicates that ultrasonication not only suppresses the decomposition of LiPF6 but also promotes the formation of a more passivating inner SEI layer, effectively reducing interfacial reactions. Moreover, the intensity of LiF2− fragments in the inner layer is significantly higher than that of PO3- and C2HO−, suggesting that ultrasonication enhances the reaction priority of FEC, thereby leading to the formation of a bilayer SEI primarily composed of FEC decomposition products.

Furthermore, to better illustrate the properties of the SEI films formed under ultrasound, the characteristics of the SEI films in both systems after 400 cycles were also characterized. First, the electrode materials and SEI films after prolonged cycling were examined by XRD to evaluate the protective effects of the SEI films on the graphite electrode during long-term cycling (Fig. S4, Supporting Information). The (002), (010), and (004) reflections are characteristic of the graphite electrode material. Compared with the pristine graphite electrode, the characteristic graphite reflections of both cycled electrodes remain identifiable, and no new crystalline bulk phase is detected. The FEC-Non electrode shows a 0.04° shift of the characteristic peak after long-term cycling (from 25.54° to 25.58°), whereas the shift in FEC-Us is smaller, 0.02° (from 25.54° to 25.56°). This small difference is used only as a qualitative comparison of the cycled electrodes and is not used to quantify graphite lattice contraction, interlayer-spacing regulation, or SEI architecture. It is also worth noting that the peak intensity of the FEC-Us system is lower than that of the FEC-Non system. Such intensity differences may be related to changes in the interfacial layer and cycled electrode surface state, but are not assigned solely to X-ray absorption by an amorphous SEI layer [40], [46]. Previous studies have shown that amorphous inorganic or polymeric interfacial components can contribute to mechanical buffering, interfacial passivation, and Li+ transport within the SEI [47]. Therefore, the XRD comparison is considered together with the electrochemical and interfacial characterization results rather than used as a standalone structural proof. This interfacial “trade-off” mechanism agrees with the superior cycling stability and rate performance of the FEC-Us system, supporting that the ultrasound-assisted formation process favors a more robust and uniform SEI film during long-term cycling.

Subsequently, KPFM was employed to investigate the electronic insulation properties of FEC-Us and FEC-Non after 400 cycles. The results show that a more concentrated distribution of electron work function on the surface of FEC-Us compared to FEC-Non (Fig. 6a,b). Notably, the electron work function distribution of FEC-Non appears to indicate the presence of cracks (indicated by gray lines), leading to a dispersed work function distribution, which is consistent with the SEM results. It is worth noting that after cycling, the surface electronic insulation properties of FEC-Us and FEC-Non tend to converge. This convergence can be attributed to the continuous repair process of FEC-Non during cycling, enabling it to achieve electronic insulation performance similar to that of FEC-Us (Fig. 6c,d). However, SEM characterization reveals that FEC-Us exhibits no obvious surface cracks after cycling and maintains a clean, intact interface after cycling, whereas FEC-Non displays extensive cracks. Based on the preceding conclusions, it can be inferred that FEC-Us forms an inner layer of amorphous nano-inorganic materials, which effectively accommodates volume changes during Li+ de/intercalation. Conversely, the inorganic components in FEC-Non do not undergo morphological changes upon further cycling. The loose interfacial connections between crystalline inorganic materials result in a larger mean square value, resulting in a decline in the mechanical properties of the SEI film (Fig. 6e) [48]. Notably, the mechanical properties of FEC-Non cannot be improved by repair and reconstruction during subsequent cycles, as evidenced by the appearance of obvious cracks in FEC-Non after approximately 400 cycles (Fig. 6f). This suggests that the Li+ de/intercalation process during subsequent cycles causes irreparable damage to FEC-Non, further decreasing its capacity after around 400 cycles (Fig. 2a) [49].

Ultrasound-Driven Preferential Growth of the SEI Film Spatial Structure

To elucidate the effect of ultrasound on the spatial evolution of the SEI film, in situ distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis combined with TEM characterization was performed to track the film formation process. Both techniques revealed markedly different interfacial behaviors within the potential range where ultrasound was applied. In the sonication region (1.4-1.0 V, Fig. 7a,b), the time constants became unobservable, indicating an increase in liquid-phase entropy at the electrode surface. This phenomenon can be attributed to ultrasound-induced perturbations in the electrolyte, which significantly enhance the disorder of both the near-surface region and the bulk solution. After applying DRT deconvolution to the impedance spectra, the corresponding time constants were extracted. Typically, each time constant represents a specific reaction or phase transition; the SEI-related relaxation process on the graphite anode lies within 10-4-10-3 s, while the range of 10-2-10-1 s corresponds to charge-transfer processes at the electrode interface. Upon discharging both FEC-Non and FEC-Us systems to 1.0 V, the peak intensity associated with the SEI film suggests that the SEI layer in the FEC-Non system is thicker than that in FEC-Us (Fig. 7c,d). While both systems demonstrate peak splitting beyond 1.0 V, indicating enhanced SEI heterogeneity and progressive densification, it is noteworthy that FEC-Us exhibits splitting in the high-frequency region (<10-3 s), whereas FEC-Non exhibits splitting in the lower-frequency region. This indicates that FEC-Us forms a stratified SEI structure after sonication, while FEC-Non can not be consistent with the ToF-SIMS results. Subsequent TEM characterization further confirms these observations. The FEC-Us sample exhibits a uniform and compact interface with continuous dark contrast and no discernible crystalline grains or voids, whereas the FEC-Non sample still shows dispersed crystalline particles with clear grain boundaries, revealing a relatively loose microstructure (Fig. 8g,h). These results demonstrate that in the absence of ultrasound, crystalline domains persist within the SEI, whereas ultrasonic treatment promotes the formation of a denser and more homogeneous interfacial structure. Moreover, the potential-resolved in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PRIs-EIS) during the initial charging and subsequent discharging processes also reveal that the impedance variation is significantly reduced after the application of ultrasound (Fig. 7e-j).[50]

Subsequently, XPS was employed to investigate the compositional evolution of the SEI film during the sonication process (Fig. 8a-f). XPS measurements were performed on samples discharged to 1.0 V under both normal and ultrasonic conditions to characterize the variations in SEI components. The results indicate that, compared with FEC-Us, the FEC-Non sample generates a greater amount of LiF after discharging to 1.0 V (Fig. 8a,b). This suggests that ultrasonic stimulation accelerates the initial FEC reduction reactions, leading to a more effective interfacial coverage that passivates the electrode surface, suppresses subsequent electrolyte decomposition, and ultimately results in a lower LiF content. In addition, the FEC-Us sample exhibits a lower organic content (38.47% in FEC-Us versus 60.29% in FEC-Non). These findings imply that the contribution of inorganic species such as LiF to the SEI film still requires further investigation; a higher LiF content does not necessarily correlate with improved interfacial properties [51], [52]. TEM interfacial characterization further reveals that granular crystalline particles are observed in the FEC-Non sample within this potential range (Fig. 8g-i), whereas the FEC-Us sample displays an amorphous morphology. This observation indicates that ultrasound enhances interfacial perturbation, thereby altering the nucleation behavior of inorganic SEI components and transforming their growth mode from a three-dimensional (3D) to a two-dimensional (2D) planar structure [53]. In other words, when comparing the “quantity” and “quality” of LiF within the SEI, the formation of high-quality LiF is far more significant for optimizing SEI properties than merely increasing its amount. Accordingly, the role of ultrasound within the FEC decomposition potential window can be interpreted as follows: under normal conditions, the low FEC concentration and slow electrolyte diffusion kinetics near the electrode surface favor the aggregation and crystallization of nuclei into crystalline inorganic phases, forming a mosaic-like SEI structure. In contrast, the application of ultrasound accelerates mass transport near the electrode surface, facilitating FEC decomposition and promoting the formation of a bilayer SEI structure that effectively suppresses parasitic reactions at the electrode interface.

To further substantiate the above inference, KPFM was employed to analyze the insulating properties of the SEI film formed after discharge at 1.0 V. The results indicate that the FEC-Us film exhibits a more uniform work function distribution (Fig. 9a,b), whereas the FEC-Non film shows a localized distribution (Fig. 9d,e). Notably, in the mosaic-structured SEI film formed in the FEC-Non system, there exist regions with uneven suppression of electron transfer (green line). This can be attributed to the incomplete coverage of certain protruding graphite areas during SEI formation, resulting in locally weaker passivation (blue and green lines). These findings suggest that, under ultrasonic treatment, the morphological transformation of inorganic components leads to the formation of a bilayer SEI film with enhanced passivation capability. Moreover, the histogram of the work function distribution reveals that the FEC-Non film has a broader range (Fig. 9f), while the FEC-Us film displays a narrower, more concentrated distribution, indicating that the FEC-Us film possesses superior electronic insulation characteristics (Fig. 9c).

In summary, bath ultrasound applied during the FEC-associated reduction region of the first formation discharge improves interfacial renewal and local mass transport at the graphite/electrolyte interface (Fig. 10a,b). Without ultrasound, slower and less uniform near-surface transport allows soluble or weakly attached reduction products to accumulate near graphite, leading to heterogeneous FEC access, nonuniform inorganic nucleation, and disordered three-dimensional growth of inorganic/organic components throughout a compositionally mixed mosaic SEI. In contrast, bath ultrasound renews the near-surface electrolyte, disperses weakly attached interfacial products, and improves local FEC supply at active graphite sites. This transport-regulated interfacial environment promotes more uniform FEC-derived inorganic nucleation and a more ordered two-dimensional inorganic growth close to the graphite surface, forming a compact LiF-rich inorganic inner layer beneath an organic-rich outer layer. The resulting bilayer SEI improves interfacial passivation, facilitates Li+ transport, suppresses continuous electrolyte decomposition, and contributes to enhanced rate capability and cycling stability.

Conclusion

In summary, we have demonstrated that 45 kHz ultrasound applied only during the 1.4 to 1.0 V vs. Li/Li+ FEC-associated reduction region of the first formation discharge can regulate FEC-derived SEI growth on graphite. This first-formation-discharge ultrasound step transforms the conventional compositionally mixed mosaic SEI into a more organized bilayer architecture, comprising a compact amorphous inorganic inner layer and an organic-rich outer layer. The inorganic inner layer enhances interfacial passivation and Li+ transport, while the organic outer layer contributes to interfacial mechanical stability. As a result, the ultrasound-treated Li||Gr cells retain 80.77% capacity after 500 cycles at 1.0 C, and 1 Ah NCM811/graphite pouch cells retain 94.47% capacity after 350 cycles at 1 C. Mechanistically, ultrasound renews the near-surface electrolyte, disperses soluble or weakly attached interfacial products, and improves FEC access to active graphite sites, thereby promoting more uniform inorganic inner-layer formation and subsequent organic outer-layer growth. This work provides a scalable physical-field strategy for regulating battery interphase architecture and highlights the potential of external-field-assisted formation to complement chemical additive design in durable lithium-ion batteries.
