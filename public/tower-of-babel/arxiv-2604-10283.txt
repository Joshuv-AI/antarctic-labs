Descriptor-Injected Cross-Modal Learning: A Systematic Exploration of Audio–MIDI Alignment via Spectral and Melodic Features
Mariano Fernández Méndez

Abstract
Cross-modal retrieval between audio recordings and symbolic music representations (MIDI) remains challenging due to the fundamental modality gap between continuous waveforms and discrete event sequences. We present a systematic exploration of descriptor injection —the practice of augmenting modality-specific encoders with hand-crafted domain features—as a mechanism for bridging this gap. Through a three-phase experimental campaign spanning 13 descriptor × \times mechanism combinations, 6 architectural families, and 3 training schedules, we identify a configuration ( d4a4 ) that reaches a mean S = 84.0 % ± 2.7 ​ pp S{=}84.0\%\pm 2.7\,\text{pp} across five independent training seeds, a + 8.8 +8.8 percentage point improvement over the descriptor-free baseline. Causal ablation reveals that the audio-side descriptor (A4, octave-band energy dynamics) is entirely responsible for this improvement in the top dual models ( d4a4 , d4-a4r ): zeroing A4 collapses performance by 75 75 – 78 ​ pp 78\,\text{pp} , while ablating the MIDI-side descriptor (D4, local pitch intervals) changes S by only − 0.4 -0.4 to + 0.6 ​ pp +0.6\,\text{pp} despite improving training dynamics. We propose reverse cross-attention , a mechanism where descriptor tokens serve as queries attending to encoder features, achieving 163 × \times fewer attention operations than the standard formulation while remaining competitive. Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) analysis reveals that descriptors double the representational alignment between audio and MIDI transformer layers ( 0.435 → 0.794 0.435\to 0.794 , + 82 % +82\% ), suggesting that the mechanism of improvement is cross-encoder representational convergence rather than simple feature injection. Perturbation analysis shows that high-frequency octave bands (750–12000 Hz) carry the majority of the discriminative signal, and that this information is encoded non-linearly ( | r | < 0.05 |r|<0.05 correlations with output). All experiments use the MAESTRO v3.0.0 piano dataset with a structured evaluation protocol controlling for composer and piece similarity.



1
Introduction

Music exists simultaneously in multiple modalities: as audio waveforms captured by microphones, as symbolic sequences encoded in MIDI files, as visual score images, and as cognitive representations in the listener’s mind. The ability to align representations across these modalities—particularly between audio and symbolic music—has direct applications in music retrieval, score following, automatic transcription evaluation, and musicological analysis.

The fundamental challenge of audio–MIDI alignment lies in the
modality gap
: audio is a continuous-time signal shaped by instrument timbre, room acoustics, performer expression, and recording conditions, while MIDI is a discrete sequence of pitch, velocity, and duration events that abstracts away all acoustic detail. Traditional approaches bridge this gap through hand-crafted intermediate representations—chromagrams
(
Ellis 2007
;
Ewert and Müller 2012
)
, audio fingerprints
(
Wang 2003
)
, or dynamic time warping on spectral features
(
Hu et al. 2003
)
—but these require extensive domain engineering and are brittle to variations in performance style and recording quality.

Recent advances in self-supervised audio representation learning
(
Baevski et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2024
)
and cross-modal contrastive objectives
(
Bardes et al. 2022
;
Radford et al. 2021
;
Wu et al. 2023
)
offer a principled alternative: learn a shared embedding space where matched audio–MIDI pairs are close and unmatched pairs are distant. However, naïve application of contrastive learning to audio–MIDI pairs faces a bootstrapping problem: the modality gap is so large that early training gradients are noisy and convergence is slow.

Beyond these practical applications, the question of whether audio and symbolic music share discoverable structural invariants is of broader interest. Frequency-based descriptors—capturing spectral dynamics, harmonic interval patterns, or energy distribution across octave bands—offer a tractable way to test whether certain relational features can serve as cross-modal bridges. If such descriptors prove effective, this would suggest that some aspects of musical information remain legible across modalities through frequency-structured relations rather than through learned abstractions alone.

Hypothesis.

We hypothesize that
domain-specific descriptors
—hand-crafted features capturing known invariances within each modality—can serve as a bridge during cross-modal learning. Specifically, spectral dynamics descriptors for audio and melodic interval descriptors for MIDI may provide the encoder with structured prior information that accelerates the discovery of cross-modal correspondences.

Approach.

We conduct a systematic three-phase exploration:

1.

Screening
(5-epoch short-horizon): 13 descriptor
\times
injection mechanism combinations, plus 11 alternative architectures across 3 families (FiLM, Mixture-of-Experts, Third Tower).

2.

Confirmation
(30–60 epochs): The top candidates are trained to convergence with optimized learning rate schedules.

3.

Scientific validation
(Gate 5B): A battery of 8 tests—causal ablation, transposition invariance, CKA/RSA alignment, perturbation sensitivity, linear probing, and embedding visualization—applied to the 4 canonical models.

Contributions.

1.

A comprehensive taxonomy of 13 descriptor
\times
mechanism combinations for cross-modal music learning, with systematic screening results (
section

4
).

2.

The
reverse cross-attention
mechanism, where descriptor tokens serve as queries attending to encoder features, achieving
163\times
fewer attention operations than standard cross-attention while delivering competitive performance (
section

3.5
).

3.

Causal analysis demonstrating that the audio-side descriptor (A4) drives the retrieval gains, while the MIDI-side descriptor (D4) acts mainly as a training regularizer and has only weak inference-time effect in top dual models (
section

5.2
).

4.

CKA analysis revealing that descriptors double cross-encoder representational alignment, suggesting the mechanism is representational convergence rather than feature concatenation (
section

5.4
).

2
Related Work

2.1
Audio–MIDI Matching

Classical audio–MIDI alignment relies on intermediate representations that abstract away modality-specific details. Chromagram-based methods
(
Ellis 2007
;
Müller 2015
)
project both modalities into a shared pitch-class energy space, then apply DTW
(
Hu et al. 2003
)
or subsequence matching. Audio fingerprinting approaches, pioneered by Shazam
(
Wang 2003
)
, extract sparse time-frequency landmarks and match via hash voting, achieving real-time performance but requiring explicit feature engineering. Score-informed source separation
(
Ewert and Müller 2012
)
uses MIDI as a guide for audio decomposition, demonstrating that symbolic information can meaningfully constrain audio processing.

2.2
Self-Supervised Audio Representations

The success of BERT-style pre-training
(
Devlin et al. 2019
)
has inspired self-supervised approaches for audio. wav2vec 2.0
(
Baevski et al. 2020
)
learns speech representations via masked prediction over quantized latent features. MERT
(
Li et al. 2024
)
extends this paradigm to music, pre-training on 160K hours of music audio and achieving strong performance on downstream MIR tasks. CLAP
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
and MuLan
(
Huang et al. 2022
)
learn joint audio-language spaces via contrastive objectives, demonstrating that cross-modal alignment can emerge from large-scale training.

2.3
Cross-Modal Contrastive Learning

VICReg
(
Bardes et al. 2022
)
regularizes representation learning by jointly optimizing
variance
(preventing collapse),
invariance
(aligning matched pairs), and
covariance
(decorrelating dimensions). Unlike InfoNCE-based methods
(
He et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2020
)
, VICReg does not require negative pairs or large batch sizes, making it suitable for settings with limited data diversity. Barlow Twins
(
Zbontar et al. 2021
)
achieves similar goals by minimizing cross-correlation redundancy. CLIP
(
Radford et al. 2021
)
scales contrastive learning to 400M image-text pairs, demonstrating that cross-modal alignment can produce powerful zero-shot representations.

2.4
Feature Conditioning in Neural Networks

Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM)
(
Perez et al. 2018
)
conditions network activations via learned affine transformations
\gamma\odot F+\beta
, originally proposed for visual reasoning. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
(
Shazeer et al. 2017
)
routes inputs through specialized sub-networks via learned gating. Cross-attention
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
;
Jaegle et al. 2021
)
allows one set of representations to attend to another, enabling flexible information flow between modalities or feature streams.

2.5
Representational Similarity Analysis

Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)
(
Kornblith et al. 2019
)
provides a principled measure of representational similarity between neural network layers, invariant to orthogonal transformations and isotropic scaling. Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA)
(
Kriegeskorte et al. 2008
)
compares representations via pairwise distance correlations. Linear probing
(
Alain and Bengio 2017
)
evaluates the linear decodability of specific features from frozen representations, providing a complementary view to geometric similarity measures.

Method

3.1
Problem Formulation

Given an audio segment
a\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{a}}
sampled at
f_{s}=24
kHz (
T_{a}=96{,}000
for 4-second segments) and a corresponding MIDI segment
m=\{(p_{i},v_{i},d_{i},o_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
where
p_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,127\}
is pitch,
v_{i}\in\{0,\ldots,127\}
is velocity,
d_{i}
is duration, and
o_{i}
is onset time, we seek to learn encoders
f_{a}:\mathbb{R}^{T_{a}}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
f_{m}:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}^{D}
mapping both modalities into a shared
D
-dimensional embedding space (with
D=256
) such that

\text{sim}(f_{a}(a),f_{m}(m))\gg\text{sim}(f_{a}(a),f_{m}(m^{\prime}))\quad\text{for matched }(a,m)\text{ and unmatched }m^{\prime}\neq m.

(1)

We define the primary evaluation metric as

S=\min\!\big(\text{R@10}_{a{\to}m},\;\text{R@10}_{m{\to}a}\big),

(2)

where
\text{R@10}_{a{\to}m}
is Recall@10 for audio-to-MIDI retrieval and
\text{R@10}_{m{\to}a}
is the reverse. The
\min
ensures that both retrieval directions are simultaneously effective.

MIDI encoder.

The MIDI encoder processes discrete event sequences through learned embeddings and a pre-norm Transformer. Each note event’s pitch (
\mathbb{R}^{256}
), velocity (
\mathbb{R}^{128}
), and duration (
\mathbb{R}^{128}
) are independently embedded, concatenated to form a 512-dimensional vector, linearly projected, and LayerNorm-ed. Sinusoidal positional encodings are added, and the sequence is processed by a 4-layer pre-norm Transformer (
d_{\text{model}}{=}512
,
n_{\text{heads}}{=}8
,
d_{\text{ff}}{=}2048
, GELU, dropout
0.1
). The output
z_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times 512}
is obtained via masked mean pooling over valid (non-padding) positions, followed by a final LayerNorm.

Projection heads.

Both encoders are followed by 3-layer MLP projection heads with BatchNorm and ReLU after each hidden layer, mapping to the shared 256-dimensional space. The audio head has dimensions
1024\to 512\to 512\to 256
; the MIDI head is
512\to 512\to 512\to 256
.

Parameter counts.

The base model (D0, no descriptors) contains approximately 74.2M parameters:
{\sim}60
M in the audio encoder,
{\sim}13
M in the MIDI encoder, and
{\sim}1.2
M in both projection heads.

3.3
VICReg Loss

We train with VICReg
(
Bardes et al. 2022
)
, which jointly optimizes three terms over a batch of
B
matched pairs
\{(z_{a}^{(i)},z_{m}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{B}
projected to embeddings
\hat{z}_{a}=g_{a}(z_{a})
and
\hat{z}_{m}=g_{m}(z_{m})
:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{VICReg}}=\lambda_{\text{inv}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}+\lambda_{\text{var}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}+\lambda_{\text{cov}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}},

(3)

The invariance term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{inv}}
minimizes the MSE between matched embedding pairs; the variance term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{var}}
applies a hinge loss on the per-dimension standard deviation to prevent representational collapse; and the covariance term
\mathcal{L}_{\text{cov}}
penalizes off-diagonal elements of the embedding covariance matrix to decorrelate dimensions
(see
Bardes et al. 2022
, for full definitions)
. We use
\lambda_{\text{inv}}=10
,
\lambda_{\text{var}}=10
,
\lambda_{\text{cov}}=1
throughout all experiments.

Descriptors

We design two domain-specific descriptors for the modalities represented in Gate 5B. Additional descriptors explored during screening are detailed in
appendix

A
.

3.4.1
A4: Octave-Band Energy Dynamics (Audio)

The A4 descriptor captures temporal dynamics of spectral energy across logarithmically-spaced frequency bands. Given an audio waveform, we compute:

1.

STFT
: Compute the magnitude spectrogram with
n_{\text{FFT}}=2048
, hop length
=512
, yielding frequency resolution
\Delta f\approx 11.72
Hz/bin at
f_{s}=24
kHz. The output has
T_{\text{stft}}=188
frames for a 4-second segment.

2.

Log-magnitude
:
M[k,t]=\log(1+|X[k,t]|)
, where
X
is the STFT.

3.

Octave-band aggregation
: We define 8 bands with logarithmically-spaced boundaries (
table

1
), and average the log-magnitude within each band:

\bar{M}[b,t]=\frac{1}{k_{b}^{\text{end}}-k_{b}^{\text{start}}}\sum_{k=k_{b}^{\text{start}}}^{k_{b}^{\text{end}}-1}M[k,t].

(4)

4.

Temporal delta
:
\Delta M[b,t]=\bar{M}[b,t+1]-\bar{M}[b,t]
.

5.

Normalization
: Per-band z-score normalization within each sample:

A4[b,t]=\frac{\Delta M[b,t]-\mu_{b}}{\sigma_{b}+\epsilon},\quad\mu_{b}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}\Delta M[b,t],\quad\sigma_{b}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t}(\Delta M[b,t]-\mu_{b})^{2}}.

(5)

The resulting descriptor
A4\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times 188\times 8}
captures how spectral energy
changes
over time in each frequency band, providing the audio encoder with explicit temporal dynamics information.

3.4.2
D4: Local Pitch Intervals (MIDI)

The D4 descriptor encodes local melodic context for each MIDI note using 4 features derived from pitch differences with neighboring notes:

D4_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}(p_{i}-p_{i-1})/24\\[2.0pt]
(p_{i+1}-p_{i})/24\\[2.0pt]
\text{clamp}\!\big((p_{i}-p_{i-1})/12,\;-2,\;2\big)/2\\[2.0pt]
\text{clamp}\!\big((p_{i+1}-p_{i})/12,\;-2,\;2\big)/2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{4},

(6)

where
p_{i}
is the MIDI pitch (0–127). The first two features capture raw semitone intervals normalized by two octaves, while the latter two apply clamping to emphasize intervals within
\pm 2
octaves. Boundary notes (
i=1
or
i=N
) use zero-padding.

The output
D4\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times N\times 4}
provides the MIDI encoder with explicit intervallic context, analogous to how music theory emphasizes relative pitch over absolute pitch.

3.5
Injection Mechanisms

We explore four mechanisms for injecting descriptors into encoders.

3.5.1
Concatenation

The simplest approach concatenates the descriptor with encoder-internal features and projects back to the original dimension:

h^{\prime}_{t}=\LayerNorm\!\big(W_{\text{proj}}[h_{t}\,\|\,d_{t}]\big),

(7)

where
h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}
is the encoder feature at time
t
,
d_{t}
is the descriptor value, and
W_{\text{proj}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times(d_{\text{model}}+d_{\text{desc}})}
.

For A4 injection into the audio encoder:
h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1024}
,
d_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}
, so
W_{\text{proj}}\in\mathbb{R}^{1024\times 1032}
. The concatenation occurs after the CNN and before the Transformer.

For D4 injection into the MIDI encoder:
e_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{512}
,
d_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, so
W_{\text{proj}}\in\mathbb{R}^{512\times 516}
. The concatenation occurs after event embedding and before the Transformer.

3.5.2
Standard Cross-Attention

In standard cross-attention, encoder features serve as queries attending to descriptor features:

\text{XAttn}(F,D)=\softmax\!\left(\frac{Q_{F}\,K_{D}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V_{D},

(8)

where
Q_{F}=F\,W_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{F}\times d_{k}}
,
K_{D}=D_{\text{proj}}\,W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{D}\times d_{k}}
,
V_{D}=D_{\text{proj}}\,W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{D}\times d_{v}}
. Here
T_{F}=2400
(CNN output frames) and
T_{D}
depends on the descriptor.

The attention complexity is
O(T_{F}\cdot T_{D}\cdot d_{k})
, dominated by the
T_{F}=2400
query tokens.

3.5.3
Reverse Cross-Attention (Key Contribution)

We propose
reverse cross-attention
, where
descriptor tokens serve as queries
attending to encoder features:

\text{RevXAttn}(D,F)=\softmax\!\left(\frac{Q_{D}\,K_{F}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V_{F},

(9)

where
Q_{D}=D_{\text{proj}}\,W_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{D}\times d_{k}}
,
K_{F}=F\,W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{F}\times d_{k}}
,
V_{F}=F\,W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{F}\times d_{v}}
.

For A4 reverse cross-attention:
T_{D}=188
(native STFT frames) and
T_{F}=2400
(CNN output). The descriptor is projected to
d_{\text{model}}=1024
and augmented with learnable positional embeddings before serving as queries.

Complexity advantage.

The critical difference is in the
output
sequence length: reverse cross-attention produces
T_{D}=188
tokens (which are then mean-pooled), while standard cross-attention produces
T_{F}=2400
tokens. Since subsequent Transformer self-attention has
O(T^{2})
complexity, processing 188 vs. 2400 tokens yields:

\text{Speedup}=\frac{T_{F}^{2}}{T_{D}^{2}}=\frac{2400^{2}}{188^{2}}\approx 163\times.

(10)

This is conceptually related to the Perceiver architecture
(
Jaegle et al. 2021
)
, which uses a small set of latent tokens as queries to compress high-dimensional inputs. In our case, the “latent” tokens are not learned from scratch but carry structured domain knowledge via the descriptor.

3.5.4
Combined Mechanisms

Two combined configurations are evaluated in Gate 5B:

•

d4a4
: D4 concatenation in MIDI encoder + A4 concatenation in audio encoder.

•

d4-a4r
: D4 concatenation in MIDI encoder + A4 reverse cross-attention in audio encoder.

3.6
Training Protocol

Dataset.

We use MAESTRO v3.0.0
(
Hawthorne et al. 2019
)
, containing 1,276 classical piano performances with aligned audio and MIDI (
{\sim}200
hours). Audio–MIDI pairs are segmented into 4-second windows with 1-second hop, yielding
{\sim}13{,}000
training segments at
f_{s}=24
kHz.

Optimizer.

AdamW
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2019
)
with weight decay
0.01
,
\beta=(0.9,0.999)
, and batch size 16.

Learning rate schedule.

We employ a cosine-with-tail schedule
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
: linear warmup for 500 steps, cosine annealing with reference period of 30 epochs, a floor of
10\%
of base LR, and a linear tail decaying to
2\%
in the final epoch.

Hardware.

Primary experiments on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GB). Multi-seed validation and parameter-matched controls on a cluster of 36
\times
NVIDIA A30 (24 GB each) via SLURM. Full hyperparameters and scheduler details are provided in
appendix

D
.

6
Discussion

6.1
The A4 Mechanism: Spectral Dynamics as Cross-Modal Bridge

The most striking finding of this study is the complete causal dependence on A4—a relatively simple 8-dimensional descriptor capturing temporal changes in octave-band energy. The model does not merely
use
A4 as supplementary information; it has reorganized its entire representational strategy around it. When A4 is removed, performance collapses to below random chance (
S<8\%
), far worse than the 73.4% achieved by D0 which never had access to A4.

This suggests that A4 provides a
bridge representation
: a feature that is simultaneously computable from audio (via STFT) and predictable from MIDI (via expected harmonic content). Temporal dynamics of spectral energy—how energy in each octave band changes from frame to frame—capture the rhythmic and harmonic contour of a performance in a way that transcends the audio/symbolic divide.

6.2
The D4 Paradox: Training Regularization Without Inference Causality

D4 consistently improves screening performance (
+3.4\,\text{pp}
at 5 epochs) and contributes to d4a4 achieving the all-time record, yet in top dual models its ablation effect at inference is near-zero (
-0.4
to
+0.6\,\text{pp}
). In the D4-only control arm, the effect is small but non-zero (0.8–1.2
pp
). We propose three complementary explanations:

1.

Curriculum effect
: D4 provides explicit interval context that helps the MIDI encoder discover useful features faster, but the encoder eventually learns equivalent representations from raw note sequences.

2.

Gradient diversity
: D4 features create additional gradient pathways during backpropagation, improving optimization landscape navigation without remaining necessary in the forward pass.

3.

Implicit regularization
: The projection from 516 to 512 dimensions (
eq.

7
) acts as a bottleneck that encourages the encoder to compress descriptor information into its internal representations.

6.3
Reverse Cross-Attention: Informational Bottleneck as Feature

In the canonical seed-42 comparison, the reverse cross-attention mechanism (a4r) achieves
S=82.0\%
—competitive with the
83.8\%
record of d4a4—while being
163\times
cheaper in attention operations. Beyond computational efficiency, a4r shows the best transposition invariance (+23.6
pp
advantage over D0 at
\pm 3
semitones). We attribute this to the
informational bottleneck
created by using only 188 descriptor tokens as queries: by forcing all encoder information to flow through spectral-dynamics-shaped queries, the model is encouraged to learn representations structured around how energy changes across frequency bands—a property that is inherently invariant to absolute pitch.

6.4
Alignment and Retrieval Are Not Monotonic

The CKA analysis reveals a counterintuitive dissociation: d4-a4r has the highest cross-encoder alignment (CKA = 0.794) but not the highest retrieval performance (
S=79.8\%
vs.
83.8\%
for d4a4 with CKA = 0.659). This suggests a non-monotonic relationship between representational alignment and task performance. We hypothesize that moderate alignment preserves sufficient modality-specific information for discrimination, while excessive alignment homogenizes representations at the cost of discriminative detail.

6.5
Noise Robustness and Descriptor Vulnerability

Descriptor-augmented models show larger relative
S
drops than D0 at moderate noise levels (
\geq 20
dB SNR), but at 5 dB the ranking reverses and descriptor models retain higher absolute
S
(
fig.

7
). The noise robustness profile is thus non-monotonic: A4 acts as both a strength and a vulnerability—it provides a powerful cross-modal bridge but creates a new attack surface through the audio-computed descriptor pathway. At moderate noise, A4 corruption dominates; at extreme noise, the superior cross-modal alignment of descriptor models (CKA 0.66–0.79 vs. 0.44 for D0,
section

5.4
) provides residual matching ability through the noise-invariant MIDI pathway. Applications in noisy environments should consider the full robustness profile alongside peak performance.

6.6
Limitations

1.

Single dataset
: All experiments use MAESTRO v3.0.0, which contains exclusively classical piano. Generalization to other instruments, genres, or polyphonic settings is unknown.

2.

Headline replication is now closed
: The flagship d4a4 arm has been replicated across five independent training seeds, yielding
84.0\%\pm 2.7\,\text{pp}
. The observed five-seed ranges do not overlap with D0, and the effect size against the baseline remains large (Cohen
d=3.51
,
p<0.001
).

3.

Parameter-matched controls are now closed
: Test 02 isolates descriptor content from parameter-count inflation within the same architectural family. The real arm reaches 83.0%, while the parameter-matched zero, random, and shuffled controls fall to 75.0%, 73.6%, and 73.6%, respectively.

4.

Audio encoder partially frozen
: The MERTEncoderLite architecture is partially initialized from pre-trained weights, potentially confounding the analysis of descriptor effects with pre-training biases.

5.

No input augmentation
: All models are trained on unperturbed data. Velocity scaling and octave transposition cause catastrophic performance drops (
S<18\%
), suggesting that data augmentation during training could substantially improve robustness (see Test 09,
section

5.6
).

6.7
Future Directions

1.

Other instruments and genres
: Testing on datasets with broader timbral diversity (e.g., MusicNet
(
Thickstun et al. 2017
)
).

2.

Descriptor discovery
: Using the A4 success as a template for systematic descriptor search, potentially via learned descriptors initialized from hand-crafted ones.

3.

Scaling
: Exploring whether the descriptor injection paradigm scales to larger encoders and datasets.

4.

Robustness through augmentation
: Incorporating velocity scaling, octave transposition, and audio corruption during training to test whether the current fragilities can be reduced without erasing the descriptor-guided advantage.

7
Conclusion

We have presented a systematic exploration of descriptor injection for cross-modal audio–MIDI learning, spanning 24 experimental configurations across 3 phases of increasing rigor. Our findings challenge the assumption that cross-modal improvement requires complex architectural innovations: a simple 8-dimensional spectral dynamics descriptor (A4), injected via concatenation, yields a replicated mean advantage of
+8.8
percentage points over a strong VICReg baseline.

The scientific validation reveals that this improvement is not merely additive feature concatenation. Causal ablation shows the model has become
entirely dependent
on A4, reorganizing its representations around spectral dynamics. CKA analysis reveals that the mechanism is
representational convergence
: descriptors cause the audio and MIDI encoders to develop similar internal representations, effectively teaching both modalities a shared language grounded in spectral dynamics.

The reverse cross-attention mechanism offers both practical (
163\times
cheaper) and scientific (enhanced transposition invariance) advantages, demonstrating that structured information bottlenecks can improve generalization. The D4 paradox—clear training benefit with weak inference-time causality in this model family—suggests that domain-specific descriptors may play a role as training regularizers even when their forward-pass contribution is limited at deployment.

These results position descriptor injection as a lightweight, effective paradigm for cross-modal learning in the music domain, and suggest that the principle may generalize to other cross-modal settings where domain knowledge can be encoded as structured features. This work is situated within a broader theoretical program—Harmonic Information Theory—which examines the hypothesis that frequency ratios may function as informational carriers across physical modalities. A companion volume develops the full theoretical framework
(
Fernández Méndez and
Echániz 2026
)

A.2
A8: Onset-Weighted Chroma

The A8 descriptor computes a chroma representation weighted by onset strength, emphasizing transient events where note onsets provide the strongest pitch information.

1.

Compute STFT magnitude
|X[k,t]|
.

2.

Map STFT bins to 12 pitch classes via log-frequency:

\text{pc}(k)=\left\lfloor 12\cdot\log_{2}\!\left(\frac{k\cdot f_{s}/n_{\text{FFT}}}{f_{\text{ref}}}\right)\right\rfloor\bmod 12,

(15)

where
f_{\text{ref}}=32.7
Hz (C1).

3.

Accumulate energy per pitch class:
C[t,c]=\sum_{k:\,\text{pc}(k)=c}|X[k,t]|^{2}
.

4.

Compute spectral flux (onset strength):

F[t]=\sum_{k}\max\!\big(0,\;|X[k,t]|-|X[k,t{-}1]|\big).

(16)

5.

Normalize flux to
[0,1]
and gate chroma:
A8[t,c]=C[t,c]\cdot F[t]/\max(F)
.

6.

Normalize per frame:
A8[t,:]\leftarrow A8[t,:]/(\sum_{c}A8[t,c]+\epsilon)
.

Output:
A8\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times T_{\text{stft}}\times 12}
.

A.3
A9: IDF-Weighted Rational Attractor

A9 extends A7 with inverse document frequency (IDF) weighting to suppress ubiquitous ratio activations:

1.

Compute raw A7 activations
A7_{\text{raw}}[t,c]
(before normalization).

2.

Compute document frequency:
\text{df}_{c}=|\{t:A7_{\text{raw}}[t,c]>\tau\}|/T
, with
\tau=0.05
.

3.

Compute IDF:
\text{idf}_{c}=\text{clamp}\!\big(\log(1/(\text{df}_{c}+10^{-3})),\;0,\;5\big)
.

4.

Weight and normalize:
A9[t,c]=A7_{\text{raw}}[t,c]\cdot\text{idf}_{c}/\sum_{c^{\prime}}A7_{\text{raw}}[t,c^{\prime}]\cdot\text{idf}_{c^{\prime}}
.

The IDF weighting suppresses ratio activations that appear in
>50\%
of frames (e.g., octave/unison), emphasizing rarer harmonic relationships.
