Immunomodulatory role and therapeutic potential of thrombospondin-1 during infectious diseases and autoimmunity
Authors: Francisca Román, Aillairlyn Jara, Agnes Jara-Collao, Felipe Novoa, Cynthia Moreno-Quidecoy, Valentina Chavez, Fernanda Urzúa, Daniel Moreno, Susan M. Bueno, Pablo A. González, Alexis M. Kalergis, Hernán F. Peñaloza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infectious diseases and autoimmunity are leading causes of mortality worldwide. The lack of vaccines and resistance to current treatments by microbes highlight the importance of developing novel therapeutics. Similarly, treatments for autoimmune diseases are limited and require improved approaches. Identifying relevant proteins involved in host defense and tolerance to self-antigens is key to designing novel therapeutic strategies. Thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) is a host glycoprotein involved in host defense and inflammation. Here, we analyze how TSP-1 interacts with different ligands involved in immunity. Then, we discuss the roles of TSP-1 in infection and autoimmunity to finally examine the therapeutic potential of TSP-1.

Thrombospondin-1 in host defense and immune regulation

Thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1) is a matricellular host glycoprotein that interacts with a variety of ligands, including surface cell receptors such as CD36 and CD47, growth factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), cytokines such as transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β), and proteases such as neutrophil elastase (NE) [1]. TSP-1 was first discovered in the lumen of platelet α-granules that, upon activation, can be either extracellularly released or allocated in the surface of platelets [2]. Nowadays, it is well known that multiple cell types, including endothelial cells, fibroblasts, adipocytes, smooth muscle cells, myeloid cells (monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells), and tumoral cells, such as malignant glioma cells, can secrete TSP-1 under physiological conditions [3–5].

In the context of infectious diseases and autoimmunity, several studies have demonstrated that TSP-1 regulates host defense and inflammation. Studies in mouse models of infection have shown that TSP-1 can either mitigate harmful inflammation or suppress neutrophil microbicidal functions, resulting in opposite outcomes depending on the pathogen [6–8], properties that indicate the potential therapeutic use of TSP-1 in specific contexts. In this review, we describe and discuss current insights regarding the immunoregulatory roles of TSP-1 during infection and autoimmunity, as well as its therapeutic potential in inflammatory diseases.

Thrombospondin-1 and multidomain interactions

TSP-1 is a multidomain protein found as a homotrimer in the extracellular matrix (ECM) that regulates cell–matrix and cell–cell interactions [9–11]. It is a homotrimer cross-linked by disulfide bonds in the region that is between the N-terminal domain and the procollagen region (Fig. 1) [10, 12]. Each TSP-1 monomer has an N-terminal globular domain, linked by a type-I procollagen homology region (PC), which is followed by three repeated sequence domains known as Type I, Type II, and Type III repeats, and a C-terminal globular domain [2, 12] (Fig. 1). Studies focused on understanding the structural basis of TSP-1’s interactions with other ligands have contributed to a deeper understanding of the structural complexity of this protein (Fig. 1). Crystallization and bioinformatic analyses of purified recombinant domains of human TSP-1 obtained by transfection of an expression vector have enabled the study of some structural properties of TSP-1 [13–15].

The structure of the N-terminal domain of TSP-1 consists of a globular β sandwich with 13 antiparallel β-strands and 1 irregular strand-like segment classified as a member of the concanavalin A-like lectins/glucanases superfamily. Positively charged residues allow interactions with negatively charged molecules such as heparin. At the same time, the flexible linker region (PC region) may enable the N-terminal domain to adopt different orientations between monomers of TSP-1, facilitating ligand binding [13]. In vitro data have shown that the N-terminal domain of TSP-1 binds to ligands involved in cell adhesion/extravasation, ECM remodeling, or lipoprotein metabolism [16–41] (Table 1). The TSR structure (Type I repeat domain) includes an antiparallel 3-stranded fold consisting of alternating stacked layers of tryptophan and arginine residues from each strand, capped by disulfide bonds on each end. Further, the TSR contains a right-handed, positively charged spiral that may recognize anionic ligands such as glycosaminoglycans, a significant component of the extracellular matrix implicated in wound healing and angiogenesis [14, 41–61, 74].

Regarding the ligands that bind the Type II repeat of TSP-1, little information has been reported; the known ligands are the voltage-dependent calcium channel subunit α2δ−1 and integrin β1 (Table 1) [61, 62]. Notably, the crystal structure of the Type II repeat domain has not yet been elucidated, and the mechanism by which ligands bind to this region remains unclear. This lack of knowledge explains the limited information and functional studies related to ligands of the Type II repeat domain, except for those mentioned above (Table 1).

The Type III repeat domain is organized around a calcium-binding core, two DxDxDGxxDxxD motifs between repeats, and the integrin recognition motif Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) (Fig. 1), that binds to αIIbβ3 and αvβ3 [15]. The Type III domain also binds and inhibits neutrophil elastase (NE) and cathepsin G (Cat-G), limiting extracellular matrix degradation and collateral injury during neutrophilic responses [15, 63–71, 74] (Table 1). The C-terminal portion of TSP-1 forms a lectin-like β sandwich structure that contains four strictly conserved calcium-binding sites [15]. These calcium-binding sites could be involved in conformational changes of TSP-1 as mutations in these sites cause protein misfolding and potential loss of function [15]. The C-terminal domain of TSP-1 interacts with the surface receptor CD47 [72, 73] (Fig. 1, Table 1), which is involved in immunomodulation during different biological processes, such as phagocytosis, and cellular migration events such as transendothelial and transepithelial neutrophil migration [72].

The homotrimeric conformation and intrinsic multidomain structure of TSP-1 enable its interaction with multiple ligands simultaneously and form cross-linked complexes that facilitate several cellular processes. In vitro studies using the breast carcinoma cell line MDA-MB-231, the M14-derived melanoma cell line MDA-MB-435, Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), and human lung carcinoma cells (OH-1 cells) showed that the interaction between fibronectin and TSP-1 induces a conformational change in TSP-1 that facilitates cell adhesion through interaction with integrin α3β1. This interaction is not observed in the absence of fibronectin [75]. Other in vitro studies using peripheral blood T-lymphocytes and T cell leukemia cell lines (TALL cells) revealed that TSP-1 produced by T cells simultaneously interacts with calreticulin (CRT) and the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1 receptor or CD91), both of which are expressed on the surface of these cells, via its N-terminal domain. As a result, TSP-1 forms a complex that mediates cell adhesion in vitro [76].

TSP-1, in association with CD47, can interact with other ligands to modulate T cell function. In vitro studies using Jurkat cells and primary human CD4+ T cells demonstrated that TSP-1 can either stimulate or inhibit T cell adhesion, chemotaxis, and matrix metalloproteinase gene expression, depending on the production and availability of the integrin α4β1 and CD47. Whereas TSP-1 binding to α4β1 promotes cell adhesion, chemotaxis, and matrix metalloproteinase expression, its interaction with CD47 inhibits T cell proliferation and antigen-T cell receptor-mediated signaling in vitro [77]. Likewise, in vitro studies with autoreactive T cell clones isolated from rheumatoid arthritis patients, which lack the conventional costimulatory receptor CD28, and cultured with synovial fibroblasts as antigen-presenting cells, revealed that TSP-1 forms a trimolecular complex by simultaneously binding to CD47 expressed on T cells and CD36 on antigen-presenting cells. This complex serves as an alternative costimulatory pathway that lowers the threshold for T cell activation, promoting their activation [78].

The interaction of TSP-1 with other ligands may also mediate the activation of latent transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β). In vitro assays using human breast cancer cells (T47-D) have shown that the simultaneous interaction of TSP-1 with CD47 and integrin αvβ3 is necessary for TGF-β activation, as neutralization of either CD47, integrin αvβ3, or TSP-1 blocks TGF-β activation in cells cultured under conditions of estrogen depletion [79]. In vitro assays with alveolar macrophages have also shown that the interaction of CD36 and plasmin with TSP-1 is involved in TSP-1-mediated TGF-β activation in these cells [80, 81].

This data shows that TSP-1 crosslinking with more than one ligand simultaneously may be required in a variety of cellular processes involved in T cell and macrophage activation.

The complex structure of TSP-1 and its high capacity to interact with diverse proteins and molecules suggest that TSP-1 participates in several physiological processes in health and disease. In the following section, we focus on the role of TSP-1 in immunity (Fig. 1), identifying the TSP-1 ligands involved in host defense and injury during infection and autoimmune diseases.

TSP-1 is a complex matricellular protein involved in immunity

TSP-1 can bind to integrins, surface receptors, and soluble factors involved in immunity [1, 82] through interactions mediated by the N-terminal domain, the Type I repeat domain, the Type III repeat domain, and the C-terminal domain.

Control of leukocyte trafficking and adhesion dynamics

Integrins are heterodimeric transmembrane molecules known to be [14] involved in critical biological processes, such as cell proliferation, cell migration, apoptosis, tissue repair, inflammation, immunity, and angiogenesis [83, 84]. The laminin-binding integrins α3β1 and α6β1 can interact with the N-terminal domain of TSP-1 (Fig. 1), mediating cell-adhesion and angiogenesis of human dermal microvascular endothelial cells, and pulmonary, breast, bone marrow, or glioblastoma carcinoma lines in vitro [23–25, 28, 85]. In the presence of human platelets-purified TSP-1 or TSP-1-derived synthetic peptides, these cells adhere and proliferate, interacting with each other through these integrins [23–25, 28, 85]. However, the phenotype described was diminished in the presence of inhibitors or neutralizing antibodies against α3β1 and α6β1 where the adherence and proliferation of these cells is blunted [23–25, 28, 85]. The α4β1 and α9β1 integrins bind to fibronectin and the N-terminal domain of TSP-1 simultaneously (Fig. 1) [26, 27, 29]. The α9β1 integrin is known to regulate hematopoiesis [86], and the interaction between α9β1 and TSP-1 in vitro regulates cell adhesion and proliferation in α9-transfected SW480 cells (human colon cancer cell line) and human microvascular endothelial cells [29]. In addition, the α9β1-TSP-1 interaction promotes angiogenesis through neovascularization in an in vivo mouse model [29]. α4β1 integrin plays an important role in lymphocyte migration to inflammatory sites and can contribute to the modulation of T cell behavior through the interaction with TSP-1 and CD47 [27, 77]. The neutralization of α4β1 with an antibody or an antagonist impairs the migration and adhesion of human primary CD4+ T cells and Jurkat T cells (an immortalized line of human T cells) in TSP-1- and CD47-coated plates due to impaired surface adhesion [27, 77].

The αIIbβ3 and αVβ3 RGD-recognizing integrins interact with the RGD motif from the Type III repeat domain of TSP-1 (Fig. 1) [70]. The αIIbβ3 integrin participates in platelet activation, while αVβ3 has significant roles in angiogenesis, such as regulation of the ECM, vascular smooth muscle cell migration, and osteoclast adhesion to the bone marrow [84, 87]. In addition, the interaction of TSP-1 with CD47 in vitro modulates the adhesion and activation of platelets through αIIbβ3 integrin activation, which could be measured with the binding of the antibody PAC-1 to the surface of platelets [88].The αVβ3 integrin regulates the migration of macrophages and neovascularization in the vessel wall of atherosclerotic rabbits; both could be involved in the wound repair [89]. Similarly, the function of integrin αVβ3 can be in vitro modulated by the TSP-1-CD47 interaction in C32 human melanoma cells and A2058 melanoma cells [72, 90].

β1-integrins have a fundamental role in signal pathways involved in immune cell activation, such as the pathway mediated by PI3K/AKT, which is relevant for wound repair processes, such as with the migration and cell adhesion to the lungs in a scratch injury model [91]. β1-integrins can bind to TSP-1 through the Type I and Type II repeat domains (Fig. 1). In vitro, the TSR domain of TSP-1 can inhibit vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-induced migration in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC), a process mediated by β1- integrin via PI3K, as its downregulation or the inhibition of the PI3K pathway reduced the inhibitory effect of TSP-1 in the VEGF-induced migration of these cells [92]. As noted, integrins can interact with other TSP-1 ligands while binding with TSP-1 (Table 1). These findings evidenced the role of these proteins from the ECM in the formation of complex through crosstalk interactions that can play roles in different cell processes.

TSP-1 interactions with surface receptors involved in inflammation

Among the surface receptors involved in immunity that interact with TSP-1, the Type I repeat domain binds CD36 and CD148 (Fig. 1). CD36 is a multi-ligand glycoprotein receptor known as a transporter sensor of free fatty acids and a scavenger receptor that participates in host defense during acute pulmonary infection in mice [93, 94]. TSP-1/CD36 interaction has been proven necessary in different processes, including IL-10 production and efferocytosis of apoptotic neutrophils by alveolar macrophages during the resolution phase of lung inflammation caused by bacterial lipopolysaccharide in vivo [95]. Similarly, the interaction between TSP-1 and CD36 mediates the efferocytosis of human apoptotic fibroblasts by macrophages through the induction of FasL in vitro. This effect was ameliorated after the neutralization of either TSP-1 or CD36 through monoclonal antibodies [96].

TGF-β is an anti-inflammatory cytokine involved in cell growth, differentiation, and immune modulation [97, 98]. It is secreted in a latent form, bound to the latency-associated peptide (LAP) complex, which keeps it inactive [99, 100]. In vitro data show that TSP-1 can bind to the latent precursor of TGF-β, interacting with LAP through a KRFK (RFK)-like sequence between the type I repeats of TSP-1, inducing a conformational change in LAP that exposes the active domain of TGF-β, allowing it to become biologically active [101]. In the context of specific inflammatory conditions, in vitro studies using alveolar macrophages have established that neutralizing TSP-1 with a monoclonal antibody inhibited TGF-β activation by these cells obtained from bleomycin-induced lung injury in rats, where immunofluorescence analyses showed colocalization between TGF-β and CD36 on the surface of these cells [80], suggesting that CD36 is required for TSP-1-dependent TGF-β activation in macrophages. In addition, the same research group showed that plasmin, an active tryptic protease derived from the proteolysis of plasminogen, is also involved in the activation of latent TGF-β [81]. Using an in vivo model of bleomycin-induced fibrosis in rats, they showed that, in vitro, alveolar macrophages in the presence of TSP-1 induced higher activation of TGF-β in the supernatant, an effect that was reduced in the presence of a plasmin inhibitor. These data suggest that plasmin and TSP-1 have significant roles in activating TGF-β. As mentioned above, the interaction of the latent form of TGF-β with TSP-1 and CD36 would allow spatial localization of this process with plasmin on the surface of these cells [80].

Another study also provided evidence regarding the potential regulatory role of TSP-1 over TGF-β activation through mechanisms mediated by the plasminogen/plasmin protease system in pancreatic cancer [102]. Plasmin is a tryptic protease derived from the proteolysis of plasminogen. The mentioned study showed that the treatment of the pancreatic cancer cell lines ASPC-1 and COLO-357, with TSP-1 and TGF-β promoted the upregulation of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), a protein that inhibits the action of the plasminogen activator necessary to regulate excessive urokinase plasminogen activator-induced extracellular matrix degradation required for tumor cell invasion during metastasis [102]. Interestingly, blocking the action of TSP-1 with specific neutralizing antibodies in vitro decreases PAI-1 expression in the adenocarcinoma cell lines ASPC-1 and COLO-357 [102]. Similar findings were observed when these cells were cultured in the presence of neutralizing TGF-β antibodies. This data suggests that TSP-1 and TGF-β upregulate PAI-1 expression in pancreatic cancer cell lines [102].

Supramolecular attack particles are stable, cytotoxic, multiprotein structures produced and released by cytotoxic T lymphocytes and Natural Killer cells into the intercellular space, inducing apoptosis in cells recognized as infected by microorganisms or as tumor cells [103–105]. These structures consist of a shell (surface) rich in TSP-1 and TSP-4 and a core rich in granzymes and perforin [103–105]. While granzymes and perforin are the effector proteins that induce cytotoxicity, TSP-1 and TSP-4 stabilize the structure and facilitate the transfer of cytotoxic particles to target cells through interactions with surface receptors such as CD36 [103–106].

CD148 is a receptor-type protein tyrosine phosphate involved in the negative regulation of growth factors, suppressing cell proliferation and transformation [59]. In vitro assays with human renal microvascular endothelial cells (HRMEC) and HUVEC cells stimulated with exogenous TSP-1 reveal that CD148 co-immunoprecipitated with TSP-1 [59]. Furthermore, in vitro cell binding assays in HEK23 cells showed that the deletion of the extracellular region of CD148 impaired the binding of TSP-1 [59]. Further experiments using truncated forms of TSP-1 demonstrated that TSP-1 binds to CD148 through the first repeat of the TSR domain [60]. CD148 has been described to inhibit, in vitro, the proliferation of HRMEC cells and the proliferation of A431D cells, a cell line resembling epidermoid cervical carcinoma [59, 60]. CD148 mediated the proliferation of A431D cells by TSP-1, and the downregulation of CD148, obtained through CD148-targeting sgRNA lentiviral particles, in A431D cells showed normal proliferation [59, 60]. Inhibition of the proliferation of endothelial cells and angiogenesis mediated by the binding of TSP-1 to CD148 was also observed in mice using a model of subcutaneous implantation of gel-foam sponges loaded with VEGF in the presence or absence of TSP-1. Interestingly, the inhibition of angiogenesis was not observed when the sponge was implanted in CD148−/− mice [60].

Another receptor involved in the immune response that binds the C-terminal of TSP-1 is CD47. CD47 is an integrin-associated protein from the immunoglobulin superfamily, heavily glycosylated and expressed on the surface of most cells, including monocytes, platelets, T cells, and B cells [107–109]. In vitro, assays indicate that CD47 signaling is required to produce IL-12 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) in primary human dendritic cells (DCs), as its neutralization decreased the production of these cytokines following stimulation with Staphylococcus aureus Cowan I strain (SAC) [110]. In addition, the ligation between TSP-1 and CD47 may negatively affect T cell activation in vitro [77, 111, 112]. In vitro assays of Jurkat cells proliferation showed increased proliferation of CD47-deficient cells in the presence of purified TSP-1 when compared with WT cells [111]. The decreased proliferation of WT Jurkat cells was consistent with a decreased expression of the activation markers CD69, Erg-1, and PAC-1 [111]. Similar effect was observed when cells were treated with an anti-CD47 neutralizing antibody [111], indicating that the TSP-1 and CD47 binding modulates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in myeloid cells and T cell activation, potentially being a major inflammatory control switch during infection and inflammation.

Inhibition of soluble proteases by TSP-1

NE and Cat-G are proteases produced by neutrophils during infection [113] that interact with TSP-1 through the Type III repeat domain [65, 66] (Fig. 1). NE and Cat-G are relevant components of neutrophil azurophilic granules and participate in the nonoxidative pathway of intracellular and extracellular pathogen destruction [113]. TSP-1 can inhibit the protease activity of NE and Cat-G, downmodulating the inflammatory response and tempering tissue injury during infection [6]. For example, inhibiting NE and Cat-G by TSP-1 protects from lung injury during acute Pseudomonas aeruginosa lung infection [6].

Role of TSP-1 in the immune response against infection

Given the interaction between TSP-1 and several ligands involved in immunity and inflammation, numerous in vivo and in vitro findings show that TSP-1 has a significant role in the immune response during infection. However, whether TSP-1 enhances or impairs host defense and inflammation varies among pathogens and sites of infection.

Protective roles of TSP-1 during infection

In some infection models, TSP-1 improves host defense, limits tissue injury, and contributes to the resolution of the infection. During mouse acute lung infection with Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa), a Gram-negative bacterium characterized by causing proteolytic pneumonia [114], TSP-1 protects the lung tissue from excessive bacterial- and neutrophilic-driven inflammation [6], and enhances host defense, as full body TSP-1 deficient (Thbs1−/−) mice (Table 2) showed increased susceptibility, impaired lung bacterial clearance, and elevated neutrophil recruitment and activation to the airspace when compared to WT mice [6]. Notably, in this infection model, TSP-1 showed inhibitory properties against the Pseudomonas metalloprotease, LasB [6], indicating that the inhibitory action of TSP-1 over proteases is not restricted to host proteases, but it can also affect the proteolytic activity of microbial proteases. Mechanistically, the inhibition of neutrophil- and P. aeruginosa-derived proteases by TSP-1 reduces the activation of IL-36γ, a main pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in neutrophil activation [117]. IL-36γ belongs to the IL-1 superfamily and needs to be fully cleaved in the N-terminal region to be fully active [8]. In vitro data show that both NE and LasB can cleave the complete form of IL-36γ and generate two different truncated forms of the protein. While NE cleaves IL-36γ proximal to Y16, LasB cleaves it proximal to M18 [117]. IL-36γ neutralization in Thbs1−/− mice enhanced lung P. aeruginosa clearance and reduced neutrophil activation to equivalent levels to those observed in WT mice [117]. This data indicates that during acute pulmonary P. aeruginosa infection, TSP-1 regulates lung neutrophilic inflammation and facilitates host defense by restraining the extracellular proteolytic environment required for IL-36γ activation. In addition to these regulatory mechanisms governing neutrophil activation, a recent study has revealed, both in vitro and in vivo, further regulatory effects of TSP-1 on the immune response against P. aeruginosa in the lungs, consisting in the induction of IL-10 by anti-inflammatory neutrophils through a mechanism dependent on the transcription factor peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma [115]. This study showed that TSP-1-induced IL-10 production by these cells is required for host defense and for reduction of the lung micro-permeability, as Thbs1−/− mice transferred with IL-10+/+ neutrophils showed lower lung bacterial burden, reduced lung micro-permeability, and increased lung IL-10 levels when compared with nontransferred Thbs1−/− mice, or mice transferred with IL-10−/− cells at 24 h postinfection [115]. In addition, a recent study has described that TSP-1 produced by megakaryocytes and platelets, but not by myeloid or endothelial cells, downmodulates lung neutrophil activity and prevents disruption of the lung alveolar barrier by stabilizing the levels of COL6A1 in the airways following injury caused by P. aeruginosa [116]. These data show that during pulmonary infection, TSP-1 is involved in the modulation of the inflammatory status of myeloid cells such as neutrophils and in the prevention of barrier disruption through stabilization of the extracellular matrix.

A similar protective role by TSP-1 is observed during herpes simplex one virus (HSV-1) infection (Table 2), a DNA virus from the Orthoherpesviridae family that also includes other relevant viruses for human health, such as HSV-2, varicella-zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, and Epstein-Barr virus, among others [145]. This family of viruses causes numerous diseases, including ocular keratitis, also known as herpes simplex keratitis (HSK), which leads to blindness, and skin infections that subsequently allow viral persistence in the nervous system in a latent form [146, 147]. In vitro studies showed that HSV-1 and HSV-2 downregulate the expression of TSP-1 by human keratocytes during the first 24 h of infection [118]. TSP-1 is critical in downregulating corneal angiogenesis during corneal disease [148]. Therefore, it can be speculated that in vivo, the suppression of TSP-1 during HSV-1 infection may contribute to corneal stromal vascularization, increasing the severity of the disease [118]. An in vivo study on HSK in mice described that the C-terminal domain of TSP-1 had antiviral effects against HSV-1 in the eye [119]. Pretreatment or postinfection subconjunctival injection of the C-terminal domain of TSP-1 in HSV-1-infected mice reduced ocular injury and viral replication, improving host survival compared to infected mice that received vehicle [119]. The topical administration of the C-terminal domain of TSP-1 in mice during HSK increased the number of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as DCs and M1 macrophages expressing iNOS, CD86, TNF, IL-1β, and IL-6 in comparison to vehicle-treated mice [119]. The increased APCs observed in response to TSP-1 in HSK mice correlated with an increased number of CD8+ T cells in the draining lymph nodes [119]. All these data suggest that C-terminal domain of TSP-1 improved the host immune response during HSK infection in mice.

In addition, in vitro studies have shown a promising protective role of TSP-1 against Hantavirus (Table 2), a single-stranded RNA virus commonly found in a particular type of rodents. In humans, Hantavirus causes hemorrhagic fever with renal syndrome and cardiopulmonary syndrome [149]. In vitro, HUVEC cells infected with the pathogenic strains of Hantavirus Andes 23 and Hantaan 76–118 showed impaired TSP-1 expression and extracellular accumulation in a virus replication-dependent manner [120]. In healthy circumstances, endothelial cells regulate blood homeostasis by expressing several proteins, including ICAM, heparin, and fibrinogen [150, 151]. The interaction between TSP-1 and these proteins leads to several processes, such as actin cytoskeleton reorganization, focal adhesion, endothelial cell proliferation suppression, and apoptosis [37, 92, 152]. We, therefore, speculate that TSP-1 dysregulation by Hantavirus could contribute to the viral pathogenesis, eventually resulting in severe injury, internal and external bleeding, and, subsequently, hemorrhagic fever. However, in vivo studies are needed to evaluate the potential protective role of TSP-1 during Hantavirus infection.

Dengue virus is a single-positive-stranded RNA virus member of the Flaviviridae family that causes dengue fever or dengue hemorrhagic fever [153]. In patients diagnosed with non-severe dengue, whole blood microarray analyses revealed reduced mRNA expression of TSP-1, elastase 2, lactoferrin, and neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin compared to healthy controls [123] (Table 2). Although no differences in TSP-1 at protein levels were found in non-severe dengue patients when compared with healthy controls [123], patients with severe dengue, characterized by plasma leakage, hemorrhage, or organ dysfunction, did show reduced plasma levels of TSP-1 compared to healthy controls and non-severe dengue patients [121, 122]. The reduction of TSP-1 levels in Dengue patients shows a positive correlation with platelet count, and a negative correlation with aspartate aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase levels, as well with the hematocrit [122]. Both studies suggest that the TSP-1 levels can be attributed to leukopenia and thrombocytopenia during infection and suggest that TSP-1 could be exploited as a disease severity marker.

Pathogenic roles of TSP-1 during infection

Interestingly, in some infection models, TSP-1 has been shown to have pathogenic roles. In vitro, data of human immune cells infected with the commensal bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) [124] reveals that human peripheral blood mononuclear cells-differentiated DCs showed an increased expression of CD47 and TSP-1 following infection with a rifampicin-resistant E. coli K1 isolate (ECK1) in a mechanism dependent on the outer membrane protein A (Table 2) [154]. The blockage of the interaction between CD47 and TSP-1 with an anti-CD47 antibody resulted in increased expression of CD40, HLA-DR, and CD86, typical DCs activation markers, increased antigen presentation, and increased production of TNF, IL1-β, IL-6, and IL-12 [154]. Similar results were observed in ECK1-infected DCs treated with a siRNA against either CD47 or TSP-1, suggesting that ECK1 prevents DC maturation, antigen presentation, and cytokine production in a mechanism that involves the ligation between CD47 and TSP-1 [154]. Moreover, in vivo assays using a model of ECK1 meningitis in newborn mice showed that the knockdown of TSP-1 or CD47 using specific siRNAs increased survival compared to infected mice treated with the control RNA [154]. In addition, the downregulation of CD47 or TSP-1 in mice infected with ECK1 reduced the bacterial burden in the bloodstream at 24 h postinfection compared with the RNA control treated mice [154]. This data suggests that the interaction between TSP-1 and CD47 has a pathogenic role in the immune response during meningitis caused by E. coli K1, impairing the correct function of DCs.

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) (Table 2) is a Gram-negative encapsulated bacterium of substantial clinical relevance as it is one of the leading causes of pneumonia in hospital environments [155]. In vivo acute respiratory infections with K. pneumoniae ATCC 43816 have observed that inoculated TSP-1 deficient mice (Thbs1−/−) showed improved lung bacterial clearance and survival compared to WT mice [7]. Interestingly, the clearance of K. pneumoniae from the lungs was correlated with an increase of NE and Cat-G activity in Thbs1−/− mice and with reduced production of IL-6, IL-10, G-CSF, GM-CSF, KC, and MCP-1 compared to WT mice [7]. The improved host defense observed in Thbs1−/− mice was reversed after the exogenous administration of TSP-1 [7]. Further, in vitro assays demonstrated that Thbs1−/− neutrophils were much more efficient at clearing K. pneumoniae when compared with WT neutrophils, presumably due to the enhanced activity of NE and Cat-G [7]. This data suggests that microbial killing induced by NE and Cat-G, downregulated by TSP-1, is required for the host during a K. pneumoniae infection.

TSP-1 is also a critical host factor involved in the immune response against the influenza A virus (IAV) (Table 2), one of the most common causes of lower respiratory infections during the winter season in the human population [156]. During IAV infection in mice, increased levels of TSP-1 were observed both at transcript and protein levels in lung megakaryocytes and platelets that peaked at day 3 postinfection [157]. In addition, another study has reported that during IAV infection in mice, alveolar macrophages (AM) upregulate CD47 in their surface compared to uninfected mice [125]. Interestingly, neutralization of CD47 in mice during an IAV infection increased the numbers of AM at day 3 postinfection, decreased the viral burden, and decreased the production of IL-1β, TNF, and IFN-γ at 7 days post-infection in the lungs [125]. Furthermore, adoptive transfer of CD47-deficient AM into AM-depleted WT mice effectively reduced lung viral burden at 5 days post-IAV infection [125]. Mechanistically, ex vivo assays have demonstrated that the binding between CD47 and TSP-1 in AM negatively regulates the production of hemoglobin in these cells [125]. This is particularly important as the subunit beta of human hemoglobin regulates the antiviral response in a mechanism that involves RIG-I and MDA5 [126]. These data suggest that recognizing TSP-1 by CD47 in AMs negatively modulates their antiviral response, impacting the host defense against IAV.

Due to the difficulty of finding suitable in vivo models to replicate the infection observed in humans, studies focused on studying the immune response against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) (Table 2) [127] have relied primarily on in silico and in vitro assays using human samples from patients infected with SARS-CoV-2. During infection, endothelial cells are highly affected by SARS-CoV-2, and coagulation abnormalities have been identified in several infected patients [127]. TSP-1 is involved in the coagulation pathway as it binds fibrinogen and fibrin through the TSR region [158] and contributes to the formation of thrombi through the regulation of the von Willebrand Factor (vWF) [159]. Proteomics-based quantitative approaches determined that the serum of COVID-19 asymptomatic patients presented upregulated levels of TSP-1 and proteins involved in coagulation, such as fibrinogen-α and the vWF [128]. These proteins are procoagulant and thrombo-inflammatory markers in severe COVID-19 patients and are associated with long COVID syndrome [128, 129]. A transcriptomic analysis in PBMCs from healthy controls and COVID-19 patients with severe and mild disease showed that severe COVID-19 patients experienced upregulation of Il17ra and Thbs1 transcripts when compared with mild COVID-19 and healthy controls and a downregulation of the miR-29a-3p, a small non-coding RNA (miRNA) that negatively regulates the expression of TSP-1 in THP-1 cells (a cell line resembles human monocytes and macrophages) [130]. These data suggest that the increased thrombosis observed in patients undergoing severe COVID-19 may be due to reduced levels of miR-29a-3p and dysregulated production and/or activity of TSP-1 [130].

Likewise, studies focused on understanding the immune response against the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), a virus from the retroviridae family that causes acquired immune deficiency syndrome [131], reveal the relation and impact of TSP-1 in the immune response against this virus through in vitro assays with cells obtained from HIV positive case (HIVpos) and negative (HIVneg) patients (Table 2). Interestingly, PBMCs from HIVpos subjects express higher levels of CD47 compared with PMBCs from HIVneg subjects, especially in NK cells, which have a significant antiviral role regulated by the expression of CD47 [132, 160]. Overexpression of CD47 may be mediated by the Toll-like receptor 7/8 (TLR7/8) pathway, activated through the recognition of the HIV proteins Gag and Env as the treatment of NK cells with TRL7/8 agonists resulted in the upregulation of CD47 in NK cells isolated from HIVpos subjects in comparison to non-treated cells [160]. This study also showed that HIVpos subjects showed elevated expression of TSP-1 in plasma when compared to HIVneg controls [160]. Treatment of NK cells derived from HIVneg subjects with recombinant TSP-1 for 24 h resulted in reduced expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67, the activation marker CD69, and IFN-γ compared to untreated cells [160]. Finally, the neutralization of CD47 and the inhibition of STAT3 with the inhibitor stattic restored IFN-γ production by HIVpos NK cells [160]. This data suggests that during HIV infection, the interaction of CD47 with TSP-1 suppresses the activation of NK cells through the JAK-STAT3 pathway, potentially leading to an aggravation of the infection. However, in vivo experiments are still needed to corroborate this hypothesis.

There is little information on the effect of TSP-1 during fungal infections. However, in the context of Candida albicans infection, one of the most common causes of mycotic infections in immunocompromised patients [161], TSP-1 impairs host defense and infection resolution (Table 2) [162]. During a systemic infection of C. albicans in mice, TSP-1 deficiency led to improved host survival, improved fungal phagocytosis, reduced fungal burden, reduced accumulation of iNOS+ monocytes, higher production of IL-10 in the kidney but lower levels of IL-6 and TNF, and reduced neutrophil levels when compared with WT mice [162]. Interestingly, CD47 deficiency in mice increases their susceptibility to C. albicans compared to WT mice [133]. CD47−/− mice showed increased fungal burden in the brain and kidneys and elevated expression of NLRP3, increased expression of chemoattractants such as MIP-2α and MIP-2β, and increased production of the cytokines IL-6, TNF, and IL-10 when compared to WT mice [133]. The susceptibility to C. albicans observed in CD47-deficient mice contrasts with the enhanced immune response in TSP-1-deficient mice. This data suggests that the mechanisms by which TSP-1 downmodulates the pro-inflammatory response during candidiasis are independent of CD47, but may be mediated by other surface receptors or soluble factors.

In cases of intracellular parasites, data have been reported on the role of TSP-1 in parasitic infections, such as Plasmodium spp., protozoan species that cause malaria and can affect mammals, birds, and reptiles, transmitted by female mosquitoes of the genus Anopheles and other Culicidae [134]. In the context of Plasmodium falciparum infection in humans [163], TSP-1 was originally described as a receptor involved in the cytoadherence of parasite-infected erythrocytes in vitro [163]. TSP-1 acts as a transient mediator that promotes the adhesion of infected erythrocytes to the vascular endothelium. This binding prevents the clearance of infected cells in the spleen, allowing the parasite to persist and proliferate within the host [135]. In vitro studies using single- molecule force spectroscopy to evaluate the binding affinity between TSP-1 or CD36 to P. falciparum-infected red blood cells (RBCs) and uninfected RBC revealed that TSP-1 binds more strongly than CD36 to these cells, but CD36 plays a role in complementing erythrocyte adhesion, making it a stronger and more lasting adhesion [137, 164]. On the other hand, CD47 is expressed on RBCs and acts as a marker that regulates the phagocytosis of aged or damaged cells. Whereas younger RBCs, which are preferred by the parasite, exhibit higher CD47 expression, which progressively declines with cellular aging, leading to their phagocytic clearance via ligation of signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on macrophages [138]. In vivo studies demonstrated that CD47-deficient mice are highly resistant to malaria, showing a significant decrease in parasitic burden and a faster resolution of infection compared with WT mice, which exhibit greater parasitemia in the spleen when infected with Plasmodium yoelii [138, 163]. In this context, the data invites to speculate that the parasite may benefit from erythrocyte expression of CD47 to avoid SIRPα-mediated elimination of infected RBCs by macrophages. Since TSP-1 can also interact with CD47, it may impair the recognition of CD47 by SIRpa’s, contributing to the persistence of the infection.

Undetermined roles of TSP-1 during infection

Even though it is well known that TSP-1 plays significant roles in host defense during infection, the specific contribution of TSP-1 against major pathogens remains to be fully elucidated. In vitro evidence suggests promising potential roles for TSP-1 in infection by major Gram-positive bacteria of the upper respiratory tract microbiota, such as Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus), Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis), and Streptococcus pneumoniae (S. pneumoniae) [136, 165]. Available data suggest that TSP-1 facilitates the adhesion and colonization of the microbe on respiratory epithelial cells through direct interaction with bacterial surface proteins (Table 2). For example, two repeated regions in the C-terminus of the staphylococcal autolysins AtlA and AtlE, present in S. aureus and S. epidermidis, respectively, mediate interactions between these bacteria and TSP-1, facilitating bacterial adhesion to epithelial and endothelial cells [140]. Similar results showed that the peptidoglycan of S. pneumoniae, Streptococcus pyogenes, and Listeria monocytogenes bind to TSP-1 in vitro [139, 165]. These studies determined that increasing concentrations of TSP-1 improved S. pneumoniae adherence to a protein-coated plate and to A549 cells (human type II pneumocytes), Detroit 562 cells (human pharynx carcinoma), HEp-2 cells (human larynx carcinoma), and human brain-derived endothelial cells (HBMECs) [139]. A second study showed that the virulence factors PavB and PspC of S. pneumoniae in vitro bind to TSP-1 in a dose-dependent manner since the adhesion of mutant strains lacking these virulence factors was reduced compared with the WT isogenic strain [136]. Even though this data has been obtained exclusively through in vitro assays and needs to be further validated in vivo, it suggests that TSP-1 is used by Gram-positive bacteria as a bridge to reach epithelial cells, mediating bacterial adhesion and potentially promoting the translocation of the epithelial barrier during infection.

Likewise, the impact of TSP-1 during parasite infections is a primarily unexplored field of research, and the few existing studies have reported the role of TSP-1 during parasitic infections. In the context of Trypanosoma cruzi, an obligate intracellular parasite that causes Chagas disease [166] (Table 2), a gene transcription microarray analysis in infected primary human coronary artery smooth muscle (HCASM) cells revealed the overexpression of Thbs1 along with several other transcripts whose protein products participate in cell differentiation, growth, and adhesion [141]. Downregulation of TSP-1 using RNA interference in T. cruzi HCASM-infected cells reduced cell infection and parasite proliferation compared with cells transfected with the empty vector [141]. T. cruzi can secrete Calreticulin (TcCRT), a calcium-binding chaperone also secreted by humans, known to interact with TSP-1 through its N-terminal domain [142]. It is observed that T. cruzi uses TcCRT to bind TSP-1 on its surface, facilitating the infection of mouse embryo fibroblasts (MEF) [142]. Significantly, the deletion of TSP-1 in MEF cells and the neutralization of TcCRT reduced the infection rate of these cells by T. cruzi. At the same time, the exogenous addition of TSP-1 improved infection [142]. These in vitro data could suggest a possible pathogenic role of TSP-1 during T. cruzi infection, although in vivo experiments are needed to validate this conclusion.

For Toxoplasma gondii (T. gondii), an apicomplexan parasite of clinical relevance, the physiological role of TSP-1 remains largely unexplored. In vitro studies using human foreskin fibroblast (HFF) cells and recombinant proteins demonstrated that the parasite’s micronemal protein MIC2, a member of the TRAP family (thrombospondin-related anonymous proteins), contains an integrin-like A-domain and thrombospondin type 1 repeats (TSP-1-like domains) that bind heparin and HFF cells, suggesting a role in host cell attachment [143]. Additionally, bioinformatic analyses have revealed that various T. gondii adhesins contain well-conserved TSP-1 domains, which are thought to facilitate invasion [144]. These in vitro findings suggest that TSP-1-like domains in T. gondii proteins may be critical for infection. However, further studies are needed to elucidate their contribution during infection fully and to determine whether host TSP-1 plays any direct role in toxoplasmosis.

clinical data

Given TSP-1’s role as a regulator of TGF-β activation [97, 101, 175], TGF-β activation can lead to hepatic fibrogenesis in rat hepatic stellate cells, thereby promoting their differentiation into myofibroblast-like cells that produce collagen, fibronectin, and other ECM components [176]. The accumulation of newly synthesized ECM generates scarring/fibrosis that reduces liver function and leads to cirrhosis [167, 175]. Consistent with this, a clinical study has shown that patients with autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) who were subsequently diagnosed with cirrhosis have increased TSP-1 levels in serum and worse disease prognosis compared with non-cirrhotic AIH patients [167, 168]. These data suggest that during AIH, TSP-1 may be a reliable biomarker for liver fibrosis and damage.

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex, multifactorial, chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by heterogeneous symptoms and prognosis that vary by the affected organ, driven by the generation of autoantibodies [171]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that serum TSP-1 levels in patients with SLE are significantly lower than those in healthy controls, and this decrease correlates with greater disease activity and severe clinical manifestations [171–173]. Interestingly, a recent study has shown that SLE patients with anti-TSP-1 autoantibodies (ATSA) had increased total IgG, antinuclear antibodies, anti-dsDNA, and anti-ribosome antibodies, suggesting that, in SLE, TSP-1 protects against the generation of autoantibodies [172]. The mechanism by which TSP-1 prevents the generation of autoantibodies may involve activation of latent TGF-β, leading to the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Foxp3+). Thus, the neutralization of TSP-1 observed in SLE ATSA+ patients would be mediated by a reduction in the equilibrium between regulatory T cells and autoreactive T cells [82, 173, 177, 178].

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is one of the most common chronic autoimmune diseases, characterized by immune dysfunction, synovitis, and inflammation and destruction of both bone and cartilage in the joints [179, 180]. A previous study has shown that RA patients have higher levels of TSP-1 in synovial tissue and blood samples than in those of healthy controls [169]. Although this data suggest that TSP-1 is involved in the pathogenesis of RA, interestingly, this phenotype was replicated in a rat model of collagen-induced arthritis, opening the door for future in vivo and in vitro studies aimed at validating this hypothesis.

Semaphorin 5 A (SEMA5A) is a protein composed of a sema domain, a plexin-semaphorin-integrin domain, and a unique TSP-1 domain. In vitro studies have shown that SEMA5A might play a key role in RA [170]. In vitro and in silico studies demonstrated that neutralizing the TSP-1 domain of SEMA5A impaired its recognition by the receptor PLXNB3 [170]. Although promising, the therapeutic potential of the TSP-1 domain of SEMA5A in RA must be validated in vivo before being tested in clinical studies.

Psoriasis is another autoimmune disease characterized by chronic skin inflammation and exacerbated, abnormal keratinocyte differentiation. This process is accompanied by infiltration of cytokine-producing inflammatory cells in the dermis and by subsequent modifications of its superficial microvasculature [181, 182]. Studies have shown that TSP-1 acts as a negative regulator of inflammation in psoriasis. RT-PCR analysis of clinical skin samples from patients with psoriasis has shown lower levels of TSP-1 mRNA and its receptor CD47 compared to healthy skin from control subjects, as well as dysregulated angiogenesis [174]. TSP-1 is required to maintain skin homeostasis, as the exogenous administration of either TSP-1 or an antibody that mimics TSP-1 binding to CD47 inhibited the differentiation of CD4+ T cells into TH17 cells while it promoted the generation of functional Treg cells [174].

Therapeutic potential of TSP-1

Given TSP-1’s diverse functional properties, its capacity to bind diverse ligands involved in immune response and other biological processes, and its role in infectious diseases and immune syndromes, exploring its therapeutic potential constitutes an interesting objective.

As described above, NE and Cat-G are serine proteases that bind the TSP-1 Type III repetition domain. Both can act cooperatively with reactive oxygen species within phagolysosomes to degrade phagocytosed pathogens [113, 183] or be secreted to the extracellular space to increase the tissue permeability required for immune cell recruitment during infection. However, this defense mechanism can also cause injury to host tissues if not properly regulated [7, 184]. During acute pulmonary K. pneumoniae infection in mice, the administration of a synthetic peptide derived from the TSP-1 Type III repetition domain (DV-9) restrained NE and CG activity in neutrophil lysates obtained from C57BL6 WT mice in a dose-dependent manner [7] (Fig. 2). In contrast to the pathogenic role of TSP-1 described in the context of K. pneumoniae infection, during acute pulmonary P. aeruginosa infection, TSP-1 protects the lung from unwarranted neutrophilic inflammation and improves host defense [6]. During this model of infection, administration of purified TSP-1 and platelet releases rich in TSP-1 enhanced P. aeruginosa clearance in the lungs and reduced neutrophil recruitment and activation (reflected in NE and MPO expression) in P. aeruginosa-infected mice [6]. This data suggests a potential therapeutic role of TSP-1 during acute P. aeruginosa lung infection. Even though DV-9 has not been used in the context of acute P. aeruginosa infection in mice, it emerges as a feasible therapeutic tool to prevent neutrophil-mediated lung inflammation and injury in this infection model.

Like DV-9, PKHB1, a synthetic peptide based on the C-terminal binding domain of TSP-1, possesses immunomodulatory properties during HSK induced in mice by HSV-1 (Fig. 2). This peptide’s topical and subconjunctival administration before and after the ocular infection with HSV-1 in mice enhanced the immune response and reduced viral burden [119]. Notably, the decrease in the burden of HSV-1 correlated with increased recruitment of antigen-presenting cells to the site of infection, such as DCs and M1 macrophages, as well as with substantial production of TNF, IL-1β, and IL-6, and an increase of CD8+ T cells in the surrounding lymph nodes [119]. Interestingly, the PHKB1 peptide has also been investigated as a therapeutic candidate against cancer due to its anti-tumoral effects through the induction of cell death in breast cancer and lymphoblastic leukemia, inducing tumor regression that is correlated with improved survival in comparison to untreated mice [185–187]. The fact that a therapeutic target whose potential has been described during infectious diseases can be used for more than one type of disease, such as cancer, broadens the therapeutic possibilities of PKHB1.

Some therapeutic approaches based on TSP-1 mimetic peptides have shown promissory effects on different components of the immune response. ABT-510 and ABT-898 are mimetic synthetic peptides from the TSR domain of TSP-1 that act like a CD36-target inhibitor of angiogenesis [188–191]. Despite their role being mostly assessed in angiogenesis in several cancer models, including glioblastomas, sarcoma, renal cell carcinoma, epithelial ovarian cancer, and medulloblastoma [192–197], the ABT-510 peptide administered to WT and TSP-1 deficient mice treated with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) to induce colitis led to a reduction of the severity of disease and inflammation, evidenced in a reduction of myeloperoxidase (MPO), TGF-β, basic fibroblast growth factor, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), TNF, and metalloproteinases production in the colon of TSP-1 deficient mice compared to TSP-1-deficient mice treated with vehicle [198] (Fig. 2).

Likewise, compared to both, vehicle-treated WT and TSP-1-deficient mice with DSS, the administration of ABT-898 reduced the severity of disease, leukocyte infiltration, and inflammation score in colon [199] (Fig. 2). Additionally, it was reported that there was a reduction in IL-6 and STAT3 levels, key signaling pathways involved in inflammation regulation [199] (Fig. 2). These results demonstrated that during colitis, TSP-1-derived peptides can exert anti-inflammatory effects in a mechanism that is most likely mediated by its interaction with CD36, providing an interesting axis to evaluate whether these synthetic peptides could modulate the inflammatory response during infectious diseases.

The use of fragments of TSP-1 as potential therapeutics is not restricted to infectious diseases or cancer. In a mouse model of inflammatory bowel disease induced by DSS, the in vivo administration of recombinant proteins containing domains of TSP-1 ameliorated the inflammation and subsequent injury caused by DSS [200]. Three TSP-1-derived recombinant proteins were generated: a version that includes only the three type I repeats of the TSP-1 (3TSR), a second version that contains the second Type I repeat (TSR2), and a third version that included the TSR2 in addition with an RFK sequence (TSR2/RFK) [200]. The administration of 3TSR to DSS-treated mice significantly reduced fecal bleeding, showed better fecal composition by day 7 post-DSS, and reduced inflammation in the intestine at cellular level when compared with mice that received a saline solution or the other generated versions of TSP-1 [200] (Fig. 2). In addition, immunohistochemical analyses revealed that administering both 3TSR and TSR2 reduced neutrophils and monocytes in the colon.

In contrast, the administration of TSR2/RFK reduced the infiltration of macrophages to this organ compared to saline-treated mice [200]. 3TSR is a peptide that binds to l-TGF-β inducing the activation of TGF-β to its mature form [201] (Fig. 2), and due to its nature, we speculate that 3TSR would also be capable of interacting with CD36 to induce an anti-inflammatory response. Although more studies are needed to evaluate the effect of 3TSR interacting with these two ligands, these data support the potential of TSP-1-derived peptide 3TSR to protect the intestinal epithelium during DSS-induced colitis in mice and open the door to evaluate whether 3TSR can be used as a therapeutic tool to protect the intestinal tissue during bacterial and viral infections.

A synthetic peptide derived from the CD47-binding C-terminal domain of TSP-1 (4N1K) emerged as a successful therapeutic tool in a mouse model of Sjögren’s syndrome-associated ocular surface inflammation. In vivo topical preventive and therapeutic administration of 4N1K reduced autoimmune ocular inflammation. Preventive intraperitoneal injection with 4N1K (10 µg) reduced the proportion of CD4+IL-17+ T cells and increased the numbers of CD4+FoxP3+ T cells in lymph nodes. At the same time, topical bilateral administration of 4N1K (5 µl/eye) reduced corneal injury and preserved MUC5AC production, two key factors for corneal homeostasis in mice. These findings suggest that TSP-1 and 4N1K are effective at inducing regulatory T cells, inhibiting the production of the pro-inflammatory mediators IL-12 and IL-23 by antigen-presenting cells, and improving the maintenance of corneal barrier integrity and mucin production in animal models of Sjögren’s syndrome. However, there is no human clinical data available that supports these findings, and therefore further research is needed. This could imply a therapeutic use for the TSP-1-derived peptide 4N1K in future studies [202].

On the other hand, potential therapies focused on using molecules that compete with TSP-1 for the ligand interaction has also been tested. TAX2 is a synthetic CD47-derived peptide that binds to TSP-1 and antagonizes TSP-1 with CD47 interaction [203]. TSP-1 has been described in the regulation of thrombosis via interaction with CD47, which leads to thrombus formation in cardiovascular tissue by platelet-epithelium adhesion [204]. TAX2 administration to C57BL/6 J WT mice inhibited platelet interaction with collagen and aggregation, reducing thrombus formation dose-dependently [203] (Fig. 2). TAX2 dosage in WT and Thbs1−/− mice was well tolerated (400 mg/kg), and the dosage reduced platelet interaction and cardiovascular compromise [203]. Given that during IAV infection in mice, TSP-1 negatively modulates the antiviral response through CD47, TAX2 emerges as a potential therapeutic strategy during IAV infection aimed to restore or improve the antiviral response against IAV in vivo.

The thrombospondin-related anonymous protein (TRAP), also known as sporozoite surface protein 2 (SSP2), is an essential Plasmodium spp. protein for sporozoite motility and hepatocyte invasion [205]. Structurally, TRAP contains a thrombospondin type 1 repeat (TSR) domain that binds sulfated glycoconjugates on the surface of liver cells [206]. Accordingly, antibodies raised against TRAP have been shown to inhibit sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes in vitro [205, 206]. However, in vivo studies in mice showed that anti-TRAP monoclonal antibodies alone conferred minimal protection against Plasmodium yoelii infection (only 15.4% of infected mice), and no protection was observed in a humanized mouse model of Plasmodium falciparum infection (0%) [207]. Notably, when an anti-TRAP antibody was administered in combination with a sub-protective dose of an anti-circumsporozoite protein antibody, protection increased significantly to 87.5% in mice infected with P. yoelii [207]. These findings indicate that although anti-TRAP antibodies have limited therapeutic efficacy on their own against Plasmodium infection, when combined with secondary antibody-mediated therapies they may synergize, inducing a better protective response.

Finally, another method in which TSP-1 can be inhibited is through the inhibition of thbs1 gene expression. Septic patients with acute kidney injury (AKI) present high production of TNF, IL-1β, and IL-18; elevated TSP-1; and increased expression of upstream transcription factor 2 (USF2) [208]. This transcriptional factor binds to the thbs1 promoter region and induces its expression [209–211]. In vivo assays in LPS sepsis-induced AKI model in mice showed that TSP-1 knockdown via adenovirus-delivered sh-TSP1 significantly reduced cell pyroptosis [208]. Similarly, the transfection with sh-USF2 successfully inhibited the expression of USF2 and TSP-1 at the protein level; decreased TGF-β activation; and reduced the expression of TNF, IL-1β, and IL-18, protecting the kidney from cell apoptosis and tissue damage in AKI-induced mice [208] (Fig. 2). These findings support that TSP-1 expression positively correlates with inflammation and pyroptosis in AKI patients. The downregulation of TSP-1 mediated by reduced levels of USF2 resulted in ameliorated sepsis-induced AKI mice.

Concluding remarks and open questions

Throughout this review, we have highlighted the critical role of TSP-1 in modulating the immune response during infectious diseases caused by several microbes, and in autoimmune diseases, exploring in the associated mechanisms, and its therapeutic potential. Given that TSP-1 has important but opposite roles in infections caused by bacteria (P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae), viruses (HSV-1, IAV, SARS-CoV-2, HIV), fungus (C. albicans), and parasites (T. cruzi, Plasmodium spp.), we hypothesize that TSP-1 could have significant roles in the host immune response against other pathogens such as respiratory syncytial virus and human metapneumovirus, that due to their relevance in human health remain relevant to study.

A significant observation from our discussion is the opposite role of TSP-1 during different infections. As with TSP-1, interleukin-10, a major anti-inflammatory cytokine that modulates the function of several pro-inflammatory mediators [212], has protective or pathogenic roles during bacterial infection [212, 213]. The modulation of the immune response during infection needs to be carefully regulated, as strong anti-inflammatory environments can lead to ineffective pathogen clearance. In contrast, a potent pro-inflammatory reaction can lead to uncontrolled tissue injury. In this context, the consequences of the pathogenicity strategies of each microbe in the anti- and pro-inflammatory balance may determine the protective or pathogenic role of immunomodulatory molecules such as TSP-1.

In addition, TSP-1 is a key factor in autoimmune diseases, and its production also shows opposite phenotypes across different autoimmune diseases. Understanding the effect of TSP-1 on the immune response in different models of infection and autoimmune diseases, and the mechanisms associated with its function in immune responses, could promote the development of effective and novel therapies against clinically relevant diseases for which current therapies are limited.
