Hierarchical Epoxy Structures via Tunable Polymerization-Induced Phase Separation Combined with Additive Manufacturing
Authors: Kylie E. Van Meter, Lina N. Ghanbari, Michael J. Ford, Nathan R. Bays, Ryan D. Davis, Alina M. Martinez, Francesca C’deBaca, Alexandra M. Golobic, Brad H. Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymerization-induced phase separation (PIPS) allows for the control of thermoset morphologies and properties, enabling the tuning of domain sizes and thermomechanical response. However, its use in generating substructural features in additively manufactured materials has been limited. In this work, we combine epoxy PIPS with UV curable acrylate and rheological modifiers to print nano- to macro-phase separating materials via a two-step, dual-cure approach. This method enables direct ink write printing of hierarchical structures with both controlled morphologies through phase separation and macroscale architecture through print design. We find that formulations for phase-separating materials require judicious incorporation of additives to enable printability and to provide sufficient green strength. Atomic force microscopy-nano infrared mapping reveals tunable, reticulated nano- to micron-scale domains of the resultant multiphase materials and their morphology changes due to additives, resulting in alterations to thermomechanical and tensile properties. Shape memory behavior is also demonstrated through multimaterial additive manufacturing of epoxies with functionally graded internal morphology using active mixing techniques, highlighting this method’s ability to fabricate complex architectures with controlled morphologies and thermomechanical response.

Introduction

Over the last several decades, additive manufacturing (AM) has transformed the material design and synthesis space. AM allows for the fabrication of three-dimensional objects out of a wide range of materials, and with complex geometries that cannot be easily generated through traditional manufacturing methods. , However, for current AM methods based on extrusion (direct ink write (DIW), fused deposition modeling (FDM)), vat polymerization, and sintering, the creation of architectures bearing substructural features remains challenging. The introduction of features at nm to μm length scales is a classic, powerful approach to optimize the thermal, chemical, and mechanical properties of materials. − To print structures with features at this length scale, there are two primary methods used: (1) two-photon polymerization (TPP) and (2) polymerization induced phase separation (PIPS). TPP relies on focusing a laser in a highly confined region of a photosensitive resin, locally polymerizing the resin and generating submicron voxels. , Printing speeds can be increased via scanning modes, but this method has been difficult to adopt outside of research laboratories due to high operation costs, low fabrication rates, and challenges with size control of voxels.

PIPS is an attractive option for creating nano- to micron-scale features in additively manufactured structures, relying on thermodynamics of monomers in an initially homogeneous mixture to drive structure formation as opposed to more complex instrument control and time-intensive processes in TPP. A wide variety of morphologies and feature length scales can be generated via PIPS, allowing for the design of materials with unique functionalities and mechanical properties. − In systems utilizing block polymers, composition and molecular weight dictate the domain size and shape. PIPS has also been used to improve properties of thermosetting polymers, most often through the introduction of a secondary polymer which may or may not be reactive with the thermosetting phase. − This approach can be advantageous for toughening brittle, low fracture toughness materials like epoxy resins through the incorporation of reactive rubbers, although control over morphology has been historically difficult to achieve, particularly in the nanoscale regime. −

We have recently developed a PIPS approach for epoxy materials that allows for the control of domain size from the nano- to micron-scale. − This approach introduces binary mixtures of resins, curatives, and/or secondary polymers, leveraging differences in their reactivity to alter phase separation. The resulting materials exhibit highly tunable morphologies ranging from nanophase to macrophase regimes, which profoundly influence their thermomechanical and tensile properties. The macrophase epoxies exhibit two distinct glass transition temperatures that are characteristic of the individual constituent phases. In contrast, as domain sizes decrease toward the nanoscale, these transitions merge into single, broad glass transition. The morphological variations also influence tensile behavior, where nanophase materials exhibit higher stiffness and ductility, whereas macrophase materials are softer and less ductile. This system presents a unique platform for precisely tailoring material properties and morphology through a single compositional parameter.

While there is extensive literature on PIPS across many different polymeric systems, the adoption of PIPS in AM structures has been limited. Bobrin et al. reported nanoscale morphological control via a mixture of multifunctional monomers and a macromolecular chain transfer agent, printed using digital light processing (DLP), and they identified a scaling law for chain length and volume fraction effects on domain size. Xie et al. created a method to print elastomers derived from bottlebrush polymers that can self-assemble into ordered body-centered cubic phases, using photo cross-linker to UV cure the structure postextrusion during DIW printing. Nanoscale phase-separated epoxy polyacrylates were 3D printed in work by Konuray et al. using masked image processing stereolithography, utilizing UV curable acrylate in conjunction with a thermally cured epoxy. Indeed, “dual cure” systems have been explored to enable AM of epoxies – which often exhibit slow polymerization kinetics that preclude printability – either through the addition of rapid UV curable components like acrylates − or through rapid thermal curing agents incorporated by active mixing. While cationically cured epoxies can be additively manufactured in their pure form, modification with secondary polymers is required to improve mechanical properties , and printing of phase separated systems has not been explored. In other noteworthy work, researchers have demonstrated intricate channel-pore structures and geometries of 3D printed scaffolds; however, such approaches are limited in structural control at the submicron level, as well as the degree to which material properties can be tuned, both locally and globally. In sum, there is a clear need to demonstrate AM epoxies with controllable internal structure, so as to enable like-for-like advanced materials for the many applications where epoxies are traditionally used, such as electronics encapsulation and reinforced structural components.

In this work, we leverage our previously developed PIPS framework − to generate epoxy materials with nano- to micron-scale control of domains in DIW AM macroscale structures (Figure ). We have created a dual-cure system in which a small amount of UV curable acrylate material is incorporated into the PIPS formulations. The acrylate is cured during the printing process to fix the print structure and ensure print fidelity, while the epoxy-amine portion undergoes PIPS during a postprint isothermal curing step. Fumed silica is also utilized as a rheological modifier to control the yield stress of the print inks prior to polymerization. This process gives AM rubber-toughened epoxy materials with tunable internal structure and in-kind control of thermomechanical properties. We also illustrate that PIPS combined with AM can be used to program intelligent response in materials, exemplified by functionally graded shape memory behavior. This demonstration is intended to highlight the potential to harness these materials for applications requiring multifaceted, programmable thermomechanical properties. Indeed, the ability to control the hierarchical structure and response with precisely printed 3D structures is advantageous for deployable complex medical devices, aerospace applications, and robotics , among many others. This method provides a unique approach to additively manufacture spatially graded, phase separated epoxy structures with locally tunable morphology and thermomechanical properties.

Method for generating dual-cure direct ink write (DIW) printed samples. (a) The ink is formulated by combining part A (UV curable acrylate formulation), part B (thermally curable epoxy-amine formulation), and a fumed silica rheological modifier. (b) The ink is printed using a direct ink write method, where the ink is extruded through the nozzle under the exposure of a UV light. The fumed silica agglomerates to form a network gel and imparts shear thinning properties, while the acrylate cures to form a network, partially cross-linking the ink. (c) After printing, part B is fully cured through higher temperature thermal curing. (d) Finally, the fully cured printed structure exhibits phase separated characteristics as investigated through DMA.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The combined materials used to generate samples can be divided into three distinct categories: (1) epoxy-amine chemistries, (2) UV cured acrylates with photoinitiators, and (3) shear-thinning additives. For the epoxy-amine materials (1), EPON 828, a diglycidyl ether of bisphenol A (DGEBA), was obtained from Miller-Stephenson (Sylmar, CA). Jeffamines D230 and T3000 were obtained from Huntsman Corporation (The Woodlands, TX). Technical grade triethylenetetramine (TETA) was obtained from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA). For the UV cured acrylates with photoinitiators (2), trimethylolpropane triacrylate (TMPTA), glycidyl methacrylate (GMA), and the photoinitiators 2,2-dimethoxy-2-phenylacetophenone (DMPA) were obtained from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA). 2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyldiphenylphosphine oxide (TPO) was obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co (TCI) (Portland, OR). A rheological modifier (3), CAB-O-SIL M5 fumed silica, was obtained from Cabot. All materials were used as received. Chemical structures are shown in Figure S1.

The selection of epoxy-amine constituents and their corresponding PIPS was based on previous work. − Combinations of resin (DGEBA), binary curative (D230 + TETA), and rubber (T3000) were prepared such that amine hydrogens and epoxies maintained stoichiometric equivalence (where r = [-NH-]0,total/[epoxide]0,total = 1). The content of rubber in each formulation was maintained as defined by the value m = 0.2 = [-NH-]0,T3000/[-NH-]0,total. The total amine hydrogens in the mixture and those contributed by T3000 are represented by [-NH-]0,total and [-NH-]0,T3000, respectively. The binary curative provided the remaining amine hydrogens, defined by n = [-NH-]0,TETA/([-NH-]0,D230 + [-NH-]0,TETA), where [-NH-]0,D230 and [-NH-]0,TETA are the initial molar concentrations of amine hydrogens from the D230 and TETA, respectively.

There are three epoxy-amine formulations used in this study that can be described as follows: homogeneous epoxy (m = 0 and n = 0, or m0n0, i.e., DGEBA cured solely with D230), macrophase epoxy (m = 0.2 and n = 0.8, or m2n8), and nanophase epoxy (m = 0.2 and n = 0, or m2n0). Weight percent compositions of these formulations are shown in Table S1.

To introduce a UV curable component to these formulations, a mixture of 95 wt % TMPTA and 5 wt % GMA was combined, hereafter described as the photocurable portion. The photoinitiator DMPA was added in a 1 wt % amount relative to the total weight of the epoxy-amine and photocurable formulation. This was done to ensure availability of photoinitiator throughout the overall formulation. Fumed silica was added to the formulations to impart shear thinning properties and material yield stresses appropriate for DIW printing. Weight percent compositions of these formulations are shown in (Table S2, S3).

Bulk Sample Preparation

The amines and photoinitiator were combined and mixed in a Thinky planetary mixer. During a second round of mixing, the Epon 828 resin and acrylates were added. If the mixture included a fumed silica component, this was incorporated last with a third round of mixing. For materials which did not include a photocurable component, the amines were combined and mixed, with a second mix step to incorporate the resin. Each mixing step was typically performed for 90 s at 2000 rpm, with the final step including a 60 s degas at 2200 rpm. The combined formulation was measured by rheology as described below or cast into molds to create specimens for mechanical testing. In the latter case, materials with a photocurable portion were cured using a 365 nm UV lamp at an intensity of 0.4 mW/cm2 for 10 min. All samples were isothermally cured at 100 °C for 24 h, during which phase separation occurs, followed by a 200 °C postcure step for 30 min under a nitrogen environment to ensure full curing above the glass transition of the material. No significant shape change was observed as a result of thermal cure/post cure, although several % shrinkage should be expected associated with epoxy curing. −

Rheology

Parallel plate rheology measurements were performed on strain-controlled TA Instruments (New Castle, DE) ARES-G2 rheometer. Oscillatory strain sweeps were collected at a frequency of 1 Hz and a range of strains from 0.1–100% with storage and loss moduli and oscillatory stress measured at 10 points per decade of strain. Twenty-five mm diameter aluminum plates with a 1 mm gap were used. Additionally, steady viscosity measurements were performed at a shear rate of 1 s–1, using 50 mm stainless steel plates with a 1 mm gap. Viscosity (Pa-s) data were measured for 5 min (at an interval of 3 data points per minute), and the average viscosity and standard deviation were calculated over the duration of the measurement. All measurements were performed under nitrogen at 25 °C.

UV Rheology

Parallel plate rheology measurements were performed on a strain-controlled TA Instruments (New Castle, DE) ARES-G2 rheometer using 20 mm diameter plates with a 0.5 mm gap. The bottom plate was aluminum, and the top plate was acrylic. UV light is transmitted through the acrylic plate and into the sample, allowing for the real time collection of cure profiles. A 365 nm UV light with an intensity of 10 mW/cm2 was used to cure the samples, and the light was turned on at the time data collection begins. Dynamic time sweeps were performed, with a 1 Hz oscillation at 1% strain. Measurements were performed in lab air on a controlled temperature stage held at 25 °C.

Dynamic Mechanical Analysis (DMA)

A strain-controlled TA Instruments (New Castle, DE) ARES-G2 rheometer operating in small amplitude oscillatory shear was used to perform DMA. Rectangular plaques (30 mm × 3 mm × 1 mm) were tested with torsional deformation under a strain amplitude of 1% and a fixed oscillation of 1 Hz. The dynamic shear storage (G′) and loss (G″) moduli and loss tangent (G″/G′) were measured during temperature sweep measurements over a temperature range of −75 to 200 °C, heating at a rate of 3 °C per minute.

Tensile Testing

An Instron load frame was utilized to perform tensile testing. ASTM 638–14 Type V standard dog bone samples were subjected to uniaxial tension and an extensometer was utilized to directly measure strain. Stress–strain curves were generated at a relatively low strain rate of 5%/min and tests were performed at room temperature. For each composition, three tensile bars were tested.

Atomic Force Microscopy with Nano-Infrared (AFM-NanoIR)

Samples were prepared for AFM by first embedding cured phase separated epoxy samples using UltraBed embedding kit (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Samples were sectioned using an EM UC7 Ultramicrotome (Leica) with an Ultra 45° Diamond Blade (Diatome). A final sectioning cut of 100 nm thick on the ultramicrotome was used to create a smooth surface on the bulk sample to expose internal phases.

The Bruker Anasys nanoIR3, equipped with a MIRcat-QT-2400 laser, operates within the wavelength range of 5.376 to 13.422 μm. The system utilizes contact mode with a Bruker contact mode near-infrared (NIR) probe tip, characterized by a resonance frequency of 13 ± 4 kHz and a spring constant of 0.2 N/m. The spectral analysis begins at 758 cm–1 and concludes at 1846 cm–1, with a resolution of 2 cm–1 per point. Prior to each sample analysis, the infrared optics are calibrated using a Bruker PMMA on epoxy standard. Background measurements are obtained as an average of three scans, each consisting of 32 coaverages, and are collected daily. For each spectra, six scans are averaged. For imaging, a trace rate of 0.25 Hz (utilized for mapping) and a retrace rate of 1 Hz are used. The scan window is typically set to 5 μm × 5 μm, and the resolution is configured to 1000 × 400 pixels (horizontal × vertical). The color scale for each AFM NanoIR absorption map is independently normalized to maximize spatial contrast between phase separated domains for direct morphological comparison between samples, regardless of variations in absolute IR absorbance.

Direct Ink Write Printing

To generate 3D printed structures, formulations were first mixed to create the printable ink (Figure a). Homogeneous and nanophase inks were mixed, degassed and loaded into 10 cm3 syringes (Nordson EFD), and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 3 min to remove air bubbles. The ink filled syringe was then secured to the printing manifold. Macrophase inks were printed via two component active mixing at a 60/40 volumetric ratio of component 1 (Epon 828, DMPA, TMPTA/GMA, 5% M5 Silica) to component 2 (T3000, TETA, D230, 5% M5 Silica). All inks were printed on a custom Aerotech 3D printer using a software-based machine controller (A3200) coupled to a positioning stage (Aerotech NANOpositioner), with material extrusion controlled using either an Ultimus V pressure box (Nordson EFD) in the case of homogeneous and nanophase inks or electromechanical drive (Parker) for active mixing of the macrophase ink. For all prints, inks were extruded through a 500 μm nozzle. Methods of printing are fully described in Ortega et al.

The structures were exposed to UV light during printing, causing the acrylate portion to cure and create a network that partially cross-linked the ink (Figure b). The fumed silica helped to retain the shape of the extruded bead, while the acrylate helped to prevent sagging, retain print fidelity, and impart green strength. After the structures were printed, they were removed from the printer, placed in an oven and cured according to the profiles described in Section (Figure c), with no significant shape change observed as a result of thermal cure. The final structure, illustrated in Figure d, displayed macroscale architecture controlled through the print parameters, and nano- to micron-scale features controlled through PIPS to generate a hierarchical structure.

Results and Discussion

Rheology

To perform DIW AM, print inks must be able to transition from liquid-like to solid-like behavior. During the print process, the ink must (1) behave like a liquid during extrusion under high shear rates in the nozzle, (2) solidify upon deposition to maintain print shape, and (3) have enough strength to support the weight of additional layers of printed material. To satisfy these requirements, DIW inks typically fall into three categories based on their liquid-to-solid transition mechanism, including yield stress, time-dependent solidification, and combined solidification inks, the latter having both a yield stress and time-dependent solidification. For this study, we investigated yield stress and combined solidification inks to print structures undergoing internal PIPS.

To formulate inks with ideal shear thinning behavior for printing, dynamic strain sweeps were performed on yield stress inks, comprised of the epoxy PIPS formulations with varying contents of fumed silica (Figure a). If the material is consistently liquid-like in nature, the storage modulus (G′) will not exceed the loss modulus (G″), regardless of strain applied. This is the case for the 1 wt % silica, which is liquid-like at very low to very high strains (0.1 – 100%). However, inks are considered ideally shear thinning when they exhibit a transition from solid-like (G′ > G″) to liquid-like (G″ < G′) as a result of applied shear stress. − For inks with a content of 2 wt % silica and higher, there is a clear crossover point at strains greater than 10%, demonstrating the desired shear thinning behavior. The storage and loss moduli of these results can also be plotted against the measured oscillatory stress, shown in Figure b, and the corresponding stress at which the crossover occurs for each ink can be defined as the ink yield stress, indicated by the stars. The presence of a yield stress in these materials indicates the fumed silica is forming a three-dimensional network through hydrogen bonding between surface silanol groups.

Shear storage and loss modulus vs (a) strain or (b) stress for the macrophase formulation with varied silica loading. Crossover points where G′ = G″ are indicated by the stars. (c) yield stress as a function of silica content for the macrophase, nanophase, and homogeneous formulations. (d) Dynamic viscosity of each of the neat epoxy-amine formulations (no silica).

The ink yield stresses were measured for the three different epoxy PIPS formulations used in this study: macrophase, nanophase, and homogeneous. It should be noted that the formulation nomenclature used in this study refers to the final structure of the cured material, not the structure of the ink itself. If we consider the yield stress as a function of the silica content (Figure c), a power law function can be fit to each formulation indicated by the solid lines for each data set. Previous studies on flocculated alumina suspensions have also found a power law relationship between yield stress and particle content. − Our formulations follow a power law exponent of 3.71 ± 0.12 but have highly varied coefficients (Table S4), suggesting that the strength of the silica network depends significantly on the specific composition of the epoxy-amine formulation.

Each formulation without silica was also found to have a different initial viscosity, increasing from homogeneous to nanophase to macrophase (Figure d), mirroring the differences in yield stress. The viscosity and yield stress trends are likely connected to the molecular weight of the constituents; the macrophase and nanophase formulations contain ∼30 wt % Jeffamine T3000, a high viscosity polyetheramine with a molecular weight of ∼3000 g/mol, whereas the homogeneous structure does not have Jeffamine T3000. Higher viscosity formulations have a greater internal resistance to flow as compared to lower viscosities, and are found to require less fumed silica to generate a given yield stress. In the context of DIW printing, the yield stress of an ink must be ∼100 Pa or greater to maintain the shape of a print strand or bead, ,, but more complex prints require higher yield stresses to prevent deformation of the structure under its own weight. In our system, the yield stress of each formulation is highly tunable over a multiple order of magnitude range (10–103 Pa) with relatively small changes in content of fumed silica from 1 to 10 wt %.

While the addition of a rheological modifier like fumed silica can be necessary to impart shear thinning behavior in DIW inks, high loadings of silica can lead to stiffening and reduced ductility of polymeric materials. , The use of a secondary solidification method, such as a time-dependent solidification component, can be used to mitigate these issues and minimize the content of fumed silica. Therefore, we also explored the use of a photocurable acrylate component to allow for partial solidification of the ink under UV during printing, creating a dual-cure ink. UV rheology was performed on the macrophase formulation without silica with two different photoinitiators: (1) TPO and (2) DMPA (Figure a). The epoxy-amine and acrylate components comprised 90 and 9 wt %, respectively, of the formulation, while the photoinitiator was added at 1 wt % (Table S3). During these measurements, the crossover of the storage and loss modulus was defined as the gel time, indicated by the stars in Figure a.

(a) UV rheology of macrophase formulation with 9% acrylate and 1% of two different photoinitiators, TPO and DMPA. (b) Yield stress of inks with 5 wt % silica, and inks with 5 wt % silica +10 wt % UV curable material (acrylate and photoinitiator).

The gel time of the formulation with DMPA is ∼ 5× faster and the storage modulus increases more rapidly when compared to the formulation with TPO. A significant decrease in the gel time when using DMPA is also observed in the homogeneous and nanophase formulations (Figure S2). Because both initial macrophase formulations are identical other than the photoinitiator, this suggests that less acrylate was cured in the TPO than in the DMPA sample at the same time point. The slow cure behavior of the TPO photoinitiator is likely due to the susceptibility of acylphosphine oxides to nucleophilic attack by amines. This causes the solvolytic cleavage of the carbon–phosphorus bond in the TPO molecule. It is likely that since ∼40 wt % of the PIPS formulation contains primary amines, some amount of the phosphine oxide-based photo initiator is consumed through interactions with abundant amines in the formulation. This results in less photoinitiator molecules available to participate in polymerization of the acrylate.

DMPA was selected as the photoinitiator to use for AM due to its faster gel time. The storage moduli of the epoxies after UV cure were found to be greater than 100 Pa (Figure a, Figure S2), which is sufficient to hold the shape of a print bead. However, the gel time is not fast enough to eliminate the need for a shear thinning additive, necessitating that we simultaneously introduce the UV curable component and silica rheological modifier. However, our goal in this initial work was to modify the epoxy-amine formulation as minimally as possible, so as to maintain the ability to induce and control PIPS postprinting. Given this context, a composition of 5 wt % fumed silica with 10 wt % UV curable material was found to produce yield stresses at or above 100 Pa (Figure b) in all of the studied formulations prior to UV curing, which allows for the retention of the print bead shape during extrusion and was selected as the dual-cure print formulation.

Characterization of Phase Separated Morphology

AFM nanoIR maps of microtome-sectioned phase separated epoxy specimens are shown in Figure . Here, we measure the intensity of the 1038 cm–1 wavenumber across the sample (Figure S3), which corresponds to a N–C stretch associated with aliphatic amines. , Since the N–C bond is associated with cross-link points in the epoxy network, and the concentration of these points will be different between the epoxy and rubber phases, contrast between phases is evident from the absorption intensity. The macrophase epoxy (Figure a) has domains that are spherical in shape with apparent diameters between ∼2 to 6 μm. These domains have a higher concentration of N–C bonds (i.e., epoxy phase), indicated by the lighter color, while the matrix material has a lower concentration (i.e., rubber phase). For the nanophase epoxy, we again see intensity differences, but with apparent domain length scales from ∼ 50 to 150 nm. The AFM nanoIR map of the macrophase epoxy is consistent with our previous work on the identical material, where we visualized this morphology through chemical etching and scanning electron microscopy. However, in the previous work, we were unable to visualize the morphology of the nanophase epoxy through chemical etching, although X-ray scattering measurements clearly indicated the presence of nanoscale structure. Here, the AFM nanoIR map confirms this notion and further reveals this structure to be bicontinuous in nature. The homogeneous epoxy shows consistent intensity across the map, lacking any micron to nanometer scale features (Figure S4).

AFM nanoIR intensity maps of the 1038 cm–1 wavenumber for the (a) macrophase epoxy, (b) nanophase epoxy, (c) macrophase epoxy with 5 wt % silica, (d) nanophase epoxy with 5 wt % silica, (e) printed macrophase epoxy with 10 wt % UV curable material and 5 wt % silica, and (f) printed nanophase epoxy with 10 wt % UV curable material and 5 wt % silica. Note that the scale of the nanophase epoxy scan is smaller as to visualize nanometer scale features. Color bars are independently scaled and represent arbitrary intensity units.

Interestingly, the addition of 5 wt % silica to the macrophase epoxy significantly alters the morphology of the phases (Figure c) from spherical domains with a clear sea–island morphology, to smaller (∼1 to 4 μm) and more irregular shapes. Similar trends have been observed in polymer blends driven by the accumulation of fumed silica at the interfaces of phases, attributed to hindered diffusion between phases and decreased phase separation rate. The exact location of the fumed silica in the system cannot be mapped due to the overlap of characteristic bands of the silica and epoxy-amine system. However, the irregular shapes still exceed 1 μm in length, suggesting that macrophase separation still occurs even in the presence of 5 wt % silica. When 5 wt % silica is added to the nanophase epoxy, nanometer scale features are retained, with apparent length scales of ∼100 nm (Figure d). It appears that the weak phase separation and material morphology are not significantly affected by the incorporation of silica, whereas silica interacts with the large, macrophase domains, changing their shape during phase separation.

When macrophase epoxy is printed with 10 wt % photocurable material and 5 wt % silica (Figure e), the morphology is again altered, where we observe rounded domains with apparent sizes from 0.7 to 3 μm. The printed morphology of the nanophase material (10 wt % photocurable material and 5 wt % silica, Figure f) appears to be a combination of features ranging from the nanoscale to macroscale. As discussed in the following sections, this printed material is optically transparent and exhibits similar thermomechanical properties to the neat phase separated material, suggesting that the nanophase separation is maintained even with additives incorporated in the formulation. Overlapping absorbance in the acrylate (C–O–C) and epoxy material (N–C) at 1038 cm–1 is likely obscuring the true morphology of the phase separated epoxy domains on account of the photothermal mechanism used in the technique combined with the small scale of the features being measured.

Thermomechanical Properties

We next used DMA to better understand the effects of ink formulation on their PIPS behavior and thermomechanical properties (Figure ). Formulations compared included epoxies without silica, silica loaded, and UV + silica. The macrophase formulations exhibit two discrete glass transitions near −10 and 115 °C, associated with the soft and hard phases resulting from PIPS, respectively (Figure a,b). This is consistent with our previous observations, wherein PIPS proceeds such that very little of the rubbery T3000 component is incorporated into the remainder of the epoxy network during phase separation (and vice versa), leading to distinct glass transitions that nearly coincide with the corresponding pure epoxy and rubber phases. The tan delta peak corresponding to the rubbery phase is modified substantially by incorporating silica, whereas the peak corresponding to the rigid phase remains relatively stable. This shift is likely due to the preferential accumulation of silica in the rubbery phase. The incorporation of silica into this phase can alter its glass transition through disrupting efficient chain packing and increasing free volume of the rubbery phase. A similar shift has been observed in other epoxy systems. The decreased magnitude of the rubbery peak can likely be attributed to the changes in morphology observed in the AFM nanoIR maps (Figure a,c), where the addition of silica changes the shape and sizes of the domains. In particular, the transition from a dispersed rigid phase in a rubbery matrix (without silica) to a network-like structure (with silica), implies that the rubbery phase would be less predominant in the macroscopic mechanical response of the material.

DMA temperature sweeps of the neat, silica filled, and UV curable + silica filled (a, b) macrophase, (c, d) nanophase, and (e, f) homogeneous epoxies.

When both photocurable material and silica are incorporated into the macrophase epoxy (Figure a,b in orange), the magnitude of the loss tangent is decreased for the softer phase transition, while the harder phase transition is less affected as compared to samples with only silica. The material is also stiffer in between the two glass transitions, which can be attributed to the acrylate that is ∼5× stiffer than the macrophase neat epoxy at room temperature (Figure S5). For the macrophase epoxy cured with UV + silica, it is likely that the acrylate network formed prior to the full cure of the epoxy amine formulation restricts chain mobility of the long rubber segments in the soft phase. Also, between the two glass transitions, there is an increase in the magnitude of the loss tangent (Figure b), which can be attributed to the broad glass transition of TMPTA that spans nearly a 200 °C range (Figure S5). Comparable behavior is observed for macrophase epoxy samples with only UV curable material and no fumed silica (Figure S6a,b). Both cast and printed samples with UV curable material and silica also displayed similar material signatures (Figure S7). Nevertheless, the observation of two discrete glass transitions indicates that the incorporation of acrylate does not inhibit macrophase separation as seen in the AFM nanoIR maps (Figure e).

The nanophase epoxy (Figure c,d) has only one broad glass transition peak (T g = 49 °C), which can be associated with weak phase separation and a high degree of intermixing between the epoxy and rubber phases. The appearance of a low temperature shoulder corresponds to the relaxation of rubber-rich domains. Much like the macrophase epoxy, these key features are maintained across all silica-loaded and UV-curable formulations, suggesting that the nanophase structure persists. The addition of silica also tends to decrease the strength of the glass transition and increase the modulus at temperatures above the glass transition, such that we again hypothesize a network of fumed silica inhibiting chain mobility. However, this stiffening effect is less pronounced in the nanophase epoxy material as compared to the macrophase epoxy material. This trend aligns with our observation through AFM nanoIR that nanophase separation is maintained even in the presence of silica, and the structure is not significantly altered (Figure b,d). When both photocurable material and silica are incorporated into the nanophase epoxy, the overall glass transition broadens slightly, and the material stiffens at higher temperatures. This behavior can be associated, as in the macrophase epoxy, to a superposition of the glass transitions of the independent acrylate and epoxy-amine networks.

From a composition standpoint, the only significant difference between the macrophase and nanophase epoxies is their ratio of curatives (TETA and Jeffamine D230). Their disparate domain sizes are the primary factor influencing their significant differences in mechanical properties. We have previously attributed the increased modulus of the nanophase to behavior observed in multiblock polymers with alternating rigid and soft blocks. ,− Modulus and strength are improved through cooperative chain motion across multiple domains and compositional heterogeneity, and greater interfaces between domains. In the case of the macrophase epoxy, significant stiffening of the rubbery phase occurs in the presence of acrylate or fumed silica, which restrict chain mobility. In the nanophase epoxy system, mobility of the rubbery phase is already limited through cooperative motion of hard and soft domains, and thus the incorporation of additives may have a dampened effect.

In the homogeneous epoxy (Figure e), nearly all contents of silica and photocurable content have similar storage moduli throughout the test with minor stiffening due to additives. However, when 10 wt % silica content is incorporated, the material has a ∼10× increase in stiffness after the glass transition (T g = 82 °C) as compared to the neat material. A nonlinear increase in stiffness due to higher fumed silica loadings has been attributed to reaching the percolation threshold, where the percolation threshold is the filler content at which liquid-to-solid like behavior occurs. However, from strain sweeps performed on these materials in Section , liquid-like to solid-like transitions can occur at as low as 2 wt % content. At higher fumed silica loadings, the network of fumed silica may become more continuous and interconnected as compared to lower silica loadings that are still above the percolation threshold. It appears that only high loadings of silica significantly decrease the magnitude of the loss tangent (Figure f). We note that phase separation and silica organization (percolation) may mutually hinder each other, leading to more disordered structures, which could also explain, in part, the various changes in loss tangent features noted above. Ultimately, the understated effects of fumed silica on the material properties for the homogeneous epoxy are similar to the effects on the material properties for the nanophase epoxy indicating the weakly phase separated nature of the latter.

(DIW) Printing And Mechanical Properties

The printability and mechanical properties of the epoxy formulations were evaluated through DIW printing. Figure depicts the shape of an ASTM 638–14 Type V tensile bar geometry and three examples of DIW printed tensile bars of varied formulations of the nanophase epoxy. The tensile bars were manufactured with print strands oriented perpendicular to the applied tensile force. At a fumed silica content of 5 wt % (Figure b, (i)), but with no photocurable component, the formulation exhibited poor printability where the extruded layers spread under their own weight, resulting in ill-defined tensile bars. While the yield stress of this material is high enough to extrude print strands, it is not high enough to retain the structure under multiple print layers. Increasing the fumed silica content to 10 wt % (Figure b, (ii)) significantly improved print quality; printed tensile bars maintained their intended geometry and individual strands were clearly visible, showing dimensional stability and adequate print resolution. The formulation containing 5 wt % silica and 10 wt % photocurable material also maintained proper dimensions and showed improved cohesion between layers, indicated by a lack of highly defined print strands (Figure b,iii and Figure S8b). The print fidelity of this formulation suggests that the addition of a photocurable component can compensate for lower silica content by rapidly solidifying the material postextrusion, thus supporting the printed shape, interlayer adhesion, and providing green strength prior to fully curing the structure. A significant difference in opacity is also observed between the printed nanophase (clear, Figure S8b) and the macrophase (white and opaque, Figure S8c), demonstrating the contrasting optical properties when nanometer or micrometer-scale features are present.

The nanophase printed materials are slightly stiffer than the neat nanophase epoxy and their ductility falls somewhat short of the neat material by an elongation difference of ∼20% (Figure c), resulting in a lower toughness (Table S5). However, it should be noted that the printed tensile bar can experience extremely high strains in excess of 50% before breaking, and the stress–strain profiles of printed and neat nanophase epoxy are similar in behavior. Indeed, in the case of the homogeneous epoxy, elongation at break and toughness (Table S5) is also reduced while its stiffness is increased slightly (Figure d). The reduction in ductility can be attributed to the incorporation of relatively brittle acrylate in the material composition or fumed silica restricting chain movement in the plastic regime.

This trend in reduced elongation at break is continued in the macrophase epoxy (Figure b), where its elongation at break is ∼40% lower than the neat epoxy. On the other hand, the decrease in the elongation at break is coupled with a nearly 3× increase in the elastic modulus, which results in a nearly matched material toughness (Table S5). This is indicative of a significant degree of stiffening of the soft rubber matrix phase through restriction of chain mobility from fumed silica and acrylate, although the morphological changes noted in Figure likely also play a significant role. More generally, these results indicate that when modifying PIPS to tune length scales, there will likely be more significant effects to stiffness in systems with larger domains, which will diminish with decreasing domain size. It should be noted that all printed compositions exhibit a decreased elongation at break compared to their neat counterparts. In addition to changes in ductility due to additives, the presence of print interfaces perpendicular to the applied tensile force may contribute to the reduced elongation at break. The successful printing and mechanical testing of phase separated epoxies demonstrates a method for generating materials with tuned mesoscale features through PIPS along with macroscale architectural control through DIW printing.

Gradient Printing Demonstration

A unique feature of these materials and printing technique is that different epoxy formulations with distinct glass transitions can be printed in tandem. To demonstrate this, we printed homogeneous epoxy (with blue dye) and nanophase epoxy (with yellow dye) using a multimaterial DIW setup (Figure a,b).

Epoxy components exhibiting a gradient of material properties were successfully fabricated into plaques and lattices using multimaterial direct ink write (DIW) 3D printing (Figure c–e). The graded composition lattices (Figure d,e) had initial target dimensions of 20 mm × 20 mm × 3.5 mm, with strand diameters of 0.58 mm. The printed strand diameters varied minimally by ∼0.03–0.05 mm, while the postprinted widths were found to increase by ∼1 mm. The printed structure height decreased by ∼0.2 mm from the target dimensions, likely due to some minor material sag between large spans in the lattice (Table S6).

By spatially varying the composition of the printed epoxies, we produced parts with regions of distinct glass transition temperatures (corresponding to the homogeneous and nanophase epoxies), enabling unique and programmable one-way shape-changing behavior. In the first demonstration (Figure f, Supporting Movies A and B), a printed part with nanophase and homogeneous epoxies in the opposite quadrants was taken above 100 °C, folded into a smaller square and quenched with room temperature water. It was then exposed to a 45 °C environment (Supporting Movie A). The corners unfolded sequentially, with each region with the lowest glass transition (pure nanophase epoxy, T g = 49 °C) unfolding first, and the highest glass transition (pure homogeneous epoxy, T g = 82 °C) unfolding last illustrating controlled, site-specific actuation. The unfolding time for each quadrant is recorded in Table S7. The hierarchical unfolding is controlled by the polymer chains acquiring increased mobility with temperature application. The regions maintaining a lower glass transition undergo this change first (unfolding at 22 s) while the regions that maintain a higher glass transition are unaffected until enough thermal input provides for chain mobility (unfolding at 136 s), yielding controlled actuation. In the folded and quenched configuration, the polymer chains are in a stressed state until approaching T g during heating, where that stress is relieved during the phase transition and the polymer chains have the ability to recover to their original shape, thus the mechanism to unfold occurs. When the same part was placed in an 80 °C environment, the overall unfolding process was accelerated to a total time of 6 s, confirming the temperature-dependent nature of the shape change (Supporting Movie B).

In the second demonstration (Figure g, Supporting Movies C,D), the part was exposed to 100 °C and rolled such that the homogeneous epoxy was positioned either on the inside or the outside of the roll. This configuration resulted in faster (84 s with homogeneous epoxy on the inside, Supporting Movie C) or slower (146 s with homogeneous epoxy on the outside, Supporting Movie D) unrolling further highlighting the ability to program different shape transformations by grading the glass transition profile within the printed structure. These results demonstrate the versatility of multimaterial DIW printing combined with PIPS for creating epoxy components with tailored, responsive properties enabling the development of advanced actuators, soft robotics, or adaptive structures that respond precisely to environmental stimuli. Future studies will explore optimizing gradient designs for targeted, programmable shape transformations in complex applications.

Conclusion

In summary, the AM of hierarchical epoxies is realized through a tunable, PIPS framework combined with UV cured acrylate and rheological modifiers. This dual-cure system enables the creation of structures where print design controls macroscale architecture while phase separation determines substructural features and morphology. Rheological properties and gel time are carefully controlled through fumed silica content and the selection of photoinitiator, allowing for the printed structure to retain green strength during the print and prior to the final thermal cure. AFM nanoIR mapping revealed the morphology of the phase separated epoxies consisting of nano or micron scale domains of the hard and soft phases. Mapping of formulations with additives showed that phase separation is maintained, but the morphology of the macrophase epoxy domains is altered both in size and shape. Changes to the morphology result in altered thermomechanical responses, and materials with larger domain sizes were found to be more dramatically affected by the incorporation of additives. Printed structures demonstrated higher elastic moduli and lower elongation at break as compared to the neat counterparts, illustrating the trade-offs of printability and mechanical properties. Finally, multimaterial DIW of phase separating materials was implemented to generate programmable shape memory structures, exemplifying the ability of this method to create complex hierarchical structures with tailorable thermomechanical response.

While the stiffness and strength of the phase separated epoxies are lower than those of the homogeneous epoxy, the primary goal of this work was to develop printable phase separated epoxies with precise control of their material properties. Notably, elongation at break and toughness was significantly improved in the nanophase epoxy compared to the homogeneous epoxy. This enhancement could be advantageous when printing flexible electronics or in soft robotics where large, repeated deformations are necessary. Indeed, although the rubber content was fixed at a single value in the phase separated materials studied, our previous work indicates that further variation in rubber content provides a convenient handle to modulate stiffness, strength, and ultimate strain of these materials spanning extremes from glassy thermosets to soft elastomers. Furthermore, the shape memory tuning and functional grading that this work demonstrates could again be beneficial in these applications, where grading of chemical composition enables interface-free transitions between materials with distinct morphologies and material properties. Finally, these materials show promise for use in damping and impact applications, where the tunability of glass transition and therefore energy dissipation mechanisms combined with hierarchical structural control would be advantageous.
