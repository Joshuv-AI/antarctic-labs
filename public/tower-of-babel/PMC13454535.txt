Global long-term agricultural experiments reveal consequences of mineral fertilization for soil microbiomes
Authors: Shengwen Xu, Manuel Delgado-Baquerizo, Yongxiang Yu, Haoxin Fan, Limei Zhang, Xiaoyan Tang, Zhi Zhang, Shuai Wu, Nataliya Bilyera, Yakov Kuzyakov, Christoph C Tebbe, Graeme W Nicol, Joann K Whalen, Huaiying Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMineral fertilizers have sustained food security for decades, yet the long-term impacts on soil microbial communities underpinning soil health remain virtually unknown. We combine standardized field surveys with a meta-analysis to assemble a global dataset of 501 long-term agricultural experiments (≥5 years, median duration 25 years) to evaluate the impacts of sustained mineral fertilization on soil properties and microbial communities. Long-term mineral fertilization increases soil organic carbon by 14% and decreases soil pH by 0.31 units on average relative to unfertilized controls. Soil organic carbon accumulation largely explains increased microbial biomass carbon and living bacterial biomass. Mineral fertilizer-induced acidification primarily reshapes dominant bacterial taxa, with the relative abundance of Proteobacteria increasing and Firmicutes declining, whereas fungal community composition remains stable. Virulent bacteriophages increase in association with shifts in bacterial hosts. Microbial activities reveal a decoupling of more nitrogen- and phosphorus-acquisition enzymes from the unchanged production of carbon-mineralization enzymes. Microbial communities are taxonomically reorganized without reducing richness or promoting fungal pathogens. These responses are evaluated in the context of agroecosystem type, fertilization regime, and cropping regime. Our findings provide global-scale evidence for the consequences of long-term mineral fertilization on soil health, which is integral to guiding fertilizer management for sustainable agriculture.Subject terms: Agroecology, Microbial ecology, Biodiversity, Soil microbiology

Introduction

Mineral fertilizers have revolutionized global agriculture since the 1950s, fueling unprecedented increases in crop yields that now sustain more than half of the world’s population1,2. While mineral fertilizers are calibrated to support crop productivity and quality, the nutrients also impact soil properties, microbiomes, and functions3. Routine use of mineral fertilizers has transformed agricultural soils, as intermittent inputs of water-soluble nutrients and sustained increases in plant productivity have altered soil properties and shaped distinctive belowground microbial communities3. These changes in soil properties and microbial communities are increasingly associated with functions central to fertilized agroecosystems, including the decomposition of organic matter and the sequestration of organic carbon4. Adding mineral forms of nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and other nutrients overcomes the natural limitations to soil microbial growth and influences associated soil functions. However, excessive fertilization may lead to long-term outcomes such as soil acidification and biodiversity loss4,5. Mineral fertilizers may also increase the prevalence of fungal pathogens, a risk factor for plant disease6. While such outcomes are widely hypothesized and raise legitimate concerns, the consequences of long-term mineral fertilization for microbial responses linked broadly to soil health and functions have not yet been comprehensively assessed at the global scale7,8.

The existence of long-term experiments (LTEs; ≥5 years since establishment) across diverse agroecosystems offers a unique opportunity to assess the long-term effects of mineral fertilizers on soil microbiomes under realistic field conditions3,9. Many LTEs, such as the iconic Broadbalk experiment at Rothamsted, were established to address production-oriented questions (e.g., crop yield and fertilizer performance) and follow regional agronomic practices, making them robust proxies for commercial farming systems across thousands of hectares10,11. With consistent protocols that are maintained for decades, LTEs are relevant for soil microbial community analysis because of their sustained management and experimental continuity9. While the bacterial microbiome, microbial biomass and activity have been evaluated in LTEs3,12, the fungal microbiome responses are inconsistently reported, and soil viruses, which are emerging agents for soil microbial turnover and nutrient cycling, have been historically ignored13,14. Given the central role of the soil microbiome in maintaining soil health and agroecosystem function, and the importance of soil biota for resilient cropping systems under climate change, it is vital to evaluate soil properties and microbial responses in LTEs with mineral fertilization15,16. A better understanding of mineral fertilizer impacts on soil health will be informative for decision-makers and guide sustainable agriculture strategies under global change.

Here, we combine standardized field surveys in LTEs with literature synthesis to assemble a global dataset of 501 agricultural experiments, enabling investigation of the impacts of long-term (median duration 25 years) mineral fertilization on soil properties, functions, and microbial communities. Our study explicitly considers physicochemical properties (e.g., soil pH, carbon and nitrogen pools), agroecosystem functions (e.g., crop yield, organic matter decomposition, fungal pathogen prevalence) and microbial groups (bacteria, fungi, and viruses) (see Methods). Our work reveals that decades of mineral fertilization leave a persistent and globally consistent imprint on soil properties, ecosystem functions, and microbial communities, with implications for sustainable agriculture under climate change.

Results and discussion

Long-term mineral fertilization acidifies soils but increases organic carbon

We first examined changes in soil physicochemical properties, which are underlying drivers of consistent microbial responses and represent foundational variables in LTEs. To this end, we evaluated the global-scale impacts of 501 long-term fertilization experiments (LTEs; median duration 25 years) on soil properties. This database encompassed diverse fertilization regimes and cropping systems across a broad gradient of fertilizer intensities (Fig. 1). Long-term mineral fertilization had both detrimental and beneficial effects on agricultural soils. On the one hand, long-term fertilization resulted in soil acidification. On average, soil pH declined by 0.31 units (95% CI: −0.42 to −0.19), with soil acidification resulting from N fertilizer alone and compound mineral N fertilizers (e.g., NP, NK, or NPK; Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Acidification was more pronounced in upland than in paddy soils (p < 0.05), due to greater H+ production via nitrification and base cation leaching (Supplementary Fig. 1)17. On the other hand, mineral fertilization increased soil organic carbon (SOC) content (lnRR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.10–0.16, Fig. 2a). Croplands have considerable potential for carbon sequestration, yet SOC levels remain low in these systems18. Here, long-term mineral fertilization resulted in a 14% (i.e., lnRR = 0.13) increase in SOC content on average. This magnitude of increase is comparable to that reported in a previous assessment (lnRR = 0.12)3. Compound mineral fertilizers (e.g., NP, NK, or NPK) supported greater SOC accumulation than sole N application (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 1). This increase was associated with higher crop productivity (lnRR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.78–1.16) and microbial biomass (lnRR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.23) under compound fertilization regimes compared with sole N fertilizer application (p < 0.001, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 3). These concurrent increases suggest that nutrient inputs from compound fertilizers may be more effective than N fertilizer alone at offsetting SOC losses in intensively managed croplands19,20. As expected, compound fertilizers increased total N (lnRR = 0.15, 95% CI: 0.12–0.17), available P (lnRR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.03–1.41), and available K (lnRR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.28–0.51) compared with sole N fertilizers (p < 0.001, Supplementary Fig. 1). While not the focus of our analysis, these co-benefits reinforce the role of balanced mineral fertilization (N with P and/or K) in sustaining agroecosystem productivity in the long term.

Long-term mineral fertilization enhances microbial N and P acquisition

Microbial processes that mediate organic matter decomposition are essential to maintain soil fertility and nutrient release, and are highly sensitive to mineral N fertilization21. Microbial respiration (lnRR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.01–0.35), dehydrogenase activity (lnRR = 0.40, 95% CI: 0.06–0.74), and the activities of N- (leucine aminopeptidase: lnRR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.02–0.60; N-acetyl-glucosaminidase: lnRR = 0.37, 95% CI: 0.17–0.58) and P-acquisition enzymes (phosphatase: lnRR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.10–0.34) increased under long-term mineral N fertilization (p < 0.05, Fig. 2b). By contrast, C-mineralizing enzymes, including hydrolytic enzymes such as β-glucosidase, β-xylosidase, cellobiohydrolase, and invertase, as well as oxidative enzymes like phenoloxidases, were unaffected by long-term mineral fertilization (Fig. 2b). Increased N- and P-acquisition enzyme activities and basal respiration were observed with compound fertilization, but not under sole mineral N application (Supplementary Fig. 2). The absence of microbial enzymatic responses under N-only fertilization reveals that nutrient limitations constrain microbial enzyme production, as P limitation is increasingly acknowledged to be pervasive in soil microbial communities22,23. According to resource allocation theory, microbial investment in enzyme production depends on both nutrient demand and metabolic capacity24. When only N is supplied, other nutrients, particularly P, may become limiting and restrict microbial growth and enzymatic activity22. In contrast, compound fertilization (NP or NPK) provides additional nutrients (P and/or K), which can alleviate nutrient constraints, stimulate microbial activity and nutrient demand, and thereby increase investment in nutrient-acquisition enzymes25. Recent evidence suggests that extracellular enzyme activities primarily reflect microbial resource allocation under nutrient stoichiometric constraints rather than a general increase in decomposition capacity26. Long-term mineral fertilization enhances crop production and increases the N:P ratio of plant litter, which can aggravate P limitation for microorganisms27. Higher crop yield results in higher C input to the soil, which increases microbial biomass C across all LTEs (Fig. 2c). As microbial biomass C increases under nutrient imbalances, microbes allocate more resources to N- and P-acquisition enzymes to maintain their internal C:N:P stoichiometric balance. Higher basal respiration under long-term fertilization may indicate greater energy input to microbial metabolism, which could be associated with increased microbial growth rates or nutrient imbalance (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 11b). This is consistent with studies showing that higher microbial growth rates are associated with increased production of N- and P-acquisition enzymes rather than C-acquiring enzymes26. In contrast, C-acquiring enzyme activities remained unchanged, suggesting that C availability was not the primary constraint on microbial metabolism under N enrichment28. This may be because long-term N fertilization increases plant biomass and plant-derived C inputs to soils, including greater root exudation29. Root exudates consist largely of low-molecular-weight, water-soluble compounds that are directly assimilable by microorganisms, reducing microbial reliance on complex organic C compounds that must undergo extracellular enzymatic decomposition30. Overall, such nutrient-driven C flow to the soil increases microbial growth efficiency and promotes the incorporation of C into microbial biomass, which produces byproducts and cellular debris that accumulate in SOC pools under compound mineral fertilization31,32.

Long-term fertilization increases bacterial biomass

Consistent with previous studies, long-term compound mineral fertilization increased soil microbial biomass C (lnRR = 0.13, 95% CI: 0.03–0.23) and N (lnRR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.05–0.37) content compared with sole N fertilization (p < 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 3)3,33. We further examined the response of soil phospholipid fatty acids (PLFAs), which turn over rapidly in soils and thus reflect living cells34. Total bacterial PLFAs increased under long-term mineral fertilization (lnRR = 0.14, 95% CI: 0.01–0.26), whereas fungal PLFAs remained unchanged (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 11a). Croplands are subject to regular disturbances (e.g., tillage, harvesting, residue management and fungicide application), which disproportionately disrupt filamentous fungal hyphal networks, whereas bacteria are less affected35. In addition, long-term fertilization increases mineral N availability, which generally favors fast-growing bacteria over fungi due to their slower growth and longer generation times36. The increase in bacterial PLFAs was associated with enhanced crop-derived C inputs under long-term fertilization, with elevated SOC coinciding with increased substrate availability and a higher abundance of copiotrophic Gammaproteobacteria (Figs. 3, 4)37,38. The unchanged fungal PLFA abundance suggests no increased reliance on fungal-mediated organic matter processing pathways under nutrient-enriched conditions39. Our results also show an absence of microbial biomass response in paddy soils, likely due to high baseline biomass, slow microbial turnover, and oxygen-limited conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3)40. Our study demonstrates that increases in microbial biomass under long-term mineral fertilization are primarily driven by bacterial components and depend on agroecosystem types3.

Long-term fertilization maintains microbial richness without promoting pathogenic fungi

A widely held assumption is that long-term mineral fertilization, via soil acidification or ammonium oxidation (which releases H+ ions), inevitably reduces microbial α-diversity41. However, our analysis revealed no consistent decline in α-diversity, challenging the generality of this assumption in croplands (Fig. 2d). Interpretations of α-diversity metrics require caution. Estimators based on discrete counts are sensitive to sequencing depth, which can complicate comparisons across studies42. In our synthesis, effect sizes were derived from paired observations within individual studies, thereby minimizing biases associated with differences in sequencing depth. Nevertheless, estimators such as Chao1 and ACE remain susceptible to inflation by singletons and sequencing artifacts43. Apparent α-diversity increases or decreases should not be overinterpreted as (un)favorable microbial consequences of long-term mineral fertilization44. To complement α-diversity patterns, we assessed microbial community similarity based on relative abundance data, which capture variation in both composition and taxon dominance. Long-term mineral fertilization consistently shifted microbial community composition across sites (i.e., centroid shift), while preserving within-treatment similarity (i.e., unchanged β-dispersion) and α-diversity (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 5)45. The stability of α-diversity and β-dispersion despite a clear centroid shift points to turnover among dominant taxa without erosion of overall diversity. This pattern is consistent with trophically structured, niche-differentiated soil microbial communities that accommodate nutrient enrichment through compositional reweighting rather than stress-induced attrition of specific groups46. Together, these findings demonstrate that long-term mineral fertilization induces structured shifts in soil microbial composition by reshaping dominant taxa, yet when it is precisely calibrated within well-managed croplands it remains compatible with the maintenance of biodiversity8.

We further examined fungal guilds to determine whether long-term mineral fertilization alters plant-microbe trophic interactions in croplands. A global synthesis in grassland ecosystems reported that mineral fertilization favors pathogenic over mutualistic fungi, yet this pattern did not emerge in our cropland LTE dataset (Fig. 3b)47. Although some site-level studies have reported pathogen increases, the absence of a general trend across LTEs likely reflects differences in cropping systems, crop rotations, and baseline pathogen prevalence48. While our analyses reveal compositional patterns of fungal guilds, a recent large-scale cropland study has complemented such approaches with soil suppressiveness assays to assess pathogen activity beyond community composition49. Overall, mineral fertilization alone does not explain variation in pathogenic fungal guild abundance in croplands, underscoring that pathogen risk cannot be inferred from fertilization history or community composition alone.

Long-term mineral fertilization drives major shifts in soil microbial taxa dominance

Our results revealed a consistent shift in the relative abundance of specific phyla in response to long-term mineral fertilization, notably increased Proteobacteria (lnRR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.04–0.17) and decreased Firmicutes (lnRR = −0.18, 95% CI: −0.36 to −0.01; Fig. 3 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Soil pH was associated with Proteobacteria (β = −0.09, p < 0.05) and Firmicutes (β = 0.51, p < 0.001), emphasizing the dominance of pH in structuring bacterial communities (Fig. 4b).

Our standardized field surveys confirmed increased Proteobacteria and decreased Firmicutes, with class-level responses largely consistent with these patterns (Fig. 4c). While overall viral diversity remained unchanged, the abundance of virulent bacteriophages increased (lnRR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.04–0.17, Fig. 3b). Co-occurrence network analysis identified Actinobacteria as the predominant predicted hosts of virulent phages in mineral-fertilized soils. Virulent phages with predicted Proteobacteria hosts showed higher mean degree, whereas those with predicted Firmicutes hosts showed lower mean degree (Fig. 4d). These changes in connectivity are consistent with the observed increases in Proteobacteria and decreases in Firmicutes under long-term fertilization, pointing to a role of virulent phages in mediating bacterial community shifts. Consistent with previous reports of increased Proteobacteria under mineral fertilization12, our results further indicate that soil acidification is a primary driver of shifts in bacterial taxa, with virulent bacteriophages potentially contributing to microbial turnover. However, the increase in virulent bacteriophage abundance could not be explained by the measured soil properties. This suggests that additional processes may be involved, such as microscale resource hotspots induced by fertilization that enhance virus-host encounters and lytic activity50.

Environmental-context dependence of soil microbial responses to mineral fertilization

We showed that fertilizer-induced soil acidification and SOC accumulation independently contributed to shifts in bacterial phyla and increases in microbial biomass (Fig. 4). We therefore examined whether the responses of soil acidification and SOC accumulation to mineral fertilization were consistent across LTEs. We found that the magnitude of pH decline depended strongly on baseline soil pH, and the magnitude of SOC accumulation depended on the baseline SOC level. Acidification was strongest at neutral baseline pH (6.5–7.5) but weaker in already acidic or alkaline soils, while SOC accumulation was greatest at low baseline SOC ( < 15 g kg−1) and decreased as baseline SOC increased (Fig. 5a). Soil acidification in LTEs was consistent with broader terrestrial patterns, whereby acidification increases rapidly with N inputs and is most severe in neutral-pH soils with weak buffering capacity51. SOC accumulation under long-term mineral fertilization was more pronounced in carbon-poor croplands than in carbon-rich soils, indicating a pattern of diminishing returns with increasing baseline SOC19. Microbial biomass was positively associated with fertilizer-induced SOC accumulation yet negatively associated with baseline SOC, pointing to smaller responses in carbon-rich soils (Fig. 5b). Although some microbial responses showed no significant overall change, they remained sensitive to changes in soil pH, including bacterial Shannon diversity (r = 0.39, p < 0.001) and the relative abundance of specific phyla (e.g., Acidobacteria, Nitrospirae, Glomeromycota), underscoring the broad role of pH in shaping microbial diversity and composition (Fig. 5b). Thus, long-term microbial responses to mineral fertilization are governed by initial soil conditions. This baseline dependence provides a mechanistic basis for globally consistent yet context-specific shifts in soil microbiomes.

Global spatial predictions of consistent microbial responses

To evaluate the broader relevance of microbial responses observed in LTEs, we developed spatial prediction models for microbial biomass carbon and the relative abundance of Proteobacteria and Firmicutes in cropland soils. Soil pH and SOC content are considered the primary explanatory factors of these microbial responses (Fig. 6). Microbial biomass carbon and Proteobacteria abundance were predicted to increase across most croplands, with the strongest responses in intensively managed regions such as eastern China, northern India, regions of Western Europe, and the eastern United States. These regions are characterized by sustained mineral N additions, which may contribute to long-term shifts in soil acidification and increases in organic carbon content (Supplementary Fig. 12)52. In contrast, Firmicutes abundance declines in the same areas, aligning with their sensitivity to acidification. Tropical croplands with inherently acidic and carbon-poor soils, including those in South America and sub-Saharan Africa, showed weaker or opposite changes53. Proteobacteria and Firmicutes respond consistently to soil acidification, while microbial biomass increases with SOC accumulation, highlighting their potential as complementary metrics for assessing soil health in mineral-fertilized croplands54,55.

Implications and limitations

Through our comprehensive synthesis of soil microbial responses to long-term mineral fertilization, we found globally consistent microbial and biogeochemical changes across LTEs: (i) Microbial biomass carbon increased with fertilizer-induced SOC accumulation, while the relative abundance of Proteobacteria increased and Firmicutes decreased under widespread soil acidification. Spatial predictions confirmed these consistent responses across intensively fertilized croplands worldwide. (ii) Functional responses showed a decoupling of the elevated nitrogen- and phosphorus-acquisition enzymes from the constant production of carbon-mineralization enzymes, indicating that simple plant-derived carbon substrates could reduce the production of enzymes capable of decomposing complex organic matter. (iii) Long-term mineral fertilization consistently shifted microbial community composition while maintaining within-treatment similarity and α-diversity, reflecting taxonomic reorganization without biodiversity loss. (iv) Microbial functional guild analysis showed no increase in the prevalence of plant-pathogenic fungi, suggesting that pathogen dynamics and plant disease risk are more strongly driven by local crops and management than by fertilization. (v) Metagenomic data revealed enrichment of virulent bacteriophages under long-term mineral fertilization, pointing to a previously underrecognized viral role in regulating microbial turnover in croplands (Fig. 7). Thus, our results clarify long-standing assumptions about the ecological costs of mineral fertilization and offer new insights into its long-term legacy in agricultural soils.

Nonetheless, the synthesis remains limited due to the uneven geographic representation of LTEs, the coarse resolution of microbial taxa, and the transient nature of functional indicators such as enzyme activities that are sensitive to seasonal variation56. Our findings are complementary to other literature debating the ecological consequences of organic and mineral fertilization strategies57. Previous meta-analyses comparing the two types of fertilization have reported higher microbial biomass and activity under organic fertilization, commonly attributed to enhanced carbon stocks, lower soil acidification, and additional organic inputs58–60. Our results show that long-term mineral fertilization alone can also increase microbial biomass and activity, while maintaining microbial diversity without changing the occurrence of plant pathogenic fungi. Thus, our synthesis provides complementary evidence that sustained mineral fertilization reshapes soil microbiomes in croplands in ways that are not necessarily detrimental to key aspects of soil biological integrity. Combining organic inputs with mineral fertilization may help to sustain crop productivity while preserving soil biodiversity61,62. Addressing remaining uncertainties will require global coordination in the integration of long-term data, as well as a greater emphasis on temporal resolution in microbial monitoring. One way to achieve this would be through collaborative networks such as the Global Long-Term Agricultural Experiment Network (GLTEN)9,63. Despite these limitations, our synthesis leverages the unique temporal depth of long-term agricultural experiments to provide rare global evidence of how the belowground microbiome is reshaped by decades of mineral fertilization. This offers critical insights for assessing soil health and managing fertilizers sustainably64.

Methods

Global dataset curation

We assembled a global dataset to investigate the impacts of long-term mineral fertilization (defined as more than five years of urea, ammonium nitrate, or related N fertilizer application) on soil properties and belowground microbial communities3. The dataset includes variables grouped into six categories: (i) Soil physicochemical properties, including pH, soil organic carbon (SOC), dissolved organic carbon (DOC), total nitrogen (TN), the C/N ratio, concentrations of ammonium and nitrate, available phosphorus and potassium (AP, AK), and bulk density. Crop yield was included separately as a proxy for agricultural soil productivity; (ii) Microbial activities relevant to organic matter decomposition were assessed via basal respiration, metabolic quotient (qCO2), and a set of extracellular enzymes. These included those involved in carbon (e.g., β-glucosidase, cellobiohydrolase), nitrogen (e.g., N-acetylglucosaminidase, urease), and phosphorus (e.g., acid and/or alkaline phosphatase) cycling, as well as redox-related enzymes (phenoloxidase); (iii) Microbial biomass was estimated using both extractable microbial carbon and nitrogen (MBC, MBN), and phospholipid fatty acid (PLFA) profiling, with PLFA profiling providing a more accurate reflection of the living microbial community; (iv) Microbial diversity was assessed using observed richness, Shannon diversity, and β-dispersion, while directional shifts in community composition were quantified via centroid shift derived from ordination; (v) Microbial taxa were represented by the relative abundances of dominant bacterial (e.g., Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria), fungal (e.g., Ascomycota, Basidiomycota), and viral (e.g., Uroviricota) phyla; (vi) Functional classifications included fungal trophic guilds (symbiotrophs, saprotrophs, pathotrophs) and viral life strategies, distinguishing temperate phages (lysogenic potential) from virulent phages (obligately lytic). Site-level metadata included geographic coordinates, cropland type (upland and paddy), fertilization duration, mean annual temperature and precipitation, cropping regime, fertilizer type, and annual application rate. Data sources comprised both literature synthesis and targeted field sampling. Our field-collected data helped fill gaps in the literature, particularly for soil viral communities and other underrepresented variables (Supplementary Fig. 7). The complete dataset is provided in the Supplementary Data 2; Source data are provided as a Source Data file.

Field data collection

Soil samples were collected in April 2024 from 11 long-term fertilization trials across China, with paired plots receiving either no fertilizer (control, CK) or mineral nitrogen fertilizer (minN). Each site followed a consistent fertilization regime in a strip-plot design. Detailed site information is available in Supplementary Fig. 6 and Source Data. At each site, bulk soil samples (0–20 cm) were collected from three replicate plots (n = 3) for both CK and minN treatments using a soil probe. Samples were homogenized and stored appropriately (fresh at 4 °C, air-dried, freeze-dried, or frozen at −80°C) depending on the planned analysis. DNA extraction and soil enzyme measurements were conducted within two weeks of sampling. The soil physicochemical properties described above were measured using standard laboratory protocols, as detailed in Supplementary Method 1.

Microbial community profiling

Microbial composition was profiled using PLFA, amplicon, and metagenomic sequencing. PLFAs were extracted from 2 g of freeze-dried soil using a modified Bligh and Dyer method65. PLFA biomarkers were assigned to major microbial groups based on established nomenclature66. The detailed procedures for PLFA extraction and analysis are provided in Supplementary Method 2.

Soil DNA was extracted from 0.5 g of freeze-dried soil using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) and used for both amplicon and metagenomic library preparation. The 16S rRNA gene (V4-V5 region) was amplified using primers 515F and 907R, while fungal ITS1 regions were amplified using primers ITS1F and ITS2. Amplicons were barcoded and pooled in equimolar concentrations for sequencing. The amplicon pool was purified with AMPure XP beads and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 250 bp) at G&C Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Fungal trophic guilds were identified using the FUNGuild database based on ITS amplicon sequences67. Further details are provided in Supplementary Method 2.

Metagenomic libraries were prepared from high-quality genomic DNA using a ligation-based protocol. DNA was fragmented to ~200–600 bp, size-selected with magnetic beads (Transgen, China), quantified using the Library Quantification Kit (KAPA Biosystems, USA), and quality-checked on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA). Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus platform (2 × 150 bp), generating approximately 15 Gb of paired-end reads per sample. Quality-filtered reads were assembled using ‘MEGAHIT’ with multiple k-mer sizes, and contigs ≥500 bp were retained and subsequently de-replicated using ‘MMseqs2’ to generate a non-redundant contig set. Both amplicon and metagenomic sequencing were performed in technical duplicate to ensure reproducibility. Sequencing depth was considered sufficient. Rarefaction curves for the amplicon data (based on the lowest read number across 132 samples) showed a saturation plateau, and metagenomic contig counts and N50 values indicated good assembly quality (Supplementary Fig. 8). Detailed descriptions of additional library preparation materials, methodology, and the analysis pipeline can also be found in Supplementary Method 2.

Viral genomes were de novo assembled from metagenomes using the ‘ViOTUcluster’ workflow68. Contigs ≥ 3,000 bp were selected for viral identification. Putative viral sequences were detected using ‘VirSorter2’69, ‘geNomad’70, and ‘viralVerify’71, operated in non-concentrated mode, which is recommended for bulk metagenomes not enriched for viral particles. Viral sequences were subsequently passed to ‘CheckV’ for quality control and trimming of host-derived regions72. High-quality viral contigs were binned into viral metagenome-assembled genomes (vMAGs) using ‘vRhyme’, followed by de-replication with ‘dRep’ to generate a non-redundant genome set. Viral abundance was estimated via read mapping with ‘Bowtie2’ and normalized to TPM. High-confidence viral contigs were clustered into viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) according to the Minimum Information about an Uncultivated Virus Genome guidelines73. Representative vOTU genomes were analyzed with ‘PhaBOX2’ to reconstruct a phylogenetic tree, classify viral lifestyle (virulent or temperate) based on hallmark genes and genome features, and predict potential hosts74.

Microbial activities

Basal respiration was determined after 24 h incubation at 25 °C in sealed serum bottles, with CO2 quantified by gas chromatography (7890B, Agilent, USA). The metabolic quotient was calculated as the ratio of CO2-C to microbial biomass carbon. Enzyme activities were measured using colorimetric microplate assays (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, USA). Detailed procedures and enzymatic substrates can be found in Supplementary Method 3.

Literature data collection

Literature search

To ensure comprehensive retrieval of peer-reviewed papers reporting data on long-term fertilization trials, we first developed a search string based on expert knowledge, and then refined it using a semi-automated method based on text mining and keyword co-occurrence networks, as implemented in the ‘litsearchr’ R package75. This approach helped identify additional relevant and synonymous terms to improve search recall. Based on this strategy, we developed the following search string for Web of Science (www.webofscience.com), while acknowledging the potential for inadvertent omissions: TS = (Soil AND (microbial community OR bacterial community OR fungal community OR bacteria OR fungi OR microbial diversity OR bacterial diversity OR fungal diversity OR microbial composition OR bacterial composition OR fungal composition OR microbiota OR microbiome OR microbial communities OR virus OR viral) AND (nitrogen fertilization OR nitrogen addition OR nitrogen input OR nitrogen fertilizer) AND (long-term OR long term) AND (cropland OR farmland OR agricultural OR arable OR cultivated OR farming) NOT (review)). The last search was done on 14 June 2024 and retrieved 1,697 records.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We specified the following criteria for the initial screening: (i) Primary studies must include both no fertilizer control and a treatment group receiving only mineral N fertilizer (optionally mineral P and K). Studies involving combined applications with manure, compost, straw, biochar, or other organic amendments were excluded; (ii) Field experiments only (not incubation studies); (iii) Fertilization duration must be at least 5 years to qualify as a long-term experiment3; (iv) Experiments must be conducted in cropland ecosystems; managed plantations (e.g., tea plantations, orchards, vineyards) were excluded; (v) Rhizosphere-focused studies were excluded due to the direct effect of roots on microbial communities76; (vi) Only bulk topsoil samples collected within the cultivated (plough) layer were included, typically corresponding to sampling depths within 0-20 cm (occasionally extending to 30 cm depending on mollic horizon). Studies involving soil replacement or deeper layers were excluded; (vii) Studies involving quick liming, reductive soil disinfestation, or nitrification inhibitors were excluded. After screening titles and abstracts, 359 full-text articles meeting the above criteria were retrieved. Full texts were independently assessed for eligibility by two authors (S. X. and Y. Y.). Eventually, 225 primary studies published from 2003 to 2024 were included. Detailed justifications for the 134 excluded studies are provided in Supplementary Table 1. A completed PRISMA checklist is provided.

Data collection

We extracted the sample size and corresponding data from the text or figures using the WebPlotDigitizer. In cases where site-level mean annual temperature and precipitation were not reported, these data were retrieved from ClimateCharts77. To ensure consistency, annual application rates were standardized and reported in terms of elemental N, P, and K contents. Microbial α-diversity data were extracted specifically for observed richness and Shannon indices. We did not include nonparametric richness estimators such as Chao1 or ACE, despite their frequent use in studies based on amplicon sequence variant data. Given that these indices are highly sensitive to singleton sequences, many of which are artifacts, we did not consider them appropriate for interpretation43. Microbial community β-dispersion and centroid shift were quantified based on unconstrained ordination (e.g., PCoA) using dissimilarity matrices78. β-dispersion was calculated as the average Euclidean distance among replicate samples within each group (CK and minN), reflecting within-group heterogeneity. Centroid shift was measured as the Euclidean distance between group centroids, indicating a directional change in community composition. A schematic illustration of both metrics is provided in Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10, with a detailed explanation in Supplementary Note 1. For all variables, means (M), standard deviations (SD), and sample sizes (n) for the control and treatment groups were recorded, respectively. SDs were calculated from standard errors (SE) when necessary using the formula SD=SE×n. We imputed missing SDs using either Bracken’s coefficient of variation-based method for unit-consistent variables, or a nearest-neighbor hot-deck method for variables with methodological variability (e.g., enzyme activities)79.

Risk of publication bias

To assess and adjust for potential publication bias and effect-size dependence, we applied the two-step robust estimation procedure81. In the first step, a fixed-effect model incorporating the variance-covariance matrix (FE-VCV) was fitted to obtain bias-resistant point estimates, thereby reducing the influence of small or selectively reported studies. In the second step, cluster-robust variance estimation (CRVE) was applied to the FE-VCV to adjust the standard errors for within-study clustering of effect sizes. This approach provides valid inference even under misspecification of the dependence structure, by relying on study-level clustering to estimate robust standard errors. Egger’s regression plots and bias-corrected estimates (via two-step robust estimation) were provided to assess potential publication bias for each response variable (Supplementary Figs. 13 and 14).

Spatial prediction of microbial responses to mineral fertilization

To quantify global-scale responses of microbial biomass C and bacterial phyla (Proteobacteria and Firmicutes) to mineral N fertilization, we developed generalized additive models (GAMs) based on lnRR and predictors from the meta-analysis dataset. Each GAM followed the structure: lnRR = s(pH) + s(Soil organic C) + s(Mean annual temperature) + s(Mean annual precipitation) + s(Annual rate of mineral N addition) + s(Longitude, Latitude). The term s() denotes a smooth function capturing the non-linear influence, and s(Longitude, Latitude) is a two-dimensional thin-plate spline accounting for spatial autocorrelation. Soil pH and soil organic C represent proximal soil regulators, mean annual temperature and precipitation capture large-scale climatic constraints, and the annual rate of mineral N addition quantifies fertilization intensity. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), which ranged from 1.09 to 5.28, indicating no severe multicollinearity. Because the highest VIF occurred for mean annual precipitation, we further evaluated its potential influence by comparing alternative models excluding either mean annual temperature or precipitation. As these models showed comparable performance, the full model was retained for prediction to preserve the complete set of relevant predictors (Supplementary Table 2). To visualize the relationships between lnRR and individual predictors, we plotted partial effects for the relevant explanatory variables. We then assembled gridded covariate layers at 5 arc-minute resolution for global prediction. Soil organic C and pH data were sourced from SoilGrids 2.0, mean annual temperature and precipitation from WorldClim, and mineral N addition rates from a global N fertilization dataset (2010s average)82–84. N inputs derived from crop residues and manure were excluded from the dataset, and a revised raster representing mineral N inputs was generated accordingly (Supplementary Fig. 12). All layers were reprojected to a common grid. The trained GAMs were applied to these layers to predict global lnRR and associated standard errors, with visualization via bivariate classification maps.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). Meta-analyses were conducted using the R packages ‘metafor’, ‘metagear’, ‘corpcor’, ‘orchaRd’, and ‘multcomp’85,86. The ‘metafor’ package was used to fit multilevel meta-analytic models with variance-covariance matrices and random effects. Missing standard deviations were imputed using ‘metagear’, and variance-covariance matrices were adjusted to ensure positive definiteness using ‘corpcor’. Subgroup contrasts were evaluated using ‘multcomp’, and orchard plots were generated using ‘orchaRd’ to visualize effect sizes and confidence intervals. Package ‘tidyverse’ was used for data processing, and ‘tidyplots’ was used for basic plotting87. Co-occurrence networks of host-associated virulent phage vOTUs were inferred using Spearman correlations implemented in the R package ‘ggClusterNet’, with abundance data organized using ‘phyloseq’. Spatial raster processing was performed using ‘tidygeocoder’, ‘terra’, and ‘sf’. Generalized additive models were implemented using the ‘mgcv’ package, including smoothing terms for environmental predictors and a two-dimensional spline for geographic coordinates to account for spatial autocorrelation. The fitted models were applied to global gridded environmental layers to generate spatial predictions. Predictor multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors. Predictor importance was further assessed using drop-one model comparisons by excluding each covariate and comparing model performance. Partial effects of predictors were visualized from the fitted GAMs. Inverse-variance weighted regressions were used to examine relationships between statistically significant changes in soil physicochemical properties and microbial response variables, with weights equal to the inverse of the sampling variance.
