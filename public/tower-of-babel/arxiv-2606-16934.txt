Exploring Extrinsic and Intrinsic Properties for Effective Reasoning with Code Interpreter
Patomporn Payoungkhamdee patomporn.p_s21@vistec.ac.th, Napat Laosaengpha napat.r@vistec.ac.th, Jenta Wonglertsakul jenta.w@live.ku.th, Pittawat Taveekitworachai pittawat@scb10x.com, Pume Tuchinda pumet_pro@vistec.ac.th, Panjapong Poobanchuen panjapong.pooba@kmutt.ac.th, Ekapol Chuangsuwanich ekapol.c@chula.ac.th, Can Udomcharoenchaikit canu_pro@vistec.ac.th, Samuel Cahyawijaya samuelcahyawijaya@cohere.com, Peerat Limkonchotiwat peerat@aisingapore.org, Sarana Nutanong snutanon@vistec.ac.th

Abstract
Reasoning with a Code Interpreter (CI) has emerged as an effective paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) through executable computation and iterative verification. Despite its growing adoption, the behavioral properties underlying effective code reasoning remain largely underexplored. In this work, we investigate code reasoning from two distinct perspectives inspired by prior studies of natural language reasoning: extrinsic properties, represented by crucial tokens , and intrinsic properties, represented by code-specific cognitive behaviors . Across multiple LLMs, we find that stronger CI reasoning models consistently exhibit a higher prevalence of crucial tokens and cognitive behaviors, particularly verification, backtracking, and backward chaining. Building on these observations, we examine how these properties can be leveraged during both inference and training. At inference time, appending code-specific crucial tokens improves performance on several reasoning capabilities, including mathematical, ordering, and optimization, while yielding limited benefits elsewhere. At training time, augmenting a state-of-the-art framework with code-specific cognitive behaviors improves supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning performance in two of three evaluated models. Further analysis shows that these behaviors reduce overthinking in incorrect responses and improve token efficiency, while also revealing factors that limit gains in a certain model. Our findings provide the first systematic characterization of effective reasoning with CI and demonstrate both the potential and limitations of leveraging key properties to improve CI-based reasoning.

2.2
Key Properties in Natural Language Reasoning

To systematically investigate the properties of reasoning in CI-based systems, building on prior studies of natural language reasoning, we define the extrinsic property as interactions with
crucial tokens

(
16
)
and the intrinsic property as the dynamics of
cognitive behaviors

(
8
)
.

Extrinsic Properties.

Early work on exploiting the extrinsic properties of extended reasoning in LLMs explored inference-time interventions that encourage models to continue reasoning after reaching a completion signal, such as repeatedly appending a
wait
token
(
22
)
. Although this simple strategy shows performance improvements on mathematical benchmarks, subsequent studies questioned its robustness and generalization.
40
showed that gains from repeated
wait
injections are highly inconsistent, with models often oscillating between answers or producing nearly identical reasoning traces despite additional computation. Extending the analysis to knowledge-intensive tasks,
47
found no consistent benefit from budget forcing and reported increased hallucination rates for stronger reasoning models, while
10
observed a non-monotonic scaling effect in which excessive deliberation can degrade performance through overthinking. More recent work argues that test-time scaling gains depend less on reasoning length and more on the informational content of reasoning trajectories. The 80:20 hypothesis suggests that only a small fraction of reasoning tokens contribute disproportionately to performance improvements
(
33
)
, while
24
identifies “critical tokens” that coincide with key reasoning transitions and carry outsized influence on final predictions. Similarly,
16
argues that improvements stem from epistemic verbalization, the explicit expression of uncertainty, doubt, and self-correction, rather than from specific trigger tokens or longer chains of thought alone.
However, these studies focus predominantly on natural language reasoning, leaving the extrinsic properties of reasoning with external tools, particularly code interpreters, largely unexplored.

Intrinsic Properties.

A growing line of research examines the intrinsic cognitive behaviors and emergent exploration tendencies of LLMs that dictate their capacity for reinforcement learning (RL) and test-time scaling. An influential study in this area,
8
empirically demonstrated that foundational cognitive habits, such as verification, subgoal setting, backtracking, and backward chaining, serve as important priors for RL-driven self-improvement, while models exhibiting these behaviors scale effectively under RL, behavior-deficient models quickly plateau unless primed with behavior-rich trajectories. Building on this premise, recent works focus on how to systematically instill, condense, or manipulate these internal mental mechanics.
31
introduces a self-distillation framework to isolate and transmit these pure structural reasoning behaviors to smaller base models to prevent early RL stagnation, while
1
draws from human cognitive psychology to compress verbose reasoning traces into efficient, dense internal intermediaries via adaptive sketching. To maximize the utility of these habits during inference,
46
identifies specialized internal attention heads dedicated to non-linear reasoning steps and introduces test-time steering mechanisms to balance overthinking and underthinking. Furthermore, models like Satori
(
30
)
manifest these intrinsic behaviors explicitly by framing verification and backtracking as autoregressive “meta-actions” within a unified chain-of-action-thought. Extending this paradigm beyond pure text,
26
;
38
demonstrate that these linguistic cognitive habits structurally generalize to visual reasoning through iterative grounding and verification under RLVR. Collectively, these studies showed that a model’s intrinsic cognitive architecture governs its learning efficiency. However, these dynamics remain underexplored in tool-integrated environments like reasoning with CI, where external compiler feedback introduces distinct, deterministic feedback loops.

3.1
Reasoning with Code Interpreter

Natural language reasoning considers an input problem
x
and a generated response
\hat{y}
, where the response is sampled from a model policy
\pi_{\theta}
parameterized by
\theta
, i.e.,
\hat{y}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x)
.
The generated response typically consists of a reasoning trajectory (rationale)
\hat{r}
and a final prediction
\hat{a}
, where the answer is commonly formatted within a predefined pattern, such as
\boxed
{
answer
}.

Unlike traditional natural language reasoning, where a model has to tackle both high-level problem-solving and actual computation, reasoning with CI lets the model generate code, commonly Python, to leverage the computational capability from CI, letting the model focus solely on problem-solving.
Conventionally, for the first turn, the model has to generate both reasoning trajectory
\hat{y}
and code
\hat{c}
from a given problem, represented as
(\hat{y}_{0},\hat{c}_{0})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)
.
After that, the code will be parsed by CI, resulting in an output
\hat{o}_{0}\sim\text{CI}(\hat{c}_{0})
. The model then takes code execution output and generate the next turn
(\hat{y}_{1},\hat{c}_{1})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\hat{o}_{0},\hat{y}_{0},\hat{c}_{0},x)
, typically called multi-turn interaction with CI until reaching the final turn (
T
), generally represented as

(\hat{y}_{T},\hat{c}_{T})\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|\mathbf{\hat{o}}_{t<T}\mathbf{\hat{y}}_{t<T},\mathbf{\hat{c}}_{t<T},x)

(1)

Similar to typical reasoning where
\hat{y}_{T}
contains rationale
\hat{r}
and final answer
\hat{a}
.
A representative trace of this structure is illustrated in
Figure

7
.

3.2
Test-Time Scaling

Test-time scaling is an emerging paradigm that improves downstream performance by allocating additional computational resources during inference
(
22
;
35
)
. Existing approaches can be broadly categorized into parallel and sequential scaling
(
44
)
. Parallel scaling generates multiple independent reasoning trajectories and aggregates or selects among them, whereas sequential scaling devotes computation to extending a single trajectory, enabling iterative refinement and error correction.
To study how models interact with extrinsic factors, we focus on sequential test-time scaling in the context of CI-based reasoning and investigate its scaling behavior across different capabilities and model variants.

To investigate these dynamics, we manipulate the test-time scaling budget by overriding the model’s self-termination signal. Typically, reasoning models signify the completion of a reasoning by generating a specific token, such as
</think>
. We intervene by replacing this token with a
crucial token
(
w
), such as
wait
, which compels the model to extend its reasoning. Formally, given an initial reasoning
\hat{r}_{0}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)
, we iteratively inject
N
such tokens to produce an extended trajectory:

\hat{r}_{N}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid w,\hat{r}_{N-1},\dots,w,\hat{r}_{0},x)

(2)

The final code,
\hat{c}
, is then sampled conditioned on the full extended reasoning:

\hat{c}\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\hat{r}_{N},w,\hat{r}_{N-1},\dots,w,\hat{r}_{0},x)

(3)

This methodology allows us to systematically analyze the scaling behavior of model accuracy as a function of the total tokens allocated to the reasoning phase, forcing the model to further explore the problem space or verify its logic before committing to a computational solution.

3.3
Supervised Fine-Tuning and Reinforcement Learning

Following prior work that studied CI-based reasoning with LLMs
(
7
;
5
)
, the training process is divided into two main phases. The first phase uses supervised fine-tuning (SFT) as a cold start to help the model understand the basic structure of multi-turn CI tasks.
In the second phase, reinforcement learning (RL) is used to further optimize the model through multi-turn interaction with the CI. During this stage, the model learns by writing code and interacting with a real CI environment over several turns. This enables the model to delegate compute-intensive operations, such as arithmetic and recursive computations, to the CI while focusing on higher-level reasoning before producing a final solution.

Supervised Fine-Tuning.

In cold-start SFT for multi-turn CI interactions, the model is typically trained on a curated dataset denoted as
\mathcal{D}^{\text{SFT}}=\{(x_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{i},\mathbf{o}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
N
represents the total number of training samples. Each individual sample
X_{i}
is a tuple consisting of a natural language instruction
x_{i}
, a gold-standard response
\mathbf{y}_{i}
, the corresponding code snippets
\mathbf{c}_{i}
, and the resulting execution outputs
\mathbf{o}_{i}
.
To ensure the model focuses its learning capacity on generative tasks, specifically, the text responses and executable code. The training objective utilizes a masked loss function.
For a given training sample
i
, the SFT loss over a sequence of
J
tokens is formulated as:

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{SFT}}=-\frac{1}{\sum_{j}\delta_{i,j}}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{i,j}\log P_{\theta}(X_{i,j}|X_{i,<j})

(4)

In this objective, a delta function
\delta_{i,j}
serves as a binary mask, where
\delta_{i,j}=1
if the token
X_{i,j}
belongs to the set of target outputs
\{\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{i}\}
, and
\delta_{i,j}=0
otherwise. This approach effectively isolates the gradient updates to the model’s primary generative tasks.

Reinforcement Learning.

Following the ReTool framework
(
7
)
, the RL pipeline employs the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) paradigm
(
28
)
. In this specific implementation, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
29
)
is utilized to refine the policy.
The RL training dataset, denoted as
\mathcal{D}^{\text{RL}}
, comprises pairs of problem (
x
) and their corresponding ground-truth answers (
a
). The optimization objective for the policy update is defined as:

\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{GRPO}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}\frac{1}{|\hat{y}_{g}|}\sum_{j=1}^{|\hat{y}_{g}|}\left\{\min\left[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{g,j}\mid x,\hat{y}_{g,<j};\text{CI})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\hat{y}_{g,j}\mid x,\hat{y}_{g,<j};\text{CI})}\hat{A}_{g,j},\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{g,j}\mid x,\hat{y}_{g,<j};\text{CI})}{\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(\hat{y}_{g,j}\mid x,\hat{y}_{g,<j};\text{CI})},1-\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon\right)\hat{A}_{g,j}\right]\right\}

(5)

In this formulation, the objective maximizes the lower bound of the clipped surrogate advantage. The probability ratio between the current policy
\pi_{\theta}
and the behavior policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
is constrained by the hyperparameter
\varepsilon
to ensure stable updates, while the advantage estimator
\hat{A}_{g,j}
guides the policy toward sequences that yield higher relative rewards within the CI environment.

In alignment with the ReTool framework, the reward signal is formulated as a binary outcome contingent on the accuracy of the final derived answer. This sparse reward mechanism incentivizes the model to converge on the correct solution via valid reasoning and code execution. Formally, the reward function
R(a,\hat{a})
is defined such that
R(a,\hat{a})=1
if the predicted final answer
\hat{a}
matches the ground-truth label
a
, and
-1
otherwise.
Within the GRPO framework, the advantage
\hat{A}_{g,j}
for a given output sequence is determined by evaluating its reward relative to the mean performance of a group of
G
sampled outputs for the same query. Specifically, for each trajectory
o_{g}
within a sampled group
\{o_{1},o_{2},\dots,o_{G}\}
, the advantage is computed as the z-score of the group’s reward distribution
R(x,\hat{y}_{g})
. This relative advantage estimation utilizes the group’s collective performance as a dynamic baseline, eliminating the need for a separate value function (critic) and thereby streamlining the RL process for CI-based tasks.

4.2
Intrinsic Property

Motivation.

Prior work demonstrates that high performing models exhibit key cognitive behaviors, including verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, and backward chaining, which are largely absent in weaker models
(
8
)
. These behaviors enable models to validate intermediate results, revise unsuccessful reasoning paths, decompose complex problems into manageable subproblems, and reason backward from desired outcomes. Motivated by these findings, we investigate whether such behaviors also emerge in CI-based reasoning and the extent to which they contribute to solving challenging tasks.

Setup.

Following
Section

4.1
, we employ the same set of models and benchmark for our empirical analysis.
To investigate internal reasoning processes, we adopt an LLM-as-a-Judge framework, extending the approach of
8
.
However, preliminary experiments reveal that the original prompts, designed to evaluate cognitive behavior in natural language reasoning, do not consistently yield reliable outcomes in code-oriented reasoning contexts.
We therefore refine the probing prompt to better suit scenarios involving reasoning with a code interpreter (see
Appendix

B
for further details).
For the Oracle LLM, we use Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Instruct
(
41
)
, which has demonstrated strong performance on mathematical problem-solving and coding tasks.

Result and Discussion.

Figure

3
shows that Qwen3 exhibits the highest frequency across all cognitive behavior types. In particular, verification, subgoal setting, and backward chaining appear in at least 96% of its responses. Backtracking is less frequent in Qwen3 (44%), though still markedly higher than in Qwen2.5 and Llama3.1 (9% and 7%, respectively).
This pattern contrasts with typical natural language reasoning, where backtracking is most prevalent. It is the least frequent behavior in reasoning with CI. In contrast, subgoal setting dominates in our settings, where planning is implicitly encouraged, thereby increasing its prevalence.
The overall trend is consistent with
8
. These findings suggest that such cognitive behaviors are also foundational for solving challenging problems in reasoning with CI, as reflected in downstream performance. Building on this insight, we incorporate these behaviors into an existing state-of-the-art training framework, designed for reasoning with CI, as detailed in

5.1
Test-Time Scaling with Crucial Tokens.

Motivation.

Sequential test-time scaling for NL reasoning has been extensively studied across diverse settings
(
22
;
40
;
47
)
.
However, its effectiveness and limitations in CI-based reasoning remain under-investigated.
To address this, we conduct an empirical study of scaling behavior under
crucial token

Setup.

For test-time scaling in reasoning with CI, we follow the implementation introduced in
Section

3.2

We adopt SymBench as the evaluation benchmark to analyze downstream scaling behavior across six reasoning capabilities.

To further examine the variance across model families, we incorporate DeepSeek-R1

Figure

The scaling effect is particularly pronounced and consistent in mathematical reasoning, order reasoning, and optimization tasks, where the

Despite these task-dependent variations, the overall results suggest that the

These findings are partially consistent with

Furthermore, our results indicate that, in certain capabilities such as spatial reasoning, logical reasoning, and search, simply appending

6
Analysis

6.1
Investigating the Lack of Performance Improvements in Qwen3-8B

Although Qwen3-8B was explicitly primed with the desired intrinsic property, we verify that
CoBe
-trained models consistently exhibit the intended behaviors during downstream evaluation (see
Appendix

F
). We observe no downstream performance improvement.
We hypothesize that this phenomenon may be confounded by two factors: (1) discrepancies in response-length distributions introduced during SFT, and (2) the insufficient preservation of the extrinsic property.
Regarding response length, the left panel of
Figure

5
shows that outputs produced by both ReTool-SFT and ReTool-SFT with
CoBe
are substantially shorter than the corresponding zero-shot generations. While response length itself may not directly determine downstream accuracy, such a substantial distributional shift may negatively affect model behavior. This observation is consistent with the degraded AIME performance of both SFT variants reported in
Table

2
.

We further assess the relationship between performance and the extrinsic property through an analysis of
crucial token
frequency. As illustrated in the right panel of
Figure

5
, ReTool-RL exhibits the higher length-normalized frequency of
crucial tokens
on both AIME24 and AIME25, aligning with its superior performance. By comparison, ReTool-RL with
CoBe
yields lower
crucial token
frequencies and correspondingly weaker results.
Overall, this finding highlights that maintaining both intrinsic and extrinsic properties is also important for preserving strong reasoning performance.

To further disentangle these factors, we conduct an additional empirical study using Qwen3-8B-Base instead of the post-trained Qwen3-8B model. We apply both ReTool-SFT and ReTool-SFT with

These results suggest that
CoBe
can be beneficial when applied to the base model, but its effectiveness may vary for models that have already undergone extensive post-training. Further investigation is therefore required to understand how cognitive-behavior augmentation interacts with existing model capabilities.

6.2
The Impact of Cognitive Behaviors on Reasoning With CI Beyond Task Performance

Prior work
(
36
)
has observed that incorrect reasoning trajectories are often associated with excessively long generations, suggesting that LMs may overthink when faced with difficult problems. Motivated by this insight, we investigate whether incorporating
CoBe
into the training pipeline can encourage more structured reasoning and thereby mitigate inefficient generation behaviors.

Figure

6
shows that integrating
CoBe
consistently reduces response lengths for incorrect samples across both the SFT and RL stages. This trend indicates that
CoBe
enables the model to reason more efficiently, particularly in cases where the model ultimately fails to reach the correct solution.

We further compare the training dynamics of ReTool-RL with and without
CoBe
under identical training settings. The
CoBe
-enhanced variant outperforms the standard ReTool-RL baseline in 8 out of 10 checkpoints on AIME24, while consistently generating shorter responses.
In contrast, standard ReTool-RL exhibits a bias towards longer responses with limited accuracy improvements, reflecting the length bias identified by
21
.
These results suggest that extending length alone is insufficient for reasoning with a code interpreter; instead, the quality and structure of intermediate reasoning behaviors are also critical.

7
Conclusion

Although reasoning with code interpreter is increasingly adopted, the underlying extrinsic
crucial tokens
and intrinsic cognitive behaviors that facilitate effective reasoning remain largely underexplored. To address this, we conduct a systematic study of how these properties manifest across multiple LLMs, focusing on
crucial tokens
and cognitive behaviors such as verification, backtracking, subgoal setting, and backward chaining.
Our results show that both properties can be leveraged to improve downstream performance in certain conditions. In particular,
crucial token
intervention, especially with
let
, enables effective test-time scaling, while incorporating code-specific cognitive behaviors into training improves performance during both supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning in most cases. Beyond accuracy gains, these behaviors also improve token efficiency and reduce overthinking in incorrect responses, highlighting the importance of intrinsic behavior rather than simply extending generation length. At the same time, the performance degradation observed in a certain specialized model suggests that the effectiveness of behavioral augmentation depends on its interaction with existing model capabilities, motivating further investigation into factors that constrain gains in advanced reasoning models.

Limitations and Potential Risks

Although this work provides a systematic characterization of extrinsic and intrinsic properties in code-interpreter-based reasoning, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, both the evaluation process and data synthesis pipeline rely on high-capacity Oracle LLMs, which may introduce model-specific biases and variability in generated outputs. Moreover, our interventions investigate these properties independently: training primarily targets intrinsic behaviors (
CoBe
), whereas test-time scaling focuses on
crucial tokens
associated with extrinsic properties. As demonstrated in our analysis of Qwen3-8B, reductions in
crucial token
frequency can adversely affect task performance even when intrinsic reasoning behaviors are maintained. Consequently, this study does not provide a unified framework that jointly leverages both intrinsic and extrinsic properties.
Second, the observed findings may not readily generalize to broader tool-augmented agent settings, such as web browsing, multi-tool orchestration, or open-ended API interactions. In addition, our conclusions are derived from a specific set of publicly available datasets and evaluation frameworks, which may limit their applicability to other domains and tasks.
Finally, this study involves the execution of LLM-generated code as part of the evaluation process. While all executions are performed within controlled sandbox environments and are unlikely to produce harmful effects, the possibility of unintended or unanticipated behaviors arising from model-generated code cannot be entirely eliminated.

Appendix D
Training Configurations and Computational Cost

All experiments were conducted using the training scripts provided in the official repository
4
4

4

https://github.com/verl-project/verl-recipe/tree/main/retool
.
For supervised fine-tuning, models were trained for six epochs with a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of
10^{-6}
. The maximum sequence length was set to 16,384 tokens, and Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP) was employed to enable efficient distributed training.
For reinforcement learning, we adopted the GRPO algorithm with an actor learning rate of
10^{-6}
. The training was performed using a batch size of 64. To support long-chain-of-thought reasoning, the maximum prompt length and response length were set to 2,048 and 16,384 tokens, respectively. During rollout, 16 responses were sampled for each prompt. No KL-divergence regularization term was applied throughout training.
The total computational expenditure, including both training and evaluation, amounted to approximately 1,608 GPU hours on NVIDIA A100 (80GB) GPUs.
