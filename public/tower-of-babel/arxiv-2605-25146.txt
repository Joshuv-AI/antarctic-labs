Kernel Embedding for Operator-Valued Measures and Its Application to Quantum Tomography
Philipp N. Mayer, Ho Yun

Abstract
This paper introduces the Quantum Covariance Embedding, which embeds Positive Operator-Valued Measures into a tensor product of a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space and the quantum state space via a tensorized Bochner integral. This construction induces the Quantum Maximum Discrepancy that metrizes the space of quantum measurements. Applying this framework to Quantum State Tomography, we reformulate density estimation as a tensorized kernel regression, enabling optimal inference without the basis-dependent sparsity constraints that restrict existing methods. We develop a unified geometric design theory for quantum Gram superoperators, establishing that Unitary Designs are strictly E-optimal experimental designs and thus statistically superior to Pauli observables. For general structure-free estimation, we derive the exact minimax lower bound and prove that our tensorized estimators achieve this optimal rate. Furthermore, we introduce the QUAntum Regression with Kernels (QUARK) estimator to accommodate the spectral geometry of physical implementations, deriving central limit theorem and concentration inequalities. To facilitate practical estimation, we establish the exactness of trace-preserving projections and demonstrate efficient estimation under mutually unbiased bases via the fast Walsh-Hadamard transform.

Introduction

Quantum technology has matured from theoretical curiosity to experimental reality, a shift underscored by the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics. As quantum platforms scale, verifying and characterizing these systems has emerged as a central challenge. Fundamentally, this is a statistical problem: latent parameters must be inferred through macroscopic evidence, and one must rigorously quantify the associated uncertainties
[
24
,
17
,
22
]
. To address this, this paper develops the theory of kernel embeddings for quantum mechanics. By mapping the measurement process itself into an infinite-dimensional feature space, we provide a unified methodology for quantum state estimation that bridges nonparametric statistics with quantum physics.

Whereas classical statistics views data as samples directly generated from a probability distribution, the probability law driving the data-generating mechanism in quantum mechanics is determined by the interaction of a quantum state with a physical apparatus
[
39
,
18
,
46
]
. Mathematically, the physical apparatus is modeled by an
operator-valued
Borel measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}
that acts upon an unobservable state of the system
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. The physical measurement process fuses these two objects together via the Born rule, reducing the operator-valued measure into a classical, scalar probability distribution
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
over the measurement outcomes
[
37
,
57
]
. The statistician is then presented with a finite set of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations drawn from
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
. If the state is known and the apparatus is uncertain, the task is Quantum Detector Tomography. On the other hand, if the apparatus is known and the state is uncertain, the task is Quantum State Tomography (QST).

The problem of Quantum State Tomography has attracted a vast literature, establishing minimax estimation rates
[
13
,
61
]
, exploring compressed sensing for low-rank states
[
33
,
34
,
27
]
, and developing estimators under a range of structural assumptions
[

,

,
34
,
1
,

,

,

,
15
,
29
]
. In particular, the predominant framework centers on tensorized Pauli observables
[
1
,
15
,
29
]
, where its sharpest statistical guarantees hold under sparsity in the Pauli basis. Yet, this restriction carries a severe fundamental consequence: by the Gottesman-Knill theorem
[
31
]
, quantum states that are sparse in the Pauli basis admit efficient simulation also on classical computers, offering no true quantum advantage. Consequently, existing sparsity-based estimators may suffer from substantial bias precisely in the regimes where quantum computation is genuinely powerful. Furthermore, least-squares approaches treat measurement outcomes as abstract categorical labels, ignoring the underlying spectral geometry of the physical hardware
[

This paper takes a different route based on the theory of kernel embeddings for scalar probability measures

Yet, extending classical kernel methods to quantum mechanics is non-trivial, as standard arguments for scalar measures do not readily apply to the non-commutative nature of the Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM) that governs the measurement process. To resolve this, our central construction introduces the
Quantum Covariance Embedding (QCE)

A profound consequence of this embedding is the derivation of the
Quantum Maximum Discrepancy (QMD)
. Extending the Maximum Discrepancy of kernel methods
[

,
56
,
5
]
to the operator level, the QMD establishes a novel metrization on the space of POVMs. This provides a statistical tool to quantify the distance between distinct quantum measurement processes — or between a theoretical apparatus and its finite-sample empirical counterpart — independent of the underlying quantum state.
Applied to QST, this embedding recasts tomography as a kernel regression. However, because our estimand is a density operator rather than a vector, this regression is inherently
tensorized
. This lifts the problem to a tensor-operator equation, aware of the spectral geometry of the measurement design, enabling estimation without imposing specific classical simulability assumptions such as Pauli sparsity.
By bridging quantum theory with tensorized kernel regression, this paper makes three primary contributions in the context of QST:

1.

Unified Design Theory:
We develop a basis-independent formulation for QST, characterizing statistical identifiability through the design tensor. By analyzing the action of quantum Gram superoperators via representation theory
[
25
]
, we establish that
Unitary Designs
(including Mutually Unbiased Bases
[
43
]
), which serve as the quantum analogue of an orthogonal design matrix, are E-optimal experimental designs
[
49
]
. We quantify the fundamental loss of information caused by eigenvalue multiplicities via an explicit damping coefficient, proving analytically why entangled, isotropic measurements are statistically superior to local measurements such as tensorized Pauli observables.

2.

Minimax Optimality:
Moving away from low-rank and basis-sparse assumptions, we address the fundamental limit of general density estimation. Under an identifiable design with
n
observables and
r
independent shots per observable, we derive the exact minimax lower bound for the squared-error risk, establishing that the information-theoretic limit scales as
O(q^{2}/(nr))
for a state space of dimension
q
. We further prove that our tensorized estimators achieve this optimal parametric rate in the dense regime where classical sparsity-based methods fail.

3.

Kernel Regression:
We introduce the
QUAntum Regression with Kernels (QUARK)
estimator, which encodes the metric-aware topology between spectra to accommodate the geometric reality of physical implementations. With a Dirac 0–1 kernel, QUARK natively recovers the standard unconstrained least-squares estimator; with a generic smooth kernel, it enforces spectral smoothness, yielding an estimator analogous to operator-level ridge regression. We establish a Quantum Representer Theorem that bounds the Schatten-norm discrepancy of kernel embeddings, and we derive a formal Central Limit Theorem, along with Bennett-type concentration inequalities.

To ensure our theoretical estimators are practically realizable, we provide algorithmic implementations. We prove that applying the computationally efficient Trace-Preserving Projection algorithm
[
54
]
to our relaxed estimator yields the exact, globally optimal solution to the fully constrained physical problem. Additionally, we demonstrate how the least-squares estimate under Mutually Unbiased Bases can be computed in
O(q\log q)
time via fast Walsh-Hadamard transforms
[
26
]
, in contrast to Pauli tomography
O(q^{2})
.

2.2
Quantum Measurements

To model the probabilistic nature of microscopic phenomena, quantum theory describes the states and observables of a system through operator algebra. We review the minimal axiomatic framework of quantum mechanics required to develop our statistical theory; for more extensive treatments, we refer to
[
39
,
37
,
57
,
18
,
46
]
.

In (non-relativistic) quantum mechanics, the state space of a physical system is modeled over a complex separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{Q}
, which we call the
Quantum Hilbert Space
(QHS). A
pure state
corresponds to a one-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{Q}
, uniquely represented by a rank-one orthogonal projection
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\psi\psi^{*}
, where
\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
is a unit vector.
To account for mixtures of pure states, this framework generalizes to
density operators
, defined as non-negative definite, unit-trace operators
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
. Geometrically, the space of density operators
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
forms a closed convex set whose extreme points are precisely the pure states
[
39
]
. Consequently, if
\boldsymbol{\rho}
has a rank strictly greater than one, it cannot be an extreme point and is termed a
mixed state
. Notable examples of the underlying QHS include:

•

\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
for a
q
-level system, called a qudit. It is called a qubit (e.g., the spin of an electron) when
q=2
. Throughout the work, we assume
q\geq 2
for non-triviality.

•

\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
for wavefunctions
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
(e.g., the spatial wavefunction of a massive particle on a real line).

Crucially, the quantum state itself resides in the microscopic regime and is not directly observable. Information can only be extracted through its interaction with a macroscopic apparatus, which constitutes the physical definition of measurement.
An
observable
is a physical quantity measured by an experimental apparatus, associated with a self-adjoint operator
\mathbf{O}
on
\mathcal{Q}
.
As a motivating example, consider an observable
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{C}^{q\times q}
for the
q
-level system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
. Assuming a non-degenerate spectrum,
\mathbf{O}
admits the spectral decomposition:

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\lambda_{k}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k},\quad\text{where}\quad\sigma(\mathbf{O})=\{\lambda_{k}:k=1,\cdots,q\}\subset\mathbb{R},\quad\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}=\psi_{k}\psi_{k}^{*}.

The measurement outcome
Y
is a random variable taking values in the spectrum
\sigma(\mathbf{O})
. The
Born rule
dictates that its probability mass, conditioned on the apparatus
\mathbf{O}
and the input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, is governed by the trace:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\{\lambda_{k}\}):=\mathbb{P}(Y=\lambda_{k}|\boldsymbol{\rho})={\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\rho}\psi_{k}},{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}\right].

This formulation guarantees a legitimate probability law since
\sum_{k=1}^{q}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\{\lambda_{k}\})=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]=1
. It also follows that the conditional expectation of the measurement outcome is
\mathbb{E}(Y|\boldsymbol{\rho})=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{O}\right]
. Throughout the work, all probabilistic statements regarding
Y
are
conditional
on the input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.
Note that the possible values of the measurement outcome are solely determined by the apparatus
\mathbf{O}
, while the density operator
\boldsymbol{\rho}
determines the probability mass. To decouple the input state from the apparatus, we consider the operator-valued measure

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}:\mathscr{B}(\sigma(\mathbf{O}))\to\mathbb{C}^{q\times q},

that acts on the density
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
via
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(B)=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}(B)\right]
for any Borel set
B\subseteq\sigma(\mathbf{O})
. Mathematically, this measure maps subsets of the spectrum to orthogonal projections onto the corresponding eigenspaces.

While this logic extends naturally to compact operators as they possess discrete spectra, physical observables such as position and momentum are represented by operators with continuous spectra. Consequently, the notion of a
Projection-Valued Measure
(PVM) serves as a necessary generalization, as evaluating an atomless measure at individual points trivially yields zero
[
37
]
. A PVM is a Borel measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}:\mathscr{B}(X)\to\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})
over a locally compact Hausdorff (LCH) space
X
, satisfying
\boldsymbol{\nu}(X)=\mathbf{I}_{\mathcal{Q}}
and the idempotence property
\boldsymbol{\nu}(B)^{2}=\boldsymbol{\nu}(B)
for any Borel set
B
. Idempotency implies orthogonality, meaning
\boldsymbol{\nu}(B)\boldsymbol{\nu}(B^{\prime})=\mathbf{0}
whenever
B\cap B^{\prime}=\emptyset
. By the
Spectral Theorem
, there is a one-to-one correspondence between self-adjoint observables
\mathbf{O}
and PVMs
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}
over
X=\sigma(\mathbf{O})\subseteq\mathbb{R}
in the following sense:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(B):=\mathbb{P}(Y\in B|\boldsymbol{\rho})=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}(B)\right],\quad B\in\mathscr{B}(\sigma(\mathbf{O})),\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})).

When there is no risk of confusion regarding the observable
\mathbf{O}
, we will simply denote the measure as
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and omit the superscript.

In practice, measurement processes are frequently subject to noise or involve interactions with a larger environmental system (See
Section

3.3
for concrete examples). This necessitates the following definition, which relaxes the idempotence requirement while preserving the validity of the induced probability distributions.

\boldsymbol{\nu}(\emptyset)=\mathbf{0}
and

2.

For all countable collections

of mutually disjoint Borel sets,

\boldsymbol{\nu}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}B_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\boldsymbol{\nu}(B_{k}),

where the convergence is with respect to the Strong Operator Topology (SOT)

We denote the space of POVMs by

Given a POVM

via:

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(B):=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nu}(B)\right],\quad B\in\mathscr{B}(X).

Additionally, for any

Note that

Given a POVM

2.3
Related Work

The POVM framework presents a largely unexplored opportunity for statistical generalization. While there is an abundant literature on kernel
mean
embeddings for scalar-valued probability measures
[
28
,
45
,
53
,
55
,
56
,
16
,
32
]
, a rigorous kernel embedding framework for operator-valued measures is virtually non-existent.
In contrast to a classical probability measure, a quantum state
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
is a unit-trace, non-negative definite operator, rendering it structurally analogous to a covariance matrix rather than a mean vector. Consequently, extending kernel methods to the quantum setting fundamentally requires
covariance embedding
proposed in
[
5
]
.

While
[
5
]
leverages quantum information theory tools,
that analysis assumes the underlying QHS itself possesses a reproducing kernel. Yet, canonical QHSs (such as
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C})
) generally lack this RKHS structure, and our work addresses a critical theoretical gap by formulating covariance embeddings that capture the non-commutative interplay between general operator-valued measures
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and density operators
\boldsymbol{\rho}
without requiring the QHS to be an RKHS.
Developing this operator-valued embedding theory is our primary focus. We apply this framework to Quantum State Tomography (QST), focusing on the statistical reconstruction of an unknown state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
given a known measurement apparatus
\boldsymbol{\nu}
. We select QST because this state-estimation problem naturally parallels classical statistical inference, distinguishing it from dual tasks like quantum detector tomography (estimating an unknown apparatus
\boldsymbol{\nu}
), which we leave for future work.

QST is an ill-posed inverse problem. Since individual quantum states are unobservable, reconstruction requires repeated measurements on an ensemble of identically prepared systems. Moreover, a single observable is mathematically insufficient: for
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
, the real affine dimension of
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
is
q^{2}-1
, yet one observable yields at most
q
distinct spectral values.
To resolve this dimensional deficit, the quantum physics literature has identified specific measurement schemes, such as Pauli measurements
[
46
,
15
]
and Mutually Unbiased Bases
[
43
]
. To reconstruct the state from these fixed designs, specialized statistical approaches have been developed, such as nuclear-norm penalized compressed sensing to promote low-rankness
[
33
]
, or Bayesian regression utilizing unitarily invariant priors
[
7
,
44
]
.
However, we caution against the application of standard classical high-dimensional regression techniques (such as LASSO) to this inverse problem. Because quantum mechanics is unitarily invariant, imposing
\ell_{1}
-parsimony on the coefficients of a fixed design (e.g., assuming the state is sparse in the Pauli basis
[
15
]
) may lack physical justification unless the specific environment inherently breaks that symmetry.
To provide a rigorous foundation free of basis-dependent artifacts, we devote
Section

4.1
to translating the physics concept of informational completeness into the formal statistical language of identifiability and design tensors (see
Proposition

4.3
). Furthermore,
Theorem

4.16
establishes the minimax lower bound applicable to any identifiable design, delineating the fundamental information-theoretic limits in its own right.

3.1
RKHS Framework

Definition 3.1

(RKHS)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a Hilbert space of
\mathbb{F}
-valued functions defined on a set
X
. A bivariate function
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{F}
is called a reproducing kernel for
\mathcal{R}
if:

1.

For any
x\in X
, the feature map
k_{x}:=K(\cdot,x):X\to\mathbb{F}
belongs to
\mathcal{R}
.

2.

For any
f\in\mathcal{R}
and
x\in X
, the reproducing property holds, i.e.,
f(x)=\langle f,k_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Such a Hilbert space is called a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS).

The Moore–Aronszajn theorem
[
3
]
establishes a one-to-one correspondence between positive definite kernels and RKHSs through
K(x,y)=k_{y}(x)={\left\langle{k_{y}},{k_{x}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}}
, justifying the notation
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(K)

[
47
,
41
]
.
The following theorem establishes the fundamental operator that will serve as our embedding by proving its existence and uniqueness via sesquilinear forms.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X
be an LCH space and
K
be a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel. Then, for any POVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
, there exists a unique bounded operator
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q})
satisfying

{\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(f\otimes\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}}=\int_{X}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x),\quad\forall f,g\in\mathcal{R}(K),\,\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}.

It holds that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq\sup_{x\in X}K(x,x)
. Additionally,
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q})
if and only if
\int_{X}K(x,x)\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})
. In this case, the trace-norm is given by:

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\int_{X}K(x,x)\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}.

The bounded operator in
Theorem

3.2
can be equivalently defined as the tensorized Bochner integral
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\int_{X}k_{x}k_{x}^{*}\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
, as discussed in
Section

A
.
Also, applying
Theorem

3.2
to
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathscr{P}(X)
guarantees the existence of
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K))
, given by

\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}:=\int_{X}k_{x}k_{x}^{*}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x)=\mathbb{E}[k_{Y}k_{Y}^{*}|\boldsymbol{\rho}],

(2)

which acts as an integral operator:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}f(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)\overline{k_{x}(Y)}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\int_{X}K(x,y)f(y)\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(y),\quad f\in\mathcal{R}(K),

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)}=\int_{X}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x)=\mathbb{E}[f(Y)\overline{g(Y)}|\boldsymbol{\rho}],\quad f,g\in\mathcal{R}(K),

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}=\int_{X}K(x,x)\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(x)=\mathbb{E}[K(Y,Y)|\boldsymbol{\rho}].

Analogous to classical kernel embedding
[
53
]
, a quantum covariance embedding (QCE) can be defined naturally as follows:

Definition 3.3

(Quantum Covariance Embedding)
.

Let
X
be LCH and
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{F}
be a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel. We define the
Quantum Covariance Embedding
(QCE) as the map:

\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})\,\mapsto\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}).

Furthermore, we define the
conditional QCE

given the density
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
as the map:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})\,\mapsto\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)).

(3)

It follows immediately that the conditional QCE is closed under convex combination of density operators: for
\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
and
t\in[0,1]
, we have:

\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}}}=(1-t)\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}}}+t\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}}},\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}_{t}:=(1-t)\boldsymbol{\rho}_{0}+t\boldsymbol{\rho}_{1}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})).

(4)

We also remark that when the PVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
is associated with a compact observable
\mathbf{O}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{k}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}(\mathcal{Q})
, then both QCEs reduce to

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k_{\lambda_{k}}k_{\lambda_{k}}^{*})\otimes\mathbf{\Pi}_{k},\quad\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k_{\lambda_{k}}k_{\lambda_{k}}^{*}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right],

where the convergence is with respect to the SOT due to Vigier’s theorem.
As a guiding example, we provide below a matrix represenation of QCEs for a finite-dimensional case:

Example 3.4

(Leveled Quanta)
.

Consider a qudit
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
and an observable
\mathbf{O}=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\lambda_{k}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
,
where
X=\{\lambda_{k}\}_{k=1}^{l}\subset\mathbb{R}
are distinct eigenvalues (
l<q
in case of multiplicities) and
\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}
are the associated eigenprojections. The corresponding PVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}
can be viewed as an
l\times q\times q
tensor with
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{kij}=[\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}]_{ij}
.
Let
K(x,y)=\mathds{1}(x=y)
be the 0–1 kernel so that the evaluation map
f\in\mathcal{R}(K)\cong\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{F}^{l}
is isometrically isomorphic, where
\mathbf{f}_{k}:=f(\lambda_{k})

[
47
]
. This induces an isomorphism for the tensor product space, equipped with the Frobenius inner product:
f\otimes\phi\in\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}\cong\phi\overline{\mathbf{f}}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times l}
.
Under this identification, the QCE acts as a column-wise projection on
\mathbb{F}^{q\times l}
:

\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}}:[\phi_{1}|\dots|\phi_{l}]\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times l}\,\mapsto\,[\mathbf{\Pi}_{1}\phi_{1}|\dots|\mathbf{\Pi}_{l}\phi_{l}]\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times l}.

To see this, note that for any
f,g\in\mathcal{R}(K)
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{F}^{q}
,

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}}(f\otimes\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\mathbf{f}_{k}\overline{\mathbf{g}_{k}}{\left\langle{\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{F}^{m}}=\int_{X}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x).

Similarly, one can show that the conditional QCE behaves as a diagonal matrix:

\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\operatorname{Diag}(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{1}\right],\dots,\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{l}\right])\in\mathbb{F}^{l\times l}\cong\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{R}(K)),\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q}),

of which the trace norm satisfies:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]=1<q=\sum_{k=1}^{l}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right]={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1},

consistent with
Theorem

3.2
. The operator norms satisfy:

{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq k\leq l}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right]\leq 1=\max_{\phi_{k}\in\mathbb{F}^{q}\backslash\{0\}}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\phi_{k}\|^{2}}{\sum_{k=1}^{l}\|\phi_{k}\|^{2}}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty},

where equality holds if and only if
\boldsymbol{\rho}
lies in the convex hull of the pure eigenstates of
\mathbf{O}
.

For a general kernel other than the 0–1 kernel, we refer to
Example

C.1
.

3.2
Characteristic Kernel

Unlike QST where the goal is to estimate
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, the focus of this subsection lies in identifying the measurement processes
\boldsymbol{\nu}
(or their marginalizations
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
).

Definition 3.5

(Characteristicity)
.

Let
X
be an LCH space and
\mathcal{Q}
be a QHS. A
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel
K
is called
characteristic
if the associated QCE is injective. That is, for any two POVMs
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
, we have
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}
.

The following theorem establishes that characteristicity in the quantum setting is equivalent to the usual definition for classical probability measures
[
28
,
56
]
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
X
be an LCH space and
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{F}
be a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel.

1.

Given POVMs
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
, their QCEs are identical if and only if their conditional QCEs are identical for every density operator
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\quad\iff\quad\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})).

2.

K
is characteristic for
\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
if and only if it is characteristic for
\mathscr{P}(X)
.

Theorems

3.6
and
3.2
naturally motivates us to metrize the space
\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
via the discrepancy of QCEs in the operator norm:

Definition 3.7

(Quantum Maximum Discrepancy)
.

Let
X
be LCH,
\mathcal{Q}
be a QHS, and
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{F}
be a
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel. For any POVMs
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
, we define their
Quantum Maximum Discrepancy
(QMD) as:

\operatorname{QMD}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}):={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

For a fixed density operator
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
, we define the
conditional QMD
as:

\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}):={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

It follows immediately from
Theorem

3.6
that the QMD induces a valid metric on
\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
if and only if
K
is characteristic. However, even in this case, the conditional QMD for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\rho}
may not be a metric in general, as
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
does not imply
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\boldsymbol{\nu}
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
X
be LCH and
\mathcal{Q}
be a QHS. Then, for any
\mathcal{C}_{0}
-kernel
K
and POVMs
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
, the following inequality holds:

\operatorname{QMD}_{K}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu})\geq\sup_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))}\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}).

Crucially, while the full QMD provides a universal upper bound, we focus on the conditional QMD for two primary reasons. First, it carries far greater statistical significance:
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
represent the actual physical probability distributions governing the observed macroscopic data given an input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. Second, from a technical standpoint, the full QMD presents analytical challenges as it operates over the larger tensor product space.
Furthermore, the conditional QMD provides a natural framework to address a practical question:
given two physical apparatuses represented by POVMs
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}
, which input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
maximizes the discrepancy between their induced probability measures?
Because the map
\boldsymbol{\rho}\mapsto\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
is affine (see (
4
)), its composition with the operator norm yields a convex function. Consequently, the supremum on the right-hand side of
Proposition

3.8
is necessarily achieved at an extreme point of the convex state space
\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))
, which corresponds precisely to a pure state
[
39
]
. Thus, there always exists a
maximally distinguishing
pure state that maximizes the conditional discrepancy, i.e.,

\displaystyle\exists\,\phi\in\mathcal{Q}\quad\text{such that}\quad\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{Q}}=1,\quad\phi\phi^{*}\in\argmax\{\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\nu}):\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{Q}))\}.

Remark 3.9
.

For later applications in quantum state tomography, we remark that the conditional QMD can be generalized to any
s
-Schatten norm with
s\in[1,\infty)
instead of the operator norm, since
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}},\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K))
due to (
3
). Hence, if another norm is more convenient for statistical inference, one may substitute the operator norm accordingly, e.g., adopting the Hilbert-Schmidt norm with
s=2
in
Theorem

4.18
. Indeed, because any norm is a convex function, the guarantee that there always exists a maximally distinguishing pure state remains valid under this generalization.

3.2.1
Pauli Measurements for Qubits

A fundamental orthogonal basis for the space of Hermitian matrices on a single qubit
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
is formed by the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}_{2}
and the three Pauli matrices
[
46
]
:

\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\vphantom{-1}\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\imath\\
\imath&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}.

These matrices are traceless, unitary, and involutory (
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}^{2}=\mathbf{I}_{2}
), with spectra
X=\{\pm 1\}
.
Consequently, any unit-trace Hermitian matrix admits a unique decomposition known as the
Bloch sphere representation
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+a_{x}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x}+a_{y}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{y}+a_{z}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}),\quad\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}.

(5)

This establishes a correspondence between the unit-trace Hermitian matrix and a real vector
\mathbf{a}=(a_{x},a_{y},a_{z})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
, called the
Bloch vector
. Since the eigenvalues of
\boldsymbol{\rho}
are
\lambda_{\pm}=(1\pm\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}})/2
, the n.n.d. condition for the density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2})
restricts the Bloch vector
\mathbf{a}
to the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{3}

[
51
]
. Furthermore, it holds that:

\displaystyle(\mathbf{a}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})(\mathbf{b}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})=(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})\mathbf{I}_{2}+\imath(\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b})\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}},\quad\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

thus
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2})
represents a pure state if and only if
1=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}^{2}\right]=(1+\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2})/2
, i.e.,
\mathbf{a}
lies on the surface
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. In short, the norm of the Bloch vector
\mathbf{a}
for any density matrix satisfies
\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\leq 1
with equality if and only if it represents a pure state. In particular, the maximally mixed state
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\mathbf{I}_{2}/2
corresponds to the origin
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
.

We now demonstrate a decoupling phenomenon specific to single qubits (
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
). While the absolute magnitude of the conditional QMD is linked to the chosen kernel geometry, the maximally distinguishing pure states are intrinsic to the Pauli measurements and independent of the kernel choice. This invariance arises due to the binary nature of their spectra. Because the measurement outcomes are strictly two-dimensional, the optimal input states that maximize the statistical discrepancy are governed solely by the algebraic difference of the Pauli observables themselves:

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
,
X=\{\pm 1\}
, and
K:X\times X\to\mathbb{C}
be a kernel with associated Gram matrix
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2}
. For any distinct pair of Pauli indices
i\neq j\in\{x,y,z\}
, the conditional QMD between the associated PVMs
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j}
is bounded by:

\displaystyle\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{j})=\frac{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}}{2}|\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})\right]|\leq\frac{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}}{\sqrt{2}}.

Equality holds (i.e., the state is maximally distinguishing) if and only if
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is either of the two pure states:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}_{2}\pm\frac{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2}).

Remark 3.11

(Indistinguishability and the Pauli-Z matrix)
.

Theorem

3.10
implies that the probability measures induced by Pauli matrices
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j}
are identical if and only if the Bloch vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{B}^{3}
of the input state is orthogonal to the difference vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{j})\right]={\left\langle{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{e}_{i}-\mathbf{e}_{j}}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}},\quad\boldsymbol{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}).

Furthermore, the appearance of the Pauli-Z matrix arises naturally from the binary nature of the spectra. In this context,
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}=\operatorname{Diag}(1,-1)
does not act on the quantum state, but rather computes the contrast
|f(+1)|^{2}-|f(-1)|^{2}
, thus capturing how effectively the kernel discriminates between the two possible measurement outcomes.

3.3
Noise Quantification of Quantum Measurements

As discussed in
Section

2.2
, physical measurement devices frequently deviate from idealized PVMs due to finite detector efficiency, thermal fluctuations, or environmental coupling. Consequently, the POVM framework is necessary to model the statistical corruption inherent in realistic apparatuses
[
11
,
10
]
. To illustrate the utility of the Quantum Maximum Discrepancy (QMD), we demonstrate how this metric quantifies classical bit-flip noise in qubit systems. An extension of this analysis to discrete modular errors in general qudit systems
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
is provided in
Section

C.1
.

3.3.1
Bit-flip Noise in Qubits

Let
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
be an observable in a qubit system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Without loss of generality, we assume the spectrum is
X=\sigma(\mathbf{O})=\{\pm 1\}
(e.g., a Pauli matrix), or equivalently,
\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
for some unit vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The eigenprojections associated with the eigenvalues
\pm 1
are given by
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\pm}=(\mathbf{I}_{2}\pm\mathbf{u}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})/2
.
Consider a scenario where the measurement outcome is subject to a symmetric bit-flip error, a standard noise model in quantum information
[
46
]
. Let
Y
denote the ideal measurement outcome for an input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2})
, and let
\varepsilon
be an independent multiplicative noise variable. The observed outcome is given by:

\displaystyle Y_{\eta}=Y\cdot\varepsilon,\quad\text{where }\quad Y=\begin{cases}+1&\text{w.p. }\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{+}\right]\\
-1&\text{w.p. }\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{-}\right]\end{cases},\quad\varepsilon=\begin{cases}+1&\text{w.p. }1-\eta\\
-1&\text{w.p. }\eta\end{cases}.

Here,
\eta\in[0,1/2]
represents the error rate (or flip probability). We restrict our attention to this regime because an error rate
\eta>1/2
merely corresponds to re-labeling the outcomes. The boundary case
\eta=1/2
represents a
completely
uninformative measurement: the observed outcome
Y_{1/2}
follows a uniform distribution over
\{\pm 1\}
regardless of the input state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, rendering any statistical inference on the underlying state fundamentally impossible.
The POVM governing the noisy outcome
Y_{\eta}
is constructed by mixing the original projections:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O},\eta}=\delta_{+1}[(1-\eta)\mathbf{\Pi}_{+}+\eta\mathbf{\Pi}_{-}]+\delta_{-1}[\eta\mathbf{\Pi}_{+}+(1-\eta)\mathbf{\Pi}_{-}].

Crucially, it is
not
a PVM as it violates the idempotence property. Furthermore, this noise process dampens the effective observable, defined as the first moment of the POVM:

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{\eta}:=\int_{X}x\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O},\eta}(x)=[(1-\eta)\mathbf{\Pi}_{+}+\eta\mathbf{\Pi}_{-}]-[\eta\mathbf{\Pi}_{+}+(1-\eta)\mathbf{\Pi}_{-}]=(1-2\eta)\mathbf{O}.

While
\mathbf{O}_{\eta}
shares the same eigenvectors as
\mathbf{O}
, its spectrum is attenuated by a factor of
|1-2\eta|
. Hence, the POVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O},\eta}
is distinct from the spectral measure of
\mathbf{O}_{\eta}
(which is a PVM).

Proposition 3.12
.

Given an observable
\mathbf{O}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
with the spectrum
X=\sigma(\mathbf{O})=\{\pm 1\}
, the conditional QMD between the ideal PVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}}
and the noisy POVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O},\eta}
with error rate
\eta\in[0,1]
is bounded by

\displaystyle\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O}},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{O},\eta})=\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}|\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{O}\right]|\leq\eta{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

Moreover, the maximally distinguishing states correspond to the eigenprojections
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\pm}
of
\mathbf{O}
.

The theorem below unifies
Theorems

3.10
and
3.12
:

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
\mathbf{O},\tilde{\mathbf{O}}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
be two observables with spectrum
X=\{\pm 1\}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{O}=\mathbf{u}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{O}}=\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
for some
\mathbf{u},\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\eta}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{\nu}}_{\tilde{\eta}}
denote the corresponding noisy POVMs subject to error rates
\eta,\tilde{\eta}\in[0,1]
, respectively.
Define
\mathbf{v}:=(1-2\eta)\mathbf{u}-(1-2\tilde{\eta})\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The conditional QMD between these measurements is given by:

\displaystyle\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\eta},\boldsymbol{\tilde{\nu}}_{\tilde{\eta}})

\displaystyle={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\left|\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\frac{(1-2\eta)\mathbf{O}-(1-2\tilde{\eta})\tilde{\mathbf{O}}}{2}\right]\right|

(6)

\displaystyle\leq{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\frac{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}{2},

provided
\mathbf{v}\neq\mathbf{0}
. In this case, the maximally distinguishing pure states are given by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{I}_{2}\pm\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{v}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\right).

Note that
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{0}
if and only if two noisy measurement processes are statistically identical (
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{\nu}}_{\tilde{\eta}}
). Consequently, the QMD is identically zero and the bound vanishes, meaning no quantum state is capable of distinguishing between the two apparatuses.

Remark 3.14

(Discrepancy vs. Uncertainty)
.

Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle characterizes the physical consequences of measurement ordering (non-commutativity), quantified by the Lie bracket
[\mathbf{O},\tilde{\mathbf{O}}]:=\mathbf{O}\tilde{\mathbf{O}}-\tilde{\mathbf{O}}\mathbf{O}=2\imath(\mathbf{u}\times\tilde{\mathbf{u}})\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

[
57
]
. In contrast, statistical inference for QST rather relies on distinguishability: the capacity to differentiate data-generating processes, which our QMD explicitly captures. To see that these are profoundly different notions, consider the ideal setting without apparatus error (
\eta=\tilde{\eta}=0
). Let
\theta=\angle(\mathbf{u},\tilde{\mathbf{u}})
denote the angle between the unit Bloch vectors. The statistical discrepancy and the quantum uncertainty metrics diverge strictly as a function of this angle:

Conditional QMD Bound

\displaystyle\propto\|\mathbf{u}-\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}=2|\sin(\theta/2)|,

Uncertainty Bound

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2})}\left|\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}[\mathbf{O},\tilde{\mathbf{O}}]\right]\right|=2\|\mathbf{u}\times\tilde{\mathbf{u}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}=2|\sin(\theta)|.

While both quantities vanish for identical observables (
\theta=0
), their behaviors diverge elsewhere. Uncertainty peaks at
\theta=\pi/2
(e.g., Pauli-X vs. Pauli-Z), whereas the QMD peaks for anti-parallel observables (
\theta=\pi
). Because anti-parallel observables strictly commute (
[\mathbf{O},\tilde{\mathbf{O}}]=\mathbf{0}
) yet yield perfectly disjoint measurement outcomes, this demonstrates that statistical discrepancy is maximized precisely where quantum uncertainty vanishes.

4
Quantum State Tomography

Building upon the kernel embedding framework in
Section

3
, we now address the statistical inverse problem of estimating an unknown density operator from measurements performed on an ensemble of identically prepared quantum systems - a process known as
Quantum State Tomography
(QST).
For the remainder of this work, we restrict our attention to finite-dimensional QHSs
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
with
q\geq 2
. To streamline our statistical derivations, we adopt the following finite-dimensional conventions. The space of linear operators on
\mathcal{Q}
(which coincides across all Schatten classes
\mathcal{B}_{p}
in finite dimensions) is simply the space of
q\times q
matrices, denoted
\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
. We denote the real vector space of self-adjoint matrices (symmetric if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
, Hermitian if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
) by
\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
. We further denote its traceless linear subspace by
\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
, and the affine hyperplane of unit-trace matrices by
\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
. The convex cone of n.n.d. matrices is denoted
\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q}\subsetneq\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
. Consequently, the quantum state space is precisely
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})=\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q}\cap\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
.
Finally, we employ
fraktur
fonts (e.g.,
\mathfrak{D},\mathfrak{H}
) to denote operators acting on matrix spaces, commonly called
superoperators

[
52
]
.

4.1
Measurement Design

Our goal is to estimate the unknown density matrix. Since a single observable is mathematically insufficient for full reconstruction, we consider a design consisting of a collection of
n
self-adjoint observables
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
.
For each observable
\mathbf{O}_{i}
, let its spectral decomposition and associated PVM be given by:

\mathbf{O}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\lambda_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik},\quad\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\delta_{\lambda_{ik}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik},

where
\{\lambda_{ik}\}_{k=1}^{q_{i}}
are distinct eigenvalues and
\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}
are the corresponding eigenprojections with rank (multiplicity)
m_{ik}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right]\in\mathbb{N}
. Throughout, we assume
q_{i}\geq 2
(otherwise
\mathbf{\Pi}_{i1}=\mathbf{I}_{q}
, yielding no information). We note the following orthogonality and trace properties:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik^{\prime}}\right]=m_{ik}\mathds{1}(k=k^{\prime}),\quad\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{q_{i^{\prime}}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}\right]=q.

To handle eigenvalue multiplicities, we define the index map
k_{i}:\{1,\dots,q\}\to\{1,\dots,q_{i}\}
, which maps the global flat index
\ell
to the distinct eigenspace index
k
:

\displaystyle k=k_{i}(\ell)\in\{1,\cdots,q_{i}\}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}m_{ik^{\prime}}<\ell\leq\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k}m_{ik^{\prime}},\quad\ell\in\{1,\cdots,q\}.

The standard tomography protocol proceeds as follows. For each
\mathbf{O}_{i}
, an ensemble of
r
independent systems is prepared in the state
\boldsymbol{\rho}
. The measurement outcomes
\{Y_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{r}
are i.i.d. random variables governed by the Born rule:

\mathbb{P}(Y_{ij}=\lambda_{ik}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho})=\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}(\{\lambda_{ik}\})=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right],\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq j\leq r,\,1\leq k\leq q_{i}.

Since the convex cone of density matrices
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
is not a linear space, we consider a linear operator by extending the domain:

\displaystyle d_{ik}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbf{S}\mapsto d_{ik}[\mathbf{S}]:=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right],\quad 1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq k\leq q_{i}.

Since
\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
under the Frobenius inner product is a real Hilbert space, the functional
d_{ik}
can be identified with the projection
\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}
by the Riesz representation theorem.
Because the maximally mixed state
\mathbf{I}_{q}/q\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
produces entirely uninformative measurement statistics (
m_{ik}^{-1}d_{ik}[\mathbf{I}_{q}/q]\equiv 1/q
), it acts as a non-directional baseline. In other words,
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
is identifiable if and only if the traceless component
\boldsymbol{\rho}-\mathbf{I}_{q}/q\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
is identifiable via variations in measurement statistics. This naturally motivates the
trace-centering
decomposition to isolate the statistically identifiable signal:

\mathbf{S}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\oplus\left[\mathbf{S}-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\right]\quad\in\quad\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}=\spann\{\mathbf{I}_{q}\}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}.

(7)

Definition 4.1

(Complete Design)
.

A collection of observables
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
is said to be a
complete design

[
12
]
, if for any traceless self-adjoint
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
:

d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\Delta}]=0\,\text{ for all }\,1\leq i\leq n,\,1\leq k\leq q_{i}\quad\iff\quad\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\mathbf{0}.

Informational completeness
is the physical equivalent of statistical identifiability:

Proposition 4.2

(Identifiability)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\rho},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
be two density matrices. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

1.

\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
is a complete design.

2.

d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]=d_{ik}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}]
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
, and
k=1,\cdots,q_{i}
, implies
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
.

Analogous to the design matrix in linear regression, we define the
quantum design tensor

\mathfrak{D}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
as the linear map: for a self-adjoint matrix
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
,

\mathfrak{D}[\mathbf{S}]=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{d}_{1}[\mathbf{S}]|\mathbf{d}_{2}[\mathbf{S}]|\cdots|\mathbf{d}_{n}[\mathbf{S}]\end{pmatrix}^{\top},\quad\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]:=\left[d_{ik_{i}(\ell)}[\mathbf{S}]/m_{ik_{i}(\ell)}\right]_{1\leq\ell\leq q},

(8)

where each vector
\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is formed by taking the weighted expectation
d_{ik}[\mathbf{S}]/m_{ik}
and repeating it exactly
m_{ik}
times for each eigenspace index
k=1,\dots,q_{i}
. We now characterize the complete design:

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
. The adjoint of the quantum design tensor is given by:

\mathfrak{D}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{A}\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\bar{a}_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik},\quad\bar{a}_{ik}=\frac{1}{m_{ik}}\sum_{\{\ell:k_{i}(\ell)=k\}}a_{i\ell},

i.e.,
\bar{a}_{ik}
is the average of the
m_{ik}
entries in the
i
-th row of
\mathbf{A}
corresponding to the eigenspace
\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}
. Additionally, the following are equivalent:

1.

\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
is a complete design.

2.

\spann\{\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}:i=1,\cdots,n,\,k=1,\cdots,q_{i}\}=\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
.

3.

The quantum design tensor
\mathfrak{D}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
is injective.

Propositions

4.2
and
4.3
imply that QST is ill-posed if
\dim\mathcal{Q}=\infty
without structural restrictions (e.g., low rank), justifying our finite-dimensional focus.

Remark 4.4
.

Given a fixed design
\mathscr{O}
, the unidentifiable directions are given by

\displaystyle\ker(\mathfrak{D})=\spann\{\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}:i=1,\cdots,n,\,k=1,\cdots,q_{i}\}^{\perp}\subset\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q},

(9)

For instance, measuring only Pauli-X and Y on
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
leaves
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}
unidentifiable because
\ker(\mathfrak{D})=\spann\{\mathbf{\Pi}_{x,\pm},\mathbf{\Pi}_{y,\pm}\}^{\perp}=\spann\{\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z}\}
.

Consider the following map, which we call the
Gram superoperator
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}}{n}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{S}\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right]}{m_{ik}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik},

(10)

Regardless of the design of observables,
\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
and
\spann\{\mathbf{I}_{q}\}
form invariant subspaces:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}}{n}:\mathbf{S}=\omega\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\oplus\boldsymbol{\Delta}\ \mapsto\ \omega\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\oplus\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}}{n}\boldsymbol{\Delta},\quad\omega\in\mathbb{R},\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}.

Roughly speaking, a complete design amounts to a full-rank matrix in linear regression, where
\mathbf{I}_{q}/q
acts as the intercept term. In this regard, we now introduce the quantum analogue of an orthogonal design matrix so that
\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
and
\spann\{\mathbf{I}_{q}\}
become irreducible subspaces.

Definition 4.5

(Unitary Design)
.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
be a collection of observables on
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
. The collection is said to be an
\alpha
-
unitary design
if there exists a constant
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}\right)\boldsymbol{\Delta}=\alpha\boldsymbol{\Delta}

for any
\boldsymbol{\Delta}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
.

Unitary designs can be characterized as follows.

Theorem 4.6

(Invariance)
.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
.

1.

\mathscr{O}
is an
\alpha
-unitary design if and only if the rotated collection
\{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{O}_{i}\mathbf{V}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
is also an
\alpha
-unitary design for any matrix
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}
with
\mathbf{V}\mathbf{V}^{*}=\mathbf{I}_{q}
(i.e., orthogonal if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and unitary if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
).

2.

The constant
\alpha
is exclusively determined by the QHS
\mathcal{Q}
and the average number of distinct eigenvalues
\bar{q}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}/n
:

\displaystyle\alpha=\eta_{\mathscr{O}}\cdot\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}},\quad\text{where}\quad\eta_{\mathscr{O}}=\frac{\bar{q}-1}{q-1},\quad\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}=\begin{cases}(q/2+1)^{-1}&\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}\\
(q+1)^{-1}&\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}\end{cases}.

Note that
\alpha>0
since
\eta_{\mathscr{O}}\geq 1/(q-1)
. For rank-1 measurements (
q_{i}\equiv q
),
\eta_{\mathscr{O}}=1
and
\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
recovers the known frame bounds for projective 2-designs in the context of the Clifford algebra
[
52

38
]
.
Theorem

4.6
generalizes this, isolating the attenuation of signal detection via the
multiplicity damping factor

\eta_{\mathscr{O}}
caused by utilizing observables with degenerate eigenvalues.

Remark 4.9

(Approximate Unitary Design)

For arbitrary dimensions

4.2
Tensorized Linear Regression

While Maximum Likelihood Estimation is a valid choice for a complete design, it becomes computationally prohibitive as the system dimension
q
grows; 8 qubits (
q=2^{8}
) can require weeks of computation
[
33
,
36
]
. Consequently, a version of Least Squares Estimation (LSE)
[
54
]
has emerged as a standard, tractable alternative in state tomography. We now demonstrate how our framework provides a rigorous, tensorized formulation of this classical approach. By expressing the inverse problem strictly through basis-independent linear superoperators, we seamlessly recover the standard unconstrained LSE while providing closed-form geometric insights into its covariance structure, mean-square error, and statistical optimality.

For each
i=1,\cdots,n
, we denote the empirical frequency for the outcome
\lambda_{ik}
by:

\displaystyle p_{ik}:=\frac{\sharp\{1\leq j\leq r:Y_{ij}=\lambda_{ik}\}}{r}\geq 0,\quad k=1,2,\cdots,q_{i},\quad p_{i1}+\cdots+p_{iq_{i}}=1.

Consequently, the empirical distribution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i,\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\delta_{Y_{ij}}=\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}p_{ik}\delta_{\lambda_{ik}}

serves as a proxy for the true probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i,\boldsymbol{\rho}}
generated by the unknown state
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
. The expectations satisfy
d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right]=\mathbb{E}[p_{ik}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]
. Since the counts
r\cdot(p_{i1},\dots,p_{iq_{i}})
follow
\text{Multinomial}(r,(d_{i1}[\boldsymbol{\rho}],\dots,d_{iq_{i}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]))
, the covariance structure is given by:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(p_{ik},p_{i^{\prime}k^{\prime}}|\boldsymbol{\rho})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]-d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]^{2})/r&\quad i=i^{\prime},k=k^{\prime}\\
-d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]d_{ik^{\prime}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]/r&\quad i=i^{\prime},k\neq k^{\prime}\\
0&\quad i\neq i^{\prime}.\end{cases}

(12)

To formulate the estimation as a linear regression problem, we construct the data matrix
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q}
incorporating the multiplicities, analogous to the design tensor (
8
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{p}_{1}|\mathbf{p}_{2}|\cdots|\mathbf{p}_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times q},\quad\mathbf{p}_{i}:=\left[p_{ik_{i}(\ell)}/m_{ik_{i}(\ell)}\right]_{1\leq\ell\leq q},

where each vector
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
is formed by taking the scaled empirical frequency
p_{ik}/m_{ik}
and repeating it exactly
m_{ik}
times for each eigenspace index
k=1,\dots,q_{i}
. We then define the quadratic loss function:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Loss}[\mathbf{S}]:={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathfrak{D}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{P}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2},\quad\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}.

(13)

The estimator is defined as the minimizer over the unit-trace affine hyperplane:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}\in\argmin\{\mathrm{Loss}[\mathbf{S}]:\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}\}.

(14)

Note that we explicitly relax the n.n.d. constraint
\boldsymbol{\rho}\succeq 0
. If the resulting estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}
lies outside the convex set
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
, it can be projected back onto the set of valid density matrices, which can be computed straightforwardly using the Trace-Preserving Projection algorithm in
Section

B.1
. Since this projection is a contraction in the Frobenius norm, it preserves the statistical consistency and asymptotic convergence rates of the unconstrained estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}
.

Theorem 4.12

(Linear Regression)
.

Given the data matrix
\mathbf{P}
and a collection of observables
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
, the set of minimizers in
\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
is given by the affine space:

\argmin\{\mathrm{Loss}[\mathbf{S}]:\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}\}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}\oplus\ker(\mathfrak{D}),

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=(\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D})^{\dagger}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
is the minimum Frobenius norm solution, and the null space corresponds to the unobservable directions from (
9
). If the design
\mathscr{O}
is complete, the minimizer is uniquely given by
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=(\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbf{P}
.

For a complete design
\mathscr{O}
, the LSE is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=(\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{P}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=(\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\boldsymbol{\rho}
for any density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
.
Surprisingly, the two-step procedure, unconstrained estimation followed by trace-preserving projection, is mathematically equivalent to solving the fully constrained optimization problem for unitary designs.

Corollary 4.13
.

Let
\mathscr{O}
be an
\alpha
-unitary design on
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
with
\alpha>0
. The unique projection of the relaxed estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
onto
\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
is precisely the exact global solution to the constrained regression problem:

\displaystyle\argmin\{{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}:\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})\}=\argmin\{\mathrm{Loss}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]:\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})\}.

While the trace-preserving projection in
[
54
]
was originally proposed as a computationally efficient proxy for the MLE,
Corollary

4.13
rigorously proves that it induces the exact constrained least-squares solution. For unitary designs, we can further derive the explicit expression for the mean-square error (MSE):

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]:=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\boldsymbol{\rho}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right].

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
\mathscr{O}
be an
\alpha
-unitary design with
\alpha>0
. Then, the LSE simplifies to:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbf{P}}{n\alpha}-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbf{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{p_{ik}}{m_{ik}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}.

(15)

If the design consists exclusively of rank-1 projections (
m_{ik}\equiv 1
), then
\alpha=\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
in
Theorem

4.6
, and the mean-squared error for any density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\frac{1}{nr\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)-\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}^{2}\right]-\frac{1}{q}\right)\right].

Recall that
1/q\leq\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}^{2}\right]\leq 1
, with equality holding if and only if
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is the maximally mixed state (
\mathbf{I}_{q}/q
) or a pure state, respectively. Hence, under the
\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
-unitary design, the MSE strictly varies with the state purity:

\displaystyle\frac{1-\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}}{nr\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\leq\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]\leq\frac{1}{nr\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right).

This demonstrates that the MSE achieves the standard parametric rate
O((nr)^{-1})
and scales with the dimension of the operator space as
O(q^{2})
.
We briefly remark on the
curse of dimensionality
: even with a unitary design where the measurement sensitivity is geometrically isotropic, the signal is inherently dampened by the factor
\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
, causing the MSE to scale inversely with
\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}\asymp q^{2}
. Furthermore, the relative gap between the best-case (pure state) and worst-case (maximally mixed state) error is determined precisely by
1-\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
, a quantity which vanishes asymptotically as
q\to\infty
.

Proposition 4.15

(Fixed Budget)
.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
be a design on
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
. The trace of the superoperator is determined solely by the average number of distinct eigenvalues
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\tfrac{1}{n}\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}\right]=\bar{q}=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}
.
Consequently, the smallest eigenvalue
\tau_{\min}
of the Gram superoperator satisfies the upper bound
\tau_{\min}\leq\alpha=\eta_{\mathscr{O}}\cdot\alpha_{\mathcal{Q}}
, with equality holding if and only if
\mathscr{O}
is
\alpha
-unitary.

We now establish the minimax lower bound of QST. The result below applies to any complete design and any unknown density, with the lower bound scaling precisely as
q^{2}/(nr)
.

Theorem 4.16

(Lower Bound)
.

Let
\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a complete design on
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
. In the asymptotic regime where the sample size is sufficiently large relative to the dimension, specifically
r\geq qc_{0}\operatorname{Tr}\left[(\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D})^{-1}\right]
for some universal constant
c_{0}>0
, the minimax risk for estimating the density matrix satisfies:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\in\mathbb{F}^{q\times q}}\sup_{\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})}\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]\geq C\frac{q^{2}}{nr},

where
C>0
is a universal constant independent of
q,n,r
.

A closer inspection of the proof reveals that the unitary design is optimal (up to constant), as it maximizes the separation of the packing set for a fixed budget
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}/n\right]=\bar{q}
in
Proposition

4.15
. Specifically, the unitary design maximizes the minimum eigenvalue of the Gram superoperator, a property analogous to E-optimality in classical experimental design
[
49
]
, despite the non-Gaussian nature of the quantum setting.
We also note that a similar minimax rate for the trace norm discrepancy has been considered in
[
14
]
albeit without explicit dependency on the dimension of the QHS,
q
.

Example 4.17

(Real Qubit System)
.

Although usual quantum mechanics uses
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
, we consider the lowest dimensional QHS, the real qubit (
rebit
) system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
to explicitly illustrate the geometry of the regression. We adopt the
Bloch disk representation
to parametrize the space of unit-trace symmetric matrices:

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}_{2}+\frac{s}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&\sin(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)&-\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}_{sa,1}^{2\times 2},\quad z:=se^{\imath\theta}\in\mathbb{C},

where
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
represents the orientation. Note that
\mathbf{S}\succeq\mathbf{0}
is a valid density matrix if and only if
s\in[0,1]
, where
s
corresponds to the purity of the state (
s=1
for pure states).
We construct a measurement design using
n
rotation matrices:

\mathbf{U}_{i}=\mathbf{U}(\theta_{i}):=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta_{i}/2)&\sin(\theta_{i}/2)\\
-\sin(\theta_{i}/2)&\cos(\theta_{i}/2)\end{pmatrix}\in SO(2),\quad\theta_{i}\in[0,2\pi),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

Let the seed observable be
\mathbf{O}=\operatorname{Diag}[+1,-1]
with eigenvectors
\mathbf{e}_{+}=[1,0]^{\top}
and
\mathbf{e}_{-}=[0,1]^{\top}
. The rotated observables are
\mathbf{O}_{i}=\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{O}\mathbf{U}_{i}
, which form a complete design provided the angles
\theta_{i}
are not all identical. The expected outcome probabilities are given by:

\displaystyle d_{i,\pm}[\mathbf{S}]=\langle(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{\pm}),\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{e}_{\pm})\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}=\frac{1\pm s\cos(\theta-\theta_{i})}{2}.

Due to the symmetry
|d_{i,+}[\mathbf{S}]-p_{i,+}|=|d_{i,-}[\mathbf{S}]-p_{i,-}|
, the loss function simplifies to:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Loss}[\mathbf{S}]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|d_{i,+}[\mathbf{S}]-p_{i,+}|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left|s\cos(\theta-\theta_{i})-\left(2p_{i,+}-1\right)\right|^{2}}{2}.

Using the Bloch disk representation, the loss can be rewritten in terms of two complex parameters
\bar{D}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{\imath 2\theta_{i}}
and
\bar{P}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2p_{i,+}-1)e^{\imath\theta_{i}}
:

\displaystyle\text{Loss}(z)=\frac{n}{4}|z|_{\mathbb{C}}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Re(z^{2}\bar{D}^{*})-\Re(z\bar{P}^{*})+\text{const}.

Only for this example, we reserve the bar notation
\bar{\cdot}
for sample averages to align with statistical convention; thus, we use
∗
to denote the complex conjugate, and
\Re
and
\Im
for the real and imaginary parts. The data average variable
\bar{P}\in\mathbb{C}
, being an average of complex numbers in the unit disk, also satisfies
|\bar{P}|_{\mathbb{C}}\leq 1
.
Solving the loss function for
z
yields the explicit LSE from
Theorem

4.12
:

\displaystyle\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}=\frac{2(\bar{P}-\bar{D}\bar{P}^{*})}{1-|\bar{D}|^{2}}\in\mathbb{C}\quad\iff\quad\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\Re(\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}})&\Im(\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}})\\
\Im(\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}})&-\Re(\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

In particular, if the angles
\theta_{i}=2\pi i/n
are uniformly spaced, then
\bar{D}=0
. This corresponds to a
1/2
-unitary design, and the LSE reduces to
\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}=2\bar{P}
.
Therefore, all we need for the estimation procedure to compute is essentially
\bar{P}
, as displayed in the bottom left of
Fig.

In this case,
Theorem

4.14
gives:

\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\frac{2}{nr}\left(1-\frac{s^{2}}{2}\right).

Note that the LSE violates the n.n.d. condition if and only if
|\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}|_{\mathbb{C}}>1
(or, equivalently,
|\bar{P}|_{\mathbb{C}}>1/2
). In this case, the exact constrained optimal solution is obtained by simple radial shrinkage due to
Corollary

4.13
:
\hat{z}_{+}^{\text{LSE}}=\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}/|\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}|_{\mathbb{C}}
, which yields a pure state.

4.3
Tensorized Kernel Regression

While standard linear regression in
Section

4.2
suffices when spectra are treated as abstract logical labels, it fails to capture the geometric reality of physical implementations. In hardware based on continuous variables — such as angular momentum
[
2
]
or discrete phase
[
48
]
— measurement outcomes possess an intrinsic metric where errors manifest as local displacements rather than categorical flips
[
30
]
. To reflect this structure, we introduce a reproducing kernel
K:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{F}
to encode the geometry over the spectrum. In this view, standard linear regression is recovered as the specific choice of the 0–1 kernel
K(x,y)=\mathds{1}(x=y)
, corresponding to a “metric-free” topology over spectra.

We define the
i
-th Gram matrix associated with
\mathbf{O}_{i}
incorporating the multiplicities:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q},\quad[\mathbf{K}_{i}]_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}:=K(\lambda_{ik_{i}(\ell)},\lambda_{ik_{i}(\ell^{\prime})}),\quad 1\leq\ell,\ell^{\prime}\leq q.

(16)

Note that we index by
\ell\in\{1,\dots,q\}
to account for eigenvalue multiplicities. We now relate the functional discrepancy of conditional QCE to a spectral matrix norm.

Theorem 4.18

(Quantum Representer Theorem)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}^{q}
. For each
i=1,\dots,n
, let
\mathbf{K}_{i}
be the Gram matrix defined in (
16
). Given any
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
, define the diagonal matrices of expectations and empirical frequencies:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]:=\operatorname{Diag}(\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}])\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q},\quad\mathbf{P}_{i}:=\operatorname{Diag}(\mathbf{p}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q},

where
\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}],\mathbf{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
are the
i
-th rows of the design tensor
\mathfrak{D}[\mathbf{S}]
and data matrix
\mathbf{P}
respectively as in (
8
). Then, for any
s\in[1,\infty]
, the
s
-Schatten norm of the discrepancy between the population QCE
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i,\mathbf{S}}}
and the empirical QCE
\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i,\boldsymbol{\rho}}}
is given by:

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i,\mathbf{S}}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i,\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{s}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}_{i}^{1/2}(\mathbf{D}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{P}_{i})\mathbf{K}_{i}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{s}<\infty.

The finite-dimensional reduction effectively projects out the orthogonal complement, conceptually mirroring the classical representer theorem in kernel ridge regression
[
47
]
. As noted previously in
Remark

3.9
, we adopt the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (
s=2
) for the conditional Quantum Maximum Discrepancy to yield a tractable convex loss function:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Loss}_{K}[\mathbf{S}]:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i,\mathbf{S}}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{i,\boldsymbol{\rho}}}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}_{i}^{1/2}(\mathbf{D}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{P}_{i})\mathbf{K}_{i}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2},\quad\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}.

We then define the
QUARK (QUAntum Regression with Kernels)
estimator by relaxing the n.n.d. constraint:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}\in\argmin\{\mathrm{Loss}_{K}[\mathbf{S}]:\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}\},

(17)

which generalizes the tensorized linear regression derived in
Section

4.2
. Specifically, if we select the 0–1 kernel, the loss reduces to the standard squared error provided
m_{ik}\equiv 1
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Loss}[\mathbf{S}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{D}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{P}_{i}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{p}_{i}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}^{2}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathfrak{D}[\mathbf{S}]-\mathbf{P}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}.

To derive the normal equation for a general kernel, recall the linear map
\mathbf{S}\mapsto\mathbf{d}_{i}[\mathbf{S}]
. Unlike the tensorized linear regression, we will shortly see that the multiplicities
m_{ik}
cancel out. Consider the following operator and its adjoint:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}},\,\mathbf{S}\mapsto\left[d_{ik}[\mathbf{S}]\right]_{1\leq k\leq q_{i}},\quad\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{a}\mapsto\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}a_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}.

For estimation within the RKHS framework, the squared kernel
|K|^{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
naturally governs the estimation procedure. Letting
\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{q_{i}\times q_{i}}
be the Gram matrices evaluated at the spectra of observables
\mathbf{O}_{i}
, i.e.,
[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}]_{kk^{\prime}}=|K(\lambda_{ik},\lambda_{ik^{\prime}})|^{2}
, then the
kernel Gram superoperator
and the effective data matrix become:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{H}_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}\mathfrak{D}_{i}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\ \mathbf{S}\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{q_{i}}[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}]_{kk^{\prime}}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik}\right]\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{K}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathfrak{D}_{i}^{*}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}\left[p_{ik}\right]_{1\leq k\leq q_{i}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k,k^{\prime}=1}^{q_{i}}[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}]_{kk^{\prime}}p_{ik}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ik^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}.

Proposition 4.19
.

Analogous to
Proposition

4.15
, the trace of the kernel Gram superoperator is determined solely by the eigenvalue multiplicities and the diagonal evaluation of the kernel, independent of the specific eigenprojections:

\displaystyle{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathfrak{H}_{K}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{1}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}m_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}]_{kk}.

However, unlike the Gram superoperator for tensorized linear regression, the kernel Gram superoperator generally preserves neither the identity nor the trace, despite being a n.n.d. tensor. Consequently, the inversion inherently induces a trace shrinkage:

Theorem 4.20
.

Let
\mathscr{O}=\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}:i=1,\cdots,n\}
be a complete design. If the squared kernel
|K|^{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly positive definite (p.d.), then so is the kernel Gram superoperator, and the QUARK estimator in (
17
) is uniquely given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}=\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{P}_{K})+(1-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{P}_{K})\right])\frac{\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{q})}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{q})\right]}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}.

As the kernel Gram superoperator is not trace-preserving, the QUARK estimator performs a version of shrinkage estimation at the matrix level:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}}\mathrm{Loss}_{K}[\mathbf{S}]\quad\text{with respect to}\quad\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]=1,

and the penalty parameter in the Lagrange multiplier is explicitly fixed by the unit-trace constraint to achieve the shrinkage effect.
Indeed, the QUARK estimation procedure is homogeneous with respect to the kernel scaling; inference depends on the relative differences of the kernel values rather than their absolute magnitudes. To see this, consider a scalar multiple of a kernel
cK
for some
c\in\mathbb{F}
. Then,
\mathfrak{H}_{cK}=|c|^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{K}
and
\mathbf{P}_{cK}=|c|^{2}\mathbf{P}_{K}
, thus
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{cK}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}
.

Corollary 4.21

(Unbiased)
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.20
, the QUARK estimator in (
17
) is unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\boldsymbol{\rho}
for any density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{+}^{q\times q})
.

As in
Section

4.2
, in the case where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q}
does not satisfy the n.n.d. constraint, one may apply the trace-preserving projection algorithm, which preserves consistency and asymptotic convergence rates.
To tackle the asymptotic behavior of the QUARK estimator under the fixed complete design, we introduce two superoperators with respect to the kernel:

Proposition 4.22
.

Let
\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a complete design, and the squared kernel
|K|^{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be strictly p.d.. Define the
kernel trace-centering superoperator
by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{K}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{S}\mapsto\mathbf{S}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{q})\right]}\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{q}).

Then, its adjoint is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}_{K}^{*}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{S}\mapsto\mathbf{S}-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{S})\right]}{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}_{q})\right]}\mathbf{I}_{q}.

Consequently, the
kernel centered-inversion superoperator

\mathfrak{A}_{K}:=\mathfrak{H}_{K}^{-1}\mathfrak{C}_{K}^{*}
is self-adjoint, and the QUARK estimator can be decomposed as:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}=\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\oplus\mathfrak{A}_{K}\left[\mathbf{P}_{K}-\frac{\mathbf{I}_{q}}{q}\right]\quad\in\quad\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}=\spann\{\mathbf{I}_{q}\}\oplus\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}.

To derive the Central Limit Theroem (CLT) of the QUARK estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}
in the sample size per observable
r
, we first compute the covariance structure. In what follows, given a self-adjoint operator
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
, we denote the superoperator via outer product by

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\otimes 2}:\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}\to\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q},\,\mathbf{S}\mapsto{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}}\right\rangle}_{2}\mathbf{M}.

Proposition 4.23
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

4.20
, the mean of the effective data matrix
\mathbf{P}_{K}
is given by
\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{P}_{K}|\boldsymbol{\rho})=\mathfrak{H}_{K}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]
, and its
scaled
covariance superoperator
\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}:=r\cdot\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf{P}_{K}-\mathfrak{H}_{K}[\boldsymbol{\rho}])^{\otimes 2}]

is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigg[\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}](\mathbf{M}_{ik}^{K})^{\otimes 2}-\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]\mathbf{M}_{ik}^{K}\Big)^{\otimes 2}\Bigg],

where
\mathbf{M}_{ik}^{K}:=\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{q_{i}}[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{i}]_{kk^{\prime}}\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{ik^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
.

It follows from
Propositions

4.22
and
4.23
that the operator MSE of the QUARK estimator is given by:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\mathbb{E}\left[{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}-\boldsymbol{\rho}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\operatorname{Cov}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K})\right]=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathfrak{A}_{K}\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\mathfrak{A}_{K}\right]}{r}.

Moreover, the following central limit theorem is trivial:

Theorem 4.24

(CLT)
.

Under the assumptions of
Proposition

4.23
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\sqrt{r}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}-\boldsymbol{\rho})\xrightarrow{d}N\left(\mathbf{0},\mathfrak{A}_{K}\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\mathfrak{A}_{K}\right),

as
r\rightarrow\infty
, where
\mathfrak{A}_{K}
is the kernel centered-inversion in
Proposition

4.22
.

Finally, we provide a Bennett-type non-asymptotic bound for the QUARK estimator:

Theorem 4.25

(Directional Concentration Inequality)
.

Assume the setup of
Theorem

4.20
. Then, for any
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
and
t>0
, the directional concentration inequality holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left.{\left\langle{\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{K}-\boldsymbol{\rho}},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}>t\right|\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\mathfrak{M}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}[\mathbf{S}]}{r}h\left(\frac{t}{\mathfrak{M}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}[\mathbf{S}]/r}\right)\right),

where
h(x):=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
and
\mathfrak{M}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}[\mathbf{S}]={\left\langle{\mathfrak{A}_{K}\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\mathfrak{A}_{K}\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}
.

Furthermore, using the inequality
h(\tfrac{t}{m_{2}})\geq\tfrac{t^{2}}{2(m_{2}+t/3)}
for every
t>0
and
m_{2}\geq 0
, the simpler Bernstein-type inequality follows from the previous result:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[{\left\langle{\mathbf{P}_{K}-\mathfrak{H}_{K}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}>t|\boldsymbol{\rho}]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2(m_{2}+\frac{t}{3})}\right),

where

\displaystyle m_{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}r}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big[\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]\,{\left\langle{\mathbf{M}_{ik}^{K}},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}^{2}-\big(\sum_{k=1}^{q_{i}}d_{ik}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]\,{\left\langle{\mathbf{M}_{ik}^{K}},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}\big)^{2}\Big].

6
Discussion

This work establishes the kernel embedding theory to quantify how the geometry of the measurement process affects statistical efficiency. We demonstrate its utility for Quantum State Tomography, natively incorporating the least-squares approach under a metric-free topology.

Our theory of unitary design and minimax analysis, providing an analytical bound for the penalty incurred by eigenvalue degeneracies, clarifies the statistical price of quantum state recovery. Crucially, our analysis sheds a light on the fundamental tension between hardware implementation and statistical optimality: while local measurement schemes (e.g., tensorized Pauli) may yield optimal accuracy given strict sparsity assumptions tailored to that design, they incur a severe, exponential variance penalty. Achieving fundamental statistical efficiency strictly requires entangled observables, such as Mutually Unbiased Bases.

In contrast, by leveraging the spectral geometry of the measurement process rather than design-dependent sparsity, the proposed QUARK estimator provides a minimax-optimal framework for density estimation even in the highly entangled regimes where genuine quantum supremacy is realized.

A
Tensorized Bochner integral

The usual Bochner integral for a general Hilbert (or Banach) space with respect to a scalar probability measure is typically constructed via a limiting argument over step functions
[
63
,
41
]
. Here, we develop a
tensorized
Bochner integration where the measure is a Positive Operator-Valued Measure (POVM) and the Banach space of interest is the space of bounded operators on a Hilbert space, a formulation that natively supports our operator-theoretic framework.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{2}
be Hilbert spaces. We define the inner product on elementary tensors by

{\left\langle{f_{1}\otimes f_{2}},{g_{1}\otimes g_{2}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}}:={\left\langle{f_{1}},{g_{1}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}{\left\langle{f_{2}},{g_{2}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}},\quad f_{1},\,g_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1},\ f_{2},\,g_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}.

For
\mathbf{T}_{1}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1})
and
\mathbf{T}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{2})
, there exists a unique bounded operator, denoted by
\mathbf{T}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{2}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})
, such that

(\mathbf{T}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{2})(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})=(\mathbf{T}_{1}f_{1})\otimes(\mathbf{T}_{2}f_{2}),

and it holds that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{1}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{T}_{2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}

[
37
, Proposition A.67]
. Therefore, the quadratic form with respect to the elementary tensor entirely characterizes the tensorized operators. That is, for an operator
\mathbf{T}:(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})\to(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})
, it holds that
\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{T}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{2}
if and only if

\displaystyle{\left\langle{(\mathbf{T}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{T}_{2})(f_{1}\otimes f_{2})},{g_{1}\otimes g_{2}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2}}={\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{1}f_{1}},{g_{1}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{1}}{\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{2}f_{2}},{g_{2}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}_{2}},

for any
f_{1},g_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
f_{2},g_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Hence, the formal definition below is well-posed:

Definition A.1
.

Let
\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{H}
be Hilbert spaces. Let
(X,\mathscr{B}(X))
be an LCH space equipped with Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
be a POVM. Consider the locally convex topological vector space
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
equipped with the Weak Operator Topology (WOT). A measurable function
\mathbf{F}:X\to\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be tensorized Bochner integrable if there exists a bounded
operator on
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{Q}
, denoted by
\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
, such that

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\left(\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\right)(f\otimes\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}=\int_{X}{\left\langle{\mathbf{F}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x),

(19)

for any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
. We call
\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
the tensorized Bochner integral of
\mathbf{F}
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\nu}
.

In the case where
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{F}
or
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{F}
, this naturally reduces to the standard Bochner integral and functional calculus, respectively, where it is customary to write
\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
instead of
\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)
.
Unlike the standard Bochner integral, we employ a topology strictly weaker than the norm topology. The WOT is sufficient to uniquely determine the integrated operator via its associated quadratic form. Furthermore, this choice allows us to leverage the Banach–Alaoglu theorem, which guarantees that the closed unit ball in
(\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}),{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\cdot\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty})
is compact with respect to the WOT. Consequently, if
\mathbf{F}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\sup_{x\in X}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{F}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}<\infty
, then it is automatically tensorized Bochner integrable since (
19
) remains bounded for all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
. In this bounded regime, we obtain the inequality:
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\int_{X}\mathbf{F}\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq\sup_{x\in X}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{F}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}
. For a measurable simple function

\mathbf{S}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{B_{i}}(x)\mathbf{S}_{i},\quad B_{i}\in\mathscr{B}(X),\quad\mathbf{S}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

its tensorized Bochner integral is trivially given by
\int_{X}\mathbf{S}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{S}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{\nu}(B_{i})\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{Q})
, which is independent of the particular representation of the simple function
\mathbf{S}
.
Alternatively, one can construct the tensorized Bochner integral globally using a standard limiting argument:

Theorem A.2

(Lebesgue Dominated Convergence)
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a Hilbert space, and
\boldsymbol{\nu}\in\mathscr{P}(X,\mathcal{Q})
. An operator-valued measurable function
\mathbf{F}:X\to(\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}),\mathrm{WOT})
is tensorized Bochner integrable if there exists a sequence of measurable simple functions
\{\mathbf{S}_{n}:X\to(\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}),\mathrm{WOT})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that:

1.

There exists a scalar function
h\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(X,\boldsymbol{\nu})
(meaning
h
is integrable with respect to the scalar positive measures
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\phi}
for all
\phi\in\mathcal{Q}
) satisfying
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq h(x)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in X
.

2.

For each
x\in X
,
\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)\to\mathbf{F}(x)
as
n\to\infty
with respect to the WOT in
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H})
.

In this case, we have
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{F}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq h(x)
for any
x\in X
, and

\displaystyle\int_{X}\mathbf{F}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x),

where the limit converges with respect to the WOT in
\mathcal{B}_{\infty}(\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{Q})
.

Proof.

First, it is trivial that
{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{F}(x)\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq h(x)
for any
x\in X
, since for any
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
,

|{\left\langle{\mathbf{F}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}|=\lim_{n\to\infty}|{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}|\leq h(x)\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Fix
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
. Because
|{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}|\leq h(x)\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}
and
h\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(X,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi})
, we can apply the standard Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to obtain:

\displaystyle\int_{X}{\left\langle{\mathbf{F}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{X}{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\left\langle{\left(\int_{X}\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)\otimes\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\right)(f\otimes\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{Q}}.

It suffices to show that the bounding limit is well-behaved for all
f,g\in\mathcal{H}
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
. Define the bounded positive operator
\mathbf{H}:=\int_{X}h(x)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{Q})
, which is well-defined by the assumption
h\in\mathcal{L}_{1}(X,\boldsymbol{\nu})
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for POVMs, the total variation of the complex measure satisfies
\mathrm{d}|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}|(x)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\phi}(x)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\psi,\psi}(x)}
.

Therefore, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for integrals, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{X}{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}_{n}(x)f},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{H}}\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x)\right|\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}\int_{X}h(x)\mathrm{d}|\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}|(x)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}\left(\int_{X}h(x)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\phi}(x)\right)^{1/2}\left(\int_{X}h(x)\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\psi,\psi}(x)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}{\left\langle{\mathbf{H}\phi},{\phi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{1/2}{\left\langle{\mathbf{H}\psi},{\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{Q}}^{1/2}\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{H}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{Q}}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{Q}}.

This uniform bound over all
n
strictly guarantees that the sesquilinear form defines a bounded operator on the tensor product space, completing the proof.
∎

Theorem B.1

(Sum-Preserving Threshold Algorithm (SPTA))
.

Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{q})
be a non-increasing real-valued sequence with
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}>0
. Consider the following constrained optimization problem:

\min\sum_{k=1}^{q}(a_{k}-b_{k})^{2}\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\quad b_{1}\geq\cdots\geq b_{q}\geq 0,\text{ and }b_{1}+\cdots+b_{q}=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}.

Define the truncation index and the truncated negative tail average by

\displaystyle t:=t(\mathbf{a})=\max\{1\leq k\leq q:ka_{k}+a_{k+1}+\dots+a_{q}\geq 0\},\,v:=v(\mathbf{a})=-\frac{a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{q}}{t}.

Then, we have
0\leq v\leq a_{t}
, and the unique solution of the primal problem is achieved at
\mathrm{SPTA}(\mathbf{a}):=\mathbf{b}^{*}=(b^{*}_{1},\cdots,b^{*}_{q})
with

b^{*}_{k}=\begin{cases}a_{k}-v&,\quad k=1,2,\cdots,t,\\
0&,\quad k=t+1,t+2,\cdots,q.\end{cases}

Proof.

We may assume that
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}=1
, as we can normalize the problem. We may further assume that
a_{q}<0
, otherwise the result is trivial as
t=q
,
v=0
and
\mathbf{b}^{*}=\mathbf{a}
. Consider the sequence

s_{k}:=ka_{k}+a_{k+1}+\dots+a_{q},\quad k=1,2,\cdots,q.

Then
\{s_{k}:k=1,2,\cdots,q\}
is a monotonically non-increasing sequence with
s_{1}=1
, since
s_{k}-s_{k+1}=k(a_{k}-a_{k+1})\geq 0
. This shows that
t:=t(\mathbf{a})=\max\{1\leq k\leq q:s_{k}\geq 0\}
is well-defined. Also, the definition of
t
yields
v=-(a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{q})/t\leq a_{t}
. Note that

(t+1)a_{t+1}+a_{t+2}+\dots+a_{q}<0\quad\iff\quad v=-\frac{a_{t+1}+\cdots+a_{q}}{t}>a_{t+1},

hence
v\geq 0
. Otherwise, we have
a_{t+1}<0
, but the monotonicity of
\mathbf{a}
yields
v>0
, which is a contradiction.

We now show that the sequence
\mathbf{b}^{*}
satisfies the KKT conditions for the dual problem
[
9
]
:

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{u},v)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{q}(a_{k}-b_{k})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{q}u_{k}(-b_{k}+b_{k+1})+v(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\mathbf{b}-1),

where
b_{q+1}:=0
. Let
v^{*}:=v
,
u_{0}:=0
, and
u^{*}_{k}:=kv-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-b^{*}_{i})
for
k=1,2,\cdots,q
.

1.

(Stationarity) For each
k=1,\cdots,q
,

\frac{\partial L(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{u}^{*},v^{*})}{\partial b_{k}}\Big|_{b_{k}=b^{*}_{k}}=-(a_{k}-b^{*}_{k})-(u^{*}_{k}-u^{*}_{k-1})+v^{*}=0.

2.

(Primal Feasibility) We have

\displaystyle b^{*}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq b^{*}_{t}=a_{t}-v\geq 0=b^{*}_{t+1}=\cdots=b^{*}_{q},\,\sum_{k=1}^{q}b^{*}_{k}=\sum_{k=1}^{t}a_{k}-tv=\sum_{k=1}^{q}a_{k}=1.

3.

(Dual Feasibility) For
k=1,\cdots,t
,
u^{*}_{k}=0
. Also, we have
u^{*}_{q}=qv\geq 0
and

\displaystyle u^{*}_{t+1}=u_{t}^{*}+(v-a_{t+1}+b^{*}_{t+1})=v-a_{t+1}=-\frac{(t+1)a_{t+1}+a_{t+2}+\cdots+a_{q}}{t}\geq 0,

by the construction of
t
, which yields

\displaystyle u^{*}_{k}=kv-\sum_{i=1}^{k}(a_{i}-b^{*}_{i})=(k-t)v-\sum_{i=t+1}^{k}a_{i}\geq(k-t)(v-a_{t+1})=(k-t)u^{*}_{t+1}\geq 0,

for
k=t+2,\cdots,q-1
.

4.

(Complementary Slackness) For
k=1,\cdots,t
, we have
u^{*}_{k}(-b^{*}_{k}+b^{*}_{k+1})=0
since
u^{*}_{k}=0
. For
k=t+1,\cdots,q
, we also have
u^{*}_{k}(-b^{*}_{k}+b^{*}_{k+1})=0
since
b^{*}_{k}=b^{*}_{k+1}=0
.

∎

Theorem B.2

(Trace-preserving projection)
.

Let
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{U}\operatorname{Diag}[\mathbf{a}]\mathbf{U}^{*}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
be the spectral decomposition of a self-adjoint matrix, where
\mathbf{U}
is unitary (orthogonal if
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
) matrix and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{q})
is the vector of its real eigenvalues, sorted in non-increasing order.
Then the optimization problem

\argmin_{\mathbf{T}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{T}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\quad\mathbf{T}\succeq\mathbf{0}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{T}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right],

has a unique solution given by
\mathbf{U}\operatorname{Diag}[\mathrm{SPTA}(\mathbf{a})]\mathbf{U}^{*}
.

Proof.

Using spectral decomposition, we solve the optimization problem in two steps:

\min_{\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{Diag}[\mathbf{b}]}\left(\min_{\mathbf{V}\in SU(q)}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{*}-\mathbf{S}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}\right)\quad\text{ w.r.t. }\quad\mathbf{D}\succeq\mathbf{0}\text{ and }\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{D}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right],

where
\mathbf{b}
is also sorted in non-increasing order. For a fixed diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}\succeq\mathbf{0}
with
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{D}\right]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]
, the inner optimization problem becomes

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{V}\in SU(q)}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{*}-\mathbf{S}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{D}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}+{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{S}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{2}^{2}-2\max_{\mathbf{V}\in SU(q)}{\left\langle{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}^{*}},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}

by the von-Neumann trace inequality
[
40
]
, and equality holds if and only if
\mathbf{U}=\mathbf{V}
. Consequently, the outer optimization problem simplifies to
Theorem

B.1
.

B.2
Tomography with Mutually Unbiased Bases

Let
GF(2^{k})
be the Galois Field of order
q=2^{k}
equipped with operations
(\bullet,+)
. We view elements of
GF(2^{k})
strictly as polynomials modulo an irreducible polynomial
p_{k}(x)
of degree
k
. To identify field operations with vector operations, we assume the choice of a
self-dual basis
for
GF(2^{k})
. Under this basis, the field trace creates an isomorphism to the standard Euclidean dot product:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{GF}(\mathbf{a}\bullet\mathbf{b})=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}a_{j}b_{j}\pmod{2},\quad\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}\in\{0,1\}^{k}

For instance,
(011)\bullet(111)=(010)
, because
(x+1)(x^{2}+x+1)\equiv x\pmod{p_{3}}
, where the arithmetic is modulo the irreducible polynomial
p_{3}(x)=x^{3}+x+1
.
The phase space is the vector space
GF(2^{k})\times GF(2^{k})
, and its elements are denoted by
\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b})
. Under the self-dual basis, we can define the
symplectic inner product
consistently for both the field and the vector space:

{\left\langle{(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b})},{(\mathbf{c}|\mathbf{d})}\right\rangle}_{sp}=\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{b}^{\top}\mathbf{c}\pmod{2}.

(20)

This phase space corresponds to the Pauli operators of the
k
-qubit system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
, having size
q^{2}=2^{2k}
:

\mathbf{I}_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{Y}^{phy}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\imath\\
\imath&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}_{sa,0}^{2\times 2}.

We define the
mathematical
Pauli Y matrix by
\mathbf{Y}^{math}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{Z}
so that
\mathbf{Y}^{phy}=\imath\mathbf{Y}^{math}
. For the
k
-qubit system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
with
q=2^{k}
, the
mathematical
tensorized Pauli operators can be identified with the symplectic vector space
(\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2k},{\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle}_{sp})
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}}^{math}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{k}\mathbf{X}_{j}^{a_{j}}\mathbf{Z}_{j}^{b_{j}},\quad\mathbf{u}=(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}).

For instance, as
\mathbf{I}_{2}\leftrightarrow(0|0)
,
\mathbf{X}\leftrightarrow(1|0)
,
\mathbf{Y}^{math}\leftrightarrow(1|1)
,
\mathbf{Z}\leftrightarrow(0|1)
, the vector
\mathbf{u}=(1010|0011)
determines
\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}}^{math}=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{I}_{2}\mathbf{Y}^{math}\mathbf{Z}
. In this association, the commutative relationship between Pauli operators can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{v}}=\imath^{{\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}_{sp}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}}=(-1)^{{\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}_{sp}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}}\,\Rightarrow\,{\left\langle{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}_{sp}=\begin{cases}0&,\quad\text{commute},\\
1&,\quad\text{anti-commute},\end{cases}

(21)

and here the choice of physical or mathematical convention does not matter for commutativity.

A clique is a maximal subgroup of mutually commutative Pauli operators. There are
q+1
cliques consisting of
q-1
traceless Pauli operators and the identity
\mathbf{I}_{q}
. Each clique is associated with a label
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\cup\{\infty\}
. The generators for these cliques are derived from the field structure. In the bitwise representation with
\mathbf{e}_{0}=(100\cdots 0),\mathbf{e}_{1}=(010\cdots 0),\cdots,\mathbf{e}_{k-1}=(000\cdots 01)
, these are given by:

•

(
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\infty
)
The clique is given by
\mathcal{C}_{\infty}=\{(\mathbf{0}|\mathbf{b}):\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}
, generated by
\mathcal{G}_{\infty}=\{(\mathbf{0}|\mathbf{e}_{j}):1\leq j\leq k\}
. This is the computational basis, as the generator simply matches the Pauli Z operators for each qubit.

•

(
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
)
The clique is given by
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\{(\mathbf{a}|\lambda\bullet\mathbf{a}):\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}
, generated by
\mathcal{G}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\{(\mathbf{e}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{e}_{j}):0\leq j\leq k-1\}
.

Let us denote the
physical
generators by
\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},j}^{phy}=\mathbf{P}_{(\mathbf{e}_{j}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{e}_{j})}^{phy}
(it is obvious for
\mathbf{Z}_{j}=\mathbf{G}_{\infty,j}^{phy}
). Because the operators in a clique commute, they share a common eigenbasis, which serves as an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{C}^{q}
. Each clique determined by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
amounts to the
physical
eigenspace:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}^{phy}=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}+(-1)^{a_{j}}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},j}^{phy}}{2}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in GF(2^{k})}(-1)^{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{b})\mathbf{P}_{(\mathbf{b}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{b})}^{phy}

where
\phi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{b})\in\{\pm 1\}
is the phase correction arising from (
21
):

\displaystyle\prod_{j:b_{j}=1}\mathbf{G}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},j}^{phy}=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{b})\mathbf{P}_{(\mathbf{b}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{b})}^{phy}.

The
q+1
collection of orthonormal bases (with identifications via the eigenspace)
\{\mathbf{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}^{phy}:\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is called the MUB.

Any self-adjoint matrix on the
k
-qubit system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
can be decomposed into the Pauli basis

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\rho}=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in\{0,1\}^{2k}}\mathbf{r}_{\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{P}_{\mathbf{u}}^{phy}\in\mathbb{C}_{sa}^{q\times q}.

Hence,
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{q^{2}}
is called the generalized Bloch vector. If
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{q\times q})
is a density matrix, then
\mathbf{r}_{(\mathbf{0}|\mathbf{0})}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]=1
, and the coefficients give the expected probability of the Bernoulli distribution under the Pauli observable. In contrast, under the MUB tomography, the expected probabilities are given by the Walsh–Hadamard Transform (WHT) of the Bloch vector: for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\cup\{\infty\},\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
,

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\text{Tr}(\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}^{phy})=\frac{1}{2^{k}}\sum_{\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{b})\mathbf{r}_{(\mathbf{b}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{b})},

which is the Walsh–Hadamard Transform of the Bloch vector restricted to the clique defined by
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
.
If
p_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}\sim\text{Binom}(r,d_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}])/r
is the estimate for
d_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]
, then the LSE under the MUB tomography is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\cup\{\infty\}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}p_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}-\mathbf{I}_{q}.

To simply compute its generalized Bloch vector, we can use the inverse WHT: for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\cup\{\infty\},\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{k}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
,

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{(\mathbf{0}|\mathbf{0})}^{\text{LSE}}=1,\quad\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{(\mathbf{b}|\boldsymbol{\lambda}\bullet\mathbf{b})}^{\text{LSE}}=\phi_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}(\mathbf{b})\sum_{\mathbf{a}\in\{0,1\}^{k}}(-1)^{\mathbf{a}^{\top}\mathbf{b}}p_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{a}}.

Finally, similar to the FFT algorithm, we employ the fast WHT algorithm that reduces the computational complexity from
O(2^{2k})
to
O(k\cdot 2^{k})
.

Example C.1

(Generic Kernel)
.

Let
X=\{1,2,\cdots,l\}
to simplify the notation, and consider a POVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}=\{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{\nu}_{l}\}
on a quantum system
\mathcal{Q}
. Given a positive semi-definite Gram matrix
\mathbf{K}=[K(i,j)]_{i,j=1}^{l}\in\mathbb{F}^{l\times l}
defining a scalar-valued reproducing kernel
K
on
X
, it is convenient to associate any function
f:X\to\mathbb{F}
with a vector via evaluation:

(f:X\to\mathbb{F})\,\cong\,\mathbf{f}\in\mathbb{F}^{l},\quad\mathbf{f}_{i}=f(i),\ i=1,2,\cdots,l.

We can identify the RKHS
\mathcal{R}(K)
with the column space of the Gram matrix,
\mathrm{Ran}(\mathbf{K})\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{l}
, equipped with the inner product
{\left\langle{\mathbf{f}},{\mathbf{g}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)}=\mathbf{g}^{*}\mathbf{K}^{\dagger}\mathbf{f}
. Under this vectorization, the point evaluation feature map
k_{i}=K(\cdot,i)\in\mathcal{R}(K)
simply corresponds to the
i
-th column of
\mathbf{K}
, denoted by
\mathbf{K}_{\cdot i}
.
For any density matrix
\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{F}_{sa,1}^{q\times q})
, the classical covariance embedding of the induced probability measure
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}\mathbf{w}_{i}k_{i}k_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)),\quad\mathbf{w}_{i}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\right]

Because the rank-one operator
k_{i}k_{i}^{*}
acts on
f
via
{\left\langle{f},{k_{i}}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)}k_{i}=f(i)k_{i}
, its matrix representation on
\mathbb{F}^{l}
maps
\mathbf{f}\mapsto\mathbf{f}_{i}\mathbf{K}_{\cdot i}
. Consequently, the full operator acts as
\sum_{i=1}^{l}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\right]\mathbf{f}_{i}\mathbf{K}_{\cdot i}
, which precisely corresponds to the matrix multiplication
\mathbf{K}\operatorname{Diag}(\mathbf{w})
.
Lifting this to the Quantum Covariance Embedding (QCE) framework, the tensorized operator becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(k_{i}k_{i}^{*})\otimes\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{\infty}^{+}(\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}).

Its action on an elementary tensor
f\otimes\phi\in\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}
yields:

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(f\otimes\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(k_{i}k_{i}^{*}f)\otimes(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{l}(f(i)k_{i})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\phi).

We can explicitly verify that this construction recovers the tensorized Bochner integral identity. For any
f,g\in\mathcal{R}(K)
and
\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{Q}
:

\displaystyle{\left\langle{\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}}(f\otimes\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}{\left\langle{(f(i)k_{i})\otimes(\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\phi)},{g\otimes\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)\otimes\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{l}f(i){\left\langle{k_{i}},{g}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)}{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\sum_{i=1}^{l}f(i)\overline{g(i)}{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\nu}_{i}\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{Q}}=\int_{X}f(x)\overline{g(x)}\ \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\phi,\psi}(x).

.
Given an unknown density
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}})\in\mathbb{S}(\mathbb{C}_{+}^{2\times 2})
with Bloch vector
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{B}^{3}
, the expected outcome probabilities are:

\displaystyle d_{i,\pm}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}\pm\mathbf{u}_{i}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}\right]=\frac{1\pm{\left\langle{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{u}_{i}}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}}{2}.

Then, the estimated Bloch vector
\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}
for
\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}
is obtained by the standard OLS inversion:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}=\bar{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\bar{\mathbf{p}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad\bar{\mathbf{D}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top},\quad\bar{\mathbf{p}}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2p_{i,+}-1)\mathbf{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{B}^{3},

which follows from the geometric action of the Gram superoperator derived in
Theorem

4.8
:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}=\left(\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}}{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathbf{P}}{n}\right)=\left(\frac{\mathfrak{D}^{*}\mathfrak{D}}{n}\right)^{-1}\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}+\bar{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}=\frac{\mathbf{I}_{2}+\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}\cdot\operatorname{vec}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}}{2}.

Because
\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}-\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}|\mathbf{a}\right]
, the operator MSE can be explicitly mapped to the design geometry:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\frac{1}{2nr}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\bar{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\right]-\operatorname{Tr}\left[\bar{\mathbf{D}}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{a})^{2}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]\right).

In the special case where
\{\mathbf{O}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
forms a
1/3
-unitary design (i.e.,
\bar{\mathbf{D}}=\mathbf{I}_{3}/3
), the estimator simplifies directly to
\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}=3\bar{\mathbf{p}}
, and the MSE elegantly reduces to the isotropic bound derived in
Theorem

4.14
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]=\frac{3(3-\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2})}{2nr}.

Analogous to the rebit case (
|\hat{z}^{\text{LSE}}|_{\mathbb{C}}>1
), if
\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}>1
, the constrained optimal solution is strictly obtained by mapping the vector back to the physical boundary:
\hat{\mathbf{a}}_{+}^{\text{LSE}}=\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}/\|\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, amounting to a pure state.

Finally, incorporating the classical bit-flip noise defined in
Section

3.3.1
, the attenuated expectation becomes
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{\text{LSE}}|\mathbf{a}]=(1-2\eta)\mathbf{a}
, and the corresponding corrupted
\mathrm{MSE}[\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}^{\text{LSE}}|\boldsymbol{\rho}]
is:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2nr}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left[\bar{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}\right]-(1-2\eta)^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\bar{\mathbf{D}}^{-2}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{a})^{2}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\right)\right]\right)+4\eta^{2}\|\mathbf{a}\|_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}^{2}.

C.1
Discrete Modular Error in Qudits

Consider a qudit system
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{q}
with the orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{0},\cdots,\phi_{q-1}\}
. Let
\mathbf{N}
be the number operator defined by:

\displaystyle\mathbf{N}=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}k\ \mathbf{\Pi}_{k},\quad\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}=\phi_{k}\phi_{k}^{*},\ X=\sigma(\mathbf{N})=\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

(22)

While the ideal PVM is
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}=\sum_{k}\delta_{k}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}
, consider a scenario standard in high-dimensional quantum communication where detector outcomes are subject to
modular additive noise

[
30
]
:

\displaystyle Y_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:=(Y+\varepsilon)\pmod{q},\quad\mathbb{P}(Y=\lambda_{k}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho})=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right],\quad\varepsilon\sim\text{Categorical}(\boldsymbol{\eta}),

where
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{0},\dots,\eta_{q-1})
represents the noise profile satisfying
\eta_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\eta_{k}=1
. The resulting POVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}}
is the convolution of the ideal PVM with the noise:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\{j\})=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\mathbb{P}(Y_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=j|Y=k)\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}\eta_{(j-k)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}.

Proposition C.3

(QMD of Modular Noise)
.

Let
\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{C}_{+}^{q\times q}
be a Gram matrix associated to a kernel
K:\mathbb{Z}_{q}\times\mathbb{Z}_{q}\to\mathbb{C}
. For each basis index
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, define the diagonal discrepancy matrix

\mathbf{E}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
with entries
[\mathbf{E}_{k}]_{jj}=\delta_{jk}-\eta_{(j-k)\pmod{q}}
.
The conditional QMD between the ideal PVM
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}
and the noisy POVM in (
22
) is given by:

\displaystyle\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N}},\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}})={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right](\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{E}_{k}\mathbf{K}^{1/2})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}\leq\max_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{E}_{k}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

The upper bound is achieved by the pure state
\mathbf{\Pi}_{j}
where

\displaystyle j=\argmax\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}}{\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{E}_{k}\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

In particular, if
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{I}_{q}
, the maximum is attained by
any
basis state
\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
.

Proof of
Proposition

C.3
.

For each
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, let
\mathbf{\Pi}_{j}=\phi_{j}\phi_{j}^{*}
be the ideal projections. The effective operators for the noisy POVM are
\mathbf{\Pi}_{\boldsymbol{\eta},j}=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\eta_{(j-k)}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}
.
Given a density
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, let
\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the probability vector where
p_{k}=\operatorname{Tr}\left[\boldsymbol{\rho}\mathbf{\Pi}_{k}\right]
. The distribution of the ideal outcome is
\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\{j\})=p_{j}
, and the noisy outcome is the convolution
\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\{j\})=(\boldsymbol{\eta}\ast\mathbf{p})_{j}
. For any function
f\in\mathcal{R}(K)
, the inner product is:

\displaystyle{\left\langle{(\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}}-\mathbf{T}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}}})f},{f}\right\rangle}_{\mathcal{R}(K)}=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}|f(j)|^{2}(\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N}}(\{j\})-\boldsymbol{\nu}^{\mathbf{N},\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\{j\}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}|f(j)|^{2}\left(p_{j}-\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\eta_{(j-k)}p_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}p_{k}\left[|f(k)|^{2}-\sum_{j=0}^{q-1}\eta_{(j-k)}|f(j)|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}^{*}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}p_{k}\mathbf{E}_{k}\right)\mathbf{f},

where
\mathbf{f}=[f(0),\dots,f(q-1)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{C}^{q}
. Using the Hilbert space isomorphism
\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{u}
with
\|\mathbf{u}\|_{2}\leq 1
as in the proof of
Theorem

3.10
, the conditional QMD for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\rho}
is:

\displaystyle\operatorname{QMD}_{K}^{\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\boldsymbol{\nu},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}})

\displaystyle={\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\kern-1.07639pt\left|\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}p_{k}(\mathbf{K}^{1/2}\mathbf{E}_{k}\mathbf{K}^{1/2})\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|\kern-1.07639pt\right|}_{\infty}.

To find the maximally distinguishing state, we maximize this norm over the probability simplex
\mathbf{p}
. The spectral norm is a convex function; thus, its maximum over the simplex is attained at one of the vertices, and the bound follows immediately.
∎

Recall from
Theorem

3.10
that for qubits (
\mathcal{Q}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
), the maximally distinguishing states are independent of the kernel choice. However, for higher-dimensional qudits
(q>2)
, this invariance breaks down, and the optimal state index becomes coupled to the geometry of the kernel. Specifically, the kernel imposes a non-uniform metric over the outcome space
\mathbb{Z}_{q}
, assigning different statistical weights to the discrepancy of each eigenstate.

Lemma D.1

(Haar Measure Expectations on the Stiefel Manifold)
.

Let
\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{F}^{q}
be mutually orthogonal unit vectors (
{\left\langle{\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{F}^{q}}=0
) and let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}
.

1.

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbf{U}
follows a Haar measure on
O(q)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)},{(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathbf{S}}{q(q+2)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)},{(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\phi)}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{q}}(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\psi)(\mathbf{U}^{\top}\psi)^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{2\mathbf{S}}{q(q+2)(q-1)}.

2.

If
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
and
\mathbf{U}
follows a Haar measure on
U(q)
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)},{(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbf{S}}{q(q+1)},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)},{(\mathbf{U}^{*}\phi)}\right\rangle}_{\mathbb{C}^{q}}(\mathbf{U}^{*}\psi)(\mathbf{U}^{*}\psi)^{*}\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathbf{S}}{q(q+1)(q-1)}.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}:=\mathbf{U}\phi
and
\mathbf{w}:=\mathbf{U}\psi
. Since
\mathbf{U}
is Haar-distributed (and thus
\mathbf{U}
,
\mathbf{U}^{\top}
, and
\mathbf{U}^{*}
share the same distribution) and
{\left\langle{\phi},{\psi}\right\rangle}=0
, the pair
(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{w})
is uniformly distributed on the Stiefel manifold. We compute the expectations component-wise using the moment formulas for the Stiefel manifold
[
19
]
.

1.

Real Case (
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
):

The
(k,l)
-th entry of the target matrix is
\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{S}_{ij}\mathbb{E}[v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}v_{l}]
.
Using the fourth-order moment formula for the unit sphere:

\mathbb{E}[v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}v_{l}]=\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}}{q(q+2)}.

Substituting this into the summation:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}]\right)_{kl}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}\frac{\mathbf{S}_{ij}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})}{q(q+2)}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\delta_{kl}+\mathbf{S}_{kl}+\mathbf{S}_{lk}}{q(q+2)}.

Since
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]=0
and
\mathbf{S}
is self-adjoint (
\mathbf{S}_{lk}=\mathbf{S}_{kl}
), this simplifies to
\frac{2\mathbf{S}_{kl}}{q(q+2)}
.
For the orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{w}
, we require the joint moments. By
O(q)
-invariance, the general form for the tensor is:

\mathbb{E}[v_{i}v_{j}w_{k}w_{l}]=C_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+C_{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}).

The constants are determined by conditioning:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}\delta_{ij}=\mathbb{E}[v_{i}v_{j}{\left\langle{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle}]=\sum_{k=1}^{q}[C_{1}\delta_{ij}+2C_{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jk})]=(qC_{1}+2C_{2})\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle 0=\mathbb{E}[v_{i}w_{l}{\left\langle{\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle}]=\sum_{k=1}^{q}[C_{1}\delta_{ik}\delta_{kl}+C_{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{il})]=(C_{1}+(q+1)C_{2})\delta_{il},

yielding
C_{2}=-[q(q-1)(q+2)]^{-1}
and
C_{1}=-(q+1)C_{2}
.
The expectation is:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{\top}]\right)_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}\mathbf{S}_{ij}[C_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+C_{2}(\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}+\delta_{il}\delta_{jk})]

\displaystyle=C_{1}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\delta_{kl}+C_{2}(\mathbf{S}_{kl}+\mathbf{S}_{lk})=2C_{2}\mathbf{S}_{kl}=-\frac{2\mathbf{S}_{kl}}{q(q+2)(q-1)}.

2.

Complex Case (
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
):

The
(k,l)
-th entry is
\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{S}_{ij}\mathbb{E}[\bar{v}_{i}v_{j}v_{k}\bar{v}_{l}]
.
Using the Weingarten formula for
U(q)

[
19
]
:

\mathbb{E}[\bar{v}_{i}v_{j}v_{k}\bar{v}_{l}]=\frac{\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}}{q(q+1)}.

Substituting this into the summation:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{*}]\right)_{kl}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}\frac{\mathbf{S}_{ij}(\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl})}{q(q+1)}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\delta_{kl}+\mathbf{S}_{kl}}{q(q+1)}.

Since
\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]=0
, we obtain
\frac{\mathbf{S}_{kl}}{q(q+1)}
.
Again, for the orthogonal vectors
\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{w}
, the joint moment takes the form:
\mathbb{E}[\bar{v}_{i}v_{j}w_{k}\bar{w}_{l}]=C_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+C_{2}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}
.
Using
\mathbb{E}[\bar{v}_{i}v_{j}{\left\langle{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{w}}\right\rangle}]=q^{-1}\delta_{ij}
and
\mathbb{E}[\bar{v}_{i}w_{k}{\left\langle{\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}]=0
, we find
C_{2}=-\frac{1}{q(q^{2}-1)}
and
C_{1}=-qC_{2}
.
The expectation is:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}[{\left\langle{\mathbf{S}\mathbf{v}},{\mathbf{v}}\right\rangle}\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}^{*}]\right)_{kl}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\mathbf{S}_{ij}(C_{1}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+C_{2}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl})

\displaystyle=C_{1}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}\right]\delta_{kl}+C_{2}\mathbf{S}_{kl}=C_{2}\mathbf{S}_{kl}=-\frac{\mathbf{S}_{kl}}{q(q+1)(q-1)}.

∎

Corollary D.2

(Haar Measure Expectations for Projections)
.

Let
\mathbf{\Pi}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa}^{q\times q}
be a projection operator of rank
m
and let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{F}_{sa,0}^{q\times q}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{U}^{*})\right]\mathbf{U}\mathbf{\Pi}\mathbf{U}^{*}\right]=\frac{q-m}{q-1}\frac{\alpha_{q}\mathbf{S}}{q},\quad\alpha_{q}:=\begin{cases}(q/2+1)^{-1}&,\quad\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R},\\

,

∎

\mathbf{d}_{i}=[d_{i1}[\boldsymbol{\rho}],\cdots,d_{iq_{i}}[\boldsymbol{\rho}]]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i}=[\beta_{i1},\cdots,\beta_{iq_{i}}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{q_{i}}
. We then use the Cramér–Chernoff method to obtain the concentration inequality
[
8
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[Z\geq t\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]\leq\exp(-g(t)),\quad g(t):=\sup_{\lambda>0}(\lambda t-\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda Z}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]).

For each
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq r
, note that
\mathbb{E}[Z_{ij}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}]=0
, thus

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda Z_{ij}}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left(1+\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda Z_{ij}}-\lambda Z_{ij}-1\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log\left(1+\mathbb{E}[Z_{ij}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}]\left(e^{\lambda}-\lambda-1\right)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{ij}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}]\left(e^{\lambda}-\lambda-1\right),

and thus

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda Z}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda Z_{ij}}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}\right]\leq\frac{m_{2}}{r}\left(e^{\lambda}-\lambda-1\right),

where

\displaystyle m_{2}:=r\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{r}\mathbb{E}[Z_{ij}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}]=r\operatorname{Cov}(Z\mid\boldsymbol{\rho})={\left\langle{\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}[\mathbf{T}]},{\mathbf{T}}\right\rangle}_{2}={\left\langle{\mathfrak{A}_{K}\mathfrak{S}_{K,\boldsymbol{\rho}}\mathfrak{A}_{K}[\mathbf{S}]},{\mathbf{S}}\right\rangle}_{2}.

Consequently,
[
8
, (2.10)]
yields the concentration inequality:

\displaystyle g(t)\geq\sup_{\lambda>0}\left(\lambda t-\frac{m_{2}}{r}(e^{\lambda}-\lambda-1)\right)=\frac{m_{2}}{r}h\left(\frac{t}{m_{2}/r}\right)\geq\frac{t^{2}}{2(m_{2}/r+t/3)}.

∎
