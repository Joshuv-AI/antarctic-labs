Seizures associated with clozapine augmentation by other antipsychotics: a pharmacovigilance–pharmacodynamic analysis using VigiBase

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Antipsychotics are the primary treatment options for schizophrenia.1,2 However, approximately one-third of patients are considered to have treatment-resistant schizophrenia (TRS), 3 which is defined as insufficient response to at least two antipsychotics. 4 Clozapine is the only antipsychotic approved for the management of TRS, and it demonstrates superior efficacy compared to that of other antipsychotics. 5 Nevertheless, its efficacy is limited, with only about 40% of patients with TRS responding to treatment. 6 For patients who show insufficient response to clozapine, augmentation with additional antipsychotics may be considered. In particular, combining clozapine with aripiprazole has been suggested to be associated with a decreased risk of psychiatric rehospitalization. 7 Previous reports have also suggested that clozapine augmentation with aripiprazole is generally well tolerated. 8 However, the risk of specific adverse events associated with combinations of clozapine and individual antipsychotics remains unclear, and careful monitoring is therefore warranted during such administration.

Clozapine is associated with a high risk of central nervous system (CNS) abnormalities. Reports have indicated that approximately 1–5% of patients receiving clozapine treatment experience seizures. 9 In addition, a systematic review has indicated that clozapine is more frequently associated with electroencephalogram abnormalities and epileptiform discharges than other antipsychotics. 10 Moreover, elevated plasma concentrations of clozapine are associated with an increased risk of seizures. 11 Furthermore, a previous pharmacovigilance study suggested that concomitant use of clozapine and other antipsychotics potentially contributes to the occurrence of seizures. 12 However, the risk of seizures caused by drug–drug interactions (DDIs) between clozapine and individual antipsychotics and the pharmacological mechanisms underlying these seizures have rarely been evaluated. In addition, the receptor occupancy profiles that may contribute to this association remain unclear.

Given that the prescribing of clozapine requires strict monitoring and regulation, its use in clinical practice remains limited, 13 making it difficult to collate data from cases involving the use of clozapine combined with other antipsychotics. To address this limitation, we conducted an analysis using VigiBase, the largest global pharmacovigilance database maintained by the World Health Organization (WHO), which contains over 39 million individual case safety reports from more than 130 countries. Signal detection using spontaneous reporting systems is useful for identifying new potentially causal associations or novel aspects of known drug–adverse event relationships. 14 We adopted a pharmacovigilance–pharmacodynamic approach, which integrates pharmacovigilance data with pharmacological information, such as receptor occupancy.15,16 This approach has been applied previously to explore the potential mechanisms underlying adverse drug reactions. Specifically, we conducted this study to evaluate safety signals for seizures associated with DDIs between clozapine and individual antipsychotics and to analyze whether seizures associated with clozapine augmentation may be explained by the neurotransmitter receptor occupancy profiles of co-administered antipsychotics.

Methods

This study was a pharmacovigilance–pharmacodynamic analysis using a spontaneous reporting system. This study was reported in accordance with the REporting of A Disproportionality analysis for drUg Safety signal detection using individual case safety reports in PharmacoVigilance (READUS-PV) statement. 14

Data source

This pharmacovigilance study was conducted using VigiBase, a global database of individual case safety reports maintained by the WHO. We included all individual case safety reports recorded in VigiBase between 1990 and 2024. We constructed a relational database based on the UMCReportId consisting of three data tables: DEMO (case/demographic information), DRUG (drug information), and ADR (reaction information). Suspected duplicates in VigiBase were excluded using an automatic algorithm vigiMatch.

Target adverse events and antipsychotics

Adverse events were coded using preferred terms (PTs) according to the Japanese version of the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA/J), version 27.0. Seizures were defined based on the narrow scope of the Standardized MedDRA Query (SMQ) for convulsions (SMQ20000079), which includes 116 PTs (Supplemental Table 1).

Antipsychotics were selected based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification code N05A (excluding lithium). Drugs with no reported cases of seizures when used in combination with clozapine were excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

We assessed potential signals of DDIs using the Ω shrinkage measure model, 25 which is considered one of the most conservative algorithms for detecting such interactions. 26 To calculate signal values, we constructed 4 × 2 contingency tables that classified case reports based on whether each drug was used alone, in combination, or not at all (Supplemental Table 4). The observed count (n111) represents the actual number of cases in which the adverse event (seizure) occurred when the two drugs were used in combination. The association between neurotransmitter receptor occupancy and DDI signals was evaluated using a linear regression model. The point estimate of each signal value was set as the dependent variable, whereas the natural logarithm of the receptor occupancy was set as the independent variable. p-Values were two-sided, and values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Antipsychotics for which receptor occupancy could not be estimated due to missing data were excluded from the analysis.

To evaluate the robustness of the results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using four frequency statistical models for the assessment of DDIs: additive, 27 multiplicative, 27 combination risk ratio, 28 and chi-square statistics models. 29 The methods used for calculating signal values in these four models are summarized in Supplemental Table 4.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 38,758,077 reports were recorded in VigiBase between 1990 and 2024, with 230,371 (0.6%) involving clozapine. Among 161,128 seizure cases, 1877 (1.2%) were associated with clozapine. The antipsychotics most commonly used in combination with clozapine were risperidone (n = 6967) and olanzapine (n = 6127). Details of the population characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Signals of drug–drug interactions

We analyzed 38 antipsychotics for which at least one case of seizures was reported when used in combination with clozapine. The Ω shrinkage measure model indicated that 10 antipsychotics showed DDI signals with clozapine (Figure 1). These antipsychotics were amisulpride, lurasidone, penfluridol, zotepine, chlorpromazine, haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone, sulpiride, and zuclopenthixol. The Ω025 values for amisulpride, lurasidone, penfluridol, and zotepine were 0.74, 0.44, 1.26, and 0.82, respectively. These signals were consistently detected across all other frequency statistical models. The Ω025 values for chlorpromazine, haloperidol, olanzapine, risperidone, sulpiride, and zuclopenthixol were 0.04, 0.06, 0.17, 0.11, 0.64, and 0.19, respectively. These signals were observed in the additive, multiplicative, and chi-square statistics models. The sensitivity analysis results for all antipsychotics, obtained using the alternative frequency statistical models, are presented in Supplemental Table 5.

Association between receptor occupancy and signals of drug–drug interactions

Of the 38 antipsychotics evaluated, 22, for which receptor occupancy estimates were available, were included in the analysis. The results of the linear regression models evaluating the association between receptor occupancy and the point estimates of signal values in the Ω shrinkage measure model are summarized in Table 2. The results showed that dopamine D4 receptor occupancy was significantly associated with signal values (β = 0.295, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.119–0.471, p = 0.002); the corresponding scatter plot is shown in Figure 2. This significant association between D4 receptor occupancy and DDI signal values was consistently observed across all other frequency statistical models (Supplemental Table 6). In addition, dopamine D1 receptor showed a significant association with signal values in the Ω shrinkage measure model (β = 0.222, 95% CI: 0.049–0.395, p = 0.015); however, consistent associations were not observed in the additive, multiplicative, and combination risk ratio models (Supplemental Table 6).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the safety signals of seizures associated with concomitant use of clozapine with other antipsychotics. In addition, we analyzed potential relationships between these signals and neurotransmitter receptor profiles. Only a few studies have been conducted to systematically evaluate the safety of combining clozapine with other antipsychotics. Given that clozapine monotherapy itself is associated with a clinically relevant risk of seizures, understanding the safety implications of concomitant antipsychotic use is particularly important. Clozapine augmentation is generally considered when adequate efficacy has not been achieved with clozapine treatment and, in some cases, for the management of adverse effects associated with clozapine, including weight gain or sedation. In such cases, patients may be exposed to higher clozapine plasma concentrations, greater cumulative exposure, and longer treatment durations, all of which may be associated with an increased risk of CNS abnormalities, including seizures. Evaluating the safety of adding other antipsychotics under these clinical conditions is expected to provide clinically meaningful insights.

Given the distinct pharmacological profiles of antipsychotics, the risk of adverse effects, including seizures, is likely to vary depending on the specific combination used. Although concomitant use of clozapine with other antipsychotics is associated with a risk of seizures, 12 the degree to which each agent contributes to this risk may differ substantially. In the present study, DDI signals with clozapine were detected for only 10 antipsychotics classified under ATC code N05A: amisulpride, chlorpromazine, haloperidol, lurasidone, olanzapine, penfluridol, risperidone, sulpiride, zotepine, and zuclopenthixol. These antipsychotics showed consistent signals in at least three of the four frequency models, supporting the robustness of our findings. In phase II and III clinical trials, clozapine, olanzapine, and quetiapine showed significantly higher seizure incidence ratios than placebo, whereas no significant differences were observed for aripiprazole, risperidone, or ziprasidone. 30 These findings are somewhat inconsistent with the results of the present study, suggesting that the risk of seizures associated with co-administration of clozapine with other antipsychotics may not necessarily reflect the risk of seizures associated with each antipsychotic when used alone.

Evidence regarding the safety of clozapine augmentation with other antipsychotics remains limited. A systematic review on various clozapine augmentation strategies indicated that the quality of the included studies was low, which precluded definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of any particular antipsychotic. 31 One approach to selecting antipsychotics for augmentation is to consider agents with pharmacological profiles that complement that of clozapine, such as drugs with high affinity for dopamine D2 receptors. 32 In a nationwide cohort study of patients on clozapine augmentation therapy, risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, and aripiprazole were reported as the most frequently prescribed antipsychotics. 33 In the present study, each of these antipsychotics was frequently co-administered with clozapine, with approximately 5000 or more cases recorded, reflecting their common clinical use. However, given that signals of DDIs were detected for risperidone and olanzapine in our analysis, caution should be exercised when combining clozapine with these agents. In several randomized controlled trials conducted to investigate the addition of risperidone to clozapine treatment, only commonly observed adverse effects such as extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinemia were reported.34 –36 However, given the small sample sizes of these studies, their ability to detect rare adverse events such as seizures may have been limited. Although the present study was conducted using a spontaneous reporting system, it was a large-scale pharmacovigilance analysis that enabled identification of potential associations between concomitant use of clozapine and other antipsychotics and the occurrence of seizures. Notably, antipsychotics for which no safety signals were detected cannot be assumed to carry a low risk of seizures. Although the absence of signals may suggest that these antipsychotics are associated with a comparatively lower risk of seizures, further research is needed to confirm their safety profiles.

In the present study, a significant association was observed between DDI signal values for seizures and dopamine D4 receptor occupancy. The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying clozapine-associated seizures have not been completely elucidated. Although direct evidence linking dopamine D4 receptor occupancy to seizure development is unavailable, several lines of evidence from human and experimental studies suggest the biological plausibility of D4 receptor involvement. Postmortem human brain studies have demonstrated D4 receptor mRNA or binding sites in the hippocampal formation, medial temporal cortex, entorhinal cortex, and amygdala,37 –39 which are regions implicated in limbic seizure generation. 40 A human genetic study in patients with temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis reported an association between DRD4_VNTR and family history of epilepsy, providing limited clinical evidence for the potential involvement of DRD4-related pathways in epilepsy. 41 In vitro studies have shown that dopamine D4 receptors activate G protein-regulated inwardly rectifying potassium channels. 42 In addition, dopamine D4 receptor-deficient mice exhibit increased spontaneous synaptic activity and more frequent, prolonged paroxysmal discharges induced by epileptogenic agents compared with wild-type controls. 43 Clozapine has substantially higher affinity for dopamine D4 receptors than for dopamine D2 and D3 receptors. 44 Collectively, these findings suggest that concomitant use of antipsychotics with high D4 receptor affinity may increase the risk of seizures when combined with clozapine. In contrast, aripiprazole, which exhibits partial agonist activity at the D4 receptor, 45 showed no signals of DDIs in the present study, supporting the role of D4 receptor antagonism in the occurrence of seizures. Conversely, no association between signals of DDIs and the dopamine D2 receptor, the principal target of most antipsychotics, was observed in the present study. This result may be explained by the low affinity of clozapine to the dopamine D2 receptor, suggesting that seizures associated with concomitant use of antipsychotics are unlikely to be mediated by dopamine D2 receptors.

Several antipsychotics showed slight deviations from the regression line in the present study. For example, brexpiprazole, which has a dopamine D4 receptor occupancy estimated to be comparable to that of haloperidol, exhibited no signal of DDIs in any of the frequency statistical models. One possible explanation for this finding is that its partial agonistic activity at serotonin 5-HT1A receptors, which is associated with an increased seizure threshold, may have attenuated seizure-related signals. However, no Ki values obtained using the reference radioligand were available for brexpiprazole; therefore, its serotonin 5-HT1A receptor affinity was not included in the analysis. Other hypotheses regarding the pathophysiology of clozapine-associated seizures involve glycine receptors, 46 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, 47 and gamma-aminobutyric acid type A receptors, 48 indicating that the mechanism underlying the occurrence of the seizures cannot be explained solely by dopamine D4 receptor activity. It should also be noted that only approximately 140 cases in the present study involved concomitant use of brexpiprazole and clozapine; therefore, the possibility of a false-negative finding cannot be ruled out.

Dopamine D1 receptor occupancy showed a potential association with signal values in the Ω shrinkage measure model. However, this finding was not consistently reproduced across three of the four frequency statistical models, indicating a lack of robust statistical support. Increased dopamine D1 receptor function has been implicated in the pathophysiology of limbic epilepsy. 40 Given that most antipsychotics act as dopamine D1 receptor antagonists, this observed association appears somewhat contradictory. This apparent inconsistency, combined with the limited reproducibility of the finding, suggests that the observed signal may not reliably reflect a true pharmacodynamic relationship and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

The present study is valuable as, to the best of our knowledge, it is the first to evaluate the association between clozapine augmentation with other antipsychotics and the occurrence of seizures. However, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, this study was focused on the evaluation of DDIs based on frequency statistical models; therefore, clozapine dose, plasma concentration, concomitant antipsychotic exposure, and other factors that may be associated with seizure occurrence could not be considered. CNS abnormalities caused by clozapine are closely related to the dose and plasma concentration of the drug.11,49 In addition, younger age, concomitant use of lithium, and a history of convulsive disorders may be potential risk factors for clozapine-associated seizures.9,12,50 Consistent with these findings, data used in the present study indicated that seizures were observed more frequently in patients aged 17 years or younger. Second, our pharmacodynamic approach, which is based on receptor affinity and occupancy derived from in vitro data, may not fully capture pharmacological effects in clinical settings. Receptor occupancy was estimated using receptor occupancy theory rather than direct imaging-based methods such as PET. Therefore, the estimated values should not be interpreted as actual in vivo receptor occupancy but rather as relative indicators of target engagement. In particular, actual receptor occupancy was not measured in individual patients, and variability in receptor occupancy resulting from differences in clozapine or concomitant antipsychotic exposure could not be assessed. Furthermore, data on brain unbound drug concentrations, which are considered better predictors of receptor occupancy, 51 were unavailable; therefore, CU was estimated using total plasma concentrations. In addition, although Ki values have been reported in various studies, we limited our data to those provided by the PDSP to ensure consistency and reliability. Furthermore, we standardized the use of radioligands by selecting the same reference ligand for each receptor type. However, this approach resulted in missing Ki values for certain antipsychotics. Finally, as only cases with adverse events are registered in the spontaneous reporting system, denominator information (i.e., the total number of patients treated with clozapine) was not available. 52 Moreover, as not all adverse events are reported in the system, the possibility of reporting bias cannot be completely ruled out. Therefore, the signal values cannot be directly compared, and the results of this study should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Given the limited generalizability of these findings to clinical settings, further large-scale epidemiological studies are warranted to establish any causal relationships.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that amisulpride, chlorpromazine, haloperidol, lurasidone, olanzapine, penfluridol, risperidone, sulpiride, zotepine, and zuclopenthixol may be associated with a potential risk of seizures when combined with clozapine. In addition, antipsychotics associated with seizure signals when combined with clozapine tended to exhibit higher estimated dopamine D4 receptor occupancy. These observations suggest a possible association between dopamine D4 receptor occupancy and seizure signals observed during clozapine augmentation. Further large-scale epidemiological studies are needed to validate these findings.
