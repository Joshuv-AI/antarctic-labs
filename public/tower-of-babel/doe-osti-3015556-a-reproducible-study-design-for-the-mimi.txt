 ABSTRACT
Open, tabular electronic health record (EHR) datasets such as MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV have become
critical resources for developing machine learning (ML) models addressing clinical prediction tasks, in-
cluding hospital readmission, length of stay, and in-hospital mortality (IHM). While MIMIC-III has ben-
efited from well-established preprocessing pipelines and standardized feature sets, MIMIC-IV remains
comparatively challenging to work with because there are no standardized benchmarks to support repro-
ducibility and comparability across studies. To address this limitation, we present a rigorously curated
MIMIC-IV custom feature set optimized for IHM prediction, constructed through a reproducible prepro-
cessing pipeline and feature selection strategy. Our pipeline integrates three core components: (1) adap-
tation of the flexible MIMIC-IV-Data Pipeline for data cleaning and normalization; (2) a custom feature
selection framework to reduce dimensionality while preserving the clinical signal; and (3) a clinically in-
formed grouping of variables using established indicators such as the Charlson Comorbidity Index. This
integrated approach ensures computational efficiency, mitigates overfitting to rare events, and emphasizes
clinically meaningful predictors. The resulting feature set provides a transparent, reproducible foundation
for benchmarking ML models on MIMIC-IV, supporting standardized evaluation and facilitating clinical
translation of predictive modeling research.
 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 BACKGROUND
Predicting in-hospital mortality (IHM) using electronic health record (EHR) data has been a longstanding
focus in clinical machine learning (ML). Numerous studies have explored methods for cohort construction,
feature extraction, and model evaluation, particularly leveraging the MIMIC-III database (Johnson, Pol-
lard, and Mark 2016; A. E. Johnson et al. 2016). These works have established reproducible preprocessing
frameworks and baseline tasks that foster comparative research in clinical prediction.
However, the release of MIMIC-IV introduced a substantially expanded and restructured dataset (Johnson
et al. 2023; A. Johnson et al. 2024). Compared with MIMIC-III, MIMIC-IV includes a larger set of hetero-
geneous clinical variables, especially within the dynamic, time-series chart data, encompassing over 400
potential measurement types. Many of these variables are sparse, redundant, or semantically overlapping,
posing challenges for feature dimensionality reduction, interpretability, and computational tractability. Ad-
dressing these challenges requires robust, standardized preprocessing pipelines and clinically informed
feature selection strategies that can serve as benchmarks for reproducible research on MIMIC-IV.
Several pipelines have been developed to facilitate standardized EHR preprocessing. The MIMIC-Extract
pipeline introduced by Wang et al. 2020 provided a reproducible framework for MIMIC-III, implementing
unit normalization, outlier handling, and feature aggregation to produce structured multivariate time-series
suitable for machine learning tasks. This pipeline became a de facto standard for mortality and other com-
mon benchmarks. Building on this foundation, Gupta et al. 2022 introduced the MIMIC-IV-Data Pipeline,
a data processing pipeline for MIMIC-IV that adapted some of the MIMIC-Extract principles to the up-
dated database schema and supports multiple prediction tasks. Although the MIMIC-IV-Data Pipeline
supports flexible, customizable extraction settings and feature sets, this flexibility can reduce cross-study
reproducibility: researchers who select different configurations may produce results that cannot be directly
compared.
Using MIMIC-III, benchmark tasks such as the one proposed by Harutyunyan et al. 2019 established con-
sistent definitions for IHM prediction, including prediction windows, cohort criteria, and standardized
feature subsets. However, no equivalent benchmark feature set currently exists for MIMIC-IV, leaving a
significant gap in reproducible and comparable IHM modeling.

 1

1.2 GAPS AND MOTIVATION
The need for standardized, interpretable, and reproducible feature sets in IHM prediction is underscored
by recent work emphasizing pipeline consistency, fairness, and feature interpretability. Using the MIMIC-
Extract outputs, Suriyakumar et al. 2021 demonstrated the importance of pipeline alignment when apply-
ing differentially private learning. Their study highlights the risks of retaining rare or long-tailed features
and motivates rigorous feature filtering strategies. Similarly, Meng et al. 2022 conducted an interpretability
and fairness analysis of deep learning models on MIMIC-IV, identifying features most influential across
multiple attribution techniques. Their findings highlighted how reliance on demographic or long-tail fea-
tures can compromise fairness and generalizability. Complementary to these interpretability efforts, statis-
tical filtering techniques such as divergence-based measures (e.g., Kullback–Leibler and Jensen–Shannon
divergence) have been used with MIMIC-III by Yoon et al. 2023 to identify chart variables whose distri-
butions differ most between clinical outcome classes. Although not yet widely applied to MIMIC-IV, such
methods offer an objective basis for assessing variable discriminability.
Time-series interpretability also has been explored through recurrent neural networks (RNNs). Na Pat-
talung, Ingviya, and Chaichulee 2021 demonstrated the value of including derived features – such as trends,
slopes, and deltas – in explaining temporal contributions to mortality risk, reinforcing the importance of
dynamic features beyond static values. In parallel, Wang, Yin, and Zhang 2024 examined multimodal fu-
sion approaches integrating physiological signals, clinical notes, and imaging, identifying procedural fea-
tures that are particularly predictive of patient outcomes. Collectively, these works reveal a common lim-
itation: while MIMIC-III pipelines and benchmarks have matured, MIMIC-IV lacks a unified, clinically
validated feature framework for IHM prediction. Current pipelines either prioritize flexibility or predictive
performance, but they do not provide a standardized option to serve as a common benchmark for a given
task.

1.3 CONTRIBUTIONS
To address these limitations, we propose a rigorous, reproducible preprocessing and feature selection study
design for IHM prediction using MIMIC-IV. Our approach introduces:
 1. Standardized preprocessing, based on an adaptation of the MIMIC-IV-Data Pipeline, ensuring
 reproducible data cleaning, normalization, and cohort construction.
 2. Custom feature selection, integrating MIMIC-III/IV literature insights, statistical measures, and
 expert clinical review to reduce redundancy while retaining meaningful signal.
 3. Clinically informed grouping, leveraging established indices (e.g., the Charlson Comorbidity In-
 dex) to prioritize variables with known relevance to mortality outcomes.
This framework bridges the gap between flexible pipelines and static, overly constrained feature lists. By
emphasizing reproducibility, interpretability, and clinical grounding, our curated feature set aims to serve
as a benchmark foundation for standardized IHM modeling on MIMIC-IV, enabling transparent compari-
son and progress toward clinically translatable machine learning tools.

 2. METHODS
2.1 DATASET
MIMIC-IV (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV) is an open-access (training required, Gold-
berger et al. 2000) medical dataset that comprises de-identified EHRs from patients admitted to the Beth
Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, MA, USA from 2008–2019 (Johnson et al. 2023; A. Johnson
et al. 2024). We used MIMIC-IV v2.2 (01/06/2023), which contains EHRs from 299,712 patients across
431,231 admissions and 73,181 ICU stays. The dataset includes structured clinical data from diagnoses,
medications, procedures, output events, chart events (lab results, vital signs), and admission statistics.

2.2 PREPROCESSING PIPELINE
Preprocessing the MIMIC-IV dataset for the IHM task was accomplished using the MIMIC-IV Data Pipeline
by Gupta et al. 2022 along with our custom feature selection and groupings integrations.

2.2.1 MIMIC-IV pipeline
The MIMIC-IV Data Pipeline Gupta et al. 2022 is a standardized preprocessing tool for the MIMIC-IV
dataset that provides four main modules: data extraction, data preprocessing, predictive modeling, and
model evaluation. We utilized its data extraction and data preprocessing steps to prepare the dataset for
IHM task-specific ML experiments. By default, the pipeline applies a filtering to adult patients (age ≥ 18)
and converts unit measurements. Other key configurations we used for the IHM task included: (i) using
the ICU data without disease-specific filters, (ii) time-series extraction limited to the first 48 hours of ICU
admission, and (iii) features sourced from diagnosis, output events, chart events, procedures, and medica-
tions. Outliers in chart events were imputed (98th percentile) and then a forward fill and mean imputation
was applied across missing values. Table 1 provides a summary of the specific pipeline settings used.

2.2.2 Feature selection and analysis
Feature subsets were selected based on clinical relevance, data completeness, and prior work on IHM pre-
diction. Key metrics used to evaluate the features included percent missingness and divergence scores:
Kullback-Leibler (KL) and Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence. The KL and JS divergence scores quantify
the distance between the two probability distributions: IHM positive and negative labels. The selected fea-
tures from medications, procedures, output events, and chart events were provided to the preprocessing
pipeline as itemid lists for each category. All diagnosis features were retained initially and later filtered
and aggregated into a single comorbidities score (see section 2.2.3). A detailed analysis of feature selec-
tion, including correlation checks and domain validation, is presented in the Results section.

2.2.3 Custom groupings
We implemented a custom Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) calculation using the diagnosis features.
The CCI score, which weights 19 comorbidity categories (e.g., diabetes, heart failure) and incorporates
age-based adjustments, was selected for its established utility in mortality risk prediction (Charlson et
al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). The clinical grouping of diagnosis features to the CCI score also significantly
reduced feature dimensionality (from 1520 available diagnosis features to 1 CCI score). We computed
the CCI score using the ICD-10 codes and a custom Python version of the SQL-based CCI calculation
(A. E. W. Johnson et al. 2018).
The original 33 ethnicity categories were custom grouped into 6 broad categories (White, Black, Hispanic,
Asian, Unknown/Declined, Other/Multiple) to reduce sparsity and align with prior studies.

 3. RESULTS
The experimental design proceeded in two stages: feature analysis and model evaluation, with a focused
objective of quantifying feature utility, constructing machine learning-ready datasets, and benchmarking
predictive performance for IHM prediction. The results below detail the rationale for feature selection, the
statistical evaluation of chart features, and the initial performance of benchmark models on the curated
datasets.

3.1 FEATURE ANALYSIS
To address the high dimensionality of MIMIC-IV’s total available features (static and dynamic) and to
enhance the generalizability of IHM models, we employed a feature selection workflow integrating prior
research findings, medical domain knowledge, and empirical signal evaluation. This iterative process in-
volved three key steps: 1) initial feature seeding based on prior literature, 2) subsequent refinement through
clinical review, and 3) statistical screening. The following subsections delineate this methodology.

3.1.1 Step 1: Seeding with Prior Literature
To establish an initial, comprehensive candidate set of relevant features, we began by compiling a union of
features referenced across multiple IHM modeling studies utilizing MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV data [1–8].
This literature-based approach allowed us to leverage existing knowledge and identify features that have
demonstrated value in previous research. Recognizing the substantial number of available chart features
(>400) and their temporal dynamics, we prioritized the evaluation and filtering of these variables. Our ap-
proach was informed by the observation that IHM modeling studies demonstrated considerable variability
in feature selection – ranging from broad groupings to highly specific subsets – often driven by feature im-
portance analyses.
Studies that applied feature importance, such as Meng et al. 2022, were particularly helpful in selection,
as they provided insight into the relative contribution of individual features to IHM prediction models.
Specifically, for chart features, we identified variables commonly employed in MIMIC-III IHM tasks (e.g.,
heart rate, mean arterial pressure, creatinine) and those consistently ranked highly in interpretability anal-
yses, such as those reported by Meng et al. 2022. For procedural features, we selected a pre-defined set,
mirroring that included in Wang et al. [8], focusing on high-impact interventions – notably mechanical
ventilation and surgical procedures. All diagnosis features were retained at this stage and consolidated into
a single comorbidity grouping score, as described below.
A challenge in evaluating features across MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV is that these datasets do not retain
the same labels (itemid) for features, leading to ambiguity in cross-study comparisons. To address this
issue, we conducted keyword regex within MIMIC-III feature lists to identify potential equivalent features
within MIMIC-IV. This approach enabled us to identify and incorporate features that, while differing in
nomenclature, captured the same underlying clinical concept.

3.1.2 Step 2: Clinical Review in Pruning
Building upon the initial feature set, we incorporated a clinical perspective to prioritize variables with a
high clinical relevance and low potential for data quality issues. Based on this clinical review, we con-
structed a short list of chart event keywords deemed particularly salient for IHM prediction and possess-
ing a low anticipated missing rate: diastolic blood pressure, systolic blood pressure, mean arterial blood
pressure, heart rate, temperature, respiration rate, oxygen saturation, fraction of inspired oxygen, blood pH,

 5

and blood glucose. These variables were selected due to their frequent documentation in clinical records,
direct physiological relevance to mortality outcomes, and established use in prior IHM models.
By evaluating the diagnosis features, we decided to implement a CCI score grouping. The MIMIC-IV-
Data Pipeline does not provide this grouping option, but it does allow for ICD-9 to ICD-10 conversion.
We incorporated the CCI score, given its strong predictive value in IHM across various patient popula-
tions (Charlson et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2023). The CCI score details and implementation statistics are
presented below.
We also incorporated features from other clinically meaningful categories, including input (medications),
output, and procedures. Input features were selected based on frequency of administration across the co-
hort and documented associations with mortality outcomes in prior studies. Output events, including gas-
tric tube drainage, stool, and chest tube, were retained as they represent direct measures of fluid loss and
organ function. These features provide insight into gastrointestinal activity, stability, and recovery, which
are all considered key indicators of clinical trajectory and mortality risk. Procedural features we adopted
from Wang, Yin, and Zhang 2024 include: PICC line, dialysis, and intubation. These interventions often
mark critical stages of illness severity and clinical escalation. Although these non-chart feature categories
were not the primary focus of this iteration, their inclusion supports a more comprehensive representation
of the clinical course. Future work should refine and expand these domains to enhance interpretability and
generalizability across predictive modeling tasks.

3.1.3 Step 3: Statistical Screening
The remaining features underwent additional statistical screening to ensure that only the most informative
variables remained in the feature set. We specifically prioritized the chart event features, which are tempo-
ral and represent about 20% of the total number of variables. Pruning this subset had the greatest potential
to reduce dimensionality. Similarly, we prioritized grouping diagnoses into the CCI score, given the large
number of 1520 total diagnosis codes and their well-documented role as risk factors for IHM. Below, we
describe our strategies for filtering chart events and grouping diagnoses into the CCI score to reduce fea-
ture dimensionality while preserving clinically relevant information for ML training and predictions.
First, we assessed the overall statistics for all chart features in MIMIC-IV. To prioritize features, we uti-
lized two key metrics:
 • Percent missingness provided an initial assessment of data sparsity and potential data quality is-
 sues. Temporal features with high missingness may negatively impact predictive power due to data
 sparsity, though some features may remain valuable upon imputation.
 • KL/JS divergence metrics quantified the discriminative power of features relative to IHM labels.
 Features with extreme divergence (e.g., rare events) may require dropping to avoid overfitting.
Histograms for percent missingness, KL divergence, and JS divergence across all available chart features
were constructed to visualize these distributions before any selection. The percent missingness distribu-
tion (Figure 1) revealed 43 features with missingness rates ≥ 10% and a strong positive skew (calculated
skewness = 3.7, where 0 is a normal distribution and within −1 to 1 indicates minor skew). Both KL and
JS divergence plots (Figure 2 and Figure 3, respectively) indicate that roughly half (∼200) of the features
are potentially moderately correlated with the IHM task. The divergence plots showed skewness = 0.90
and 2.45 for KL and JS divergence, respectively.
These metrics guided feature selection, prioritizing clinically relevant variables with low missingness and
moderate divergence from IHM labels. Yoon et al. 2023 also used KL/JS divergence to select top features

CCI score grouping. Grouping scores, such as the CCI computed from diagnosis features, are valuable
for dimension reduction while preserving rich information for the IHM task. The CCI score quantifies the
overall health burden from pre-existing chronic conditions and has demonstrated strong predictive value in
IHM across various patient populations. Figure 7 provides a histogram of CCI score distributions across
all patients in the IHM task cohort. The distribution is centered around 6 to 7, with scores ranging from 0
to 20. Figure 8 shows a boxplot of CCI scores separated by five CCI age groups, with outliers indicated as
open circles. As anticipated, the median CCI score increases consistently with age, reflecting the cumula-
tive burden of comorbidities over time.
Feature set consolidation and characterization. The culmination of these steps resulted in a curated set
of 89 features, encompassing static, dynamic, and demographic variables. The CCI score served as a crit-
ical proxy for comorbidity burden, providing a single, informative representation of patient health status.
As demonstrated in Table 2, this represents a substantial reduction from the original 2,244 features in the
MIMIC-IV dataset, while retaining a representative subset of key variables across each category. Indeed,
we retained a representative set of features for each category (chart, input, output, procedures), with ∼10%
of the original events in the final set. The full list of selected features across these categories is given in
Appendix A. All admission features were retained, with a grouping of ethnicities. A single CCI score fea-
ture was calculated using a subset of diagnosis features.

3.2 MODEL EVALUATION
ML model training was conducted on the preprocessed data to evaluate performance on the IHM predic-
tion task.

3.2.1 Dataset construction
The final cohort for the IHM task included 34,347 patients, with a class imbalance: 87.8% negative, 12.2%
positive.
Two versions of the dataset were constructed:
 1. Aggregated features: a total of 97 static variables were obtained by mean aggregation of the time-
 series features (combining the 48 hourly time bins into 1 value).
 2. Full time-series features: a total of 4045 static and dynamic variables were retained, preserving the
 temporal resolution of the data.
To prevent data leakage, a stratified patient-level (subject_id) train/test split was implemented using
StratifiedKFold from scikit-learn, ensuring no patient overlap between splits. Both the aggregated and
time-series datasets were split into 80:20 train:test sets for ML training. Patient identifiers (stay_id) were
preserved to enable future multimodal studies combining EHR data with radiology notes from the MIMIC-
IV-Note dataset for the IHM task.

3.2.2 Benchmark model performance
Three models – Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost – were evaluated on both dataset ver-
sions. Data normalization was applied using the scikit-learn MinMaxScaler. To address the class imbal-
ance, a "balanced" class weight setting was applied to both Logistic Regression and Random Forest fits.
For XGBoost, a positive class weight of 7.33 was used.
All models achieved reasonable performance on both the aggregated (Table 3) and time-series (Table 4)
datasets. On the aggregated dataset, Random Forest achieved the highest test F1 macro (78.02%) and F1

 4. DISCUSSION
This study presents a carefully curated MIMIC-IV feature set designed to streamline the clinical prepa-
ration phase for ML research using the IHM prediction task. The preprocessing and feature selection ac-
complished here should accelerate research focused on model development, application, and benchmark-
ing. By integrating prior research on MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV, empirical signal analysis, and clinical
judgment, our feature set bridges the gap between raw EHR data and actionable insights, ensuring that
subsequent ML efforts are grounded in clinically meaningful variables. The consolidation of over 2,200
original features into 89 representative variables – including static, dynamic, and demographic features –
was accomplished through a balance of reducing feature dimensionality while maintaining critical clini-
cal signals. This approach not only facilitates standardized comparisons across studies but also provides a
reproducible foundation for future research.
A central strength of this work lies in its methodological structure, which builds upon the flexibility of the
MIMIC-IV-Data Pipeline to prescribe task-optimized preprocessing options and a well-characterized fea-
ture set that allow ease of reproducibility and benchmarking. We also prioritized variables with demon-
strated utility in capturing cumulative comorbidity burdens, such as the CCI, which aligns with established
clinical frameworks. These efforts underscore our focus on generalizability over narrow model optimiza-
tion, ensuring that selected features avoid overfitting to rare events or non-representative patterns. By em-
phasizing clinical relevance, our feature set aligns with real-world healthcare priorities and offers models
that are statistically robust.
This work opens avenues for future research, particularly in extending the feature set to multimodal appli-
cations. The preservation of patient identifiers (stay_id) in our dataset enables integration with comple-
mentary data sources, such as radiology notes from the MIMIC-IV-Note dataset, paving the way for multi-
modal studies that combine EHR data with unstructured clinical notes. Additionally, the time-series reso-
lution retained in our full dataset offers opportunities to explore advanced temporal modeling approaches,
which could further enhance predictive accuracy. As clinical ML research evolves, our framework and fea-
ture set invites collaboration to refine and expand its utility in addressing complex healthcare challenges.
