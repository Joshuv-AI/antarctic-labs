A rare case of fulminant talquetamab-induced myocarditis presenting as a STEMI mimicker
Authors: Eric J. Kim, Sara Diaz Saravia, Gagan Sahni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune-mediated myocarditis is a rare and potentially fatal complication of oncologic immunotherapy. While most reported cases have been associated with the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), novel immunotherapy agents may also pose cardiac risks that warrant further attention. We report a case of fulminant, steroid-refractory myocarditis following initiation of talquetamab, a bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) targeting CD3 and GPRC5D used for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM).Case PresentationA 69-year-old man with MM refractory to multiple lines of treatment presented to his outpatient cardio-oncologist with syncope and intermittent chest pain 4 weeks after starting talquetamab. He was found to have elevated high-sensitivity troponin and new inferior Q waves on EKG. He presented to the hospital, where he was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit. New ST-segment elevations in the inferior leads were subsequently observed on serial EKGs. Transthoracic echocardiography showed a decreased left ventricular (LV) ejection fraction with new regional wall motion abnormalities. Left and right heart catheterization and endomyocardial biopsy were unremarkable. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed segmental late gadolinium enhancement, myocardial edema, increased T1 time, and increased extracellular volume. Acute myocarditis due to talquetamab was presumed, and intravenous steroid therapy was initiated. His symptoms and biomarkers initially improved but later worsened despite steroid therapy. He received additional immunosuppressive therapies including tocilizumab, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin, and ruxolitinib. However, his biomarkers and LV systolic function continued to worsen, ultimately resulting in death.ConclusionsThis case underscores the potential for immune-mediated myocarditis associated with talquetamab to follow a fulminant and treatment-refractory course despite guideline-directed escalation of immunosuppression, with important implications for clinical recognition and management. It also illustrates the diagnostic challenge of immune-mediated myocarditis presenting as an ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) mimic with non-obstructive coronary arteries. Finally, it emphasizes the complementary role of cardiac MRI in establishing the diagnosis when endomyocardial biopsy is non-diagnostic.

Background

Talquetamab is a bispecific T-cell engager (BiTE) targeting CD3 and GPRC5D and has emerged as an effective therapy for relapsed or refractory multiple myeloma (MM) [1]. Indeed, given its novelty, much remains to be elucidated regarding its efficacy and safety profile, with its phase 3 trial, MonumenTAL-3, still underway [2]. BiTEs potentiate T-cell recruitment and activation against malignant cells by binding simultaneously to T-cells and malignant cells; Talquetamab treats multiple myeloma in this way by binding to both CD3 and GPRC5D, which is found particularly among MM plasma cells [3, 4].

BiTEs are most commonly associated with systemic reactions such as cytokine release syndrome (CRS), Immune effector Cell-Associated Neurotoxicity Syndrome (ICANS), and cytopenias [5]. However, the cardiovascular toxicities of BiTEs remain largely unknown and continue to be uncovered. Talquetamab, specifically, does not have any published case reports linking it to acute myocarditis. This case thus represents a rare instance of talquetamab-induced fulminant myocarditis refractory to multiple lines of treatment including pulse steroids, tapering oral steroids, tocilizumab, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), and ruxolitinib. This case report brings forth the possibility of cardiotoxicity of BiTE agents, the challenges in treatment, and the mimickers of acute coronary syndrome in patients undergoing cancer treatments.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old man with a history of MM refractory to multiple lines of treatment, prostate cancer (on active surveillance only), mild left ventricular (LV) dysfunction with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 51%, patent foramen ovale, and mild non-obstructive coronary artery disease seen on a recent coronary computed tomography angiography presented to his outpatient cardio-oncologist after one episode of syncope while walking in his apartment and intermittent chest pain since initiation of BiTE therapy 4 weeks prior to presentation. Chest pain had been associated with fatigue, postural dizziness, episodes of presyncope, and epigastric pain. In the office, blood pressure was noted to be 97/69, and positive for orthostatic changes. An EKG revealed new inferior Q waves along with an elevated high sensitivity troponin I (hs-TnI) of 4,823 ng/L performed that day with outpatient labs. He was referred to the emergency department due to a concern for acute myocardial infarction (MI).

Regarding his previous cardiac history, the patient had been noted to have a mildly reduced LVEF and moderate LV hypertrophy 2 years prior to this hospital presentation. Cardiac MRI done 7 months before presentation revealed an LVEF of 50% with 1% basal inferolateral late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), elevated T1 time, and extracellular volume (ECV) of 34%, suggestive of a fibrotic or infiltrative process, though not necessarily diagnostic of cardiac amyloid. Fat pad biopsy and bone marrow biopsy performed at that time did not reveal any amyloid deposits. A CTA coronary study demonstrated non-obstructive coronary artery disease with a calcium score of 27 (50th percentile for age, gender, and ethnicity), ruling out an ischemic etiology. Cardiac biopsy for the evaluation of cardiac amyloid had been deferred pending further worsening of his LVEF, and he had been referred for genetic testing to evaluate for genetic causes.

The patient had previously undergone multiple lines of treatment for MM, including cyclophosphamide/bortezomib/dexamethasone with the later addition of daratumumab 2 years prior to this hospital presentation. A year prior to presentation, the patient had been treated with courses of daratumumab and lenalidomide maintenance therapy, dexamethasone/cyclophosphamide/etoposide/cisplatin, and pomalidomide/dexamethasone with carfilzomib or bortezomib. He also received teclistamab but ultimately underwent an autologous stem cell transplant with melphalan. His MM remained refractory to these therapies, so he was initiated on talquetamab with a four-dose step-up regimen one month before presentation. Talquetamab initiation was complicated by rhinovirus pneumonia and CRS grade 1, for which he received tocilizumab and continued on his maintenance talquetamab treatment two weeks later – which was one week prior to this hospital presentation. The patient’s other outpatient medications were clopidogrel 75 mg daily, rosuvastatin 10 mg nightly, finasteride 5 mg daily, tamsulosin 0.4 mg daily, and acyclovir 400 mg twice daily. He had no relevant surgical, substance use, or family medical history.

Hospital course

Upon arrival to the Emergency Department, the patient’s temperature was 37.1 Celsius, blood pressure 125/80 mmHg, heart rate 80/minute, respiratory rate 15/minute, and O2 saturation 97% on room air. His physical exam was unremarkable, with lungs clear to auscultation bilaterally, normal S1/S2 heart sounds with regular rate and rhythm, and warm and well-perfused extremities without peripheral edema. N-terminal Pro B-type natriuretic peptide was 4,378 pg/mL, and his repeat hs-TnI was 4,704 ng/L with unchanged inferior Q waves on EKG (Fig. 1). His creatine kinase-MB was 13.3 ng/mL, and his creatine phosphokinase was 50 micrograms/L. Other remarkable labs were a hemoglobin of 9.4 g/dL (baseline 10–11 g/dL) and a venous lactate of 1.0 mg/dl. Complete blood counts were otherwise within normal limits, including his white blood cell count (7.2 × 103/µL), absolute neutrophil count (6.0 × 103/µL), and eosinophil count (0.3 × 103/µL). Only his lymphocyte count was low at 0.7 × 103/µL. Basic metabolic panel and hepatic function panel were within normal limits. Respiratory viral panel, including COVID-19 and rhinovirus, was negative. C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 5.7 mg/L, though both ferritin and fibrinogen were normal at 127 ng/mL and 354 mg/dL, respectively. A chest X-ray done on admission showed no acute cardiopulmonary pathologies.

The patient was admitted to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU) for a possible acute MI versus acute myocarditis associated with recent immunotherapy with talquetamab. Approximately 12 h after being admitted to the unit, he developed new inferior ST elevations on his EKG without any chest pain (Fig. 2). Bedside transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) in the CICU showed a newly reduced LVEF of 44%, down from his prior LVEF of 51%, with severe hypokinesis of the basal, inferior, and lateral segments.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed the presence of hypokinesis in the basal, inferior, and lateral segments, as seen on TTE. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) of the subepicardium was noted in the basal to mid inferolateral segments, as well as enhancement of the bilateral papillary muscles in a non-ischemic pattern, myocardial edema in the basal to mid lateral walls on T2-weighted imaging, increased T1 time predominantly in the basal to mid lateral segments, and an extracellular volume (ECV) of 41% (Fig. 4), fulfilling the 2018 Lake Louise criteria for diagnosis of acute myocarditis [6]. Given the negative respiratory viral panel and the absence of lymphocytic infiltrates and CD3 + staining on cardiac biopsy, a presumptive diagnosis of acute myocarditis associated with talquetamab was made over viral myocarditis.

The patient received dexamethasone 10 mg IV every 6 h for 4 days, with hs-TnI dropping to 2,172 ng/L about 3 h following the first dose. Over the next 5 days, he improved significantly in his symptoms and tolerance of guideline-directed medical therapy. He was started on colchicine and discharged with a plan for rapid follow-up. Upon evaluation by his outpatient cardio-oncologist 2 days later, hs-TnI increased to 5,478 ng/L with increases in IL-6 to 1,432 pg/mL and CRP to 8.5 mg/L. He was re-admitted to the CICU for further management and was started on methylprednisolone 1 gram daily for 3 days. Hs-TnI decreased to 2,343 ng/L, IL-6 to 412 pg/mL, IL-8 to 6.4 pg/mL, and CRP to 4.7 mg/L. The patient was briefly trialed on oral prednisone. However, hs-TnI rose again to 10,098 ng/L, so he was restarted on methylprednisolone for 3 more days, followed by tapering oral steroids. Hs-TnI continued to rise despite CRP’s decreasing to undetectable levels. In accordance with the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on cardio-oncology’s general approach to immunotherapy-induced myocarditis [7], additional immunosuppressive therapies were given over a total of three weeks. These included a dose of tocilizumab, 6 days of IV mycophenolate mofetil, 5 days of IVIG, and 8 days of ruxolitinib. However, the hs-TnI levels remained elevated throughout, between 30,000 and 48,000 ng/L (Figs. 5 and 6).

The patient was evaluated for advanced heart failure therapies eligibility, but he was determined not to be a candidate for a left ventricular assist device or heart transplant as destination therapies given his active malignancy and uncertainty about progression of his advanced myeloma. His overall prognosis was considered poor in the absence of durable long-term support. A third right heart catheterization while on 2.5 mcg/kg/minute of dobutamine (performed on hospital day 27) showed stable elevated filling pressures and decreased pulmonary vascular resistance compared to prior, along with preserved cardiac output (Table 3). Accordingly, he was not considered for temporary mechanical circulatory support, including Impella, intra-aortic balloon pump, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Discussions regarding goals of care were initiated to pursue a comfort-centered approach. However, on hospital day 37, the patient developed ventricular tachycardia with stable blood pressure initially and was treated with an amiodarone bolus followed by continuous infusion. He subsequently progressed to pulseless electrical activity, at which point cardiopulmonary resuscitation was initiated. Cardiopulmonary resuscitation efforts were unsuccessful.

Discussion and conclusions

This patient’s challenging case raises several important questions regarding the development and etiology of his fulminant myocarditis. Firstly, the patient had pre-existing borderline cardiomyopathy, for which a cardiac MRI revealed findings suggestive of a fibrotic or infiltrative process. Ischemic evaluation with non-invasive imaging had been unremarkable. Ultimately, the etiology of the patient’s pre-existing cardiomyopathy had not yet been ascertained; it may have been related to one of his earlier chemotherapy agents such as cyclophosphamide and cisplatin, though those agents cause myocardial dysfunction only rarely [8]. Although his LV had only mild systolic dysfunction, the fibrotic or infiltrative changes demonstrated by his cardiac MRI suggest the presence of a potential inflammatory process that may have exacerbated the cardiotoxic effects he suffered from talquetamab.

Secondly, an alternative etiology of the patient’s myocarditis may have been his rhinovirus infection. Indeed, he had tested positive for rhinovirus 1 month prior to his hospital presentation; although he had tested negative for rhinovirus during this presentation, viral myocarditis can arise weeks after the initial infection [9]. However, rhinovirus has very rarely been documented as causing myocarditis [10]. Moreover, viral myocarditis is typically marked by the presence of lymphocytic infiltrates and CD3 + staining [11], such that the absence of both findings argues against the diagnosis of rhinovirus-induced myocarditis. It was concluded that talquetamab was the more likely culprit of this patient’s fulminant myocarditis.

Talquetamab is a BiTE against CD3 and GPRC5D used to treat relapsed/refractory MM. While immunotherapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now well recognized to cause myocarditis with a reported incidence of 0.3%-2.2% in contemporary series [12], the cardiovascular toxicities of BiTEs remain incompletely characterized with only isolated cases reported across multiple agents and myocarditis remaining exceedingly uncommon in pharmacovigilance databases [13]. Moreover early published clinical trials of talquetamab did not discuss any cardiovascular events, including myocarditis [1]. This case represents a rare and severe presentation of probable talquetamab-associated myocarditis, notable for its fulminant course, resistant to multi-agent immunosuppression, and initial presentation mimicking an acute ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction.

A recent survey into the US Food and Drug Administration’s Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) by Sayed et al. explored the cardiovascular events of five BiTEs: blinatumomab, teclistamab, mosunetuzumab, glofitamab, and epcoritamab. Of 3,668 included cases, 747 (20.4%) involved cardiovascular adverse events, but only 8 cases of myocarditis were observed – of which 5 of them were due to a single agent (viz., teclistimab) [13]. In general, BiTEs promote systemic inflammation by activating CD4 + and CD8 + T-cells, as well as increasing myeloid populations, and ultimately releasing numerous cytokines including tumor necrosis factor-α, interferon-γ, IL-6, and interleukin-1β [14, 15]. The biological plausibility of myocarditis by BiTEs therefore incorporates robust T-cell activation and cytokine release, including IL-6, interferon-γ, and tumor necrosis factor-α, all of which have been implicated in myocardial inflammation and injury.

It is worth considering the correlation between talquetamab’s half-life and the patient’s clinical course. Talquetamab has a half-life of only 7.56 days at initial treatment and 12.2 days at steady state [16]. The patient had received 4 doses of step-up therapy 1 month prior to presentation, followed by a maintenance dose 1 week prior to presentation. Thus, he had likely achieved a steady state, and his initial recovery and discharge coincided with when his talquetamab reached its half-life. His subsequent worsening cardiotoxicity that prompted readmission may therefore have correlated with a delayed inflammatory insult, though the mechanism of this observation remains unclear.

This case also highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by immune-mediated myocarditis, particularly when it presents with electrocardiographic and biomarker features suggestive of acute coronary syndrome. The ST-segment elevations, regional wall motion abnormalities, and marked troponin elevation [17, 18], as seen in this case, also fall within the spectrum of myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) [19]. While myocarditis often produces diffuse electrocardiographic and echocardiographic abnormalities, focal myocarditis may mimic territorial ischemia [20–23], as observed in this case.

Endomyocardial biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard for myocarditis [24], but its sensitivity is limited by patchy distribution of information and sampling error [25]. In this case, 2 biopsies failed to demonstrate any inflammatory infiltrates despite progressive clinical deterioration and concordant imaging findings. Cardiac MRI therefore played a central diagnostic role with serial studies demonstrating myocardial edema, nonischemic lead gadolinium enhancement, elevated T1 values and expansion of extracellular volume consistent with active myocarditis by the 2019 late use criteria [6]. The discordance between biopsy and imaging findings underscores the limitations of histopathology in immune-mediated myocarditis and supports the integration of advanced imaging into diagnostic algorithms.

This case illustrates the potential for severe myocarditis associated with immunotherapy to be refractory to guideline-recommended immunosuppressants strategies, including prompt steroid initiation [7, 26]. While data are lacking to recommend any specific second-line immunosuppressive agents in particular, various management strategies have been described in the literature regarding steroid-refractory, ICI-induced myocarditis [27]. Mycophenolate mofetil has been reported to accelerate the decline in cardiac biomarkers with a trend toward decreased mortality [28]. Doms and colleagues reported on the successful treatment of steroid-refractory myocarditis with IL-6 blockade using tocilizumab [29], while Vockenhuber and colleagues accomplished the same with JAK2 inhibition using ruxolitinib [30]. Other case reports have described the successful use of abatacept, a CTLA-4-Ig fusion protein that inhibits CD28-mediated T-cell costimulation, both alone and with ruxolitinib [31, 32]. All of these therapies were applied to the case presented here, except for abatacept. Unlike ICIs, talquetamab directly activates T-cells in a CD28-independent manner, so abatacept was deemed unlikely to be beneficial.

However, despite early initiation of high-dose corticosteroids and escalation to IL-6 blockade, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulin and JAK2 inhibition with ruxolotinib, the patient experienced relentless myocardial injury, progressive systolic dysfunction and ultimately fatal arrhythmia. He had a persistent elevation of troponin levels despite declining CRP, suggesting ongoing myocardial injury despite partial suppression of systemic inflammation. This dissociation raises the possibility that direct cytotoxic T-cell mediated myocardial damage may persist independently of cytokine signaling pathways and may be less responsive to currently available immunosuppressive regimens.

Future strategies for the management of relapsed or refractory MM should involve heightened vigilance regarding talquetamab’s potential cardiotoxicities, including fulminant myocarditis – which, though rarely reported as in this case, certainly highlights the need for more diligent pharamcovigilance data to assess the true burden of such rare cardiotoxicities. Had the patient survived, it also poses the clinical conundrum of safe rechallenges with other types of immunotherapy such as CAR-T cell therapy that has its own risk of CRS and potential cardiotoxicity [33]. Other treatment options that could be considered include selinexor, an exportin 1 inhibitor that has shown promise in clinical trials for refractory MM without immune-mediated cardiotoxicities [34, 35].

In summary, this case expands the spectrum of cardiovascular toxicities associated with bispecific T-cell engager therapy and highlights several key clinical lessons: myocarditis may present as a mimicker of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in patients receiving immunotherapy with talquetamab; cardiac MRI is essential when biopsy findings are non-diagnostic; and immune-mediated myocarditis may follow a fulminant and treatment-refractory course despite aggressive immunosuppression. As the use of bispecific T-cell engagers continues to expand, heightened awareness of their potential cardiotoxic effects and early multidisciplinary collaboration will be critical to improving recognition and outcomes.
