Chronic exposure to LED lighting impairs prefrontal cortex-dependent cognitive functions in adolescent rats
Authors: Jing Wang, Xiaona Wang, Yingying Han, Chong Sun, Shunqi Wang, Chaolin Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Light-emitting diodes (LEDs) have already replaced conventional lighting sources due to their energy efficiency and environmental benefits. Although previous studies suggest that chronic exposure to LED may affect human physiological health, its impact on cognitive functions remains unclear. To simulate typical human lighting conditions, we established an animal model which Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats exposed to LED light daily from 18:00 to 22:00 (ZT12-ZT16) for one month. Cognitive functions were assessed using social interaction, Morris water maze, and sound-location-association tasks. Results indicated that chronic LED exposure impaired social interaction ability, spatial learning and memory, and auditory-spatial associative learning. Immunohistochemical and Western blot analyses revealed a reduction in myelination levels and a decreased number of mature oligodendrocytes in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). These findings suggest that chronic LED exposure impairs mPFC-dependent cognitive functions, possibly through downregulation of myelin integrity in the mPFC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58723-4.

Introduction

A light-emitting diode (LED) is a semiconductor device that emits monochromatic light within a narrow spectral range. Contemporary white LEDs typically utilize a blue LED chip to excite yellow phosphors, thereby producing white light1,2. The emission spectrum of white LED lighting differs significantly from that of traditional light sources, with a dominant wavelength around 482 nm—within the short-wavelength blue light range3,4. This wavelength is near the peak sensitivity of melanopsin (∼480 nm), a photopigment involved in non-visual light responses, distinguishing it from incandescent lighting5–8. Modern lifestyles often involve prolonged exposure to artificial light, particularly from electronic screens in the evening and artificial lighting at night3. The effects of blue-enriched light on children and adolescents are of growing concern9,10, especially since many serious mental disorders emerge before the age of 1811.

Growing evidence indicates that artificial light at night and other forms of light pollution can profoundly affect brain function beyond visual processing. Experimental studies have shown that abnormal lighting conditions disrupt circadian organization, alter sleep patterns, and impair learning and memory performance in rodents12–15. For example, exposure to dim artificial lighting in the evening induces coordinated changes in circadian rhythms, sleep architecture, and short-term recognition memory performance in mice13. Likewise, abnormal lighting conditions can produce temporal desynchronization among circadian oscillators in different brain regions, leading to dissociable impairments in object recognition and spatial memory tasks12. Recent studies further demonstrated that nighttime exposure to blue-enriched light impairs spatial learning and memory, accompanied by elevated corticosterone levels, neuroinflammation, and neuronal dysfunction within memory-related brain circuits15. In addition, distinct retina-brain pathways have been identified through which light influences mood and learning, highlighting the broad impact of environmental lighting on higher-order brain functions14. Collectively, these findings suggest that light pollution may adversely affect neural systems involved in cognition, particularly the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and hippocampus, two regions critically involved in executive function, recognition memory, and learning. However, the cellular mechanisms underlying light-induced cognitive impairment, especially during adolescent brain development, remain incompletely understood.

The prefrontal cortex (PFC), located in the anterior portion of the frontal lobe, serves as an association cortex. In rats, the PFC comprises medial, lateral, ventral, and orbital regions, each containing multiple subregions16. It plays a critical role in higher-order cognitive processes, including working memory, attention control, behavior inhibition, and executive planning16–18. Dysfunction of the PFC is associated with cognitive impairments, such as working memory (WM) deficits in schizophrenia attentional difficulties in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)19,20.

In the central nervous system (CNS), oligodendrocytes (OLs) migrate along axons and form myelin sheaths, which are essential for efficient neural conduction21. Myelin basic protein (MBP) is a key structural component of the myelin sheath22.OLs exhibit adaptive plasticity in response to environmental stimuli, influencing myelin remodeling throughout development, health, and disease23,24. Myelin integrity is influenced by environmental factors, and deficits in myelination have been linked to neuropsychiatric conditions such as schizophrenia, depression, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and multiple sclerosis25–29. Region-specific myelin abnormalities can lead to distinct cognitive deficits30. For instance, previous work demonstrated that myelin deficiency in the PFC results in impairments in social behavior, learning, and memory31.

To investigate whether chronic LED exposure affects cognitive function, this experiment was designed using adolescent male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats, which were exposed to either LED or incandescent light at an intensity of 350 lx. Then, anxiety-like behavior, novel object recognition, social interaction, spatial learning and memory, and associative learning through behavioral assays, and assessed myelination levels in the PFC were evaluated separately. Because myelination within the medial prefrontal cortex continues throughout adolescence, we first characterized the developmental trajectory of mPFC myelination in rats across postnatal development. This developmental profile provided a framework for determining whether adolescent LED exposure occurs during a period of active myelin maturation. The results indicate that prolonged LED exposure impairs cognitive function, likely by disrupting myelination in the medial prefrontal cortex. Thus, chronic LED light exposure during developmental periods of myelination may adversely affect PFC-dependent cognitive functions.

Materials and methods

Animal

Male SD rats were used in this study. Only males were included to minimize potential variability associated with the estrous cycle and related hormonal fluctuations, thereby reducing experimental variability in behavioral and neurobiological assessments. For the developmental myelination analysis shown in Fig. 1-6, SD rats of different postnatal ages (P10–P90) were obtained either from the Animal Experiment Center of Nanchang University or from our in-house breeding colony. For other experiments (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 and 8), only in-house bred male rats were used and randomly assigned to the experimental groups. Prior to weaning, rats were housed 3–4 per cage. After experimental group allocation at P21, animals were housed three per cage throughout the study. The rats were housed in cage with ad libitum access to food and water, under a 12/12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 06:00, ZT0; lights off at 18:00, ZT12) at a controlled temperature of approximately 25 °C. All experimental procedures involving animals were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nanchang University and were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.” This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Euthanasia and Tissue Collection

At the end of the behavioral tests, rats were deeply anesthetized for tissue collection. Anesthesia was induced using a mixture of ketamine (80 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) administered intraperitoneally (i.p.). The depth of anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal and corneal reflexes. Once unresponsive, rats were transcardially perfused with ice-cold saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for immunohistochemical analysis. For Western blot analysis, a separate cohort of rats was euthanized by rapid decapitation under deep anesthesia as described above, and the mPFC tissue was freshly dissected.

Lighting settings

Commercially available LED and incandescent bulbs were selected for this study. The spectral output of each bulb type was measured using a spectrometer. Ambient illuminance at the cage level was maintained at 350 ± 50 lx, as verified by an illuminance meter, and was checked every three days throughout the experiment.

Light exposure protocol

Male SD (Postnatal day 21, P21) rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10 per group at study initiation): control (CON), LED-exposed (LED), and incandescent lamp-exposed (ILE). Ten animals were initially assigned to each experimental group. Because different subsets of animals were used for behavioral, histological, and biochemical analyses, the sample size varied across experiments. The exact n value for each analysis is reported in the corresponding figure legends. Rats were housed three per cage with ad libitum access to food and water. All groups were maintained in the same animal room under the same environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, and ventilation) and on the same cage racks throughout the study. The LED and ILE compartments were located within the same temperature-controlled room (approximately 25 °C). Outside the 4-h exposure period, the LED, ILE, and CON groups were maintained under identical standard 12-h light/12-h dark housing conditions.

During the dark phase, the LED and ILE groups were transferred daily from 18:00 (ZT12) to 22:00 (ZT16) to isolated light-controlled compartments located within the same animal room for specific light exposure. The compartments were physically separated to prevent light contamination of the main housing area. No detectable light leakage from the exposure compartments reached the control animals. Following the 4-h exposure period, the animals were returned to their original cages and maintained under standard dark-phase conditions. This lighting regimen continued for 30 consecutive days (P22-P51). Behavioral testing began on P52, followed by tissue collection for histological and biochemical analyses after completion of all behavioral experiments.

Behavioral tests

Behavioral testing was initiated after completion of the 30-day light exposure protocol. Behavioral assays were conducted in the following order: Open field test (OFT), Elevated plus maze (EPM), Novel object recognition (NOR), Three-chamber social interaction test, Morris water maze (MWM), and Sound-location association task. The order was selected to minimize potential carryover effects, with less stressful tests performed before more demanding behavioral paradigms. Not all animals participated in every behavioral test; therefore, sample sizes varied among experiments. The exact number of animals used for each test is indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Open field test (OFT)

The open field apparatus consisted of a square arena (50 × 50 × 45 cm). A camera mounted above the arena recorded the rats’ activity. Parameters such as total distance moved, time spent in the center, duration of stereotypic, and resting time were analyzed to assess locomotor activity and anxiety-like behavior32–34. One hour before testing, rats were brought to the testing room for acclimation. Each rat was then placed in the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 10 min. Behavior was recorded and analyzed using Activity Monitor™ and Video Tracking Interface™ software.

Elevated plus maze (EPM)

The EPM consisted of two open arms (60 × 10 cm) and two enclosed arms (60 × 10 × 40 cm), elevated 100 cm above the floor. The test exploits the conflict between a rat’s innate curiosity for novel environments (open arms) and its preference for dark, enclosed spaces (closed arms) to assess anxiety. After a 1-hour acclimation period in the testing room, each rat was placed in the central zone of the maze, facing an open arm, and allowed to explore for 10 min. The number of entries into and time spent in each arm were recorded using Video Maze™ and Video Tracking Interface™ software.

Novel object recognition (NOR)

The NOR test was conducted in a square arena (50 × 50 × 45 cm) to assess recognition memory. After a 1-h acclimation to the testing room, the procedure consisted of three phases over three consecutive days: Habituation (Day 1), the rat was placed in the empty arena for 10 min. Sample Phase (Day 2), two identical objects were placed in opposite corners of the arena, and the rat was allowed to explore for 10 min. Test Phase (Day 3), one familiar object was replaced with a novel object of similar size but different shape and texture, and the rat was allowed to explore for 10 min. The exploration time for each object was recorded across all sessions. For the NOR test, the interaction ratio was calculated as the time spent exploring the novel object divided by the time spent exploring the familiar object (Novel/Familiar).

Social Interaction

The test was performed in a rectangular arena (90 × 40 × 45 cm) virtually divided into three equal zones (30 × 40 cm). Two identical wire mesh cages (15 cm diameter, 20 cm height) were placed at either end. The procedure was as follows: Habituation (Day 1), the experimental rat was placed in the center of the empty arena (without cages) for 10 min. Sociability Test (Day 2), one cage contained a novel stranger rat (Rat), while the other contained an inanimate object. The experimental rat was placed in the center and allowed to explore for 10 min. Social Novelty Preference Test (Day 3), one cage contained the now-familiar rat (Familiar), and the other contained a new stranger rat (Novel). The experimental rat was again placed in the center for a 10-min session. The time spent exploring each zone (containing the object, Rat, Familiar, or Novel) was recorded. For the sociability phase, the interaction ratio was calculated as the time spent investigating the chamber containing a rat divided by the time spent investigating the chamber containing an object (Rat/Object). For the social novelty preference phase, the interaction ratio was calculated as the time spent investigating the novel rat divided by the time spent investigating the familiar rat (Novel Rat/Familiar Rat).

Morris water maze (MWM)

The MWM test was used to assess spatial learning and memory. A circular pool (1.8 m diameter) was filled with opaque water (maintained at 25 ± 2 °C) and divided into four quadrants. A hidden platform was submerged 3 cm below the water surface in the center of the target quadrant (T). Habituation: Rats were placed on the platform for 60 s. Acquisition Training (5 d): Rats underwent two trials per day (with a 5-hour interval) from varying start points. They were given 120 s to locate the hidden platform. If successful, they remained on it for 60 s; if not, they were guided to it and remained for 60 s. Escape latency and path were recorded. Probe Trial (Day 6): The platform was removed. Rats were released from the quadrant opposite the target (Q3) and allowed to swim freely for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant (T) and swimming path were recorded.

Sound-location association

Rats were water-restricted for this task. Body weight was monitored regularly throughout the experimental period and was used to calculate individual daily water requirements. Supplemental water was provided after each training session to maintain hydration status. Daily water allowance was adjusted according to body weight and animal welfare guidelines to maintain normal hydration and body weight throughout training. No animals exhibited excessive weight loss or signs of dehydration during the study. Apparatus: A T-maze was used. Habituation: Rats freely explored the T-maze for 10 min with water available in both arms. Pre-training: Rats underwent 40 trials. Upon entering the start area and breaking an infrared beam, a 7 kHz tone was played. A water reward was given regardless of the arm chosen. Training (8 d): Rats completed 60 trials per day. Two tones were used: Tone 1 (3 kHz) and Tone 2 (12 kHz), presented semi-randomly. For half the rats, Tone 1 signaled a reward in the left arm and Tone 2 in the right arm; this rule was reversed for the other half. A correct choice was rewarded with water and a positive sound cue; an incorrect choice resulted in no reward and a negative sound cue. The daily correct response rate was recorded.

For histological and biochemical analyses of Figs. 7 and 8, tissues were collected from animals that had previously undergone behavioral testing. Following completion of the behavioral assessments, a subset of animals was randomly selected for perfusion and immunofluorescence analyses, whereas another subset was used for Western blot experiments.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed according to the previous study35,36, with minor modifications. In brief, 30-µm-thick cryosections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Sections were blocked with a solution containing 0.3% Triton X-100, 1% BSA, and 5% goat serum to prevent non-specific binding. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against MBP (1:1000; Abcam, UK), NG2 (1:200; Millipore, USA), CC1 (1:100; Millipore, USA), or Olig2 (1:1000; Millipore, USA). After 24 h, sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies (anti-mouse, 1:500; Thermo Fisher) for 2 h at room temperature. Images were captured using a laser-scanning confocal microscope (Olympus FV1000) equipped with a 20× objective lens. For quantitative analyses, all sections were imaged using identical acquisition settings, including laser intensity, detector gain, offset, and pinhole size, to ensure valid comparisons among experimental groups.

Western blot

Western blot was performed as previously described37,38, with minor modifications. Briefly, tissue homogenates were boiled and separated on 12% SDS-PAGE gels, then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20) for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibody against MBP (1:1000; Abcam, UK) overnight at 4 °C. After washing, membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:3000; kangwei, China) for 1.5 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a SuperSignal West Pico HRP substrate kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) and quantified by densitometry. Band intensities were normalized to GAPDH as a loading control.

Statistical analysis

Sample sizes varied among experiments because not all animals were used for every behavioral, histological, or biochemical analysis. The exact sample size (n) for each experiment is indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 6.0). Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate, followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons post hoc test. Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Chronic nighttime LED light exposure induces anxiety-like behavior in adolescent rats

The experimental timeline is shown in Fig. 1. To explore the effects of LED light on body weight, locomotor activity, and anxiety levels, OFT and EPM were executed39. Body weight was monitored throughout the 30-d lighting period. We found that nighttime light exposure for 30 d did not significantly affect body weight (Fig. 2A). In the EPM test, rats in the LED group spent significantly less time exploring the open arms compared to those in the ILE and control groups (CON: 109.7 ± 19.92, n = 8; ILE: 104.8 ± 22.31, n = 8; LED: 50.2 ± 12.92, n = 8. CON vs. ILE: P = 0.8716; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0250; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0499) (Fig. 2B). However, in the OFT, no significant differences were observed among the three groups in total distance moved, time spent in the center, movement time, duration of stereotypic behavior, or resting time (Fig. 2C-F).

Chronic nighttime LED light exposure impairs novel object recognition and social behavior

NOR test was executed to assess recognition memory (Fig. 3A). Rats in the LED group exhibited a significantly lower Novel/Familiar interaction ratio compared with the CON and ILE groups, indicating impaired recognition memory. Consistent with this finding, animals in the CON and ILE groups spent significantly more time exploring the novel object than the familiar object, whereas no such preference was observed in the LED group. Both the ILE and control groups explored the novel object significantly longer than the familiar object (CON: 1.948 ± 0.2268, n = 8; ILE: 1.731 ± 0.2736, n = 8; LED: 1.161 ± 0.1307, n = 8. CON vs. ILE: P = 0.5523; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0095; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0301) (Fig. 3B, C). Social behavior was evaluated using the three-chamber social test (Fig. 3D). During the sociability phase, the LED group spent similar amounts of time exploring the chamber containing a rat (R) and the chamber containing an object (O). In contrast, the ILE and control groups spent significantly more time exploring the rat chamber (CON: 2.226 ± 0.3008, n = 10; ILE: 2.497 ± 0.4218, n = 10; LED: 1.446 ± 0.2228, n = 10. CON vs. ILE: P = 0.6273; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0450; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0375) (Fig. 3E, F). In the social novelty preference phase, the LED group showed no preference between a novel rat (NR) and a familiar rat (FR), whereas the ILE and control groups spent significantly more time exploring the chamber with the novel rat (CON: 1.706 ± 0.2317, n = 10; ILE: 1.686 ± 0.2165, n = 10; LED: 1.112 ± 0.1735, n = 10. CON vs. ILE: P = 0.9508; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0494; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0492) (Fig. 3G, H). These results indicate that chronic nighttime LED exposure impairs novel object recognition and social behavior in adolescent rats.

Spatial learning and memory are impaired by LED exposure

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the MWM test (Fig. 4A). During the training phase (Fig. 4B), rats in the LED group took significantly longer to locate the hidden platform compared to the ILE and control groups (Fig. 4D), indicating impaired spatial learning. In the probe trial (Fig. 4C), the LED group spent less time in the target quadrant (T) than the other two groups, suggesting deficits in spatial memory retention (CON: 20.96 ± 1.392, n = 8; ILE: 20.15 ± 1.354, n = 8; LED: 16.60 ± 1.319, n = 8. CON vs. ILE: P = 0.9067; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0393; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0221) (Fig. 4E).

Auditory-spatial associative learning is disrupted by LED exposure

To assess cognitive function independent of visual cues, an auditory-spatial associative learning task was employed (Fig. 5A). The results showed that the accuracy of the LED group was significantly lower than that of the ILE and control groups on days 4–6 of training (Day 4: CON vs. ILE: P = 0.9482; CON vs. LED: P = 0.0013; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0008; Day 5: CON vs. ILE: P = 0.7420; CON vs. LED: P = 0.1079; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0209; Day 6: CON vs. ILE: P = 0.9599; CON vs. LED: P = 0.1029; ILE vs. LED: P = 0.0445) (Fig. 5B).

Developmental characterization of mPFC myelination and effects of chronic nighttime LED exposure

To determine whether the LED exposure period coincided with a critical stage of mPFC myelin development, we first characterized the developmental trajectory of MBP expression across postnatal ages (P10–P90) using immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6A-D) and Western blotting (Fig. 6E). Consistent with normal developmental trajectories, MBP expression was barely detectable at P10, followed by a rapid increase from P20 onwards. Myelination reached a peak around P50 and remained relatively stable through P90 (Fig. 6B-E), reflecting the establishment of mature myelin networks in the adolescent and young adult mPFC. After exposure to chronic nighttime LED light, however, a marked reduction in myelination was observed (Fig. 7). Specifically, immunostaining showed that the MBP-positive area in the mPFC was significantly decreased in the LED group compared with the ILE and control groups (Fig. 7A–C). Western blotting further confirmed the downregulation of MBP protein levels (Fig. 7D–E). Intriguingly, this impairment appeared region-specific, as no significant changes in MBP levels were detected in other brain areas such as the motor cortex, corpus callosum, or striatum (Fig. 7F–I). These findings indicate that the adolescent mPFC is selectively vulnerable to the effects of aberrant light exposure during nighttime. Although the reduction in MBP immunoreactivity appeared visually more prominent in deeper cortical layers in some representative sections, the present analysis was performed across the entire PrL and IL regions and was therefore not designed to assess layer-specific effects.

Impaired oligodendrocyte maturation underlies LED-induced hypomyelination

To investigate the cellular basis of myelination deficits, we examined oligodendrocyte lineage markers by immunofluorescence. NG2, a marker for oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), was significantly increased in the mPFC of LED-exposed rats (Fig. 8A–B). In contrast, the number of CC1+/Olig2+ mature oligodendrocytes was significantly reduced (Fig. 8C–D). This reciprocal change suggests that chronic LED exposure disrupts the normal progression of OPC differentiation into mature myelinating oligodendrocytes. Instead of advancing through the lineage, OPCs appear to accumulate, reflecting a blockade in their developmental transition. Such impairment in oligodendrocyte maturation provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed hypomyelination in the adolescent mPFC. Collectively, these results point to a previously unrecognized risk of nighttime LED exposure in altering myelin development, which could have long-lasting consequences for prefrontal cortical function.

Discussion

In recent years, LED light sources have been widely adopted due to their energy efficiency, long lifespan, and cost-effectiveness40. Given their spectral properties, which are enriched in blue wavelengths3, the pervasive use of LED-based devices (e.g., computers, smartphones, TVs) has raised public health concerns regarding potential biological risks4,6,39,41.

Methodological considerations and experimental design strengths

This study employed a well-controlled experimental paradigm that mimics real-world nighttime exposure to LED light while maintaining consistent light intensity between LED and incandescent conditions. This design isolates the spectral composition as the key variable, thereby strengthening causal inferences. Previous studies often failed to distinguish between intensity-dependent and wavelength-dependent effects of artificial light42,43. By maintaining equivalent illuminance (350 ± 50 lx), our protocol ensured that observed behavioral and molecular alterations were specifically attributable to the spectral characteristics of LEDs, rather than total light energy.

Moreover, we selected adolescent rats (P21–P52), a developmental window corresponding to human adolescence, when myelination in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) undergoes rapid maturation44,45. Behavioral assessments—covering multiple cognitive domains (social behavior, recognition memory, spatial learning, and associative learning)—provide converging evidence that LED exposure induces broad and specific PFC-dependent dysfunctions. In addition, the use of both visual and non-visual tasks (e.g., sound-location association) effectively controls for potential visual fatigue or retinal damage, increasing the reliability of the conclusions.

Reliability and biological relevance of results

The reproducibility and biological validity of our findings are reinforced by the convergence of behavioral, histological, and biochemical evidence. Chronic LED exposure impaired multiple cognitive domains, consistent with previous findings that disrupted prefrontal myelination contributes to cognitive inflexibility and social deficits46,47. Importantly, the observed reduction in myelination was most evident in the mPFC, whereas no significant alterations were detected in the motor cortex, corpus callosum, or striatum under the present experimental conditions. This finding suggests that the adolescent mPFC may be particularly vulnerable to nighttime LED exposure during the developmental period examined in this study. However, myelination proceeds along distinct temporal trajectories across brain regions, and the mPFC is among the last regions to reach full maturation48. Consequently, the apparent regional specificity observed here may reflect a developmental window of heightened vulnerability rather than an absolute resistance of other brain regions to LED exposure. It remains possible that earlier or later exposure paradigms could affect additional neural circuits, particularly those undergoing active myelination during those periods48,49. Future studies examining multiple developmental stages will be important for defining the temporal and regional specificity of LED-induced myelin disruption.

Immunostaining and Western blot analyses consistently demonstrated reduced MBP expression, accompanied by increased NG2+ OPCs and reduced CC1+ mature oligodendrocytes. This reciprocal relationship implies an arrest in oligodendrocyte differentiation, rather than loss of precursor proliferation, indicating a developmental blockade50. Such mechanisms are well-documented in models of stress-induced or circadian disruption-induced demyelination15,51, reinforcing the plausibility of LED-induced interference in oligodendroglia maturation.

Potential mechanisms underlying LED-induced cognitive impairment

The mechanisms by which LED light disrupts myelination are likely multifactorial. One plausible pathway involves circadian rhythm dysregulation. Blue-enriched light (dominant wavelength ~ 480 nm) is known to suppress melatonin secretion through melanopsin activation in intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs)52. Chronic suppression of melatonin during adolescence—a period critical for brain maturation—has been associated with reduced oligodendrocyte differentiation and impaired myelin formation53.

Another potential mechanism involves oxidative stress and neuroinflammation. Excessive blue-light exposure increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in neural tissues, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and myelin protein oxidation15,54. Furthermore, studies have shown that chronic light exposure alters glial activation and inflammatory cytokine expression in the PFC55,56, potentially impairing oligodendrocyte lineage homeostasis.

Additionally, sleep disturbance secondary to LED exposure could indirectly contribute to PFC dysfunction. Chronic circadian disruption and sleep deprivation have been shown to reduce myelin gene expression and synaptic plasticity in rodents57,58. These mechanisms likely converge to impair prefrontal oligodendrogenesis, leading to the observed cognitive deficits.

Translational implications for human health

Although this study was conducted in rats, the findings have significant implications for adolescents and young adults, who are increasingly exposed to LED-based light sources during nighttime activities. Epidemiological studies have linked excessive evening screen exposure to reduced sleep quality, mood dysregulation, and attention deficits in adolescents10,59. The mPFC, which continues to mature through late adolescence, plays a crucial role in executive control and social cognition60,61. Thus, the observed prefrontal myelin deficits in rats may translate to subtle but cumulative neurodevelopmental alterations in humans.

Furthermore, our findings highlight the potential risks associated with widespread use of LED-based environmental lighting and electronic devices in schools and households. Preventive strategies, such as using blue-light filters, warmer spectral lighting, and avoiding LED exposure in the hours before sleep, may help mitigate the adverse neural consequences of excessive nighttime illumination. Notably, the timing of light exposure may be as important as its overall duration.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, only male rats were included in this study; given known sex differences in myelination and circadian regulation62,63, future research should include both sexes. Second, the study did not directly assess sleep patterns or melatonin levels, which could provide mechanistic insights. Third, although the behavioral assays are robust, longitudinal studies assessing whether the observed myelin impairments persist into adulthood would help clarify the long-term impact of adolescent LED exposure.

Fourth, the present study is that the control animals were not subjected to the same daily transfer procedure as the LED- and ILE-exposed groups. Although the LED and ILE groups experienced identical handling, transfer, and housing conditions, allowing direct comparison of light source-specific effects, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that repeated transfer or environmental novelty contributed to some differences observed relative to the control group. Future studies incorporating a sham-transfer control group will help further distinguish the effects of light exposure from potential handling-related stress.

Fifth, Although the novel object recognition task suggested impaired recognition memory following LED exposure, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that increased anxiety-like behavior contributed to reduced exploration of the novel object. Future studies incorporating more detailed analyses of object exploration patterns or additional memory paradigms may help further dissociate cognitive impairment from anxiety-related behavioral changes.

In addition, MBP quantification was performed across the entire PrL and IL regions of the mPFC. Although representative images suggest that deeper cortical layers may be particularly affected by LED exposure, layer-specific analyses were not performed in the present study. Future studies examining myelination across individual cortical layers may provide further insight into the regional vulnerability of mPFC oligodendrocytes to nighttime light exposure.

Moreover, future research should also examine whether interventions—such as melatonin supplementation or environmental light modulation—can rescue LED-induced myelin deficits. At the molecular level, transcriptomic or single-cell analyses could elucidate downstream pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, mitochondrial dynamics, or myelin gene transcription) underlying the observed cellular phenotypes.

Conclusions

The results demonstrate that chronic nighttime LED light exposure induces anxiety-like behavior and impairs multiple cognitive functions, including novel object recognition, social behavior, and spatial and associative learning in adolescent rats. These behavioral deficits are associated with a specific reduction in myelination and oligodendrocyte maturation in the mPFC. This study establishes a link between a prevalent environmental factor (LED light) and prefrontal cortex-dependent cognitive function, offering important insights into the potential neurological impacts of artificial lighting.
