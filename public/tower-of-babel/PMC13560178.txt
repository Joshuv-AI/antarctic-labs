Predicting heterosexual reactions to coming out in China
Authors: Gu Li, Xiaoyu Huang, Cong Peng, Jiayu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor many Chinese sexual minority men, coming out—disclosing their sexual orientation—is stressful because of concerns about how heterosexual recipients may react. This study examined predictors of those reactions and applied machine learning to model them. Between August 2022 and January 2023, we screened 54,902 individuals on a Chinese crowdsourcing platform and recruited 650 Chinese cisgender heterosexual participants who had experienced a coming-out encounter with a sexual minority man. Participants reported on 85 personal, contextual, and behavioral factors surrounding the encounter. Bivariate analyses and machine learning models revealed that background characteristics—such as the recipient’s sex, age, and relationship to the discloser—were the strongest predictors of reactions. Smaller contributions emerged from select contextual parameters and coming-out strategies: negative reactions were linked to disclosures involving a love confession, assault, or substance use by disclosers; positive reactions were associated with recipients anticipating the disclosure and disclosers referencing a same-sex romantic relationship. The machine learning models captured complex interactions among predictors. These models achieved moderate predictive accuracy (ACC = 0.62–0.65, R2 = 0.20–0.31), comparable to that reported in prior machine learning studies in social and personality psychology. While recipients’ reactions are predictable, improving coming-out experiences in high-stigma contexts requires attention to the recipient-side processes that sustain or challenge heteronormative norms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05032-0.Keywords: Coming out, Disclosure, Sexual minority, LGB, CART, Random forests

Introduction

As sexual minority individuals develop and understand their sexual orientation, many choose to disclose it to others—a process known as “coming out” [1, 2]. In heteronormative societies, such disclosures can elicit diverse reactions from recipients. For heterosexual recipients in particular, these reactions may involve immediate behavioral responses (e.g., rejection) and longer-term changes in relational dynamics (e.g., distancing) and social attitudes (e.g., prejudice). While the consequences of heterosexual recipients’ reactions for disclosers are well theorized [3] and documented [4–11], much less is known about what predicts these reactions (reviewed in [12]). Which factors matter most, and how accurately can these reactions be predicted?

Addressing these questions has theoretical and practical significance. The coming-out process has been primarily examined through the disclosure process model [3, 12], which relies on sexual minority individuals’ retrospective accounts of their disclosure motivations, strategies, and outcomes. Incorporating heterosexual recipients’ perspectives can reveal patterns of support, discomfort, or misunderstanding that disclosers may not perceive, thereby enriching existing frameworks. It also extends intergroup contact theory [13, 14] to the coming-out context by specifying when and how intergroup contact facilitates or inhibits stigma reduction. Practically, many sexual minority individuals must balance desires for self-expression with anticipated social risks when making decisions about to whom, when, and how to come out [3, 5]. Improving the ability to anticipate recipients’ reactions may therefore help sexual minority individuals navigate these tradeoffs.

The social risks of coming out are especially salient in high-stigma contexts like China, where heteronormativity prevails at multiple levels. At the interpersonal level, nearly 80% of adults disapprove of same-sex sexual behaviors (with higher disapproval among older adults) [15], and 20%–50% of sexual minority individuals report victimization in families, schools, workplaces, or healthcare systems [4, 16–18]. At the structural level, LGBTQ+-protective laws are absent [19], and state restrictions have curtailed LGBTQ+ visibility through suspending pride events, suppressing advocacy, and censoring online spaces [20]. At the cultural level, Confucian values such as filial piety and collectivism intensify heteronormative expectations surrounding marriage and child-rearing [21, 22]. Within this context, we focused on coming-out processes involving Chinese sexual minority men, who face harsher public attitudes [23], stronger heteronormative pressures [24], and more pronounced identity conflicts [25] than sexual minority women. Consequently, negative reactions from heterosexual recipients remain both common and consequential [4, 17, 18, 26], underscoring the need to examine how such reactions can be predicted.

Making accurate predictions raises two methodological challenges. The first is selecting appropriate statistical techniques. Predicting real-world phenomena requires methods capable of handling large numbers of predictors, detecting nonlinear associations, and capturing complex interactions among variables [27–29]. Traditional regression approaches, such as ordinary least squares and standard logistic regression, are often less well suited to accommodate these demands simultaneously because they typically require researchers to specify nonlinear terms and interactions a priori and may become unstable when model complexity is high relative to sample size [28, 29]. In contrast, tree-based machine learning algorithms can flexibly model nonlinearities and higher-order interactions with fewer assumptions about underlying data structure, and they have frequently demonstrated superior predictive performance in complex behavioral prediction tasks [27–31]. Therefore, we employ tree-based machine learning to model heterosexual recipients’ reactions.

The second challenge concerns which predictors to include. Although machine learning can detect novel predictors, it has been criticized for producing results that are atheoretical or difficult to interpret [27, 29]. To address this concern, the present study integrated two complementary theoretical frameworks to guide predictor selection. The intergroup contact theory [13, 14] highlights recipients’ characteristics, situational conditions, and interaction management [32–34], whereas the disclosure processes model [3] emphasizes who is involved, where disclosure occurs, and how disclosure is managed. Together, these frameworks informed the inclusion of predictors across three domains: recipient and discloser background, contextual parameters, and coming-out strategies.

Within each domain, we selected predictors using a hybrid strategy that combined hypothesis-driven and exploratory considerations. Hypothesis-driven predictors were directly derived from theories (e.g., recipients’ prior contact with LGBTQ+ individuals, derived from the intergroup contact theory [35]) or empirical evidence (e.g., recipients’ age [23]). Exploratory predictors were conceptually relevant but underexamined, such as specific verbal disclosure strategies implied by the disclosure processes model [3] but lacking empirical validation (see Coming-Out Strategies section). Meanwhile, some factors were excluded for reasons of ecological and predictive utility. For example, psychological traits such as essentialist beliefs or right-wing authoritarianism may shape heterosexual reactions [36, 37]. Disclosers’ perceptions of such traits may also influence disclosure expectations and decisions. However, because the present study surveyed heterosexual recipients rather than sexual minority disclosers, we could not directly assess disclosers’ perceptions of those traits. In addition, because our primary aim was to evaluate how accurately reactions can be forecast based on information accessible to the discloser, we excluded latent psychological traits to maximize the applied relevance of the predictive models. This hybrid strategy offered broader and more systematic coverage than prior work (reviewed in [12]) and informed the expanded predictor pool outlined below.

Recipient and discloser background

The social positions and personal histories of both recipients and disclosers shape the interpersonal context of a coming-out encounter [3, 14]. Recipients’ demographic characteristics—such as age, sex, and education—predict attitudes toward sexual minority individuals [23, 36, 38]. Consistent with the intergroup contact theory [13, 14], heterosexual recipients with prior positive contact or who reside in LGBTQ+-supportive communities tend to hold favorable attitudes [35, 39]. Disclosers’ characteristics also matter: Bisexual individuals often receive less validation than lesbian or gay individuals [40]. Relationship type (e.g., family, friend, acquaintance, or stranger) and closeness can further shape reactions, with supportive responses more likely in close relationships (reviewed in [41]). Therefore, we hypothesize:

Contextual parameters

The spatial and temporal contexts of disclosure determine where and when it occurs, both of which influence recipients’ reactions [3]. Private or casual settings can ease tension, while formal contexts may heighten discomfort [42]. Timing also matters. Spontaneous disclosures, especially during LGBTQ+-affirming conversations, often elicit more positive reactions than disclosures made under pressure (e.g., when being forced to come out after being overheard or confronted [42]). Reactions tend to be more positive when recipients have previously suspected the discloser’s sexual orientation [43], echoing the evidence that imagined contact reduces prejudice [44]. In contrast, unexpected disclosures for heterosexual recipients—especially those involving perceived sexual or romantic interest—may provoke discomfort or rejection [45]. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H2. Respectful contexts that minimize direct conflicts, encourage meaningful conversations on LGBTQ+-affirming topics, and allow recipients to anticipate the disclosure are conducive to positive reactions; disrespectful contexts involving assault or unexpected confessions of romantic or sexual interest are expected to elicit negative reactions (Table 1).

Respectful contexts that minimize direct conflicts, encourage meaningful conversations on LGBTQ+-affirming topics, and allow recipients to anticipate the disclosure are conducive to positive reactions; disrespectful contexts involving assault or unexpected confessions of romantic or sexual interest are expected to elicit negative reactions (Table 1).

Coming-out strategies

Disclosure strategies define how coming-out interactions are managed [3, 14]. In-person verbal disclosure is common [46], and greater narrative depth (e.g., sharing plans to build a family with a same-sex partner) may foster empathy [3]. Mediated verbal disclosure—via messages, letters, or digital communication—can afford greater self-expression and emotional depth, sometimes strengthening relational bonds with recipients (reviewed in [41]). Alternatively, disclosing through a supportive third party may further soften recipients’ reactions (e.g., [47]). Group-based disclosure, including introducing LGBTQ+ friends or sharing affirmative content on social media, can normalize sexual minority experiences [48, 49]. However, disclosures through group chats may create a “many minds problem,” in which communication among many recipients can dilute message clarity and reduce opportunities for meaningful dialogue [50]. Nonverbal cues (e.g., appearance or body language) may be interpreted by others as signaling sexual orientation [51, 52], although such interpretations can reflect recipients’ stereotypes and may be associated with negative reactions [53]. Accordingly, we hypothesize:

H3. Strategies that facilitate empathetic and meaningful communication are likely to elicit positive reactions, whereas strategies that reinforce stereotypes about gender and sexuality may elicit negative reactions (Table 1).

The present study

The present study examined heterosexual recipients’ reactions to first-time coming-out encounters with a sexual minority man. Our first goal was to identify predictors of heterosexual recipients’ reactions using bivariate analyses and machine learning. Bivariate analyses (e.g., correlations) reveal main effects, whereas machine learning identifies salient predictors, nonlinear associations, and interactions [30, 31]. We tested three sets of nonpreregistered hypotheses for the bivariate analyses (summarized above and detailed in Table 1). Because machine learning is not designed for hypothesis testing [30, 31], we did not propose hypotheses about nonlinear associations or interactions. Our second goal was to predict heterosexual recipients’ reactions using two complementary tree-based machine learning techniques: classification and regression trees (CART) and random forests [30]. Both models handle large, complex data sets and allow for nonlinearities and complex interactions among predictors. In addition to identifying key predictors, they help evaluate predictive accuracy and visualize predictor interactions.

Method

Transparency and open science

The research question, study materials, procedure, and analytic plan were preregistered prior to data collection on the Open Science Framework (OSF) at https://osf.io/eb3rk/?view_only=a7464e5da3364209ac287145318917dc. The preregistration form, along with documents detailing changes to the preregistered plan, as well as other study materials such as questionnaires, data, and computer codes, are available on this website. We report how we determined our sample size, all data exclusions, all manipulations, and all measures in the study. ChatGPT-5 [54] was used to proofread this manuscript.

Participants

All procedures and materials were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at NYU Shanghai (2022-031; study title: “Coming Out in China II”). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before the study.

The analytic sample comprised 650 Chinese cisgender heterosexual adults who had experienced at least one coming-out event from a sexual minority man. Participants were recruited via Credamo, a Chinese online sampling platform. The geographic and demographic diversity of Credamo’s sampling pool, as well as its data quality, have been supported by prior large-scale research [55]. Credamo implemented graphic CAPTCHA tasks for bot detection and allowed us to prevent duplicate responses from the same IP address.

We employed a two-stage screening process. In the prescreening stage, 54,902 individuals were assessed against preregistered inclusion criteria (age ≥ 18 years, cisgender identity, exclusively heterosexual orientation, and prior experience of a coming-out event from a sexual minority man). Eligible individuals were then invited to the main survey, where eligibility was reassessed alongside additional preregistered requirements (e.g., Chinese nationality and residence). Participants who did not meet the inclusion criteria or failed preregistered attention checks were excluded.

Data were collected between August 25, 2022 and January 31, 2023. Data collection was extended 1 month beyond the preregistered plan to approach the target sample size (see “Changes to Preregistration” on OSF). Additional nonpreregistered exclusion criteria were applied to enhance data quality (e.g., removing participants who reported experiencing a coming-out event at an age older than their current age; removing participants who reported incongruent sex assigned at birth in the prescreening and main surveys; see “Changes to Preregistration” on OSF), yielding the final sample of 650 participants (see Figure S1 for the flow diagram).

Among the final sample, 411 (63.2%) were cisgender women and 239 (36.8%) were cisgender men. The mean age was 25.17 years (SD = 6.05, range = 18–57). Full participant characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Procedure

The main survey comprised four sections. In the first section, participants provided demographic information. In the second section, eligible participants rated 32 coming-out strategies based on their effectiveness in conveying a sexual minority man’s sexual orientation1. Answering these questions early in the survey familiarized participants with the survey’s broad focus on coming-out experiences.

In the third section, participants recalled their first experience of a sexual minority man coming out to them. They reported (a) background characteristics about the discloser, (b) contextual parameters of the event, (c) coming-out strategies used by the discloser and their own feelings about each strategy used2, and (d) outcomes of the event.

Participants who had experienced multiple coming-out events completed a fourth section, where they recalled their experience of a second sexual minority man coming out to them using the same set of questions. Given the limited number of participants reporting a second event (n = 324), these findings are presented in the Supplemental Material. The smaller sample size may limit the replicability of these results. Upon completing the survey, participants were debriefed on the study’s objectives.

Measures

Predictors

Table S1 presents the 85 predictors3 related to participants’ first coming-out encounter with a sexual minority man. These predictors fall into three categories: (a) participant and discloser background, (b) contextual parameters of the coming-out event, and (c) coming-out strategies.

Participant and discloser background

Participants reported their demographic background, including sex assigned at birth, education, and relationship status. They also provided details about the coming-out event, such as their age at the time, the discloser’s sexual orientation, and their relationship closeness with the discloser. This category includes 22 predictors.

Contextual parameters

Participants recalled various contextual aspects of the event (25 predictors), including the medium of disclosure (e.g., in person, instant messaging), occasion (e.g., casual, formal), venue (e.g., private, public), and context (e.g., whether the discloser was pressured to come out or if the discloser assaulted the participant). Participants also indicated whether they had previously suspected, imagined, or hoped that the discloser would come out to them. An “I cannot remember” option was provided for each question. This response was treated as a valid response in the analyses rather than as a missing value, because (1) it more accurately reflected participants’ recall status than missing values, and (2) treating it as missing can contribute to split selection bias in CART models [56].

Coming-out strategies

Participants reported whether the discloser used any of the five broad categories of coming-out strategies (0 = No, 1 = Yes): in-person verbal disclosure, mediated verbal disclosure, group-based disclosure, disclosure through appearance (e.g., make-up, clothing, accessories, or hairstyle), and disclosure using other nonverbal cues (e.g., voices, facial expressions, gestures, or body language). A “none of the above” option was also available.

If participants selected a broad category, they were prompted to specify individual strategies within that category. For example, under “in-person verbal disclosure,” options included “direct reference to his sexual identity (‘I am gay.’)” and “direct mention of his romantic relationship (e.g., ‘I am in love with a man.’).” If a broad category was not selected, all individual strategies within that category were coded as “No.” This two-stage approach minimized participant burden. In total, 38 predictors were derived, including the five broad categories, the “none of the above” option, and 32 individual strategies.

Outcomes

Descriptive statistics for the three outcome variables of the first coming-out event are presented in Table S1: (a) participant’s immediate behavioral reactions, (b) impact on the participant-discloser relationship, and (c) impact on the participant’s attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people.

Participant’s immediate behavioral reactions

Participants selected their immediate behavioral reactions to the coming-out event from 22 options (e.g., “I had verbal or physical conflict with him”; “I changed the subject”; or “I told him that I would like to help him as much as he needs”). Latent class analysis (LCA) [57] identified three behavioral response patterns: negative, neutral, and positive (Tables S3 and S5).

Impact on the participant-discloser relationship

Participants rated how the coming-out event impacted their relationship with the discloser on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 5 (very positive), with an “I cannot remember” option. No participants selected this “I cannot remember” option for the first coming-out event.

Impact on the participant’s attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people

Participants rated how the coming-out event influenced their attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 5 (very positive), with an “I cannot remember” option. As only one participant selected this option for the first coming-out event, their data were excluded from subsequent analyses concerning this outcome.

Analytic plan

Descriptive analysis

All analyses were preregistered unless otherwise noted.4 Before conducting the primary analysis, descriptive statistics were computed for all variables.

Hypothesis testing of bivariate associations

To test H1–H3, bivariate associations between predictors and outcomes were examined using Pearson’s correlation, Spearman’s correlation, analysis of variance, and chi-square analysis. Bonferroni adjustment was applied to control for familywise Type I error. Although less conservative procedures such as the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure control the false discovery rate [58], we applied the Bonferroni adjustment because it provides stricter control of the familywise error rate, thereby reducing the likelihood of false-positive findings across the large number of bivariate tests conducted.

Classification and regression trees (CART)

CART is capable of accommodating nonlinear associations and complex interactions among predictors. Analyses were conducted separately for each of the three outcomes using the R package “rpart” (version 4.1.19) [59], with all 85 predictors entered into each model (see Supplemental Material for technical details). Including all available predictors without prior screening was consistent with our preregistered analysis plan (see OSF), thereby enhancing transparency and minimizing researcher-driven predictor selection decisions. In addition, because the present study was exploratory, retaining the full predictor set allowed the models to detect potentially important nonlinearities and higher-order interactions that may have been missed through manual variable selection [28, 31]. Prescreening predictors based on bivariate significance was also avoided because such procedures may exclude predictors that contribute only conditionally through interactions or nonlinear effects [28, 31]. Tree-based machine learning methods are designed to accommodate relatively large predictor sets through data-driven variable selection during model construction [28, 31].

The CART algorithm iteratively splits participants into increasingly homogenous groups based on the predictor variable that maximally reduces impurity at each step [30, 31]. This process continues until stopping criteria—such as impurity reduction thresholds or minimum node sizes—are met, generating a decision tree that illustrates nonlinear interactions among predictors.

To evaluate how effectively our models predicted the outcomes, several performance metrics were employed [30]. For continuous outcomes (in regression trees), we used mean squared error (MSE) and its root-square derivative (RMSE) to represent the average discrepancy between the model’s prediction and the observed outcome; lower values indicate a more accurate model. In addition, R2 was calculated to measure the percentage of the outcome’s variance explained by the model, with cut-off values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 indicating small, medium, and large effects [60]. For categorical outcomes (in classification trees), accuracy (ACC) was reported to represent the percentage of correct classifications, which was compared against the “no information rate” (NIR; the accuracy one would achieve by pure chance); larger discrepancy indicating better model performance. In addition, Cohen’s kappa (κ) was used to assess accuracy while accounting for the possibility of correct guesses by chance, with cut-off values of 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80 indicating fair, moderate, substantial, and almost perfect agreement [61].

Random forests

Random forests, another machine learning technique, enhances predictive accuracy by aggregating multiple decision trees [28, 30, 31]. Unlike CART, which generates a single tree, random forests introduce randomness in predictor selection across trees, thereby improving generalizability. However, because the algorithm does not produce an “average tree,” visualizing interactions among predictors is difficult. Instead, variable importance estimates summarize each predictor’s contribution. Analyses were conducted using the R package “randomForest” (version 4.7–1.1) [62]. Full methodological details are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted for both LCA and CART. For LCA, random-starts values were varied to evaluate classification stability. For CART, robustness was examined by (a) including participants initially excluded based on nonpreregistered data-quality criteria (see “Participants”), (b) restricting analyses to data collected by the preregistered end date, and (c) varying seed numbers to assess the influence of randomness.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Table S1 presents the descriptive statistics for all variables related to participants’ first coming-out encounter with a sexual minority man. On average, participants were 21.08 years (SD = 4.50, range = 12–48) at the time of this encounter. Most disclosers were individuals within participants’ social circles (71.7%), followed by those from their workplace or school (13.9%). Most disclosers identified as gay (64.9%) or bisexual (17.9%) according to the participants, while the remaining disclosers were “heterosexual with same-sex sexual attraction or same-sex sexual behavior” (7.4%), “sexual minority but not sure to which of the above groups he belonged” (9.2%), or “other” (0.6%). Regarding contextual parameters, disclosures most occurred one-on-one (50.8%), within casual conversations (69.9%), and were spontaneous (74.8%). The most frequently used coming-out strategies from the broad categories included in-person verbal disclosure (50.2%) and mediated verbal disclosure (49.2%).

For outcome variables, 31.7%, 28.0%, and 40.3% of participants reported negative, neutral, or positive immediate behavioral reactions. The perceived impact of the coming-out event on participants’ relationships with disclosers was, on average, neutral (M = 2.88, SD = 0.78), as was its impact on participants’ attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people (M = 3.01, SD = 0.77).

Hypothesis testing of bivariate associations

H1: participant and discloser background as predictors

Table S7 presents the bivariate analyses examining associations between background characteristics and outcomes. Supporting H1a, the participant’s sex assigned at birth significantly correlated with all three outcomes: immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 74.66, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.34; relationship impact (i.e., impact on the participant-discloser relationship), r(648) = 0.38, padj. < 0.001; and attitudinal impact (i.e., impact on attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people), r(647) = 0.32, padj. < 0.001. Female recipients (63.2% endorsement among participants; Table S1) exhibited more favorable immediate behavioral reactions than male recipients (36.8%), with moderate-to-large effect sizes. Supporting H1b, younger participants responded more positively, as indicated by negative correlations between their age and immediate behavioral reactions, ρ(648) = –0.30, padj. < 0.001, and between age and attitudinal impact, r(647) = –0.16, padj. = 0.009; these effects were in the moderate and small ranges, respectively. However, inconsistent with H1c–e, participants’ education level, prior exposure to LGBTQ+ individuals, and provincial-level attitudes toward same-sex sexual behaviors in their current residence did not significantly correlate with the outcomes after Bonferroni adjustment, padj. > 0.05.

Supporting H1f, participants who encountered disclosure from a gay man (64.9%) reported more positive immediate behavioral reactions than those whose encounter involved a bisexual man (17.9%) or a heterosexual man with same-sex sexual attraction or behavior (7.4%), χ2(8, N = 650) = 103.56, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.28 (a large effect; Table S7). Supporting H1g, greater closeness with the discloser was associated with more positive immediate behavioral reactions, ρ(648) = 0.21, padj. < 0.001, and more positive relationship impact, r(648) = 0.17, padj. = 0.006; these effects were in the small range. Table 1 summarizes the results of hypothesis tests.

Among the nonhypothesized predictors, married participants (24.8%) reported more negative outcomes than their unmarried counterparts, including more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 107.60, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.41 (a large effect), more negative relationship impact, r(648) = –0.18, padj. < 0.001 (a small effect), and more negative attitudinal impact, r(647) = –0.16, padj. = 0.007 (a small effect).

H2: contextual parameters as predictors

Table S7 also presents the bivariate analyses examining how contextual parameters of the coming-out event relate to the outcomes. Inconsistent with H2a–b, coming out in private venues (22.5%) or during casual occasions (47.5%) did not significantly predict the outcomes after Bonferroni adjustment, padj. > 0.05. However, supporting H2c–d, spontaneous disclosure (74.8%) was associated with more positive immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 26.08, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.20. Similarly, discussing LGBTQ+-affirming topics prior to disclosure (22.0%; e.g., LGBTQ+ celebrities or LGBTQ+-themed movies) was associated with more positive immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 22.08, padj. = 0.004, V = 0.18, and more positive attitudinal impact, r(647) = 0.15, padj. = 0.025. All effects for H2c–d were in the small range. Supporting H2e, participants who had previously hoped for the discloser to come out (5.7%) reported more positive immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(8, N = 650) = 83.77, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.25, as well as more positive relationship impact, F(4, 645) = 23.96, padj. < 0.001, ω2 = 0.12, and attitudinal impact, F(4, 644) = 21.62, padj. < 0.001, ω2 = 0.11; effect sizes ranged from moderate to large. Overall, these findings suggest that, while evidence for physical settings is scarce, disclosures made at a time following spontaneous conversations about LGBTQ+-affirming topics or when recipients can anticipate them are associated with more positive outcomes.

Conversely, supporting H2f, disclosures in which the discloser confessed love to the participant (12.5%) were associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 56.39, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.29, as well as more negative relationship impact, r(648) = –0.26, padj. < 0.001, and attitudinal impact, r(647) = –0.20, padj. < 0.001; effects were in the small-to-moderate range (Table S7). Similarly, supporting H2g, being assaulted by the discloser (6.6%) was associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 38.61, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.24, more negative relationship impact, r(648) = –0.25, padj. < 0.001, and attitudinal impact, r(647) = –0.27, padj. < 0.001; effects were again in the small-to-moderate range. These findings supported H2, indicating that unexpected or disrespectful behaviors during disclosure are associated with more negative outcomes (see Table 1 for a summary).

Two additional, nonhypothesized contextual parameters were significantly associated with at least two outcomes (Table S7). First, disclosing to a group (40.8%), rather than individually, was associated with more favorable immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(4, N = 650) = 73.98, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.24 (a moderate effect), as well as more positive relationship impact, F(2, 647) = 11.07, padj. = 0.005, ω2 = 0.030 (a small effect), and attitudinal impact, F(2, 646) = 10.03, padj. = 0.013, ω2 = 0.027 (a small effect). Second, coming out via a call (8.9%) was associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 33.82, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.23 (a moderate effect), and more negative relationship impact, r(648) = –0.16, padj. = 0.014 (a small effect).

H3: coming-out strategies as predictors

Overall, coming-out strategies exhibited weaker associations with the outcomes than background characteristics and contextual parameters, with one notable exception (Table S7). Supporting H3a, in-person verbal disclosure referencing the discloser’s same-sex romantic relationship (26.0%) was associated with more positive immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 27.72, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.21, indicating a moderate effect.

Contrary to H3b, mediated verbal disclosure was negatively or negligibly associated with the outcomes (Table S7). Specifically, (1) asynchronously mediated disclosure (e.g., through recorded video or letter; 37.5%) was associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 35.17, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.23 (a moderate effect), and more negative attitudinal impact, r(647) = –0.17, padj. < 0.001 (a small effect); (2) synchronously mediated disclosure (e.g., through phone call; 18.5%) was associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 25.20, padj. < 0.001, V = 0.20 (a small-to-moderate effect); and (3) disclosure through another person (8.5%) did not significantly predict any outcomes after Bonferroni adjustment, padj. > 0.05. Additionally, contrary to H3c, group-based disclosure in which the discloser advocated for LGBTQ+ rights (11.1%) was associated with more negative immediate behavioral reactions, χ2(2, N = 650) = 20.78, padj. = 0.008, V = 0.18, reflecting a small effect. Finally, inconsistent with H3d, nonverbal disclosure did not significantly predict any outcomes after Bonferroni adjustment, padj. > 0.05. Together, these findings suggest that strategies promoting meaningful communication may facilitate recipient acceptance, but this effect appears limited to in-person conversations that elicit empathy and minimize value conflict (see Table 1 for a summary).

Predicting the participant’s immediate behavioral reactions

The first CART model, predicting the participant’s immediate behavioral reactions, demonstrated moderate fit, κ = 0.46. Its predictive accuracy was 25% higher than the baseline model, ACC = 0.65, 95% CI [0.62, 0.69], NIR = 0.40. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the primary split occurred by the participant’s marital status, with unmarried participants responding more positively and married participants reacting more negatively. Among unmarried participants, the closeness between the participant and the discloser emerged as a key predictor, with closer relationships associated with more positive immediate behavioral reactions. Further splits revealed that among those with close relationships, females reacted more positively, whereas males responded more negatively. Additional splits suggested that females reacted more negatively when the discloser confessed love to them while coming out (e.g., as bisexual), whereas males reacted more positively when the discloser mentioned having a same-sex romantic relationship.

Discussion

Coming out can be a profound yet stressful experience for Chinese sexual minority men [21]. When met with acceptance, it can foster mutual understanding and support [5, 48]. This study provides an exploratory examination of heterosexual recipients’ reactions to coming-out encounters. These reactions encompass immediate behavioral reactions, impact on the recipient-discloser relationship, and impact on the recipient’s attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people.

Important predictors of heterosexual recipients’ reactions

Our first goal was to identify informative predictors using bivariate analyses and machine learning. Several robust predictors emerged across the three domains: discloser and recipient background, contextual parameters, and coming-out strategies.

Consistent with H1, key background characteristics included the recipient’s sex assigned at birth, age, age at the coming-out encounter, and marital status; the discloser’s sexual orientation; and the closeness of their relationship—all in line with prior research on demographic correlates of homophobia and biphobia [23, 36, 38, 40]. In addition, provincial-level attitudes toward same-sex sexual behaviors in the recipient’s region of residence were an important predictor in machine learning models predicting the impact on the recipient-discloser relationship. This finding echoes prior work [39] and underscores broader sociocultural influences on heterosexual recipients’ reactions to coming out.

Consistent with H2, contexts that foster respect, minimize conflict, or allow recipients to anticipate disclosure predicted positive reactions. For example, spontaneous disclosures following prior LGBTQ+-affirming conversations were associated with positive reactions, likely because such discussions allowed disclosers to gauge recipients’ attitudes beforehand and prepared recipients for a more receptive exchange. This strategy aligns with a culturally specific approach known in the Chinese LGBTQ+ community as “pu lu” (literally “paving the road”), which refers to a preparatory process intended to facilitate successful disclosure [47]. In contrast, conflict-laden contexts were linked to negative reactions, such as those involving a confession of romantic feelings, aggression, or substance use by the discloser. These findings suggest that recipients’ acceptance depends on supportive environments that enable respectful, stigma-free communication.

Among the coming-out strategies, in-person verbal disclosure with detailed narratives, especially those referencing a same-sex relationship, predicted positive reactions. Supporting H3, this finding suggests that sharing personal experiences fosters deeper communication and empathy in heterosexual recipients [3, 42]. It may also alleviate recipients’ concerns about potential unwanted sexual or romantic interest from the discloser [45]. In contrast, mediated verbal disclosures (e.g., disclosing via call, letter, or a third party) did not show the expected positive associations, reinforcing parallel findings on contextual parameters that favor in-person over mediated communication. Group-based disclosure was generally associated with positive outcomes but yielded counterproductive effects when coupled with LGBTQ+ advocacy, indicating that their effectiveness depends on group dynamics and discussion topics [50]. Finally, nonverbal disclosure was not significant in bivariate analyses but occasionally emerged in machine learning models, suggesting that their effects may depend on interactions with other factors.

Predictive power of machine learning models

Our second goal was to evaluate the predictive performance of the CART and random-forests models. Both achieved accuracy rates above 60% in predicting immediate behavioral reactions, exceeding the 40% chance benchmark (indexed by the NIR). The κ coefficients exceeded 0.40, indicating moderate agreement between predicted and observed classifications [61]. For continuous outcomes, prediction error was modest: On a 1–5 scale, average absolute deviations between predicted and observed values were under 0.75 units. The models also explained up to 31% of the variance in relationship impact and 20% in attitudinal shifts—effect sizes that are considered large in psychological research [60].

Although imperfect, these modest predictive accuracy rates are notable when viewed alongside comparable machine learning applications in psychology. Prior work predicting relatively stable constructs, such as personality traits, typically explains only 10%–25% of the variance [63, 64]. Machine learning studies of more contextualized human experiences, such as initial romantic attraction [65] and long-term relationship quality [66], reported up to 27% and 18% of explained variance, respectively. Achieving comparable or stronger performance for a highly contextualized social behavior suggests that heterosexual recipients’ reactions are not idiosyncratic, but follow predictable patterns when personal, contextual, and behavioral factors are jointly modeled.

Strengths and implications

This study extends prior research in three ways. First, it applies tree-based machine learning to model complex interactions among many predictors—patterns difficult to specify or estimate with regression. For example, the CART models (Figs. 2 and 3) demonstrated an interaction among the discloser’s sexual orientation, the recipient’s sex assigned at birth, and the recipient’s anticipation of disclosure: female recipients who had not anticipated the disclosure tended to react more negatively to bisexual disclosers. One possible interpretation is that unexpected disclosures of bisexuality may be perceived as ambiguous and may involve potential romantic or sexual interest from the male discloser, which may trigger discomfort in the female recipient [67]. This illustrates how machine learning can uncover complex conditional patterns—where the effect of one predictor depends on the levels of several others—that would be difficult to hypothesize a priori.

Second, incorporating heterosexual recipients’ perspectives complements prior research centered on sexual minority disclosers. Many findings from both perspectives converged, suggesting shared psychological processes in how coming-out encounters are interpreted. Meanwhile, recipients’ accounts revealed novel insights that challenge assumptions in the current literature. For example, private or casual settings commonly emphasized by disclosers [42] carried less weight for recipients. In addition, adverse disclosure practices, notably confession of romantic interest, emerged as robust predictors of negative reactions, despite being rarely discussed in sexual minority narratives (cf. [45]).

These discrepancies highlight relational and structural asymmetries in coming-out encounters. Sexual minority disclosers engage in greater stigma-related vigilance, whereas heterosexual recipients, situated in relative social privilege, may be less attuned to contextual cues. In addition, the unidirectional nature of disclosure, where only sexual minority individuals must reveal a stigmatized identity, positions heterosexual recipients reactively and may amplify their sensitivity to perceived interpersonal threat [68–70]. For example, heterosexual men’s negative reactions to same-sex romantic confessions may reflect concerns over threats to their heterosexual masculinity, a socially privileged and tightly policed status [45, 71, 72]. These findings extend the disclosure processes model [3] by showing that outcomes depend not only on disclosers’ motivations and strategies but also on recipients’ characteristics, expectations, and interpretations, underscoring the need to consider both parties’ perspectives when examining coming-out processes.

Third, this study situates coming out within China’s distinct sociocultural context. Several findings diverged from the Western literature: Recipient characteristics such as education and prior intergroup contact did not significantly predict reactions [23, 35, 38], likely reflecting limited LGBTQ+ content in education, persistent stigmatization, and few opportunities for meaningful intergroup contact [15, 19, 20, 73]. Moreover, background characteristics outweighed contextual parameters and coming-out strategies, suggesting that structural and cultural constraints strongly shape heterosexual recipients’ reactions in high-stigma environments, where negative responses often reflect broader social forces rather than solely disclosers’ actions.

Limitations

This study has several methodological limitations. First, compared with other psychological studies employing tree-based machine learning (e.g., [74]), our sample size was moderate relative to the number of predictors. Simulation studies suggest that tree-based methods remain sensitive to sample size relative to model complexity, and that performance instability may persist when many predictors are modeled simultaneously [75]. However, CART and random forests algorithms incorporate built-in regularization mechanisms—including cost-complexity pruning and bootstrap aggregation—that constrain model complexity and reduce the risk of modeling random noise rather than meaningful patterns in the data [28, 29, 76]. Residual overfitting was further mitigated through cross-validation and out-of-bag error estimation to guide pruning decisions [29, 30]. Critically, the convergence of findings across CART, random forests, and sensitivity analyses increases confidence in the overall pattern of results. Nonetheless, future research should recruit larger and more diverse samples to strengthen generalizability. Achieving a substantially larger sample in the present study was constrained by the rarity of the target population, as 54,902 individuals were screened to identify the cisgender heterosexual participants who had experienced a coming-out encounter with a sexual minority man.

Second, the focus on heterosexual recipients excluded discloser-related factors such as social support, coming-out preparation, and disclosure goals (e.g., [5, 42]), which could further improve model performance and theoretical completeness. Third, this study examined only first-time coming-out encounters, though acceptance often unfolds across repeated disclosures to the same recipient [3]. Fourth, because the study focused exclusively on encounters involving sexual minority men, the findings may not generalize to encounters involving sexual minority women. Finally, the data relied on retrospective accounts of potentially remote and emotionally charged events, which may introduce recall bias. Some details might have been forgotten [77], whereas emotionally salient disclosures might be overrepresented, influencing variable importance rankings in the machine learning models [78]. Additionally, recipients who had since developed closer relationships with the discloser might reinterpret the event more positively, further complicating recall accuracy [77].

Future directions

Building on our exploratory findings, several directions merit future investigation. First, future research could employ longitudinal methods to examine how immediate behavioral reactions develop into longer-term relational and attitudinal changes. Second, dyadic designs pairing disclosers and recipients would enable the study of coming out as a reciprocal interaction, potentially within an intergroup contact framework [13, 14]. Such designs could more directly examine how recipients’ psychological traits, disclosers’ perceptions of those traits, and discrepancies between the two shape disclosure experiences and outcomes. Third, future research could explore how predictive models may be integrated into supportive digital tools that offer LGBTQ+ individuals personalized insights for navigating disclosure situations with greater agency.

At the policy and practical levels, our findings highlight the need for tailored, recipient-focused interventions [47, 79–82]. Recipients’ background characteristics were significantly associated with their reactions to disclosure, suggesting that LGBTQ+-inclusive content in education and public messaging may be more effective when strategically adapted to demographic and social subgroups. Accordingly, these findings may help inform the development of targeted programs that address group-specific concerns, such as distinct sources of discomfort or cognitive biases. Further, institutions may consider implementing evidence-based guidelines that equip heterosexual allies with concrete strategies for supporting disclosures.

Conclusion

This study advances understanding of heterosexual recipients’ reactions to coming-out encounters by integrating theory-driven and data-driven approaches within the Chinese cultural context. By jointly modeling personal, contextual, and behavioral predictors, the findings identify important predictors and reveal that heterosexual reactions can be predicted with moderate accuracy. These results extend disclosure processes and intergroup contact theories by incorporating recipients’ perspectives in the interactive coming-out processes. Methodologically, the study demonstrates the utility of tree-based machine learning for uncovering complex interaction structures in social psychological data. Broadly, this work underscores that improving coming-out experiences requires attention not only to disclosers’ motivations and strategies but also to the recipient-side processes that sustain or challenge heteronormative norms.
