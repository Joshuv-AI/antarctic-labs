Efficient evaluation of the k k -space second Chern number in four dimensions
Xiang Liu, Xiao-Xia Yi, Zheng-Rong Liu, Rui Chen, Bin Zhou

Abstract
We propose an efficient numerical method to compute the k k -space second Chern number in four-dimensional (4D) topological systems. Our approach employs an adaptive mesh refinement scheme to evaluate the Brillouin-zone integral, which automatically increases the grid density in regions where the Berry curvature is sharply peaked. We compare our method with the 4D lattice-gauge extension of the Fukui-Hatsugai-Suzuki method and a direct uniform grid integration scheme. Compared with these approaches, our method (i) achieves the same accuracy with substantially fewer diagonalizations, and thus runs faster; (ii) requires minimal memory to execute, enabling calculations for larger systems; and (iii) remains accurate even near topological phase transitions where conventional methods often face challenges. These results demonstrate that the adaptive subdivision strategy is a practical and powerful tool for calculating the k k -space second Chern number.

II
Numerical methods for the Second Chern number

II.1
The second Chern number

We consider a translationally invariant 4D lattice system with Bloch
Hamiltonian
H(\bm{k})
, where
\bm{k}=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
spans the 4D
Brillouin zone (BZ). Let
\{\ket{u_a(\bm{k})}\}_{a=1}^{N_{\rm occ}}
denote an
orthonormal basis of the occupied-band subspace.
The non-Abelian Berry connection and curvature are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{ab}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=i\langle u_{a}(\bm{k})|\partial_{k_{\mu}}u_{b}(\bm{k})\rangle,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=\partial_{k_{\mu}}\mathcal{A}_{\nu}-\partial_{k_{\nu}}\mathcal{A}_{\mu}-i[\mathcal{A}_{\mu},\mathcal{A}_{\nu}],

(2)

where
\mu,\nu\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. The second Chern number
C_{2}
is given by the integral:

C_{2}=\frac{1}{32\pi^{2}}\int_{\rm BZ}d^{4}k\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\text{Tr}\left[\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\mathcal{F}_{\rho\sigma}(\bm{k})\right].

(3)

Here,
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}
denotes the four-dimensional totally antisymmetric Levi-Civita symbol, and the Einstein summation convention is implied for repeated indices. Using the antisymmetry
\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}=-\mathcal{F}_{\nu\mu}
, the
integrand can be written in terms of three pairings, e.g.

\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\Tr\!\left[\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}\mathcal{F}_{\rho\sigma}\right]=8\,\Tr\!\left(\mathcal{F}_{12}\mathcal{F}_{34}+\mathcal{F}_{41}\mathcal{F}_{32}+\mathcal{F}_{31}\mathcal{F}_{24}\right).

(4)

In gapped phases,
C_{2}
is
integer-quantized and changes only when the bulk gap closes.

II.2
The three methods

We introduce three methods to evaluate
C_{2}
. It should be emphasized that the primary
difficulty in computing
C_{2}
lies in the numerical evaluation of this four-dimensional
integral.

II.2.1
Method I: the extended FHS approach

For comparative purposes, let us first employ the lattice-gauge approach as a benchmark (Method I), which is a four-dimensional generalization of the FHS method
Mochol-Grzelak et al. 2018
;
Fukui et al. 2005
. This formulation ensures gauge invariance on a discrete lattice by constructing Wilson loops defined on elementary plaquettes. The detailed mathematical derivation and implementation of this method are presented in Appendix
A
.

II.2.2
Method II: direct uniform grid integration (Riemann sum)

To avoid the numerical differentiation of eigenstates in Eq. (
1
), we express the Berry connection elements directly in terms of the Hamiltonian derivatives
Xiao et al. 2010
:

\mathcal{A}_{\mu}^{ab}(\bm{k})=i\frac{\langle u_{a}(\bm{k})|\frac{\partial H}{\partial k_{\mu}}|u_{b}(\bm{k})\rangle}{E_{b}(\bm{k})-E_{a}(\bm{k})},\quad(a\neq b),

(5)

where
a
and
b
are band indices. This expression allows us to evaluate the Berry connection in Eq. (
1
) and
subsequently the Berry curvature in Eq. (
2
), at any arbitrary
\bm{k}

point without the need to construct a continuous gauge or compute numerical
derivatives of the wavefunctions.

We discretize the 4D Brillouin zone into a uniform hypercubic grid with
N

points along each dimension, resulting in a grid spacing of
\Delta k=2\pi/N
. The continuous integral for the second Chern number is approximated
by the discrete Riemann sum:

C_{2}\approx\frac{(\Delta k)^{4}}{32\pi^{2}}\sum_{\bm{k}\in\text{grid}}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\mathcal{F}_{\mu\nu}(\bm{k})\mathcal{F}_{\rho\sigma}(\bm{k})\right].

(6)

Here, the summation runs over all
N^{4}
points in the
Brillouin zone.

II.2.3
Method III: Adaptive mesh refinement

By inspecting Eq. (
5
), one observes that the Berry curvature is inversely proportional to the energy difference between occupied and unoccupied bands. In the vicinity of a topological phase transition, the bulk gap closes, causing the Berry curvature to exhibit extremely sharp divergences within the Brillouin zone. A uniform grid fails to resolve these localized singularities, leading to unstable and non-quantized values of
C_{2}
. Thus, Method II encounters significant difficulties near topological phase transitions.

To address this, we propose an adaptive mesh refinement scheme (Method III), as schematically illustrated in Fig.
1
. Instead of a static grid, this method dynamically concentrates computational resources solely in regions where the Berry curvature varies rapidly. Specifically, the strategy employs a recursive approach. For each hypercube in the integration domain, we construct a local error estimator by comparing the integral contribution calculated at its geometric center, referred to as the coarse estimate [Fig.
1
(a)], with a refined value obtained by subdividing the cell into sixteen sub-cells, the fine estimate [Fig.
1
(b)].

The discrepancy between these two estimates serves as a local error indicator. Hypercubes where this discrepancy exceeds a dynamic threshold are identified as regions of high curvature gradient and are physically split into smaller daughter cells. This process, shown as the adaptive refinement in Fig.
1
(c), repeats iteratively. By comparing the refined values of the sub-cells with their own subsequent subdivisions, the grid is allowed to automatically concentrate on the singularities until the global accumulated error converges to the desired tolerance. This dynamic allocation of grid points ensures both numerical stability and computational efficiency. The detailed algorithm is presented in Appendix
B
. Furthermore, the complete code of the adaptive mesh refinement scheme is available in Supplemental Material.

II.3
Performance Analysis

Before proceeding, we analyze the computational complexity and memory requirements of the three strategies. Across all methods, we have implemented the antisymmetry of the Berry curvature tensor in Eq. (
4
) to minimize matrix multiplications.

Computational Complexity

Since the diagonalization of the Hamiltonian is the most time-consuming part, we define the complexity as the total number of diagonalizations,
N_{k}
, required for convergence. For both the lattice-gauge approach (Method I) and the uniform grid integration (Method II), the complexity scales rigidly as
N_{k}=N^{4}
, where
N
is the grid density for each dimension. To resolve sharp curvature peaks near phase transitions,
N
must be large globally, leading to excessive computational waste in flat regions.
In contrast, the adaptive mesh method (Method III) decouples computational cost from global grid density. By dynamically allocating resources only to diverge regions, it achieves high precision with a significantly reduced number of diagonalizations.

Memory usage

Memory usage further distinguishes the methods. Method I is inherently non-local, involving link variables between neighboring
k
points. To avoid redundant diagonalizations, one must cache eigenstates for the entire grid, incurring a prohibitive memory cost proportional to
O(N^{4})
. Alternatively, one could choose not to store these link variables or wavefunctions to save memory; however, this would come at the expense of a significant increase in computational cost, as each Hamiltonian would need to be re-diagonalized up to
2^{4}=16
times.

Conversely, Methods II and III are strictly local. Both the perturbative Berry connection formula and the adaptive integration require information only at the current
k
-space point, independent of its neighbors. Consequently, they eliminate the need for global wavefunction storage, and thus requires a minimal
O(1)
memory usage.

III.3
Strategic Trade-off and Performance Limitations

Based on the comparative analysis, we conclude that Method III is
the most robust approach for characterizing topological phases,
particularly near critical boundaries. However, it is essential
to note a specific limitation: once the precision reaches the

10^{-3}
threshold, the rate of convergence towards the exact
theoretical value slows down significantly.

This result is due to our adaptive strategy, which concentrates grid points near the singularities. While this effectively captures the sharp peaks near the transition, it uses fewer points in regions where the Berry curvature is smooth, leading to minor errors there. Despite this, Method III remains the best choice for mapping the phase diagram, as the precision is sufficient to determine the topological invariants.

V
Conclusion

In conclusion, our comparative study establishes the adaptive mesh refinement scheme (Method III) as the superior numerical strategy for characterizing high-dimensional topological phases. While the lattice-gauge formulation (Method I) serves as a stable benchmark and uniform grid integration (Method II) offers expediency in deep gapped phases, both struggle with the computational demands of resolving sharp Berry curvature divergences near phase transitions. Method III overcomes these limitations by dynamically concentrating computational resources on topological singularities, thereby achieving high quantization precision with a computational cost reduced by orders of magnitude compared to traditional approaches. Validated through the complex spectral gaps of the 4D Quantum Hall system, this adaptive strategy provides a robust, memory-efficient framework for the automated discovery and classification of 4D topological insulators.

Furthermore, we emphasize that the applicability of our method extends well beyond the second Chern number. It is fundamentally suited for evaluating any physical observable or topological invariant that requires the integration of geometric quantities over the Brillouin zone. For instance, it holds great potential for addressing more numerically demanding tasks, such as calculating the third Chern number in six dimensions
Petrides et al. 2018
, and evaluating the nonlinear Hall effect associated with the Berry curvature dipole
Sodemann and Fu 2015
;
Du et al. 2021
or quantum metric
Wang et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2021
. We will systematically explore these broader applications in future work.

Appendix A
Details of the extended FHS method

We begin by defining the link tensor
Mochol-Grzelak et al. 2018
;
Fukui et al. 2005
, which describes the phase acquired
during the parallel transport between two neighboring sites in the
lattice momentum space. For occupied states
\psi^{\alpha}
, the link
variable is given by:

\left(U_{\mu}\right)^{\alpha\beta}(k_{l})=\left\langle\psi^{\alpha}(k_{l})\mid\psi^{\beta}\left(k_{l}+\Delta\hat{\mu}\right)\right\rangle,

(8)

where
k_{l}
denotes a discrete point on the hyperlattice. Using these
link variables, we construct the Wilson loop around an elementary
plaquette spanning directions
\mu
and
\nu
:

U_{\mu\nu}^{P}(k_{l})=U_{\mu}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)U_{\nu}^{P}\left(k_{l}+\hat{\mu}\right)U_{\mu}^{P}\left(k_{l}+\hat{\nu}\right)^{-1}U_{\nu}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)^{-1},

(9)

where
U_{\mu}^{P}(k)\equiv PU_{\mu}(k)P
involves the projection onto
the relevant bands.

The lattice field strength (non-Abelian Berry curvature) on each
plaquette is extracted via the matrix logarithm of the Wilson loop:

\tilde{F}_{\mu\nu}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)=\ln\left(U_{\mu\nu}^{P}(k_{l})\right).

(10)

To evaluate the second Chern number, we compute the contribution from
each hypercube cell of the hyperlattice. The total invariant

\tilde{C}_{2}
is obtained by summing the trace of the symmetrized field
strength products over all grid points
\{k_{l}\}
:

\tilde{C}_{2}\left(\varepsilon_{F}\right)=\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{\left\{k_{l}\right\}}\operatorname{Tr}\tilde{F}_{l}^{P},

(11)

where the local contribution
\tilde{F}_{l}^{P}
is defined by the cyclic
permutation of indices:

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{l}^{P}

\displaystyle=\tilde{F}_{xy}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)\tilde{F}_{zw}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)+\tilde{F}_{wx}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)\tilde{F}_{zy}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)

\displaystyle+\tilde{F}_{zx}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right)\tilde{F}_{yw}^{P}\left(k_{l}\right).

(12)

This formulation ensures that the flux is calculated in a gauge-invariant
manner on the discrete mesh, providing a robust integer index provided
the lattice discretization is sufficiently fine to capture the topology.

The algorithm utilizes a dynamic domain decomposition strategy, as summarized in Algorithm
5
. Instead of employing a static uniform grid, which is computationally wasteful in flat curvature regions, the Brillouin zone is recursively subdivided based on a local error estimator.

B.1
Local Error Estimation Strategy

The central component of the AMR scheme is the local evaluation routine (
EvaluateBox
in Algorithm
5
). To determine whether a given hypercube
B
with volume
V
requires refinement, we compare two approximations of the integral. First, we compute a
coarse estimate

I_{\text{coarse}}
by evaluating the Chern density
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{k}_{c})
at the single geometric center of the hypercube. This zeroth-order approximation is computationally inexpensive but lacks resolution. Second, to gauge the local variation, we calculate a
fine estimate

I_{\text{fine}}
. This is achieved by virtually subdividing the hypercube into
2^{4}=16
congruent sub-cubes and summing the contributions from the center of each sub-cube.

The local error indicator is then defined as the absolute difference between these two values,
\varepsilon=|I_{\text{fine}}-I_{\text{coarse}}|
. A large
\varepsilon
signals that the curvature
\mathcal{Q}(\bm{k})
varies non-linearly within the hypercube—indicating a potential singularity or domain wall—thus necessitating physical subdivision of the cell.

B.2
Algorithm Workflow

The numerical procedure begins with an initialization phase (Lines 1–6), where the 4D Brillouin zone is partitioned into a moderately dense uniform grid (e.g.,
8^{4}
hypercubes). This initial step is crucial to ensure that the mesh captures the global topology and does not inadvertently miss narrow peaks located between coarse grid points.

Following initialization, the algorithm enters an iterative adaptive refinement loop (Lines 7–21). In each iteration, the algorithm sorts the existing hypercubes and identifies those with the largest local errors. To maximize efficiency on multi-core architectures, we employ a batch processing strategy rather than refining cells sequentially. A batch of
N_{\text{batch}}
worst-performing boxes is selected and physically subdivided into 16 daughter hypercubes. These new cells are then evaluated in parallel and returned to the active pool. This cycle repeats until the total accumulated error
E_{\text{total}}
falls below the user-defined tolerance
\epsilon_{\text{tol}}
, ensuring the computed invariant
C_{2}
converges to its quantized integer value.
