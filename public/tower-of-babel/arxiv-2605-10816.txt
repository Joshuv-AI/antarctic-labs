Policy Gradient Methods for Non-Markovian Reinforcement Learning
Avik Kar, Siddharth Chandak, Rahul Singh, Soumitra Sinhahajari, Eric Moulines, Shalabh Bhatnagar, Nicholas Bambos

Abstract
We study policy gradient methods for reinforcement learning in non-Markovian decision processes (NMDPs), where observations and rewards depend on the entire interaction history. To handle this dependence, the agent maintains an internal state that is recursively updated to provide a compact summary of past observations and actions. In contrast to approaches that treat the agent state dynamics as fixed or learn it via predictive objectives, we propose a reward-centric formulation that jointly optimizes the agent state dynamics and the control policy to maximize the expected cumulative reward. To this end, we consider a class of Agent State-Markov (ASM) policies, comprising an agent state dynamics and a control policy that maps the agent state to actions. We establish a novel policy gradient theorem for ASM policies, extending the classical policy gradient results from the Markovian setting to episodic and infinite-horizon discounted NMDPs. Building on this gradient expression, we propose the Agent State-Markov Policy Gradient (ASMPG) algorithm, which leverages the recursive structure of the agent state dynamics for efficient optimization. We establish finite-time and almost sure convergence guarantees, and empirically demonstrate that, on a range of non-Markovian tasks, ASMPG outperforms baselines that learn state representations via predictive objectives.



1
Introduction

Reinforcement Learning (RL) provides a general framework for sequential decision-making and has enabled major advances across domains ranging from large language models (LLMs)
(
38
)
to robotics
(
25
)
and healthcare
(
56
)
. A large body of work models the environment as a Markov decision process (MDP), where the dynamics and rewards depend only on the current state and action, and not on the past history. This assumption enables tractable analysis and algorithm design. However, in many real-world settings, the Markov property fails to hold, as the dynamics and rewards depend on the entire history of observations and actions
(
17
;
39
)
. Such environments are inherently “non-Markovian” and cannot be accurately captured by standard MDP formulations.

A canonical example of non-Markovianity arises in partially observable environments, where the agent must infer latent states from a history of observations to act optimally
(
24
)
. An intuitive instance appears in dialogue systems
(
26
)
, where appropriate responses depend on the full conversation history rather than the most recent utterance alone. Similar challenges arise in domains such as recommender systems
(
54
)
, financial markets
(
28
)
, and healthcare
(
49
)
, where the underlying dynamics depend on latent factors or long-term dependencies. In such settings, effective control requires extracting and maintaining a representation of relevant information from the trajectory.

Learning such representations, however, poses several challenges. While the full history is sufficient, its size grows with time, rendering direct use of history computationally intractable. Moreover, representation learning is tightly coupled with control, requiring the agent to jointly learn both a state representation and a policy. A common approach in non-Markovian RL is to learn such representations by optimizing them to predict future observations based on the current state
(
50
;
13
)
. These predictive representations aim to summarize the history in a way that is sufficient for modeling the environmental dynamics. However, learning such predictive representations can be challenging, as accurate prediction may require a high-dimensional state representation and can be difficult to learn in practice.

In this work, we take a different approach, motivated by the view that predicting future observations is merely an intermediate objective. Instead, we bypass this step and directly learn a state representation and policy optimized for maximizing reward, the ultimate objective in reinforcement learning. To this end, we introduce a class of Agent State-Markov (ASM) policies, where the agent follows a Markov policy with respect to an agent state that summarizes the history and is recursively updated. We extend the policy gradient theorem to this class of policies, which enables us to develop the
Agent State-Markov Policy Gradient (ASMPG)
algorithm for both episodic and infinite-horizon discounted non-Markovian decision processes (NMDPs). Empirically, we demonstrate that in various non-Markovian environments, ASMPG outperforms methods that learn state representations via predictive objectives.

1.1
Main contributions

We summarize the main contributions of this work below.

Reward-centric optimization of agent state dynamics and control policy.

We formulate policy search over the joint parameters of the recursive agent-state dynamics and the state-dependent control policy. In contrast to existing approaches that treat the agent state dynamics as fixed
(
19
)
or learn it via auxiliary predictive objectives
(
50
)
, we propose a novel reward-centric formulation that departs from predictive objectives and directly optimizes the agent state dynamics and the control policy for the control objective.

Policy gradient theorem for NMDPs and ASMPG algorithm.

To enable joint optimization, we establish a novel policy gradient theorem for non-Markovian RL, extending classical policy gradient results beyond the Markovian setting to both episodic and infinite-horizon discounted NMDPs. The resulting gradient admits a simple form that exploits the recursive structure of the agent state dynamics, depending on the history only through the previous state. This leads to the ASMPG algorithm, an efficient method for jointly learning the agent state dynamics and control policy.

Theoretical guarantees and empirical performance.

Under standard assumptions, we establish a finite-time convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{K})
for the time average of the squared gradient norm, together with almost-sure convergence of the gradient norm to zero under decreasing stepsizes. We evaluate ASMPG on five non-Markovian environments and compare it with the AIS-KL and AIS-MMD baselines of
50
under comparable representation sizes. Across these tasks, ASMPG performs favorably, supporting the benefit of a reward-centric formulation.

Related work

Reinforcement learning in non-Markovian environments is largely organized around how the growing history is compressed. A classical case is partial observability: in a POMDP, the belief over latent states is a sufficient statistic for optimal control when the model is known or accurately estimated

. These methods reduce history dependence, but typically rely on a fixed representation class or provide limited guarantees for end-to-end reward optimization.

More structured approaches explicitly define agent-side states.

. In contrast, our formulation is reward-centric: the recursive agent-state dynamics and the control policy are optimized jointly for cumulative reward.

The closest literature is policy-gradient learning with memory. GPOMDP estimates gradients for POMDPs without observing the latent state

)
; finite-state controllers and structured POMDP policies have been optimized with gradient or actor-critic methods

;

)
; sufficient statistics such as beliefs and PSRs have been combined with policy gradients
(

; and recurrent policy-gradient or natural actor-critic methods optimize RNN-based memory policies

;
12

11
)
. Policy gradient method for MDPs is now well understood
(

;
3
;

. Our contribution is to derive an ASM policy-gradient theorem for general NMDPs and to use it for joint optimization of the agent-state dynamics and the control policy, rather than optimizing only a fixed memory, a model-based sufficient statistic, or a generic recurrent policy.

Episodic non-Markovian decision process

An episodic NMDP is specified by a five-tuple
(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},\{p_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H-1},\{r_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H},H)
, where
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{A}
denote the finite observation and action spaces,
H
is the episode duration, and
\{p_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
and
\{r_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
are the sequence of transition kernels and reward functions. The initial observation is sampled as
O_{1}\sim\mu
. At each step
t\in[H]
, the agent observes
O_{t}\in\mathcal{O}
and then chooses
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
. We write
O_{1:t}\coloneqq(O_{1},\ldots,O_{t})
and
A_{1:t}\coloneqq(A_{1},\ldots,A_{t})
. The process is non-Markovian because the next observation and the reward function depend on the entire history until the current time-step, i.e., for all
t\in[H-1]
,
O_{t+1}\sim p_{t}(\cdot\mid O_{1:t},A_{1:t})
, and the agent receives a reward
r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t})
. We assume throughout that rewards are uniformly bounded, i.e., there exists
r_{\max}>0
such that
\left|r_{t}(\cdot)\right|\leq r_{\max}
for every
t\in[H]
. For
1\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq H
, define the partial return

\displaystyle R_{t_{1}:t_{2}}\coloneqq\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t}),

with the convention that an empty sum is zero.

Let
\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}
denote the class of history-dependent randomized policies. A policy
\rho\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}
is a sequence
\rho=\{\rho_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
with
\rho_{t}:(\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{A})^{t-1}\times\mathcal{O}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
, where
\rho_{t}(\cdot\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t-1})
is the action distribution used after observing
(o_{1:t},a_{1:t-1})
. The benchmark objective is

\displaystyle\rho^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\rho\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{\rho,\mu}
denotes expectation under the trajectory distribution
induced by
\rho
and
O_{1}\sim\mu
.

2.1
Agent state and ASM policies

To avoid conditioning directly on the growing history, the agent maintains an
agent state

S_{t}\in\mathcal{S}
at each time
t
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space
(
19
)
. The state is updated recursively according to a stochastic kernel

\displaystyle\nu_{t}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{S}},\qquad S_{t}\sim\nu_{t}(\cdot\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t}),\quad t\in[H],

with initialization
(S_{0},A_{0})=(s_{0},a_{0})
, where
(s_{0},a_{0})
is a fixed dummy state-action pair used only to start the recursion. We write
\nu=\{\nu_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
and call
\nu
the
agent state dynamics
. A deterministic update is included as the special case in which
\nu_{t}(\cdot\mid s,a,o)
is a Dirac mass. Through the recursion,
S_{t}
is a summary of the interaction history available before choosing
A_{t}
.

Given an agent state, the control policy is Markovian in
S_{t}
. Specifically, let
\phi=\{\phi_{t}:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\}_{t=1}^{H}
and sample

\displaystyle A_{t}\sim\phi_{t}(\cdot\mid S_{t}),\quad t\in[H].

The pair
(\nu,\phi)
induces a history-dependent policy, denoted
\phi\circ\nu
. We refer to such policies as
Agent State-Markov (ASM) policies
.

2.2
ASM policy gradient for episodic NMDPs

We now derive a policy-gradient identity for parameterized ASM policies. It is convenient to combine the agent state dynamics and control policy into a single kernel
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}
, where
\pi_{t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)
is the conditional distribution of
(S_{t},A_{t})
given
(S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})=(\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)
. Thus,
\pi_{t}
can be factorized in the following manner.

\displaystyle\pi_{t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)=\nu_{t}(s\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\,\phi_{t}(a\mid s),\quad s,\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S},\ a,\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A},\ o\in\mathcal{O}.

(2)

Consider a differentiable parametric class

\pi_{\theta}=\{\pi_{\theta,t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
, with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and define

\displaystyle J(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right],

which is the expected sum of rewards over an episode that we wish to maximize over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We now state the corresponding policy gradient theorem.

Theorem 2
(ASM Policy Gradient Theorem for Episodic NMDPs)

Let
\{\pi_{\theta}=\{\pi_{\theta,t}\}_{t=1}^{H}\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a class of parameterized episodic ASM policies that are differentiable with respect to
\theta
, and have support independent of
\theta
for all
t\in[H]
. Then,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},\mu}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}R_{t:H}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta,t}(S_{t},A_{t}\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})\right].

(3)

See Appendix
B.1
for the proof. The above theorem implies that from a single trajectory generated by
\pi_{\theta}
, the agent can compute

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{H}R_{t:H}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta,t}(S_{t},A_{t}\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t}),

which is the unbiased stochastic gradient estimator of
\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)
.
This reward-to-go form is similar to REINFORCE
(
53
)
, where the rewards earned before time
t
do not contribute to the conditional score at time
t
. This yields the episodic Agent State-Markov Policy Gradient algorithm (Algorithm
1
), an online policy gradient algorithm for episodic NMDPs. Due to the factorizability property of ASM policies (
2
), we can decompose the gradient in the following form.

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta,t}(S_{t},A_{t}\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})=\nabla_{\theta}\log\nu_{\theta,t}(S_{t}\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})+\nabla_{\theta}\log\phi_{\theta,t}(A_{t}\mid S_{t}).

This enables us to use decoupled kernels for the agent state
dynamics and the control policy, and update both of them separately.

3
Infinite-horizon discounted non-Markovian decision process

An infinite-horizon discounted NMDP is described by a five-tuple
(\mathcal{O},\mathcal{A},\{p_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty},\{r_{t}\}_{t=1}^{\infty},\gamma)
, where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor. As in the finite-horizon case,
O_{t}\in\mathcal{O}
is the observation and
A_{t}\in\mathcal{A}
is the action at step
t
, but now the process evolves indefinitely. The observation process evolves as
O_{t+1}\sim p_{t}(\cdot\mid O_{1:t},A_{1:t})
, for
t=1,2,\ldots
, and at the
t
-th step the agent receives the reward
r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t})
. For
1\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}<\infty
, define the discounted partial return

\displaystyle R^{\gamma}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}\coloneqq\sum_{t=t_{1}}^{t_{2}}{\gamma^{t-t_{1}}r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t})},

with the convention that an empty sum is zero, and define
R^{\gamma}_{t_{1}:\infty}=\lim_{t_{2}\to\infty}{R^{\gamma}_{t_{1}:t_{2}}}
. The limit exists due to discounting and the boundedness of the rewards. The benchmark objective is to maximize the expected value of the cumulative discounted rewards
R^{\gamma}_{1:\infty}
, i.e., solving the following infinite-horizon discounted optimization problem,

\displaystyle\pi^{\star}\in\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\text{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R^{\gamma}_{1:\infty}\right],

where once again
\Pi_{\text{HR}}
is the class of history-dependent policies, and a policy from the set
\Pi_{\text{HR}}
at each step
t
prescribes an action as a function of the entire history until
t
. Note that, in this case, employing a general history-dependent policy is practically impossible due to an exponentially growing history space up to an indefinite time. On the other hand, a stationary ASM policy
\pi=\phi\circ\nu
, that generates the agent states and actions using time-independent functions, i.e.,
S_{t}\sim\nu(\cdot\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})
and
A_{t}\sim\phi(\cdot\mid S_{t})
for all
t\geq 1
, can be efficiently implemented for an infinite-horizon environment.

3.1
ASM policy gradient for infinite-horizon discounted NMDPs

In this section, we first present the policy gradient theorem for infinite-horizon discounted NMDPs that allows us to maximize the discounted reward over the space of given parametric stationary ASM policies. Under a stationary ASM policy
\pi
, we have
(S_{t},A_{t})\sim\pi(\cdot,\cdot\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t}),t\geq 1

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{J_{\gamma}(\theta)},\quad\mbox{where }J_{\gamma}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta},\mu}\left[R^{\gamma}_{1:\infty}\right],

(4)

where

,

We obtain analogous convergence guarantees for the infinite-horizon discounted setting, where

We evaluate ASMPG on five non-Markovian environments and compare it with the AIS-KL and AIS-MMD methods of

We briefly describe the NMDP environments considered in our experiments; detailed descriptions are provided in Appendix

CheeseMaze.

The HallwayNavigation task is again a partially-observable navigation problem introduced by
32

5.0

HealthcareTreatment.
We consider a treatment planning environment

29

The agent must choose one of three treatment regimes at each decision epoch to maximize the patient’s overall health, along with a binary outcome-oriented large terminal reward or penalty.

MachineRepair.
We consider a machine maintenance environment
(
20

The deterioration of the machine’s health and the effectiveness of the repair depend on a hidden wear level that evolves with the usage and repair history. 5
Conclusion

We studied policy-gradient methods for reinforcement learning in NMDPs, where observations and rewards may depend on the full interaction history. To handle this history dependence, we introduced Agent State-Markov policies, in which the agent maintains a recursively updated internal state and acts in a Markovian manner with respect to this state. Unlike approaches that fix the agent-state dynamics or learn representations through auxiliary predictive objectives, our formulation directly optimizes both the agent-state dynamics and the control policy for the expected-return objective.

We derived policy-gradient identities for episodic and infinite-horizon discounted NMDPs and used them to develop the ASMPG algorithm. We also established finite-time convergence bounds and almost sure convergence to the set of stationary points under standard regularity assumptions. The simulation results show that ASMPG performs favorably compared with baselines across several non-Markovian environments, supporting the benefit of reward-centric joint optimization of agent state dynamics and control policy.

The proposed method is based on REINFORCE-type gradient estimators and may therefore suffer from high variance. A natural direction for future work is to develop actor-critic variants for ASM policies that reduce variance while preserving the same reward-centric objective. Another important direction is to study conditions under which policy-gradient methods for non-Markovian reinforcement learning converge to globally optimal policies, extending recent global convergence results from the Markovian setting.

Appendix A
Extended related work

Non-Markovian reinforcement learning has been studied through several complementary notions of state, memory, and temporal structure. The classical example is the POMDP, where the environment has a Markov latent state, but the agent observes only a noisy signal. If the model is known, the belief over latent states is a sufficient statistic, reducing the problem to a fully observed belief-state MDP
(
4
;
48
;
24
;
42
)
. This reduction is conceptually powerful, but belief construction requires a known or accurately estimated transition-observation model and may be computationally expensive.

When the latent model is unavailable, one can replace the full history with a learned or hand-designed memory. Early approaches include finite observation windows
(
30
)
and finite-state controllers
(
41
)
. Predictive state representations avoid latent variables by representing state through predictions of future tests
(
27
;
46
)
. In deep RL, recurrent architectures provide a flexible way to encode histories into latent states
(
44
;
22
;
45
;
5
)
. Recent empirical work shows that recurrent model-free RL can be a strong baseline for many POMDPs when architectures and hyperparameters are carefully tuned
(
37
)
, and POPGym provides a benchmark suite for comparing memory mechanisms in partially observable RL
(
36
)
. These approaches are expressive, but their learned memory is often difficult to analyze.

A more structured line of work studies agent-side state representations.
19
introduce recursively updated agent states and analyze efficient learning when the state dynamics are fixed.
47
also use fixed agent states and learn periodic policies for POMDPs. Approximate information states (AIS)
(
50
)
provide a general framework in which a history representation is approximately sufficient for rewards and future prediction, yielding approximate planning and learning guarantees.
13
use related information-state ideas to quantify the error caused by non-Markovianity in Q-learning. Our approach is closest in spirit to these agent-state and information-state methods, but differs in the optimization objective: rather than fixing the state dynamics or learning them through an auxiliary sufficiency/prediction criterion, we optimize the recursive state dynamics directly for cumulative reward.

Another complementary direction uses automata or formal languages to encode temporal structure. Regular decision processes and their offline/online variants model history-dependent behavior using regular-language structure
(
9
;
1
;
43
;
16
;
18
)
. Reward machines encode non-Markovian reward structure as finite-state machines and can expose decompositions useful for learning
(
23
)
. These approaches are most natural when the relevant temporal abstraction is specified, symbolic, or inferred as an automaton. ASM policies instead treat the agent state as a stochastic, differentiable memory mechanism that is optimized jointly with the policy.

Our analysis is also closely related to policy-gradient methods under partial observability. GPOMDP provides simulation-based gradient estimates for average-reward POMDPs without access to the latent state
(
7
)
. Gradient methods for finite-state controllers learn both action choices and stochastic memory updates
(
35
)
, while actor-critic methods for structured POMDP policies estimate policy gradients using value-function approximation
(
57
)
.
2
combine policy gradients with sufficient-statistic tracking, including beliefs and predictive state representations. Recurrent policy gradients optimize RNN or LSTM policies directly through memory
(
52
)
, and recent work gives finite-time analyses for natural actor-critic methods with finite memory or recurrent policies in POMDPs
(
12
;
11
)
. These works show that policy-gradient methods can be applied under partial observability, but they are formulated for POMDPs, sufficient statistics, finite-state controllers, or generic recurrent policies. The present work targets general NMDPs and derives a policy-gradient theorem for the ASM class, enabling joint reward-centric optimization of the agent-state dynamics and the control policy.

Finally, our convergence analysis builds on the mature theory of policy-gradient methods for fully observed MDPs, including classical likelihood-ratio estimators
(
53
;
51
)
and recent non-asymptotic/global convergence analyses
(
3
;
58
;
33
;
34
)
. The main technical difference is that in NMDPs, rewards and observations depend on the entire history; the ASM recursion provides enough structure to obtain a usable gradient expression and finite-time stationarity guarantees without reducing the environment to a Markov state observed by the learner.

We now differentiate this identity. To make the differentiation precise, write the expectation over
the full prefix

\mathcal{H}_{t}:=(O_{1:t},S_{0:t},A_{0:t}).

The probability of such a prefix under
\pi_{\theta}
is a product of the environment kernels, which
do not depend on
\theta
, and the ASM policy kernels

\prod_{j=1}^{t}\pi_{\theta,j}\left(S_{j},A_{j}\mid S_{j-1},A_{j-1},O_{j}\right).

Hence, on the support of the prefix law,

\nabla_{\theta}\log P_{\pi_{\theta}}(\mathcal{H}_{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{t}Z_{j}(\theta).

Using the regularity assumptions from Theorem
2
, we may differentiate under the finite trajectory
expectation. Therefore,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})B_{t}(\theta)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}Z_{t}(\theta)\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}{Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})Z_{t}(\theta)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{t}Z_{j}(\theta)\right)^{\top}}\right].

Here and below, gradients are viewed as column vectors. If the opposite Jacobian convention is used, the displayed matrices are transposed, which does not affect the spectral-norm bounds.

Taking spectral norms and using the triangle inequality together with
\|uv^{\top}\|_{2}=\|u\|_{2}\|v\|_{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J(\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left|Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})\right|\|B_{t}(\theta)\|_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}\|Z_{t}(\theta)\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left|Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})\right|\|Z_{t}(\theta)\|_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{t}\|Z_{j}(\theta)\|_{2}\right].

We next bound each term on the right-hand side. Since each reward is bounded by
r_{\max}
in
absolute value,

\left|Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})\right|\leq r_{\max}(H-t+1).

Therefore the first term is bounded by

T_{1}:=\sum_{t=1}^{H}r_{\max}(H-t+1)M=r_{\max}M\frac{H(H+1)}{2}.

It remains to bound
\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}
. For
t=H
,

Q_{H}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:H},a_{0:H},s_{H})=r_{H}(o_{1:H},a_{1:H}),

which does not depend on
\theta
, and hence

\nabla_{\theta}Q_{H}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:H},a_{0:H},s_{H})=0.

For
t\leq H-1
, by the definitions of
Q_{t}^{\pi}
and
V_{t+1}^{\pi}
,

Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})=r_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{1:t})+\sum_{o_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}}p_{t}(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t})V_{t+1}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t+1},a_{0:t},s_{t}).

The reward function and the environment kernel do not depend on
\theta
. Thus,

\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})=\sum_{o_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}}p_{t}(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t})\nabla_{\theta}V_{t+1}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t+1},a_{0:t},s_{t}).

Applying the
Q
-function form of Proposition
7
to
V_{t+1}^{\pi_{\theta}}
gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}Q_{\ell}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:\ell},A_{0:\ell},S_{\ell})Z_{\ell}(\theta)\,\Bigg|\,O_{1:t}=o_{1:t},A_{0:t}=a_{0:t},S_{t}=s_{t}\Bigg].

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q_{t}^{\pi_{\theta}}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}\left|Q_{\ell}^{\pi_{\theta}}(O_{1:\ell},A_{0:\ell},S_{\ell})\right|\|Z_{\ell}(\theta)\|_{2}\,\Bigg|\,O_{1:t}=o_{1:t},A_{0:t}=a_{0:t},S_{t}=s_{t}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}r_{\max}(H-\ell+1)G.

The same bound also holds for
t=H
, with the convention that the empty sum is zero. Hence, the second term in the Hessian bound is at most

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{H}G\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}r_{\max}(H-\ell+1)G

\displaystyle=r_{\max}G^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}(H-\ell+1).

We compute

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{H}\sum_{\ell=t+1}^{H}(H-\ell+1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{H}(\ell-1)(H-\ell+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{H(H-1)(H+1)}{6}.

Therefore,

T_{2}=r_{\max}G^{2}\frac{H(H-1)(H+1)}{6}.

Finally, the third term is bounded by

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{t=1}^{H}r_{\max}(H-t+1)G\sum_{j=1}^{t}G

\displaystyle=r_{\max}G^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{H}t(H-t+1).

Using

\sum_{t=1}^{H}t(H-t+1)=\frac{H(H+1)(H+2)}{6},

we obtain

T_{3}=r_{\max}G^{2}\frac{H(H+1)(H+2)}{6}.

Combining the three bounds,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J(\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}

\displaystyle=r_{\max}M\frac{H(H+1)}{2}+r_{\max}G^{2}\frac{H(H-1)(H+1)}{6}+r_{\max}G^{2}\frac{H(H+1)(H+2)}{6}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\max}H(H+1)}{6}\left(3M+G^{2}\bigl((H-1)+(H+2)\bigr)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\max}H(H+1)}{6}\left(3M+G^{2}(2H+1)\right).

Thus,

\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J(\theta)\right\|_{2}\leq\beta,\qquad\beta:=\frac{r_{\max}H(H+1)}{6}\left(3M+G^{2}(2H+1)\right).

This proves that
J
is
\beta
-smooth.

B.3
Convergence results

Theorem 9
(Extended version of Theorem
5
)

Consider the iterates
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
generated by Algorithm
1
. Suppose that the
episodic ASM policy parameterization satisfies the regularity conditions of Theorem
2
and
Assumption
1
. Let

\Delta_{1}:=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\theta)-J(\theta_{1}),

and let
\beta
be the smoothness constant from Lemma
4
. Define

C_{H}:=r_{\max}GH(H+1).

Then the following statements hold.

(a)

Fix
K\geq 1
. If

\alpha_{k}=\alpha:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{2}{C_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta_{1}}{\beta K}}\right\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K,

then

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2K}}.

(b)

Fix
K\geq 1
and
\delta\in(0,1)
. With probability at least
1-\delta
,
the following bounds hold for the corresponding choices of stepsizes.

(i)

If

\alpha_{k}=\alpha:=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta},\frac{1}{C_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\beta K}}\right\},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K,

then

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+5C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}}+\frac{12C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{K}.

(ii)

If

\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{k}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,K,

then

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}+3C_{H}^{2}(1+\log K)+12C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{K}}.

(c)

If
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
is any deterministic positive stepsize sequence satisfying

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty,

then

\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Proof

Let
\tau_{k}:=(O^{k}_{1:H},S^{k}_{0:H},A^{k}_{0:H})
denote the trajectory generated during
episode
k
, and let

\mathcal{G}_{k}:=\sigma(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{k-1})

be the sigma-field generated by all trajectories sampled before episode
k
. In particular,

\theta_{k}
is
\mathcal{G}_{k}
-measurable.

Let
\widehat{g}_{k}
denote the stochastic gradient estimator computed in Algorithm
1
, namely

\widehat{g}_{k}=\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left(\sum_{\ell=t}^{H}r_{\ell}(O^{k}_{1:\ell},A^{k}_{1:\ell})\right)\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta_{k},t}\left(S_{t}^{k},A_{t}^{k}\mid S_{t-1}^{k},A_{t-1}^{k},O_{t}^{k}\right).

By Theorem
2
, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, the estimator is unbiased:

\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]=\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k}).

For notational simplicity, write

g_{k}:=\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k}),\qquad\xi_{k}:=\widehat{g}_{k}-g_{k}.

Then

\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]=0.

We first record deterministic bounds on the estimator and on the noise. Since

|r_{t}|\leq r_{\max}
and Assumption
1
gives

\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta_{k},t}\left(S_{t}^{k},A_{t}^{k}\mid S_{t-1}^{k},A_{t-1}^{k},O_{t}^{k}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq G,

we have, almost surely,

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{H}\left|\sum_{\ell=t}^{H}r_{\ell}(O^{k}_{1:\ell},A^{k}_{1:\ell})\right|\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta_{k},t}\left(S_{t}^{k},A_{t}^{k}\mid S_{t-1}^{k},A_{t-1}^{k},O_{t}^{k}\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{H}r_{\max}(H-t+1)G

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\max}GH(H+1)}{2}=\frac{C_{H}}{2}.

Consequently, by Jensen’s inequality and conditional unbiasedness,

\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{g}_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\right\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\leq\frac{C_{H}}{2}.

Therefore, almost surely,

\left\|\xi_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}+\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{H}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\xi_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}-g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]-\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{C_{H}}{2}\right)^{2}.

Since
J
is
\beta
-smooth by Lemma
4
, for every
k
,

J(\theta_{k+1})\geq J(\theta_{k})+\left\langle g_{k},\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\beta}{2}\left\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Using the ASMPG update
\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}+\alpha_{k}\widehat{g}_{k}
and

\widehat{g}_{k}=g_{k}+\xi_{k}
, this gives

\displaystyle J(\theta_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq J(\theta_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\widehat{g}_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\beta\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=J(\theta_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\beta\alpha_{k}^{2}}{2}\left\|\widehat{g}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq J(\theta_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle-\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Equivalently,

\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq J(\theta_{k+1})-J(\theta_{k})-\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Summing from
k=1
to
K
and using

J(\theta_{K+1})\leq\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\theta)
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\Delta_{1}-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

We now prove part (a). Taking expectations in the preceding inequality, the martingale
difference term vanishes because
g_{k}
is
\mathcal{G}_{k}
-measurable and

\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}]=0
. Hence, for every deterministic stepsize sequence,

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\Delta_{1}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

For the constant stepsize in part (a), this implies

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\alpha K}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\alpha.

If
\Delta_{1}=0
, then
\theta_{1}
is a global maximizer of the differentiable function
J
,
so
g_{1}=0
; with the displayed constant stepsize the iterates do not move, and the claim is
immediate. Thus assume
\Delta_{1}>0
. Define

\overline{\alpha}:=\frac{2}{C_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta_{1}}{\beta K}},\qquad\alpha=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta},\overline{\alpha}\right\}.

Then

\frac{1}{\alpha}\leq\beta+\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha}}=\beta+\frac{C_{H}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta K}{2\Delta_{1}}},

and
\alpha\leq\overline{\alpha}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta_{1}}{K}\left(\beta+\frac{C_{H}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta K}{2\Delta_{1}}}\right)+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\overline{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+\frac{C_{H}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2K}}+\frac{C_{H}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2K}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{2K}}.

This proves part (a).

We next prove the high-probability bounds in part (b). Fix
K\geq 1
and let

\eta_{K}:=\max_{1\leq k\leq K}\alpha_{k}.

Define

S_{K}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2},\qquad A_{K}:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2},\qquad Z_{K}:=-\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle.

The preceding descent inequality can be written as

S_{K}\leq\Delta_{1}+Z_{K}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}A_{K}.

We now bound
Z_{K}
. Conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{k}
, the scalar random variable

\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\rangle
has mean zero and satisfies

\left|\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\right|\leq C_{H}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}.

Hence, by Hoeffding’s lemma, for every
\lambda>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\lambda\alpha_{k}\left\langle g_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\leq\exp\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}\alpha_{k}^{2}C_{H}^{2}}{2}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right).

Iterating conditional expectations gives

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(\lambda Z_{K}-\frac{\lambda^{2}C_{H}^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]\leq 1.

Therefore, by Markov’s inequality, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

Z_{K}\leq\frac{\lambda C_{H}^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_{k}^{2}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\lambda}.

Since
\alpha_{k}^{2}\leq\eta_{K}\alpha_{k}
for all
k\leq K
, we have

Z_{K}\leq\frac{\lambda C_{H}^{2}\eta_{K}}{2}S_{K}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\lambda}.

Choosing
\lambda=(C_{H}^{2}\eta_{K})^{-1}
yields

Z_{K}\leq\frac{1}{2}S_{K}+C_{H}^{2}\eta_{K}\log(1/\delta).

Combining this estimate with the bound on
S_{K}
, we obtain, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

S_{K}\leq 2\Delta_{1}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{4}A_{K}+2C_{H}^{2}\eta_{K}\log(1/\delta).

We first apply this bound to the constant stepsize in part (b)(i). Let

\overline{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{C_{H}}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\beta K}},\qquad\alpha=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta},\overline{\alpha}\right\}.

If
\Delta_{1}=0
, the claim is immediate as above. Thus assume
\Delta_{1}>0
.
For constant stepsize,
\eta_{K}=\alpha
,
A_{K}=K\alpha^{2}
, and
S_{K}=\alpha\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|g_{k}\|_{2}^{2}
. Hence, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\Delta_{1}}{\alpha K}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{4}\alpha+\frac{2C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{K}.

Moreover,

\frac{1}{\alpha}\leq\beta+\frac{1}{\overline{\alpha}}=\beta+C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta K}{\Delta_{1}}},\qquad\alpha\leq\overline{\alpha}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+2C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}}+\frac{C_{H}}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}}+\frac{2C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{K}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}+5C_{H}\sqrt{\frac{\beta\Delta_{1}}{K}}+\frac{12C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{K}.

This proves part (b)(i).

We now prove part (b)(ii). Let
\alpha_{k}=1/(\beta\sqrt{k})
for
k=1,\ldots,K
.
Then

\eta_{K}=\frac{1}{\beta},\qquad A_{K}=\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{k}\leq\frac{1+\log K}{\beta^{2}}.

The high-probability bound on
S_{K}
gives

S_{K}\leq 2\Delta_{1}+\frac{C_{H}^{2}}{4\beta}(1+\log K)+\frac{2C_{H}^{2}}{\beta}\log(1/\delta).

Also, since
\alpha_{k}\geq\alpha_{K}=1/(\beta\sqrt{K})
for
k=1,\ldots,K
,

\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq S_{K}.

Consequently, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta S_{K}}{\sqrt{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}+\frac{C_{H}^{2}}{4}(1+\log K)+2C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{K}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\beta\Delta_{1}+3C_{H}^{2}(1+\log K)+12C_{H}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{K}}.

This proves part (b)(ii).

It remains to prove part (c). Assume that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}=\infty,\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty.

Taking conditional expectation in the smoothness inequality gives

\mathbb{E}\left[J(\theta_{k+1})\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\geq J(\theta_{k})+\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Let

J^{\star}:=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J(\theta),\qquad Y_{k}:=J^{\star}-J(\theta_{k}).

Then
Y_{k}\geq 0
and

\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{k+1}\mid\mathcal{G}_{k}\right]\leq Y_{k}-\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{8}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Since
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
, the Robbins–Siegmund almost
supermartingale theorem implies

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely.}

We now show that this implies
\|g_{k}\|_{2}\to 0
almost surely. Since
J
is

\beta
-smooth,

\left\|g_{k+1}-g_{k}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k+1})-\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}\leq\beta\left\|\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}\right\|_{2}.

Using
\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k}=\alpha_{k}\widehat{g}_{k}
and

\|\widehat{g}_{k}\|_{2}\leq C_{H}/2
, we get

\left\|g_{k+1}-g_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\frac{\beta C_{H}}{2}\alpha_{k}.

Let

v_{k}:=\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\|g_{k}\|_{2}\leq C_{H}/2
for all
k
, we have

\displaystyle|v_{k+1}-v_{k}|

\displaystyle=\left|\left\|g_{k+1}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|g_{k+1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}\right)\left\|g_{k+1}-g_{k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta C_{H}^{2}}{2}\alpha_{k}.

Set
L:=\beta C_{H}^{2}/2
. Thus

|v_{k+1}-v_{k}|\leq L\alpha_{k}.

We already know that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{k}v_{k}<\infty\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Fix an outcome on which this finite-sum property holds. Suppose, for contradiction, that

v_{k}
does not converge to
0
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

v_{k}\geq\varepsilon
for infinitely many
k
. Since

\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}=\infty
while
\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}v_{k}<\infty
, the sequence
v_{k}
cannot be
eventually larger than or equal to
\varepsilon/2
. Hence there are infinitely many
upcrossings from below
\varepsilon/2
to above
\varepsilon
.

Choose one such upcrossing interval
[r,q]
, with
r<q
, such that

v_{r}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2},\qquad v_{q}\geq\varepsilon,

and
r
is the last index before
q
for which
v_{r}<\varepsilon/2
. Then

v_{j}\geq\varepsilon/2
for all
j=r+1,\ldots,q
. Also,

\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\leq v_{q}-v_{r}\leq\sum_{j=r}^{q-1}|v_{j+1}-v_{j}|\leq L\sum_{j=r}^{q-1}\alpha_{j}.

Because
\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}^{2}<\infty
, we have
\alpha_{k}\to 0
. Hence, for all sufficiently
large upcrossings,

\alpha_{r}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4L},

and therefore

\sum_{j=r+1}^{q-1}\alpha_{j}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{4L}.

On the same indices
j=r+1,\ldots,q-1
, we have
v_{j}\geq\varepsilon/2
. Thus each
sufficiently late upcrossing contributes at least

\sum_{j=r+1}^{q-1}\alpha_{j}v_{j}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{j=r+1}^{q-1}\alpha_{j}\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{8L}

to the sum
\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}v_{k}
. Infinitely many disjoint upcrossings would therefore force

\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}v_{k}=\infty
, contradicting the finite-sum property. Hence

v_{k}\to 0
almost surely, i.e.,

\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}=\left\|g_{k}\right\|_{2}\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{almost surely.}

This proves part (c), and completes the proof.

C.1
Policy gradient theorem

Recall the definition of the discounted reward
Q
-functions for an ASM policy
\pi
,

\displaystyle Q^{\gamma,\pi}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:\infty}\mid O_{1:t}=o_{1:t},A_{0:t}=a_{0:t},S_{t}=s_{t}\right],

where
t\geq 1
,
R^{\gamma}_{t:\infty}=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}r_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{1:t^{\prime}})}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}
denotes the expectation taken considering that the agent states and actions from time
t
onward are generated by
\pi
. Likewise, define the discounted-reward value function for a stationary ASM policy
\pi
, as follows:

\displaystyle V^{\gamma,\pi}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t-1},s_{t-1})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:\infty}\mid O_{1:t}=o_{1:t},A_{0:t-1}=a_{0:t-1},S_{t-1}=s_{t-1}\right].

for
t\geq 1
. The next proposition provides expressions for the gradient of the value functions with respect to policy parameters.

Proposition 10

Let
\{\pi_{\theta}\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
be a class of parameterized stationary ASM policies that are differentiable with respect to the parameter vector
\theta
, and the support of
\pi_{\theta}
is independent of
\theta
. Suppose that there exists
r_{\max}>0
such that
|r_{t}(\cdot)|\leq r_{\max}
for every
t\geq 1
, and there exists
G>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\right)\right\|_{2}\leq G

for every
s,\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}
,
a,\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}
,
o\in\mathcal{O}
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for every
t\geq 1
,
o_{1:t}\in\mathcal{O}^{t},a_{0:t-1}\in\mathcal{A}^{t}
, and
s_{t-1}\in\mathcal{S}
,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}{V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t-1},s_{t-1})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid o_{1:t},a_{0:t-1},s_{t-1}\right]

(10)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid o_{1:t},a_{0:t-1},s_{t-1}\right].

(11)

Proof

Fix
t\geq 1
and let

\displaystyle h_{t}\coloneqq(o_{1:t},a_{0:t-1},s_{t-1}).

For
T\geq t
, define the truncated return and the truncated value function as

\displaystyle V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:T}\mid h_{t}\right],

where
R^{\gamma}_{t:T}=\sum_{\ell=t}^{T}{\gamma^{\ell-t}r_{\ell}(O_{1:\ell},A_{1:\ell})}
. We first prove the desired identity for
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})
and then let
T\to\infty
.

Denote
\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{T}(\cdot\mid h_{t})
the conditional law of the finite-length trajectory
(O_{t+1:T},A_{t:T},S_{t:T})
induced by
\pi_{\theta}
given
h_{t}
. For a finite-length trajectory
\tau_{T}\coloneqq(o_{t+1:T},a_{t:T},s_{t:T})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{T}(\tau_{T}\mid h_{t})=\left(\prod_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\pi_{\theta}(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}}\mid s_{t^{\prime}-1},a_{t^{\prime}-1},o_{t^{\prime}})}\right)\left(\prod_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T-1}{p_{t^{\prime}}(o_{t^{\prime}+1}\mid o_{1:t^{\prime}},a_{1:t^{\prime}})}\right),

where the second product is interpreted as one when
T=t
. On the support of this finite-length trajectory law, its logarithm satisfies

\displaystyle\log\left(\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{T}(\tau_{T}\mid h_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}}\mid s_{t^{\prime}-1},a_{t^{\prime}-1},o_{t^{\prime}})\right)}+\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T-1}{\log\left(p_{t^{\prime}}(o_{t^{\prime}+1}\mid o_{1:t^{\prime}},a_{1:t^{\prime}})\right)},

where only the first term depends on
\theta
. Thus,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{T}(\tau_{T}\mid h_{t})\right)=\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(s_{t^{\prime}},a_{t^{\prime}}\mid s_{t^{\prime}-1},a_{t^{\prime}-1},o_{t^{\prime}})\right)}.

Since the support of
\pi_{\theta}
is independent of
\theta
, the support of
\mathbb{P}^{\pi_{\theta}}_{T}(\cdot\mid h_{t})
is also independent of
\theta
. Hence, applying the log-derivative trick to the finite-length trajectory
\tau_{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:T}\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right].

(12)

Note that, for every
t^{\prime}\in\{t,\ldots,T\}
,

\displaystyle R^{\gamma}_{t:T}=R^{\gamma}_{t:t^{\prime}-1}+\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:T}.

Denote the
\sigma
-field

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}}\coloneqq\sigma(O_{1},S_{1},A_{1},O_{2},S_{2},A_{2},\ldots,O_{t^{\prime}-1},S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}}).

Note that
R^{\gamma}_{t:t^{\prime}-1}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}}
-measurable. For every
t^{\prime}\in\{t,\ldots,T\}
, using the tower property of conditional expectation, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:t^{\prime}-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)\mid h_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t:t^{\prime}-1}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}}\right]\mid h_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\Bigg[R^{\gamma}_{t:t^{\prime}-1}\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}:\\
\pi_{\theta}(s,a\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})>0\end{subarray}}{\pi_{\theta}(s,a\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})}\mid h_{t}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=0.

The last equality follows because the support of
\pi_{\theta}(\cdot,\cdot\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})
is independent of
\theta
, and the sum of the policy probabilities over this support is one. Summing the above identity over
t^{\prime}=t,\ldots,T
and using (
12
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right].

(13)

It remains to pass to the limit
T\to\infty
. First, by boundedness of the rewards,

\displaystyle\left|V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})-V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})\right|\leq\sum_{\ell=T+1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{\ell-t}r_{\max}}=\frac{r_{\max}\gamma^{T+1-t}}{1-\gamma}.

Hence
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})\to V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})
uniformly in
\theta
.

Next, for
L>T
, applying the same finite-length trajectory log-derivative argument to
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,L}(h_{t})-V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})
gives

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,L}(h_{t})-\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})\right\|_{2}\leq Gr_{\max}\sum_{\ell=T+1}^{L}{(\ell-t+1)\gamma^{\ell-t}}.

The right-hand side goes to zero as
T\to\infty
, uniformly in
L
and
\theta
. Therefore,
\{\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})\}_{T\geq t}
is uniformly Cauchy. Since
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t})
converges uniformly to
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})
, the limit
V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})
is differentiable and

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})=\lim_{T\to\infty}\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t,T}(h_{t}).

Finally, by the boundedness of the rewards and the score,

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:T}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\right\|_{2}\leq\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{T}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}G}\leq\frac{Gr_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}.

Therefore, by dominated convergence in (
13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(h_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right].

This proves (
10
). Now, we will prove (
11
). By definition,

\displaystyle Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\mid O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}}\right].

Moreover, by the recursive structure of ASM policies, conditioning on the earlier agent-state history beyond
(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})
does not change the conditional distribution of the future trajectory. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\right]=Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}}).

Also, note that
\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)
is
\sigma(\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}})
-measurable. Since the series in (
10
) is absolutely integrable, we may apply the tower property term by term. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\right]}\mid h_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[R^{\gamma}_{t^{\prime}:\infty}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\right]\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid h_{t}\right].

This completes the proof.

Note that

C.2
Smoothness of policy gradient objective

We make the following standard smoothness assumption on the class of parametrized stationary ASM policies.

Assumption 2

The class of parameterized stationary ASM policies,
\{\pi_{\theta},\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
satisfies the following: There exists
G,M>0
such that for every
s,\tilde{s}\in\mathcal{S}
,
a,\tilde{a}\in\mathcal{A}
, and
o\in\mathcal{O}
,

\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\|_{2}\leq G,\mbox{ and }\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\pi_{\theta}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\|_{2}\leq M.

Under the above assumption, the parameterized infinite-horizon discounted policy gradient objective (
4
) is uniformly smooth, which is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 11

Let the ASM policy parameterization satisfy Assumption
2
and
r_{\max}>0
be such that
|r_{t}(\cdot)|\leq r_{\max}
, for all
t\geq 1
. Then,
J_{\gamma}(\theta)
is
\beta_{\gamma}
-smooth in
\theta
, i.e.,
\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J_{\gamma}(\theta)\|_{2}\leq\beta_{\gamma}
, where

\displaystyle\beta_{\gamma}\coloneqq\frac{r_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\left(M+\frac{G^{2}(1+\gamma)}{1-\gamma}\right).

(14)

Proof
[
Proof of Lemma
11
]
From the policy gradient theorem (Theorem
6
),

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t-1}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{0:t},S_{t})\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta}(S_{t},A_{t}\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t})}\right].

Differentiating the above, we get,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J_{\gamma}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)(\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t})^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\left(\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right)^{\top}\right].

Applying
2
-norm above,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J_{\gamma}(\theta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right|\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right\|_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\gamma^{t-1}\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right|\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{t}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\right].

(15)

We will produce a uniform upper bound on the r.h.s. of the above inequality. From Assumption
2
, we have
\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq G
and
\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq M
. Also, note that
\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right|\leq\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}
which follows from the assumption
|r_{t}|\leq r_{\max}
. It remains to bound
\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})\right\|_{2}
in order to derive a uniform bound on
\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta)\right\|
.

Since

\displaystyle R^{\gamma}_{t:\infty}=r_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{1:t})+\gamma R^{\gamma}_{t+1:\infty},

we have

\displaystyle Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})

\displaystyle=r_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{1:t})

\displaystyle\quad+\gamma\sum_{o_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}}{p_{t}(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t})V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t+1}(o_{1:t+1},a_{0:t},s_{t})}.

Differentiating both sides with respect to
\theta
, and using that
r_{t}
and
p_{t}
do not depend on
\theta
, gives

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})=\gamma\sum_{o_{t+1}\in\mathcal{O}}{p_{t}(o_{t+1}\mid o_{1:t},a_{1:t})\nabla_{\theta}V^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t+1}(o_{1:t+1},a_{0:t},s_{t})}.

Applying Proposition
10
in the above, we obtain,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)}\mid o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t}\right].

(16)

Using Jensen’s inequality, Assumption
2
and the bound on
\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}\right|
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}(o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[\sum_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t^{\prime}}(O_{1:t^{\prime}},A_{0:t^{\prime}},S_{t^{\prime}})\right|\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}(S_{t^{\prime}},A_{t^{\prime}}\mid S_{t^{\prime}-1},A_{t^{\prime}-1},O_{t^{\prime}})\right)\right\|_{2}}\mid o_{1:t},a_{0:t},s_{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t^{\prime}=t+1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-t}\cdot\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\cdot G}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma Gr_{\max}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}.

Substituting the bounds on
\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta}\right)\right\|_{2}
,
\left|Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right|
and
\left\|\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\gamma,\pi_{\theta}}_{t}\right\|_{2}
in (
15
), we obtain,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}J_{\gamma}(\theta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{\max}M}{1-\gamma}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t-1}}+\frac{r_{\max}G^{2}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t-1}}+\frac{r_{\max}G^{2}}{1-\gamma}\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{t\,\gamma^{t-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{\max}M}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+\frac{r_{\max}G^{2}\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}+\frac{r_{\max}G^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}=\beta_{\gamma},

which completes the proof.

C.3
Convergence results

Next, we establish finite-time expected and high-probability convergence rates for the time-averaged gradient norm of ASMPG (Algorithm
2
) iterates with constant stepsize, along with finite-time and almost sure convergence results under decreasing stepsizes.

Theorem 12
(Finite-time convergence of discounted ASM policy gradient)

Consider the iterates
\{\theta_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
generated by Algorithm
2
. Suppose that the stationary ASM policy parameterization satisfies Assumption
2
. Let
\Delta_{1}:=\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}J_{\gamma}(\theta)-J_{\gamma}(\theta_{1})
, and let
\beta_{\gamma}
be the smoothness constant in Lemma
11
. Let
C_{\gamma}:=\frac{2r_{\max}G}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
.
Then, the following statements hold.

1.

If
\alpha_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta_{\gamma}},\frac{2}{C_{\gamma}}\sqrt{\frac{2\Delta_{1}}{\beta_{\gamma}K}}\right\},~\forall k
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\|\nabla_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta_{k})\|_{2}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{2\beta_{\gamma}\Delta_{1}}{K}+C_{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{\gamma}\Delta_{1}}{2K}}.

2.

With probability at least
1-\delta
, the iterates satisfy the following bounds:

(a)

If
\alpha_{k}=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\beta_{\gamma}},\frac{1}{C_{\gamma}}\sqrt{\frac{\Delta_{1}}{\beta_{\gamma}K}}\right\},~\forall k
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{2\beta_{\gamma}\Delta_{1}}{K}+5C_{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\beta_{\gamma}\Delta_{1}}{K}}+\frac{12C_{\gamma}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{K}.

(b)

If
\alpha_{k}=\frac{1}{\beta_{\gamma}\sqrt{k}},~\forall k
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\frac{2\beta_{\gamma}\Delta_{1}+3C_{\gamma}^{2}(1+\log K)+12C_{\gamma}^{2}\log(1/\delta)}{\sqrt{K}}.

3.

If
\{\alpha_{k}\}
is any positive sequence satisfying
\sum_{k}{\alpha_{k}}=\infty
and
\sum_{k}{\alpha^{2}_{k}}<\infty
, then with probability
1
,
\left\|\nabla_{\theta}J_{\gamma}(\theta_{k})\right\|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

Proof

We first derive a bound on
\left\|\widehat{g}_{\gamma,k}\right\|_{2}
. Since
|r_{t}|\leq r_{\max}
and by Assumption
2
, we have almost surely

\displaystyle\left\|\widehat{g}_{\gamma,k}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\left|\sum_{t^{\prime}=t}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t^{\prime}-1}r_{t^{\prime}}(O^{k}_{1:t^{\prime}},A^{k}_{1:t^{\prime}})}\right|\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(\pi_{\theta_{k}}(S^{k}_{t},A^{k}_{t}\mid S^{k}_{t-1},A^{k}_{t-1},O^{k}_{t})\right)\right\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}{\gamma^{t-1}\frac{r_{\max}G}{1-\gamma}}

\displaystyle=\frac{r_{\max}G}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}=C_{\gamma}/2.

The above bound implies a bounded-variance (and sub-Gaussian) noise condition with parameter
C_{\gamma}
. From here the proof follows from the proof of Theorem
5
, by substituting
C_{H}
with
C_{\gamma}
and
\beta
with
\beta_{\gamma}
.

Appendix D
Condition for ASM policies to be optimal

In this section, we identify a sufficient condition under which the restriction to the class of ASM policies while optimizing for the expected cumulative reward incurs no loss of optimality. Equivalently, there is an ASM policy that is optimal. This condition is that the agent state generated by the agent state dynamics is a sufficient statistic of the history for both rewards and future observations.

Definition 13
(Ideal agent state dynamics)

Consider an agent state dynamics
\nu=\{\nu_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
and let the agent state process

\{S_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}
be generated recursively as

\displaystyle S_{t}\sim\nu_{t}(\cdot\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t}),\quad t\in[H],

with
(S_{0},A_{0})=(s_{0},a_{0})
, where
(s_{0},a_{0})
is a designated initial
state-action pair. We say that
\nu
is an
ideal agent state dynamics
if, for every
t\in[H]
, there exists
\bar{r}_{t}:S\times A\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t})=\bar{r}_{t}(S_{t},A_{t}),

and for every
t\in[H-1]
, there exists
\bar{p}_{t}:S\times A\to\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
such that

\displaystyle p_{t}(\cdot\mid O_{1:t},A_{1:t})=\bar{p}_{t}(\cdot\mid S_{t},A_{t}).

Definition
13
states that, given the current action, the agent state
S_{t}
contains all information from the history that is relevant for the current reward and for predicting the next observation. Thus, once
S_{t}
is known, the rest of the past observations and actions can be discarded.

The following lemma shows that once an ideal agent state dynamics is known, it suffices to optimize over only those decision rules that use only the current agent state. A closely related result appears in
(
50
)
.

Lemma 14
(Optimality of ASM policies under an ideal agent state dynamics)

Suppose there exists an ideal agent state dynamics

\nu^{\star}=\{\nu_{t}^{\star}\}_{t=1}^{H}
. Then, there is no loss of optimality in restricting attention to the class of ASM policies that use
\nu^{\star}
while maximizing the expected cumulative reward. In
particular,

\displaystyle\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\circ\nu^{\star},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right]=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right],

where
\phi=\{\phi_{t}:\mathcal{S}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}\}_{t=1}^{H}
ranges over
agent-state-dependent Markov decision rules.

Proof

Every ASM policy
\phi\circ\nu^{\star}
induces a history-dependent
policy. Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\circ\nu^{\star},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right]\leq\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right].

(17)

It remains to prove the reverse inequality. Fix an arbitrary history-dependent policy
\pi=\{\pi_{t}\}_{t=1}^{H}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}
. Now consider the ideal agent state dynamics
\nu^{\star}
which uses

S_{t}\sim\nu_{t}^{\star}(\cdot\mid S_{t-1},A_{t-1},O_{t}).

We will now construct an ASM policy
\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}
such that it will induce the same probability as that done by
\pi\circ\nu^{\star}
. Let
\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}
denote the probability measure induced by policy
\pi
and agent state dynamics
\nu^{\star}
. For each
t\in[H]
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
, define

\displaystyle\phi_{t}^{\pi}(a\mid s):=\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(A_{t}=a\mid S_{t}=s),

whenever
\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s)>0
. If
\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s)=0
, define
\phi_{t}^{\pi}(\cdot\mid s)
arbitrarily. We will show that the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a)=\mathbb{P}^{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a),\quad\forall(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A},\forall t\in[H].

(18)

We prove the claim by induction on
t
.

The base case
t=1
: the distribution of
S_{1}
is the same under both policies because
S_{1}
is generated from the same initial pair
(S_{0},A_{0})=(s_{0},a_{0})
, the same initial observation distribution, and the same kernel
\nu_{1}^{\star}
. Moreover, by construction,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}(A_{1}=a\mid S_{1}=s)=\phi_{1}^{\pi}(a\mid s)=\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(A_{1}=a\mid S_{1}=s)

on the support of
S_{1}
. Hence, the claim holds for
t=1
.

Now suppose that the (
18
) holds at time
t
. Since
\nu^{\star}
is ideal, the
conditional law of
O_{t+1}
depends on the history only via
(S_{t},A_{t})
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(O_{t+1}=o\mid O_{1:t},A_{1:t})=\bar{p}_{t}(o\mid S_{t},A_{t}).

Therefore, under the fixed agent state dynamics
\nu^{\star}
, the one-step transition kernel of the agent state is

\displaystyle\bar{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a):=\sum_{o\in\mathcal{O}}\bar{p}_{t}(o\mid s,a)\nu_{t+1}^{\star}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a,o).

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(S_{t+1}=s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,a}{\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a)\bar{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,a}{\mathbb{P}^{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}(S_{t}=s,A_{t}=a)\,\bar{P}_{t}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}^{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}(S_{t+1}=s^{\prime}),

where the second equality uses the induction hypothesis. Finally, by the
definition of
\phi_{t+1}^{\pi}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}(A_{t+1}=a^{\prime}\mid S_{t+1}=s^{\prime})=\phi_{t+1}^{\pi}(a^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}^{\pi,\nu^{\star}}(A_{t+1}=a^{\prime}\mid S_{t+1}=s^{\prime})

on the support of
S_{t+1}
. Hence, the joint distribution of

(S_{t+1},A_{t+1})
also agrees under the two policies. This completes the
induction.

Using the reward condition in Definition
13
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu^{\star}}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}r_{t}(O_{1:t},A_{1:t})\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{H}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\nu^{\star}}\left[\bar{r}_{t}(S_{t},A_{t})\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{H}{\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}}\left[\bar{r}_{t}(S_{t},A_{t})\right]}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right].

Thus, for every history-dependent policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}
, there
exists an ASM policy
\phi^{\pi}\circ\nu^{\star}
with the same value.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right]\leq\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\circ\nu^{\star},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right].

(19)

Combining the two (
17
) and (
19
) yields

\displaystyle\max_{\phi}\mathbb{E}_{\phi\circ\nu^{\star},\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right]=\max_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{HR}}}\mathbb{E}_{\pi,\mu}\left[R_{1:H}\right].

Lemma
14
implies that an ideal agent state dynamics reduces the optimization problem from the class of all history-dependent policies to the class of ASM policies. In particular, if
S_{t}
takes values in a low-dimensional space, then the agent state dynamics acts as an efficient compressor of the trajectory.

Remark 15
(Examples of Ideal agent state dynamics)

A canonical example is a POMDP, where the hidden environment state is not observed directly. However, the belief state, i.e., the conditional distribution of the hidden state given the observation-action history, is an information state. The belief-update recursion is therefore an ideal agent state dynamics, and Lemma
14
recovers the classical fact that it suffices to optimize over policies that depend only on the belief state.

Another example is a velocity-only CartPole system. Suppose the agent observes only the linear and angular velocities, while the initial positions are known and the observations are noise-free. The position variables can then be reconstructed recursively by integrating the velocity over time. In this case, the agent state dynamics is a deterministic integrator that reconstructs a sufficient state from the observation history, and the resulting agent state is sufficient for predicting future observations and rewards.

Appendix E
Properties of softmax ASM policies

Let
\mathcal{S}
,
\mathcal{A}
, and
\mathcal{O}
be finite sets and let
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|\mathcal{S}\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{A}\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{O}\right|H}
. The softmax ASM policy
\pi_{\theta}
, parameterized by
\theta
, is defined as,

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta,t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)=\frac{\exp(\theta(\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o,s,a,t))}{\sum_{(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\exp(\theta(\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o,s^{\prime},a^{\prime},t))},

for every
(\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O}
,
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and
t\in[H]
. The class of softmax ASM policies satisfies the uniform score and Hessian bounds required in Assumption
1
, as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 16
(Lipschitz continuity and smoothness)

Consider the class of softmax ASM policies
\{\pi_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|\mathcal{S}\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{A}\right|^{2}\left|\mathcal{O}\right|H}\}
. For every

t\in[H]
,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\pi_{\theta,t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\left(\pi_{\theta,t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\right)\right\|_{2}\leq 1.

Consequently, the softmax parameterized ASM policy class satisfies
Assumption
1
with
G=\sqrt{2}
and
M=1
.

Proof

Fix
t\in[H]
and an input triple

x=(\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{O}
.
For notational convenience, write
y=(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and

\displaystyle p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t):=\pi_{\theta,t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o).

The parameter block corresponding to the input
x
is

\{\theta(x,y^{\prime},t):y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\}
.

For any
y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, the derivative of the log-softmax is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta(x,y^{\prime},t)}\log\left(p_{\theta}(y\mid x)\right)=\mathbf{1}\{y^{\prime}=y\}-p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x;t).

For parameter coordinates corresponding to input triples
x^{\prime}\neq x
, the
derivative is zero. Hence

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t)\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}}\left(\mathbf{1}\{y^{\prime}=y\}-p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x;t)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t))^{2}+\sum_{y^{\prime}\neq y}p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x;t)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t))^{2}+\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}\neq y}{p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x;t)}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2(1-p_{\theta}(y\mid x))^{2}\leq 2.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\log\left(p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t)\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{2}.

Next, the Hessian with respect to the active parameter block is

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta(x,\cdot,t)}^{2}\log\left(p_{\theta}(y\mid x;t)\right)=-\left(\operatorname{diag}(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t))-p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)^{\top}\right).

All mixed derivatives involving parameter blocks
x^{\prime}\neq x
are zero. Thus, it suffices to bound the spectral norm of

\displaystyle\operatorname{diag}(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t))-p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)\,p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)^{\top}.

For any vector
v
with
\left\|v\right\|_{2}=1
,

\displaystyle v^{\top}\left(\operatorname{diag}(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t))-p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)\,p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x;t)^{\top}\right)v

\displaystyle=\sum_{y^{\prime}}p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)v_{y^{\prime}}^{2}-\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}}p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)v_{y^{\prime}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y^{\prime}}p_{\theta}(y^{\prime}\mid x)v_{y^{\prime}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{y^{\prime}}v_{y^{\prime}}^{2}=1.

Since the above matrix is positive semidefinite, its spectral norm is at most
1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{2}\log\left(\pi_{\theta,t}(s,a\mid\tilde{s},\tilde{a},o)\right)\right\|_{2}\leq 1.

This proves the claim.

HallwayNavigation.
HallwayNavigation is a deterministic, partially observed maze-navigation task introduced by
32
. The latent state is the agent’s position in a
7\times 4
grid containing two interior blocked regions (See Figure
5
). The agent starts uniformly at one of the four corners and must reach the central goal cell. The available actions are north, east, south, and west. If an action would move the agent into a wall or a blocked cell, the agent remains in the same place. The agent does not observe its absolute grid position. Instead, it observes whether there is a wall immediately to the north, east, south, and west, giving up to
2^{4}=16
possible observations. Reaching the goal gives a reward of
5.0
, attempting to move into a wall gives a reward of
-1.0
, and all other transitions give a reward of
-0.1
.

Note that different grid cells can have identical local wall configurations. Thus, the agent needs to take different actions to reach the goal from cells with the same observation. Therefore, the current observation is not a sufficient statistic for control, and the agent must use history to disambiguate its position.

HealthcareTreatment.
Reinforcement learning has been applied to treatment planning and dynamic treatment regimes, typically using Markovian state-based formulations
(
29
;
15
;
14
)
. In realistic treatment settings, however, improvement/deterioration of health can depend on intervention history, since repeated therapy may accumulate toxicity and induce resistance, thereby altering the effect of future treatment
(
49
)
. Motivated by this, we consider a sequential treatment-planning environment in which the agent observes the patient’s current health state and chooses one of three interventions: no treatment, mild treatment, or aggressive treatment. These interventions have different efficacy-cost trade-offs: aggressive treatment has the largest immediate effect but also the highest cost. The environment also maintains two hidden variables: an accumulated toxicity burden and a treatment-resistance variable. Toxicity refers to the cumulative side effects of previous interventions. It increases after treatment, especially after aggressive treatment, and decays gradually over time. Treatment resistance represents reduced effectiveness of future interventions after repeated aggressive treatment. It increases when aggressive treatment is used and also decays gradually over time.

At each step, the patient’s health is updated by combining three effects. First, the chosen intervention provides a treatment benefit. Second, this benefit is attenuated by the current hidden resistance level, so repeated aggressive treatment can make future treatments less effective. Third, accumulated hidden toxicity contributes a negative effect on health. Thus, aggressive treatment can be useful in the short term because it has the largest nominal treatment effect, but excessive aggressive treatment may be harmful in the long term because it increases both toxicity and resistance. This environment is non-Markovian because the agent observes only the current health state, not the hidden toxicity or resistance variables.

The reward is the post-treatment health minus the intervention cost. The episode terminates if the patient’s health crosses either a recovery or failure threshold. In our implementation, crossing the upper recovery threshold
h=2.0
gives a terminal reward of
+50
, while crossing the lower failure threshold
h=-1.0
gives a terminal penalty of
-50
. Episodes start from health
h=0.5
with zero toxicity and zero resistance.

MachineRepair.
MachineRepair is a maintenance-control environment
(
20
)
for a single deteriorating machine. Maintenance models often go beyond Markovian dynamics by allowing the degradation of the machine to depend on accumulated age or wear, and by allowing repairs to be imperfect rather than as-good-as-new
(
40
;
21
)
. Motivated by this, we consider a machine maintenance environment in which the agent observes only a coarse machine condition, healthy or degraded, and chooses between continuing operation and performing repair. In addition to the observed condition, the simulator maintains an unobserved wear variable that evolves with the usage and repair history: wear increases during operation and is only partially reduced by repair. The transition probabilities depend on this hidden wear level, so that a healthy machine becomes more likely to degrade as wear increases, a degraded machine becomes more likely to remain degraded when wear is high, and repair becomes less effective as accumulated wear grows. Consequently, two machines with the same observed condition can exhibit different transition behavior depending on their past usage and maintenance history. Continuing to operate the machine in the healthy state yields a positive reward, whereas in the degraded state, it yields a negative reward; repairs incur a fixed unit cost. This provides a simple maintenance benchmark with history-dependent dynamics in the spirit of age- and wear-dependent degradation and partially observed deterioration models
(
39
)
.

VelocityOnlyCartPole.

VelocityOnlyCartPole
(
36
)
is a partially observed variant of the classic CartPole control problem
(
6
;
10
)
. In the standard CartPole system, the state vector consists of the cart position, cart velocity, pole angle, and pole angular velocity. The agent applies either a leftward or rightward force to the cart, and the objective is to keep the pole balanced while keeping the cart within the allowed track.

In the velocity-only version, the agent observes only velocity information: the cart velocity and the pole angular velocity. The cart position and pole angle are hidden. The agent receives a positive reward at each time step for keeping the pole upright and the system within its stability limits, and the episode terminates if the pole angle becomes too large, the cart leaves the allowed track, or the maximum episode length is reached.

Two trajectories with the same velocity components may require different actions depending on the cart and pole positions. The task is therefore non-Markovian, and effective control requires a history of past observations and actions. We note that the position variables can be reconstructed iteratively by integrating the corresponding velocity component, which aligns with the recursive structure of the agent state dynamics.
