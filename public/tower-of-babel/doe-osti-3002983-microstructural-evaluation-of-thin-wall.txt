ABSTRACT
Laser powder bed fusion (LBPF) is a method of additive manufacturing (AM) that offers a means to
fabricate complex components with features which rely upon the mechanical integrity of thin-wall
(less than 0.4 mm thick) structures. The microstructure of a 316L AM thin wall is investigated as
metallographically prepared from a cylindrical shell. Optimization is pursued through mechanical
polishing and chemical etching to minimize the effects of surface roughness. Microanalysis
measurements of the sectioned component reveal the sample has high microstructural fidelity,
uniform composition and phase content across grain as well as powder layer boundaries within the
thin wall. The results are attributed to a uniform thermal gradient at the part scale during rapid
solidification.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The additive manufacturing (AM) of 316L thin wall structures is accomplished using a laser powder
bed fusion process. Microanalysis measurements reveal high microstructural fidelity with a uniform
homogeneous composition and phase content across grain as well as powder layer boundaries. This
result is accomplished without evidence of composition segregation or the formation of secondary
phases. These results confirm the prospective use of AM to produce structural components with
fine scale features, high aspect ratios, and established mechanical property behavior.

1. INTRODUCTION
The use of additively manufactured (AM) structures present opportunities to produce as-built
components with complex geometries and material composition. The control of composition in
source materials across the dimensions of components is achievable through AM methods such as
laser powder-bed fusion (LPBF). In addition, LPBF provides a method for composite material
blends [1] as well as prescribed [2] microstructures and properties. The present challenge to produce
thin wall sections with high structural integrity that are just a few-tenths of a millimeter thick is
especially relevant for AM structures synthesized by LPBF. Thin wall sections are sought as formed
from the fusion of thin powder layers wherein the variation in mechanical behaviors as strength and
elongation to failure are found [3] to be intrinsically related, as e.g., to variations in thickness and
build direction. To this purpose, the sample microstructure of 316L AM material is evaluated as
prepared for microanalysis using a well-defined metallographic methodology. The investigation
includes microstructural feature analysis to reveal grain morphology, crystalline phase, and
composition – with particular attention to transits across grain boundary features.

Both scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and laser interferometry are used along with
metallographic optical imaging to reveal surface finish, grain morphology, and microstructure. In
addition to the use of SEM electron back scattering diffraction (EBSD) to assess crystalline phase
formation, electron dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) trace analysis is used to determine elemental
composition and variations across the thin wall section. These attributes are achieved through an
optimized metallographic polishing process wherein surface damage is removed, and the integrity of
the constituent microstructure remains.

The first key step for a successful metallographic sample preparation is to produce a smooth surface
followed by an etch that removes surface damage from mechanical polishing but does not attack the
grain structure or preferentially deplete the grain boundary structure of constituent alloying
elements. Details for the sample preparation and characterization methods are provided along with
corresponding experimental results that will demonstrate uniform composition and phase content
across both grain and powder layer boundaries within the 316L AM thin wall.

2. EXPRIMENTAL METHODS

2.1. Sample Synthesis
The preparation of thin-wall, right-circular cylinders is accomplished using LPBF on a 3D Systems,
Inc. PROX300 Additive Manufacturing Metal 3D-printer. The instrument is equipped with a 500 W
laser and a powder layering roller that promotes powder particle compaction to facilitate laser energy
transfer between particles at a faster rate. The cylinder wall thickness is only 0.400 mm thick,
whereas the height and radius are 5.5 mm. Synthesis occurs under a controlled inert atmosphere of
argon with less than 250 ppm of oxygen to prevent oxidation. The contours/outline feature of the
PROX300 is used to build the thin walls with parameters of a lower beam energy and scan speed to
enhance both laser stability and accuracy, instead of utilizing a hatch strategy. To establish boundary
overlap (also known as skywriting or compensation) before setting the laser parameters, it’s verified
that the vectors aren’t passing through the outlines. If the vectors pass the outlines, then it can
adversely affect the surface finish. Conversely, if the outlines aren’t contacted, then internal porosity
may result. Adjustments are made as needed since a 45° rotation is used after each layer. The laser
parameters used during synthesis of the thin walls include a 200 W power, 1000 mm×s-1 scan speed,
50 µm spot size, and 40 µm powder layer thickness. The thin-walled hollow cylinder samples are
grown orthogonally to the build plate with an 11x11 layout array of samples. Only three passes of
the incident laser are needed to fuse the powders across the width of the thin wall. Microscopy of
witness cylinders evidence only 0.35% and 0.28% porosity in longitudinal and transverse views,
respectively. The nominal composition (wt.%) of the elements in the 316L alloy source powder is
16-18 Cr, 10-14 Ni, 2-3 Mo, < 2 Mn, < 0.75 Si, and balance Fe. The addition of Mo distinguishes
316L from the 304L alloy.

2.2. Metallography
The cylinders are metallographically sectioned along the build axis to reveal a radial view of the thin
walls. The samples are vacuum potted in an epoxy resin prior to mechanical sectioning. The cross-
sectioned surface is examined as mechanically finished using a vibratory polish and as
electrochemical etching progresses with time. The mechanical polishing progresses using: 400, 600,
1200, 4000 grit silicon carbide (SiC) for intervals of 30-120 s at each step; followed by 6, 1 µm
diamond for intervals of 300 s each; colloidal silica for 300 s; and a vibratory polish for 1 h with a
colloidal-silica suspension (at a pH ~9) that can induce some topological relief with grain
orientation. The electrochemical etch is performed using a solution composition by volume of 55 ml

 11

H20, 60 ml 15.8M (70%) HNO3, and 15 ml 0.1M HCl. The sample is the anode in the
electrochemical cell where the etch is performed under a driving potential of 1.15V for time periods
of 7, 90, and 120 s at a current density of ~50 mA×mm-2.

The surface topology is measured at each step of the polishing and subsequent chemical etching
processes to map the surface topology and concurrent change in roughness. The laser scanning
confocal microscopy (LSCM) method combines white light with a laser light source. A Keyence
VK-250 LSCM is used to scan both the polished and etched surfaces to acquire optical images and
high-resolution surface data. The heights of surface features are measured by analyzing the reflected
laser intensity relative to the z-position of the laser source. The surface is scanned with C-Laser
digital interference contrast (DIC) to provide gray-scale imaging, and color-scale limited surface
images. The surface area A of the polished material scanned is 1.5 mm2. The surface roughness
computed for the Keyence LSCM is measured by the maximum height Sz, the arithmetic mean
height Sa and the root-mean-square (rms) height Sq using eqns. (1-2) where the profile height

2.3. Scanning Electron Microscopy
The elemental composition is measured in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) using electron
dispersive spectrometry (EDS). A JEOL 7600 (with an Oxford Silicon Drift Detector) operated at
20 keV is used for trace analysis across the full section of the thin wall, whereas an FEI Apreo loVac
(with a Bruker XFlash 6160 detector) operated at 15 keV is used for fine spatial resolution in area

 12

maps. The intensity of characteristic x-rays generated off the sample surface as exposed to an
energetic incident electron beam is used to determine the elemental composition of the material
considering factors such as the atomic number (Z), absorption (A), and fluorescence (F) excitation
effects. One characteristic peak designated by the Siegbahn notation is selected for each element to
determine the semi-quantitative composition measurement. The Ka x-ray peaks of 6.40 keV Fe, 5.41
keV Cr, 7.47 keV Ni, 5.90 keV Mn, and 1.74 keV Si are used initially for each element except for Mo
where the 2.29 keV La peak is used. Carbon and oxygen are considered surface contaminants and are
excluded from the analysis. For example, if the oxygen Ka x-ray peak at 0.525 keV is included, then
values <0.3 wt.% are computed.

From the database for the absorption coefficients [4], >95% of the characteristic Ka x-rays for these
elements are calculated to be emitted from distances up to 1.7-2.5 µm from the incident beam. For
x-ray energies less than 5 keV, a lateral spatial resolution as small as 0.3 µm is reported [5] as the
result of Monte Carlo simulations for identifying the presence of these elements. The determination
of the characteristic x-ray attenuation length normal to the sample surface is readily computed [6]
assuming the intensity of x-rays falls to e-1 of its value at the surface as recommended for semi-
quantitative composition analysis. For even finer spatial resolution, the La peaks of 2.29 keV Mo,
852 eV Ni, 705 eV Fe, 637 eV Mn, and 573 eV Cr are used for semiquantitative analysis. The
computed attenuation lengths for these La peaks normal to the surface are 423, 79, 72, 73, and 64
nm, correspondingly. Since these La peaks are located near the dominant 705 eV Fe peak, the La
peaks are close to the limiting 130 eV spectral resolution (full-width half-maximum intensity) of the
detector used to acquire the EDS spectra for accurate compositional analysis. Note that the O Ka x-
ray peak at 525 eV is within this threshold for measurement of the Cr La x-ray peak. For these
reasons, the finest step interval of 0.2 µm is used in the EDS analysis of the alloy composition with
La x-ray peaks across the microstructure of the AM section. For this study, variations in composition
below this spatial resolution should only be attributed to boundary scattering rather than resolvable
composition effects. The use of electron-transparent foils, such as scanning transmission electron
microscopy (STEM) specimens, would permit finer spatial resolution as penetration depths would
be less than 0.1 µm.

Electron back-scatter diffraction (EBSD) is used to determine phase content using scattered
electrons in the reflected mode. In principle, the spacing and geometric pattern for an array of
‘parallel’ bands are used to assess the crystalline symmetry and lattice spacing of the diffracting

 13

volume to determine the crystalline phase and orientation. From the collected diffraction data,
inverse-pole figures (IPF) are generated to display a color-coded map of crystalline orientation
within an axial view of the grain structure.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Polishing
The merits of evaluating the EDS trace are achieved through an optimized polishing process. The
adverse effects of surface damage accumulated during mechanical polishing is removed but the
integrity of the constituent microstructure remains. The steps in this process are to produce as
smooth a surface as reasonably possible by mechanical means, followed by a chemical etch that
removes surface damage from mechanical polishing but does not attack the grain structure nor
preferentially attack the grain boundary structure and deplete it of constituent alloy elements. An
example of the progression through the mechanical polishing steps is seen in the (top row) C-Laser
DIC and (bottom row) scale-limited surface images of Fig. 1 for sample no. 39.

3.2. Phase Identification
The EBSD maps are acquired using a 20 keV incident beam voltage with a 70° specimen tilt. An
inverse pole figure EBSD map of the central portion of the thin wall is shown in Fig. 4a. The
corresponding electron band contrast image of the grain structure is shown in Fig. 4b. These images
reveal the three passes of the laser that are used to form the thin wall. The crystalline content of the
microstructure is identified as indexed to a face-center-cubic (fcc) phase with a lattice parameter ag
of 0.359 nm, and a body-center-cubic bcc phase with a lattice parameter ab of 0.287 nm. The phase
content map in Fig. 4c is indexed to the phase fraction, as computed to 96.65% fcc austenite (shown
in red), 0.15% bcc ferrite (shown in pink), and 3.20% zero solution (as shown in black that
corresponds to grain boundary regions). For all regions sampled, the EBSD results indicate that the
as-deposited structure austenitic with less than 0.2% ferrite. Within the EBSD map of Fig. 4a, the
base colors of red, green, blue represent crystalline orientations of [001], [101] and [111],
respectively.

3.4. Microhardness Behavior
Vickers microhardness measurements Hv are taken using a 10g load at six positions along the
centerline of the thin-wall at 125-175 µm separations from the metallographic cross-section of
sample no. 39. The indenter tip is a four-sided diamond pyramid with an internal angle of 136°. For
an average indent diameter d, the microhardness Hv equals 1.8544∙P/d2. For this sample the average
diagonal is 9.62±0.22 µm producing an Hv value of 200.8±9.3 kgf/mm2 (1.97±0.09 GPa). For
comparison, 33 microhardness measurements along all three principal directions of bulk 316L AM
samples using a 25 g indenter load have an average value of 208.8±22.9 kgf/mm2 (2.05±0.10 GPa).
Measurement of a 214 kgf/mm2 (2.10 GPa) standard produced a value of 216.1±12.0 kgf/mm2
(2.12±0.12 GPa). At a much higher indenter load of 200 g, an average Vickers microhardness of
207.0±6.1 kgf/mm2 (2.03±0.06 GPa) is measured at ten different locations within the cross-section
of a wrought 316L test coupon. These bulk measurement values compare well with the average
value of 201.2±9.5 kgf/mm2 (1.97±0.09 GPa) for the thin wall section.

4. DISCUSSION
Significant efforts are made to develop as well as refine both the mechanical polishing and chemical
etching processes to produce a smooth continuous surface suitable for a submicron scale of
resolution in the detailed microscopy and microanalysis of the material. This objective is especially
relevant for accomplishing an analysis of the full cross-section to the thin wall 316L AM material as
required for the evaluation structural components.

The columnar growth morphology seen in Fig. 4 is commonly observed [7-8] for 316L AM material
along the build direction. In addition, the formation of a cellular sub-grain structure is generally
reported for rapidly solidified 316L AM [9-11]. A similar structure is clearly seen in the SEM images
of Figs. 10 and 12. The composition values for the 316L thin-wall AM structures listed in Table 3
are consistent with comparable source 316L powder stock wherein inductively coupled plasma mass
spectrometry (ICP-MS) measurements [12] reveal (wt.%) values of 67.8±0.3 Fe, 16.84±0.34 Cr,
10.81±0.22 Ni, 2.05±0.20 Mo, 1.20±0.12 Mn, 0.65±0.10 Si, 0.21±0.03 Cu, and 0.10±0.02 Co. Vickers
microhardness measurements of this powder stock revealed an average value of 261.0±21.4
kgf/mm2 (2.56±0.21 GPa). Differences in the Vickers microhardness are consistent with the effects
of applying 1000 °C anneal treatments [13] where values for LPBF 316L AM material decrease from
260±13 to 205±7 kgf/mm2 (2.55±0.13 to 2.01±0.07 GPa).

A subcellular structure within 316L AM is reported [14] from high-resolution transmission electron
microscopy (HRTEM) images with <10 nm Mn-Si-O particles and 4 wt.% enrichments of Cr within
20-40 nm wide regions of grain boundaries. The lack of a segregation effect is reported as dependent
upon the source material processing. Only 20nm thick bands of Cr, Mo, Ni and Mn enrichment
were imaged by TEM EDS [15] at the boundaries of 0.2 µm sub-cells of 316L. Bands of Cr and Mo
enrichment 30-50nm wide are reported in LBPF 316L [16] using scanning TEM analysis at
boundaries of 0.2-0.4 µm sub-cells. Similar results are reported [17] for directed energy deposits
(DED) of 304L AM where EDS mapping show: Cr enriched solidification cell boundaries <0.1 µm
thick; Ni enriched solidification cell cores ~1 µm in width; and Mn-Si-O enriched oxide inclusions
<0.2 µm in diameter. EDS line traces for the 316L DED material revealed a ~2 wt.% increase at
cellular boundaries but did not show any statistically observable Ni variation across cellular
boundaries. The formation of Mn-Si-oxides between laser melt regions can be resolved [18] in 316L
LPBF material at the 0.1-0.5 µm scale, along with elemental variations in composition across finer-

 25

scale discrete boundaries in material subjected to pitting corrosion. The segregation of composition
within selected laser melted (SLM) 316L AM can be significant as Cr variation is reported [19] to
change yield strength through micromechanical crystal plasticity modeling. STEM EDS maps and
atom probe tomography show [11] small amounts (~0.5-1 at.%) of Cr and Mo enrichment at
boundaries as attributed to dislocation cell wall structure in as-deposited LPBF 316L AM.

At present, there is no statistically significant evidence for composition segregation as revealed by
the EDS line traces of Figs. 5-8 across the full cross-section to the thin walls of the 316L LPBF AM
material. In addition, there is a lack of sub-micron composition segregation either within or across
the grain boundaries as seen at the finer scale 60-80 nm resolution attained using characteristic La x-
ray peaks as seen in Figs. 10-12. This present result, as well as those reported that show segregation,
are a function of the synthesis process parameters.

The effects of macrosegregation by solute rejection at subcellular and grain boundaries is reported
[20] in the directional solidification of Pb-Sn alloys under a thermal gradient. This result supports an
assertion that a thermal gradient during rapid solidification of AM materials is needed for solute
segregation to boundaries in ultra-fine scale microstructures. Microscopic modeling efforts [21] to
demonstrate regrowth from laser induced melting show solute uptake at the liquid-solid interface
during directional solidification. The solute concentration at the solid-liquid interface is higher than
its equilibrium composition. A transition to solute trapping from equilibrium segregation occurs as
the velocity of the solidification front exceeds the diffusive speed of the solute in the liquid phase.
The localization of solute enrichment is reported [22] as well for an Al-20 wt.% Cu alloy during its
directional solidification. Fragmentation of the dendritic microstructure results at the solidification
front from arrival of the solute-rich liquid phase. Loss of solute, in turn, causes a remelting of the
dendritic root that furthers a detachment of dendritic branches.

A postulate for the segregation of high melt temperature (Tm) elements to grain and cell boundaries
should consider the melt process itself during the 316L AM synthesis. If the centers of the powders
are at higher temperature during the melt process than the exterior surface, then the high Tm
elements would migrate under the thermal gradient to solidify, i.e. move towards the cooler surface
of the boundary exterior. Also, temperature gradients that form at the solid-liquid interface would
further drive cellular structure formation and solute rejection from the tips of the solidification
front. For 316L stainless steel, segregation around the cellular structures would create an enrichment
of Cr and Mo at the boundaries, where the extent of the temperature gradient enhances solute

 26

segregation within the melt layer. However, the uniform composition in the thin wall section would
indicate uniform heating in a controlled melt to rapid solidification of the powder layer occurs
without discrete temperature gradients within the melt layer.

Rapid solidification of the thin-walled 316L produces the subcellar dendritic AM microstructure
independent of micro-segregation. The lack of detectable segregation is indicative of the fast
solidification rate associated with the AM process without thermal gradients. This uniform cooling
during rapid solidification may be a result of the thin wall structure itself functioning to radiate heat.
The uniformity within the microstructure of the thin wall sections of 316L as achieved using the
LPBF AM process is optimized to ensure a high-density solid (<0.2% porosity) with minimal
surface roughness. Attention was given to use of the contours/outline build feature to solidify
0.4mm thin walls using a lower beam energy and scan speed that enhance laser stability and
positional accuracy. With a 45° rotation after each layer, attention is paid to control beam path for
ensuring boundary overlap and avoid straying past boundary outlines. The lack of latent segregation
as function of build height demonstrates that a high degree of temperature management was
achieved during the synthesis process without thermal gradients at the part scale. The wrought-like
mechanical behavior evidenced through the lower Vickers microhardness values of ~2 GPa is
representative of the homogeneous composition measured throughout the microstructure.

 27

5. SUMMARY
The additive manufacturing of thin wall structures with high structural fidelity is accomplished using
a laser powder bed fusion process. The microstructure of a 316L AM thin wall section, only 0.4 mm
thick, is investigated as sampled from a cylindrical shell. A detailed metallographic preparation
procedure is developed to produce smooth surfaces with only a 0.3 µm rms roughness across the
entire section for conducting microscopy and microanalysis. Scanning electron microscopy reveals a
columnar growth from the rapid solidification of powders in the build direction with a constituent
submicron-cellular structure. Electron back scattering diffraction reveals solely an austenitic phase
without evidence for the formation of secondary crystalline phases. Microanalysis measurements of
the component using characteristic x-rays of energy dispersive spectroscopy reveal the high
microstructural fidelity has a uniform homogeneous composition and phase content across grain as
well as powder layer boundaries within the thin wall. This is accomplished without evidence of
composition segregation or the formation of secondary phases at a finer spatial resolution on the
order of 50 nm. These results confirm the prospective use of additive manufacturing to produce
structural components with fine scale features and established mechanical property behavior.
