Robot-assisted resection of abdominal lymphatic malformations in children: a novel minimally invasive strategy for complex lesions
Authors: Yi Chen, Duote Cai, Xiaoxiao Xuan, Sai Chen, Shuhao Zhang, Weiwei Chen, Qingjiang Chen, Zhigang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) represents a promising emerging alternative to conventional laparoscopic surgery (LAS) for abdominal lymphatic malformations (ALMs). We aimed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of robotic-assisted resection of ALMs in pediatric patients. A retrospective analysis was performed on 134 children with ALMs who underwent surgery from December 2018 to December 2025 at the Department of General Surgery, Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine. Patients were stratified into two groups according to the surgical modality: the robot-assisted surgery (RAS) group (n = 57) and the laparoscopic surgery (LAS) group (n = 77). The RAS group achieved a higher rate of total intracorporeal cyst dissection (33 vs. 13) and intracorporeal intestinal resection with anastomosis (21 vs. 1), whereas the LAS group more commonly used extracorporeal procedures (31 vs. 0; 32 vs. 3), with significant differences in surgical approach distribution (P < 0.001). The total hospitalization cost was significantly higher in the RAS group than in the LAS group [68286 (60183, 72551) yuan vs. 19261 (15651, 23869) yuan, P < 0.001]. Postoperatively, four complications occurred in the RAS group, while seven complications were observed in the LAS group. RAS appears to be a safe and feasible minimally invasive approach for pediatric ALMs. For complex lesions, RAS may offer advantages in intracorporeal dissection, intestinal preservation, and perioperative recovery. Introduction

Lymphatic malformations are benign congenital disorders stemming from lymphatic system dysplasia, typically presenting in early infancy with an incidence of 1 in 6,000 to 1 in 16,000 live births [1–3]. These lesions may involve diverse anatomical sites across the body, with the head and neck being the most commonly affected region [4, 5]. Abdominal lymphatic malformations (ALMs) are relatively uncommon, comprising less than 10% of all cases. Most affected children are asymptomatic, and lesions are often only identified following the onset of complications [6–8]. Clinical manifestations include abdominal pain, distension, palpable mass, vomiting, and constipation, resulting mainly from mass effect exerted by large cysts on adjacent abdominal organs, as well as secondary hemorrhage and infection [6–8]. Anatomically, approximately 70% of ALMs arise in the mesentery, followed by the greater omentum and retroperitoneum [7, 9–12].

In imaging assessment, ultrasound is the first-line screening for ALMs, while computed tomography (CT) is commonly used for emergency evaluation of acute abdominal pain [13]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can accurately delineate lesion boundaries and adjacent structures, with unique advantages for retroperitoneal lesions [7, 14, 15]. Although ALMs are benign lesions, delayed or inadequate intervention may lead to severe complications, including intestinal obstruction, intracystic hemorrhage, infection, peritonitis, and cystic torsion with subsequent necrosis [14, 15].

Complete surgical resection is the standard curative treatment for ALMs, as it effectively reduces the risks of postoperative recurrence and complications [16, 17]. Laparoscopic surgery (LAS) has become the primary minimally invasive approach for ALMs[18–21]. Nevertheless, complex lesions such as retroperitoneal malformations, deeply infiltrating lesions, and those surrounding major vessels or vital organs are difficult to resect laparoscopically, frequently requiring conversion to open surgery. Although sclerotherapy is commonly adopted as a palliative alternative for such intractable cases, it cannot achieve radical resection.

Robotic platforms have expanded minimally invasive treatment options for complex ALMs [22]. Featuring 3D high-definition vision, stable operation, high-precision dissection and flexible articulated instruments, robotic systems have been widely used in multiple surgical fields [23–33]. Currently, large comparative studies on robotic-assisted surgery (RAS) for pediatric ALMs remain scarce.

Unlike standard laparoscopy, which is suitable for simple lesions, RAS serves as a refined minimally invasive alternative to open surgery for complex ALMs. Herein, we retrospectively analyzed data from 134 pediatric ALM patients. As one of the largest cohorts to date, this study compares perioperative outcomes, operative time, hospital stay and medical costs between RAS and LAS. Our goal is to assess the safety and efficacy of RAS and provide evidence for clinical practice.

Methods

Clinical characteristics and grouping

A retrospective analysis was conducted on clinical data from 134 pediatric patients with ALMs who were hospitalized in the Department of General Surgery, Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, between December 2018 and December 2025. The study cohort included 74 males and 60 females. The surgical approach was determined by the guardians based on family economic conditions and personal preferences. Patients were divided into two groups by surgical modality: the RAS group (57 patients, 36 males and 21 females) with a median age of 59 (38, 84) months, and the LAS group (77 patients, 38 males and 39 females) with a median age of 57 (30, 86) months. All operations were performed by two senior attending surgeons from the same surgical team with extensive clinical experience in pediatric abdominal surgery. Relevant clinical data were collected, including demographic characteristics (age, sex), medical history, perioperative parameters, postoperative complications, and clinical outcomes.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Initial diagnosis and treatment at the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine; (2) Diagnosis of ALMs based on clinical presentation, ultrasonography, and confirmatory cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI); (3) Receipt of either RAS or LAS; (4) Postoperative pathological confirmation of lymphatic malformation.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Primary open surgery or sclerotherapy as the initial intervention; (2) A history of previous abdominal surgery; (3) Coexistence of other congenital malformations or systemic diseases.

Docking positions and surgical procedure for ALMs resection

RAS group: All patients underwent general anesthesia with tracheal intubation and were placed in the supine position. According to cyst location, a slight Trendelenburg or reverse Trendelenburg position was adopted, with a 15° rightward or leftward tilt. Pneumoperitoneum was established in accordance with standard protocols. An 8-mm umbilical midline incision was made for the camera (Port 2). Two additional 8-mm incisions were created as working ports, positioned along the vertical line between the lesion and the umbilicus, 5–8 cm from the umbilicus (Ports 1 and 3). If required, a 5-mm assistant port was also placed (Port 4) (Fig. 1). Surgical strategies were individualized based on the lesion location. For omental cysts, aspiration-assisted decompression was performed first via puncture, followed by en bloc complete resection. For mesenteric cysts, management was tailored to lesion complexity: the cyst was either dissected from the mesenteric tissue (Fig. 2A) or resected en bloc with the involved bowel segment, followed by primary intestinal anastomosis (Fig. 2H). For retroperitoneal lymphatic malformations, the surgical goal was to maximally preserve adjacent organ function. Following meticulous dissection and protection of adjacent major blood vessels and vital organs, complete cyst resection was attempted, with concomitant resection of involved bowel segments if indicated.

LAS group: Anesthesia and patient positioning were consistent with those used for RAS. Following pneumoperitoneum establishment, a 5-mm incision was made at the left paraumbilical margin for insertion of the primary laparoscope. According to the cyst location, two additional 5-mm incisions were created on the bilateral lateral abdominal walls as working ports. Surgical strategies were individualized according to the lesion location: for omental cysts, en bloc resection was readily achieved, with an extended umbilical incision utilized if necessary. For mesenteric cysts, the cyst and involved bowel segment were exteriorized from the abdominal cavity, followed by either isolated cyst dissection or combined resection of the involved bowel segment as clinically indicated. For retroperitoneal lymphatic malformations, timely conversion to open surgery was performed if laparoscopic dissection was technically challenging, surgical visualization was inadequate, or vital structures were at risk of injury.

Postoperative management and follow-up

All patients underwent postoperative follow-up at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and then every 6 months thereafter. Follow-up assessments included clinical examinations and abdominal ultrasonography. CT or MRI was performed if clinically indicated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 Categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson’s chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, with frequencies presented as percentages of the respective groups. Continuous variables were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, and the results were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Clinical characteristics and surgical outcomes in RAS and LAS groups

A total of 134 pediatric patients with ALMs were enrolled in the present study, including 57 patients who underwent RAS and 77 who received LAS. The LAS group comprised 38 males and 39 females, while the RAS group consisted of 36 males and 21 females. No significant intergroup differences were detected in age [59 (38, 84) months vs. 57 (30, 86) months, P = 0.707] or weight [18 (15, 24) kg vs. 16 (12, 22) kg, P = 0.222]. Notably, preoperative imaging revealed that the LAS group harbored significantly larger cysts than the RAS group [10 (7, 15) cm vs. 8 (6, 12) cm, P = 0.011], indicating a more complex baseline anatomical condition in the LAS cohort. Clinical presentations varied substantially across patients, with abdominal pain being the most prevalent symptom (63%). Cysts were located in the greater and lesser omentum, the mesentery of the small intestine and colon, and the retroperitoneum (Table 1).

Comparations of clinical characteristics RAS group and LAS group

With respect to surgical approaches, the RAS group had a significantly higher number of complete intracorporeal cyst dissection procedures (33 vs. 13) and intracorporeal bowel resection and anastomosis (21 vs. 1). In comparison, the LAS group exhibited a greater proportion of extracorporeal cyst dissection (31 vs. 0) and extracorporeal bowel resection and anastomosis (32 vs. 3). The overall distribution of surgical techniques differed significantly between the two groups (P < 0.001). Regarding the extracorporeal procedures, one patient in the RAS group required conversion to open surgery, and two patients required extension of the umbilical incision; in the LAS group, 25 patients underwent open conversion, and 38 patients received extended umbilical incisions. Total operative time, postoperative fasting period, and postoperative hospital stay were comparable between the two cohorts. Despite similar median intraoperative blood loss, the RAS group demonstrated a significantly lower blood loss distribution than the LAS group [5 (2, 5) mL vs. 5 (2, 10) mL, P = 0.030]. The total hospitalization cost was significantly higher in the RAS group than in the LAS group [68286 (60183, 72551) yuan vs. 19261 (15651, 23869) yuan, P < 0.001]. Additionally, the LAS group had a significantly longer postoperative follow-up duration than the RAS group [39 (17, 66) months vs. 32 (17, 43) months, P = 0.019] (Table 2).

Analysis of postoperative complications in LAS and RAS groups (Table 3)

No intraoperative adjacent organ injury occurred in the RAS group, whereas two severe visceral injuries requiring surgical repair were documented in the LAS group. Both patients recovered uneventfully. The RAS group had three minor postoperative complications (two chylous fistulas, one encapsulated peritoneal effusion), all resolving with conservative management. The LAS group had two chylous fistulas requiring prolonged nutritional support, one intestinal obstruction requiring reoperation, and two cyst recurrences requiring secondary resection. No recurrence was observed in the RAS group.

Discussion

The clinical manifestations of ALMs vary greatly and lack pathognomonic signs, with their clinical presentation largely determined by lesion size and anatomical location within the abdomen [9, 34]. Clinical features also correlate with patient age: young infants and toddlers frequently present with abdominal distension and palpable abdominal masses, whereas older children are often asymptomatic[35, 36]. Acute abdominal symptoms, including abdominal pain, vomiting and fever, usually occur secondary to complications such as intracystic hemorrhage, infection, intestinal obstruction or intestinal volvulus [7, 9, 22]. In our cohort of 134 pediatric patients, 16% of cases were detected incidentally during routine physical examination or unrelated imaging tests, consistent with previous studies reporting the indolent clinical course of ALMs [7, 9, 17]. Abdominal pain constitutes the most common initial complaint, occurring in 63% of our patients. This result further indicates that ALMs should be considered a key differential diagnosis in pediatric patients with unexplained recurrent abdominal pain.

Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for initial screening, suitable for both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, and helps distinguish intra-abdominal from retroperitoneal lesions. However, its diagnostic efficacy is limited for large or deeply located cysts, often requiring further cross-sectional imaging. Both CT and MRI provide superior anatomical resolution, enabling precise localization and clear characterization of cyst contents and surrounding tissue involvement [11, 37].

Surgical planning depends primarily on lesion location. Omental cysts can generally be completely resected via laparoscopy without injuring adjacent organs, while mesenteric lesions frequently require concurrent bowel resection. For retroperitoneal malformations or lesions adjacent to major vessels, conventional laparoscopy is constrained by two-dimensional vision and limited instrument mobility. These limitations may lead to missed small lesions and impede precise dissection. Surgeons often need to make extended incisions for extracorporeal manipulation, which increases the risk of iatrogenic injury to vital structures [17, 18, 22].

The robotic surgical system provides a novel minimally invasive approach for managing such complex lesions. Its high-definition magnified visualization and three-dimensional stereoscopic imaging enable surgeons to clearly delineate the anatomical boundaries between the cyst wall and adjacent tissues. Combined with seven-degree-of-freedom wristed instruments—offering enhanced flexibility, precision, and physiological tremor filtration—surgeons can perform meticulous dissection and complete cyst resection within confined anatomical spaces without significantly prolonging operative duration [17, 18, 22]. These strengths are particularly evident when managing large cysts, allowing complete intracorporeal dissection and intracorporeal bowel resection with primary anastomosis. It avoids the extended incisions often required in standard laparoscopy due to poor surgical exposure [17, 18]. Overall, the robotic surgical system may offer two key benefits: first, eliminating the necessity for bowel exteriorization, and second, substantially reducing unnecessary bowel resection with anastomosis. These advancements may lower the risk of intraoperative iatrogenic injury and open conversion, as well as reduce postoperative complications such as intestinal adhesions, obstruction, and fistula.

Compared with LAS, the core advantage of RAS resides in achieving a higher proportion of total intracorporeal procedures—particularly in complex cases involving mesenteric root involvement, large cyst size, and dense adhesions to surrounding tissues. Based on the clinical experience of our institution, the primary intraoperative challenges in such scenarios include: (1) extremely limited intra-abdominal working space secondary to large cyst volume and the small abdominal cavity of young children; (2) dense adhesions between the cyst wall and intestinal loops, as well as mesenteric vasculature; and (3) blurred anatomical planes that increase the risk of misidentification. Consequently, conventional laparoscopic instruments face substantial limitations in performing multi-directional and multi-angled precise dissection within the constrained operative field [17].

To address these challenges, the key operative techniques for robotic surgery can be summarized as follows: First, we leverage three-dimensional stereoscopic visualization to establish clear anatomical planes, identify mesenteric root vessels, lymphatic ducts, and intestinal blood supply branches (Fig. 2B), accurately delineate the anatomical boundaries between the cyst and adjacent vital structures (Fig. 2C), and perform progressive dissection from the relatively free edge of the cyst toward its pedicle. Second, we utilize seven-degree-of-freedom wristed instruments to achieve precise tissue retraction and dissection, thereby avoiding cyst rupture or injury to blood vessels/lymphatic ducts caused by forceful traction. For densely adhesive regions, we adopt a “thin-layer progressive dissection technique”—skeletonize the vasculature (Fig. 2D and G), perform meticulous dissection to achieve complete cyst resection whenever feasible, and preserve a minimal amount of cyst wall tissue to protect intestinal blood supply when necessary, followed by electrocautery ablation of the residual tissue. When intestinal resection and anastomosis are indicated, the entire anastomotic procedure can be completed intracorporeally, eliminating the risks associated with extended abdominal incisions or extracorporeal bowel manipulation (Fig. 2H). Through the implementation of these strategies, robotic surgery ensures adequate cyst resection, maximizes preservation of mesenteric integrity and intestinal viability, reduces the risk of postoperative complications (e.g., hemorrhage, chylous fistula, intestinal necrosis), significantly enhances the success rate of total intracorporeal surgery, and optimizes the minimally invasive benefits for patients.

The findings of this study support this clinical advantage. The RAS group exhibited a significant superiority in the success rate of intracorporeal cyst dissection (58%), which was statistically higher than that of the LAS group (13%). Better intracorporeal manipulation helps preserve intestinal function and minimize intraoperative trauma. For complex cases in the RAS group, most operations were completed entirely intracorporeally, with few cases requiring incision extension or open conversion. This indicates that RAS delivers reliable minimally invasive performance for large ALMs, overcoming the drawbacks of conventional laparoscopy that frequently relies on extracorporeal tissue handling [33].

These technical differences were further reflected in perioperative complications: four complications were documented in the RAS group (two chylous fistulas, one encapsulated peritoneal effusion, one incomplete intestinal obstruction), all resolving with conservative management. In contrast, the LAS group experienced seven complications, including two intraoperative adjacent organ injuries (ureteral injury, combined pancreatic tail-splenic vessel laceration) with severe sequelae, two cyst recurrences requiring reoperation, and two chylous fistulas. This discrepancy is attributable to inherent laparoscopic limitations—two-dimensional imaging lacking depth perception (hindering identification of concealed structures like ureters) and straight rigid instruments with limited degrees of freedom and tremor amplification (compromising delicate dissection precision). The robotic system addresses these shortcomings via 3D high-definition imaging and stable, multi-articulated instruments. These technical merits are particularly valuable in the narrow abdominal cavity of children, helping reduce iatrogenic trauma and perioperative complications, which is consistent with previous studies [22, 25, 26, 32, 33].

Despite the above advantages, the robotic platform has obvious limitations. System setup traditionally takes extra time, though no significant difference in total operative time was found between the two groups in this cohort, in line with earlier reports [22, 25, 26, 33]. At our center, robotic system preparation currently takes 15–20 min on average. With the accumulation of surgical experience and progression along the learning curve, setup time is expected to decrease further.

In terms of postoperative recovery, the RAS group exhibited a slightly shorter postoperative hospital stay than the LAS group, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. The trend of shorter hospitalization further reflects the favorable minimally invasive recovery profile of robotic surgery. However, RAS presented distinct economic disadvantages. The total hospitalization cost in the RAS group was substantially higher than that in the LAS group, mainly attributed to robotic system usage fees, specialized consumables, and equipment maintenance expenses. The higher economic burden greatly limits the widespread and routine application of robotic surgery, especially in resource-limited medical centers. Therefore, conventional laparoscopy remains the cost-effective first choice for uncomplicated and simple ALMs, whereas RAS is more reasonably reserved for highly complex lesions that are technically challenging for standard laparoscopic manipulation.

This study has several inherent limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study. Surgical approaches were determined mainly by parental preference and economic considerations rather than random grouping, so selection bias cannot be fully excluded. In addition, baseline differences such as larger cyst size in the LAS group may also affect intergroup comparison outcomes. Second, all operations were performed by two attending surgeons, and individual operative experience and technical habits may have influenced clinical results. Third, the follow-up period was relatively short (minimum 5 months), which is insufficient for long-term outcome assessment including late intestinal obstruction and cyst recurrence. Further multicenter, prospective, randomized controlled trials with prolonged follow-up are required to generate high-level evidence for clinical practice.

In conclusion, RAS appears to be a safe and feasible minimally invasive approach for pediatric ALMs. Supported by 3D visualization and flexible articulating instruments, RAS is associated with a higher rate of complete intracorporeal procedures and successful cyst resection, as well as reduced intraoperative blood loss, milder tissue trauma and fewer postoperative complications. It shows particular value in intestinal preservation during minimally invasive surgery while maintaining surgical safety. For complex pediatric ALMs, RAS may serve as a favorable alternative to open surgery, though its higher cost limits widespread use in routine clinical practice.
