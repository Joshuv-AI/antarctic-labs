Stabilizing Oroxylin A Transfersomes by Caffeic Acid–Chitosan Functionalization for Superior Photoaging Protection
Authors: Huanhuan Wang, Mengjiao Li, Weijia Zhang, Junyang Zhu, Minglei Sun, Yuan Yan, Qiaoli Kong, Xueyan Zhang, Jinlong Zong, Xue Liu, Xibei Zhang, Yan Liu, Qingmei Xia, Nan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ultraviolet (UV)-radiation-induced intracellular oxidative stress is a key mechanism driving skin photoaging. Nanodelivery systems employed to alleviate photoaging of the skin have been hindered in clinical application due to their inherent instability and functional limitations. To address this issue, we prepared caffeic acid-grafted chitosan (CA-g-CS). Subsequently, oroxylin A-loaded transfersomes were modified with CA-g-CS to obtain CA-g-CS-coated oroxylin A-loaded transfersomes (CA-g-CS-OA-TFs). In vitro studies demonstrated that this system significantly enhanced the transferosome stability and produced a synergistic antioxidant effect. The modification of CA-g-CS markedly suppressed lipid oxidation, increasing the DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacities to 89.71% and 72.39%, respectively. Furthermore, it improved OA transdermal delivery and suppressed UVB-induced HEK cell senescence. In vivo, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs, with their enhanced stability and synergistic antioxidant activity, significantly restored skin barrier function, attenuated collagen and elastic fiber damage, downregulated senescence-associated genes, and relieved oxidative stress in mice with UV-induced photoaging. Collectively, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs represent a promising nanodelivery strategy for superior photoaging protection.

Introduction

Functioning as the primary protective barrier of the human body, the skin defends against ultraviolet (UV) radiation. − Prolonged UV exposure induces excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, disrupting redox balance and inhibiting endogenous antioxidant enzymes (e.g., peroxidase, glutathione reductase). , This process not only impairs skin barrier function (elevated transepidermal water loss, reduced hydration) but also accelerates the degradation of collagen and elastic fibers, ultimately leading to dryness, desquamation, and wrinkle formation, , which represent the core pathological features of skin photoaging.

Oroxylin A (OA), a flavonoid primarily isolated from Scutellaria baicalensis, exhibits a diversity of pharmacological actions including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory impacts. − It effectively alleviates UV-induced skin photoaging in mice by reducing oxidative stress, which is mediated via activation of the ERK1/2-Nrf2-ARE and Sirt1/Nrf2 signaling pathways. Our previous work demonstrated that OA transfersomes (OA-TFs) can effectively penetrate the stratum corneum barrier due to their excellent deformability, thereby enabling efficient delivery of OA into deeper skin layers and significantly enhancing transdermal efficiency. However, the phospholipid components in these vesicles are susceptible to oxidation and degradation during storage, − which leads to particle aggregation and sedimentation, consequently compromising the stability of the transfersomes. ,

Constructing a protective layer on the surface of TFs is an effective strategy to improve their stability. Chitosan (CS) adheres to phospholipid membranes through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonds, creating a protective film on the TF surfaces. − This film mitigates oxidative phospholipid decomposition and improves nanocarrier stability. Nevertheless, CS utilization is severely constrained because it dissolves only under acidic conditions. Chemical modification can improve its water solubility, and the abundance of free amino groups in CS provides potential sites for surface functionalization. As a polyphenolic compound, caffeic acid (CA) contains an oxidized quinone structure that can react with the highly active amino groups of CS. Caffeic acid-grafted chitosan (CA-g-CS) was designed as a multifunctional modifier: it overcomes CS’s poor water solubility via CA grafting, enabling stable surface binding to TFs through electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding; CA’s phenolic hydroxyl groups scavenge free radicals and inhibit lipid peroxidation, while its ability to downregulate UVB-induced MAPK/NF-κB pathways synergizes with OA’s Nrf2-mediated antioxidant effects.

In this study, transfersomes of the antiphotoaging drug OA were fabricated using the thin-film dispersion technique, followed by surface modification with caffeic acid-grafted chitosan to enhance the system’s stability and integrate synergistic antioxidant capacity, thus developing a novel OA nanodelivery system (CA-g-CS-OA-TFs). The in vitro antiphotoaging effect of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was evaluated using HEK cells, and the in vivo skin photoaging effect of this system was investigated through animal experiments.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Chlorogenic acid (CA, 99% pure) and potassium persulfate came from Meryer Biochemical Tech. Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Chitosan (CS, deacetylation degree >95%, viscosity 500 mPa·s) was supplied by Macklin Biochemical Tech. Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. DPPH, ABTS, and Tween 80 were provided by Yuanye Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Oroxylin A (OA, 98% purity) was obtained from Puyi Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China. 1-(3-(Dimethylamino)­propyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) was sourced from Dibai Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Soybean lecithin (SPC), hydrogenated lecithin (HSPC), and sodium (2S)-2,3-dihydroxypropyl 2,3-dihydroxypropyl phosphate (EPG) were supplied by AVT Pharmaceutical Tech. Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Human epidermal keratinocytes (HEK) were procured from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA). DMEM and PBS were sourced from Gibco BRL (Gaithersburg, MD, USA). SA-β-Gal staining kit was supplied by Solarbio (Beijing, China).

Synthesis and Identification of CA-g-CS

CA-g-CS was prepared according to the method described by Wang et al. with some minor modifications. In brief, CS (0.20 g) was dissolved in 1% HCl, and the pH was adjusted to 5.0. An ethanol solution of CA (0.10 g) was added, followed by rapid mixing with an aqueous ethanol solution of EDC (0.15 g). The mixture was dialyzed (MWCO: 3500 Da) against an acetic acid solution (pH 5.0) for 48 h with frequent solution changes and then freeze-dried to obtain CA-g-CS.

The samples were characterized using UV–vis spectroscopy (UV-6100, Shanghai Mapada, China), FT-IR analysis (Tensonor27, Bruker, Germany), 1H NMR (JEOL, Bruker, Germany), XRD (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany), and TGA (TGA550, TA, American).

Assessment of Aqueous Solubility Enhancement

The water solubility of CS and CA-g-CS was measured by following the procedure of Huang et al. and making slight modifications. An excess amount of CS (30.5 mg, M 0) and CA-g-CS (31.8 mg, M 0) was accurately weighed and dissolved separately in 10 mL of deionized water. After stirring for 24 h to reach dissolution equilibrium, saturated aqueous solutions of CS and CA-g-CS were obtained. The saturated solutions were centrifuged at 10 000 rpm for 10 min. Preparation and Characterization of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs

OA transfersomes (OA-TFs) were prepared by the thin-film hydration method. A lipid film composed of soybean phosphatidylcholine, hydrogenated soybean phosphatidylcholine, egg phosphatidylglycerol, Tween 80, and OA was formed via rotary evaporation and hydrated with PBS (pH 7.4). The suspension was probe-sonicated and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. For coating, the OA-TFs were added to an equal volume of CA-g-CS solution (0.2% w/v) under stirring to obtain CA-g-CS-OA-TFs. Blank formulations (TFs and CA-g-CS-TFs) were prepared accordingly. All samples were stored at 4 °C.

CA-g-CS-OA-TFs were appropriately diluted with deionized water and analyzed at 25 °C using a Zetasizer Nano ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). The average particle size, PDI, and Zeta potential were recorded.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading capacity (DL) of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs were determined as follows. An accurately measured volume of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was mixed with methanol and sonicated for 30 min to disrupt the vesicles. After centrifugation, the supernatant was obtained to quantify the total OA content (W 0). To determine the free OA content (W 1), an equal volume of the formulation was subjected to ultrafiltration using centrifugal filter tubes (5000 rpm, 20 min). The upper liquid was discarded, and 20% v/v ethanol solution was added, followed by a second ultrafiltration step. The resulting filtrate was collected for analysis. W s is the total amount of nanocarrier material. The amount of OA was quantified via HPLC on an Agilent 1200 system, employing isocratic elution with 0.1% aqueous formic acid and acetonitrile (35:65 v/v) and UV detection at 272 nm. The EE and DL were derived from the following equations: EE=W0−W1W0×100% DL=W0−W1Ws×100% CA-g-CS-OA-TFs were characterized using FT-IR analysis (Tensonor27, Bruker, Germany), XRD (D8 ADVANCE, Bruker, Germany), TGA (TGA550, TA, American), and transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1200EX, Japan).

Evaluation of In Vitro Drug Release and Skin Permeation

The release behavior under in vitro conditions for free OA, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs, and OA-TFs was assessed via a dialysis membrane technique. In brief, each formulation (2 mL) was transferred into preactivated dialysis bags (MWCO: 3500 Da). After sealing, the bags were submerged in 12 mL of a release medium composed of phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) containing 0.5% w/v Tween 80. The entire system was maintained at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 100 rpm. At 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h, 1 mL aliquots of the external medium were removed and promptly exchanged with an equivalent volume of fresh medium. OA content was assayed using HPLC, and cumulative release percentage was calculated and plotted over time.

After removing hair and subcutaneous tissue, the abdominal skin of SD rats was mounted on Franz diffusion cells with an effective diffusion area of 1.77 cm2. The donor compartment received free OA solution or CA-g-CS-OA-TFs solution, while the receptor chamber was filled with 30% ethanol aqueous solution. The system was held at 37 °C with agitation at 600 rpm. At fixed time intervals, 2 mL of the receptor solution was withdrawn and substituted with an equal volume of new medium. The OA content was quantified to calculate the cumulative transdermal amount.

After the transdermal study, the skin was carefully rinsed to remove residual formulation, cut into small pieces, and homogenized in a normal saline. The homogenate underwent acetonitrile extraction and was centrifuged to obtain a supernatant. The OA content in the supernatant was quantified, and the total retention of skin was calculated.

Layered skin analysis is a crucial method for assessing drug delivery efficiency in transdermal administration. To further determine the retention of OA in different skin layers, skin samples were collected 24 h after transdermal administration and analyzed sequentially by tape stripping and heat separation following the method of Li et al. Briefly, a strip of tape was adhered to the skin’s surface, held under compression for ten seconds, and then removed; OA adhered to the tape was dissolved in methanol for quantitative analysis. After 30 cycles of tape stripping, the skin specimens were subjected to thermal delamination: a hairdryer was used to warm the tissue to 50 °C for 60 s, after which the epidermis was scraped off with a scalpel to separate it from the underlying dermis. Finally, OA in each skin layer was extracted and quantified using the method described above.

Good deformability allows the formulation to traverse interstices smaller than its own diameter, enhancing transdermal delivery efficiency. The relative deformability of conventional OA liposomes (OA-Lips) and CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was evaluated following the method of Chaudhary et al. In short, vesicles were forced through a 50 nm pore-sized polycarbonate filter using an externally driven liposome extruder. For a duration of 5 min, the volume of the resulting extruded dispersion was recorded. Additionally, particle sizes were assessed both prior to and subsequent to the filtration step. The extent of vesicle deformability was then computed using the following equation: D=J(Rv50)2 where D is the deformability index of the vesicle membrane, J is the volume of suspension extruded within 5 min, and R v is the vesicle size after filtration.

Stability Assessment

The average particle size, PDI, and zeta potential of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and OA-TFs were studied for 21 days at 4 °C conditions.

Antioxidant Performance

The antioxidant activities of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and OA-TFs were evaluated by determining their scavenging capacities against the DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion radicals.

The DPPH radical scavenging assay was performed according to the method described by Higashi et al. , with slight modifications. Briefly, 0.1 mL of each sample at different concentrations (0.5–10.0 mg/mL) was mixed with 0.1 mL of freshly prepared DPPH ethanol solution (0.2 mM). After incubation in the dark for 30 min, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm.

The ABTS radical scavenging assay was adapted from the protocol described by Yan et al. Briefly, equal volumes of 7 mM ABTS and 2.45 mM potassium persulfate were mixed and then incubated in the dark for 12 h to prepare an ABTS+ stock solution. This stock was subsequently diluted with PBS to reach an absorbance of 0.60–0.70 at 734 nm, yielding the working solution. Thereafter, 200 μL of the ABTS+ working solution was combined with 20 μL of sample (0.5–10.0 mg/mL), and the absorbance was measured at 734 nm.

Superoxide anion radicals, a major reactive oxygen species generated during oxidative stress, was also evaluated. Briefly, 1 mL of sample (0.5–10.0 μg/mL) was combined with 2 mL of Tris buffer (0.5 mM, pH 8.0) and held at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 0.1 mL of pyrogallol (7 mM) was supplemented, and the absorbance was recorded at 320 nm. The scavenging rate was calculated as scavengingrate=(1−As−AbAc)×100% where A s represents the absorbance of the experimental group, A c the absorbance of the control group, and A b the absorbance of the mixture without any sample.

In addition, DPPH radical scavenging assays were separately performed on CA, OA, and the combination of CA and OA at the same formulation ratio as that used in the transfersome preparation, following the experimental procedures described above.

Biocompatibility of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs

The biocompatibility of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was evaluated in vitro using a red blood cell hemolysis assay. Fresh blood was collected from rabbits, and red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated and reconstituted as a 2% v/v suspension in physiological saline. The RBC suspension was mixed separately at a 1:1 ratio with physiological saline (negative control), deionized water (positive control), and CA-g-CS-OA-TFs at various concentrations (2.7–13.5 μg/mL). After incubation at 37 °C for 4 h, hemagglutination and hemolysis were visually inspected. Subsequently, the mixtures were spun for 10 min at 1500 rpm, and the optical density of the collected supernatant was recorded at 540 nm. The percentage of hemolysis was then computed using the formula below: hemolysisrate=As−AnAp−An×100% where A s denotes the absorbance of the sample group, A n the absorbance of the negative control group, and A p the absorbance of the positive control group.

UVB-Induced Cellular Senescence Model and Therapeutic Intervention

Effects on the UVB-Induced HEK Cell Model

After HEK cells adhered and reached 70–80% confluence, they were pretreated with different concentrations of the samples. The experimental groups included a control group and a UVB model group. Following 24 h of incubation, the UVB group and sample-treated groups were exposed to UVB irradiation at a dose of 60 mJ/cm2, while the control group remained unexposed. After an additional 24 h of culture, the supernatant was discarded and the cells were washed with PBS. Cell viability was then assessed by using the CCK-8 assay.

Cellular Oxidative Stress Levels

HEK cell suspensions were first subjected to ultrasonic disruption. The resultant lysate was then employed to quantify malondialdehyde (MDA) levels as well as the enzymatic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) using corresponding commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). All measurements were recorded with a microplate reader.

Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase Staining

Senescence of cells was evaluated with SA-β-Gal staining kits following the manufacturer’s instructions. For each well, six randomly chosen microscopic fields were captured under bright-field illumination. In every field, the total cell count and the number of cells showing blue staining were determined.

Reverse-Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent was used to isolate total RNA from cells that had been treated. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA employing a cDNA reverse transcription kit. The cDNA was then amplified using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master in separate PCR reactions. mRNA expression levels were normalized to those of ACTB or GAPDH. The primer sequences used were as follows: ACTB sense, 5′-CCT­GGC­ACC­CAG­CAC­AAT-3′; ACTB antisense, 5′-GGG­CCG­GAC­TCG­TCG­TCA­TAC-3′; p16 sense, 5′-CGT­GGA­CCT­GGC­TGA­GGAG-3′; p16 antisense, 5′-CAT­CTA­TGC­GGG­CAT­GGTT­ACTG-3′; p21 sense, 5′-CCT­GCC­GCC­GCCT­CTTC-3′; p21 antisense, 5′-CTC­CAG­TGG­TGT­CGG­TGAC-3′; GAPDH sense, 5′-GGT­TGT­CTC­CTG­CGA­TTCA-3′; GAPDH antisense, 5′-TGG­TCC­AGG­GTT­TCT­TAC­TCC-3′.

Amelioration of UV-Induced Skin Photoaging In Vivo

ICR mice, aged 4 weeks and specific pathogen-free (SPF), were acquired from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (license SCXK (Beijing) 2021-0006) for this investigation. All animal handling protocols received approval from the Animal Ethics Committee at Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval TCM-LAEC2024127m1021). The mice were then randomly divided into six groups: Control, Model, Vitamin E (VE), CA-g-CS-OA-TFs, OA-TFs, and OA solution. After dorsal hair removal in mice from each group, all except the Control group received UV irradiation (UVA cumulative dose: 94.5 J/cm2, UVB cumulative dose: 9.45 J/cm2). The irradiation lasted for 9 weeks, with exposure administered 5 days per week followed by 1-day rest intervals, and the dose was progressively increased each week. Concurrently with modeling, the corresponding formulations were topically applied. Successful model establishment was confirmed by the presence of characteristic skin lesions, which demonstrated significant roughness, dryness, stiffness, and wrinkling.

Transepidermal water loss (TEWL), skin density, and skin hydration were measured using a Cutometer MPA 4 (CK Electronic GmbH, Germany). Dorsal images of the mice were captured with a tissue viability imager (TiVi 700, WheelsBridge AB, Sweden), and wrinkle formation was analyzed.

Skin tissue samples were taken for paraffin embedding and sectioning. The sections were stained with H&E (to observe tissue structure), Masson (to assess collagen fibers), and Weigert’s (to observe elastic fibers), respectively.

The levels of MDA and the enzymic activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and CAT in serum and skin tissues as well as GSH-Px activity in skin were determined using commercial assay kits. The hydroxyproline (HYP) content in skin was also measured with a commercial kit to evaluate collagen levels.

Skin tissue RNA was extracted from the mice, and the expression of skin-aging-related genes was detected according to the method described in “2.9.4”.

The primer sequences used were as follows: P16 sense, 5′-CAG­GTG­ATG­ATG­ATG­GGC­AACG-3′; P16 antisense, 5′-CGG­GCG­GGA­GAA­GGT­AGTG-3′; P21 sense, 5′-CCG­TGG­ACA­GTG­AGCA­GTTG-3′; P21 antisense, 5′-CCT­CCA­GCG­GCGT­CTCC-3′; GAPDH sense, 5′-GGT­TGT­CTC­CTG­CGA­TTCA-3′; GAPDH antisense, 5′-TGG­TCC­AGG­GTT­TCT­TAC­TCC-3′.

Data and Statistical Analysis

Differences between treatment conditions were evaluated by using a one-way ANOVA, adopting a significance level of P = 0.05. A minimum of three independent replicates were conducted for each experiment, with the outcomes presented in the figures.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of CA-g-CS with Improved Water Solubility

CA-g-CS was successfully synthesized through a covalent coupling reaction. Comprehensive characterization techniques confirmed the success of the grafting reaction and revealed consequent improvements in water solubility. The FT-IR spectrum of CA-g-CS (Figure S1) exhibited a significant weakening or disappearance of the absorption at 1596 cm–1, consistent with the consumption of primary amine groups (−NH2) during covalent coupling. Additional weakening at 1420 and 1323 cm–1 further supports the reaction between the −NH2 groups of CS and the phenolic hydroxyl groups (−OH) of CA. Simultaneously, a new absorption peak appeared at 1558 cm–1, attributed to the CC stretching vibration of the CA skeleton. Crucially, these spectral changes were absent in the physical mixture, confirming covalent grafting rather than physical adsorption. The 1H NMR results provided further evidence (Figure B). Five new proton signals appeared in the range of 6.0–7.4 ppm in the CA-g-CS spectrum, corresponding to the aromatic protons of the grafted CA (labeled C-h, C-e, C-f, C-b, and C-g). This confirmed the successful grafting of the CA structure onto the CS backbone. UV–vis (Figure C) showed that CS itself had no absorption peaks in the range of 220–550 nm, while CA-g-CS exhibited characteristic absorption peaks at 248 and 331 nm. Compared to the absorption peaks of free CA (217 and 324 nm), obvious red shifts were observed, indicating changes in the conjugated system within the benzene ring and an increase in electron cloud density after grafting, which further demonstrated the success of the conjugation reaction. XRD analysis (Figure D) was employed to investigate how CA grafting affects the physical structure of CS. The diffraction peak of CA-g-CS shifted from 19.7° for pure CS to 20.8°, accompanied by peak broadening and reduced intensity. This indicated that the introduction of CA disrupted the intermolecular hydrogen bonds in CS, reducing its crystallinity and structural orderliness, which was consistent with the FT-IR and 1H NMR results. Thermal analysis (Figure E,F) showed that the thermal behavior of CA-g-CS was intermediate between that of CA and CS. Its maximum decomposition temperature (310.13 °C) was lower than that of CS (366.52 °C), indicating decreased thermal stability. This was ascribed to the introduction of phenolic hydroxyl groups disrupting the regularity of the CS molecular chains, reducing crystallinity and hydrogen bond density, and leading to a more loosened structure. This was consistent with the XRD results, collectively proving that CA was successfully grafted onto the CS molecular chains.

Crucially, the structural modification confers a dramatic enhancement in the water solubility. The water solubility of CA-g-CS was determined to be 82.08% (2.6 mg/mL), a substantial increase over the 6.56% (0.2 mg/mL) solubility of unmodified CS. This was attributed to two factors: on one hand, the introduction of additional hydrophilic groups (carboxylic acid and hydroxyl groups) from CA enhanced the hydration capacity; on the other hand, grafting CA inserted hydrophobic benzene rings between CS chains, disrupting the hydrogen bonding arrangement, making the molecular chains looser and more easily penetrated by water molecules.

Enhanced Stability and Synergistic Antioxidant Effect of OA-TFs by CA-g-CS Coating

The OA-TFs were successfully coated with CA-g-CS to form CA-g-CS-OA-TFs. The resulting dispersion was homogeneous and transparent, exhibiting a faint blue opalescence and a distinct Tyndall effect (Figure S1), which are typical characteristic of a nanoscale colloidal system. Dynamic light scattering measurements revealed: a particle size of 85.74 ± 0.71 nm, a low PDI of 0.269 ± 0.004, and a positive zeta potential of +32.6 ± 1.91 mV (Figure B,C). The EE and DL were 81.28 ± 1.23% and 1.57 ± 0.41%, respectively. Compared to unmodified OA-TFs (Figure S2), the increased particle size and the reversal of zeta potential from negative to positive confirm the successful surface coating with CA-g-CS, attributed to electrostatic interaction between the protonated amino groups (−NH3 +) of CS and the negatively charged phosphate groups (−PO4 –) of phospholipids. The highly positive zeta potential further enhanced the colloidal stability via electrostatic repulsion. TEM imaging revealed that CA-g-CS-OA-TFs adopted a spherical or near-spherical morphology with a roughened surface (Figure D), consistent with the presence of a CA-g-CS coating.

FT-IR and XRD provided structural evidence for the successful encapsulation of OA and the coating with CA-g-CS. The FT-IR spectrum of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs showed characteristic absorption bands of phospholipids and a red-shifted amide I band of CS. Notably, the characteristic peaks of free OA were absent in CA-g-CS-OA-TFs but remained clearly visible in the physical mixture, indicating that OA was encapsulated within the transfersomes rather than simply adsorbed onto the surface (Figure E). XRD further confirmed that the crystalline diffraction peaks of OA disappeared in CA-g-CS-OA-TFs, indicating an amorphous state of the drug within the carrier. The broad peak at 20.9° corresponded to CA-g-CS, confirming its presence on the TF surface (Figure F). Thermal analysis (TGA/DTG) confirmed the significantly improved thermal stability of OA after encapsulation. Pure OA began to volatilize rapidly around 200 °C, while the thermal decomposition curve of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs exhibited a two-stage profile: the CA-g-CS decomposition stage (25–290 °C) and the phospholipid degradation stage (290–510 °C), with no obvious OA volatilization stage observed. In contrast, the physical mixture still exhibited the independent thermal behavior of its individual components, confirming that the transfersome structure effectively protected OA and that this enhanced stability could not be achieved by simple mixing (Figure G,H).

The in vitro release profile showed that CA-g-CS-OA-TFs achieved a cumulative release of 70.38% within 24 h, which was lower than that of OA-TFs (80.99%) but significantly higher than free OA (36.02%). This demonstrates the ability of TFs to promote drug release. The slower release rate from CA-g-CS-OA-TFs is likely due to the protective CA-g-CS coating, which connects with phospholipid molecules via hydrogen bonding on the transfersome surface, restricting the fluidity of the phospholipid layer and thereby retarding drug release and leakage (Figure I). To investigate whether Tween 80 in the release medium disrupts the structural integrity of transfersomes, we incubated OA-TFs and CA-g-CS-OA-TFs in PBS containing 0.5% Tween 80 for 48 h. The particle size and PDI of both formulations remained unchanged, indicating that the vesicle structure remained stable during the release experiment and that the release medium did not compromise transfersome integrity (Figure S3). In addition, in vitro release assays were performed with reduced Tween 80 concentrations. The results showed that both OA solution and CA-g-CS-OA-TFs exhibited slower release rates and lower cumulative release (Figure S4). Given that reducing Tween 80 concentration also affects sink conditions, the stability data support that the observed release profiles reflect true drug release rather than vesicle disintegration. Skin permeation experiments showed that the transfersomes significantly enhanced the cumulative permeation amount of OA over 24 h: CA-g-CS-OA-TFs reached 19.66 ± 2.41 μg/cm2, while free OA was only 7.99 ± 0.58 μg/cm2, representing an increase of approximately 2.5-fold (Figure J). In addition, transfersomes also increased the skin retention of OA (Figure S5). The results of retention in different skin layers showed that CA-g-CS-OA-TFs exhibited significantly higher retention in the epidermis compared to free OA, and OA was also detectable in the dermis. In contrast, due to its poor water solubility, free OA was mainly retained in the stratum corneum (SC), with limited distribution in the epidermis and almost undetectable in the dermis (Figure K). Notably, the retention of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs in the epidermis was significantly higher than that in the dermis, which could be attributed to the electrostatic interaction between the positively charged transfersomes and negatively charged epidermal cells, as well as the hydrogen bonds and π–π interactions formed by the catechol structure of caffeic acid with cells, thereby collectively enhancing the adhesion of transfersomes in the epidermis. Furthermore, the relative deformability experiment demonstrated that the deformability index of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was 3.25 times that of conventional rigid liposomes (OA-Lip) (Figure S6). These results indicate that CA-g-CS-OA-TFs significantly enhance the transdermal permeation of OA by virtue of their excellent deformability.

The CA-g-CS coating conferred excellent colloidal stability. Over 21 days of storage, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs maintained consistent particle size, PDI, and zeta potential, while unmodified OA-TFs showed visible precipitation after 9 days (Figure L–N).

Finally, the CA-g-CS coating endowed a synergistic antioxidant effect. CA-g-CS-OA-TFs exhibited superior, concentration-dependent scavenging capacity against DPPH, ABTS, and superoxide anion radicals compared to OA-TFs. The DPPH scavenging rate reached 89.71%, approximately 2.3 times that of OA-TFs, and it also showed superior antioxidant performance in the ABTS assay. Notably, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs achieved nearly complete scavenging of superoxide anion radicals even at a low concentration (2.5 μg/mL). These results indicate a synergistic antioxidant effect between the encapsulated OA and the CA-g-CS polymer coating (Figure O–Q). Furthermore, the radical scavenging activity of the CA+OA combination (94.35 ± 0.29%) was higher than that of CA (49.82 ± 1.10%) or OA (35.32 ± 1.26%) alone, suggesting a potential synergistic trend between the two compounds (Figure S7).

CA-g-CS-OA-TFs Inhibit UVB-Induced Senescence in HEK Cells and Alleviate Oxidative Damage

CA-g-CS-OA-TFs demonstrated superior efficacy in mitigating UVB-induced photodamage in the HEK cells. UVB irradiation significantly reduced cell viability, which was effectively restored by treatment with CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and, to a lesser extent, with OA-TFs (Figure A). The CA-g-CS-OA-TFs also exhibited a stronger capacity to scavenge intracellular ROS induced by UVB (Figure B,C). These advantages may be attributed to the CA-g-CS coating, which imparts higher stability to the transfersomes, reducing the premature drug leakage in the cell culture environment. This allows for a more effective restoration of cell viability and clearance of UVB-induced excess ROS. Furthermore, the enhanced antioxidant activity benefits from the synergistic effect between CA and OA-TFs in the antioxidant activity.

The SA-β-Gal staining results further support the above conclusions (Figure D,E). CA-g-CS-OA-TFs were more effective than OA-TFs in mitigating UVB-induced cellular senescence. Gene expression analysis (Figure F,G) revealed that UVB irradiation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of the senescence-associated genes P16 and P21. Both 0.1 μg/mL CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and OA-TFs effectively inhibited this process, with CA-g-CS-OA-TFs performing more notably, consistent with the SA-β-Gal staining results. This further confirms that CA-g-CS modification enhances transfersome stability, reduces the degradation of active ingredients, and thereby more persistently and effectively inhibits the cellular senescence process.

CA-g-CS-OA-TFs Effectively Mitigate UV-Induced Skin Barrier Damage and Structural Disruption in Mice

The therapeutic efficacy of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs was evaluated in a murine model of UV-induced skin photoaging. All treatment groups showed a continuous increase in body weight, indicating no systemic toxicity (Figure B). Assessment of skin barrier function revealed that CA-g-CS-OA-TFs effectively reversed UV-induced damage, showing superior restoration of TEWL, skin hydration, and skin density compared to the OA-TFs group (Figure C–E). The efficacy of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs in repairing the skin barrier was comparable to that of the positive control (VE) group.

A macroscopic evaluation showed that UV irradiation caused dry, rough skin with visible erythema and wrinkles in the model group. Topical application of CA-g-CS-OA-TFs resulted in the most significant improvement among the treatment groups, nearly restoring the skin to a healthy state, while the free OA solution showed a minimal effect (Figure F). Quantitative wrinkle assessment revealed that UV irradiation led to decreased skin elasticity and deepened wrinkles. Both CA-g-CS-OA-TF and OA-TF treatments significantly improved wrinkle depth and volume, effectively alleviating UV-induced damage, with CA-g-CS-OA-TF showing a superior trend (Figure G–I).

Histopathological analysis provided microscopic evidence of the therapeutic effects. H&E staining (Figure J,K) showed that UV exposure caused significant and uneven epidermal thickening in the Model group. CA-g-CS-OA-TFs treatment significantly reduced epidermal thickness, with efficacy similar to the VE group and superior to the OA-TFs group. Masson’s and Weigert’s staining (Figure L–N) showed neatly arranged and uniformly distributed collagen and elastic fibers in the Control group, while the Model group exhibited disordered and fragmented fibers, accompanied by a significant decrease in the OD value of collagen fibers. Compared to the Model group, both the CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and OA-TFs groups showed reduced fiber breakage and a significant recovery in collagen content.

In summary, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs demonstrated excellent efficacy in repairing the skin barrier, improving epidermal structure, and promoting the regeneration of collagen and elastic fibers. The presumed origins of these benefits lie in the protective CA-g-CS layer, which bolsters nanocarrier stability, curtails leakage and release of the drug, and thereby decelerates its in vivo clearance. This interpretation is corroborated by skin permeation and in vitro release data. Consequently, it enables a more sustained and effective antiphotoaging effect in vivo.

CA-g-CS-OA-TFs Alleviate Skin Photoaging In Vivo by Reducing UVB-Induced Oxidative Stress and Senescence-Associated Genes

To explore the antioxidant mechanism, key oxidative stress markers in skin tissue were evaluated. Figure A–D reveals that, relative to the Control group, the Model group following UVB exposure displayed a substantial elevation in MDA levels and notable reductions in the enzymatic activities of SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px. Treatment with CA-g-CS-OA-TFs effectively reversed these alterations, significantly reducing MDA levels and restoring the activities of these antioxidant enzymes. The OA-TFs group also showed some regulatory effects, but these were weaker than those in the CA-g-CS-OA-TFs group. Oxidative stress indicators in serum more broadly reflect the systemic oxidative stress status. A similar regulatory trend on these oxidative stress markers was observed in serum, indicating a systemic antioxidant effect (Figure E–G).

UV radiation can induce the expression of matrix metalloproteinases, promote collagen degradation, and consequently reduce the content of its characteristic amino acid HYP. The effect on collagen degradation was assessed by measuring the HYP levels. The skin HYP content in the model mice was markedly lower than that in the control mice, suggesting a substantial collagen loss. Treatment with CA-g-CS-OA-TFs effectively mitigated this decrease, demonstrating its antiphotoaging capacity (Figure H).

At the molecular level, the expression of key senescence-associated genes, p21 and p16, was analyzed. UVB irradiation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression levels of p21 and p16 in the skin of the model group. Notably, topical application of both CA-g-CS-OA-TFs and OA-TFs significantly downregulated the mRNA levels of these two genes, with CA-g-CS-OA-TFs exhibiting a stronger trend, indicating their potent antiaging potential (Figure I,J).

In summary, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs demonstrated superior in vivo efficacy compared to OA-TFs in combating oxidative stress and downregulating senescence markers, consistent with our previous cellular study findings. This enhanced antiphotoaging performance is attributed to the improved stability of the nanocarrier and the synergistic antioxidant effect between CA-g-CS and OA.

Conclusions

In this study, an OA-loaded transfersome system modified with CA-g-CS was developed. Covalent grafting of CA onto CS was confirmed, significantly improving the polymer’s water solubility. The CA-g-CS coating stabilized the transfersomes via electrostatic interactions, resulting in enhanced colloidal stability and synergistic antioxidant effects, as shown by free radical scavenging experiment. In a UV-induced photoaging mouse model, CA-g-CS-OA-TFs improved skin barrier function and mitigated collagen and elastic fiber damage. The formulation exerted antioxidant and antiphotoaging effects by reducing MDA levels, increasing SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px activities, and downregulating p16 and p21 mRNA expression.

Unlike previous studies that employed polyphenol-grafted chitosan-coated rigid liposomes , or other polyphenol-based nanocarriers, our work focuses on deformable transfersomes. The high deformability of transfersomes endows them with superior capacity to penetrate the stratum corneum, making them particularly suitable for skin drug delivery against photoaging. However, the preparation of transfersomes is technically more challenging than that of conventional liposomes, and they are prone to drug leakage. To address this, we coated the transfersomes with CA-g-CS. This coating not only improves the structural integrity of the transfersomes and prevents premature leakage of the encapsulated OA but also overcomes the acid-dependent solubility limitation of chitosan due to the excellent water solubility of CA-g-CS, thereby enabling a uniform and biocompatible shell. Importantly, CA in the shell may exert synergistic antioxidant effects with OA in the core, jointly combating photoaging. Nevertheless, the preliminary DPPH data obtained in this study are insufficient to fully elucidate the synergistic antioxidant mechanism between the CA and OA. Further in-depth mechanistic investigations are, therefore, necessary to confirm and characterize the nature of this synergy. Accordingly, we plan to conduct additional mechanistic studies in our future work to comprehensively explore the synergistic interplay between the CA and OA.

In conclusion, the developed CA-g-CS-OA-TFs represent a promising delivery system characterized by excellent stability, remarkable antioxidant activity, and efficient skin permeation, demonstrating a strong potential for mitigating UV-induced skin photoaging.
