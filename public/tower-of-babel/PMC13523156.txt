Mortality risk prediction from dense PICU data from patients with suspected infection: data-derived physiological trajectories outperform expert assessments when temporal resolution is high
Authors: Tom Velez, Oluwakemi Badaki-Makun, Danielle Hirsch, Danielle Claire Mercurio, Holly Depinet, Maya Dewan, Rishikesan Kamaleswaran, Jocelyn Grunwell, Maria Triantafyllou, Nora Wolff, Fehima Abdelrahman, Charles Macias, Ioannis Koutroulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Early identification of mortality risk in critically ill children with suspected infection remains challenging. Whether severity scores (SIRS, pSOFA, Phoenix) add incremental value beyond data-derived physiologic features in machine-learning models is uncertain. We compared the predictive value of physiologic dynamics, treatment features, and continuous laboratory trends expert-derived severity representations for 24-hour PICU mortality prediction, and derived a parsimonious, interpretable model.Study Design:We analyzed 3310 PICU encounters (107 deaths, 3.2% mortality) using first-24-hour data. A staged, domain-structured feature selection strategy was used to build sequential XGBoost models evaluated via 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Performance was assessed by AUROC, AUPRC, Brier score, and specificity at 80% sensitivity, with SHAP-guided pruning at each stage.Results:Vital sign extrema provided baseline discrimination. Incorporation of variability and slope features improved calibration and specificity. Vasopressor and supplemental oxygen timing and intensity features produced substantial gains in AUROC and AUPRC. Continuous laboratory extrema and temporal trends reflecting evolving organ dysfunction yielded further improvement. Expert-threshold-derived metrics, including SIRS, pSOFA, and Phoenix composite scores, did not improve performance beyond data-derived features.Conclusion:Routinely collected hourly PICU data from the first-24-hour are sufficient for XGBoost to extract deterioration patterns strongly predictive of early mortality, without requiring severity thresholds.

Introduction

Early risk stratification for mortality in critically ill children with a suspected infection remains a central challenge in pediatric intensive care. Although overall sepsis-associated mortality in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) is relatively low, early identification of children at elevated risk within the first 24 hours of admission is clinically valuable for triage, escalation of care, and resource allocation [1]. Contemporary PICU environments generate dense, time-resolved electronic health record (EHR) data streams that include frequent vital signs, repeated laboratory assessments, and detailed treatment records [2]. These data create an opportunity for machine learning (ML) models to detect patterns in evolving physiologic instability not captured by single “worst-value” assessments.

Early identification and risk stratification in pediatric sepsis management has traditionally relied on expert-derived thresholds and composite severity scores. Established frameworks such as SIRS [3], pSOFA [4–6], and Phoenix [7,8] encode physiologic and laboratory assessments into interpretable summaries of illness severity and organ dysfunction, and are widely used for bedside decision support, quality benchmarking, and research. These scores commonly use age-adjusted reference ranges for vital signs and laboratory values to quantify physiologic derangement and mortality risk [9]. These constructs were developed to standardize assessment and reduce cognitive burden in settings such as Emergency Departments where data are sparse, irregular, or difficult to integrate in real time.

However, the availability of dense, high-frequency physiologic data fundamentally alters the prediction problem. Threshold-based representations discretize continuous measurements and may discard information about magnitude, trajectory, and response to therapy, features that modern ML models can exploit effectively when trained on temporally resolved data. Composite scores also impose fixed weighting schemes and organ system abstractions that may not align with patient history, local patient populations, practice patterns, or evolving clinical trajectories. They may also obscure short-term dynamics critical for early risk prediction.

Although these scoring systems share a reliance on worst-value (extrema) physiologic and laboratory abnormalities relative to age-adjusted norms, they differ in their incorporation of treatment variables. While SIRS is intervention-independent, pSOFA and Phoenix incorporate cardiovascular and respiratory therapies, specifically vasopressor use and mechanical ventilation as indicators of organ dysfunction and septic shock, alongside expert-defined thresholds on physiologic and biochemical variables [5,7].

Recent work has demonstrated that early use of vasopressor or invasive mechanical ventilation is strongly associated with in-hospital mortality in PICU patients with a suspected infection [10]. Another recent ML study predicting Phoenix criteria in PICU patients found pSOFA to be the most important predictive feature [8]. However, it remains unclear whether predictive performance attributed to pSOFA derives primarily from its embedded intervention content, expert threshold defined physiologic abnormalities, or their composite structure.

More broadly, the value of expert knowledge in ML-based clinical prediction remains an active area of investigation. Expert-augmented machine learning approaches suggest that incorporating domain knowledge in the form of iterative expert rankings of machine generated rules may improve predictive model performance, particularly under dataset shift or limited sample sizes [11,12]. However, the benefit of a priori expert knowledge was not evaluated here.

A further, often under-recognized challenge is that commonly used pediatric severity scores—including pSOFA, and Phoenix—incorporate neurologic components that are frequently unobserved or inconsistently documented in infection-driven PICU admissions without trauma or primary neurologic disease. Formal neurologic assessments such as the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) are often omitted in routine care when neurologic injury is not suspected, reflecting standard clinical workflow rather than data quality limitations. Although scores such as Phoenix are designed to permit calculation in the absence of documented neurologic assessments, missing neurologic data implicitly contribute no neurologic dysfunction points, potentially underrepresenting neurologic involvement in this population. Neurologic dysfunction may therefore remain latent or incompletely captured in EHR-derived datasets. This motivates evaluation of alternative feature representations—physiologic trajectories, laboratory dynamics, and treatment timing—that can capture early deterioration when formal neurologic scoring is unavailable or incomplete.

Here, we examined whether externally defined expert-knowledge features provide meaningful incremental predictive value for early mortality risk when paired with time-resolved PICU data and modern gradient-boosted tree models (XGBoost) [13]. Expert features considered include threshold-based abnormality flags, burden metrics capturing persistence and duration of vital-sign abnormality [14], and established risk-stratifying severity scores.

To address this question, we implemented a staged, domain-structured modeling strategy that incrementally introduces distinct classes of features while monitoring discrimination, calibration, and feature importance. Using data from the first 24 hours of PICU admission, we compared static extrema measures, dynamic physiological trajectories, treatment-based variables, continuous laboratory features, and expert-derived representations to determine when–and to what extent–expert knowledge augments the predictive performance of contemporary machine learning models for pediatric critical illness.

Methods

Study design and cohort:

We conducted a retrospective observational study of children aged >3 months and <18 years with suspected infection admitted to the PICU at a single tertiary, freestanding pediatric hospital during the period March 2022-February 2025. Suspected infection was defined by the presence of a blood culture order. A total of 3310 PICU encounters met inclusion criteria, of which 107 (3.2%) resulted in in-hospital mortality. Predictor variables were derived exclusively from data available within the first 24 hours of PICU admission. Mortality occurring within the initial 24-hour window was excluded from the prediction target to ensure temporal separation between feature extraction and outcome ascertainment. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality, identified by either a documented death timestamp or discharge disposition of “expired.” All data were deidentified and collected under a Pearl IRB-approved protocol (22-CTAI-101)

Data Sources:

Time-stamped EHR data were extracted across multiple domains, including demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, microbiology, medications, and respiratory support. Vital signs were recorded at high temporal density (median >20 measurements per variable over 24 hours), and laboratory and treatment data were captured as recorded in routine clinical care.

Feature engineering and domain construction.

Preliminary statistical screening

Prior to model development, candidate features were compared between survivors and non-survivors using Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables. Features demonstrating minimal univariate separation were deprioritized but not excluded solely on statistical significance, as multivariable interactions were evaluated during model training. These univariate comparisons were used to support feature screening rather than as formal hypothesis tests and were therefore reported descriptively without adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Sequential model development

Model development followed a staged, domain-structured approach. An initial model based on vital sign extrema served as the baseline. At each subsequent stage, candidate features from a new domain were added to the best-performing model from the prior stage, allowing explicit assessment of incremental predictive value.

At each stage, feature retention was guided by changes in discrimination (ΔAUROC), precision–recall performance (AUPRC), calibration (Brier score), and specificity at a prespecified sensitivity of 0.80. This approach ensured that each feature domain was evaluated in the context of previously retained information rather than through independent re-optimization.

Feature pruning and parsimony

Within each stage, feature pruning was performed using a combination of quantitative and clinical criteria. Feature contributions were assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and features with consistently low importance or high collinearity were iteratively removed. When multiple features captured overlapping physiologic constructs, preference was given to features that improved interpretability or preserved performance with fewer predictors. Because gradient-boosted trees employ greedy split selection that assigns each split to the single most informative predictor, collinear features typically contribute little marginal information and are surfaced as low-SHAP features within the staged framework; SHAP-based pruning therefore served as the primary mechanism for identifying and removing redundancy, supplemented by clinical judgment for features capturing overlapping physiologic constructs (e.g., hours-above-threshold and area-above-threshold metrics derived from the same signal).

After evaluation of all domains, a final SHAP-guided reduction was performed to derive a parsimonious model minimizing dimensionality while preserving discrimination and calibration.

Model training and evaluation

Mortality prediction models were developed using Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) [13]. Class imbalance was addressed using the scale_pos_weight parameter, defined as the ratio of non-events to events in the training data. XGBoost’s native handling of missing values was used, allowing informative missingness patterns common in clinical workflows to be incorporated without explicit imputation.

Models were trained using five-fold stratified cross-validation, generating out-of-fold (OOF) predictions for all encounters. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) and the area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC). Calibration was evaluated using the Brier score. Sensitivity was fixed near 0.80 to reflect a clinically relevant rule-out operating point, with corresponding specificity and negative predictive value reported.

Bootstrap resampling of OOF predictions (500 iterations) was used to estimate 95% confidence intervals for AUROC and ΔAUROC. All models used consistent hyperparameters across experiments (learning rate 0.05, maximum tree depth 4, 300 boosting iterations).

Model interpretability

SHAP values were computed to quantify global feature importance and the directionality of feature effects, enabling transparent comparison between models using continuous physiologic trajectories, threshold-based representations, and expert-derived severity constructs.

Table 3 summarizes the stepwise evaluation of feature domains for early prediction of PICU mortality, highlighting changes in discrimination, calibration, and clinical operating characteristics as progressively richer clinical information was incorporated.

At Stage 0, a baseline model using only vital sign extrema demonstrated modest discrimination (AUROC 0.66, 95% CI: 0.61–0.71), consistent with prior observations that static physiologic snapshots provide limited prognostic resolution early in critical illness. Despite low positive predictive value (PPV 0.04), the model achieved high negative predictive value (NPV 0.98), indicating utility primarily for ruling out near-term mortality risk. In Stage 1, the addition of vital sign dynamics—including variability (standard deviation) and slopes—resulted in a statistically meaningful improvement in discrimination (AUROC 0.72, ΔAUROC +0.06, 95% CI: 0.01–0.10). This finding underscores the importance of short-term physiologic trajectories over isolated extrema, particularly for respiratory and hemodynamic variables. Improvements were also observed in specificity at fixed sensitivity (47% at 80% sensitivity), with preserved calibration (Brier score 0.05).

The inclusion of severity and burden metrics in Stage 2 did not materially improve model performance (ΔAUROC −0.00, 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.03). While these features captured cumulative physiologic derangement, their prognostic signal appeared largely redundant with already incorporated dynamic vital sign features. Nonetheless, these variables were retained for subsequent stages to preserve clinical completeness and assess downstream interactions.

A substantial performance gain emerged in Stage 3 with the addition of early treatment and respiratory support features. Discrimination increased markedly (AUROC 0.82, 95% CI: 0.77–0.86), accompanied by a pronounced rise in AUPRC (0.17 vs 0.08–0.09 in prior stages). This stage yielded the largest absolute improvement in clinical utility, with specificity increasing to 66% at 80% sensitivity and PPV rising to 0.07, while maintaining excellent NPV (0.99). These results suggest that early treatment intensity and response encode latent severity information not fully captured by physiologic data alone.

Further gains were observed in Stage 4 following the addition of selected laboratory features, with AUROC increasing to 0.86 (ΔAUROC +0.05, 95% CI: 0.01–0.08) and AUPRC reaching 0.21. Notably, laboratory trends and extrema (e.g., bicarbonate variability, platelet nadir, renal indices) improved discrimination without degrading calibration (Brier score 0.03), reinforcing their complementary role in early mortality risk stratification.

In contrast, the introduction of binary laboratory abnormality flags (Stage 5) did not confer additional benefits and slightly reduced discrimination (AUROC 0.85). Similarly, incorporation of SIRS-based features in Stage 6 resulted in a statistically significant decrement in AUROC (ΔAUROC −0.02, 95% CI: −0.04 to −0.001), indicating limited incremental prognostic value once granular physiologic, treatment, and laboratory data were available.

Composite organ dysfunction scores were evaluated in Stages 7-9. Addition of approximate pSOFA scores preserved discrimination (AUROC 0.86) and modestly improved AUPRC (0.23), whereas Phoenix composite scores did not yield further gains. However, a single Phoenix respiratory component (minimum S/F ratio) demonstrated comparable performance to the full score, supporting the notion that select subdomains may be more informative than aggregate indices in early prediction tasks.

Finally, Stage 10 presents the parsimonious model, in which clinically redundant features were removed, reducing dimensionality from 29 to 10 predictors. Although discrimination decreased modestly (AUROC 0.83), performance remained robust with preserved calibration and strong negative predictive value (0.99). This trade-off was considered acceptable given substantial gains in interpretability, stability, and feasibility for real-time clinical deployment.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (Figure 1A) are shown for the best-performing all-domain model incorporating 29 features across vitals, dynamics, treatment, laboratory values, and expert-derived thresholds (solid blue line) and for the reduced parsimonious model limited to 10 data-driven features (dashed orange line). The all-domain model demonstrated higher overall discrimination (AUROC 0.864 [95% CI: 0.823–0.896]) compared with the parsimonious model (AUROC 0.833 [95% CI: 0.789–0.875]), with a modest but statistically significant reduction in AUROC (ΔAUROC −0.030 [95% CI: −0.064 to −0.002]). Despite feature reduction, the parsimonious model preserved sensitivity at the prespecified operating point (~80%) and maintained excellent negative predictive value (>0.99), supporting its suitability for early risk stratification with improved interpretability and deployment feasibility. SHAP analysis of the full feature model (Stage 9) demonstrated that early mortality risk was primarily driven by markers of hemodynamic instability, metabolic derangement, respiratory failure, and early treatment intensity rather than static thresholds alone. Delayed vasopressor initiation emerged as the strongest contributor to increased predicted risk, followed by markers of metabolic acidosis (bicarbonate variability), systolic blood pressure trajectories, pulse variability, and thrombocytopenia, reflecting both physiologic instability and evolving organ dysfunction. Laboratory features such as AST elevation, worsening renal indices, and markers of infection and inflammation contributed to additional prognostic signal, while respiratory impairment was captured through minimum S/F ratios and their temporal trends. Notably, dynamic features (slopes, variability, and delay metrics) consistently exerted greater influence than corresponding extrema, supporting the importance of short-term physiologic trajectories in early risk stratification. Composite scores (e.g., pSOFA) contributed comparatively modest incremental information once granular physiologic and treatment features were included.

SHAP analysis of the parsimonious Stage 10 model (Figure 1B–C) demonstrated that a compact set of 10 features preserved the dominant risk signals identified in the full model, with delayed vasopressor initiation remaining the strongest contributor to early mortality risk. Measures of acid-base disturbance, thrombocytopenia, cardiovascular and autonomic variability (pulse variability, systolic blood pressure slope, MAP slope), and evolving renal and hepatic dysfunction (BUN slope, AST maximum) continued to exert substantial influence on model output. Notably, the retained features were predominantly dynamic or trend-based rather than static extrema, reinforcing the importance of short-term physiologic trajectories. Several of these slope-based features (sbp_slope, map_slope, bun_slope, q_hourly_slope) exhibited weak univariate associations with mortality in the supplementary tables but contributed substantially to multivariable model performance, consistent with slope features deriving their prognostic value through interaction with concurrent extrema, variability measures, and treatment context rather than as independent predictors. For example, a declining MAP slope carries different prognostic weight depending on whether vasopressor support has already been initiated—an interaction captured naturally by XGBoost’s tree-based architecture, which can condition feature effects on other variables. Despite the reduction in feature dimensionality, the relative ordering and directionality of SHAP effects were consistent with the full model, supporting the robustness, interpretability, and clinical plausibility of the parsimonious approach.

Discussion

We evaluated the incremental predictive value of clinically derived, knowledge-based features—burden metrics, laboratory abnormality flags, and composite organ dysfunction scores—within a modeling framework using dense, high-frequency physiologic and treatment data from children with suspected infection. While our cohort focused on pediatric sepsis, the findings have implications for other forms of life-threatening critical illness. Our principal finding is that when rich temporal data are available, data-driven feature dynamics dominate predictive performance, and the addition of expert-derived summaries provides little, if any, incremental benefit. The predominance of dynamic features in our models closely mirrors routine clinician reasoning at the bedside, where decisions are driven less by isolated values and more by whether physiologic and laboratory measures are improving, stable, or deteriorating over time.

Knowledge-based features, including burden metrics and composite scores, were developed to address data sparsity and cognitive load in clinical settings where continuous monitoring or repeated laboratory assessments are unavailable. In contrast, when granular temporal data are available, machine learning models can directly exploit raw physiologic trajectories and treatment responses without intermediate expert summarization. Under these conditions, the model learns its own representations of severity and deterioration, rendering handcrafted abstractions largely redundant.

Clinically, the proposed approach is viewed as a continuously updating risk stratification tool, rather than a static severity score. Rather than assigning patients to fixed risk categories based on worst observed values, the model evaluates response to therapy over time, identifying patients whose physiology is failing to improve despite intervention—even when conventional thresholds for hypotension or organ dysfunction have not yet been crossed. This framing aligns naturally with real-time clinical decision-making and supports early recognition of treatment non-response.

An additional finding of clinical relevance was the dominant contribution of vasopressor initiation delay to early mortality risk. Current pediatric resuscitation practices commonly emphasize repeated fluid boluses prior to vasopressor initiation, particularly in the absence of overt hypotension. While intended to optimize preload, this approach may delay vasoactive support in children with evolving, fluid-refractory shock. The strong predictive signal associated with vasopressor delay suggests that treatment timing itself captures latent severity not fully reflected in contemporaneous vital signs or laboratory values. This signal may also reflect delayed recognition of shock physiology rather than treatment intensity alone. Vasopressor initiation timing reflects not only patient physiology and individual clinician recognition but also institutional escalation thresholds and resuscitation practices. The predictive contribution of treatment-timing features should therefore be interpreted as associational rather than causal: SHAP values quantify how strongly these features predict mortality given the observed data, not the effect of altering treatment timing on outcomes.

Traditional scoring systems such as SIRS, pSOFA, and Phoenix remain valuable for standardized assessment and retrospective benchmarking but may be less effective for early decision support in data-dense environments. These scores rely on discretization of continuous variables and fixed weighting schemes that do not explicitly incorporate rich, high-frequency physiologic trajectories or treatment timing. Moreover, composite scores often commingle direct indicators of clinical intervention (e.g., vasopressor use) with expert-defined thresholding of physiologic measurements, making it difficult to disentangle evolving severity from clinician response. In contrast, the present approach models physiologic dynamics and treatment timing directly, allowing risk estimates to evolve continuously as new data become available.

Several respiratory rate–based features contributed less to model performance than might be anticipated clinically. Likely contributors include the inherent measurement variability of pediatric respiratory rate, redundancy with stronger oxygenation features such as minimum S/F ratio and SpO2 burden, and a cohort in which mortality was dominated by hemodynamic rather than primary respiratory pathophysiology—with minimum respiratory rate (often reflecting peri–arrest bradypnea) remaining a notable exception (P < 0.001).

Sepsis provides a clear, illustrative use case. Current clinical workflows emphasize repeated reassessment during early resuscitation, yet existing tools offer limited support for determining when a patient is failing to respond to fluids and may require earlier escalation to vasoactive therapy. The strong predictive signal associated with vasopressor initiation delay in this study suggests that trajectory-based models could serve as a decision support adjunct. Such models could flag patients with persistent metabolic acidosis, worsening hemodynamics, or lack of physiologic improvement despite treatment—signals that may precede overt shock. Importantly, this approach is not limited to sepsis and may generalize to other forms of pediatric critical illness characterized by evolving cardiopulmonary or metabolic instability.

The model relies exclusively on routinely collected PICU data, including high-frequency vital signs, commonly ordered laboratory tests, and time-stamped treatment events. No specialized devices, novel biomarkers, or institution-specific data elements are required, and the approach is agnostic to EHR vendor. Required resources are limited to real-time data extraction, periodic model inference, and integration into existing clinical dashboards or alerting workflows.

These findings support a shift from static, threshold-based scoring toward trajectory-aware decision support that augments clinician judgment rather than replacing it. By formalizing how clinicians already assess response to therapy, data-driven models such as the one presented here may enable earlier recognition of treatment failure and more timely escalation of care, with potential implications for improving outcomes across a broad range of critical illnesses.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant emphasis. First, this is a single-center study conducted at a tertiary, freestanding pediatric hospital. Several of the most influential features in our models—vasopressor initiation timing, respiratory support type and timing, and laboratory ordering frequency—are closely tied to local clinical practice patterns and escalation thresholds. Practice variability across institutions may therefore influence both the absolute importance of individual features and the calibration of model outputs. Although the broader finding that dynamic, treatment-aware representations outperform threshold-based summaries in dense-data settings is likely to be robust, external validation in geographically and operationally diverse multi-center cohorts will be required before clinical deployment, and recalibration may be needed at sites with materially different patient populations or workflows.

Second, our primary outcome mortality was selected because it is objective, clinically meaningful, and comparable across institutions and prior literature. However, mortality is a coarse endpoint that may fail to capture clinically relevant deterioration short of death—such as new-onset organ dysfunction, escalation to extra-corporeal support, or prolonged mechanical ventilation—that may be equally relevant for early triage and decision support. Future work should evaluate composite deterioration outcomes that better reflect the full spectrum of severe illness.

Finally, the 3.2% mortality rate poses challenges of training-data imbalance. We mitigated this through stratified five-fold cross-validation and class-weight adjustment in XGB training, and characterized the residual uncertainty through AUPRC reporting alongside AUROC and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals on all metrics. Even with these measures, performance estimates carry wider uncertainty than at higher event rates, and PPV is bounded by prevalence (0.115 and 0.090 for the full and parsimonious models at sensitivity 0.804)—appropriate for decision support rather than diagnosis. Small between-model differences should be interpreted in light of overlapping intervals, and external validation in higher-event-rate cohorts will further test stability.
