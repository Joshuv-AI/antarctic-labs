Introduction

The Cox proportional hazards model is the most widely used semi-parametric framework in survival analysis [1]. By estimating covariate effects via partial likelihood without specifying the baseline hazard function parametrically, it has become the standard approach in clinical research [2]. However, the model assumes a linear relationship between the log-hazard and covariates. This assumption often proves inadequate for capturing non-linear biomedical relationships, such as genetic interactions, dose-dependent saturation effects, or the U-shaped risk associations frequently observed in biomarkers.

To relax the linearity assumption, kernel-based Cox regression was proposed [3]. In this approach, the log-hazard function is defined within a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS), and by the Representer Theorem, the solution is expressed as a finite combination of kernel functions [4]. Nevertheless, this method requires a dense kernel matrix, and its computational cost scales cubically with the number of observations. This limitation hinders its applicability to large cohort datasets [5, 6].

Random Fourier Features (RFF) mitigate the computational burden of kernel methods by approximating stationary kernels in a finite-dimensional feature space, an approach grounded in sampling from the spectral density of the kernel function [7, 8]. Despite this potential, the integration of RFF-based approaches with censored survival data remains limited in the literature. Two primary methodological barriers currently impede applications in this domain. First, the performance of the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel is highly sensitive to the bandwidth parameter (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}). Yet, determining an optimal value for this parameter in the context of censored data structures typically necessitates cross-validation, a process that incurs substantial computational costs. Second, and more broadly, many non-linear survival models fail to provide closed-form analytical measures of uncertainty for individual predictions. Consequently, assessing the uncertainty of model predictions often relies on computationally expensive resampling techniques, such as the bootstrap.

Several approaches have addressed non-linearity in the Cox model. Kernel Cox regression [3] provides theoretical flexibility but incurs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} computational cost for large cohorts. DeepSurv [9] offers universal approximation but lacks closed-form uncertainty measures, requiring resampling for confidence intervals. Tree-based methods (RSF, XGBoost) capture interactions implicitly [10] but do not provide formal hypothesis testing. The RFF-Cox framework addresses these gaps by combining \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(n{m}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} scalability, analytical uncertainty quantification through the Fisher information matrix, and a Taylor-expansion-based inference framework enabling covariate-level Wald tests and interaction detection.

This study proposes the RFF-Cox framework, designed to derive individual risk estimates from censored survival data in a manner that is more flexible, efficient, and interpretable. By leveraging random Fourier features, the method effectively models complex, non-linear risk relationships, thereby overcoming the limitations of classical linear approaches while offering a scalable and rapid prediction process suitable for large-scale datasets. Furthermore, the framework introduces a Taylor-expansion-based inference procedure that bridges the classical linear Cox model and the non-linear RFF-Cox model, enabling covariate-level hazard ratio estimation, Wald-type significance testing, and formal interaction detection [11, 12]. The framework also enables the quantitative assessment of uncertainty in individual survival estimates through a multivariate Delta method that propagates variance from both parameter estimation and baseline hazard estimation [13, 14], allowing for the derivation of analytical confidence intervals (CIs) for each prediction. In these respects, RFF-Cox offers a practical survival modeling framework capable of capturing non-linear risk structures, operating with high computational efficiency, and explicitly quantifying individual prediction uncertainty.

Theoretical background

This section systematically summarizes the theoretical foundations underpinning the proposed method: the classical Cox model, kernel-based generalizations, and the Random Fourier Features (RFF) approach.

Cox proportional hazards model

The reference model in survival analysis is the Proportional Hazards (PH) model, introduced by Cox [1]. Throughout this work, d is assumed to be fixed. For a covariate vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x\in\:{R}^{d}$$\end{document}, the model defines the conditional hazard function as:

1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h(t \mid x) = h_0(t) \exp(x^\top \beta_{\textit{cox}})$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{0}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} denotes the non-parametric baseline hazard function, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{cox}\in{R}^{d}$$\end{document} represents the coefficient vector capturing the constant effects of covariates on the log-hazard.

The most distinct feature of this model is that the parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta_{cox}$$\end{document} can be estimated via the partial likelihood method without specifying the form of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{h}_{0}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. This framework was later extended by Norman Breslow to estimate the baseline hazard function [15]. However, the assumption of a linear relationship (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${x}^{\top}\beta_{cox}$$\end{document}) between the log-hazard and covariates can be a limiting factor when modeling complex non-linear phenomena, such as genetic epistasis, saturation points in dose-response curves, or U-shaped risk associations observed in biomarkers. While Therneau and Grambsch have detailed the standard applications [2], the linearity constraint remains a known limitation.

Functional generalization and Kernel Approach

When the linear predictor in the Cox model is generalized to a nonparametric function, the log-hazard function is expressed as:

2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{log}h\left(t\mid{x}\right)=\mathrm{log}{h}_{0}\left(t\right)+\eta\:\left({x}\right)$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left(\cdot\:\right)$$\end{document} is an unknown function defining the non-linear relationship between covariates and risk. This problem can be formulated as a regularized optimization problem within a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS). The Representer Theorem, established by Kimeldorf and Wahba [4], demonstrates that for such problems, the optimal solution can be expressed as a linear combination of a finite number of kernel functions [4, 5]. Lin et al. (2011) adapted this approach to the Cox model, proposing kernel-based Cox regression and showing that the non-linear risk function could be estimated within the RKHS framework via partial likelihood maximization [3].

Random fourier feature approximation of shift-invariant Kernels

While kernel methods offer a powerful framework for modeling non-linear relationships, their classical implementations require computing and storing a dense \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\:\times\:n$$\end{document} Gram matrix for n observations. This results in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} memory and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} computational complexity [6, 7]. Due to this computational burden, approximating kernel functions via finite-dimensional feature maps has gained prominence.

For shift-invariant kernels, the theoretical basis of this approach relies on Bochner’s Theorem [16]. According to the theorem, any continuous, positive-definite kernel \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\left({x}-{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)$$\end{document} can be expressed as the Fourier transform of a probability measure [6, 16]:

3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\left({x},{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)=k\left({x}-{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)={\int}_{{\mathrm{R}}^{d}}p\left({\omega\:}\right){e}^{i{{\omega\:}}^{{\top\:}}\left({x}-{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)}d{\omega\:}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\left({\omega\:}\right)$$\end{document} is a probability distribution representing the spectral density of the kernel function. By taking the real part of the complex exponential and applying Euler’s formula and trigonometric identities, this expression can be transformed into the following expected value form involving a random phase variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:b\sim\:\mathrm{Uniform}\left(\mathrm{0,2}{\uppi\:}\right)$$\end{document}:

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\left({x},{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)={E}_{{\omega\:},b}\left[\mathrm{cos}\left({{\omega\:}}^{{\top\:}}{x}+b\right)\mathrm{cos}\left({\{\omega\:}^{{\top}}{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}+b\right)\right]$$\end{document}

Rahimi and Recht proposed reducing this infinite-dimensional integral representation to a finite-dimensional feature map via Monte Carlo sampling [7]. Specifically, for the Radial Basis Function (RBF) Gaussian kernel:

5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k\left({x},{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\frac{\parallel{x}-{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\parallel^{2}}{2{{\upsigma\:}}^{2}}\right)$$\end{document}

the spectral density distribution is Gaussian: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\left({\omega\:}\right)=\mathcal{N}\left(0,{{\upsigma\:}}^{-2}{{I}}_{d}\right)$$\end{document}. Due to the inverse variance transformation in the Fourier pair, sampling the weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{w}$$\end{document} satisfies the condition \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Var}\left({w}\right)={{\upsigma\:}}^{-2}$$\end{document}, which corresponds directly to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{w}}_{j}\sim\:\mathcal{N}\left(0,{{\upsigma\:}}^{-2}{{I}}_{d}\right)$$\end{document}, for j = 1,…,m, in practice.

By drawing m independent frequency vectors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\omega\:}}_{j}$$\end{document} and phases \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{j}$$\end{document} from this spectral distribution, the following finite-dimensional feature map is constructed:

6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:\left({x}\right)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}\:\left[\begin{array}{c}{cos}\left({{\omega\:}}_{1}^{{\top\:}}{x}+{b}_{1}\right)\\\:.\\\:.\\\:.\\\:{cos}\left({{\omega\:}}_{m}^{{\top\:}}{x}+{b}_{m}\right)\end{array}\right]\:\in\:{R}^{m},$$\end{document}

By the Law of Large Numbers, this transformation ensures that the inner product in the feature space converges to the original kernel value. This approach is based on the principle of approximating infinite-dimensional kernel maps with finite-dimensional stochastic projections [11]:

7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}&{\mathrm{E}}_{{\omega\:},b}\left[\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\phi\:\left({{x}}^{{\prime\:}}\right)\right]=\mathrm{k}\left({x},{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)\\&\Rightarrow \:k\left({x},{{x}}^{{{\prime\:}}}\right)\approx\:\:\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\phi\:\left({{x}}^{{\prime\:}}\right)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

For a data matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{X}\in\:{R}^{n\times\:d}$$\end{document}, computing the explicit feature matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Phi\:}\in\:{R}^{n\times\:m}$$\end{document} has a complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\left(ndm\right)$$\end{document}. This approach eliminates the necessity of constructing an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\:\times\:n$$\end{document} dense kernel matrix, reducing computational complexity from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} in classical methods to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{O}\left(n{m}^{2}+{m}^{3}\right)$$\end{document}. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m\:\ll\:n$$\end{document}, this structure provides near-linear scalability for large datasets [7, 17]. Furthermore, since the kernel method is thereby mapped to an explicit linear feature space, it allows for the direct application of standard linear optimization algorithms. These advantages constitute the core computational efficiency and practical applicability of the proposed RFF-Cox method.

Proposed method: RFF-cox framework

This section details the RFF-Cox method, developed by integrating the theoretical components presented in previous sections. The proposed framework introduces three key innovations: scalable optimization, data-driven automatic bandwidth selection, and asymptotic uncertainty quantification. This approach reduces the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} computational cost inherent in classical kernel methods, eigen-decomposition and matrix symmetrization, and ensures statistical reliability through full covariance matrix derivation.

RFF-based cox model formulation

The linear component \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{x}}^{\top\:}{{\beta\:}}_{{c}{o}{x}}$$\end{document} in the classical Cox model is redefined in the explicit feature space obtained via RFF as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}$$\end{document}:

8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:h\left(t\mid{x}\right)={h}_{0}\left(t\right)\mathrm{exp}\left(\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}\right)$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upphi\:}\left({x}\right)\in\:{R}^{m}$$\end{document} denotes the Random Fourier Features transformation defined in Sect. This formulation preserves the proportional hazards (PH) assumption, as the hazard ratio between any two individuals remains independent of time. Thus, the non-linear risk structure is reduced to a linear parameter estimation problem in an m-dimensional feature space (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m\:\ll\:n$$\end{document}).

The partial likelihood function is expressed as:

9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:l\left({\beta\:}\right)={\sum\:}_{i:{E}_{i}=1}\left[\phi\:{\left({{x}}_{{i}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}-\mathrm{log}\left({\sum\:}_{j\in\:R\left({t}_{i}\right)}\mathrm{exp}\left(\phi\:{\left({{x}}_{j}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}\right)\right)\right]$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:E_{i}\in\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}$$\end{document} denotes the event indicator for observation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:i,t_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the observed time, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left(t_{i}\right)=j:t_{j}\ge\:t_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the risk set at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t_{i}$$\end{document} Defining the model dimension by the number of features m rather than the sample size n eliminates the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} memory requirement associated with dense kernel matrices, thereby enhancing the scalability of the method.

Newton–Raphson optimization for penalized partial likelihood

To prevent overfitting and improve estimation stability, a Ridge (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{2}$$\end{document}) penalty is added to the objective function [2, 18, 19]. The penalized log-likelihood function to be optimized is defined as:

10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{l}_{\mathrm{p}}\left({\beta\:}\right)=l\left({\beta\:}\right)-\frac{{\uplambda\:}}{2}\left|\right|{\beta\:}{\left|\right|}_{2}^{2},\:$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:>\:0$$\end{document} is the parameter controlling the regularization strength. The optimization is performed using the Newton–Raphson algorithm, which utilizes second-order derivative information. The iterative update, using the gradient vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}\left({\beta\:}\right)$$\end{document} and the Hessian matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{H}\left({\beta\:}\right)$$\end{document}, is given by:

11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\beta\:}}^{\left(k+1\right)}={{\beta\:}}^{\left(k\right)}-{H}{\left({{\beta\:}}^{\left(k\right)}\right)}^{-1}{U}\left({{\beta\:}}^{\left(k\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

Since the dimension of the model parameters is limited to m following the Random Fourier Features transformation, the cost of computing the gradient and Hessian at each iteration is approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(n{m}^{2}\right)$$\end{document}, while inverting the Hessian matrix requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({m}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} complexity [7, 17, 19]. In cases where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m\:\ll\:\:n$$\end{document}, this approach offers a scalable solution for large datasets compared to classical kernel methods.

To reduce computational costs, risk set sums are calculated in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(n\right)$$\end{document} complexity using the reverse cumulative sum method [2]. To prevent floating-point overflow in exponential computations, values are clipped to the range [− 700,700] as a standard IEEE 754 safeguard. Within this framework, the variance weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\mu\:}}_{{i}}\:$$\end{document} forming the Fisher information matrix for the i-th observation is derived according to the Breslow method:

12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upmu\:}}_{i}={r}_{i}{\sum\:}_{j:{t}_{j}\le\:{t}_{i},{E}_{j}=1}\frac{1}{R\left({t}_{j}\right)}={r}_{i}{H}_{i},$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{r}_{i}=\mathrm{exp}\left(\phi\:{\left({{x}}_{{i}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}\right)$$\end{document} represents the individual risk score, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left(t_{j}\right)={\varSigma\:}_{l\in\:R\left(t_{j}\right)}r_{\mathrm{l}}$$\end{document} is the total risk score at event time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t_{j}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:H_{i}={\varSigma\:}_{j:t_{j}\le\:t_{i},E_{j}=1}1/R\left(t_{j}\right)$$\end{document} is the cumulative Breslow weight that accumulates the inverse risk-set contributions from all events at or before \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t_{i}$$\end{document}. The term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the observation-specific variance contribution in the Hessian matrix of the partial likelihood. This value is non-zero not only for events (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{i}=1$$\end{document}) but also for censored observations (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{i}=0$$\end{document}) that contribute while present in the risk set; this is critical for accurately reflecting the covariance structure of the Fisher information matrix. This sum is computed simultaneously for the entire sample using vectorized cumulative sum operators rather than loop-based procedures.

Using the obtained vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}$$\end{document}, and following the Hessian decomposition of the Cox partial likelihood [2, 14], the observed Fisher information matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}\left({\beta\:}\right)=-{H}\left({\beta\:}\right)$$\end{document} can be approximately expressed in the form of a variance-weighted design matrix, thanks to the linear model structure after RFF transformation (see Appendix A for the full derivation):

13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}\left({\beta\:}\right)={{\Phi\:}}^{{\top\:}}\mathrm{diag}\left({\mu\:}\right){\Phi\:}+\lambda\:{{I}}_{m}$$\end{document}

This constitutes a Breslow-type approximation obtained by omitting the positive semi-definite rank-1 correction terms from the exact Hessian (Appendix A, Eq. A5–A6). Since the omitted terms are positive semi-definite, this approximation overestimates the Fisher information matrix (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{I}}_{approx}\geq{{I}}_{exact}$$\end{document}) which may lead to marginally anti-conservative confidence intervals.

To facilitate efficient inversion and uncertainty quantification, the eigendecomposition of the regularized information matrix is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}\left({\beta\:}\right)={V}{\Lambda\:}{{V}}^{{\top\:}}$$\end{document} where V is the orthogonal matrix of eigenvectors and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\Lambda\:}=\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{g}\left({\lambda\:}_{1},\dots\:,{\lambda\:}_{m}\right)$$\end{document} contains the corresponding positive eigenvalues.

In numerical implementation, to avoid constructing an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\times\:n$$\end{document} diagonal matrix, the expression \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Phi\:}}^{\top\:}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{g}\left({\mu\:}\right)$$\end{document} is evaluated by first constructing the row-weighted matrix whose i-th row is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mu\:}_{i}\cdot{\varphi\:\left({x}_{i}\right)}^{{\top\:}}$$\end{document} and then computing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Phi\:}}^{\top\:}$$\end{document} times this weighted matrix, thereby reducing memory usage. Here, Φ represents the design matrix.

Since the symmetry of the matrix may be compromised due to numerical rounding errors, the matrix to be used in the Newton step is symmetrized as:

14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{I}}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\frac{1}{2}\left({I}+{{I}}^{{\top\:}}\right)$$\end{document}

Using this matrix, the Newton–Raphson update is performed as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\beta\:}}^{\left(k+1\right)}={{\beta\:}}^{\left(k\right)}+{{I}}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{-1}{U}\left({{\beta\:}}^{\left(k\right)}\right)$$\end{document}

This stabilization aids the reliable convergence of optimization in the high-dimensional RFF space. According to maximum likelihood theory, the inverse of the Fisher information matrix at the point of convergence provides the approximate covariance matrix of the parameter estimates. Thus,

15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Var}\left({\beta\:}\right)={{I}}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{-1}$$\end{document}

is obtained. Using this covariance matrix, both standard errors of parameter estimates and confidence intervals for linear predictors can be calculated. Consequently, the model not only produces point estimates but also enables the quantitative assessment of statistical uncertainty for individual risk predictions.

Automatic bandwidth selection via laplace approximation

The performance of the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel depends significantly on the bandwidth parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document}. In censored survival data, traditional cross-validation methods incur high computational costs. In this study, a Laplace approximation based on penalized partial likelihood is adopted for selecting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}$$\end{document} parameter.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathcal{D}=\{\left({x}_{i},\hspace{0.17em}{t}_{i},\hspace{0.17em}{E}_{i}\right){\}}_{i=1}^{n}$$\end{document} denote the observed survival dataset, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{x}}_{{i}}\in\:{R}^{d}$$\end{document} is the covariate vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{t}_{i}>0$$\end{document} the observed time, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{i}\in\:\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}$$\end{document} the event indicator for subject i. Under a Gaussian prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}\sim\:\mathcal{N}\left(0,\hspace{0.17em}{{\uplambda\:}}^{-1}{{I}}_{m}\right)$$\end{document} the penalized log-likelihood\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{l}_{p}\left({\beta\:}\right)=l\left({\beta\:}\right)-\frac{{\uplambda\:}}{2}\left|\right|{\beta\:}{\left|\right|}_{2}^{2}$$\end{document} (Eq. 10) corresponds to the log-posterior density up to an additive constant.

Since the marginal likelihood of the model, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p\left(D\mid{\upsigma\:}\right)=\int\:\mathrm{exp}\left({l}_{p}\left({\beta\:}\right)\right)d{\beta\:}$$\end{document}, cannot be solved in closed form, it is approximated using a second-order Taylor expansion around the MAP estimate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}$$\end{document} [20]:

16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{log}p\left(D\mid{\upsigma\:}\right)\approx\:{l}_{\mathrm{p}}\left(\widehat{{{\beta\:}}_{{\upsigma\:}}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{log}\mathrm{det}{I}\left(\widehat{{{\beta\:}}_{{\upsigma\:}}}\right)+C$$\end{document}

This expression can be interpreted as an evidence maximization criterion, where the bandwidth parameter is selected to maximize the approximate marginal likelihood. The subscript \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\beta\:}_{{\upsigma\:}}}$$\end{document} indicates that the MAP estimate depends on the bandwidth parameter through the RFF feature map. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{l}_{\mathrm{p}}$$\end{document} denotes the penalized log-likelihood, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:I\left(\cdot\:\right)$$\end{document} is the Fisher information matrix, and C is a constant term. The determinant term (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{log}\mathrm{det}{I}$$\end{document}) acts as a regularization component penalizing model complexity; thus, the resulting score serves as a model selection criterion balancing data fit and model simplicity. For numerical stability, the determinant is calculated via eigen-decomposition [21] (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{I}={V}{\Lambda\:}{{V}}^{{\top\:}}$$\end{document}) where V is the matrix of eigenvectors and {\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\uplambda\:}}_{j}$$\end{document}} are the positive eigenvalues of I, as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sum\:\mathrm{log}{{\uplambda\:}}_{j}$$\end{document} rather than by direct computation.

The optimal bandwidth \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upsigma\:}}^{*}$$\end{document} is determined via numerical optimization over a bounded range in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:log-{\upsigma\:}$$\end{document} space to maximize this score, ensuring positive-valued and scale-invariant exploration of the bandwidth parameter. To efficiently scan the search space, the median heuristic is employed, consistent with the suggestion by Schölkopf and Smola [6]. By calculating the median of pairwise Euclidean distances between data points (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upsigma\:}}_{\mathrm{med}}$$\end{document}), the search range is restricted to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[0.1{{\upsigma\:}}_{\mathrm{med}},10{{\upsigma\:}}_{\mathrm{med}}\right]$$\end{document}. This constraint prevents the evaluation of meaningless extreme values and accelerates convergence.

To further reduce computational load, random subsampling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{n}^{{\prime\:}}=min\left(n,300\right)$$\end{document}) is applied for large-scale datasets (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\:>\:300$$\end{document}), balancing computational efficiency with adequate representation of the censoring distribution. During the optimization process, the Newton–Raphson iteration for each candidate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\sigma\:$$\end{document} value is limited to only 5 steps; the goal is not full convergence but rapid comparison of relative scores among σ candidates, an early stopping strategy justified because the purpose is relative ranking rather than full convergence at each candidate. Empirical evaluations confirming the robustness of both approximations are presented in Appendix B (Table S1).

Baseline hazard and survival function estimation

Once the model coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document} are obtained, the non-parametric baseline cumulative hazard function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is calculated using the Breslow estimator [15]. In the presence of tied events, the Breslow approximation is applied:

17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)={\sum\:}_{i:{T}_{i}\le\:t,{E}_{i}=1}\frac{{d}_{i}}{{\sum\:}_{j\in\:R\left({T}_{i}\right)}\mathrm{exp}\left(\phi\:{\left({{x}}_{{j}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}\right)}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{i}$$\end{document} denotes the number of events occurring at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{i}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left({T}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} represents the risk set at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{i}$$\end{document}.

The individual survival function for a new covariate vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}$$\end{document} is then derived as:

18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{S}\left(t\mid{x}\right)=\mathrm{exp}\left(-\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)\mathrm{exp}\left(\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}\right)\right)$$\end{document}

To facilitate downstream uncertainty quantification, the variance of the baseline cumulative hazard estimator is additionally required. To account for tied events and ensure numerical stability in finite samples, a Greenwood-type variance estimator is employed for the baseline cumulative hazard. The variance of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document} is computed as:

19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)\right)=\sum\:_{i:{T}_{i}\le\:t,{E}_{i}=1}\frac{{d}_{i}}{R\left({T}_{i}\right)\left(R\right({T}_{i})-{d}_{i})}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{i}$$\end{document} is the number of events at time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{i}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:R\left({T}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} is the size of the risk set. This formulation reduces to the standard Nelson-Aalen [22, 23] variance \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({d}_{i}/R{\left({T}_{i}\right)}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{d}_{i}\ll\:R\left({T}_{i}\right)$$\end{document} but provides better coverage properties when ties are present or risk sets are small. This estimator is consistent with the large-sample counting-process theory established by Andersen and Gill (1982) [13] and is standard in the survival-analysis literature [14]. In the present implementation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)\right)$$\end{document} is computed at each unique event time and linearly interpolated for arbitrary prediction horizons.

To maintain interpretability in point estimates and ensure compatibility with standard practices in the literature, the linear predictor is used directly in the form \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{exp}\left(\eta\:\right)$$\end{document} without applying any moment corrections.

Uncertainty quantification and confidence intervals

Reliable deployment of the proposed RFF-Cox framework in clinical decision support systems necessitates the systematic quantification of uncertainty for individual risk predictions.

Observed information matrix and stabilized covariance estimation

Maximum likelihood estimators obtained under the Cox partial likelihood function are asymptotically normally distributed in large samples [1, 2]. Consequently, the covariance matrix of the parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}$$\end{document} is approximated by the inverse of the observed information matrix, derived from the second derivatives of the Cox partial log-likelihood function [14]:

20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}\left(\widehat{{\beta\:}}\right)={I}{\left(\widehat{{\beta\:}}\right)}^{-1}$$\end{document}

When Random Fourier Features (RFF) are employed, the model reduces to a linear parameter structure, and the Fisher information matrix takes the variance-weighted form given in Eq. 13. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\Phi\:}}^{\top\:}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{i}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{g}\left({\mu\:}\right){\Phi\:}$$\end{document} is positive semi-definite and the ridge penalty \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:{{I}}_{m}$$\end{document} contributes strictly positive eigenvalues, positive definiteness is guaranteed for any \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:>0$$\end{document}. The covariance matrix is therefore obtained directly as:

21\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}={{V}{\Lambda\:}}^{-1}{{V}}^{{\top\:}}$$\end{document}

Unlike diagonal approximations, this study utilizes the full covariance structure, which preserves correlations between parameters, providing a more robust foundation for calculating Wald statistics. Finally, the variance of the linear predictor for a new observation is calculated using:

22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Var}\left(\eta\:\left({x}\right)\right)={\upphi\:}{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}{\upphi\:}\left({x}\right)$$\end{document}

Since this expression accounts for the entire covariance structure among parameters, it directly enables the calculation of confidence intervals for individual risk predictions.

Variance propagation via the multivariate Delta method

The survival function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{S}\left(t\mid{x}\right)$$\end{document} depends on two quantities that are estimated with sampling uncertainty: the linear predictor \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left({x}\right)=\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document} and the baseline cumulative hazard \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)$$\end{document}. To obtain a comprehensive variance estimate, the multivariate Delta method ([12, 24]) is employed, propagating variability from both sources simultaneously:

23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{S}\right)\approx\:{\left(\frac{\partial\:S}{\partial\:\eta\:}\right)}^{2}\cdot\:\hspace{0.17em}\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\eta\:\right)+{\left(\frac{\partial\:S}{\partial\:{H}_{0}}\right)}^{2}\cdot\:\hspace{0.17em}\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{{H}_{0}}\right)$$\end{document}

The cross-term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Cov(\eta\:,\:H_0)$$\end{document} is omitted under the standard working assumption that parameter estimation uncertainty and baseline hazard variability are approximately independent for moderate sample sizes [14]. While both quantities are estimated from the same data, this independence approximation is commonly adopted in the survival-analysis literature and becomes increasingly accurate as the sample size grows. Where the partial derivatives, evaluated at the estimated quantities, are:

24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{\partial\:S}{\partial\:\eta\:}=-\widehat{{H}_{0}}\left(t\right)\hspace{0.17em}\mathrm{exp}\left(\eta\:\right)\hspace{0.17em}\widehat{S},\hspace{1em}\hspace{1em}\frac{\partial\:S}{\partial\:{H}_{0}}=-\mathrm{exp}\left(\eta\:\right)\hspace{0.17em}\widehat{S}$$\end{document}

Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\eta\:\right)=\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}\hspace{0.17em}\phi\:\left({x}\right)$$\end{document} is the variance of the linear predictor derived in Sect. Observed information matrix and stabilized covariance estimation, (Eq. 22), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{{H}_{0}}\right)$$\end{document} is the Greenwood-type variance estimator defined in Eq. 19. The first term captures parameter estimation uncertainty propagated through the log-hazard structure, whereas the second term reflects the inherent variability of the non-parametric baseline hazard estimate. Inclusion of the baseline hazard variance yields more conservative—and, in small risk sets, more realistic—confidence intervals than those obtained from the single-source approximation that accounts only for parameter uncertainty.

The survival function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\left(t\mid{x}\right)$$\end{document} is naturally defined within the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(\mathrm{0,1}\right)$$\end{document} range and exhibits a distinctly asymmetric distribution. Therefore, a confidence interval based on the log-log transformation is used, which inherently preserves the asymmetric structure and prevents estimates from exceeding probability bounds [25]. The transformation is defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:u=\mathrm{ln}\left(-\mathrm{ln}\left(\widehat{S}\right)\right)$$\end{document}. Using the Delta method, the standard error \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\updelta\:}$$\end{document} of this transformation is derived as follows. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:u=\mathrm{ln}\left(-\mathrm{ln}\left(S\right)\right)$$\end{document}, the derivative with respect to S is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{du}{dS}=\frac{1}{S\cdot\:\mathrm{ln}\left(S\right)}$$\end{document} Applying the Delta method, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Var}\left(u\right)\approx\:{\left(\frac{du}{dS}\right)}^{2}\cdot\:\mathrm{Var}\left(S\right)$$\end{document}, yielding:

25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\delta\:=\frac{\sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}\left(\widehat{S}\right)}}{\widehat{S}\left|\mathrm{ln}\left(\widehat{S}\right)\right|+\epsilon}$$\end{document}

The absolute value ensures a positive standard error, as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:du/d\widehat{S}\:<\:0$$\end{document} for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S\in\:\left(\mathrm{0,1}\right)$$\end{document}. The additive \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document} prevents division by zero as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{S}\:\to\:\:1$$\end{document}. Consequently, the 95% confidence intervals are constructed as:

26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{S}_{lo},\hspace{0.17em}{S}_{up}\right]=\left[\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left(-\mathrm{exp}\left(u+1.96\hspace{0.17em}\delta\:\right)\right),\mathrm{e}\mathrm{x}\mathrm{p}\left(-\mathrm{exp}\left(u-1.96\hspace{0.17em}\delta\:\right)\right)\right]$$\end{document}

For the Hazard Ratio (HR), the standard Wald approach (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{exp}\left(\eta\:\pm\:1.96\cdot\:\mathrm{SE}\right)$$\end{document}) is adopted.

Clinical interpretation: equivalent coefficients and interaction inference

Although the RFF-Cox model operates in an m-dimensional random feature space, clinical interpretation frequently requires covariate-level effect estimates expressed in the original d-dimensional input space. This section presents two complementary procedures that bridge this representational gap: equivalent linear coefficients for main effect inference (Sect. Equivalent linear coefficients and bias correction) and second-order partial derivatives for interaction detection (Sect. Inference on second-order effects).

Equivalent linear coefficients and bias correction

To enable covariate-level interpretation, a first-order Taylor expansion of the feature map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:$$\end{document}(x) is employed around the training sample mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{{x}}$$\end{document}[11]:

27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left({x}\right)=\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}\approx\:\phi\:{\left(\stackrel{-}{{x}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}+{\left({x}-\stackrel{-}{{x}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}{{J}}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}\in\:{R}^{m\times\:d}$$\end{document} denotes the Jacobian matrix of the feature map evaluated at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\stackrel{-}{{x}}$$\end{document}. For the RBF-based Random Fourier Feature mapping defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\phi\:}_{j}\left({x}\right)=\sqrt{2/m}\mathrm{cos}\left({{\upomega\:}}_{j}^{{\top\:}}{x}+{b}_{j}\right)$$\end{document}, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left(j,k\right)$$\end{document}-th element of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{J}$$\end{document} is given by:

28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{J}}_{jk}={\left.\frac{\partial\:{{\upphi\:}}_{j}}{\partial\:{x}_{k}}\right|}_{{x}=\stackrel{-}{{x}}}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}\mathrm{sin}\left({{\upomega\:}}_{j}^{{\top\:}}\stackrel{-}{{x}}+{b}_{j}\right)\hspace{0.17em}{{\upomega\:}}_{jk}$$\end{document}

By defining the equivalent linear coefficient vector as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\beta\:}}_{\mathrm{equiv}}\:={{J}}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}\in\:{R}^{d}$$\end{document}, the linear predictor can be re-expressed as:

29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left({x}\right)\approx\:{{x}}^{{\top\:}}{\beta\:}_{\mathrm{equiv}}\:+{b}_{\mathrm{bias}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{b}_{\mathrm{bias}}=\phi\:{\left(\stackrel{-}{{x}}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}-{\stackrel{-}{{x}}}^{{\top\:}}{{\beta\:}}_{\mathrm{equiv}}\:$$\end{document} represents the residual intercept arising from the first-order approximation. This decomposition preserves the total linear predictor while enabling the interpretation of each covariate’s marginal effect through the corresponding element of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\beta\:}}_{\mathrm{equiv}}$$\end{document}, analogous to the coefficient vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}$$\end{document} in the classical Cox model [1, 2]. Consequently, the bias-corrected hazard ratio for the j-th covariate is computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{exp}\left({\beta\:}_{\mathrm{equiv},j}\right)$$\end{document}.

For uncertainty quantification of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\beta\:}}_{\mathrm{equiv}}\:$$\end{document}, the covariance matrix is derived via the standard Jacobian variance-propagation formula [12]:

30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\mathrm{Cov}}{({\beta\:}}_{\mathrm{equiv}})={{J}}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}{J}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}\in\:{R}^{m\times\:m}$$\end{document} is the stabilized covariance matrix obtained in Eq. 21. Standard errors, Wald-type Z-statistics, and two-sided p-values for each equivalent coefficient are subsequently computed from the diagonal elements of this \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:d\:\times\:d$$\end{document} matrix. This procedure facilitates formal hypothesis testing of individual covariate effects while fully accounting for the correlation structure among the parameters in the RFF domain.

Inference on second-order effects

To test for covariate interactions, the cross-partial derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\partial\:}^{2}{\upeta\:}/\partial\:{x}_{j}\partial\:{x}_{k}$$\end{document} is estimated using a centered finite-difference stencil applied to the model’s linear predictor:

31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\frac{{\partial\:}^{2}\widehat{\eta\:}}{\partial\:{x}_{j}\partial\:{x}_{k}}=\frac{\widehat{\eta\:}\left({x}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)-\widehat{\eta\:}\left({x}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)-\widehat{\eta\:}\left({x}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)+\widehat{\eta\:}\left({x}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)}{4{{\upepsilon\:}}^{2}}$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}\in\:{R}^{d}$$\end{document} denotes a perturbation vector with value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\epsilon\:$$\end{document} in the j-th position and zeros elsewhere. All interaction derivatives are evaluated at the covariate mean unless otherwise stated. These derivatives quantify local second-order interaction behavior around the evaluation point. Since \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{\eta\:}\left({x}\right)=\phi\:{\left({x}\right)}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document} is linear in the model parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document}, the delta method (Sect. Variance propagation via the multivariate Delta method) applies directly. The Jacobian of the cross-partial derivative with respect to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\beta\:}}$$\end{document} is given by:

32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{J}}_{jk}=\frac{{\upphi\:}\left({x}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)-{\upphi\:}\left({x}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)-{\upphi\:}\left({x}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}+{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)+{\upphi\:}\left({x}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{j}-{{\upepsilon\:}}_{k}\right)}{4{{\upepsilon\:}}^{2}}$$\end{document}

The variance of the interaction estimate is then computed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Var}\left({\partial\:}^{2}\widehat{{\upeta\:}}/\partial\:{x}_{j}\partial\:{x}_{k}\right)={{J}}_{jk}^{{\top\:}}\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}{{J}}_{jk},$$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\widehat{{\Sigma\:}}$$\end{document} is the stabilized covariance matrix from Eq. 21. A Wald statistic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=\left({\partial\:}^{2}\widehat{{\upeta\:}}/\partial\:{x}_{j}\partial\:{x}_{k}\right)/\mathrm{SE}$$\end{document} is subsequently calculated to formally test the significance of the interaction effect. When evaluating all pairwise interactions among d covariates, Bonferroni correction is applied to control the family-wise error rate at the nominal level.

Empirical evaluation and performance validation

In this section, the performance of the proposed RFF-Cox model is evaluated across six datasets (two simulated and four real-world). The analyses focus on four key criteria: (i) the ability to capture non-linear risk relationships and covariate interactions, (ii) empirical coverage of analytical confidence intervals, (iii) computational cost, and (iv) calibration of prediction uncertainty.

Experimental setup and data characteristics

To comprehensively assess the model’s performance across diverse data structures, we utilized a total of six datasets: two simulated datasets designed to evaluate specific modeling capabilities, and four real-world datasets representing fields such as hematology, intensive care, cardiovascular diseases, and oncology. These include the FLCHAIN hematology cohort provided by Therneau [26] (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n\:=\mathrm{7,874}$$\end{document}), the SUPPORT2 intensive care data developed by Knaus et al. [27] (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\mathrm{9,105}$$\end{document}), the WHAS cardiovascular data reported by Hosmer and Lemeshow [28] ( n=500), and the METABRIC breast cancer genomic cohort published by Curtis et al. [29] (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\mathrm{1,904}$$\end{document}).

Across these six datasets, sample sizes range from 500 to 9,105, the number of covariates varies between 6 and 52, and event rates span from 27.5% to 77.6%. This diversity allows for rigorous testing of the model’s performance under varying data scales and censoring levels.

The first simulated dataset (non-linear; n = 2,000, d = 6) was generated to evaluate the model’s capacity to capture non-linear risk structures in a controlled environment. Covariates were generated from specific distributions and standardized: Age (Uniform), BMI and Blood Pressure (Normal), Smoking Status (Gamma), LDL Cholesterol (Normal), and CRP (Log-Normal). The log-normal distribution was chosen for CRP as right-skewed biomarker distributions are frequently modeled using log-normal frameworks in the literature [30]. The true log-hazard function was defined as:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned}\:\eta\:\left(x\right)&=1.3\mathrm{tanh}\left(\mathrm{Age}\right)+0.9{\mathrm{BMI}}^{2}-1.1\left(\mathrm{SBP}\times\:\mathrm{Smoking}\right)\\&+0.5\mathrm{LDL}+0.7\mathrm{log}\left(1+{\mathrm{CRP}}^{2}\right)\end{aligned}$$\end{document}

This structure incorporates three types of non-linear effects: (i) saturation (tanh), (ii) U-shaped relationships (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}^{2}$$\end{document}), and (iii) covariate interactions (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{x}_{1\:}{x}_{2}$$\end{document}).

Event times were generated from a Weibull-based process (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{{\uplambda}}_{0}=0.008$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\rho\:=\:1.6$$\end{document}). Censoring times were drawn from an exponential distribution independent of the event process, yielding an approximate censoring rate of 77.6%.

The second simulated dataset (linear; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n=\mathrm{2,000},\:d=6$$\end{document}) was constructed to serve as a controlled baseline in which the proportional hazards assumption holds and the true log-hazard function takes a strictly linear form:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\eta\:\left(x\right)=0.5{x}_{1}-0.8{x}_{2}+0.6{x}_{3}+0.4{x}_{4}-0.5{x}_{5}+1.2{x}_{6}$$\end{document}

Event times were generated from a Weibull-based process (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda_{0}=0.01,\:\rho\:=1.5$$\end{document}), with an approximate event rate of 45.0%. This dataset evaluates whether the RFF approximation introduces performance penalty when the true relationship is linear.

Five methods were evaluated under identical training–test splits and 5-fold cross-validation: RFF-Cox, CoxPH, Random Survival Forest (RSF), XGBoost, and DeepSurv. DeepSurv is a deep learning-based survival model that extends the Cox framework with deep neural networks [9]. XGBoost is a scalable gradient boosting framework widely adopted for survival analysis [31]. For the RFF-Cox model, the number of random features was set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:m=\lceil\:\sqrt{n}\rceil\:$$\end{document} and the regularization parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\lambda\:$$\end{document} was selected from the grid {\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{0}^{-3},1{0}^{-2},1{0}^{-1},1{0}^{0}$$\end{document}}. For competing methods, hyperparameters were optimized via grid search: RSF (trees: {50, 100}, max depth: {5, 10, None}, min samples per leaf: {5, 10}); XGBoost (trees: {100, 200}, learning rate: {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}, max depth: {3, 5}); DeepSurv (hidden layers: {[64, 32], [64, 32, 16], [128, 64]}, learning rate: {\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:1{0}^{-4},1{0}^{-3}$$\end{document}}, dropout: {0.1, 0.2}). The discriminatory performance of the models was evaluated using 5-fold cross-validation with Harrell’s C-index and time-dependent integrated AUC (iAUC) metrics [32, 33]. All C-index values are reported as mean ± SD across folds. To mitigate the potential bias of high censoring rates on the C-statistic, variants based on Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) have been proposed in the literature [34]; adopting this approach ensures methodological consistency with the calibration analyses performed in this study. Table 2 presents the performance values of the models across datasets along with their mean training times.

In the synthetic dataset containing non-linear components, the RFF-Cox model achieved the highest iAUC (0.92) and a C-index of 0.83, jointly with DeepSurv (iAUC = 0.92). In contrast, the CoxPH model, which relies on the linearity assumption, yielded lower performance on the same data (C-index = 0.69). These results confirm that the RFF-Cox model offers a distinct advantage in capturing non-linear risk structures.

An examination of Table 2 reveals that while most methods exhibit comparable discriminatory performance (C-index and iAUC) across the real-world datasets, there are substantial differences in training times. RSF and DeepSurv require significantly longer training durations, particularly for large datasets, whereas the RFF-Cox model delivers similar discriminatory power at a fraction of the computational cost. In the small-sample WHAS dataset, performance metrics for all methods were closely aligned, with comparably fast training times across all approaches.

Non-linear relationship detection: main effects, interactions

Partial dependence analysis

To evaluate the method’s capacity to capture non-linear risk functions, a partial dependence analysis was conducted on the simulation dataset. The simulation design explicitly incorporated a U-shaped risk function for the BMI variable, where risk increases at both low and high BMI values while remaining lower at intermediate levels.

As shown in Fig. 1, the risk curve estimated by the RFF-Cox model closely tracks the true simulation function. The predicted curve exhibits a smooth structure across the entire BMI range, displaying only limited deviations in the tail regions where data density is sparse.

Discussion

In this study, the Cox proportional hazards model was extended into a scalable framework capable of modeling non-linear risk structures using Random Fourier Features (RFF) representation. The findings demonstrate that the method offers a more flexible prediction structure compared to other survival models in both simulation experiments and real-world applications.

In simulations, the U-shaped risk function constructed for the BMI variable clearly revealed this flexibility (Fig. 1). The synthetic covariate distributions employed, particularly the log-normal structures, were selected to reflect the characteristic features of real-world biomedical data [40]. While the classical Cox model failed to capture this curve due to its linearity constraint, RFF-Cox produced a smooth prediction surface closely approximating the true risk profile. This capability stems from the RFF transformation reducing the infinite-dimensional kernel space to a finite-dimensional feature map, thereby enabling the resolution of non-linear relationships via linear optimization tools.

Backward compatibility and covariate-level inference

An important property of the proposed framework is its backward compatibility with the classical Cox model. In the linear simulation scenario (Table 3b), the equivalent coefficients derived via the first-order Taylor expansion recover the classical Cox PH estimates with negligible differences, confirming that the RFF approximation introduces minimal penalty when the true hazard is linear. In the non-linear scenario (Table 3a), RFF-Cox outperforms CoxPH, recovering the saturated Age effect and avoiding a spurious CRP association produced by CoxPH.

The Taylor-expansion-based framework (Sect. Clinical interpretation: equivalent coefficients and interaction inference) serves as a bridge between the classical linear Cox model and the non-linear RFF-Cox model. The first-order expansion yields equivalent linear coefficients (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\beta\:}_{equiv}$$\end{document}) for covariate-level hazard ratio estimation and Wald testing. The second-order expansion enables formal hypothesis testing for covariate interactions via the Delta method with Bonferroni correction. In the simulation study, the true SBP × Smoking interaction was identified, with no surviving false positives after correction. This dual-derivative capability offers an advantage over tree-based and gradient boosting methods, where interaction detection typically relies on heuristic measures [10].

Uncertainty quantification and coverage

A further key feature of the model is the analytical calculability of prediction uncertainty. While many machine learning-based survival methods produce only point estimates, this study directly derives the parameter covariance matrix via the Fisher information matrix. The covariance matrix was computed via eigen decomposition, preserving the correlation structure among parameters. This enabled the generation of analytical confidence intervals for individual survival curves. The multivariate Delta method propagates uncertainty from both parameter estimation and baseline hazard estimation, yielding more conservative confidence intervals than those obtained from single-source approximation.

Analytical confidence intervals were validated through simulation-based coverage assessment (Sect. Hyperparameter sensitivity and coverage analysis). In the linear scenario, coverage approaches the nominal level. In the non-linear scenario, coverage stabilizes at a level closely matching the bootstrap reference. The gap between non-linear and linear coverage is attributable to the cumulative effect of the Breslow-type Hessian approximation and the chain-rule amplification of variance through the survival function. This pattern is consistent with the sensitivity of partial likelihood inference to functional form misspecification [41]. Additionally, strong regularization adversely affects coverage, suggesting that moderate regularization provides a favorable balance between discrimination and coverage.

Calibration and temporal drift

From a calibration perspective, IPCW-weighted analyses confirm that the predicted probabilities are in agreement with observed event rates. For instance, the proximity of the calibration curve obtained for the 36-month horizon to the ideal diagonal line, along with the low Integrated Calibration Error (ICI), indicates that the model does not produce systematic bias. RFF-Cox predictions are in better agreement with observed event rates than CoxPH, representing a relative improvement in mean absolute slope deviation.

Ridge parameter sensitivity analysis (Table 9) indicates that slope deviations observed at certain time points are unlikely to be primarily attributable to the RFF approximation but rather reflect the interaction between regularization strength and finite-sample behavior of the Breslow estimator. At later horizons, both models exhibit slope values exceeding 1.0, indicating overdispersion as the risk set contracts. Cross-dataset validation on SUPPORT2 (Appendix C) supports these findings, suggesting that the observed slope deviations reflect regularization-induced calibration compression rather than dataset-specific effects.

Computational efficiency

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left({n}^{3}\right)$$\end{document} Gram matrix cost inherent in traditional kernel methods is reduced to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:O\left(n{m}^{2}\right)$$\end{document} with the RFF approach (Table 2). Training times remain at the scale of seconds for large datasets, whereas competing methods require substantially longer.

Practical guidance

In real-world applications, hyperparameter analyses highlight the balance between the RFF dimension ( m) and performance (Fig. 2). Performance reaches a saturation point at moderate m values; larger values increase the computational burden without providing marginal improvement. Consequently, the optimal hyperparameter map of the model can be effectively extracted via Grid Search.

Grid search analyses across simulated and real-world datasets (Sect. Hyperparameter sensitivity and coverage analysis) yield three guidelines: (i) \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$m\approx\lceil\sqrt{n}\rceil$$\end{document} suffices for discrimination, but two to three times this value is advisable for reliable uncertainty quantification; (ii) strong regularization should be avoided for analytical CI generation; (iii) moderate regularization offers the best balance. Supplementary analyses (Appendix B) confirm that subsampling for bandwidth selection and early stopping have negligible impact on both σ selection and discriminatory performance.

Limitations

The study has certain limitations. (i) The model is limited to the RBF kernel; the impact of different kernel structures could be investigated in the future. (ii) The method focuses on a single event type; its extension to competing risks or multi-event scenarios represents an area for future research. (iii) In non-linear scenarios, analytical coverage falls short of the nominal level, reflecting cumulative approximation error. (iv) Bandwidth selection on a subsample may be suboptimal for highly heterogeneous datasets. (v) Detailed calibration analysis was conducted on one primary dataset, with supporting validation on a secondary cohort; additional datasets would further strengthen generalizability. Although the RFF transformation renders interpretability somewhat more abstract compared to classical coefficients, variable effects can be elucidated by employing partial dependence plots alongside model-agnostic explainability tools such as DALEX and SHAP.

Future directions

Future directions include: (i) extension to competing risks and multi-state models; (ii) investigation of alternative kernels (Matérn, polynomial) within the RFF framework, including connections to Gaussian process survival models [42]; (iii) broader benchmarking against recent deep learning survival models, such as DeepHit, Cox-Time, and DRSA, particularly in settings where the proportional hazards assumption may be violated; (iv) theoretical comparison of the Taylor-expansion-based inference with penalized spline [43] and GAM approaches; and (v) integration of model-agnostic explainability tools (DALEX, SHAP) for additional interpretability support.

Conclusion

The integration of the Cox model and Random Fourier Features offers a survival analysis method that is scalable, statistically transparent, and capable of modeling non-linear risk relationships. The proposed approach combines the flexibility of kernel methods with the computational efficiency of linear models.

The method degenerates to the classical Cox model under linearity while providing substantial gains under non-linearity, with no risk of degradation in either regime. Findings from simulations and real-world data reveal that the method produces more accurate predictions than the classical Cox model in scenarios where the linearity assumption does not hold, while operating significantly faster than traditional machine learning algorithms on large datasets. The fundamental contribution of the method lies in its analytical uncertainty estimation based on the Fisher information matrix and the multivariate Delta method. Thanks to this approach, the model not only performs a risk ranking but also provides confidence intervals that quantitatively assess the reliability of the prediction for each individual patient. The Taylor-expansion-based inference framework bridges the interpretability gap between the classical linear Cox model and the non-linear RFF-Cox setting, enabling covariate-level hazard ratio estimation, Wald-type significance testing, and formal interaction detection — capabilities not available in tree-based or gradient boosting survival methods. In datasets where non-linear effects are weak, RFF-Cox matches classical performance without penalty. The critical advantage in such settings is not discriminatory improvement but the availability of formal inference tools — covariate-level Wald tests, interaction detection, and analytical individual-level confidence intervals — none of which are provided by standard CoxPH or tree-based implementations.

The obtained low calibration errors and realistic uncertainty bands indicate that RFF-Cox can be utilized as a reliable tool in clinical risk prediction and biomedical data analysis. Practical guidelines for hyperparameter selection are provided to facilitate adoption. Future studies can advance this potential by extending the method to the context of competing risks, integrating different kernel functions, and conducting broader benchmarking against deep learning survival models.
