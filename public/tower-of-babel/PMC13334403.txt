A novel combination approach targeting P300 and DNA-PK exploits epigenetic dependencies in acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Srinivas Vinod Saladi, Swati Garg, Ellen L. Weisberg, Abigail E. Case, Wei Ni, Siddhika Pareek, Klaus Podar, Ravi Salgia, James D. Griffin, Richard M. Stone, Martin Sattler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is characterized by poor survival, especially in older patients with relapse/refractory disease. With an apparent lack of reliable long-term treatments, pathways involved in chromatin regulation represent potentially leverageable targets. The arginine methyltransferase CARM1 is a known dependency in AML, yet clinical grade inhibitors have remained elusive. We found that CARM1 promotes DNA repair and other pathways associated with malignant growth. Dysregulation of DNA repair pathways is pervasive in AML and linked to the transforming phenotype. Interestingly, targeting CARM1 chemosensitized AML cells for DNA-PK inhibition by peposertib, thereby blocking NHEJ (non-homologous end joining). Also, H3K27ac (histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation) active enhancer marks and marks of the corresponding histone acetyltransferase, P300, were found at the promoter region of relevant CARM1-regulated DNA repair enzymes. Consequently, our results show that CARM1 dependencies could also be exploited by utilizing inobrodib, a p300/CBP bromodomain inhibitor that synergizes with peposertib treatment in AML cells. Overall, these data demonstrate a rational approach for combination therapy by exploiting dependencies through inhibition of proximal effector function in addition to essential NHEJ repair, targeting its rate-limiting enzyme complex, thereby resulting in synergistic inhibition in primary AML.

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematologic disorder with poor survival, especially in relapsed/refractory disease.1 For most types of AML, initial treatment involves induction therapy with chemotherapy agents (e.g., cytarabine), and sometimes with targeted therapy. Venetoclax-based combinations with hypomethylating agents have improved outcomes in AML and are standard of care. However, responses are often not durable due to primary or acquired resistance, highlighting the need for complementary therapeutic strategies.2,3 Long-term survival is poor for the vast majority of older patients. Some therapies that have found great success in other hematologic malignancies, such as CAR T cell therapy, have not come to fruition in AML.4 The CARM1/PRMT4 (coactivator-associated arginine methyltransferase 1/protein arginine N-methyltransferase 4) arginine methyltransferase is a central epigenetic regulator with broad substrate specificity but few targets are relevant to its known functions.5 CARM1 expression has been associated with AML growth and an immune suppressive phenotype.6,7 CARM1 itself can also be regulated through phosphorylation by the hyperactive JAK2.V617F oncoprotein, leading to increased methylation of the RUNX1 transcription factor.8 RUNX1 methylation by CARM1 may contribute to the differentiation block in AML.9

With an apparent lack of reliable long-term treatment options, epigenetic factors are potential therapeutic targets in AML. Here, we have identified an approach that takes advantage of CARM1 dependency in AML and its interplay with the p300/CBP (p300 thereafter) histone acetyltransferase, as a chemosensitizing event for DNA-protein kinase (PK) inhibition, an enzyme involved in non-homologous end joining (NHEJ). P300 is a transcriptional coactivator with intrinsic histone acetyltransferase activity that plays central roles in regulating chromatin accessibility and gene expression programs in AML. Notably, p300 and CARM1 have been shown to functionally interact in multiple cancer models and previous studies have demonstrated bidirectional crosstalk between these enzymes, including CARM1-mediated methylation of p300 and reciprocal effects of p300-dependent acetylation on chromatin states that influence CARM1 activity.10,11 This suggests a model in which p300 and CARM1 act as coordinated epigenetic regulators rather than components of a strictly linear pathway. NHEJ is the predominant pathway that repairs double-strand breaks (DSBs) in cycling and resting cells but it usually results in mutagenic events.12 Mechanistically, Ku70/80 heterodimers bind to ends of broken DNA double strands and recruit the DNA-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit (DNA-PKcs), thereby forming an active DNA-PK complex that promotes DSB repair.13 DNA damage repair is an opportune target in AML as the disease is associated with abnormalities in DSB repair as well as elevated levels of ROS (reactive oxygen species), which are thought to contribute to disease progression.14,15,16

Results

CARM1 is required for growth in drug-resistant and drug-naive AML cells

We identified the CARM1 inhibitor TP-06417 in a cell-based assay as a compound able to reduce cell growth in cell lines that were made to be resistant to the AML standard therapy drugs, cytarabine and daunorubicin (Figure 1A). TP-064 is a chemical probe that displays IC50 values that are typically >3 μM in many models.17 We found that it exhibited broad activity (IC50 7.1–24.1 μM) in a variety of AML cell lines that are treatment-naive to cytarabine and daunorubicin (Figure 1B). AML cell lines that were more sensitive to TP-064 included in this screen had different genetic background, including SKM1 (KRASLys117Asn, TP53Arg248Gln, EZH2Tyr646Cys, ASXL1Tyr591Ter), HL60 (NRASGln61Leu, CDKN2AArg80Ter, TP53DEL)), MOLM13 (not shown)/MOLM14 (FLT3 (Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3)-ITD, KMT2A-MLLT3), MV4-11 (FLT3-ITD, KMT2A-MLLT3), and OCI-AML3 (DNMT3AArg882Cys, NPM1Trp288Cysfs∗12, NRASGln61Leu) and cell lines with growth inhibition that was somewhat less pronounced included THP1 (KMT2A-MLLT3, TP53Arg174fs∗3, NRASGly12Asp, CSNK2A1-DDX39B) and HEL (JAK2Val617Phe, TP53Met133Lys). Treatment of AML cells with TP-064 consistently reduced dimethylation levels of one of the CARM1 targets, the BAF chromatin remodeling component SMARCC1,18 relative to CARM1 and GAPDH (Figure 1C). Importantly, to further validate the effect of TP-064 on growth of AML cells, we performed additional knockdown experiments. In a four-day assay, five different hairpins significantly reduced CARM1 expression as well as reduced cell growth in SKM1 cells, compared to controls (Figure 1D). CARM1 regulates the expression of DNA damage response genes

To understand the contribution of CARM1 to cell growth in AML, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and gene expression analyses following knockdown of CARM1. Notably, we observed a significant change in genes involved in DNA damage repair pathways (Figure 1E). Knockdown of CARM1 also led to an increased expression of genes associated with pathways involved in myeloid cell function or decrease in metabolic pathways (Figure S1B). This was further confirmed by Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the RNA-seq dataset, where we observed enrichment of altered DNA repair (q = 0.026) and also enrichment for E2F (Early 2 Factor) targets (q = 0.026), G2M checkpoint (q < 0.001) and MYC targets (q < 0.01) (Figure 1F). E2F targets and MYC targets are intrinsically linked to changes in SWI/SNF (SWItch/Sucrose Non-Fermentable) complex activity, which is consistent with the functional role of CARM1 in regulating SMARCC1 activity.18 A closer look at the leading edge of the DNA repair signature shows an accumulation of genes involved in DSB repair with higher expression in the control compared to knockdown (Figure 1G, top). The majority of these genes were found to co-occur in the The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) AML dataset, which is consistent with the involvement of master regulators in the expression of DNA repair enzymes (Figure 1G, bottom). Specific genes identified were validated in SKM1 and MOLM13 cells, including CARM1 as a control, RAD51 as a hallmark DNA repair gene and MYC and MYBL2 transcription factors, both with a known role in regulating DNA repair and genomic stability (Figure 1H).19,20

Targeting DNA-PK cooperates with CARM1 inhibition

DSB repair pathways are opportune targets as we and others have already demonstrated their dysregulation through oncogenes that drive myeloid malignancies.15,21,22 We selected drugs targeting these DSB DNA repair pathways (Figure S2A) and tested them on AML cell lines. In general, these drugs had variations of IC50s values within one log scale, including the CHK1 inhibitor, prexasertib (0.9–7.2 nM), WEE1 inhibitor, adavosertib (0.15–0.62 μM), ATM inhibitor, KU-60019 (4.8–7.9 μM), DNA-PK inhibitor peposertib (0.52–4.32 μM), PARP1/2 inhibitor, olaparib (1.5–23.4 μM) and ATR inhibitor, berzosertib (0.08–0.36 μM) (Figure 2A; Figure S2B). SKM1, MOLM14, and MV4.11 cells were found to be more responsive to these inhibitors, in contrast to THP1, HEL, and OCI-AML3 cells and the IC50s for KU-60019 and olaparib were higher than those measured with the other drugs. In contrast to NHEJ mechanisms (targeted by peposertib), which can be active throughout the cell cycle and in resting cells, homologous DSB repair mechanisms are predominantly active in S/G2 phase.23 Considering the role of CARM1 in regulating DNA repair pathways and cell growth, we reasoned that CARM1-targeting would cooperate with DNA-PK inhibition by peposertib. TP-064 showed a dose-dependent reduction of growth in TP-064 in MV4-11, OCI-AML3, and SKM1 cell lines that was further enhanced by the addition of peposertib (Figure 2C, top). A formal analysis of the results demonstrated synergy with several combinations in each model (highest single agent [HSA] index >10) (Figure 2C, bottom). For comparison, we also tested an ATM inhibitor, involved in the homologous recombination pathway, and similar results demonstrating cooperation between TP-064 and ATM inhibition were observed (Figure S2C). We further validated this approach by RNA interference and generated two cell lines with doxycycline-inducible knockdown of CARM1, which had different growth rates and different levels of CARM1 expression. In both cell lines, inducible knockdown of CARM1 significantly reduced growth in a three-day assay (Figure 2D). Similar to the extent of growth inhibition with TP-064, the combination of inducible CARM1 knockdown with peposertib was also highly efficacious in reducing cell growth in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 2E). Apart from olaparib, the other previously tested DSB inhibitors also cooperated with the knockdown of CARM1 in these cell lines (Figure S2D). RNA levels were measured to confirm inducible knockdown of CARM1 as well as reduction in MYC and RAD51 expression (Figure S2E).

AML cells depend on the CARM1 effector P300

In an effort to identify factors that control the expression of CARM1 regulated genes, we queried the chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-Atlas database to identify transcription factors and co-factors that bind to their promotor regions, and H3K27ac and H3K4me3, active enhancer and promoter marks, which are associated with higher transcriptional activity.24 We found that the genes we had identified involved in DNA repair pathways, including RAD51 and PRKDC (Figure 3A) as well as FEN1, BRCA1, BRCA2, RAD50, XRCC2, XRCC4, and XRCC6 (Figure S3) are enriched with binding of P300, RUNX1 and MYC on the promoters, but not on the promoter of MYBL2 (see also Figure 1H). In the future, it would be interesting to see whether MYC and MYBL2 have different functions in CARM1 signaling. Interestingly, a similar pattern was also observed for CCND3 (cyclin D3 gene) (Figure 3A), which is commonly involved in cell cycle entry and is essential for efficient DSB repair. These results are consistent with CARM1-dependent context-specific histone acetylation through P300, methylation of the BAF component SMARCC1 (required for MYC expression) and RUNX1 regulation (Figure 3B).25 Knockout data from targets within this pathway show that AML cells are particularly dependent on EP300, SMARCC1, MYC, and MYBL2 (Figure 3C). Also, in line with the transcriptional program regulated by CARM1 and p300, we found reduced protein expression of MYC in response to the p300 inhibitor inobrodib (Figure S4). We next tested the efficacy of inobrodib in our models and found IC50 values of 0.03–0.12 μM (Figure 3D, left). For comparison, HDAC inhibitors that oppose P300 activity (such as vorinostat) and lead to pathological histone acetylation are efficacious in AML cell lines (Figure 3D, right) but have not come to fruition as monotherapy in AML. In contrast to TP-064, inobrodib and peposertib are clinical grade inhibitors that are currently evaluated as therapeutics in AML or other malignancies. Importantly, when tested in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), IC50 values for both inobrodib (1 μM) and peposertib (>10 μM) were higher (Figure 3E) compared to AML cell lines (Figures 2A and 3D). Synergism between inobrodib and peposertib

Based on these findings, we reasoned that similar to CARM1-targeting, inhibition of P300 by inobrodib would likely sensitize AML cell lines to peposertib treatment for DNA-PK inhibition. MV4.11, SKM1, and MOLM13 treatment with either CARM1 or P300 inhibitors led to a dose-dependent reduction in cell growth as well as showed cooperative effects on growth inhibition (Figure 4A). A formal analysis of drug combinations exhibited synergism in our models (Figure 4A, bottom). Interestingly, we also found cooperation between inobrodib and standard therapeutics in MV4.11 cells, suggesting that the chemosensitizing effects may not be limited to DNA-PK targeting. In our experiments, inobrodib enhanced the effects of the FLT3 inhibitor midostaurin, the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax and the chemotherapeutic antimetabolite cytarabine (Figure S5). Changes in cell growth were likely due to increased apoptosis, measured by changes in caspase 3/7 activity, and G1 cell-cycle arrest (Figures 4B and 4C). Role of PRKDC and P300 in AML

AML is a heterogeneous disease and it is not entirely clear which patient population would benefit from PRKDC or P300 targeting. We therefore first looked for differences in genes expression in AML with common genotypes, compared to healthy bone marrow using the BloodSpot database. PRKDC and MYC mRNA levels did not significantly change in AML with normal karyotype, complex karyotype, (inv16), t(15;170, t(8;21), or MLL rearrangement, but EP300 RNA levels were significantly lower in AML with normal karyotype, (inv16) and MLL rearrangements (Figure 5A, left) (Figure S6A, left). Data from the cbioportal.org database showed that levels of both PRKDC and EP300 mRNA were readily detected but showed a weak inverse correlation in expression (Spearman’s coefficient: −0.39, p = 2.17 × 10−39) (Figure 5A, right). A similarly weak inverse correlation was also found between P300 and MYC (Figure S6A, right). A moderate correlation between PRKDC and MYC expression was found as well (Spearman’s coefficient: 0.54, p = 3,05 × 10−50) (Figure 5A, right). Even though the changes are significant, the robust mRNA expression and the overall level of correlation do not hint at interactions that define responses. This is also consistent with a lack in differences of survival in AML patients with above or below median levels of PRKDC, EP300, or MYC, which is in contrast to data for cell cycle regulating CCND3 (Figure S6B). We did identify differences in overall survival for the P300 associated transcription factor CEBPB though, but not CEBPA (Figure 5B, left). Both CEBPA (low) and CEBPB (low) alteration frequency showed a higher correlation with the CARM1 target RUNX1, which would point at an association of these changes with RUNX1 function that is independent of DNA repair (Figure 5B, right). Interestingly, high SMARCC1 expression is associated with better survival (in contrast to CARM1) and diverges with CARM1 in terms of NPM1 and FLT3 alterations (Figure 5C). A similar trend is also found when CARM1 is substituted by EP300 and high CARM1 expression is also associated with low p53 (TP53) alterations (p < 0.05) (not shown), overall hinting at a complex link between genomic alterations and pathway regulation. Finally, we tested combinatorial effects of EP300 and DNA-PK inhibition on three distinct primary AML samples, using a colony formation assay approach. Discussion

AML has remained a difficult to treat cancer that is elusive to many therapeutic approaches and modalities. While venetoclax-based treatments have improved outcomes in AML, resistance and relapse remain unfortunately common and are likely driven by adaptive signaling and leukemic stem cell survival.26,27 Our study proposes a mechanistically distinct approach targeting p300 and DNA-PK, which may complement existing therapies and address resistance mechanisms. Here, we show that inhibiting the CARM1/P300 axis in AML cells may limit the activity of several cancer-associated pathways, thereby creating opportunities within signaling mechanisms that can be exploited by clinical grade inhibitors. The CBP (CREB binding protein)/P300 histone acetyltransferases complex can be in part regulated through direct CARM1 methylation.28,29 Even though P300 is only one of many CARM1 targets, its substrate specificity suggests that it is a major regulator of chromatin remodeling and gene expression.30 P300 and related proteins are known to be involved in a variety of cancers and can have substrate specificity beyond histones.31 However, the broad role of histone acetylation by P300 also suggests a highly context-specific function that involves additional regulators. This is also supported by findings that CARM1-targeting primes for anti-tumor activity in AML but has no severe myelosuppressive effect in normal hematopoiesis.6,7,32 Nevertheless, for example, inhibitors of the P300-associated transcription factor CEBP can also inhibit AML cell growth, pointing at an exploitable role of this signaling node.33 As a next step to define the CARM1/P300 axis in more detail, it will be necessary to integrate chromatin state and gene expression approaches in the context of major CARM1 methylation (H3R17me2a, H3R26me2a)34 and P300 acetylation marks. Using published datasets, we have already begun to identify H3K27ac marks in a number of DNA repair-related genes, indicating a path toward combination therapy. AML is intrinsically associated with DNA repair abnormalities, in particular DSB repair, and some of these error-prone mechanisms have been linked to the propensity of the disease to evolve into drug-resistance phenotypes.15,22 Consistent with this, current clinical trials not only focus on monotherapy of inobrodib but also on combinations with standard therapy (NCT04068597). Our analyses suggest that combination of highly targeted therapy with inobrodib can exploit additional-induced vulnerability with translational potential.

The molecular mechanisms that facilitate the repair process itself have been well described and while dysregulation may be common in AML, mutations in the proteins involved in DSB repair may not be.35,36 The MYC transcription factor is a pivotal modulator of transformation in many cancers and is also likely a key element controlling DSB repair through the CARM1/P300 axis. We have shown here that CARM1 is associated with high levels of MYC and targeting p300 inhibits MYC expression. Further, we show that CARM1 activity provides a dependency on NHEJ, which is exploitable by DNA-PK inhibition.37,38 While our study does not directly assess NHEJ or replication stress signaling, it should be noted that these pathways are closely interconnected in the cellular response to DNA damage and replication-associated lesions frequently require NHEJ for repair. Of additional interest in this context is the control of MYC in part through a BRD4-containing master-regulator complex at super enhancer sites.39 This complex requires both methylation of SMARCC1 through CARM1, and recruitment of BRD4 to the histone H3K27 site acetylated by P300.40,41 In CREBBP/EP300-mutated lymphoma cells, CARM1 is required to maintain histone acetyltransferase activity, demonstrating dependent interactions between these two mechanisms.42 MYC itself is a known substrate and regulator of DNA-PK and its activity is required for functional expression of MYC.20,40 Thus, the role of DNA-PK goes beyond its role in maintaining genomic stability through NHEJ and may affect MYC-dependent oncogenic function.

While we evaluated the synergy between p300 inhibition and DNA-PK inhibition under monoculture conditions, stromal-mediated mechanisms may affect the level of synergy. We have previously shown that stromal-mediated resistance can reduce the efficacy of targeted approaches, but our data also demonstrated that rational combination strategies can overcome this effect.43 Combinations of FLT3 inhibitors with multi-targeted kinase inhibitors or JAK inhibitors exhibited synergistic activity even in stromal-supported conditions, suggesting that certain drug combinations can retain or enhance efficacy in the presence of microenvironmental protection. In addition to stromal cells-provided cytokine-driven survival signaling through cell surface receptors, intracellular programs may include the activation of NF-kB signaling and subsequent downregulation of miR-23a-5p, thereby increasing protective autophagy.44 Whether our approach can overcome stromal protection through any of these or other mechanisms would require further evaluation.

Overall, our data suggest that the cellular environment can be optimized for effective targeted therapy in AML by enhancing the dependency on DNA repair pathways through targeting of the CARM1-P300/CBP axis may not only be effective in AML cells with broad genetic background, but it can also chemosensitize AML cell to the clinical grade DNA-PK inhibitor peposertib and possibly other therapeutics. Increasing the efficacy of dependent mechanisms through targeting of epigenetic networks warrants further investigation in AML.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Human AML cell lines SKM1, MV4.11, THP1, OCI-AML3, MOLM13, MOLM14, and HL60 were grown in RPMI 1640 (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). Cells were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) or DSMZ (Germany) and used within 6 months of culturing or cell line authentication through cell line short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (Molecular Diagnostics Laboratory, Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, Boston, MA, USA). Cytarabine- and daunorubicin-resistant cells were previously described.45 Human Lenti-X HEK-293T cells (Takara Bio USA, San Jose, CA, USA) were used for production of lentiviruses. Stable cells with doxycycline-inducible CARM1 knockdown were generated by transfection of the FH1tUTG construct containing a CARM1-targeting sequence (5′-CTATGACTTGAGCAGTGTTAT-3′).46

Chemical compounds

In some experiments, cells were treated with cytarabine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), daunorubicin hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich), TP-064 (Selleckchem, Houston, TX, USA), KU-60019 (Abcam, Cambridge, MA), peposertib/nedisertib (Selleckchem), Olaparib (Selleckchem), berzosertib (Abcam), adavosertib (ApexBio, Houston, TX, USA), prexasertib (Selleckchem), inobrodib (Selleckchem) or vorinostat (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA).

Cell growth, apoptosis, and cell cycle assays

Cell growth was measured over time with the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay Kit (Promega). Caspase 3/7 activity as a measure of apoptosis was measured using the Caspase-Glo 3/7 Assay SystemCells (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Cell cycle analysis was performed using a propidium iodide method.47

Drug combination assay

Single agents were used and combined at multiples of concentrations that caused 50% inhibition. Changes were calculated as relative growth compared to vehicle-treated controls. Synergy or antagonism was assessed with the SynergyFinder online tool (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi/) using the HSA method.48

RNA interference

Targeted knockdown was performed as described previously,49 using lentiviral constructs targeting CARM1. Five constructs, each containing short hairpin RNA (shRNA) against CARM1 (TRCN0000007166 (#66), TRCN0000007167 (#67) and TRCN0000007168 (#68) TRCN0000007169 (#69) and TRCN0000280298 (#98)) were used and compared to a non-targeting control vector (Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed using lysates prepared in RIPA buffer (Boston Bioproducts, Milford, MA, USA) using 4%–20% gradient gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% (v/v) non-fat milk in TBST (Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20) for 30–60 min at room temperature and incubated overnight with primary antibodies. Signals were detected using horseradish peroxidase-coupled secondary antibodies (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) and an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Bio-Rad). Antibodies against SMARCC1 (#11956; Cell Signaling Technology), dimethylated SMARCC1 (#ABE1339; Millipore, Danvers, MA, USA), β-actin (AC-15; Sigma-Aldrich), CARM1 (#09–818; Millipore) and GAPDH (14C10; Cell Signaling Technology) were used to measure protein expression.

AML colony formation assay

Human primary AML samples were obtained under Dana-Farber Cancer Institute IRB protocol. AML cells were resuspended in RPMI1640 and seeded into 12-well plates at 15 × 103 cells/well at a final concentration of 90% MethoCult H4034 Optimum (STEMCELL Technologies, Cambridge, MA, USA) and in the presence of drug or control treatments. Total colonies were counted 14 days post drug treatments and compared to controls.

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was isolated from cells using TRIzol (Ambion by Life Technologies, Austin, TX, USA) followed by RNeasy purification (RNeasy kit, QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA). RNA-seq and gene expression analyses were performed at Medgenome (Foster City, CA, USA) and GSEA was performed against signature databases as previously described47 or analyzed online at https://appyters.maayanlab.cloud/ using the online enrichment visualizer program.

Analysis of chromatin marks

Active histone marks, H3K27ac and H3K4me3 (histone H3 lysine 4 trimethylation), were analyzed along with marks for EP300, RUNX1, MYC, and MYBL2 in blood and AML datasets using the ChIP-Atlas Peak browser tool (https://chip-atlas.org/peak_browser) and the Integrative Genome Viewer (IGV_2.16.1) at q < 10−25 for histone marks and q < 10−5 for all other measurements.

Quantitative RT-PCR

RNA was harvested using TRIzol (Invitrogen/Thermo Fisher Scientific, Carlsbad, CA, USA) followed by RNeasy purification (RNeasy kit, QIAGEN). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using the TaqMan Reverse Transcription Reagents (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), and RT-qPCR (quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction) was performed using the Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) and primers against CARM1 (F5′-TTCCAGTCACCACTGTTCGCCA; R5′-CCAGGAGGTTACTGGACTTGGA), RAD51 (F5′-TCTCTGGCAGTGATGTCCTGGA; R5′-TAAAGGGCGGTGGCACTGTCTA), MYC (F5′-CCTGGTGCTCCATGAGGAGAC; R5′-CAGACTCTGACCTTTTGCCAGG), MYBL2 (F5′-CACCAGAAACGAGCCTGCCTTA; R5′-CTCAGGTCACACCAAGCATCAG) and GAPDH (F5′-TTGCCATCAATGACCCCTTCA; R5′-CGCCCCACTTGATTTTGGA). Data are expressed as mean fold change (n = 3).

Statistical analysis

For statistical comparison between control and treated groups the Student’s t test was used, unless related groups were analyzed and then the paired-sample t test was applied. Error bars represent standard deviation (SD) (n = 4, unless otherwise indicated). Each key experiment was replicated at least once or confirmed by related methodologies, if feasible.
