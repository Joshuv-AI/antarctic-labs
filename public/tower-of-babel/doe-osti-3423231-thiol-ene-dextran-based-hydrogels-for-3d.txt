Abstract

 Three-dimensional (3D) cell-laden hydrogels have become essential tools for tissue

engineering and disease modelling because they better recapitulate the native cell

microenvironment than two-dimensional (2D) culture systems. However, commonly used

natural hydrogels suffer from batch-to-batch variability and limited stability, while many

synthetic systems lack bioactivity or require harsh polymerization conditions. To address

these limitations, we developed a dextran-based semi-synthetic hydrogel platform

designed for 3D neuron encapsulation and brain-mimetic tissue modelling. Two hydrogel

formulations were investigated and compared to control collagen hydrogels: a Michael

addition system using functionalized dextran and a cell-degradable peptide crosslinker,

and a UV-initiated thiol-norbornene step-growth system using a commercially available

pre-functionalized dextran polymer. Both approaches were selected to enable highly

specific thiol-ene chemistry and support tunable mechanical properties through variation

of component ratios and cross-linking density. Compared with methacrylate chain-growth

polymerization, our UV-initiated system requires lower photoinitiator concentrations

(0.036 w/v% LAP) and shorter UV exposure (<0.4s of 405 nm blue light). Hence, we believe

that, with further work to improve neural viability, our hydrogel formulations may provide

cost-efficient, long-term alternatives to current hydrogel systems, and may be well-suited

for bioprinting and advanced neural tissue modelling applications.

 I. INTRODUCTION

 In the last decade, three-dimensional cell-laden hydrogels have attracted substantial
interest across an array of biomedical applications, from in vitro tumor models for studying
disease progression,1 to responsive biohybrid robotic systems designed to restore lost
function.2 As opposed to 2D tissue modeling systems, 3D systems more closely resemble
the tissue microenvironment through cell morphology, access to soluble factors, and
complex cell/cell-matrix interactions,3 thus enabling more physiologically relevant
evaluation of cell behavior.

 Natural hydrogels, such as Matrigel and Collagen (Fig. 1a and b), have served as a gold
standard for 3D cell encapsulation because they provide biologically familiar environments
to support cell attachment and remodeling. However, these materials are limited by batch-
to-batch variation and limited long-term stability, due to protease-mediated degradation .4
In contrast, synthetic polymers such as PEG (Polyethylene glycol) and PHEMA (poly(2-
hydroxyethyl methacrylate)) offer tunable and reproducible chemistry,5 but inherently lack
bioactive motifs.

 Semi-synthetic hydrogels (Fig. 1c)—hydrogels with a natural polymeric backbone,
functionalized with reactive groups and cell adhesion sites—offer a compromise between

3

bioactivity and mechanical tunability. By adjusting the cross-linking ratio and degree of
functionalization (DOF) of the polymer backbone, these systems allow for precise fine-
tuning of hydrogel mechanical properties yet still create an environment suitable for cell
survival.6,7 In particular, 3D printable cell-laden semi-synthetic hydrogels have attracted
significant attention because of their reproducibility, tunability, and ability to form complex
biomimetic structures, such as perfusable blood brain barrier models.8

 Although methacrylate-functionalized biopolymers have been considered a standard
platform for decades,9 their chain-growth polymerization mechanism requires high levels
of photoinitiator and UV exposure,10 and tends to promote heterogenous network
formation. Göckler et al. reported a gelatin-based hydrogel utilizing step-growth
polymerization,11 which reduced photoinitiator requirements. While very promising, the
gelatin backbone in that system was fully degraded by MMPs in PBS with 10% FCS within
14 days, limiting its suitability for long-term culture.

 In this work, we aim to produce a mechanically tunable, reproducible, cytocompatible
hydrogel platform that mimics the soft tissues of the brain for 3D neuron encapsulation.
Specifically, we designed and optimized a dextran-based semi-synthetic hydrogel using
highly specific thiol-ene click chemistry to minimize unwanted side reactions with cellular
components. We propose two pathways for the synthesis of a dextran-based hydrogel: a
Michael addition between functionalized dextran and a cell degradable dithiol peptide
crosslinker (Fig. 2a), and a UV-initiated step-growth polymerization occurring between a
cell degradable dithiol peptide crosslinker and norbornene-functionalized dextran polymer
(Fig. 2b).

In both pathways, efforts were made to simplify the formulation for maximum usability in
other labs. To this end, this study utilizes commercial norbornene pre-functionalized
dextran polymer (HAWORKS, Ilex Life Sciences) for the UV-initiated pathway. Thiol-
norbornene photo-click chemistry was selected because of its high specificity, low toxicity,
and ability to create a homogenous scaffold.12 Additionally, the use of cyclic alkenes such as
norbornene circumvents the undesirable isomerization of a reactive cis alkene to a less
reactive trans alkene due to ring strain.

 By leveraging dextran as a stable and chemically versatile backbone and taking
advantage of the specific nature of thiol-ene chemistry, this work aims to address the key
limitations of current natural and synthetic hydrogel systems. Given the growing
importance of 3D bioprinting, we anticipate that the UV-initiated formulation may be useful
for fabricating complex brain-like structures with reduced manual handling. Meanwhile,
the Michael addition formulation may serve as a more robust platform for cell than
physically cross-linked gels such as collagen or Matrigel.

 II.  MATERIALS AND METHODS
 A. Formulation of Michael addition hydrogel

The formulation of the Michael addition dextran-based hydrogel was adapted from vendor
specifications for TrueGel3D Hydrogel Kits (Sigma-Aldrich). Hydrogel functionality was
assessed at final polymer thiol-reactive group concentrations of 1.5, 3.0, and 6.0 mM.
CRGDS peptide (NovoPro) and recombinant human versican (R&D Systems) were added in

5

accordance with previous research, to reach final concentrations of 0.75-3.0 mM and 50
ug/mL, respectively.13 CD cell degradable cross-linker (Sigma-Aldrich) was varied in
concentration between 0.75 and 3.0 mM, ensuring equimolar amounts of thiol-reactive
groups on the polymer backbone and thiol groups from the CRGDS peptide and the peptide
crosslinker. Agar was supplemented at 10 w/v% (AgaFloat, Ilex Life Sciences) to create a
micronetwork to suspend cells without exerting shear stress on the cells via pipetting.

In 96 well plates, 50 uL of gel was added to each well. After incubating at 37°C and 5% Cᵄ2
for 25 minutes, 100 uL of seeding media (NeuCyte) was added to each well. Full media
changes occurred after 15 and 105 minutes after seeding. In 24 well plates, two 10 uL
drops of gel were added to each well and plates were flipped upside down to cure. After
incubating for 25 minutes, 1 mL of DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added
initially to each well and replenished after 15 minutes. At the second media change, 500 uL
of seeding media was supplemented.

 B. Formulation of UV-initiated dextran-based hydrogel

The UV-initiated dextran-based hydrogel was composed of N-Dextran, Lithium phenyl-
2,4,6-trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP), AgaFloat, and Phenol-red free buffer at pH 7.2,
all from Ilex Life Sciences. CD cell degradable peptide crosslinker was procured from
Sigma-Aldrich, and had a final concentration of 3 mM. CRGDS peptide was contributed at a
1.5 mM concentration, and versican was added at 50 ug/mL. N-Dextran was added at 4.5
mM. The cell suspension was added to a pre-reaction mix of N-Dextran, water, buffer,
CRGDS peptide, and versican. Agafloat was supplemented and the mixture was chilled on
ice for 5 minutes. Crosslinker and photoinitiator were added and gels were pipetted into
wells. Gels were cured and seeding media was added. Wash steps were performed at the
same time intervals and volumes as described for the Michael addition hydrogel.

 C. Formulation of collagen hydrogels

A collagen-based hydrogel served as a control for the dextran-based hydrogels and was
composed of Collagen 1 (8.85 mg/mL, Corning, Bedford, MA) diluted to final concentrations
of 1.5 and 3.0 mg/mL, and seeding media. The addition of NaOH (1.0 N, Sigma-Aldrich)
neutralized the solution to the physiological pH (7.4). This reaction mixture was combined
in a 1:1 ratio with cell suspension to reach a total volume of 50 uL per well, seeded in 96
well plates. Once seeded, gels were cured at 37°C and 5% CO2 for two hours, at which point
100 uL of seeding media was added.

 D. 3D cell encapsulation

Hydrogels were seeded at cell densities of 100,000 or 250,000 cells/well. iPSC-derived
neurons were procured from NeuCyte. Post-natal mouse cortical neurons were extracted

6

from in-house dissections. A 7:3 ratio of Glutamatergic to GABAergic neurons was utilized
for iPSC-derived neurons, and astrocytes were supplemented at a 25:75 ratio of astrocytes
to neurons. After seeding, half-media changes occurred every 2-3 days following vendor
recommended protocols.

 E. UV exposure toxicity study

Control collagen gels (3.0 mg/mL) were exposed to the same dose of UV light found to be
sufficient for UV-initiated hydrogels. Gels were seeded with 250,000 cells/50 uL of post-
natal cortical mouse neurons. Exposure occurred 30 minutes after seeding to allow the gel
to begin to cure. Brightfield images were taken using EVOS M7000 technology to observe
neurite outgrowths.

 F. Gel component cytotoxicity analysis

To analyze the biocompatibility of reactive gel components, 3.0 mg/mL collagen gels with
0.75 mM crosslinker or 1.5 mM thiol-reactive groups in SLO-Dextran were seeded in a 24
well plate format. Cultures were imaged using EVOS M7000 technology to observe neurite
outgrowth. Analysis of cell viability was conducted via ImageJ by counting the percentage
of neurons extending projections within 24 hours in a reference frame. Three reference
frames were analyzed for each condition. Only cells in focus in a reference frame were
counted. Only cells on the exterior of a cell cluster were counted, as cells near the interior
may not have visible projections.

 G. Rheology measurements

Rheology measurements were performed with a parallel plate rheometer (PP25, Anton
Paar) equipped with a UV–Vis curing system (Omnicure, S1500). 3.0 mg/mL collagen, 1.5
mM Michael addition dextran hydrogel, and the UV-initiated dextran hydrogel were
assessed. A strain sweep was performed prior to gelation over a strain range of 0.01% to
100%. Gelation kinetics were recorded over a variable time period under 0.1% strain and
10 Hz frequency. The collagen hydrogel and Michael addition hydrogel were kept at 37°C,
and gelation kinetics were recorded under 1.0% strain and 1 Hz frequency, within their
respective linear viscoelastic ranges. The UV-initiated hydrogel was exposed to 14.5
W/cm2 of 405 nm light. After gelation, a frequency sweep (0.1 to 100 Hz, 0.1% strain) and
a strain sweep (0.01% to 1000% strain) were performed. Gelation time was reported as
the crossover point between G’ and G’’.

 III. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
 A. Mechanical characterization of hydrogel systems

 1. Rheology

 Prior to gelation, the linear viscoelastic limit of the UV-initiated hydrogel, reported
as the point at which G’ drops below G’’, occurred at 0.318% strain. After gelation, the
linear viscoelastic limit was reached at 505% strain (Fig. 3c). Thus, the gel became
significantly more elastic and mechanically robust through the crosslinking process. A
frequency sweep on the cured UV-initiated hydrogel also reveals a stable network across
all tested frequencies (0.1 to 100 Hz). These properties suggest that this hydrogel is well
suited for 3D neuron culture, as it can withstand internal remodelling and cell migration
while maintaining the network integrity.
 Under high-intensity UV conditions (14.5 W/cm^2), gelation—defined by the G'/G''
crossover—occurred within the first 0.4s of UV exposure, with a plateau storage modulus
reached after approximately 7 s (Fig. 3a). The storage modulus after gelation was around
379 Pa (Fig. 3b). The pregel solution exhibited a complex viscosity of 0.16 Pa·s (Fig. 3d),

8

which is within a commonly reported practical range for stereolithography resins,
approximately 0.1 to 0.5 Pa·s under comparable measurement conditions.
 The Michael addition hydrogel reached its linear viscoelastic limit at 53.9% strain
prior to gelation, and 1750% strain after gelation (Fig. 3c). Once fully gelled, this hydrogel
reached a storage modulus of around 480 Pa and was also stable throughout a frequency
sweep (Fig. 3b).
 Meanwhile, the 3.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogel broke down at 8.3% strain before fully
gelling and 96.5% strain after gelling—significantly earlier than the semi-synthetic
hydrogels (Fig. 3c). Since collagen hydrogels rely on weak and transient hydrogen bonding
interactions, the gel is significantly less elastic compared to the chemically crosslinked
semi-synthetic hydrogels.
 It is worth noting that the 3.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogel was also significantly more
viscous prior to fully gelling (8.5 Pa·s) compared to the semi-synthetic hydrogels (Fig. 3d).
This makes the collagen gels significantly more difficult to work with and causes more
bubbles and gaps to form when pipetting into their final location.

 2. Degradation study

 Collagen hydrogels exhibited a significantly faster decrease in dry weight than
dextran-based hydrogels (Fig. 4a), indicating greater susceptibility to enzymatic
degradation. Although both dextran-based hydrogels incorporated a cell-degradable

9

peptide crosslinker and therefore supported cell-mediated network remodelling, their
chemically crosslinked structure provided greater mechanical stability. Consequently,
these hydrogels are more robust and appear to be better suited for long-term cell culture
than the physically crosslinked collagen gels.

 3. Mass swelling ratio

 The 3.0 mg/mL collagen hydrogel showed the highest average mass swelling ratio
(57.5), indicating a highly hydrated and relatively open network that may support rapid
nutrient diffusion but could compromise long-term structural stability. In contrast, the
Michael addition dextran hydrogel and UV-initiated dextran hydrogel showed lower
swelling ratios of 45.7 and 31.8, respectively (Fig. 4b). While the UV-initiated dextran
hydrogel may offer improved structural robustness, its reduced hydration could also limit
mass transport and cell permissiveness. The intermediate-swelling Michael addition

 B. Collagen hydrogel

 In both 1.5 and 3.0 mg/mL collagen gels (Fig. 5), neurons were observed extending
projections within 24 hours after seeding. Cells were uniformly dispersed across the height
of the gel at both concentrations, however cells tended to aggregate more at 1.5 mg/mL.
This behavior can contribute to necrotic conditions where cells in the interior of a cluster
receive insufficient oxygen and nutrients or cannot properly remove waste, leading to cell
death. We therefore decided to use 3.0 mg/mL collagen for all future control cultures.

 2. Cytotoxicity analysis

 Once gel stiffness had been optimized, the lower cell viability observed in Michael
addition dextran hydrogels compared with collagen hydrogels could potentially be

12

explained by two factors: the absence of extracellular matrix components and adhesion
sites present in collagen, or exposure to cytotoxic gel components unique to the dextran
system. To distinguish between these possibilities, each hypothesis was evaluated
separately. First, cells were encapsulated in Michael addition hydrogels containing 1.5 mM
RGD peptide, instead of 0.75 mM, with the SLO-dextran thiol-reactive group concentration
adjusted accordingly to 2.25 mM. These gels were seeded with iPSC-derived human
neurons at 100,000 cells per 50 μL in 96-well plates. No improvement in cell viability or
aggregation behavior was observed, suggesting that limited bioactivity was not the primary
cause of reduced viability.
 To assess whether cytotoxicity contributed to the observed phenotype, a
cytotoxicity analysis was performed as previously described (Fig. 7d). Prior to seeding,
viability averaged approximately 70% for all cells. This was comparable to the percentage
of cells extending projections within 24 hours in the collagen control gels and crosslinker
exposed collagen gels (Fig. 7a). In contrast, collagen cultures exposed to SLO-dextran
showed substantially reduced projection outgrowth (Fig. 7c), with only 40% of cells
extending processes. Meanwhile, approximately 57% of cells in the Michael addition
dextran hydrogels extended projections. Together, these data suggest that unreacted thiol-
reactive groups in SLO-dextran (identity not disclosed by vendor) may contribute to
reduced neuronal viability. It is also possible that the peptide thiol groups are mildly
detrimental, but any exposure may be reduced by the smaller size and faster diffusion of
the crosslinker relative to the dextran polymer.

 IV. CONCLUSION

 In this study, two dextran-based hydrogels were developed and optimized to
support 3D neuron cultures. Our UV-initiated dextran hydrogel enabled mild curing
conditions with low photoinitiator concentrations and short UV exposure conditions,
offering a promising alternative to standard methacrylate bioinks. Both the UV-initiated
and Michael addition hydrogels experienced reduced enzymatic degradation compared to a
control collagen hydrogel---the gold standard of 3D cell culture.

 While neuron viability in these dextran hydrogels was reduced compared to
collagen hydrogels, we were able to identify SLO-Dextran (dextran functionalized with
reactive alkenes) as a likely contributor to cytotoxicity. With this discovery, we were able
to improve neuron viability by decreasing the gel volume and increasing the number of
wash steps to facilitate the removal of unreacted groups via diffusion. Future work will
focus on further improving biocompatibility in these semi-synthetic hydrogels by
supplementing decellularized ECM to provide more cell adhesion sites and signalling
factors to promote neural extensions.
