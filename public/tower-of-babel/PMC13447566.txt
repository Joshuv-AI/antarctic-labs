Nodal metastasis from an occult duodenal gastrinoma amid an overlap of xanthogranulomatous and type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis: A case report
Authors: Eun Sun Choi, Hyeon Ji Jang, Halim Song, Tae Jun Song, Dae Wook Hwang, Seung-Mo Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastrinomas are uncommon neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) that often present as occult primary lesions. Their coexistence with xanthogranulomatous pancreatitis (XGP) and type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP) is exceptionally rare. Here, we report the case of a 69-year-old man who presented with acute epigastric pain. Initial computed tomography (CT) revealed a 5.5-cm minimally enhancing pancreatic head mass with fat stranding and regional lymphadenopathy. Magnetic resonance imaging showed peripheral T1 hyperintensity without solid enhancement, suggesting an inflammatory or hemorrhagic lesion. Endoscopic ultrasound–guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy was suggestive of a NET. However, 68Ga-DOTATOC positron emission tomography-CT demonstrated no somatostatin receptor (SSTR) uptake in the pancreatic mass but intense uptake in a regional lymph node. Follow-up CT showed interval reduction (3.5 cm) with rim enhancement, further supporting an inflammatory lesion. Pancreaticoduodenectomy was performed. Histopathologically, the pancreatic head mass exhibited overlapping features of XGP and type 1 AIP. Additionally, a 0.6-cm occult duodenal gastrinoma with nodal metastases was identified. This case emphasizes that SSTR-avid nodal disease with an SSTR-negative pancreatic mass warrants meticulous duodenal evaluation. Introduction

Gastrinomas are uncommon neuroendocrine tumors (NETs), with approximately 90% arising within the gastrinoma triangle, which is bounded superiorly by the confluence of the cystic and common bile ducts, inferiorly by the junction of the second and third portions of the duodenum, and medially by the neck and body of the pancreas [1]. Although these tumors are often subcentimeter in size, particularly when originating in the duodenum, they frequently manifest initially with nodal metastasis [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. Owing to their small size, primary gastrinomas may remain occult on imaging and are often detected only during surgical exploration or detailed histopathologic examination [5].

Accurate diagnosis becomes increasingly complex when these occult neoplasms coexist with inflammatory processes that mimic malignancy. In the pancreas, several inflammatory conditions, including xanthogranulomatous pancreatitis (XGP) and type 1 autoimmune pancreatitis (AIP), are well-recognized mimics of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. XGP represents an exceedingly rare variant of chronic pancreatitis, histologically characterized by lipid-laden foamy histiocytes accompanied by lymphoplasmocytic infiltration [[7], [8], [9]]. The precise pathogenesis of XGP remains incompletely understood; however, available evidence suggests that it results from recurrent cycles of pancreatic inflammation, infection, and hemorrhage [[9], [10], [11]]. On cross-sectional imaging, XGP may appear as either a solid or cystic mass, rendering differentiation from pancreatic neoplasms particularly challenging [9,12,13]. Similarly, type 1 AIP, the pancreatic manifestation of immunoglobulin G4 (IgG4)–related disease, is characterized by lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, storiform fibrosis, abundant IgG4-positive plasma cells, obliterative phlebitis, and elevated serum IgG4 levels [[14], [15], [16]]. AIP represents a well-known diagnostic pitfall, especially when it manifests as a focal mass-forming lesion that closely resembles pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [17]. The coexistence of XGP and type 1 AIP is distinctively rare, with only a single case previously reported in the literature [18].

Herein, we report a unique case of XGP with overlapping features of type 1 AIP occurring concurrently with an occult duodenal gastrinoma and regional lymph node metastasis, which was initially misinterpreted as a pancreatic head mass with nodal metastasis. This case posed a diagnostic conundrum due to discordant imaging findings, including a somatostatin receptor (SSTR)–negative pancreatic inflammatory mass coexisting with SSTR-avid regional lymphadenopathy.

Case report

A 69-year-old man initially presented to a secondary care hospital with acute epigastric pain and vomiting. The clinical timeline is summarized in Table 1. His initial contrast-enhanced abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed a 5.5-cm, well-defined, minimally enhancing exophytic mass in the pancreatic head, accompanied by perilesional fat stranding (Figs. 1A and B). A few enlarged mildly enhancing perilesional lymph nodes were also identified (Fig. 1B). The main pancreatic duct was normal. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a noncystic, hyperechoic mass with a hypoechoic rim (Fig. 1C). Pancreatic protocol magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed central T2-hypointensity and peripheral T2- and T1-hyperintensity without definite post-contrast enhancement (Figs. 1D-F), suggesting necrotic or hemorrhagic components. A few prominent, avidly enhancing perilesional lymph nodes were also noted (Fig. 1F). Diffusion-weighted imaging showed peripheral high signal intensity corresponding mainly to the T2-hyperintense rim, without convincing diffusion restriction throughout most of the lesion (Figs. 1G and H). However, a focal peripheral area showed corresponding low signal intensity on the apparent diffusion coefficient map, suggesting focal restricted diffusion without corresponding postcontrast enhancement (Figs. 1G and H). Endoscopic ultrasound–guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy (EUS-FNAB) of the pancreatic mass was performed at the outside institution (Fig. 2A), and the initial pathologic interpretation favored a NET; however, only a single hematoxylin and eosin–stained slide was available for review at our institution. 18F-fludeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT) showed minimal peripheral FDG uptake in the pancreatic head mass, while the suspected regional lymph nodes were metabolically inactive (Fig. 2B).

The patient was referred to our institution one month later for further evaluation and management. Baseline laboratory tests showed normal liver function. Serum C-reactive protein was mildly elevated (1.03 mg/dL; normal, <0.6 mg/dL), and serum lipase was at the upper limit of normal (61 U/L; normal, <60 U/L). Tumor markers were within reference ranges, including carcinoembryonic antigen (1.8 ng/mL) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (<2.0 U/mL). Serum IgG4 and fasting gastrin levels were not measured, representing a limitation that precluded further biochemical characterization of the inflammatory and neuroendocrine components, respectively. The patient had no exposure to proton pump inhibitors. There was no known personal or family history of MEN1-associated tumors, including parathyroid, pituitary, or pancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms, and formal MEN1 genetic testing was not performed. Apart from the acute epigastric pain at presentation, the patient reported no history of chronic diarrhea or recurrent peptic ulcer disease. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy performed at the outside institution revealed no evidence of peptic ulcer disease. Upon review of the single available hematoxylin and eosin–stained slide at our institution, cytologic examination revealed scattered monomorphic epithelial cells with a fine chromatin pattern, favoring a neoplastic lesion. According to the outside pathology report, immunohistochemical staining showed diffuse synaptophysin and focal chromogranin positivity. Taken together, these findings were considered most consistent with a NET. 68Ga-DOTATOC PET-CT revealed no appreciable SSTR expression within the pancreatic head mass but demonstrated avid uptake in one regional lymph node (Krenning score 3; maximum standardized uptake value [SUVmax], 18.5; liver SUVmax, 6.9) (Fig. 2C). No additional sites of abnormal 68Ga-DOTATOC uptake were identified in the pancreas or gastrointestinal tract. On preoperative CT obtained approximately 2.5 months after the initial presentation, the mass had decreased in size to 3.5 cm and developed thick rim enhancement (Fig. 2D). Although the interval size reduction and rim enhancement favored an inflammatory etiology, the combination of neuroendocrine cytology and SSTR-avid lymphadenopathy raised concern for nodal metastasis from a NET in the pancreatic head, thus prompting surgical resection for definitive diagnosis and treatment.

The patient underwent a pylorus-preserving pancreaticoduodenectomy. The surgical resection specimen revealed a 3.5-cm, well-defined, ovoid mass in the pancreatic head that was centrally soft with purulent contents and peripherally firm with multifocal yellow nodules (Fig. 3A). Microscopically, the lesion exhibited mixed inflammatory cell infiltration mainly consisting of lipid-laden foamy histiocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, plasma cells, as well as peripheral fibrosis (Fig. 3B). From the center to the periphery of the lesion, there was a transition in the inflammatory cell composition: the central areas mainly contained foamy histiocytes and neutrophils, which was consistent with an organizing abscess; more peripherally, the foamy histiocytes were changing to a predominance of lymphoplasmacytic infiltration (Fig. 3C). The lesion’s periphery demonstrated lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates accompanied by storiform fibrosis (Fig. 3D). Additionally, there was total obliteration of a muscular vein by fibrosis and lymphoplasmacytic infiltration, a condition known as obliterative phlebitis (Fig. 3E). Immunohistochemical staining for IgG4 demonstrated more than 142 IgG4-positive plasma cells per high power field (HPF) (Fig. 3F) and IgG4/IgG ratio was about 45%. Collectively, the pancreatic head lesion exhibited overlapping histopathologic features of XGP and type 1 AIP.

One enlarged peripancreatic lymph node was identified (Fig. 3A). On microscopic examination, the lymph node was largely filled with epithelial cells arranged in a trabecular architecture (Fig. 4A). Residual nodal structures, including a fibrous capsule, subcapsular sinus, and scattered lymphocytes, confirmed the lesion as a lymph node. The tumor cells showed diffuse immunoreactivity for the neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin (Fig. 4B) and chromogranin, and focal positivity for gastrin (Fig. 4C), confirming nodal metastasis from a NET. However, no identifiable NET was present in the pancreas, and the pancreatic head lesion was inflammatory, demonstrating features of XGP and type 1 AIP. Therefore, a meticulous additional gross examination of the surgical resection specimen was performed, involving 36 additional tissue blocks from the duodenum and pancreas. This subsequent analysis revealed a 0.6-cm mass in the duodenal wall submucosa with partial invasion into the proper muscle layer. The tumor predominantly exhibited a trabecular architecture and contained some solid areas (Fig. 4D), which were histologically similar to the metastatic peripancreatic lymph nodes. Immunohistochemically, the tumor cells were diffusely positive for neuroendocrine markers, namely synaptophysin (Fig. 4E) and chromogranin, and focally positive for gastrin (Fig. 4F), while being negative for glucagon, insulin, somatostatin, and pancreatic polypeptide. The Ki-67 labeling indices of the duodenal tumor and nodal metastasis were 1.8% and 4%, respectively. Overall, 3 of 77 lymph nodes harbored metastases, with the largest nodal metastasis measuring 1.2 cm. The same histologic features and immunohistochemical staining patterns for synaptophysin, chromogranin, and gastrin expression in both the duodenal submucosal mass and the peripancreatic lymph node indicate a duodenal gastrinoma with peripancreatic lymph node metastasis. Accordingly, the final diagnosis was a clinically occult 0.6-cm duodenal gastrinoma with a 1.2-cm peripancreatic nodal metastasis.

These findings successfully reconcile the discordant imaging findings: an SSTR-negative inflammatory mass in the pancreatic head and an SSTR-avid nodal metastasis arising from an occult duodenal gastrinoma. The patient had an uneventful postoperative course and remained free of recurrence at a 10-month follow-up, as confirmed by cross-sectional imaging and serum IgG4 levels within normal limits. Postoperative fasting gastrin measurements were not obtained, representing an additional limitation in the biochemical surveillance of this patient. Longer follow-up with serial serum gastrin measurement and cross-sectional imaging is warranted.

Discussion

This case demonstrates a rare clinicopathologic triad within the gastrinoma triangle: XGP with overlapping features of type 1 AIP, coexisting with a subcentimeter duodenal gastrinoma and SSTR-avid nodal metastasis. The key diagnostic challenge arose from discordant functional imaging findings, specifically an SSTR-negative pancreatic head mass alongside a single SSTR-avid lymph node, which was ultimately resolved through integrated radiologic-pathologic correlation (Table 2 summarizes the multimodal imaging findings and final pathologic diagnosis).

Inflammatory conditions, such as XGP and AIP, do not generally overexpress SSTR and therefore appear SSTR-negative on 68Ga-DOTATOC PET-CT. In contrast, well-differentiated NETs, including gastrinomas, typically overexpress SSTR, with even microscopic duodenal primaries being capable of giving rise to disproportionately SSTR-avid nodal metastases [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. In our patient, the discordant SSTR pattern initially suggested a pancreatic NET with nodal metastasis. On imaging, pancreatic NETs typically present as well-defined hypervascular solid masses with avid arterial enhancement, whereas inflammatory pancreatic lesions may exhibit rim-like enhancement, perilesional fat stranding, hemorrhagic or proteinaceous components, and interval morphologic change on follow-up imaging. In our patient, although the initial cytology suggested NET, several imaging findings favored an inflammatory process: absence of convincing solid enhancement, intrinsic T1 hyperintensity suggesting hemorrhagic or proteinaceous contents, interval size reduction from 5.5 cm to 3.5 cm, and development of thick rim enhancement. These findings, together with the lack of SSTR uptake in the pancreatic mass, favored an inflammatory mimic rather than the primary NET. The ultimate surgical pathology confirmed this imaging interpretation, revealing a 0.6-cm duodenal gastrinoma that was not appreciably SSTR-avid, in contrast with a disproportionately large SSTR-avid lymph node metastasis. This finding supports the well-recognized behavior of gastrinomas, which often manifest as small, subcentimeter primary tumors accompanied by substantial nodal disease [5]. It highlights the ongoing debate over primary nodal gastrinomas vs nodal metastases from an occult primary [4]. Because extensive and systematic duodenal sampling in our case led to the identification of the gastrinoma, these findings favored nodal metastasis from an occult duodenal primary rather than a primary nodal tumor. Clinically, this discordant pattern should prompt meticulous duodenal exploration, as occult gastrinomas often elude standard preoperative imaging and biopsy. In such a setting, inflammatory pancreatic mimics, including XGP and AIP, should also be actively entertained in the differential diagnosis.

The unexpected NET identified on EUS-FNAB remains incompletely explained. Inadvertent sampling of an adjacent metastatic lymph node rather than the pancreatic parenchyma itself cannot be confirmed, but remains a plausible explanation. The EUS images from the outside institution demonstrated targeting of the pancreatic mass without documented intention to sample lymph nodes. However, the possibility that the needle trajectory traversed the perilesional metastatic lymph node en route to the pancreatic lesion should be considered, given the close anatomic proximity demonstrated on the surgical specimen (Fig. 3A). This case illustrates a practical diagnostic pitfall in the evaluation of peripancreatic lesions. When EUS-FNAB yields unexpected neuroendocrine cytology from a lesion with imaging features favoring an inflammatory process, careful review of the needle trajectory in relation to cross-sectional imaging anatomy is essential to determine whether the target lesion or an adjacent structure was inadvertently sampled. Such diagnostic pitfalls highlight the challenges of pancreatobiliary cytopathology and underscore the necessity of integrating cytologic findings with imaging features and clinical context.

XGP demonstrates variable imaging appearances depending on the stage of disease evolution [19]. In the early phase, lesions may appear as lobulated cystic masses with irregular thick walls, thus mimicking solid pseudopapillary neoplasm, intraductal papillary mucinous neoplasm, or pseudocysts [9]. As the disease progresses, the cystic component is gradually absorbed and replaced through proliferative fibrosis with foamy macrophages, resulting in a more solid, heterogeneous mass with delayed progressive enhancement and diffusion restriction. These features can closely simulate pancreatic cancer [9]. In our case, the MRI findings (i.e., T1-hyperintensity with central T2-hypointense debris and absence of true solid enhancement) were more consistent with the inflammatory nature of XGP, explaining the evolution from a 5.5-cm minimally enhancing lesion with hemorrhagic features to a smaller, thick-walled, rim-enhancing mass over the two-month interval.

Although the pathogenesis of XGP remains incompletely understood, by analogy with xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis and pyelonephritis, conditions frequently associated with chronic obstruction and infection [7], our patient’s XGP may have been precipitated by the underlying type 1 AIP–related inflammation. In this context, the resection specimen fulfilled the criteria for definite type 1 AIP according to the International Consensus Diagnostic Criteria for AIP, based on level-1 histology findings, including lymphoplasmocytic infiltration, abundant IgG4-positive plasma cells (142/HPF), storiform fibrosis, and obliterative phlebitis [20,21].

The coexistence of xanthogranulomatous inflammation and IgG4-related sclerosing disease has been described in the gallbladder. Hong et al. [19] reported that 16% of cases of xanthogranulomatous cholecystitis exhibited histologic overlap with IgG4-related cholecystitis; however, it remains uncertain whether these findings represent true coexistence or whether xanthogranulomatous inflammation merely mimics IgG4-related sclerosing disease. In the pancreas, XGP with morphologic overlap with type 1 AIP has been reported only once [18], making our case the second such description in the literature. Both XGP and type 1 AIP can be considered histologic subtypes of chronic pancreatitis. Whether these cases reflect a genuine overlap between IgG4-related disease and xanthogranulomatous inflammation or instead represent xanthogranulomatous inflammation simulating IgG4-related disease remains a subject of debate and requires careful clinicopathologic correlation.

A limitation of this case is the absence of preoperative serum IgG4 and fasting gastrin measurements. Elevated serum IgG4 levels could have provided biochemical support for type 1 AIP and strengthened consideration of an inflammatory pancreatic mimic in the preoperative differential diagnosis. Similarly, markedly elevated fasting gastrin levels could have raised earlier suspicion for a functional gastrinoma and guided more targeted preoperative evaluation of the duodenum. However, given the clinical concern for a NET with nodal metastasis based on imaging and cytologic findings, prioritizing surgical exploration for definitive diagnosis was a reasonable approach.

In summary, this case highlights the diagnostic complexity of rare pancreatic inflammatory processes coexisting with occult duodenal neuroendocrine neoplasms. Discordant SSTR expression, specifically SSTR-avid lymphadenopathy with an SSTR-negative pancreatic mass, constitutes a critical diagnostic pitfall within the gastrinoma triangle. This pattern should prompt meticulous duodenal evaluation for an occult gastrinoma alongside active consideration of inflammatory mimics such as XGP or AIP. Furthermore, the histologic overlap between XGP and AIP expands the recognized spectrum of type 1 AIP that can mimic pancreatic malignancy. Therefore, integrated functional and morphologic imaging, extensive duodenal sampling, and careful clinicopathologic correlation are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and guide the extent of surgical resection and subsequent follow-up.
