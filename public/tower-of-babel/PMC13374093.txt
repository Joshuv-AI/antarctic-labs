Characterizing Heat Health Outcomes and Informing Public Health Interventions with Medical Claims Data in California
Authors: I. Avery Bick, Gina Hervey, Jenny Suckale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat is now the deadliest weather hazard in the United States. California faces this hazard in two forms, acute heat waves affecting millions and chronic exposure, which affects its large agricultural workforce. The state directs climate resilience resources using tools such as composite vulnerability indices and competitive grant programs. Whether these instruments reach the communities actually experiencing heat-related illness has not been systematically tested against health outcomes. We map heat-related Medicaid claims across California ZIP codes from 2011 to 2019 to evaluate who bears the health burden of heat, how well existing vulnerability indices capture it, and whether state grant funding reaches the highest-burden communities. We show that ZIP codes with the highest claim rates have lower median incomes, more farmworkers, and more mobile homes than the state average. Heat-related claim rates rise 24.4% per 1 °C in majority-cropland ZIP codes, compared with 20.6% per 1 °C in majority built-up areas. Of three vulnerability indices tested, only the CDC heat and health index, which itself incorporates emergency room data, correlates strongly with observed claim rates. Our analysis suggests that State Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program funding broadly tracks county-level claim counts, but several high-burden counties, including Kern, Fresno, and Imperial, are substantially underfunded. We conclude that using medical claims data in conjunction with indices could lead to a more effective allocation of funding to communities experiencing heat risk in California than considering indices alone.

Introduction

Heat exposure now causes more deaths in the United States than any other weather event. From 2004 to 2018, an average of 702 people died annually from heat-related causes, increasing to 1930 deaths annually between 2019 and 2023. This trend in heat-related deaths is projected to continue in the coming years, both in the United States and worldwide. − Increased mortality from heat is only one of many heat-related health impacts. Heat exposure can also cause dehydration, heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and acute kidney failure, all of which are preventable in principle and, with improved methods of identifying high heat risk, could be minimized. California is particularly exposed: it faces heat waves that regularly affect millions of people as well as significant chronic heat exposure, notably for low-income workers in the agricultural sector. ,

In response to increasing heat risk, California has built a heat-resilience policy infrastructure that channels state funding to communities identified as priorities. The 2022 passage of AB 2238 required the state to develop a statewide extreme heat ranking system, and the same year, the California Governor’s Office released an Extreme Heat Action Plan outlining state actions toward heat resilience. A central component of this plan is the Extreme Heat and Community Resilience Program (EHCRP), which awards competitive grants to local and regional governments and nonprofits for projects such as shade structures, urban greenery, and local heat action plans. , Priority is given to projects serving SB 535 “disadvantaged communities,” which are identified through the CalEnviroScreen index. A central question for the effectiveness of this policy infrastructure is whether the resulting funding allocation reaches the communities bearing the largest heat health impacts.

Here, we use medical claims data to evaluate whether California’s heat-resilience funding reaches the communities experiencing the largest heat health impacts. We use complete patient-level claims data from 2011 to 2019 for Medi-Cal, , California’s Medicaid system, considering both inpatient and outpatient claim counts and rates for heat-related illnesses within the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) category “Effects of Heat and Light” on a ZIP-code scale. This ICD category includes heatstroke, heat exhaustion, heat edema (heat-induced swelling), and heat syncope (heat-induced fainting and dizziness). The data set captures approximately 13 million certified eligibles in 2019, about 32% of the state’s population that year.

Assessing whether current state funding reaches the communities with the highest risk of heat-related health impacts requires establishing which communities bear the largest burden of heat-related illness and whether they share any socioeconomic or land-use characteristics. We source socioeconomic data and ZIP code geometries from the 2015 to 2019 U.S. American Community Survey data provided by the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) National Historical Geographic Information System (GIS) tool and records of active mobile home parks in 2024 from the California Department of Housing and Community Development. We also rely on daily maximum air temperature from the Daymet data set and on land-cover data from the European Space Agency. We then compare county-level EHCRP grant distributions against observed heat-related illnesses across counties. Because EHCRP relies on CalEnviroScreen to identify priority communities, we also assess the degree to which observed heat-related illnesses correlate with different vulnerability indices including CalEnviroScreen.

Whether existing tools identify those communities accurately is unclear. A recent assessment of California heat action plans found that many would benefit from a more deliberate focus on health outcomes and epidemiological data. California currently identifies at-risk communities through several composite vulnerability indices including the California heat assessment tool (CHAT) heat health action index, the CDC heat and health index, and CalEnviroScreen. These indices composite heat-vulnerability covariates such as temperature, age, and occupation, but they do not incorporate empirically measured heat-related medical outcomes, and prior work has shown that composite vulnerability indices are often unvalidated against observational data and should be used cautiously in policy settings, or used alongside measures of health outcomes of heat. CalEnviroScreen, the index that drives EHCRP prioritization, contains no heat-specific covariates at all. EHCRP’s own scoring criteria reference climate data but not epidemiological data.

To characterize heat health outcomes at a granular scale, we calculate total heat-related claims and claim rates (per Medicaid eligible) on the ZIP-code scale. While this approach affords a detailed comparison of communities across California, some ZIP codes have few Medicaid eligibles, introducing significant noise into the estimation of claim rates. We mitigate this issue through a Bayesian spatial smoothing technique that borrows information from surrounding ZIP codes. This analysis reveals that the largest burden of heat-related illness in California falls on agricultural and low-income communities, that the indices currently driving funding decisions identify these communities only imperfectly, and that EHCRP funding broadly tracks county-level burden but leaves several of the highest-burden counties substantially underfunded. Together, these findings indicate that medical claims data, used alongside existing indices, could help direct state heat-resilience funding more effectively toward the communities most affected.

Methods

Medicaid Claims

We source heat-related illness claims from complete Medicaid inpatient and outpatient data from 2011 to 2019 within California, supplemented by Medicaid demographic and eligibility data. The combined data set includes claims for patients at outpatient facilities, such as urgent care, emergency rooms, and physicians’ offices, as well as patients who underwent inpatient stays at hospitals. It also includes the age and residential ZIP codes of patients. Medicaid only covers a subset of Californians: low-income communities and those under age 65. At age 65, patients can enroll in Medicare, which then becomes the primary payer for medical services before Medicaid, so many claims from elderly patients are not captured here.

The Medicaid database utilizes the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system, a standardized system for classifying diseases that is often applied for reporting medical claims. ICD diagnosis codes are primarily used for billing purposes, and thus do not always represent instances of bona fide diagnoses; they can also include errors resulting from medical coder inexperience or miscommunication between doctors and patients. Still, ICD codes are commonly applied in epidemiological studies. In October 2015, as part of a national effort, California shifted from reporting medical claims in ICD version 9 to version 10. During the same year, the use of the Medicaid Analytic eXtract (MAX) system for the compilation and distribution of Medicaid data was discontinued in favor of the Analytic File (TAF) system of the transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System, which is generally more comprehensive with higher data quality.

We aggregate both TAF and MAX data and counted all codes falling under the ICD class “Effects of Heat and Light”, denoted by the prefixes “992” in ICD-9 and “T67” in ICD-10. This class includes heatstroke, heat cramps, heat exhaustion, heat fatigue, heat edema (swelling in extremities), and heat syncope (dizziness or fainting). Each individual outpatient claim can have up to two diagnosis codes, while inpatient claims can have up to 12. If a single claim had multiple relevant ICD codes, it is still counted as a single heat-related claim. Medical claims data is subject to restrictions to protect patient privacy that limit the display of any cell with a size of 15 or less for outputs that include individuals under 18. Thus, any count of Medicaid claims or unique patients between 1 and 15 in a ZIP code is displayed as “1–15”, though counts of 0 are shown. We also exclude rates calculated using cell sizes of 1–15.

We perform all processing of Medicaid data on the secure Redivis cloud platform. First, we filter all MAX and TAF inpatient and outpatient claims data for residents of California. Next, we drop visits that did not have a documented patient residential ZIP code, a total of 547 claims, less than 1% of the total of 100,928 relevant heat-related claims. We then aggregate heat-related claims based on the residential ZIP codes of patients for that year, as reported in the Medicaid personal summary data set. Finally, we prepare count data by aggregating heat-related illness claims by patient residential ZIP code.

To develop a crude average annual claim rate by ZIP, we divide the claim counts by the mean monthly Medicaid eligibles in each ZIP across our study period (see Figure b), based on data provided by the California Department of Health Care Services. We then divide by nine, the number of years in our study period, 2011–2019, to calculate the crude rate. Since ZIP codes differ significantly in the mean of monthly Medicaid eligibles, we employ spatial smoothing to reduce noise from small-number bias by borrowing information from neighboring regions. More specifically, we use the BYM (Besag, York and Mollié) hierarchical Bayesian model designed for the spatial smoothing of epidemiological data. The BYM model accounts for spatial patterns using an intrinsic conditional autoregressive model (ICAR), which assumes that neighboring regions have similar disease rates, while also accounting for unstructured, random variation in each region. We apply a reparameterized version of the model, BYM2, which introduces a mixing parameter that quantifies the proportion of variance associated with spatial patterns versus unstructured, random variation.

(a) Majority land cover, (b) Medicaid enrollment at the ZIP code scale in California, and (c) average daily maximum temperature (°C) by ZIP, 2011–2019. County borders shown in black.

We define the neighbors of each ZIP as the 10 nearest ZIPs based on geographic centroids. The neighborhood structure in BYM2 is captured by a symmetrized adjacency matrix. The symmetrization process can create more neighbors for some regions, as regions selected by others can gain additional relationships. This larger neighbor sample can decrease variance for certain regions. Within the BYM2 model, this is corrected by scaling the ICAR spatial component based on the average neighborhood size. This process ensures fair comparison between spatial patterns and random noise. In our BYM2 implementation, we specify a negative binomial distribution of likelihood to account for overdispersion in disease counts, common with real-world disease data 1 μi=Ei·exp(β0+σ[1−ρ·θi+ρs·ϕi]) where: μ i = expected claim count in ZIP i, E i = baseline expected count for ZIP i (ZIP eligible population × global rate), β0 = intercept, σ = scaling parameter, ρ = mixing parameter, θ i = unstructured random effect, ϕ i = spatial pattern random effect, s = scaling factor.

After calculating the expected claim count, μ i , by ZIP i, we calculate smoothed rates by dividing the expected claim count by the mean Medicaid eligible population in each ZIP.

Geospatial Data Sets

We source socioeconomic data and ZIP code geometries from the 2015 to 2019 U.S. American Community Survey data provided by the Integrated Public Use Microdata Series (IPUMS) National Historical GIS (geographic information system) tool. This data set represents ZIP code geographies as ZIP code tabulation areas (ZCTA), an approximation of ZIP postal codes used by the U.S. Census. These data sets include median household income (IPUMS field ALW1E001) and the number of workers in farming, forestry, and fishing by sex (IPUMS variables ALY6E031 and ALY6E067). We calculate the overall fraction of the workforce in the farming, forestry, and fishing industries by ZIP by summing fields ALY6E031 and ALY6E067 and dividing by the total workforce size (IPUMS field ALY6E001).

We source daily maximum air temperature from the 1 km2 resolution Daymet data set, then calculate the mean maximum daily air temperature for each ZCTA across our study period of 2011–2019 (see Figure c). We derive the land cover of each ZCTA from the 10 m resolution ESA WorldCover 2020 global land cover product, which is based on imagery from the Sentinel-1 and 2 satellites (see Figure a). Using zonal statistics, we calculate the relative fraction of each ZCTA covered by each land use type. We focus on three land cover types that have been shown in literature to modulate heat-related illness risk: ,− cropland, defined as land sowed and harvested at least once per year, built-up areas, covered by man-made structures and roads, and tree cover, which characterizes areas with at least 10% tree cover.

We also investigate associations of heat-related claim rates with relative rates of mobile homes by ZIP code. We derive the number of mobile home spaces per ZIP code from records of active mobile home parks in 2024 from the California Department of Housing and Community Development. We assume that the 2024 data is indicative of the years 2011–2019, as historical data was not available. We calculate relative rates of mobile homes per ZIP code by dividing the total number of active mobile home spaces by the mean Medicaid eligible count in each ZIP code across the study period.

Vulnerability Indices

We compare our ZIP heat-related claim rates against the California heat assessment tool (CHAT) heat health action index, the CDC heat and health index, and the CalEnviroScreen population characteristics score. The CDC heat and health index and the CalEnviroScreen data are available at ZIP-code resolution, and thus directly comparable against heat-related claim rates. However, the CHAT heat health action index data is constructed at a census tract scale. We use a 2019 ZIP to tract crosswalk file from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development to convert the census tract-scale index to ZIP-scale. This crosswalk provides the fractions of residential addresses in each ZIP that come from census tracts within that ZIP. Thus, we calculate a ZIP-scale CHAT heat health action index by performing a weighted average of the tract-scale heat health action index values based on the fraction of ZIP code residential addresses contained within each census tract.

Analyses

Evaluating the Relationship of Socioeconomic and Environmental Characteristics to Heat Risk

To determine whether the distributions of these demographic indicators were significantly different in high- and low-heat risk ZIP codes, we first split all ZIP codes into groups of relative high risk (those within the top 10% of BYM2-smoothed heat-related claim rates) and low risk (the remaining ZIP codes). We apply a one-sided permutation tests with 1000 resamples and mean statistics (scipy.stats.permutation_test) to evaluate whether the distributions of median household income, percentage of farm workers in the workforce, ZIP code land use coverage fraction (tree cover, cropland, and built-up area), and mobile home space rates are significantly different between low- and high-heat risk ZIP codes. We also use Spearman rank correlation tests to evaluate the direction, strength, and significance of monotonic relationships between BYM2-smoothed heat-related claim rates and mean daily maximum temperature and the three aforementioned vulnerability indices.

Heat Wave Analysis

To determine whether Medicaid claim data can capture signals of heat health events such as heat waves, we calculate the daily Medicaid claim count before, during, and after the 2017 heat wave in the Central Valley of California. We first subset California ZIP codes to those that fall at least 50% within the bounds of the Central Valley. In the absence of official Central Valley borders, we use the borders as described by the 2003 Central Valley Historic Mapping Project. We identify the start date (2017-06-17) and the end date (2017-06-27) of the heat wave based on a 2024 report from the California Department of Insurance. For the heat wave analysis, daily heat-related claim counts are calculated in a similar fashion as previously described in the main analysis. We first filter inpatient and outpatient claims to remove claims without residential ZIP codes, as well as claims with residential ZIP codes outside the designated Central Valley region. We then sum and compare the counts of heat-related claims during the 11 days of the heat wave to the 11 days before and after the heat wave.

Heat Claims by Age and Gender

To compare heat-related claim counts of men and women by age, we first filter inpatient and outpatient claims to California and removed claims without patient residential ZIP code data. We then calculate the age of the patient at the date of service using the birth date provided in the Medicaid personal summary data set. We drop any claims without information on date of birth, date of service, or patient sex. Finally, we aggregate total heat-related claim counts by integer age for male and female patients separately across the study period into a histogram.

Regression Model

Following the hypothesis that farm workers face high heat risk, we fit a multiple linear regression model using ordinary least-squares (OLS) to model log-transformed heat claim rates as a function of temperature and land use. We calculate the log-transformed heat claim rates by first aggregating heat-related claim counts by ZIP code and day, dropping any claims without data on patient residential ZIP codes. We then aggregate the daily ZIP counts by daily maximum temperature (in 0.5 °C buckets) and dominant land cover type. We set the temperature range of interest between 20 and 48 °C, as few heat-related illness claims occurred below this range in “cropland” areas or above this range in both “cropland” and “built-up” areas. We then filter the analysis to ZIP codes that are either majority “built-up” or majority “cropland”. We also calculate the number of person-days of heat exposure to each 0.5 °C temperature bucket for each land cover type by summing the mean Medicaid eligible count for each ZIP and day.

After log-transforming the claims per person-day data (r), we applied the OLS model with a categorical variable (C) based on whether dominant land cover (LULC) was cropland or built-up: log(r)∼T+C(LULC)+T:C(LULC) where the C(LULC) term creates separate y-intercepts for baseline heat illness claims per person-day categorized by land cover type. We also included an interaction term T:C(LULC), which allows for different slopes for each land cover type, allowing temperature (T) to affect claims per person-day differently in built-up and agricultural areas. To account for heteroscedasticity in model residuals, we report heteroscedasticity-robust standard errors using HC3 covariance estimation. Model parameters are reported in the Supporting Information.

Heat Resilience Grant Funding

We compare grant funding from EHCRP’s Round 1 Grant Awards for the 2024–2025 fiscal year against the total 2011–2019 heat-related health claims at a county level in order to determine whether heat resilience grant awards were broadly directed toward regions most in need. We aggregate TAF and MAX inpatient and outpatient claims, counting all claims including codes falling under the ICD class “Effects of Heat and Light”, based on the patient’s county of residence. We manually reviewed the EHCRP grant funding to determine the target county for each grant, based on the grant recipient organizations. We then sum the total grant funding allocated for each county. Lastly, we apply a Spearman correlation to determine whether county-level EHCRP grant funding is associated with the total heat-health claims by county.

Results

Heterogeneous Spatial Distribution of Heat-Related Claims and Rates

Between 2011 and 2019, we identify 98,456 outpatient claims stemming from 27,079 beneficiaries for heat-related illnesses billed to California Medicaid and 1925 inpatient heat-related claims from 1706 patients. We map claim counts and smoothed claim rates on the ZIP code scale in Figure a,b, respectively. Heat-related claim counts cluster in the southeast, parts of the Central Valley, and generally in rural ZIP codes (Figure a). The ZIP codes with the highest claim counts (92243, 93307, 92227, 92201, and 93706 respectively) contain a mix of built-up and cropland areas, with relatively high mean air temperatures (Figure c) and low tree cover (Figure a).

The BYM2 spatial smoothing model converged well, with all parameters achieving a Gelman–Rubin statistic (R̂) of 1.00 (Table S1). The spatial distribution of rates (Figure b) is even more heterogeneous than the count (Figure a) across ZIP codes (σ = 4.56, 95% HDI: [3.94, 5.19]). We find that 98.8% of variation in heat-related claim rates explained by spatial or neighborhood effects and only 1.2% of variation due to random effects in individual ZIP codes (Table S1). The primary clusters of high heat-related claim rates are located in the agricultural Imperial Valley and Kern County areas, San Bernardino County, and select rural ZIP codes in Northern California (Figure b).

Among more populated ZIP codes with at least 1000 mean monthly Medicaid eligibles, smoothed heat-related claim rates were generally highest in southeastern regions that contain a mix of agricultural, urban, and desert landscapes (Figure a). These areas include ZIP codes within Imperial County (92257, 92283, 92227, 92243, 92281), San Bernardino County (92363), and Riverside County (92225). We also note high rates in some more urban areas, such as Santa Clara County (95113) and in sparsely populated rural ZIP codes in Northern California in Trinity (95595), Humboldt (95511), and Colusa (95955) counties.

Socioeconomic and Environmental Characteristics of High-Risk ZIP Codes

To identify the socioeconomic and environmental characteristics of high-heat risk ZIP codes, we compare the top 10% of ZIP codes by heat-related claim rates (high-risk ZIPs) between 2011 and 2019 to the remaining ZIP codes (low-risk ZIPs). In this comparison, we only consider ZIP codes with at least 1000 mean monthly eligibles (Figure ). Via permutation test (see Methods), we find that high-risk ZIP codes tend to have higher percentages of farm workers in the workforce than low-risk ZIP codes (N highrisk = 142, N lowrisk = 1214, test stat.: 0.0187, p = 0.008) (Figure b), lower median household income (N highrisk = 140, N lowrisk = 1204, test stat.: −$24,553, p < 0.001) (Figure d), and a significantly higher rate of mobile home spaces per Medicaid eligible (N highrisk = 112, N lowrisk = 781, test stat.: 0.0673, p < 0.001) (Figure b).

Interestingly, we find that high-risk ZIPs tend to have slightly higher tree cover fraction (N highrisk = 143, N lowrisk = 1221, test stat.: 0.0660, p = 0.002) (Figure e) and significantly lower fraction of built-up area (N highrisk = 143, N lowrisk = 1221, test stat.: −0.311, p < 0.001) than low-risk ZIPs (Figure f). One possible explanation is that rural areas face higher heat risk, potentially due to a greater fraction of outdoor workers or other lifestyle or demographic differences, despite also having more tree cover. The urban heat island effect associated with built-up areas may be counteracted by other factors, such as fewer outdoor workers or greater air conditioning access. We also note that heat-related claim counts are similar for young men and women, but are higher for middle-aged men compared to middle-aged women (Figure ). Indeed, the median age of patients with heat-related claims is 35 years old, and approximately 58% are male. A potential reason is that middle-aged men are generally more likely to work outdoors. −

Heat-Related Claims Increase with High Temperatures and Heat Wave Events

We identified a significant, though weak-to-moderate, positive monotonic relationship between ZIP code average maximum daily air temperature and the corresponding monthly rates of heat-related illnesses in Figure c (Spearman corr.: 0.183, p < 0.001, N = 1364). This weak correlation suggests that claim rates are likely confounded by variables other than maximum daily temperature. For additional verification of the suitability of claims data to monitor heat-related illness rates, we analyzed claims during the 2017 Central Valley heat wave, which occurred from June 17 to 27. We observed a 10-fold increase in heat-related inpatient and outpatient claims compared to the previous 11 days, an increase from 111 to 1143 claims (see Figure S2). The highest claim counts occurred in ZIP codes in Bakersfield (93307, 93304) and Fresno (93728, 93726, 93706).

Vulnerability Indices Have Mixed Associations with Medical Claim Outcomes

The high spatial variability of heat-related claim rates in Figure b poses a policy challenge. Measurably reducing heat risk with limited public resources requires decision-makers to identify and prioritize high-heat risk communities in programs like EHCRP. One common option for identifying high-risk communities is through composite vulnerability indices such as CalEnviroScreen, raising the question of whether high-risk communities are correctly identified through these kinds of algorithms.

In Figure , we compare the CDC heat health index (a), the CalEnviroScreen population characteristics score (b) and the heat health action index from the California heat assessment tool (c) against the annual heat-related claims at the ZIP code level. Only the CDC heat health index increases with claims count (ρ = 0.393, p < 0.001, N = 1359). This index differs from the others, because it includes historical heat health events, health sensitivities to heat, demographic sensitivities such as age and income, and natural and built environment conditions such as mobile home occupancy and air quality.

In contrast, we find a weak but significant negative Spearman correlation between ZIP code claim rates and the CHAT heat health action index (ρ = −0.099, p < 0.001, N = 1351). The CHAT heat health action index also uses information on demographic sensitivities, health sensitivities, and the natural and built environment, but does not incorporate any data on prior heat-related illnesses. For comparison, the CDC heat health action index incorporates emergency room visit data. While not a heat health index, we also compare smoothed ZIP code heat-related claim rates against the population characteristics score of CalEnviroScreen, which is intended to represent the vulnerability of communities to pollution, finding a weak positive monotonic agreement (ρ = 0.220, p < 0.001, N = 1351).

Heat Resilience Grant Funding Is Broadly Associated with Medical Claim Outcomes

Comparing county-level EHCRP heat resilience grant funding for fiscal year 2024–2025 to heat-related Medicaid claim counts by county, we find that EHCRP funding is broadly associated with heat-related health claims (Spearman ρ = 0.590, p < 0.001, N = 57) (Figure ). Of the total of $31.9 million in grant funds distributed, Los Angeles County received the largest amount of funding by far ($6.81 Million), but also had the largest count of heat-related claims (17,466). Similarly, Riverside County had the second largest amount of funding ($4.48 Million) and the second largest count of heat-related claims (9019). Still, organizations within the counties with the fourth and fifth-largest counts of heat-related claims, Kern (7058 claims) and Fresno (5930 claims), received no EHCRP grant funding in fiscal year 2024–2025. Imperial County, the sixth largest by claim count (4,979), received only $0.532 million. Discussion

California, like many other regions in the United States and worldwide, faces rapidly increasing heat risk. , Effective state response to this risk requires knowing who and where is most affected. Existing public-facing heat metrics estimate vulnerability through composite indices that combine demographic and environmental covariates , or report heat-related illness counts aggregated to regional scales, and much of the existing literature emphasizes population-wide outcomes such as total mortality or emergency department visits. Here, ZIP code-scale inpatient and outpatient Medicaid claims data enable us to identify the specific communities bearing the largest heat-related illness burden, evaluate whether the indices currently driving California’s heat-resilience funding identify those communities, and assess whether state grant funding reaches them.

Across California, the top 10% of ZIP codes by smoothed heat-related Medicaid claim rates had, on average, a $24,553 lower median household income and an 86.5% higher rate of mobile home spaces per Medicaid eligible than other ZIP codes (0.145 versus 0.0778 spaces per eligible) (Figure ). The income gradient we observe is consistent with prior work finding that low-income households face higher heat exposure not because they encounter hotter outdoor temperatures but because they cannot afford to mitigate indoor heat. ,, Poor housing quality compounds this effect. ,− The mobile home association we identify is a particularly direct expression of this mechanism: mobile homes typically have poor insulation and limited cooling infrastructure, so the elevated claim rates we observe in ZIP codes with high mobile home density likely reflect the combined effect of housing stock and household income that prior work has identified as drivers of indoor heat exposure.

We find that ZIP codes with high heat risk have, on average, a 66% larger share of agricultural, forestry, and fishing workers in their workforce relative to ZIP codes with low heat risk (Figure b), and heat-related claim rates increase faster in majority cropland ZIP codes than in majority built-up ZIP codes (Figure ). These patterns are consistent with prior epidemiological work showing that outdoor workers face heat-related illness rates well above the general workforce, with farmworkers approximately 35 times more likely to experience heat illness than workers in other occupations. − Our observation that younger and middle-aged men have elevated heat-related claim counts (Figure ) likely reflects the demographic composition of California’s outdoor agricultural workforce, which is predominantly young to middle-aged and male, − , rather than an underlying physiological difference.

The agricultural and housing patterns described above have a spatial expression: the heaviest heat-related illness burden in California falls on rural communities rather than the metropolitan areas that have been the focus of much previous literature. , Los Angeles County had the largest absolute count of heat-related Medicaid claims between 2011 and 2019 (17,466), but rural ZIP codes face disproportionately high claims per person (Figures b and a). Among ZIP codes with at least 1000 Medicaid eligibles, Imperial County alone contains 6 of the 10 top ZIP codes (92257, 92283, 92227, 92243, 92281, 92249), several of which contain substantial cropland, consistent with the higher temperature sensitivity of agricultural areas shown in our cropland-area regressions. The California Department of Public Health independently reached a similar conclusion by classifying Imperial County as having the highest rate of occupational heat-related illness emergency department visits of all California counties between 2016 and 2023. Several rural ZIP codes in Northern California also have elevated heat-related claim rates despite lower average temperatures, consistent with prior reports of occupational heat risk in the regional forestry industry and possible comorbidities from combined heat and wildfire smoke exposure.

Whether California’s funding for heat resilience reaches these at-risk communities depends on whether the vulnerability indices that drive grant prioritization identify these communities in the first place. CalEnviroScreen, the index used to designate SB 535 “disadvantaged communities” and thereby determine EHCRP prioritization, , does so only weakly. Of the three indices tested (CHAT’s heat health action index, the CDC heat and health index, and CalEnviroScreen), only the CDC heat and health index, which itself incorporates emergency room data, correlates strongly with smoothed heat-related claim rates; the CalEnviroScreen population characteristics score correlates only weakly (Figure b). This is consistent with CalEnviroScreen being a composite of various environmental and socioeconomic variables that do not directly relate to heat exposure or heat-related health outcomes. The generic view of vulnerability embedded in CalEnviroScreen, unrelated to a particular stressor, has been criticized extensively in the scientific literature. −

At the county level, EHCRP grant funding broadly tracks heat-related claim counts (Spearman ρ = 0.590, p < 0.001, N = 57), suggesting that the program is, in aggregate, directing resources toward counties with elevated heat-related illness. However, several high-burden communities are not currently prioritized. Of the approximately $32 million distributed by EHCRP in Round 1, only one small planning grant of $531,764 (1.7% of total funds distributed) was awarded within Imperial County, roughly what each of California’s 58 counties would receive under equal distribution. Other counties with high heat-related claim counts, such as Kern and Fresno, had no organizations receiving EHCRP grants in fiscal year 2024–2025. We note that EHCRP’s Round 1 grant funding criteria did not explicitly consider heat-related health outcomes, , and the awarding of extra points to grantees based on CalEnviroScreen , means it inherits CalEnviroScreen’s lack of heat-specific covariates.

Our results suggest that incorporating epidemiological data into grant scoring could help direct state funding to the communities currently facing the largest heat health impacts, complementing rather than replacing existing vulnerability indices. SB 1000 provides an analogous opening at the local level, requiring environmental justice plans that draw on “historical data on natural events and hazards, including locally prepared maps of areas subject to previous risk, areas that are vulnerable, and sites that have been repeatedly damaged”, language that fits claims data naturally. Such integration could build on the prioritization criteria already in use rather than requiring new policy infrastructure.

The case for using claims data in policy must be weighed against the populations the data do not capture. Medicaid covers low-income Californians under age 65, so heat-related illnesses among elderly residents and undocumented workers, including a substantial fraction of California’s farmworkers, are systematically undercounted. Workplace conditions may further compound this undercount: a 2021 UC Merced survey of 1200 farmworkers found that 20% lacked temperature monitoring on hot days and 22% reported no heat illness monitoring above 95 °F, suggesting that heat illness may go unrecognized in the workplace before it would generate a medical claim. The agricultural-workforce finding is therefore a lower bound on actual heat exposure. Finally, the analysis does not capture heat-induced exacerbations of other conditions such as renal or mental health issues , and aggregates illnesses of varying severity within the ICD “Effects of Heat and Light” category. Total heat-related health impact in California is almost certainly larger and more uneven than Medicaid claims reflect.

Even with these limitations, the spatial and demographic patterns in heat-related Medicaid claims are clear and policy-relevant. Agricultural and low-income communities, particularly in Imperial County and other rural parts of the state, bear the largest share of California’s heat health burden, and the vulnerability indices and grant programs currently used to direct heat-resilience funding do not consistently identify and support them. Medical claims data offers California a way to test and refine the prioritization mechanisms the state already has in place, without requiring new policy infrastructure. As heat risk continues to grow, using observed health outcomes to guide resilience investment will become an increasingly valuable complement to the index-based approaches that currently shape funding decisions.
