Association of interleukin-33 gene polymorphisms with susceptibility to Plasmodium falciparum infection
Authors: Nada H Aljarba, Fatimah Alghnnam, Arwa A Al-Qahtani, Tahani Mohamed Al-Hazani, Abdullah Al Marzan, Marie Fe F Bohol, Fatimah Alhamlan, Mohammed I Shafeai, Fuad H Rudiny, Ali M Motaen, Saad Alkahtani, Ahmed A Al-Qahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHost immune genetic variation contributes to inter-individual differences in susceptibility to Plasmodium falciparum malaria and parasite-burden response. Interleukin-33 (IL-33), an IL-1 family alarmin signaling through ST2, regulates innate and adaptive immune responses during infection. Although IL-33/ST2 signaling has been implicated in malaria-related immune regulation, the contribution of IL-33 genetic variation to human P. falciparum susceptibility remains insufficiently defined.MethodsThis case–control study included 250 individuals with active P. falciparum malaria and 200 healthy controls from the Jazan region of Southwestern Saudi Arabia. Thirteen IL-33 SNPs were genotyped, and associations were evaluated in relation to malaria susceptibility, parasite-density strata, age-defined patient groups, haplotypes, and luciferase reporter activity.Resultsrs10118795 CT was associated with increased malaria susceptibility (OR = 1.584, 95% CI 1.068–2.350; p = 0.022), whereas rs55726619 CT showed a protective association (OR = 0.042, 95% CI 0.002–0.725; p < 0.001). rs55726619 showed the most consistent parasite-burden association, including with the highest parasite-density category. In children aged 1–5 years, rs10815393 CC was strongly enriched compared with patients aged ≥ 6 years (100.0% vs. 2.1%; χ2 = 100.8; p < 0.001; adjusted OR = 650.46, 95% CI 32.88–12869.02; Fisher’s p = 7.12 × 10⁻11), as was rs55726619 CC (100.0% vs. 7.8%; χ2 = 62.04; p < 0.001; adjusted OR = 172.69, 95% CI 9.50–3138.11; Fisher’s p = 5.91 × 10⁻⁸). rs1332290 GG showed a nominal age-associated difference (28.6% vs. 16.5%; OR = 0.098, 95% CI 0.005–2.093; p = 0.046). Haplotype set 4 was significantly associated with malaria susceptibility (p = 2.76 × 10⁻⁶), and luciferase reporter assays showed allele-dependent differences in IL-33 regulatory activity.ConclusionIL-33 variants, particularly rs55726619 and rs10815393, may influence P. falciparum susceptibility, parasite-burden response, and age-dependent host genetic effects, supporting IL-33/ST2 signaling as a relevant pathway for further validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12936-026-05996-9.Keywords: Malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, Interleukin-33, Single nucleotide polymorphisms, Gene expression, Luciferase reporter assay

Introduction

Malaria remains a major global health challenge, affecting millions annually and contributing to significant morbidity and mortality. It is caused by Plasmodium protozoan parasites, which are transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1]. Of the six Plasmodium species currently recognized to infect humans [2] (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale curtisi, P. ovale wallikeri, P. malariae, and P. knowlesi) P. falciparum is the most life-threatening, accounting for the majority of severe cases and deaths, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa [3].

The pathogenesis of P. falciparum malaria is complex, involving multiple factors such as parasite virulence, host immune response, and environmental conditions [4]. While the host immune response is essential for controlling parasite replication and clearing the infection, it also contributes to severe complications, including cerebral malaria and severe malarial anemia [5]. The clinical manifestations and outcomes of malaria result from complex interactions between the parasite, host, and environment. A key modulator of these interactions is the host immune response, which can be both protective and pathogenic. Malaria immunity involves both innate and adaptive components and is mediated by various cytokines, small proteins that regulate inflammation and immune cell activity [6].

Cytokines play a critical role in malaria pathogenesis by influencing parasite clearance, immunity development, and disease severity [7]. Their effects vary depending on the type, timing, and magnitude of production. For instance, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) contribute to parasite control but can also induce fever, anemia, and tissue damage [8]. Anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), help mitigate inflammation and tissue damage but may also hinder parasite clearance and immune response development [9].

Interleukin-33 (IL-33) is a cytokine of the IL-1 family, expressed by various cell types, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells [10]. It binds to its receptor, ST2, which is expressed on multiple immune cells, such as mast cells, basophils, eosinophils, Th2 cells, and regulatory T cells. Depending on the cellular context and immune environment, IL-33 can exert both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects [11]. While the immunological role of IL-33 has been elucidated in experimental malaria models, its specific genetic impact on human malaria susceptibility remains under-investigated. Some studies suggest that IL-33 may confer a protective effect against malaria by enhancing the Th2 immune response, promoting the production of anti-parasitic antibodies, and facilitating eosinophil-mediated parasite clearance [12]. However, other research indicates that IL-33 could have detrimental effects, such as driving the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF and IL-6, which may exacerbate inflammation and tissue damage [13].

Genetic polymorphisms in the human host can influence susceptibility to P. falciparum malaria and the severity of disease outcomes [14]. Several genes have been linked to different malaria phenotypes, including sickle cell trait, glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency, and the Duffy blood group antigen [14]. However, these genetic factors do not fully explain the variation in malaria susceptibility and severity, suggesting the involvement of additional genes or pathways.

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which are single-base changes in the DNA sequence, represent a potential source of genetic variation that may influence the host response to malaria. SNPs can modify gene expression or function, thereby affecting the production or activity of cytokines and other immune-related molecules [15]. IL-33 gene polymorphisms have been linked to various inflammatory and allergic diseases, including asthma, atopic dermatitis, and rheumatoid arthritis [16]. However, their role in malaria susceptibility and severity remains unexplored.

In this study, we examined the association between IL-33 gene polymorphisms and P. falciparum malaria pathology in a cohort of patients from Saudi Arabia, where malaria remains endemic in certain regions and is targeted for elimination. Additionally, we explored potential mechanisms by which IL-33 polymorphisms may influence malaria pathogenesis through the modulation of IL-33 gene expression.

Methods

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Review Committee of King Fahad Central Hospital (KFCH), Jazan, Saudi Arabia (Registry Number 041). The study was performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and for minors, consent was provided by their legal guardians. To ensure confidentiality, patient identifiers were removed from data and biological samples. Demographic and clinical information was recorded and securely stored on a password-protected computer, accessible only to authorized research staff.

Study design

This case–control study included blood samples from 250 individuals with active P. falciparum malaria and 200 healthy controls from the Jazan region of Southwestern Saudi Arabia. Malaria cases were recruited from the Malaria Center in Jazan at the time of active infection. Although the malaria control program in Saudi Arabia, first initiated in 1948 in the Eastern province, later jointly implemented with the World Health Organization in 1952, and formally established nationwide in 1956 [17], has substantially reduced malaria cases, the disease remains prevalent in Southwestern regions such as Jazan and Aseer. A recent study reported that P. falciparum accounted for 94.3% of malaria cases in the Jazan region.

P. falciparum infection in cases was confirmed using both Giemsa-stained blood smear microscopy and species-specific nested polymerase chain reaction (nested PCR), thereby ensuring parasitological and molecular confirmation of infection. Healthy controls were recruited from the same geographic community in the Jazan region to reduce differences in regional exposure background.

At enrollment, all controls were screened using the same diagnostic approaches, including Giemsa-stained blood smear microscopy and nested PCR, and were confirmed negative for P. falciparum infection. In addition, controls had no reported history of previous malaria infection. Participants were excluded if they were pregnant, immunocompromised, or had received antimalarial therapy before enrollment.

Available demographic and malaria-related clinical information was recorded at enrollment, with age and microscopically quantified parasite density used as the analyzable host and disease-burden variables for genotype–phenotype assessment. Age was evaluated through predefined biologically relevant strata among infected patients, including early childhood, broader pediatric age, and adolescent/young-adult versus adult comparisons. Parasite density was used as the objective malaria-burden phenotype because it was measured directly from thick blood smears and reflected graded parasite biomass across predefined microscopy-based categories.

The case–control comparison was used to evaluate genetic susceptibility to active P. falciparum infection, whereas the parasite-density and age-stratified analyses were conducted within infected patients to evaluate genotype associations with malaria burden and age-related immune-development patterns. Age was available for stratified analysis; however, sex-stratified, ethnicity-specific, socioeconomic, detailed symptom-level, and prospective clinical outcome classifications such as asymptomatic infection, uncomplicated malaria, cerebral malaria, or severe malarial anemia were not part of the analyzable genotype–phenotype dataset. Accordingly, symptom–parasitemia correlation was not modeled as a separate endpoint; instead, the available objective disease-burden phenotype, parasite density, was analyzed across three predefined parasite-burden contrasts and further integrated with age-stratified, haplotype, and functional reporter evidence in the proxy disease-burden reanalysis.

Parasitemia levels were assessed by examining thick blood smears and counting P. falciparum parasites per 200 white blood cells (WBCs). The P. falciparum malaria patients were grouped into four categories on the basis of parasite density: (1) Group 1: 1 to 10 malarial parasites per 100 thick blood smear fields; (2) Group 2: 11 to 100 malarial parasites per 100 thick blood smear fields; (3) Group 3: 1 to 10 malarial parasites per single thick blood smear field; and (4) Group 4: > 10 malarial parasites per single thick blood smear field [18]. These microscopy-based cut-offs reflect a graded increase in parasite burden. Groups 1 and 2 represent lower parasite densities detected across 100 thick-smear fields, whereas Groups 3 and 4 represent higher parasite densities detectable within individual thick-smear fields. Group 4 (> 10 parasites/single thick-smear field) was therefore considered the highest parasitemia category and used as an operational proxy for the greatest parasite biomass in the present genotype–parasite burden analysis. Importantly, this grouping was used to stratify parasite density and was not intended to independently define clinical severe malaria in the absence of additional severity markers.

Selection of SNPs for assessment

Genomic organization of the IL-33 gene on chromosome 9 and the locations of the 13 SNPs genotyped for association analyses are shown in Fig. 1. Thirteen IL-33 gene SNPs (rs12336076, rs10975514, rs4742170, rs8172, rs10975520, rs10815393, rs10118795, rs55726619, rs1332290, rs10975516, rs1317230, rs1929992, and rs7044343) were selected for genotyping using a candidate-gene/tag-SNP approach.

To our knowledge, these individual IL-33 SNPs had not previously been directly evaluated for association with P. falciparum malaria susceptibility. Therefore, SNP selection was based primarily on biological plausibility and genetic coverage of common IL-33 variation rather than on prior SNP-specific malaria association evidence.

IL-33 was selected as a biologically relevant candidate gene because IL-33/ST2 signaling has been implicated in malaria-related immune regulation, including experimental cerebral malaria, innate immune activation, regulatory T-cell responses, macrophage polarization, and parasite-burden-associated inflammatory responses [19, 20]. The SNP panel was selected to capture common variation across functionally relevant regions of the IL-33 gene, including promoter, intronic, and regulatory regions with potential influence on gene expression or immune function. The selection criteria included: (i) minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 5%; (ii) linkage disequilibrium pruning to avoid redundant markers, using an LD threshold of r2 < 0.8; (iii) location within or near potentially regulatory IL-33 regions; and (iv) availability of validated commercial TaqMan genotyping assays. Thus, the prior malaria-related evidence supporting SNP selection refers to the IL-33/ST2 pathway and IL-33 expression/serum-level biology, not to previously established malaria associations for each individual SNP. Details of the IL-33 gene SNPs used in this study are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Genotyping of SNPs

DNA was extracted from blood samples using the DNeasy commercial kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. SNP genotyping was performed using commercially available predesigned TaqMan SNP genotyping assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Because these assays contain proprietary primer/probe sequences, the individual probe sequences are not provided; instead, the corresponding commercial TaqMan Assay IDs are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Each genotyping reaction was carried out in a 20-µl volume containing 20 ng of genomic DNA and the assay-specific TaqMan primer/probe mix. The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Genotype calls were generated using Thermo Fisher TaqMan Genotyper Software. Assay performance and genotype clustering were evaluated using ΔRn fluorescence values for the allele-specific probes. Quality control measures included duplicate genotyping of 10% of samples, exclusion of samples with a call rate below 95%, and verification of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) in the control group. Importantly, no restriction fragment length polymorphism (RFLP)-based genotyping was performed. The restriction enzymes BglII, XhoI, SacI, and XbaI were used only during preparation of luciferase reporter constructs and were not used for SNP genotyping.

Luciferase reporter assays

The luciferase reporter assay constructs were generated by cloning IL-33 regulatory fragments containing the relevant SNP variants into reporter vectors; restriction enzymes BglII, XhoI, SacI, and XbaI were used exclusively for restriction digestion/cloning and construct verification during the luciferase reporter assay workflow, not for SNP genotyping.

For the luciferase reporter assays, IL-33 regulatory fragments encompassing the 5′UTR SNPs rs12343534 (A/C) and rs73639580 (C/G), as well as the 3′UTR SNPs rs55726619 (C/T) and rs1048274 (A/G), were PCR-amplified and cloned into luciferase reporter plasmids. The 5′UTR regulatory fragments were cloned into the pGL3 Basic Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), while the 3′UTR fragments were cloned into the pmirGLO Luciferase Reporter Vector. Primers contained 5′ restriction-enzyme recognition sequences to facilitate directional cloning. The 5′UTR fragments were digested using BglII and XhoI, whereas the 3′UTR fragments were digested using SacI and XbaI. These restriction enzymes were used exclusively for reporter construct generation and were not used for SNP genotyping. Details of the primers, reaction conditions, and chromosomal positions of the cloned 5′UTR and 3′UTR fragments are provided in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

PCR amplification was performed using HotStar PCR (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α-competent cells, and colony selection was carried out on ampicillin-containing plates. Genomic DNA was extracted from selected clones using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The plasmids were then transfected into HEK293 cells (Human Embryonic Kidney 293 cells) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Qiagen). After 48 h, transfected cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was measured.

Statistical analysis

SNPs in the IL-33 gene were analyzed using Haploview software version 4.0 (Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard) to compute haplotype frequencies, minor allele frequencies (MAF), and linkage disequilibrium patterns. Haplotype analysis was performed using Haploview based on linkage disequilibrium patterns among the genotyped IL-33 variants. Haplotypes were estimated from SNPs that formed an informative LD-defined block and were present at sufficient frequency for comparison between cases and controls. The final haplotype analysis included five IL-33 SNPs—rs4742170, rs10975514, rs10975516, rs1317230, and rs10975520—which showed suitable LD structure and generated 11 observed haplotype combinations for case–control comparison. Haplotype frequencies were compared between P. falciparum-infected patients and healthy controls, and haplotypes with sufficient frequency were interpreted in relation to malaria susceptibility. Genotypic association testing was conducted using the DeFinetti program. To assess potential inheritance patterns, genotype, allele, dominant, and recessive model analyses were performed. The genotypic model was used to evaluate genotype-specific effects, the allelic model was used to assess allele-burden effects, and dominant/recessive models were included to evaluate biologically plausible inheritance patterns and to improve interpretability where genotype counts were sparse, particularly for low-frequency variants. Association results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was assessed in the control group to evaluate deviation from expected genotype distributions.

Statistical significance was initially defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05. Because multiple SNPs, genetic models, and malaria-related phenotypes were evaluated, additional post hoc sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the strongest signals. For the primary SNP-level susceptibility analysis, a Bonferroni-adjusted SNP-level threshold of p < 0.00385 was used for 13 SNPs. Because genotype, allele, dominant, and recessive models are biologically related and statistically correlated, model-specific p-values were interpreted within this predefined SNP-level framework rather than as independent unrelated tests. For age-stratified comparisons with small cell counts, key genotype associations were further evaluated using Fisher’s exact test with Haldane–Anscombe adjustment for odds ratio estimation where zero cells were present. A conservative Bonferroni sensitivity correction was additionally applied across all age-stratified genotype/model comparisons reported in Tables 5, 6, 7. Using 234 comparisons as the most conservative denominator, the Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold was p < 2.14 × 10⁻4. For haplotype analysis, a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < 0.0045 was used for 11 haplotypes.

To address statistical power, a post hoc minimum detectable effect-size analysis was performed using the available case–control sample size of 250 malaria cases and 200 controls. Based on the observed MAF range of 0.068–0.496, the study had approximately 80% power to detect allele-level odds ratios of approximately 1.46–1.91 at α = 0.05 and approximately 1.65–2.29 after SNP-level correction across 13 SNPs. Results

IL-33 genotype and allele frequencies in P. falciparum-infected patients versus healthy controls

To investigate the relationship between genotypic variations and the pathomechanism of malaria, the genotypic distributions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were analyzed in both the control group and the patient group (Table 1). Among the SNPs examined, rs10118795, rs55726619, and rs10975516 exhibited significant genotypic variations associated with malaria infection. Specifically, the heterozygous CT genotype of rs10118795 was significantly linked to an increased risk of malaria infection (OR = 1.584, 95% CI 1.068–2.350, χ2 = 5.24; p = 0.022). Under the dominant model, the T allele was significantly associated with an increased risk of malaria infection (OR = 1.520, 95% CI 1.044–2.214, χ2 = 4.79; p = 0.029). Analysis of the rs55726619 SNP revealed that the heterozygous CT genotype was significantly associated with protection against malaria infection (OR = 0.042, 95% CI 0.002–0.725, χ2 = 11.0; p < 0.001), suggesting that individuals carrying this genotype have a reduced likelihood of developing malaria. In contrast, while the genotypes of the rs10975516 SNP did not show a significant association with malaria risk, the dominant model for A allele revealed that carriers of A allele (AG + AA) had significantly higher odds of malaria compared with GG homozygotes (OR = 1.595, 95% CI 1.028–2.475, χ2 = 4.38; p = 0.036).

A post hoc statistical sensitivity summary is provided in Supplementary Table 4. The minimum detectable effect-size analysis indicated that the available case–control sample size was adequate to detect moderate-to-large allele-level genetic effects across the observed MAF range. The strongest findings also remained robust under predefined correction or sensitivity thresholds. In the case–control susceptibility analysis, rs55726619 remained significant after SNP-level correction across 13 IL-33 SNPs. In the parasite-density analyses, rs55726619 showed the most consistent association with parasite-burden strata, including robust associations in the lowest-versus-higher parasite-burden comparison and in the highest parasite-burden comparison. In the age-stratified analysis, rs10815393 and rs55726619 remained significant after Fisher’s exact sensitivity testing and conservative Bonferroni correction. In the haplotype analysis, haplotype set 4 remained significant after correction across 11 haplotypes. These findings indicate that the principal results were supported by post hoc robustness assessment and were not limited to isolated nominal associations.

A proxy disease-burden reanalysis is summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Because symptom-level clinical severity categories, systemic inflammatory markers, and hemoglobin/severe-anemia measurements were not available as analyzable retrospective endpoints, parasite density was used as the objective malaria-burden phenotype. Patients were evaluated across three predefined parasite-burden contrasts: Group 1 vs Groups 2 + 3 + 4, Groups 1 + 2 vs Groups 3 + 4, and Groups 1 + 2 + 3 vs Group 4. This integrated analysis showed convergent evidence for rs55726619 across parasite-density distribution, highest parasite-burden comparison, age-dependent enrichment in infected children aged 1–5 years, and 3′UTR luciferase reporter activity, supporting rs55726619 as the most consistent IL-33 variant linking susceptibility, parasite burden, age-dependent response, and regulatory activity.

Association of IL-33 genotype and allele frequencies with parasite density in P. falciparum-infected patients

To identify SNPs associated with parasitemia or parasite burden, patients were categorized into four groups based on escalating parasite density: Group 1 (lowest density, n = 14), Group 2 (n = 24), Group 3 (n = 52), and Group 4 (highest density, n = 160). The association between 13 selected polymorphisms and varying parasitemia levels in P. falciparum-infected individuals was then analyzed. Allelic and genotypic frequencies were compared between Group 1 and Groups 2, 3, and 4 (Table 2). The findings revealed that two SNPs, rs12336076 and rs55726619, were associated with parasite burden. For rs12336076, carriers of AC and CC genotypes had markedly reduced odds of being in the Group 2 + 3 + 4 compared with other genotypes (OR = 0.096, 95% CI 0.006–1.659, χ2 = 4.83; p = 0.028) and (OR = 0.102, 95% CI 0.005–1.936, χ2 = 4.21; p = 0.040), respectively. Under the dominant model, the rs12336076-C allele was also associated with lower parasite burden (OR = 0.101, 95% CI 0.006–1.712, χ2 = 4.68; p = 0.030). Similarly, for rs55726619, both the CT and CC genotypes were associated with reduced parasitemia (OR = 0.000, 95% CI 0.000–0.004, χ2 = 231; p < 0.001 and OR = 0.028, 95% CI 0.001–0.717, χ2 = 11.52; p < 0.001, respectively). Interestingly, in the allelic model, the T allele was significantly associated with reduced risk of high parasite density (OR = 0.072, 95% CI 0.032–0.162, χ2 = 60.91; p < 0.001). Under the dominant model, rs55726619 C-carrier status also showed a strong association with parasite-density grouping (OR = 0.003, 95% CI 0.000–0.051, χ2 = 101.03; p < 0.001), indicating that rs55726619 contributed substantially to differentiation between the lowest parasite-burden group and higher parasite-burden categories.

Association of IL-33 genotype and allele frequencies with age in P. falciparum-infected patients

To evaluate whether IL-33 genotype distributions differed across biologically relevant stages of anti-malarial immune development, age-stratified analyses were performed among P. falciparum-infected patients using three predefined comparisons. The 1–5 years versus ≥ 6 years comparison was used to evaluate early childhood, when anti-malarial immunity is least developed; the 1–9 years versus ≥ 10 years comparison was used to evaluate the broader pediatric age range; and the 10–20 years versus ≥ 21 years comparison was used to compare adolescent/young-adult patients with adults, who are more likely to have acquired partial immunity through repeated exposure. These analyses were presented in separate tables to avoid overcrowding and to allow each age threshold to be interpreted within its corresponding biological context. We first compared children aged 1–5 years with patients aged ≥ 6 years (Table 5).

The strongest age-dependent signals were observed for rs10815393 and rs55726619. For rs10815393, the CC genotype was markedly enriched in the 1–5 years group compared with the ≥ 6 years group (100.0% vs. 2.1%; χ2 = 100.8; p < 0.0001). Because zero cells were present in this comparison, Fisher’s exact testing with Haldane–Anscombe correction was performed as a sensitivity analysis and confirmed a very strong association for rs10815393 CC versus non-CC genotypes (Haldane–Anscombe-adjusted OR = 650.46, 95% CI 32.88–12,869.02; Fisher’s exact p = 7.12 × 10⁻11). This association remained significant after conservative Bonferroni correction across age-stratified genotype/model comparisons.

A similarly robust age-dependent association was observed for rs55726619. The CC genotype was also enriched in children aged 1–5 years compared with patients aged ≥ 6 years (100.0% vs. 7.8%; χ2 = 62.04; p < 0.0001). Fisher’s exact sensitivity analysis confirmed this enrichment for rs55726619 CC versus non-CC genotypes (Haldane–Anscombe-adjusted OR = 172.69, 95% CI 9.50–3138.11; Fisher’s exact p = 5.91 × 10⁻⁸), which also remained significant after conservative Bonferroni correction. Additional nominal age-associated differences were observed for rs1332290 and rs7044343, suggesting that IL-33 genetic variation may contribute to age-dependent differences in malaria susceptibility or parasite-burden response, with the most statistically robust signals observed for rs10815393 and rs55726619.

Table 6 displays the genotypic distribution of the genetic variants analyzed for their association with age, comparing age groups 1–9 years and ≥ 10 years. The GT genotype of rs1332290 showed a significant association with the 1–9 years age group (72.2% vs. 48.7%; OR = 0.217, 95% CI 0.048–0.990, χ2 = 4.61; p = 0.032). Additionally, the G allele was also predominant in number in 1–9 years age group under the dominant model (OR = 0.237, 95% CI 0.053–1.059, χ2 = 4.14; p = 0.042). For rs10975516, the AG genotype was higher in frequency in 1–9 years age group (genotype frequency 77.8% vs. 55.6% OR = 0.162, 95% CI 0.021–1.259, χ2 = 3.89; p = 0.049). The heterozygous CT genotype of rs7044343 was significantly more frequent in malaria infection cases in the age group 1–9 years (genotype frequency 77.8% vs. 48.3%, OR = 0.182, 95% CI 0.040–0.821, χ2 = 6.05; p = 0.014). Under the dominant model, the T allele was more frequent in age group 1–9 years (OR = 0.205, 95% CI 0.046–0.911, χ2 = 5.21; p = 0.022).

We further analyzed the genotypic distribution of the genetic variants to assess their association with age, comparing the 10–20 years age group with those above 21 years in malaria-infected subjects (Table 7). The AC genotype of rs1317230 was significantly more common in the older age group (≥ 21 years) than in the 10–20 years group (55.1% vs. 42.6%; OR = 1.960, 95% CI 1.002–3.834, χ2 = 3.93; p = 0.047). Additionally, under the dominant model, the A allele was more likely to be present in individuals with > 21 years consistently(OR = 1.899, 95% CI 1.011–3.569, χ2 = 4.04; p = 0.044). Overall, the strongest corrected age-dependent signals were observed in the early-childhood comparison for rs10815393 and rs55726619, whereas additional associations in the broader pediatric and adult-comparison analyses were weaker and interpreted as supporting age-related genotype distribution patterns.

Haplotype frequencies of IL-33 in the patient and control groups

Based on LD structure among the genotyped IL-33 variants, haplotype analysis was performed using five informative SNPs likely to be inherited together: rs4742170, rs10975514, rs10975516, rs1317230, and rs10975520. The observed haplotype combinations were categorized into 11 distinct sets, as presented in Table 9. Statistical analysis revealed that four out of the eleven haplotypes exhibited a significant frequency of occurrence (SNP sets 2, 4, 8, and 9; p = 0.021, 2.76 × 10⁻⁶, 0.025, and 0.009, respectively). Haplotype set 2, comprising the TGGCG variant, was more prevalent in the control group than in P. falciparum patients (0.260 vs. 0.195, χ2 = 5.372; p = 0.021), suggesting that this haplotype may confer decreased risk. In contrast, haplotype sets 4 (0.071 vs. 0.007), 8 (0.026 vs. 0.006), and 9 (0.026 vs. 0.004) were more frequently observed in P. falciparum patients.

The influence of IL-33 gene variants on IL-33 gene expression at 5'UTR region was explored through luciferase reporter assay (Fig. 2). As depicted in Fig. 2A, a substantially high mean luciferase activity, RLU of group F1R1 was observed at 5'UTR region of IL-33, containing variant rs73639580 and rs12343534. To examine if the haplotype rs73639580 C/G and rs12343534 A/C contribute to IL-33 5’UTR gene expression, we took one clone F1R1 and changed one nucleotide as shown in Fig. 2B. The IL-33 5'UTR activity of the variant rs12343534 allele A with rs73639580 allele G and the variant rs12343534 allele C with rs73639580 allele C were measured afterwards through luciferase reporter gene assay. Discussion

This study provides valuable insights into the genetic determinants of malaria susceptibility by investigating the association between IL-33 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and P. falciparum infection. The findings reveal significant associations between specific SNPs and key aspects of malaria pathogenesis, including infection susceptibility, parasite burden, and age-related susceptibility patterns. Furthermore, functional assays highlight the potential influence of these genetic variations on IL-33 gene expression, offering mechanistic insights into their role in malaria progression. The present study extends prior IL-33/ST2 malaria literature from cytokine biology and experimental models into human host genetic association analysis. Previous studies have implicated IL-33/ST2 signaling in malaria-related immune regulation and cerebral malaria biology, and IL-33 levels have been reported to vary with parasite burden and disease phenotype [12, 19, 20]. However, direct evaluation of common IL-33 genetic variants in human P. falciparum susceptibility has been limited. The present SNP panel was therefore designed to test whether common regulatory variation in IL-33 contributes to infection susceptibility, parasite-burden distribution, age-dependent genotype patterns, haplotype structure, and reporter-based regulatory activity.

The distribution of specific genetic variants (alleles) within a population plays a crucial role in determining individual susceptibility to malaria. Certain genotypes can either increase the risk of infection or confer resistance. For example, the sickle-cell allele (HbS) provides protection against specific malaria strains when inherited in a heterozygous state (one normal allele and one HbS allele). However, malaria susceptibility is influenced by a complex interplay of multiple genetic factors. In this study, genotype–phenotype association analysis identified several SNPs, including rs10118795, rs55726619, and rs10975516, that showed significant correlations with malaria infection risk. Notably, the heterozygous CT genotype of rs10118795 was associated with an increased risk of malaria infection, whereas the CT genotype of rs55726619 appeared to confer protection against the disease. These findings suggest that specific genotypic variations may either predispose individuals to malaria or provide resistance, underscoring the intricate relationship between host genetics and malaria pathogenesis. This aligns with previous studies indicating that SNPs in various cytokine genes influence susceptibility to malaria infection [21, 22]. Furthermore, Fan et al. (2014) reported a significant association between rs10118795 and susceptibility to ankylosing spondylitis [23]. Interestingly, evidence in the literature suggests a link between ankylosing spondylitis and malaria, with P. falciparum-induced selective pressure on the HLA-B27 gene potentially leading to specific SNPs that are common to both diseases. This genetic adaptation may serve as a protective mechanism against these conditions [24].

The analysis of genotype and allele frequencies in relation to parasite burden provided additional insights into the genetic determinants of disease severity. Notably, SNPs such as rs12336076 and rs55726619 were significantly associated with parasite burden, with specific genotypes conferring a reduced risk of high parasitemia. In particular, the rs55726619-C allele was linked to a lower likelihood of developing high parasitemia, suggesting its potential role as a protective genetic marker against severe malaria. Similar associations between cytokine gene polymorphisms and parasitemia levels in P. falciparum malaria have been reported in previous studies. Aljarba et al. demonstrated that IL-27 SNPs, such as rs181206, rs181209, and rs26528, are significantly associated with parasite burden in malaria patients. Specifically, individuals carrying the rs181206-C minor allele are more likely to experience low to moderate disease severity [21]. Likewise, the rs544354-A allele of IL-18 has been linked to an increased risk of high parasite burden in malaria patients [22].

The age-stratified findings provide one of the strongest signals in this study and suggest that IL-33 genetic variation may be particularly relevant during early childhood, when anti-malarial immunity is still developing. The enrichment of rs10815393 CC and rs55726619 CC genotypes in infected children aged 1–5 years was not only statistically significant in the primary analysis but also remained robust in Fisher’s exact sensitivity testing and after conservative multiple-comparison correction. This strengthens the biological inference that IL-33 regulatory variation may influence malaria susceptibility or parasite-burden response most prominently in young children.

The molecular basis for this age-dependent pattern is biologically plausible. Malaria susceptibility varies with age, with young children being particularly vulnerable because of limited previous exposure to P. falciparum and incomplete acquisition of protective anti-malarial immunity [25]. Children aged 1–5 years are relatively immune-naïve and require repeated infections to develop a broader repertoire of immune cells capable of recognizing antigenically diverse malaria-derived proteins [26]. They are also more likely to experience inadequate modulation of pro-inflammatory responses, which may contribute to immunopathology during infection [27]. Young children in endemic settings often carry higher parasite densities and remain more susceptible to severe malaria-related pathology until partial resistance is gradually acquired over repeated exposure [28]. With increasing age, cumulative P. falciparum exposure promotes partial anti-malarial immunity, reducing infection frequency, parasite burden, and disease severity in older children and adults [29].

In this immune-naïve early-childhood setting, innate and alarmin-mediated pathways may exert a proportionally greater influence on parasite control and inflammatory pathology. IL-33 is an IL-1 family alarmin released during cellular stress, epithelial/endothelial injury, and inflammatory activation. Through ST2 signaling, IL-33 can regulate innate lymphoid cells, macrophages, Th1/Th2 balance, cytotoxic T cells, and regulatory T cells, all of which are relevant to the balance between parasite clearance and inflammatory tissue damage [12]. IL-33/ST2 signaling has also been implicated in cerebral malaria pathogenesis and immune regulation during malaria infection [20]. Therefore, regulatory IL-33 variants such as rs10815393 and rs55726619 may be more consequential in young children because genotype-dependent differences in IL-33 expression or IL-33/ST2 pathway activation would act at a stage when adaptive immune buffering is incomplete.

In older children and adults, repeated parasite exposure progressively induces broader antibody repertoires, memory immune responses, and improved regulation of inflammatory responses. These acquired mechanisms may reduce parasite burden and partially mask the effect of individual regulatory variants. Thus, the age-SNP associations observed in this study support a model in which IL-33 regulatory variation has its strongest detectable impact during the early immune-naïve period, before acquired anti-malarial immunity becomes established.

The age-stratified analyses were conducted within the infected patient group because the objective was to determine whether IL-33 genotype distributions differed across age-defined stages of malaria exposure and immune maturation among individuals with active P. falciparum infection. The healthy control group was used for the primary susceptibility analysis against infected cases, whereas the age-stratified analyses were designed as within-case genotype–phenotype comparisons rather than separate age-matched case–control analyses. This approach reduces interpretive ambiguity by linking age-related genotype patterns directly to infected individuals and parasite-burden-related phenotypes. This analytic separation allowed the primary susceptibility analysis to remain case–control based, while the age-stratified analyses focused specifically on genotype distribution across immune-development stages among infected individuals. Larger age-matched control datasets in future studies could further evaluate whether similar age-dependent IL-33 genotype patterns are detectable among malaria-negative individuals from the same endemic community.

The haplotype analysis of IL-33 gene variants (rs4742170, rs10975514, rs10975516, rs1317230, and rs10975520) in both control and patient groups provided valuable insights into the genetic factors influencing susceptibility to P. falciparum malaria. Significant differences in haplotype frequencies between the two groups highlighted the potential role of these genetic variants in modulating malaria susceptibility. For example, the higher prevalence of haplotype set 2 (TGGCG variants) in the control group suggests a potential protective effect against P. falciparum infection associated with this haplotype. In contrast, haplotypes sets 4, 8, and 9, which are more frequent in P. falciparum patients, may reflect a genetic predisposition to increased susceptibility to malaria. Haplotypes within particular genes are often capable of exposing genetic combinations which can moderate or interact to produce effects that are not observable with individual genotypes. For instance, our previous study demonstrated significant associations between two IL-27 haplotypes and P. falciparum, highlighting the critical role of genetic factors in malaria pathogenesis [30]. IL-33 haplotypes have been shown to confer susceptibility to immune-mediated conditions, with haplotypic analysis of IL-33 SNPs demonstrating significant associations with allergic rhinitis risk in the Chinese population, suggesting that combinations of IL-33 variants collectively influence IL-33-mediated immune responses [31]. The IL-33 haplotype associations identified in the present study extend this paradigm, suggesting that combinations of IL-33 variants may collectively modulate cytokine expression and downstream innate immune responses in ways that influence P. falciparum susceptibility.

Functional assays, such as luciferase reporter activity assays, have been crucial in uncovering how genetic variations influence IL-33 gene expression, providing valuable mechanistic insights into the role of these SNPs in malaria pathogenesis. The results from these assays emphasize the regulatory effects of variants in both the 5' and 3' UTRs of the IL-33 gene, suggesting their potential role in modulating gene expression. Specifically, the observed modulation of IL-33 gene expression by SNPs within the UTR regions suggests their role in influencing immune responses during malaria infection. Similar findings have been reported for IL-18 and IL-27, where malaria-associated SNPs within the 3' and 5' UTRs were shown to regulate the gene expression of these cytokines [21, 22]. This is biologically significant in the context of P. falciparum malaria, given that IL-33 serum levels have been reported to be elevated in children with severe P. falciparum malaria compared with unaffected children, to correlate with parasite load, and to decline after parasite clearance. [12, 20]. Furthermore, IL-33 is the driver of cytotoxic T cells, Th1, and Treg cells, and can independently stimulate CD4 + and CD8 + T cells and boost cytotoxic T lymphocytes responses [20]. Therefore, SNP-associated alterations in IL-33 expression levels, as demonstrated by our reporter assay, may have downstream consequences, potentially influencing individual susceptibility to P. falciparum infection and disease severity. Together, these findings emphasize the importance of regulatory mechanisms controlling IL-33 gene expression in the context of malaria infection.

Taken together, the genetic association, parasite-burden, age-stratified, haplotype, and reporter-assay findings support a convergent model in which IL-33 regulatory variation may influence malaria susceptibility and parasite-burden response through modulation of IL-33 expression and downstream IL-33/ST2-mediated immune signaling. Among the analyzed variants, rs55726619 showed the most consistent evidence across complementary analyses: it was associated with malaria susceptibility in the case–control comparison, remained robust in parasite-density contrasts, showed age-dependent enrichment in infected children aged 1–5 years, and was linked to altered 3′UTR reporter activity. This convergence strengthens the biological plausibility that rs55726619 may affect IL-33 post-transcriptional regulation and thereby influence the balance between parasite control and inflammatory pathology. In parallel, rs10815393 showed a strong corrected age-dependent signal in early childhood, supporting the concept that IL-33-related regulatory variation may be particularly consequential during the immune-naïve period before repeated P. falciparum exposure establishes broader adaptive immune protection. The haplotype findings further suggest that combinations of IL-33 variants, rather than isolated SNPs alone, may contribute to susceptibility patterns by capturing regulatory variation across the IL-33 locus.

The present study focused on IL-33 genetic variation in relation to malaria susceptibility, parasite burden, age-dependent genotype distribution, haplotypes, and functional reporter activity. Detailed symptom-level variables, socioeconomic indicators, systemic inflammatory markers, and hemoglobin/severe-anemia parameters were not available as analyzable retrospective endpoints. To address clinical disease-burden interpretation using available data, parasite density was analyzed as the objective malaria-burden phenotype and integrated with age-stratified, haplotype, and luciferase reporter evidence in the proxy disease-burden reanalysis. This integrated analysis identified rs55726619 as the most consistent IL-33 variant linking susceptibility, parasite burden, age-dependent response, and regulatory activity. Future prospective studies integrating IL-33 genotyping with detailed clinical phenotyping, inflammatory markers, hemoglobin levels, and longitudinal outcomes would further refine the clinical interpretation of these findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identifies IL-33 genetic variation as a biologically plausible contributor to P. falciparum malaria susceptibility and parasite-burden response in the Jazan cohort. The most consistent signal was observed for rs55726619, which showed evidence across case–control susceptibility, parasite-density, age-stratified, and functional reporter analyses, supporting its potential role as a regulatory variant affecting IL-33-mediated malaria immune responses. The strong age-dependent enrichment of rs10815393 CC and rs55726619 CC genotypes in infected children aged 1–5 years further suggests that IL-33 regulatory variation may be especially relevant during early childhood, when acquired anti-malarial immunity is still developing. Haplotype analysis also indicated that combinations of IL-33 variants may contribute to malaria susceptibility beyond single-SNP effects. Overall, these findings support IL-33/ST2 signaling as a biologically relevant host immune pathway in P. falciparum infection and highlight IL-33 variants, particularly rs55726619 and rs10815393, as candidates for validation in larger, clinically phenotyped, and ethnically diverse malaria cohorts.
