Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

High performance computing data centers will increasingly need to rely on automation to keep
pace with exascale growth in compute capability and to manage and optimize the data center
environment and facility resources. Artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches
provide the means to improve HPC data center operational efficiency, by learning historical
trends and training models to operate on real-time data collected from both IT and facilities
sources. NREL has developed methods of real-time collection, aggregation and streaming of
these data in the ESIF HPC Data Center and has collected a significant dataset of relevant
metrics across computer systems, racks, environmental, building and utility sources for research
into various predictive analytics problems. HPE’s Advanced Technology Group (ATG) is doing
comprehensive research into exascale monitoring and management for High Performance

 1

Computing (HPC) systems (hereinafter HPE’s Data Monitoring/ Management Technology).
NREL and HPE will collaborate to add Artificial Intelligence (AI) to NREL’s real-time data
collection/ aggregation/ streaming system and HPE’s Data Monitoring/ Management System,
with the goal of improving the operational efficiency of NREL’s Energy Systems Integration
Facility (ESIF) HPC Data Center through data analytics on both historical and real-time data
from IT systems and facilities operations. This collaboration will consist of efforts in Data
Management, Data Analytics, and AI/ML Optimization for both manual and autonomous
intervention in data center operations. This will be a multi-year, multi-staged effort with a goal
towards building capabilities for an Advanced Smart Facility, and demonstration of these
techniques in the NREL ESIF HPC Data Center.

In Year 2, the team will be focused on the following high-level tasks:

3.2.1 Predictive Scheduling of Cooling

NREL uses a system from Delta for its cooling infrastructure. The AIOps at NREL effort will be
designed to work with the Delta system.

Current data center cooling management systems do not take future IT load and behavior into
account. Since there is a large amount of thermal inertia built into a large data center such as
NREL’s, the system can take a long time to cool down or warm up. From the collected data
streams (in year-1) on each application’s power profile, machine learning methods can be used to
predict the footprint of each such job, whether they be capacity (single-node jobs) or capability
(large-scale, multi-node jobs) or somewhere in between. Then apriori, we will have an idea on
the impending power/energy footprints of the collection of jobs that will be scheduled to run, in
which a maximum amount of the cooling resources will be needed to enable the job to
successfully run, the ramp rate of the job will be much faster than the cooling system can get up
to steady-state. This can lead to a sharp increase in power consumption as more and more
cooling resources are added, which in turn can lead to a massive over-provisioning of cooling
capacity. If the job suddenly consumes half of its peak power, the cooling infrastructure will
continue to run at full capacity for a substantial amount of time since the cooling infrastructure
cannot ramp down as rapidly as the IT system power can change. To prevent this, the cooling
system can be ramped up ahead of time in anticipation of a large job coming online. Similarly, if
the power profile is expected to drop, the cooling system will be ramped down at a prescribed
rate in anticipation of the IT system ramping down. This ramping up/down of the cooling system
can be done each day at the appropriate time, given the anticipated cooling needs of the job
collection that’s expected to run or come online as scheduled.

 3

The cooling system ramp time, amount, and type of cooling resources, etc. will be determined by
the Data Analytics module. The adjustment to the cooling system will initially be done manually.
At a later stage, the team will demonstrate autonomous control.

Specifically, we will focus on the following tasks:

 • Establish power profiles from HPC applications
 • Investigate machine learning approaches to power aware scheduling.

3.2.2 Instrumentation resiliency, integrity, and anomaly detection

malfunction, in ways that are difficult to detect. This task will focus on characterizing and
tracking instrumentation behavior over time. Anomalous behavior will be flagged after which
servicing and maintenance can occur.

3.3.1 Power and Energy Consumption Optimization

NREL acquires its power and energy consumption data using a variety of systems. Key amongst
these systems are:

 • Data acquisition from facility power meters (typically GE) through a Delta system
 (480VAC, typically 400A and 800A feeds)
 • VFD power measured by the various pump/fan VFDs
 • ModbusTCP data acquisition from Emon/Dmon, Satec, and Schneider smart breakers
       (480VAC/277VAC, 3-phase circuits, 20A to 150A)
 • Schneider/APC power distribution (415VAC/240VAC, 3-phase circuits 20A or 40A)
 • Power measured at rack PDUs (most)
 • In some cases, power measured at the sub-rack level (e.g., via Ursa Major for Peregrine)
 and node-level for the HPC platforms.
 • This task will focus on investigating power and energy consumption optimization. To
 start, NREL and HPE will focus on one or more of the following areas:
 • Energy-aware job scheduling
 A. Apply machine learning approaches to predict the application/job power/energy
 footprint.
 B. Optimizing the SLURM resource manager, it has the application profile
 information from the data analytics tool so it can schedule the job at the
 appropriate time.
 • Managing power consumption to minimize utility demand charges
 • Dialing back power usage when time-of-use rates are high

Task 3.2.8: Predictive Scheduling of Cooling

Current data center cooling management systems do not take future IT load and behavior into
account. Since there is a large amount of thermal inertia built into a large data center such as
NREL’s, the system can take a long time to cool down or warm up when running various
applications, e.g., jobs or workloads such as WRF (Weather Research Forecasting), OpenFoam,
etc. For this task, NREL will collect power footprints from all the applications that run on Eagle.
Using this historical power consumption data, Machine Learning models will then be trained to
predict in real-time the power consumption footprint of each type of application, whether they be
capacity (single-node jobs) or capability (large-scale, multi-node jobs) applications, or
somewhere in between.

Based on this prediction of the required power/energy footprints from the batch of scheduled
jobs, an accurate estimate of the maximum amount of cooling resources needed can then be
calculated. The cooling system can be ramped up ahead of time in anticipation of a large job
coming online. Similarly, if the power consumption for the collection of jobs is expected to drop,
then the cooling system will be ramped down at a prescribed rate in anticipation of the IT system
ramping down. This ramping up/down of the cooling system can be done each day at the
appropriate time, given the anticipated cooling needs of the job collection that’s expected to run
or come online as scheduled. This approach, in addition, will in all likelihood also allow for a
more accurate prediction of the daily/weekly PUE prediction, setting the stage for better resource
utilizations at NREL. This will also allow us to concurrently optimize power utilization from the
local utility.

Task 5: IT/Facility Optimization – Predictive Scheduling of Cooling (NREL)

In Year 2, NREL has been able to classify application type and estimate the average power
consumption for certain types of jobs – this ties in the Eagle jobs, power, and XALT data. Using
this data, NREL has been able to calculate on a limited basis, the cooling system ramp time and
correlated that to the application power usage. An initial test case for predictive ramp up/ramp
down of the cooling system has been identified. An AI model strategy to predict the cooling
system ramp time in advance of some significant power usage by a job was not completed in
Year 2, so it has been rolled over into Year 3.

 • Work with HPE to develop an AI model strategy to predict the cooling system ramp time
 • Create a dataset of metrics used to train the AI model
 • Integrate application profile information from Year 2 into SLURM for use in the AI
 model
 • Work with HPE to evaluate effectiveness of the power-aware prediction model for
 cooling system ramp up/ramp down time.

 • HPE to work with NREL to create the correct data set for the AI model. This data will be
 used to train the model.
 • HPE to develop an AI-based model using NREL’s strategy to predict cooling system
 ramp/ramp down time in advance of large and power-hungry jobs.
 • HPE and NREL to run at least two test cases for power hungry jobs in order to verify the
 capability of the model to effectively predict the ramp up/ramp down of the cooling
