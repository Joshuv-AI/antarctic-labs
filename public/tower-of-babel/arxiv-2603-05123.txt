First-principles calculation of coherence length and penetration depth based on density functional theory for superconductors
Mitsuaki Kawamura, Takuya Nomoto, Niklas Witt, Ryotaro Arita

Abstract
We develop a first-principles framework for evaluating the fundamental length scales of superconductivity, namely the coherence length ξ 0 \xi_{0} and the magnetic penetration depth λ L \lambda_{\mathrm{L}} , within superconducting density functional theory (SCDFT). By incorporating finite-momentum Cooper pairs, we formulate a microscopic scheme that enables a consistent and parameter-free determination of ξ 0 \xi_{0} , λ L \lambda_{\mathrm{L}} , and the superconducting transition temperature T c T_{\mathrm{c}} on the same theoretical footing. Applying the method to representative elemental superconductors, the A15 compound V 3 Si, and H 3 S under high pressure, we obtain results in good agreement with available experimental data. Furthermore, the unified access to ξ 0 \xi_{0} and λ L \lambda_{\mathrm{L}} allows us to construct the Uemura plot entirely from first principles, demonstrating that conventional elemental superconductors systematically exhibit small T c T_{\mathrm{c}} / T F T_{\mathrm{F}} , while higher- T c T_{\mathrm{c}} systems are characterized by the simultaneous realization of strong pairing and large phase stiffness. Our results establish a predictive first-principles route to superconducting length scales and provide a microscopic interpretation of empirical correlations in superconductivity.



Introduction—

Establishing a first-principles description of the superconducting state and quantitatively predicting its physical properties have been central challenges in solid state physics
Sanna et al. 2018
;
Flores-Livas et al. 2020
;
Boeri et al. 2022
;
Pellegrini and Sanna 2024
;
Margine and Giustino 2013
;
Pellegrini et al. 2023
;
Lucrezi et al. 2024
. Beyond the evaluation of the superconducting transition temperature (
T_{\mathrm{c}}
), a microscopic and parameter-free determination of quantities characterizing the superconducting state itself is of fundamental importance, not only for deepening our understanding of superconductivity but also for guiding materials design
Cerqueira et al. 2024
;
Sanna et al. 2025
and technological applications
Dobrovolskiy et al. 2026
.

In this context, superconducting density functional theory (SCDFT)
Oliveira et al. 1988
;
Kreibich and Gross 2001
;
Sanna 2017
, which extends density functional theory to the superconducting state, has emerged as a powerful theoretical framework. Since its explicit formulation in 2005, SCDFT has been successfully applied mainly to conventional phonon-mediated superconductors
Lüders et al. 2005
;
Marques et al. 2005
;
Floris et al. 2005
;
Profeta et al. 2006
;
Tresca et al. 2022
, enabling quantitative first-principles calculations of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
. More recently, systematic improvements of the exchange-correlation functional
Sanna et al. 2020
, including the effects of plasmons
Akashi and Arita 2013
;
Davydov et al. 2020
and spin fluctuations
Essenberger et al. 2014
;
Essenberger et al. 2016
;
Kawamura et al. 2020
, have significantly broadened the range of materials accessible within this framework
Boeri et al. 2022
;
Pellegrini and Sanna 2024
.

Despite these advances, a first-principles evaluation of fundamental length scales characterizing the superconducting state—namely, the coherence length (
\xi_{0}
) and the magnetic penetration depth (
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
)—has not yet been established within SCDFT. These quantities play a pivotal role in classifying superconductors into type-I and type-II regimes, and directly determine critical magnetic fields, depairing currents, and superconducting stiffness. Therefore, these length scales are indispensable for both fundamental characterization and practical assessment of superconducting materials
Tinkham 1996
.

One microscopic approach to evaluate the coherence length and penetration depth on the same footing has been previously explored within dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT)
Witt et al. 2024
. In that study, Cooper pairs carrying a finite center-of-mass momentum (
\mathbf{Q}
) are introduced, and the
\mathbf{Q}
-dependence of the superconducting order parameter and the supercurrent carried by the Cooper pairs is analyzed to extract
\xi_{0}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
.

In the present work, we incorporate this finite-momentum Cooper-pair approach into the framework of SCDFT and develop a first-principles formulation for evaluating
\xi_{0}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
. Specifically, we derive a gap equation for Cooper pairs with finite
\mathbf{Q}
within SCDFT and extract the relevant physical quantities. Since the characteristic length scales are typically more than an order of magnitude larger than the lattice constant, it is essential to treat extremely small
\mathbf{Q}
in a numerically stable manner. We introduce computational techniques that ensure numerical stability in this regime and further enable the calculation of supercurrents in the superconducting state.

We apply the developed methodology to representative elemental superconductors, including Al, Nb, and Pb, and perform detailed comparisons with available experimental data. For all these materials, we find excellent agreement for both
\xi_{0}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
, demonstrating the quantitative reliability of our approach. We further extend our analysis to the A15
Cucciari and Boeri 2026
superconductor V
3
Si and to H
3
S, a high-temperature superconductor stabilized under high pressure
Drozdov et al. 2015
;
Flores-Livas et al. 2020
, and again obtain results in very good agreement with experimental estimates.

In particular, H
3
S becomes superconducting only above pressures of approximately 100 GPa
Drozdov et al. 2015
;
Flores-Livas et al. 2020
, making experimental determinations of
\xi_{0}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
extremely challenging. In such cases, the first-principles SCDFT-based simulations developed in this work provide a powerful and indispensable tool for characterizing the superconducting state and predicting its properties under extreme conditions.

Method—

The superconducting singlet order parameter is computed as the expectation value of the product of annihilation operators,
\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})
, as
Oliveira et al. 1988
:

\displaystyle\chi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle{\hat{\psi}}_{\uparrow}(\mathbf{r}){\hat{\psi}}_{\downarrow}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\{u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})v_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})f\left(-E_{n\mathbf{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})v_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\ast}(\mathbf{r})f(E_{n\mathbf{k}})\},

(1)

where
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
,
v_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
, and
E_{n\mathbf{k}}
are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Kohn–Sham–Bogoliubov–de Gennes eq.

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}}{2}+V_{\mathrm{KS}}(\mathbf{r})-\mu&{\hat{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{KS}}\\
{\hat{\Delta}}_{\mathrm{KS}}^{\dagger}&\frac{\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{2}}{2}-V_{\mathrm{KS}}(\mathbf{r})+\mu\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\\
v_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\end{matrix}\right)

\displaystyle=E_{n\mathbf{k}}

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\\
v_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})\end{matrix}\right).

(2)

Next, we consider
\chi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime})
which is periodically modulated with a finite momentum
\mathbf{Q}

(see Supplemental Material of Ref.
Witt et al. 2024
),

\displaystyle\chi(\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}+\mathbf{R})=e^{i\mathbf{Q}\cdot\mathbf{R}}\chi(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(3)

This assumption leads to the decoupling approximation
Lüders et al. 2005
together with the generalized Bloch theorem
Sandratskii 1998
;
Alase et al. 2017
;
Witt et al. 2024
,

\displaystyle v_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\approx v_{n\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{n\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}/2}(\mathbf{r}),

(4)

\displaystyle u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle\approx u_{n\mathbf{k}}\varphi_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}(\mathbf{r}),

(5)

where
\varphi_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{r})
is the normal-state Kohn–Sham orbital at band
n
and wavenumber
\mathbf{k}
.
We obtain the scalar coefficients
u_{n\mathbf{k}}
and
v_{n\mathbf{k}}
by solving the
2\times 2

\displaystyle\left(\begin{matrix}\xi_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}&\Delta^{(\mathbf{Q})}_{n\mathbf{k}}\\
\Delta^{(\mathbf{Q})*}_{n\mathbf{k}}&-\xi_{n\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}/2}\\
\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}u_{n\mathbf{k}}\\
v_{n\mathbf{k}}\\
\end{matrix}\right)=E_{n\mathbf{k}}\left(\begin{matrix}u_{n\mathbf{k}}\\
v_{n\mathbf{k}}\\
\end{matrix}\right),

(6)

where
\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}
is the Kohn–Sham eigenvalue measured relative to the Fermi level.
To stabilize the numerical
\mathbf{k}
-point integration, we introduce an auxiliary energy-dependent gap function
Kawamura et al. 2017
, which satisfies
\Delta^{(\mathbf{Q})}_{n\mathbf{k}}\equiv\Delta^{(\mathbf{Q})}_{n\mathbf{k}}(0)
, and

\displaystyle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int{d\xi^{\prime}}\sum_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\delta(\xi^{\prime}-{\bar{\xi}}_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})})

\displaystyle\times\frac{K_{n\mathbf{k}n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})}{1+Z_{n\mathbf{k}}(\xi)}\frac{\Delta_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}{E_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}\tanh{\left(\frac{E_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}{2T}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\frac{1-\tanh^{2}{\left(\frac{d_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}{2T}\right)}}{1-\tanh^{2}{\left(\frac{E_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}{2T}\right)}\tanh^{2}{\left(\frac{d_{n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi^{\prime})}{2T}\right)}},

(7)

where we ignore the
\mathbf{Q}
dependence of
K_{n\mathbf{k}n^{\prime}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}
and
Z_{n\mathbf{k}}
, and

\displaystyle E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi)

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\xi^{2}+\left|\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi)\right|^{2}},

(8)

\displaystyle{\bar{\xi}}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\xi_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}+\xi_{n\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}/2}}{2},

(9)

\displaystyle d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\xi)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\left\langle\left(\xi_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}-\xi_{n\mathbf{k}-\mathbf{Q}/2}\right)\delta(\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}-\xi)\right\rangle}{2\left\langle\delta\left(\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}-\xi\right)\right\rangle}.

(10)

For materials with a coherence length much larger than the lattice constant (such as Al and Nb), we need to compute at tiny
\mathbf{Q}
relative to the
\mathbf{k}
-grid spacing.
Therefore, the Kohn–Sham energy at the
\mathbf{k}\pm\mathbf{Q}/2
point, which is slightly shifted from the original grid point, is evaluated using the Taylor expansion as

\displaystyle\xi_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}

\displaystyle=\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}+\sum_{\alpha=x,y,z}{\frac{\partial\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}}{{\partial k}_{\alpha}}\frac{Q_{\alpha}}{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}=x,y,z}{\frac{\partial^{2}\xi_{n\mathbf{k}}}{{\partial k}_{\alpha}{\partial k}_{\alpha^{\prime}}}\frac{Q_{\alpha}}{2}\frac{Q_{\alpha^{\prime}}}{2}}.

(11)

To compute the penetration depth, we need the spatial average of the supercurrent density

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\textrm{sc}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}

\displaystyle\equiv\bar{\mathbf{j}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}-\bar{\mathbf{j}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}\left(\Delta=0\right),

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{j}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{V}\int dr^{3}\mathbf{j}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\mathbf{r}),

(13)

where
\mathbf{j}^{(\mathbf{Q})}(\mathbf{r})
is the expectation value of the current density,

\displaystyle\mathbf{j}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\sigma}\left\langle\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\boldsymbol{\nabla}\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})-\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}(\mathbf{r})\boldsymbol{\nabla}\hat{\psi}_{\sigma}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r})\right\rangle

(14)

for a finite-momentum superconducting state.
With the same approximation as derived above, we obtain

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\textrm{sc}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{V}\int{d\xi}\sum_{n\mathbf{k}}\delta(\xi-{\bar{\xi}}_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})})\mathbf{v}_{n\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{Q}/2}

\displaystyle\times\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\{\tanh\left(\frac{d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)+E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)}{2T}\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad+\tanh\left(\frac{d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)-E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)}{2T}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\xi}{2E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)}\left\{\tanh\left(\frac{d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)+E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)}{2T}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\qquad-\tanh\left(\frac{d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)-E_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)}{2T}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\tanh\left(\frac{d_{n\mathbf{k}}^{\mathbf{Q}}(\xi)+\xi}{2T}\right)\right],

(15)

where
\mathbf{v}_{n\mathbf{k}}
is the Fermi velocity.

Results—

We apply the methodology outlined above to Nb, a prototypical conventional superconductor. As an initial benchmark, we perform the standard SCDFT calculation for Cooper pairs with
{\mathbf{Q}}=\mathbf{0}
and evaluate
T_{\mathrm{c}}
using Superconducting-Toolkit
Kawamura et al. 2020
. The calculated transition temperature is 8.09 K, in good agreement with the experimental value of 9.50 K. This level of agreement demonstrates the reliability of the numerical framework adopted in the present study.

Next, we calculate the gap function
\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
for Cooper pairs with finite
{\mathbf{Q}}
following Eq. (
7

1
shows the momentum dependence of the Fermi-surface-averaged gap function
\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}\rangle
for temperatures ranging from 1 K to 7 K. At temperatures close to

,
\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
decreases monotonically, following the Ginzburg–Landau expression

\displaystyle\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}\rangle=\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q=0})}\rangle\sqrt{1-\xi_{0}^{2}Q^{2}},

(16)

where
Q=|\mathbf{Q}|
. Following Ref.
Witt et al. 2024
, we define

as the wave number at which
\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q})}\rangle
is reduced to
1/\sqrt{2}
of its zero-momentum value
\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{Q=0})}\rangle
, and evaluate the coherence length as
\xi_{0}=1/(\sqrt{2}Q_{2})
. Although Eq. (
16
) is strictly valid only close to
T_{\mathrm{c}}
, the
Q_{2}
criterion provides a reasonable estimate even at lower temperatures. Indeed, applying this approach and the temperature dependence
\xi(T)=\xi_{0}(1-T/T_{\mathrm{c}})^{-1/2}
, we extract a zero-temperature coherence length
\xi_{0}=34
nm which is in very good agreement with the experimental estimate of
39\pm 1
nm,

We also evaluate the Pippard coherence length from the ratio of
\langle\Delta_{n\mathbf{k}}^{(\mathbf{0})}\rangle
to the Fermi-surface-averaged Fermi velocity (
\langle|\mathbf{v}_{n\mathbf{k}}|\rangle
). This procedure yields a value of 79.6 nm. Taking into account the electron–phonon coupling constant
\lambda_{\rm ep}=1.34
for Nb, the Fermi velocity is effectively renormalized by a factor of
(1+\lambda_{\rm ep})
. Including this renormalization reduces the Pippard coherence length to 34 nm, which is consistent with the value of
\xi_{0}
obtained above.

We next evaluate the magnetic penetration depth. Using Eq. (
15
), we calculate the supercurrent density
\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\rm sc}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
carried by Cooper pairs with finite
\mathbf{Q}
. The results are shown in Fig.
1
. The current density
\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\rm sc}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
increases linearly for small
\mathbf{Q}
, reaches a maximum, and then decreases as
\mathbf{Q}
increases further. From the slope of
\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\rm sc}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
in the vicinity of
\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{0}
, the magnetic penetration depth is evaluated as

\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=\left.\left(2\mu_{0}\frac{\partial\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{(Q)}}{\partial Q}\right)^{-1/2}\right|_{Q=0},

(17)

where
\mu_{0}
denotes the vacuum permeability. This procedure yields
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=34
nm, which lies within the experimental range of 27–39 nm
Suter et al. 2005
;
McFadden et al. 2026
;
Kittel 2004
.

Alternatively, the magnetic penetration depth can also be evaluated by combining the depairing current
J_{\rm dp}
, i.e., the maximum value of
\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\rm sc}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
, with the zero-temperature coherence length
\xi_{0}
as

\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=\sqrt{\frac{\Phi_{0}}{3\sqrt{3}\mu_{0}\xi_{0}J_{\rm dp}}},

(18)

where
\Phi_{0}
is the flux quantum.
This approach yields
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}=40
nm, again in good agreement with experimental values, demonstrating the internal consistency of the present method.

We note that a particular strength of our framework is that
J_{\mathrm{dp}}
is directly obtained from
\bar{\mathbf{j}}_{\rm sc}^{(\mathbf{Q})}
without any further assumption. The depairing current is a theoretical upper maximum to achievable critical currents
J_{\mathrm{c}}
, as they
crucially depend on sample geometry and defect densities

;
Bardeen 1962
. For Nb, we obtain
J_{\mathrm{dp}}=19\times 10^{7}
A/cm
2
which is about 25 times larger than measured

A/cm
2
(see Table S2 in the Supplemental Material

).

We have applied the same procedure to the set of superconductors listed in Table

For all materials considered, the calculated coherence lengths and magnetic penetration depths show good agreement with available experimental data. A key feature of the present approach is that the three key quantities,
T_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\xi_{0}
, and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
, can be evaluated consistently within a single first-principles framework. One intriguing outcome of this unified treatment is the ability to construct the so-called Uemura plot

;
Uemura et al. 1991
, which compares
T_{\mathrm{c}}
with the Fermi temperature

. The latter characterizes the phase stiffness of the superconducting condensate and is in three dimensions given by

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{1}{2}(3\pi^{2})^{2/3}\frac{n_{\mathrm{s}}^{2/3}}{m^{*}}

(19)

where

is the effective mass (containing the band-structure part
m^{*}_{\mathrm{band}}
and electron-phonon mass-enhancement), the superfluid density
n_{\mathrm{s}}
is related to
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
as
n_{\mathrm{s}}=m^{*}c^{2}/(4\pi\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}^{2})
, and
c
is the speed of light.
Our first-principles calculations naturally reproduce the experimentally observed trend that elemental superconductors, which are well described by conventional pairing mechanisms, systematically exhibit small values of
T_{\mathrm{c}}/T_{\mathrm{F}}
(Fig.
2

3

\xi_{0}

\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
, reflecting a large phase stiffness. These results demonstrate that the Uemura plot, which has traditionally been discussed in an experimental or semi-phenomenological context, can be understood and predicted on a fully first-principles basis that explicitly connects the pairing scale and the phase stiffness of the superconducting state.

Summary—

We have developed a first-principles methodology to evaluate the coherence length and magnetic penetration depth of superconductors within SCDFT by explicitly incorporating finite-momentum Cooper pairs. This approach enables a consistent determination of
T_{\mathrm{c}}
,
\xi_{0}
, and
\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}
without phenomenological input and reproduces experimental trends across a wide range of superconducting materials. By constructing the Uemura plot directly from first principles, we demonstrate that empirical correlations between transition temperature, pairing strength, and phase stiffness can be understood on a microscopic basis. Our work establishes superconducting length scales as predictive quantities in first-principles theory and provides a foundation for exploring superconductivity under extreme conditions where experiments are challenging.
