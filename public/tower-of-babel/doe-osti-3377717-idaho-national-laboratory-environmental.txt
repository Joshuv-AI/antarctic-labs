 ABSTRACT
 This document provides details on the environmental and geoarchaeological context of the
Idaho National Laboratory (INL) Site for use by archaeologists within the INL Cultural Resource
Management Office. This includes a brief background to the geological formation processes of the
modern landscape as well as a discussion of climate, flora and fauna, and the geomorphic contexts
that comprise the INL Site. Geomorphic contexts consist of floodplains and fan deposits, lacustrine
deposits and playa sinks, and basalt plains. The impact of fire and its potential drastically alter the
landscape is also discussed.

 INL Environmental and Geoarchaeological Context
INTRODUCTION
 The Idaho National Laboratory (INL) Site is part of the Eastern Snake River Plain (ESRP),
an elongate, low relief topographic depression that extends from the Wyoming border to near Twin
Falls, Idaho and follows the track of the Yellowstone volcanic hotspot. It is one of the most
prominent geomorphic features of eastern Idaho, being underlain by a thick sequence of Tertiary
and Quaternary rhyolitic and basaltic volcanic rocks (Gianniny et al. 2002: 79). The INL Site and
its immediate environs are primarily composed of Quaternary basaltic lava flows ranging in age
from 1.1 mya to 2.1 ka, mantled with a veneer of loess, aeolian sand, and fluvial, playa, and
lacustrine deposits (Bestland et al. 2002: 28; Ostenaa et al. 2002: 92; Kuntz et al. 1986, 1994).
Kuntz et al. (1992) identify as many as nine volcanic rift zones across the ESRP, four of which
intersect the INL Site, including the Arco-Big Southern Butte, Howe-East Butte, Lava Ridge-
Hell’s Half Acre, and Circular-Kettle Butte volcanic rift zones. The Axial Volcanic Zone is a
topographic high that forms the central axis of the ESRP (Mark and Thackray 2002: 62).

 Lands of the INL Site are within the core area of the Pioneer Basin, a closed drainage
system fed by montane watersheds to the north (Nace et al. 1972, 1975). Three of the largest
streams, the Big Lost River, the Little Lost River, and Birch Creek converge on the INL Site in a
series of playas and “sinks” where they disappear below ground into the Snake River aquifer.
These natural features are remnants of Lake Terreton, a Pleistocene cryptorheic lake that once
covered much of the northern INL Site (Gianinny et al. 2002). To the south, three volcanic domes
rise abruptly from a rolling plain of superimposed basalt flows extruded from low-relief shield
volcanoes (Bonnichsen and Breckenridge 1982). Big Southern Butte is located about 10 miles
south of the Big Lost River, just outside the INL Site. Webb Springs, on the north side of Big
Southern Butte, was a rare source of water on the plain and an important stop on the Jeffrey-
Goodale Cutoff of the Oregon Trail. The Twin Buttes (East Butte and Middle Butte) are located
within the INL Site near the route linking present-day Arco and Idaho Falls.

 Controlled access to the INL Site has helped preserve much of the sagebrush steppe habitat
of the Pioneer Basin since the 1940s. Long-term climatic fluctuations and more recent changes in
land use have altered the landscape of the INL Site significantly since the arrival of the first people
to the region ca. 13,000 years ago. One of the most notable changes would have been the
disappearance of Lake Terreton by the Early Holocene. Other dramatic changes have occurred
more recently since the early 1900s, when an upswing in agricultural activity on land within and
around the INL Site boundaries led to the construction of a number of dams, including the Mackay
Dam on the Big Lost River, and canal projects that substantially altered the landscape and greatly
reduced the inflow of water to the region. Further water diversion efforts such as the construction
of the INL Diversion Dam and associated “Spreading Areas” in the 1950s and 1960s also led to
changes in the flow of the Big Lost River, the main source of water to the INL Site. Over time,
this diversion of surface water has contributed to changes in habitat on the floodplain and Terreton
basin, including loss of meadows and riparian vegetation and the expansion of upland steppe
communities more tolerant of arid conditions. On the basalt plains flanking the floodplain, long-
term vegetation monitoring has tracked a marked decline in sagebrush cover, increased grass
cover, and an increased prevalence of invasive annuals (Forman 2024). These changes are thought

to be driven by direct loss to fire over the past 25 years, decreasing snow cover and precipitation
over the past century, and a sharp reduction in grazing after ca. 1930 (see Forman 2024; French
and Mitchell 1983). The combined effect of these anthropogenic changes and natural processes is
a much drier and less productive landscape compared to the distant and recent past.

CLIMATE
 The contemporary climate of the Pioneer Basin is typical of high-altitude cold deserts or
shrub steppe of the Great Basin and Columbia Plateau. Elevations range from 4,780 feet at Mud
Lake to 7,550 feet at the summit of Big Southern Butte. The area receives an average annual
precipitation of 23 cm (9 in.). Monthly precipitation typically peaks in May, then drops with rising
temperatures in July and August. Snowfall between October and February provides most available
soil moisture to desert plants for the year. The low precipitation of this arid environment is
supplemented by spring runoff from mountains to the north, supporting a comparative abundance
of resources relative to the vast interior of the Snake River Plain.

 The availability of regional environmental data for the terminal Pleistocene is currently
limited (see INL CRMO 2026: 10). The Bølling–Allerød Interstadial (B-A), often referred to as
the “Clovis Drought” by archaeologists, was initially recorded in the Greenland ice sheet as a
relatively lengthy warm period immediately following the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM).
Although paleoclimatic records across the globe differ, the LGM is suspected of ending around
18,000 years ago (Hughes and Gibbard 2015). Existing data suggest that the B-A ended abruptly,
marked by the re-advance of glaciers in many parts of Europe and North America (Meltzer and
Holliday 2010). This return to glacial conditions is referred to as the Younger Dryas (YD)
Chronozone. Recent investigations in Yellowstone and Grand Teton National Parks provide much
higher resolution data regarding terminal Pleistocene climate change in the Intermountain West
(Larsen et al. 2016; Millspaugh et al. 2000). Based on the charcoal stratigraphy of Cygnet Lake,
B-A vegetation communities in the greater Yellowstone region were primarily characterized by
tundra and grassland (Millspaugh et al. 2000) while sediments in Cygnet Lake contained little or
no organic matter. The YD likely correlates with the advance of lodge pole pine forests between
12,800 and 11,300 calibrated years before present (cal. BP [before 1950]). Likewise, sediment
cores from Jenny Lake, located on the east side of the Teton Mountain Range and just south of
Yellowstone Park, reflect similar terminal Pleistocene /Early Holocene climatic shifts (Larsen et
al. 2016) with an influx of terrestrial carbon beginning at roughly 11,500 cal. BP.

 Beginning in the late 1970s, Bright and Davis (1982) collected sediments from a variety of
regional sources around the INL Site, including dry ephemeral ponds, sand dunes, caves, packrat
middens, and beneath lava flows. Although sediment samples from such open-air sources as ponds
and dunes provided relatively limited information, Middle Butte and Rattlesnake caves produced
continuous climatic records. Pollen records from both Middle Butte Cave and Rattlesnake Cave
indicate that maximum aridity occurred in their immediate surroundings around 7,000 years ago
(Bright and Davis 1982; Davis and Bright 1983; Steadman et al. 1994). Pollen samples from this
timeframe contain higher percentages of cheno-ams (plants of the amaranth and pigweed families,
including shadscale), suggesting that higher temperatures, lower precipitation, or possibly both
resulted in a greater prevalence of shadscale vegetation than exists in the area at present (Bright
and Davis 1982: 31). Although the plant remains from Rattlesnake Cave suggest a xeric peak at
roughly 7500 cal. BP. (Davis 1981), vertebrate species associated with riparian and lacustrine

 2

habitats continued to be deposited in the cave during this period, suggesting that temperatures,
although higher, were insufficient to eliminate nearby wetland habitats entirely (Steadman et al.
1994). Moisture conditions gradually improved by about 5,200 years ago in the immediate vicinity
of Middle Butte Cave, and shadscale communities became established at lower elevations (Bright
and Davis 1982; Davis and Bright 1983).

 According to data examined by Bright and Davis (1982), ESRP environmental conditions
spanning the last 5,000 years have remained relatively stable. Subsequent research has produced
comparable results. Column samples from Scaredy Cat Cave in the Craters of the Moon National
Monument and Preserve closely resemble the components of the sagebrush community that
currently surround the site, although periods of increased moisture may have occurred between
7,200 and 6,500 years ago and 4,700 and 4,200 years ago, as indicated by slightly higher
percentages of grass pollen (Wigand 1997). Pollen samples from Kelvins and Tomcat caves also
produced coarse-grained results. These pollen profiles were not unexpected because aeolian cave
deposits appeared to be mixed (Cummings 2002; Cummings and Puseman 2005). As a
consequence, these data are inadequate for pinpointing Middle and Late Holocene climatic
episodes that likely resulted in the expansion or contraction of Holocene plant communities and
the subsequent responses of regional fauna.

 Although efforts to investigate Holocene climate change on the ESRP have been relatively
unrevealing, relevant data are being generated in adjacent regions. For example, within the
Sawtooth Range of central Idaho Whitlock et al. (2011) document long-term climatic changes
based on pollen records, highlighting the ebb and flow of lodge pole pine and Douglas fir forests
and inferring five drought episodes, including those at 8200 cal BP and during the Medieval
Climate Anomaly. To the south in the eastern Great Basin, Coltraine and Stafford (1999) argue
that after about 2000 cal. BP, growing seasonal precipitation increased as a result of a westward
shift in the southwestern monsoon system. This shift provided sufficient summer moisture (and
warmth) to support horticulture in northern Utah. However, wetlands surrounding the Great Salt
Lake were also very productive during this time frame, allowing for a broad range of subsistence
options that included both foraging and farming (Simms 1999). By 800 cal. BP, deteriorating
climatic conditions, possibly associated with the onset of the Medieval Warm Period (MWP),
prompted a return to full-time foraging in northern Utah (Coltraine and Stafford 1999). Whether
referred to as the MWP or the Medieval Climatic Anomaly, evidence of an epic drought has been
well documented in western North America. Cook et al. (2004) describe a period of
“unprecedented drought severity and duration” spanning roughly 400 years between 1100 and 700
cal. BP. While other climatologists have generated slightly different age ranges for the MWP, there
are sufficient data to conclude that the ESRP experienced significant decreases in precipitation
during this period.

 Holocene climatic changes are also documented in the Yellowstone region east of the
ESRP, whereby fossil and pollen data suggest that central and southern Yellowstone became drier
during the Early Holocene while northern Yellowstone became wetter. Such regional nuances can
partly be explained by differences in topography and local responses to the effects of greater
summer solar radiation (Whitlock and Bartlein 1993). Similarly, a strong link between changes in
climate and variations in fire frequency are highlighted at Cygnet Lake in the Yellowstone region
by Millspaugh et al (2010), who show that fire frequency was moderate during the late glacial

 3

period, highest during the Early Holocene, and decreased after 7000 cal BP. A correlation between
summer insolation and regional climatic shifts is suggested (ibid 2000: 213).

 In addition to the MWP, paleoecologists in both North America and Europe have noted
evidence of another potentially significant Late Holocene climatic event referred to as the Little
Ice Age (LIA) (Bradley and Jones 1993; Hereford 2002; Hotchkiss et al. 2007). Although regional
variations exist, the onset of the LIA is thought to have occurred between roughly 500 and 200
years ago, resulting in an overall cooling trend and shorter growing seasons across North America,
with temperatures potentially dropping to their lowest during the AD 1700s. Historical journals,
especially those associated with fur traders of the Hudson’s Bay Company, have been used to
support existing climate data (Bradley and Jones 1993; Hotchkiss et al. 2007). How the LIA may
have altered vegetative communities on the ESRP is currently unknown, but if sagebrush
communities expanded as a result of the cooling trend, the availability of cool-season grasses may
have prompted an increase in resident bison populations during this period.

FLORA AND FAUNA
 Plant life across the INL Site is strongly influenced by climate and topography and is
generally similar to other cool desert environments of the Great Basin and Columbia Plateau.
Communities range from shadscale steppe at lower altitudes, to several sagebrush- and grass-
dominated communities, to juniper woodland along the foothills of the nearby mountains and
buttes. Although the relative dominance and boundaries of these general communities have
expanded and contracted in response to variation in available moisture and temperature regimes,
palynological data from Rattlesnake and Middle Butte caves indicate their continued presence
since the Late Pleistocene glacial periods (Davis and Bright 1983). A total of 20 to 22 distinct
vegetation cover types have been identified on the present-day INL Site (McBride et al. 1978),
typically consisting of an overstory of shrubs and an understory of grasses and forbs. Big sagebrush
(Artemisia tridentata) is by far the most common shrubs, occurring on approximately 80% of the
vegetation (Harniss and West 1973; Shive et al. 2019), but 43 other species of shrubs have also
been identified on the INL Site and the adjacent Big Southern Butte (Anderson et al. 1996).
Perennial grasses are the most abundant understory plants in shrub-dominated communities and
are the dominant plants in grassland communities where shrubs are scarce.

 Ethnographic and ethnohistorical evidence indicate that a wide range of plants are collected
and used by Indigenous communities, both today and in the past, possessing profound cultural,
spiritual, and practical significance. Some of the earliest written accounts of Shoshone-Bannock
plant use come from the Lewis and Clark Expedition, in which Meriweather Lewis in August 1805
described women gathering berries in the Salmon River Country (Moulton 2002). On the INL Site,
sage (Artemisia spp.), juniper (Juniperus spp.), desert parsley (Lomatium spp.), ragweed (Ambrosia
acanthicarpa), coyote tobacco (Nicotiana attenuata), aster (Aster spp.), fleabane (Erigeron spp.),
rabbit brush (Chrysothamnus spp.), and dogbane (Apocynum cannabinum) are just some of the
plants known to be used for medicinal purposes (see Anderson et al. [1996] for a more thorough
ethnobotanical review). Biscuitroot (Cymopterus spp.), sweet cicely (Osmorhiza spp.), prickly pear
(Opuntia polyacantha), false dandelion (Agoseris glauca), thistle (Cirsium spp.), hawksbeard
(Crepis spp.), and sunflowers (Helianthus spp.) are also important food sources, while grasses such
as basin wildrye (Leymus cinereus),beardless wildrye (Leymus triticoides), and Indian ricegrass

 4

(Achnatherum hymenoides) were used for their grain and other purposes, including medicine,
ornamentation, and material for basketry (Anderson et al. 1996; Smoak 2025: 274). Wood and bark
from willow (Salix spp.), birch (Betula spp.) and lodgepole pines (Pinus contorta) were also key
sources of fuel and material for shelter and the creation of a range of tools.

 Over 200 resident and seasonal vertebrate species have been documented on the INL Site
(Arthur et al. 1984; Reynolds et al. 1986). Birds constitute the largest single class of wildlife,
although many of these are migratory, while small mammals are the most common year-round
residents. Of particular cultural interest are species that are known or expected to have been utilized
by people. Many of these, including mammoth (Mammuthus spp.) and camel (Camelops spp.), are
now extinct in North America. However, nearby archaeological sites such as Bison and Veratic
Rockshelters (Swanson 1972), Owl Cave (Butler 1978, 1986; Miller 1982, 1990), and Jaguar Cave
(Dort 1975; Guilday and Adams 1967; Kurten and Anderson 1972) provide evidence of these
animals’ past presence on the INL Site.

Today, the most abundant big game animals on the INL Site are pronghorn (Antilocapra
americana) and elk (Cervus canadensis), which established a resident herd on the INL Site in the
1980s. Mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus) are also occasionally observed at the INL Site. Mountain
sheep (Ovis canadensis) were historically abundant in the Lost River, Lemhi, and Beaverhead
ranges to the north. Other big game animals, such as bison (Bison bison), no longer inhabit the
area. Evidence that bison were utilized by Native American populations on the Snake River Plain
is found at Bison and Veratic Rockshelters (Swanson 1972), Owl Cave (Butler 1978, 1986; Miller
1982, 1990), and Wilson Butte Cave (Gruhn 1961) in the form of bison remains with associated
cultural materials. Bison bone is also commonly encountered at archaeological sites across the INL
Site. Smaller animals of cultural significance include rabbits, badgers (Taxidea taxus), yellow-
bellied marmots (Marmota flaviventris), squirrels, chipmunks, and birds such as sage grouse, owls,
and eagles. Most of these species were exploited as sources of food, but others like owls and eagles
also served other functions (Smoak 2025: 239–241).

INL ENVIRONMENTAL AND GEOMORPHIC CONTEXTS
 Lands within the INL Site can be classed into three broad environmental zones
distinguished by depositional environment, soil development, and vegetation potential (Olson et
al. 1995; Shive et al. 2011: 1–6). These include: (1) alluvial floodplains and fan deposits; (2)
lacustrine deposits and playa sinks; and (3) basalt plains overlain by aeolian sands and loess
(Figure 1; Table 1). This system of classification is similar to what is discussed by INL Cultural
Resource Management Office (CRMO) (2026), who divide the INL Site into alluvial deposits,
colluvial deposits, playa deposits, and the basalt plain. For INL CRMO (2026), these features serve
as the basis for discussing the integrity of archaeological surface assemblages, determining buried
site potential, and understanding differential site formation processes and how they intersect with
cultural resource integrity determinations.

Alluvial Floodplains and Fan Deposits
 The largest perennial stream in the Pioneer Basin by volume is the Big Lost River. The
floodplain of the Big Lost forms a broad arc running southwest to northeast along the base of the
Big Lost Range from Arco to Howe, where it converges with the floodplain of the Little Lost River
and Birch Creek at the Lost River Sinks. On the INL Site, both the Big and Little Lost are
intermittent streams that peak with spring runoff between April and June and run dry from late
summer through early winter. Under drought conditions, the Big Lost River is often dry throughout
the year on the INL Site. Stream gauge data collected at the INL Diversion Dam show that no
water from the Big Lost River reached the INL Site for 20 of the past 40 years from 1984 to 2024
(United States Geological Survey 2025). Upstream diversions for irrigation are partially
responsible for reduced flows in recent decades, but periodic drought impacted discharge
throughout the late-19th and early-20th centuries, undermining the viability of homesteads in the
Pioneer Basin.

 Floodplain soils on the INL Site range from shallow (< 50 cm) to deep (> 150 cm), well-
drained, silty to sandy loams over alluvial gravels (Olson et al. 1995) deposited between 40,000
and 10,000 years ago (Hackett and Smith 1992; Kuntz et al. 1994; Morgan and Hackett 1989).
Alluvial bottoms support a Historical Climax Plant Community (HCPC) of basin big sagebrush,
thickspike wheatgrass, and basin wildrye (NRCS 2020l). Older alluvial terraces support an HCPC
of Wyoming big sagebrush and bluebunch wheatgrass (NRCS 2020i). The contemporary plant
community is generally similar in that basin big sagebrush, thickspike wheatgrass, western
wheatgrass, and basin wildrye are prevalent (Shive et al. 2019). However, near the northern
terminus of the Big Lost River, what was once a dry meadow community dominated by western
wheatgrass (see French and Mitchell 1983) has been significantly diminished due to impacts from
grazing and a general aridification of the landscape due to upstream water diversion.

 South of Arco, the Big Lost River flows out of the alluvium filled Big Lost River Valley
and across the sand and loess covered Quaternary basalt of the ESRP. Once the river enters Box
Canyon, the river is incised 5–30 m into the basalt, and only small amounts of alluvium are present
(Ostenaa et al. 2002: 95). Downstream of Box Canyon, the river channel and other Holocene
fluvial deposits (including remnants of a ~400 year old paleoflood) are inset within a broad surface
of Late Pleistocene alluvium (Ostenaa et al. 2002: 106). Holocene deposits generally consist of
finer grained sands and silts compared to the Pleistocene, with gravel present only as small bars in
the channel. This area of the Big Lost River floodplain is also home to peculiar Late
Pleistocene/Early Holocene geological features that Tullis (1995) refers to as “earth mounds.”
These mounds are typically round or oval in shape, less than half a meter in height, with a diameter
of between 8–14 meters. They are found in several contexts across the ESRP, including on alluvial
terraces of the Big Lost River, distal alluvial fan segments of the Lost River Range, and loess
sediments overlying basalt flows. They formed through the heaving of sediment by the growth of
ice lenses during a climatic period of intense seasonal freezing and subsequent pedogenesis
(Ostenaa et al. 2002: 96; Tullis 1995: 85) and can be used today as a temporal marker of terraces
of the Big Lost River floodplain. The gradient of the floodplain decreases as it continues to flow
northward and transitions from a terraced to a braided delta system between State Butte (north of
the Naval Reactors Facility and the sinks, where dozens of abandoned stream channels are super-
imposed upon one another.

 The northern quarter of INL Site is covered by a broad, gently sloping alluvial fan deposited
by Birch Creek during the Pleistocene. In contrast to the Big Lost, Birch Creek is fed primarily by
groundwater, allowing it to maintain a more consistent level of discharge throughout the year
(Kjelstrom and Berenbrock 1996). Before it was diverted for irrigation in the early 20th century
and a hydroelectric power station installed in 1987 (Swanson et al. 2003: 3), Birch Creek flowed
south along a braided channel that terminated in sinks at the present site of Test Area North. Soils
on the Birch Creek fan are shallow to deep, well-drained to excessively drained gravelly loams
formed in metamorphic and sedimentary limestone gravels from the Lemhi and Beaverhead
mountains. Deeper soils are dominated by an HCPC of Wyoming big sagebrush and bluebunch
wheatgrass (NRCS 2020i). Shallow soils are dominated by an HCPC of low sagebrush, bluebunch
wheatgrass, and Indian ricegrass (NRCS 2020j). Soils at the terminus of Birch Creek fan deposits
are more alkaline, supporting an HCPC of shadscale saltbush, Indian ricegrass, and needle-and-
thread (NRCS 2020k). Current vegetation is similar to HCPC communities in that Wyoming big
sagebrush and bluebunch wheatgrass are co-dominant. At the terminus of Birch Creek, shadscale
saltbush is the dominant shrub, but other shrubs such as winterfat and plains pricklypear are also
found. Bud sage, spiny hopsage, and greasewood are also present in lesser amounts (Shive et al.
2019).

 Geomorphic and hydrological studies indicate that Birch Creek Fan sediments were
primarily deposited during the Pinedale glaciation (22–13 thousand years ago). Funk (1976, as
cited in Pierce and Scott 1982: 692) describes particle-size distributions in the Birch Creek Valley
and concludes that “deposition of the fan gravels occurred in a high-energy, braided, fluvial system
characterized by fluctuating water and sediment discharges, high-regime flow, and rapid
aggradation.” Pierce and Scott (1982: 696) provide a date of between 25,000 and 11,000 years ago
for the younger gravel deposits and note their transportation by "sustained seasonal flows, probably

 8

at least 10 times larger than discharges of present streams" (ibid. 1982: 685). These high velocity
flows held glacial gravels in suspension, depositing them in deep layers across the Birch Creek
Valley (Berenbrock and Kjelstrom (1997: 3) describe the composition of the lower Birch Creek
Valley as largely coarse sand, gravel, and cobbles that are at least 20 feet deep (ibid. 1997: 6).

 There is further evidence of the Pleistocene age of the Birch Creek Fan in that fossil camel
remains were excavated by researchers at Idaho State College from only a few feet below the
surface in Birch Creek gravels (Nace et al. 1956: 125). Additionally, the more recent stability of
this surface is attested by the presence of regularly spaced, low-relief earth mounds like those of
the Big Lost River floodplain (Tullis 1995).

 Since the Pleistocene, the gravels of the Birch Creek alluvial fan have remained stable due
to drastically reduced stream flow (Pierce and Scott 1982). The sediments within the Birch Creek
Fan are highly transmissive and, unless the sediments are frozen, the rate of infiltration greatly
exceeds the snowmelt or precipitation rate. The Birch Creek channels within the braided system
are, therefore, very shallow with gravel floors. Viseras and Fernandez (1994) note that there is a
lack of terrace formation along distributary channels due to lateral channel migration; however,
there may be some very small, incipient terrace formation in some areas.

Lacustrine Deposits and Playa Sinks
 During cooler, less arid periods in the region’s climate history, runoff exceeded infiltration
at the sinks, and waters rose to join those of what is now Mud Lake to form Lake Terreton
(Gianniny et al. 2002). Lacustrine sediments left by Lake Terreton are hundreds of meters deep in
some areas, recording a long history of wet-dry climate cycles spanning the Late Pliocene and
Quaternary (Bestland et al. 2002). The most recent highstands of the lake date between the LGM
(~22 ka) and Late Pleistocene deglaciation (~11 ka) (Gianniny et al. 2002). Under these conditions,
Lake Terreton and the perennial streams that supplied it would have supported rich riparian and
wetland habitats, presenting a tremendous attraction to both people and the game on which they
relied. As the climate became warmer and dryer by the Early to Middle Holocene, waters receded,
and high winds reworked lakebed sediments into vast fields of shallow, partially stabilized, linear
dunes and playa lunettes (Forman and Pierson 2003). Under these conditions, mixed meadow and
shrub-steppe communities around the sinks would have remained an attractive resource for large
game.

 The Big Lost River, with headwaters in the Pioneer Mountains, is the most significant
tributary to Lake Terreton (Geslin et al. 1999: 295; Kuntz et al. 2003: 6). The river flows southeast
out of the Big Lost River Valley, then curves north toward Lake Terreton at the ESRP, meeting
the extinct Lake Terreton shoreline just south of the tip of the Lemhi Range. This portion of the
Big Lost River is commonly referred to as the Big Lost Trough, where low-relief and underfilling
of the subbasin created fanned-out braided channels flowing north into an area where the river
channels would have once met the lake (Gianniny et al. 2002: 78), perhaps forming a delta. Today,
this location south of the Lemhi Range is called the Big Lost River Sinks, where porous basalt
rocks allow surface water seepage, recharging the ESRP Aquifer (Zinsser 2021: 3–4). During
periods of high fill in antiquity, the total Lake Terreton surface area included the Big Lost Trough
subbasin and Mud Lake subbasin to the east. The Mud Lake subbasin is primarily an aquifer
discharge location, and its main contemporary tributaries are the small, south-flowing Beaver

 9

Creek and Camas Creek (Spinazola 1994: 14). Even at its peak extents, the lake was relatively
shallow; during highstand estimates of 1,463 and 1,468 meters above sea level (Forman and
Kaufman 1997; Stearns et al. 1939), the majority of the lake would not have exceeded 10 meters
in depth.

 Alluvial sediments from the trough were found to be dominantly of Big Lost River origins
(Geslin et al. 1999: 295), and Big Lost River sediments and lacustrine Lake Terreton sediments
are indistinguishable north of State Butte (Kuntz et al. 2003: 6). Fluctuations in lake levels are
closely linked to changes in environmental conditions in the mountains north of the plain, such as
deglaciation occurring at the Big Lost River headwaters in the Pioneer Mountains. While minor
late glacial advances (15,000–11,000 cal. BP) have been documented in the neighboring Sawtooth
Mountains due to moisture delivery by westerly winds (Mijal 2008; Sherard 2006; Thackray et al.
2004), cosmogenic nuclide dating showed that the onset of glacial retreat from the Pioneer
Mountains began around 20,000 cal. BP with nearly all glaciers disappearing before 15,000 cal.
BP (Warner 2020: 42). Thus, Lake Terreton fluctuations during periods of human occupation
(post-13,050 cal. BP; Waters et al. 2020: 9) would not have been largely dependent on glacial melt,
as hypothesized by Amidon (2016). Rather, rates of precipitation relative to evaporation were
likely the primary controls over fluctuations in lake levels throughout the Holocene (Gianniny et
al. 2002: 78).

 While environmental data on pluvial Lake Terreton do not characterize the range of
seasonal fluctuations, some data exist on the lake’s extent at singular points in time during the
Pleistocene and Holocene. A highstand was recorded at both the Big Lost Trough subbasin and
the Mud Lake subbasin ca. 120,000–160,000 cal. BP when the subbasins were likely connected
(Gianniny et al. 2002: 88). At 80,000–100,000 cal. BP, a subsequent highstand was recorded in
the Mud Lake subbasin (Gianniny et al. 2002: 89). Researchers documented another highstand at
the Mud Lake subbasin through lacustrine sediments containing aquatic gastropods located as high
as 10 meters above the modern Mud Lake level (~1,468 meters), radiocarbon dated to 25,400 cal.
BP (21,176 ±513 radiocarbon years before present (RCYBP), median age calibrated with IntCal20
from Forman and Pierson [2003, 193]). Researchers found that lacustrine gastropods at North
Lake, a desiccated portion of the Mud Lake subbasin, dating to 13,458 cal. BP (11,600 ±70
RCYBP, median age calibrated with IntCal20 from Gianniny et al. (2002: 87) showed a shoreline
recessed to 1,460 meters.

 Bright and Davis (1982: 25) confirmed a Late Holocene refilling of Lake Terreton through
a lacustrine snail dated to 656 cal. BP recovered from the northern-most part of the Big Lost
Trough, the “Birch Creek Playa.” Subsequent investigations by Levish et al. (1999) concluded that
the Late Holocene lacustrine conditions were confined to the playa, an area approximately 10 km2,
and that the lacustrine sediments outside the playa were probably correlated to a Late Pleistocene
highstand. This 10 km2 playa corresponds to the 1,458-meter elevation contour. If a lake level of
1,458 meters was extrapolated to the rest of the lake’s basin, Mud Lake would extend slightly
beyond its modern shoreline, and small ponds or lakes would have been scattered along the
“embayments,” where basalt ridges formed by two Pleistocene lava flows (Kuntz et al. 1994) met
the Pleistocene Lake Terreton shoreline.

 10

 Since the lake was sensitive to changes in precipitation and temperature, it fluctuated
drastically, not only between longer climate cycles lasting decades or centuries (Forman and
Pierson 2003), but within a single season as well. Gypsum-rich sediment deposits, as well as
evidence of an intermittently active aeolian system across the lakebed, reflect stark lake level
fluctuations between wet and arid climate cycles (Geslin et al. 1999: 295; Geslin et al. 2002: 11).
Moreover, ostracodes in the Mud Lake subbasin dating to the B-A were a species tolerant of
considerable seasonal fluctuations typical in shallow marshes (Gianniny et al. 2002: 85, 88). This
suggests that during cooler, wetter periods in the region’s climate history, the lake would have
been surrounded by rich wetland, wet meadow, dry meadow, and riparian communities
comparable to those near present-day Mud Lake and the Camas National Wildlife Refuge (see
Kittel et al. 2012).

 Since the terminal Pleistocene, the Terreton lakebeds on the INL Site have been reworked
into well-delineated parabolic and linear dunes. Dune fields are found across the ESRP and are
generally the result of later Holocene aridity and associated aeolian processes (Forman and
Kaufman 1997; Forman and Pierson 2003). Most are oriented southwest to northeast and formed
by regular, diel shifts in spring/summer wind direction (Forman and Pierson 2003). Forman and
Kaufman (1997) document more recent dune formations in the vicinity of Mud Lake where 2–4 m
high exposures reveal a sequence of lacustrine silt and sand unconformably capped by aeolian
sand, revealing a series of “hairpin” parabolic dunes that likely formed as late as the 19th century.

 Soils formed on Lake Terreton sediments vary in texture, drainage, and vegetation
potential. Dune field soils are formed in deep (0.5 to 2 meters), excessively-drained sand overlying
lacustrine silty clay loams (Olson et al. 1995: 14, 30–31). Deeper dunes support an HCPC of basin
big sagebrush and needle-and-thread (NRCS 2020g). Shallow dunes support an HCPC of basin
big sagebrush, Indian ricegrass, and needle-and-thread (NRCS 2020c). Soils formed in mixed
aeolian and lacustrine sediments exposed between dunes are deep, well-drained, alkaline silty clay
loams, loams, or sandy loams supporting an HCPC of basin big sagebrush and bluebunch
wheatgrass (NRCS 2020b). Contemporary vegetation studies reveal broad similarities to the
HCPCs described above, with basin big sagebrush and needle-and-thread being common among
dune formations of the Lake Terreton basin. However, an increase in green rabbitbrush, thickspike
wheatgrass, and introduced annual forbs has also been documented, consistent with changes in
annual precipitation (Forman 2024; Shive et al. 2019).

Basalt Plains
 South of Pleistocene Lake Terreton, the INL Site east and west of the Lost River Floodplain
is a patchwork of Pleistocene basalt flows ranging in age from >730 to 200 ka. The Cerro Grande
flow between Big Southern Butte and the Twin Buttes is more recent, dating to 13,380 ± 350 BP
(Kuntz et al. 1994). Olson et al. (1995) distinguish between two broad soil complexes formed on
older Pleistocene basalts. Soils in the Grassy Butte series and Malm-Bondfarm-Matheson complex
are formed in aeolian sands that occur throughout central portions of the INL Site. Soils in the
Pancheri series and Coffee-Nargon-Atom complex are loess-derived loams. These occur primarily
to the south and west of the Lost River Floodplain at slightly higher elevations. Differences in
climax vegetation communities between the two soil types are subtle, but sandy soils are more
susceptible to invasive species and altered fire regimes than loamy soils derived from aeolian loess.

 11

 Significant loess deposition on the ESRP occurred during distinct episodes followed by
periods of stability that are generally coincident with the pluvial lake and glacial cycles in the
region (Forman et al. 1993). The most recent episodes date between ca. 80,000 and 60,000 years
ago and between 40,000 and 10,000 years ago, separated by a prolonged period of more than
20,0000 years of pedogenesis (Forman et al. 1993). Loess-derived soils on basalt plains of the
ESRP vary in depth and texture from shallow to deep loamy, loamy basins, or shallow and stony
(NRCS 2020a, 2020b, 2020d, 2020f). They are well-drained, but permeability ranges with slope,
texture, and depth from impermeable to moderate. Runoff and erosion hazard are low to high.
Wind erosivity varies with plant cover from low to moderate. Fine shallow to deep loamy soil
supports an HCPC of basin big sagebrush and bluebunch wheatgrass (NRCS 2020a, 2020b).
Shallow stony loams support an HCPC of Wyoming big sagebrush and bluebunch wheatgrass
(NRCS 2020f). Deep silty loams in shallow basins between lava flows support an HCPC of basin
big sagebrush and basin wildrye (NRCS 2020d).

 Portions of the basalt plains east and north of the Big Lost River Trough are overlain by
sandy dunes of variable thickness. Similar dune fields near St. Anthony, Blackfoot, and American
Falls are derived from sediments associated with Late Pleistocene deglaciation in the Snake River
basin. Those within the Pioneer Basin are likely associated with similar processes on the Big Lost
and Little Lost Rivers (Forman et al. 1993; Hoover et al. 2018). Sandy soils are highly mobile,
moderately deep to deep, well-drained to excessively drained with moderate to rapid permeability,
depending on depth to bedrock. Susceptibility to runoff and water erosion varies with slope from
slight to severe (NRCS 2020c). Wind erosivity varies with plant cover from moderate to very
severe (NRCS 2020c). These soils support an HCPC dominated by basin big sagebrush, Indian
ricegrass, and needle-and-thread. Low basins between lava flows may collect deeper, finer
sediments through ephemeral ponding, hosting an HCPC of basin big sagebrush and basin wildrye
(NRCS 2020d). A high incidence of wildfire between 1994 and 2020 has led to a loss of sagebrush
cover over much of the basalt plains, but total vegetation cover has rebounded. Sagebrush and
needle-and-thread grassland are still common, but contemporary studies of vegetation cover also
document extensive cheatgrass ruderal grassland and a preponderance of green rabbitbrush and
thickspike wheatgrass across much of the basalt plain, particularly on the central and eastern half
of the INL Site, much of which can be tied directly to post-fire revegetation (Pink and Fugitt 2024;
Shive et al. 2019). Loss of shrub cover has led to an increase in perennial bunchgrasses, such as
Indian ricegrass, which occurs in consistent but low densities in recently burned areas.

 Although it is easy to characterize the basalt plain as a flat and homogenous sagebrush
steppe, the topography is actually quite variable, consisting of a patchwork of ephemeral ponds,
grassy flats, hilltop vent craters, and lava tube caves. For example, basalt flows were often fed
through lava tube systems that hardened after volcanic activity, and over time entrances to these
underground tubes have formed through roof collapses. In some cases, the collapses formed basalt
rock shelters with little to no underground caverns, and in other cases long lava tubes are exposed.
In these contexts, it is possible for aeolian and alluvial accumulation to create some of the best
potential for buried archaeological materials in stratified contexts with integrity. Similarly, past
predictive models by Ringe (1995) and Plager et al. (2004) highlight craters, lava tube caves, and
lava edges (particularly those along volcanic ridges or lava embayments offering wind protection)
as some of the areas with the highest potential for cultural resources to be present.

FIRE
 It is important to note that the more localized conditions within these larger
geomorphological and environmental units are not static; rather, they are in a constant state of flux
due to ongoing deposition, erosion, and other formation processes. One prominent process is fire,
which has the potential to eliminate entire vegetal communities and drastically alter the landscape.
Over the past several decades, fire has been particularly prevalent across the basalt plains, resulting
in declining sagebrush cover, increased grass cover, and an increase in invasive annuals. In these
contexts, small plants and bushes help to anchor sediments in place, and loss of vegetation cover
from fires can result in severe soil erosion from regional high winds, causing deflation (Sankey et
al. 2009; Wagenbrenner et al. 2013).

 Range fires are a natural process, yet more recent efforts to document fires on the INL Site
have demonstrated that fires repeatedly affect large swathes of land. Furthermore, the frequency
of fires on the INL Site over the past quarter century is several times higher than the estimated
historical fire frequency (Table 1; see Pink and Fugitt 2024). In the absence of normal fire
frequency, sagebrush, bitterbrush, and juniper may begin to crowd out grasses. As fires become
more frequent than historic return intervals, shrub cover can be reduced or eliminated, and – under
extreme conditions – the INL Site may transition to a new state dominated by cheatgrass and other
invasive annuals (NRCS 2020c). For the most part, however, post-fire vegetation on the INL Site
is composed primarily of native perennial shrubs and grasses that recover quickly following fire.
Dominant species include green rabbitbrush, spineless horsebrush, thickspike wheatgrass, and
Indian ricegrass (Shive et al. 2019). Such habitat is less suitable for sagebrush obligates such as
sage grouse and pygmy rabbits but may be more favorable to ungulate species such as pronghorn
and elk. Invasive annuals such as cheatgrass, desert alyssum, and Russian thistle are common, but
are most prevalent near roads and disturbed areas.
