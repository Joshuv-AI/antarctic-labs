Patient health outcomes for evaluation of pharmacist-led primary care: a scoping review
Authors: Claire Keenan, Amy Grant, Andrea Bishop, Kristy Hancock, Natalie Kennie-Kaulbach, Hannah MacConnell, Alex Robart, Noel Guscott, Charmaine Cooke, Rachel Giacomantonio, Mina Tadrous, Lisa Woodill, Jennifer E Isenor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPharmacists’ role in primary care has rapidly evolved in recent years, driven by pressures on primary care and supported by increased government funding, further enabling full scope of practice while reducing patient costs. To optimize healthcare delivery, the patient outcomes of pharmacist-led primary care in community settings must be evaluated.AimThis scoping review aimed to identify and explore the breadth of patient health outcomes used to assess the quality, effectiveness, and safety of pharmacist-led primary care.MethodA comprehensive search was conducted in four databases and Google for articles published from 1997 to July 2023, updated in February 2025. Database records were screened independently by two reviewers at two stages in Covidence. Google search results were screened by one reviewer, with selections verified by a second reviewer. Studies were included if they involved pharmacist-led primary care and reported patient health outcomes related to quality, effectiveness, and/or safety. Extracted data on study design, disease state, clinical services, and patient outcomes were categorized by two reviewers. Data are reported descriptively (frequencies, percentages) and are displayed using heat maps. Results were reported using PRISMA-ScR guidelines.ResultsA total of 299 studies (297 peer-reviewed, 2 grey literature) were included from 10,498 database records and 300 grey literature search results. Experimental study designs were the most common (61%) and increased in prevalence over the study period. Patient populations primarily comprised those with chronic diseases, primarily endocrine (27%) and cardiovascular (26%), with the volume of studies focused on these groups also increasing over time. Studies often evaluated multiple types of clinical service, with more focusing on education or counselling (29%) and chronic disease management (23%). A wide variety of outcomes were measured, with more than 70% being positive. The number of studies reporting outcomes increased markedly after 2002, particularly those examining clinical measures (29%), resource use (19%), and laboratory measures (14%).ConclusionThis scoping review identified patient health outcomes used to evaluate pharmacist-led primary care. Although studies on pharmacist-led clinical services have increased, the most assessed services may not reflect current practice. These findings should inform health system metrics grounded in outcomes that matter to patients to ensure safe, effective pharmacist-led care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11096-026-02179-z.Keywords: Community pharmacy, Evaluation, Patient outcomes, Pharmacy, Primary care, Scoping review

Introduction

Pharmacists are among the most accessible primary healthcare professionals [1], and they have been increasingly incorporated into primary care delivery in recent decades [2–4]. In Canada, this evolution has included authority to prescribe (new medications, adjustments, substitutions, or renewals), administer vaccines, and manage chronic disease drug therapy [2, 4–6]. The incorporation of pharmacists into primary care through legislative changes has accelerated in response to system-wide challenges and shortages of other healthcare professionals [7, 8]. In 2024, over 7 million Canadians (17%) did not have access to a regular primary care provider [9], and 74% could not access a same-day or next-day appointment [10]. Similar enablement of scope has been seen internationally, with immunization, diagnosis, and point-of-care testing becoming more common [11–14].

The incorporation of pharmacists in primary care delivery accelerated during the COVID-19 pandemic, with pharmacists playing a more significant role in patient care [15–17]. Contributing factors included decreased primary care access during the initial wave of the pandemic [18], avoidance of physicians or hospitals due to fear of infection [19, 20], and changes in government funding to ensure continued access to essential medications [21–23]. Pharmacists are now among the most frequently accessed healthcare providers in some jurisdictions [1, 24]. For example, in Canada, approximately half the population visits a pharmacy weekly and see a pharmacist up to ten times more often than their family physician [1, 20, 21]. In the United States, over half of Americans turn to pharmacies first for non-emergent care [27, 28], while approximately 10% of those living in the European Union visit pharmacies daily [29].

Pharmacist-led primary care services have emerged as a key strategy to address workforce shortages [30] and the growing burden of chronic diseases worldwide [31]. Pharmacists play a key role in global health efforts and are essential players in working towards the United Nation’s (UN) third Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 3), which aims to “ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages” [32]. While often unrecognized, pharmacists contribute significantly to primary care in limited resource settings [33].

Given the evolving role of pharmacists in primary care [4], regulators and decision-makers face a growing need for metrics that speak to the safety, quality, and outcomes of pharmacist care. Measurement efforts to date have been focused on dispensing and workflow [34–36] or on specific medical conditions (e.g., osteoporosis, asthma management, medication review) [37–41]. While individual, disease and condition specific studies have demonstrated that pharmacist-led primary care can improve medication adherence, blood pressure control, and patient-reported outcomes [37, 39, 42, 43], as well as system-level impacts such as reduced hospitalizations [44, 45], there is a need for meaningful examination of pharmacist-led care.

Aim

This scoping review aimed to identify and explore the breadth of patient health outcomes used to assess the quality, effectiveness, and safety of pharmacist-led primary care.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was informed by methodologies from Arksey and O’Malley [46], Levac and colleagues [47], and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) [48]. Results are reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extensions for scoping reviews [49] and literature searches [50]. The review protocol was registered on Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/WZ8UM).

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed and executed by a health sciences librarian (KH). The initial search was developed in MEDLINE (Ovid) and peer reviewed by a second health sciences librarian using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guideline [51]. The finalized MEDLINE search was adapted for CINAHL Full Text (EBSCO), Embase (Elsevier), and Scopus (Elsevier). The database searches were first conducted on July 13, 2023, and updated on February 6, 2025. Relevant grey literature was identified through three Google search queries developed using select terms from the database searches. The Google search results were limited to PDF files only to retrieve reports and exclude webpages describing or promoting primary care pharmacist services. Grey literature searches were conducted on July 4 and 5, 2024. All searches are reported in Supplementary Material 1.

No study design or date limits were applied to the original searches; however, during screening, the decision was made to exclude sources published before 1997, as this is when the term/practice ‘pharmaceutical care’ became the standard of care [52]. Search results, screening and selection are summarized in a PRISMA flow diagram [53] (Fig. 1).

This review considered all literature on any patient receiving pharmacist-led clinical services in primary care settings (e.g., community pharmacies, primary care clinics, general practices, health centres). Articles were excluded if the services were not in a primary-care setting (e.g., specialist clinics, specialized ambulatory care, long-term care). Articles were also excluded if the intervention was not led by a pharmacist, if services were performed collaboratively without the role and impact of the pharmacist reported separately (e.g., in a primary care clinic with a physician making recommendations alongside the pharmacist), if the service was aimed at other healthcare providers (e.g., a pharmacist educating a physician on optimal prescribing), if the services were provided by a trainee or resident pharmacist, or if the focus was on technical aspects of dispensing/drug distribution or a medication regimen review without interacting with patients. Both peer reviewed and grey literature with abstracts in English were eligible for inclusion. If a non-English article made it to full text review based on the English abstract being included, a translated version was obtained for review. All study designs were included (e.g., experimental, quasi-experimental, observational), except for editorials, commentaries, conference abstracts, letters, narrative reviews, and systematic or scoping reviews. Exploring the breadth of the clinical outcomes of the pharmacist-led care provided was the primary concept of interest. Articles were excluded if the outcomes were focused on the larger pharmacy system and costs or solely involved feasibility, satisfaction, or prevalence. All inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Supplementary Material 2.

Study selection

All database citations were uploaded into Covidence review software by Veritas Health Innovation [54] to facilitate screening and extraction; duplicates were automatically removed. When the updated search was conducted in February 2025, the new citations were uploaded to a separate Covidence project where duplicates were removed before being added to the main Covidence project. All records were then screened independently by a team of two reviewers at both title/abstract and full text levels (HM, AR, JEI, NKK, RG, AG, CK, OH, AB). Any conflicts were resolved by a third reviewer (JEI, AG, AB, NKK, CK, OH). The grey literature search was conducted by one reviewer (KH) by executing each query and screening the first 10 pages of search results. Potentially relevant sources were opened in a new browser tab for further scrutinizing, and relevant sources documented in an Excel spreadsheet, with a second reviewer (JEI) verifying the eligibility of the sources.

Data extraction and synthesis of results

The research team developed a standardized data extraction form in Covidence. Extracted data elements included country, setting (e.g., urban, rural), practice setting (e.g., community pharmacy), study objective, study design (e.g., descriptive, experimental), target patient population (e.g., adolescents, adults, older adults), disease state (e.g., cardiovascular, respiratory), pharmacist-led clinical service (e.g., chronic disease management, education), evaluation information (e.g., patient consultation, focus groups/interviews), and outcomes. The data extraction form was piloted in Covidence by the team working in assigned pairs. Six reviewers (CK, OH, KW, JC, AR, HM) then completed the single-reviewer data extraction, and two reviewers (AB and NKK) conducted an audit of 10% of the data to validate the extraction.

Four main elements were extracted for analysis: study design, clinical service, disease state, and clinical outcomes. Due to the heterogeneity of the data, each extraction element was further analyzed and recategorized to support systematic reporting. Study design was initially extracted based on how it was reported in the original studies and was re-categorized as either experimental (quasi-experimental (no random assignment; however, an intervention is tested) or randomized trial) or observational (descriptive, observational analytic, or cross-sectional) [55]. Clinical services provided were grouped into higher-level categories, adapted from the Pharmacy Association of Nova Scotia [56]: chronic disease management, education/counselling, immunizations, medication review, pharmaceutical care, prescribing, referrals, and testing. Disease state was grouped into conditions by body-system or clinical domain to best capture the breadth of disease states and conditions studied, with categories reported as: cardiovascular, endocrine, infectious disease, mental health and addictions, pain, respiratory, or other (e.g., obesity, Parkinson’s). Lastly, clinical outcomes were adapted from a review of clinical outcomes reported in clinical pharmacy intervention studies [57]. Categories reported include: clinical measures/assessment (e.g., risk scores, change in clinical outcomes), resource utilization (e.g. hospital admission, ED use), medication regimen characteristics (e.g., smoking status, time in therapeutic range), laboratory measures (e.g., blood glucose, HIV screening), adherence to medication, patient satisfaction, identified/resolved drug therapy problems (e.g., change in medication based on drug therapy problems), adherence to guidelines (e.g., correct dosing based on clinical guidelines), receipt of immunization, and adverse drug events/reactions. Clinical service and outcome category definitions and examples

Once the main extraction elements were identified and categories established, the data were re-categorized and validated by two members of the research team (CK and AG), with all discrepancies resolved by a third team member (JEI). Many studies met the criteria for multiple categories and were double coded for disease state, clinical service, and clinical outcomes. However, studies were not double coded if they included more than one disease or clinical service under the same category (e.g., Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) and asthma coded only once as ‘Respiratory’). Heat maps were generated to illustrate when extracted elements were reported, and how these patterns changed over time. Heat maps allow for pattern detection and exploratory, descriptive analysis in large datasets [58]. Study design was reported by the frequency of each research design type, while the target patient population were identified based on the number of different diseases studied in each article. The latter approach was also applied to clinical service and clinical outcomes, where each category represented the number of services or outcomes measured rather than total number of studies.

Patient/public involvement

A person with lived and living experience (PWLLE) was involved as a member of the research team. This PWLLE reviewed and provided feedback on the methods, extraction of data, and reviewed knowledge translation products (e.g., presentations, manuscript). The team met approximately four times a year. Contribution to activities on the project was tailored to the PWLLEs preferences and time available to contribute.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

A total of 10,498 studies were retrieved through database searches and 300 through the grey literature search. After removing 4,904 duplicates, the remaining 5,594 articles were screened at the title/abstract level, followed by full text screening of 486 articles with 299 studies included (Fig. 1).

Characteristics of included studies are described in Supplementary Material 3. Briefly, over half (n = 193) were published after 2017, most were in English (n = 296), and most (n = 193) were conducted in a community pharmacy setting. The majority of the studies were published in the United States (39.1%), European countries (26.8%), Oceania (11.0%) and Canada (9.4%). There were fewer studies from other regions, including Asia (7.0%), the Middle East (3.3%), South America (1.7%), and Africa (1.7%). All included studies are listed in Supplementary Material 4.

Study design

There was an increase in the frequency of all study design types over time (Fig. 2). The volume of studies using experimental designs was highest overall (n = 182; 60.9%), which included randomized trials (n = 78; 26.1%) and quasi-experimental studies (n = 105; 35.1%) and increased the most over the study period. There were fewer observational studies (n = 115; 38.5%), with descriptive studies (n = 55; 18.4%) being the most common.

Target population

The most commonly reported target patient population studied involved patients with endocrine (n = 104; 27.2%) and cardiovascular (n = 101; 26.4%) diseases, with the volume increasing over time (Fig. 3). Studies exploring pain and infectious diseases remained low, aside from a slight increase in infectious disease studies from 2017–2021. Prior to 2017, there were few studies focusing on mental health/addictions (n = 9 pre-2017) and respiratory diseases (n = 19 pre-2017). While there was a consistent increase over time for most other target populations, apart from those categorized as ‘Other’, both mental health/addictions (n = 28 post-2017) and respiratory diseases (n = 33 post-2017) increased largely from 2017 onwards.

Target patient population by time.

Other – included osteoporosis, obesity, travel-related illnesses/vaccines, pregnancy, sleep health, HIV, minor ailments, Parkinson’s disease, hepatitis, cancer, contraception, eye disease, tetanus, frailty, and Alzheimer’s disease. Each ‘Other’ population was identified fewer than 6 times.

Clinical service

The types of clinical services studied over time are depicted in Fig. 4. Education/counselling was the most commonly studied clinical service (n = 142; 28.8%), followed by chronic disease management (n = 112; 22.7%), medication review (n = 66; 13.4%), pharmaceutical care (n = 62; 12.6%), testing (n = 53; 10.8%), and prescribing (n = 40; 8.1%). Referrals (n = 6; 1.2%) and immunizations/injections (n = 12; 2.4%) were less frequently studied, but the volume still increased over time.

Outcomes

There were a total of 1326 clinical outcomes of pharmacist-led primary care reported (Fig. 5). The most frequent outcomes were focused on clinical measures/assessment by pharmacists (n = 381; 28.7%) and the most infrequent categories involved receipt of immunization (n = 27; 2%) and adverse drug events/reactions (n = 19; 1.4%). Most outcomes (n = 959; 72.3%) were reported to have positive change (demonstrating evidence of improvement), while 24% (n = 327) were neutral (no evidence of change) and very few (n = 40; 3%) were negative (demonstrating evidence of adverse change). Individual outcome categories were compared descriptively, given the low number of neutral and negative outcomes within each category (Supplementary Material 5). There were more neutral adverse drug events outcomes (e.g., number of drug reactions identified), and a higher proportion of positive patient satisfaction outcomes. There was also a higher proportion of negative outcomes related to adherence to medication (e.g., reported non-adherence) and a higher proportion of neutral clinical measures/assessment by pharmacists’ outcomes (e.g., no change in recorded blood pressure rates).

Discussion

This scoping review showed a consistent increase in published studies on pharmacist-led primary care over time. Although the number and diversity of studies have increased—particularly since 2017—the findings demonstrated that evaluations continued to focus largely on traditional pharmacist services. These services included education, counselling, and condition-specific management (e.g., diabetes). While research on the current full scope of pharmacy practice is increasing, these areas remain less frequently assessed. Of note, prescribing was not commonly measured (8% of all the clinical services measured), and it has only increased slightly (9.4%) since 2017.

Despite an increasing number of studies measuring pharmacist-led care, the types of services measured do not represent the shift in provision of care by pharmacists. Early concepts of ‘pharmaceutical care’ [52, 59] focused on medication dispensing. Since then, pharmacists’ scope has expanded to include prescribing and renewing prescriptions, administering vaccines, ordering lab tests, managing common ambulatory conditions (also referred to as “minor ailments”), and supporting chronic disease management [2–4, 6]. These regulatory changes have been in place for more than 10 years in some jurisdictions [2, 11–13, 42, 60]. However, the peer-reviewed literature has not kept pace with this change. As pharmacist scope of practice continues to evolve internationally, more studies are needed on the interventions and outcomes of this care. Future research should consider whether changes in the types of interventions delivered and related outcomes have kept pace with the shift in care provision.

More studies targeted patients with endocrine and cardiovascular conditions. This reflects both the prevalence of these diseases and the established role of pharmacists in managing chronic conditions. In 2023, 46% of Canadians reported living with at least one major chronic disease. The most common conditions included arthritis (20.6%) and high blood pressure (19.9%), a risk factor for many cardiovascular diseases [61]. Globally, chronic diseases are the leading cause of death and disability, accounting for 43 million deaths in 2021 [62]. Pharmacists have the skills and expertise to play a larger role in chronic disease management, where medication management is a cornerstone of treatment [4]. Of note, research on respiratory diseases and mental health/addictions was relatively limited before 2017 but has increased modestly in the past several years. Overall, the findings indicated that while pharmacist-led care is being evaluated across a range of medical conditions, the research remains concentrated in areas where pharmacist interventions are well-established.

The clinical outcomes reported in this review were largely positive (or demonstrating evidence of improvement), particularly for patient satisfaction, which was measured alongside other outcomes measured and pharmacist-led clinical measurement. Outcomes also varied widely across studies. The most frequently measured included clinical measures/assessment by pharmacists, resource utilization, and laboratory measures. These findings are consistent with prior literature demonstrating that pharmacist interventions can improve clinical parameters, adherence to medication and pharmaceutical guidelines, and overall quality of care in chronic disease management [63–65]. Some negative outcomes were reported, most often related to adherence or adverse drug events. However, these results were generally comparable to previous reports of adherence outcomes, [64] with outcomes reported being similar to usual care (e.g., care from a family physician) [65]. The remainder of outcomes were largely positive. Positive patient satisfaction aligns with evidence that patients value the accessibility, counselling, and education provided by pharmacists, supporting their evolving role in primary care [66].

Notably, there was an absence of patient-reported experience and outcome measures. Despite a growing body of literature on the clinical role of pharmacists and its positive impact on patient health, there is little data to reflect experience and outcome measures that are important to patients. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), which assess the patient’s health condition directly from the patient [67], and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs), which evaluate patients’ perceptions of their experience with healthcare services [64], are essential components to enhancing patient-centred care. While satisfaction has traditionally been a primary outcome measure [69, 70], and was frequently identified in this review, further analysis into patient experiences of accessing pharmacy care, affordability, and quality and utility of service received would facilitate more accurate assessments. Development of a conceptual framework for classifying PROMs/PREMs within pharmacist-led primary care would support embedding patient-reported measures into routine care. Without evaluation of PROMs/PREMs, care may not align with patient priorities, and patient harms or burdens may go unmeasured [71]. This may limit the ability to fully measure impact [72] and underscores an important gap in the literature.

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review followed an established rigorous method for conducting scoping reviews [46–48]. The research team met regularly to discuss data extraction, and each extraction element was analyzed and categorized systematically. As an inherent limitation of a scoping review, this study explored the breadth rather than depth of current research [73]. However, this breadth was appropriate for the study objective of mapping the current literature on pharmacist-led primary care rather than evaluating the rigor and quality of included studies. As a descriptive, rather than evaluative study, certain topics were beyond scope, such as measuring and comparing efficacy based on outcomes measured. Most included studies were published in the United States, Europe, Oceania and Canada, which limits the ability to describe pharmacist-led primary care in other jurisdictions and lower income countries. There is also the potential of publication bias, which may result in an overrepresentation of positive results. Studies included in this scoping review were eligible if the abstract was in English. While efforts were made to find translations of non-English articles, this criterion may have led to the exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages.

Unlike the database searches that were conducted twice, the grey literature search was not executed a second time, and only 2 search results were retained. Although a systematic search was conducted, the search strategy and inclusion criteria may have resulted in missing relevant results. Finally, findings in this review are up to date as of February 2025, and additional relevant studies may have been published since the search was conducted. However, this scoping review provides a comprehensive view of studies on pharmacist-led services in primary care that have been published in the past 30 years.

Conclusion

This review consolidates the current literature around pharmacist-led primary care and identifies the shifting focus of outcomes research over the past 30 years. This study found an increase in pharmacist-led primary care research over time with primarily positive outcomes. However, significant gaps in measurement exist as literature to date has not kept pace with pharmacists’ full scope. Studies have focused on measuring traditional services, such as counselling, education, and chronic disease management, and less on the breadth of current practice including prescribing, vaccination, and point-of-care testing.

Future evaluation of pharmacist-led primary care should incorporate outcomes that capture full scope and meaningful patient-centred metrics to more accurately reflect pharmacists’ current contributions to primary care. Developing a consistent and comprehensive patient-centered outcome measurement framework is essential to support safe, high-quality, and well-integrated pharmacist provided care within the wider healthcare system [35, 74]. These frameworks should consider and include outcomes that are important to patients in order to improve quality of care [71, 75].
