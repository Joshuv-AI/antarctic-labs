Risk factors for revision amputation and infection after definitive closure of traumatic amputations: a retrospective cohort study at a Level I academic trauma center
Authors: Jaquelyn Kakalecik, Konstantin Brnjos, Ayman Habib, Terrie Vasilopoulos, Jennifer E. Hagen, Thomas A. Krupko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:The purpose of this study was to identify risk factors for revision amputation and postoperative infection after definitive closure of traumatic amputations.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients presenting with traumatic lower extremity amputation or injuries requiring amputation during the initial operative intervention at a level one trauma center between January 2005 and April 2024. Patients under 18 or without an acute fracture in the amputated limb were excluded. Collected variables included mechanism of injury, time to antibiotics and debridement, and final amputation level. Computed tomography angiography abnormalities, tourniquet use, gross contamination, injury severity score, and body mass index (BMI) were compared using Fisher exact and Mann–Whitney nonparametric tests.Results:Eighty-six patients (mean age 44.9 ± 16.9 years) met inclusion criteria. Nearly half were smokers (48%) with an average BMI of 29.3 ± 7.1; the median injury severity score was 22. Tourniquets were present on arrival in 40% of cases. Although most presented with tibia-level amputations, 37% ultimately required above- or through-knee amputation. These patients were more likely to have American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status scores ≥4 (39% vs 13%, P = 0.006) and present with Gustilo–Anderson type IIIC injuries (70% vs 48%, P = 0.045). Below-knee amputations were associated with higher ambulation rates (72% vs 53%, P = 0.010) and reduced requirement for assistive devices (24% vs 53%, P = 0.009). Patients undergoing further debridement after final closure had higher BMI (P = 0.009) and increased risk of revision amputation (52% vs 11%, P < 0.001).Conclusion:Revision amputation after definitive stump closure was associated with both patient and treatment-related factors, particularly higher BMI and the need for additional debridement. When feasible, preservation of a below-knee amputation was associated with better functional outcomes, underscoring the role of thoughtful surgical planning and attention to infection control in optimizing outcomes after traumatic lower extremity amputation.

1. Introduction

Lower extremity amputations are performed for a variety of indications, including tumors, vascular insufficiency, infection, or trauma. Although ischemia, diabetes, and peripheral arterial disease are the most common causes of amputation, trauma is also a significant contributing etiology. The initial priority in trauma care is stabilizing the patient, with limb considerations addressed once the patient is hemodynamically stable. The decision to amputate a traumatized extremity is multifactorial and should be reflective of the patient as a whole: including resuscitation status, injury severity score (ISS) as representative of damage to the entire person, perfusion status of the limb, patient and family preferences, and capabilities of the trauma center/receiving facility.

Patients with traumatic amputations often require multiple operative debridements before definitive closure. Fracture zone of injury, contamination and lack of adequate soft tissue coverage can result in a more proximal amputation may ultimately be required than initially anticipated. Generally, more distal amputations, especially those that preserve a functional knee, are preferred, as they are associated with better rehabilitation potential and improved functional outcomes.1,2 Surgeons aim to preserve as much limb length as possible to optimize recovery. Below-knee amputations (BKAs) have been shown to result in better SF-36 scores, higher rates of prosthesis use, and lower pain levels compared with above-knee amputations (AKAs).2 However, BKAs are not without complications, with reoperation rates approaching 24% and conversion to AKA occurring in approximately 2% of cases.1

Although traumatic amputations have been well studied in military populations and mass casualty settings,3–6 there is a relative paucity of literature documenting final amputation levels in civilian trauma patients. The purpose of this study was to identify risk factors for revision amputation and postoperative infection in patients presenting with traumatic amputations at our academic medical center. Secondarily, we examined prosthesis utilization, ambulatory status, use of assistive devices, and the need for further surgical debridement after final closure.

2. Materials and methods

Before performing this retrospective cohort study, institutional review board (IRB) approval was obtained. We retrospectively reviewed the charts of adult patients who presented to a single level one trauma center between January 2005 and March 2024 with traumatic lower extremity amputations with a minimum of 3 months of clinical follow-up. This included patients who presented with complete traumatic amputations and those with severely mangled limbs who underwent amputation during the initial trip to the operating room, without a preceding attempt at limb salvage. We excluded patients who were under 18, did not have an acute fracture in the amputated limb, had nontraumatic indications for amputation (e.g., chronic wounds or acute limb ischemia without revascularization options), or underwent amputation after the initial trip to the operating room or after attempts at limb salvage. Only amputations performed by orthopaedic surgeons were included; cases performed by vascular or general surgery were excluded. The initial amputation was performed by the orthopaedic surgeon on call (not necessarily trauma fellowship trained), and subsequent serial debridements and definitive stump closure were performed by the orthopaedic trauma service. Definitive closure was completed once the wound was deemed clean and viable by the attending orthopaedic trauma surgeon.

Deidentified patient data were acquired from our institutional electronic medical record (EPIC Systems, Verona, WI) using Current Procedural Terminology codes 27880, 28805, 28800, 27889, 27888, 27882, 27884, 27886, 27590, 27592, 27596, 27594, and 27295. Patients' charts were reviewed to collect demographic data including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), injury severity score (ISS), mechanism of injury, substance abuse history, chronic kidney disease, or diabetes mellitus, Gustilo–Anderson classification. Time to antibiotic administration, time to operative debridement, days until definitive wound closure, total number of surgical procedures performed on the injured limb during the index hospitalization, presence of a tourniquet upon hospital arrival, presence of gross contamination, and the need for secondary coverage were recorded. Where applicable, we reviewed the number of intact vessels in the injured limb as seen on CT angiography.

The initial level of amputation at hospital arrival was compared with the final amputation level, using anatomical segments to standardize the measurement of limb shortening. For example, if a patient presented with a traumatic below-knee amputation but ultimately required an above-knee amputation distal to the femoral isthmus, this was classified as a 2-segment reduction. For patients requiring a more proximal amputation than their initial injury, the number of segments shortened was documented. In addition, when applicable, the distance from the final amputation site to the nearest joint was measured using postoperative radiographs.

We also assessed postoperative outcomes, including hospital readmissions for superficial infection or cellulitis and the need for repeat surgical debridement within 1 year. At final follow-up, we documented ambulatory status and prosthesis use.

Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 17 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary NC). Continuous measures were summarized as means with standard deviations and medians with 25th to 75th percentiles. Categorical measures were summarized as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were analyzed using Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, Fisher exact tests, and z-tests for proportions. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

Eighty-six patients met inclusion criteria. The mean age was 44.9 ± 16.9 years, and mean BMI was 29.3 ± 7.1. Most patients were male (73%) and White (73%), with smoking history in 48% and diabetes in 13%. The median ISS was 22 (IQR 9.5–31.5), and most patients were ASA 3 (48%) or ASA 4 (21%). A tourniquet was present on arrival in 40% of cases.

The most common mechanism of injury was motor vehicle or motorcycle collision (77%), with smaller numbers from falls, chainsaw injuries, farm equipment, all-terrain vehicles, crush injury, explosion, and other causes. Gustilo–Anderson classification was IIIA in 7%, IIIB in 35%, and IIIC in 53%, and gross contamination was noted in 48%. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) showed no vessel runoff in 56% of patients. Secondary coverage was required in 22%, with a median of 4 OR trips (range 1–8). Median time to antibiotics was 22 minutes, and time to operative debridement was 207 minutes (range 112–365.5).

The most common initial amputation level on arrival were tibia below isthmus (26%), tibia above isthmus (21%), and tibia at isthmus (15%), with only 9% presenting with amputations above or through the knee (Table 1). However, 37% of patients ultimately required conversion to above- or through-knee amputations. Final level of above- or through-knee amputation was significantly associated with tourniquet use on arrival (59% vs 28%, P = 0.006), ASA ≥4 (39% vs 13%, P = 0.006), and Gustilo–Anderson IIIC injuries (70% vs 48%, P = 0.045). Although not statistically significant, those with above- or through-knee amputations were less likely to have any vessel runoff (29% vs 54%, P = 0.069). Patients with above- or through-knee amputations had a higher median ISS (27, IQR 17–34) compared with those with below-knee amputations (14, IQR 9–27, P = 0.016). The number of segments shortened from initial to final amputation ranged from 0 to 6 (median: 2), with 8 patients having no segment shortening (Table 2). The final amputation level was not found to be significantly associated with 1-year readmission rates, postoperative infections after definitive closure, the need for repeat surgical debridement, or the requirement for revision amputation after index hospitalization (Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/OTAI/A137).

Patients who developed an infection after definitive closure had a shorter median time to initial debridement (154 vs 214 minutes, P = 0.033) and showed a trend toward greater ambulatory aid use at follow-up (53% vs 30%, P = 0.091). However, time to antibiotics, number of patent vessels on CTA, and the presence of gross contamination did not significantly increase the need for debridement after final closure. 4. Discussion

This retrospective study aimed to identify risk factors for revision amputation and postoperative infection after definitive amputation closure in civilian patients presenting with traumatic lower extremity injuries at a Level 1 academic trauma center. Secondary objectives included evaluating functional outcomes, including ambulatory status and use of assistive devices, and assessing whether amputation level influenced these outcomes.

We found that 37% of patients ultimately required an above- or through-knee amputation, despite only 9% initially presenting with one. Use of a tourniquet on arrival, higher ASA scores, and Gustilo–Anderson IIIC injuries were associated with a need for a revision to a more proximal amputation. Importantly, tourniquet use likely serves as a marker of injury severity and arterial bleeding. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence against appropriate tourniquet application, which should remain dependent on hemorrhage control needs and patient physiology. Patients requiring additional surgical debridement after initial closure had higher BMI, increased 1-year readmission rates, and were more likely to develop postoperative infections after final closure. Patients who developed postoperative infections after definitive closure had higher rates of unplanned reoperations, revision amputations, and 1-year readmissions. Interestingly, this group was more likely to have at least one intact vessel visible on initial CTA. Regarding functional outcomes, patients with BKAs were significantly more likely to be ambulatory at final follow-up compared with those with AKAs (72% vs 53%). Ambulatory status was defined as the ability to ambulate without wheelchair dependence, regardless of the use of assistive devices. Patients with AKAs demonstrated significantly greater reliance on ambulatory aids, whereas patients with BKAs were more likely to ambulate without the need for assistive devices.

In our cohort, only 55% of patients with BKA were documented as using a prosthesis at final follow-up. This rate is lower than many published series and may reflect several factors. First, our follow-up is limited to documentation within the electronic medical record, and prosthesis use may have occurred outside our system without documentation. Second, the cohort includes a high proportion of patients with severe injury patterns, significant comorbidities, and higher BMI, which may limit candidacy for prosthetic fitting or tolerance. Finally, some patients may rely on ambulatory aids rather than a prosthesis because of comfort, mobility needs, or access to prosthetic services.

The existing literature on outcomes and final amputation levels after lower extremity amputations largely focuses on diabetic, vasculopathic, and military populations, with relatively few studies reporting on outcomes in civilian cohorts.2,7–12 Among those that do, outcome measures for traumatic limb amputations often vary widely, lack standardization, and frequently omit patient-centered metrics such as mobility and ambulation.13,14 Low et al,12 in a large retrospective analysis using a national trauma database, reported a 14% rate of conversion from transtibial to transfemoral amputation—comparable with our observed rate of 19%. However, their rate of at least one revision amputation at university teaching hospitals (46%) was notably higher than ours (21%). Our rate of postoperative infection rate of 20% aligns closely with the 24% reported in a UK civilian cohort and is substantially lower than the 69% seen in military populations.10 Our findings are consistent with existing literature, which indicates that higher ISS, elevated ASA classifications, tourniquet use upon arrival, and vascular injury are all associated with increased complication rates and more proximal final amputation levels.10,12,15–20 The Stop the Bleed Program, which aims to teach civilians living saving techniques to address trauma, includes use of tourniquets. Civilians, as with first responders, should be encouraged to place the tourniquets as distal as functional, to safely preserve function. The superior ambulatory outcomes seen in our BKA subgroup compared with the AKA subgroup mirror both historical and contemporary biomechanical and functional studies.2,11,21,22

Given the high rates of revision to more proximal amputation observed in both our study and prior research, the clinical question arises: should earlier, more proximal amputation be considered for high-risk patients? Although this approach may reduce the need for further surgery and associated hospital costs, studies comparing limb salvage to primary amputation in the United States have consistently shown that the lifetime cost of amputation—estimated at $500,000—is significantly higher. This is largely driven by the recurring expenses of prosthetic maintenance ($3700/y) and replacement (averaging $10,200 every 2 years).23–25 Given that average AKA prostheses are more costly than BKA prostheses, any short-term cost savings from earlier proximal amputation may ultimately be outweighed by the greater long-term financial burden.

Beyond economics, literature from 1976 to the present have consistently demonstrated that patients with longer residual limbs achieve better mobility and functional outcomes. Patients with AKAs face higher energy demands for ambulation, leading to slower walking speeds and reduced endurance compared with patients with BKA—even with modern prosthetic advancements.2,11,21,22 These findings suggest that rather than advocating for earlier proximal amputation in high-risk individuals, greater emphasis should be placed on strategies to reduce the need for reamputation—such as antibiotic-impregnated implants or enhanced wound management protocols.

Further study is needed to clarify how initial amputation technique and timing affect outcomes after definitive stump closure. Although our cohort included both guillotine and circular amputations, the numbers were not sufficient to analyze these approaches separately. Future studies could explore whether an initial guillotine amputation changes infection risk, need for revision, or final amputation level. In addition, as more trauma centers move toward specialized limb amputation or limb salvage teams, prospective research is needed to determine whether these multidisciplinary pathways improve outcomes by better timing definitive closure, standardizing debridement practices, and supporting functional recovery.

To our knowledge, this is the first and largest study to date focusing exclusively on traumatic lower extremity amputations in a civilian population, examining final amputation level, ambulatory outcomes, and predictors of unplanned reoperation and postoperative infection. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design introduces potential bias, and data regarding injury severity, fracture classification, functional outcomes, and vascular status were limited by the quality of documentation and availability of trauma bay and intraoperative photographs in the electronic health record. Furthermore, this study was conducted at a single tertiary academic medical center, which may limit generalizability. Prior studies have shown higher reamputation rates at university-affiliated hospitals, likely because of referral patterns and the complexity of cases treated at such institutions.12 Finally, long-term follow-up was limited, precluding evaluation of important time-dependent, patient-centered outcomes such as chronic pain, return to work, and sustained prosthetic use, which may differ significantly based on final amputation level.

5. Conclusion

In this civilian series of traumatic lower extremity amputations, more than one-third of patients underwent revision to a more proximal amputation level than was initially planned. Several patient and injury factors, including higher ASA classification, presentation with a tourniquet in place, and Gustilo–Anderson IIIC injuries, were associated with proximal revision. Functional outcomes were better among patients who ultimately underwent below-knee amputation, with higher rates of ambulation and less dependence on assistive devices. These findings highlight the need for careful consideration of initial amputation level and appropriate counseling regarding the risk of subsequent revision, particularly in higher-risk patients. Ongoing work should focus on perioperative decision making, wound management, and infection prevention to improve outcomes after traumatic amputation.
