AI-assisted digital breast tomosynthesis as a decision-support tool for post-neoadjuvant surgical planning in breast cancer
Authors: Adnan Gündoğdu, Nur Betül Ünal Özdemir, Ceyda Sönmez Wetherilt, Merve Aktaş, Damiano Gentile, Bülent Çomçalı, Osman Cem Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurate assessment of residual disease after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is essential for surgical planning and prevention of incomplete excision. This study evaluated artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) as a decision-support tool for post-NAC assessment in breast cancer.MethodsThis retrospective study included 151 patients treated with NAC followed by surgery. Post-NAC DBT images were analyzed using Lunit INSIGHT DBT. An AI score threshold of 70 was used to classify patients as having low (< 70) or high (≥ 70) scores. In a paired subgroup of 70 patients, pre- and post-NAC AI scores were compared. Diagnostic performance and univariable and multivariable analyses were performed.ResultsThe overall pathological complete response (pCR) rate was 41.1% (62/151). Patients with low AI scores had higher pCR rates than those with high scores (49.5% vs. 28.3%, p = 0.010). High AI scores were associated with persistent microcalcifications and residual ductal carcinoma in situ (p < 0.001). Overall accuracy was 58.3% and varied by molecular subtype, with higher performance in triple-negative breast cancer and limited discrimination in HR+/HER2 − tumors. In multivariable analysis, AI score showed borderline significance (p = 0.070). In the paired cohort, AI scores decreased after NAC (p < 0.001), but delta change did not differ between pCR and non-pCR groups (p = 0.877).ConclusionAI-assisted DBT may have potential complementary value as a decision-support tool for post-NAC surgical planning, particularly in triple-negative breast cancer and in cases with residual microcalcifications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04116-0.

Introduction

Currently, breast cancer represents the most common malignancy and persists as a leading cause of cancer mortality in women [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) has become the standard treatment for locally advanced and early-stage breast cancer requiring systemic therapy, increasing breast-conserving surgery rates and guiding axillary management. Pathological complete response (pCR) is achieved in 20–60% of cases (varying by molecular subtype), with significantly improved prognosis and potential for surgical de-escalation strategies in these patients [2, 3]. However, accurate preoperative prediction of pCR is critical for preventing unnecessary radical surgeries and improving patient quality of life.

The accuracy of conventional imaging modalities in assessing NAC response is limited [4, 5]. A study demonstrated magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sensitivity of 77.8% and mammography sensitivity of 44.7% in predicting pCR [6]. In recent years, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms into breast imaging has opened new avenues for predicting NAC response [7]. Deep learning models, particularly when applied to digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT) images, have the potential to predict pCR by analyzing microcalcifications, stromal changes, and tumor heterogeneity patterns [7, 8].

The aim of this study was to evaluate the potential role of AI-assisted digital breast tomosynthesis as a complementary decision-support tool for identifying residual radiologic patterns after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer patients, by comparison with histopathological findings. Rather than serving as a standalone predictor of pathological complete response, this study focuses on the ability of AI-assisted DBT to highlight imaging features relevant to post-NAC surgical planning, including residual disease patterns that may influence the extent of breast and axillary surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a high-volume breast clinic, from which all patients included in the study were consecutively recruited between January 2022 and May 2025. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (Decision No: 202, Date: 04.06.2025) and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion criteria were histopathologically confirmed invasive breast cancer, completed NAC protocol, post-NAC DBT images available with AI scoring (Cohort 1, n = 151), availability of both pre-NAC and post-NAC DBT images with AI scoring for Cohort 2 (n = 70), and age ≥ 18 years. Patients were excluded if they had incomplete NAC treatment, bilateral breast cancer, male breast cancer, prior breast cancer treatment, metastatic disease at presentation, inadequate image quality for AI analysis, or missing pathological response data.

The study evaluated 151 breast cancer patients who received NAC and underwent subsequent surgery. Among these, 70 patients had pre-NAC DBT with integrated AI assessment available for analysis. Two analytical cohorts were defined. Cohort 1 (Post-NAC DBT Group, n = 151) included patients who underwent DBT with AI scoring after completing NAC and prior to surgery. Cohort 2 (Paired Pre- and Post-NAC DBT Group, n = 70) included patients who underwent both baseline (pre-NAC) and post-treatment DBT with AI scoring.

Clinical staging and assessment

Initial clinical staging (cT and cN) was performed using a multimodal assessment, including physical examination, mammography, and breast/axillary ultrasonography, with breast MRI added when clinically indicated.

Axillary staging relied primarily on ultrasound, with tissue sampling of suspicious lymph nodes; MRI findings were taken into account in selected cases requiring additional locoregional assessment.

These evaluations were conducted independently of DBT, as DBT with AI scoring was investigated solely as a predictor of pathological treatment response.

Data collection

All data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records and the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) of the study center. Extracted information included clinical and demographic data (age, menopausal status, family history, and clinical TNM stage at diagnosis), histopathological data (tumor type, histologic grade, immunohistochemical markers including ER, PR, HER2, Ki-67, and molecular subtype classification), imaging data (post-NAC DBT findings with AI scores for Cohort 1 and both baseline and post-NAC DBT findings with AI scores for Cohort 2), and surgical and pathological outcomes (type of surgery performed and final pathological assessment of pCR vs. non-pCR).

DBT imaging and AI assessment

Since 2022, the study center has integrated Lunit INSIGHT DBT (Lunit Inc., South Korea), a CE-marked and FDA-cleared AI system, into routine clinical practice. The system is based on a deep convolutional neural network specifically developed for digital breast tomosynthesis [9]. The algorithm analyzes standard four-view mammographic projections (bilateral craniocaudal and mediolateral oblique) and provides per-breast abnormality scores ranging from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating an increased algorithm-estimated likelihood of malignancy, together with heatmap overlays highlighting suspicious regions. All AI assessments were performed prospectively at the time of image acquisition (Fig. 1). No retrospective AI scoring was conducted on previously obtained DBT images.

Post-NAC DBT was performed after completion of all planned chemotherapy cycles and before definitive surgery. The imaging was obtained 2–4 weeks after the last chemotherapy dose and 1–2 weeks before planned surgery.

To determine the optimal threshold for predicting pCR, diagnostic accuracy metrics were evaluated at cutoff values of 60, 70, and 80. At a cutoff of 60, sensitivity was 52.8% and specificity was 62.9% with an accuracy of 57.0%; at 70, sensitivity was 48.3% and specificity was 72.6% with an accuracy of 58.3%; at 80, sensitivity was 38.2% and specificity was 77.4% with an accuracy of 54.3%. The cutoff of 70 was selected as it provided the highest overall accuracy and optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity. This threshold selection is consistent with emerging evidence that AI thresholds should be calibrated according to specific clinical applications, as different clinical purposes may require different threshold optimization strategies [10]. Since pCR prediction benefits from higher specificity to minimize false-positive assessments of residual disease, a higher threshold was considered appropriate for this application. Patients were therefore categorized as having either a low AI score (< 70) or a high AI score (≥ 70). The AI output was not used for clinical decision-making and was evaluated solely for its association with pathological response and surgical outcomes.

Neoadjuvant treatment protocol

Treatment decisions were made by our institutional multidisciplinary tumor board according to current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Arbeitsgemeinschaft Gynäkologische Onkologie (AGO) guidelines [11, 12]. Standard neoadjuvant regimens were administered based on molecular subtype: anthracycline and taxane-based chemotherapy for HR+/HER2 − tumors, chemotherapy plus dual HER2 blockade (trastuzumab and pertuzumab) for HER2-positive tumors, and anthracycline-taxane with or without platinum compounds for triple-negative tumors.

Pathological response assessment

Pathological complete response was defined as ypT0/is ypN0 (absence of invasive carcinoma in both breast and axillary lymph nodes, with residual ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) allowed) [13].

All surgical specimens were evaluated according to standardized protocols. Immunohistochemical assessment followed American Society of Clinical Oncology / College of American Pathologists (ASCO/CAP) guidelines for ER/PR (≥ 1% positivity) and HER2 status [14, 15].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, when appropriate. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and compared using Student’s t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on distribution. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of pCR. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariable analysis were included in the multivariable model. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the subset of 70 patients with both pre- and post-NAC DBT, paired comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables and McNemar’s test for categorical variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Reporting guidelines

This study was reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies [16].

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 151 patients were included in the post-NAC DBT analysis (Cohort 1), of whom 70 had both pre- and post-NAC DBT available (Cohort 2). The median age was 47 years (IQR 41–56). The most common molecular subtype was HR+/HER2− (45.7%), followed by HR+/HER2+ (22.5%), triple-negative (15.9%), and HR−/HER2+ (15.9%). Overall pCR rate was 41.1% (62/151).

AI score and clinicopathological features

Patients were categorized as low (< 70) or high (≥ 70) AI score. High AI scores were significantly associated with microcalcification on DBT (75% vs. 15.4%, p < 0.001), residual mass (28.3% vs. 13.2%, p = 0.021), and suspicious axillary lymph nodes (16.7% vs.4.4%, p = 0.019) (Table 1). Discussion

Beyond overall pathological response rates, the AI-derived DBT score appeared to capture post-treatment imaging features that are directly relevant to surgical decision-making. Higher AI scores were predominantly associated with persistent microcalcifications, residual masses, and suspicious axillary findings, all of which are known to be associated with the presence of residual disease at the time of surgery [6]. Conversely, lower AI scores were more frequently observed in patients demonstrating radiologic regression and favorable pathological outcomes. These findings suggest that AI-assisted DBT may help identify patients at increased risk of residual disease after NAC, thereby supporting more tailored surgical planning rather than serving as a standalone predictor of pathological complete response. Importantly, surgical procedure selection itself did not differ between AI groups, likely reflecting that operative decisions were based on multidisciplinary conventional assessment rather than AI output. In practical terms, these associations may contribute to surgical planning discussions by identifying patients with imaging features suggestive of persistent residual disease after NAC.

The presented AI-derived DBT score reflected post-treatment morphologic patterns associated with residual disease rather than serving as a direct indicator of pathological complete response. From a surgical standpoint, residual microcalcifications and small residual masses are major contributors to re-excision after breast-conserving surgery, making their preoperative identification potentially relevant for surgical planning [2, 17]. In the present study, higher AI scores were predominantly associated with persistent microcalcifications and the presence of ductal carcinoma in situ, both of which represent well-established markers of residual disease at the time of surgery. These findings are consistent with a recent DCIS study [18] which demonstrated that deep learning–based image analysis can reliably exclude invasive components with a high negative predictive value. Previous studies evaluating NAC response using MRI or conventional mammography have reported variable performance, with limited data available on DBT and AI-based assessment [19–21]. Notably, although microcalcifications often persist after NAC, treatment-related changes in architectural distortion and calcification morphology may contribute to lower post-treatment AI scores, supporting the role of AI-assisted DBT in nuanced post-NAC imaging interpretation. AI-DBT and conventional clinicopathological factors may address different aspects of treatment response assessment and could therefore provide complementary rather than fully overlapping information.

Performance varied substantially by molecular subtype (Fig. 3). In hormone receptor–positive/HER2 − tumors, AI-assisted DBT demonstrated limited discrimination, likely reflecting persistent fibrotic changes, scattered residual disease, and in situ components that remain radiologically detectable despite favorable pathological response [22, 23]. Although the absolute pCR rate in this subgroup was low and the sample size limited, AI scoring still trended in the expected direction (Fig. 2). However, reliable identification of the minority of HR+/HER2 − patients who achieve pCR remains challenging and is unlikely to be achieved by AI alone, likely requiring multimodal approaches integrating AI with molecular markers and on-treatment imaging changes. In contrast, triple-negative breast cancer showed clearer separation between responders and non-responders, consistent with the known chemosensitivity of this subtype and the pronounced morphologic regression observed after neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The loss of statistical significance of the AI score in multivariable analysis (p = 0.070) may be explained by heterogeneous subgroup distribution, limited sample sizes within molecular subtypes, and overlapping biological information captured by molecular subtype and AI-derived morphologic features. Together, these findings suggest that current AI algorithms may be insufficient for luminal tumors and highlight the need for future subtype-specific model development in larger, balanced cohorts [24]. This finding is consistent with our framing throughout the manuscript, in which AI-DBT is positioned as a complementary decision-support indicator rather than an independent predictor of pCR.

In the paired cohort analysis (n = 70), AI scores decreased significantly after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in both pCR and non-pCR groups (p < 0.001); however, the magnitude of change did not differ between groups (Δ − 20.7 vs. − 20.1, p = 0.877). This finding suggests that the absolute post-treatment AI score may be more informative for post-NAC imaging assessment than delta change alone. The multivariable model achieved an AUC of 0.851, comparable to recent imaging-based response assessment studies, including DBT-based and contrast-enhanced mammography approaches [7, 25]. Notably, higher performance reported in selected subtypes in prior studies further underscores the importance of molecular stratification when interpreting imaging-based AI tools [8]. From a practical standpoint, AI-assisted DBT may offer clinical value in specific scenarios. Although MRI and contrast-enhanced spectral mammography (CESM) are considered reference standards for post-NAC response assessment, they are not universally performed in routine practice; in the present cohort, all patients underwent post-NAC DBT as part of standard workflow, while MRI was available only in a subset based on clinical indication. AI-assisted DBT may therefore offer added value in this large patient population, including in settings where MRI is unavailable or contraindicated, in supporting decision-making in triple-negative breast cancer, and in facilitating preoperative identification of residual microcalcifications to guide surgical margin planning.

According to the IDEAL (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, Long-term study) framework for evaluating surgical innovation, the present study represents an early exploratory phase (Stage 2a–2b), focusing on feasibility and potential clinical utility rather than definitive effectiveness. The findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant further prospective validation before broader clinical adoption.

This study has several limitations. The modest overall accuracy (58.3%) and low sensitivity (48.3%) limit broad clinical applicability. The retrospective, single-center design with 151 patients restricts generalizability. The molecular subtype subgroups were imbalanced (TNBC n = 24; HR+/HER2 − n = 69), and subgroup-specific findings — particularly the apparent discrimination observed in TNBC — should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive, given the limited statistical power within smaller subgroups. The absence of external validation further limits interpretation. Direct head-to-head comparison with MRI or contrast-enhanced spectral mammography — considered reference standards for post-NAC response assessment in the literature — was not feasible by study design, as DBT was routinely performed in all study patients while MRI was available only in a subset based on clinical indication. Despite these limitations, this exploratory study provides preliminary evidence that AI-assisted DBT may have potential complementary value in specific scenarios, particularly in TNBC and for identifying residual microcalcifications. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger, balanced cohorts and external validation are needed before routine clinical implementation.

Conclusion

AI-assisted digital breast tomosynthesis demonstrated potential complementary clinical utility as a decision-support tool for post-neoadjuvant surgical planning, with performance varying substantially across molecular subtypes. TNBC appeared to show the strongest discrimination in our cohort, while luminal tumors showed limited benefit. Beyond pCR assessment, the ability of AI-assisted DBT to identify residual microcalcifications and in situ components may be potentially relevant for guiding surgical strategy. Future multicenter prospective studies with subtype-specific algorithms are warranted to further define its role in clinical practice.
