Sub-femtosecond figure-of-merit millimeter-wave switches via solution-processed MoS2 for 6G radio-frequency front-ends
Authors: Changwoo Pyo, Sungmoon Park, Seungchan Lee, Dohyun Lee, Juho Son, Joohoon Kang, Deji Akinwande, Myungsoo Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Next-generation 6G communication requires radio-frequency components capable of operating above 100 GHz with low loss, high isolation, and zero static power—requirements that challenge complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) and microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) technologies. Here, we report transfer-free, large-area millimeter-wave (mmWave) switches based on solution-processed MoS2. While solution-processed 2D materials are often viewed as inferior to their crystalline counterparts due to high defect densities, we demonstrate that their edge-rich morphology is, in fact, a performance enabler. These edge defects act as intrinsic templates that confine Cu-filament pathways, enabling rapid (76 ns) and low-energy switching (1.57 nJ) with > 2000 cycles and uniform zero static-power operation, as corroborated by Kelvin probe force microscopy, conductive atomic force microscopy and low-temperature studies. The resulting switches achieve an low insertion loss ( < 0.1 dB) and high isolation ( > 35 dB) at 67 GHz. Notably, they exhibit a switching figure-of-merit (RON·COFF) of ~ 0.8 fs (fco ~ 187 THz), surpassing previously reported 2D switches. Importantly, by adopting an inverse-state operational scheme in a SHUNT architecture, we mitigate self-switching and achieve improved power handling (P0.1dB > 10 dBm) and linearity (IIP3 > 42.1 dBm). Finally, we demonstrate the platform’s circuit-level viability by integrating the switches into a true-time-delay and hybrid-coupled phase shifters targeting 30 GHz mmWave applications.

Introduction

The advancement of wireless communication toward 6G standards necessitates high data rates (up to 100 Gbits−1) and operation in the mmWave spectrum above 100 GHz1–3, which demands high-performance, low-power radio-frequency (RF) components4. Analog switches serve a function in signal routing and processing to reconfigure communication systems, yet conventional technologies fall short5–8. Field-effect transistors (FETs) are hindered by intrinsic parasitic capacitance that degrades high-frequency performance, while microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) suffer from high actuation voltages and large footprint issues9–11. A notable drawback for both is the requirement for static power.

To address these limitations, emerging memory-based switches are gaining prominence for mmWave communication. Considering the conventional communication architecture used in cellular networks, their nonvolatile nature enables low-power operation by consuming only a few nanojoules during each Tx (UL)/Rx (DL) transition, in contrast to conventional switches that continuouslyconsume milliwatts of power. However, leading memristive candidates face significant trade-offs. Phase-change memory (PCM), despite achieving complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) integration, is hampered by the thermal crosstalk of its heaters and a performance ceiling of RON·COFF ~ 10 fs (fco = 1/2πRONCOFF ~ 15 THz)12–17. Oxide-based (e.g., HfOx, TiOx, VOx) and polymer-based (e.g., Nafion, pV3D3) memristors are likewise limited at high frequencies, where large parasitic capacitance arising from high dielectric constants compromises isolation, and high ON-state resistance penalizes insertion loss5,18–23. In contrast, switches based on two-dimensional (2D) materials like hBN and MoS2 have demonstrated figures of merit (FoM), achieving RON·COFF ~ 2 fs (fco ~ 120 THz) in small unit devices3,24–27. Although many of these studies have employed mechanically exfoliated or CVD-grown films, solution processing offers a distinct materials route for memristive RF switches. Beyond its compatibility with low-cost and wafer-scale deposition, the flake-assembled microstructure of solution-processed films naturally provides abundant edge-rich defect sites, which can promote ion migration and conductive filament formation28. Nevertheless, challenges remain for this emerging platform, including an incomplete understanding of configuration-dependent performance, scaling implications, and reliability bottlenecks such as limited endurance and uniformity, as well as practical integration hurdles for RF front-ends.

A key bottleneck for 2D switches remains their limited cycling endurance, typically capped at a few hundred cycles29. This work overcomes these challenges by developing a low-power and sub-femtosecond mmWave switch platform based on a transfer-free, large-area Au/solution-processed MoS2/Cu device. We demonstrate that the vacancy-rich edge sites inherent to solution-processed MoS2, where sulfur vacancies (VS) guide Cu ions, serve as preferential pathways for Cu ion migration, effectively engineering the filament formation energy30. We identified this mechanism as electrochemical metallization (ECM) via comprehensive analysis using conductive atomic force microscopy (cAFM), Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), and low-temperature I–V analysis. This mechanism facilitates rapid and low-power operation, confirmed via real-time pulse measurements to be approximately 76 ns and 1.57 nJ per switching event with 2000 cycles of endurance, while improving switching uniformity by 77% compared to the exfoliated sample26,29,31. We demonstrate the platform’s versatility across series, shunt, and series-shunt (SS) configurations. We further demonstrate that implementing an inverse-state operational scheme in a shunt topology for memristive switches resolves the self-switching reliability bottleneck3,24,25. By mapping the memristor’s low resistance state to the mmWave switch’s OFF-state, we render the device immune to accidental triggering by high-power RF signals, a known reliability issue in conventional memristive switches. The shunt configuration exhibits a low insertion loss (<0.1 dB), high isolation (>35 dB) at 67 GHz, a P0.1dB > 10 dBm, high linearity (IIP3 > 42.1 dBm), and an extrapolated RON·COFF ~ 0.8 fs (fco ~ 187 THz). This sub-femtosecond performance represents an advancement over previously reported 2D material-based RF switches25. Furthermore, the series-shunt architecture surpasses the scaling limits of the series configuration, enabling higher isolation. The practical viability of this platform is demonstrated by integrating these switches into a functional single-pole double-throw (SPDT), 2-bit true time delay (TTD) phase shifter operating over a 16 GHz bandwidth with low error (RMS insertion loss error ≤ 0.3 dB, RMS group delay error ≤ 4 ps), and a 2-bit mmWave hybrid-coupled phase shifter (HCPS) targeting 30 GHz, confirming their suitability for essential components in next-generation communication systems.

Results

Furthermore, AFM topology (Supplementary Fig. 3) verifies the formation of a uniform film with a thickness of ~30 nm. The device’s active area is defined by the overlapping region of the top electrode (TE) and bottom electrode (BE) (Supplementary Fig. 4). Figure 1d displays I–V curves, clearly demonstrating the device’s non-volatile resistive switching (NVRS) characteristics. Both the RESET (low-resistance state (LRS) to high-resistance state (HRS)) and SET processes (HRS to LRS) occur within a low operating voltage window of −1 to 2 V. The device exhibits a stable retention time exceeding 106 s at room temperature (Fig. 1e). Importantly, both the LRS and HRS also remained stable at 85 °C over 12 h without noticeable degradation, confirming the thermal robustness of the programmed states under representative communication-chip operating conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6). The device also shows an endurance of 2000 cycles (Fig. 1f), a improvement over previously reported MoS2-based RF switches24,29. To minimize stress, endurance measurements were conducted without back-tracing, an improvement over the standard double-sweep method (Supplementary Fig. 7)26. The stability of the resistance states is further evidenced by the cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) for the LRS and HRS (Fig. 1g and h). The low coefficients of variation (CV = σ/μ, where σ is the standard deviation and μ is the average) for both retention (0.88%) and endurance (28.6%) highlight the good uniformity of the LRS. This uniformity is particularly encouraging, as the LRS in memristive RF switches directly dictates the insertion loss (IL)26. Also, RLRS could be enhanced via compliance current (CC) without specialized techniques such as pulsed-voltage stress (Supplementary Fig. 8)26,33.

With zero static power consumption owing to NVRS, the energy per switching event is the key performance metric. To be viable for modern communication systems, this non-volatility must be paired with high speed and low energy consumption (Supplementary Note 2). To quantify these real-time switching characteristics, a 1 GHz, 0 dBm continuous wave (CW) signal was applied to the device simultaneously with a voltage pulse (Supplementary Fig. 9). The device achieved rapid switching, with a SET time of 64 ns (Fig. 1i) and a RESET time of 76 ns (Fig. 1j), using ~3 V pulses, limited by the bandwidth of the measurement instrument. The measured switching time is sufficient for 6G-relevant RF applications, which generally require sub-µs-level switching34,35. Prior reports of faster nanosecond- and sub-nanosecond-scale switching in 2D-material devices further suggest room for improvement beyond the present instrument-limited values3,36. By analyzing the simultaneous I–V data (Supplementary Fig. 10), the switching energy was calculated to be 2.38 nJ for the SET (Fig. 1k) and 1.57 nJ for the RESET (Fig. 1l).

The switch’s isolation (IS) performance is strongly correlated with OFF capacitance (COFF), which is dominated by the metal–insulator–metal (MIM) structure. The device’s overlap area is therefore a key design parameter, making it crucial to verify that the NVRS characteristics are preserved at smaller device dimensions24,37. To investigate this scalability, point switching was performed using cAFM with a tip radius of 32.20 nm (Supplementary Fig. 11). This demonstrates that the switching phenomenon can be confined to an area as small as ~0.03 μm2. The higher SET and RESET voltages observed during cAFM measurements (Supplementary Fig. 12) are attributed to an increased voltage drop from the higher contact resistance of the nanoscale contact area. The cAFM line confirms this localized NVRS characteristic (Supplementary Fig. 13). Additional cycling data and individual point switching graphs are presented in Supplementary Figs. 14–16.

Spatially resolved evidence for filament formation was obtained by conductive AFM (cAFM) following removal of the TE, where discrete, highly conductive spots appear within the active region. Successful TE removal with minimal damage to the switching layer was confirmed by AFM topography, which showed negligible difference between the as-deposited and TE-removed surfaces (Supplementary Fig. 17). Increasing the CC from 5 to 40 mA systematically enhances the conductivity of these spots, consistent with progressive filament reinforcement (Supplementary Fig. 18). Such CC-dependent evolution can be rationalized by positive feedback in ECM-type growth: once a conductive nucleus forms, the local resistance drops, current crowds into that region, and Cu incorporation is further accelerated. The proportional scaling between the peak current and the applied CC (Supplementary Fig. 19) indicates that filament development is reproducible and controllable, producing an effective ON-state resistance that decreases approximately inversely with CC.

The filament geometries were further resolved using high-resolution cAFM (Fig. 2c) together with pixel-by-pixel Ohmic fitting maps (R2) (Fig. 2d) over a 140 × 140 nm2 area. The R2 value approaches ~1 at the filament center, indicative of nearly ideal metallic conduction, but gradually decreases toward the periphery, implying a transition to a mixed transport regime where Schottky-like components become increasingly influential. This spatial trend is consistent with a conical filament profile and agrees with the elevated Schottky-fitting R2 observed near the filament boundary (Supplementary Fig. 20). Complementary confirmation of both composition and three-dimensional structure was obtained by simultaneous cAFM/KPFM at 10 mA CC (Fig. 2e), where a pronounced work–function contrast emerges: the periphery remains MoS2-like (~4.36 eV), whereas the conductive core shifts toward a Cu-like value (~4.53 eV)40. This localized work-function gradient provides strong evidence that the conductive pathway is Cu-rich and adopts a conical growth morphology. Importantly, the simultaneously collected topography shows no discernible surface protrusion, indicating that the filament forms within the MoS2 layer rather than as a raised feature on the surface. (Supplementary Fig. 21).

To understand the physical origin of the suppressed α at LRS, we analyzed pristine, isolated MoS2 flakes by KPFM (Fig. 2h). The edge regions exhibit higher surface potential (lower work function) compared to the basal plane, consistent with a higher density of VS that act as electron donors41. Based on this observation, we suggest that vacancy-rich flake edges provide energetically favorable channels for Cu ion migration42, thereby promoting more localized and efficient filament nucleation/growth and ultimately reducing the required switching voltages. This mechanism-level advantage is directly reflected in the switching statistics when comparing exfoliated and solution-processed MoS2 devices with the same Cu/MoS2/Au stack (Supplementary Fig. 22). As Fig. 2g and h shows the solution-processed devices switch at lower average voltage, with RESET decreasing from 1.04 V (exfoliated) to 0.71 V (solution-processed) and SET decreasing from 1.79 to 1.36 V. They also exhibit improved LRS resistance uniformity with the distribution narrowing from 128% to 28.6% when moving from exfoliated to solution-processed devices. This trend remained consistent even in comparison with CVD-grown 2D switches (Supplementary Fig. 23). Moreover, unlike the exfoliated counterpart, the solution-processed switch shows a clearly defined switching window with robust LRS/HRS separation, enabling more deterministic state assignment. Consistent with this enhanced stability, endurance improves from ~100 cycles (exfoliated) to ~2000 cycles (solution-processed). We emphasize that this reduction in LRS/HRS dispersion is not merely a device-level metric but a key enabler of RF reliability: tighter RON and COFF distributions suppress C2C drift in IL and IS, thereby stabilizing mmWave front-end performance over cycling.

mmWave performance

To characterize the mmWave performance, we evaluated the scattering parameters (S-parameters) from 10 MHz to 67 GHz using a vector network analyzer (VNA) with standard short-open-load-thru (SOLT) calibration and de-embedding procedure (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). We fabricated series, shunt, and SS switch architectures on characteristic impedance-matched coplanar waveguide (CPW)(Supplementary Fig. 26). As shown in Fig. 3a, all configurations demonstrate ON-state IL < 0.5 dB up to 67 GHz, surpassing leading technologies including PCM (−2 dB at 60 GHz)15, RF silicon-on-insulator (RF-SOI) metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistors (−1.3 dB at 50 GHz)43 and a commercial switch (−3.6 dB at 67 GHz)44. The shunt achieved a low IL < 0.1 dB at 67 GHz, exceeding even hBN-based switches (−0.4 dB at 67 GHz)26. Regarding IS, all devices achieved >20 dB up to 67 GHz, comparable to the benchmarks (Fig. 3b), suggesting that even a standalone series switch can approach the isolation levels typically targeted by CMOS SS cells, thereby enabling a more compact implementation with reduced footprint13. The shunt, leveraging a low RLRS via CC control, demonstrated even higher IS > 35 dB at 67 GHz. The lumped circuit model reveals a low COFF of 0.72 fF, making the signal leakage to ground virtually negligible and thereby alleviating a key practical drawback of the shunt configuration. This yields an FoM (RON·COFF) of ~0.8 fs. This sub-femtosecond metric corresponds to an fco of 187 THz, indicating that the intrinsic material response will not be the bottleneck even as carrier frequencies push deep into the terahertz regime required for 6G communications37. This high fco behavior was consistently reproduced in repeated measurements of the same shunt device (Supplementary Fig. 28). The extracted εr of 2.15 also aligns well with ellipsometry results. Furthermore, the SS achieved >35 dB of IS at 67 GHz, making it suitable for demanding applications such as phased-array antennas, low-power channel selectors, and reconfigurable circuits45. While the IL is inherently higher (0.3 dB at 67 GHz) due to its low-pass topology, this SS still vastly outperforms conventional CMOS equivalents. The measurement data before and after the de-embedding process are presented in Supplementary Fig. 29. The matching performance, characterized by the return loss (S11), showed favorable results up to 67 GHz (Supplementary Fig. 30).

Device-to-device (D2D) variability was assessed for the series and shunt configurations (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Fig. 31), showing uniformity comparable to commercial PCM technology15,46,47. The devices demonstrated highly uniform fco with low CV of 5.2% and 4.8% for series and shunt, respectively26. A notable feature is that CC-tunable RLRS allows the device to function as a variable attenuator in the series (Fig. 3e and f) and as a varactor-like element in the shunt (Fig. 3g and h).

Power handling capabilities were investigated for each configuration. The shunt, which routes the ON-state signal along the primary metal line, exhibited a 0.1 dB gain compression point (P0.1dB) > 10 dBm (the instrument limit) (Fig. 3c). This demonstrates linearity compared to the series (10 dBm) and series-shunt (6 dBm). In the OFF-state, the shunt avoided self-switching up to 10 dBm (Fig. 3d), unlike the series, which self-switched at ~8 dBm3,5,24,25. This robustness is attributed to the asymmetry of the switching voltages combined with an inverse-state mapping between the memristor (LRS/HRS) and the shunt switch (OFF-/ON-state), which mitigates self-switching under high RF power. As a separate stability test, the programmed state showed negligible change in the measured S-parameters under continuous-wave RF stress at 10 GHz and 0 dBm for ~6 h, confirming robust RF-state retention during prolonged signal loading (Supplementary Fig. 32). Nevertheless, because mmWave AC signals inherently include periodic negative half-cycles, the possible influence of long-term reverse-bias stress on device lifetime remains an important subject for future investigation. Linearity was further assessed via two-tone intermodulation distortion (IMD) measurements (f1 = 2.365 GHz and f2 = 2.415 GHz). The shunt achieved an input third-order intercept point (IIP3) of 42.1 dBm, corresponding to an ~22.4% improvement over the series (Fig. 3i). At an input power of 0.325 dBm, the shunt suppressed 3rd IMD (IM3) products to −84.6 dBc, better than the −75.7 dBc of the series (Supplementary Fig. 33). This linearity is a direct result of the shunt design, where the signal bypasses the nonlinear MoS2 channel. Scaling characteristics of the OFF-state were also explored via COMSOL, projecting performance for devices scaled down to 0.03 μm2 (the minimum switchable active area identified by cAFM) (Fig. 3k). Simulations predict that performance converges due to CPW geometry limitations, a trend matching experimental data. While this issue has historically limited the series scaling37, our work shows the SS architecture extends this scaling limit by an order of magnitude, enabling further isolation improvements.

Mechanical reliability for flexible applications was confirmed via cyclic and static bending tests. Under 103 bending cycles (30 mm radius), all configurations maintained stable ON- and OFF-state performance, with low endurance CV of 1.14% and 0.9%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 35). Notably, observed failures during prolonged testing are attributed to the mechanical limits of the CPW metal lines, not degradation of the MoS2 switching element itself, confirming the intrinsic robustness of the active material (Supplementary Note 4, Supplementary Fig. 36). This interpretation is further supported by AFM topography of solution-processed MoS2 films on the same PI substrate before and after identical cyclic bending, which showed no noticeable cracking and only minor changes in surface roughness (Supplementary Fig. 37). Subsequently, the shunt device exhibited stable characteristics when subjected to a progressively decreasing static bending radius from 40 down to 5 mm (Fig. 3j).

Memristive circuit for RF application

To validate that these atomistic advantages translate to system-level performance, we integrated the switches into reconfigurable RF circuits. Leveraging the nonvolatile, zero-static-power operation of the switch, these circuits reduce standby power relative to actively biased diode- or transistor-based reconfigurable RF circuits. Separately, the low RON·COFF and low insertion loss of the present platform compare favorably with PCM-based implementations in dense arrays. First, we implemented a single-pole double-throw (SPDT) circuit, a fundamental topology for reconfigurable systems using both series and SS configurations (Supplementary Fig. 38)48. This circuit is commonly used to route signals between a Tx path (power amplifier) and an Rx path (low-noise amplifier) for a single antenna. Both configurations effectively routed and isolated the Tx and Rx path signals, which were consistent with electromagnetic (EM) simulations (Supplementary Fig. 43). However, dynamic metrics relevant to practical Tx/Rx operation, including path-switching settling time and channel-to-channel transient crosstalk, were not evaluated in the present setup and will be systematically investigated in future work. Notably, the series-only switches already provided sufficient IS, replacing the conventional SS topology—typically adopted to maximize IS—with the series-only configuration improves IL, increases integration density, and reduces switching energy (Supplementary Note 5, Supplementary Fig. 39).

Next, we integrated the switches into phase shifter circuits: a 2-bit true-time-delay (TTD) phase shifter and a 2-bit hybrid-coupled phase shifter (HCPS). The TTD architecture is important for applications such as beamforming antennas and reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RIS), as it prevents beam squint across wide bandwidths by ensuring all frequency components propagate coherently13,45,49. The zero static power consumption of our switch is a key enabler for such large-scale, energy-efficient time-space-coded RIS. Simultaneously, the HCPS integration demonstrates the switch’s value for the low-power edge devices, particularly Internet of Things (IoT) devices, where its efficiency is crucial for extending battery life.

Benchmarking of mmWave switches

A comprehensive benchmarking of our device against other switching technologies is presented in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 1. The radar chart (Fig. 5a) highlights the key strengths of the present platform in a class-specific manner: zero static power compared with conventional active switch technologies, low switching energy and low actuation voltage compared with emerging memory-based RF switches, and high fco as an advantage that remains favorable across both conventional and nonvolatile RF switch platforms. A normalized comparison against leading conventional foundry technologies such as RF SOI, RF Silicon-on-Sapphire (SOS), high electron mobility transistor (HEMT), and GaN in Fig. 5b reveals a performance advantage for our device in all configurations9,43,50–64. Notably, the shunt achieves an RON·COFF FoM of ~0.8 fs, placing it firmly in the sub-femtosecond regime and confirming its potential for mmWave applications. This performance gap is fundamental: conventional switches are limited by substrate parasitic capacitance from their front-end-of-line (FEOL) fabrication. Furthermore, integrating these FEOL devices with back-end-of-line (BEOL) components like antennas and inductors introduces interconnect losses15. In contrast, our solution-processed switch is fully compatible with BEOL integration, enabling it to maintain intrinsic high performance and achieve more favorable, low-loss integration with the RF front-end without expensive SOI technologies. Discussion

In summary, we have developed a sub-femtosecond mmWave switch platform based on solution-processed MoS2. We demonstrated that a shunt delivers high performance, including a low insertion loss (<0.1 dB), high isolation (>35 dB), and an extrapolated RON·COFF time of ~0.8 fs (fco ~ 187 THz)—an advancement over prior 2D material-based switches. Comprehensive mechanistic analyses confirmed that the edge-rich morphology of the solution-processed film acts as a natural defect-mediated template, facilitating efficient filament formation via VS, achieving an endurance of 2000 cycles and low, nanojoule-scale switching energy with enhanced uniformity of resistance. We further demonstrated the platform’s versatility by realizing variable attenuators and varactors and introduced an operational scheme to ensure high linearity (IIP3 = 42.1 dBm) and power handling (P0.1dB > 10 dBm) by eliminating self-switching. The successful integration of these switches into complex SPDT, 2-bit TTD, and hybrid-coupled phase-shifter circuits confirms their viability for practical circuit integration. This work provides a scalable, high-performance solution for advancing future mmWave communication.

Methods

Device and RF circuits fabrication

The fabrication process began with the definition of a 50 Ω characteristic impedance-matched CPW via photolithography on a 430 μm sapphire wafer. An electron-beam evaporator was used to deposit the BE and ground pads, consisting of a Cr/Au (5/55 nm) stack. For the active layer, a ~30 nm-thick MoS2 film was created by spin-coating a 0.5 mg/mL solution (synthesized via THAB-assisted electrochemical intercalation and mixed in isopropyl alcohol, see Supplementary Fig. 1) at 2500 rpm for 40 s, followed by a 5 min bake at 70 °C. This MoS2 layer was then patterned into the active region using photolithography and reactive-ion etching (RIE). The device was completed with the deposition of the TE stack of Cr/Cu (5/85 nm). An identical procedure was used for the RF circuits, with the addition of an in-house Cu electroplating process for the phase shifter pads with 2 μm thick (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Material characterization

Confocal Raman spectroscopy was performed using a WITec Alpha 300R system equipped with a 532 nm laser source (2 mW power) and an 1800 grooves/mm grating. Confocal Raman was calibrated using a Si/SiO2 sample. Samples were prepared for analysis by coating the material onto a sapphire substrate, following the same procedure used for device fabrication. At eight arbitrary points on the sample surface, spectra were acquired by accumulating 10 integrations with an exposure time of 1 s per integration. cAFM and KPFM analyses were performed using an NX7 (Park Systems). For cAFM measurements, an NSC18/Cr–Au tip was used. The TE removal was performed through diluted Cu etchant with 0.5 Å/s of etch rate. To ensure a reliable conductive pathway, the bottom electrode of the device was electrically connected to the sample disk with silver paste, and the bias voltage was applied directly to the sample disk. For KPFM measurements, MoS2 flakes were spin-coated onto a Si wafer, which was then electrically connected to a grounded sample disk with silver paste. An NSC36/Cr–Au C tip was utilized for KPFM measurements. Prior to scanning, the system was calibrated to 4.6 eV using a highly oriented pyrolytic graphite (HOPG) substrate. During the scan, topology and surface potential data were acquired simultaneously. The topology was measured via the first lock-in amplifier (modulation frequency 54 kHz), while the KPFM data was obtained from the second lock-in amplifier (drive voltage 1 V, AC tip bias 17 kHz).

DC and low-temperature characterization

A Keysight B1500A semiconductor parameter analyzer, supplemented with a B1530A WGFMU pulsing module, was the primary instrument for DC electrical characterization at room temperature in a dark shield environment. Low-temperature analysis was conducted with a Keithley 4200-SCS system with Lakeshore cryogenic probe station CRX-4K, where the temperature was swept from 100 to 300 K under high vacuum (10−6 torr). The top electrode was consistently biased for all electrical tests.

Hot switching measurements

Hot switching measurements were conducted on a probe station equipped with T-plus TP26 GSG RF probes. A Keysight P5024A vector network analyzer, operating in CW time mode with 1 GHz and 0 dBm, served as the continuous wave RF source, while a Keysight B1530A WGFMU supplied the input pulse to the device. A Tektronix PSPL5585 bias tee was employed to distinguish the pulse signal and the RF signal. To capture the transient switching event, a Keysight MSOX6004A oscilloscope was connected to monitor both the RF signal transmitted through the device and a reference signal from the WGFMU’s remote sensing unit (RSU) monitor line, using a trigger. Any residual pulse artifacts in the captured RF signal were subsequently removed through post-processing with custom-developed software. For a detailed diagram of the measurement setup, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 8.

RF mmWave measurements

On-wafer measurements are performed using the Cascade Infinity GSG probes and Keysight N5247A vector network analyzer from 10 MHz to 67 GHz. For small-signal measurements, the RF power was set to a nominal level of −20 dBm. Before actual device measurement, the SOLT calibration was carried out from 10 MHz to 67 GHz using a Cascade 138-356 Impedance Standard Substrate and Cascade WinCal software. Scattering parameters were measured for each device in both the ON- and OFF-state, and parameter extraction was carried out to determine the ON- and OFF-state equivalent lumped circuit component models. Power handling was also evaluated in both the ON- and OFF-state by measuring scattering parameters as the devices were gradually exposed to RF power levels from −20 to +10 dBm. The bending measurement was conducted using a 3D-printed curved structure. For a detailed diagram of the measurement setup, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 21.

IMD measurement

A two-tone test was conducted to assess the third-order intermodulation distortion. A Keysight P5024A VNA was used to both generate two input signals (f1 = 2.365 GHz, f2 = 2.415 GHz) and to measure the power levels of the output signals. These tones were combined by an RFCM APDS2-0580-01S power divider and delivered to the device through T-plus GSG probes on a probe station. The input power was swept across a range of –30 to +10 dBm. Accurate power levels were ensured by calibrating the VNA with a Keysight U8487A power sensor. The output signals at the fundamental and the 2.465 GHz (2f2–f1) intermodulation frequency were captured using the Keysight Network Analyzer software. For a detailed diagram of the measurement setup, please refer to Supplementary Fig. 21.

Full EM-wave and high-frequency circuit simulations

Electromagnetic simulations were conducted using Keysight ADS 2020, and COMOSL software.The model incorporated a 430 µm-thick sapphire substrate with properties given in ADS (dielectric permittivity εr = 9.3, loss tangent tan δ = 0.00002). The conductors were modeled as copper (Cu) and gold (Au) with a conductivity of σCu = 59.6 × 106 and σAu = 41.1 × 106 S m⁻¹, respectively. The thickness was equivalent to that of the electron-beam evaporated films to ensure consistency with the fabricated devices.
