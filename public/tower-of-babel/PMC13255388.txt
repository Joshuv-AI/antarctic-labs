Associational pathway analyses of depression and impulsivity in the relationship between internet addiction and non-suicidal self-injury among adolescents: a cross-sectional case–control study
Authors: Alaa A Abdalla, Magda Fahmy, Khaled Anwar, Ahmed Elarabi Hendi, Kathryn R Cullen, Omneya Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents undergo important developmental changes, characterized by profound biological and psychosocial changes. This period is also associated with increased vulnerability to mental health challenges and engagement in risky/problematic behaviors. Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) and internet addiction (IA) are increasingly recognized as prevalent and interconnected behavioral concerns among adolescents, particularly those with depressive disorders. Impulsivity has also been identified as a key psychological factor in both IA and NSSI. This study aimed to investigate the cross-sectional interrelationships among depression, impulsivity, IA, and NSSI in adolescents.MethodsA total of 110 adolescents (55 diagnosed with major depressive disorder [MDD] and 55 healthy controls) were recruited from the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic at Suez Canal University Hospital. The severity of depression was assessed using the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI). All participants completed the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), Deliberate Self-Harm Inventory (DSHI), and Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11). Statistical analyses included group comparisons, correlation analyses, logistic regression. Cross-sectional pathway analyses using structural equation modeling (SEM) was conducted to examine indirect associations between IA and NSSI through depression and impulsivity.ResultsAdolescents with MDD reported significantly higher levels of NSSI, IA, and impulsivity compared to controls. Significantly positive correlations between IAT, BIS-11, and CDI scores and lifetime NSSI frequency were demonstrated among the depression group. IA and NSSI were significantly associated by indirect pathways through both depression and impulsivity. In serial associational models, the cross-sectional indirect pathway from impulsivity to depression (B = 0.370) was stronger than the reverse direction (B = 0.310).ConclusionOur findings highlighted the interrelated roles of IA, impulsivity, and NSSI among adolescents with MDD. Impulsivity may be a critical factor linking IA to emotional dysregulation and self-injurious behavior. These results highlight the need for integrated screening and prevention strategies targeting impulsivity and these risk behaviors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00646-5.Keywords: Non-suicidal self-injury, Internet addiction, Adolescents, Impulsivity, Depression, Associational pathway analyses

Background

Adolescence is a unique and sensitive phase of life with a leap in physical, cognitive, and psychosocial development [1, 2]. Despite being an extraordinary window of opportunities [3], it is also a period with vulnerabilities with a greater propensity to imbalance. This imbalance manifests as increased emotional reactivity and impulsivity in susceptible individuals, where the balance and proper modulation of their emotions and behaviors while interacting within their social and intellectual environment get interrupted due to various brain developmental processes [1, 3]. This is when some concerning behaviors begin to develop and manifest, and vulnerable adolescents become at greater risk for poorer outcomes [2].

The current work focuses on two problematic behaviors that commonly emerge during the adolescent period. First, non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) is defined as the damage of one’s own body tissues without the intention of dying by suicide. The lifetime prevalence rates range from 17% to 60% [4–7], with higher prevalence, severity, and poorer future outcomes tending to occur in clinical settings [8–11], depression being one of the most encountered [12–15]. Second, internet addiction (IA), which is defined by excessive or poorly regulated preoccupations, urges, or behaviors related to computer and internet use [16], is now highly prevalent among adolescents [17–20] and is linked to poor mental health in adolescents, especially depression [21–24] and NSSI [25–27]. Therefore, recent research attention has been directed to the study of both NSSI and IA in adolescents within the context of depression and other associated factors [28–31].

The association between IA and NSSI in adolescent populations has received growing empirical support in recent years. Prospective longitudinal research demonstrates that excessive screen time and problematic internet use are associated with subsequent engagement in self-harm behaviors [32, 33]. Several mechanisms may account for this association. Online exposure to self-harm communities can normalize NSSI and provide explicit methods [34], while digital stressors such as cyberbullying and social comparison intensify emotional distress that may trigger self-harm as a coping mechanism [35]. Additionally, excessive internet use displaces protective offline activities including face-to-face social interaction, physical exercise, and adequate sleep [36, 37], factors known to buffer against NSSI risk.

One common construct implicated in both NSSI and IA in adolescence is impulsivity.

Some research has demonstrated that NSSI is predicted by impulsivity [38], and in the context of negative affect, NSSI frequency is greater in individuals with greater impulsivity [33, 34]. Prior systemic reviews have concluded that while initiation of NSSI is mood-based, maintaining self-harm is related to cognitive aspects of impulsivity [39, 40]. Impulsive individuals are also more likely to engage in excessive online behaviors as a means of immediate gratification, emotional escape, or reward-seeking without adequately considering the negative consequences [41]. Neurocognitive studies consistently show that adolescents with elevated impulsivity scores exhibit impaired inhibitory control, heightened sensitivity to reward, and dysregulation in prefrontal regions involved in self-regulation—factors that increase vulnerability to IA [42].

Importantly, longitudinal evidence supports impulsivity as a prospective predictor of depressive symptoms in adolescents, among impulsivity facets, negative urgency—the tendency to act rashly when experiencing negative emotions—has emerged as the strongest predictor of internalizing psychopathology [43]. Recent prospective research demonstrates that negative urgency is associated with increases increases in emotion dysregulation over time in adolescents [44], with this impulsivity-related difficulty in modulating emotional responses serving as a key mediating pathway to depression [45].

Collectively, this evidence establishes impulsivity, particularly negative urgency, as an upstream vulnerability factor that creates conditions conducive to the emergence and maintenance of depressive symptoms.

The Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution (I-PACE) model offers a comprehensive framework for understanding these processes by integrating psychological and neurobiological mechanisms underlying internet addiction [46]. According to this model, individuals with IA often demonstrate deficits in cognitive control, elevated impulsivity, and maladaptive reward processing, all of which increase susceptibility to excessive internet engagement [47]. The I-PACE model suggests that frequent exposure to highly stimulating online environments (e.g., social media, online gaming) reinforces maladaptive behavioral patterns, thereby intensifying impulsive traits and impairing attentional regulation over time [48–50]. Thus, impulsivity is not only a predisposing trait in IA but also exacerbated by the addictive process itself, while negative mood both triggers and is worsened by compulsive use. As such, IA may contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle of impulsivity and emotional dysregulation, which are also central mechanisms of NSSI.

While the I-PACE model provides valuable insights into IA development, it does not explicitly address self-harm behaviors, necessitating integration with NSSI-specific theoretical frameworks. The Four-Function Model (FFM) posits that NSSI is maintained primarily through automatic-negative reinforcement—reducing unbearable negative emotions—with contemporary extensions emphasizing emotion dysregulation as the primary pathway to NSSI in adolescents [51, 52]. Complementing this, the Emotional Cascade Model proposes that NSSI occurs when rumination amplifies emotional distress to intolerable levels, creating pressure for immediate relief through impulsive self-harm [53]. Integrating I-PACE with these NSSI-specific frameworks reveals three pathways through which IA may contribute to self-injury: (1) IA further depletes pre-existing emotion regulation capacity, increasing reliance on NSSI as an alternative regulatory strategy [46, 54]; (2) repeated engagement with highly rewarding online stimuli produces neuroadaptations that compromise inhibitory control, increasing impulsive responding to self-harm urges [46, 55, 56]; and (3) IA is associated with exacerbated depressive symptoms through disrupted sleep and impaired social relationships [47, 57], creating the affective context in which NSSI may become appealing for immediate relief [58].

It is important to acknowledge that alternative directional pathways warrant consideration. Impulsivity and IA may exert independent effects on NSSI, with each representing distinct risk mechanisms—impulsivity as a neurocognitive vulnerability and IA as a behavioral pattern—that could additively increase self-harm risk. Bidirectional relationships likely exist, wherein pre-existing impulsivity may increase vulnerability to developing IA through difficulties with self-control [59–61], while excessive internet use may subsequently be associated with exacerbated impulsive tendencies through neuroplastic changes in reward and control systems [55, 62]. Additionally, IA could serve as an intervening variable in the relationship between impulsivity and NSSI, suggesting that impulsive adolescents may develop problematic internet use patterns that increase self-harm risk through mechanisms such as online social comparison, cybervictimization, or circadian rhythm disruption [25, 63]. Despite these plausible alternatives, we prioritized the serial associational model (IA → impulsivity → depression → NSSI) based on theoretical frameworks positioning IA as an environmental exposure that may activate latent vulnerabilities [46] and longitudinal evidence showing screen time is associated with subsequent increases in depression symptoms [64, 65]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that reciprocal pathways likely operate concurrently, and our cross-sectional design cannot definitively establish temporal precedence or causality.

To date, the association between IA, impulsivity, depression, and NSSI in adolescents has not been sufficiently studied within an integrated theoretical framework. As impulsivity represents an inherent risk factor for both NSSI and internet addiction in adolescents, the aim of the present study was to examine the cross-sectional interrelationships among IA, impulsivity, depression, and NSSI. Specifically, we sought to determine whether IA and NSSI were statistically associated through indirect pathways involving impulsivity and depressive symptoms using structural equation modeling (SEM), consistent with an associational variable analysis framework for cross-sectional data [66]. It was hypothesized that (1) adolescents with MDD would report significantly higher levels of IA, impulsivity, and NSSI compared to healthy controls; (2) the association between IA and NSSI would be statistically accounted for through both impulsivity and depression in cross-sectional pathway models; and (3) alternative serial associational models specifying different orderings of impulsivity and depressive symptoms would demonstrate significant indirect pathways linking IA and NSSI, without implying temporal or causal precedence.

Methods

Participants and procedures

This cross-sectional, case-control study was conducted at the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic of the Suez Canal University Teaching Hospital. Participant recruitment occurred between January 2022 and June 2023. Prior to data collection, the study protocol was approved by the Ethics and Clinical Research Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Suez Canal University (Approval ID: 4588/2021). All procedures adhered to the ethical standards of the committee and the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and its subsequent amendments [67]. Written informed consent was obtained from caregivers, and verbal and written assent was obtained from adolescent participants prior to enrollment.

A pilot study that included 10 participants to identify the average time needed to perform all the study steps and procedures and identify any hurdles in applying the data collection tools. Participants were asked to complete the questionnaire, followed by an interview to discuss any difficulties faced. Feedback from the pilot study was used to refine the final version of the predesigned demographic data sheet and the other study questionnaires and tools. Those participants were not included in the analysis. Sample size was determined using Epi Info Version 6 for unmatched case-control studies, yielding a required total of 110 participants (55 in each group).

Participants were recruited using a consecutive sampling approach. The clinical group (cases) included 55 adolescents (aged 10–17 years) who were first-time attendees at the outpatient clinic, drug-naïve, and newly diagnosed with Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) according to DSM-V criteria. Diagnosis was confirmed by the clinic (Child and Adolescent Psychiatry) consultant, using a structured clinical interview. Eligible participants had a full-scale IQ ≥ 90, as determined by the Arabic version of the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC).

After the initial evaluation for the suitability for inclusion regarding the control group, they were invited to the clinic for evaluations by the same clinic consultant. The control group included 55 adolescents who were matched for age, intellectual, educational, and socioeconomic status with the recruited cases group with no current or past psychiatric diagnoses. These participants were recruited from local youth groups within the community in the catchment area of the hospital and relatives of the health care workers at the hospital.

Exclusion criteria for both groups included any history of neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, Rett syndrome), intellectual disability (e.g., Down syndrome), neurological conditions (e.g., epilepsy), chronic or severe medical illness, substance use disorders, current use of stimulants or psychotropic medications, and any other DSM-IV psychiatric diagnoses.

All adolescent individuals who were presenting to the clinic within the assigned age range and time period were screened for the purpose of their presentation to the clinic. Those whose presenting symptoms were solely depressive symptoms were subjected to thorough general and neurological examinations to exclude any physical or neurological disorders. Individuals who conformed with our set of criteria for inclusion were further assessed. Each assessment for every individual took around 2.5 to 3 h to complete.

Measures

1. Demographic questionnaire

A structured self-reported demographic questionnaire was predesigned for the purpose of this study, assessing demographic characteristics including age, gender, own and parents’ educational level, perception of family’s social status and living conditions, and their parents’ occupation. The questionnaire was defined so that the input information from adolescents is in fixed answer categories.

2. Kiddie SADS present and lifetime english arabic version (K-SADS-PL)

The Arabic version of the K-SADS-PL was used to assess current and past psychiatric diagnoses [68]. It is a semi-structured diagnostic interview widely used in clinical and research settings and has demonstrated good inter-rater reliability and diagnostic validity across a range of disorders [69]. In this study, it was administered by trained clinicians to confirm the diagnosis of MDD (cases’ group) or its absence (control group) and rule out any comorbid psychiatric disorders other than those under study.

3. Children’s depression inventory (CDI)

The CDI [70] is a 27-item scale that is self-rated and symptom-oriented. The 27 items of the CDI are grouped into five factor areas, including negative mood, interpersonal problems, ineffectiveness, anhedonia, and negative self-esteem, each with three response options reflecting increasing symptom severity. Higher total scores indicate more severe depressive symptomatology. The Arabic version [71] has shown adequate internal consistency and construct validity, and we administered it to our sample to assess the severity of depressive symptoms.

4. Deliberate self-harm inventory (DSHI)

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) was assessed using the Arabic version of the Deliberate Self-Harm Inventory (DSHI), originally developed by Gratz (2001) [72]. We used the translated Arabic version [73] that has been used in previous research on self-harm in Egypt [74]. The DSHI is a self-report questionnaire that assesses the frequency and types of intentional self-injurious behavior without suicidal intent. Participants are asked whether and how many times they were involved in various kinds of behaviors on purpose and the age of the first time and last time they were involved in these behaviors. A dichotomous DSH variable was designed by putting a score of ‘‘1’’ to the participants who reported having engaged in DSH and a score of ‘‘0’’ to those who did not report engaging in any of the behaviors of the DSHI. The lifetime frequency was calculated once as a categorical variable divided into none, once, two times, and more than two times, and as a continuous variable for the number of times deliberating self-harm. The Arabic version has demonstrated strong internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and criterion validity in both clinical and non-clinical samples.

5. Internet addiction test (IAT)

Internet addiction was assessed using the Arabic version of the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), developed by Young in 1989 [75], and the Arabic version was validated [76] and was used in many research studies investigating youth Internet addiction in many Arabic countries [77–81]. The IAT consists of 20 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale (0 = does not apply to 5 = always), with higher scores denoting a higher level of problems related to internet use, measuring the degree of compulsivity, escapism, and dependency related to internet use. The Arabic version has been validated and shows acceptable psychometric properties in adolescent samples.

6. Barratt impulsiveness scale (BIS-11)

Trait impulsivity was measured using the Arabic version [82] of the Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11) [83], a widely used 30-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess attentional, motor, and non-planning impulsivity. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale, and total scores range from 30 to 120, with higher total sum-scores denoting higher trait impulsivity. The Arabic translation has demonstrated good internal consistency and construct validity. The Arabic version has been used in previous research studies in Egypt [84, 85].

Data management

All collected data, including sociodemographic variables and psychometric scores, were entered into a secure electronic database. Data were reviewed for completeness and accuracy prior to statistical analysis. Unique identifiers were used to ensure participant confidentiality and data integrity throughout the research process.

Statistical analysis

Participants’ socio-demographic characteristics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to assess the normality of continuous variables. Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact test or Monte Carlo correction when more than 20% of cells had expected counts less than 5 were used to compare categorical variables between groups. Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test were employed to compare normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables between groups, respectively. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests were applied for normally and non-normally distributed variables, respectively.

Bivariate associations between psychological variables (depression, impulsivity, and internet addiction) and NSSI lifetime frequency as an outcome were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Group differences in psychological and behavioral measures, including internet addiction (IAT scores), impulsivity (Barratt Impulsiveness Scale scores), and NSSI characteristics, were assessed using appropriate statistical tests based on data distribution and variable type.

The associations between internet addiction and lifetime NSSI presence were examined using logistic regression models. Crude models were first established, followed by adjusted models controlling for potential covariates. Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Results were presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied to assess model adequacy.

To examine the roles of depressive symptoms (Children’s Depression Inventory scores) and impulsivity (Barratt Impulsiveness Scale scores) as mediating (indirect pathway) variables in the cross-sectional relationship between IA and NSSI, pathway analyses were conducted using structural equation modeling (SEM). Given the cross-sectional design, in which IA, depressive symptoms, impulsivity, and NSSI were assessed at the same time point, these analyses assessed statistical direct and indirect associations and cannot establish temporal precedence or causality. In line with Weems (2025) [66], these models were interpreted as associational pathway analyses rather than causal mediation models, and indirect effects were used to quantify the extent to which associations between variables were statistically accounted for by intermediate variables.

Direct and indirect pathways were estimated, and unstandardized regression coefficients (B), standard errors, t-values, and p-values were reported. Bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for indirect pathways were obtained using bootstrap procedures with 5,000 resamples. Age and sex were included as covariates in all SEM models, including single-indirect and sequential pathway models, to account for potential demographic confounding and sex-related differences in NSSI risk. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and AMOS version 23 (IBM Corp.) for SEM analyses. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Our study included 110 adolescents, with 55 in the depression cases group and 55 in the control group. There was no significant difference in the mean age between both groups (15.36 ± 2.05 vs. 15.00 ± 2.22 years, p = 0.374). A significantly higher proportion of females was observed in the case group (72.7%) compared to the control group (50.9%) (p = 0.019). Most participants in both groups lived in urban areas (87.3% of cases vs. 96.4% of controls), and the majority of them were in middle or high school.

While most parents were married in both groups, a higher percentage of divorced parents was reported among cases (16.4%) compared to controls (3.6%). There were no significant differences in other sociodemographic variables, including parental education, occupation, family income, and family size (Table 1).

Group differences in internet addiction, impulsivity, and NSSI

There were significant differences observed between both groups in terms of internet addiction, impulsivity, and lifetime NSSI frequency (Fig. 1). The cases group demonstrated moderate to severe internet addiction (47.3%) compared to the controls (21.8%; p < 0.001); additionally, its IAT mean scores were higher (54.87 ± 24.32 vs. 37.55 ± 17.88). Regarding impulsivity, the case group had a higher prevalence of moderate BIS-11 scores (78.2% vs. 56.4%), while low impulsivity was more common among controls (43.6%) compared to cases (16.4%). Furthermore, the mean BIS-11 score was significantly higher in the case group (70.85 ± 10.89) than in controls (60.24 ± 13.70; p < 0.001). The frequency and clinical severity of lifetime NSSI were also significantly greater among cases. Most of the case group reported more than two lifetime NSSI episodes compared to controls (63.6% vs. 41.8%; p = 0.001, respectively). The mean number of lifetime episodes was higher in cases (6.76 ± 6.91) than controls (2.78 ± 3.72; p < 0.001). In addition, 29.1% of cases had received treatment or hospitalization for NSSI, compared to none of the controls (p < 0.001). Complete results are presented in Tables S1–3 and Supplemental Results.

Psychological profiles within the case group

Between the 55 adolescents diagnosed with MDD, CDI scores ranged from 15 to 46, with a mean of 27.24 ± 7.08 and a median of 26.0 (IQR: 22.0–33.5), indicating a moderate level of depressive symptoms overall. Based on CDI classification, 38.2% of participants fell within the mild depression range, 23.6% had moderate depression, and 38.2% were classified as having severe depression. Although IAT scores did not significantly differ across depression subgroups (p > 0.05), a non-significant increase in IA with higher depression severity was observed (mean IAT scores: 48.86 in mild, 54.77 in moderate, and 60.95 in severe groups) (Table 2). In contrast, impulsivity significantly increased with depression severity (p = 0.008), where participants with severe depression had the highest mean BIS-11 scores (76.43 ± 10.46), compared to the moderate (68.62 ± 8.61) and mild (66.67 ± 10.59) groups. Although the categorical distribution of impulsivity did not differ significantly (p = 0.115), severe impulsivity was observed only in the severe depression group (14.3%) (Table 3). Lifetime NSSI frequency also varied with depression severity. Participants with severe depression reported more frequent lifetime episodes (85.7% had > 2 episodes) compared to those with moderate (76.9%) and mild depression (33.3%; p = 0.011). While age at onset and recency of NSSI did not differ significantly, earlier and more recent episodes were evident in the severe group. Hospitalization or treatment for NSSI was more common among those with higher CDI scores, though not statistically significant (Table 4).

Correlation and regression analyses

Table 5 presents the correlations between psychological variables in the case and control groups. In the case group, higher IAT scores were significantly associated with increased impulsivity (Barratt score; r = 0.281, p = 0.037), more frequent lifetime NSSI (r = 0.340, p = 0.011), and greater depression severity (CDI scores; r = 0.345, p = 0.010). Impulsivity was also positively correlated with lifetime NSSI frequency (r = 0.269, p = 0.047) and depression severity (r = 0.419, p = 0.001), while lifetime NSSI frequency showed a strong positive correlation with depressive symptoms (r = 0.477, p < 0.001). Additionally, younger age of first NSSI was significantly associated with more severe depression (r = − 0.424, p = 0.003).

Indirect pathway analyses (exploring the cross-sectional mediating roles)

To better understand the psychological mechanisms linking IA to NSSI, a series of structural equation models (SEM) were conducted to examine the direct and indirect associations linking IA to NSSI, evaluating whether depressive symptoms and impulsivity statistically accounted for this association through indirect pathways. All models were adjusted for age and sex.

In Model 1, depressive symptoms were examined as a mediator in the cross-sectional association between IA and NSSI. The indirect association of IA on NSSI through depressive symptoms was statistically significant (B = 0.042, p = 0.002), whereas the direct was not statistically significant (B = 0.036, p = 0.136). This pattern indicates that, within this cross-sectional model, the IA–NSSI association was statistically accounted through depressive symptoms (Figure S1; Table S4, Supplementary Materials).

Model 2 evaluated impulsivity as an indirect pathway variable. A statistically significant indirect association of IA on NSSI through impulsivity was observed (B = 0.039, p = 0.003), while the direct association between IA and NSSI remained non-significant (B = 0.038, p = 0.123). These findings suggest that impulsivity also statistically accounted for the IA–NSSI association in this cross-sectional framework (Figure S2; Table S5).

To evaluate whether the indirect association between IA and NSSI was robust when depressive symptoms and impulsivity were considered simultaneously, two plausible alternative cross-sectional sequential pathway models were examined.

In Model 3, depressive symptoms and impulsivity were included concurrently, with depressive symptoms specified earlier in the sequential pathway. Significant specific indirect associations were observed for the following pathways:

IA → depressive symptoms → NSSI (B = 0.032, p = 0.009).

IA → depressive symptoms → NSSI (B = 0.032, p = 0.009).

IA → impulsivity → NSSI (B = 0.019, p = 0.037).

IA → impulsivity → NSSI (B = 0.019, p = 0.037).

IA → depressive symptoms → impulsivity → NSSI (B = 0.009, p = 0.026).

IA → depressive symptoms → impulsivity → NSSI (B = 0.009, p = 0.026).

The total indirect association was statistically significant, while the direct association between IA and NSSI was not (B = 0.017, p = 0.484), indicating that indirect pathways involving depressive symptoms and impulsivity statistically accounted for the observed association in this model (Fig. 2; Table 7).

Discussion

This study highlights the complex interrelationships among IA, impulsivity, depression, and NSSI in adolescents, with a specific focus on those diagnosed with major depressive disorder (MDD). Our findings offer novel insight into how IA may indirectly contribute to NSSI through distinct but interrelated psychological mechanisms, underscoring the importance of integrated screening and intervention strategies.

Consistent with prior studies, adolescents with MDD in our sample reported significantly higher levels of IA compared to healthy controls, suggesting that problematic internet use may be more common among clinically depressed adolescents [86–88]. Previous research has shown certain symptoms of depression, such as the tendency to social isolation and negative self-perception, cause an individual to prefer Internet communication to face-to-face communication for emotional regulation and social avoidance, using digital interactions to act as a regulator of emotional distress and perceived safer alternatives to real-world engagement [89–92]. Furthermore, these findings suggest that IA could be maintained by a negative reinforcement mechanism, such as a means to alleviate distress or sadness [93]. Similarly, adolescents with negative emotions may resort to coping mechanisms, such as NSSI, to alleviate their psychological pain and facilitate an escape from reality [94, 95]. In our sample, NSSI was highly prevalent among adolescents with MDD, with 89.1% reporting at least one lifetime episode compared to 56.4% in healthy controls (p < 0.001). This elevated prevalence is in line with prior studies [15, 96, 97] where they found that adolescents with MDD had a history of NSSI, rates significantly higher than those observed in general adolescent populations. The heightened prevalence may reflect the role of depressive symptoms in increasing NSSI risk [98, 99]. Additionally, the higher frequency of repeated NSSI in the depressed group raises concerns about behavioral habituation, where negative physical sensations diminish over time, and self-injury becomes increasingly reinforcing [100]. This pattern may elevate suicide risk, consistent with Anestis et al.‘s findings that more frequent NSSI is associated with heightened suicidality [101].

Impulsivity also emerged as a key differentiating factor between depressed adolescents and controls. Adolescents with MDD demonstrated significantly greater impulsivity, aligning with prior findings by Onat et al. who reported that impulsivity was higher in MDD group than in controls [87]. Further findings link impulsivity to both mood instability and poor behavioral regulation [102, 103]. One hypothesis is that higher impulsivity may contribute to the risk of developing depressive episodes. Neurobiological evidence suggests that deficits in serotonergic function and impaired prefrontal control may underpin this association [80], highlighting shared mechanisms between impulsivity and depressive symptoms.

Notably, in our study, depression severity, impulsivity, and IA were all positively correlated with higher lifetime frequency of NSSI. These findings are consistent with Cheng et al. [104], who reported significant positive correlations between NSSI behaviors, depression, and a positive diagnosis of IA. Similarly, a large-scale survey involving 15,623 Chinese adolescents found that both possible and confirmed IA were associated with increased odds of engaging in NSSI behaviors compared to non-addicted peers [105]. However, while depression, impulsivity, and IA were significantly associated to lifetime NSSI frequency in univariate analyses, only female gender remained a significant in the multivariate model. This pattern suggests overlapping or indirect effects among these psychological variables. Our findings are in line with those of Zhong et al. [106], who identified female gender as a key risk factor for NSSI, potentially due to differences in biological vulnerability, emotion regulation strategies, and psychosocial stressors.

To explore these cross-sectional dynamics further, we used structural equation modeling to test whether IA affects lifetime NSSI frequency indirectly through impulsivity (BIS-11 score) and depression severity (CDI score). In both single and serial pathway models, we found no significant direct association of IA on NSSI, but significant indirect association through both depression and impulsivity. These results are in line with previous literature, including the findings by Ma et al., who reported that depressive symptoms mediated the association between IA and NSSI, suggesting that adolescents with IA may not directly engage in self-injury but may first develop depressive symptoms that increase the risk of NSSI [29]. Similar findings were reported in other studies, where the link between IA and self-injurious behavior was shown to be mediated by emotional and cognitive vulnerabilities rather than being a direct effect [107–109]. While the cross-sectional nature of these data can not confirm definitive conclusions about temporal ordering, this ordering supports the hypothesis that impulsivity may act as an initiating factor, exacerbated by internet overuse, and subsequently triggering depressive symptoms. Prior research supports this pathway, suggesting that frequent internet exposure, particularly through social media and online gaming, can reinforce impulsive behavioral patterns [49, 50]. The critical role of impulsivity in this pathway is consistent with findings by Cassels et al., who reported that heightened impulsivity prospectively predicts the onset of NSSI behaviors in adolescents and young adults. Furthermore, their study showed that depression may mediate the relationship between impulsivity and NSSI, indicating that adolescents high in impulsivity may be especially vulnerable to engaging in self-injury when they also experience depressive symptoms [38].

In summary, our findings highlight the interconnected roles of IA, impulsivity, depression, and NSSI among adolescents. Impulsivity appears to function as a critical indirect associative factor, amplifying emotional distress and maladaptive coping, while depression further contributes to the risk of NSSI. These insights underscore the importance of early identification and intervention targeting both impulse control and mood regulation in youth with problematic internet use.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

By integrating multiple risk factors into a comprehensive analysis and using a case-control design with our cases being a clinically diagnosed sample of adolescents with depression and a healthy community adolescent sample as our controls, our study provides important insights into the interrelation between NSSI, IA, and impulsivity within the context of depression. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to further validate these findings and explore potential preventive measures.

Although we obtained some self-reported data that might have carried lurking recall or social desirability biases, we did not rely only on self-reported measures and performed thorough clinical assessments of all participants through qualified child and adolescent clinicians to ensure adherence to the inclusion and exclusion criteria and provide a comprehensive evaluation for each adolescent involved in the study. We used reliable and validated tools that were used extensively in prior research projects investigating adolescent challenges, but we believe that instead of using child (or adult) scales, designing sensitive adolescent scales, and more ideally culturally specific ones, would yield more precise responses that could better capture and represent the unique mental challenges faced by adolescents in future research.

Our sample consisted of both male and female adolescents, but they were not evenly distributed within our samples; accordingly, we were not able to investigate gender differences directly. Girls constituted most of our cases’ group.

We collected our “cases” sample from one hospital, but as it is a pooling hospital for a large area with diverse characteristics, our sample and pooled results might not hold a sample-collection bias. Our cases were recruited only from the outpatient clinic; it would have been more informative to obtain data from inpatients as well.

Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships between NSSI, IA, depression, and impulsivity in adolescents to inform the development of effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Our study limitations lie in the fact that it has a cross-sectional design that prevents the establishment of causal relationships and limits the understanding of temporal or directional effects. The reliance on self-reported measures may introduce bias, including underreporting or social desirability, especially for sensitive behaviors like NSSI. We did not integrate other related psychosocial and behavioral variables within our analyses, such as further family dynamics, parenting practices, social determinants of health, or peer influences. These might confound the observed associations; that’s why we recommend taking them into account while designing future research to clarify the influence of these inputs on the studied relationships.

Input from schools and healthcare providers could enhance contextual understanding and facilitate translation of findings into prevention and intervention efforts. Importantly, engaging adolescents and young people with lived experience of depression, NSSI, and problematic internet use would provide critical insight into the subjective meaning, contextual triggers, and lived pathways linking these experiences. Such participatory approaches may strengthen the ecological validity of research findings and inform the development of more acceptable, relevant, and effective mental health programs for adolescents.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that among this sample of adolescents, depression and impulsivity fully mediate the link between internet addiction and NSSI. Another key distinction of our study is identifying female gender as a significant associating factor to NSSI in a multivariate model. In our sample, adolescents with severe depression exhibited higher impulsivity and more frequent and severe NSSI. These findings stress the need for early identification and tailored interventions addressing depression, Internet addiction, and impulsivity to mitigate self-harm risks.

Schools and healthcare providers should implement mental health programs aimed at early detection and intervention for adolescents exhibiting impulsivity, depression, or PIU to reduce the risk of NSSI. As to practical implications, mental health promotion and prevention programs should also include teaching adolescents how to handle impulsivity.
