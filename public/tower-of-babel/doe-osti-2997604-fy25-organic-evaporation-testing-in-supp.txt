 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Hanford Waste Treatment and Immobilization Plant (WTP) is being built to treat and immobilize the
approximately 56 million gallons of radioactive mixed waste stored in 156 underground storage tanks. The
baseline method for immobilization of Low-Activity Waste (LAW) is vitrification, but additional
immobilization capacity is needed to supplement the initial LAW melters. Additionally, efforts are being
undertaken to accelerate the disposition of Pre-Treated Waste (PTW) in Hanford’s West Area. An
alternative cementitious waste form is being investigated as an alternative immobilization method.
However, one impediment to a cementitious waste form is the presence of Land Disposal Restricted (LDR)
organic chemicals in tank waste, which are regulated on a concentration-based standard in the final waste
form. Hence, if the quantity of organics in PTW and LAW is high enough, treatment may be needed to
destroy or remove said organics to make a waste form compatible with disposal in a mixed low level waste
landfill. This work evaluates evaporation as a potential avenue for treatment of LDR organics to eliminate
the impediment and permit possible use of a cementitious waste form.

Vacuum evaporation testing to remove LDR organics consisted of preparing non-radioactive PTW
simulant, spiking that simulant with organic chemicals, and evaporating the mixture via differential
distillation. The apparatus was a laboratory-scale vacuum evaporator operated at 60 ± 5 torr absolute
(vacuum evaporation). The PTW simulant represented the liquid expected to be retrieved from the Hanford
tank farms, approximately 4.0 M [Na+] total sodium ion concentration.1 The concentration of the organic
chemicals added was significantly higher than typically found in the tank waste samples since the higher
levels were necessary to assist in analytical measurement and tracking of the spiked species. One set of the
evaporation tests involved adjustment to a lower pH of 4 with sulfuric acid followed by evaporation to
remove compounds that are ionic at high pH.

The following are the key observations from vacuum evaporation tests performed for simulated Hanford
tank waste without pH adjustment:
 • Nitrobenzene showed good removal in duplicate tests. The tests had an incomplete mass balance
 for nitrobenzene due to difficulty quantifying the nitrobenzene that moved beyond the initial
 condenser.
 • 2,4,6-Trichlorophenol and p-nitrophenol removal was not expected and test results confirmed no
 apparent removal.
 • Phenol and o-cresol removal was not expected and did not appear to occur; however, evaporation
 test results could not be confirmed because of High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)
 preparation complications.
 • Pyridine was not quantifiable in this matrix due to poor resolution in HPLC. Proton Nuclear
 Magnetic Resonance (1H-NMR) analysis confirmed the removal of pyridine from the evaporator
 pot.
 • Vacuum evaporation remains a viable option to treat the LDR organic compounds that have
 relatively high volatility and that do not deprotonate at the PTW high pH condition.

The following are the key observations from vacuum evaporation tests performed for simulated Hanford
tank waste adjusted to a pH of approximately 4 through the addition of sulfuric acid.
 • Nitrobenzene removal appeared complete based on the intermediate and ending pot sample
 analysis.
 • 2,4,6-Trichlorophenol was not present at detectable levels in the intermediate and final evaporated
 low pH simulant; however, the amount of 2,4,6-trichlorophenol measured in the condensate and
 KOP was less than expected to close a mass balance. Acidification improved the removal of
 2,4,6-trichlorophenol.

 vi

 • p-Nitrophenol exhibited no apparent removal. This is consistent with its relatively high Henry’s
 law coefficient (Kh) ratio of 362 compared to that of N-nitrosomorpholine (Kh ratio of 182).
 • Phenol and o-cresol experienced nitration. There is evidence of partial evaporation of both phenol
 and o-cresol. However, larger fractions of the initial phenol and o-cresol likely underwent nitration
 reactions.
 • Pyridine was not quantifiable in this matrix due to poor resolution by HPLC. The 1H-NMR analysis
 confirmed removal of pyridine from the evaporator pot. This was unexpected because pyridine was
 expected to be pyridinium and not removed at pH near 4.

Several observations were made during acid addition for pH adjustment and subsequent evaporation testing
that indicate the risk of potential difficulties in such a process:
 • Solids are precipitated, which are likely gibbsite. The evaporator and subsequent processes would
 need to handle a dilute slurry rather than a liquid.
 • Gases are evolved during pH adjustment, including NOx compounds and likely including CO2. The
 NOx may require treatment and/or permitting.
 • Phenol and o-cresol, and potentially other phenolic compounds, will likely undergo partial or
 complete nitration during acidification, which may in turn result in persistent nitrated LDR organics
 in the acidified product.
 • Foaming was encountered during vacuum evaporation of the low pH material that makes control
 of the process more difficult.

1.0 Introduction
The primary mission of the U. S. Department of Energy (DOE) River Protection Project (RPP) is to protect
the Columbia River by removing and treating the legacy defense waste stored in Hanford’s underground
tank system. Vitrification at the Waste Treatment Plant (WTP) for both high-level waste (HLW) and low-
activity waste (LAW) is the current treatment approach. The LAW vitrification system is currently being
commissioned, and hot operations are planned for 2025; however, the WTP does not have the capacity to
immobilize all of Hanford’s LAW. Additionally, efforts are being undertaken to accelerate disposition of a
portion of the waste in Hanford’s West Area.2 The analogous waste in West Area is chemically similar to
LAW, but is referred to as Pre-Treated Waste (PTW).

One option under investigation for alternate treatment capacity is to solidify/immobilize waste in a
cementitious waste form that meets both DOE Order 435.1 low level waste requirements and Resource
Conservation and Recovery Act (RCRA) Land Disposal Restriction (LDR) requirements. The proposed use
of an ambient temperature immobilization method, however, will not remove or destroy the various
regulated organic chemicals in the waste to the same extent as thermal treatment. These regulated organic
chemicals may need to receive alternate treatment to allow disposal in a mixed low level waste landfill.
Alternate treatment of PTW by solidification/immobilization in a cementitious waste form is being
investigated as a potential lower cost alternate option.3

One treatment method that has been proposed to support ambient temperature solidification/immobilization
of PTW is to remove the organics from the aqueous stream by evaporation with subsequent treatment of
the aqueous condensate containing the removed organics in the RCRA-permitted Hanford 200 Area
Effluent Treatment Facility (ETF). This approach would likely be similar to the current evaporative volume
reduction of tank waste performed by the Hanford 242-A evaporator. Hanford Tank Waste Operations &
Closure, LLC (H2C), formerly Washington River Protection Solutions, LLC (WRPS), requested that
Savannah River National Laboratory (SRNL) perform laboratory-scaled evaporator tests to strengthen
process knowledge on the ability to remove or destroy regulated organics in Hanford tank waste.

The evaporator testing reported here is a continuation of previous evaporator experiments performed at
SRNL.4-6 The objectives for this current testing are the following:

 • Investigate adjustment to lower pH followed by evaporation to remove compounds that are ionic
 at high pH. This includes determining the effectiveness for removal of the compounds expected to
 be positively and negatively impacted by pH adjustment.
 • Investigate evaporation for removal of compounds for which conflicting or incomplete information
 exists.

3.3 Simulant pH Adjustment
Sulfuric acid was chosen for the pH adjustment acid due to the lesser acid volume needed, compared to a
similar acid such as hydrochloric acid, as well as the minimal impact to grout.

Simulant pH adjustment trials were performed using both concentrated (18 M) and 2 M sulfuric acid to
determine the optimal approach for larger scale simulant pH adjustment. Observations made during the trial
pH adjustments included gas and heat generation, solid precipitation, and color changes of the simulant.
The 18 M sulfuric acid was ultimately selected as the gas and heat generation, while present, was
manageable while significantly reducing the volume increase in comparison to the 2 M sulfuric acid.

The pH was adjusted by adding 23 mL of concentrated sulfuric acid dropwise to a stirring solution of
approximately 380 mL simulant while monitoring pH via a pH meter. NOx gas was observed upon the
introduction of sulfuric acid. Sulfuric acid was added over a period of approximately 30 minutes until the
simulant reached pH 4. After 45 minutes, the pH of the simulant solution was re-measured and found to
have increased to 4.3. As a result, additional sulfuric acid was added to adjust the pH back to 4. This process
was repeated to produce a total of two approximately 400 mL pH 4 simulant solutions.

3.4 Choice of Regulated Organic Chemicals and Surrogates
The basis for selection of organics to be used in the testing was as follows:
 • On the reduced list of 132 LDR organics
 • Priority given to detected compounds (detection priority Categories 1, 2, and 3)8
 • Have one or more of the following qualities:
 o A pKa for deprotonation in the range of 13 or lower (where lower pH may promote
 evaporation),
 o A pKa for ionization above 5 (where lower pH may hinder evaporation), or
 o An information gap with conflicting or incomplete information.

 3

Phenol and various substituted phenols are the compounds on the reduced list of 132 LDR organic
compounds for which lowering the pH would be most beneficial. Phenol as well as one isomer each of a
mono- or poly-substituted methyl-, nitro-, or chloro-phenol were selected. 2,4-dinitrophenol was not chosen
for this study despite its high detection priority category (Category 1) because it has low water solubility.

Additionally, pyridine was selected as a compound to represent those for which removal may be inhibited
at low pH. Pyridine is in detection priority Category 2 and has already been shown to be removed by vacuum
evaporation in Hanford waste simulants. In acid, however, pyridine can form the cation pyridinium (pKa =
5.25), which may reduce the ability to remove it through evaporation. This is a similar case for several
anilines and amines. Vacuum evaporation has already been shown to be ineffective for 4-nitroaniline at the
high pH of the waste. Thus, 4-nitroaniline was not selected because it is expected that lowering the pH
would not improve evaporation.

From Aspen modeling, nitrobenzene evaporation resulted in an apparent azeotrope that prevented removal
from the evaporator concentrate to less than the non-wastewater RCRA limit for nitrobenzene.9 There is
some indication from literature references that steam stripping should remove nitrobenzene from water.10
Because both evaporation and steam stripping processes should be similarly impacted by an azeotrope, this
conflicting information clouds the expectation of how nitrobenzene will behave during waste evaporation.
Thus, nitrobenzene warrants experimental evaporation investigation as a detection priority Category 3
compound.

3.5 Evaporation Testing
The vacuum evaporation testing described herein was performed using the same evaporator apparatus and
procedure as used in the previous testing. A schematic of the evaporator setup is shown in Figure 3-1, and
an image is given in Figure 3-2. The setup consists of a 1 L glass evaporator pot resting on a hot plate/stirrer.
The evaporator pot contained 4 ports connected to the following:
 • A thermocouple connected to a temperature controller used to monitor the temperature in the
 evaporator pot (Temperature LCD readout, as per Figure 3-1),
 • A sample port used to sample the feed in the evaporator pot,
 • A pressure transducer and vacuum gauge with a readout to monitor the pressure within the pot,
 and
 • A stainless-steel tubing that connects downstream to the condenser.

Downstream from the evaporator pot is a condenser, a graduated cylinder used to collect condensate, a
secondary knock-out pot (KOP) that was cooled by dry ice, and a vacuum pump.

The lab-scale evaporator is not a continuous feed system but is operated in batch mode. Conversely, the
Hanford 242-A evaporator is operated in a continuous fashion where the pot has higher sodium
concentration than is found in the feed during processing. Differences between a batch and continuous
process, including the effects of temperature and sodium molarity over time, should result in higher organic
removal in a continuous process. For example, batch processing starts at 4 M sodium for the current work
and rises to ~8 M over ~3 hours, at which point the experiment is stopped. In contrast, continuous
evaporation starts and remains at the high value (i.e., 8 M sodium). The higher salt content of the continuous
process results in a higher boiling temperature and more salting-out. Both the steady higher temperature
and steady higher salt content will tend to increase organic partitioning to the evaporator overhead.

A vacuum evaporation test entailed the following: the feed was added to the evaporator pot and the system
was sealed, the system was then evacuated to 60 torr (± 5 torr), and the feed was then brought to boil while
stirring using a Teflon-coated magnetic stir-bar. The system was monitored as the pot contents were boiled
down until target volumes of condensate were collected. For each run, the feed consisted of 400 mL of the
spiked feed simulant (pH adjusted for low pH tests) - an exception to this was the first run (high pH R1), in
which ~450 mL of the spiked feed simulant was added. During the first concentration phase, the simulant
was concentrated until ~100 mL of condensate was generated. At this point, boiling was paused and a ~30
mL sample was withdrawn from the evaporator pot, and the accumulated condensate was collected. Pot,
condensate, and KOP samples at this point are referred to as “intermediate” samples in this work. The
system was sealed and boiling resumed until an additional 75 mL of condensate was generated. At this
point, boiling was stopped, and a final sample was withdrawn from the evaporator pot and the accumulated
condensate was collected. Pot, condensate, and KOP samples at this point are referred to as “end” samples
in this work.

During the design and construction of the evaporator, care was taken to have as few polymer parts as
possible. This would prevent the simulant in the evaporator pot and the off-gas from coming into contact
with polymeric materials. This was a precautionary measure to minimize loss of organic components that
might absorb (partition) into such materials.

The apparatus was constructed almost entirely of glass and stainless-steel tubing. All testing was conducted
inside a fume hood in a laboratory at SRNL. The evaporator pot was a 1 L modified glass beaker. The
simulant was heated using a hot plate/stirrer and continuously stirred with a Teflon-coated magnetic stir
bar. The absolute pot pressure was displayed using an electronic pressure transducer. The pressure was kept
at ~60 torr and manually adjusted as needed. The vapors traveled unrestricted to the glass condenser. There
was no engineered demisting element designed into the off-gas line to remove entrained particles, but the
stainless-steel line was approximately 58 cm high, which should have precluded entrainment based on
engineering judgement. As the gases cooled in the condenser, the condensate drained into the glass
condensate vessel, which was a graduated cylinder with appropriate ground glass fittings.

The condenser was cooled using a VWR® Scientific recirculating water bath. The chiller, supplemented
with periodic additions of ice, maintained the cooling water at approximately 4-5 °C. Any vapors that
passed through the condenser were condensed in the glass trap used as a secondary KOP. The KOP was
submersed in a Dewar vessel with dry ice, where the temperature was maintained at approximately -78 °C.
The vacuum in the system was created by a Vacuubrand® diaphragm vacuum pump, Type: MZ 2C.

The contents collected inside the KOP were always frozen when it was disconnected. KOP vessels had to
be thawed before they could be transferred to the sample collection bottle. The inlet and outlet were covered
with a cap to prevent the entry of air and humidity during thawing to room temperature. Sample liquid was
transferred to a glass container as soon as it thawed to minimize vapor losses. During the test campaign,
two separate KOPs were used; when one is removed, thawed, and emptied, the other is connected, placed
into the dry ice, and used in the test. All sample vials and bottles were glass and all caps used on these
sample containers were lined with Teflon. Environmental I-Chem® containers were used for both vials and
bottles.

Before simulant testing, water runs were performed to ensure that the equipment and instruments were
operating correctly. The system operated at 60 ± 5 torr and a temperature of 42 °C. This compares well with
CRC Steam Tables that show a boiling point of water at 108.0 °F (42.2 °C) at 62.2 torr.11

3.6 Methods of Sample Analysis

3.6.1 High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)
Samples for HPLC analysis were processed by organic extraction following pH adjustment. A 1 mL sub-
aliquot of the unfiltered well-mixed parent sample was diluted into 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer (H2PO4- and
HPO42-) for pH adjustment. Further adjustment with phosphoric acid or sodium hydroxide was performed
to attain a pH range of 5.5 – 7.5, ideal to avoid damage to the analytical equipment. Organic extraction was
performed on the buffered sample by the addition of 6.5 mL of HPLC-grade acetonitrile. The organic
supernate was removed for analysis. For HPLC samples, analysis was performed on an Agilent® 1260
Infinity II with a DyChrom Chemcosorb 5-ODS-UH (3.2 mm x 250 mm) column using a gradient method
utilizing water and acetonitrile for the mobile phase. The analytes were measured by a UV-Vis detector
over a broad UV wavelength range of 190 – 400 nm, with a 4 nm bandwidth and a reference wavelength of
360 nm with a 20 nm reference bandwidth. Quantitation was performed by optimal wavelength selection
for each analyte and collection of a 5-point calibration curve. Laboratory-prepared quality control (QC)
standards were analyzed as spikes in DI water. QC samples were analyzed with each sample batch to ensure
quantitative control and retention time matching.

3.6.2 NMR
Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR) measurements were performed on a Bruker® 500 MHz
ASCEND® AVANCE NEO Spectrometer. In a typical experiment, 400 µL of liquid samples were
transferred to a glass Wilmad® Precision NMR sample tube of 5.0 mm outer diameter combined with a
stem coaxial insert (WGS-5BL, outer diameter = 2.0 mm, stem height = 55 mm) containing 50 µL of a
locking solvent with 10 mM reference analyte (3-(Trimethylsilyl)propionic-2,2,3,3-d4 acid sodium salt
(TMSP-d4) in D2O, which is used to lock the magnet on a known signal (deuterium) and prevent drift in the
magnetic field. TMSP-d4 was selected as an axis-calibration reference where the associated signal (9H-
singlet) which provide a precise zero-point for the chemical shift scale, allowing for accurate, reproducible
analysis12. The zg pulse program was selected, and 256 scans were acquired with a relaxation delay of 1.0
s and pulses of 10 µs increments to ensure high spectral resolution. The samples that required water
suppression used a zgesgp pulse program and 256 scans were acquired with a relaxation delay of 5.0 s and
pulses of 9.5 µs increments to ensure high spectral resolution. All spectra were collected at 298.0 K without
further dilution in an effort to maintain the maximum signal-to-noise ratio possible. Spectral acquisition,
processing, and analysis were conducted through the software TopSpin® V4.3.0. All samples were filtered
prior to analysis due to incompatibility of solids with the analytical method. TMSP-d4 was selected as an

 7

axis-calibration reference where the associated signal (9H-singlet) was referenced to 0.00 ppm. All spectra
were collected at 300.0 K without further dilution in an effort to maintain the maximum signal-to-noise
ratio possible.

3.6.3 Other Methods
Components of the simulant including anions and elementals were measured by inductively coupled plasma
emission spectroscopy, ion chromatography (pH 4 PTW simulant samples were filtered), total organic and
inorganic carbon analyzer, and titrations. The pH meter measurements were performed with an Orion Star®
A215 pH meter utilizing a three-point standard calibration prior to sample measurements.

4.0 Results and Discussion

4.1 PTW Simulant pH Adjustment and Organic Spikes
The 4.0 M Na+ PTW simulant was adjusted with concentrated sulfuric acid to a pH of approximately 4. As
shown in A of Figure 4-1, the PTW simulant is a clear bright yellow solution due to the chromate in the
solution. As concentrated sulfuric acid was added to the solution, the color of the solution changed from a
bright yellow to a mustard yellow and the stirred simulant became opaque due to the formation of solids,
likely gibbsite, that started appearing around pH 11. A brown gas indicative of NOx formation was evident
throughout the later stages of pH adjustment (Figure 4-1, B). Additionally, there appeared to be a colorless
gas generated during the pH adjustment based on bubbles forming at solution surface that was likely carbon
dioxide (Figure 4-1, C). Around 6 - 7 g of the approximately 450 g of each simulant was lost through gas
generation. By the end of the adjustment to pH 4, the solution was a green-brown color. This color changed
to a blue solution within a couple of hours. The color change of the solution appears to be due to the
reduction of Cr(VI) in the solution to a Cr(III) species from the pH adjustment. During the simulant pH
adjustment trials, the pH was measured and had risen to 4.7 after two days of sitting. The rise in pH in the
simulant was most likely due to gibbsite dissolution. It should be noted that NOx gas generation continued
well after the pH adjustment had concluded. Inset F in Figure 4.1 shows the gas in the headspace of the
container turning brown when the cap was removed the day after pH adjustment.

4.2.3 o-Cresol
The pKa for o-cresol is 10.32 and it is expected to not be removed without pH adjustment from the
evaporator pot, due to the conjugate base o-cresolate being the predominate species in the high pH PTW
simulant.

As shown above in Figure 4-5, upon o-cresol addition to the low pH PTW simulant, there was a color
change from blue to brown, suggesting a reaction of o-cresol with the acidic simulant. The 1H-NMR spectra
in Figure 4-5 give the aromatic region for the o-cresol standard in the PTW simulant (green), pH 4-adjusted
PTW organic spiked simulant (blue), and the o-cresol standard in the pH 4-adjusted PTW simulant (red).
The large peak around 8.5-8.3 ppm in each spectrum is from the formate in the simulants. The expected
peak splitting for the o-cresol aromatic hydrogens can be seen in the PTW simulant o-cresol standard. This
same peak splitting is not seen in either of the pH 4 spectra. Instead, there are multiple peaks in the pH 4
o-cresol standard, suggesting that o-cresol has been nitrated into several different nitromethylphenol
species. This is not surprising, since o-cresol is susceptible to nitration under acidic conditions. The reaction
occurring is most likely an electrophilic aromatic substitution in which the sulfuric acid is activating nitric

 15

acid to form a nitronium ion which reacts with the aromatic ring of the organic compound. Electron donating
groups such as hydroxyl and methyl groups further promote the electrophilic aromatic substitution reaction.
Withdrawing groups such as nitro groups tend to deactivate this reaction. One such compound, 4,6-dinitro-
o-cresol, is also an LDR organic compound on the reduced list of 132 LDR organic compounds, with
detection priority Category 3 and a pKa of 5.

For the HPLC data, o-cresol was not present at the expected retention time in the pH 4 evaporator runs due
to the nitration discussed above. For the high pH PTW sample HPLC results, the samples were treated with
phosphoric acid during HPLC sample preparation, and it is believed to have cause the nitration of most of
the o-cresol in the samples. The results that could be obtained for o-cresol are given in Table 4-2 and should
not be considered quantitative. What can be gleaned is that it appears that o-cresol, as expected, is not
removed in the PTW simulant vacuum evaporator runs. This is supported by 1H-NMR given in Appendix
B: o-cresol is not present in either of the condensate samples (Figure B-1 and B-2) but is present in the end
pot sample (Figure B-3). As for the nitrated products formed, no results are given by HPLC due to a lack
of calibration standards. In general, nitrated aromatics are expected to be harder to remove by vacuum
evaporation than the non-nitrated organics. The intermediate pot 1H-NMR result shown in Figure C-3 shows
that there is at least one nitrated o-cresol still in the intermediate evaporator pot. It should also be noted that
there appears to be some o-cresol in the intermediate KOP from low pH Run 1, which suggests that a small
amount of o-cresol is removed prior to nitration in the low pH simulant.

4.2.5 p-Nitrophenol

data for p-nitrophenol is given in Table 4-7. While the pot concentration values are higher than expected,
there was no p-nitrophenol above detection in any of the condensate/KOP samples. This suggests that pH
adjustment does not help with the removal of p-nitrophenol during vacuum evaporation. p-Nitrophenol has
the lowest Henry’s law coefficient (Kh) of the compounds tested in this study, at 1.3E-8 atm-m3/mol.

Previous vacuum evaporation testing postulated that non-ionic compounds with Kh ratio (defined as the
ratio of the Kh of methanol to Kh of the compound) below that of N-nitrosomorpholine would be effectively
evaporated while compounds with a Kh ratio above that of N-nitrosomorpholine would not.5,6 In these terms,
p-nitrophenol has a Kh ratio of 362, which is greater than the Kh ratio of N-nitrosomorpholine of 186, while
phenol has a Kh ratio of 13.3. Thus, the observations for the acidic vacuum evaporation of phenol and p-
nitrophenol are consistent with the previous statements that a Kh ratio of 186 or below is needed for effective
evaporation.

4.2.7 Nitrobenzene
There is no conjugate base for nitrobenzene causing issues with removal from the PTW simulant during

4.3 Implications of a Low pH Evaporation Process
A complete treatment process for pH adjustment followed by evaporation for removal of LDR organics has
not been defined. It is thought that it could best fit after the filtration and ion exchange for cesium removal
and prior to grouting and/or transfer of the waste offsite. This holds true for the evaporation process with
or without acidification.

The implementation of acidification adds some complicated options to consider. The pH adjustment could
take place in whatever new stainless-steel tank is placed between the end of the ion exchange process and
the vacuum evaporator. The tank would need agitation, the ability to add chemicals, the ability to remove
precipitated solids, and the ability to abate undesired emissions. Rather than monitoring acid addition using
a pH probe, the likely method for acid addition would involve adding a calculated amount of acid based on
titration analysis of a sample upstream in the treatment process, such as from Tank SY-101 or SY-102. A
pH probe or sample measurement can be used for confirmation prior to treated waste evaporation. The
process may attempt to employ other acids or more mildly acidic conditions to potentially alter some of the
reactions that were observed during this testing.

There were several observations made during acid addition for pH adjustment that would indicate that
potential difficulties could be encountered in a process to evaporate acidified waste. First, fine solids are
precipitated during pH adjustment. These solids are likely gibbsite forming from the high soluble aluminum
content of the waste, as aluminum has low solubility as neutral pH is approached from either direction.
These solids persist at pH 4, meaning that the evaporator and subsequent processing would need to handle
a dilute slurry rather than a liquid. Second, gases are evolved during pH adjustment. These gases include
NOx compounds formed from the acidification of nitrite and likely CO2 from the reaction of the added acid

 23

with carbonate and bicarbonate. Ventilation and potentially off-gas permitting or treatment would be
needed if the NOx gas is deemed significant from the process. Third, phenol and o-cresol, and potentially
other phenolic compounds, will likely undergo nitration during acidification, which may create organic
compounds that are more difficult to remove and/or more energetic. Some of the likely reaction products
are LDR organic compounds themselves.

Operation of the vacuum evaporator at low pH was also more challenging than evaporation of the high pH
PTW simulant. Significant foaming was noted for evaporation of the low pH material. This may have been,
at least in part, due to the presence of fine solids in the evaporator pot slurry. The heating rate and vacuum
pressure needed to be precisely controlled to prevent foam-over of material into the condenser while still
maintaining an acceptable evaporation rate. It may be possible to employ an anti-foaming agent, but that
would require additional investigation.

With the observations made during this testing, it is unlikely that an evaporation process preceded by
acidification of the waste could be implemented without further significant testing and development to
understand and address the risks that are introduced. Thus, it is not recommended that the acidification
option be pursued. There are alternate additional processes, such as immobilization or chemical oxidation,6,8
that may hold more promise for treatment of the organic compounds than acidification for compounds for
which high pH vacuum evaporation is not effective.

5.0 Conclusions
Two vacuum evaporation tests were performed for simulated Hanford tank waste without pH adjustments.
The following are the key observations:
 • Nitrobenzene showed good removal in duplicate tests. The tests had an incomplete mass balance
 for nitrobenzene due to difficulty quantifying the nitrobenzene that moved beyond the initial
 condenser.
 • 2,4,6-Trichlorophenol and p-nitrophenol removal was not expected and test results confirmed no
 apparent removal.
 • Phenol and o-cresol removal was not expected, and did not appear to occur. However, evaporation
 test results could not be confirmed because of HPLC preparation complications.
 • Pyridine was not quantifiable in this matrix due to poor resolution by HPLC. The 1H-NMR analysis
 confirmed removal of pyridine from the evaporator pot.

Two vacuum evaporation tests were performed for simulated Hanford tank wastes adjusted to a pH of
approximately 4 through the addition of sulfuric acid. The following are the key observations:
 • Nitrobenzene removal appeared complete based on the intermediate and ending pot sample
 analysis.
 • 2,4,6-Trichlorophenol was not present at detectable levels in the intermediate and final evaporated
 low pH simulant. However, the amount of 2,4,6-trichlorophenol measured in the condensate and
 KOP was less than expected to close a mass balance. Acidification improved the removal of
 2,4,6-trichlorophenol.

 25

 • p-Nitrophenol exhibited no apparent removal. This is consistent with its relatively high Kh ratio of
 362 compared to that of N-nitrosomorpholine (Kh ratio of 182).
 • Phenol and o-cresol experienced nitration. There is evidence of partial evaporation of both phenol
 and o-cresol. However, larger fractions of the initial phenol and o-cresol likely underwent nitration
 reactions.
 • Pyridine was not quantifiable in this matrix due to poor resolution by HPLC. The 1H-NMR analysis
 confirmed removal of pyridine from the evaporator pot. This was unexpected because pyridine was
 expected to be pyridinium and not be removed at pH near 4.

Several observations made during acid addition for pH adjustment and subsequent evaporation testing that
indicate the risk of difficulties in such a process:
 • Solids are precipitated, which are likely gibbsite. The evaporator and subsequent processes would
 need to handle a dilute slurry rather than a liquid.
 • Gases are evolved during pH adjustment, including NOx compounds and likely including CO2. The
 NOx may require treatment and/or permitting.
 • Phenol and o-cresol, and potentially other phenolic compounds, will likely undergo partial or
 complete nitration during acidification, which may in turn result in persistent nitrated LDR organics
 in the acidified product.
 • Foaming was encountered during vacuum evaporation of the low pH material that makes control
 of the process more difficult.

6.0 Recommendations
The following are recommendations resulting from this work and previous evaporation work:

Vacuum evaporation remains a viable option to treat some of the LDR organic compounds, primarily those
with relatively high volatility and those that do not deprotonate in the high pH waste condition. Additional
testing should be pursued as necessary to aid in evaporator selection and flowsheet design, or to close data
gaps that may be identified.

With the observations made during this testing, it is not recommended that the acidification option be
pursued. Potential adjustments to the acidification process, such as the acid used and the pH endpoint, may
improve some difficulties, but is unlikely to mitigate all of the risks that acidification introduces to
processing.
