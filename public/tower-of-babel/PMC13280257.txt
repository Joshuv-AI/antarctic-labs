ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic peripheral nerve injuries can result in severe disability, due to varying degrees of neuropathic pain, sensory deficits, motor impairment, or a combination of these symptoms, often with a profound impact on quality of life.Accurate clinical evaluation and appropriate diagnostic investigations are essential for the timely identification of such injuries and for determining the most suitable treatment strategy.The surgical management of peripheral nerve injuries encompasses a range of surgical techniques, which may be used individually or in combination, with the goal of restoring neurological function and improving patient outcomes. Introduction

Traumatic peripheral nerve injuries (PNI) occur in approximately 3-10% of patients with polytrauma, predominantly affecting young adult males (Noble et al., 1998; Martins et al., 2013; Robinson, 2000a).

The mechanisms of injury are primarily mechanical-related, including laceration, compression, and stretching. Depending on the mechanism and severity, spontaneous recovery may be possible, due to the intrinsic regenerative capacity of peripheral nerves (PN)—provided the proximal segment remains connected to the central nervous system (CNS) (Martins et al., 2013; Socolovsky et al., 2018; Robla-Costales et al., 2022).

Indications for surgical intervention depend on multiple factors, including the level, severity, and extent of the injury; the time elapsed since trauma; the presence of associated non-neural injuries; the features and intensity of neuropathic pain; and clinical examination findings. In recent years, radiological investigations— such as ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)— have become instrumental in identifying lesions that may warrant early surgical exploration (Martins et al., 2013; Robla-Costales et al., 2022; Wijntjes et al., 2020; Padua et al., 2013; Elshewi et al., 2023).

Core principles for understanding and managing peripheral nerve trauma

Peripheral nerve microanatomy and physiology

Understanding the microstructural organization of PN is essential for interpreting the mechanisms of nerve injury, whether due to acute trauma, chronic compression, or tumor-related processes. In the context of traumatic lesions, this knowledge is critical for accurately assessing injury severity, guiding classification, and determining the most appropriate conservative or surgical management strategy.

PN are composed of three main structural components: 1) Connective tissue (CT), 2) Nervous tissue (NT), and 3) Neural support tissue (NST). Each component plays a distinct and complementary role in maintaining the functional integrity and regeneration capacity (Robla-Costales et al., 2022; Menorca et al., 2013; Myers, 1991; Rigoard et al., 2009).

Connective tissue

PN are structurally supported by four concentric layers of CT, organized from the inside out: 1) Endoneurium, surrounds each individual axon and Schwann cell (SC) in unmyelinated fibers, and the axon/myelin unit in myelinated fibers; 2) Perineurium, encases a fascicle, which are bundles of axons, each individually enveloped by a concentric myelin sheath; 3) Epineurium, which surrounds the entire nerve trunk and; 4) Mesoneurium, the outermost layer composed of loose areolar tissue, located between the peripheral nerve and surrounding tissues.

Overall, the first three CT layers serve as a structural scaffold, providing tensile strength, elasticity, nourishment, metabolic support, and maintenance of the internal microenvironment. In addition, tight junctions within the perineurial cells and endoneurial capillaries establish the blood-nerve barrier, which regulates the diffusion of substances into the nerve.

The mesoneurium facilitates nerve gliding during limb movement, protecting the nerve from stretch-related mechanical stress. Embedded within the perineurium and endoneurium is a repetitive architectural feature known as the spiral bands of Fontana—transverse and oblique fibers that allow the nerve to elongate and recoil safely under tension.

Furthermore, the CT/NT ratio varies along the nerve's course. In regions more susceptible to mechanical stress—such as superficial locations near joints or under traction— the number of fascicles increases and the CT layers become thicker, offering additional mechanical protection against compression and stretch.

PN are also supported by a robust vascular network, comprising external and internal vascular systems, with extensive anastomoses across all CT layers and prominent endoneurial capillaries. These features provide relative resistance to ischemia, allowing PN to tolerate longer periods of compromised perfusion compared to the brain or spinal cord (Rigoard et al., 2009; Topp and Boyd, 2012; Pavelka and Roth, 2010; Peltonen et al., 2013; Sunderland, 1965).

Nervous and neural support tissues

The other two essential components of PN microstructure—nervous tissue (NT) and neural support tissue (NST)— are intimately involved in the conduction of nerve impulses.

The axons, which constitute the NT component, are long cytoplasmic extensions of neuronal cell bodies that transmit efferent and afferent signals between the PN and the CNS.

They are enveloped by the myelin sheath, a key element of the NST, which is produced by individual SC in the peripheral nervous system.

The myelin sheath serves to electrically insulate axons from one another and to enhance the speed and efficiency of impulse conduction. This is achieved through the presence of regularly spaced nodes of Ranvier, which are unmyelinated gaps between adjacent SCs that facilitate saltatory conduction. Groups of myelinated and unmyelinated axons are bundled into fascicles surrounded by the perineurium, and constitute the basic functional unit of a peripheral nerve (Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2018) (see Fig. 1).

Microstructural arrangement of peripheral nerves.

Efferent (motor) signals are transmitted through motor units, which consist of an alpha motor neuron, its axon, and the innervated extrafusal muscle fibers. While afferent (sensory) input is conveyed through sensory receptors, their axons, and their cell bodies located in the dorsal root ganglia.

At rest, nerve fibers maintain a resting membrane potential, generated by a differential distribution of ions across the axolemma (portion of the plasma membrane surrounding the axon). Upon stimulation with a supramaximal electrical input, there is a rapid influx of positive ions, leading to membrane depolarization. This depolarization reduces the potential difference across the membrane, thereby triggering an action potential, which propagates the nerve impulse along the axon (Myers, 1991; Rigoard et al., 2009; Topp and Boyd, 2012; Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2018; Cravioto, 1965; Sunderland, 1990).

Understanding the pathophysiological basis and classification of traumatic nerve lesions

Following any traumatic injury—regardless of the etiology or mechanism—two physiological processes will occur, either independently or simultaneously: degeneration and regeneration.

Degenerative and regenerative process

Any disruption of axonal integrity triggers a cascade known as Wallerian degeneration (WD), involving the disintegration of the distal axonal segment. This degeneration can extend proximally to the nearest node of Ranvier (anterograde degeneration), further toward the neuronal soma (chromatolysis), and distally to the target organ. WD is characterized by the breakdown of the axonal cytoskeleton and degradation of the myelin sheath, typically beginning within 24 to 36 h post-injury and completing by approximately 21 days.

Importantly, before completion of WD, the affected axon may still exhibit electrical excitability. Therefore, neurophysiological evaluation (i.e., electromyography) should be deferred until the third to fourth week post-trauma to ensure accurate assessment of lesion severity.

The regenerative process is orchestrated by intact SCs and macrophages, which collaborate to remove cellular debris. SCs subsequently proliferate and align into longitudinal structures known as bands of Büngner, which express neurotrophic factors and adhesion molecules to guide regenerating axons. Meanwhile, the proximal axonal stump generates growth cones—sprouting extensions driven by intracellular signaling and attracted by factors secreted by SCs.

If the growth cone successfully enters the distal endoneurial tube, axonal regeneration proceeds at an average rate of approximately 1 mm per day, potentially leading to target organ reinnervation and functional recovery. However, if there is a disruption in continuity—such as a nerve gap or intervening scar tissue— regenerating axons may fail to reach the distal segment, resulting in persistent denervation. Over time, this leads to irreversible structural and functional changes in the denervated organ (Robinson, 2000a; Robla-Costales et al., 2022; Menorca et al., 2013; Sunderland, 1990; Burnett and Zager, 2004; Jacques and Kline, 2000). Nerve injury grading

The severity of PNI is typically classified using two widely accepted systems: 1) Seddon's classification and 2) Sunderland's classification. Both are based on the degree of damage sustained by the nerve fibers, CT and NST, and reflect the likelihood of spontaneous recovery and the need for surgical intervention (Kim et al., 2008; Seddon, 1943; Sunderland, 1978) . Fig. 3.

Grading of nerves injuries.

Seddon's system defines three degrees of nerve injury: 1) Neurapraxia (Sunderland's grade I), which is the mildest form of nerve injury and results from segmental demyelination at the injury site without axonal disruption or WD. It causes a transient conduction block, and spontaneous recovery is expected within weeks to two months, as remyelination by intact SCs restores function. Clinical recovery is usually complete. 2) Axonotmesis (Sunderland's grade II) refers to axonal and myelin disruption with preserved CT sheaths. This is a moderate form of nerve injury, characterized by the development of WD, but the intact CT scaffold permits axonal regeneration toward the distal target. Spontaneous recovery can be anticipated but is delayed, often requiring 4 to 6 months or longer. 3) Neurotmesis (Sunderland's grade V) represents complete transection of the nerve, including all structural components—axons, myelin and CT—. This is the maximum degree of injury. Although the proximal stump may generate axonal sprouts, the gap and absence of a guiding structure prevent reinnervation of the target. Thus, spontaneous recovery is not possible, and microsurgical repair is required.

Sunderland expanded Seddon's model to include five grades of nerve injury, offering greater anatomical detail and prognostic accuracy: 1) Grade III involves disruption of axons, myelin and endoneurium, and 2) Grade IV, include additional disruption of the perineurium.

In both cases, regenerating axons may become misdirected, leading to inefficient reinnervation and poor functional recovery. Prognosis is unpredictable and surgery may be required. This establishes the anatomical basis for a commonly observed pathological condition known as neuroma in continuity, which occurs in closed injuries.

Key elements in the clinical evaluation of peripheral nerve injuries

Clinical evaluation

Gathering history and conducting a physical examination require a careful and systematic approach, typically guided by the patient's complaints. Current symptoms, their onset, and the mechanism of injury (e.g., closed trauma, open trauma, etc.) must be assessed, along with their distribution, frequency, progression, associated symptoms, aggravating and alleviating factors, any previous treatments and medications, past medical history, family history, and handedness. If pain is present, its features, location, and radiation are crucial for determining whether it is neuropathic pain linked to peripheral nerve disease.

Similarly, the physical examination should be guided by the data gathered during history taking. It can be categorized into a global examination and a focused examination, with an emphasis on specific tests and assessments (e.g., provocative maneuvers). The components to include are: 1) inspection, 2) palpation, 3) assessment of tone, 4) range of passive movements, 5) muscle strength reflexes (when applicable), 6) range of active movements and evaluation of muscle strength, 7) sensory examination, and 8) provocative tests (when applicable) and Tinel's sign.

Regardless of gender, culture, or religious beliefs, it is essential to fully expose the affected area before the physical exam, comparing both sides, starting with the unaffected one. Consequently, understanding the innervation and function of each nerve is crucial. See Tables 1A and 1B.

During inspection, the examiner should look for a range of clinical indicators suggestive of PNI. These include visible signs of trauma (e.g., scars, open wounds, or ecchymosis), muscle atrophy, abnormal postures such as foot drop, claw hand, or the waiter's tip position. The presence of atypical signs—including Horner's syndrome or Froment's sign—should also be noted, as well as structural deformities like ankylosis or joint misalignment. Further attention should be paid to asymmetry between limbs, visible masses along the course of the nerve, and subtle signs of autonomic dysfunction, such as changes in skin moisture, hair distribution, or skin color. Palpation of the affected areas should be performed to detect tenderness, focal swelling, or previously unnoticed masses along the anatomical course of the nerve. Additionally, assessment of pulses, temperature, and local swelling is essential to rule out vascular compromise, particularly in traumatic injuries.

During tone and reflexes evaluation, signs of lower motor neuron dysfunction associated with PNI include weakness, atrophy, hyporeflexia, and reduced muscle tone.

Before proceeding with active movement and strength testing, a careful assessment of the passive range of motion should be performed to exclude musculoskeletal injuries (e.g., joint dislocation, fractures, rotator cuff injuries, etc.), which can mimic nerve injuries. Painful or restricted passive joint motion may also be associated with compensatory movement patterns or suboptimal physical therapy, leading to mechanical limitations.

Active range of motion and muscle strength are then assessed systematically, ideally using the Medical Research Council (MRC) grading system to evaluate weakness. Testing should include all muscles innervated by the suspected nerve or corresponding spinal myotome, depending on the level of injury (see Table 1).

The sensory examination evaluates superficial (light touch, pain, temperature) and deep sensations (vibration, two-point discrimination and proprioception). Sensory function should be compared to the contralateral, unaffected side, and mapping should follow dermatomal or peripheral nerve territories, depending on the clinical suspicion. Fig. 5.

Dermatomes distribution.

Certain clinical findings may assist in localizing the level of injury within the nervous system: 1) the nervous system becomes more specific as it progresses distally; 2) radiculopathy typically displays diffuse sensory changes, with less pronounced weakness or atrophy; 3) peripheral neuropathies, in contrast, show a localized sensory impairment and are often associated with distinct weakness and atrophy in the affected territory.

Diagnostic studies

While the diagnosis of peripheral neuropathy is primarily clinical, complementary diagnostic tools—such as magnetic resonance imaging, ultrasound, and electrodiagnostic studies— are essential to confirm the diagnosis, identify the site of injury and guide management decisions (Socolovsky et al., 2018; Russell, 2015; John et al., 2022a, 2022b; Campbell, 2008; Kim et al., 2013; O'Brien, 2010). Decision-making in nerve surgery: indications and timing

Following a PNI, the resulting functional impairment manifests through varying degrees of three key symptoms: 1) neuropathic pain, 2) sensory disturbances, and 3) weakness. As previously noted, indications for surgical management, as well as the optimal timing, depend on several critical factors, including: mechanism of injury (e.g., stretching, laceration, etc.), severity and classification of the injury (e.g., neurapraxia, neurotmesis, etc.), the nature of the injury (e.g., open or closed), anatomical level (e.g., brachial plexus avulsions or distal injuries of terminal branches), elapsed time since injury (e.g., more or less than six months), presence of intractable neuropathic pain, and patient-specific factors (e.g., age, associated injuries, etc.), among others.

Initial clinical decision: surgery vs non-operative management

The first step in evaluating a traumatic PNI is to determine whether spontaneous recovery is likely or whether surgical intervention will be required. Evidence shows that up to 90% of nerves that will recover do so within the first 4 months post-injury. Depending on the degree of injury, spontaneous recovery is commonly expected in Sunderland Grades I and II, and in some cases of Grade III.

In contrast, Grades IV and V, and certain Grade III injuries, have a low likelihood of spontaneous recovery, making surgical exploration essential.

One common approach to determine the ideal surgical timing is based on the “Rule of three plus one”. This clinical guide stratifies timing based on injury type (see Fig. 7), and the principles are as follows:

Rule of three.

1) Immediate surgery (within 3 days), indicated for clean, sharp open injuries with minimal soft tissue damage, allowing for direct, tension-free neurorrhaphy under optimal conditions; 2) Early surgery (within 3 weeks), reserved for blunt or contused open injuries with irregular edges. These often have undefined zones of injury and elevated inflammation.

When identified during acute exploration, these injuries can be challenging, as premature reconstruction risks incorporating damaged tissue. Therefore, the nerve stumps should be tagged, and definitive repair delayed; 3) Delayed surgery (after 3 months), pertains to closed injuries with in-continuity lesions (e.g., neuroma formation) where spontaneous functional recovery is not observed. Surgical exploration is warranted if no clinical or electrodiagnostic improvement occurs by this point and; 4) Chronic/late repair, applied to long-standing or previously untreated injuries, as well as failed primary repairs. In these cases, secondary procedures such as tendon transfers, free muscle transfers, or skeletal realignment may be required to restore function.

In summary, open injuries universally warrant urgent surgical intervention, whereas closed injuries are initially managed conservatively, with surgery reserved for lack of recovery within the expected timeframe. The best functional outcomes are consistently achieved when surgery is performed within the first 6 months after trauma, before irreversible changes develop in the target organ (muscle).

Certain situations require modified timing strategies: 1) If surgery is already planned for associated injuries (e.g., vascular reconstruction, osteosynthesis), concurrent nerve exploration is recommended, as it may influence the treatment course. 2) Gunshot wounds, although considered penetrating trauma, are managed like closed injuries because complete transections are rare. Many exhibits partial injury with potential for recovery, warranting initial observation.

In summary, for PNI that do not require immediate surgical repair, a structured follow-up protocol is essential. This includes: a comprehensive history and physical examination, serial clinical, neurophysiological and imaging assessments. These tools aid in identifying recovery trends, detecting failure of regeneration, and guiding timely and appropriate surgical intervention (Martins et al., 2013; Bhandari, 2019; Jack et al., 2022; Grinsell and Keating, 2014; Griffin et al., 2014; Dubuisson and Kline, 1992; Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2016).

Guiding surgical decision-making in neuromas in continuity: the role of multimodal intraoperative monitoring

Multimodal intraoperative monitoring (MIOM) refers to the use of continuous or evoked neurophysiological techniques to assess and preserve the functional integrity of neural structures. These modalities are invaluable in differentiating viable from non-viable nerve tissue, guiding decisions in microsurgical reconstruction, protecting functional fascicles during nerve decompression or tumor resection, and assisting in the selection of non-functional fascicles during diagnostic biopsies. Among these applications, MIOM plays a particularly critical role when addressing neuromas in continuity, where intraoperative evaluation of conduction across the lesion can determine the appropriate surgical strategy and prevent unnecessary sacrifice of functional fibers. (Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2018; Cravioto, 1965).

Nerve action potential

Nerve action potential (NAP) is a neurophysiological intraoperative technique used to assess axonal conduction across an injured segment of nerve, particularly in cases of neuroma in continuity. This method involves proximal stimulation of the nerve trunk or specific fascicles, with simultaneous distal recording to evaluate whether electrical conduction persist through the site of injury.

NAP represents the summated electrical response of all conducting fibers within the nerve and requires a supramaximal stimulus and a minimum of 3000 to 4000 axons to elicit a measurable response. Technically, stimulation is performed using bipolar hook electrodes, and recording is achieved with tripolar hook electrodes, which help to isolate the nerve from surrounding tissues and fluids, thereby reducing artifacts and avoiding current dispersion.

Electrode placement is essential for accurate results: interelectrode spacing should be at least 3 mm for small nerves and 5-7 mm for larger nerves. Additionally, the distance between stimulating and recording sites must exceed 4 cm to prevent contamination by stimulus artifact. These technical requirements can make NAP assessment challenging in proximal injuries, where available nerve length may be limited.

Functionally, NAP monitoring provides insight into the motor pathway and is especially useful in assessing neuromas in continuity, frequently encountered in closed traumatic injuries. The presence of a reproducible NAP suggests preserved conduction and supports nerve preservation, while its absence may indicate the need for internal neurolysis or resection and grafting. Consequently, when appropriately applied, NAP plays a critical role in intraoperative decision-making (Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2018; Rodríguez-Aceves et al., 2016; Zelenski et al., 2023; Rodríguez-Aceves et al.; Oberle et al., 1997). Surgical management of nerve injuries: surgical techniques to restore nerve function (primary surgery)

The surgical strategies for managing PNI are generally categorized into two main groups: 1) Techniques aimed at restoring nerve function (primary surgery) and 2) Techniques aimed at compensating for loss of function (secondary or palliative surgery). The selection between these groups depends primarily on the time elapsed since the injury and the likelihood of functional reinnervation.

The first group comprises four main surgical techniques, each addressing specific microsurgical challenges and following strict anatomical and physiological principles (Dvali and Mckinnon, 2007; Dahlin, 2008a; Isaacs, 2010; Dahlin, 2008b; Robinson, 2000b; Harris and Tindall, 1991; Dvali and Mackinnon; Spinner and Kline, 2000) (see Fig. 9):

1)Neurolysis refers to the circumferential (360°) surgical release of an injured nerve from surrounding scar tissue, fibrosis, or compressive structures. Because compression is a well-recognized factor that impairs axonal regeneration and normal nerve conduction, neurolysis is primarily indicated in closed injuries presenting with neuropathic pain and/or functional sensorimotor deficits. The procedure may be performed as external neurolysis, involving dissection around the epineurium, or as internal neurolysis, involving dissection within the nerve, and between fascicles when external release is insufficient. Technically, surgical dissection should begin over a zone of normal anatomy, and progresses toward the injury site to minimize the iatrogenic trauma and ensure safer exposure. Extensive devascularization should be avoided to minimize the risk of postoperative complications and preserve nerve viability. See Fig. 10.

2)Direct neurorrhaphy involves the precise anatomical alignment of the proximal and distal nerve stumps to restore continuity of the nerve trunk. This technique offers the most efficient route for regenerating axons to reach the distal stump and is therefore indicated in nerve lacerations or short-gap defects following neuroma resection. The following technical considerations are necessary: proper debridement of nerve ends to ensure coaptation between viable tissues; correct fascicular alignment, especially in mixed nerves to align motor and sensory fascicles appropriately; tension-free coaptation, as mechanical tension compromises both vascular perfusion and axonal regrowth. Suturing is performed using non-absorbable monofilament microsutures (i.e., 8-0 to 10-0) with atraumatic needles, under microscopic magnification. Coaptation may be either epineural or perineurial, with no significant differences in clinical outcomes. Fibrin glue may be used to reinforce the suture line. See Fig. 11

3)Nerve grafting is employed when direct, tension-free coaptation of nerve stumps is not feasible, typically due to segmental loss or resection of non-viable tissue. This technique involves the interposition of bridging materials to restore anatomical continuity and enable axonal regeneration. Three types of grafting materials are currently available: autografts (nerve harvested from the same individual), allografts (cadaveric nerve, usually processed to reduce antigenicity), and nerve conduits (biological or synthetic). Among these, autografts remain the gold standard due to their histocompatibility, ability to provide an optimal microenvironment (physical, chemical and biological), and lack of need for immunosuppression. The sural nerve is the most commonly used donor, offering adequate length, caliber, and easy harvesting with minimal donor-site morbidity. In addition to the principles described for other microsurgical repairs, maintaining a clean and well-vascularized surgical bed is essential for successful reinnervation. An extra graft length of 15-20% is recommended to compensate for postoperative shrinkage and to avoid tension. A key limitation of grafting is graft length: in defects exceeding 8-10 cm, outcomes may be suboptimal due to delayed or incomplete reinnervation, caused by limited revascularization, axonal dispersion, and the prolonged time required for axons to reach the target organ. Additional considerations

In all surgical techniques, a meticulous microsurgical approach is essential, involving the use of optical magnification and dedicated microsurgical instruments throughout all stages of nerve reconstruction. Proper hemostasis must be achieved using gentle compression, hemostatic agents, or precise bipolar coagulation, in order to avoid additional trauma to the nerve trunk and surrounding tissues.

To preserve nerve excitability intraoperatively, total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) is preferred, and short-acting muscle relaxants should be used judiciously.

The application of tourniquets is generally discouraged, except in select cases where their benefit clearly outweighs the risks.

Following surgery, when any type of nerve coaptation or anastomosis is performed, it is recommended to maintain the operated limb immobilized for 7 to 10 days to reduce the risk of postoperative suture line dehiscence or rupture. Additionally, the success of nerve repair is closely tied to a structured and personalized rehabilitation program, including physical and occupational therapy, which should be initiated as soon as clinically appropriate.

Outcomes and prognostic factors

Functional recovery following surgical reconstruction of traumatic peripheral nerve injuries remains highly variable and depends on multiple injury-, patient-, and treatment-related factors (Bhandari, 2019; Jack et al., 2022; Grinsell and Keating, 2014; Griffin et al., 2014). Among these, the timing of surgical intervention is one of the most important determinants of outcome.

Earlier reconstruction generally yields superior results, particularly when reinnervation occurs before irreversible degeneration of motor endplates and target muscles.

The mechanism, severity, and anatomical level of injury also significantly influence prognosis. Distal injuries tend to demonstrate better outcomes than proximal lesions because of the shorter distance required for axonal regeneration (Grinsell and Keating, 2014; Griffin et al., 2014). Likewise, clean lacerations repaired primarily usually achieve superior functional recovery compared with crush, avulsion, or traction injuries, which are frequently associated with extensive intraneural damage and scar formation.

Patient-related variables such as age, comorbidities, smoking status, and adherence to postoperative rehabilitation further affect recovery. Younger patients generally exhibit greater regenerative capacity and cortical plasticity than older individuals.

Among reconstructive procedures, direct nerve repair remains the gold standard whenever tension-free coaptation can be achieved. Nerve grafting can provide meaningful recovery in segmental defects; however, outcomes progressively decline with increasing graft length, particularly in defects exceeding 8-10 cm (Dvali and Mckinnon, 2007; Dahlin, 2008a; Isaacs, 2010; Dahlin, 2008b; Robinson, 2000b; Harris and Tindall, 1991; Dvali and Mackinnon; Ray and Mackinnon, 2009). In contrast, nerve transfers have emerged as an effective strategy for proximal injuries and brachial plexus lesions by shortening the distance to the target organ and reducing denervation time.

Systematic reviews of nerve transfer procedures have reported favorable functional outcomes, particularly in upper brachial plexus injuries. For example, Oberlin-type transfers frequently achieve MRC grade M3 or greater elbow flexion recovery in more than 80% of patients, with many series reporting M4 strength in a substantial proportion of cases (Fisher et al., 2024; Czarnecki et al., 2024). Similar success has been reported for spinal accessory-to-suprascapular nerve transfers for shoulder abduction and external rotation restoration (Czarnecki et al., 2024).

Despite advances in microsurgical techniques, outcomes remain less predictable in proximal injuries, delayed reconstructions, severe avulsion injuries, and elderly patients.

Consequently, careful patient selection, timely intervention, meticulous surgical technique, and structured rehabilitation remain essential to maximize functional recovery and long-term quality of life.

Contemporary advances and future directions

The field of peripheral nerve surgery has experienced substantial advances during the last decade, expanding reconstructive options beyond traditional neurorrhaphy, grafting, and conventional nerve transfers.

One of the most important developments has been the increasing use of processed nerve allografts for selected sensory and mixed nerve defects. These grafts eliminate donor-site morbidity and have demonstrated encouraging outcomes in short- and medium-length defects, although autologous nerve grafts remain the reference standard for extensive reconstructions (Ray and Mackinnon, 2009; Lin et al., 2013; Isaac et al., 2023). Similarly, biological and synthetic nerve conduits continue to evolve, with ongoing efforts focused on improving their mechanical properties, biocompatibility, and ability to support axonal regeneration through the incorporation of growth factors, extracellular matrix components, and bioactive scaffolds (Isaac et al., 2023; Lans et al., 2023; Stocco et al., 2023).

Nerve transfer surgery has also undergone significant refinement. Novel donor-recipient combinations, expanded indications, and improved understanding of cortical plasticity have increased the role of nerve transfers in brachial plexus reconstruction, complex PNI and even after spinal cord injuries (Rasulić et al., 2026; Moltaji et al., 2021). In addition, supercharged end-to-side (SETS) nerve transfers have emerged as a promising strategy to augment regeneration while preserving native nerve continuity in selected cases (Dunn et al., 2021; Abakhron et al., 2024). Another rapidly growing area is the management of neuroma-related pain and post-amputation syndromes. Techniques such as targeted muscle reinnervation (TMR) and regenerative peripheral nerve interfaces (RPNI) have demonstrated encouraging results in reducing neuroma formation and phantom limb pain while simultaneously improving prosthetic control in amputees (Yuan et al., 2024; Mauch et al., 2023).

Technological advances are also transforming diagnosis and surgical planning. High-resolution ultrasound and magnetic resonance neurography now allow increasingly precise visualization of nerve pathology, facilitating earlier diagnosis and improved operative decision-making (Chhabra et al., 2018; Baal et al., 2024; Le Corroller, 2024). Furthermore, artificial intelligence and machine-learning algorithms are being investigated to assist image interpretation, prognostic modeling, and outcome prediction (Qian et al., 2024; Buldu et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2023).

Future research is expected to focus on tissue engineering, stem-cell therapies, bioengineered nerve constructs, neurotrophic factor delivery systems, and advanced biomaterials designed to enhance axonal regeneration and functional recovery. Although many of these technologies remain investigational, they represent promising avenues that may further improve outcome following traumatic peripheral nerve injuries.

Conclusions

Traumatic injuries of peripheral nerves encompass a wide spectrum of clinical, radiologic, and electrophysiological manifestations, ranging from mild deficits to complete loss of sensorimotor function, frequently accompanied by varying degrees of neuropathic pain. These injuries often have profound social and economic implications for affected individuals.

Accurate diagnosis relies heavily on meticulous history-taking and comprehensive clinical examination, with particular attention to identifying lesions that may benefit from early surgical intervention—timing remains one of the most critical determinants of successful functional recovery.

Advancements in imaging modalities and electrodiagnostic techniques now offer valuable complementary data, enhancing the ability to stratify injury severity and tailor management strategies accordingly.

Currently, four primary surgical techniques are available to restore peripheral nerve function, each with specific indications, contraindications, technical nuances, and inherent limitations. When appropriately selected and expertly executed, these procedures can achieve meaningful functional recovery. Nonetheless, outcomes remain highly dependent on multiple variables, including injury type and severity, patient factors, timing of denervation, and the quality of postoperative care and rehabilitation.
