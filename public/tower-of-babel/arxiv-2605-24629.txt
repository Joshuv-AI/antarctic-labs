Abstract
This paper started as a review of seven results pertaining to a family of bilinear models with rank one NGM introduced by Fall, Iggidr, Sallet and Bonzi, which utilize the explicit eigenvectors of the NGM to compute the unique endemic equilibrium (EE), and Lyapunov functions at both the disease free equilibrium (DFE) and EE, and of results of Shuai and Van den Driessche (2013), which essentially deal with the same “DFE -EE stability exchange” in the non-rank one case, when the eigenvectors are not explicit. Recently, these results were complemented by Earn and McCluskey (2025), who proved as well a “strong threshold theorem”, namely that when the DFE is unstable, a second equilibrium which is globally asymptotically stable must exist. Below, we obtain in Theorem 3 some results that extend beyond rank one. For example, a nontrivial positive equilibrium exists if and only if the spectral equation ρ ​ ( K ~ ​ ( S ) ) = 1 \rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1 , admits a strictly positive solution. Also, we showed that Bonzi-Iggidr-Sallet bilinear models with rank one NGM may be classified in two classes, with slight variations in the eigenvector formulas, and that extensions in the presence of feedback from infectious to susceptible are possible. Another takeout from the previous works, which we clarify in a revisit of the seven results in the rank one case, is that Lyapunov functions for both the DFE and the EE may be constructed using as weights the left Perron eigenvector π ⁡ ( S ) \pi(S) of the next generation matrix (NGM) K = F ⁡ ( S ) ​ V − 1 K=F(S)V^{-1} , where S S denote all the non-infectious variables, and when a positive ODE leaves a siphon face, it does so along the right Perron eigenvector w ⁡ ( S ) w(S) of K ~ = V − 1 ​ F ​ ( S ) \widetilde{K}=V^{-1}F(S) . The question of whether this continues to be true beyond rank one is explored in ongoing work.

1
Introduction

This paper started as a review of several recent results in
mathematical epidemiology (ME). One is an elegant proof of the celebrated next generation matrix (NGM) method, which was shown recently
[
11
,
10
]

to be an outcome of the fact that the siphon property used in chemical reaction networks (CRN) forces the Jacobian at any “reasonable” boundary equilibrium to have a triangular block form. If in addition the transversal-block is a Metzler matrix, then stability is amenable to next-generation analysis (which is analog to the
regular splitting
theory
[
44
]
in the numerical analysis literature).

Another result, due to Earn and McCluskey
[
20
]
, is that a general class of bilinear ME models, first featured in papers like
[
28
,
21
,
14
]
, have an explicit Lyapunov function which guarantees that the unique strictly positive “endemic equilibrium” (EE) is globally asymptotically stable (GAS).

Note also a determinant law
[
12
,
5
]
, which states that the sum of the determinants of the Jacobians at the DFE and EE equals 0, and had been proved for a SAIRS model only (but conjectured to hold more widely, together with other explicit formulas/laws concerning both the EE and the DFE, which hold for certain rank one models, and go back to
[
28
,
21
,
14
]
).

While writing the review, we completed it with various remarks not stated explicitly in our sources, like the fact that there are two classes of
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models, and while five of the “laws” hold for both of them, sometimes with small variations, the determinant law and the Lyapunov function at the endemic point hold only when there is just one susceptible class, in which the two classes of
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models coincide.

1.1
Positive dynamical systems

A dynamical system is said to be positive
if it leaves invariant the positive orthant
[
38
]
.

The biological interaction networks (BIN) sciences, which include
population dynamics,
ecology,
mathematical epidemiology (ME),
viro-immunology,
chemical reaction network(CRN), etc,
are all concerned with positive dynamical systems, and
share common objects of study, such as multi-stationarity, local and global stability, bifurcations, persistence, permanence, extinction, global stability and the construction of Lyapunov functions. For interesting historical references on BIN, as well as recent results on persistence and global stability, the reader is referred to the CRN papers
[
17
,
23
,
48
,
47
,
18
,
16
,
45
]
.

While studying quite similar problems, the BIN fields put emphasis on distinct aspects.

The ME literature focuses often on non-polynomial models, on the effect of delays, and that of replacing ODEs by IDEs and PIDEs, which fit more realistically epidemiological data.

The CRN literature focuses a lot on “structural results”, which are independent of the ODE rates, as long as these are smooth and monotone; it uses often graphs whose edges are labeled by the rates, where the exact form of the rates may be unknown.

There are very few results which are well-known and used equally in both fields (one exception being the works of Li and Muldowney
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
), and our hope is to bring the two fields closer to each other.

Below, we review and advance a chapter of the ME literature, which could be called the “laws of the DFE to EE stability relay”, but also use on purpose occasionally the language of CRNT. While the theoretical results here do not rely on CRNT,
we also offer two example notebooks, using our Mathematica package Epid-CRN,
available at
https://github.com/florinav/EpidCRNmodels
, and readers willing to take advantage
of a “hands on approach” will need some rudiments of CRNT.

1.2
The (RHS,var), and the (RN,rts,var) mass-action representation of positive ODEs

For parameterizing ME or CRN ODE models,
there are three natural choices:

1.

The parametrization used traditionally for all ODE models is
X^{\prime}=RHS(X)
. A model is thus a pair (RHS, var=X), where RHS denotes the set of formulas for the derivatives, and var are the variables whose rates of change we study.

2.

Chemical reaction networks theory starts by breaking the RHS as

X^{\prime}=RHS(X)=\Gamma.{\bf R}(X).

(1)

A model is now defined as a triple
(\Gamma,{\bf R}(X),X)
. The RHS has thus been broken into two parts:

(a)

\Gamma
is the “stoichiometric matrix” (SM), whose columns represent directions in which several species/compartments change simultaneously;
it is also called the “structure” of the model.

(b)

{\bf R}(X)
is the vector of rates of change associated to each direction (assumed all to be non-negative), also known as kinetics. They are separated from
\Gamma
, since they are less certain; this fact, well accepted in CRNT, is equally true in ME.

Example 1

(stoichiometric matrix of SIRS)
.

For example, the simplest
SIRS ODE without inflows and outflows (=demography) is defined by the triple
(\Gamma,{\bf R},X)
:

X^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}s^{\prime}\\
i^{\prime}\\
r^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0&1&-1\\
1&-1&0&0\\
0&1&-1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta si\\
\gamma_{i}i\\
\gamma_{r}r\\
\gamma_{s}s\end{pmatrix}:=\Gamma{\bf R}(X).

(2)

Note this representation is also a natural first step towards defining an associated CTMC model (continuous-time Markov chain) on the integers.

We formalize the above as follows.

Definition 1

(Stoichiometric and chemical representation)
.

A stoichiometric representation of
f
is a pair
(\Gamma,r)
such that

f(x)=\Gamma r(x),\qquad r(x)\geq 0,

where
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n_{R}}
is constant and
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{R}}_{\geq 0}
is locally Lipschitz.

It is called chemical if

\Gamma_{i\rho}<0\implies r_{\rho}(x)=x_{i}\tilde{r}_{\rho}(x),\qquad\tilde{r}_{\rho}(x)\geq 0,

and, moreover,
r_{\rho}
depends only on variables in
\operatorname{supp}(r_{\rho})\subseteq\Gamma^{-}_{i}
whenever
\Gamma_{i\rho}<0
.
Furthermore, we assume that each rate
r_{\rho}(x)
is monotone non-decreasing with respect to each variable
x_{k}
for
k\in\operatorname{supp}(r_{\rho})
.

Definition 2

(Identification of consumption and production supports)
.

Let
f
be chemical in the sense of Definition
1
. For each
i
, the sets

\displaystyle\Gamma^{-}_{i}:=\{\,j:f_{i}^{-}(x)\ \text{depends nontrivially on }x_{j}\,\}=\bigcup_{\rho:\,\Gamma_{i\rho}<0}\operatorname{supp}(r_{\rho}),

\displaystyle\Gamma^{+}_{i}:=\{\,j:f_{i}^{+}(x)\ \text{depends nontrivially on }x_{j}\,\}=\bigcup_{\rho:\,\Gamma_{i\rho}>0}\operatorname{supp}(r_{\rho})

are called consumption and production supports.

Assumption 1

(stoichiometric representability)
.

All systems considered in this paper are assumed to
admit a chemical stoichiometric representation (CSR) as above.

3.

We introduce the third “sources-products” parametrization, the one most used in CRNT, by an example. For SIRS, it is:

\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\text{RN}&\text{rts}\\
\hline\cr\text{i}+\text{s}\to 2\text{i}&\beta\,is\\
\text{i}\to\text{r}&\gamma_{i}\,i\\
\text{r}\to\text{s}&\gamma_{r}\,r\\
\text{s}\to\text{r}&\gamma_{s}\,s\\
\end{array}\right)

(3)

Here each column of
\Gamma
is replaced by a “reaction” (or “interaction”. For example, the second column in (
2
), representing a transfer from the row of
i
towards that of
r
, is replaced
by
i->r
.

The
s+i\to 2i
in the first row is the CRNT “mass-action representation” (not reviewed here). Note that
this provides a quite natural phenomenological representation of infections at the individual level: the meeting of a susceptible and an infectious results in two infectious.

Remark 1
.

Note that the (RN,rts) representation of an ODE is not unique. These representations (any of them) have however the advantage of being the only ones which take into account the positivity of an ODE (for polynomial ODEs, positivity is equivalent to the existence of a mass-action representation).

1.3
Some interactions between CRN and epidemiology

CRNT has focused considerably on graph structures associated with reactions networks, and on simplifications available when these graphs are (weakly) reversible.
On the other hand, the most encountered infection reaction in epidemiology,
``s"+``i"\to 2``i"
is irreversible, which explains maybe why
classic CRN and ME
have not interacted much in the past (but
SI, SIS, SIRS models, appear sometimes in the CRN literature for the purpose of illustrating various results about autocatalytic models – see for example
[
39
,
1
]
).

However, the key fact that Epidemiologic Strains, Minimal Self-Replicable Siphons, and Autocatalytic Cores are conceptually related seems to have passed unnoticed until

[
6
]
.

We believe that more information transfer between CRN and ME could be useful to both, as well as to the other positive system subfields.
Let us illustrate this by a few examples:

1.

The
positivity criterion
of
[
26
]
seems unfortunately unknown outside CRNT, and has been reproved in particular cases an uncountable number of times.

2.

Especially relevant is the notion of
siphons
. These are in one-to-one correspondence with forward invariant boundary faces, cf. Angeli et al.
[
3
]
,
and in fact this yields their simplest definition. However, for completeness, we add
(and complete for our purpose) the definition given in the CRN literature:

Definition 3
.

(siphon, minimal siphon, total/DFE siphon, critical siphon, and DFE closure/infection)

(a)

A subset
S
of species is a siphon/semi-locking set if every reaction that produces a species in S also consumes at least one species in
S
.

(b)

A siphon is said to be minimal if it does not properly contain any
other siphon.

(c)

The union of all minimal siphons will be called the total/DFE siphon.

(d)

A siphon is said to be critical if it does not contain the support of any
linear conservation (“P-flow”).

(e)

The set of all species which are automatically zero when all the species
in the DFE are zero will be called DFE-closure siphon, and any eventual fixed point on the corresponding face will be called DFE.

Siphons are also related to the existence and stability of fixed points on the boundary of the positive orthant.
Indeed, Angeli et al.
[
3
]
proved that if a boundary point is an
\omega
-
limit
point, then the species set
\Sigma
in which every species has zero concentration must be a siphon.

Proposition 1
.

[
3
, Prop 1]
,
[
2
, Thm 2.5]
,
[
29
, Lem 2.1]
,
[
22
, Prop. 3.1]
If an
\omega
-limit
w
of a strictly positive initial point belongs to a face

w\in L_{\Sigma}:=\{X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}:x_{i}=0,i\in\Sigma\},

then
\Sigma
is a siphon.

Remark 2
.

While the total/DFE siphon is clearly a siphon (since its corresponding face is the intersection of all minimal siphons, hence a siphon), the DFE-closure needs not be a priori a siphon. However, it is so in all epidemic models, because in epidemic models a DFE must exist, and be an
\omega
-limit of strictly positive initial points when
R_{0}<1
, where
R_{0}
is the basic reproduction number defined as the spectral radius of the next generation matrix(this siphon is also critical, since it is unstable when

R_{0}>1
, and hence criticality holds by a further result of
[
3
]
).

Our Mathematica package starts any analysis by inputting the model as a set of reactions, then determines the minimal siphons (using the Julia algorithm provided by Vincent Du
[
35
]
) as a first, easier step towards computing boundary fixed points residing on each siphon-face
[
10
]
.

3.

The remarkable NGM theorem of ME states that the stability of the disease free equilibrium (DFE)
fixed point of the system
is guaranteed by

R_{0}<1.

This result has fascinated ME researchers (a review
[
24
]
focusing solely on measles identified more than 10000 citations that employ
R_{0}
), due to its probabilistic interpretations, and also because of the significant simplification with respect to the Routh-Hurwitz conditions. Despite their importance,
R_{0}
and NGM seem to have never been mentioned in CRN theory, until
[
13
,
9
]
. Recently, we have shown that the natural condition for the NGM theorem to hold is that the boundary face under investigation, the DFE, is a siphon
[
11
,
10
]
.

2
Balanced bilinear models

The works of
[
21
,
28
,
14
]
, followed by
[
41
]
, building on the celebrated
[
43
]
, suggested that several connections between the stability of the DFE and that of the EE might exist. In retrospect, this connection is not surprising, since
the DFE and EE are often members of a “stability relay team” — one takes over the stability from the other –see
[
10
]
for further remarks in this direction. This connection translates into a list of seven laws concerning both the EE and the DFE for rank-one balanced bilinear models presented below, which use the same common building bricks.

Let us mention that some of these laws are continually being reproved in particular examples in the ME literature, without making reference to the common structure of
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models
[
28
,
21
,
14
]
, which shows the interest in the unification we propose.

The models we study here are of the form:

Definition 6

(balanced bilinear model)
.

A positive ODE system is called a

if, after a permutation of coordinates, it can be written as

\begin{cases}S^{\prime}=\varphi(S)-\diag(S)\,BI+CI,\;\varphi(S)=\Lambda+A_{S}S\\[5.69054pt]
I^{\prime}=P\,\diag(S)\,BI+AI,\end{cases}

(4)

where

S\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m},\qquad I\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n},

and:

1.

I\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
are
n
DFE closure/infection compartments.

2.

S\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
are
m
non-infection/susceptible compartments (the complement of
I
).

3.

C\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m\times n}
yields feedback from
I
to
S
.

4.

\varphi(S)
is the dynamics in the
S
compartments, in the absence of the
I
compartments. Below we always assume
\varphi(S)=\Lambda+A_{S}S,\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m},A_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m,m}
.

5.

A_{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m},A\in\mathbb{R}^{n,n}
are stable Metzler matrices modeling the intra flows between S and I compartments, respectively. The second is precisely the matrix appearing in the NGM formula
K=F(-A)^{-1}
.

6.

is the WAIFW (Who Acquires Infection From Whom) matrix, and

denotes the contact and infectivity rate of

F
from the NGM formula.

7.

I

Hypothesis 1

•

Any susceptible compartment is accessible from a susceptible compartment with inflow/recruitment.

•

Any infected-infectious compartment is accessible from at least one entry-point for infection.

2.2
Independence of the two rank–one assumptions:ExP.wl,ExB.wl

Recall that the infection dynamics in (
7
) may be written

I^{\prime}=F(S)I+AI,\qquad F(S):=P\,\diag(S)\,B,

with simplifying cases

\text{(P)}\quad P={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}),\qquad\text{(B)}\quad B={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{m}\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}.

We show now that the two assumptions are algebraically independent.

Lemma 1

(Rigidity of the factorization)
.

Let
F(S)=P\diag(S)B
.
Assume that every row of
B
and of
\widetilde{B}
is nonzero.
If two column-stochastic pairs
(P,B)
and
(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{B})

generate the same infection operator for all
S
,

P\diag(S)B=\widetilde{P}\diag(S)\widetilde{B}\qquad\forall\,S\in\mathbb{R}^{m},

then necessarily

P=\widetilde{P},\qquad B=\widetilde{B}.

Proof.

Write

P\diag(S)B=\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}\,p_{i}\,\vec{b}_{i},

where
p_{i}
is the
i
th column of
P
and
\vec{b}_{i}
the
i
th row of
B
.
The same expansion holds for
(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{B})
:

\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}\,p_{i}\,\vec{b}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}S_{i}\,\tilde{p}_{i}\,{\tilde{\vec{b}}_{i}}\qquad\forall\,S.

(8)

Since the coefficients
S_{i}
are independent, we obtain

p_{i}\,\vec{b}_{i}=\tilde{p}_{i}\,{\tilde{\vec{b}}_{i}}\qquad\text{for each }i.

Since
P
and
\widetilde{P}
are column-stochastic, each column satisfies

\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}p_{i}=1
and
\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}\tilde{p}_{i}=1
.
Because
\vec{b}_{i}\neq 0
and
\tilde{\vec{b}_{i}}\neq 0
, the outer products above have rank one, hence

p_{i}=\lambda_{i}\tilde{p}_{i}

for some scalar
\lambda_{i}>0
.
Applying both unit-sum conditions gives
\lambda_{i}=1
, hence
p_{i}=\tilde{p}_{i}
.
It then follows from
p_{i}(\vec{b}_{i}-{\tilde{\vec{b}}_{i}})=0
and
p_{i}\neq 0
that

\vec{b}_{i}={\tilde{\vec{b}}_{i}}
.
Hence
P=\widetilde{P}
and
B=\widetilde{B}
.
∎

This lemma shows that the infection dynamics uniquely determines the
column-stochastic pair
(P,B)
, so one cannot transform a model satisfying one of the
two assumptions into the other without changing the dynamics.

Theorem 1

(Existence of Case (P) without Case (B))
.

There exist systems of the form (
4
) with

P={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}})
but
B
not of rank one.

Proof.

Consider
m=n=2
and

{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\
2/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad P={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(1,1)=\begin{pmatrix}1/3&1/3\\
2/3&2/3\end{pmatrix}.

Let

B=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
3&4\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\det B=-2\neq 0,

so
\mathrm{rank}(B)=2
.
Hence the model satisfies Case (P) but not Case (B).

By Lemma
1
, no alternative rank-one matrix

\widetilde{B}
can reproduce the same infection operator

F(S)=P\diag(S)B
.
∎

Theorem 2

(Existence of Case (B) without Case (P))
.

There exist systems of the form (
4
) with
B
rank one but
with no representation satisfying
P={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}})
.

Proof.

Again take
m=n=2
and define

{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{m}=\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\
2/3\end{pmatrix},\qquad\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}=\begin{pmatrix}3&9\end{pmatrix},\qquad B={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{m}\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}=\begin{pmatrix}1&3\\
2&6\end{pmatrix}.

Thus
B
has rank one.

Now take

P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

This matrix is column-stochastic but its columns are not proportional,
so it cannot be written in the form
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}})
.

Suppose that the same infection operator could be written using

\widetilde{P}={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}})
.
Then since B is common to both representations;

P\diag(S)B=\widetilde{P}\diag(S)B\qquad\forall\,S.

By Lemma
1
this would imply
P=\widetilde{P}
, which is
impossible because the columns of
P
are not proportional.

Hence Case (B) does not imply Case (P).
∎

Conclusion
:
The two simplifying assumptions

P={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}(\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}),\qquad B={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{m}\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}

are independent structural properties of the bilinear epidemic model.
Neither can be deduced from the other without changing the infection
dynamics.

2.3
Perron-Frobenius strong threshold theorem for ABP models without feedback

The following result, motivated by
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models introduced in Section
2
, is independent of the dimension of
S
and does not rely on rank-one assumptions. Previous constructions of endemic equilibria in this spirit were formulated only for particular cases; see for example
[
14
,
28
,
41
]
, where explicit rank-one representations are used.

We show that such constructions may be replaced by a two-step procedure based on Perron–Frobenius theory. First, one determines
S
by solving the scalar equation

\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1,

where
\widetilde{K}(S)
is the next-generation operator associated with the infected subsystem. Second, one recovers the infected equilibrium direction from a positive left Perron eigenvector of
\widetilde{K}(S)
, and determines its amplitude through the susceptible subsystem.

This yields a constructive characterization of endemic equilibria under the sole assumption that the infected Jacobian block admits a regular splitting and is irreducible. While the argument extends to all epidemiological models satisfying these conditions, we formulate it here for
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models in order to relate it to subsequent results in the rank-one setting.

Theorem 3

(Perron-Frobenius strong threshold theorem for ABP models without feedback)
.

Fix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}
, and consider the linear equilibrium equation

0=AI+P\,\diag(S)\,BI,

(9)

where
A
is Metzler–Hurwitz and
P,B\geq 0
. Introduce:

\widetilde{K}(S):=\widetilde{G}\,\diag(S),\qquad\widetilde{G}:=B(-A)^{-1}P,

and assume that
\widetilde{G}
is irreducible. Then:

(i)

For the fixed
S
, Equation (
9
) admits a nonzero solution
I{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}>}0
if and only if

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)=1.

In particular,
I=0
is always a solution of (
9
). Moreover, it is the unique nonnegative solution if and only if
\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)<1
.

(ii)

If
\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1
and
S\gg 0
, then all nonzero nonnegative solutions of (
9
) form a one-dimensional positive ray:

I=k\,(-A)^{-1}P\,\diag(S)\,v(S),\qquad k>0,

where
v(S)\gg 0
is a Perron eigenvector of
\widetilde{K}(S)
, unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar.

(iii)

Let
S_{0}\gg 0
be the disease-free value, and set

R_{0}:=\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S_{0})\big).

If
R_{0}<1
, then for every
0\leq S\leq S_{0}
,

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)\leq R_{0}<1.

Hence, for every
0\leq S\leq S_{0}
, equation (
9
) admits only the trivial nonnegative solution
I=0
.

(iv)

If
R_{0}=1
, then
S_{0}
itself lies on the threshold hypersurface

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)=1.

(v)

If
R_{0}>1
, then there exists a unique equilibrium point on the ray
tS_{0}
,
t\geq 0
, with

\bar{S}:=\frac{1}{R_{0}}\,S_{0},

and moreover

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(\bar{S})\big)=1.

(vi)

More generally, if
S^{*}\gg 0
satisfies

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S^{*})\big)=1,

then the
I
-equation determines a unique positive ray

I=k\,I_{*}(S^{*}),\qquad k>0,

where

I_{*}(S^{*}):=(-A)^{-1}P\,\diag(S^{*})\,v(S^{*}).

Remark 6

(
\widetilde{K}
is cyclic permutation of the next-generation matrix)
.

At
S=S_{0}
, the operator
\widetilde{K}(S_{0})
is the cyclically permuted form of the
usual next-generation matrix

K(S_{0})=P\,\diag(S_{0})\,B(-A)^{-1}.

Indeed,

\widetilde{K}(S_{0})=B(-A)^{-1}P\,\diag(S_{0}),

and the matrices
K(S_{0})
and
\widetilde{K}(S_{0})
have the same nonzero spectrum. Hence

R_{0}=\rho\big(K(S_{0})\big)=\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S_{0})\big).

This is why Theorem
3
applies also with the standard next generation matrix replacing
\widetilde{K}
.

Remark 7

(Meaning of the threshold ray)
.

Fixing an
S^{*}\gg 0
such that

\begin{cases}0=AI+P\,\diag(S^{*})\,BI\\
\rho(\widetilde{K}(S^{*}))=1\end{cases}

like in the last subpoint does not determine a unique equilibrium vector
I
, but only a one-dimensional
positive ray

I=k\,I_{*}(S^{*}),\qquad k>0,

where
I_{*}(S^{*})
is fixed up to normalization.

In particular:

•

the
profile
of the infected state (i.e. the ratios between components of
I
)
is uniquely determined by the Perron eigenvector of
\widetilde{K}(S^{*})
;

•

the
magnitude
of
I
is not determined at the linear level, and remains
free up to a positive scalar factor.

Thus, at threshold, the system selects a unique infection pattern but not its amplitude.
The latter is fixed only when coupling with the nonlinear
S
-equation.

Proof.

Since
A
is Hurwitz, it is invertible, and since
A
is Metzler, one has

(-A)^{-1}\geq 0.

Thus (
9
) is equivalent to

I=(-A)^{-1}P\,\diag(S)\,BI.

(10)

Applying
B
gives

u=\widetilde{K}(S)\,u,\qquad u:=BI\geq 0.

(11)

To prove (i), suppose first that (
9
) admits a nonzero solution

. Then
u=BI\neq 0
, and (
11
) shows that
1
is an eigenvalue of the nonnegative matrix
\widetilde{K}(S)
with a nonzero nonnegative eigenvector. Hence

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)=1.

Conversely, assume that
\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1
, and let

\widetilde{K}(S)\,v(S)=v(S).

Define

For (ii), assume that

and

. Since

\widetilde{K}(S)=\widetilde{G}\,\diag(S),

and
\diag(S)
is positive diagonal,
\widetilde{K}(S)
has the same zero pattern as
\widetilde{G}
, hence is irreducible. By Perron–Frobenius, the eigenspace of
\widetilde{K}(S)

is one-dimensional and generated by a vector

, unique up to multiplication by a positive scalar.

Let
I\geq 0
,
I\neq 0

9
), and set

. Then

and (
11
) gives

for some

) yields

Conversely, every vector of this form satisfies (

For (iii), if

, then

\diag(S)\leq\diag(S_{0}),

hence

\widetilde{G}\geq 0
. By monotonicity of the spectral radius on nonnegative matrices,

The last statement follows from (i).

Part (iv) is immediate from the definition of

.

For (v), if

, then

\widetilde{K}(tS_{0})=\widetilde{G}\,\diag(tS_{0})=t\,\widetilde{G}\,\diag(S_{0})=t\,\widetilde{K}(S_{0}),

so

Hence

if and only if

at the unique point

.

Finally, (vi) is an immediate application of (ii) with

.
∎

Remark 8

(DFE corollary for the coupled model)
.

For the coupled balanced bilinear system

\begin{cases}S^{\prime}=\Lambda+A_{S}S-\diag(S)\,BI+CI,\\[2.84526pt]

with

(iii) provides the

.

Remark 9

(Spectral mechanism and CEP reduction)
.

Theorem
3
isolates the essential structure of the equilibrium problem:

\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))<1\iff I=0,\qquad\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1\iff\exists\,I\neq 0.

Hence every nontrivial equilibrium must lie on the hypersurface

\rho\big(\widetilde{K}(S)\big)=1.

On this hypersurface, the

In particular, if the spectral equation admits a unique solution

, then the

CEP mechanism beyond rank one.

Remark 10

(Loop interpretation)
.

The operator

\widetilde{K}(S)=B(-A)^{-1}P\,\diag(S)

represents one full circulation through the positive feedback loop

If

z=\widetilde{K}(S)\,z.

Thus
\rho(\widetilde{K}(S))=1
expresses exact balance between dissipation through
A
and
amplification through the feedback loop. This is the structural origin of the threshold.

3.2
Fifth Law: An explicit Lyapunov function establishing global asymptotic stability of the DFE for
rank-one balanced bilinear model (Case P) when
R_{0}<1

The following result is for Case (P) (

).

Theorem 5

(Stability of DFE for (P) rank-one balanced bilinear models)
.

For an

-
S
ODE

\displaystyle\begin{cases}S^{\prime}=\varphi(S)-\diag[S]\,BI,\\
I^{\prime}=\left[{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}SB+A\right]I,\end{cases}

let
R_{0}
be defined by the natural regular splitting of the Jacobian matrix of the invasion vector field with respect to the invasion variables into
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}SB
and

.
The following holds:

•

If
{R}_{0}>1
, then the disease-free equilibrium is unstable.

•

If
{R}_{0}\leq 1
, and

is a strictly negative diagonal matrix, then

(21)

is a Lyapunov function, whose minimum is attained uniquely at the DFE, which is therefore
globally asymptotically stable.

Remark 13

(Lyapunov weights are related to the left NGM eigenvector)
.

The function (
21

\mu H(S)+L(I),\qquad\mu=R_{0},

with tangential Goh–Volterra–Horn–Jackson term

and
with transversal Perron term

whose weights
are a left Perron eigenvector of

in both cases (P) and (B).

Proof of Theorem
5
.

At the DFE one has

S_{0}=(-A_{S})^{-1}\Lambda,\qquad A_{S}=-\diag(\mu_{S}),

hence

\Lambda=\diag(\mu_{S})S_{0},\qquad\varphi(S)=\Lambda+A_{S}S=-\diag(\mu_{S})(S-S_{0}).

Set

H(S):={\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}S-S_{0}\ln(S),\qquad L(I):=S_{0}\Pi I,\qquad\Pi:=B(-A)^{-1}.

Then

V_{DF}(I,S)=R_{0}\,H(S)+L(I).

We compute the two derivatives separately.

First,

\dot{W}={\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\,S^{\prime}-S_{0}\frac{S^{\prime}}{S}={\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\bigl(\varphi(S)-\diag(S)BI\bigr)-S_{0}\frac{\varphi(S)-\diag(S)BI}{S}.

Since

{\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\diag(S)BI=S\,BI,\qquad S_{0}\frac{\diag(S)BI}{S}=S_{0}\,BI,

this gives

\dot{W}={\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\varphi(S)-S_{0}\frac{\varphi(S)}{S}-S\,BI+S_{0}\,BI.

Next,

\dot{Q}=S_{0}\Pi I^{\prime}=S_{0}\Pi({\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}SB+A)I=S_{0}\Pi{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n}\,SBI+S_{0}\Pi AI.

Using

R_{0}=S_{0}\Pi{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{n},\qquad\Pi A=B(-A)^{-1}A=-B,

we obtain

\dot{Q}=R_{0}\,S\,BI-S_{0}\,BI.

Therefore

\dot{V}_{DF}=R_{0}\dot{W}+\dot{Q}=R_{0}\Bigl({\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\varphi(S)-S_{0}\frac{\varphi(S)}{S}\Bigr)+(R_{0}-1)S_{0}\,BI.

Since

{\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}}\varphi(S)-S_{0}\frac{\varphi(S)}{S}=(S-S_{0})\frac{\varphi(S)}{S},

and

\varphi(S)=-\diag(\mu_{S})(S-S_{0}),

it follows that

\dot{V}_{DF}=-R_{0}\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{S,j}(S_{j}-S_{0,j})^{2}}{S_{j}}+(R_{0}-1)S_{0}\,BI.

(22)

If
R_{0}<1
, both terms in (

) are nonpositive, with equality only if

S=S_{0},\qquad I=0.

Hence
V_{DF}
is a strict Lyapunov function and the DFE is globally asymptotically stable.

If
R_{0}=1
, then

\dot{V}_{DF}=-\sum_{j}\frac{\mu_{S,j}(S_{j}-S_{0,j})^{2}}{S_{j}}\leq 0.

On the set
\{\dot{V}_{DF}=0\}
one has
S=S_{0}
, and then

0=S^{\prime}=-\diag(S_{0})BI,

so
BI=0
. Therefore

I^{\prime}=AI.

Since
A
is Hurwitz, the only invariant point is
I=0
. By LaSalle’s invariance principle, the DFE is globally asymptotically stable also when
R_{0}=1
.

If
R_{0}>1
, the unstable infected block has spectral bound
\lambda_{\max}(J_{DFE})>0
, where
\lambda_{\max}(J_{DFE})
is the largest real part of eigenvalues of Jacobian of infected equations with respect to infected variables, evaluated at the DFE, so the DFE is unstable.
∎

3.4
Seventh Law: A determinant law for rank–one balanced bilinear models with one–susceptible class

The determinant identity proved in
[

for the SAIR model

\det(J_{EE})=-\det(J_{DFE}),

extends to the whole class of rank–one balanced bilinear models with one susceptible class (in this case (P) and (B) coincide):

\begin{cases}S^{\prime}=\Lambda-\mu_{S}S-S\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}I,\\[5.69054pt]
I^{\prime}={\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\,S\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}I+AI,\end{cases}\qquad{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+},\ \vec{\mathbf{1}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}=1,

(33)

Theorem 7

(Determinant law)
.

Consider (
33

R_{0}>1
, where

R_{0}=\frac{\Lambda}{\mu_{S}}\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}(-A)^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}.

Let

and

denote the Jacobians at the disease–free and endemic equilibria.
Then

\det(J_{EE})=-\det(J_{DFE}).

(34)

More explicitly,

\det(J_{DFE})=-\mu_{S}\det(A)\,(1-R_{0}),\qquad\det(J_{EE})=\mu_{S}\det(A)\,(1-R_{0}).

(35)

Proof.

At the DFE,
S_{0}=\Lambda/\mu_{S}
and
I=0
, hence

J_{DFE}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{S}&-S_{0}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\\
0&A+S_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\end{pmatrix},

so

\det(J_{DFE})=-\mu_{S}\,\det(A+S_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}).

By the matrix determinant lemma,

\det(A+S_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}})=\det(A)\bigl(1+S_{0}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}A^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\bigr).

Since

it follows that

\det(A+S_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}})=\det(A)(1-R_{0}),

which yields the first identity in (
35
).

At the endemic equilibrium
(\bar{S},\bar{I})
, define
q^{*}:=\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\bar{I}
. Then

J_{EE}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{S}-q^{*}&-\bar{S}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\\
q^{*}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}&A+\bar{S}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\end{pmatrix}.

Perform the row operation
R_{i+1}\mapsto R_{i+1}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}_{i}R_{1},i=1,...,n
to obtain

\widetilde{J}_{EE}=\begin{pmatrix}-\mu_{S}-q^{*}&-\bar{S}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\\
-\mu_{S}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}&A\end{pmatrix}.

Hence, by Schur’s formula

\det(J_{EE})=\det(A)\Bigl(-\mu_{S}-q^{*}-(-\bar{S}\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}})A^{-1}(-\mu_{S}{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}})\Bigr).

Using the endemic identity

A\bar{I}=-{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\,\bar{S}\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}\bar{I},

we obtain

\det(J_{EE})=\det(A)\bigl(-\mu_{S}-q^{*}+\mu_{S}\bigr)=-q^{*}\det(A).

From
0=\Lambda-\mu_{S}\bar{S}-\bar{S}q^{*}
and
\bar{S}=S_{0}/R_{0}
, it follows that

q^{*}=\mu_{S}(R_{0}-1)
, hence

\det(J_{EE})=\mu_{S}(1-R_{0})\det(A),

which proves (

).
∎

Remark 15

(Index interpretation)
.

Assume that all equilibria are hyperbolic.
The local index of an equilibrium is
\mathrm{sign}(\det J)
.

At the DFE,

\det(J_{DFE})=-\mu_{S}\det(A)(1-R_{0}),

and since
A
is Hurwitz,
\det(A)=(-1)^{n}|\det(A)|
.
Thus the index of the DFE changes sign at
R_{0}=1
.

At the EE,

\det(J_{EE})=-\det(J_{DFE}),

so the two equilibria have opposite index.

This is consistent with degree theory: on any positively invariant compact region of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n+1}
containing both equilibria and no others, the sum of indices is zero. Hence the appearance of the endemic equilibrium at
R_{0}=1
is accompanied by a sign change of the Jacobian determinant, and the two equilibria necessarily have opposite stability types.

Remark 16

(Limitations)
.

The identity
\det(J_{EE})=-\det(J_{DFE})
relies on:

1.

the rank–one infection structure
{\mbox{\boldmath$\alpha$}}\,S\,\vec{{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}}I
;

2.

the single susceptible equation, which allows elimination of the rank–one perturbation at the EE.

For multi–susceptible systems, this mechanism fails and no general determinant sign law is expected.

For a conservative irreducible generator

Theorem 9

(Kirchhoff–Perron formula for regular splittings)

Let

1.

(Perron–Frobenius.)

2.

(Adjugate factorisation at the Perron root.)

The matrix

6
Conclusion

We reviewed a class of mathematical epidemiology (ME) models that we call
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models, for which explicit formulas for the interior fixed point and the basic reproduction number, together with elegant Lyapunov functions establishing the asymptotic stability of the disease-free equilibrium (DFE) and endemic equilibrium (EE), are available when certain matrices have rank one.

Several themes emerge.

First, rank one plays two rather different roles. On the one hand, it yields explicit formulas for Perron eigenvectors, endemic equilibria, and Goh–Volterra coefficients. On the other hand, the DFE theory itself is not inherently rank-one: the Perron Lyapunov weight persists for higher-rank NGMs, although it is no longer explicit. Thus rank one is best viewed not as the source of the theory, but as the regime in which the general theory becomes closed-form.

Second, the relation between DFE and EE stability is stronger than is usually emphasized. In the rank-one
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear model setting, both rely on the same building blocks: the Perron data of the NGM, the dwell-time vector, and a normalization law. This suggests that DFE and EE Lyapunov theories should not be developed separately, but rather as two parts of a common “stability relay”. At present this relay is fully understood only in special cases.

Third, the comparison with Shuai–van den Driessche shows a sharp distinction between what remains valid at general rank and what does not. The DFE Lyapunov weight remains Perron-theoretic, but EE Lyapunov weights are generally no longer Perron eigenvectors; they are instead related to Kirchhoff-type cofactors. Thus one should expect a genuine bifurcation in the theory at higher rank: Perron theory controls the DFE, while graph-theoretic constructions control the EE.

These observations lead to the following open problems.

Problem 1

(General Lyapunov functions for bilinear ME models)
.

Is it possible to extend both Lyapunov functions in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
to a wider class that includes both

(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear model and multi-strain models, including models whose NGM has rank greater than one?
In particular, can the Lyapunov functions of
[
37
,
15
]
be obtained from some unifying general formulas?

A first subproblem is to determine whether the DFE Perron term and the EE Goh–Volterra term can be completed into a single general construction, valid beyond rank one. A second is to decide whether the higher-rank EE coefficients should be sought through Perron theory, through Kirchhoff cofactors, or through a genuinely new mechanism.

Problem 2

(General theory for multistrain models)
.

Do formulas
[
13
]
like

\begin{cases}R_{i}=R_{i}(E_{0}),R_{0}=\max\left[R_{1},R_{2}\right],\\
{\mathcal{R}}_{\widetilde{i}}^{j}=R_{j}(E_{i}),\\
R_{2}<R_{2,c}:=\frac{R_{2}(E_{0})}{R_{2}(E_{1})}\Longrightarrow E_{1}\text{ is LAS }\end{cases}

(40)

where
R_{0},R_{i}
denote the reproduction numbers,
{\mathcal{R}}_{\widetilde{i}}^{j}
the invasion numbers of resident strain
i
by strain
j
, and
R_{i}(x)
the reproduction functions,
hold for some general class of epidemic models?

More generally, one may ask whether reproduction functions admit a structural interpretation analogous to that of NGMs, and whether invasion criteria can be derived from Jacobian factorizations without requiring explicit knowledge of the competing endemic equilibria.

Problem 3

(Structural characterization of balanced bilinear epidemic models within CRN theory)
.

Characterize
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear models within CRN theory, and investigate extensions of the bilinear results under rates which are admissible in the sense of
[
3
]
.

This includes at least three natural questions: how to recognize
(A,B,P,\varphi)
balanced bilinear model structure directly from a reaction network; which parts of the seven-law theory survive under admissible non-bilinear rates; and whether siphons, minimal siphons, or related CRN objects provide the correct structural substitute for the rank-one assumptions.

Problem 4

(Persistence and boundary structure beyond the single-siphon case)
.

Extend the persistence part of Shuai–van den Driessche’s theory from the case of a single minimal siphon to models with several minimal siphons.

A natural conjecture is that persistence above threshold should hold whenever every minimal siphon supports a repelling boundary face, equivalently whenever the restriction of the infection dynamics to each such face has spectral radius greater than one. Formulating and proving such a criterion would provide a boundary counterpart to the higher-rank DFE theory.

Problem 5

(Determinant laws, relay identities, and inheritance beyond rank one)
.

Determine whether the determinant law, relay relations between DFE and EE weights, and related inheritance phenomena extend beyond the one-susceptible rank-one setting.

Even partial extensions would clarify which features are genuinely tied to rank one and which are manifestations of a broader algebraic structure underlying epidemic Jacobians.
