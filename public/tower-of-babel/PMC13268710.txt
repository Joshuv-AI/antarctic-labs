Continuous video-based postural tracking for Parkinson’s disease: a pose estimation approach with landmark calibration
Authors: Yuki Kondo, Ippei Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] We aimed to develop and validate an automated, continuous, video-based postural tracking system using pose estimation in people with Parkinson’s disease. [Participants and Methods] This cross-sectional validation study included 14 people with Parkinson’s disease with postural abnormalities. Participants performed a 2 min standing endurance test while being recorded using iPads. We developed Continuous Video AutoPosture for Parkinson’s disease, an automated system with optional operator-guided initial-frame landmark calibration integrating MediaPipe pose estimation and DeepLab semantic segmentation to track postural angles at 1 s intervals. We measured the lumbar anterior trunk flexion, thoracic anterior trunk flexion, and lateral trunk flexion and manually calculated ground-truth postural angles from 140 sampled frames using NeuroPostureApp for validation. An optional initial-frame landmark calibration feature was implemented. [Results] Following calibration, the system demonstrated excellent reliability for lumbar (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.98) and thoracic (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.96) anterior trunk flexion and good reliability for lateral trunk flexion (intraclass correlation coefficient=0.80). Continuous tracking captured gradual postural deterioration during the standing task. [Conclusion] The system demonstrated excellent-to-good reliability and enables continuous postural tracking with superior temporal resolution compared with conventional assessments, advancing clinical evaluation and research on postural abnormalities in people with Parkinson’s disease.

INTRODUCTION

Postural abnormalities (PA) represent a prominent and often debilitating manifestation of Parkinson’s disease (PD), affecting approximately 20% of the affected population1) and substantially impairing physical function and quality of life. The primary postural abnormalities in people with Parkinson’s disease (PwPD) manifest in three distinct forms2, 3), each associated with unique pathophysiological mechanisms and clinical presentations. Camptocormia, commonly termed “bent spine syndrome”, is characterized by excessive forward flexion of the thoracolumbar spine that becomes prominent during standing or walking and notably improves or resolves when the patient assumes a supine position4). This dissociation between standing and supine posture is a critical diagnostic feature that distinguishes camptocormia from structural spinal deformities. Dropped head syndrome, clinically referred to as anterocollis, presents with severe forward flexion of the neck and head. Pisa syndrome manifests as characteristic lateral trunk flexion to one side2,3,4).

The traditional gold standard method for evaluating PA involves measuring postural angles from static photographs captured at a single time point during clinical examination2, 3). However, this approach has several limitations. First, photographic assessment reflects only a brief moment and may be influenced by voluntary effort or posture optimization during image capture, potentially failing to represent the individual’s habitual postural pattern during daily activities. Thus, photo-based measurements capture only momentary effort and may not accurately reflect posture during daily life, limiting their clinical utility for understanding habitual postural behavior. Second, this method provides no temporal information regarding the progression or severity of postural changes during sustained standing tasks, thereby missing critical insights into postural endurance and fatigue-related deterioration. Relying solely on static images does not offer a holistic understanding of postural changes over time. Therefore, a more comprehensive approach is required to measure axial PA during dynamic tasks5).

To address these limitations, we previously developed the Two-minute standing endurance test (2M-SET)5), which evaluates postural angles at predetermined time points (0, 10, 30, 60, and 120 s) during a controlled 2 min standing task. The 2M-SET protocol requires participants to voluntarily correct their axial PA to the best of their ability, without using their upper limbs for support, before initiating the task. Participants are then instructed to maintain an upright standing position for 2 min while avoiding mid-task postural adjustments. Although this method demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity for capturing progressive postural deterioration, it presents two important limitations. First, the analysis is extremely time-consuming, requiring extraction of individual screenshots from video recordings followed by meticulous manual landmark selection using specialized software (NeuroPostureApp; https://www.neuroimaging.uni-kiel.de/NeuroPostureApp) for postural angle calculation, a process that may require several minutes per image. Second, measurements are limited to five predetermined time points per participant, leaving substantial temporal gaps and potentially missing postural variations occurring at intermediate time points.

Recent advances in deep learning-based pose estimation technology have enabled automatic measurement of postural angles from RGB images captured using single commercial cameras in PwPD. The development of automated tools such as AutoPosturePD (APP) has demonstrated robust performance across varying participant body characteristics and image properties, offering substantial improvements in efficiency and objectivity compared with manual assessment methods6, 7). These automated approaches employ advanced machine learning algorithms to identify anatomical landmarks and calculate postural angles with high accuracy and reliability.

The purpose of this study was to develop and validate an automated method with optional operator-guided initial-frame landmark calibration for continuous postural angle measurement using advanced pose estimation technology applied to entire video recordings from the 2M-SET. Continuous measurement throughout the full standing task enables detailed temporal assessment of postural changes that cannot be captured by conventional point-in-time evaluations. This approach may provide a more accurate and comprehensive evaluation of postural endurance, fatigue-related postural deterioration, and disease progression in PwPD. This method has the potential to transform the clinical assessment of PA by offering unprecedented temporal resolution while substantially reducing the manual effort currently required for comprehensive postural analysis.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

This cross-sectional validation study was conducted at the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (NCNP) in Tokyo, Japan, between September 2022 and April 2023. During this period, participants performed the 2M-SET while being recorded5). The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards of NCNP and Kanto Gakuin University (approval number: A2024-044). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The automated postural measurement was validated in 2025. This study adheres to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational studies8).

Participants were recruited from patients admitted to the NCNP. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of PD according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria9,10,11); (2) a Movement Disorder Society-unified Parkinson’s disease rating scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III posture item score of at least two points, indicating clinically relevant PA12); (3) age 50 years or older; and (4) ability to provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria included: (1) structural postural deformities that did not improve in the supine position, suggesting fixed spinal pathology rather than functional postural abnormality; (2) Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score of 24 points or lower, indicating cognitive impairment that could affect understanding of instructions; (3) severe balance or motor impairment precluding safe participation in the 2 min standing task; and (4) additional neurological conditions that could confound postural assessment. Of 27 patients assessed for eligibility from the original 2M-SET validation study5), 14 were included in the present analysis. Thirteen were excluded after visual inspection of video recordings owing to therapist-patient overlap or occlusion that would preclude reliable pose estimation. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this study was designed as an exploratory validation of a novel automated measurement system. Given the preliminary nature of this study, the sample was considered sufficient to provide initial estimates of reliability and systematic bias.

Comprehensive demographic and clinical data were collected from all participants, including age, biological sex, and disease duration. Clinical assessments comprised the following: (1) Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging to assess overall disease severity in both the “on” and “off” medication states13); (2) MDS-UPDRS Part III in the on-medication state to evaluate motor function and activities of daily living12); (3) calculation of levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) to quantify antiparkinsonian medication use14); and (4) MMSE to assess global cognitive function15). Experienced movement disorders specialists performed all clinical assessments.

Participants were instructed to stand with their feet shoulder-width apart and to voluntarily correct their postural abnormality to the best of their ability using the core musculature alone, without using the upper limbs for support. After this initial correction, participants were instructed to maintain an upright standing position for exactly 2 min while avoiding additional postural adjustments during the task, according to the 2M-SET protocol5). To minimize non-physiological motion artifacts, participants were instructed to tuck their shirts into their pants, and adhesive tape was applied to clothing when necessary. When the seventh cervical vertebra (C7) landmark was obscured by hair, a headband was applied to ensure clear visualization of the posterior cervical region without impairing landmark detection. The test was performed during the “on” medication state to ensure consistent measurement conditions and minimize medication-related variability in motor symptoms.

The task was simultaneously recorded from two fixed camera positions using iPads (iPad Air, 4th Generation; Apple Inc., Cupertino, CA, USA): one positioned 5 m from the frontal plane and the other 3.5 m from the sagittal plane, both at a standardized height of 82.5 cm above floor level. This dual-angle recording system enabled comprehensive capture of anterior–posterior and medial–lateral postural components.

Continuous Video AutoPosture for PD (CVAPP) was developed as an automated system with optional operator-guided initial-frame landmark calibration for processing video recordings and calculating postural angles at high temporal resolution. The system extracts individual frames from video recordings at 1 s intervals and applies computer vision algorithms to calculate postural angles. CVAPP was implemented in Visual Studio Code using the following technology stack: PyTorch 2.3.0 for deep learning operations, OpenCV 4.8.0 for image processing and computer vision tasks, Matplotlib 3.7.1 for data visualization, and MediaPipe 0.10.0 for pose estimation. MediaPipe Pose, using the BlazePose model developed by Google LLC (Mountain View, CA, USA), detects 33 human body landmarks in images, identifying key anatomical positions to analyze posture with outputs in both 2D image coordinates and 3D world coordinates16, 17). DeepLab semantic segmentation, also developed by Google LLC, classifies each pixel in an image into predefined categories, enabling precise identification and separation of body regions from background elements18, 19). A fundamental challenge in applying standard pose estimation keypoints to postural angle measurement is that the default MediaPipe keypoints do not include the specific anatomical landmarks required by clinical postural assessment protocols. Therefore, we implemented a method to extrapolate the anatomical positions of C7, the fifth lumbar vertebra (L5), and the fulcrum point (FC) based on approaches described in prior research by Artusi and colleagues6). These extrapolated positions were calculated as follows:

- C7 identification: A line perpendicular to the line connecting the right ear and right shoulder was drawn in the posterior direction. The intersection of this perpendicular line with the participant’s silhouette edge, as determined by semantic segmentation, was defined as the C7 position.

- L5 identification: A point was defined at 20% of the vertical distance from the right hip joint to the right knee joint, measured from the right hip joint position (designated as L5′). The intersection of a vertical line drawn from L5′ to the posterior silhouette edge was defined as the L5 position.

- FC identification: The fulcrum point was defined as the contact point of the tangent line to the back that is parallel to the C7–L5 line. This point represents the most posterior aspect of spinal curvature in the thoracic region and serves as the fulcrum for thoracic camptocormia measurement.

The CVAPP system calculated three types of postural angles according to established MDS Task Force consensus criteria and international recommendations2, 3):

- Lumbar Anterior Trunk Flexion (lATF) Angle

The lumbar ATF angle represents camptocormia with a lumbar fulcrum and is calculated as the external angle between two lines: (1) the line connecting C7 and L5, and (2) the line connecting L5 and the lateral malleolus (ankle reference point). This measurement assesses forward trunk flexion with primary bending at the lumbar spine level. Anatomical keypoints from the participant’s body silhouette are identified using semantic segmentation techniques. According to international consensus criteria, lATF measurements exceeding 30° from the vertical position are considered diagnostic of camptocormia with lumbar fulcrum3).

- Thoracic Anterior Trunk Flexion (tATF) Angle

The tATF angle represents camptocormia with a thoracic fulcrum and is calculated as the external angle between two lines: (1) the line connecting the fulcrum point (FC) and L5 and (2) the line connecting FC and C7. The fulcrum point is automatically identified as the point most distant from the straight line connecting C7 and L5, representing the apex of thoracic spinal curvature. This measurement captures forward trunk flexion with primary bending at the upper spine level. Image segmentation is used to detect the most posterior point of spinal curvature from the participant’s silhouette. Measurements exceeding 45° from the vertical position are considered diagnostic of camptocormia with thoracic fulcrum2).

- Lateral Trunk Flexion (LTF) Angle

The LTF angle represents Pisa syndrome and is calculated as the external angle between two lines: (1) the line connecting C7 and L5, representing the vertical spinal axis, and (2) the line connecting the midpoint between the lateral malleoli (MA) and L5. This measurement assesses the lateral deviation of the trunk to the left or right side, producing the characteristic leaning posture of Pisa syndrome. LTF measurements exceeding 10° from the vertical midline are considered diagnostic of Pisa syndrome2).

The tool provides interactive height-based adjustment parameters for the initial frame: file selection and output directory settings; keypoint position adjustment panel with three sliders for C7 Offset Y, K1 Percent (L5 position), and MA Offset Y (ankle position); preview of the first frame with normalized coordinate display; and analysis mode selection allowing automated calculation at three temporal resolutions: every second (time series with 120 measurements), at standard 2M-SET time points (0, 10, 30, 60, and 120 s) or at custom time point intervals. - For ATF measurements (sagittal plane), operators can adjust the vertical positions of C7, L5, and the lateral ankle landmark (lateral malleolus) using dedicated sliders. The thoracic fulcrum point (FC) is automatically recalculated from the adjusted C7 and L5 positions and body silhouette information.

- For LTF measurements (frontal plane), operators can adjust the vertical positions of C7, L5, and the ankle midpoint (midpoint between medial malleoli) using dedicated sliders.

All vertical adjustments are constrained to follow the body silhouette, ensuring anatomically plausible landmark positioning. Once adjusted in the initial frame, all subsequent frames are automatically calculated based on these calibrated positions. This semi-automated approach preserves automation while improving accuracy in challenging cases.

For concurrent validation purposes, manual postural angle calculations were performed using a strategic sampling approach rather than analyzing all frames, as comprehensive manual analysis of the entire continuous dataset would be impractical and prohibitively time-consuming. Screenshot images were extracted from the iPad video recordings, and ten images per participant were analyzed: (1) five images corresponding to established 2M-SET standardized time points (0, 10, 30, 60, and 120 s) to maintain consistency with prior validation studies5) and enable longitudinal comparison, and (2) five additional randomly selected time points to assess accuracy across the entire measurement period and reduce systematic bias related to specific time points. This sampling approach resulted in a comparative analysis of 140 images across 14 participants between CVAPP and manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements.

All extracted images underwent visual inspection prior to validation analysis to exclude cases with obvious pose estimation failures, including occlusion artifacts, erroneous keypoint detection on non-human objects, or severe image quality degradation. Two experienced physical therapists specializing in movement disorders, each with more than ten years of clinical experience, performed manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements. All 140 images were assigned unique numerical identifiers and randomly reordered using the RAND function in Microsoft Excel before independent analysis to eliminate potential order effects. Both therapists independently analyzed images to calculate postural angles. When discrepancies greater than 15% occurred between the two assessments, the therapists jointly reviewed the images and reached consensus on final postural angle measurements through discussion and application of standardized measurement protocols.

To assess reliability and agreement of the CVAPP system, statistical analyses were performed comparing CVAPP automated measurements with manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements. Analyses were conducted for both unadjusted and adjusted CVAPP measurements to evaluate the impact of the landmark adjustment feature. The following statistical approaches were employed.

ICC estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement to evaluate consistency between CVAPP and manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements across all postural angle types. Separate ICC analyses were performed for (1) unadjusted automated CVAPP measurements and (2) adjusted CVAPP measurements following initial-frame landmark calibration. ICC values were interpreted according to established standards: ICC<0.50=poor reliability; ICC 0.50–0.75=moderate reliability; ICC 0.75–0.90=good reliability; ICC>0.90=excellent reliability20). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Bland–Altman analysis was conducted to assess agreement between CVAPP and manual measurements for both unadjusted and adjusted CVAPP data. This analysis included calculation of (1) mean differences (bias) with 95% CI; (2) 95% limits of agreement (mean difference ± 1.96 × SD); and (3) evaluation of fixed and proportional bias21). Proportional bias was assessed by calculating the correlation coefficients between measurement differences and mean values of the two methods to determine whether measurement error varied systematically with angle magnitude22). Visual inspection of Bland–Altman plots was used to identify systematic patterns in agreement. All statistical analyses were performed using R 4.4.2, with significance level α=0.05.

DISCUSSION

This study developed and validated CVAPP, an automated continuous postural angle measurement system using pose estimation technology to assess PA in PwPD. CVAPP demonstrated high reliability compared with manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements and enabled continuous postural angle assessment throughout the entire 2M-SET, providing unprecedented temporal resolution for detecting postural change patterns that cannot be captured by conventional point-in-time clinical evaluations. These reliable continuous measurements represent a methodological advancement in postural assessment for PD and have important implications for both clinical practice and research.

The CVAPP system demonstrated excellent to good reliability across postural angle types compared with manual NeuroPostureApp© measurements. Unadjusted CVAPP measurements showed excellent reliability for lATF (ICC=0.96, 95% CI: 0.95–0.97) and tATF (ICC=0.94, 95% CI: 0.91–0.95) and good reliability for LTF (ICC=0.79, 95% CI: 0.72–0.84). Following initial-frame landmark calibration, reliability improved for lATF (ICC=0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.98) and tATF (ICC=0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.97), whereas LTF showed minimal improvement (ICC=0.80, 95% CI: 0.73–0.85). These ICC values, particularly for adjusted measurements, are comparable with prior studies using pose estimation-based tracking for postural assessment in PD.

The recent APP study by Artusi et al. reported excellent agreement for lATF (ICC=0.99), tATF (ICC=0.93), and LTF (ICC=0.96) when validated against NeuroPostureApp© measurements6). Adjusted CVAPP measurements achieved comparable reliability to APP for lATF (0.98 vs. 0.99) and tATF (0.96 vs. 0.93), demonstrating the robustness of pose estimation-based tracking for sagittal-plane measurements under realistic clinical conditions. However, LTF reliability differed substantially between systems (CVAPP adjusted: 0.80 vs. APP: 0.96), and this discrepancy warrants explicit acknowledgment. This difference is likely attributable to the more challenging measurement conditions in this study, in which participants wore regular daily clothing against complex clinical backgrounds, whereas the APP study employed standardized undergarments and uniform backgrounds. Furthermore, frontal-plane LTF measurement is inherently more susceptible to hip joint detection errors, which are amplified under realistic clinical conditions. These findings suggest that while CVAPP performs robustly for sagittal-plane measurements in clinical settings, LTF measurement accuracy remains a specific limitation requiring further development. This robustness is further enhanced through initial-frame landmark calibration, which functions as an adaptive refinement mechanism to address challenges such as occlusion, anatomical variation, and complex background scenarios, issues that are well recognized in multi-object and robust tracking contexts.

The fundamental accuracy of automated postural angle calculation using pose estimation is preserved when applied to continuous video-based assessment, supporting the validity of extending single-time-point evaluation to continuous frame-by-frame tracking paradigms. The manual landmark adjustment feature represents a practical hybrid approach that complements automated detection rather than replacing it entirely.

Bland–Altman analysis revealed systematic biases that warrant detailed discussion. Following initial-frame landmark calibration, lATF measurements showed minimal fixed bias with a mean difference of −0.09° (95% CI: −1.28 to 1.09°), indicating near-unbiased measurement. In contrast, LTF measurements demonstrated persistent positive bias with a mean difference of 4.99° (95% CI: 4.22 to 5.77°), reflecting systematic overestimation. In addition, proportional bias was observed for LTF, with measurement differences increasing at larger postural angles (r=0.563, p<0.001). The clinical implications of this LTF bias warrant explicit discussion. The mean bias of 4.99° represents approximately 50% of the internationally accepted diagnostic threshold for Pisa syndrome (>10° from the vertical midline)^3)^. Consequently, patients whose true LTF angle falls in the range of approximately 5–10° may be systematically misclassified as meeting the diagnostic criterion for Pisa syndrome when CVAPP measurements are used without manual correction. Based on the 95% limits of agreement (−4.69° to 14.66°), even patients with true LTF values below 5° could theoretically exceed the 10° threshold due to measurement error alone. Therefore, CVAPP LTF measurements should not be used as a direct substitute for manually validated measurements in diagnostic decision-making without systematic re-evaluation or the application of an appropriate correction factor. Regarding the feasibility of a simple fixed correction, subtracting the mean bias of approximately 5° from CVAPP LTF measurements might appear to offer a straightforward mitigation strategy. However, because proportional bias is present (r=0.563, p<0.001), whereby measurement error increases systematically with lATF magnitude, a uniform fixed correction would be insufficient and potentially misleading for patients with severe sagittal-plane postural abnormalities. A more appropriate correction approach would require individual calibration accounting for both fixed and proportional components of bias, which should be investigated in future studies with larger samples.

These systematic differences can be attributed to several technical factors. First, keypoints become progressively obscured as postural angles increase, leading to landmark estimation errors that particularly affect severely flexed postures. Second, the MediaPipe BlazePose model may not be optimally trained on individuals with pronounced PA, potentially reducing accuracy in severe cases. Third, hip joint detection accuracy remains problematic23), as reported in movement analysis literature, affecting LTF precision. Fourth, during lATF and tATF measurement, silhouette analysis combined with landmark adjustment can partially compensate for imprecise position estimation, whereas LTF calculation is more directly affected by hip keypoint errors without comparable compensation.

An important distinction of this validation is that it was conducted under realistic clinical conditions in which participants wore regular daily clothing rather than specially designed undergarments against uniform backgrounds, as in prior validation studies6, 7). This setting reflects actual conditions under which the system would be deployed in clinical practice. Although wider limits of agreement compared with prior research reflect these more challenging real-world conditions, the excellent reliability of lATF measurements (ICC=0.98) achieved under these circumstances demonstrates sufficient precision for continuous postural monitoring in typical clinical settings. This finding is particularly relevant given cultural and practical considerations in Japan, where underwear-only photography is not feasible. The ability to obtain high-quality measurements under realistic clothing conditions substantially enhances the clinical utility and ecological validity of CVAPP.

From a clinical perspective, the continuous measurement capability of CVAPP offers several advantages over traditional assessment methods. First, the automated system reliably detects subtle postural deterioration patterns during sustained standing tasks, which is particularly valuable for understanding postural endurance and fatigue-related changes in PwPD. Continuous measurements at 1-second intervals provide high temporal resolution, yielding 120 measurements per 2-minute task compared with five standardized time points (0, 10, 30, 60, and 120 s) using manual methods. This resolution enables detection of postural dynamics that may not be apparent during brief conventional clinical examinations. The resulting temporal data provide insights into the rate and pattern of postural deterioration, which may serve as a sensitive biomarker for disease progression or treatment response. In addition, the automated nature of CVAPP reduces inter-rater variability and potential bias associated with manual landmark selection while substantially reducing the time-intensive analysis required by traditional methods. Conventional postural assessments may require several minutes per image for manual landmark placement and angle calculation, whereas CVAPP requires only video upload and optional initial-frame keypoint adjustment, after which postural angles across the entire 2-minute recording are calculated automatically for any time point. Although precise processing time data were not formally recorded in this study, this streamlined workflow represents a substantial reduction in the manual effort required for conventional frame-by-frame analysis and may improve efficiency for both routine clinical use and large-scale research studies.

Limitations and implementation considerations should be acknowledged when deploying CVAPP in clinical practice. First, like all pose estimation-based tracking systems, CVAPP exhibits reduced robustness in cases of severe PA. Critical keypoints may become occluded or difficult to track accurately, potentially leading to incorrect landmark estimation, a challenge inherent to long-term tracking of deforming anatomical structures. This limitation reduces suitability for patients with pronounced postural deformities where keypoint tracking becomes unreliable. Second, occlusion by extraneous objects or persons can adversely affect tracking accuracy when caregivers or other individuals are positioned in front of or behind the patient during recording, obscuring essential anatomical landmarks. This occlusion challenge is well recognized in multi-object tracking contexts and requires careful scene control during clinical deployment. Third, for LTF measurements, inherent hip joint detection limitations identified in our analysis require particular attention to patient positioning and image quality. These challenges reflect fundamental constraints in current pose estimation models for accurate lower-body keypoint localization during complex postural tasks. The manual landmark adjustment feature serves as a practical adaptive refinement mechanism, allowing operators to optimize keypoint detection on the initial frame and compensate for tracking failures in challenging cases. Although this semi-automated approach introduces limited manual intervention into an otherwise automated process, it aligns with hybrid tracking methodologies that combine automated detection with selective human-in-the-loop validation to improve robustness in real-world deployment. Fourth, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. Although the reliability methodology literature recommends a minimum of 30 participants for adequate ICC confidence interval estimation20), this study was designed as an exploratory validation. The sample of 14 participants was therefore considered sufficient to provide initial estimates of reliability and systematic bias. Fifth, the 140 observations used in the reliability analysis consisted of 10 repeated measurements nested within 14 individual participants. The ICC(2,1) analysis did not explicitly account for this within-subject correlation structure, which may have resulted in slightly narrower confidence intervals than would be obtained using a hierarchical model. This limitation should be addressed in future studies with larger independent samples. Sixth, the validation analysis was based on 10 of approximately 120 available frames per participant (approximately 8.3% of the continuous record). However, as validation was performed on individually extracted static images rather than continuous video sequences, temporal continuity between frames did not directly affect measurement accuracy. The strategic sampling of five standardized time points combined with five randomly selected time points was designed to provide representative coverage across the full duration of the 2M-SET.

From a methodological perspective, it is important to recognize that CVAPP provides estimated postural angles derived through computer vision algorithms rather than directly measured angles obtained using traditional anatomical methods. Clinical implementation should therefore include appropriate validation procedures and institutional awareness of both the systematic biases identified in this analysis and the inherent limitations of pose estimation technology. Comprehensive staff training on proper video recording conditions, patient positioning, occlusion avoidance, and system limitations is essential for optimal tracking performance and reliable clinical application.

In conclusion, this study developed and validated CVAPP, a continuous video-based postural tracking system using pose estimation technology to assess PA in PwPD, achieving excellent-to-good reliability (ICC: 0.80–0.98) compared with manual reference measurements. By enabling continuous, frame-by-frame postural tracking throughout the 2M-SET duration, CVAPP provides superior temporal resolution for characterizing postural endurance and fatigue-related deterioration patterns beyond conventional discrete time-point evaluations. Despite systematic biases identified under realistic clinical conditions, CVAPP improves assessment efficiency by automating analysis and reducing time-intensive manual work. Implementation of CVAPP in clinical settings may enhance the sensitivity and efficiency of postural assessment and support large-scale research on PA in PD.
