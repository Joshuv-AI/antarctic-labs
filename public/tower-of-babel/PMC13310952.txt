Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar Syndrome With Higher Brain Dysfunction in Which Arterial Spin Labeling Revealed Abnormal Cerebral Blood Flow: A Case Report
Authors: Go Yoshimichi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTreatment for hyperglycemic hyperosmolar syndrome (HHS) was initiated for a transferred unconscious patient. Aphasia and agnosia remained even after metabolic improvement. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) indicated increased blood flow in the left temporal and parietal lobes. ASL may provide useful information regarding cerebral perfusion abnormalities in patients with HHS.Keywords: aphasia, arterial spin labeling, cerebral perfusion, diabetes mellitus, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar syndrome

Introduction

Hyperglycemic hyperosmolar syndrome (HHS) is an acute complication seen in diabetic patients. HHS can be difficult to diagnose and treat when the patient is unconscious. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) is considered a useful imaging modality for investigating epilepsy. We report herein the case of a patient with HHS and higher brain dysfunction in which ASL revealed abnormal blood flow.

Case History and Examinations

A 64‐year‐old woman had been receiving outpatient treatment for type 2 diabetes at another hospital for a long time, but had stopped attending outpatient appointments around 6 months earlier. A history of hypertension, lipidemia, malignant lymphoma, and bilateral nonfunctional adrenal tumor was elicited. On June 202X, hemoglobin (Hb) A1c was 7.1%. She was therefore classified as having stage G3aA3 chronic kidney disease and type A2 diabetic retinopathy according to the Fukuda classification.

Family Composition

The patient lived in a house with her husband, daughter, and son but tended to stay overnight in a shelter with 18 cats.

Occupation

The patient ran a shelter for cats.

Diet

The patient reported irregular meal times due to her busy schedule. When unable to eat a meal, she would supplement with juice or fruit.

Alcohol Consumption

The patient occasionally drank alcohol until 30 years old, but had since ceased drinking.

Smoking

She was a current smoker, with a 35‐year history of smoking 5–10 cigarettes/day.

On December 25, 202X, a bystander found the patient lying near her front door and called an ambulance. On arrival, the ambulance team gauged her level of consciousness as a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E3V5M6 and was gradually deteriorating. She was immediately transferred to our hospital.

On arrival at our hospital, the patient was unconscious (a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E1V1M1). Right conjugate deviation of gaze was observed, along with facial spasms. Twenty milligrams of diazepam was administered intravenously. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed no obvious cerebral hemorrhage or infarction (Figure 1). Diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) showed subtle areas of hyperintensity in both hemispheres. Arterial spin labeling (ASL) indicated increased blood flow (Figure 2). Blood glucose level was 708 mg/dL and HbA1c was 16% (Table 1). Testing for urinary ketones yielded negative results. The attending physician in the emergency department consulted our department regarding treatment of the high blood glucose level as a possible cause of the disturbance of consciousness.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The physical characteristics of the patient were as follows: height, 155.5 cm; weight, 62.3 kg; body mass index, 25.8 kg/m2; and body temperature, 35.6°C. Blood pressure was significantly high, at 220/115 mmHg, accompanied by tachycardia (heart rate, 106 beats/min). However, peripheral oxygen saturation remained normal, at 98% in room air.

Regarding the level of consciousness, assessments showed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of E4V1M1, indicating altered consciousness when we checked the patient.

Physical examination of the head and neck revealed right‐directed horizontal nystagmus. Seizures, mainly on the right side of the face, were observed and 20 mg of diazepam was administered. Neither pale conjunctiva nor thyroid gland enlargement was observed. Auscultation of the lungs detected no abnormal sounds, and heart auscultation showed a regular rhythm without murmurs. The abdomen was soft and flat, with normal bowel sounds. No swelling was evident in the lower legs.

Plasma osmotic pressure was 314 Osm/L and urinary osmolality was 561 Osm/L. Based on these findings, HHS was diagnosed (Diabetes Treatment Guidelines in Japan). Treatment with massive fluid replacement and continuous venous insulin lispro infusion was initiated. On Day 2, the patient appeared to speak only in nonsensical syllables, such as “bububu” and “sesesese.” On Day 3, an insulin regimen on a sliding scale was started and vildagliptin was prescribed. The patient showed gradual improvements in level of consciousness, opening her eyes and moving her limbs, although she remained aphasic. The diagnosis was hyperglycemia‐induced encephalopathy with the aphasia likely resulting from dominant involvement of the left hemisphere.

Metformin was initiated on Day 4. The patient attempted to drink some soup, but ended up biting into the bowl. Speech and conduct remained incoherent, with the patient making statements such as, “I drink in the meantime,” “This is neat,” and “Calorie. Calorie.”

By Day 9, speech and walking had stabilized. We prescribed 3 units of subcutaneous insulin lispro before each meal and 8 units of subcutaneous insulin glargine at bedtime, along with 100 mg/day of empagliflozin. On Day 10, fasting blood glucose level before breakfast fell to 131 mg/dL, compared to 213 mg/dL before lunch earlier that day and 187 mg/dL before dinner that day. We discontinued insulin lispro and gradually decreased the dose of insulin glargine. By Day 13, all insulin had been discontinued and the patient was discharged in an ambulant state. In addition to diabetes treatment, we managed seizures using antiepileptic drugs from the early stages of hospitalization. Specifically, levetiracetam was administered by intravenous infusion at 1000 mg/day on Days 2–4. Fosphenytoin sodium hydrate was administered by intravenous drip at 750 mg on Day 2 and 250 mg/day on Days 3 and 4. Levetiracetam (1000 mg/day) was prescribed from Day 5. On Day 10, the dose of levetiracetam was decreased to 500 mg/day for 7 days, and levetiracetam was discontinued on Day 17.

Conclusion and Results

We encountered a diabetic patient with impaired consciousness attributed to hyperglycemia. ASL may provide useful information regarding abnormalities of cerebral perfusion in patients with HHS presenting with impairment of consciousness. The link between glucose metabolism and cerebral perfusion thus provides an alternative perspective on this pathophysiology.

Discussion

Among diabetic patients, the evaluation of perfusion in lower extremities showing ulcers and evaluation of renal blood flow and fibrosis in patients with chronic kidney disease can reportedly be performed without using contrast medium [1, 2, 3]. Other papers have reported the use of ASL to identify reduced regional perfusion in the occipital and parietal lobes among patients with type 2 diabetes compared to healthy individuals, and have indicated associations between reduced regional perfusion and the visual and cognitive dysfunctions commonly seen in diabetic patients [4].

In the present case, utilizing ASL provided important information regarding regional cerebral perfusion abnormalities in the patient with HHS. The neurological symptoms that occur in HHS are diverse. Positive symptoms such as seizures are caused by depletion of gamma‐aminobutyric acid from nerve cells, while negative symptoms such as hemianopia are considered to result from dehydration of glial cells due to high osmotic pressure and metabolic disorders [5, 6] However, the exact causes remain contentious. Despite the variety of symptoms, specific examinations and markers remain lacking and the neurological findings are difficult to identify promptly. In this case, however, ASL allowed identification of hyperperfusion in the left cerebral hemisphere, but was not apparent from other imaging studies. This finding suggested that the language disorder may have been associated with left hemispheric dysfunction including both Broca's area and Wernicke's area.

The patient exhibited facial seizures on arrival at our hospital, so it is possible that the hyperperfusion detected by ASL may have reflected peri‐ or postictal changes. On the other hand, metabolic encephalopathy associated with HHS may also have contributed to the neurological manifestations and perfusion abnormalities. In the absence of electroencephalography (EEG) and follow‐up ASL imaging, the relative contributions of these mechanisms could not be determined. In such cases, by the time the patient arrives at the hospital, the seizure has commonly already stopped, and witnesses are often unavailable. Because non‐convulsive status epilepticus can cause fatal or serious sequelae if not treated promptly and appropriately, EEG is necessary for diagnosis. However, EEG can only be performed and analyzed 24 h/day in hospitals with epilepsy centers, not usually in general emergency departments. On the other hand, the usefulness of ASL in the early diagnosis of cerebral infarction, epileptic overlap, and dementia has been reported in recent years [7]. Because MRI of the head is often performed to rule out intracranial disease in cases of impaired consciousness, adding ASL to the routine may facilitate prompt diagnosis [8].

This report has several limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting the findings. First, a standardized aphasia battery was not applied, limiting our ability to objectively characterize the language dysfunction. Second, because EEG was not performed, the potential for subclinical seizure activity to have influenced cerebral perfusion cannot be fully evaluated. Finally, the lack of follow‐up ASL imaging prevented confirmation of the normalization of left‐hemisphere hyperperfusion, leaving definitive causal relationships between HHS, postictal changes, and observed perfusion patterns partially obscured.

Clinical applications of ASL will likely expand further as MRI equipment becomes more available and powerful and imaging techniques continue to improve. We expect that this technology will make significant contributions to precise diagnosis and treatment planning for HHS patients.
