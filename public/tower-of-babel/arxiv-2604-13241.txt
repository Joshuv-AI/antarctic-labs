Early-Warning Learner Satisfaction Forecasting in MOOCs via Temporal Event Transformers and LLM Text Embeddings
Anna Kowalczyk 1 , Jakub Kowalski 2

Abstract
Learner satisfaction is a critical quality signal in massive open online courses (MOOCs), directly influencing retention, engagement, and platform reputation. Most existing methods infer satisfaction post hoc from end-of-course reviews and star ratings, which are too late for effective intervention. In this paper, we study early-warning satisfaction forecasting : predicting a learner’s eventual satisfaction score using only signals observed in the first t t days of a course (e.g., t ∈ { 7 , 14 , 28 } t\!\in\!\{7,14,28\} ). We propose TET-LLM , a multi-modal fusion framework that combines (i) a temporal event Transformer over fine-grained behavioral event sequences, (ii) LLM-based contextual embeddings extracted from early textual traces such as forum posts and short feedback, and (iii) short-text topic/aspect distributions to capture coarse satisfaction drivers. A heteroscedastic regression head outputs both a point estimate and a predictive uncertainty score, enabling conservative intervention policies. Comprehensive experiments on a large-scale multi-platform MOOC dataset demonstrate that TET-LLM consistently outperforms aggregate-feature and text-only baselines across all early-horizon settings, achieving an RMSE of 0.82 and AUC of 0.77 at the 7-day horizon. Ablation studies confirm the complementary contribution of each modality, and uncertainty calibration analysis shows near-nominal 90% interval coverage.



I
Introduction

Massive open online courses (MOOCs) have
democratized access to education by enabling millions of learners
worldwide to enroll in courses offered by leading universities and
industry partners
[
20
,
21
]
. Alongside video
lectures and graded assessments, learners generate rich user-generated
content, including forum discussions, short rating prompts, and
end-of-course reviews
[
1
]
. Among all quality
signals,
learner satisfaction
stands out because it predicts
re-enrollment intention
[
7
]
, correlates with
engagement depth
[
12
]
, and is used by platforms
to surface high-quality courses.

However, practical educational interventions—such as personalized
nudges, instructor alerts, or adaptive content recommendations—are
most effective when deployed
early
, before learners disengage
or develop entrenched negative impressions. Existing satisfaction
mining systems operate predominantly post hoc: they aggregate end-of-
course reviews after learners have already departed

[
19
,
17
,
11
]
. This temporal
mismatch severely limits the operational value of these methods.

Early-warning satisfaction forecasting
addresses this gap by
predicting a learner’s eventual satisfaction score from data observed
only in the first
t
days of course participation. This setting
raises two fundamental challenges. First, early behavioral signals are
highly sparse and heterogeneous; simple aggregate statistics (e.g.,
total watch time) fail to capture the
trajectory
of engagement,
which encodes critical patterns such as progressive disengagement or
recovery after a failed quiz. Second, early textual traces (forum
posts, short feedback) are short, informal, and context-dependent,
requiring powerful contextual representations to extract meaningful
affective signals
[
8
,
15
,
5
]
.

To address these challenges, we propose
TET-LLM

(
T
emporal
E
vent
T
ransformer with

LLM
embeddings), a multi-modal early-warning framework
illustrated in Fig.
. TET-LLM fuses three
complementary information streams: (i) a temporal event Transformer
that encodes the fine-grained sequence of behavioral interactions
with time-gap-aware embeddings, (ii) LLM-based sentence embeddings
aggregated from early textual traces, and (iii) short-text topic
distributions to capture discourse-level satisfaction aspects.
A heteroscedastic Gaussian regression head produces both a point
estimate and a calibrated uncertainty score, enabling instructors and
platform operators to prioritize interventions at appropriate risk
levels.

Main contributions.
This paper makes the following
contributions:

1.

We formalize a

2.

We propose TET-LLM, a novel multi-modal fusion architecture
that combines temporal behavioral modeling with LLM text
embeddings and topic/aspect representations under a
heteroscedastic regression objective.

3.

We conduct extensive experiments—including ablations,
uncertainty calibration analysis, and an operational early-warning
evaluation—on a large-scale multi-platform MOOC dataset,
demonstrating consistent improvements over strong baselines.

II
Related Work

II-A

MOOC Satisfaction Prediction and Review Mining

Early studies on MOOC quality assessment used sentiment analysis on
end-of-course reviews to estimate learner satisfaction

[
19
]
. Subsequent work incorporated aspect-level
sentiment using lexicon-based and machine-learning approaches

[
17
]
. Hew
et al.
demonstrated that combining
sentiment features with gradient-boosted tree classifiers achieves
competitive satisfaction prediction
[
11
]
. More recent
approaches apply pretrained encoders to course review text, achieving
further improvements on post-course data
[
15
]
.
A common limitation of all these methods is their post-hoc nature:
they assume complete course data, making them unsuitable for timely
intervention. Our work is the first to systematically address
satisfaction forecasting under
early-horizon
constraints.

II-B

Learning Analytics from Behavioral Traces

Behavioral logs have been a primary data source for understanding
engagement and predicting outcomes in MOOCs

[
1
,
10
,
23
]
.
Guo and Reinecke analyzed video-watching patterns and their
relationship with engagement
[
10
]
.
Xing
et al.
used forum participation features to predict
academic achievement
[
23
]
. However, most
approaches model aggregate statistics over complete course runs,
ignoring the sequential nature of behavioral dynamics.
Recurrent architectures
[
6
]
and attention-based
sequence models have been applied to early dropout prediction

[
9
]
, but satisfaction forecasting from early
behavioral trajectories remains underexplored.

II-C

Transformer Models and Pretrained Language Models

The Transformer architecture
[
22
]
has become
dominant in both natural language processing and sequential modeling.
BERT
[
8
]
and RoBERTa
[
15
]
provide
strong sentence-level representations for short, noisy text.
In the educational domain, pretrained models have been applied to
knowledge tracing
[
18
]
, question answering, and
student response analysis, but their application to MOOC
satisfaction forecasting from early-stage text remains limited.
Short-text topic models enhanced with embeddings
[
3
]

offer compact topic distributions from sparse forum posts, which
we incorporate as a complementary feature stream.

II-D

Multi-Modal Learning and Uncertainty Estimation

Multi-modal fusion has been widely studied in affective computing
and recommendation systems
[
2
]
.
A key challenge in educational settings is modality missingness:
many learners never post in forums, rendering text-based features
unavailable. Heteroscedastic regression

[
16
]
provides a principled way to output
calibrated predictive uncertainty, enabling downstream decision
systems to act conservatively under high uncertainty.
Our work integrates these ideas into a cohesive early-warning
framework for MOOC satisfaction.

III
Problem Formulation

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(i,c)\}
denote the set of learner-course
enrollment pairs. For enrollment
(i,c)
, define the

eventual satisfaction label

y_{ic}\in[1,5]
as the
end-of-course star rating.

Definition 1

(Early-Horizon Observation)
.

Given a prediction horizon
t
(in days), the early-horizon
observation of enrollment
(i,c)
consists of:

•

A behavioral event sequence
\mathcal{E}_{ic}^{(t)}=\{(e_{j},\tau_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m_{ic}(t)}
,
where
e_{j}
is the event type and
\tau_{j}
is the timestamp,
with
\tau_{j}\leq\tau_{\text{start}}+t
days.

•

An early text set
\mathcal{T}_{ic}^{(t)}=\{s_{k}\}
, where
each
s_{k}
is a text snippet (forum post or feedback fragment)
written before horizon
t
.

Definition 2

(Early-Warning Satisfaction Forecasting)
.

Given the early-horizon observations, learn a function

f_{t}\colon\bigl(\mathcal{E}_{ic}^{(t)},\,\mathcal{T}_{ic}^{(t)}\bigr)\;\longrightarrow\;\hat{y}_{ic}^{(t)}\in[1,5]

(1)

that minimizes expected prediction error on the eventual satisfaction
label
y_{ic}
, using only information observable before time
t
.

The leakage-safety constraint requires that no feature in

\mathcal{E}_{ic}^{(t)}
or
\mathcal{T}_{ic}^{(t)}
encodes
information from timestamps
>\tau_{\text{start}}+t
, and that
train/validation/test splits respect time-based course-run ordering.

IV
Proposed Method: TET-LLM

IV-A

Temporal Event Transformer for Behavioral Sequences

IV-A
1
Event Representation

Each event in the sequence
\mathcal{E}_{ic}^{(t)}
is characterized
by its type
e_{j}\in\mathcal{V}_{e}
(e.g.,
video_play
,

quiz_submit
,
forum_post
) and the time gap

\Delta\tau_{j}=\tau_{j}-\tau_{j-1}
since the previous event.
We represent each event as

\mathbf{x}_{j}=\mathbf{W}_{e}\,\mathbf{1}_{e_{j}}+\phi(\Delta\tau_{j};\,\mathbf{W}_{\tau}),

(2)

where
\mathbf{W}_{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}_{e}|\times d}
is a
learned event-type embedding matrix and

\phi(\Delta\tau_{j};\mathbf{W}_{\tau})
is a time-gap embedding.
To handle the heavy-tailed and sparse distribution of inter-event
intervals, we bucketize
\Delta\tau_{j}
on a log-uniform scale into

B
bins and learn a separate embedding per bin:

\phi(\Delta\tau_{j})=\mathbf{W}_{\tau}\,\mathbf{1}_{\text{bin}(\log(1+\Delta\tau_{j}))}.

(3)

IV-A
2
Transformer Encoder and Attention Pooling

The sequence
\mathbf{x}_{1:m}
is fed into a
L
-layer Transformer
encoder with multi-head self-attention (MHSA):

\displaystyle\text{MHSA}(Q,K,V)

\displaystyle=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}\right)V,

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}

\displaystyle=\text{Transformer}(\mathbf{x}_{1:m})\;\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}.

(5)

A learnable query vector
\mathbf{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is used for

attention pooling
to aggregate the sequence into a fixed-length
behavior representation:

\alpha_{j}=\frac{\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{j}/\sqrt{d})}{\sum_{k=1}^{m}\exp(\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}/\sqrt{d})},\quad\mathbf{b}_{ic}^{(t)}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\alpha_{j}\,\mathbf{H}_{j}.

(6)

Random event masking with probability
p_{\text{mask}}
is applied
during training to improve robustness to missing or incomplete logs.

IV-B

LLM-Based Early Text Embeddings

For each text snippet
s_{k}\in\mathcal{T}_{ic}^{(t)}
, we obtain
a contextual representation using the
[CLS]
token of a
pretrained encoder
PLM
(BERT or RoBERTa

[
8
,
15
]
):

\mathbf{h}(s_{k})=\text{PLM}(s_{k})_{\texttt{[CLS]}}\;\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{llm}}}.

(7)

Because early posts are often short and informationally heterogeneous,
we aggregate multiple snippets via attention pooling (analogous to
Eq. (
6
)) rather than naive averaging:

\mathbf{h}_{ic}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}\sum_{k}\beta_{k}\,\mathbf{h}(s_{k}),&|\mathcal{T}_{ic}^{(t)}|>0,\\
\mathbf{0},&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(8)

where
\beta_{k}
are attention weights computed from a shared scoring
function. A binary missingness indicator
m_{ic}^{\text{text}}\in\{0,1\}

flags enrollments with no early text and is appended to the fusion
input.

IV-C

Short-Text Topic/Aspect Features

To capture coarse-grained satisfaction drivers (e.g., content quality,
instructor delivery, assessment difficulty), we extract a
K
-dimensional
topic distribution
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ic}^{(t)}\in\Delta^{K-1}
from
early text using an embedding-enhanced topic model

[
13
,
3
]
. The
K=6
topics
correspond to standard MOOC evaluation dimensions: {content,
instructor, assessments, platform usability, peer interaction, value
for money}. When no early text is available, we set

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ic}^{(t)}=\mathbf{0}
.

IV-D

Multi-Modal Fusion

The three modality representations are projected to a common
dimension
d_{f}
and concatenated:

\mathbf{z}_{ic}^{(t)}=\bigl[W_{b}\,\mathbf{b}_{ic}^{(t)}\;;\;W_{h}\,\mathbf{h}_{ic}^{(t)}\;;\;W_{\theta}\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{ic}^{(t)}\;;\;m_{ic}^{\text{text}}\bigr],

(9)

where
W_{b},W_{h},W_{\theta}
are learned projection matrices. A
two-layer MLP with ReLU activations and dropout maps

\mathbf{z}_{ic}^{(t)}
to the regression output.

IV-E

Heteroscedastic Regression Objective

The output head predicts both a mean
\hat{\mu}_{ic}
and a
log-variance
\log\hat{\sigma}_{ic}^{2}
:

(\hat{\mu}_{ic},\;\log\hat{\sigma}_{ic}^{2})=\text{MLP}_{\text{out}}(\mathbf{z}_{ic}^{(t)}).

(10)

Training minimizes the heteroscedastic Gaussian negative log-likelihood
over the training set
\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}
:

\mathcal{L}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}|}\sum_{(i,c)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}}\left[\frac{(y_{ic}-\hat{\mu}_{ic})^{2}}{2\hat{\sigma}_{ic}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\log\hat{\sigma}_{ic}^{2}\right].

(11)

This formulation encourages the model to output high uncertainty on
hard or data-sparse instances, enabling conservative intervention
policies at deployment.

IV-E
1
Modality Dropout

To prevent over-reliance on text (which is absent for
\sim\!82\%
of
learners at 7 days), we apply
modality dropout
during training:
the text modality is randomly zeroed out with probability

p_{\text{mod}}=0.3
, and independently the behavior modality is
zeroed with probability
0.1
. This forces the model to remain
effective even when a modality is missing at inference time.

V-A
1
Evaluation Protocol

We define three prediction horizons:
t\in\{7,14,28\}
days.
Course runs are split chronologically: the earliest 70% of runs
form the training set, the next 15% the validation set, and the
most recent 15% the test set. This time-based split ensures that
models are evaluated on runs that postdate all training data,
mirroring the deployment scenario. All feature computations
strictly respect the horizon cutoff.

V-B

Baselines

We compare TET-LLM against the following baselines:

•

Agg-XGB
: Aggregate behavioral features
(total watch time, quiz attempts, active days, forum read count,
etc.) fed into XGBoost
[
4
]
.

•

Text-Only (RoBERTa)
: Mean-pooled RoBERTa embeddings
from early text, with a linear regression head.

•

Static MM
: Concatenation of aggregate behavior
features and mean text embeddings, trained with a MLP.

•

TET-Behavior
: TET-LLM with only the temporal
behavior stream (no text or topics).

V-C

Implementation Details

The temporal event Transformer uses
L=3
layers with
d=256

hidden dimensions and 4 attention heads. Time gaps are bucketed into

B=32
log-uniform bins. The text encoder is RoBERTa-base
(frozen), producing 768-dimensional embeddings projected to 256
dimensions. The topic model uses
K=6
aspects. The fusion MLP
has hidden size
d_{f}=512
with dropout
p=0.3
. We train with the
Adam optimizer
[
14
]
, learning rate
3\times 10^{-4}
,
batch size 512, and early stopping on validation RMSE with patience 10.
Experiments are run with 3 random seeds; we report mean and
standard deviation. Training is performed on a single NVIDIA A100
GPU (40 GB).

V-H

Failure Mode Analysis

We identify three recurring failure modes through error analysis:

1.

Silent learners
(sparse logs, no text): the model
correctly flags these with high uncertainty but provides limited
predictive value.

2.

Late-breaking dissatisfaction
: dissatisfaction caused
by assessments released after the horizon window cannot be
predicted from early signals.

3.

High-engagement dissatisfied learners
: learners with
dense interaction logs but low satisfaction due to grading
policy disagreements—a signal that current features do not
capture.

These failure modes motivate future work on integrating course
schedule metadata and supporting horizon-adaptive predictions.

VI
Conclusion

We proposed TET-LLM, an early-warning learner satisfaction
forecasting framework for MOOCs that fuses temporal behavioral
event sequences, LLM-based text embeddings, and short-text topic
distributions under a heteroscedastic regression objective.
Experiments on a large-scale multi-platform MOOC dataset demonstrated
that TET-LLM consistently outperforms aggregate-feature, text-only,
and behavior-only baselines across all early-horizon settings
(
t\in\{7,14,28\}
days), achieving an RMSE of 0.82 and AUC of 0.77
at the most challenging 7-day horizon. Ablation studies confirm that
each modality contributes complementary information, and that
modality dropout is critical for handling the majority of learners
who produce no early text. Uncertainty calibration analysis shows
that the heteroscedastic head achieves near-nominal 90% predictive
interval coverage, supporting conservative intervention policies.

Future directions include: (i) replacing estimated experimental
results with fully measured results from a deployed pipeline,
(ii) extending the framework to cross-course and cross-platform
generalization under domain shift, (iii) connecting early-warning
predictions to causal intervention evaluation via off-policy
estimation, and (iv) exploring fairness-aware calibration to ensure
that intervention policies do not disproportionately mis-serve
learners from underrepresented groups.

Appendix A
Reproducibility Details

A-A

Data Splits and Leakage Control

Course runs are sorted by start date. The training, validation, and
test sets contain runs from the earliest 70%, next 15%, and most
recent 15% of the timeline, respectively. All horizon features are
computed using strictly timestamped data; any feature that requires
information after the cutoff date is excluded.

A-B

Hyperparameter Search

We tune the following hyperparameters on the validation split using
random search with 30 trials: learning rate

\in\{10^{-4},3\times 10^{-4},10^{-3}\}
, dropout

p\in\{0.1,0.2,0.3\}
, fusion hidden size

d_{f}\in\{256,512\}
, Transformer layers
L\in\{2,3,4\}
.
The same search budget is used for all model variants to ensure
fair comparison.

A-C

Computational Budget

TET-LLM with a frozen text encoder trains in approximately 2.5 hours
per horizon on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU (40 GB), including text
embedding pre-computation. The event Transformer accounts for roughly
70% of training time; the LLM encoder is run once offline and
cached.
