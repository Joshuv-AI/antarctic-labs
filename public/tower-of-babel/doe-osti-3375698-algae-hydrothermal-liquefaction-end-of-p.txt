 Summary
 This document is a close-out report for two Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) projects
 funded by the Alternative Fuels and Feedstocks Office (formerly the Bioenergy Technologies
 Office) of the Department of Energy (DOE).

 The two projects are:
  Thermochemical Interface (DOE ref. no. 1.3.4.101, PNNL ref. no. 64490)
  HTL Model Development (DOE ref. no. 1.3.5.202, PNNL ref. no. 71612)

 At the end of fiscal year 2025, both projects ended. Additional activities related to the topics of
 algae, hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL), and the techno-economic assessment of HTL of algal
 feedstocks will be studied in other projects.

 Key technical findings of the projects were published in early 2025 [1] and highlights will be
 summarized in this document. A summary of recent technical findings from 2025 are also included
 herein. This close-out report summarizes key outcomes from both projects highlighting the value
 and technical impact achieved.

 1.0 Introduction
 In September 2025, researchers at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) completed two
 key projects related to the hydrothermal liquefaction (HTL) of algae to produce biofuels. The first
 project, initiated in 2013 as “Thermochemical Interface,” focused on the technology development
 for HTL processing of algal slurries. The overall objective of the project was to address scale-up
 barriers and further advance the commercialization of HTL processing and the subsequent
 upgrading of the HTL products. Throughout the project, several improvements were discovered
 to improve the technical feasibility and economic viability of HTL with algae as a feedstock. A
 large variety of micro- and macro-algal feedstocks were also tested to prove their viability.

 The second project, “HTL Model Development” was started in 2017 and complemented the
 Thermochemical Interface project by providing focused techno-economic assessments that
 guided and tracked the technological developments through economic metrics. For several years,
 both projects contributed to the publication of state-of-technology (SOT) reports. Six consecutive
 reports were published, starting in 2020, with each report summarizing key technology and/or
 process improvements to HTL. The improvement in breakeven selling price (minimum fuel selling
 price [MFSP]) was reported to quantify the value of the technical improvements for a
 conceptualized industrial-scale process.

 The purpose of this document is to highlight the value and technical impact of the project achieved
 through publications, patents, and licenses.

Introduction 1

 2.0 Key Research Outcomes
 Through both projects, key innovations and research outcomes were produced. The following
 paragraphs highlight key findings and the relevant publications that detail each finding.

 Minimizing Costs

 One of the objectives of the annual SOT reports was to quantify the technical improvements as a
 reduction in MFSP for the finished fuel product from algal HTL. The SOT reports highlighted how
 cost of equipment and materials influenced the final fuel cost. Ultimately, the cost of the feedstock
 was determined to be the most significant contributor to the cost of the fuel. The cost contribution
 of pond-cultivated microalgae was at least $5.54/gasoline gallon equivalent (GGE) (2025$).
 Developing cost-advantaged or zero-cost algae improved cost calculations, resulting in MFSP
 values as low as $3.73/GGE (2025$). Wastewater-grown algae provided the best cost outcomes,
 achieving low MFSP values while maintaining a reasonable yield of biocrude and scalability
 comparable to pond-cultivated algae. Figure 1 summarizes MFSP calculations from several SOT
 reports, showing the relationship between scale of the HTL system and feedstock type with
 respect to the MFSP (2020$) [1]. The figure shows two categories of feedstocks, zero-cost
 feedstocks, shown in the gray-shaded region, and purchased feedstocks (pond-cultivated),
 shown in the blue-shaded region. Because of the higher cost of farm-grown feedstock, a facility
 with a larger production capacity is needed to achieve the benefits of economies of scale.

 Figure 1. Biomass conversion scale, minimum product selling price, and feedstock source
 for HTL scenarios modeled between 2014 and 2024. Scenarios marked with an
 asterisk (*) exclude the cost of hydrotreating biocrude to finished fuel products. Costs
 shown are in 2020$; additional details about the figure are published previously [1].

Key Research Outcomes 2

 Storage and Pretreatment

 Research in the project demonstrated the stability of the algal biomass for HTL processing after
 >100 days of anaerobic storage with the addition of Lactobacillus plantarum [2]. Demonstrating
 storage stability ensures that seasonally produced algae can be later processed with comparable
 performance to fresh feedstocks. HTL biocrude from the stored and fresh algae showed negligible
 differences in yield and composition.

 As feedstocks shifted toward low-cost but high-ash biomass, such as wastewater-grown algae
 and algal bloom biomass, pretreatment methods were examined to remove ash precipitates prior
 to HTL processing. Brown et al. successfully demonstrated a 21 wt. % reduction in ash content,
 with minimal loss (8 wt. %) of organic content, using pH adjustment (9.5) and hydrocyclone
 processing [3]. The reduced ash content is expected to facilitate HTL processing, reducing the
 total volume of material to be processed and enabling higher carbon retention in the biocrude.

 Scale-up

 Experimental data and process expertise significantly contributed to the design and operation of
 the engineering-scale HTL system at PNNL. The process knowledge for HTL, which was gained
 mostly from processing algal feedstocks, was used to inform scale-up of the bench-scale HTL
 equipment (up to 5 liters per hour) to the modular hydrothermal liquefaction system ([MHTLS] up
 to 16 liters per hour) [4]. The MHTLS is part of the process development unit capability at PNNL
 for hydrothermal conversion, shown in Figure 2. The MHTLS is a focal point for HTL research at
 PNNL, enabling scaled-up, large-volume demonstrations of HTL conversion across a variety of
 biomass feedstocks.

 Figure 2. Photo of the MHTLS at PNNL.

Key Research Outcomes 3

 Jet Fuel

 The project reported the first ever production of synthetic aviation fuel (SAF) from algae via HTL
 [5]. Picochlorum celeri was hydrothermally processed and the resulting biocrude was
 hydroprocessed to finished fuels. The jet fuel cut was isolated and assessed for jet fuel properties,
 showing that many of the properties were within specification; Figure 3 shows the results of the
 jet fuel testing. In later research, SAF was also produced from wastewater-grown algae, producing
 a similar quality of fuel products [6].

 Figure 3. Characterization results from the SAF sample derived from P. celeri showing (a) the
 distribution of carbon number and hydrocarbon type and (b) distillation profile [5].

 Co-Products

 Although the production of biocrude as a fuel intermediate was the primary goal for HTL research,
 several valuable and scalable co-products were also considered to improve the overall value of
 HTL processing. Extractable sugars for fermentation [7] or even precipitated fertilizer products
 (e.g., struvite) were examined in the project [8]. Seed germination studies proved the viability of
 the direct usage of HTL solids as a fertilizer substitute. Figure 4 shows results of the seed
 germination study as seeds were planted with different concentrations of HTL solids, up to 100
 wt. %. All the seeds at lower concentrations (5, 10, 25 wt. %) successfully germinated, and only
 the seeds in 100 wt. % solids were unable to germinate.

Key Research Outcomes 4

 Figure 4. Seedlings fertilized with HTL solids increasing up to 100 wt. % concentration.

 Alternatively, the HTL solids from high-ash algae, such as wastewater-grown algae or algal bloom
 biomass, produced a material suitable for use as a cement additive [9]. Specifically, HTL solids
 were blended into cement formulations as a supplemental cementitious material (SCM). The HTL
 solids from algal bloom biomass were particularly high in concentration for aluminum, silicon, and
 calcium, which help improve pozzolanic activity. The use of HTL solids would supplement other
 SCM materials, such as coal fly ash. This was the first published use of HTL solids as an SCM.

 The biocrude was examined as an asphalt binder for pavement applications [10-13]. The novel
 use of biocrude in this way eliminated the need for additional refinement or hydrotreatment after
 HTL. In fact, the blending of algal biocrudes into bitumen formulations improved the moisture
 resistance, self-healing capability, and thermal cracking (at low temperatures). The improved
 characteristics have been attributed to the fatty acids and amides from the algae. Analysis and
 Modeling

 Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FTICR-MS) was utilized to
 understand the transformation of biomass into crude-like molecules. Using the precision of
 FTICR-MS assisted in understanding how porphyrins from algae decomposed during HTL [14].
 Porphyrins are specific to chlorophyll-rich biomass, such as blue-green microalgae, and it was
 important to understand how the release of iron from the porphyrin complex could impact
 downstream catalytic upgrading of the biocrude to finished fuels. The removal of iron before
 hydrotreatment was highlighted as a priority to limit catalyst deactivation in hydrotreatment
 processes.

Key Research Outcomes 5

 HTL biocrudes from pine wood and algae were assessed via FTICR-MS. The analysis revealed
 synergistic reactions that occur during HTL when feedstocks are blended together, producing new
 compounds that are not present from single feedstocks [15]. The addition of algae to pine wood
 during HTL was found to be beneficial in moderating the pH and preventing certain polymerization
 reactions that would ultimately produce more solids than biocrude.

 The data generated from several algae-HTL experiments were used to develop a predictive model
 for the yield and elemental composition of the HTL products from algae [16]. Creating a simple
 model to predict HTL outcomes enabled the use of Monte Carlo simulation to examine thousands
 of possible processing scenarios and estimate uncertainty in the predicted values for MFSP.
 Uncertainty in the MFSP was ±12%, which equates to variations of up to ±$0.50/GGE for
 predicted values.

 3.0 Scientific and Commercial Impact
 Overall, the two projects provided scientific products, external awards, and commercial patents.

 Between the two projects, numerous publications, reports, and presentations were produced,
 providing contributions toward the technical knowledge of HTL within the scientific community. A
 listing of the peer-reviewed publications (18), technical reports (11), and conference presentations
 and invited talks (24) are provided in Appendix A.

 In addition to the published technical content, two patents were issued for technology
 developments that were generated by the project team. Both patents have been successfully
 licensed for commercial utilization.

 1. Elliott, D., J. Oyler. Issued on November 4, 2014. “Methods for Sulfate Removal in Liquid-
 Phase Catalytic Hydrothermal Gasification of Biomass.” U.S. Patent #8,877,098.

 2. Thorson, M., L. Snowden-Swan, A. Schmidt, T. Hart, J. Billing, D. Anderson, and R.
 Hallen. Issued March 2022. “Hydrothermal liquefaction system.” U.S. Patent #11,279,882.

 The issued patents and subsequent licensing were recognized by two external organizations. In
 2015, PNNL was recognized with an R&D100 award for “Hydrothermal Processing to Convert
 Wet Biomass into Biofuels” [17]. The R&D100 awards recognize the innovation and significance
 of new technologies available for licensing.

 Also in 2015, the Federal Laboratory Consortium for Technology Transfer (FLC) awarded the
 project team for “Excellence in Technology Transfer.” The FLC was created by Congress in 1974
 to recognize the work accomplished by federal laboratories and research centers. The Excellence
 in Technology Transfer Award recognizes outstanding work in transferring federally developed
 technology into the commercial sector. The award specifically recognized the successful licensing
 of PNNL-developed HTL technology to Genifuel [18].

 The foundational research for algae HTL led to collaborations with research consortia, national
 laboratories, universities, and commercial organizations throughout the project’s lifetime.
 Additional parallel projects continued to develop the topic in novel and valuable research
 directions. One example is from PNNL’s contributions to the National Alliance for Advanced
 Biofuels and Bio-products. Early research examined coupling HTL of algae biomass with lipid
 extraction or catalytic hydrothermal gasification to boost the total yield of energy products [19].
 Another example is a parallel project funded by the U.S. Army Engineer Research and
 Development Center (ERDC) to examine the HTL of algal bloom biomass harvested from
 freshwater lakes. The project with ERDC led to a separate FLC award, in 2023, in the category
 of impact, recognizing tangible and lasting impact from successful technology transfer [20].

 4.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
 Research at PNNL has contributed to the advancement and technology development for algal
 fuels and products produced via HTL. Innovations and findings in storage stability, preprocessing,
 fertilizer and cement co-products, and the first demonstrated production of algae-derived SAF
 have expanded the technical feasibility of algae HTL while strengthening its economic viability.
 Advanced analytical studies and modeling have improved understanding of product compositions,
 uncertainty quantification, and predictive modeling.

 The projects have delivered impactful scientific contributions through high-quality publications,
 patents, and awards for technology transfer. The accomplishments at PNNL highlight the
 substantial technical value and broad impact from the projects. The research for algae HTL at
 PNNL was fundamental for developing additional research topics and projects in HTL.

 In the 2024 report, three key recommendations were proposed for continued improvements for
 the technical and economic feasibility of algae HTL [1].

 1. Reducing capital intensity

 There are still opportunities to innovate the commercial-scale design for HTL, maximizing
 biocrude yield and quality (low nitrogen and oxygen content) while minimizing equipment cost and
 complexity. Reducing operating temperature and pressure, maximizing resource (carbon and
 energy) utilization, or catalyzing HTL reactions will reduce costs at any process scale (pilot to
 commercial).

 2. Other co-products or alternatives

 The HTL biorefinery will benefit from a broad variety of co-products to achieve economic viability.
 Initial investigations for fertilizers and SCMs are promising, but additional research is needed to
 fully validate their potential. Other co-products should be considered for technical and economic
 viability.

 3. Scaling cost-advantaged feedstocks via blending opportunities

 The cost of algal feedstock is a limiting factor for utilization. Continued investigation of alternative,
 cost-advantaged algae or other feedstocks will reduce the MFSP. However, as low-cost
 feedstocks are considered, the trade-offs with respect to quality and availability will also need
 consideration.
