Abortion policies in DRC and Nigeria: public health impacts

The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and Nigeria, two of Africa’s largest and most populous nations, present unique yet interconnected narratives on abortion law and women’s reproductive rights. Both countries grapple with alarmingly high maternal mortality rates, a significant proportion of which is driven by complications from unsafe abortions.1 These challenges, rooted in restrictive legal frameworks, inadequate healthcare infrastructure and sociocultural barriers, highlight the critical need for reform in reproductive health policies and practices.

In the DRC, maternal mortality stands at an estimated 427 deaths per 100 000 live births.2 In Nigeria, the case is even worse, with maternal mortality rates reaching 993 deaths per 100 000 live births.3 One of the significant factors for these figures is unsafe abortion, especially in rural and underserved areas where access to quality reproductive healthcare remains restricted. According to the WHO, unsafe abortion contributes to 13% of global maternal deaths,4 but in Sub-Saharan Africa, it accounts for over 30%.5 These numbers highlight the significance of unsafe abortion as a public health crisis in the region, which demands urgent attention and action.

The African Union’s decision to adopt the Maputo Protocol in 2003 laid the groundwork for legal protections around women’s reproductive health, including access to safe abortion.6 While both the DRC and Nigeria have ratified the protocol, their strategies for implementation have followed drastically different trajectories. The DRC has significantly improved access to safe abortion, standing out among francophone African nations.7 Nonetheless, structural issues, including inadequate medical facilities, untrained personnel and cultural stigma, continue to obstruct full implementation of the protocol.8 For Nigeria, not being able to domesticate the protocol has kept abortion laws substantially unchanged, creating a resultant clandestine market of unsafe abortions.9 This policy paralysis reflects a missed opportunity to address one of the leading causes of maternal mortality.

Legal reforms in DRC: progress, challenges and opportunities

The DRC’s abortion laws, inherited from Belgian colonial rule, were among the most restrictive in Africa.10 The 1940 Penal Code criminalised abortion to promote population growth for labour exploitation, imposing harsh penalties on both women and providers.11 Despite subsequent reforms, access to safe abortion remains limited due to outdated legislation, stigma and weak healthcare infrastructure.12

Early legal exceptions and population control efforts

Like many African nations, the DRC’s restrictive abortion laws are the colonial artefacts of the Belgian Penal Code of 1940, which prohibited both contraception and abortion.13 Legislative progress came slowly, but the 1970 Code of Medical Ethics authorised abortion only for congenitally malformed foetuses and an immediate threat to the mother’s life, which had little practical effect due to the lack of healthcare professionals. It was not until the 1973 decree by the National Council for the promotion of desirable birth that contraceptive use was permitted, although strictly confined to birth spacing purposes.13

The cumulative toll of conflict and violence against Congolese women forced the government to adopt Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights as its priority following the 2006 post-conflict elections.14 Although the initial goal was to improve contraceptive uptake and address the crisis of unsafe abortions, advocates pushed their efforts on legal policy changes which would address the impunity, reparations for rape victims and access to safe abortion care, shifting the abortion question from a marginal legal and health issue into a central focus of human rights policy.7

The Maputo Protocol and its legal supremacy in the DRC

The DRC signed the Maputo Protocol in 2003 and ratified it in 2008, committing to Article 14’s reproductive health provisions, despite the strong opposition of the Catholic Church. The delay of nearly 5 years before official publication enabled the coming together of groups such as government institutions, NGOs and civil society organisations (CSOs) to assess the impacts of unsafe abortion and create a case for implementation.13

The Maputo Protocol’s publication in the DRC’s Journal Officiel in March 2018 was the legal turning point, as Article 215 of the Congolese Constitution dictates that ratified treaties automatically supersede domestic law on publication, giving them immediate force. This principle was then reaffirmed by a Constitutional Court mandate,15 which officially integrated the right to safe abortion into national law for cases of rape, incest, foetal anomalies and threats to a woman’s life or physical and mental health.16 Although the DRC’s reforms are one of the most comprehensive efforts on the African continent, accomplished concurrently for decriminalisation and service scale-up, practical access remains limited by low public awareness, weak healthcare infrastructure and enduring cultural stigma.13

Expanding reproductive rights: policy and reforms

Recognising the limitations of highly restrictive abortion frameworks, the DRC has implemented extensive reproductive health reforms that align with WHO recommendations.13 Unlike nations that impose narrow legal exceptions, the DRC has moved towards broader access, ensuring equity in abortion care for vulnerable women and girls.7

The process of domesticating the Maputo Protocol has catalysed a far-reaching transformation of women’s rights across the DRC, extending well beyond abortion access. Over the past decade, the government has enacted several progressive laws aimed at protecting and empowering women and girls.7 Key legislative changes include laws that mandate compensation for survivors of gender-based violence, amendments to electoral laws to promote women’s greater participation in civic and political life, and critical changes to the Family Code, such as eliminating the requirement for spousal consent for women to seek employment, thereby fostering economic autonomy. Additionally, programmes promoting ‘positive masculinity’ have been initiated to reduce gender-based discrimination and cultivate more equitable relationships between men and women throughout society.7

Challenges to implementation

Despite these legal milestones, several challenges persist. The 1940 Penal Code, with its outdated and restrictive provisions, remains unrevised, creating inconsistencies within the legal system. Healthcare providers and legal professionals often lack awareness about the Maputo Protocol’s provisions and its legal primacy, limiting its practical enforcement.17 Moreover, the dissemination of new guidelines has been insufficient, leaving many women and healthcare professionals uninformed about the legal grounds for safe abortion.17

Nigeria’s abortion landscape: legal fragmentation and access barriers

Nigeria’s legal framework is inherently complex, characterised by its dual legal system that reflects the country’s colonial legacy and sociocultural diversity. The dual system comprises the Criminal Code, rooted in British common law, which governs the 17 southern states, and the Penal Code, influenced by Scottish common law and Islamic jurisprudence, which applies to the 19 northern states and the Federal Capital Territory.18

This legal fragmentation creates significant disparities in the interpretation and application of abortion laws across the country, with further complications arising from the overlay of customary and religious laws, particularly in the northern region where Sharia law operates alongside the Penal Code.18

Failure to implement the Maputo Protocol

Both the Criminal and Penal Codes criminalise abortion, except to save a woman’s life, leaving no room for exceptions in cases of rape, incest or fetal anomalies.19 These frameworks impose penalties of up to 14 years’ imprisonment for individuals who attempt to procure an abortion. These restrictions not only violate women’s reproductive rights but also drive many to seek unsafe abortions, contributing to Nigeria’s high maternal mortality rate.9

Under Section 12 of the 1999 Nigeria Constitution, international treaties do not automatically become law unless ratified by the National Assembly.19 This requirement has stalled the Maputo Protocol’s implementation despite being ratified in 2004, as conservative lawmakers strongly oppose any attempt to broaden abortion access.20 Many lawmakers, influenced by religious and cultural conservatism, view abortion as a contentious moral issue rather than a public health necessity, leaving Nigeria’s abortion laws among the most prohibitive in Africa.9

Religious lobbying and stigma

The structural difference in religious demographics is an important factor in understanding the divergent paths in abortion reform between Nigeria and the DRC, although the mechanism is rooted in political influence rather than mere population size. Nigeria is characterised by a near-equal balance of Christianity and Islam (approximately 45% each),21 while the DRC has a predominantly Christian population (over 95%) with a small Muslim minority (around 2%).7

Consequently, religious parity creates a political deadlock in Nigeria. An attempt to liberalise abortion legislation, such as the domestication of the Maputo Protocol, is considered a compromise and an imposition of the ‘Western agenda’ on ‘moral’ norms.22 This opposition is institutionally reinforced with Sharia law in northern states prescribing harsh punishment and stigma alongside the Penal Code, while powerful Christian groups in the south lobby policymakers and influence healthcare providers.4

In the case of the DRC, the predominant Catholic Church strongly opposed reform.7 Nevertheless, due to the unprecedented extent of conflict-related sexual violence, which has been documented as a public health and humanitarian crisis,23 arguments were successfully reframed from moral debate to one of urgent public health and victim protection, neutralising religious opposition and creating political space for the Maputo Protocol’s domestication.9

DRC, Democratic Republic of Congo.

The Maputo Protocol, adopted in 2003, remains one of the most significant legal instruments for advancing women’s reproductive rights in Africa.20 Article 14(2)(c) of the protocol explicitly mandates that state parties ensure the right to medical abortion in cases of sexual assault, rape, incest or when the pregnancy endangers the mental or physical health of the mother.6 Despite these clear directives, implementation across African nations has been inconsistent, with countries like the DRC and Nigeria exhibiting starkly different responses. While the DRC has ratified the protocol and integrated its provisions into national law, systemic barriers continue to impede effective implementation.24 Nigeria, on the other hand, ratified the protocol but has failed to domesticate its provisions, leaving highly restrictive abortion laws in place and exacerbating maternal health risks.25

The DRC’s efforts to implement the Maputo Protocol have been heavily influenced by the alarming levels of sexual violence in the country.14 Often referred to as the ‘rape capital of the world’, the DRC has some of the highest reported rates of sexual violence globally.26 Women and girls, particularly in conflict-affected areas, face extreme violations of their rights, including mass rape, sexual slavery and forced pregnancies.26 According to humanitarian reports, thousands of women and girls are raped each year in the eastern provinces of the DRC, with sexual violence being used as a weapon of war by armed groups. The prevalence of unwanted pregnancies resulting from these rapes has fuelled urgent advocacy for the implementation of the Maputo Protocol, particularly in humanitarian regions where women’s rights are highly violated.27

Recognising the gravity of this crisis, advocacy organisations and civil society groups played a pivotal role in pushing for the domestication of the protocol. Organisations such as IPAS and the Youth Alliance for Reproductive Health (YARH) have been instrumental in championing abortion rights and ensuring that safe abortion services are made available to survivors of sexual violence.11 These organisations have engaged in extensive community outreach, educating women and healthcare providers about their legal rights under the Maputo Protocol. Through partnerships with healthcare facilities, they have supported the integration of safe abortion care into broader reproductive health services. YARH’s MASHUJAA Referral Network, in particular, stands out as a practical model for connecting women with timely, safe and confidential abortion care.

Nigeria presents an even more restrictive landscape. Although the country ratified the Maputo Protocol in 2004, its abortion laws remain among the most prohibitive in Africa due to strong political and religious opposition.9 Nigeria’s dual legal system creates inconsistencies in abortion laws. The religious divide between Christianity in the south and Islam in the north further impedes legal reform.22 As a result, abortion remains illegal except when necessary to save the mother’s life. This rigid stance has fuelled the growth of clandestine abortion practices, contributing to Nigeria’s alarmingly high maternal mortality rate.28

The impact of these restrictive policies is reflected in the severe public health challenges both countries continue to face. Unsafe abortion remains a major factor contributing to maternal deaths in the region,1 highlighting the urgent need to expand access to safe abortion as an essential component of reproductive healthcare. In both countries, lack of awareness about legal abortion provisions exacerbates the problem, leading many women to seek unsafe procedures performed by unqualified individuals.29 Healthcare providers often hesitate to offer post-abortion care due to stigma and fear of prosecution, further endangering women’s lives.30

Beyond mortality rates, the restrictive abortion laws in the DRC and Nigeria impose substantial social and economic burdens. Women who suffer complications from unsafe abortions often require emergency medical intervention, placing additional strain on already overburdened healthcare systems.31 The financial costs of treating abortion-related complications divert resources from other critical healthcare needs, including maternal and neonatal care. Additionally, women who experience post-abortion health complications may face long-term physical disabilities, limiting their ability to work and provide for their families.32 In many cases, young women and adolescent girls who become pregnant and lack access to safe abortion services are forced to drop out of school, perpetuating cycles of poverty and gender inequality.

Advancing safe abortion access: lessons from DRC and implications for Africa

Nigeria and other African countries can draw valuable lessons from the DRC’s approach to abortion law reform and the implementation of the Maputo Protocol. While significant challenges persist, the DRC has demonstrated that political will, strategic advocacy and strong civil society engagement can drive progress in reproductive rights.7 To advance safe abortion access across the continent, African nations must consider a multi-faceted approach that integrates legal reform, healthcare system strengthening, public education and sustainable funding strategies.

Legal and policy frameworks

One of the key takeaways from the DRC’s experience is the importance of aligning national laws with international human rights frameworks.16 Domestication of the Maputo Protocol in the DRC has expanded legal abortion access beyond colonial-era restrictions. Nigeria, in particular, should take concrete steps to incorporate the provisions of the Maputo Protocol into national legislation, ensuring that abortion is legally accessible under broader conditions, including cases of rape, incest, fetal anomalies and risks to a woman’s physical or mental health. Legal reform must also include clear guidelines for healthcare providers, preventing ambiguity in service provision and reducing the fear of legal repercussions among medical professionals.

Role of CSOs

CSOs play a critical role in driving policy change, raising public awareness and delivering essential reproductive health services.33 In the DRC, organisations like IPAS and YARH have been instrumental in increasing access to safe abortion services through community education, hospital partnerships and medical training programmes. Similar collaborations in Nigeria and other African nations can help bridge gaps in reproductive healthcare by fostering partnerships between government institutions, advocacy groups and professional medical associations.

Community engagement and cultural sensitivity

Community engagement has been a defining feature of the DRC’s approach to expanding abortion access.33 Advocacy efforts have successfully involved local leaders, religious figures and women’s rights activists, enabling meaningful dialogue in regions where abortion stigma remains deeply rooted. Other restrictive nations must prioritise culturally sensitive outreach programmes that address misinformation, challenge harmful gender norms and engage key stakeholders in discussions about reproductive rights and public health.

Strengthening healthcare infrastructure

Expanding access to safe abortion services requires significant investment in healthcare infrastructure as part of universal health coverage.34 The DRC’s experience highlights the need for targeted investments in hospital facilities, medical equipment and provider training. African nations seeking to improve abortion care must ensure that healthcare centres, particularly in rural and underserved areas, are equipped with the necessary resources, including safe abortion technologies such as manual vacuum aspiration and medication abortion. Training healthcare providers in evidence-based abortion care, including post-abortion care, is crucial to reducing complications and ensuring high-quality services. Additionally, integrating safe abortion into primary healthcare services can enhance accessibility and reduce barriers faced by women in seeking care.

Public education and awareness campaigns

Misinformation and stigma surrounding abortion remain major obstacles to access in many African countries.35 Public education campaigns, as seen in the DRC, can help dispel myths, provide accurate medical information and empower women to make informed choices about their reproductive health. These campaigns should use diverse communication channels, including radio, social media, community theatre and peer-led discussions, to reach a wide audience. Educational initiatives should also target healthcare providers, law enforcement agencies and policymakers to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the legal and medical aspects of abortion care.

Sustainable funding and international support

Expanding abortion services requires sustainable funding mechanisms.34 The DRC’s progress in reproductive rights has been supported by international donors, multilateral organisations and philanthropic foundations that prioritise maternal health and family planning. African governments must allocate domestic resources to reproductive health programmes and explore partnerships with international agencies such as the WHO and the United Nations Population Fund. Establishing public-private partnerships can also mobilise additional resources to expand service delivery and ensure the sustainability of abortion care programmes.

Addressing barriers for vulnerable populations

Young people, women from economically disadvantaged backgrounds and people with disabilities face higher risks associated with unsafe abortion practices, due to limited decision-making power and increased stigma.35 The DRC’s approach has included these groups in its framework, using targeted interventions to ensure that legal provisions translate into meaningful access. Nigeria and other African nations should implement youth-friendly reproductive health services, create confidential and non-judgemental spaces for care and address financial and logistical barriers that prevent marginalised women from obtaining safe abortion services.

Conclusion: moving towards reproductive justice

The DRC and Nigeria illustrate the challenges and opportunities in advancing abortion rights in Africa. Achieving meaningful progress requires sustained legal reform, strengthened healthcare systems, community-driven advocacy and widespread public education. The DRC’s evolving framework offers valuable lessons for Nigeria and other African nations seeking to shape progressive reproductive health policies. Aligning national laws with international human rights standards, fostering civil society collaboration and combating stigma through culturally sensitive approaches are essential to safeguarding women’s rights and reducing maternal mortality. While the Maputo Protocol serves as a critical guiding framework, its effectiveness depends on strong political commitment, persistent advocacy and grassroots mobilisation. To address unsafe abortion and advance reproductive justice, Nigeria and other African countries must prioritise the full domestication of the Maputo Protocol, while the DRC should focus on improving healthcare infrastructure and raising public awareness to ensure effective implementation. Without decisive action, preventable maternal deaths will persist, undermining efforts to improve women’s health and rights across Africa.
