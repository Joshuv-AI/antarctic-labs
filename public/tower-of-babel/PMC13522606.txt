Patterns of compliance with COVID-19 preventive measures in Armenia: results from a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Tsovinar Harutyunyan, Varduhi Hayrumyan, Zhanna Sargsyan, Anya Agopian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile numerous studies have explored adherence to individual COVID-19 preventive behaviors and their predictors, their combinations and clustering have been less frequently investigated. We explored the patterns and socio-demographic correlates of mask-wearing, hand washing, maintaining social distance, avoiding gatherings and vaccination against COVID-19 in Armenia. We enrolled participants using stratified two-stage cluster sampling. Telephone interviews were conducted among 3,483 respondents in May-September 2021. A hierarchical cluster analysis and multivariable logistic regression were applied. The most common protective behavior was hand washing (91%), while the least common was maintaining 1.5 m distance (42%). About 12% of the sample was vaccinated. The respondents in the “low protection” cluster (42.3%) had lower engagement in preventive behaviors, while those in the “high protection” cluster (57.7%) mostly adhered to behavioral recommendations. “High protection” cluster membership was associated with increasing age (aOR = 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02–1.03) and being a female (aOR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.01–1.44). Those with higher monthly family expenditures had lower odds of “high protection” cluster membership (aOR = 0.78; 95% CI: 0.63–0.96). This cross-sectional study stresses the importance of exploring patterns of compliance with multiple preventive behaviors and developing targeted interventions facilitating uptake of multiple behaviors in specific socio-demographic groups.Keywords: COVID-19, Armenia, Compliance with multiple preventive behaviors, Cluster analysis, VaccinationSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

Since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, the World Health Organization has provided guidance on large-scale public health measures, including several personal protective behaviors, to manage the transmission of the virus and alleviate its impact on the population1. While COVID-19 vaccines, with 13.6 billion doses administered globally2, have been a highly effective instrument in controlling the pandemic3, physical distancing, face mask use, and hand hygiene have also been shown to be effective, and particularly important in regions with low vaccination rates4–6. Several studies have evidenced that the success of preventive measures largely depended on the stringency of the corresponding regulations in different countries as well as individual engagement and compliance6–10. For example, some studies have shown that compliance with mask-wearing requirements varied across countries substantially, resulting in compliance rates ranging from 5 to 15% in Scandinavian countries to almost 95% in Japan in 202010.

A number of demographic, socio-economic and psycho-social factors have been shown to impact each individual COVID-19 protective behavior7–9. Yet, the combinations and clustering of protective behaviors have been less frequently studied11,12. Studying adherence to multiple infection prevention measures is important, as only simultaneous practice of several behaviors might provide adequate protection levels13.

The risk compensation hypothesis14 posits that those who perform a particular protective behavior may be less inclined to protect themselves via other recommended behaviors because of lowered risk perceptions. In the context of infectious diseases, and particularly COVID-19, this might imply that those who adhere to one of the recommended behaviors, i.e., mask wearing, should be less likely to get vaccinated or maintain social distancing, because they might feel protected by masks, and vice versa. However, the evidence provided by the research to date is inconclusive13. Several studies found links between multiple COVID-19 protective behaviors, including mask-wearing, vaccination, hand washing and social distancing, with individuals performing several behaviors in combination12,13,15,16. The exploration of the patterns of adherence to COVID-19 protective measures and the socio-demographic correlates of specific behavioral patterns can provide insights into the common bases of different behaviors and help develop appropriate approaches and targets for health communication messages and interventions in future infectious disease outbreaks. Such investigations could be even more valuable in the context of less developed countries where preventive health-related behaviors have rarely been studied integrally.

The Republic of Armenia is a higher middle-income country17 with an estimated population of 2.9 million18. Armenia reported its first COVID-19 case on March 1, 2020 and experienced its first COVID-19 peak in June 202019. The large-scale war initiated by Azerbaijan against Nagorno Karabakh on September 27, 2020 further exacerbated the situation, putting enormous strain on healthcare resources in Armenia19–21. As of May 30, 2021, Armenia had more than 192 thousand cases of COVID-19 and about 3,500 deaths22.

Since the start of the pandemic, the Ministry of Health (MoH), in accordance with the World Health Organization’s advice, recommended several protective behaviors, such as wearing face masks, social distancing, and frequent handwashing23. A mandate to wear face masks in all outdoor public areas, such as public parks, historical and cultural sites, and others was issued on May 3, 2020. The policy included circumstances under which wearing a face mask was not obligatory (e.g., children under the age of five, while engaging in sports or cycling, while being in beaches, mountains, or forests, and individuals with specific health conditions supported by relevant medical documentation)24. Similar exceptions were allowed for when the mandate was extended to indoor areas24. For those who were vaccinated, the requirement was lifted for public outdoor areas on June 1, and for indoor areas on July 1, 202125. Vaccination started in March 2021 and was rolled out in stages. Despite governmental initiatives to vaccinate the population, including awareness raising and promotion campaigns, as of May 2021, the proportion of people who received at least one dose was 1.1%22. The main types of vaccines administered in Armenia at that time included AstraZeneca and Sputnik V (Gam-COVID-Vac)26,27.

This study represents a secondary analysis of a subsample of respondents enrolled in a telephone survey as part of a larger study of COVID-19 seroprevalence, which included blood testing for the presence of COVID-19 antibodies in the Armenian population. Prior publications using the survey dataset focused on health behavior theory-based factors associated with COVID-19 vaccination intent22 and mask-wearing28, infectious disease literacy29 and delay/avoidance of medical care during the pandemic30; however, general patterns of compliance to multiple protective behaviors in the Armenian population remained largely unexplored.

The present study aimed to answer the following research questions: (1) What were the patterns of COVID-19 protective behaviors such as mask-wearing, hand washing, social distancing, avoidance of social gatherings, and getting vaccinated against COVID-19 in the adult population of Armenia between May-September 2021? and (2) What were the socio-demographic profiles of the population groups with specific behavioral patterns?

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional telephone survey of 3483 adults was conducted as part of a larger study of COVID-19 seroprevalence in Armenia. It was implemented primarily to provide an evidence base for the programmatic activities of the USAID-funded “Support to control COVID-19 and other infectious disease outbreaks” project carried out by the Turpanjian College of Health Sciences of the American University of Armenia (AUA/TCHS)31. The sample size for the seroprevalence study was 3,832, based on the formula for estimating a population proportion assuming 30% estimated prevalence of anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies, 1.45% margin of error, 0.05 type 1 error, and 95% confidence level. Stratified two-stage cluster sampling via randomly selected primary health care facilities across the country was applied to identify the study participants. One-third of the facilities in each of 11 administrative regions in Armenia, including capital Yerevan, were selected based on the size of the adult population served by each facility. Participants from various age groups were sampled in the facilities in proportion to their representation in the general population, using the database of the national e-health registry of patients (ARMED)32. Recruitment continued until the desired sample size was reached. Those having a contraindication to venipuncture, being in quarantine or self-isolation for less than 10 days, or being unable to attend the sampling site because of physical disability, were excluded from the seroprevalence study. Written informed consent was obtained for blood testing and the phone survey at the facility. Telephone interviews were conducted by ten AUA/TCHS-trained interviewers in May-September 2021 among 3,483 adults out of the total of 3,832 recruited for the seroprevalence study (Fig. 1). The exclusion criteria for the phone interviews were having mental and hearing problems and inability to speak Armenian. The survey was administered via electronic tablets using the Alchemer online tool (https://www.alchemer.com/). Survey participants were compensated for their time with a 1000 AMD (US$2.0) top-up of their phone cards. Periodic spot checks were conducted by the senior researchers of the AUA/TCHS to ensure the quality of collected data.

Data collection and study tool

The structured interviewer-administered questionnaire gathered information in multiple domains, focusing on knowledge, attitude and practices related to COVID-19 and COVID-19 experience. The questionnaire was constructed based on the review of tools used in similar studies conducted in Armenia and other countries33–36. The questionnaire was translated into Armenian and pretested before fielding. We used questions on participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, COVID-19-related protective behaviors, and vaccination status for the secondary data analysis.

The American University of Armenia’s Institutional Review Board approved the study (#AUA-2021-005).

Study measures

The behaviors were measured by asking “How often have you done the following in the past 14 days: 1) wore a mask in public areas in the past 14 days; 2) washed your hands with soap and water for about 20 seconds; 3) participated in a social gathering with anyone other than your immediate household; 4) maintained social distance of at least 1.5 meters.” The response options were “always”, “usually”, “sometimes”, “rarely”, and “never”. For the combination analysis, the variables were dichotomized as follows: the response options for questions about mask-wearing, handwashing, and maintaining social distance were grouped into “always/usually” versus “sometimes/rarely/never”, while participation in social gatherings was categorized as “always/usually/sometimes” versus “rarely/never.” The dichotomization aimed to differentiate between a consistent performance of protective behaviors and random or no performance, as well as generate reasonably large population segments for meaningful interpretation and applicability. In the case of social gatherings, the consistent avoidance of risky behavior was contrasted with casual/no avoidance. Vaccination status was measured by asking “Have you been vaccinated against COVID-19?” with “yes” or “no” answer options, thus tallying respondents receiving either one or two doses of COVID-19 vaccine. The variable on compliance with multiple behaviors was created by summing the binary values of five protective behaviors, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 5. This score reflected the number of protective behaviors practiced by each participant. The total behavioral compliance score was categorized into three groups: 0–1 behavior, 2–3 behaviors, and 4–5 behaviors.

Place of residence (urban/rural), gender (male/female), age (continuous), education level (categorical), employment status (categorical), and average monthly expenditures (categorical) were included as socio-demographic variables.

Data management and analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21 was used for data analysis (IBM Corp. Released 2012. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). The descriptive analysis assessed the proportions of the population practicing each protective behavior including hand-washing, mask-wearing, participating in social gatherings, maintaining social distance, and getting vaccinated against COVID-19, as well as highlighted specific patterns of co-occurrence of different behaviors.

We conducted a hierarchical cluster analysis to group the participants by their performance of the behaviors and to determine the optimal number of clusters using the five-point Likert-scale behavioral variables and vaccination status (binary). The Ward’s method with squared Euclidean distance was employed to minimize the within-cluster variance (Fig. 2). The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the dendrogram and agglomeration schedule. Subsequently, we conducted K-means analysis to refine these clusters and to assign participants to the most appropriate cluster based on their behavioral patterns. The identified clusters were described in terms of the patterns of performing the examined behaviors and sociodemographic characteristics using bivariate analysis (e.g., Pearson Chi2 test and t-test). To assess the independent associations of socio-demographic characteristics with cluster membership, multivariable binary logistic regression was conducted. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were low (mean VIF = 1.13), indicating no evidence of multicollinearity. Observations with missing values in regression variables were excluded from the multivariable analysis. The adjusted model explained a modest proportion of the variance in cluster membership (Nagelkerke R²=0.054). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was not statistically significant (p = 0.101), suggesting adequate model fit. The link test showed that the squared predicted values were not statistically significant (p = 0.420), providing no evidence of model misspecification, and residual diagnostics did not indicate substantial departures from model assumptions.

Results

A total of 3,483 individuals participated in the telephone survey; however, the present analysis is limited to 3,347 respondents who provided complete responses on all five COVID-19-related protective behaviors.

The mean age of participants was 49.4 years (SD = 14.77). The majority were female (71.5%), lived in urban areas (88.4%), and had vocational (12–13 years) or university-level education (68.9%). More than half of the respondents were employed (54.7%). The majority of the sample reported monthly household expenditures between 101,000 and 400,000 AMD (approximately $228–$900 USD) (Table 1).

The most commonly reported behavior was hand washing, with 91.4% of respondents indicating they always or usually washed their hands with soap and water for about 20 s in the past 14 days. About 74.0% reported that they had rarely or never participated in social gatherings with people outside of their immediate household in the past 14 days. Mask-wearing in public areas was always or usually practiced by 52.0% of participants, while 42.1% consistently maintained a distance of at least 1.5 m. At the time of the survey, a small proportion of respondents (11.6%) had received one or two doses of COVID-19 vaccine.

About 13.9% of respondents practiced one or none of the recommended behaviors, 59.2% practiced two or three behaviors, and 26.9% reported engagement in four behaviors (Table 1). Only 3.4% practiced four protective behaviors and were vaccinated (Table 2). The most common combination was the simultaneous practice of all four behaviors — mask-wearing, handwashing, avoiding social gatherings, and maintaining a distance of 1.5 m (19.7%), followed by the combination of handwashing and avoiding social gatherings (19.6%), and the combination of mask-wearing, handwashing, and avoiding gatherings (13.3%) (Table 2). These three combinations accounted for more than half of all multi-behavior patterns reported.

Based on the cluster analysis, two clusters of respondents were identified (Table 1). Cluster 1 (“low protection”), comprised of 1,416 participants (42.3%), was characterized by lower engagement in preventive behaviors. Participants in this cluster mainly reported never (34.7%) or rarely (29.4%) wearing masks in public areas, always (54.1%) or usually (29.4%) washing hands with soap and water, relatively frequently participating in social gatherings (always/usually/sometimes: 39.5%), and never (39.4%) or rarely (26.8%) maintaining social distance. About 9% of this cluster was vaccinated against COVID-19. Cluster 2 (“high protection”) included 1,931 participants (57.7%) and was characterized by more consistent engagement in preventive behaviors. Participants in this cluster predominantly reported always (51.6%) or usually (28.7%) wearing masks in public areas, always (77.0%) or usually (20.2%) washing hands with soap and water, never (60.1%) or rarely (23.9%) participating in social gatherings, and always (30.9%) or usually (33.9%) maintaining a distance of at least 1.5 m. About 14% of respondents in Cluster 2 were vaccinated. Significant differences between clusters were observed across all preventive behaviors (p < 0.001). In addition, compared to Cluster 1, Cluster 2 contained a substantially higher proportion of individuals simultaneously practicing 4 or more behaviors (46.0% versus 0.7%, p < 0.001).

The “high protection” cluster had a higher proportion of female respondents compared to Cluster 1 (73.4% versus 69.0%, respectively; p = 0.005) and was significantly older, with almost 5.5 years difference in the mean age between clusters (p < 0.001). Also, Cluster 2 membership was associated with being unemployed (p = 0.010) and lower monthly expenditures (p < 0.001).

Table 3 presents the results of multivariable logistic regression examining the association between cluster membership and socio-demographic characteristics. Females had higher odds of membership in the “high protection” cluster compared to males (aOR = 1.21; 95% CI: 1.01–1.44; p = 0.040). Increasing age was associated with higher odds of membership in “high protection” cluster, with each additional year increasing the odds by 3.0% (aOR = 1.03; 95% CI: 1.02–1.03; p < 0.001). Participants reporting monthly family expenditures from 101,000 to 400,000 AMD had significantly lower odds of belonging to the “high-protection” cluster compared to those reporting less than 100,000 AMD expenditure (aOR = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.63–0.96; p = 0.021). The relationship between employment status and cluster membership observed in the bivariate analysis was not maintained in the adjusted analysis. No statistically significant associations were observed between cluster membership and place of residence or education level.

Discussion

This study represents the first attempt to analyze compliance with multiple preventive behaviors in the context of an infectious disease outbreak in Armenia. The most commonly practiced protective behavior in our sample was washing hands with soap and water, with 91.4% of respondents reporting doing it always or usually. Maintaining social distance was the least common behavior, as only 42.1% had done so regularly in past 14 days. Wearing a mask always or usually was practiced by only about half of the sample. Overall, compared to the reports from other parts of the world, we observed poorer adherence to some important protective measures in Armenian population. The behavioral patterns observed in our study were closer to those reported from Greece, where similarly low adherence was reported particularly to mask wearing and social distancing behaviors15, and dissimilar to findings from a study conducted in the UK, where the highest compliance was observed for mask wearing and indoor and outdoor social mixing, while hand washing and social distancing were practiced less frequently12. A survey of G7 countries in March 2020 has shown that, overall, 89.1% reported keeping a social distance. At the same time, “washing hands more often/for longer” was reported by 76.5% of respondents, while only 26% used a mask37. It should be noted, however, that any such comparisons to behaviors in other countries have limited utility because the epidemic curves have been different, and policy approaches have fluctuated considerably across the globe, thus creating differences in the adherence to recommended behaviors even during the same time intervals. In Armenia, the mask-wearing mandate was active in May 2021 when our data collection started, but was lifted for outdoor areas and indoor areas for those who were vaccinated shortly afterwards, in June and July 2021, respectively. Therefore, while we present the aggregate prevalence for the survey period, the rate of mask-wearing behavior could have fluctuated in our data depending on the month of data collection. As a general trend, pandemic fatigue led to a gradual reduction of protective behaviors with time in many countries around the world38–42. However, cultural factors might also play a role in terms of which particular behaviors might be more adhered to in a specific context. For example, maintaining social distance during the outbreak might be more challenging in those countries where normally smaller interpersonal space is preferred in social encounters43.

Our analysis revealed two distinct clusters in the Armenian population: those with higher and lower engagement in protective behaviors, with the “high protection” cluster constituting the majority of the sample (57.7%). While our results are not fully comparable to the findings from other studies in the literature because of different analytic methodologies, data collection periods (and thus pandemic phases), and the range of assessed protective measures, some consistency in reported patterns was observed. A cluster analysis study conducted in Greece found that almost 50% of citizens (a group labeled “Meticulous Proactive Citizens”) undertook all protective measures during the height of the pandemic in that country15. Similarly, in the UK population, a latent class analysis identified 52.5% of the sample as “full compliers” with the recommended behaviors12. While in Hungary, about 37% of the population belonged to the group adhering to protective measures44.

At the same time, we were able to identify only two large distinct clusters in the population with respect to protective behaviors, contrary to the majority of the studies mentioned, which distinguished four and more groups. Presumably, this means that the Armenian population is more behaviorally homogenous compared to other studied populations, which is in line with its remarkably high ethnic and religious homogeneity45 and tendency to comply with social norms28.

Notably, we observed quite small proportions of those who “always” (as opposed to “usually”) complied with a specific recommended behavior in the “high protection” cluster and larger differences in these proportions across the behaviors compared to a few other studies which reported analogous proportions11,12. For example, only 51.6% of participants in the “high protection” cluster reported “always” wearing masks in public areas, and 30.9% “always” maintained a distance of at least 1.5 m. At the same time, only a small proportion of our overall sample (27%) “always” or “usually” adhered to at least four recommended protective behaviors, while only 3.4% practiced all four protective behaviors and were vaccinated against COVID-19. While the low rate of full compliance may be partially explained by less stringent regulations in Armenia, a more viable explanation could be the timing of data collection. Our survey was conducted in May-September of 2021, when the most acute phase of the pandemic was over. In a study conducted in Belgium between April 2021 and December 2021, a modest 28.1% of the population was categorized into high protective behavior adherence group based on the joint performance of hand washing, mask wearing, distancing and social contact limitation46.

The multivariable analysis of socio-demographic factors associated with cluster membership revealed that females, older, and less affluent individuals were more likely to comply with the recommended behaviors. This is to a great extent in line with the cluster analysis study conducted in Greece where the group of most proactive respondents had higher proportions of females, as well as older individuals.15 Wright et al. have found that high compliance was strongly related to older age in their latent class analysis of a large nationwide sample in the UK12. Similarly, in Hungary, older age and being a female were associated with higher compliance44 to several preventive measures. Similar findings were reported in Iran47.

Sex disparities in adherence behaviors have been largely explained by lower perceived risk among men48,49. In younger adults and adolescents, in addition to lower perceived risk, predisposition to risk taking for social and emotional stimulation and underestimation of the long-term consequences of their behavior may also decrease adherence to preventive behaviors48. Further exploration of factors that can facilitate the adoption of preventive measures in males and younger people, and focused awareness-raising interventions in these population groups during infectious disease outbreaks can help with boosting the levels of protection across the country.

We could find only limited evidence for confirming our findings related to lower odds of membership in “high protection” cluster in more affluent respondents in the literature. A study in the US has shown poorer adherence to mask wearing behavior with higher income50. Wright et al., have reported an association between higher education, higher household income and unemployment and lower compliance in the UK that grew across time (April-August 2020) as the regulations became less stringent51. The association was partially explained by a more relaxed approach to compliance driven by the sense of privilege and lack of fear over financial penalties as the pandemic continued, which might be the case in Armenia as well. This finding along with possible explanatory mechanisms should be explored and confirmed in further investigations on this topic.

While our study of behavioral patterns was largely descriptive, our findings seem to support the assumption that certain groups of individuals might want to enhance their protection through adhering to all or most of the recommended measures simultaneously52. It has been proposed that some of the preventive behaviors might work as cues to initiate other measures53 or serve as gateways to other similar behaviors54. These assumptions are further supported by the fact that while in our sample the overall proportion of those who practiced at least four behaviors was smaller than the proportion of those who practiced two to three behaviors, the most common behavioral combination was simultaneous mask wearing, hand washing, maintaining social distance and avoiding gatherings (19.7%). Further investigations should focus on individuals who adhere to multiple recommendations or their specific combinations to gain insights into factors that motivate and enable their protective behaviors.

This study is subject to several limitations. First, the phone survey was conducted as part of a larger COVID-19 seroprevalence study, which could have led to overrepresentation of those respondents who were most concerned with their seroprevalence status and, thus, more likely to comply with recommended preventive measures. Second, our sample included a larger proportion of females, people with higher education, and urban residents than the overall Armenian population18, which could have affected overall estimates of the behavioral performance and limited the generalizability of the results. Third, since the estimates of the performance of protective behaviors were based on the respondents’ self-report and not on actual observations, recall and social desirability biases cannot be ruled out. Finally, COVID-19-related behaviors have been shown to be time-sensitive and fluctuating in accordance with morbidity and mortality patterns in a particular context; therefore, our results do not necessarily reflect the situation in the country through all phases of the pandemic.

This study provided unique evidence on the patterns of COVID-19 preventive behaviors in a region where similar investigations have rarely been carried out. Although the COVID-19 pandemic is over, the potential for new infectious disease outbreaks globally and in Armenia remains high. Our results stress the importance of exploring general patterns of compliance instead of focusing on individual preventive behaviors, and developing interventions which could facilitate and enable behaviors in concert. This approach has been widely applied to non-communicable disease behavior change54. Our study shows that multiple behavior change could be similarly valuable in the context of infectious diseases. Identifying socio-demographic characteristics of the population subgroups based on their general compliance patterns can help with segmenting the population and tailoring the content of health communication accordingly. This approach can be particularly important for countries like Armenia, where limited resources preclude multiple direct assessments and comprehensive analysis of individual behaviors.
