Global in Time Estimates for Multi-phase Muskat Problem
Zirui Wang

Abstract
We establish global-in-time decay estimates for the multi-phase Muskat problem in the case where the density takes exactly n + 1 n+1 distinct constant values. We first linearize the system around a flat stable configuration, followed by the study of associated linearized operator. The asymptotic behavior at low frequencies of eigenvalues yields the decay rate of ( 1 + t ) − s / 2 − 1 / 4 (1+t)^{-s/2-1/4} for Wiener norm ‖ f ‖ s \|f\|_{s} , which is slower than the classical case, where the decay rate is ( 1 + t ) − s + ν (1+t)^{-s+\nu} . Afterwards we bound the nonlinear term to close the argument.



1
Introduction

We consider the multi-phase Muskat problem in 2D. In particular, it describes the motion of fluids in incompressible porous media (IPM)
[
2
,
24
]
when the density takes exactly

n+1
distinct constant values. The density is carried by a viscous incompressible flow governed by Darcy’s law in the field of gravity:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+u\cdot\nabla\rho=0,\quad u+\nabla p=-(0,\rho),\quad\text{div}u=0,

(1.1)

where gravity points downward in the
y
direction. Here
\rho
represents the density,
u
is the velocity field,
p
is the pressure. And we have normalized dynamic viscosity, the permeability of the medium, and the gravitational constant in Darcy’s law to unity. This equation is an active scalar, where the velocity has the same level of regularity as
\rho
. In
\mathbb{R}^{2}
this can be seen from the following Biot-Savart law

u=\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1}\partial_{x}\rho,\quad\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{y},\partial_{x}).

(1.2)

There have been numerous results concerning the well-posedness of classical Muskat problem (
n=1
). In this case the interface can be denoted by a function
f(x,t)
satisfies the following

f_{t}=\frac{\rho_{1}-\rho_{0}}{2\pi}\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{y\left(f_{x}(x)-f_{x}(x-y)\right)}{y^{2}+\left(f(x)-f(x-y)\right)^{2}}dy.

(1.3)

In this classical setting, local well-posedness has been established for initial data in
H^{3}
in
[
15
]
, which was later improved to
H^{2}
in
[
9
]
. Further local results were obtained for the case with viscosity jump in
[
13
,
14
]
, as well as for small or monotone initial data in
[
20
,
27
]
. For the half-plane setting, local well-posedness was established in
[
30
]
recently. We also mention here that
H^{\frac{3}{2}}
is in fact a critical space in
[
1
]
. For global results, well-posedness was given in
[
10
]
when the Wiener norm of initial data
\|f_{0}\|_{1}
is less than
1/3
for 2D and
1/5
for 3D (see also
[
11
]
). Based on this, large time decay rate was given in
[
26
]
of exact
(1+t)^{-s+\nu}
for Wiener norm
\|f\|_{s}
. In
[
12
]
, the authors proved local well-posedness for initial data in
W^{2,p},p\in[1,\infty)
, and further gained global well-posedness given the smallness of the slope of initial surface. Besides, global regularity results in critical space
\dot{W}^{1,\infty}
for small data is given in
[
5
,
6
]
. We also mention related works on self-similar solutions in 2D and 3D
[
22
,
25
]
as well as singularity formation
[
7
,
18
,
23
,
29
,
30
]
, while the question of finite-time singularity formation in the stable regime with bounded slope remains open.

For the multi-phase Muskat problem, local well-posedness has been established in
[
3
]
for the case of two interfaces, and later extended in
[
4
]
to the setting with different viscosities. In addition, the interfaces are known not to touch each other, which rules out the formation of squirt singularities, see
[
16
,
21
]
. This is different from one-phase Muskat problem in which such blowup exists
[
19
,
17
]
. Very recently, uniform lifespan for local solutions of three-phase Muskat problem was established in
[
8
]
. In contrast to the extensive theory available for the classical Muskat problem, the global theory for the multi-phase setting remains less developed. In this paper, we study the large-time decay of global solutions to the multi-phase Muskat problem. We begin by introducing the system of equations in the following.

1.1
Setting of the problem

We consider the fluids characterized by
n+1
different constant densities
\rho_{j}
for
j=0,1,\dots,n
. Thus the density is represented by

\rho(\textbf{x},t)=\rho_{j},\quad\text{if}\quad\textbf{x}\in\Omega_{j},\quad j=0,1,\dots,n,

(1.4)

where
\Omega_{j}
are connected regions separated by
n
interfaces which are characterized by the functions
y=f_{j}(x,t),j=1,2,\dots,n
. To be specific the regions are defined as

\Omega_{j}=\{\textbf{x}=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|f_{j}(x,t)<y<f_{j+1}(x,t)\},\quad j=0,1,\dots,n

(1.5)

with
f_{0}(x,t)=-\infty,f_{n+1}(x,t)=+\infty
. We assume that the interfaces are ordered and remain separated, namely

f_{k}(x,t)<f_{j}(x,t),\quad\text{for all}\quad k<j.

(1.6)

Substituting the above ansatz into the IPM system (
1.1
) we are able to derive the equation for all interfaces as below

\partial_{t}f_{k}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{P.V.}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{y\left(\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t)-\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)\right)}{y^{2}+\left(f_{j}(x-y,t)-f_{k}(x,t)\right)^{2}}dy,\quad k=1,2,\dots,n.

(1.7)

Here, each term in the summation represents the contribution of the
j
-th interface to the evolution of the
k
-th interface. In other words, the dynamics of
f_{k}
are determined by the nonlocal interactions with all interfaces in the system, weighted by the corresponding density jumps
\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j}
. We left the detailed derivations in Section
2
.
We remark for system (
1.7
) in order to work in the stable regime and ensure well-posedness, we assume that the lighter fluid lies above the denser one as in
[
15
]
:

\rho_{k}>\rho_{j},\quad\text{for all}\quad k<j.

(1.8)

And the problem is ill-posed if this condition fails, see
[
15
]
. We now observe, as in the classical case, that flat interfaces form a stationary solution to (
1.7
). Thus, in this stable regime, it is natural to study the dynamics near such a solution.
Motivated by this, we consider perturbations of a flat layered configuration. More precisely, we assume that the
k
-th interface is a small perturbation of the horizontal line
y=d_{k}
, where

d_{k}<d_{j}\qquad\text{for }k<j.

Accordingly, we introduce the perturbation variables

g_{k}(x,t)=f_{k}(x,t)-d_{k},

(1.9)

and assume that
g_{k}
remains small in a suitable sense. In the following context, we treat
\rho_{k},d_{k}
as fixed constants that do not change in our analysis.

We next linearize the system around the steady state. In Fourier variables, the resulting linearized dynamics for the perturbation vector
\vec{g}(x,t)=(g_{1}(x,t),\dots,g_{n}(x,t))^{T}
takes the form

\partial_{t}\hat{\vec{g}}(\xi,t)=-|\xi|A(\xi)\hat{\vec{g}}(\xi,t),

(1.10)

where the
n\times n
matrix
A
is defined as

A(\xi)=\left(a_{kj}(\xi)\right)_{n\times n},\quad a_{kj}(\xi)=\frac{\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j}}{2}e^{-|d_{k}-d_{j}||\xi|}.

(1.11)

The study of the linear matrix (
1.11
) plays a key role in our analysis and the computation of exact decay rate. We left the analysis in Section
3
.

1.2
Notations

We consider the following Wiener norm introduced in
[
10
]

\|f\|_{s}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{s}|\hat{f}(\xi)|d\xi,

as well as the weighted norm for a positive function
w(\xi)

\|f\|_{w}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\xi)|\hat{f}(\xi)|d\xi,

(1.12)

where
\hat{f}
is the standard Fourier transform of
f

\hat{f}(\xi)=\mathscr{F}(f)(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-ix\xi}dx.

We define the convolution of two functions as usual as

(f*g)(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(y)g(x-y)dy.

For functions
f,g
defined in
\mathbb{R}
, we take

\displaystyle\Delta_{y}f(x)=\frac{f(x)-f(x-y)}{y},

(1.13)

\displaystyle[f,g]_{y}(x)=f(x)-g(x-y).

We use
\tilde{f}
to denote the initial data, that is

\tilde{f}(x)=f(0,x).

(1.14)

Finally we use the notation
f_{1}\lesssim f_{2}
if there exists uniform constant
C>0
such that
f_{1}\leq Cf_{2}
. Also
f_{1}\approx f_{2}
means that
f_{1}\leq Cf_{2}
and
f_{2}\leq Cf_{1}
.

1.3
Main results

Given a priori assumption of the well-defined fluid interface that exists globally in time, we will study its long-time
behavior in this paper. In particular we have the following main. theorem

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
. Let
\{f_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
be a solution to system (
1.7
) and
\{g_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
is defined in (
1.9
). Further suppose
g_{k}\in C([0,\infty);H^{s^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}))
for
s^{\prime}\geq 3
and all
1\leq k\leq n
. Then there exists universal constant
\gamma
depends on
l,\{d_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n},\{\rho_{k}\}_{0\leq k\leq n}
, such that if the initial data is small enough to satisfy

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{0}+\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{1}\right)\leq\gamma,

(1.15)

we have the uniform in time estimate for any
0\leq s<s^{\prime}-1
:

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|g_{j}(t)\|_{s}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}.

(1.16)

Remark 1.1
.

In the classical one-interface case, global well-posedness and asymptotic behavior have been extensively studied in
[
26
]
. In particular, the long-time decay rate of a single interface is given by

\|f\|_{s}\sim(1+t)^{-s+\nu}.

(1.17)

It is notable that this is different from the case
n\geq 2
, where the decay rate becomes
(1+t)^{-s/2-1/4}
. This distinction is from the spectral structure of the linearized matrix
A(\xi)
. In the one-interface case,
A(\xi)
has only one eigenvalue, which behaves like
-|\xi|
as
\xi\to 0
. By contrast, when
n\geq 2
, there is still one eigenvalue with behavior
-|\xi|
, but the remaining
n-1
eigenvalues behave like
-|\xi|^{2}
. It is precisely the slower decay in low frequencies that leads to less decay in the multi-phase setting.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we rigorously derive the system for multi-phase Muskat problem and prove the
L^{2}
maximum principle. In Section
3
we analyze the linear operator
A(\xi)
and derive the asymptotic behavior of its eigenvalues. Finally in Section
4
we estimate the nonlinear term using the properties of Wiener algebra following
[
14
]
. This allows us to complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly derive the equation for the multi-phase system (
1.7
). By the Biot–Savart law (
1.2
), the velocity field can be expressed in terms of the density as follows:

u(\mathbf{x},t)=(-K_{1}*\partial_{x_{1}}\rho,K_{2}*\partial_{x_{1}}\rho)=(-K_{2}*\partial_{x_{2}}\rho,K_{2}*\partial_{x_{1}}\rho),

(2.1)

where we use the notation
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2})
. Here the kernel
K
is of Calderon-Zygmund type:

K(\mathbf{x})=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\frac{x_{2}}{|x|^{2}},\frac{x_{1}}{|x|^{2}}\right)=\left(K_{1}(\mathbf{x}),K_{2}(\mathbf{x})\right),

(2.2)

(see
[
28
]
). Since
\rho
satisfies the settings in (
1.4
)
we have

\nabla\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})(\partial_{x_{1}}f_{j}(x_{1},t),-1)\delta(x_{2}-f_{j}(x_{1},t))

(2.3)

where
\delta
is the Dirac distribution. Using again the expression of velocity (
2.1
) we obtain

u(x,x_{2},t)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(y,y\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)}{y^{2}+\left(x_{2}-f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)^{2}}dy.

(2.4)

The equation above is valid when
x_{2}\neq f_{k}(x,t)
and the principal value is taken at infinity (see again
[
28
]
). As
x_{2}
approaches
f_{k}(x,t)
in the normal direction, discontinuity arises since the vorticity is concentrated on the interfaces. Thus for
\epsilon>0
we define

u^{1}\left(x,f_{k}(x,t),t\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}u\left(x-\epsilon\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t),f_{k}(x,t)+\epsilon,t\right),

(2.5)

and

u^{2}\left(x,f_{k}(x,t),t\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}u\left(x+\epsilon\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t),f_{k}(x,t)-\epsilon,t\right).

(2.6)

Then it follows

\displaystyle u^{1}\left(x,f_{k}(x,t),t\right)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(y,y\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)}{y^{2}+\left(f_{k}(x,t)-f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)^{2}}dy

(2.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k})\frac{\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)\left(1,\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)\right)}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)^{2}}},

(2.8)

and

\displaystyle u^{1}(x,f_{k}(x,t),t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{(y,y\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t))}{y^{2}+(f_{k}(x,t)-f_{j}(x-y,t))^{2}}dy

(2.9)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k})\frac{\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)(1,\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t))}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)^{2}}}.

(2.10)

We further argue that the velocity in tangential directions only moves particles on the line
f_{k}(x,t)
and does not make contribution to the dynamics of the interface. Thus it follows

u\left(x,f_{k}(x,t),t\right)=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left(y,y\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)}{y^{2}+\left(f_{k}(x,t)-f_{j}(x-y,t)\right)^{2}}dy,

(2.11)

due to the fact that

\frac{1}{2}(\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k})\frac{\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)(1,\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t))}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)^{2}}}

(2.12)

is in tangential directions for the interface
f_{k}
. Finally, we use the fact that the interface
f_{k}
evolves according to

\left(0,\partial_{t}f_{k}(x,t)\right)\cdot\frac{\left(-\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t),1\right)}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)^{2}}}=u\left(x,f_{k}(x,t),t\right)\cdot\frac{\left(-\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t),1\right)}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)^{2}}}.

(2.13)

We obtain

\partial_{t}f_{k}=-\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathrm{P.V.}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{y\left(\partial_{x}f_{j}(x-y,t)-\partial_{x}f_{k}(x,t)\right)}{y^{2}+(f_{j}(x-y,t)-f_{k}(x,t))^{2}}dy.

(2.14)

Therefore we have derived (
1.7
).

We then prove the
L^{2}
maximum principle for system (
1.7
). The result is fundamentally physical since it can be expressed in terms of the conservation law for gravitational potential energy.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
\{f_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
be solution to (
1.7
) and
\{g_{k}\}_{1\leq k\leq n}
defined in (
1.9
) satisfies
g_{k}\in H^{3}(\mathbb{R}),1\leq k\leq n
. Then the following inequality holds:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\|f_{j}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)\leq 0.

(2.15)

Proof.

Let

\Gamma_{k}(t):=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|x_{2}=f_{k}(x_{1},t)\}

(2.16)

to be the graph of
k
-th interface. And we use the following notations for different domains

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}^{\pm}(t)=\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|\pm d_{k}<\pm x_{2}<\pm f_{k}(x_{1},t)\},

(2.17)

\displaystyle\Omega_{0}(t)=\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|x_{2}<f_{1}(x_{1},t)\},\quad\Omega_{n+1}(t)=\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|x_{2}>f_{n}(x_{1},t)\},

(2.18)

\displaystyle\Omega_{k}^{\prime}=\{\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|d_{k}<x_{2}<d_{k+1}\},\quad 0\leq k\leq n,\quad\text{with}\quad d_{0}=-\infty,\quad d_{n+1}=+\infty.

(2.19)

Thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}g_{k}(x,t)\partial_{t}g_{k}(x,t)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{f}}\frac{(x_{2}-d_{k})\partial_{t}f_{k}(x_{1},t)}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x_{1},t)^{2}}}d\sigma(\textbf{x})

(2.20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{f}}(x_{2}-d_{k})(0,\partial_{t}f_{k}(x_{1},t))\cdot\frac{(-\partial_{x}f_{k}(x_{1},t),1)}{\sqrt{1+\partial_{x}f_{k}(x_{1},t)^{2}}}d\sigma(\textbf{x})

(2.21)

We now recall (
2.13
). The second vector on both sides in (
2.13
) is the unit outer normal
n(\textbf{x})
to
\Omega_{k}^{+}
when
x
lies on
\partial\Omega_{k}^{+}
, and it equals
-n(\textbf{x})
to
\Omega_{k}^{-}
when
x
lies on
\partial\Omega_{k}^{-}
. The above observation yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|f_{k}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{+}}(x_{2}-d_{k})u(\textbf{x})\cdot n(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}-\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}^{-}}(x_{2}-d_{k})u(\textbf{x})\cdot n(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}

(2.22)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{k}^{+}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}-\int_{\Omega_{k}^{-}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}

(2.23)

where we use the incompressible condition
\text{div}(u)=0
. This leads to the following

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\|f_{j}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\left(\int_{\Omega_{k}^{+}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}-\int_{\Omega_{k}^{-}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}\right)

(2.24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j})\left(\int_{\Omega_{k}^{+}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}-\int_{\Omega_{k}^{-}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}\right)+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\rho_{j}\int_{\Omega_{j}^{\prime}}u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}

(2.25)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(\textbf{x})u_{2}(\textbf{x})d\textbf{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\rho(\textbf{x})\partial_{x_{1}}(-\Delta)^{-1}\rho_{x_{1}}(\textbf{x},t)=-\|\rho_{x_{1}}\|_{\dot{H}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}\leq 0

(2.26)

Hence we complete the proof of Theorem
2.15
.
∎

3
Linear Estimate

We linearize system (
1.7
) assuming that
g_{k}
is small enough

\partial_{t}g_{k}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(T_{k,j}g_{j}(x,t)+N_{k,j}(x,t)\right).

(3.1)

Here
T_{k,j}
denotes the linear operator and can be written explicitly as below

\displaystyle T_{k,j}v(x,t)=-\frac{\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j}}{2\pi}\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{y\partial_{x}v(x-y,t)}{y^{2}+(d_{k}-d_{j})^{2}}dy,\quad k\neq j,

(3.2)

\displaystyle T_{k,k}v(x,t)=-\frac{\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k}}{2\pi}\mathrm{P.V.}\int\frac{\partial_{x}v(x-y,t)-\partial_{x}v(x,t)}{y}dy,

(3.3)

The remaining terms are nonlinear, and we treat them as perturbative terms by recalling (
1.13
)

\displaystyle N_{k,j}(x,t)=\frac{\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j}}{2\pi}\int\frac{y\partial_{x}[g_{k},g_{j}]_{y}(x,t)\left((d_{k}-d_{j})^{2}-(d_{k}-d_{j}+[g_{k},g_{j}]_{y}(x,t))^{2}\right)}{(y^{2}+(d_{k}-d_{j})^{2})(y^{2}+(d_{k}-d_{j}+[g_{k},g_{j}]_{y}(x,t))^{2})}dy

(3.4)

for
k\neq j
and

N_{k,k}(x,t)=\frac{\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k}}{2\pi}\int\partial_{x}\Delta_{y}g_{k}(x,t)\frac{\left(\Delta_{y}g_{k}(x,t)\right)^{2}}{1+\left(\Delta_{y}g_{k}(x,t)\right)^{2}}dy.

(3.5)

In the argument below, we omit the time variable
t
whenever no confusion arise, for simplicity. We now study the linearized system in Fourier space, since the linear operator can be expressed as a Fourier multiplier. In particular we have

\widehat{T_{k,j}v}(\xi)=-\frac{\rho_{j-1}-\rho_{j}}{2}|\xi|e^{-|d_{k}-d_{j}||\xi|}\hat{v}(\xi),\quad 1\leq k,j\leq n.

(3.6)

Therefore we are ready to write the system in Fourier space as

\partial_{t}\widehat{\vec{g}}(\xi,t)=-|\xi|A(\xi)\widehat{\vec{g}}(\xi,t)+\widehat{\vec{N}}(\xi,t)

(3.7)

with the definition of linear matrix
A(\xi)
in (
1.11
). The nonlinear term
\vec{N}(x,t)=(N_{1},\dots,N_{n})^{T}(x,t)
is defined as

N_{k}(x,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}N_{k,j}(x,t).

(3.8)

We now analyze the behavior of the matrix
A(\xi)
. To begin with, we present the following lemma, which provides a criterion for its positive definiteness.

Lemma 3.1

(Bochner Theorem)
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
to be an even function with the condition that
\hat{\phi}(\xi)\geq 0
. Then for any given
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
, the following matrix

M=(\phi(x_{i}-x_{j}))_{n\times n}

(3.9)

is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Proof.

For a given real vector
v=(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n})^{T}
we compute

\displaystyle v^{T}Mv

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}v_{j}v_{k}\phi(x_{j}-x_{k})

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}v_{j}v_{k}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(x_{j}-x_{k})\xi}\hat{\phi}(\xi)d\xi

(3.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}e^{ix_{j}\xi}\right|^{2}\hat{\phi}(\xi)d\xi\geq 0,

(3.12)

which completes the proof.
∎

The following proposition is at the heart of our linear estimates.

Proposition 3.1
.

The matrix
A(\xi)
is diagonalizable for all
\xi
with
n
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(\xi)\leq\lambda_{2}(\xi)\dots\leq\lambda_{n}(\xi)
satisfies:

(i)

For all
\xi\neq 0
we have

\lambda_{k}(\xi)>0,\quad\forall 1\leq k\leq n

(3.13)

and

\lambda_{1}(0)=\dots=\lambda_{n-1}(0)=0,\quad\lambda_{n}(0)=\frac{\rho_{0}-\rho_{n}}{2}.

(3.14)

(ii)

Asymptotic behavior: There exists
\epsilon,\alpha>0
and a permutation

of
\{1,\dots,n\}
such that for all

\lambda_{1}(\xi)\geq\alpha\xi

(3.15)

and

\lim_{\xi\rightarrow+\infty}\lambda_{\tau(k)}(\xi)=\frac{\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k}}{2},\quad 1\leq k\leq n.

(3.16)

Proof.

We notice

A(\xi)=A_{0}(\xi)Q(\xi)

(3.17)

with

A_{0}(\xi)=(e^{-|d_{j}-d_{k}||\xi|})_{n\times n},\quad Q(\xi)=diag(\frac{\rho_{k-1}-\rho_{k}}{2})_{1\leq k\leq n}.

(3.18)

Then matrix
A
is similar to
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}A_{0}Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
. Since
A_{0}
is symmetric and
Q^{\frac{1}{2}}
is diagonal matrix with all components large than zero, we only need to prove all the above properties for
A_{0}(\xi)
. We compute

\varphi(x)=e^{-|x|},\quad,\widehat{\varphi}(\xi)=\frac{2}{1+\xi^{2}}.

(3.19)

Then
A_{0}(\xi)
is positive semi-definite due to Lemma
3.1
by noticing

A_{0}(\xi)=(\varphi(d_{k}\xi-d_{j}\xi))_{n\times n}.

(3.20)

Furthermore when
\xi=0
,
A_{0}(\xi)
is the all-ones matrix. Hence it has
n-1
zero eigenvalues and one nonnegative eigenvalue equals
n
. When
\xi\neq 0
, it is straightforward to compute the determinant of
A_{0}(\xi)
by replacing the
k
-th row
R_{k}
with
R_{k}-e^{-(d_{k}-d_{k-1})|\xi|}R_{k-1}
for
k=n,n-1,\dots,2
successively to obtain

\text{det}(A_{0}(\xi))=\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(1-e^{-2(d_{j+1}-d_{j})|\xi|}\right)>0.

(3.21)

This implies
A_{0}(\xi)
is positive definite for
\xi\neq 0
, therefore yields the first part of the proposition.

When
\xi\rightarrow 0^{+}
we use the following expansion

A_{0}(\xi)=J+B\xi+O(\xi^{2}).

(3.22)

Here

J=A_{0}(0)=\textbf{1}\textbf{1}^{T},\quad B=(-|d_{k}-d_{j}|)_{n\times n},\quad\textbf{1}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}.

(3.23)

We decompose the vector field as

\mathbb{R}^{n}=V\oplus V^{\perp}

(3.24)

where
V
denotes the eigenspace corresponding to the only nonzero eigenvalue of
J

V=\{\textbf{1}\}.

(3.25)

Thus it is straightforward to decompose a unit vector

into

v=av^{\prime}+bv^{\prime\prime},\quad v^{\prime}\in V,v^{\prime\prime}\in V^{\perp}

(3.26)

with the normalization

a^{2}+b^{2}=1,\quad\|v^{\prime}\|=1,\quad\|v^{\prime\prime}\|=1.

(3.27)

Direct computation shows

v^{T}A_{0}v=a^{2}\left((v^{\prime})^{T}Jv^{\prime}+O(\xi)\right)+\xi\left(2ab(v^{\prime})^{T}Jv^{\prime\prime}+b^{2}(v^{\prime\prime})^{T}Bv^{\prime\prime}\right)+O(\xi^{2})

(3.28)

Choosing a large constant
C
such that when

v^{T}A_{0}v\geq\frac{n}{2}C^{2}\xi-C\xi+O(\xi^{2})\geq C\xi

(3.29)

holds for all small
\xi
. Further, when
a\leq C|\xi|^{\frac{1}{2}}
, we observe

\displaystyle(v^{\prime\prime})^{T}Bv^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}-|d_{j}-d_{k}|v^{\prime\prime}_{j}v^{\prime\prime}_{k}

(3.30)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}(d_{j+1}-d_{j})\left(\sum_{k=1}^{j}v^{\prime\prime}_{j}\right)^{2}\geq 0

(3.31)

where we use
\sum_{j=1}^{n}v^{\prime\prime}_{j}=0
. Note the equality holds if and only if
v^{\prime\prime}=\textbf{0}
, then there exists
\mu>0
such that

(v^{\prime\prime})^{T}Bv^{\prime\prime}\geq\mu.

(3.32)

This implies

v^{T}A_{0}v\geq a^{2}(n+O(\xi))+O(\xi^{\frac{3}{2}})+\frac{\mu}{2}\xi+O(\xi^{2})\geq\frac{\mu}{4}\xi

(3.33)

for sufficiently small
\xi
. The above argument shows by choosing
\alpha=\min\{C,\mu/4\}
and sufficiently small

we have for all
0<\xi<\epsilon
,

\lambda_{1}(\xi)\geq\alpha\xi.

(3.34)

For the asymptotic behavior of
A_{0}(\xi)
at infinity we simply point out that
A_{0}(\xi)
tends to identity matrix as
\xi\rightarrow\infty
. This completes the proof of proposition.
∎

Given the asymptotic behavior in the above proposition we can also take an even function

\frac{\alpha}{2}|\xi|\leq\tilde{\lambda}(|\xi|)\quad\text{for}\quad\xi\rightarrow 0,\quad\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)\quad\text{non-decreasing and}\quad\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)\leq\lambda_{1}(\xi)\quad\text{for all}\quad\xi>0.

(4.51)

\displaystyle\lesssim(2^{s+1}C_{0})^{l}(\|g_{k}\|_{0}+\|g_{j}\|_{0})^{l}(\|g_{k}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}}+\|g_{j}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}})

(4.52)

\displaystyle+(2^{s+1}C_{0})^{l}(\|g_{k}\|_{1}+\|g_{j}\|_{1})(\|g_{k}\|_{0}+\|g_{j}\|_{0})^{l-1}(\|g_{k}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s}}+\|g_{j}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s}})

(4.53)

where the second inequality follows by expressing the integral in convolution form and reapplying inequality (
4.17
).
∎

4.3
Large time decay estimate

To begin the analysis of large time decay, we introduce the simplified notation

\displaystyle E_{0,s}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}(t)\|_{s},

\displaystyle E_{1,s}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}(t)\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s}},

(4.54)

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{0,s}(t)=F_{0,s}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}(t)\|_{1}\right)=F_{0,s}\left(E_{0,1}(t)\right)

\displaystyle,\quad\tilde{F}_{1,s}(t)=F_{1,s}(E_{0,0}(t)),\quad\tilde{F}_{2,s}(t)=F_{2,s}(E_{0,0}(t)).

Recalling equation (
3.7
), (
3.37
) and
(
3.35
) we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\vec{h}(\xi,t)=-|\xi|D(\xi)\vec{h}(\xi,t)+P^{-1}(\xi)\widehat{\vec{N}}(\xi,t).

(4.55)

It follows

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\|h_{i}\|_{s}

\displaystyle=\int|\xi|^{s}(\partial_{t}\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)\overline{\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)}+\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)\partial_{t}\overline{\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)})/(2|\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)|)d\xi

(4.56)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda_{i}(\xi)\int|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}(\xi)|d\xi+C\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int|\xi|^{s}|\mathscr{F}(N_{k,j})(\xi)|d\xi

\displaystyle\leq-\|h_{i}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}}+C\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int|\xi|^{s}|\mathscr{F}(N_{k,j})(\xi)|d\xi.

for each
1\leq i\leq n
. Then we set
a\delta=1
where constant
a>\frac{1}{2}
will be fixed later to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left((1+\delta t)^{a}\|h_{i}\|_{s}\right)\leq(1+\delta t)^{a}\left((1+\delta t)^{-1}\|h_{i}\|_{s}+\frac{d}{dt}\|h_{i}\|_{s}\right),

(4.57)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left((1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}\|h_{i}\|_{s}\right)\leq(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{2a-1}{2a}(1+\delta t)^{-1}\|h_{i}\|_{s}+\frac{d}{dt}\|h_{i}\|_{s}\right).

(4.58)

We decompose the integral for
\|h_{i}\|_{s}
into two parts
|\xi|<r
and
|\xi|>r
:

\displaystyle\|h_{i}\|_{s}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\xi|<r}|\xi|^{s}|\hat{h_{i}}(\xi)|d\xi+\frac{2}{\alpha}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\int_{|\xi|>r}\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h_{i}}(\xi)|d\xi

(4.59)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{|\xi|\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}|\xi|^{s}|\hat{h_{i}}(\xi)|d\xi+\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta t)\|h_{i}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\delta t)^{-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(1+\delta t)\|h_{i}\|_{\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}}.

Here we gain the first inequality by using the asymptotic behavior in (
3.38
). The second inequality holds if we take
r=c(1+\delta t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for some universal constant
c
and the third inequality is due to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with a universal constant
C
. For the bound of nonlinear terms we apply Proposition
4.1
, Proposition
4.2
and notation (
4.54
) to gain

\displaystyle\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\int|\xi|^{s}|\mathscr{F}(N_{k,j})(\xi)|d\xi\leq C\left(\tilde{F}_{0,s}E_{0,s+1}+\tilde{F}_{1,s}E_{1,s+1}+E_{0,1}\tilde{F}_{2,s}E_{1,s}\right)

(4.60)

\displaystyle\leq\left(2\epsilon+C\tilde{F}_{1,s}+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+\frac{C}{\epsilon}E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)E_{1,s+1}+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\left(\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)E_{0,s}.

Here we use the Cauchy-Schwarz estimate

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{0,s}E_{0,s+1}

\displaystyle=\tilde{F}_{0,s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\epsilon\int\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}\int\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)^{-1}|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi\right)

(4.61)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\epsilon\int\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}\int\left(|\xi|^{-1}+\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)\right)|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi\right)

(4.62)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\left(\epsilon+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}\right)\int\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}\int|\xi|^{s}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi\right)

(4.63)

\displaystyle=\left(\epsilon+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}\right)E_{1,s+1}+\frac{C}{\epsilon}\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}E_{0,s}

(4.64)

and

\displaystyle E_{0,1}\tilde{F}_{2,s}E_{1,s}

\displaystyle=E_{0,1}\tilde{F}_{2,s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)|\xi|^{s}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi\leq CE_{0,1}\tilde{F}_{2,s}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int|\xi|^{s+1}|\hat{h}_{i}|d\xi

(4.65)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\epsilon+\frac{C}{\epsilon}E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)E_{1,s+1}+\frac{C}{\epsilon}E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}E_{0,s}

(4.66)

where we use the properties of
\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)
:

\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)^{-1}\lesssim|\xi|^{-1}+\tilde{\lambda}(\xi),\quad\tilde{\lambda}(\xi)\lesssim|\xi|

(4.67)

which is obvious from (
3.38
).
Combining the inequalities (
4.56
), (
4.57
), (
4.58
), (
4.59
) and (
4.60
) by choosing a sufficiently small constant
\epsilon
we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\left((1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}\right)

(4.68)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}-(1+\delta t)^{a}\left(\frac{1}{4}-C\left(\tilde{F}_{1,s}+\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)\right)E_{1,s+1}

\displaystyle+(1+\delta t)^{a}C\left(\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)E_{0,s}

and similarly

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\left((1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}E_{0,s}\right)

(4.69)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}-(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{4}-C\left(\tilde{F}_{1,s}+\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)\right)E_{1,s+1}

\displaystyle+(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}C\left(\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)E_{0,s}.

These computations lead to the following proposition.

Proposition 4.3
.

There exists a universal constant
\gamma
such that if the initial data satisfies

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{0}+\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{1}\right)\leq\gamma,

(4.70)

then we have large time decay estimates

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|g_{j}(t)\|_{0}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{1}{4}},\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|g_{j}(t)\|_{1}\lesssim(1+t)^{-\frac{3}{4}}.

(4.71)

Proof.

By Remark
3.1
it is equivalent to deal with
h_{i}
. We aim to control the following

\tilde{E}(t):=(1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,1}(t)+(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}E_{0,0}(t)=(1+\delta t)^{a}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{1}+(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{0}

(4.72)

with bootstrap assumption

\tilde{E}(t)\leq c_{0}(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{3}{4}}

(4.73)

for a small constant
c_{0}
. This leads to

E_{0,1}(t)\leq c_{0},\quad E_{0,0}(t)\leq c_{0}

(4.74)

which implies we can choose
c_{0}
small enough such that

\frac{1}{4}-C\left(\tilde{F}_{1,s}+\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)\geq 0.

(4.75)

Then using (
4.68
) and (
4.69
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{E}(t)\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{7}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\left(\tilde{F}_{0,s}^{2}+E_{0,1}^{2}\tilde{F}_{2,s}^{2}\right)\tilde{E}

(4.76)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{7}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\left(E_{0,1}^{4}+E_{0,1}^{2}\right)\tilde{E}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{7}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+(1+\delta t)^{-2a}(\tilde{E}^{4}+\tilde{E}^{2})\tilde{E}

where in the second inequality we utilize the asymptotic approximations that
F_{0,s}\approx x^{2},F_{2,s}\approx 1
near
x=0
. To arrive at this conclusion we set
a>\frac{3}{4}
first, using assumption (
4.73
)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{E}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{7}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+(1+\delta t)^{-2a+5(a-\frac{3}{4})}(c_{0}^{5}+c_{0}^{3})

(4.77)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{7}{4}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+c_{0}^{5}+c_{0}^{3}\right)

(4.78)

where the second inequality holds if we take
a-\frac{7}{4}\leq-2a+5(a-\frac{3}{4})
, as well as
a\leq 1
. Therefore we take arbitrary
a
satisfying
\frac{3}{4}<a\leq 1
with
\delta=a^{-1}
. Theorem
2.15
provides
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\gamma
. Hence integral the above expression we gain

\displaystyle\tilde{E}(t)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{3}{4}}\left(\gamma+(1+\delta t)^{-a+\frac{3}{4}}\tilde{E}(0)+c_{0}^{5}+c_{0}^{3}\right)

(4.79)

\displaystyle\leq(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{3}{4}}(2\gamma+c_{0}^{5}+c_{0}^{3}).

(4.80)

This implies we can first set
\gamma
is uniformly small such that there exists small
c_{0}>0
satisfying

2\gamma+c_{0}^{5}+c_{0}^{3}\leq C^{-1}c_{0}

(4.81)

and then let
\gamma
to satisfy
\tilde{E}(0)\leq 2\gamma\leq c_{0}
. Therefore we have closed the bootstrap and proved the proposition.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

For any constant
\gamma>0
, there exists
\gamma_{0}>0
, such that if

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\tilde{g}_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{g}_{i}\|_{0}+\|\tilde{g}_{i}\|_{1}\leq\gamma_{0},

(4.82)

then

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|g_{i}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\|g_{i}(t)\|_{0}+\|g_{i}(t)\|_{1}\leq\gamma

(4.83)

holds for all time
t
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Choose uniformly small
\gamma
such that Proposition
4.3
holds. Use (
4.68
) and (
4.69
) again to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left((1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}(t)\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\left(F_{0,s}^{2}+(\|u\|_{1}+\|v\|_{1})^{2}F_{2,s}^{2}\right)(1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}

(4.84)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+\left(E_{0,1}^{4}+E_{0,1}^{2}\right)(1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}

(4.85)

\displaystyle\lesssim(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|h_{i}\|_{L^{2}}+(1+\delta t)^{-\frac{3}{2}}(1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}

(4.86)

Note firstly the inequality

\frac{d}{dt}F\leq(1+\delta t)^{-\frac{3}{2}}

means F is uniformly bounded in time
t
. Second the inequality

\frac{d}{dt}F\leq(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{5}{4}}

means F has the growth of
(1+\delta t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}
at most if we set
a>\frac{s}{2}+\frac{1}{4}
. Hence we have

(1+\delta t)^{a}E_{0,s}(t)\lesssim(1+t)^{a-\frac{s}{2}-\frac{1}{4}},

which finishes the proof.
∎
