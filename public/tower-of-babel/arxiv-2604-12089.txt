Dynamics of density fluctuations in atomic nuclei
Francesca Bonaiti, Gaute Hagen, Thomas Papenbrock

Abstract
We study the spatiotemporal patterns of density fluctuations in 16,24 O and 48 Ca using nuclear interactions from chiral effective field theory and the time-dependent coupled-cluster method. We find that two-particle–two-hole excitations generate small-amplitude fluctuations that are fast, short-ranged and of stochastic character.



Introduction.—
Nuclear dynamics involves fascinating processes such as fission where a heavy nucleus splits into lighter fragments. The time evolution of this process is complicated because it involves vastly different time scales and complex quantum many-body states, see Refs.
Simenel and Umar 2018
;
Sekizawa 2019
;
Schunck and Regnier 2022
for recent reviews. The inverse process, fusion, is similarly complicated when heavy nuclei are involved and plays a key role in the synthesis of superheavy elements.

The dynamics of fission and fusion is usually modeled with time-dependent Hartree Fock (Bogoliubov) methods and density functional theory. The recent computations
Goddard et al. 2015
;
Bulgac et al. 2016
;
Sekizawa and Yabana 2016
;
Goddard et al. 2020
;
Hasegawa et al. 2020
;
Abdurrahman et al. 2024
revealed that the time scales involved in fission typically range from tens to hundreds of fm
/c
, with equilibration being slowest and of the order of
1~\mathrm{zs}=10^{-21}~\mathrm{s}\approx 300~\mathrm{fm}/c
. Similarly, relevant experimental timescales for nuclear reactions are also of the order of 0.3 zs
Jedele et al. 2017
;
Simenel et al. 2020
.

These timescales are clearly visible in movies about nuclear collisions, as shown, e.g., by
Sekizawa and Yabana 2016
. Their movies show how densities evolve in time. These were produced at about 30 frames per second, and a second of showtime corresponds to
60~\mathrm{fm}/c
. Thus, the time step between frames is about 2 fm
/c
, and this is obviously much shorter than the time scales over which the densities change. One sees colliding nuclei that – dependent on the impact parameter – fuse or separate again, with the reaction products slowly vibrating and/or rotating.

How does nuclear dynamics look in an
ab initio
approach
Ekström et al. 2023
? Hamiltonians from effective field theories of quantum chromodynamics typically have momentum cutoffs
of 400–500 MeV
/c
. This corresponds to nucleon kinetic energies that are about 100 MeV and relevant time scales could be as short as 2 fm
/c
, which is much shorter than what is observed in time-dependent mean-field computations. Furthermore,
ab initio
computations include two-particle–two-hole excitations and thereby differ from the mean-field methods that are limited to one-particle–one-hole excitations. So, one clearly expects shorter time scales and more complex dynamics in an
ab initio
approach.

In this Letter, we study basic aspects of nuclear dynamics in an
ab initio
setting. The long-term goal is to compute fission and fusion within this framework but that requires further advances. Time-independent
ab initio
computations have reached the spherical nuclei
208,266
Pb
Hu et al. 2022
;
Bonaiti et al. 2025
and deformed nuclei in the mass
A\approx 80
region
Hu et al. 2024
. In contrast, time-dependent
ab initio
computations of nuclei, however, are less advanced
Pigg et al. 2012
. One reason was the insufficient computational resources available in the past. In time-dependent coupled-cluster theory
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1978
;
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1979
;
Kvaal 2012
;
Sverdrup Ofstad et al. 2023
, for example, one has to solve coupled-cluster equations for each time step; as we will see below, time steps are of the order of 0.2 fm
/c
, and studying a single time evolution to hundreds of
fm
/c
thus becomes a thousand times more expensive than a single static solution. However, present-day computing resources are now making this possible
Bonaiti et al. 2026a
.

We want to study the dynamics of nuclear density fluctuations. We are interested in their time scales and their amplitudes.
To do so, we use time-dependent coupled-cluster theory as formulated in Refs.
Kvaal 2012
;
Pigg et al. 2012
.
Our approach is based on Hamiltonians from chiral effective field theory
Epelbaum et al. 2009
;
Machleidt and Entem 2011
;
Hebeler et al. 2011
;
Hammer et al. 2020
. Our time evolutions start from densities that resulted from time-independent Hartree-Fock computations of the nucleus under consideration. Such densities are close to those obtained from more accurate coupled-cluster computations. (Their charge radii, for instance, differ only on the percent level
Koszorús et al. 2021
;
Companys Franzke et al. 2025
.) We then let such a density evolve in time, solving the time-dependent Schrödinger equation with the coupled-cluster method. As we will see, the density oscillates around the density that is a solution of the time-independent coupled-cluster equations. (This density is an equilibrium fixed point in the time evolution.) On time scales of tens of fm
/c
, the oscillations are qualitatively similar to what is seen in the movies of Ref.
Sekizawa and Yabana 2016
. However, in addition to these oscillations there are also short-time fluctuations, which are small in amplitude, of short range, and somewhat stochastic. This is the exciting discovery we report in this Letter.

Time-dependent coupled cluster theory.—

Our coupled-cluster computations start from a Hartree-Fock product state

|\phi_{0}\rangle\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{A}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}|0\rangle\ .

(1)

which serves as the reference state.
Here,
|0\rangle
is the zero-particle vacuum state, and
\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}
creates a nucleon in the single-particle state
|p\rangle\equiv\hat{a}_{p}^{\dagger}|0\rangle
. In Eq. (
1
) and in what follows, we used the convention that labels
i,j,k,\ldots
(
a,b,c,\ldots
) refer to occupied (unoccupied) states, while
p,q,r,\ldots
label any state.
In this work, the creation and annihilation operators are taken to be time-independent (see Refs.
Kvaal 2012
;
Pigg et al. 2012
;
Sverdrup Ofstad et al. 2023
for formulations with time-dependent orbitals).

In coupled-cluster theory, the ket ground-state is written as

|\psi(t)\rangle=e^{\hat{T}(t)}|\phi_{0}\rangle\ ,

(2)

where
\hat{T}(t)=\hat{T}_{1}(t)+\hat{T}_{2}(t)+\ldots+\hat{T}_{A}(t)
with

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{ia}t_{i}^{a}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{a}\hat{a}_{i}\ ,

(3)

\displaystyle\hat{T}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ijab}t_{ij}^{ab}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{b}\hat{a}_{j}\hat{a}_{i}\ ,

(4)

and so on. The bra ground-state is parameterized as

\langle\widetilde{\psi}(t)|=\langle\phi_{0}|\left[1+\hat{L}(t)\right]e^{-\hat{T}(t)}\ .

(5)

Here
\hat{L}(t)=\hat{L}_{1}(t)+\hat{L}_{2}(t)+\ldots+\hat{L}_{A}(t)
is a de-excitation operator with

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{ia}l^{i}_{a}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}_{a}\ ,

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{L}_{2}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ijab}l^{ij}_{ab}(t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{a}_{b}\hat{a}_{a}\ ,

(7)

and so on. The bra ground state is not the adjoint of the ket, and the coupled-cluster equations stem from a bi-variational principle
Kvaal 2012
;
Kvaal et al. 2025
. In what follows, we will consider two approximations, namely coupled-cluster singles and doubles (CCSD), where
\hat{T}=\hat{T}_{1}+\hat{T}_{2}
and
\hat{L}=\hat{L}_{1}+\hat{L}_{2}
are truncated at the two-particle–two-hole level, and coupled-cluster doubles (CCD), where only
\hat{T}_{2}
and
\hat{L}_{2}
are retained.

As we focus on density fluctuations, our interest is in the time-dependent density

\rho(r,t)=\langle\phi_{0}|\left(1+\hat{L}(t)\right)e^{-\hat{T}(t)}\hat{\rho}(r)e^{\hat{T}(t)}|\phi_{0}\rangle\ ,

(8)

where
\hat{\rho}(r)
is the one-body density operator.

The dynamical coupled-cluster equations can be derived from a variation of an action functional
Kvaal 2012
;
Kvaal et al. 2025
with respect to the parameters of
\hat{L}
and
\hat{T}
. In the CCSD approximation one finds

\begin{split}i\hbar\dot{t}_{0}(t)&=\langle\Phi_{0}|\overline{H}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\ ,\\
i\hbar\dot{t}_{i}^{a}(t)&=\langle\Phi^{a}_{i}|\overline{H}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\ ,\\
i\hbar\dot{t}_{ij}^{ab}(t)&=\langle\Phi^{ab}_{ij}|\overline{H}|\Phi_{0}\rangle\ ,\end{split}

(9)

and

\begin{split}-i\hbar\dot{l}_{0}(t)&=0\ ,\\
-i\hbar\dot{l}_{i}^{a}(t)&=\langle\Phi_{0}|L(t)\overline{H}|\Phi^{a}_{i}\rangle\ ,\\
-i\hbar\dot{l}_{ij}^{ab}(t)&=\langle\Phi_{0}|L(t)\overline{H}|\Phi^{ab}_{ij}\rangle\ .\end{split}

(10)

Here,

\overline{H}\equiv e^{-\hat{T}(t)}He^{\hat{T}(t)}

(11)

is the similarity-transformed Hamiltonian. In the CCD approximation one sets
t_{i}^{a}=0=l^{i}_{a}
at all times.

Our initial condition is the Hartree-Fock state (
1
). Thus,
t_{i}^{a}(0)=0
,
t_{ij}^{ab}(0)=0
,
l^{i}_{a}
=0, and
l^{ij}_{ab}(0)=0
. The time evolution will then introduce changes in the density via particle-hole-excitations.
We define density fluctuations as

\delta\rho(r,t)\equiv\rho(r,t)-\rho_{\rm av}(r)\ .

(12)

Here the time-averaged density
\rho_{\rm av}(r)
is

\rho_{\mathrm{a}v}(r)\equiv\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm max}-\tau_{\rm min}}\int\limits_{\tau_{\rm min}}^{\tau_{\max}}dt\;\rho(r,t)\ .

(13)

This average is approximately independent of the integration limits
\tau_{\rm min}
and
\tau_{\rm max}
for sufficiently large times
\tau_{\rm min},\tau_{\rm max}
, of the order of
100
fm/
c
.

We note that an equilibrium (or fixed) point of the dynamics appears when the right-hand side of the Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) vanishes. At this fixed point, the
\hat{T}
and
\hat{L}
amplitudes fulfill the time-independent coupled-cluster equations
Shavitt and Bartlett 2009
. One can linearize the equations in the vicinity of the fixed point and then obtains harmonic oscillations (for which the long-time average is the fixed point). While the Hartree-Fock density is close to the fixed-point density (see Supplemental Material), one is generally not in the linear regime. Consequently, the time averaged density (
13
) differs slightly from the CCSD density.

We use
cvode
, an adaptive solver from the
Sundials

Hindmarsh et al. 2005
;
Gardner et al. 2022
library, to integrate the set of ordinary differential equations (
9
–
10
). During the time evolution, we employ time steps of 0.2 fm
/c
in
cvode
and record results at increments of 1 fm
/c
.

Hamiltonian and model space.—
In this work, we employ two chiral effective field theory interactions, NNLO
sat

Ekström et al. 2015
and
\Delta
NNLO
GO
(394)
Jiang et al. 2020
. These interactions are formulated at next-to-next-to-leading order in the chiral expansion, and include three-body forces.
NNLO
sat
and
\Delta
NNLO
GO
(394) exhibit a momentum cutoff of
450
MeV/
c
and
394
MeV/
c
, respectively. Since we focus on short-time and short-range phenomena in this work, the difference in cutoff scales allows us to illustrate their model dependence.

Our time-dependent coupled-cluster calculations are performed in a Hartree–Fock basis. The model space consists of a single-particle basis from the spherical harmonic oscillator with excitations up to and including
N_{\rm max}\hbar\omega
. We use
N_{\rm max}=8
and the harmonic oscillator frequency
\hbar\Omega=16
MeV. While such a basis is not large enough to fully converge the ground-state energy, it is sufficient to reproduce collective excitations
Sun et al. 2025
;
Papenbrock 2024
.

Discussion.—
We discuss our results in the context of
(i)
beyond-mean-field simulations of nuclear dynamics and
(ii)
stochastic behavior in nuclei.
First, the short-range, short-time oscillations reported in this Letter are induced by two-particle two-hole correlations. As such they cannot be detected in a mean-field approach, which does not yield any fluctuations in observables
Balian and Vénéroni 1984
;
Simenel 2025
. The inclusion of these quantum fluctuation requires beyond-mean-field methods
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1978
;
Hoodbhoy and Negele 1979
;
Balian and Vénéroni 1992
;
Simenel 2025
. Examples are the beyond-mean-field approaches
Goutte et al. 2005
;
Regnier et al. 2016
to fission product distributions where collective wave packets were evolved in time, or time-dependent random phase approximation calculations
Simenel et al. 2020
. One can also introduce stochastic aspects
Abe et al. 1996
;
Fröbrich and Gontchar 1998
by employing an ensemble of initial conditions
Ayik 2008
.
In contrast, time-dependent coupled-cluster theory provides us with a systematic expansion to include many-body correlations beyond the mean field.

Second, the observed short-range, short-time fluctuations look somewhat stochastic. To analyze this further we compute the power spectrum
P(E)=|\widetilde{\delta\rho}(E)|^{2}
of the time-dependent signal (
12
) at a fixed radial distance
r_{0}
via a Fourier transform. Since our focus is on short-range effects, we choose
r_{0}\approx 10^{-4}
fm (we verified that results are qualitatively similar for other
r_{0}
values below
1
fm). Figure
3
shows the results from CCD computations of
P(E)
for
16,24
O and
48
Ca obtained with both the NNLO
sat
and the
\Delta
NNLO
GO
(394) interactions.

For all nuclei, the power spectrum appears to be almost energy-independent, comparable to white noise. This suggests that the dynamics of short-range density fluctuations is intrinsically stochastic. NNLO

This raises the question if one could interpret the stochastic behavior of density fluctuations as a signature of chaos in atomic nuclei. Traditionally, this relation rests on the fact that random matrix theory describes the statistics of nuclear spectra and resonance widths
Verbaarschot et al. 1985
;
Zelevinsky et al. 1996
;
Papenbrock and Weidenmüller 2007
;
Weidenmüller and Mitchell 2009
;
Mitchell et al. 2010
;
Kawano et al. 2016
. It is eye-opening to see stochastic aspects directly in nuclear dynamics.

Summary.–

Ab initio
computations of nuclear dynamics reveal the presence of short-range, short-time density fluctuations, which are stochastic in character. These fluctuations emerge across different nuclei and for different interactions. They are as short as
3
fm/
c
in typical interactions from chiral effective field theory. Their presence in nuclear dynamics is universal and unavoidable when correlations beyond the mean field are taken into account.
