Prevalence of and factors influencing early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth in the mountainous regions of Karnali, Nepal
Authors: Sona Shrestha, Vishnu Khanal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite the well-established benefits of initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of birth and of exclusive breastfeeding, many newborns across low- and middle-income countries do not receive timely or exclusive breastfeeding during the early neonatal period. Delayed initiation of breastfeeding and the introduction of prelacteal feeds remain common and are influenced by a range of maternal, neonatal, and health system factors. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth (EBF-2d) has recently been introduced as a new infant feeding indicator; however, its prevalence and associated factors are not well understood in Nepal. This study aimed to examine the prevalence of, and factors associated with early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted using a structured questionnaire among 423 randomly selected mothers of children aged 6–23 months in a remote mountainous region of Nepal. Prevalence was reported using frequency distribution, and associations were examined using Chi-square tests followed by multiple logistic regressions.ResultsChildren had lower odds of receiving EBF-2d if they were born low birthweight (Adjusted odds ratio (AOR): 0.33; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17, 0.63), born at home (AOR: 0.23; 95% CI: 0.08, 0.61), or their mothers had health issues hindering breastfeeding (AOR: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.23,0.90). Conversely, children had higher odds of receiving EBF-2d when mothers were assisted by a skilled health worker during birth (AOR: 3.23; 95% CI: 1.33, 7.82) and when mothers received antenatal counselling on exclusive breastfeeding (AOR: 2.18; 95% CI:1.16, 4.08). Similarly, children had lower odds of receiving EIBF if they had low birthweight (AOR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.19, 0.68) but they had higher odds when childbirth was assisted by skilled health workers (AOR: 9.15; 95% CI: 3.84, 21.8), were born at health facility (AOR: 16.08; 95% CI: 5.80, 44.5), and when mothers received antenatal counselling on breastfeeding on demand (AOR: 2.55; 95% CI: 1.48, 4.41), antenatal counselling on exclusive breastfeeding (AOR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.28, 4.23), and advice on avoiding artificial nipples (AOR: 4.49; 95% CI: 2.07, 9.75).ConclusionBreastfeeding practices in remote Karnali remain suboptimal, particularly among low birth weight infants and those born at home. Skilled birth attendance, institutional childbirth, and antenatal breastfeeding counselling were associated with improved early breastfeeding practices. Strengthening facility-based childbirth, ensuring skilled birth attendance, and enhancing antenatal counselling on optimal breastfeeding practices are essential for improving early breastfeeding outcomes.

Introduction

Breastfeeding is a fundamental life-saving practice that plays a critical role in ensuring optimal nutrition and survival of newborns [1]. Initiating breastfeeding within an hour of birth and maintaining exclusive breastfeeding (EBF) for the first six months significantly reduce neonatal and infant mortality, primarily by lowering the risk of infectious diseases [2]. The WHO and UNICEF recommend that breastfeeding begin within the first hour after birth and that infants receive only breastmilk, except for medicines and oral rehydration solutions during the first six months of life [1, 3]. Early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF) refers to feeding the newborn with breastmilk within the first hour after birth [4], while exclusive breastfeeding in the first two days after birth (EBF-2d) discourages pre-lacteal feeds, and supports practices such as skin-to-skin contact and rooming-in [5]. Beyond being a new indicator, EBF-2d is programmatically important because feeding newborns anything other than breast milk can delay critical early mother-infant contact and hinder the establishment of successful long-term breastfeeding [6].

Exclusive breastfeeding has the potential to lower under-five mortality by up to 11.6% in developing countries if 90% of infants were exclusively breastfed [7]. Newborns who are exclusively breastfed have a lower chance of dying or developing infections in their first month compared to those who receive only partial breastfeeding, reducing their vulnerability to conditions such as sepsis, diarrhea, and respiratory infections [8]. Breastfeeding within the first hour allows the newborn to receive colostrum, which is nutrient-rich first milk filled with antibodies and immune-protective factors [9]. Increasing breastfeeding globally to near-universal levels could save approximately 823,000 lives of under five children each year and prevent around 20,000 maternal deaths from breast cancer [10]. Early initiation of breastfeeding alone has the potential to avert about 33% of neonatal deaths [11].

Nepal Demographic and Health Survey 2022 (NDHS) showed that 55% of children received early breastfeeding and 59% were exclusively breastfed for the first two days after birth based on the 2-day recall indicator [12]. The prevalence of 24-hour-recall EBF indicator (0–5 months) has declined sharply between 2011 and 2022 from 70% to 56% [13]. While national trends suggest suboptimal breastfeeding practices overall, there is limited subnational evidence, particularly from remote provinces, on both EIBF and EBF-2d [14].

As of 2022, 25% of children under five in Nepal were stunted, with the Karnali region reporting the highest prevalence (36%) among the seven provinces of the country [12, 15]. The likelihood of children becoming wasted increases when breastfeeding after birth is delayed, prelacteal feeds are given, and exclusive breastfeeding is not maintained [16]. Nepal has introduced various initiatives to enhance breastfeeding practices, such as the Mother’s Milk Substitutes (Control of Sale and Distribution) Act of 1992 and the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) [17] yet breastfeeding practice remains suboptimal. A 2023 systematic review estimated national EBF prevalence at 43% [18], significantly below the national target of over 90% by 2030 [19].

Despite growing global interest, EBF-2d is a relatively new infant-feeding indicator [6] with limited evidence in Nepal, and no published studies from remote mountainous regions. To date, only NDHS 2022 has reported EBF-2d at the national level, with no district-level analyses [12]. Karnali’s challenging mountainous terrain, sparse distribution of health facilities, limited comprehensive obstetric care such as caesarean section (CS) capacity, and referral barriers may plausibly influence both EIBF and early breastfeeding support [20]. Given these gaps, this study provides place-based evidence from a remote mountainous district of Karnali Province, aiming to assess the prevalence of and factors associated with early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF) and exclusive breastfeeding for first two days after birth (EBF-2d).

Methods

Study setting and context

This study was conducted in Kalikot district which is located in the upper western mountainous region of Karnali Province, Nepal. The district, which ranges in elevation from 180 to 7,348 m above sea level, is characterized by rugged mountainous terrain and is largely rural, comprising eight administrative divisions: three municipalities and five rural municipalities [21]. This study was conducted in Khadachakra Municipality, which is the largest administrative centre of the district. Figure 1 presents the study map, highlighting the geographical boundaries of the study area [22]. Despite being officially classified as a municipality, the area is primarily a rural community in terms of infrastructure, available services, and daily living conditions. According to the National Census 2021, Khadachakra comprises 4,101 households and has a total population of 22,274, including 11,222 males and 11,062 females [23]. The current study is part of a larger study carried out with the objective of assessing the coverage of and factors associated with the regular use of Fortified Blended Flour (a supplementary food) among children aged 6–23 months [24].

Participants and procedure

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from February to March 2024 in a setting comprising 11 wards. A list of eligible children aged 6–23 months was compiled using local sources such as the vitamin A register covering nearly 100% of children under five and verified through the knowledge of health workers and female community health volunteers. The local female community health volunteers are the residents of the local community, actively engaged in maternal and child health and other social activities, are likely to know the hard-to-reach communities, and are able to identify 6-23-month-old children in their communities [22].

A sample size of 423 mother-child pairs was obtained based on a single population proportion formula using an assumed prevalence of 50%, a 95% confidence level, and a nonresponse rate of 10%, and marginal error of 5%. The final calculated sample size was 423 and 423 participants were successfully enrolled [25].

Participants were selected using probability proportional to population size (PPS) across the 11 wards. To implement PPS sampling, all wards were listed with their population of eligible children. The number of required samples for each ward was determined using the formula (each ward/Target population*100). Participants were then selected using random sampling from the list of each ward. Each selected ward contributed participants, proportional to its population size [24]. Mothers were the preferred respondents, and in their absence, primary caregivers were interviewed. No one refused to participate when they were invited. When there was more than one child aged 6–23 months in the same household, the youngest child was included. Participants who were unable to respond due to severe health issues or cultural constraints were excluded. In total, 423 mother-child pairs were included in the study.

Face-to-face interviews were conducted at the participants’ homes using structured questionnaires. The breastfeeding variables were adapted from the WHO/UNICEF Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) guideline, ‘Indicators for assessing infant and young child feeding practices: definitions and measurement methods’ [6] and NDHS 2022 [12]. We pretested the tool among 10% (n = 43) of a similar population in nearby municipalities and modified the flow and wording of the Nepali version of the questionnaire before use.

Ethics

Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Medicine, Maharajgunj, Kathmandu (22 December 2023/286). Participants were informed in the local language about the study's purpose and assured that their participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw at any time. Written consent was obtained before conducting interviews, and mothers provided consent on behalf of themselves and their infants.

Variables

Outcome variables

Two IYCF indicators were included for this study: Early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF) and exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth (EBF-2d) [6]. Early initiation of breastfeeding measures the percentage of children who were put to the breast within one hour of birth [6, 26]. Mothers were asked “How soon after birth was your child first put to the breast?” Responses were recorded in hours and categorized into: early initiation (within one hour) and after one hour [6, 12]. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth measures the percentage of children who were exclusively breastfed for the first two days after birth [6, 27]. Mothers were asked “in the first two days after birth, what did you feed your child?” A detailed list of commonly used prelacteal feeds in this setting (formula, animal milk, sugar water, honey, ghee, butter) was read aloud. The definition explicitly excluded breast milk from another mother and any food or liquids besides breastmilk, unless medically indicated which was in line with the definition of IYCF guideline [6]. Mothers’ responses were coded to determine whether the newborn received only breastmilk with the exception of medicines and oral rehydration solution; i.e., ‘exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth’ or ‘non-exclusive breastfeeding’.

Independent variables

We collected data on exposure variables, and sociodemographic variables (background variables) based on extensive literature review [28–31]. The exposure variables included low birth weight; place of childbirth; assistance during childbirth; mode of birth; maternal health issues hindering breastfeeding; maternal experience of breast redness or blisters; child experiencing difficulty during breastfeeding; mothers receiving advice on breastfeeding on demand; mothers receiving advice on avoiding artificial nipples; receiving advice on where to seek help for child feeding problems; and receiving advice on exclusive breastfeeding during antenatal care (ANC). Baby- Friendly Hospital Initiative recommended counselling variables were also as exposures of interest consistent with breastfeeding promotion frameworks that conceptualize counselling as an intervention influencing breastfeeding practices [32–34]. Similarly, key background socio-demographic variables included mother’s age; gender of the child; religion; caste; father’s education; mother’s education; number of children in the household; first child status; type of family; father’s occupation; mother’s occupation; and wealth quintile. Information on maternal occupation and health issues was collected at the time of the interview and was not specifically referenced to the time of birth. To assess economic status, a wealth index was developed using principal component analysis (PCA), based on indicators such as access to drinking water, sanitation, cooking fuel, presence of a separate kitchen, type of flooring, and ownership of household items like electricity, radio, television, mobile phones, sofas, and cupboards [35]. The resulting wealth quintiles were grouped into three categories: poor (40%), middle (40%), and rich (20%) [36]. Prelacteal feeding refers to the practice of giving a newborn any food or liquid before breastfeeding is initiated. This does not include medicines, oral rehydration solutions, vitamin drops, or breast milk from another mother [37].

Parental education was categorized into three levels: no formal education, school-level education, and higher education [22]. Fathers’ occupations were classified into four groups: employed in formal sector (i.e., jobs or services), employed in semi-formal sector (i.e. small businesses or labor), foreign employment, and not employed in a paid job, while mothers’ occupations were grouped into three categories: employed in formal sector (i.e., jobs or services), employed in semi-formal sector (i.e. small businesses or labor), or not employed in a paid job (household and agricultural work) [38].

While a Hindu majority country, Nepal is home to diverse faiths. According to the National Census 2021, Nepal is home to diverse religious groups, including Hinduism, Buddhism, Islam, Kiranti, Christianity, Prakriti, Bon, Jainism, Bahai, and Sikhism [23]. Given the small number of non-Hindu participants, the religion variable was consolidated into two groups: Hindu and Non-Hindu [22]. Similarly, caste was classified into three main categories: Brahmin/Chhetri, Dalit, and Janajati, with an additional group for other castes such as Shahi and Malla [39].

Statistical analysis

The prevalence of EIBF and EBF-2d were reported using frequency distribution. Univariate associations between these breastfeeding practices and their individual association with independent variables were investigated using Chi-square tests. The factors with p < 0.10 were further subjected to multiple logistic regression [40]. Multiple logistic regression models were conducted for each exposure variable, adjusting for background demographic characteristics. This approach was adopted to avoid 'Table 2 Fallacy' following the recommendations from available literature [41, 42]. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was set at a p-value of < 0.05. A sensitivity analysis was also performed among 6–11 months sub- group to examine if these factors remained consistent. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with all values below 10, indicating no serious collinearity. Model fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (p = > 0.05). Data were entered into Epi-data and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, Version 22).

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the characteristics of the 423 participants. The mean age of the children was 15.06 months (SD: 5.46). Regarding education, 59.1% of fathers and 53.4% of mothers had secondary education. One in four (40%) households were in the poor quintile, with only 17.8% in the rich quintile.

Prevalence of early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth

Overall, 84.4% (95% CI: 0.80, 0.87) of newborns were breastfed within the first hour of birth, and 83.9% (95% CI: 0.80, 0.87) were exclusively breastfed for first two days after birth. Only 6.1% (95% CI: 0.04, 0.08; n = 26) of infants received prelacteal feeds. Of these 26 infants who received prelacteal feeds, 11 received cow or buffalo milk as the most common prelacteal feed. The other prelacteal feeds were powdered milk (packaged dairy milk powder intended for general household consumption, not infant formula), ghee (refined butter), and fruit juice.

Factors associated with early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding for first two days after birth

Tables 1 and 2 show the distribution of early initiation of breastfeeding (EIBF) and exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth (EBF-2d) by various characteristics. Childbirth in a health facility, assistance by a skilled attendant, and vaginal delivery were common in the study sample (95.3%, 94.1%, and 96.5%, respectively). Seven exposure variables were significantly associated with EIBF after controlling for background variables (Table 3). Children born in health facilities (AOR: 16.08; 95% CI: 5.80, 44.5), and with the assistance of skilled birth attendants (AOR: 9.15; 95% CI: 3.84, 21.8) had higher odds of receiving EIBF. Children whose mothers were counselled not to use artificial teats or pacifiers (AOR: 4.49; 95% CI: 2.07, 9.75), whose mothers received counselling on breastfeeding on demand (AOR: 2.55; 95% CI: 1.48, 4.41), and whose mothers received antenatal counselling on exclusive breastfeeding during antenatal care (AOR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.28, 4.23) were also likely to receive EIBF. Conversely, children who were born with low birthweight had lower odds of initiating breastfeeding early (AOR: 0.36; 95% CI: 0.19, 0.68).

For EBF-2d, six factors remained significant after controlling for background variables (Table 4). Children born with the assistance of skilled birth attendants (AOR: 3.23; 95% CI: 1.33, 7.82), whose mothers were counselled on exclusive breastfeeding during antenatal care (AOR: 2.18; 95% CI: 1.16, 4.08) and on breastfeeding on demand (AOR: 1.67; 95% CI: 0.97, 2.89), had higher odds of being exclusively breastfed during the first two days after birth. Conversely, children with low birth weight (AOR: 0.33; 95% CI: 0.17, 0.63), those whose mothers had health issues hindering breastfeeding (AOR: 0.45; 95% CI: 0.23, 0.90), and those born at home (AOR: 0.23; 95% CI: 0.08, 0.61) had lower odds of being exclusively breastfed during the first two days after birth.

Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence of and factors associated with early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding for first two days after birth. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study conducted in the upper mountains of Nepal reporting these two breastfeeding indicators.

More than 80% of children received the recommended EIBF and EBF-2d. While reports on exclusive breastfeeding for the first two days after birth in Nepal are rare, our finding is higher than that reported in the NDHS 2022 (59%) [12], India (62.3%) [43] and Bangladesh (62%) [44]. The prevalence of EIBF was also notably higher in our study compared to those reported from Kaski district (46.3%) [45], Rupandehi district (42.2%) [46] and a large study including 21 districts of Nepal (41.8%) [47]. The relatively high rates of breastfeeding practices in this study setting may be explained by the remoteness of and a stronger cultural preference to breastfeeding. In this area, access to infant formula, and other commercial feeding alternatives is limited; therefore, mothers are more likely to rely on breastfeeding immediately after childbirth [48]. Despite these encouraging findings, gaps in optimal breastfeeding practices remain. Recognizing such challenges, the Government of Nepal emphasized the need for supportive breastfeeding environments during World Breastfeeding Week 2025 (Fig. 2), highlighting barriers faced by mothers, particularly when returning to work, and underscoring that creating a supportive environment for breastfeeding is a shared responsibility. This programmatic message aligns with our findings, which emphasize the importance of health system support and counselling in promoting optimal breastfeeding practices.

Skilled birth attendance was found to be associated with both outcome variables, EIBF and EBF-2d. Positive influence of skilled birth attendance on breastfeeding outcomes have been previously reported by studies conducted in western Nepal [46] and from India [49, 50]. Evidence from facility-based breastfeeding interventions further demonstrates that support from trained healthcare providers and skilled staff can improve both early initiation and exclusive breastfeeding in the immediate postnatal period [51–53]. In addition, a study from the WHO European Region countries also reported that exclusive breastfeeding within 24–48 h at the time of discharge from hospital was associated with receiving support from a skilled birth attendant [54].

Low birth weight was negatively associated with both outcome variables. Evidence from Nepal (46), India (50) and Sri-Lanka (54) indicates that mothers delivering low birth weight infants had lower odds of initiating breastfeeding early, a critical determinant of exclusive breastfeeding during the first two days after birth. Since early initiation is a prerequisite for maintaining exclusive breastfeeding in the immediate postnatal period, this may have also influenced EBF-2d negatively. Low birth weight infants often experience feeding difficulties and clinical complications requiring medical attention, which may delay breastfeeding initiation and necessitate supplementation, thereby reducing exclusive breastfeeding even in the earliest days after birth [55].

Key breastfeeding promotion and counselling variables, including antenatal counselling on exclusive breastfeeding, breastfeeding on demand, and avoiding artificial nipples, were associated with early initiation of breastfeeding. Similarly, antenatal counselling on exclusive breastfeeding and breastfeeding on demand were associated with exclusive breastfeeding during the first two days after birth. These findings align with evidence that structured breastfeeding counselling, particularly when delivered through Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI) recommended practices, has a sustained positive effect on breastfeeding outcomes [51]. Evidence from Nepal, has shown that BFHI-consistent counselling indicators improve both exclusive and predominant breastfeeding practices [56, 57]. Similar associations have also been reported from India, Brazil, Ethiopia, and Ghana, where antenatal breastfeeding counselling improved early initiation and early exclusive breastfeeding [58–61].

Children born in health facilities had higher odds of initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of life than those born at home. This finding is consistent with evidence from Nepal, other South Asian countries, Ethiopia, and Ghana, highlighting the positive influence of institutional childbirth on timely breastfeeding [59, 62–64]. The presence of skilled birth attendants facilitates immediate counselling, practical support, early skin-to-skin contact, and adherence to postnatal care guidelines, all of which promote early initiation of breastfeeding [65]. Our study found that being born in a health facility increased the likelihood of EBF-2d. This finding is supported by a study conducted in Vietnam, which reported that infants born in health facilities, particularly district hospitals, had higher odds of being exclusively breastfed during the hospital stay, which is reflective of EBF-2d [66].

Finally, our study shows that the children whose mothers had health issues during childbirth had lower odds of EBF-2d. While other studies on EBF-2d are scarce, poor maternal health status and adverse breastfeeding outcomes have been previously reported [67, 68]. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses [69, 70] have demonstrated that cesarean delivery and other maternal complications are associated with delayed initiation of breastfeeding and reduced exclusive breastfeeding rates. Such poor breastfeeding outcomes can be attributed to delayed maternal recovery, separation of mother and infant, and physiological or procedural barriers to early breastfeeding [71]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis found that delayed onset of lactation and breastfeeding problems were common among mothers who experienced health issues [70].

This study has several notable strengths. To the best of our knowledge, it is the first empirical study to report factors influencing EBF-2d in Nepal. The use of primary data collected directly from mothers allowed for a contextual understanding of infant feeding practices. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design restricts the ability to establish causal relationships between the associated factors and breastfeeding practices. Recall bias may have occurred, as mothers were asked to retrospectively report feeding practices among children aged 6–23 months. To assess whether recall over a 23-month window may have influenced the findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis, which suggests that the impact of recall bias on the main results is likely limited. Nonetheless, retrospective maternal reporting remains the most feasible population-based approach recommended by the WHO IYCF measurement guidelines [6]. Social desirability bias is also possible, given the strong promotion of breastfeeding in the community and the involvement of public health professionals in data collection. Additionally, residual confounding from factors such as maternal intentions, cultural norms, and family support may persist despite adjustment. Although the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit tests indicated acceptable calibration for our multivariable models, the high prevalence of facility delivery, skilled attendance, and vaginal delivery should be taken into account. Furthermore, as the findings are based on data from a single geographic and cultural context, their generalizability to other regions of the country is limited. Further place-based studies using standard methodologies are recommended to enhance comparability.

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study offers important insights into breastfeeding challenges in the remote Karnali region and underscores the need for targeted interventions. Beyond encouraging facility births, priority should be given to improving the quality of ANC counselling, strengthening post-caesarean delivery lactation support, and ensuring workplace protections such as paid maternity leave, breastfeeding rooms, and flexible working hours [72, 73]. Public health offices may consider simple implementation checklists, aligned with the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding and the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI)—including routine audits of ANC counselling quality; ensuring BFHI-aligned lactation support following caesarean delivery; establishing breastfeeding or lactation rooms in public institutions; and engaging employers to promote breastfeeding-friendly workplaces [74]. At a societal level, structural changes are essential to enable working mothers to exclusively breastfeed, including government-supported paid maternity leave and workplace breastfeeding spaces in both public and private sectors [75–77]. The burden of breastfeeding should not rest solely on mothers; rather, society at large and the health system must share responsibility for creating a supportive environment to enable optimal infant feeding outcomes.

Conclusion

This study assessed the prevalence of, and factors associated with, early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding during the first two days after birth in the remote Kalikot district. Overall, the prevalence of both indicators was relatively high, indicating generally positive breastfeeding practices in this hard-to-reach mountainous setting. However, important gaps persist among mothers experiencing health complications during childbirth, those with low birth weight infants, and in situations where antenatal breastfeeding counselling is limited (e.g., home births). These findings highlight the critical role of health system support in improving early breastfeeding practices, particularly skilled early postnatal care and BFHI-recommended counselling, education, and support. As this study is based on a single district, further multi-district research is recommended. Nonetheless, these findings contribute valuable evidence to support Nepal’s progress toward its 2030 breastfeeding and child health goals.
