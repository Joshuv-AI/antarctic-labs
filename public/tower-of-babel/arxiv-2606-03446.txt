Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials on the Ball and Radial Extensions
Lidia Fernández, Juan Antonio Villegas

Abstract
A primary method for constructing orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball consists of combining a Jacobi-type radial component with a spherical harmonic angular part. Building upon this framework and using Jacobi-Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials, this paper introduces Type I and Type II multiple orthogonal polynomials on the multidimensional ball. To demonstrate the practical utility of these definitions, we establish multivariate extensions of several fundamental results from univariate multiple orthogonality. Finally, we extend the construction to more general domains by introducing multiple orthogonality with respect to radial weights. Keywords: Multiple orthogonal polynomials, multivariate orthogonal polynomials, radial bases, Spherical harmonics. MSC: 33C45, 33C50, 33C55, 42C05.



1
Introduction

Multiple orthogonal polynomials arise when the classical notion of orthogonality is extended to settings involving several measures instead of a single one. In this framework, a polynomial is required to satisfy a collection of orthogonality conditions, each associated with a different measure, and these conditions are organized according to a multi‑index. This naturally leads to two complementary formulations, usually referred to as Type I and Type II, which together capture the structure of the underlying system (see
[
18
,
22
,
28
]
for general settings in the univariate case).

The theory gained prominence as it became clear that these polynomials appear in a variety of contexts where several influences or sources interact simultaneously. They play a central role in Hermite–Padé approximation, where one seeks to approximate vector‑valued functions in a coordinated way
[
27
]
. They also surface in problems from probability and mathematical physics, such as random matrix models with an external field or systems of non-intersecting paths
[
5
,
11
]
. In these settings, the recurrence relations and analytic properties of the polynomials provide an effective means of describing the behavior of the models.

While univariate multiple orthogonality and classical multivariate orthogonality have long been, and continue to be, the focus of extensive research, work on multivariate multiple orthogonality is still in its early stages. In
[
15
]
, the authors introduced a general framework for defining Type I and Type II bivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials on a two‑dimensional lattice, along with several extensions and illustrative examples. More recently,
[
20
]
examined the mixed‑type case, focusing on bivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials along the step‑line. In
[
14
]
, the authors further contributed to the field by developing a Rodrigues-type formula for multiple orthogonal polynomials defined on the simplex and, in addition, they established a connection with a Hermite–Padé type approximation of the kind introduced in
[
25
]
.

When studying systems of orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball, one of the most commonly used bases consists of polynomials obtained as the product of a Jacobi‑type polynomial in the radial variable and a spherical harmonic. One of the main objectives of this work is to build on this idea in order to construct Type I and Type II multiple orthogonal polynomials on the ball. To this end, we replace the radial component with Jacobi–Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials while retaining the spherical harmonic. In this way, we show that the resulting family forms a basis of polynomials satisfying multiple orthogonality relations on the ball. Using this methodology, we also aim to extend orthogonal polynomials associated with general radial weights to the multiple orthogonal setting, providing an example illustrating how multiple Laguerre polynomials can be used to construct multivariate polynomials. In addition, we generalize existing results on multiple orthogonality to this multivariate framework.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section
2
provides essential preliminary tools, detailing properties of Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials, with a specific focus on the Jacobi-Piñeiro and multiple Laguerre polynomials, as well as an overview of spherical harmonics and orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball. In Section
3
, we present extensions of orthogonal polynomials on the ball to the multiple orthogonal framework, explicitly describing the case for two variables (the disk). Afterwards, in Section
4
, these newly developed constructions and their properties are applied to establish multivariate extensions of two fundamental results from univariate multiple orthogonality: the biorthogonality relation and Nearest Neighbor Relations. Finally, we study an extension to the multiple setting for orthogonal polynomials for radial weights in Section
5
, introducing MOPs for multivariate Hermite Weights as an example.

2
Preliminaries

With the aim of introducing an extension of MOPs in multi-dimensional domains, in this section we recall some tools and fix some notations: generalities about multiple orthogonal polynomials in one variable, focusing on the special case of Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials, spherical harmonics, and orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball.

2.1
Multiple Orthogonal polynomials

In the Orthogonal Polynomials setting, multiple orthogonality is a generalization of standard orthogonality where, rather than fixing a single measure and, in turn, an integral inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\omega}
, several measures are provided, so that polynomials, named Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials (MOPs) satisfy orthogonality relations with respect to a system of measures and, in turn, a set of inner products
[
28
,
18
,
3
]
.

More precisely, consider a set of positive weight functions
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r}
and their associated integral inner products

\langle f,g\rangle_{l}:=\langle f,g\rangle_{\omega_{l}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)g(t)\omega_{l}(t)\mathrm{d}t,

for
f,g\in\bigcap_{l=1}^{r}L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\omega_{l})
. The orthogonality relations with respect to these inner products, as well as the degree of the polynomials, are determined by a multi-index
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{0}
. We will denote the modulus or the norm of a vector
\vec{v}=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
as
|\vec{v}|=|v_{1}|+\cdots+|v_{m}|
, so that
|\vec{n}|=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{r}
.

Fixing
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}_{0}
, there are two ways of defining multiple orthogonal polynomials, known as Type I and Type II multiple orthogonal polynomials. Type I MOPs are
r
polynomials often represented in a vector
(A_{\vec{n},1},\dots,A_{\vec{n},r})
, such that for
l=1,\dots,r
the degree of
A_{\vec{n},l}
is at most
n_{l}-1
and they satisfy

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\langle A_{\vec{n},j},t^{k}\rangle_{l}=\delta_{k,|\vec{n}|-1},\qquad 0\leq k\leq|\vec{n}|-1.

(1)

On the other hand, Type II MOP is a polynomial (usually considered monic)
P_{\vec{n}}
of degree
|\vec{n}|
which is orthogonal to polynomials up to degree
n_{l}-1
with respect to the
l
-th measure. This means

\langle P_{\vec{n}},t^{k}\rangle_{l}=0,\quad 0\leq k\leq n_{l}-1,\quad\text{ for }l=1,\dots,r.

(2)

These different ways of constructing multiple orthogonal polynomials are, indeed, equivalent for a fixed multi-index
\vec{n}
, which is said to be
normal
if the existence of both Type I and Type II polynomials holds. If every multi-index is normal for a set of weights
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{r}
and the associated measures, this system of measures is called a
perfect system
.

Multiple orthogonality has several results extending some others of the standard orthogonal polynomials theory: existence and uniqueness theorems, Christoffel-Darboux formulas or Hermite-Padé Approximation, among others, see
[
18
, Section 23.1]
, or
[
28
, Section 1]
. In particular, we will spotlight the Nearest Neighbour Recurrence Relations (NNRR), which extend the three-term recurrence relation satisfied by univariate orthogonal polynomial sequences.

From now on, assume that every mentioned multi-index is normal and let
\vec{n},\vec{m}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
. The multi-indices
\vec{n}
and
\vec{m}
are said to be
neighbours
if either
\vec{n}=\vec{m}+\vec{e}_{l}
or
\vec{n}=\vec{m}-\vec{e}_{l}
for any
l\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, where
\vec{e}_{l}=(0,\cdots,0,\overset{(l)}{1},0,\cdots,0)
is a vector whose components are zero excepting the one in the
l
-th position, which is one. If we fix
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, and consider
\vec{m}_{1},\dots,\vec{m}_{r},\vec{m}_{r+1}=\vec{n}
a path of neighbour multi-indices from
\vec{m}_{1}=(n_{1}-1,\dots,n_{r}-1)
to
\vec{n}
and choose
\vec{n}+\vec{e}_{k}
for any
k\in\{1,\dots,r\}
, the following relation holds
[
18
, Theorem 23.1.7]
:

tP_{\vec{n}}(t)=P_{\vec{n}+\vec{e}_{k}}(t)+a_{\vec{n},0}P_{\vec{n}}(t)+\sum_{l=1}^{r}a_{\vec{n},l}P_{\vec{m}_{l}}(t).

(3)

A different relation concerning the closest multi-indices
\vec{n}-\vec{e}_{l}
for
l=1,\dots,r
would be
[
28
, Section 3.2]

tP_{\vec{n}}(t)=P_{\vec{n}+\vec{e}_{k}}(t)+b_{\vec{n},0}P_{\vec{n}}(t)+\sum_{l=1}^{r}b_{\vec{n},l}P_{\vec{n}-\vec{e}_{l}}(t).

(4)

2.2
Jacobi-Piñeiro Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials

In this document, we will center our attention on a specific system of measures which gives rise to the so-called
Jacobi-Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials
, a multiple orthogonal extension of the well-known Jacobi polynomials.

Most of the time, Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials are introduced as those satisfying (
1
) and (
2
) with the weights (see
[
28
, Section 3.7]
)

\tilde{\omega}_{l}(t):=t^{\alpha_{l}}(1-t)^{\gamma},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,\quad 1\leq l\leq r.

These polynomials were first introduced in
[
24
]
for the case
\gamma=0
. In this paper, we map the variable
t
so that
t\longmapsto 1-t
. With this change, we obtain different expressions – but still computable using the standard ones – that are more suitable for our concerns.

Then, given
\alpha,\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{p}>-1
such that
\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
whenever
i\neq j
, consider the following weight functions supported on
[0,1]
:

\omega_{l}(t):=t^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\gamma_{l}},\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,\quad 1\leq l\leq r.

(5)

The condition
\gamma_{i}-\gamma_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
whenever
i\neq j
guaranties that this set of weights composes an AT-System, which is a particular case of a perfect system
[
18
, Section 23.1.2]
. In this way, the existence of both types of MOPs is ensured for every
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
.

Denote
\vec{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{r})
and consider a multi-index
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
. The Type II Jacobi-Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials can be computed as
[
2
]

P^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n}}(t)=\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{r}(t)}\dfrac{d^{n_{r}}}{dt^{n_{r}}}\omega_{r}(t)(1-t)^{n_{r}}\right)\cdots\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{2}(t)}\dfrac{d^{n_{2}}}{dt^{n_{2}}}\omega_{2}(t)(1-t)^{n_{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{1}(t)}\dfrac{d^{n_{1}}}{dt^{n_{1}}}\omega_{1}(t)(1-t)^{n_{1}}\right)t^{|\vec{n}|}.

(6)

These polynomials have degree
|\vec{n}|
and are orthogonal to every polynomial of degree lower than
n_{l}
with respect to
\omega_{l}
. More precisely,

\langle P^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n}}(t),t^{k}\rangle_{l}=\int_{0}^{1}P^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n}}(t)\,t^{k}\,t^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\gamma_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}t=0\qquad\text{ if }0\leq k\leq n_{l}-1,\qquad 1\leq l\leq r.

(7)

This representation in terms of a Rodrigues-type formula was introduced in
[
2
]
, together with other expressions for Type II MOP with respect to classical weights. Owing to their reduced complexity, Type II MOPs have historically received greater attention than their Type I counterparts. Consequently, explicit hypergeometric representations for Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials have only recently emerged. Initial formulations for the two-measure case (
r=2
) were established in
[
8
]
and
[
7
]
, with subsequent generalizations for an arbitrary number of measures
r\geq 2
provided in
[
9
]
:

A^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n},l}(t)=\dfrac{\prod_{k=1}^{r}(\gamma_{k}+\alpha+|\vec{n}|)_{n_{k}}}{(n_{l}-1)!\prod_{k=1,k\neq l}^{p}(\gamma_{k}-\gamma_{l})_{n_{k}}}\dfrac{\Gamma(\gamma_{l}+\alpha+|\vec{n}|)}{\Gamma(\alpha+|\vec{n}|)\Gamma(\gamma_{l}+1)}\times\\
\times{{}_{r+1}}F_{r}\left(\begin{matrix}-n_{l}+1,\gamma_{l}+\alpha+|\vec{n}|,(\gamma_{l}+1)\vec{1}_{r-1}-\vec{\gamma}^{(l)}-\vec{n}^{(l)}\\
\gamma_{l}+1,(\gamma_{l}+1)\vec{1}_{r-1}-\vec{\gamma}^{(l)}\end{matrix}\,;\,1-t\right),

(8)

where we denote
\vec{1}_{r-1}=(1,\dots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{(r-1)}
and given
\vec{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
\vec{a}^{(l)}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{l-1},a_{l+1},\dots,a_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r-1}
is the vector
\vec{a}
with the
l
-th component removed.

As mentioned in the previous section, the polynomials
A^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n},l}
have degree at most
n_{l}-1
,
1\leq l\leq r
, and all together satisfy

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\langle A^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n},l},t^{k}\rangle_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{1}A^{(\alpha,\vec{\gamma})}_{\vec{n},l}\,t^{k}t^{\alpha}(1-t)^{\gamma_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}t=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }0\leq k\leq|\vec{n}|-2,\\
1&\text{ if }l=|\vec{n}|-1.\end{cases}

(9)

Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials have attracted significant attention within the field of multiple orthogonality since their introduction. Various representations in terms of hypergeometric functions were established in
[
2
,
4
]
, reflecting their analytical versatility. Beyond their theoretical properties, they play a crucial role in several practical contexts, including rational approximation
[
3
,
28
]
, electrostatic partners
[
21
]
, number theory
[
16
]
, and even random walks
[
7
]
, urn models
[
17
]
and related stochastic processes
[
10
]
, see also
[
22
]
.

2.3
Multiple Laguerre polynomials

Another commonly used family of multiple orthogonal polynomials is that extending classical Laguerre polynomials to this multiple orthogonal setting. Recall that, given
\alpha>-1
, Laguerre polynomials
\{L_{n}^{(\alpha)}\}_{n\geq 0}
are those orthogonal with respect to the weight
t^{\alpha}e^{-t}
on
[0,\infty)
. We may extend Laguerre polynomials to the multiple case by varying the parameter
\alpha
, considering
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{r}>-1
, giving rise to multiple Laguerre polynomials
of the first kind
, see
[
2
, Section 3.2]
,
[
18
, Section 23.4.1]
or
[
28
, Section 3.6.1]
. However, we will work with a different extension, where the exponential decay at infinity is
e^{-t}
is replaced by
e^{-c_{l}t}
with parameters
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}
, obtaining multiple Laguerre polynomials
of the second kind

[
6
, Section 3.1]
.

More precisely, let
\alpha>-1
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{r}>0
with
c_{i}\neq c_{j}
whenever
i\neq j
, and define the weights
[
28
, Section 3.6.2]

\omega_{l}(t):=t^{\alpha}e^{-c_{l}t},\qquad t\geq 0,\quad 1\leq l\leq r.

Then, the Type II multiple Laguerre polynomial of the second kind, for
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
, denoted by
L^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n}}
where
\vec{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})\in(0,+\infty)^{r}
, is monic and satisfies

\langle L^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n}},t^{k}\rangle_{l}=\int_{0}^{\infty}L^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n}}(t)\,t^{k}\,t^{\alpha}e^{-c_{l}t}\,\mathrm{d}t=0\qquad\text{ if }0\leq k\leq n_{l}-1,\quad 1\leq l\leq r.

(10)

On the other hand, the Type I multiple Laguerre polynomials of the second kind are denoted by
B^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n},1},\dots,B^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n},r}
with
\deg B^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n},l}\leq n_{l}-1
and satisfy

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\langle B^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n},l},t^{k}\rangle_{l}=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}B^{(\alpha,\vec{c})}_{\vec{n},l}(t)\,t^{k}\,t^{\alpha}e^{-c_{l}t}\,\mathrm{d}t=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }0\leq k\leq|\vec{n}|-2,\\
1&\text{ if }k=|\vec{n}|-1.\end{cases}

(11)

Explicit expressions as well as several properties and integral representations of these polynomials, together with applications in random matrices (Wishart ensemble) can be found in
[
2
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
18
, Section 23.4.2]
, or
[
28
, Section 3.6.2]
. Furthermore, the asymptotics of multiple Laguerre polynomials, as well as their zero distribution, have been studied in
[
19
]
and
[
23
]
.

Once multiple orthogonal polynomials have been introduced, we return to the single-measure but multivariate setting. In the following sections, we introduce what will become a key tool: orthogonal polynomials on the unit sphere and on the unit ball.

2.4
Spherical harmonics

Let
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the euclidean norm
\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}}
. Denote by
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}
the space of real homogeneous polynomials of degree
n
, and by
\Pi_{n}^{d}
the space of real
d
-variate polynomials of degree at most
n
. Recall that
[
12
, Section 1.1]

\displaystyle\dim\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}

\displaystyle=\binom{n+d-1}{n},

\displaystyle\dim\Pi_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d}{n}.

(12)

Now, the Laplace operator is a two order differential operator defined as

\Delta=\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}+\cdots+\dfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{d}^{2}}.

With these notations, we introduce the following definitions

Definition 1
.

For
n\geq 0
, the space of
real harmonic polynomials
of degree
n
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as

\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}:=\{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}:\Delta P=0\}.

The
spherical harmonics
are the restrictions of the real harmonic polynomials to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Indeed, spherical harmonics are the restrictions of the elements of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
to the sphere, but, following the standards
[
12
]
, we will call
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
the space of spherical harmonics too. The dimension of this space is
[
12
, Corollary 1.1.4]

\dim\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}=\dim\mathcal{P}_{n}^{d}-\dim\mathcal{P}_{n-2}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-3}{n-2},

(13)

where it is convenient to consider
\dim\mathcal{P}_{n-2}^{d}=0
if
n=0,1
.

Recall the Lebesgue measure on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Fixing the dimension
d
and
\{Y_{\nu}^{n}:1\leq\nu\leq\dim\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}\}
,
\{Y_{\eta}^{m}:1\leq\eta\leq\dim\mathbb{H}_{m}^{d}\}
orthogonal bases of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
and
\mathbb{H}_{m}^{d}
respectively, then
[
12
, Theorem 1.1.2]

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}Y_{\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{x})Y_{\eta}^{m}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\qquad\text{ if }n\neq m\text{ or }\nu\neq\eta.

(14)

Now, we show explicit expressions for spherical harmonics.

2.4.1
Spherical harmonics of two variables

Whenever the dimension is
2
, in this section and in the whole paper, we will eventually express the points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
using their polar coordinates, so that
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\equiv(\rho,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
where

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}x&=&\rho\cos\theta\\
y&=&\rho\sin\theta\end{array}\right\},\qquad 0\leq\rho\leq 1,\quad 0\leq\theta<2\pi.

(15)

The Jacobian of this transformation is
\rho
. This information will be useful for future changes of variable, see Section
2.5.1
.

In this bivariate setting,
\dim\mathbb{H}_{n}^{2}=2
. An orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{2}
is given, in polar coordinates, by
[
12
, Section 1.6.1]

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{n}(\rho,\theta)

\displaystyle=\rho^{n}\cos n\theta,

\displaystyle Y_{2}^{n}(\rho,\theta)=\rho^{n}\sin n\theta.

(16)

Indeed, if we restrict the spherical harmonics to the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, they reduce to

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{n}(1,\theta)

\displaystyle=\cos n\theta,

\displaystyle Y_{2}^{n}(1,\theta)=\sin n\theta.

(17)

2.4.2
Spherical harmonics of three variables

In the
3
-variate case, we use spherical coordinates to express points on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, so that we use the identification
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)\equiv(\rho,\theta,\phi)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
where

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}x&=&\rho\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
y&=&\rho\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
z&=&\rho\cos\theta\end{array}\right\},\qquad 0\leq\rho\leq 1,\quad 0\leq\theta\leq\pi,\quad 0\leq\phi<2\pi.

(18)

In this case, the Jacobian of the transformation to spherical coordinates is
\rho^{2}\sin\theta
.

The space of spherical harmonics of degree
n
and three variables
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{3}
has dimension
2n+1
. In spherical coordinates, an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}^{3}_{n}
is (see
[
12
, Equation 1.6.5]
and
[
13
, Theorem 4.1.4]
).

\displaystyle Y_{k,1}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)=\rho^{n}\sin^{k}\theta\,C_{n-k}^{k+1/2}(\cos(\theta))\,\cos(k\phi),\quad 0\leq k\leq n

(19)

\displaystyle Y_{k,2}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)=\rho^{n}\sin^{k}\theta\,C_{n-k}^{k+1/2}(\cos(\theta))\,\sin(k\phi),\quad 1\leq k\leq n,

where
\{C_{n}^{(\alpha)}(t)\}_{n\geq 0}
are the Gegenbauer polynomials
[
26
, Section 4.7]
, which are orthogonal on
[-1,1]
with respect to the weight
(1-t^{2})^{\alpha-1/2}
.

Eventually, we will denote these polynomials as
Y^{n}_{\nu}
, where

\displaystyle Y_{\nu}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=Y_{\nu,1}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)=\rho^{n}\sin^{\nu}(\theta)\,C_{n-\nu}^{\nu+1/2}(\cos(\theta))\,\cos(\nu\phi),

\displaystyle 0\leq\nu\leq n

(20)

\displaystyle Y_{\nu}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)

\displaystyle=Y_{\nu-n,2}^{n}(\rho,\theta,\phi)=\rho^{n}\sin^{\nu-n}(\theta)\,C_{2n-\nu}^{\nu-n+1/2}(\cos(\theta))\,\sin((\nu-n)\phi),

\displaystyle n+1\leq\nu\leq 2n,

so that
\{Y_{\nu}^{n}:\nu=0,\dots,2n\}
is an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{3}
. In addition, the restriction of the spherical harmonics to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
reduces to
Y_{k,1}^{n}(1,\theta,\phi)
and
Y_{k,2}^{n}(1,\theta,\phi)
.

2.4.3
Spherical harmonics of
d
variables

Now, in the general case, an arbitrary number
d\geq 2
of variables is considered. For an arbitrary tuple
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we will consider its spherical polar coordinates, which generalize (
15
) and (
18
) identifying
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\equiv(\rho,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})
, where

\left.\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{1}&=&\rho\cos\theta_{d-1},\\
x_{2}&=&\rho\sin\theta_{d-1}\cos\theta_{d-2},\\
&\vdots&\\
x_{d-1}&=&\rho\sin\theta_{d-1}\cdots\sin\theta_{2}\cos\theta_{1}\\
x_{d}&=&\rho\sin\theta_{d-1}\cdots\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{1}\end{array}\right\},\quad\begin{array}[]{l}0\leq\rho\leq 1,\quad 0\leq\theta_{1}<2\pi,\\
0\leq\theta_{k}\leq\pi,\ \ k=2,\dots,d-1.\end{array}

(21)

The Jacobian of this transformation is

\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\rho^{d-1}(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{d-k-1}.

(22)

In order to define an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
, the space of spherical harmonics in
d
variables of degree
n
, we will work with multi-indices
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
such that
|\mathbf{m}|=n
and
m_{d}\in\{0,1\}
. The number of possible tuples satisfying these conditions is exactly
\dim\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
, given in (
13
), see
[
13
, Section 4.1]
. In spherical polar coordinates, an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{n}^{d}
is given by
\{Y_{\mathbf{m}}^{n}(\mathbf{x}):|\mathbf{m}|=n,\,m_{d}\in\{0,1\}\}
, where

Y_{\mathbf{m}}^{n}(\rho,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})=\rho^{n}\,g(\theta_{1})\,\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\left[(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{\beta_{k}}\,C_{m_{k}}^{\lambda_{k}}(\cos\theta_{d-k})\right],

(23)

with
g(\theta_{1})=\cos((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=0
or
g(\theta_{1})=\sin((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=1
,
\beta_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}m_{i}
, and
\lambda_{k}=\beta_{k}+(d-k-1)/2
. Again, the restriction of these polynomials to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is
Y_{\mathbf{m}}^{n}(1,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})
.

Despite the generality of (
23
) for any dimension
d\geq 2
, in the 2 or 3-variable setting it is common to use the expressions introduced in Sections
2.4.1
and
2.4.2
for the sake of simplicity; see next two sections.

2.5
Orthogonal polynomials on the Unit Ball

One of the most studied domains in the theory of multivariate orthogonal polynomials is the unit ball of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as

B^{d}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq 1\}.

(24)

Choose
\mu>-1
and define the following weight function on
B^{d}
:

W_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})=w_{\mu}\,(1-\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2})^{\mu}=w_{\mu}\,(1-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{d}^{2})^{\mu},\qquad\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in B^{d},

(25)

where the normalization constant
w_{\mu}
is

w_{\mu}=\left(\int_{D}W_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma(\mu+1+\frac{d}{2})}{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(\mu+1)}.

This weight leads to the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}:=\int_{B^{d}}f(\mathbf{x})g(\mathbf{x})W_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

(26)

for
f,g\in L^{2}(B^{d};W_{\mu})=\left\{u(\mathbf{x}):\int_{B^{d}}(u(\mathbf{x}))^{2}W_{\mu}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}<\infty\right\}

[
13
, Section 5.2]
.

Fixed this weight and this inner product, we denote

\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}=\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}(W_{\mu})=\{P\in\Pi_{n}^{d}:\langle P,Q\rangle_{\mu}=0,\;\forall Q\in\Pi_{n-1}^{d}\},

the space of polynomials of
d
variables and degree
n
which are orthogonal to lower degree polynomials with respect to the weight
W_{\mu}
.

There exist several bases of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}
, expressed in terms of Gegenbauer polynomials or Appell polynomials (see
[
13
, Sections 5.2.1, 5.2.2]
), but we will focus on the one given explicitly in terms of spherical harmonics.

More precisely, consider
n\geq 0
,
0\leq j\leq n/2
and
\{Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}:1\leq\nu\leq\dim\mathbb{H}_{n-2j}^{d}\}
an orthogonal basis of
\mathbb{H}_{n-2j}^{d}
. Then, the polynomials

P_{j,\nu}^{n}(\mathbf{x})=P_{j}^{(\nu,n-2j+(d-2)/2)}(2\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-1)\,Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(\mathbf{x}),

(27)

where
P^{(\alpha,\beta)}_{j}
denotes the Jacobi polynomial with parameters
\alpha,\beta>-1
and degree
j
, form an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{d}
, see
[
13
, Proposition 5.2.1]
. In particular, if we use the basis
\{Y_{\mathbf{m}}^{n}\}
given in (
23
), for
\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})
with
|\mathbf{m}|=n-2j
and
m_{d}\in\{0,1\}
, we get the polynomials

P_{j,\mathbf{m}}^{n}(\mathbf{x})=P_{j}^{(\nu,n-2j+(d-2)/2)}(2\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-1)\,Y_{\mathbf{m}}^{n-2j}(\mathbf{x}).

(28)

In spherical polar coordinates:

P_{j,\mathbf{m}}^{n}(\rho,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})=P_{j}^{(\nu,n-2j+(d-2)/2)}(2\rho^{2}-1)\,\rho^{n-2j}\,g(\theta_{1})\,\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\left[(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{\beta_{k}}\,C_{m_{k}}^{\lambda_{k}}(\cos\theta_{d-k})\right],

(29)

where
g(\theta_{1})=\cos((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=0
or
g(\theta_{1})=\sin((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=1
,
\beta_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}m_{i}
, and
\lambda_{k}=\beta_{k}+(d-k-1)/2
.

The main purpose of this paper is to extend this constructions to the multiple orthogonal setting. This means, construct multivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball, leveraging the explicit expression (
27
), but substituting the standard Jacobi polynomials by one of their multiple orthogonal analogs: the Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials already introduced in Section
2.2
.

In the following section, we will introduce the simplest and most common case where
d=2
, that is, orthogonal polynomials on the unit disk.

2.5.1
Orthogonal polynomials on the Unit Disk

Fix
d=2
, so that
\mathbf{x}=(x,y)\equiv(\rho,\theta)
in polar coordinates (
15
). We denote by

D=B^{2}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;:x^{2}+y^{2}\leq 1\},

(30)

the unit disk of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

[
13
, Section 2.3]
. Regarding the previous notations in (
25
), (
26
), we consider the following weight function

W_{\mu}(x,y)=w_{\mu}(1-x^{2}-y^{2})^{\mu},\qquad\mu>-1,

(31)

where
w_{\mu}=(\mu+1)/\pi
, and its associated integral inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{D}f(x,y)g(x,y)W_{\mu}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y,

(32)

for
f,g\in L^{2}(D;W_{\mu})
.

If we express points on the disk
D
using their polar coordinates (
15
), and use this change of variables on the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
, then (
32
) might be written as

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}:=w_{\mu}\,\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}f(\rho,\theta)g(\rho,\theta)\,r(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu}\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

(33)

where
f(\rho,\theta)\equiv f(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
and
g(\rho,\theta)\equiv g(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
are the functions
f
and
g
expressed in polar coordinates.

Within this setup, we define the following polynomials, which are particular cases of (
27
) for
d=2
using (
16
).

Definition 2
.

Let
n\geq 0
. For
(x,y)\equiv(\rho,\theta)\in D
, define

\displaystyle P^{n}_{j,1}(\rho,\theta)=P_{j}^{(n-2j,\mu)}(1-2\rho^{2})\rho^{n-2j}\cos((n-2j)\theta),\quad 0\leq j\leq n/2

(34)

\displaystyle P^{n}_{j,2}(\rho,\theta)=P_{j}^{(n-2j,\mu)}(1-2\rho^{2})\rho^{n-2j}\sin((n-2j)\theta),\quad 0\leq j<n/2

It is known
[
13
, Proposition 2.3.3]
that
P^{n}_{j,1}
,
P^{n}_{j,2}
are polynomials of degree
n
in Cartesian coordinates. Moreover, as mentioned before, they form an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}(W_{\mu})
, that is:

\displaystyle\langle P^{n}_{j,1},P^{m}_{k,1}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=h_{j,1}^{n}\,\delta_{n,m}\delta_{j,k},

\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq n/2,\ 0\leq k\leq m/2,

(35)

\displaystyle\langle P^{n}_{j,2},P^{m}_{k,2}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=h_{j,2}^{n}\,\delta_{n,m}\delta_{j,k},

\displaystyle 0\leq j<n/2,\ 0\leq k<m/2,

\displaystyle\langle P^{n}_{j,1},P^{m}_{k,2}\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq n/2,\ 0\leq k<m/2,

where the squared norm
h_{j,\nu}^{n}
is given by

h_{j,\nu}^{n}=\langle P^{n}_{j,\nu},P^{n}_{j,\nu}\rangle_{\mu}=\dfrac{(\mu+1)}{2^{n-2j+\mu+2}\,\pi}\|P_{j}^{(n-2j,\mu)}\|^{2}\,\|Y_{\nu}^{n-2j}(1,\theta)\|^{2},\qquad\nu=1,2,

with:

\|P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\|^{2}=\dfrac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\dfrac{\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{n!(n+\alpha+1)_{\beta}}

(36)

(see
[
1
, Section 22.2.1]
), and

\displaystyle\|Y_{1}^{n}(1,\theta)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\pi(1+\delta_{n,0})

\displaystyle\|Y_{2}^{n}(1,\theta)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\pi

(37)

Within this preliminaries, once we studied Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials, spherical harmonics, as well as orthogonal polynomials on the Unit Ball, it is time to gather these definitions and introduce Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials on the Unit Ball.

3
Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials on the Unit Ball

Starting from the theoretical foundations of multiple orthogonality and the properties of orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball, this section aims to integrate the multiple orthogonal properties of Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials with the multivariate orthogonality on the unit ball. Specifically, our construction employs Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials to govern the radial component, while spherical harmonics represent the angular part.

Firstly, we will describe the case with
2
variables. By providing an inductive approach to both the definitions and the multiple orthogonality relations, we aim to facilitate a clearer understanding of the generalization to higher-dimensional settings.

3.1
The bivariate case

Let
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r}>-1
such that
\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
if
i\neq j
and denote
\vec{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{r})
,
W_{l}(x,y):=W_{\mu_{l}}(x,y)
, and the inner products
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{l}:=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu_{l}}
,
l=1,\dots,r
, where
W_{\mu}
is defined in (
31
) and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mu}
in (
32
). In an analogous way as above, we define several functions in polar coordinates, which are polynomials in Cartesian coordinates and satisfy multiple orthogonal properties.

3.1.1
Type II MOP on the disk

Within the described setting, we will introduce an extension of orthogonal polynomials on the disk satisfying orthogonality relations with respect to the considered weights at the same time, playing a similar role as Type II MOPs.

Definition 3
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r}),\,\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
. Consider the Jacobi-Piñeiro Type II MOP
P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(t)
, we define

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},1}^{\vec{n}}(\rho,\theta)=P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\cos((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta),\qquad 0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2,\\[8.61108pt]
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},2}^{\vec{n}}(\rho,\theta)=P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\sin((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta),\qquad 0\leq|\vec{j}|<|\vec{n}|/2.\end{array}

(38)

Now, recall from Euler’s formula
(x+i\,y)^{m}=\rho^{m}(\cos m\theta+i\sin m\theta)
that, as shown in
[
13
, Proof of Proposition 2.3.3]
, the functions
\rho^{m}\cos m\theta
and
\rho^{m}\sin m\theta
are polynomials of degree
m
in Cartesian coordinates
(x,y)
. As a consequence, both
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},1}^{\vec{n}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},2}^{\vec{n}}
are polynomials of degree
|\vec{n}|
in
(x,y)
.

Remark 4
.

Observe that, as mentioned above,
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(\rho,\theta)=P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,Y_{\nu}^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}(\theta)
, which bears an analogy with (
27
), substituting Jacobi polynomials by Jacobi-Piñeiro MOP. Besides, notice that Jacobi polynomials in (
27
) are evaluated in
1-2\rho^{2}
as they are orthogonal with respect to a weight supported on
[-1,1]
, whereas Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials in (
38
) – and below – are evaluated in
\rho^{2}
, since the multiple weights in (
5
) are supported on
[0,1]
.

Now we present the multiple orthogonal properties satisfied by the polynomials in (
38
).

Proposition 5
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
and
\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
and consider the polynomials
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}
,
\nu=1,2
, defined in (
38
). Then

\langle\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(x,y),\,x^{a}y^{b}\rangle_{l}=\int_{D}\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(x,y)\,x^{a}y^{b}\,W_{l}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y=0\qquad\text{ if }0\leq a+b<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+2j_{l},

(39)

for
1\leq l\leq r
and
\nu=1,2
. Moreover,

\langle\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(x,y),\,x^{a}y^{b}\rangle_{l}=0\qquad\text{ if }a+b-|\vec{n}|+2|\vec{j}|\text{ is odd},\qquad 1\leq l\leq r,\ \nu=1,2.

Proof.

From the definition of
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}
for
\nu=1,2
given in (
38
), and using (
33
) and (
15
), the integral in (
39
) expressed in polar coordinates reads

I=w_{\mu_{l}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\cos((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)\,\rho^{a+b+1}\,\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\theta\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}\theta,

if
\nu=1
, and

I=w_{\mu_{l}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\sin((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)\,\rho^{a+b+1}\,\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\theta\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\;\mathrm{d}r\;\mathrm{d}\theta

if
\nu=2
.

This integral might be split into the product of two univariate integrals: one depending on
\rho
(radial part), and another one depending on
\theta
(angular part). As a consequence,
I=w_{\mu_{l}}\,I_{1}\times I_{2}
where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+a+b+1}\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)\,\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\,\mathrm{d}\theta&\text{ if }\nu=1,\\
\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)\,\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\,\mathrm{d}\theta&\text{ if }\nu=2.\end{cases}

If
a+b<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
or
a+b=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell
with
\ell
an odd integer, by Lemma
27
(in Appendix
A
),
I_{2}
vanishes in both cases and, in turn,
I
vanishes as well, giving us the second part of the theorem.

Now assume
a+b=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell
with
\ell
an even integer. We will focus on
I_{1}
. Using the change of variable
t=\rho^{2}
,
\mathrm{d}t=2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
,
I_{1}
can be rewritten as

I_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(t)t^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell/2}(1-t)^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using the multiple orthogonality of
P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}
(see (
7
)), we get
I_{1}=0
if
\ell/2<j_{l}
, that is, if
a+b<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+2j_{l}
,
1\leq l\leq r
, which completes the proof.

∎

Once the definition and the properties of the Type II MOP on the disk are established, we present a clarifying example.

Example 6
.

For simplicity, in this and the following examples we will consider only
r=2
measures. As an example, let
\vec{n}=(2,4)
,
\vec{j}=(0,1)
, so that
M=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|=4
. Polynomials
\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),1}(x,y)
and
\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),2}(x,y)
have both degree
|\vec{n}|=6
and are orthogonal to every polynomial of degree lower than
M+2j_{1}=4
(resp.
M+2j_{2}=6
) with respect to the first measure (resp. second). This means, for a bivariate polynomial
q(x,y)
:

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),\nu},q\rangle_{1}

\displaystyle=0\text{ if }\deg(q)<4,

\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),\nu},q\rangle_{2}

\displaystyle=0\text{ if }\deg(q)<6.

Moreover,

\langle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),\nu},q\rangle_{l}=0\text{ if }\deg(q)=4+(2k+1),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\quad\nu=1,2,\quad 1\leq j\leq r.

Since the Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomial
P^{(4,(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}))}_{(0,1)}(t)=5-(\mu_{2}+6)t
, the expressions in polar coordinates of these polynomials are

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),1}(\rho,\theta)=\rho^{4}\left(5-(\mu_{2}+6)\rho^{2}\right)\,\cos(4\theta)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),2}(\rho,\theta)=\rho^{4}\left(5-(\mu_{2}+6)\rho^{2}\right)\,\sin(4\theta).

In Cartesian coordinates these expressions become

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),1}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\left(-x^{4}+6x^{2}y^{2}-y^{4}\right)\left((\mu_{2}+6)\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)-5\right)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{(2,4)}_{(0,1),2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=4xy(y-x)(x+y)\left((\mu_{2}+6)\left(x^{2}+y^{2}\right)-5\right).

So far, by substituting Jacobi polynomials by Type II Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials we introduced Type II MOP on the disk. However, there is no reason to limit the extension to Type II MOP, as shown in the next section.

3.1.2
Type I MOP on the disk

Following the same approach used for Type II MOPs, we construct an extension of Type I MOPs preserving the structure of (
27
) and based on Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials.

Definition 7
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
and
\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
. Consider Type I Jacobi-Piñeiro MOPs
\left(A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},1}(t),\dots,A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},r}(t)\right)
. We define the bivariate polynomials
\left(\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(1)}^{\vec{n}},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(r)}^{\vec{n}}\right)
given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},1,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(\rho,\theta)=A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\cos((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta),\qquad 0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2,

(40)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},2,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(\rho,\theta)=A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}\,\sin((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta),\qquad 0\leq|\vec{j}|<|\vec{n}|/2,

for
1\leq l\leq r
.

Remark 8
.

Taking into account (
40
) and since the univariate Type I MOP
A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(t)
have degree at most
j_{l}-1
, the degree of the bivariate Type I MOP
\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})
is at most
|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-j_{l}+1)
.

Observe that the structures of the polynomials
\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}
and
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}
are analogous, with the Type II Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomial replaced by Type I MOPs. Consequently, the orthogonality of the angular part will remains unchanged, while the main differences in the orthogonality relations arise from the radial part.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
and
\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
and consider the Type I polynomials
\left(\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(1)}^{\vec{n}},\dots,\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(r)}^{\vec{n}}\right)
defined in (
40
). Then

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\langle\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(x,y),x^{a}y^{b}\rangle_{l}=\int_{D}\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(x,y)\,x^{a}y^{b}\,W_{l}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y=0\quad\text{ if }0\leq a+b<|\vec{n}|-2.

(41)

Moreover,

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\langle\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(x,y),x^{a}y^{b}\rangle_{l}=0\quad\text{ if }a+b-|\vec{n}|+2|\vec{j}|\text{ is odd},\quad\nu=1,2.

Proof.

Using polar coordinates, (
40
), and the linearity of the integral operator, we rewrite the target integral as

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{r}w_{\mu_{l}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{2\pi}A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+a+b+1}\,Y_{\nu}(1,\theta)\,\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\theta\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\rho

(42)

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos^{a}\theta\,\sin^{b}\theta\,Y_{\nu}(1,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta\right)\sum_{l=1}^{r}w_{\mu_{l}}\int_{0}^{1}A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+a+b+1}\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho.

where
Y_{\nu}(1,\theta)=\cos((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)
if
\nu=1
, and
Y_{\nu}(1,\theta)=\sin((|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)\theta)
if
\nu=2
. As described in the proof of Proposition
5
, we use Lemma
27
, in Appendix
A
, to deduce that the angular integral vanishes if
a+b<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
and also if
a+b=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell
with
\ell
an odd positive integer.

If
a+b=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell
with
\ell
even, let us focus on the radial sum of integrals, where we apply the change of variables
t=\rho^{2}
,
\mathrm{d}t=2\rho\,\mathrm{d}\rho
and obtain

\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{r}w_{\mu_{l}}\int_{0}^{1}A^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j},l}(t)\,t^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+\ell/2}\,(1-t)^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

(43)

a+b<|\vec{n}|-2
.
∎

Definition 12

Let

\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}

,

Proposition 13
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
and
\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
. Let
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{d}),\,\mathbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}_{0}
be such that
|\mathbf{m}|=M=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
,
m_{d}\in\{0,1\}
. Consider the polynomial
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\mathbf{m}}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})
in (
44
) and denote an arbitrary
d
-variate monomial as
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}
. Then

\langle\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\mathbf{m}}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}\rangle_{l}=\int_{B^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\mathbf{m}}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}\,W_{l}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\quad\text{ if }0\leq|\mathbf{a}|<M+2j_{l},

(45)

for
l=1,\dots,r
. Moreover, (
45
) also holds if
a_{d-k}+\cdots+a_{d}<m_{d-k}+\cdots+m_{d}
, or
a_{d-k}+\cdots+a_{d}-(m_{d-k}+\cdots+m_{d})
is an odd positive integer,
k=1,\cdots,d
.

Proof.

In this proof, we will follow an inductive procedure. First, we express the integral in (
45
) in
d
-dimensional spherical polar coordinates (
21
). To this end, the expression of
\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\mathbf{m}}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})
is written in spherical coordinates as in (
44
), the weight
W_{l}
becomes
(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}
, and the monomial
\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}
becomes

\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}=x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{a_{d}}=\rho^{|\mathbf{a}|}\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}(\cos\theta_{d-k})^{a_{k}}(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{\alpha_{k}},

(46)

where
\alpha_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}a_{i}
(observe that
\alpha_{k-1}=a_{k}+\alpha_{k})
. Also, recall the Jacobian of the transformation to spherical polar coordinates given in (
22
).

Therefore, (
45
) becomes

\displaystyle I=\int_{B^{d}}\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\mathbf{m}}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x}^{\mathbf{a}}\,W_{l}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\pi}\cdots\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P_{\vec{j}}^{(M+(d-2)/2,\vec{\mu})}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{M}

\displaystyle\times g(\theta_{1})\,\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}\left[(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{\beta_{k}}\,C_{m_{k}}^{\lambda_{k}}(\cos\theta_{d-k})\right]\,\rho^{|\mathbf{a}|}\prod_{k=1}^{d-1}(\cos\theta_{d-k})^{a_{k}}(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{\alpha_{k}}

\displaystyle\times\rho^{d-1}\prod_{k=1}^{d-2}(\sin\theta_{d-k})^{d-k-1}\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}\,\cdots\mathrm{d}\theta_{d-1}\mathrm{d}\rho,

with
\beta_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{d}m_{i}
(observe that
\beta_{k-1}=m_{k}+\beta_{k}
), and
\lambda_{k}=\beta_{k}+(d-k-1)/2
. The above integral might be split as a product of
d
univariate integrals so that
I=I_{\rho}\times I_{\theta_{1}}\times I_{\theta_{2}}\times\cdots\times I_{\theta_{d-1}}
with

I_{\rho}=\int_{0}^{1}P_{\vec{j}}^{(M+(d-2)/2,\vec{\mu})}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{M+|\mathbf{a}|+d-1}(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

(47)

I_{\theta_{1}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}g(\theta_{1})(\cos\theta_{1})^{a_{d-1}}(\sin\theta_{1})^{a_{d}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{1},

(48)

I_{\theta_{k}}=\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin\theta_{k})^{\beta_{d-k}+\alpha_{d-k}+k-1}(\cos\theta_{k})^{a_{d-k}}C_{m_{d-k}}^{\lambda_{d-k}}(\cos\theta_{k})\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{k},\quad k=2,\dots,d-1.

(49)

Recall that
g(\theta_{1})=\cos((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=0
and
g(\theta_{1})=\sin((m_{d-1}+m_{d})\theta_{1})
if
m_{d}=1
. Then, by Lemma
27
(in Appendix
A
),
I_{\theta_{1}}=0
(and in turn
I=0
) if
\alpha_{d-2}=a_{d-1}+a_{d}<\beta_{d-2}=m_{d-1}+m_{d}
or
\alpha_{d-2}=\beta_{d-2}+\ell_{1}
, with
\ell_{1}
an odd integer. Now, assume
\alpha_{d-2}=\beta_{d-2}+\ell_{1}
with
\ell_{1}=\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2}
an even integer.

Focus on
I_{\theta_{2}}
and use the change of variables
t=\cos\theta_{2}
,
\mathrm{d}t=-\sin(\theta_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}
, keeping in mind that
\sin(\theta_{2})=(1-t^{2})^{1/2}
:

I_{\theta_{2}}=\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin\theta_{2})^{\beta_{d-2}+\alpha_{d-2}+1}(\cos\theta_{2})^{a_{d-2}}C_{m_{d-2}}^{\lambda_{d-2}}(\cos\theta_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{2}=-\int_{-1}^{1}C_{m_{d-2}}^{\lambda_{d-2}}(t)\,t^{a_{d-2}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\beta_{d-2}+\alpha_{d-2})/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Gegenbauer polynomials
\{C_{n}^{\alpha}(t)\}_{n\geq 0}
are orthogonal with respect to the weight
(1-t^{2})^{\alpha-1/2}
on
[-1,1]
. Since
\lambda_{d-2}=\beta_{d-2}+1/2
, the weight associated to Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{m_{d-2}}^{\lambda_{d-2}}
is
(1-t^{2})^{\beta_{d-2}}
, so that, using the orthogonality of Gegenbauer polynomials,

I_{\theta_{2}}=-\int_{-1}^{1}C_{m_{d-2}}^{\beta_{d-2}+1/2}(t)\,t^{a_{d-2}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})/2}(1-t^{2})^{\beta_{d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}t=0

if
\deg(t^{a_{d-2}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})/2})=a_{d-2}+\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2}=\alpha_{d-3}-\beta_{d-2}<m_{d-2}
, or equivalently if
\alpha_{d-3}<\beta_{d-3}
.

Moreover, since Gegenbauer polynomials are even (resp. odd) functions if their degree is even (resp. odd) and the interval
[-1,1]
is symmetric, they are also orthogonal to monomials
x^{k}
such that
k
and the degree of the Gegenbauer polynomial have different parity,
i.e.
,

\int_{-1}^{-1}C_{n}^{\alpha}(t)x^{k}(1-t^{2})^{\alpha-1/2}\,\mathrm{d}t=0\qquad\text{ if }k<n,\quad\text{ or }k\geq n\text{ and }k-n\text{ is odd}.

(50)

Hence, if
\alpha_{d-3}=\beta_{d-3}+\ell_{2}
with
\ell_{2}
a non-negative integer, we might write the polynomial

t^{a_{d-2}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})/2}=\sum_{i=0}^{(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})/2}c_{i}t^{2i+\alpha_{d-2}},

so that

I_{\theta_{2}}=-\sum_{i=0}^{(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})/2}c_{i}\int_{-1}^{1}C_{m_{d-2}}^{\beta_{d-2}+1/2}(t)\,t^{2i+\alpha_{d-2}}\,(1-t^{2})^{\beta_{d-2}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Recalling (
50
),the

-th integral of the previous sum vanishes if
2i+a_{d-2}<m_{d-2}
. If
2i+a_{d-2}\geq m_{d-2}
, the
i
-th integral vanishes if
2i+a_{d-2}-m_{d-2}
is odd. Since
2i
is always even, then the
i
-th integral is zero, and in turn
I_{\theta_{2}}
and
I
, whenever
a_{d-2}-m_{d-2}
is odd.

Since we are assuming that
\alpha_{d-3}=\beta_{d-3}+\ell_{2}
, or equivalently
a_{d-2}+\alpha_{d-2}=m_{d-2}+\beta_{d-2}+\ell_{2}
, and also that
\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2}
is even, so that
(a_{d-2}-m_{d-2})+(\alpha_{d-2}-\beta_{d-2})=\ell_{2}
. Hence,
a_{d-2}-m_{d-2}
is odd if, and only if
\ell_{2}
is odd. Summarizing,
I_{\theta_{2}}=0
, and in turn
I=0
, if
\alpha_{d-3}=\beta_{d-3}+\ell_{2}
with
\ell_{2}
being an odd integer.

Using induction, we will assume, for
2\leq k\leq d-2
, that
I=0
if
\alpha_{d-k-1}<\beta_{d-k-1}
or
\alpha_{d-k-1}=\beta_{d-k-1}+\ell_{k}
with
\ell_{k}
an odd integer. With this assumption, we will prove that
I=0
if
\alpha_{d-k-2}<\beta_{d-k-2}
or
\alpha_{d-k-2}<\beta_{d-k-2}+\ell_{k+1}
with
\ell_{k+1}
an odd integer.

We will study what happens if
\alpha_{d-k-1}=\beta_{d-k-1}+\ell_{k}
with
\ell_{k}=\alpha_{d-k-1}-\beta_{d-k-1}
an even integer (otherwise we know
I=0
owing to the induction hypothesis). Focus on
I_{\theta_{k+1}}
and use the change of variables
t=\cos\theta_{k+1}
,
\mathrm{d}t=-\sin\theta_{k+1}\mathrm{d}\theta_{k+1}
:

\displaystyle I_{\theta_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\pi}(\sin\theta_{k+1})^{\beta_{d-k-1}+\alpha_{d-k-1}+k}(\cos\theta_{k+1})^{a_{d-k-1}}C_{m_{d-k-1}}^{\lambda_{d-k-1}}(\cos\theta_{k+1})\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{k+1}

\displaystyle=-\int_{-1}^{1}C_{m_{d-k-1}}^{\lambda_{d-k-1}}(t)\,t^{a_{d-k-1}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\beta_{d-k-1}+\alpha_{d-k-1}+k-1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\lambda_{d-k-1}=\beta_{d-k-1}+k/2
, these Gegenbauer polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the weight
(1-t^{2})^{\beta_{d-k-1}+(k-1)/2}
. Rewrite
I_{\theta_{k+1}}
as

I_{\theta_{k+1}}=-\int_{-1}^{1}C_{m_{d-k-1}}^{\lambda_{d-k-1}}(t)\,t^{a_{d-k-1}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\alpha_{d-k-1}-\beta_{d-k-1})/2}\,(1-t^{2})^{\beta_{d-k-1}+(k-1)/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

By the orthogonality of Gegenbauer polynomials, see (
50
), we know that this integral vanishes whenever
\deg(t^{a_{d-k-1}}\,(1-t^{2})^{(\alpha_{d-k-1}-\beta_{d-k-1})/2})=\alpha_{d-k-2}-\beta_{d-k-1}<m_{d-k-1}
, this means
\alpha_{d-k-2}<\beta_{d-k-2}
. If
\alpha_{d-k-2}=\beta_{d-k-2}+\ell_{k+1}
with
\ell_{k+1}
a non-negative integer, recalling (
50
) and using an argument analogous to the one in the case of
I_{\theta_{2}}
, we get that
I_{\theta_{k+1}}
, and in turn
I
, vanish if
\alpha_{d-k-2}=\beta_{d-k-2}+\ell_{k+1}
with
\ell_{k+1}
an odd positive integer, completing the induction and obtaining the second part of the theorem.

As a consequence, we finally get that
I=0
if
\alpha_{0}=|\mathbf{a}|<\beta_{0}=|\mathbf{m}|=M=|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
or
|\mathbf{a}|=M+\ell_{d-1}
with
\ell_{d-1}
an odd positive integer.

Finally, assume
|\mathbf{a}|=M+\ell_{d-1}
with
\ell_{d-1}=|\mathbf{a}|-M

. By the change of variables

Now, Jacobi-Piñeiro polynomials are multiple orthogonal with respect to the weights

,

. Rewriting the above integral as

4
Nearest Neighbour Relation in two variables

As mentioned in the preliminaries on multiple orthogonal polynomials in Section
2
, univariate families of MOPs satisfy Nearest Neighbour Recurrence Relations (
3

4
), which extend the well-known three-term recurrence relation of standard orthogonal polynomials. Once definitions and multiple orthogonal properties of multiple orthogonal polynomials in the unit ball have been established throughout Section

Although the results presented here can be extended to an arbitrary number of variables, we restrict our analysis to the bivariate case, described in Section

Before extending the NNRR, we need an essential preliminary result, which is presented in the next section.

4.1
Biorthogonality

Let us define the Type I function as

\mathbf{Q}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{l=1}^{r}\mathbf{A}_{\vec{j},\nu,(l)}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})\,W_{l}(\mathbf{x}),

(54)

which allows to write the Type I multiple orthogonality from Proposition

\langle\mathbf{Q}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(x,y),x^{a}y^{b}\rangle=\int_{D}\mathbf{Q}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(x,y)\,x^{a}y^{b}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}y=0\quad\text{ if }0\leq a+b<|\vec{n}|-2.

Where the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
(without sub-index) is the integral inner product with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
D
,
i.e.
, with the constant weight function
1
. Indeed, in this equation, the weights
W_{l}
have been omitted as they are explicitly included in the Type I function.

By simultaneously leveraging the properties of univariate Type I and Type II multiple orthogonality, we establish a biorthogonality relation between Type I and Type II MOPs on the disk.

Proposition 17
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r}),\,\vec{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r}),\,\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r}),\,\vec{k}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}

I:=\int_{D}\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{Q}_{\vec{k},\eta}^{\vec{m}}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=0\qquad\text{ if }\nu\neq\eta\text{ or }|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|\neq|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|.

(55)

Moreover, if
\nu=\eta
and

I=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\vec{k}\leq\vec{j}\quad\text{(componentwise)},\\

(56)

Proof.

From (
54

\mathbf{Q}_{\vec{k},\eta}^{\vec{m}}(\rho,\theta)=\sum_{l=1}^{r}A^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|,\vec{\eta})}_{\vec{k},l}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|}\,Y_{\eta}^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)}(1,\theta)\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}},

where
Y_{\eta}^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)}(1,\theta)=\cos((|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)\theta)
if
\eta=1
, and
Y_{\eta}^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)}(1,\theta)=\sin((|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)\theta)
if
\eta=2

38

can be expressed as

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(\rho^{2})\,\rho^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|+|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|+1}\sum_{l=1}^{r}A^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|,\vec{\eta})}_{\vec{k},l}(\rho^{2})\,(1-\rho^{2})^{\mu_{l}}\,\mathrm{d}\rho,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}Y_{\nu}^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|)}(1,\theta)\,Y_{\eta}^{(|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|)}(1,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

We know that

, which gives us the first part of the result.

For the second one, assume
\nu=\eta
and
|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|=|\vec{m}|-2|\vec{k}|
and apply the change of variables

to

Observe that the univariate Type I function for Jacobi-Piñeiro multiple orthogonal polynomials stands in

, so that

I_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}P^{(|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu})}_{\vec{j}}(t)\,Q_{\vec{k}}^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|,\vec{\mu}}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,

which, using the biorthogonality relation between Type I and Type II MOP
[

∎

This biorthogonality relation between Type I and Type II MOP on the disk is a fundamental tool in order to prove what comes next: the extension of NNRR.

4.2
Nearest Neighbour Relation

Let
\vec{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
and consider a path of neighbour multi-indices
\{\vec{m}_{k}\,:\,k=0,\dots,|\vec{n}|\}
such that
|\vec{m}_{k}|=k
,
\vec{m}_{0}=\vec{0}
,
\vec{m}_{|\vec{n}|}=\vec{n}
,

Then

\left\{\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{i},1}^{\vec{n}}:0\leq i\leq|\vec{n}|/2\right\}\cup\left\{\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{i},2}^{\vec{n}}:0\leq i<|\vec{n}|/2\right\}

(58)

is a set of
|\vec{n}|+1

|\vec{n}|
. Repeating this consideration for every multi-index
\vec{m}_{0},\dots,\vec{m}_{|\vec{n}|-1}

\bigcup_{k=0}^{|\vec{n}|}\left\{\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{i},1}^{\vec{m}_{k}}:0\leq i\leq k/2\right\}\cup\left\{\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{i},2}^{\vec{m}_{k}}:0\leq i<k/2\right\}

\Pi_{|\vec{n}|}^{2}
, the space of bivariate polynomials of degree at most
|\vec{n}|
. In this way, it is possible to express any bivariate polynomial as a linear combination of bivariate multiple orthogonal polynomials on the disk after choosing an appropriate path.

In order to simplify the notation, we collect these polynomials into vectors and denote

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left(\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{0},1}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor},1}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{0},2}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor},2}^{\vec{m}_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{if }k\text{ is odd,}

(60)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{k}

\displaystyle:=\left(\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{0},1}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{k/2},1}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{0},2}^{\vec{m}_{k}},\dots,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{m}_{k/2-1},2}^{\vec{m}_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\text{if }k\text{ is even.}

Observe that whether
k
is odd or even,
\mathbb{P}_{k}
is a vector of
k+1
linearly independent polynomials of degree

.

Combining the basis (
59

[

\vec{n}

,

\displaystyle\deg(x\,\mathbf{A}^{\vec{m}_{t}}_{\vec{m}_{s},\eta,(l)})

\displaystyle\leq 1+|\vec{m}_{t}|-2(|\vec{m}_{s}|-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l}+1)

\displaystyle=1+t-2(s-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l}+1)

\displaystyle=t-1-2(s-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l}),

where we used
|\vec{m}_{k}|=k
.

Since
(s-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l})=|\vec{m}_{s}|-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l}\geq 0
, the minimum reachable value for
(s-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l})
is
0
, arising whenever
s=0
, in whose case
\vec{m}_{s}=\vec{0}
. With this information, the inequality

\deg(x\,\mathbf{A}^{\vec{m}_{t}}_{\vec{m}_{s},\eta,(l)})\leq t-1-2(s-(\vec{m}_{s})_{l})\leq t-1

(65)

holds for every
l
.

On the other hand, we have the right hand side of (
64
). Since
\vec{j}
might be any multi-index satisfying
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
, all we can assert is that
0\leq j_{l}\leq|\vec{n}|/2
. In this way,

|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-j_{l})\geq|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|.

(66)

Gathering (
65
) and (
66
), we get that if
t-1<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
, then (
64
) holds for every
l
, and consequently
c^{(t-2)}_{s-1,\eta}=0
. Equivalently,
c^{(k)}_{i,\eta}=0
if
k<|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|-1
, completing the proof.

∎

Observe that Equations (
61

|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|
increases, since more coefficients vanish. However, these relations depend strongly on the multi-index
\vec{j}
and on its modulus. This is due to the fact that
\vec{j}
is not fixed, as it may be any multi-index satisfying
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
.

We now study a particular case of these relations by imposing a specific structure on the path. This fact allows us to control the components of the multi-indices, at the cost of reducing the freedom in their choice. Then, consider a path
\{\vec{m}_{k}:k\geq 0\}
of neighbour multi-indices such that
|\vec{m}_{k}|=k
,
\vec{m}_{0}=\vec{0}
,
\vec{m}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
,
\vec{m}_{2}=(1,1,\dots,0)
, …,
\vec{m}_{r}=(1,1,\dots,1)
,
\vec{m}_{r+1}=(2,1,\dots,1)
, …,
\vec{m}_{ra+i}=(a+1,\overset{(i)}{\dots},a+1,a,\dots,a)
, and so on; see Figure
2
for a graphic representation. This path is known as the
step-line
and is widely used to standardize the indexing of multiple orthogonal polynomials, see
[
7
,
20
]
.

Step-line muti-indices for
r=2

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
be a multi-index on the step-line, and assume that
|\vec{n}|=ar+i
, where
a=\lfloor|\vec{n}|/r\rfloor
and
i=|\vec{n}|\,\mathrm{mod}\,r
. Then
n_{j}=a+1
if
j\leq i
, and
n_{j}=a
if
j>i
. Consequently, each component of
\vec{n}
satisfies
\lfloor|\vec{n}|/r\rfloor\leq n_{j}\leq\lfloor|\vec{n}|/r\rfloor+1
. Using this characterization of the components of a multi-index on the step-line, we now present the following result.

Corollary 19
.

In the same setting as Theorem
18
, assume
\vec{n}
is in the step-line, as well as the path
\vec{m}_{0}=\vec{0},\vec{m}_{1},\dots,\vec{m}_{|\vec{n}|}=\vec{n}
goes through the step-line. Then,

\displaystyle x\,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{c}^{(|\vec{n}|+1)}\mathbb{P}_{|\vec{n}|+1}+\mathbf{c}^{(|\vec{n}|)}\mathbb{P}_{|\vec{n}|}+\sum_{k=2\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor-1}^{|\vec{n}|-1}\mathbf{c}^{(k)}\mathbb{P}_{k},

(67)

\displaystyle y\,\mathbf{P}_{\vec{j},\nu}^{\vec{n}}(\mathbf{x})=\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{(|\vec{n}|+1)}\mathbb{P}_{|\vec{n}|+1}+\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{(|\vec{n}|)}\mathbb{P}_{|\vec{n}|}+\sum_{k=2\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor-1}^{|\vec{n}|-1}\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{(k)}\mathbb{P}_{k},

where the expressions of
\mathbf{c}^{(k)},\,\tilde{\mathbf{c}}^{(k)}
are given in (
62
), (
63
).

Proof.

This proof is analogous to that of Theorem
18
, but makes use of the structure of the multi-indices to get another lower bound for
|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-j_{l})
.

Since
\vec{j}
is an element from the step-line, then
j_{l}\geq\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor
, so that

|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-j_{l})\geq|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor),

(68)

Now, we will seek an upper bound for
|\vec{j}|-\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor
. For this purpose, the function
f(t)=t-\lfloor t/r\rfloor
is defined on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This function is non-decreasing, as

f(0)=0,f(1)=1,\dots,f(r-1)=r-1,f(r)=r-1,f(r+1)=r,\dots

and, in general if
t=ar+i
with
a=\lfloor t/r\rfloor
and
0\leq i\leq r-1
, then
f(t)=a(r-1)+i
.

Then
f(|\vec{j}|)=|\vec{j}|-\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor
will reach its maximum at the maximum value of
|\vec{j}|
, which is
|\vec{n}|/2
. Then
|\vec{j}|-\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor\leq|\vec{n}|/2-\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor
. Applying this inequality in (

|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-j_{l})\geq|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{j}|-\lfloor|\vec{j}|/r\rfloor)\geq|\vec{n}|-2(|\vec{n}|/2-\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor)=2\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor.

Combining these inequalities with (
65
), we get that (

l
whenever
t<2\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor+1
. In turn,
c^{(t-2)}_{s-1,\eta}=0
whenever that condition holds, and equivalently
c^{(k)}_{i,\nu}=0
for
0\leq k<2\lfloor|\vec{n}|/(2r)\rfloor-1
, completing the proof.
∎

Observe that we have removed the dependence on
|\vec{j}|
using the knowledge about its components. Let us show an example to illustrate the possible advantages of this case.

Example 20
.

Using
r=2
, let
\vec{n}=(4,4)
,
\vec{j}=(2,1)
and consider the path
\vec{m}_{0},\dots,\vec{m}_{|\vec{n}|}=\vec{n}
in the step-line (see Figure
2
), and choose either
\vec{w}=(5,4)
or
\vec{w}=(4,5)

\mathbb{P}_{0},\dots,\mathbb{P}_{|\vec{n}|+1}

since

as

5
Multiple Orthogonal Polynomials for radial weights

In this section, the notion of MOP on the disk, studied in Section
3.1
, is extended to general radial weights. A weight function
W(\mathbf{x})
is said to be
radial
if it is of the form
W(\mathbf{x})=\omega(\rho)=\omega(\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}})
, where we use spherical polar coordinates
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\equiv(\rho,\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{d-1})
, introduced in (
21
)
[
13
, Sections 2.6.2 and 5.1.2]
.

Now consider
r
radial weights
W_{1}(\mathbf{x})=\omega_{1}(\rho),\dots,W_{r}(\mathbf{x})=\omega_{r}(\rho)
. For
k\geq 0
, define the system of weights
\rho^{k+1}\omega_{1}(\rho),\dots,\rho^{k+1}\omega_{r}(\rho)
supported on
[0,+\infty)
. Let
\vec{n}
be a normal multi-index for this system, we denote by
p_{\vec{n}}^{(k)}
the associated Type II MOP and by
q_{\vec{n},l}^{(k)}
,
1\leq l\leq r
the associated Type I MOPs. This means that

\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{\vec{n}}^{(k)}(\rho)\,\rho^{t}\,\rho^{k+1}\omega_{l}(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho=0\qquad\text{ if }0\leq t<n_{l},\quad 1\leq l\leq r,

(69)

and

\sum_{l=1}^{r}\int_{0}^{\infty}q_{\vec{n},l}^{(k)}(\rho)\,\rho^{t}\,\rho^{k+1}\,\omega_{l}(\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\rho=\delta_{t,|\vec{n}|-1},\qquad 0\leq t\leq|\vec{n}|-1.

(70)

Using these auxiliary polynomials, we now present the Type II and Type I MOPs for the radial weights
W_{1},\dots,W_{r}
.

5.1
Type II MOP for radial weights

Following the approach in Section
3.1.1
, we define polynomials associated with radial weights, analogous to the disk case.

Definition 21
.

Let
\vec{n}=(n_{1},\dots,n_{r})
and
\vec{j}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{r})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{r}
be such that
0\leq|\vec{j}|\leq|\vec{n}|/2
and consider the polynomial
p_{2\vec{j}}^{(2|\vec{n}|-4|\vec{j}|)}
satisfying (
69
), and
Y_{\nu}^{|\vec{n}|-2|\vec{j}|}

(71)

This function is a polynomial of degree
|\vec{n}|

This proof is analogous to that of Proposition

and by (

Indeed, these properties keep an analogy with the ones stablished in Proposition

6
Conclusions and future work

Drawing upon the established theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit ball
B^{d}
, we use multiple orthogonal polynomials to extend this framework to the multivariate setting. Specifically, we preserve the classical structure from
[
13
]
, where polynomials are defined as the product of a radial component and an angular component, the latter given by spherical harmonics. In our extension, the radial part, traditionally associated with Jacobi polynomials, is replaced by Jacobi-Piñeiro MOPs. This modification enables the construction of multivariate polynomials on the ball that satisfy Type I and Type II multiple orthogonality relations, thereby generalizing the univariate Jacobi-Piñeiro cases (
1
) and (
2
).

To illustrate the usefulness of this construction, we provide an extension of the Nearest Neighbor Relations using arbitrary paths and carry out a detailed study of the case in which the multi-indices follow the step-line. Finally, we address an extension of bivariate multiple orthogonality for radial weights, providing an example for Hermite-type weights where multiple Laguerre polynomials of the second kind were employed in the definitions.

In future work, we aim to extend existing constructions for bivariate orthogonal polynomials—such as those presented in
[
13
, Section 2.6.1]
—to the multiple orthogonal setting. This will enable the generalization of various bases on planar domains, providing a broader framework for multivariate multiple orthogonality.

Lemma 27
.

Consider
a,b,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos(mt)\cos^{a}(t)\sin^{b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin(mt)\cos^{a}(t)\sin^{b}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=0,

(79)

if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

•

a+b<m
, or

•

a+b=m+\ell
, with
\ell
and odd natural number.

Proof.

Despite the fact that these are real valued integrals, we will use some useful results from complex analysis to prove this result. First, recall the equalities

\displaystyle\cos(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{it}+e^{-it}}{2},

\displaystyle\sin(t)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{it}-e^{-it}}{2i},

(80)

So that

\displaystyle\cos^{a}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{a}}\sum_{j=0}^{a}\binom{a}{j}e^{it(a-2j)},

\displaystyle\sin^{b}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2i)^{b}}\sum_{h=0}^{b}(-1)^{h}\binom{b}{h}e^{it(b-2h)},

\displaystyle\cos(mt)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{imt}+e^{-imt}}{2},

\displaystyle\sin(mt)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{e^{imt}-e^{-imt}}{2i}.

Substituting these expressions in (
79
), we have

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\cos mt\cos^{a}t\sin^{b}t\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2^{a+b+1}i^{b}}\sum_{j=0}^{a}\sum_{h=0}^{b}(-1)^{h}\binom{a}{j}\binom{b}{h}\Big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{it(a+b-2(j+h)+m)}\mathrm{d}t\\
+\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{it(a+b-2(j+h)-m)}\mathrm{d}t\Big),

(81)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\sin mt\cos^{a}t\sin^{b}t\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2^{a+b+1}i^{b+1}}\sum_{j=0}^{a}\sum_{h=0}^{b}(-1)^{h}\binom{a}{j}\binom{b}{h}\Big(\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{it(a+b-2(j+h)+m)}\mathrm{d}t\\
-\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{it(a+b-2(j+h)-m)}\mathrm{d}t\Big),

(82)

On the other hand, it is easy to check that

\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{itn}\mathrm{d}t=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }n\neq 0\\
2\pi&\text{ if }n=0.\end{cases}

Now, assume
a+b<m
, so that, if
0\leq j\leq a
,
0\leq h\leq b
, observe that

a+b-2(j+h)+m\geq-a-b+m>0

and

a+b-2(j+h)-m\leq a+b-m<0,

so that both integrals in the right-hand side of (
81
) and (
82
) vanish for every value of
j
and
h
and the result follows in this case.

If
a+b=m+\ell
, with
\ell
an odd integer, the integrals in the right-hand side of (
81
) and (
82
) do not vanish only if
(2m+\ell)/2=j+h
and
\ell/2=j+h
respectively. If
\ell
is odd, these conditions will never be satisfied, so that both integrals will be zero for every value of
j
and
h
, and the result follows too.
∎
