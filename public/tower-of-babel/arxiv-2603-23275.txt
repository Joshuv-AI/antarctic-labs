Dirac Operators, APS Boundary Conditions, and Spectral Flow on a Finite Warped Cylinder
Taro Kimura, Sanchita Sharma

Abstract
We study the Dirac operator on a finite warped cylinder coupled to a background U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) gauge field. We identify the intrinsic endpoint operators defining the Atiyah–Patodi–Singer (APS) boundary condition and derive a determinant characterization of the modewise APS spectrum. In the constant-gauge, invertible setting, the endpoint reduced η \eta contributions cancel, so the APS index vanishes. For smooth gauge families, the APS projector becomes discontinuous when a boundary mode crosses zero. We therefore introduce a regularized APS-type family of self-adjoint endpoint conditions that remains continuous across such crossings. This regularized family admits a real-symplectic boundary formulation within the standard spectral-flow/Maslov framework: for nondegenerate regularization, the zero-mode set coincides with the boundary-zero set, and transverse boundary zeros give isolated regular crossings.

2
Dirac operator on a finite two-dimensional warped manifold

In this section, we derive the Dirac operator on a two-dimensional warped cylinder, fix the geometric and Clifford conventions, and include the coupling to a background
U(1)
gauge field. Throughout, we work in the Euclidean signature.
Let

M=[0,T]\times S^{1},\qquad T>0,

(2.1)

be equipped with coordinates
(t,\theta)
, where
t\in[0,T]
and
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. We endow
M
with the warped product metric

g=dt^{2}+f(t)^{2}\,d\theta^{2},

(2.2)

where
f\in C^{\infty}([0,T])
satisfies
f(t)>0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. Thus,
g
is a smooth Riemannian metric on
M
. The boundary of
M
is

\partial M=\{0\}\times S^{1}\sqcup\{T\}\times S^{1}.

(2.3)

We write

Y_{0}=\{0\}\times S^{1},\qquad Y_{T}=\{T\}\times S^{1}.

(2.4)

Hence,
M
is a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary.

Next, we fix an orthonormal frame on
M
. Following standard conventions, Greek indices
(\mu,\nu)
refer to curved coordinates, whereas Latin indices
(a,b)
refer to the orthonormal frame. Using
e^{a}=e^{a}_{\ \mu}\,dx^{\mu}
, we choose the orthonormal coframe

e^{1}=dt,\qquad e^{2}=f(t)\,d\theta,

(2.5)

with the dual frame

e_{1}=\partial_{t},\qquad e_{2}=\frac{1}{f(t)}\partial_{\theta}.

(2.6)

The metric components in the coordinate basis are recovered from the vielbein by
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{a}_{\mu}e^{b}_{\nu}\delta_{ab}
.

This frame is adapted to the warped manifold:
e_{1}
is normal to the boundary, while
e_{2}
is the tangent to the circle, scaled by the warping factor. We orient
M
by
dt\wedge d\theta
, that is, by the ordered orthonormal frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
. The inward unit normal is
\partial_{t}
at
t=0
and
-\partial_{t}
at
t=T
. In
Section 3.2
, this sign change will induce the corresponding sign change in the boundary operators on
Y_{0}
and
Y_{T}
used in the APS projector. For brevity, we often write
f
in place of
f(t)
.

2.1
Spinor bundle and Dirac operator

Let
S\to M
denote the complex spinor bundle associated with the chosen spin structure on
M
.
The two-dimensional complex spinor bundle has rank two. Since
M=[0,T]\times S^{1}
is a cylinder, once a spin structure is fixed, the spinor bundle can be trivialized in the chosen orthonormal frame. Spinors can therefore be represented by
\mathbb{C}^{2}
-valued functions, with the choice of spin structure reflected in the periodic or anti-periodic boundary condition in the
\theta
-direction.

We choose a representation of the complex Clifford algebra
\mathrm{Cl}_{2}
in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
by Hermitian
2\times 2
matrices
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
satisfying

\{\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b}\}=2\delta_{ab}.

(2.7)

the associated Hermitian Dirac operator is,

D=i\,\gamma_{a}\nabla_{e_{a}},

(2.8)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
. This convention is chosen so that the Clifford action of the induced boundary is defined below.

c(X)=-\,i\,\gamma(N)\gamma(X),\qquad X\in\mathcal{T}(\partial M),

(2.9)

is Hermitian. Here,
\mathcal{T}(\partial M)
denotes the tangent vector bundle along the boundary of
M
.

We represent the Clifford algebra using Pauli matrices, where
\gamma_{1}=\sigma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}=\sigma_{2}
. With the orientation fixed above, we define the chirality matrix by

\gamma_{3}=i\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}=-\sigma_{3}.

(2.10)

Therefore,

\gamma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\gamma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\gamma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(2.11)

The spin connection on
S
is determined by the Levi-Civita connection of
g
, with respect to the orthonormal frame
(e_{1},e_{2})
introduced above. The only non-zero coefficients of the Levi-Civita connection are

\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}e_{2},\quad\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}e_{1}.

(2.12)

Thus, the only independent non-vanishing spin connection one-form is

\omega^{1}_{2}=-\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}e^{2}=-\omega^{2}_{\ 1}.

(2.13)

These spin connection one-forms satisfy Cartan’s first structure equation
de^{a}+\omega^{a}_{b}\wedge e^{b}=0
.
The spinor covariant derivative on
S
is denoted by
\nabla^{S}
. In the chosen trivialization, it is given by

\nabla^{S}_{e_{a}}=\partial_{e_{a}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\omega_{a\,bc}\Sigma^{bc},\qquad\Sigma^{bc}=\frac{1}{4}[\gamma_{b},\gamma_{c}],

(2.14)

where
\partial_{e_{a}}
denotes directional differentiation along
e_{a}
, acting componentwise on spinors. For the warped metric under consideration, this gives;

\nabla^{S}_{e_{1}}=\partial_{t},\qquad\nabla^{S}_{e_{2}}=\frac{1}{f}\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}.

(2.15)

Function spaces and sections.

For a vector bundle
V\to M
, we write
\Gamma(V)
,
L^{2}(M;V)
, and
H^{1}(M;V)
for the spaces of smooth, square-integrable, and first Sobolev sections of
V
, defined using the Riemannian volume measure and the Hermitian bundle metric. The same notation applies to each boundary component
Y_{t_{0}}
. On
[0,T]
, we use the standard scalar Sobolev spaces
L^{2}([0,T])
and
H^{1}([0,T])
.

For each boundary component
Y_{t_{0}}
, let
S_{Y_{t_{0}}}
denote the induced boundary spinor bundle. We then write

\Gamma(S_{Y_{t_{0}}}),\qquad L^{2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}}),\qquad H^{1}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})

(2.16)

for the corresponding spaces of smooth, square-integrable, and first Sobolev boundary spinors. We also write

H^{1/2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})

(2.17)

for the standard fractional Sobolev trace space on the boundary component
Y_{t_{0}}
. Therefore,
H^{1/2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})
is the natural target of the trace map

H^{1}(M;S)\longrightarrow H^{1/2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})

(2.18)

given by restriction of spinors to
Y_{t_{0}}
.

Chirality splitting.

The two-dimensional spinor bundle splits as follows.

S=S^{+}\oplus S^{-},

(2.19)

where, under the chosen trivialization,

S^{+}\cong M\times\mathbb{C}\binom{0}{1},\qquad S^{-}\cong M\times\mathbb{C}\binom{1}{0}.

(2.20)

S^{\pm}
are the
\pm 1
-eigenbundles of the chirality operator
\gamma_{3}
. In our representation,

\gamma_{3}\binom{1}{0}=-\binom{1}{0},\qquad\gamma_{3}\binom{0}{1}=+\binom{0}{1}.

(2.21)

2.2
Coupling to a background
U(1)
gauge field

Let
E\to M
be a Hermitian complex line bundle, and fix a global unitary trivialization in which the unitary connection takes the form

\nabla^{E}=d+iA\,d\theta,

(2.22)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}
is constant. We then consider the spinor bundle
S\otimes E
coupled to the background gauge field and the corresponding Dirac operator

D:\Gamma(S\otimes E)\to\Gamma(S\otimes E).

(2.23)

From this point onward, all spinor bundles, boundary spinor bundles, and the associated spaces of sections, Sobolev spaces, and
L^{2}
-spaces are understood to be coupled to
E
. For brevity, we suppress
E
from the notation and continue to write
S
,
S^{\pm}
, and
S_{Y_{t_{0}}}
in place of
S\otimes E
,
S^{\pm}\otimes E
, and
S_{Y_{t_{0}}}\otimes E|_{Y_{t_{0}}}
, respectively.
In gauge theory notation, the connection one-form is
A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}=A\,d\theta
, so that
A_{t}=0
and
A_{\theta}=A
is constant.

The Dirac operator is defined using the tensor product connection.

\nabla^{S\otimes E}=\nabla^{S}\otimes 1+1\otimes\nabla^{E}

(2.24)

on
S\otimes E
. Thus,

D=i\gamma_{a}\nabla^{S\otimes E}_{e_{a}}.

(2.25)

In this trivialization, one obtains

\nabla^{S\otimes E}_{e_{1}}=\partial_{t},\qquad\nabla^{S\otimes E}_{e_{2}}=\frac{1}{f}(\partial_{\theta}+iA)-\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}.

(2.26)

Combining these expressions, we obtain the full Dirac operator:

D=i\gamma_{1}\left(\partial_{t}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}\right)+i\gamma_{2}\frac{1}{f}(\partial_{\theta}+iA).

(2.27)

With Hermitian Euclidean gamma matrices,
D
is self-adjoint. For the APS boundary condition, however, the relevant operator is not the tangential term appearing in the bulk decomposition, but the intrinsic self-adjoint boundary Dirac operator defined on each boundary component below.
After plugging in the Pauli matrices in the previous equation, we obtain

D=i\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{t}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}-\dfrac{i}{f}(\partial_{\theta}+iA)\\
\partial_{t}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}+\dfrac{i}{f}(\partial_{\theta}+iA)&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.28)

The term
\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}
encodes the effects of warping geometry.
Thus, we obtain the Dirac operator on the warped cylinder coupled to the background gauge field.

3
Bulk spectrum

We observe that the metric and background connection are invariant in the
\theta
-direction. Therefore, the operator decomposes into a one-dimensional radial equation parametrized by the angular mode
k
.

Remark 3.1
.

We allow either spin structure on
S^{1}
: for the periodic spin structure,
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, whereas for the anti-periodic spin structure,
k\in\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
. In what follows,
k
denotes an arbitrary allowed mode; the derivation is identical in both cases.

Thus, the bulk spectral equation reduces to a modewise family of radial equations parametrized by

m=k+A.

(3.1)

We now consider the eigenvalue equation and the associated Hilbert space of eigenfunctions.

D\psi=\lambda\psi,\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.2)

Mode Hilbert spaces.

For a fixed Fourier mode
k
, the natural radial Hilbert space induced from

L^{2}(M;S\otimes E)
is

\mathfrak{H}_{k}=L^{2}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}^{2}),

(3.3)

with the corresponding Sobolev space

\mathfrak{W}_{k}=H^{1}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}^{2}).

(3.4)

Accordingly, all modewise adjointness and self-adjointness statements in this section are understood with respect to the weighted radial inner product unless an explicit unitary conjugation to the unweighted space is made later. Writing

\psi(t,\theta)=e^{ik\theta}\begin{pmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{pmatrix},

(3.5)

we have

i\begin{pmatrix}0&\partial_{t}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}+\dfrac{k+A}{f}\\
\partial_{t}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}-\dfrac{k+A}{f}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}u(t)\\
v(t)\end{pmatrix},

(3.6)

leading to coupled differential equations

\displaystyle v^{\prime}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}v+\dfrac{k+A}{f}v

\displaystyle=-i\lambda u,

(3.7a)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{2f}u-\dfrac{k+A}{f}u

\displaystyle=-i\lambda v.

(3.7b)

The coupled system decouples into second-order scalar equations for the two spinor components. Writing
m=k+A
, we obtain for
u(t)

u^{\prime\prime}+\dfrac{f^{\prime}}{f}u^{\prime}+\left(\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{2f}-\frac{f^{\prime 2}}{4f^{2}}+\frac{mf^{\prime}}{f^{2}}-\frac{m^{2}}{f^{2}}+\lambda^{2}\right)u=0.

(3.8)

The corresponding equation for
v(t)
is obtained by replacing
m
with
-m
.

3.1
Reduction to Heun type

To place the resulting second-order equation into a standard Fuchsian form, we perform a sequence of transformations. First, we apply a Liouville transformation, which removes the first-derivative term and rewrites the equation in Schrödinger form.
In particular, if the decoupled equation is written as

u^{\prime\prime}(t)+P(t)\,u^{\prime}(t)+R(t)\,u(t)=0,

(3.9)

then setting
u(t)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int^{t}P(s)\,ds}\,w(t)
produces

w^{\prime\prime}(t)+\Bigl(R(t)-\tfrac{1}{2}P^{\prime}(t)-\tfrac{1}{4}P(t)^{2}\Bigr)\,w(t)=0.

(3.10)

In our case
P(t)=f^{\prime}(t)/f(t)
, hence
u(t)=f(t)^{-1/2}w(t)
. The explicit effective potential obtained from (
3.10
) is given in
Appendix A
.

Now we substitute
f(t)=e^{t}+\alpha e^{-t}
with
\alpha>0
, and introduce
z=e^{t}
. Since

f=\frac{z^{2}+\alpha}{z},\qquad\frac{d}{dt}=z\frac{d}{dz},

(3.11)

on the original real domain
t\in[0,T]
, we have

z=e^{t}\in[1,e^{T}]\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. For singularity analysis, we consider the normalized equation as a complex ODE in the variable
z
. Its coefficients are rational functions of
z
, so the equation is regular on

\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0,\pm i\sqrt{\alpha}\},

(3.12)

with singular points at

z\in\{0,\ \pm i\sqrt{\alpha},\ \infty\}.

(3.13)

To place these four singular points in a standard configuration, let
\beta=\sqrt{\alpha}
, and then we apply the Möbius transformation

x=\frac{z-i\beta}{z+i\beta},

(3.14)

which maps
z\in\{0,\pm i\beta,\infty\}
to
x\in\{-1,0,1,\infty\}
. In the
x
-variable, the equation can be written in the form

W^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{2x}{x^{2}-1}W^{\prime}(x)+Q(x)\,W(x)=0.

(3.15)

The explicit expressions for
Q(x)
, the Liouville-transformed potential, and the rational form of the
z
-equation are given in
Appendix A
.
Finally, we remove the double poles by factoring off the Frobenius gauge.

W(x)=x^{\rho_{0}}(x-1)^{\rho_{1}}(x+1)^{\rho_{-1}}\,y(x),

(3.16)

where
\rho_{0},\rho_{\pm 1}
are chosen from the indicial equations so that the
(x-a)^{-2}
terms cancel for
a\in\{0,\pm 1\}
. The resulting equation for
y
has four regular singular points at
x\in\{-1,0,1,\infty\}
, so the mode equation is of general Heun type. We used this observation only as structural motivation; the actual spectral condition will be formulated later using the APS boundary conditions and the associated boundary determinant.

3.2
APS boundary conditions on the warped cylinder

In the previous subsection, we reduced the eigenvalue equation

D\psi=\lambda\psi
to a second-order mode equation. To obtain a discrete spectrum of a finite cylinder, we need to specify an elliptic boundary condition. For this purpose, we impose APS boundary conditions, which require identifying the induced boundary operator on each boundary component.

The boundary is
\partial M=Y_{0}\sqcup Y_{T}
with
Y_{0}=\{0\}\times S^{1}
and
Y_{T}=\{T\}\times S^{1}
.

3.2.1
Normal vector and boundary Clifford multiplication

Let
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
be the orthonormal frame
e_{1}=\partial_{t},e_{2}=\frac{1}{f(t)}\partial_{\theta}.

Following the conventions of
[
6
]
, we use the inward-pointing unit normal
N
along each boundary
component. At
Y_{0}
the inward unit normal is
N=+e_{1}
, whereas, at
Y_{T}
the inward unit normal is

N=-e_{1}
. We continue to write
\gamma(\cdot)
for the Hermitian gamma-matrix representation in the chosen orthonormal frame, so that

\gamma(e_{1})=\gamma_{1},\qquad\gamma(e_{2})=\gamma_{2}.

(3.17)

Consequently,

\gamma(N)=\gamma_{1}\quad\text{at}\quad Y_{0},\qquad\gamma(N)=-\gamma_{1}\quad\text{at}\quad Y_{T}.

(3.18)

For the warped metric, the bulk Dirac operator is not literally of pure product form near the boundary,
because its normal part contains the additional zeroth-order term

\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{2f(t)}.

(3.19)

Consequently, we define the APS boundary operator intrinsically on each boundary component, following the hypersurface formalism of
[
6
]
. To define a Hermitian Clifford action along the boundary, we set

c(X)=-\,i\,\gamma(N)\gamma(X),\qquad X\in\mathcal{T}(\partial M).

(3.20)

Since
X\perp N
, the Hermitian matrices
\gamma(N)
and
\gamma(X)
anticommute, so
\gamma(N)\gamma(X)
is skew-Hermitian; hence
c(X)
is Hermitian. Moreover,
c(X)
satisfies the Clifford relations for the induced boundary metric.
We equip
L^{2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})
with the natural
L^{2}
-inner product

\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{L^{2}(Y_{t_{0}})}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\langle\phi(\theta),\psi(\theta)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}\,f(t_{0})\,d\theta.

(3.21)

With respect to this inner product, the tangential covariant derivative is skew-adjoint and the induced boundary Dirac operator is self-adjoint. In dimension two, the intrinsic boundary Dirac operator
\tilde{D}_{Y_{t_{0}}}
is related to the bulk Dirac operator by the hypersurface identity,

\tilde{D}_{Y_{t_{0}}}\psi=\frac{1}{2}H_{t_{0}}\,\psi-\gamma(N)D\psi-\nabla_{N}\psi,

(3.22)

for spinors
\psi
obtained by restriction from the bulk. Here
H_{t_{0}}
denotes the mean curvature of the boundary component
Y_{t_{0}}
, computed with respect to the chosen inward unit normal. In the present model, this intrinsic boundary operator is identified with the self-adjoint boundary operator
B_{t_{0}}
introduced below, and it is this operator that enters the APS projector.

Therefore, for the unit tangent vector
\mathcal{U}=e_{2}|_{t=t_{0}}
on

Y_{t_{0}}
one obtains

c(\mathcal{U})=\begin{cases}+\sigma_{3},&t_{0}=0,\\
-\sigma_{3},&t_{0}=T.\end{cases}

(3.23)

Crucially, the sign changes between the two ends because the inward normal changes sign.

3.2.2
Self-adjoint boundary Dirac operator

With the line bundle
E
fixed, we suppress it from the notation here as well. The covariant derivative along the unit tangent at
Y_{t_{0}}
is

\nabla_{\mathcal{U}}=\frac{1}{f(t_{0})}(\partial_{\theta}+iA).

(3.24)

The operator
\nabla_{\mathcal{U}}
is skew-adjoint on
L^{2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})
, so we introduce the Hermitian tangential operator

\mathcal{D}_{t_{0}}=-\,i\,\nabla_{\mathcal{U}}=\frac{1}{f(t_{0})}\bigl(-i\partial_{\theta}+A\bigr),

(3.25)

which has a real spectrum. We define the intrinsic boundary Dirac operator by

B_{t_{0}}=c(\mathcal{U})\,\mathcal{D}_{t_{0}}.

(3.26)

With the explicit expression for
c(\mathcal{U})
from (
3.23
), this becomes

B_{0}=\frac{1}{f(0)}\,\sigma_{3}\bigl(-i\partial_{\theta}+A\bigr),\qquad B_{T}=-\frac{1}{f(T)}\,\sigma_{3}\bigl(-i\partial_{\theta}+A\bigr).

(3.27)

Each
B_{t_{0}}
is self-adjoint on
L^{2}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})
with the natural domain

H^{1}(Y_{t_{0}};S_{Y_{t_{0}}})
.

We obtain the explicit mode matrices

B_{0}=\frac{m}{f(0)}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B_{T}=-\frac{m}{f(T)}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},

(3.28)

with
m=k+A
. In the invertible case relevant to the APS discussion below, henceforth we assume
m\neq 0
. Therefore, the boundary kernel is trivial, and both
B_{0}
and
B_{T}
are invertible.
The boundary eigenvalues are real and given by

\mu_{k,\pm}(t_{0})=\pm\frac{m}{f(t_{0})}.

(3.29)

The assignment of the positive eigenvalue to the upper or lower spinor component is reversed between
t=0
and
t=T
.

3.2.3
APS boundary conditions

Let
P_{>0}(B_{t_{0}})
denote the
L^{2}
-orthogonal projection onto the direct sum of eigenspaces of
B_{t_{0}}
with positive eigenvalues. Since we are working in the case
m\neq 0
, the boundary operators are invertible and
P_{>0}(B_{t_{0}})=P_{\geq 0}(B_{t_{0}})
. The APS boundary condition is

P_{>0}(B_{0})\bigl(\psi|_{t=0}\bigr)=0,\qquad P_{>0}(B_{T})\bigl(\psi|_{t=T}\bigr)=0.

(3.30)

Since
B_{0}
and
B_{T}
are diagonal in (
3.28
), the APS boundary condition reduces to a single component constraint for each
mode
m\neq 0
. Explicitly:

•

If
m>0
, then
\mu>0
corresponds to the upper component when
t=0
and to the lower component when

t=T
, hence

u(0)=0,\qquad v(T)=0.

(3.31)

•

If
m<0
, then
\mu>0
corresponds to the lower component when
t=0
and to the upper component when

t=T
, hence

v(0)=0,\qquad u(T)=0.

(3.32)

With these boundary conditions imposed at both ends, each Fourier mode defines a Fredholm boundary equation for the bulk. The compatibility of the bulk solutions with the APS constraints determines the spectral condition and hence the spectrum.

3.3
Bulk APS spectrum

We introduce first-order differential operators

\mathcal{A}_{+}=\partial_{t}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}+\frac{m}{f},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{-}=\partial_{t}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}-\frac{m}{f},

(3.33)

so that the equations (
3.7
) become
\mathcal{A}_{+}v=-i\lambda u
and
\mathcal{A}_{-}u=-i\lambda v
.

3.3.1
Decoupling and reconstruction

Eliminating
v
yields the decoupled second-order equation for
u
,

\mathcal{A}_{+}\mathcal{A}_{-}u+\lambda^{2}u=0.

(3.34)

Similarly, eliminating
u
yields
\mathcal{A}_{-}\mathcal{A}_{+}v+\lambda^{2}v=0
. For
\lambda\neq 0
, the second component can be
reconstructed from
u
using (
3.7
):

v(t)=\frac{i}{\lambda}\Big(u^{\prime}(t)+\Big(\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{2f(t)}-\frac{m}{f(t)}\Big)u(t)\Big),\qquad\lambda\neq 0.

(3.35)

Any nontrivial solution of (
3.34
) determines a unique spinor solution of
(
3.7
) after fixing an overall normalization.

3.3.2
APS boundary conditions and boundary constraints

The APS boundary condition imposes one
scalar constraint at each boundary component, and in the present basis, it takes the simple mode-by-mode form:

m>0:\quad u(0)=0,\ \ v(T)=0,\qquad m<0:\quad v(0)=0,\ \ u(T)=0.

(3.36)

Alternatively, there exist linear functionals

b_{0},b_{T}\in(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\ast}

(3.37)

such that

b_{0}\bigl(\psi(0,\lambda)\bigr)=0,\qquad b_{T}\bigl(\psi(T,\lambda)\bigr)=0.

(3.38)

With respect to the basis of the dual space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\ast}
, these functionals are

\displaystyle m>0:\quad

\displaystyle b_{0}=(1\ \ 0),\qquad b_{T}=(0\ \ 1),

(3.39a)

\displaystyle m<0:\quad

\displaystyle b_{0}=(0\ \ 1),\qquad b_{T}=(1\ \ 0).

(3.39b)

Proposition 3.2

(Modewise APS operator)
.

Fix an allowed Fourier mode
k
and assume
m=k+A\neq 0
. Let

D_{k}=i\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{A}_{+}\\
\mathcal{A}_{-}&0\end{pmatrix}

(3.40)

act on
\mathfrak{H}_{k}=L^{2}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}^{2})

with domain

\Dom(D_{k,\APS})=\begin{cases}\left\{\binom{u}{v}\in\mathfrak{W}_{k}:u(0)=0,\ v(T)=0\right\},&m>0,\\
\left\{\binom{u}{v}\in\mathfrak{W}_{k}:v(0)=0,\ u(T)=0\right\},&m<0.\end{cases}

(3.41)

Then
D_{k,\APS}
is a self-adjoint operator on
\mathfrak{H}_{k}
with compact resolvent. In particular, its spectrum is real, discrete, and of finite multiplicity.

Proof.

Since
f\in C^{\infty}([0,T])
and
f(t)>0
on
[0,T]
, the weighted Sobolev space

\mathfrak{W}_{k}=H^{1}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}^{2})

(3.42)

coincides with
H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2})
with the same norms. Let

\chi=\binom{u}{v},\qquad\nu=\binom{p}{q}\in\mathfrak{W}_{k}.

(3.43)

A direct integration by parts in the weighted inner product on
\mathfrak{H}_{k}
gives

\langle D_{k}\chi,\nu\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}_{k}}-\langle\chi,D_{k}\nu\rangle_{\mathfrak{H}_{k}}=if(0)\bigl(v(0)\overline{p(0)}-u(0)\overline{q(0)}\bigr)-if(T)\bigl(v(T)\overline{p(T)}-u(T)\overline{q(T)}\bigr).

(3.44)

If
m>0
, the APS boundary conditions are

u(0)=0,\qquad v(T)=0,

(3.45)

and the boundary form vanishes on
\Dom(D_{k,\APS})
. If
m<0
, the APS boundary conditions are
v(0)=0,\qquad u(T)=0,
and again the boundary form vanishes on
\Dom(D_{k,\APS})
. Thus
D_{k,\APS}
is symmetric.

The associated boundary trace space is a two-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{C}^{2},

and in each case, it is maximal isotropic for the full boundary form above. Therefore, the symmetric operator is self-adjoint; see, for example,
[
7
,
8
]
.

Finally, on the compact interval
[0,T]
, the graph norm of
D_{k,\APS}
is equivalent to the
H^{1}
-norm on the domain, and the embedding

H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2})\hookrightarrow L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2})

(3.46)

is compact. Hence
D_{k,\APS}
has compact resolvent. The spectral conclusions follow.
∎

3.3.3
Determinant formulation of the spectral condition

Let
\psi_{1}(t,\lambda)
and
\psi_{2}(t,\lambda)
be two linearly independent solutions of
(
3.7
) on
[0,T]
. Then any solution can be written in the form

\psi(t,\lambda)=C_{1}\psi_{1}(t,\lambda)+C_{2}\psi_{2}(t,\lambda).

(3.47)

Imposing the two APS constraints gives a homogeneous linear system for
(C_{1},C_{2})
, and a nontrivial solution exists if and only if the associated
2\times 2
boundary matrix is singular. Accordingly, we define

F_{k}(\lambda)=\det\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}\psi_{1}(0,\lambda)&b_{0}\psi_{2}(0,\lambda)\\
b_{T}\psi_{1}(T,\lambda)&b_{T}\psi_{2}(T,\lambda)\end{pmatrix}.

(3.48)

Proposition 3.3

(Determinant characterization of the modewise APS spectrum)
.

Fix an allowed Fourier mode
k
and assume
m=k+A\neq 0
. Then

\lambda\in\Spec_{\mathrm{APS}}(k)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F_{k}(\lambda)=0.

(3.49)

Moreover, the zero set of
F_{k}
is independent of the chosen fundamental pair

(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})
.

Proof.

Any solution of (
3.7
) can be written uniquely as

\psi(t,\lambda)=C_{1}\psi_{1}(t,\lambda)+C_{2}\psi_{2}(t,\lambda).

(3.50)

Imposing the APS boundary conditions at
t=0
and
t=T
gives a homogeneous linear system in
(C_{1},C_{2})
. A nontrivial solution exists if and only if the associated boundary matrix is singular, which is exactly the condition
F_{k}(\lambda)=0
.

If another fundamental pair is obtained by

(\widetilde{\psi}_{1},\widetilde{\psi}_{2})=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})M(\lambda),\qquad M(\lambda)\in\mathrm{GL}_{2}(\mathbb{C}),

(3.51)

then

\widetilde{F}_{k}(\lambda)=\det(M(\lambda))\,F_{k}(\lambda),

(3.52)

so the zero set is unchanged.
∎

In components, this reads:

\displaystyle m>0:\quad

\displaystyle F_{k}(\lambda)=u_{1}(0,\lambda)v_{2}(T,\lambda)-u_{2}(0,\lambda)v_{1}(T,\lambda),

(3.53a)

\displaystyle m<0:\quad

\displaystyle F_{k}(\lambda)=v_{1}(0,\lambda)u_{2}(T,\lambda)-v_{2}(0,\lambda)u_{1}(T,\lambda).

(3.53b)

3.3.4
Bulk Spectrum

For a fixed Fourier mode

, let

and consider the reduced first-order Dirac system (
3.7
).
To determine the APS spectrum numerically, we solve this system with the left APS boundary condition imposed at

and define a scalar terminal residual at

. When

we impose the left APS condition

and define

(3.54)

whereas when

we impose the left APS condition

and define

F_{k}(\lambda)=u(T;\lambda).

(3.55)

Thus,

is exactly the remaining APS boundary condition, so the zeros of

coincide with the APS eigenvalues in mode

.

Proposition 3.4

(Absence of zero modes in the invertible case)
.

Fix an allowed Fourier mode
k
and assume
m=k+A\neq 0
. Then
\lambda=0
is
not an APS eigenvalue for the
k
-th mode.

Proof.

For
\lambda=0
, the system (
3.7
) reduces to

\mathcal{A}_{+}v=0,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{-}u=0.

(3.56)

Hence

v(t)=C_{1}\exp\!\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}+\frac{m}{f}\right)\,ds\right),\qquad u(t)=C_{2}\exp\!\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}-\frac{m}{f}\right)\,ds\right).

(3.57)

If

, the APS conditions are
u(0)=0
and

, which force

and

. If

4
The APS boundary correction:
\eta
-invariant and cancellation

So far, we have considered the self-adjoint APS eigenvalue equation for the full Dirac operator
D
, whose modewise APS conditions constrain both components of the spinor. Now we switch to the Fredholm APS problem for the chiral operator
D^{+}
. This shift is natural because the APS index theorem is formulated for the chiral Dirac operator, whereas the previous section concerned the self-adjoint spectral problem for the full Dirac operator.

D^{+}:\Gamma(S^{+})\to\Gamma(S^{-}).

(4.1)

For the chiral Dirac operator, the APS projector is imposed only on the
S^{+}
boundary, while the complementary boundary condition appears in the adjoint problem for
D^{-}
.

By
Section 3.2.1
, the unit normal
N
is taken to be inward-pointing along each boundary component, and all boundary operators and APS projections below are understood in this convention. As in the hypersurface formalism above, the APS condition is defined from the spectral resolution of the intrinsic self-adjoint boundary Dirac operator on each boundary component. In the warped-cylinder model, these are the operators
B_{0}
and
B_{T}
in (
3.27
).

By
Section 3
, the allowed Fourier modes are

\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{Z}\quad\text{in the periodic case,}\qquad\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}\quad\text{in the anti-periodic case.}

(4.2)

4.1
The boundary
\eta
-invariant and the reduced invariant
\xi

Let
B
be a self-adjoint first-order elliptic operator on a closed manifold.
For
\Re(s)\gg 1
, its
\eta
-function is defined by

\eta(B,s)\;=\;\sum_{\mu\in\Spec(B)\setminus\{0\}}\sign(\mu)\,|\mu|^{-s}.

(4.3)

It admits a meromorphic continuation to
s\in\mathbb{C}
which is regular at
s=0
. We write
\eta(B,0)
for its value at
s=0
.

In APS index theory, the boundary correction is expressed in terms of the reduced invariant

\xi(B)\;=\;\frac{\eta(B,0)+h(B)}{2},\qquad h(B)=\dim\ker B.

(4.4)

When
B
is invertible, one has
h(B)=0
, hence,

\xi(B)=\frac{\eta(B,0)}{2}.

(4.5)

We begin with the
\eta
-invariant computation for the circle operator and then return to the warped cylinder to show cancellation of the two APS boundary contributions when the gauge is constant.

Circle model.

The intrinsic boundary Dirac operator is,

B_{A}e^{ik\theta}=(k+A)e^{ik\theta},\qquad k\in\mathcal{K}.

(4.6)

Assuming

k+A\neq 0\qquad\text{for every }k\in\mathcal{K},

(4.7)

equivalently
0\notin\Spec(B_{A})
.
We now justify the reduction to a unique parameter
\rho\in(0,1)
. The open interval is forced by the invertibility assumption
0\notin\Spec(B_{A})
, since
\rho=0
or
\rho=1
would place
0
in the spectrum in the representation (
4.13
).

If
\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{Z}
, write

A=\ell+\rho,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\rho\in(0,1).

(4.8)

Then

\Spec(B_{A})=\{n+A:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\{n+\ell+\rho:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\{m+\rho:m\in\mathbb{Z}\},

(4.9)

after relabelling
m=n+\ell
.

If
\mathcal{K}=\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
, then

\Spec(B_{A})=\Bigl\{n+\Bigl(A+\tfrac{1}{2}\Bigr):n\in\mathbb{Z}\Bigr\}.

(4.10)

Since
0\notin\Spec(B_{A})
, the number
A+\tfrac{1}{2}
is not an integer. Hence, there is a unique decomposition

A+\tfrac{1}{2}=\ell+\rho,\qquad\ell\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\rho\in(0,1),

(4.11)

and therefore

\Spec(B_{A})=\{m+\rho:m\in\mathbb{Z}\},

(4.12)

after relabelling the Fourier index.
Thus, in either spin structure, there is a unique
\rho\in(0,1)
such that

\Spec(B_{A})=\{n+\rho:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

(4.13)

Now we compute the
\eta
-function explicitly.
Using (
4.13
), for
\Re(s)>1
one has absolute convergence and can split the positive and negative parts of the spectrum:

\displaystyle\eta(B_{A},s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n+\rho>0}(n+\rho)^{-s}-\sum_{n+\rho<0}|n+\rho|^{-s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+\rho)^{-s}-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1-\rho)^{-s}.

(4.14)

Indeed, the negative eigenvalues are precisely

-(n+1-\rho),\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots.

(4.15)

Therefore, for
\Re(s)>1
,

\eta(B_{A},s)=\zeta(s,\rho)-\zeta(s,1-\rho),

(4.16)

where
\zeta(s,a)
denotes the Hurwitz zeta function. Each Hurwitz zeta function admits a meromorphic continuation to all
s\in\mathbb{C}
, with a simple pole at
s=1
of residue
1

[
37
]
. Hence both
\zeta(s,\rho)
and
\zeta(s,1-\rho)
have the same pole at
s=1
. It follows that in the difference (
4.16
) the pole parts cancel. Hence
\eta(B_{A},s)
admits a meromorphic continuation to all
s\in\mathbb{C}
and is regular at
s=0
.

Evaluating at
s=0
and using the standard formula

\zeta(0,a)=\frac{1}{2}-a,\qquad a\in(0,1),

(4.17)

one obtains

\eta(B_{A},0)=\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-\rho\Bigr)-\Bigl(\frac{1}{2}-(1-\rho)\Bigr)=1-2\rho,

(4.18)

and therefore

\xi(B_{A})=\frac{1-2\rho}{2}.

(4.19)

Relation to the actual endpoint operators.

The operator
B_{A}
above is a normalized operator on the scalar boundary mode space over
S^{1}
. After identifying the boundary components
Y_{0}
and
Y_{T}
with
S^{1}
via the coordinate
\theta
, the actual positive-chirality endpoint operators are

B_{0}^{+}=-\frac{1}{f(0)}\,B_{A},\qquad B_{T}^{+}=+\frac{1}{f(T)}\,B_{A}.

(4.20)

Since
f(0),f(T)>0
, positive rescaling does not change the reduced
\eta
-invariant:

\eta(cB,0)=\eta(B,0),\qquad\xi(cB)=\xi(B)\qquad(c>0),

(4.21)

provided
B
is invertible. Thus, only the sign matters, and therefore

\xi(B_{0}^{+})=-\xi(B_{A}),\qquad\xi(B_{T}^{+})=\xi(B_{A}).

(4.22)

This is the mechanism behind the cancellation of the two APS boundary contributions on the finite cylinder.

4.2
Fredholm chiral APS problem and the APS index

We now return to the chiral APS problem on the finite warped cylinder.

M=[0,T]\times S^{1}.

(4.23)

Let
D^{+}:\Gamma(S^{+})\to\Gamma(S^{-})
denote the positive-chirality part of the Dirac operator.
Because the metric and connection are
S^{1}
-invariant, both
D^{+}
and the induced tangential boundary operators are diagonal in Fourier modes.

A spinor may be decomposed into Fourier modes as

\psi(t,\theta)=e^{ik\theta}\binom{u(t)}{v(t)},\qquad k\in\mathcal{K}.

(4.24)

In our matrix convention,
S^{+}
is represented by the second component
v
, while
S^{-}
is represented by the first component
u
.
The Dirac operator (
2.28
) takes the form,

D=i\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathcal{A}_{+}\\
\mathcal{A}_{-}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\pm}=\partial_{t}+\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{2f(t)}\pm\frac{m}{f(t)},\qquad m=k+A.

(4.25)

Thus,

D^{+}=i\mathcal{A}_{+}:\Gamma(S^{+})\to\Gamma(S^{-}),\qquad D^{-}=i\mathcal{A}_{-}:\Gamma(S^{-})\to\Gamma(S^{+}).

(4.26)

Let
B_{0}^{+}
and
B_{T}^{+}
denote the tangential operators induced by
D^{+}
at the boundary components
t=0
and
t=T
, respectively. Similarly, let
B_{0}^{-}
and
B_{T}^{-}
denote the tangential operators induced by
D^{-}
. Under natural identification of both boundary circles with
S^{1}
, the change in boundary orientation together with the rescaling of the tangential metric implies that the endpoint operators differ by a sign and a positive constant factor.

The operators
B_{t_{0}}
introduced earlier act on the full boundary spinor bundle

S_{Y_{t_{0}}}=S^{+}_{Y_{t_{0}}}\oplus S^{-}_{Y_{t_{0}}}.

(4.27)

In the chiral APS problem, we write
B_{t_{0}}^{\pm}
for the restrictions of
B_{t_{0}}
to the corresponding chiral summands.

Chiral decomposition of the boundary operator.

Since

S_{Y_{t_{0}}}=S^{-}_{Y_{t_{0}}}\oplus S^{+}_{Y_{t_{0}}},

(4.28)

and in our convention
S^{-}
is the upper component while
S^{+}
is the lower component, the full boundary operator decomposes diagonally as

B_{t_{0}}=\begin{pmatrix}B_{t_{0}}^{-}&0\\
0&B_{t_{0}}^{+}\end{pmatrix}.

(4.29)

Thus
B_{t_{0}}^{\pm}
are simply the restrictions of
B_{t_{0}}
to the corresponding chiral summands. In particular, the modewise eigenvalues of
B_{t_{0}}^{\pm}
are read off directly from (
3.28
), and therefore

B_{0}^{+}(k)=-\frac{m}{f(0)},\qquad B_{T}^{+}(k)=+\frac{m}{f(T)},\qquad B_{0}^{-}(k)=+\frac{m}{f(0)},\qquad B_{T}^{-}(k)=-\frac{m}{f(T)}.

(4.30)

Here

U:L^{2}(Y_{0};S_{Y_{0}})\longrightarrow L^{2}(Y_{T};S_{Y_{T}})

(4.31)

denotes the unitary induced by the identification of both boundary components with

S^{1}
via the coordinate
\theta
; in the chosen trivialization, it acts by

(U\phi)(\theta)=\phi(\theta).

(4.32)

Hence, after identifying the two boundary Hilbert spaces by the unitary map
U
, the endpoint operators satisfy

B_{T}^{\pm}=-\,c\,UB_{0}^{\pm}U^{-1},\qquad c=\frac{f(0)}{f(T)}>0.

(4.33)

Global APS domain.

The APS chiral operator is,

D^{+}_{\APS}:\Dom(D^{+}_{\APS})\subset H^{1}(M;S^{+})\longrightarrow L^{2}(M;S^{-}),

(4.34)

with domain

\Dom(D^{+}_{\APS})=\Bigl\{\psi\in H^{1}(M;S^{+}):P_{>0}(B_{0}^{+})\bigl(\psi|_{t=0}\bigr)=0,\;P_{>0}(B_{T}^{+})\bigl(\psi|_{t=T}\bigr)=0\Bigr\}.

(4.35)

APS theory implies that the operator
D^{+}_{\APS}
is Fredholm.

For the positive-chirality
k
th Fourier mode, we write

\mathfrak{H}_{k}^{+}=L^{2}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}),\qquad\mathfrak{W}_{k}^{+}=H^{1}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}).

(4.36)

Modewise description.

Due to
S^{1}
-invariance,
D^{+}_{\APS}
decomposes into Fourier modes.
On the
k
-th mode, one obtains the scalar operator

D^{+}_{\APS,k}=i\mathcal{A}_{+}:\Dom(D^{+}_{\APS,k})\subset\mathfrak{W}_{k}^{+}\to\mathfrak{H}_{k}^{+},

(4.37)

acting on the
v
-component.
For
m\neq 0
, the APS boundary condition becomes,

m>0:\quad v(T)=0\ \text{(no condition at }0),\qquad m<0:\quad v(0)=0\ \text{(no condition at }T).

(4.38)

The adjoint APS conditions for
D^{-}
impose the complementary endpoint conditions on
u
:

m>0:\quad u(0)=0,\qquad m<0:\quad u(T)=0.

(4.39)

Thus, the chiral APS conditions are compatible with the two-component self-adjoint operator used in
Section 3.3.2
; modewise, they impose complementary endpoint conditions on
u
and
v
.

4.2.1
APS index formula and cancellation for constant gauge

APS index formula.

By the Atiyah-Patodi-Singer index theorem for Dirac operators on manifolds with boundary
(see
[
1
, Theorem 4.2]
; for background on conventions, see also
[
4
,
5
]
), the index is given by

\ind(D^{+}_{\APS})=\frac{i}{2\pi}\int_{M}F^{\nabla^{E}}-\xi(B_{0}^{+})-\xi(B_{T}^{+}).

(4.40)

In constant gauge, the connection is flat, so
F^{\nabla^{E}}=0
.

Geometric relation between the two endpoint operators.

Equation (
4.33
) shows that, after identifying the two boundary circles, the tangential operators at
t=0
and
t=T
are related by a sign and a positive scale factor:

B_{T}^{\pm}=-\,c\,UB_{0}^{\pm}U^{-1},\qquad c=\frac{f(0)}{f(T)}>0.

(4.41)

In particular,
B_{T}^{+}
is unitarily equivalent to
-c\,B_{0}^{+}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Sign/scale flip implies
\xi
-cancellation)
.

Let
B
be a self-adjoint elliptic operator with discrete spectrum, and assume that
B
is invertible.
If
c>0
, then

\eta(cB,0)=\eta(B,0),\qquad\eta(-B,0)=-\eta(B,0).

(4.42)

Consequently, if

B_{T}=-\,c\,UB_{0}U^{-1},\qquad c>0,

(4.43)

for some unitary
U
, then

\xi(B_{T})=-\xi(B_{0}),\qquad\text{and hence}\qquad\xi(B_{0})+\xi(B_{T})=0.

(4.44)

Proof.

When,
\Re(s)\gg 1

\eta(cB,s)=c^{-s}\eta(B,s),

(4.45)

so by meromorphic continuation
\eta(cB,0)=\eta(B,0)
.
Similarly,

\eta(-B,s)=-\eta(B,s),

(4.46)

hence
\eta(-B,0)=-\eta(B,0)
.
Unitary conjugation does not change the spectrum, so

\eta(UBU^{-1},0)=\eta(B,0).

(4.47)

If
B
is invertible, then
h(B)=0
and therefore
\xi(B)=\eta(B,0)/2
.
Applying these facts to
B_{T}=-c\,UB_{0}U^{-1}
yields (
4.44
).
∎

Proposition 4.2

(Vanishing of the APS boundary correction for constant gauge)
.

Assume that
k+A\neq 0
for every
k\in\mathcal{K}
. Then the endpoint operators
B_{0}^{+}
and
B_{T}^{+}
are invertible, and

\xi(B_{0}^{+})+\xi(B_{T}^{+})=0.

(4.48)

Consequently,

\ind(D^{+}_{\APS})=0.

(4.49)

Proof.

By assumption, one has
m=k+A\neq 0
for every

, so the modewise eigenvalues in (
4.30
) never vanish. Thus, both
B_{0}^{+}
and
B_{T}^{+}
are invertible.
By (
4.33
) and Lemma
4.1
,

\xi(B_{T}^{+})=-\xi(B_{0}^{+}).

(4.50)

Hence, the APS boundary correction cancels:

\xi(B_{0}^{+})+\xi(B_{T}^{+})=0.

(4.51)

Since
F^{\nabla^{E}}=0
, the index formula (
4.40
) reduces to

\ind(D^{+}_{\APS})=0.

(4.52)

∎

Remark 4.3

(Wall mode and parameter variation)
.

When
A
is constant, no wall mode occurs precisely when
k+A\neq 0
for every
k\in\mathcal{K}
.
The wall case
m=0
becomes relevant when one varies
A
(or, more generally, a boundary family) so that
A
crosses the lattice of allowed shifts; depending on the spin structure, this lattice is
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2}
.
In that situation,
0\in\Spec(B)
and the APS projection is no longer canonical on
\ker B
.
We return to this issue in the discussion of spectral flow.

Remark 4.4

(No APS boundary condition at infinity)
.

The cancellation above is a finite-cylinder statement: it uses the fact that
M
has two genuine boundary components, and hence two APS boundary operators
B_{0}
and
B_{T}
.
If one passes formally to the half-infinite cylinder

without imposing additional asymptotic conditions, there is no second APS boundary contribution and therefore no literal “
\eta
-invariant at infinity” canceling
\eta(B_{0},0)
.

Consequently, throughout this paper, the phrase “

” is interpreted only as the large-

limit of the finite APS problem on

, not as an APS boundary condition imposed at infinity.
In particular, for a constant

under the invertible assumption, the APS index vanishes for every finite

, and therefore also in the large-

limit.

5
Regularized boundary families, Maslov index, and zero-mode crossings

Our motivation for the analysis in this section is to isolate the crossing mechanism associated with the boundary-zero set

k+A(s)=0.

(5.1)

In the two-boundary APS setup studied in Sections
2
-
4
, the endpoint reduced
\eta
-invariant contributions cancel in the constant gauge invertible case, so the APS index does not directly provide a continuous framework for analyzing these boundary-zero crossings. For this reason, we introduce a regularized family of self-adjoint boundary conditions whose parameter dependence is continuous. This makes the standard spectral-flow/Maslov formalism accessible and enables direct analysis of the zero-mode crossing set.

Assumption.

Throughout this section, we fix a Fourier mode
k\in\mathcal{K}
and a smoothing parameter
\delta>0
. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, all operators and boundary conditions below refer to the regularized family
D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
. We assume endpoint invertibility at
s=s_{1},s_{2}
. Whenever a signed local crossing count is used, we assume the relevant crossings are isolated and simple.

Theorem 5.1

(Zero-mode criterion for the regularized family)
.

Fix a mode
k\in\mathcal{K}
and assume

\delta\neq\frac{2}{\ell(T)},\qquad\ell(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d\tau}{f(\tau)}.

(5.2)

Let
\{D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\}_{s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]}
denote the regularized
k
-mode operator family defined in
Section 5.3
. Then

0\in\Spec\bigl(D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\bigr)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k+A(s)=0.

(5.3)

Thus, zero modes of the regularized family occur exactly at the boundary zeros. If moreover

A^{\prime}(s_{*})\neq 0
at such a point
s_{*}
, then
s_{*}
is an isolated regular crossing for the regularized family, and the local spectral-flow/Maslov crossing formalism applies.

Corollary 5.2

(Global zero-mode criterion)
.

Under the same assumptions, the global regularized family has a zero mode at parameter
s
if and only if there exists
k\in\mathcal{K}
such that

k+A(s)=0.

(5.4)

Steps to prove Theorem
5.1

First, we identify the boundary-zero set and define the regularized endpoint coefficients. Then, we rewrite the mode equation in a real first-order form and describe the associated boundary symplectic space and Lagrangians. Next, we show that the resulting regularized boundary problem defines a continuous self-adjoint Fredholm family to which the standard spectral-flow/Maslov correspondence applies. Finally, we compute the zero-mode matching condition explicitly and deduce the zero-mode criterion and the regularity of transverse crossings.

5.1
Boundary zeros and regularized endpoint conditions

The regularization replaces the discontinuous sign choice in the APS boundary condition by a continuous parameter
m(s,k)=k+A(s)
. For
m(s,k)\neq 0
, the regularized endpoint family converges as
\delta\to 0
to the corresponding APS choice, while remaining continuous through
m(s,k)=0
.

Let
A=A(s)
be a smooth one-parameter family. The endpoint boundary operators are

B_{s}(0)=\frac{1}{f(0)}\,\sigma_{3}\bigl(-i\partial_{\theta}+A(s)\bigr),\qquad B_{s}(T)=-\frac{1}{f(T)}\,\sigma_{3}\bigl(-i\partial_{\theta}+A(s)\bigr),\qquad s\in[s_{1},s_{2}].

(5.5)

Since these operators are
S^{1}
-invariant, they are diagonal in Fourier modes. Writing

\mathcal{K}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{Z},&\text{periodic spin structure},\\
\mathbb{Z}+\tfrac{1}{2},&\text{anti-periodic spin structure},\end{cases}\qquad m(s,k)=k+A(s),\qquad k\in\mathcal{K},

(5.6)

their restriction to the
k
-th Fourier mode is

B_{s}(0)\big|_{k}=\frac{m(s,k)}{f(0)}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B_{s}(T)\big|_{k}=-\frac{m(s,k)}{f(T)}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(5.7)

Hence, the boundary eigenvalues in mode
k
are

\mu_{k,\pm}(s;t_{0})=\pm\frac{m(s,k)}{f(t_{0})},\qquad t_{0}\in\{0,T\}.

(5.8)

A
boundary-zero
for mode
k
is a parameter
s_{*}
such that

m(s_{*},k)=k+A(s_{*})=0.

(5.9)

We assume endpoint invertibility,

0\notin\Spec(B_{s_{1}}(t_{0})),\qquad 0\notin\Spec(B_{s_{2}}(t_{0})),\qquad t_{0}\in\{0,T\},

(5.10)

so no boundary-zero occurs at
s=s_{1}
or
s=s_{2}
.

A boundary-zero
s_{*}
is called
transverse
if

\partial_{s}m(s_{*},k)=A^{\prime}(s_{*})\neq 0.

(5.11)

In the present scalar modewise setting, every transverse boundary-zero is simple and hence regular for the local Maslov crossing form. If
A^{\prime}(s_{*})=0
, then the boundary-zero is nontransverse; it may be a genuine sign-changing crossing or merely a touching point. A sufficient local criterion for touching is

m(s_{*},k)=0,\qquad\partial_{s}m(s_{*},k)=0,\qquad\partial_{s}^{2}m(s_{*},k)\neq 0,

(5.12)

equivalently,

k+A(s_{*})=0,\qquad A^{\prime}(s_{*})=0,\qquad A^{\prime\prime}(s_{*})\neq 0.

(5.13)

Because
A(s)
is bounded on the compact interval
[s_{1},s_{2}]
, only finitely many modes satisfy
k+A(s)=0
for some
s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
.

Fix
\delta>0
. On the scalar boundary mode space over
S^{1}
, set

M_{s}=-i\partial_{\theta}+A(s),

(5.14)

and defined by functional calculus

\alpha_{0}(s)=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{M_{s}}{\delta}\Big),\qquad\alpha_{T}(s)=-\alpha_{0}(s).

(5.15)

On the
k
-th mode,

\alpha_{0}(s,k)=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Big),\qquad\alpha_{T}(s,k)=-\alpha_{0}(s,k).

(5.16)

Remark 5.3

(Why this regularization is chosen)
.

The choice

\alpha_{0}(s,k)=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Big),\qquad\alpha_{T}(s,k)=-\alpha_{0}(s,k),

(5.17)

is a choice rather than a canonical one. It gives a smooth interpolation between the two APS endpoint choices as the sign of
m(s,k)
changes, and the identity

\frac{1+\tanh z}{1-\tanh z}=e^{2z}

(5.18)

makes the zero-mode matching equation explicitly solvable.

5.2
Real formulation, boundary form, and Lagrangians

We now pass to a real first-order formulation adapted to the boundary symplectic form. Write the original mode spinor as

\psi=\binom{u}{v}.

(5.19)

Let

U=\operatorname{diag}(1,i),

(5.20)

and define

\mathcal{U}:L^{2}([0,T],f(t)\,dt;\mathbb{C}^{2})\longrightarrow L^{2}([0,T],dt;\mathbb{C}^{2}),\qquad(\mathcal{U}\psi)(t)=f(t)^{1/2}U^{\ast}\psi(t).

(5.21)

If

\phi=\mathcal{U}\psi=\binom{\tilde{u}}{w},\qquad\tilde{u}=f(t)^{1/2}u,\qquad w=-\,i\,f(t)^{1/2}v,

(5.22)

then
\mathcal{U}
is unitary. We relabel
\tilde{u}
as
u
, and write

\phi=\binom{u}{w}.

(5.23)

Conjugating by
\mathcal{U}
transforms the mode differential expression into

\mathcal{D}_{s,k}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\partial_{t}-\dfrac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}\\
\partial_{t}-\dfrac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(5.24)

Hence,

\mathcal{D}_{s,k}\phi=\lambda\phi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{aligned} u^{\prime}(t)&=\frac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}\,u(t)+\lambda\,w(t),\\
w^{\prime}(t)&=-\frac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}\,w(t)-\lambda\,u(t),\end{aligned}\qquad t\in[0,T].

(5.25)

The coefficients are real.

In these variables, the regularized endpoint conditions are

\displaystyle(1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))\,u(0)+(1-\alpha_{0}(s,k))\,w(0)

\displaystyle=0,

(5.26)

\displaystyle(1+\alpha_{T}(s,k))\,u(T)+(1-\alpha_{T}(s,k))\,w(T)

\displaystyle=0.

Lemma 5.4

(Real and complex zero modes)
.

Fix
s
,
k
, and
\delta>0
, and let
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
denote the operator associated with (
5.24
) and the endpoint conditions (
5.26
). Then, complex conjugation preserves
\Dom(\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k})
and commutes with
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
. Consequently,

\ker_{\mathbb{C}}\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\bigr)=\ker_{\mathbb{R}}\bigl(\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\bigr)\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{C}.

(5.27)

In particular, if the real kernel is one-dimensional, then the complex kernel is one-dimensional as a complex vector space.

Proof.

The coefficients of (
5.24
) and the endpoint relations (
5.26
) are real. Therefore, complex conjugation preserves both the domain and the action of the operator. If
x
is a complex zero mode, then its real and imaginary parts are real zero modes satisfying the same endpoint conditions, which gives the stated complexification identity.
∎

Let

x(t)=\begin{pmatrix}u(t)\\
w(t)\end{pmatrix},\qquad y(t)=\begin{pmatrix}p(t)\\
q(t)\end{pmatrix},

(5.28)

with
x,y\in H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Integration by parts gives

\langle\mathcal{D}_{s,k}x,y\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle x,\mathcal{D}_{s,k}y\rangle_{L^{2}}=\bigl[u(t)q(t)-w(t)p(t)\bigr]_{t=0}^{t=T}.

(5.29)

If

J^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},

(5.30)

define

\omega(\xi,\eta)=\langle J^{\prime}\xi,\eta\rangle_{\mathbb{R}^{2}},\qquad\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

(5.31)

On the endpoint trace space

\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{2},\qquad(x_{0},x_{T})=(x(0),x(T)),

(5.32)

set

\Omega\big((x_{0},x_{T}),(y_{0},y_{T})\big)=\omega(x_{T},y_{T})-\omega(x_{0},y_{0}).

(5.33)

Then (
5.29
) becomes

\langle\mathcal{D}_{s,k}x,y\rangle_{L^{2}}-\langle x,\mathcal{D}_{s,k}y\rangle_{L^{2}}=\Omega\big((x(0),x(T)),(y(0),y(T))\big).

(5.34)

Thus, separated self-adjoint boundary conditions are encoded by Lagrangian subspaces of
(\mathcal{H}_{k},\Omega)
.

We now define the two Lagrangians relevant for the zero-mode crossing problem. First,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{x=(u,w)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ (1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))u+(1-\alpha_{0}(s,k))w=0\Bigr\},

(5.35a)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{x=(u,w)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ (1+\alpha_{T}(s,k))u+(1-\alpha_{T}(s,k))w=0\Bigr\},

(5.35b)

and

\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)=\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)\oplus\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}.

(5.36)

Lemma 5.5
.

For each fixed
s
and
k
, the subspace
\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}
is Lagrangian.

Proof.

Each of
\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)
and
\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)
is a one-dimensional isotropic subspace of
(\mathbb{R}^{2},\omega)
. Their direct sum is therefore a two-dimensional maximal isotropic subspace of
(\mathcal{H}_{k},\Omega)
.
∎

Second, at
\lambda=0
the system (
5.25
) decouples:

u^{\prime}=\frac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}u,\qquad w^{\prime}=-\frac{m(s,k)}{f(t)}w.

(5.37)

Let

\ell(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d\tau}{f(\tau)}.

(5.38)

Then the transfer matrix is

x(T)=\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)\,x(0),\qquad\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)=\begin{pmatrix}e^{\,m(s,k)\,\ell(T)}&0\\
0&e^{-\,m(s,k)\,\ell(T)}\end{pmatrix}.

(5.39)

Since
\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)
is symplectic with respect to
\omega
, its graph

\Lambda_{s,k}(0)=\operatorname{graph}\bigl(\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)\bigr)=\Bigl\{(x_{0},x_{T})\in\mathcal{H}_{k}:\ x_{T}=\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)x_{0}\Bigr\}

(5.40)

is Lagrangian.

Lemma 5.6
.

For each fixed
s
and
k
, the subspace
\Lambda_{s,k}(0)\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}
is Lagrangian.

Proof.

The graph of a symplectic map is Lagrangian in the product symplectic space
(\mathcal{H}_{k},\Omega)
.
∎

Lemma 5.7

(Limit of the regularized endpoint family)
.

Fix a mode
k\in\mathcal{K}
and a parameter value
s
such that

m(s,k)=k+A(s)\neq 0.

(5.41)

Then, as
\delta\downarrow 0
, the endpoint lines
\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)
and
\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)
converge to the corresponding APS endpoint lines:

m(s,k)>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)\to\{u=0\},\qquad\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)\to\{w=0\},

(5.42)

and

m(s,k)<0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)\to\{w=0\},\qquad\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s)\to\{u=0\}.

(5.43)

Proof.

If
m(s,k)\neq 0
, then

\alpha_{0}(s,k)=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Big)\longrightarrow\sign(m(s,k))\qquad(\delta\downarrow 0),

(5.44)

and
\alpha_{T}(s,k)=-\alpha_{0}(s,k)
. Substituting these limits into (
5.26
) yields the claim.
∎

5.3
The regularized boundary and the Maslov formalism

For fixed
s
,
k
, and
\delta>0
, let
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
denote the operator associated with (
5.24
) on
L^{2}([0,T],dt;\mathbb{C}^{2})
and the regularized endpoint conditions

\Dom(\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k})=\left\{x=\binom{u}{w}\in H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2}):\begin{array}[]{l}(1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))u(0)+(1-\alpha_{0}(s,k))w(0)=0,\\
(1+\alpha_{T}(s,k))u(T)+(1-\alpha_{T}(s,k))w(T)=0\end{array}\right\}.

(5.45)

Define the corresponding operator in the original mode variables by

D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\mathcal{U}.

(5.46)

At the global level,

D^{(\delta)}_{s}=\widehat{\bigoplus}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}D^{(\delta)}_{s,k},

(5.47)

but all arguments below are modewise.

Proposition 5.8

(Fixed-
s
regularized operator)
.

Fix
k\in\mathcal{K}
,
\delta>0
, and
s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
. Then
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
is a self-adjoint operator with separated boundary conditions associated with the mode differential expression (
5.24
).

Proof.

By (
5.34
), the operator with separated boundary conditions is symmetric exactly when its endpoint trace space is isotropic in
(\mathcal{H}_{k},\Omega)
. For fixed
s
, the regularized endpoint conditions define the trace space

\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)=\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)\oplus\Lambda_{k}^{T}(s),

(5.48)

which is Lagrangian by Lemma
5.5
. Hence, the operator is symmetric and maximally isotropic, therefore self-adjoint.
∎

Corollary 5.9

(Compact resolvent)
.

For each fixed
s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
, the operators
\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
and
D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}
have compact resolvent. In particular, their spectra are real, discrete, and consist of eigenvalues of finite multiplicity.

Proof.

The operator is first order on the compact interval
[0,T]
with
H^{1}
-domain and separated homogeneous boundary conditions. Hence the graph norm is equivalent to the
H^{1}
-norm on the domain, and the embedding

H^{1}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2})\hookrightarrow L^{2}([0,T];\mathbb{C}^{2})

(5.49)

is compact. Unitary equivalence preserves compactness of the resolvent and the spectrum.
∎

Lemma 5.10

(Continuity of the regularized mode family)
.

Fix
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\delta>0
. Then the maps

s\longmapsto\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s),\qquad s\longmapsto\Lambda_{s,k}(0)

(5.50)

are continuous in the Lagrangian Grassmannian of
(\mathcal{H}_{k},\Omega)
, and the corresponding self-adjoint family

s\longmapsto\mathcal{D}^{(\delta)}_{s,k}

(5.51)

it is continuous in the gap topology. Equivalently, the family

s\longmapsto D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}

(5.52)

is gap-continuous.

Proof.

The coefficients of (
5.24
) depend continuously on
s
through
m(s,k)=k+A(s)
. The boundary coefficients

\alpha_{0}(s,k)=\tanh\!\Big(\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Big),\qquad\alpha_{T}(s,k)=-\alpha_{0}(s,k)

(5.53)

therefore vary continuously in
s
, so
\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)
varies continuously. The same is true for the zero-mode propagator
\Phi_{s,k,0}(T)
and hence for its graph
\Lambda_{s,k}(0)
. Standard results for separated self-adjoint first-order systems on a compact interval then yield gap continuity; see, for example,
[
27
,
8
]
.
∎

Corollary 5.11

(Modewise spectral flow equals Maslov index)
.

Fix
k\in\mathcal{K}
and
\delta>0
. Then

\operatorname{SF}\bigl(\{D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\}_{s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]};0\bigr)=\mu_{\mathrm{Maslov}}\!\bigl(\Lambda_{s,k}(0),\,\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)\bigr).

(5.54)

Proof.

By Proposition
5.8
, Corollary

, and Lemma

, the family

is a continuous path of self-adjoint Fredholm operators in the standard separated-boundary setting. The usual spectral-flow/Maslov correspondence therefore applies; see, for example,
[
27
, Theorem 3.14]
.
∎

Remark 5.12

(Scope)
.

The spectral-flow/Maslov correspondence is used here only for the continuous regularized family

. It is not applied to the APS family across boundary zeros

, since the APS projector is discontinuous there.

5.4
Explicit zero-mode criterion and proof of the main theorem

We now compute the zero-mode crossing condition. By the previous subsections,

0\in\Spec\bigl(D^{(\delta)}_{s,k}\bigr)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\Lambda_{s,k}(0)\cap\Lambda_{\mathrm{bc},k}(s)\neq\{0\}.

(5.55)

Equivalently, there exists a nonzero solution of the zero-mode system satisfying the regularized endpoint conditions (
5.26
).

We repeatedly use the non-degenerate condition

\delta\neq\frac{2}{\ell(T)},\qquad\ell(T)=\int_{0}^{T}\frac{d\tau}{f(\tau)}.

(5.56)

Choose a nonzero spanning vector for the left endpoint line
\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s)
:

v_{0}(s,k)=\binom{1-\alpha_{0}(s,k)}{-(1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))}\in\Lambda_{k}^{0}(s).

(5.57)

Let

x(t;s,\lambda,k)=\binom{u(t;s,\lambda,k)}{w(t;s,\lambda,k)}

(5.58)

denote the unique solution of (
5.25
) with initial condition

x(0;s,\lambda,k)=v_{0}(s,k).

(5.59)

Define the scalar matching function

F(s,\lambda,k)=(1+\alpha_{T}(s,k))\,u(T;s,\lambda,k)+(1-\alpha_{T}(s,k))\,w(T;s,\lambda,k).

(5.60)

Then
F(s,\lambda,k)=0
if and only if the corresponding solution satisfies both regularized endpoint conditions.

Proposition 5.13

(Explicit zero-mode criterion for the regularized family)
.

Fix a mode
k
and write
m(s,k)=k+A(s)
. For
\lambda=0
, the matching function (
5.60
) is

F(s,0,k)=\Bigl(1-\tanh\!\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Bigr)^{2}e^{\,m(s,k)\ell(T)}-\Bigl(1+\tanh\!\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Bigr)^{2}e^{-\,m(s,k)\ell(T)}.

(5.61)

Equivalently,

F(s,0,k)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m(s,k)\Bigl(\ell(T)-\frac{2}{\delta}\Bigr)=0.

(5.62)

In particular, under (
5.56
),

F(s,0,k)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad m(s,k)=0.

(5.63)

Hence, zero modes of the regularized family occur exactly at the boundary zeros.

Proof.

At
\lambda=0
, the system decouples:

u(t)=u(0)e^{\,m(s,k)\ell(t)},\qquad w(t)=w(0)e^{-\,m(s,k)\ell(t)},

(5.64)

where

\ell(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d\tau}{f(\tau)}.

(5.65)

With

x(0;s,0,k)=v_{0}(s,k)=\binom{1-\alpha_{0}(s,k)}{-(1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))},\qquad\alpha_{0}(s,k)=\tanh\!\Bigl(\frac{m(s,k)}{\delta}\Bigr),

(5.66)

we get

u(T)=(1-\alpha_{0}(s,k))e^{\,m(s,k)\ell(T)},\qquad w(T)=-(1+\alpha_{0}(s,k))e^{-\,m(s,k)\ell(T)}.

(5.67)

Since
\alpha_{T}(s,k)=-\alpha_{0}(s,k)
, substitution into (
5.60
) gives (
5.61
). Using

\frac{1+\tanh z}{1-\tanh z}=e^{2z},

(5.68)

the equation
F(s,0,k)=0
is equivalent to

e^{2m(s,k)\ell(T)}=\left(\frac{1+\tanh(m(s,k)/\delta)}{1-\tanh(m(s,k)/\delta)}\right)^{2}=e^{4m(s,k)/\delta},

(5.69)

which is exactly (
5.62
). The last statement follows from (
5.56
).
∎

Corollary 5.14

(Transverse boundary zeros are isolated regularized crossings)
.

Assume (
5.56

be a boundary-zero for mode

Proof.

Proof of Theorem

Appendix A
Heun reduction of the radial Dirac equation

The main text uses only the following structural facts about the radial mode equation:
(i) after decoupling, it becomes a second-order scalar ODE,
(ii) under the explicit warped function
f(t)=e^{t}+\alpha e^{-t}
its coefficients become rational after
z=e^{t}
,
and (iii) the resulting equation has four
regular singular points
, hence belongs to the general Heun class.
We therefore do
not
need closed-form Heun solutions; what matters for the spectral problem is the singularity
structure and the associated Frobenius exponents.

Throughout, we fix a Fourier mode
k
and write.

m=k+A,\qquad f(t)>0,

(A.1)

where
f
is the warping function,
m\neq 0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the spectral parameter in
D\psi=\lambda\psi
.

Our goal in this appendix is to show the Heun reduction in a self-contained way:
§
A.1
decoupling of the first-order Dirac system to a scalar ODE,
§
A.2
Liouville substitution to remove the first-derivative term,
§
A.3
-§
A.4
rational
z
-equation and identification of its singular points,
§
A.5
-§
A.7
Frobenius exponents at
z=0
,
z=\pm i\sqrt{\alpha}
and
z=\infty
,
§
A.8
-§
A.9
Summary of the Heun classification and its role in the shooting formulation used in Section 3.3.

The singularity analysis and extraction of characteristic exponents uses the standard Frobenius method for
second-order linear ODEs with regular singular points, together with the usual reduction of the point

z=\infty
via
z=1/\zeta
; see, e.g., Ince
[
38
]
. Finally, the fact that a second-order equation
with four regular singularities belongs to the general Heun class is standard; see Ronveaux
[
39
]
.

A.1
Decoupled radial equation

We begin by decoupling the first-order radial Dirac system into a single scalar equation
for
u
, which will serve as the master ODE governing each Fourier mode. In
Section 3.3
we obtained the coupled system

\displaystyle v^{\prime}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}\,v+\frac{m}{f}\,v

\displaystyle=-\,i\lambda\,u,

(A.2a)

\displaystyle u^{\prime}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{2f}\,u-\frac{m}{f}\,u

\displaystyle=-\,i\lambda\,v.

(A.2b)

Eliminating
v
yields a second-order equation for
u
. Differentiating (
A.2b
) and using
(
A.2a
) to substitute for
v
and
v^{\prime}
gives

u^{\prime\prime}+\frac{f^{\prime}}{f}\,u^{\prime}+\Bigg[\lambda^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{f^{2}}+\frac{mf^{\prime}}{f^{2}}+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}}{2f}-\frac{(f^{\prime})^{2}}{4f^{2}}\Bigg]u=0.

(A.3)

This is the master scalar equation governing each Fourier mode of the bulk spectrum.

A.2
Liouville transformation

In this section, we remove the first-derivative term by the standard Liouville substitution
u=f^{-1/2}w
; to this end, we apply the Liouville substitution

u(t)=f(t)^{-1/2}\,w(t).

(A.4)

A direct computation shows that (
A.3
) is equivalent to the Schrödinger-type equation

w^{\prime\prime}+V(t)\,w=0,

(A.5)

where the effective potential is

V(t)=\lambda^{2}-\frac{m^{2}}{f(t)^{2}}+\frac{mf^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)^{2}}.

(A.6)

In particular, (
A.5
) isolates the singular structure coming from the warp factor
f
.

A.3
Exponential change of variables

Now we convert the equation into a rational-coefficient ODE via
z=e^{t}
.
The warp factor for our metric is

f(t)=e^{t}+\alpha e^{-t}\qquad(\alpha>0),

(A.7)

and introduce the exponential coordinate

z=e^{t},\qquad z\in(1,e^{T}).

(A.8)

Then

f(t)=z+\frac{\alpha}{z}=\frac{z^{2}+\alpha}{z},\qquad\frac{d}{dt}=z\frac{d}{dz}.

(A.9)

Under this change of variables, (
A.5
) becomes

\frac{d^{2}w}{dz^{2}}+\frac{1}{z}\frac{dw}{dz}+\frac{1}{z^{2}}\,Q(z)\,w=0,

(A.10)

where

Q(z)=\lambda^{2}-\frac{m^{2}z^{2}}{(z^{2}+\alpha)^{2}}+\frac{m(z^{2}-\alpha)}{(z^{2}+\alpha)^{2}}.

(A.11)

A.4
The
z
-equation and its singular points

After the Liouville substitution
u=f^{-1/2}w
and the change of variable
z=e^{t}
, the mode equation takes the form

w_{zz}+\frac{1}{z}w_{z}+\frac{1}{z^{2}}Q(z)\,w=0,

(A.12)

where
Q(z)
is the rational function displayed in (
A.11
). The singular points of
(
A.12
) are exactly the points where the coefficients fail to be analytic:
z=0
, the zeros of

z^{2}+\alpha
(namely
z=\pm i\sqrt{\alpha}
), and
z=\infty
. Each of these points is a
regular singular

point (see
Section A.5
-
A.7
below). Hence (
A.12
) is a Fuchsian
second-order equation with four regular singularities, which places it in the (general) Heun class.

Remark.
We will not need the explicit canonical Heun parameters in the main text. What matters for our purposes
is the singularity structure and the fact that the spectral problem can be handled via the shooting formulation in
Section 3.3
without invoking closed-form Heun solutions.

A.5
Frobenius analysis at
z=0

Here, we discuss the Frobenius exponents at
z=0
, which determine the local behavior of solutions
near the left endpoint
t\to-\infty
in
z=e^{t}
coordinate.

As
z\to 0
we have
z^{2}+\alpha=\alpha+O(z^{2})
, hence expanding
Q(z)
gives

Q(z)=\lambda^{2}-\frac{m}{\alpha}+O(z^{2}).

(A.13)

Therefore, the leading model near
z=0
is

w_{zz}+\frac{1}{z}w_{z}+\frac{1}{z^{2}}\Big(\lambda^{2}-\frac{m}{\alpha}\Big)w=0.

(A.14)

Substituting a Frobenius ansatz
w\sim z^{\rho}
yields the indicial equation

\rho(\rho-1)+\rho+\Big(\lambda^{2}-\frac{m}{\alpha}\Big)=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad\rho^{2}+\Big(\lambda^{2}-\frac{m}{\alpha}\Big)=0.

(A.15)

Hence, the characteristic exponents at
z=0
are

\rho_{0}^{\pm}=\pm i\sqrt{\lambda^{2}-\frac{m}{\alpha}}\qquad\text{when}\ \lambda^{2}-\tfrac{m}{\alpha}>0,

(A.16)

and

\rho_{0}^{\pm}=\pm\sqrt{\frac{m}{\alpha}-\lambda^{2}}\qquad\text{when}\ \lambda^{2}-\tfrac{m}{\alpha}<0

(A.17)

A.6
Frobenius analysis at
z=\pm i\sqrt{\alpha}

Let
z_{\ast}\in\{+i\sqrt{\alpha},-i\sqrt{\alpha}\}
and write
z=z_{\ast}+\zeta
. Since
z^{2}+\alpha
has a
simple zero at
z=z_{\ast}
, we have

z^{2}+\alpha=2z_{\ast}\,\zeta+O(\zeta^{2}),

(A.18)

We observe
\frac{1}{z^{2}}Q(z)
has a double pole at
\zeta=0
. In particular, each of

z=\pm i\sqrt{\alpha}
is a
regular singular point
. Writing
w\sim\zeta^{\rho}
and collecting the most
singular terms yield an indicial equation of the form

\rho(\rho-1)+p_{\ast}\rho+q_{\ast}=0,

(A.19)

where
p_{\ast},q_{\ast}
depend explicitly on
(m,\alpha)
through the principal part of the Laurent expansion of

Q(z)
at
z=z_{\ast}
. We record the corresponding characteristic exponents as

\rho_{\ast}^{\pm}=\frac{1-p_{\ast}}{2}\pm\sqrt{\Big(\frac{1-p_{\ast}}{2}\Big)^{2}-q_{\ast}}.

(A.20)

(Explicit expressions for
p_{\ast},q_{\ast}
can be obtained by a direct expansion of
Q
at

z=z_{\ast}
, but are not required for the spectral analysis in the main text.)

A.7
Frobenius analysis at
z=\infty

To analyze
z=\infty
, set
z=1/\zeta
and rewrite (
A.12
) in the variable
\zeta\to 0
.
Since
Q(z)=\lambda^{2}+O(1/z^{2})
as
z\to\infty
, the leading model is

w_{zz}+\frac{1}{z}w_{z}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{z^{2}}w=0,

(A.21)

which shows that
z=\infty
is again a
regular singular point
. The corresponding asymptotic behaviors are

w(z)\sim z^{\pm i\lambda}\qquad\qquad z\to\infty

(A.22)

This confirms that
z=\infty
is again a regular singular point and that solutions have
power-law/oscillatory behavior controlled by
\lambda
.

A.8
Heun classification

Equation (
A.12
) has four regular singular points

z=0,\qquad z=+i\sqrt{\alpha},\qquad z=-i\sqrt{\alpha},\qquad z=\infty.

(A.23)

A second-order Fuchsian ODE with four regular singular points is equivalent, after a Möbius change of variable and a
gauge transformation removing characteristic exponents, to a (general) Heun equation. This justifies describing the
mode equation as
Heun-type
and explains why closed-form expressions for the bulk spectrum are not expected in
general.

A.9
Role of the Heun reduction in the spectral analysis

The Heun classification is used here only to clarify the analytic complexity of the reduced radial equation. Our
spectral analysis does not require explicit Heun solutions: instead, we use the shooting formulation in
Section 3.3
, where eigenvalues are characterized as zeros of a scalar endpoint condition. The
Frobenius data above remain useful for

(i)

checking local behavior,

(ii)

designing stable numerical integration, and

(iii)

developing asymptotic regimes (e.g. large
|m|
or large
T
).

Appendix B
Complement: Gorokhovsky–Lesch gauge-conjugation picture

A complementary gauge-conjugation viewpoint.

This discussion is logically independent of the regularized APS/Maslov analysis above. Its purpose is to record a complementary gauge-conjugation interpretation for
local elliptic self-adjoint boundary conditions
preserved by the relevant unitary gauge transformation. It should therefore be read as a parallel viewpoint, not as a second proof of the regularized crossing results.

Consider a local elliptic self-adjoint boundary condition

\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{0},\mathcal{F}_{T}),\qquad\mathcal{F}_{0}\subset H^{1/2}(Y_{0};S_{Y_{0}}),\quad\mathcal{F}_{T}\subset H^{1/2}(Y_{T};S_{Y_{T}}),

(B.1)

such that the associated bulk Dirac operator is self-adjoint and Fredholm. Writing

\gamma:H^{1}(M;S\otimes E)\to H^{1/2}(Y_{0};S_{Y_{0}})\oplus H^{1/2}(Y_{T};S_{Y_{T}})

(B.2)

for the trace map, we set

\Dom(D_{A,\mathcal{F}})=\{\psi\in H^{1}(M;S\otimes E):\gamma(\psi)\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{T}\},

(B.3)

and denote by

D_{A,\mathcal{F}}:\Dom(D_{A,\mathcal{F}})\subset L^{2}(M;S\otimes E)\to L^{2}(M;S\otimes E)

(B.4)

the resulting self-adjoint Fredholm operator.

Let

\mathfrak{g}:S^{1}\to U(1)

(B.5)

be a smooth unitary, extended trivially in the
t
-direction to
M=[0,T]\times S^{1}
, and assume that
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathfrak{g}
-invariant:

\mathfrak{g}(\mathcal{F}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{T})=\mathcal{F}_{0}\oplus\mathcal{F}_{T}.

(B.6)

Then multiplication by
\mathfrak{g}
preserves
\Dom(D_{A,\mathcal{F}})
.

We fix
A_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. The Gorokhovsky-Lesch gauge-conjugation path is

D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}(\tau)=D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}+\tau\,\mathfrak{g}^{-1}[D_{A_{0}},\mathfrak{g}],\qquad\tau\in[0,1].

(B.7)

Since
\mathfrak{g}
depends only on
\theta
,

[D_{A_{0}},\mathfrak{g}]=i\gamma_{2}\,\frac{1}{f(t)}\,\partial_{\theta}\mathfrak{g},\qquad\mathfrak{g}^{-1}[D_{A_{0}},\mathfrak{g}]=i\gamma_{2}\,\frac{1}{f(t)}\,\mathfrak{g}^{-1}\partial_{\theta}\mathfrak{g},

(B.8)

so this is a bounded zeroth-order multiplication operator. Hence (
B.7
) is a norm-continuous path of self-adjoint Fredholm operators with common domain
\Dom(D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}})
, and

D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}(1)=\mathfrak{g}^{-1}D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{g}.

(B.9)

Let

P_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}).

(B.10)

Now we define the Toeplitz operator

T_{\mathfrak{g}}=P_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}\,\mathfrak{g}\,P_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}:\Ran(P_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}})\to\Ran(P_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}).

(B.11)

Under the hypotheses above,
T_{\mathfrak{g}}
is Fredholm, and the Gorokhovsky-Lesch theorem gives

\operatorname{SF}\bigl(\{D_{A_{0},\mathcal{F}}(\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}\bigr)=\ind(T_{\mathfrak{g}});

(B.12)

see
[
30
]
.

We now specialize to

\mathfrak{g}_{N}(\theta)=e^{iN\theta},\qquad N\in\mathbb{Z},

(B.13)

whose winding number is

\wind(\mathfrak{g}_{N})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{S^{1}}\mathfrak{g}_{N}^{-1}\,d\mathfrak{g}_{N}=N.

(B.14)

Equivalently,
\mathfrak{g}_{N}
represents the class
N\in K^{1}(S^{1})\cong\mathbb{Z}
.

In our cylinder model,

\mathfrak{g}_{N}^{-1}(\partial_{\theta}+iA)\mathfrak{g}_{N}=\partial_{\theta}+i(A+N),

(B.15)

so

D_{A+N}=\mathfrak{g}_{N}^{-1}D_{A}\mathfrak{g}_{N}.

(B.16)

If
\mathcal{F}
is also
\mathfrak{g}_{N}
-invariant, then

D_{A+N,\mathcal{F}}=\mathfrak{g}_{N}^{-1}D_{A,\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{g}_{N}.

(B.17)

Therefore, whenever

A(s_{2})-A(s_{1})=N\in\mathbb{Z},

(B.18)

the endpoint operators
D_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}
and
D_{A(s_{2}),\mathcal{F}}
are gauge-equivalent, and the GL theorem yields

\operatorname{SF}\bigl(\{D_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}(\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}\bigr)=\ind\!\bigl(P_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}\,\mathfrak{g}_{N}\,P_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}\bigr),

(B.19)

where

P_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}=\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(D_{A(s_{1}),\mathcal{F}}).

(B.20)

In the present circle model, this index depends only on the homotopy class of
\mathfrak{g}_{N}
, hence, only on the winding number
N
.

The GL construction is a
gauge-conjugation
computation for gauge-equivalent endpoint operators under local boundary conditions. It is therefore complementary to, but not literally the same path as, the parameter family
s\mapsto D_{A(s),\mathcal{F}}
. In particular, it applies when the endpoint shift is an integer and the chosen local elliptic self-adjoint boundary condition is preserved by the corresponding unitary
\mathfrak{g}_{N}
.

Example 1:
A(s)=s-\tfrac{1}{2}
,
s\in[0,1]
.

Here

A(1)-A(0)=\tfrac{1}{2}-(-\tfrac{1}{2})=1.

(B.21)

Hence one chooses

\mathfrak{g}_{1}(\theta)=e^{i\theta},\qquad\wind(\mathfrak{g}_{1})=1.

(B.22)

If
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathfrak{g}_{1}
-invariant, then

D_{A(1),\mathcal{F}}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}^{-1}D_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{g}_{1},

(B.23)

and

\operatorname{SF}\bigl(\{D_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}(\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}\bigr)=\ind\!\bigl(P_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\,\mathfrak{g}_{1}\,P_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\bigr).

(B.24)

Example 2:
A(s)=3s-\tfrac{1}{4}
,

.

Here

(B.25)

Hence one chooses

\mathfrak{g}_{3}(\theta)=e^{i3\theta},\qquad\wind(\mathfrak{g}_{3})=3.

(B.26)

If
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathfrak{g}_{3}
-invariant, then

D_{A(1),\mathcal{F}}=\mathfrak{g}_{3}^{-1}D_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\mathfrak{g}_{3},

(B.27)

and

\operatorname{SF}\bigl(\{D_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}(\tau)\}_{\tau\in[0,1]}\bigr)=\ind\!\bigl(P_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\,\mathfrak{g}_{3}\,P_{A(0),\mathcal{F}}\bigr).

(B.28)

Example 3:

,

.

The cutoff excludes endpoint boundary zeros and therefore guarantees endpoint invertibility. In this case,

(B.29)

Hence one chooses

(B.30)

If
\mathcal{F}
is

and

(B.32)

Appendix C
Numerical implementation and plots

Proposition C.1

(Liouville/Wronskian identity)
.

For any fundamental matrix
\psi(t)
we have

f(t)\,\det\psi(t)=\textup{constant in }t.

(C.1)

Equivalently, for any two solutions
\psi^{(1)}=(u_{1},v_{1})
and
\psi^{(2)}=(u_{2},v_{2})
,

f(t)\,\bigl(u_{1}(t)v_{2}(t)-u_{2}(t)v_{1}(t)\bigr)

(C.2)

is
t
-independent.

Proof.

Liouville’s formula gives

\frac{d}{dt}\det\psi(t)=\mathrm{tr}\bigl(A(t;\lambda)\bigr)\det\psi(t).

(C.3)

In our system,

\mathrm{tr}\bigl(A(t;\lambda)\bigr)=(-q+p)+(-q-p)=-2q(t)=-\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}.

(C.4)

Thus,
\frac{d}{dt}\log\det\psi(t)=-\frac{f^{\prime}(t)}{f(t)}
, hence
\det\psi(t)=C/f(t)
and
f(t)\det\psi(t)=C
is constant.
∎

Numerical illustration of Proposition
C.1
.

To complement
C.1
, we plot in
Figure 5
the numerical deviation of the conserved quantity

f(t)\det\psi(t)

(C.5)

from its exact constant value. Here
\psi(t)
is the fundamental matrix of the first-order system, normalized by
\psi(0)=I
, so the exact constant is

f(0)\det\psi(0)=f(0)=2

(C.6)
