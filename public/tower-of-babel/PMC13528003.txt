PD-L1 copy number loss is associated with lower survival in women with advanced breast cancer in Indonesian cohort
Authors: Ramadhan Karsono, Muhammad Al Azhar, Anggana Dyah Utari, Adhitya Bayu Perdana, Ermita Rizki Umaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic value of PD-L1 copy number variation (CNV) in advanced breast cancer (BC) patients remains unclear. This study investigated the association between PD-L1 CNV and its prognostic significance in advanced BC.MethodsPD-L1 CNV was analyzed using real-time PCR in advanced BC patients (n = 132) who received chemotherapy or hormonal therapy, along with a non-malignant control group (n = 5) obtained from patients with histopathologically confirmed fibroadenoma used as reference controls. The association of PD-L1 CNV with overall survival and clinicopathological features was determined.ResultsPD-L1 CNVs were identified in 22% (29/132) of advanced BC cases, comprising 9 CN losses and 20 CN gains. PDL-1 CN loss was associated with low overall survival (OS) (HR = 2.61; 95% CI = 1.10–6.21; p = 0.024) and remained significant after multivariate adjustment (HR = 3.13; 95% CI = 1.23–7.94; p = 0.016). The adverse prognostic effect was pronounced in HR-positive (p = 0.004) and HER2-negative patients (p = 0.040). PD-L1 CNVs were more frequent in patients > 40 years (p = 0.027) and HER2-negative tumors (p = 0.019).ConclusionsPD-L1 CN loss was associated with lower OS, particularly in HR(+) and HER2(–) patients. This result revealed the prognostic value of PD-L1 CNV in Indonesian advanced BC who received primary systemic therapy.Keywords: Breast cancer, PD-L1, Copy number variation, Chemotherapy, Hormonal therapy

Introduction

Breast cancer remainsone of the most prevalent and challenging malignancies worldwide, with advanced stages presenting substantial hurdles in terms of treatment outcomes and patient survival [1]. Indonesia, as the archipelago countries with limited resources, faces a high incidence rate of breast cancer, accumulated to 30.1% of all cancer cases in 2022 [1]. Furthermore, disparities in healthcare access including the diagnostic challenges across regions affect the timely detection and management of breast cancer [2]. Despite the high prevalence of breast cancer in Indonesia, the prognostic significance of PD-L1 in breast cancer is still debated and not conclusively established, largely due to subtype differences, methodological heterogeneity, and inconsistent clinical correlations. In recent years, immunotherapy has emerged as a promising treatment, particularly with the development of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand, programmed cell death protein ligand 1 (PD-L1) [3, 4]. In breast cancer, the combination of ICIs and chemotherapy has demonstrated clear clinical benefits, especially in early TNBC patients, with pembrolizumab improving pathological complete response (pCR) rates by 13.6% in KEYNOTE-522 [5].

Although immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) provide significant clinical benefits, only patients with PD-L1-positive tumors are likely to respond to the therapy [5]. Consequently, PD-L1 assessment has been commonly used to predict response to ICI. However, the prognostic value of PD-L1 expression in breast cancer remains inconsistent. While some studies report a positive association between PD-L1 expression and improved clinical outcomes, others suggest it correlates with poorer survival or show no significant prognostic link [6]. A meta-analysis found that PD-L1 expression was associated with worse overall survival (OS) (p = 0.002; HR = 1.63; 95% CI: 1.07–2.46). Apart from that, higher PD-L1 gene expression predicted better survival in the full cohort (HR = 0.82; 95% CI 0.74‑0.90). Collectively, the prognostic value is not consistently demonstrated in PD-L1 expression in breast cancer. Apart from that, the predictive value of PD-L1 expression in the treatment of TNBC is still controversial, as tumours with negative PD-L1 expression still show a positive response to ICI [7]. The utility of PD-L1 as a predictive biomarker in the rest of BC subtypes also remains unclear.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) remains the gold standard for assessing PD-L1 expression across various types of cancer [8, 9]. However, its clinical application is limited by several challenges, including the selection of the appropriate antibody clone and evaluation cut-off, tissue availability, intratumoral heterogeneity, and the absence of standardized protocols for PD-L1 staining and tissue processing [8, 9]. These limitations highlight the need for more robust and reliable immunotherapy biomarkers [10, 11]. In this context, PD-L1 copy number variation (CNV) also has emerged as a potential biomarker with prognostic significance [8–11].

CNV refers to the amplification (gain) or deletion (loss) of specific genomic regions, which may lead to altered gene expression levels [12, 13]. Studies have shown that PD-L1 CNV was associated with PD-L1 expression, and the PD-L1 copy number has increasingly been recognized as an important marker in determining response to ICI [14–17]. PD-L1 gene amplification is frequently associated with positive responses to nivolumab (anti-PD-L1) therapy in Hodgkin lymphoma patients [18]. PD-L1 copy number gains have also been identified as a predictive biomarker for NSCLC treated with nivolumab [19]. In breast cancer, PD-L1 amplification and copy number gains have been shown to predict the response of Durvalumab in metastatic breast cancer [20].

Several studies have shown the association between PD-L1 CNV and prognosis in various cancer types [8, 9, 11, 21, 22]. Previous studies have provided only limited insights into the prognostic significance of PD-L1 in advanced breast cancer, and their findings have been inconsistent, indicating a need for more comprehensive evaluation. Despite its potential, the prognostic significance of PD-L1 CNV has also been scarcely explored, with limited studies conducted in Indonesia. To address this issue, we evaluated the prognostic significance of PD-L1 CNV and clinicopathological features in advanced breast cancer patients at Dharmais Cancer Hospital (National Cancer Center in Indonesia).

Materials and methods

Study population

In this retrospective study, a total of 132 breast cancer tissue and 5 non-malignant breast tissue samples were collected. To minimize potential selection bias, eligible cases were retrieved consecutively from the institutional biobank based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Only patients diagnosed with advanced disease (stage IIIB or IV) were included in this study. Tumor tissue samples were obtained during diagnostic biopsy procedures prior to the initiation of systemic therapy. After diagnosis, patients received chemotherapy or hormone therapy as their primary systemic treatment according to standard clinical practice. Detailed clinicopathological data associated with each sample were obtained from the Dharmais National Cancer Center’s pathology reports and medical records. All patients agreed to participate in the experiment by signing an informed consent form. Ethical approval was obtained from the Dharmais National Cancer Center institutional review board with approval number: DP.04.03/11.5/078/2024.

Tissue collection and storage

All specimens were fresh-frozen tissues retrieved from the biobank of Dharmais Cancer Hospital – National Cancer Center, Jakarta, Indonesia. The tissues were originally collected during diagnostic biopsy or surgical procedures. At the time of collection, a small portion of each tumor sample was divided into two parts: one was reserved for histological analysis in the Department of Anatomical Pathology at Dharmais Cancer Hospital, while the other portion was immediately placed in cryotubes containing 1mL RNAlater and stored at − 80 °C for future molecular analyses.

Non-malignant reference samples

Non-malignant tissue specimens were used as a reference control for CNV analysis. Non-malignant breast tissue specimens were obtained from patients undergoing surgical excision for fibroadenoma mammae at Dharmais National Cancer Center. The diagnosis of fibroadenoma was confirmed through histopathological examination by the Department of Anatomical Pathology. Only tissues confirmed to be benign and free of malignant features were included. These specimens were archived in the institutional biobank and used as reference control samples for copy number analysis.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included in this study if they had (1) histologically confirmed breast cancer, (2) advanced-stage disease (stage IIIB or IV), (3) available fresh frozen tumor tissue stored in the Dharmais Cancer Hospital biobank, and (4) complete clinicopathological data. Samples were excluded if the tissue quantity was insufficient for DNA extraction, if the DNA quality did not meet the requirements for real-time PCR analysis, or if the corresponding clinical or pathological data were incomplete. Sample quality was assessed during DNA extraction and quality control procedures prior to PD-L1 CNV analysis.

Tumor classification and immunohistochemical staining

The immunohistochemical staining analysis was performed by the Department of Anatomical Pathology, Dharmais National Cancer Center for tumor classification. Tumor grading was performed using the Scarff–Bloom–Richardson (SBR) system. For statistical analysis, grades were grouped into two categories: low grade (SBR grades I and II) and high grade (SBR grade III). HR positivity was defined as ER and/or PR expression > 1% of tumor nuclei. HER2 positivity was defined as IHC 3 + or IHC 2 + with gene amplification confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH); HER2 negativity was defined as IHC 0 or 1+. Advanced breast cancer was defined by the presence of Lymph node metastases in stage IIIB (T4N0-N2M0) and stage IV (T0-T4N0-N3M1) was identified by the presence of metastases in lung X-ray, bone scanning, or abdominal ultrasound.

PD-L1 copy number variation analysis

Total DNA was extracted from tissue samples using a DNeasy Blood and Tissue kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA yield and purity were measured using NanoDrop Spectrophotometer 2000 (Thermofisher Scientific, Waltman, MA, USA). DNA samples were analyzed for PD-L1 copy number using TaqMan Copy Number Assay. Real-time PCR was performed using a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystem, Foster City, CA, USA). A single reaction consisted of 18 ng of genomic DNA, TaqMan Genotyping Master Mix (Applied Biosystem), and PD-L1 TaqMan copy number assay (Hs03704252_cn) duplexed with TaqMan Copy Number Reference Assay Human RNase P. The thermal cycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min, according to the manufacturer’s protocol [23]. Copy numbers were calculated using CopyCaller V2.0 software based on the DDCt method, which analyzed the gene target copy number normalized with RNase P. The non-malignant breast tissue samples were used as reference calibrators for copy number normalization in the real-time PCR assay. A PD-L1 copy number > 2.5 was defined as copy number gain; a PD-L1 copy number < 1.5 was defined as copy number loss; and a PD-L1 copy number 1.5 to 2.5 was defined as copy number normal.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v22. The Chi-square test was used to assess the association between PD-L1 copy number and clinicopathological features. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time of diagnosis until death from any cause and was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. The prognostic value of the PD-L1 copy number on OS was analyzed using the Cox Proportional Hazard model.

Correlations type of PD-L1 CNV with overall survival of advanced breast cancer patients

All non-malignant control samples had PD-L1 copy number normal (2 copies), with copy number values ranging from 1.8 to 2.1. For breast cancer patients, 20 patients were categorized as PD-L1 CN gain, and 9 patients were PD-L1 CN loss. We observed a significant association between the PD-L1 copy number loss and the overall survival of advanced breast cancer patients. The median survival of PD-L1 CN loss was 14 months, significantly lower than the median OS of PD-L1 copy number normal (28 months) (p = 0.024) with a hazard ratio (HR) = 2.61 (95% CI 1.14–8.71) (Fig. 1). The association was also statistically significant in the subgroup of HR(+) patients (p = 0.004). Normal CN in HR(+) was associated with reduced odds of the event compared to CN loss HR = 0.215 (95% CI 0.08–0.58). Similarly, in HER2(-) was also significantly associated with outcome (p = 0.042) with normal CN associated with lower odds of the event compared to CN loss HR = 0.297 (95% CI 0.11–0.79) (Fig. 2). The overall survival of PD-L1 CN loss was still significant after adjusting to all variables of clinicopathological features using multivariate analysis (adjusted p-value = 0.016; HR = 3.13 (1.23–7.94)) (Table 2).

Discussion

Alterations in DNA copy number are closely linked to tumor progression and may serve as important prognostic biomarkers. Previous studies have reported that PD-L1 copy number (CN) gains occur frequently in bladder, head and neck, cervical, ovarian, and colorectal cancers, whereas PD-L1 CN losses are commonly observed in NSCLC and melanoma [24]. In this study, we found that PD-L1 copy number gain was more frequent in advanced breast cancer, occurring in 20 patients, while CN loss was observed in 9 patients. Although no significant association was observed between PD-L1 CNV and disease stage in our cohort (Table 3), possibly due to the limited sample size and the restriction of our study to advanced-stage patients (IIIB and IV) only, PD-L1 CN alterations may still have potential as a biomarker for patient survival stratification, as demonstrated by the significant association with overall survival in our study.

We showed that PD-L1 copy number loss was highly associated with lower survival compared to PD-L1 CN normal in all patients (Fig. 1). The result was also observed in a subset of HR(+) and HER2(-) patients (Fig. 2A and D). This finding was supported by a previous study by Hong et al. [11], which showed that PD-L1 CN loss was significantly associated with lower OS and PFS in patients with advanced NSCLC. PD-L1 CN loss was also significantly associated with decreased PD-L1 tumor staining and negative response to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy in non-squamous NSCLC [25]. Therefore, PD-L1 CN loss may be utilized as a biomarker to predict response to ICI therapy in advanced breast cancer.

On the other hand, PD-L1 CN gain did not show any significant correlation with survival in advanced BC patients. However, in a subset of HR(-) patients, gain in PD-L1 copy number tends to have higher survival compared to PD-L1 CN normal, although the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 2B). A previous report showed that amplification of PD-L1 CN has increased the response rate to nivolumab therapy significantly improved the survival of advanced NSCLC. However, several studies found that PD-L1 gain was associated with poor prognosis. In a study by Inoue et al. [21], PD-L1 CN gains were associated with lower survival in NSCLC. A study by Loharamtaweethong et al. [22] showed that PD-L1 amplification was associated with lower recurrence-free survival. Further studies with more samples with HR(-) are warranted to conclude the association between PD-L1 CN gains and survival in advanced BC.

A previous report showed that cancers with low PD-L1 CN were phenotypically cold, as indicated by reduced expression of some immune modulators, including IL-6, MMP9, IL-1B, IL-10, transforming growth factor, and IFN-g [9]. It is well-known that most “cold tumor” was associated with the ineffectiveness of ICI therapy [26]. PD-L1 CN loss may contribute to creating the cold tumor microenvironment (TME). It has been shown that PD-L1 CN loss may diminish JAK/STAT signaling in the TME, leading to reduced expression of some immunoregulatory genes and creating a cold TME [9].

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study conducted at Dharmais Cancer Hospital in Jakarta, Indonesia. The results may not be generalizable to populations in other settings. Second, the number of breast cancer patients with HR(+) and HR(-), as well as HER2(+) and HER2(-), was relatively small. Thus, the range of confidence interval (95% CI) in the hazard ratio is large in several subsets of patients. More samples with positive and negative HR and HER2 status should be increased to get more valid and accurate results. Third, this study did not include patients treated with ICI and thus did not reveal the prognostic and predictive value of PD-L1 CNV to ICI treatment. However, the result of this study showed the potential of PD-L1 CNV as an independent prognostic biomarker to predict overall survival. Additionally, several reports have shown the potency of PD-L1 CNV in predicting response to ICI therapy. Therefore, further study is warranted to investigate the potential of PD-L1 CNV as a predictive biomarker for ICI treatment, especially in HR(+) and HER2(-) advanced BC.

Conclusion

PD-L1 copy number loss is highly associated with lower survival among patients with advanced breast cancer receiving primary systemic therapy, which remains significant after multivariate analysis. PD-L1 CNV was also associated with lower survival in HR(+) and HER2(-) advanced breast cancer. PD-L1 CNV was most frequent in patients older than 40 years and HER2(-). This result revealed the prognostic significance of PD-L1 CNV in advanced breast cancer patients.
