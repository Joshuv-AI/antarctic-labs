Retrospective case-control study of pre-diagnosis observational and prescription data in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Patrick Doherty, Susan Duty, Gareth Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There are currently no disease-modifying treatments for Parkinson’s disease. Retrospective analysis of routinely collected health data may enable earlier risk detection and identify therapeutic candidates for drug repurposing. We conducted a retrospective case–control study using data from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink. Individuals diagnosed with Parkinson’s disease (n = 37,910) were matched by age, sex, and general practice to 113,730 controls. Biometric measures and prescription histories were analysed across two pre-diagnostic windows (5–10 years and 10–20 years before diagnosis). Logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between biomarkers, medication exposure, and Parkinson’s disease incidence. A composite risk score was constructed and assessed using receiver operating characteristic analysis. Individuals who developed Parkinson’s disease showed significant deviations in established vascular, metabolic, and inflammatory markers years before diagnosis, including elevated systolic blood pressure, serum urea, and creatinine. The combined risk model demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy (area under the curve = 0.73). Previously reported contrasting associations of salbutamol and propranolol with Parkinson’s disease incidence were confirmed. Hormonal therapies and triptan medications were associated with lower disease incidence but were more frequently prescribed to individuals with more favourable biometric risk profiles, suggesting potential confounding. In contrast, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists were prescribed to individuals with higher underlying risk yet were associated with reduced disease incidence. Integration of biometric and prescription histories improves interpretation of medication–disease associations in population data. These findings support glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists as candidates for further investigation in Parkinson’s disease based on observational associations and demonstrate the value of real-world data for early identification of population-level risk patterns and therapeutic discovery in neurodegenerative disease.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurological condition after Alzheimer’s disease (AD). Unfortunately, dopamine replacement therapy1 does not address the progressive neuronal loss or many of the non-motor symptoms including the later development of dementia2. Like AD, PD is characterized by a complex pathogenesis involving a combination of neurodegeneration, protein misfolding3, inflammation4, and mitochondrial dysfunction5. In a similar fashion, multiple weak genetic associations with risk have been discovered6, with evidence for a degree of commonality across neurodegenerative conditions7. The environmental8 and lifestyle risk factors9 associated with late onset are also common across neurodegenerative diseases. Notably, lifestyle factors such as regular physical exercise have been proposed as potential interventions to modify disease progression, supported by growing evidence of their neuroprotective effects and benefits for motor and cognitive function in PD and AD10,11. In this context PD12 and AD13 may be more accurately described as syndromes. This multifactorial landscape of PD has prompted interest in alternative strategies for the discovery of novel therapeutics, particularly drug repurposing, where approved medications with known safety profiles are explored for efficacy in new indications14,15. Retrospective analyses of prescription histories and disease incidence can guide drug repurposing while also highlighting the relevance of a drug’s known targets. For instance, salbutamol was identified as a potential protective agent against PD through analysis of the Norwegian NorPD prescription database, suggesting a possible role for its adrenergic targets in modulating disease risk16.

The Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD)17 is a powerful platform for epidemiological research providing curated observational and prescription data for nearly 19 million alive and currently registered with a general practice that is actively contributing data (over 60 million patients historically) across the UK. CPRD has supported investigations into dementia risk in relation to inflammatory biomarkers18, type 2 diabetes (T2D)19, and prescription history20. Using the CPRD we conducted a retrospective case-control study to examine pre-diagnosis observational and prescription data from individuals who went on to develop PD and a matched control cohort. Our analysis aimed to identify key pre-diagnostic associations and clarify the association of specific drug classes with PD incidence.

Our analysis revealed that variations in cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, and inflammatory factors are associated with future PD incidence, paralleling patterns observed in AD. By combining biometric data with prescription histories, we were able to probe the extent to which observed associations may reflect underlying risk profiles versus the potential protective effects of medications. For example, we found that biometric factors mediated the apparent association between certain drug classes, such as hormonals, and PD risk, while also strengthening the repurposing case for GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA). The latter finding aligns with growing evidence suggesting a potential disease-modifying role for GLP-1RAs in neurodegeneration21–26. This dual analysis helps differentiate correlation from possible causation and supports the development of hypotheses around new intervention strategies. However, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the associations reported here. Rather, such findings serve to motivate more controlled investigations, including prospective studies and clinical trials.

Methods

Cohort

This study utilized routinely collected data from CPRD (Aurum database version as of September 2023), comprising individuals aged 50 years or older who received a first diagnosis of PD between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2022. This ten-year diagnostic window reduces the influence of long-term secular changes in clinical recording and prescribing practices. Cases were matched with controls by age, sex, and primary care general practice at the index date, defined as the date of first recorded PD diagnosis for cases, with the same date assigned to matched controls. An average of three controls was selected per case, see Table 1. Data was made available under license from CPRD. PD diagnoses were identified using SNOMED CT Concept/Description IDs 49,049,000/81,717,011; 49,049,000/1,230,677,014; 49,049,000/2,920,648,011; 49,049,000/1,230,678,016; 49,049,000/1,230,679,012; and 715,345,007/3,302,310,019. The codes serve as the mandatory clinical terminology standard for the NHS (digital.nhs.uk/services/terminology-and-classifications/snomed-ct), ensuring consistent and accurate recording of patient data. While no independent validation of PD diagnoses was performed, potential variability in coding accuracy and clinician expertise is acknowledged and we mitigate against differences in diagnosis rates by matching controls on clinical practices as well as age and sex. However, use of SNOMED CT within the electronic health record system supports consistency in diagnostic capture across practices.

For the observation-based PD risk prediction, missing data were imputed based on a random selection from data in the same category. Missingness threshold was set at 40% resulting in 9 observations (in addition to age at index date and sex) for which 60% of the data points were complete. A PD risk score was generated using the ranger random forest R package (www.rdocumentation.org/packages/ranger). The model was tested based on a 70/30 training/testing data split, and statistics based on multiple runs with different splits and imputation. The distribution of observables over the entire PD and control cohort was visualised using a 2-dimensional Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)27 using the uwot R package (www.rdocumentation.org/packages/uwot). PD risk prediction performance was assessed by ranking individuals according to their PD prediction score and aggregating the number of true PD cases above each score threshold. The true positive rate (sensitivity) was then plotted against the false positive rate (1–specificity) to generate the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under this curve (AUC) was used as a summary measure of model discrimination. Multiple independent random imputation runs were performed, and the ROC AUC statistic reported reflects the average and standard deviation across these runs.

Observations

Observational data were collected over two retrospective periods: 5 to 10 years and 10 to 20 years prior to the index date. Biometric variables included in the analysis were restricted to those with the highest recording frequency in CPRD, to ensure robust population-level inference and minimise the impact of missing data. Values were averaged within each of the two periods to reduce the influence of short-term variability. By excluding the five years immediately preceding diagnosis, these windows were chosen to minimise the impact of prodromal disease processes and reverse causation. Observations falling outside two standard deviations from the mean were excluded as outliers to improve data quality.

The dataset included 54 routinely recorded observational measures, such as systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and multiple plasma or serum biomarkers. Not surprisingly, these measures varied in population coverage; for example, SBP was recorded in a substantially larger proportion of individuals compared to laboratory values such as serum albumin.

To maintain the integrity of the matched case–control design, individuals without a complete match on age at PD diagnosis, sex, and primary care general practice were excluded from the relevant biometric analysis.

Medication

Medication exposure was defined by prescription events for any medication listed in the British National Formulary (BNF) (www-medicinescomplete-com.apollo.worc.ac.uk/#/browse/bnf), comprising 1,364 distinct drugs. All prescription variants of a given BNF type are pooled. Consistent with the observational data, two different time ranges prior to index were analysed, thereby pooling prescription data from 5 to 10 and from 10 to 20 years prior to diagnosis.

Analysis of associations

Associations between biometric measures and PD status were quantified using Cohen’s d effect sizes. Association between drug prescriptions and PD incidence were assessed using odds ratios derived from logistic regression models. Prescription status for each drug was treated as a binary variable, defined by the presence of at least one prescription within the relevant observation period.

For medications predominantly prescribed to one sex (defined as a greater than tenfold difference in prescription frequency between men and women) analyses were restricted to the relevant sex. To adjust for individual differences in overall healthcare engagement and prescribing patterns, the total number of prescriptions was included as a covariate in the regression models.

To address the possible confounding of biometric risk with prescription type we collected the first prescription date for the given medication classes in the non-PD cohort. We matched individuals with a medication, with those without, based on having the same age at prescription date, sex and primary care general practice. Average levels of the observational measures were calculated for periods up to five years prior to first prescription. The observation levels prior to prescription in the two groups were compared and a sensitivity analysis with match group randomisation was implemented to test for robustness. Inferential interpretation was restricted to pre-specified drug classes with prior biological plausibility, and no global multiple-testing correction was applied.

Results

Biometric observations

Analysis of the biometric measures associated with increased risk of developing PD revealed an overall picture very similar to that seen in our previous study for AD28. The most significant associations are displayed in Fig. 1.

These associations span cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, and inflammatory biomarker domains. These measures were therefore combined into a multivariable predictive model, as described in the Methods. To this end and as described in the Methods section, we built a random forest predictive model for PD incidence based on these biometric data. The measures had different coverages across the cohort and we built a data matrix from measures with a minimum 60% coverage. With this 40% missingness, we obtained a matrix covering 50,705 individuals across nine observables: SBP, DBP, Serum potassium level, Serum albumin level, Serum creatinine level, Serum urea level, Neutrophil count, Serum sodium level, Serum alkaline phosphatase level. Note, the last two observables in this list did not fall into the top significances for individual measures, Fig. 1.

The 2-dimensional UMAP plot, Fig. 2, shows broad overlap of the PD (Case) and non-PD (Control) cohorts across these nine key measures at the 5 to 10 year prior index date, showing a complex and overlapping disease signature. The performance of the predictive model is shown as the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve for multiple training/testing runs (Fig. 2). The model showed moderate predictability, with the ROC area under curve (AUC) of 0.73 ± 0.003 and an Area Under Precision Recall (AUPR) score of 0.67 ± 0.006. Based on random forest categorical assignments the model has accuracy: 0.67 ± 0.002, sensitivity: 0.64 ± 0.005, and specificity: 0.69 ± 0.004, which further indicated moderate predictability of the model. Notably, the biometric associations observed here closely mirror those reported in our earlier study of AD using the same CPRD framework28. In that analysis, systolic and diastolic blood pressure showed strong positive associations with future AD incidence across long pre-diagnostic windows. Notably, for systolic blood pressure, the effect sizes were 0.89 and 0.36 for the 10–20 and 5–10 year pre-diagnostic windows, respectively, with similarly directed but attenuated effects for diastolic blood pressure. Serum albumin also exhibited a consistent negative association with future AD incidence (d = − 0.27 and − 0.34 across the same windows). These quantitative similarities support the interpretation that the vascular and metabolic associations identified here reflect broadly shared systemic correlates detectable long before clinical diagnosis, rather than disease-specific effects. By way of comparison, a prediction scheme based on the levels of 20 out of a possible 1536 blood borne proteins, measured over an entire 15-year period prior to PD diagnosis, returned an ROC AUC of 0.829.

Prescriptions

We next looked at relative drug prescription frequencies prior to index in the case and control cohorts. The results recapitulate the findings of Mittal et al.16, with salbutamol being less frequently prescribed in the PD cohort both 5 to 10 and 10 to 20 years prior diagnosis, see Table 2. We also observed opposite associations of salbutamol and propranolol with PD incidence, pointing to a target-based effect as salbutamol is an agonist and propranolol an antagonist of the same β2 adrenergic receptor. Here, Mittal et al.16 propose a direct effect of β2 agonists on SNCA whereas Fletcher et al.30 based the proposed repurposing of salbutamol for PD on its driving up the expression of striatal fibroblast growth factor 20 (FGF20).

Furthermore, nicotine and varenicline, relevant in the context of asthma and smoking, also show negative associations. Finally, we confirm established neuroprotective pathways, as evidenced by the strong negative association for the retinoid drug tretinoin.

A possible confounder in the observed association between salbutamol use and reduced PD incidence is smoking, which is itself associated with lower PD risk and a higher likelihood of being prescribed respiratory medications31. In this context, we also find that nicotine appears to be associated with a lower PD incidence. Moreover, the partial nicotinic receptor agonist and smoking-cessation medication varenicline is strongly negatively associated with PD incidence, further supporting a potential protective effect of nicotinic signalling. These findings are consistent with the established inverse association between smoking and PD risk.

In addition, beclomethasone, an alternative asthma medication with an indirect corticosteroid-based inflammation suppressive activity and no direct β2-adrenergic agonist effects, also appears to be associated with lower PD incidence, see Table 2. However, the effect size here is much lower than that linking salbutamol with reduced risk, suggesting a potential additional benefit from salbutamol in PD, above and beyond that of its link with asthma. Taken together, these observations suggest that the reduced PD incidence observed for salbutamol may, at least in part, reflect confounding by smoking-related factors rather than a direct protective pharmacological effect. In this context, we have previously shown that nicotine is protective in a rat model of PD32.

Interestingly, retinoids have also emerged as potential therapeutic candidates in AD33, and we observe a similarly consistent negative association for tretinoin, the most commonly prescribed retinoid.

As with our study of prescription rates in AD, we observed that hypertension medications (HT) (Angiotensin Converting Enzyme (ACE) inhibitors and Calcium Channel Blockers (CCB)) are associated with significantly higher PD incidence, see Table 3, reflecting vascular health as being a key risk factor for both conditions. Also, in line with our AD results we find that GLP-1RAs, hormonal drugs and the triptan migraine medication consistently associated with lower PD incidence, see Table 3.

The Associations between PD incidence and the prescriptions of anti-hypertensive medications (HT), hormonal drugs, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and triptans.

To address possible confounding effects of biometric status prior to medication we looked at biometric data in the context of future drug prescription within the Control cohort in the five years pre index, see Methods. As a positive control, we observed high SBP and DBP prior to the prescription of anti-hypertensives, explaining the higher PD incidence in those on anti-hypertensives, see Table 4. Interestingly, until recently GLP-1RAs have been prescribed exclusively for T2D, which is an established PD risk factor34–36. Our data recapitulates this observation that GLP-1RAs tend to be prescribed in populations with biometrics associated with higher PD risk (e.g. increased SBP and DBP), see Table 3. The negative association of GLP-1RAs use with PD incidence is therefore highly significant and our results highlight GLP-1RAs as prioritised candidates for hypothesis-driven follow-up studies. In contrast, both hormonals and triptans appear to be prescribed in individuals with lower SBP and DBP. Given that low BP is associated with lower PD incidence, these data suggest that the positive associations of both hormonal and triptan medication with reduced PD risk may be explained by cohort differences already apparent prior to prescription, rather than the medications themselves being associated with reduced PD risk.

Discussion

In this study we gathered prescription and observation data from CPRD for individuals going on to develop PD together with age, sex and primary care general practice matched controls. Our findings highlight vascular and inflammatory factors driving PD, in agreement with previous reports37,38 and show a biomarker and mechanistic overlap between PD and AD39–43. Notably, hypertension is a reported AD risk factor44–46, albumin levels show a negative correlation with brain beta-amyloid depositions47. Dysregulation of glucose metabolism48, and inflammatory pathways49 have also been reported in AD. These shared mechanisms highlight the possible benefits of preventive measures, such as blood pressure control, metabolic regulation, and promotion of physical activity, not only in reducing dementia risk but also in delaying or attenuating PD onset. However, given evidence that the prodromal phase of PD may extend for more than a decade, these long-range associations may partially reflect early disease processes rather than antecedent risk factors.

We further show that moderate predictive performance can be achieved using routinely recorded data from primary care, providing a quantitative indication of the level of discrimination that can be achieved within this setting. As such, the analysis should be regarded as exploratory rather than as a clinically deployable predictive tool. Its purpose is to characterise the upper limits of predictability within the constraints of routinely collected primary-care data.

The present findings therefore highlight the informational value of practical, low-cost clinical data already available in primary care, while also motivating future work that combines improved data completeness, longitudinal modelling, and integration with richer biomarker information to enhance predictive performance.

Given the extent and likely non-random nature of missing biometric data in routine records, the predictive modelling should be viewed as exploratory, and model performance estimates interpreted with appropriate caution. While the present model was based on window-averaged observations, future work may benefit from explicitly modelling interactions and temporal trajectories of clinical measures prior to diagnosis. Sequence-aware approaches could potentially improve discrimination, particularly in settings with denser longitudinal data, and represent an exciting direction for further methodological development.

While such overlap highlights the potential relevance of modifiable systemic factors, including blood pressure control, metabolic regulation, and physical activity, the observational nature of the data does not permit inference that intervention on these factors would delay or attenuate PD onset. The associations observed between vascular or inflammatory measures and future PD incidence therefore represent systemic correlates rather than evidence of direct mechanistic drivers of neurodegeneration. Accordingly, these findings are best viewed as hypothesis-generating and intended to inform future longitudinal and mechanistic investigations rather than to establish causal pathways.

The apparent protective effect of hormonal drugs on PD risk appears to be confounded by their prescription patterns. A possible explanation could be that hypertension has been a contraindication for hormone replacement therapy50–54. In this context the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS) on post 65 year old women showed no decreased risk of AD in postmenopausal women prescribed hormonals, reviewed by Coker et al.55.

We also found triptan migraine medications to be associated with a lower PD risk. However, our results show that triptans tend to be prescribed to those with lower biometric risk of developing PD, implying the drugs themselves have no protective effects. However, there have been reports of protective effects of zolmitriptan in a rat model of PD56 and sumatriptan in a mouse stroke model57, so further scrutiny is required here.

Our observational data highlight GLP-1RAs as candidates for further investigation in PD. These drugs are historically prescribed for T2D which is a risk factor for both PD and AD. Accordingly, we found that GLP-1RAs were prescribed at higher rates to control cohorts whose biometric measures, including higher SBP and DBP, were associated with higher PD (and AD) risk. These associations are notable given that GLP-1RAs are preferentially prescribed to individuals with an elevated baseline PD risk profile associated with T2D. However, they do not establish a protective, disease-modifying, or causal effect and may reflect residual confounding by indication, comorbidity, or early disease processes. The findings should therefore be regarded as exploratory signals that help prioritise GLP1-RAs for further mechanistic and longitudinal study, rather than as evidence of therapeutic benefit, particularly in light of the recent negative phase III exenatide trial results58. However, positive results have emerged for liraglutide in the context of AD, with the drug sparing grey matter and limiting ventricle growth in phase 2 year long trial of AD patients26. The recent licensing of GLP-1RAs as weight loss drugs59 may thus positively impact dementia incidence in the future, and our analyses suggest that these associations warrant further investigation in the context of PD.

Combining observational and prescription data can help in predicting the repurposing potential of drugs for complex neurodegenerative conditions. Initial broad-brush analyses can guide more focused future studies, enabling better balancing of risk factors across cohorts and thereby strengthening potential causal links between drug use and disease incidence. In particular, analyses of drugs differentially prescribed according to PD risk, determined by biometric measures, would benefit from matching controls on overall biometric risk using the predictive schemes described above.

In conclusion, it is important to emphasise that all associations reported here arise from an observational, retrospective design and remain subject to substantial residual confounding, including confounding by indication and comorbidity. Accordingly, the associations described should be interpreted as signals of interest suitable for hypothesis generation and prioritisation, rather than as evidence of causal or protective effects.

Study limitations

As with most retrospective observational studies, causal inferences cannot be drawn from the associations reported here, which should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of causal effects. For example, as shown here, the negative association with PD incidence of hormonal drugs and anti-migraine medication may be confounded by their higher prescription rates in those with biometric profiles falling in the lower PD risk group. In contrast to the analysis of biometric factors, where one is comparing numerical measures, the analysis of medication incidence is complicated by being categorical, where a null assignment for a given medication may be due to the lack of data, rather than a person being medication naive. We have mitigated against this by controlling for overall prescription frequency but ideally would balance the case and control groups on general overall medication load. More generally, exposure was defined based on recorded prescription events rather than cumulative dose or duration of use, as reliable long-term information on adherence, persistence, and cumulative exposure is inconsistently available in routine primary care records, particularly over retrospective windows extending up to two decades. Accordingly, the medication associations identified here should be interpreted as exposure signals suitable for prioritisation and hypothesis generation, rather than as evidence of dose-dependent or causal effects.

The identification of PD cases relied on diagnostic coding within electronic health records and was not independently clinically validated. Although the use of SNOMED CT supports consistency in diagnostic recording across primary care practices, it does not guarantee diagnostic accuracy. Consequently, diagnostic misclassification, including secondary or drug-induced parkinsonism, cannot be excluded and represents an additional source of uncertainty. Any such misclassification would be expected to attenuate, rather than spuriously inflate, long-range associations.

The inclusion of negative control outcomes or exposures that represent conditions or medications with minimal biological or clinical overlap with PD propensity would provide valuable falsification tests to further assess residual confounding in the observed associations. Due to data access constraints and the scope of the present study, implementation of appropriate negative controls was not feasible. We now explicitly acknowledge this limitation and identify the incorporation of negative controls as an important next step for future analyses aimed at strengthening causal interpretation. Finally, the prolonged prodromal phase of PD can predate clinical diagnosis by up to two decades11. Some pre-diagnostic features or medication patterns may therefore represent early disease manifestations rather than antecedent risk factors. This overlap between prodromal and diagnostic phases is a common challenge in retrospective and case–control studies of PD and underscores the need for longitudinal validation of potential early markers.
