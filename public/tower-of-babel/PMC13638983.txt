Attention guided fair artificial intelligence modeling for skin cancer diagnosis
Authors: Mingcheng Zhu, Mingxuan Liu, Han Yuan, Yilin Ning, Zhiyao Luo, Tingting Zhu, Nan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) has shown promise in dermatology, offering accurate and non-invasive diagnosis of skin cancer. While extensive research has addressed skin-tone bias, gender bias in dermatologic AI remains underexplored, potentially perpetuating diagnostic disparities. In this study, we developed LesionAttn, an algorithm designed to mitigate gender bias by directing model attention toward lesions, thereby mirroring clinicians’ diagnostic focus. Combined with Pareto Frontier optimization for dual-objective model selection, LesionAttn balances gender fairness and diagnostic performance. Validated on two large-scale dermatologic datasets for binary malignancy classification, LesionAttn significantly mitigated gender bias while maintaining high diagnostic performance, outperforming existing bias-mitigation algorithms. Our study demonstrates that explicitly guiding model attention to medically essential features provides a practical approach to advance both performance and fairness in dermatologic AI. By leveraging clinical priors to bridge the gap between human expertise and algorithmic optimization, this study demonstrates a feasible pathway for developing equitable and reliable diagnostic tools.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Skin cancer poses a major global health concern, with more than 6.7 million malignant cases reported annually worldwide. Its incidence has risen sharply over the past two decades1,2, highlighting the need for efficient and accurate diagnostic tools. Conventional diagnosis often relies on invasive biopsies that are time-consuming and uncomfortable for patients3. In response, Artificial Intelligence (AI) has emerged as a transformative solution, enabling non-invasive skin cancer diagnosis directly from dermoscopic images4. These models now achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to or exceeding that of expert dermatologists5 and are increasingly integrated into clinical practice6,7.

Despite their promise of accurate diagnosis, dermatologic AI models remain vulnerable to algorithmic bias, raising concerns about unequal care across patient subgroups. Prior research has primarily focused on disparities across racial and skin tone groups, showing that models trained predominantly on lighter skin often underperform on darker tones8–13. These skin tone-related biases risk exacerbating existing inequities in dermatologic outcomes14. In contrast, gender bias in AI-based skin cancer diagnosis remains relatively underexplored, perhaps because gender-specific differences, unlike visible variations in skin tone, are more subtle and less readily recognized15,16.

Gender disparities in skin cancer diagnosis may stem from both biological and behavioral differences. These include variations in skin texture, hormonal and vascular characteristics17,18, as well as gendered patterns in sun protection and skin care practices19,20. Such factors could lead to uneven performance of AI models across genders, perpetuating diagnostic inequities. Specifically, models may learn to rely on these gender-related features present in the background skin as shortcuts, rather than focusing on the disease-specific features21. Yet, few studies have systematically investigated and mitigated gender bias in dermatologic AI.

The effectiveness of existing bias mitigation algorithms in skin cancer diagnosis remains uncertain. Most were developed and validated on non-medical datasets and may not generalize to dermatologic image analysis9,14,22, where domain-specific challenges such as variable lighting, occlusion, and ill-defined lesion boundaries complicate bias mitigation23. They also usually lack interpretability, modifying model parameters in ways that are difficult to trace or explain, which limits clinical acceptance and trust24. Moreover, many achieve fairness at the expense of diagnostic accuracy (for example, by enforcing similarity in latent feature distributions across demographic subgroups), thereby discarding clinically meaningful information25–27. Such trade-offs pose a major barrier to the clinical translation of bias mitigation techniques in dermatologic AI.

To address this gap, we developed LesionAttn, a gender-equity-aware algorithm designed to direct neural network attention specifically toward clinically relevant lesion regions. This approach leverages a learnable attention mechanism that dynamically assigns spatial weights to input images, thereby prioritizing diagnostic features while actively suppressing background noise that may encode gender-related bias. In addition, we employ Pareto Frontier optimization during model training to balance diagnostic performance and fairness. By utilizing clinician-annotated lesion masks to guide spatial attention during model design, LesionAttn reduces reliance on spurious background features and mitigates gender bias without sacrificing performance. These findings offer an interpretable and clinically grounded approach to mitigating gender bias in dermatologic AI and underscore the potential of using clinical priors like annotation to advance both fairness and reliability in clinical settings.

Results

In this study, we proposed LesionAttn, a gender-equity-aware algorithm that guides attention toward clinically relevant lesion areas while optimizing both performance and fairness via Pareto Frontier optimization (Fig. 1). Further details regarding the LesionAttn algorithm are elaborated in the Methods section. LesionAttn was evaluated against existing fairness algorithms across two large-scale public dermatologic datasets—the Human Against Machine dataset (HAM)28 and the BCN20000 dataset (BCN)29 for the binary classification of malignant versus benign skin lesions. We further compared LesionAttn with a static lesion-masking approach, highlighting the importance of maintaining spatial context for both diagnostic performance and fairness.

Throughout this study, gender disparity (bias) was primarily quantified using equalized odds (EO), defined as the larger disparity between groups in either true positive rates (TPR) or false positive rates (FPR). We also denoted the difference in TPR between male and female groups as EO[TP], and the difference in FPR as EO[FP]. Model performance was evaluated using the areas under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) and precision-recall curves (AUPRC) for diagnostic performance and EO for fairness.

Gender bias mitigation using LesionAttn

The Residual Attention Neural Network (RANN) model, based on the widely adopted convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture in skin cancer diagnosis30–32, displayed marked gender bias in both internal (HAM testing set) and external (BCN dataset) cohorts. The RANN model achieved high discrimination performance on HAM (AUROC: 0.903 [0.897–0.909]; AUPRC: 0.698 [0.668-0.727]) and moderate performance on the BCN (AUROC: 0.723 [0.696–0.750]; AUPRC: 0.763 [0.742–0.784]) datasets. The corresponding ROC and PRC curves are provided in Suppl. Fig. 1. However, as shown in Fig. 2A, in both datasets, the male subgroup exhibited a significantly higher TPR and lower TNR (i.e., 1-FPR), with statistical significance compared to the female subgroups.

Disparity in the baseline model and comparison of five bias mitigation methods across datasets.

A Gender disparity of the baseline model on HAM and BCN datasets. The fairness is evaluated by comparing the difference in TPR and FPR between male and female groups. B Comparison of bias mitigation methods. Lower values of Equalized Odds indicate lower gender bias.

Critically, the RANN model yielded a substantial clinical discrepancy in the number needed to screen (NNS), defined here as the reciprocal of positive predictive value (PPV). This metric estimates the number of alerted patients the models need to flag to identify one true positive case. In HAM dataset, the discrepancy in NNS was 0.32 (1.16 for female patients vs. 1.32 for male patients), while in BCN data, the discrepancy in NNS was 0.91 (1.96 for female patients vs. 2.87 for male patients).

In the HAM dataset, LesionAttn reduced EO by 40.4% (from 0.109 [0.068–0.150] to 0.065 [0.041–0.089]; p = 0.041), while achieving an AUROC of 0.909 [0.904-0.913] and AUPRC of 0.719 [0.696–0.742], higher than the baseline model. In the BCN dataset, LesionAttn reduced EO by 23.5% (from 0.162 [0.146–0.177] to 0.124 [0.102–0.145]; p = 0.016), and improved diagnostic performance (AUROC: 0.730 [0.712–0.747]; AUPRC: 0.766 [0.749–0.782]). Notably, LesionAttn mitigated the discrepancy in NNS, reducing the gender gap from 0.32 to 0.08 in HAM dataset (1.15 for female patients vs. 1.23 for male patients) and from 0.91 to 0.60 in BCN dataset (1.93 for female patients vs. 2.53 for male patients).

We also compared LesionAttn against four established bias mitigation methods: Reweighting33, LAFTR22, ADNet14, and ShorT9, using the same model architecture of RANN. Further implementation details are provided in the Methods section. LesionAttn consistently achieved the highest diagnostic performance and fairness, across both internal (HAM testing set) and external (BCN dataset) cohorts. As shown in Fig. 2B, in the HAM dataset, LesionAttn yielded the numerically lowest mean EO of 0.065 [0.041–0.089], outperforming other bias mitigation methods on average across random seeds (Reweighting: 0.091 [0.068–0.114], p = 0.016; LAFTR: 0.090 [0.052–0.128], p = 0.197; ADNet: 0.099 [0.076–0.123], p = 0.086; ShorT: 0.077 [0.043–0.111], p = 0.295), with comparable diagnostic performance. Although the 95% CIs between LesionAttn and bias-mitigation approaches like ShorT overlap on the HAM dataset, LesionAttn consistently demonstrated an average numerical improvement in fairness across independent runs. In the BCN dataset, although all bias mitigation methods achieved better diagnostic performance relative to the baseline, only LesionAttn improved the fairness (LesionAttn: 0.124 [0.102–0.145]; Reweighting: 0.165 [0.136–0.194], p = 0.016; LAFTR: 0.178 [0.163–0.194], p = 0.001; ADNet: 0.180 [0.171–0.188], p = 0.008; ShorT: 0.154 [0.140–0.167], p = 0.037) with statistical significance.

Impacts of attention-based training

To assess the effect of attention-guided training, we examined attention maps from LesionAttn, other bias mitigation methods, and the baseline model. Spatial alignment of models’ attention and the reference lesion regions was quantified using intersection over union (IoU). As shown in Fig. 3 (left panel), LesionAttn achieved the highest median IoU (0.802) and the lowest variability (standard deviation [SD]: 0.199) over the testing set, outperforming other bias mitigation methods and the baseline (Reweighting: median 0.590, SD 0.281; LAFTR: median 0.243, SD 0.270; ADNet: median 0.578, SD 0.288; ShorT: median 0.214, SD 0.249; baseline: median 0.421, SD 0.278).

Higher heatmap intensity indicates greater attention to one pixel, while lower intensity indicates less attention to one pixel. In the image columns, (−) denotes a pathologically confirmed benign lesion, and (+) denotes a pathologically confirmed malignant lesion.

As shown in Fig. 3 (right panel), attention patterns varied across bias mitigation methods. LAFTR typically highlighted non-lesion regions in its attention maps (i.e., bright areas for the lesion regions and dark areas in the non-lesion regions). Similar attention patterns also occasionally appeared in Reweighting, ADNet, ShorT, and the baseline, particularly for benign lesions in male patients (i.e., true negative cases). In contrast, LesionAttn consistently concentrated attention on lesion areas, regardless of gender or malignancy status, suggesting a more clinically aligned model reasoning.

In addition, restricting the image to lesion regions alone was not as effective as the attention guidance employed by LesionAttn. We compared LesionAttn with a variant that replaced the learnable attention map with static lesion masks, termed LesionOnly. As shown in Fig. 4, LesionOnly achieved low EO values in both datasets at the cost of markedly lower diagnostic performance. In HAM, LesionOnly yielded an AUROC of 0.621 [0.591–0.657] and an AUPRC of 0.271 [0.253–0.290], far below both the baseline and LesionAttn. Despite LesionOnly’s reduction to EO (0.073 [0.064–0.081]) compared to the baseline model, it was still outperformed by LesionAttn. In BCN, LesionOnly significantly reduced gender disparity (0.063 [0.052-0.074]), but collapsed to an AUROC of 0.537 [0.450–0.490] and an AUPRC of 0.586 [0.547–0.626]. These results indicate that hard cropping reduced gender bias but destroyed the spatial context essential for accurate diagnosis.

Sensitivity analysis for the hyperparameter ρ that controls the soft-guidance mechanism confirmed the attenuation of background noises is critical for maintaining diagnostic capability (Suppl. Fig. 2). Compared with LesionOnly (ρ=0), diagnostic performance improved significantly even with a minimal weight assigned to the background (e.g., ρ=0.02). The final LesionAttn model was trained using the optimal hyperparameter identified via grid-searching (i.e., ρ=0.7). We observed a broad range of ρ<1 consistently outperformed the baseline model regarding bias mitigation, demonstrating the algorithmic robustness of our approach.

Discussion

While AI shows promise in early skin cancer diagnosis, its fairness across gender subgroups remains insufficiently studied. In this study, we introduced LesionAttn, an attention-guided algorithm that utilizes expert lesion annotations to guide spatial focus during model training and incorporates gender fairness in model selection. Using two large-scale dermatologic datasets, LesionAttn effectively mitigated algorithmic gender bias while maintaining strong diagnostic performance, compared to the baseline model unmitigated for bias. This study also highlights that aligning model attention with annotations from clinicians can enhance both model performance and fairness in dermatologic AI, underscoring the value of domain knowledge as a key driver of equitable and reliable diagnostic tools.

We found that the baseline model consistently exhibited lower TPRs in female patients across both internal and external cohorts. Because delayed diagnosis of melanoma can lead to rapid disease progression and worse prognosis, prioritizing high TPR is critical for triage and AI-assisted early diangosis34,35. These disparities suggest that female patients may suffer from a higher risk of missed or delayed diagnoses, raising concerns about the equitable deployment of AI models in dermatology. We hypothesized that this gender bias is primarily driven by misdirected model attention. As shown in Fig. 3, the baseline model frequently focuses on background healthy skin rather than the lesion itself when not explicitly guided36–38. Background skin inherently encodes subtle gender-specific cues due to biological variations (e.g., hair density, skin texture, vascularity)17,18 and behavioral differences (e.g., sun exposure patterns, skincare products)19,20. When the baseline model over-relies on these non-lesion regions, it inadvertently exploits these gender markers as predictive shortcuts, leading to the observed gender bias.

LesionAttn directly addresses this spurious correlation. By explicitly aligning model focus with the actual lesion regions used by clinicians, the framework prevents the model from accessing demographic information hidden in the background. Consequently, the model’s reasoning is grounded in the morphological features of the tumor, effectively mitigating gender bias while preserving diagnostic accuracy.

This reliance on intrinsic disease morphology, rather than idiosyncratic background artifacts, may be central to the framework’s superior generalizability. As illustrated in Fig. 2, existing bias-mitigation methods often failed to translate fairness gains during external evaluation due to their dependence on dataset-specific features. In contrast, the robust performance of LesionAttn across geographically distinct cohorts underscores its reliability and readiness for real-world clinical deployment.

Among the comparative bias mitigation methods, ShorT improved fairness while preserving performance by discouraging the model from exploiting visual shortcuts9. As shown in Fig. 3, its attention maps were more diffusely distributed, likely reducing bias by minimizing dependence on spurious background cues of demographics36,39. However, ShorT offers no positive guidance on where the model should focus for its diagnosis, as the model learns only what to avoid rather than what to prioritize, thereby limiting clinical interpretability. In contrast, LesionAttn adopted a fundamentally different and clinically grounded strategy by explicitly directing model attention to lesion regions. This approach not only proved more effective but also provided clear insight into where and how the model achieved its fairness status.

This study highlights the potential of integrating clinical knowledge (e.g., the localization of disease-relevant features) into AI model design to advance both fairness and accuracy. However, our comparative analysis reveals that the method of integration is critical. While hard-cropping images to strictly isolate the lesion (i.e., the LesionOnly variant) yields better fairness, it results in a catastrophic loss of diagnostic performance. This degradation underscores the necessity of maintaining contextual features rather than enforcing rigid attention constraints40. Clinically, the transition zone between a lesion and the surrounding normal skin is critical for assessing border irregularity and edge fading, key indicators of malignancy that are lost when the diagnostic boundary is removed41,42. Computationally, binary masks introduce artificial, high-frequency gradients that might disrupt feature extraction in convolutional networks, which are highly sensitive to such stark edges21. LesionAttn strikes this balance through the soft-guidance of attention, preserving spatial relationships while learning to prioritize medically relevant areas43. Future work may further optimize the integration of information across both background skin and lesion regions to refine these synergistic effects.

While LesionAttn reduced the EO on the external BCN dataset by 23.5% (from 0.162 to 0.124), the remaining disparity remains non-trivial. This translates to differential clinical screening burdens: the NNS to identify one malignancy was still 1.93 for female patients, compared to 2.53 for male patients. Notably, this higher screening burden for males occurred despite their significantly higher malignancy prevalence (58.6% vs. 42.8%), suggesting that male patients may undergo more “unnecessary” evaluations per case identified. These observations suggest that the residual disparity may be influenced by non-visual demographic and epidemiological factors. As shown in Table 1, in the BCN cohort, male patients are older on average (59.1 vs. 54.1 years) and likely presented with more complex skin textures29. Since LesionAttn intervenes exclusively on visual spatial attention, it is not designed to correct for these non-visual, demographic imbalances. Furthermore, the persistence of bias during external validation underscores that fairness is not a static model property but is context-dependent; therefore, local algorithmic auditing and site-specific mitigation still remain essential when deploying such frameworks across diverse clinical environments13.

While LesionAttn demonstrates robust performance, it currently necessitates lesion annotations during the training phase. This requirement introduces an annotation burden and inherently couples the framework’s efficacy to the quality of expert-defined masks7,28. Relying heavily on human-defined boundaries also carries a risk of supervision bias, where the model may inadvertently inherit human “blind spots” or subjective diagnostic patterns44. To mitigate the potential supervision bias, our soft-guidance mechanism allows the model to retain critical contextual features rather than strictly cropping to the annotated region, thereby reducing the impact of potential edge-case inaccuracy in the masks. Looking forward, future work could explore the integration of weakly supervised learning to generate high-fidelity pseudo-masks. Transitioning toward such automated algorithms would significantly reduce the reliance on costly human expertise and facilitate the scaling of this framework to larger, more diverse clinical datasets.

There are limitations to this study. First, while we acknowledge non-binary gender identities, the datasets used were annotated only with binary gender labels, limiting the granularity of subgroup analysis. Second, while our study demonstrates the efficacy of LesionAttn using the RANN architecture, the evaluation was conducted on a single model backbone. While the proposed fairness-enhancing mechanism is designed to be architecture-agnostic, further research is required to validate its generalizability across diverse architectures. Finally, we observed fluctuation in fairness across random seeds for all models, a phenomenon likely attributable to the inherent noise within medical imaging datasets. Future work involving larger, more diverse cohorts may help further stabilize the fairness against stochastic training variations.

This study reveals gender bias in AI-based skin cancer diagnosis and demonstrates that aligning model attention with clinically relevant lesion regions can mitigate these disparities without compromising diagnostic accuracy. LesionAttn exemplifies how incorporating clinical expertise into algorithm design promotes fairness, interpretability, and clinical reliability in dermatologic AI. Furthermore, the underlying principle of attention-guidance holds significant potential for broader application across other medical imaging domains to develop more equitable and trustworthy AI diagnostic tools.

Methods

Dataset

In this study, we focused on the binary classification of malignant versus benign skin lesions from dermoscopic images5,45. The HAM dataset comprises 10,015 dermoscopic images collected over a two-decade period from the Medical University of Vienna (Austria) and a dermatology clinic in Queensland (Australia)28. The BCN dataset comprises 19,424 dermoscopic images acquired between 2010 and 2016 at the Hospital Clinic in Barcelona, of which 12,411 images with a confirmed outcome of interest were included in this study29. Both datasets cover various skin conditions. In HAM, basal cell carcinoma (BCC), melanoma (MEL), and actinic keratoses/intraepithelial carcinoma (AKIEC) were labeled as malignant. In BCN, BCC, MEL, actinic keratoses (AK), and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) were considered malignant. All other included conditions were categorized as benign.

To provide the clinical guidance for LesionAttn, we utilized official lesion segmentation masks from the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive for both the HAM and BCN datasets7,28. These masks were curated and validated by expert dermatologists associated with the original dataset releases. The ground-truth diagnoses followed a rigorous hierarchy of evidence: histopathology for malignant cases, and expert consensus or longitudinal follow-up for benign cases. To ensure high-quality annotation, images were manually reviewed by multiple dermatologists, incorporating metadata such as anatomical site and patient age to account for clinical complexity and diagnostic nuances7,28.

Demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by gender are summarized in Table 1. In both datasets, male patients were older on average and had higher malignancy rates than female patients. To assess model robustness and generalizability, we conducted both internal and external validations. HAM was randomly split into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% internal testing sets strictly at the patient level. The entire BCN dataset was reserved for external testing to evaluate performance in an independent clinical setting. As HAM and BCN were collected from entirely different institutions and geographic locations, there is no patient-level overlap between the internal training data and the external testing cohort.

Residual Attention Neural Network (RANN) for skin cancer diagnosis

The RANN model is based on the widely adopted CNN paradigms in skin cancer diagnosis30–32 and additionally incorporates attention maps into a residual learning framework. By embedding attention maps within a residual structure, the model can dynamically refine feature representations, providing a robust but simple-implementation demonstration of attention-driven dermatology AI. We trained the RANN model following established protocols for AI-based skin cancer classification30–32.

LesionAttn algorithm for bias mitigation

We proposed LesionAttn to improve gender fairness in skin cancer diagnosis while preserving diagnostic performance. This bias mitigation approach employs two key mechanisms (Fig. 1B): (1) soft-guided attention alignment with lesion annotations, and (2) Pareto Frontier optimization.

To guide the model’s attention to clinically relevant lesion regions, we maximize the alignment between attention maps and lesion masks using cosine similarity. Model selection in fairness-aware modeling often involves balancing between competing objectives of maximizing diagnostic performance while minimizing algorithmic bias25,26. To address this, LesionAttn employed the Pareto Frontier algorithm46, a widely used approach in multi-objective optimization, to identify models representing optimal trade-offs between the objectives. Unlike approaches that use a single weighted composite loss, which forces a predetermined trade-off between gender fairness and model performance25,26, the Pareto Frontier algorithm preserves the independence of both objectives46. This also allows the training to explore models that simultaneously achieve high diagnostic performance and fairness without imposing artificial weighting.

We define the Pareto Frontier as the set of non-dominated model checkpoints during the training process, where an improvement in gender fairness cannot be achieved without a concurrent reduction in diagnostic accuracy, as visualized in Fig. 1B. The formed boundary acts like the “outer edge” of the eligible models regarding Pareto-optimality. It represents the superior trade-off between competing metrics and effectively excludes any candidate models that are objectively inferior in these two dimensions. Specifically, we simultaneously maximized diagnostic performance (Ppred, measured by AUROC) and fairness (Pfair, quantified by 1−EO). A model f is considered as Pareto-optimal (i.e., lying on the Pareto frontier) if there exists no other model f′ that performs as well as f in both objectives and strictly better in at least one:

with at least one strict inequality.

As the Pareto frontier is dynamically updated throughout training, a deterministic rule was applied to select the final model for evaluation. We selected the last model checkpoint that resided on the Pareto frontier upon the completion of training. Notably, we implemented a 10-epoch early stopping protocol, which means the training is terminated if no new non-dominated model checkpoints are discovered for 10 consecutive epochs. This deterministic approach ensures that the final selected model benefits from the most mature state of network weight convergence, while still representing a mathematically optimal trade-off between predictive performance and fairness.

Attention analysis

To examine the attention behavior, we extracted and visualized attention maps from RANN models trained with the baseline, LAFTR, ADNet, ShorT, and LesionAttn. Attention maps were obtained from the final attention layer and overlaid on the input images to compare the models’ regions of focus. Quantitatively, the attention-lesion alignment was measured using the Intersection over Union (IoU). To enable a standard IoU calculation between the continuous soft-attention map and the binary ground-truth lesion mask, we applied a dynamic, proportion-based binarization procedure. For each image, we calculated the positive pixel ratio (Pr) of the ground-truth lesion mask. We then binarized the model’s soft-attention map by assigning a positive label to the top Pr fraction of pixels with the highest attention weights, and a negative label to the rest. The standard IoU was then calculated between this binarized attention map and the ground-truth mask, ensuring the metric evaluated the spatial ranking of the model’s focus while controlling for absolute lesion size.

For illustration, we randomly selected four cases from the HAM testing set for examples: a benign lesion in a male patient, a benign lesion in a female patient, a malignant lesion in a female patient, and a malignant lesion in a male patient, respectively.

Experiment settings

To evaluate the effectiveness of LesionAttn, we compared it with four established bias mitigation methods (Reweighting, LAFTR, ADNet, and ShorT) under the same experimental settings. All models shared the same RANN architecture to ensure consistency in comparison. Additionally, to isolate the effect of learnable attention, we constructed a LesionOnly variant of RANN, in which input images were cropped using binary lesion masks rather than learned attention maps. This setup enabled assessment of whether static lesion focus alone could reproduce the fairness improvement achieved by LesionAttn.

All models underwent hyperparameter optimization via grid search, using AUROC on the validation set as the selection criterion. Hyperparameter ranges and final settings are detailed in the Suppl. Table 1. A sensitivity analysis of the hyperparameter ρ, which regulates the soft-guidance of attention, was conducted to evaluate the algorithmic robustness of LesionAttn.

Each experiment was repeated five times with different random seeds, and results are reported as mean values with 95% confidence intervals. To evaluate performance improvements, we conducted a paired one-sided t-test across the experimental seeds, with p < 0.05 defined as the threshold for statistical significance.
