Fate induction through asymmetric T cell division is modulated by chimeric antigen receptor costimulatory domains
Authors: Caitlin S Frazee, Sisi Chen, Corbett T Berry, Casey S Lee, Reid A Banciella, Lucianna Ngo, David Shon, Patrick J Herman, Jake S Fischman, Chloe L Leff, Anna Chen, Yvonne Kuo, Alexander A Shestov, Andre R Kelly, Bakir Valentic, Michael C Milone, Roddy S O’Connor, Christoph T Ellebrecht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy can result in impressive remissions in hematological malignancies, but relapse is common because a minority of infused cells acquire a long-lived, memory-like state. All US Food and Drug Administration-approved CAR T cell products incorporate either 4-1BB or CD28 costimulatory domains, each conferring distinct phenotypic outcomes, but how these signals control early fate decisions is incompletely understood. Here we show that costimulatory domains control memory fate acquisition through asymmetric cell division. CD28 CAR T cells have higher CAR surface expression and enhanced surface proteome asymmetry after the first division, yet paradoxically they show muted transcriptional, epigenetic and metabolic divergence between daughter cells, correlating with reduced long-term persistence. Conversely, 4-1BB CAR T cells have less surface polarization but more pronounced transcriptional, metabolic and epigenetic divergence, yielding proximal effector-prone and distal persistence-prone daughters. Integrating surface proteomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics and chromatin accessibility, we show how costimulatory domains tune asymmetric cell division to shape CAR T cell fate, providing a mechanistic foundation for optimizing cellular immunotherapy.Subject terms: Translational immunology, Cancer immunotherapy

Main

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapies have yielded remarkable outcomes in several hematological malignancies1 and refractory autoimmune disorders2,3, although long-term disease control remains inconsistent across patients4. Durable control depends on long-term CAR T cell persistence, sustained by the formation of long-lived memory T cells that provide ongoing immunosurveillance5–11. Asymmetric cell division (ACD) has emerged as one key mechanism of the divergence between effector and memory T cell fates12–15: upon activation, a parental T cell produces two distinct daughters—a proximal, target-facing daughter that inherits the immunological synapse and adopts a glycolytic, effector-like phenotype, and a distal daughter that is more quiescent, oxidative and memory like. This phenomenon was recently demonstrated in human CAR T cells16. How different, clinically relevant costimulatory domains engineered in CAR constructs, including 4-1BB and CD28, influence these early fate decisions remains poorly understood.

The 4-1BB-based CARs often show prolonged persistence with remissions sustained over 10 years in some patients17–19, whereas CD28-based CARs frequently yield rapid expansion and cytotoxicity but may predispose cells to reduced persistence and earlier exhaustion8,20–22. Mechanistically, 4-1BB signaling promotes oxidative or fatty acid metabolism, anti-apoptotic pathways and noncanonical nuclear factor κ-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) activity, along with increased human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II and interleukin 21 (IL-21) axis genes, whereas CD28-based CARs foster a more glycolytic, effector-like phenotype with higher expression of exhaustion markers18,23,24.

Here we integrate an adapted target-induced, molecular proximity-labeling method (LIPSTIC16,25) with multidimensional, orthogonal and single-cell analysis (surface proteomics, transcriptomics, metabolomics and chromatin accessibility) to systematically compare 4-1BB (BBζ) and CD28 (28ζ) costimulatory signals after the first CAR T cell division. Although both costimulatory domains produce effector-like (proximal) and memory-like (distal) progeny, BBζ drives more robust transcriptional and epigenetic asymmetry along with unique metabolic and survival pathways in memory-like progeny, resulting in superior long-term persistence and tumor control.

Results

ACD occurs independent of costimulatory domain and CAR expression

To investigate how different costimulatory domains influence the initial cell fate decisions in CAR T cells, we first examined the expression patterns of 4-1BB (BBζ) and CD28 (28ζ) CARs (Extended Data Fig. 1a). The 28ζ CARs consistently displayed higher surface expression levels and a strikingly more homogeneous expression pattern compared to BBζ CARs, which showed a characteristic broad distribution (Fig. 1a). This distinction was not a function of differential transduction efficiency, because both constructs achieved comparable transduction rates (Fig. 1b). The difference appeared to be intrinsic to the costimulatory domain itself, because messenger RNA electroporation (which bypasses lentiviral integration site variability) reproduced the same pattern: a narrow, homogeneous peak for 28ζ and a broad distribution for BBζ (Fig. 1a). Across multiple donors, the median fluorescence intensity was consistently higher for 28ζ CARs at similar transduction efficiencies and expression was notably more homogeneous, as demonstrated by the decreased coefficient of variation of CAR staining (Fig. 1b).

Importantly, this costimulatory, domain-dependent expression pattern is a generalizable phenomenon that extends well beyond the CARs used in this study. Multiple independent laboratories have observed the same distinction (homogeneous, high-level expression for CD28 CARs and broader, more heterogeneous expression for 4-1BB CARs) across different target antigens including mesothelin, κ light chain and CD19, and using different delivery methods, including both lentiviral transduction and mRNA electroporation18,26–28.

This staining pattern was observed in both CD4 and CD8 T cells after mRNA electroporation (Extended Data Fig. 1b), indicating that the expression differences were not subset specific. Furthermore, the coefficient of variation of the 28ζ protein surface expression remained consistently lower across a large range of transduction efficiencies, indicating a more homogeneous expression pattern (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). When we tested additional costimulatory domains, only CD28 exhibited this clear homogeneous expression pattern, whereas OX40, CD27 and CD40 all displayed broad distributions similar to BBζ (Extended Data Fig. 1e), suggesting that the CD28 domain is uniquely associated with uniform surface expression. This led us to focus our comparative analysis on 28ζ and BBζ—also notable because these are the only costimulatory domains incorporated in US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved CAR T cell products to date. Given these distinct expression patterns, we next employed our recently adapted version of target-induced molecular proximity-labeling approach called Labeling Immune Partnerships through SorTagging Intercellular Contacts (LIPSTIC)16,25,29 to track and characterize how these expression differences might influence daughter cell fate after asymmetric division (Fig. 1c).

We previously demonstrated that LIPSTIC enabled precise tracking of ACD in CAR T cells by covalently transferring a fluorescently labeled leucine–proline–glutamic acid–threonine–glycine (LPETG) peptide from target cells to the CAR T cell surface at the immunological synapse16 (Fig. 1c). Upon the first division, this label is asymmetrically inherited by the proximal daughter cell (which forms at the target-facing pole), whereas the distal daughter cell (which forms away from the target contact site) remains unlabeled. This approach allows us to isolate and characterize functionally distinct daughter cells generated through ACD. Here we used LIPSTIC to determine whether the distinct CAR expression patterns between BBζ and 28ζ domains influence the molecular and functional asymmetry generated during first division (Fig. 1c,d and Extended Data Fig. 1f).

To characterize the kinetics of LIPSTIC labeling, we performed time-course experiments at 37 °C, room temperature (23 °C) and 4 °C (Extended Data Fig. 2). Substantial labeling was detected within 30 min at all temperatures, for both CD28ζ and BBζ CAR T cells, consistent with the rapid sortase-mediated transfer kinetics reported in the original LIPSTIC system25, our previous characterization by live cell imaging16 and sortase labeling approaches that report efficient labeling of cell surface proteins at 4 °C (ref. 30). Labeling at 4 °C was, in fact, marginally higher than at 37 °C after 4 h, likely because CAR internalization is arrested at this temperature, preserving surface-accessible substrate. Importantly, prior work has demonstrated that LIPSTIC transfer in the T cell receptor (TCR)–peptide–major histocompatibility complex (MHC) context is sensitive to receptor–ligand affinity29, underscoring that labeling is a direct readout of productive receptor engagement at the immunological synapse rather than nonspecific proximity.

A potential concern with this approach is that LIPSTIC-negative, first-division cells classified as distal daughters could instead represent bystander T cells activated by paracrine cytokine signaling rather than through direct target cell engagement. To address this, we included CellTrace Yellow-labeled non-CAR-expressing T cells (incapable of recognizing target cells) alongside CellTrace Violet-labeled CAR T cells in a standard LIPSTIC coculture. At the 72-h timepoint used for first-division daughter cell collection, CellTrace Yellow-labeled control cells showed negligible to no proliferation (Extended Data Fig. 3a,b), demonstrating that ambient cytokine exposure during the assay window is insufficient to drive meaningful bystander division. These findings confirm that the LIPSTIC-negative, first-division population represents bona fide distal daughter cells arising from antigen-driven asymmetric division rather than cytokine-induced bystander activation.

As an additional control, we verified that CAR T cells were in a synchronized, quiescent state at the time of LIPSTIC coculture initiation. By the time of assay setup, cells had ceased proliferating for at least 2 d and had returned to a resting cell size of approximately 230–260 fl, consistent with conventional criteria for T cell quiescence. DNA content analysis using Vybrant DyeCycle Green staining immediately before coculture confirmed that >99% of cells resided in G0 or G1 across multiple biological donors, with only a minor population (~0.5%) of larger cells in S or G2 or M phase (Extended Data Fig. 3c,d). Importantly, this cell-cycle distribution was indistinguishable between BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells, establishing that any differences observed during the LIPSTIC assay cannot be attributed to differential proliferative states at the time of target cell encounter.

First-division daughters retaining the fluorophore–streptavidin–LPETG peptide complex (that is, first-division proximal daughters) were substantially larger in size than LPETG-negative cells (that is, first-division distal daughters), regardless of the CAR costimulatory domain (Fig. 1e,f). Consistent with hallmarks of ACD previously described in conventional T cells, proximal daughters expressed higher levels of CD98 (SLC7A5) and MYC protein compared to distal daughters (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b), both of which have been identified as asymmetrically inherited fate determinants in T cell division14,31. To determine whether CAR surface expression level influences the degree of functional asymmetry in downstream progeny of first-division daughter cells, we sorted BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells into high and low CAR-expressing populations based on a coexpressed NGFR reporter intensity before the LIPSTIC assay, using NGFR as a surrogate to avoid inadvertent T cell activation through CAR crosslinking by anti-CAR antibodies (Extended Data Fig. 4c–f). First-division proximal and distal daughters were then isolated and cultured for 3 d to assess the functional trajectory of their respective progeny. Remarkably, the pattern of functional asymmetry was consistent across CAR expression levels: the progeny of proximal daughters were larger and expressed more CD25 and proliferated more rapidly than the progeny of distal daughters to a similar degree in both high and low CAR-expressing populations for both BBζ and 28ζ constructs (Fig. 1e,f). The progeny of distal daughters from all conditions remained relatively quiescent with limited proliferation and lower CD25 expression, maintaining viability in a memory-like state (Fig. 1e,f and Extended Data Fig. 5a). This finding was surprising, because it suggested that the magnitude of CAR signaling input, varied over a substantial range of surface expression, does not proportionally scale the functional divergence propagated to downstream progeny in the first 72 h after ACD. To further characterize the differentiation state of first-division daughters, we assessed their proliferative response to homeostatic cytokines. In the absence of exogenous cytokines, proximal daughters proliferated more rapidly than distal daughters, consistent with their activated effector-like state and our prior observations16. However, when IL-2 or IL-15 was provided, distal daughters from both BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells demonstrated markedly greater responsiveness, with increased overall proliferation and a higher proportion of cells undergoing more than four divisions compared to proximal daughters (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). This enhanced cytokine responsiveness of distal daughters is consistent with a less differentiated, stem cell memory-like or central memory-like phenotype, because previous work has shown that T stem cell memory and central memory cells exhibit the greatest proliferative response to IL-15 among T cell subsets32.

These findings reveal a paradox: despite substantial differences in CAR surface expression (both within constructs and between BBζ and 28ζ), functional divergence between first-division progeny was remarkably similar, indicating that signal magnitude does not proportionally dictate fate divergence after ACD. Whether costimulatory domains nevertheless imprint molecular programs affecting long-term persistence remained an open question that we next addressed in vivo.

CAR costimulatory signaling controls T cell persistence after ACD

To test in vivo persistence, we sorted first-division proximal or distal daughter BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells and injected them into NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) mice. As controls, we included resting CAR T cells from the same-donor and transduction batch that had not undergone LIPSTIC coculture or cell division, as well as nontransduced T cells. Then, 30 d later, the mice received Nalm6 leukemia cells to assess how each subset persisted under tumor challenge (Fig. 2a).

Then, 30 d after CAR T cell injection, mice receiving BBζ distal daughters displayed the highest peripheral T cell counts (Fig. 2b), consistent with our previous report of enhanced distal daughter persistence. After a Nalm6 challenge, only BBζ distal daughters effectively controlled leukemic outgrowth (Fig. 2c). To further probe this phenotype, we employed a stress-test model in which subtherapeutic numbers of first-division daughters were injected into leukemia-bearing mice (Fig. 2d). Under these conditions, 28ζ daughters (both proximal and distal) and BBζ proximal daughters exhibited poor tumor control, whereas mice treated with BBζ distal daughters maintained sustained tumor control over an extended period (Fig. 2e). Importantly, resting (undivided) CAR T cells from both BBζ and 28ζ constructs demonstrated clear, albeit transient, therapeutic benefit compared to nontransduced controls (Fig. 2e), confirming that the limited tumor control observed with 28ζ first-division daughters is not an inherent deficiency of the CD28 costimulatory domain, but rather reflects a specific consequence of how CD28 signaling shapes daughter cell fate through ACD. Notably, in a subset of mice, resting CAR T cells (more prominent in the BBζ condition) showed delayed leukemic outgrowth (Fig. 2e), consistent with the eventual in vivo emergence of distal-like progeny after antigen encounter. However, this delayed response was insufficient for durable tumor control. As we previously demonstrated, first-division daughter cells (both proximal and distal) exhibit substantially elevated cytotoxic activity relative to resting CAR T cells, achieving more potent killing at lower effector-to-target ratios16. Resting CAR T cells require approximately 3–4 d in vivo to encounter antigen, activate and begin proliferating, effectively arriving at the functional state that first-division daughters already occupy at the time of injection. As BBζ distal daughters can deplete tumor burden within this critical early window, they prevent leukemia from surpassing the threshold beyond which tumor control is lost. The kinetic lag of resting cells, while ultimately producing similar progeny, is sufficient in this stress-test model to diverge outcomes.

In vitro cytotoxic assays further ruled out reduced cytolytic function as an explanation for poor tumor control by 28ζ and BBζ proximal daughters: both subsets demonstrated robust cytotoxicity. It is interesting that 28ζ proximal daughters appeared to kill more efficiently than BBζ proximal daughters, although, unlike BBζ, we noted a modest decrease in cytotoxicity between 28ζ proximal and distal subsets (Fig. 2f). Collectively, although ACD is detectable in both BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells, only BBζ generates a long-lived memory subset capable of controlling leukemia in our model. The failure of 28ζ distal daughters to persist despite their initial memory-like phenotype suggested that conventional surface markers cannot capture the molecular programs governing these fate decisions.

CD28 promotes greater surface protein polarization between daughters

To comprehensively understand how costimulatory domains shape the molecular landscape during ACD, we performed single-cell cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-seq) profiling on first-division daughter CAR T cells immediately after LIPSTIC sorting to simultaneously interrogate the surface proteome and transcriptome of BBζ and 28ζ daughters at the time of fate divergence (Fig. 3a). For these experiments, we used a CAR targeting the TCR δ chain (clone 5A6.E9). When we projected CD8 cell surface antibody data (205 proteins) on to a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), we observed clear segregation of resting, activated, undivided, proximal daughter and distal daughter populations for both BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells (Fig. 3a). Strikingly, 28ζ first-division daughters showed a pronounced gap between proximal and distal populations with increased Euclidean distance, a pattern that became even more apparent when populations were overlaid. This increased separation between 28ζ proximal and distal daughters was also consistently observed in CD4 T cells (Fig. 3b).

Canonical differentiation markers (CD45RA, CD45RO, CD62L, CD27, KLRG1, CD57, CD103 and CD69 in CD8+ CD25 and CD122 in CD4+) were detected (Fig. 3c,d). Unsupervised clustering and subset scoring on these markers (Fig. 3e) revealed naive-like (TN cell-like), central memory-like (TCM cell-like), effector memory-like (TEM cell-like) and tissue-resident memory-like (TRM cell-like) populations in CD8 T cells, and TN and TCM cell-like or TEM cell-like clusters in CD4 T cells (Fig. 3f). Strikingly, 28ζ CAR T cells exhibited considerably greater surface protein asymmetry between proximal and distal daughters than BBζ across all subsets (Fig. 3g and Extended Data Fig. 6a,b), with more unique differentially expressed proteins higher in 28ζ compared to BBζ for every T cell subset analyzed in both CD4 and CD8 populations. This enhanced polarization was evident in heatmaps showing differentially expressed surface proteins between proximal and distal daughters for both CD8 and CD4 T cells (Fig. 3h,i).

4-1BB drives greater transcript asymmetry despite low surface polarity

To investigate the transcriptional basis of the observed surface proteome differences, we analyzed the mRNA expression profiles from our CITE-seq data. First, we validated our T cell subset assignments by examining the expression of lineage-defining genes and their associated transcription factors. As expected, the surface protein-based subset classifications aligned with appropriate transcriptional programs: CD8 T cells showed coordinated expression of IL7R, CD27, CCR7 and IL2RA with stemness factors TCF7 and LEF1 in naive-like populations, whereas effector-like cells expressed TBX21, PRDM1, PRF1 and ITGA1 (Fig. 4a). Similarly, CD4 T cells displayed subset-appropriate expression including SELL, IL7R, CD27, IL2RA, TCF7 and LEF1 in naive-like cells, with effector populations showing MYC, TBX21 (T helper 1 (TH1) cells), GATA3 (T helper 2 (TH2) cells) and IL13 (TH2 cells) expression (Fig. 4b).

Remarkably, when we quantified transcriptional asymmetry between proximal and distal daughters, we uncovered a striking and unexpected pattern that contrasted sharply with our surface proteome findings. BBζ CD8 T cells showed enhanced asymmetric transcription compared to 28ζ, as evidenced by volcano plots revealing many more differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between BBζ proximal and distal daughters (Fig. 4c, left) versus the relatively muted transcriptional differences in 28ζ daughters (Fig. 4c, right). This same pattern of enhanced transcriptional asymmetry in BBζ cells was observed in CD4 T cells (Fig. 4d). Quantitative analysis across all T cell subsets confirmed this unexpected finding—BBζ first-division daughters consistently displayed more unique DEGs between proximal and distal cells compared to 28ζ in both the CD8 and the CD4 populations (Fig. 4e,f). Consistently, the BBζ first-division daughters showed robust transcriptional divergence whereas the 28ζ daughters exhibited more similar expression profiles between proximal and distal cells (Fig. 4g). This observation was particularly striking given the inverse relationship with our proteome data: despite 28ζ CAR T cells having higher CAR expression and greater surface protein asymmetry, they showed reduced transcriptional asymmetry compared to BBζ. Gene ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the transcriptional differences unique to BBζ daughters were functionally significant. TN cell-like BBζ daughters, but not 28ζ daughters, exhibited transcriptional asymmetries in genes involved in ATP hydrolysis, electron transport chain, methyltransferase activity, histone binding and other metabolic pathways (Fig. 4h,i), suggesting early metabolic and epigenetic differences established exclusively through 4-1BB costimulation during asymmetric division.

These findings reveal a fundamental paradox in how costimulatory signals shape ACD: increased signal strength through higher CAR expression (28ζ) drives greater surface protein asymmetry but paradoxically results in reduced transcriptional divergence between daughter cells.

Costimulation, not CAR expression, drives transcriptional asymmetry

To rigorously test whether this paradox holds across donors and CAR expression levels, we repeated the single-cell analysis using an orthogonal technology platform and returned to CD19-targeting CARs (clone FMC63) to assess reproducibility across both technology and antigen specificity. BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells (bulk CD4 and CD8) from two independent donors were sorted into high and low CAR-expressing populations based on NGFR reporter intensity during expansion (Extended Data Fig. 4c–e), before resting. LIPSTIC assays were performed separately for each donor, construct and expression level, and first-division proximal daughters, distal daughters, resting cells and activated–undivided (AU) cells were profiled by simultaneous single-cell transcriptome and surface proteome analysis using split-pool combinatorial barcoding (Parse Biosciences), yielding approximately 221,000 cells after quality filtering. Importantly, donor-specific transcriptional differences were minimal (Extended Data Fig. 7a) and high and low CAR-expressing cells did not segregate into distinct clusters (Extended Data Fig. 7b), indicating that neither donor identity nor CAR expression level was a dominant source of transcriptional variation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the four sorted populations organized along a differentiation trajectory: resting cells occupied one pole, proximal daughters the opposite and AU cells fell between proximal and distal daughters—consistent with AU cells representing an intermediate state before the diverging fates established through asymmetric division, where distal daughters remained transcriptionally closer to resting cells (Fig. 5a). Critically, when constructs were compared directly, BBζ proximal and distal daughters showed greater transcriptional separation than their 28ζ counterparts, with BBζ cells extending to the poles of the UMAP whereas 28ζ cells remained confined to a narrower range (Fig. 5c,d). Expression of canonical markers validated the biological identity of these populations: distal daughters were enriched for LEF1, IL7R and KLF2—genes associated with stemness and quiescence—whereas proximal daughters expressed higher levels of MYC, TBX21, IL2RA (CD25), CD39, TOX and ARID1A, consistent with effector activation and chromatin remodeling programs (Fig. 5e)14,31,33,34. When we quantified transcriptional asymmetry between proximal and distal daughters, BBζ CAR T cells exhibited substantially more DEGs than 28ζ in both high and low CAR-expressing populations (Fig. 5f,g). This striking consistency across expression levels provides direct evidence that it is the costimulatory domain, rather than the CAR expression level, that drives the degree of transcriptional asymmetry during ACD. Furthermore, gene set enrichment analysis revealed that metabolic pathways were again among the most enriched in BBζ-specific proximal-versus-distal DEGs, with both high and low CAR-expressing BBζ cells demonstrating similar enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism programs that were absent in 28ζ cells (Fig. 5h).

At the surface protein level, 28ζ CAR T cells again demonstrated more pronounced protein asymmetry between proximal and distal daughters, particularly in low CAR-expressing cells (Extended Data Fig. 7c), whereas both BBζ populations and high-expressing 28ζ cells also showed asymmetry but to a lesser degree. Importantly, both the 10X and Parse datasets demonstrated remarkable reproducibility in which specific markers were enriched in proximal-versus-distal daughters, reinforcing the biological validity of these observations across platforms.

Costimulation by 4-1BB, not CD28, drives metabolic asymmetry

To determine whether the transcriptional metabolic programs identified by pathway analysis resulted in functional metabolic differences, we performed real-time metabolic flux analysis (Seahorse) on sorted first-division daughters. Proximal daughters from both constructs exhibited higher oxygen consumption rates (OCRs) than their distal counterparts, consistent with their more activated metabolic state. However, when we examined the ratio of OCR to extracellular acidification rate (ECAR; a measure of the relative balance between oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis), BBζ distal daughters displayed a notably higher OCR-to-ECAR ratio compared to proximal daughters, indicating a preferential reliance on oxidative metabolism (Fig. 6a). To further resolve these metabolic differences, we generated energy phenotype plots (OCR versus ECAR) under basal conditions, ATP synthase inhibition (oligomycin) and maximal respiration (BAM15). On these plots, the position of each population relative to the diagonal reflects its OCR-to-ECAR ratio and thus its relative reliance on oxidative versus glycolytic metabolism. BBζ distal daughters maintained the highest OCR-to-ECAR ratio across all three metabolic states in three independent biological donors (Fig. 6a and Extended Data Fig. 8a). As expected, ATP synthase inhibition reduced the OCR-to-ECAR ratio in all populations; however, even under this metabolic stress, BBζ distal daughters retained a higher ratio (~0.8) than all other populations (~0.5–0.6), indicating a persistent oxidative bias. The 28ζ proximal and distal daughters clustered closely together, occupying an intermediate position between BBζ proximal and distal cells with markedly less metabolic divergence (Fig. 6a,b). We next calculated metabolic flexibility (defined as the percentage increase in OCR after BAM15 injection (oxidative flexibility) and the percentage increase in ECAR after oligomycin injection (glycolytic flexibility)). BBζ distal daughters demonstrated substantially greater increases in both oxidative and glycolytic flexibility compared to BBζ proximal daughters, indicating that distal cells retain greater metabolic reserve capacity across both pathways, a hallmark of less differentiated, memory-like T cells. Again, 28ζ proximal and distal daughters showed decreased differences, with no significant change in glycolytic flexibility between the two populations (Extended Data Fig. 8b). Collectively, these observations demonstrate that 4-1BB costimulation establishes pronounced metabolic asymmetry between first-division daughter cells whereas CD28 costimulation produces metabolically similar daughters.

To comprehensively characterize the metabolic landscape, we performed liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) metabolomics on sorted proximal and distal first-division daughters of BBζ and 28ζ CAR T cells. PCA of all detected metabolites revealed greater metabolic divergence between BBζ proximal and distal daughters along principal component 1, whereas 28ζ daughters clustered more closely together, indicating muted metabolic asymmetry (Fig. 6c). Specific metabolite analysis revealed that proximal daughters from both constructs showed elevated lactate and AMP levels—consistent with enhanced glycolysis and ATP turnover in proximal daughter cells—as well as increased proline and UDP-GlcNAc, which support protein synthesis and effector function during rapid proliferation (Fig. 6d). However, the magnitude of these differences was consistently greater in BBζ daughters. Conversely, glutamine levels were higher in distal daughters from both constructs, reflecting their oxidative metabolic preference, but again the proximal–distal difference was more pronounced in BBζ cells.

Global differential metabolome analysis by volcano plot confirmed this pattern, with BBζ daughters displaying extensive metabolic asymmetry between proximal and distal cells (Fig. 6e), whereas 28ζ daughters showed minimal metabolic polarization (Fig. 6f). Metabolites uniquely differential in BBζ but not 28ζ included key amino acids and fatty acids with distinct patterns between daughter cells. Arginine, pyruvate, isoleucine and aspartate were all elevated in BBζ distal daughters (Fig. 6g), suggesting enhanced oxidative metabolism, mitochondrial function and amino acid catabolism—metabolic features associated with memory T cell maintenance and long-term survival. In contrast, fatty acids including palmitic acid and myristic acid were significantly higher in BBζ daughters compared to 28ζ daughters, with both BBζ proximal and distal cells showing elevated levels relative to their 28ζ counterparts (Fig. 6h), indicating enhanced lipid metabolism potentially supporting membrane synthesis and cellular functions.

This metabolic profile reveals that BBζ costimulation creates metabolically distinct daughter cells: proximal daughters geared toward biosynthetic processes and proliferation, whereas distal daughters maintained oxidative and amino acid metabolism supportive of memory formation. Notably, BBζ daughters showed elevated levels of antioxidant metabolites including cysteine and glutathione compared to 28ζ daughters, with both BBζ proximal and distal cells maintaining higher levels than their 28ζ counterparts (Fig. 6i), accompanied by increased gene expression of antioxidant enzymes PRDX1 (peroxiredoxin 1), TXN (thioredoxin) and MT2A (metallothionein 2A) in both BBζ daughter populations (Fig. 6j). These enzymes are critical for managing oxidative stress in metabolically active T cells, suggesting that BBζ costimulation not only drives metabolic divergence but also equips daughter cells with distinct capacities to handle metabolic stress.

4-1BB drives epigenetic asymmetry reinforcing distinct daughter fates

Given that metabolic states can directly influence chromatin modifications through metabolite availability and flux, we investigated whether the metabolic asymmetry observed in BBζ daughters translated into distinct epigenetic landscapes. We therefore performed integrated single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with sequencing (ATAC–seq) and RNA sequencing analysis on CD19-targeting CAR T cells to simultaneously assess chromatin accessibility and gene expression in first-division daughters. Strikingly, UMAP visualization of chromatin accessibility revealed that BBζ CD8 proximal and distal daughters occupied distinct regions of epigenetic space, indicating divergent chromatin landscapes established after just one cell division (Fig. 7a; see CD4 CAR T cells in Extended Data Fig. 8c–e). In marked contrast, 28ζ proximal and distal daughters showed nearly complete overlap in their chromatin accessibility profiles, suggesting minimal epigenetic divergence. This pattern was mirrored in the transcriptional landscape, where BBζ daughters showed clear separation whereas 28ζ daughters remained transcriptionally similar (Fig. 7b). Pseudotime analysis of chromatin accessibility further confirmed this differential epigenetic programming, with BBζ daughters displaying greater ATAC signal divergence along the pseudotime trajectory compared to the constrained chromatin accessibility states of 28ζ daughters (Fig. 7c). This enhanced epigenetic asymmetry in BBζ was functionally important, resulting in substantially more differentially accessible regions and DEGs between proximal and distal daughters in both CD8 and CD4 T cells (Fig. 7d,e). The magnitude of this difference was striking: volcano plots revealed extensive epigenetic asymmetry in BBζ daughters whereas 28ζ showed minimal differentially accessible regions (Fig. 7f). Key T cell fate-determining genes, including IFNG, BATF3, IL2RA and TOX, showed asymmetric accessibility between BBζ daughters but remained similarly accessible in 28ζ daughters (Fig. 7g).

To understand the regulatory mechanisms driving these differences, we performed SCENIC+ analysis to identify gene-regulatory networks linking transcription factor binding, chromatin accessibility and target gene expression. This revealed asymmetric inheritance of regulatory networks in CD8 BBζ daughters, with distinct transcription factor activities between proximal and distal cells (Fig. 7h and Extended Data Fig. 9). The effector-associated transcription factor TBX21 showed differential activity between BBζ proximal and distal daughters, whereas 28ζ distal daughters maintained residual TBX21 transcriptional and chromatin accessibility programs, suggesting incomplete effector program suppression. Similarly, chromatin architectural factors SMC3, RAD21 and CTCF were downregulated in BBζ distal daughters, but retained residual activity in 28ζ distal cells, potentially preventing these cells from achieving or maintaining full quiescence.

Most strikingly, the memory-associated transcription factor BCL11B showed robust upregulation specifically in BBζ distal daughters with minimal activity in proximal cells, whereas this differential was markedly muted in 28ζ daughters. Consistent with prior reports that BCL11B functions as both a transcriptional activator and a repressor in T cells35, our analysis revealed that BCL11B functions as a dual regulator in BBζ distal daughters (Fig. 7i): it acts as an epigenetic activator for stemness-associated and memory-associated genes including LEF1, TCF7, NELL2, MBNL1 and DOCK9 (maintaining cells in a less differentiated state) while simultaneously repressing effector and metabolic programs by suppressing IL2RA (T cell activation), ENO1 (glycolysis) and CLIC1 (chloride channel or cell proliferation) expression.

Discussion

The clinical success of FDA-approved CAR T cell therapies depends on generating durable memory cells, yet how 4-1BB and CD28 costimulation shapes the first fate decisions after antigen encounter has remained unclear. By combining LIPSTIC-based fate tracking with multiomic profiling across three orthogonal single-cell platforms (10x Genomics 5′-CITE-seq, Parse Biosciences split-pool combinatorial barcoding and 10x Genomics Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression), four healthy donors and two distinct CAR antigen specificities (CD19 or FMC63 and TCRδ or 5A6.E9), we have shown that CAR expression controls the extent of surface protein polarization during division, whereas the costimulatory domain determines whether this polarization becomes stable functional divergence. Thus, the clinical distinction between 4-1BB and CD28 CAR T products reflects how early ACD is molecularly programmed.

These data refine established differences between the domains. CD28 CARs promote rapid effector differentiation, glycolysis and immediate cytotoxicity, whereas 4-1BB CARs favor oxidative metabolism, memory formation and persistence18,22–24. Mechanistically, 4-1BB engages TRAF-dependent noncanonical, NF-κB and THEMIS–SHP1 pathways, whereas CD28 recruits LCK for stronger proximal activation23,36; corresponding transcriptional programs include HLA class II, IL-21 axis and fatty acid metabolism enrichment in 4-1BB CAR T cells24. These distinctions help explain B cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia analyses showing greater persistence and minimal residual disease-negative remission with 4-1BB products37–39. However, they are context dependent: CD28-based axi-cel can show stronger antitumor activity in aggressive lymphoma, with greater toxicity38 and chronic 4-1BB activation can also drive dysfunction40. Extending our prior demonstration that CD8+ BBζ CAR T cells undergo ACD16, we have shown here that ACD occurs in both CD4+ and CD8+CAR T cells but is not a binary process. The higher, more homogeneous, surface expression of 28ζ CARs produces greater protein asymmetry, likely through stronger receptor clustering and synapse formation; CD28ζ CARs also signal more strongly than 4-1BBζ CARs at an equivalent antigen density41. Yet this enhanced surface polarization does not translate into durable daughter cell divergence. Instead, 28ζ proximal and distal daughters remain transcriptionally, metabolically and epigenetically similar, whereas BBζ daughters diverge at all three levels and generate the persistent distal population that helps explain the superior durability often observed for 4-1BB CARs18,19.

This dissociation indicates that signal quality, not signal magnitude, determines whether ACD produces stable effector-based and memory-biased daughters. CD28ζ provides stronger immediate activation but imposes a relatively rigid glycolytic, proliferative program. BBζ delivers less dramatic surface segregation but permits distinct transcriptional trajectories through TRAF-associated signaling, oxidative metabolism and sustained NF-κB activity. This conclusion agrees with calibrated CD28 CAR designs in which reduced CD3ζ immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif signaling preserved memory programs and improved therapeutic potency despite lower total activation42.

The metabolic data provide a mechanistic basis for this effect. Consistent with prior work linking 4-1BB to oxidative metabolism and CD28 to glycolysis18, BBζ distal daughters retained a higher OCR-to-ECAR ratio, greater metabolic reserve and metabolite profiles consistent with oxidative and amino acid metabolism, whereas BBζ proximal daughters were more biosynthetic and proliferative. In contrast, 28ζ daughters showed limited metabolic polarization despite greater surface asymmetry. As mitochondrial dynamics and metabolic flexibility shape T cell fate43, these findings suggest that costimulatory signaling actively programs the metabolic branch point generated by ACD, rather than passively reflecting inherited surface cargo.

Regulatory-network analysis further identified BCL11B as a key feature of productive memory-biased ACD. BBζ distal daughters selectively activated BCL11B, a regulator of T cell stemness and memory44, together with stemness-associated targets including LEF1, TCF7, NELL2, MBNL1 and DOCK9, while repressing activation and metabolic genes such as IL2RA, ENO1 and CLIC1. The muted BCL11B asymmetry in 28ζ daughters may therefore explain why they acquire some memory-like surface features but fail to establish durable memory function.

Our data also show that epigenetic divergence is established within one human CAR T cell division. BBζ distal daughters downregulated chromatin architectural factors, including SMC3, RAD21 and CTCF, suggesting a more adaptable chromatin state, whereas 28ζ daughters retained more similar accessibility programs45. This pattern is consistent with recent work showing that asymmetric EIF4F partitioning drives MYC-dependent fate divergence34 and that cBAF-MYC complexes can be asymmetrically inherited to remodel chromatin and affect persistence31. Together, these findings place costimulatory signaling upstream of the epigenetic machinery that stabilizes ACD-derived fate. The robustness of these conclusions is supported by convergence across orthogonal single-cell platforms (10× 5′-CITE-seq; Figs. 3 and 4), Parse split-pool barcoding (Fig. 5) and 10× Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression (Fig. 7) spanning four healthy donors and two CAR antigen specificities. The muted asymmetry of 28ζ daughters and pronounced divergence of BBζ daughters were independently recapitulated by each chemistry, strongly arguing that these differences are intrinsic to the costimulatory domain rather than platform-specific or donor-specific artifacts.

These observations argue against simply maximizing CAR expression or signaling intensity. Instead, CAR design should match signaling quality to therapeutic context: CD28 programs may be advantageous when rapid tumor clearance is needed38, whereas 4-1BB programs may better support durable immunosurveillance. Dual costimulatory designs46, affinity-tuned CARs47, metabolic conditioning and epigenetic interventions48 could be used to tune this balance. Overall, our work defines ACD as a costimulatory, domain-tunable mechanism in which early protein polarization, transcriptional remodeling, metabolism and chromatin accessibility are coordinated to determine CAR T cell persistence.

Methods

Cell culture, vectors and CAR T cell generation

Unless otherwise indicated, cells were cultured in R10 medium (Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 mM Hepes and 1% penicillin–streptomycin). Primary human T cells from de-identified healthy donors were obtained from the Human Immunology Core (HIC) at the University of Pennsylvania under applicable institutional approvals. T cells were activated with anti-CD3 or anti-CD28 Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a 3:1 bead-to-cell ratio, transduced 24 h later with third-generation, self-inactivating lentiviral CAR vectors and debeaded on day 4. Cultures were maintained at 0.8 × 106 cells ml−1 whereas IL-2 was tapered from 100 IU ml−1 to 0 IU ml−1. Transduction efficiency and CAR surface expression were measured by flow cytometry. Newly generated plasmids and engineered cell lines are available from the corresponding author under an appropriate material transfer agreement. Nalm6 cells expressing sortase-tethered target molecules were maintained in R10 medium; source and authentication method are included in Reporting Summary. All materials are available on request from the corresponding author.

CAR constructs and mRNA electroporation

CARs targeted CD19 (FMC63 scFv) or TCR δ (5A6.E9), contained either CD28ζ or 4-1BBζ signaling domains and carried an amino-terminal pentaglycine tag for LIPSTIC labeling. Construct schematics are shown in Extended Data Fig. 1a and vector maps in Extended Data Fig. 4c. For CAR-expression sorting, NGFR (CD271) was placed downstream of the CAR in the same transcript separated by a P2A sequence. Cells were stained with anti-NGFR-phycoerythrin (BioLegend, clone ME20.4, cat. no. 345106; 1:100) and anti-FMC63-FITC (ACRO Biosystems, clone Y45, cat. no. FM3-FY45; 1:100) and NGFR-high or NGFR-low cells were isolated on a BD FACSAria II. Nontransduced, donor-matched T cells served as staining controls. For transient expression, CAR sequences were subcloned into pDrive.150 poly(A)49, linearized with ClaI or SpeI and transcribed using the T7 mScript Standard mRNA Production System (Cellscript) to generate Cap 1 transcripts with an approximately 150-nucleotide poly(A) tail. The mRNA was purified with the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN), aliquoted at 1 μg μl−1 and stored at −80 °C. T cells were washed 3× in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline, resuspended at 5 × 106 cells per 100 μl in P3 Primary Cell Nucleofector Solution (Lonza), electroporated with 10 μg of mRNA using pulse EH-115 on a Lonza 4D-Nucleofector and recovered for 10 min in OptiMEM and 10% FBS before culture at 2 × 106 cells ml−1. Activated cells were debeaded before electroporation. CAR expression was analyzed 24 h later.

LIPSTIC labeling and daughter cell isolation

LIPSTIC assays were performed using Nalm6 target cells expressing sortase-tethered target molecules. Target cells were incubated with 100 μM biotinylated LPETG peptide (LifeTein) for 30 min at 37 °C in RPMI medium and 10% FBS, washed 3×, stained with fluorescent streptavidin (phycoerythrin, AF647 or allophycocyanin; BioLegend; 10 μg ml−1) for 30 min at 37 °C, washed and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells ml−1. Rested CAR T cells (14–16 d after activation; no proliferation for at least 2 d; 200–260 fl by Coulter Multisizer 4e) were stained with CellTrace Violet at 1 × 107 cells ml−1 for 10 min at 37 °C. Target cells and CAR T cells were cocultured at a 5:1 effector-to-target ratio in 6-well plates or flasks at 1 × 106 total cells per ml (approximately 0.625 × 106 cells cm−2). After 72 h, first-division proximal daughters (CellTrace diluted, LIPSTIC positive), first-division distal daughters (CellTrace diluted, LIPSTIC negative), AU cells and resting cells were isolated by FACS. Lentiviral titers were adjusted to achieve comparable transduction efficiencies between constructs (20–85%). For LIPSTIC time-course assays, cocultures were started in a staggered fashion at 37 °C, room temperature or 4 °C so that all samples were harvested, stained and analyzed together.

Cell-cycle and cytokine-response assays

Immediately before LIPSTIC coculture, resting status was verified by staining CAR T cells at 1 × 106 cells ml−1 with Vybrant DyeCycle Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were analyzed without washing on an LSR Fortessa after singlet gating; an FSC-high gate served as an internal cycling-cell control. For cytokine-response assays, sorted first-division daughters were plated in flat-bottomed 96-well plates at 1.32 × 105 cells per well in 200 μl of R10 medium and cultured with no cytokine, IL-2 (30 IU ml−1; aldesleukin (Proleukin), Iovance Biotherapeutics) or IL-15 (25 ng ml−1; STEMCELL Technologies, cat. no. 78031.1). At approximately 48 h, half the medium was replaced with fresh cytokine-containing R10 medium. Cells were analyzed at approximately 72 h by flow cytometry and proliferation was quantified from CellTrace Violet dilution in FlowJo v10.

Metabolic profiling and metabolomics

Mitochondrial function was measured on the day of daughter cell sorting with the Seahorse T Cell Metabolic Profiling Kit (Agilent). Cells were resuspended in nonbuffered RPMI 1640 medium containing 5.5 mM glucose, 2 mM L-glutamine and 1 mM sodium pyruvate; 1.0 × 105 cells were seeded per well. Plates were centrifuged at 200g for 2 min, incubated for 60 min, moved to a CO2-free 37 °C incubator during cartridge calibration and analyzed on XF96 cartridges. OCR and ECAR were measured at baseline and after 1.5 μM oligomycin, 2.5 μM BAM15 and 0.5 μM rotenone or antimycin A. Each population included 5–15 technical wells.

For LC–MS metabolomics, resting, proximal and distal CAR T cell populations were collected 1 d after sorting, washed twice in ice-cold 0.9% NaCl and extracted in 1 ml of pre-chilled acetonitrile, methanol and water (2:2:1, v:v:v) with 0.1% formic acid on dry ice for 3 min. Extracts were neutralized with 69 μl of 15% ammonium bicarbonate, spiked with 20 μl of internal-standard mix, pulse sonicated on ice and centrifuged at 21,000g for 15 min at 0 °C. Supernatants were cleaned using Agilent Captiva EMR-Lipid cartridges, dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in 100 μl of 50% acetonitrile and water. Samples were analyzed on an Agilent 6546 quadrupole time-of-flight LC–MS using hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography with positive and negative electrospray ionization. Data were processed in MassHunter Profinder vB.10.01 and MetaboAnalyst 6.050, with batch correction, feature filtering (relative s.d. > 25%, interquartile range <5%), log10(transformation), median normalization and autoscaling. Differential metabolites were identified by fold-change and two-sided Student’s t-tests with Padj < 0.05 and absolute log2(fold-change) > 0.26.

Flow cytometry and cytotoxicity assays

Flow cytometry antibodies were from BioLegend unless stated otherwise (Supplementary Table 1). LIPSTIC-sorted populations were cultured for 72 h before phenotyping with CD25-BV711 (clone M-A251, cat. no. 356138; 1:40), CD45RA-BV650 (HI100, cat. no. 304136; 1:100), CD45RO-PE-Cy7 (UCHL1, cat. no. 304230; 1:50), CD62L-PE (DREG-56, cat. no. 304840; 1:200), CCR7-FITC (BD Biosciences, 150503, cat. no. 561271; 1:100), CD4-BB700 (BD Biosciences, SK3, cat. no. 566392; 1:50) and CD8-APC-H7 (BD Biosciences, SK1, cat. no. 560179; 1:100). In vivo samples were additionally stained with CD45-PE-Cy7 (HI30, cat. no. 304016; 1:100) or CD8-BUV395 (BD Biosciences, RPA-TB, cat. no. 563795; 1:100). CountBright Absolute Counting Beads (Invitrogen) were used where indicated. Samples were acquired on LSRII, LSR Fortessa or FACSymphony A3 instruments (BD Biosciences), gated by forward or side scatter and singlets and analyzed in FlowJo v10 and GraphPad Prism v9.4.0. Cytotoxicity assays were performed 1 d after the first division by coculturing click beetle green luciferase-expressing Nalm6 cells with proximal, distal or resting CAR T cells, or donor-matched nontransduced T cells, at the indicated effector-to-target ratios.

Mouse studies

Animal experiments were approved by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol no. 805652) and followed institutional humane-endpoint policies. NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ mice (NSG; bred in house by the Penn Xenograft Core; male, age 8–12 weeks) were bred and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Bulk CD4 or CD8 T cells were used for adoptive transfer to promote engraftment. BBζ and 28ζ experiments used CAR T cells from three independent healthy donors and were performed concurrently; a subset of the BBζ arm has been reported previously16 and is included here because the 28ζ arm was run in parallel from the same donors. Sample sizes were selected from prior data rather than from formal power calculations.

For persistence experiments, mice received 2.5 × 105 proximal, distal or resting anti-CD19 CAR T cells, or nontransduced T cells, by tail-vein injection on day 0, and were challenged on day 35 with 1 × 106 Nalm6 leukemia cells. For stress-test experiments, 1 × 106 Nalm6 cells were injected on day 0, engraftment was confirmed by bioluminescence imaging on day 3 and mice were treated on day 4 with 2.5 × 105 sorted proximal or distal CAR T cells, 2.5 × 106 resting CAR T cells and 1.3 × 106 bulk restimulated CAR T cells or nontransduced controls. Group sizes are reported in the relevant figure legends. Cage assignment was randomized before treatment and T cell infusions were delivered blinded by laboratory personnel or the Human Stem Cell and Xenograft Core. Unless otherwise stated, subsequent imaging analysis and flow cytometry analysis were not performed blind to experimental group. Leukemia burden was measured 2–3× weekly using an IVIS Lumina III (PerkinElmer) and total flux was used as the tumor-burden surrogate. Peripheral blood was collected by retro-orbital bleed for flow cytometry. Mice were monitored at least twice weekly for morbidity, including weight loss, posture and body condition. Humane endpoints were >20% body-weight loss, body condition score 1 out of 5 or total tumor burden measured as bioluminescent signal exceeding 1 × 1012. The maximal tumor burden was not exceeded throughout the studies.

10X CITE-seq

First-division proximal, distal, AU and resting BBζ or 28ζ CAR T cell populations (1.5 × 105 cells per population) were isolated by LIPSTIC sorting. The 28ζ samples were processed in parallel with same-donor BBζ samples; previously reported CD8 BBζ data16 are shown with new CD4 and 28ζ data to enable direct costimulatory-domain comparisons. Cells were stained with a customized TotalSeq-C antibody panel (BioLegend, cat. no. 900000114, lot B311489; full antibody details are supplied in Supplementary Table 1) in 100 μl of staining buffer for 30 min at 4 °C, washed 3×, resuspended at 1.5 × 106 cells ml−1 and loaded at 10,000 cells per population on 10× Genomics Next GEM K chips. Gene expression and antibody-derived tag libraries were prepared using Chromium 5ʹ v2 chemistry, checked on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 and sequenced paired-end dual-index (150× 2 cycles) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 lane. FASTQ files were processed using Cell Ranger multi v6.1.2 with the GRCh38 reference (GENCODE v32 or Ensembl 98; 10× v5.0.0) and libraries were aggregated with Cell Ranger aggr. Seurat v551 was used for quality filtering (>25% mitochondrial reads, <5% ribosomal content, <500 or >40,000 transcripts or <500 genes were excluded), SCTransform v2 normalization with glmGamPoi52, ADT-based dimensionality reduction, clustering and differential expression of CD4 and CD8 subsets. UMAPs for selected genes were generated using scVelo53-denoised expression values. T cell states were assigned using ADT module scores: CD8 TN cell-like (CD45RA, CD27 and IL7R), TCM cell-like (CD62L and CD45RO), TEM cell-like (CD45RO and CD49a) and TRM cell-like (CD103, ITGA1 and CD49a); CD4 TN cell-like (CD62L and CD45RA) and TCM cell-like or TEM cell-like (CD45RO, CD57, KLRG1, CD25 and IL2RB). GO enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler v4.12.6 and org.Hs.eg.db; categories with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.2 were retained. Analyses were run in R v4.4.1 and Python v3.11.

Single-cell multiome ATAC–seq and RNA sequencing

First-division proximal, distal and resting BBζ or CD28ζ CAR T cells were FACS isolated and processed in parallel for 10× Genomics Chromium Single Cell Multiome ATAC + Gene Expression profiling (CG000369 Rev B). Nuclei were prepared using the Chromium Nuclei Isolation Kit with RNase inhibitor and 0.1× lysis buffer, inspected microscopically, diluted to 3,000–6,000 nuclei μl−1 and transposed for 60 min at 37 °C. Barcoding was performed on a Chromium X using Next GEM Chip J, followed by separate ATAC and gene-expression library construction. ATAC libraries used Single Index Kit N, Set A and GEX libraries used Dual Index Kit TT, Set A. Libraries were quality control tested on a Bioanalyzer, quantified with the KAPA Library Quantification Kit and sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 to approximately 25,000 ATAC read pairs and 20,000 GEX read pairs per nucleus. FASTQ files were processed with Cell Ranger ARC v2.0.2 using the GRCh38 v2020-A-2.0.0 reference. Seurat v5 and Signac were used for downstream analysis. Nuclei with <2,000 or >100,000 ATAC fragments, <500 or >25,000 RNA transcripts, transcription start site enrichment <1 or nucleosome signal >2 were excluded. RNA was normalized by SCTransform and CD4 and CD8 subsets were analyzed separately. Differential gene expression used Seurat FindMarkers with Wilcoxon’s rank-sum tests on SCT-normalized values (absolute log2(fold-change) >0.26; FDR-adjusted P (Padj) < 0.05; RPL or RPS genes excluded). Differential accessibility used Signac54 logistic regression on ATAC peak counts with nCount_ATAC as a latent variable (absolute log2(fold-change) >0.59; Padj < 0.05). Monocle3 pseudotime55 was calculated from ATAC-derived UMAPs rooted in resting G1-phase cells. SCENIC+ v0.12.056 was run with GRCh38 and cisTarget motif databases to infer eRegulons.

Split-pool single-cell profiling

For higher-throughput profiling, CD19-directed CD28ζ or 4-1BBζ CAR T cells from two healthy donors were sorted approximately 6 d after activation into NGFR-high and NGFR-low populations, expanded, rested and analyzed by LIPSTIC as above. For each donor, construct and expression level, proximal, distal, AU and resting cells were isolated, generating 32 conditions. Sorted cells were stained at 4 × 105 cells per 50 μl with TotalSeq-A Universal Human Cocktail v2.0 (BioLegend; 184 antibodies, listed in Supplementary Table 1) plus Human TruStain FcX for 30 min at 4 °C, washed and fixed or permeabilized using the Parse Biosciences Fixation Kit v3. All conditions were processed together with the Parse Biosciences Mega Kit v3; barcoded cells were distributed into 16 sublibraries, 8 of which were sequenced, yielding approximately 221,000 quality-filtered T cells with paired RNA and ADT measurements. Whole-transcriptome libraries followed the manufacturer’s workflow. ADT libraries57 were generated by adding the TotalSeq-A enrichment primer (5ʹ-ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGCTCTTCCGATCTCCTTGGCACCCGAGAATTCCA-3ʹ) at 0.02 μM during complementary DNA amplification, recovering short amplicons from the 0.8× KAPA Pure Bead supernatant and re-amplifying for 12 cycles with dual-indexed Illumina primers (forward: 5ʹ-AATGATACGGCGACCACCGAGATCTACAC[i5]ACACTCTTTCCCTACACGACGC-3′; reverse: 5′-CAAGCAGAAGACGGCATACGAGAT[i7]GTGACTGGAGTTCAGACGTGTGCTCTTCCGATCT-3′, where bracketed sequences denote unique sample indices). Whole-transcriptome and ADT libraries were sequenced to approximately 27,000 and 11,500 reads per cell, respectively. RNA reads were processed with a split-pipe (Parse Biosciences); ADTs were quantified using split-pipe CRISPR mode with antibody barcodes supplied as guide identifiers. Seurat v5 with BPCells was used for cells with paired RNA and ADT data. Cells were retained with 500–20,000 unique molecular identifiers, 100–5,000 genes, mitochondrial fraction <15% and ribosomal fraction >0%. RNA data were SCTransform normalized with glmGamPoi; ADTs were centered log(ratio) normalized. Donor effects were corrected by reciprocal PCA integration after comparison with Harmony, Canonical Correlation Analysis and JointPCA. Differential expression was assessed by Seurat Wilcoxon’s tests and DESeq2 pseudobulk aggregation by donor, construct, CAR expression level and sorted population. Gene set enrichment used clusterProfiler-ranked MSigDB Hallmark lists and ADT differential expression used pseudobulk DESeq2.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical tests were two sided unless otherwise indicated. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure where appropriate; q values were calculated using clusterProfiler. P < 0.05 was considered significant and exact P values have been provided where possible. Unless otherwise stated, graphs show individual data points with the mean indicated by a horizontal line; error bars and box-plot definitions are provided in figure legends. Data distribution was assumed to be normal but was not formally tested unless indicated; equality of variances was not formally tested unless stated. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes, but sample sizes were similar to those in previous publications and were chosen before data collection. Randomization and blinding for animal experiments are described above; other in vitro and sequencing experiments were not randomized or analyzed blind unless stated because groups were processed in parallel under matched conditions. No data points were excluded unless exclusion criteria are stated in the relevant methods or figure legends. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism v9.4.0 or the software pipelines described above.
