Abstract
This report summarizes activities conducted to understand and mitigate the negative
consequences that moisture ingressions have upon the operations of pyroprocessing equipment.
The objectives of the work conducted in FY25 were to characterize (1) the physicochemical
properties of particles generated as a result of moisture ingressions, and (2) the corrosion of
relevant structural materials with the introduction of moisture. Compositional and morphological
analyses of the generated particles were conducted through a suite of characterization
technologies, including X-ray diffraction, microscopy, and particle size analysis. The results
gathered this year were compared to those obtained in FY24 when studies with oxygen
ingressions were conducted on CeCl3-LiCl-KCl and UCl3-LiCl-KCl systems. Investigations
involving performance evaluations of industrially relevant structural alloys were also conducted
under moisture ingress conditions. In-line electrochemical monitoring of the bulk salt was
augmented with microscopy of alloy samples to quantify the concentration and accumulation of
corrosion products in the bulk salt. The results reported in this work provide a more holistic
understanding of the effects of atmospheric ingressions on molten chloride salt chemistry such
that effective redox control strategies may be implemented.

1. Introduction
Pyroprocessing involves a series of unit operations that utilize molten salts to enable the
recycling of radioactive nuclides from used nuclear fuel. Contaminants introduced into these unit
operations through atmospheric ingressions may promote the formation of insoluble oxide
particles [1-5]. While atmospheric ingressions can be mitigated through gas purification systems
and headspace purging, some degree of particulates will eventually form in the salt after years-
long operations. Left untreated, these particles can negatively affect material processing and
complicate safeguarding of the equipment. Moreover, the redox reactions associated with
contaminant interactions lead to the formation of aggressive salt species and gases (e.g., Cl2 and
HCl) that can corrode and limit the lifetime of the process equipment.

In FY24, we focused on characterizing particle formation in lanthanide- and actinide-bearing
salts as a result of reactions with oxygen according to typical reactions such as:

                         2CeCl3 + 2O2 → 2CeO2 + 3Cl2 (g)                                  [1]

                          2UCl3 + 2O2 → 2UO2 + 3Cl2 (g)                                   [2]

Compositional and morphological properties of the oxide particles were assessed through a suite
of tools, including laser diffraction (LD), dynamic image analysis (DIA), and X-ray diffraction
(XRD). The objective of the work conducted in FY25 built upon these earlier efforts and focused
on the characterization of particles formed in the presence of moisture. Reaction mechanisms
governing chemical and electrochemical interactions between moisture and relevant reactants
that ultimately yield insoluble particles are discussed. Interestingly, particle morphology and
characteristics appear largely unaffected by the oxide source (i.e., oxygen vs. moisture). In
contrast, the electrochemical behavior of the salt varies considerably depending upon the
ingressed gas as the introduction of moisture forms HCl which helps to maintain a reductive salt
potential compared to O2 ingression cases.

Materials performance studies relevant to fuel processing in LiCl-KCl eutectic salts were also
initiated this year. Corrosion studies related to nuclear-relevant chloride and fluoride salts have
long been performed in the literature [6-8]. Most of those studies occur in sealed configurations
where the ingression of atmospheric impurities is avoided. In contrast, our studies have focused
on understanding material performance in the context of controlled levels of atmospheric
ingressions that are representative of the incursions typically experienced in pyroprocessing
equipment during operations. Historical literature provided context for the corrosion testing. For
example, in dry molten chloride and fluoride salts, selective dealloying of both Cr and Fe from
stainless steel samples have been reported [9-11]. The presence of dissolved oxides and caustic
gases can further enhance the corrosion of stainless steel alloys. The effect of atmospheric
ingressions on these structural alloys while exposed to molten salts is an ongoing area of study
with inconclusive results on the change in corrosion rate [12, 13]. Prolonged exposure to such
conditions can further diminish the structural integrity of the unit operation and further
complicate material control and accountancy objectives. It is therefore imperative to characterize
material corrosion pathways in the presence of oxidative conditions induced by atmospheric
contaminants. As such, discussions presented will also focus on characterizing corrosion rates
through near-real time electrochemical techniques. Information gained from global, macroscopic

 1

electrochemical measurements will be augmented with local, microscopic and morphological
data collected from scanning electron microscopy (SEM) coupled with electron dispersion
spectroscopy (EDS) and particle size analysis (PSA).

Results from characterizing the physicochemical composition of generated particles, together
with the insights gained from the corrosion studies, provide a more holistic understanding of the
complex phenomena associated with the presence of atmospheric ingressions and their at-
temperature interactions with structural materials.

2. Experimental Approach
The following section includes details of the experimental approaches related to analyses of the
solid particulates and determination of corrosion rates of various metals and alloys in the
presence of controlled moisture ingressions. Earlier reports include additional details of the
experimental procedures and protocols associated with particle generation studies [14].

2.1. Characterization of Particles Formed During Ingression Experiments
All salts discussed herein were composed of LiCl-KCl eutectic with concentrations of Ce3+ or
U3+ ranging from 0.5-2 wt%, prepared through addition of CeCl3 and UCl3, respectively. Details
regarding the precise preparation of these salts prior to the introduction of moisture may be found
elsewhere [14]. Post-ingression analysis was conducted on solids isolated from the bulk salt
using previously described methodologies [15]. For brevity, only a brief description of this
procedure is discussed. First, the bulk salt was dissolved in 18.3 MΩ·cm deionized water (DI
H2O) and agitated for 90 min in an ultrasonic bath. Chloride test strips (Hach Quantab® Low
Range 30-600 ppm) were used to ensure the non-radiological particles were freed from residual
bulk salt. Provisional analysis of the prepared radiological samples omitted the removal and
subsequent testing of residual chloride salt.

The chemical composition of the particles was characterized using a Rigaku SmartLab SE X-ray
diffractometer. Patterns were collected in the 5 – 90° range with 0.01° step sizes at a scan rate
of 2°/minute. Samples were rotated at 30° per second (5 rpm) to enhance the probability of
capturing various lattice orientations signals. Physical morphology of the particles was evaluated
using LD and DIA on a Microtrac SYNC particle size analyzer equipped with a FlowSync
sample delivery device using DI H2O as the carrier fluid. Particle size distributions (PSDs) were
generated using 30 second run times and assuming absorbing particles with irregular shapes.
Particles were also characterized using a Hitachi S-3000 N SEM with an associated
backscattered electron (BSE) detector and energy-dispersive X-ray emission spectrometer
(EDS) operated at 20 kV.

2.2. Analysis of Structural Material Corrosion Rates
Samples of various industrially relevant metallic alloys were used to characterize corrosion rates
and mechanisms resulting from the atmospheric ingressions. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV)
was used to provide in situ electrochemical monitoring of the corrosion products formed as a
result of salt contact with Ar gas containing varying concentrations of H2O vapor. The working
and reference electrodes were 1 mm tungsten (ThermoShield, 99.95% purity) rods, and the
counter electrode was a 3 mm tungsten rod. All electrodes were housed in an insulative alumina

 2

sheath. The test samples were held in place with an Inconel wire. For the comparison of anti-
corrosion coatings, uncoated and Ag-plated (AMS 2411) 18-8 SS samples were compared. All
electrochemical measurements were conducted using a Gamry Interface 5000 potentiostat. LSVs
were collected at 500 mV/s and were used to quantify concentration transients as moisture
ingression experiments progressed.

The piping and instrumentation diagram (P&ID) of the ingression setup used in this work is
shown in Figure 1a. In the P&ID, the mass flow controllers (Masterflex 32907-67) were added
in-line to ensure the precise control of gas flow rates into the salt and water basin. A hygrometer
(AcuDew-P Dewpoint transmitter coupled with AcuVu-P-C hygrometer, Shaw USA) was used
to measure the humidity of the Ar/H2O gas prior to entry into the reaction vessel. A scrubber
containing calcium oxide (Sigma Aldrich, ≥95%) was installed downstream to remove
potentially caustic and corrosive gases formed during experimentation. A section view schematic
of the reaction vessel is shown in Figure 1b accompanied by the picture of the system shown in
Figure 1c. Unless otherwise specified, corrosion tests were conducted at 550°C. Samples were
prepared for post-experimental characterization by cleaning the alloy samples in a DI H2O
sonication bath to remove residual salt. The portions of the alloy submerged in the salt were then
collected and mounted in epoxy for SEM/EDS characterization. This epoxy mount was polished
sequentially using 120, 240, 600, 800, 1200, and 1200 fine-grit SiC abrasive griding paper.
Select samples were tested on SEM/EDS using a ZEISS Gemini 300 microscope operated at
10 keV.

3. Thermodynamic Predictions of Speciation: Construction
 of Littlewood Diagrams
As previously discussed, the introduction and accumulation of atmospheric ingressions can alter
the molten salt chemistry such that corrosion rates are enhanced and the formation of insoluble
solids are promoted. Littlewood diagrams are often employed to inform operational design
parameters and enable the deployment of targeted redox potential control mechanisms [16].

While Littlewood diagrams have previously been constructed for the LiCl-KCl salt systems
studied in this work, differing thermochemical values can lead to widely varying diagrammatic
representations [17]. For that reason, we have produced our own versions of the diagrams that
include updated thermochemical data and the addition of curves related to moisture impurities.
Ultimately, these diagrams serve to provide key context for the types of particles and corrosion
products that atmospheric ingressions generate.

As with the oxygen ingression experiments conducted in FY24, a series of characterization tools
were deployed this year to further elucidate discrete contributions of moisture ingressions to
reactant consumption and particle formation. In situ electrochemical monitoring approaches
were previously reported in FY25 [14], and the apparent reaction rates were extracted as the
solution-phase concentration of surrogate Ce species decayed over time in the presence of
moisture [14].

The solid particles generated during these previously reported experiments were analyzed via
XRD, LD, and DIA techniques in order to elucidate connections between process conditions and
particle characteristics. XRD patterns were first collected to determine the chemical composition
of the solid particles. Figure 5 shows patterns of particles collected at various temperatures
alongside optical images of the particles. From these patterns, we noted the prevalence of peaks
corresponding to CeOCl present at all tested temperatures. Higher order oxidation states of Ce
present in the form of CeO2 were only observed at sufficiently low temperatures (i.e., T <
550°C). The coloration gradient in the optical images is also in agreement with those reported in
literature for CeO2 and CeOCl [20].

During experiments, the salt redox potential was determined as the difference between the open
circuit potential and the reduction potential of Li. In the cases of moisture ingression
experiments, the salt potential was measured at values consistent with the presence of HCl at
low activity in the salt. Ultimately, atmospheric ingressions into the salt produce dynamic
physicochemical phenomena that necessitate the utilization of multiphysics simulations to fully
account for the overall behavior of particle generation. Complex phase transformations, thermal
convection gradients, and diffusion-reaction mechanisms all need to be integrated into the
simulations to fully characterize the phenomena governing particle formation.

Additional particle characteristics were also extracted from the DIA functionality on the
Microtrac FlowSync analyzer. Figure 9 shows the roundness distribution of the particles at the
various tested temperatures plotted alongside its corresponding Feret Length (FLength) vs. Feret
Width (FWidth) distribution curves. Roundness is used to relate the generated particle edges’
proximity to a circle. It is highly sensitive to ellipsoidal shape elongations such as those shown
in Figure 4 and ranges from 0 – 1 (where roundness = 1 is a perfect circle). In the distribution
plot, the box is determined via the interquartile range (IQR), wherein the upper and lower
whiskers correspond to 1.5× the upper and lower limits of the IQR. The discrete data points
correspond to the individual particle data, and the black solid line provides a visual guide for the
distribution of these values. As with the LD data shown in Figure 9, particle morphology was
largely independent of process temperatures under the conditions tested (2 wt% Ce3+ and 5000
ppm H2O). It was also previously determined that the Ce3+ consumption rate was also largely
independent of operating temperature [14]. This suggests a temperature-independent process
governing particle formation. This was further confirmed by plotting the FLength vs. FWidth
shown in Figure 9. Because FLength and FWidth measure the maximum length and width in
sequential images, ideally spherical particles will lie on the parity line. Figure 9, we note that the
data points largely converging to the parity line with only a limited number of outliers.

Contrary to these results, in the case of the oxygen ingressions studied in FY24, the PSD’s

4.3. Corrosion of Structural Materials in Molten Salts During Moisture
 Ingressions
In the absence of carefully controlled salt conditions, practical pyroprocessing systems can form
dynamic salt conditions that promote corrosion of structural materials. A variety of structural
materials have been evaluated for use in chloride-based molten salts, including austenitic 316
stainless steel (316 SS) and high-nickel content alloys, such as Hastelloy C-22 [22, 23].
Extensive corrosion studies involving nickel-based alloys, such as Inconel, have also been
conducted in halide salts [24, 25]. In each of these studies, the formation of depleted Cr and Fe
layers was detected at salt-alloy interfaces. Fuel processing conditions are distinct from other
molten salt equipment (e.g., molten salt reactors) in that the salt conditions are generally kept in

 18

extremely reducing conditions. However, in the presence of incursions, those salt conditions can
rapidly become oxidizing. Selection of an alloy with acceptable performance at both extremes is
essential. In reducing conditions, the selected structural alloy must avoid alloying with uranium
or other salt constituents, while in oxidizing conditions the alloy must be able to display slow
rates of oxidation. To find alloys that work well across this range of conditions, we investigated
the corrosion of 316 SS, C-22, 18-8 SS, and Ag-coated 18-8 SS under a variety of ingression
scenarios.

Redox potential control is particularly important in practical settings wherein atmospheric
ingressions drive the salt potential towards increasingly oxidative environments. Several redox
control mechanisms have been proposed to maintain the salt’s potential within its designed
operating window [26]. Among the metrics driving the establishment of this design window are
redox potentials of relevant reactions (e.g. Cr → Cr 2+ + 2𝑒𝑒 − ) associated with the oxidation of
structural materials. Towards that end, this section focuses on characterizing corrosion rates of
relevant alloys to facilitate the informed design and operation of pyroprocessing unit operations.

Commercial alloys of interest to this work are listed in Table 8 along with their major gravimetric
compositions. It should be noted that the alloys tested in this work are meant to serve as
representative examples of commonly used families of structural alloy materials. Additional
analysis of materials is ongoing through the remainder of FY25 to holistically understand factors
that contribute to corrosion of specific materials.

5. Conclusions and Future Work
The work presented herein focused on continuing the development and understanding of
atmospheric ingressions on the generation of solid-phase particles in fuel reprocessing
equipment. In FY24, the interactions of oxygen ingression at various process conditions were
studied. FY25 concentrated on continuing these studies to characterize interactions with
moisture. Solid-phase particle morphology and compositions were related to electrochemical
signatures and compared to results from FY24. Provisional characterization efforts suggested
the formation of oxychlorides and oxides in both moisture and oxygen ingression experiments.
From an operational standpoint, the data this year emphasizes the need to eliminate oxygen and
moisture ingressions to the greatest degree possible to avoid solid particle formation. If
ingressions cannot be fully eliminated through gas purification or headspace cover gas, particle
filtration mechanisms should be adopted at a regular cadence using filtration media less than 1
micron to capture and transfer the particles back into the upstream oxide reduction processes.
Doing so will ensure improved process efficiencies and proper material accounting over long-
duration operations. Salt sampling approaches should also use filters with less than 1 micron
filter sizes to ensure representative samples of the salt are not disaffected by the presence of
solids.

 25

Beyond particle studies, experimental data was collected on industrially relevant alloys under
oxidative and corrosive conditions. Intergranular corrosion and metal depletion layers were
observed microscopically and confirmed spectroscopically on alloys exposed to moisture
ingressions. In-line electrochemical sensors were used to track the dissolution of structural
materials into the bulk salt throughout these tests. Overall, the work again emphasized the need
to avoid the ingression of impurities into the salt during operations. While coatings or nickel
alloys showed improved performance compared to standard stainless steel, the long-term
viability of these alternate approaches is unclear due to cost and machinability considerations.
Ultimately, longer term testing must be conducted to understand alloy performance both in very
reducing and very oxidizing conditions to ensure that the materials adequately perform across
the full spectrum of conditions that may be encountered in fuel reprocessing equipment.

Future work remains to more fully understand corrosion and particle formation phenomena in
realistic fuel processing equipment. At present, details regarding the interplay between combined
oxygen and moisture ingression remain unexplored, and several key thermochemical and kinetic
properties for relevant species have yet to be measured (e.g., activities of oxides and other
oxygen anions in the LiCl-KCl salt). The effect of the presence of Ce and U metal during
ingression testing was examined in earlier reports, but more information regarding the presence
and use of reactive metals as sacrificial anodes should be investigated. Further examination of
new alloys and coatings is warranted to find if a suitable choice of material can be found that is
able to tolerate a full range of possible salt redox potentials. Additionally, coatings require
further investigation to understand the potential for localized corrosion at pinholes or surface
defects, and also to determine the required thicknesses. Ultimately, this year’s efforts and future
studies will serve to delineate and identify optimized structural materials capable of satisfying
the operations, maintenance, and economical requirements of commercial-scale fuel processing
operations.
