In a sticky situation: a case report on pulmonary glue embolism complicated by an inflammatory myopericardial syndrome
Authors: Nicolas Bradt, Matthias Dullaert, Peter Vanlangenhove, Michel De Pauw, Els Vandecasteele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTissue glue is used in a wide range of different medical procedures and although complications are rare, they can be challenging to manage.Case summaryA 51-year old man presented to our hospital with progressive dyspnoea, haemoptysis and fever following cyanoacrylate injection therapy for erectile dysfunction. A CT-scan revealed a non-thrombotic pulmonary embolism (NTPE) with tissue glue based on the non-contrast images showing multiple radiodense foci. Introduction

Tissue glue such as cyanoacrylate is widely used in medical practice. It was first described in 1949 for skin wound closure and later, in 2011, as treatment of vascular pathology.1 Cyanoacrylate exists in four forms: methyl-, ethyl-, butyl-, and octylcyanoacrylate. They are used industrially, commercially, and medically. The butyl- and octyl- forms are used in healthcare. These compounds remain liquid at room temperature but polymerize into a solid cast within 10–20 s upon contact with ionic solutions such as blood thereby obturating the vessel.1,2 Lysophospholipids are added as they provide radiopacity and modulate the polymerization rate.2

Although cyanoacrylate injections (CI) with n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate are generally considered safe, complications such as glue migration have been reported. Migration of glue into the pulmonary circulation results in non-thrombotic pulmonary embolism (NTPE), a rare but potentially life-threatening condition.3,4 Its true prevalence remains uncertain, as many cases are asymptomatic.2,4

We report a case of a patient who underwent a CI with a mixture of n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate (Glubran) and lysophospholipids (Lipidol) for erectile dysfunction due to venous leakage. The embolization was complicated with both pulmonary embolism and an inflammatory myopericardial syndrome (IMPS).

Case presentation

A 51-year-old man presented to the emergency department due to complaints of exertional dyspnoea, fever, and chills. One week earlier, the patient had undergone an elective interventional radiological procedure for erectile dysfunction. He was diagnosed with venous leakage of the dorsal penile vein, external pudendal vein, and prostatic venous plexus. This was treated with a retrograde venous embolization with a mixture of Glubran with Lipidol as tissue glue. The 10 millilitres (ml) of a mixture of 4 mL Glubran and 8 mL Lipidol was used in the dorsal penile vein and prostatic venous plexus. Subsequently, 3 mL of glue (ratio Glubran/Lipidol: 1/4) was used for the leakage in the external pudendal vein. During the procedure embolization to the femoral vein was observed. As part of a standard procedure, a chest X-ray was made showing embolization of the glue to the pulmonary arteries (Figure 1). As the patient remained asymptomatic, he was discharged.

Fluoroscopic chest X ray showing the multiple glue emboli in the pulmonary arteries (arrows).

At presentation the patient reported progressive dyspnoea, haemoptysis, and fever. He was tachycardic (heart rate of 118 beats per minute [bpm]) and had an oxygen saturation level of 90% at ambient air. Blood pressure was slightly reduced at 105/52milimeters of mercury (mmHg). Body temperature was 38.1 degrees Celsius. There were no cardiac murmurs but there were inspiratory crackles at both lung bases. The jugular veins were non-distended. Abdominal examination was unremarkable. There was no peripheral oedema. The puncture site was slightly inflamed.

His electrocardiogram showed a normal sinus rhythm at 84 b.p.m. The atrioventricular conduction was normal with an isoelectric PR segment. There was a slight concave ST-segment elevation possibly compatible with either benign early repolarization or an early sign of pericarditis.

Laboratory testing revealed a mild leucocytosis of 11 770 per mL with 74.7% neutrophils. There was a normal level of haemoglobin, lactate, renal function, and Troponin T. The C-reactive protein (CRP) was markedly increased at 316 mg/L. An arterial blood gas analysis showed respiratory alkalosis with a pH level of 7.47, a partial pressure of carbon dioxide of 32.7 mmHg and a hypoxaemia with a partial pressure of oxygen of 61.5 mmHg.

Due to the clinical suspicion of progressive pulmonary emboli a chest computed tomography (CT) scan was performed. While contrast-enhanced images showed homogeneous filling of the pulmonary arteries, non-contrast images revealed multiple radiodense foci within segmental and subsegmental branches (Figure 2, panel 2, A–C), consistent with NTPE due to cyanoacrylate embolization. There were some focal manifestations of pneumonitis around the affected blood vessels. Echocardiographic and CT graphic, there was no right ventricular dilation (RV/LV ratio of 0.8), pulmonary hypertension nor significant pericardial effusion.

Panel of four chest computed tomography images. Panel A: axial pulmonary artery filling phase with a normal opacification of the pulmonary trunk. Panel B: axial bone window showing multiple radiodense foci (arrows) within the pulmonary arteries. Panel C: coronal bone window showing the same radiodense foci. Panel D: bone window showing a radiodense focus within the right ventricle.

The patient was admitted to the pulmonology ward and was started on oxygen therapy combined with antibiotics (amoxicillin and clavulanic acid). The antibiotics were started for suspected puncture site infection. Clinical improvement was noted, with resolution of chest pain, dyspnoea, as well as a significant reduction in CRP levels.

On the fourth day of hospitalization, the patient experienced an increase in chest pain. The pain was sharp with pleuritic character and was severe enough to cause a syncope. After a brief loss of consciousness, he remained haemodynamically stable. Repeat electrocardiogram showed increased widespread concave ST-segment elevation (Figure 3). A transthoracic echocardiography showed a diffuse pericardial effusion with partial right atrial collapse (Figure 4, videos are visible in the online supplements). There were no signs of pericardial tamponade. Troponin levels rose to 112 pg/mL. A control CT scan of the chest was performed, with a non-contrast and a contrast window. In addition to the stable NTPE, there was also a radiodense focus in the right ventricle (Figure 2, panel D), suggesting embedded tissue glue. The patient was transferred to the cardiology department for further therapy and rhythm monitoring.

The findings were consistent with the diagnosis of an inflammatory myopericardial syndrome. Anti-inflammatory therapy with high dose aspirin (1 g t.i.d.) combined with colchicine (0.5 mg bid) was initiated. Gastric protection with a proton pump inhibitor was associated. During the hospitalization the troponin level rose to 186 pg/mL. Following administration of the anti-inflammatory treatment, there was a fast improvement in the symptoms within 24 h. The patient was hospitalized for a total of 14 days for regular ultrasound and blood test follow-up given the high troponin and CRP level and monitoring for any arrhythmic events. The troponin level normalized and the C-reactive protein level dropped to 17.7 mg/L. An echocardiographic control showed a resolution of the pericardial effusion. The patient was discharged on Day 14 of the hospitalization with an tapering aspirin regimen (500 mg reduction every 2 weeks) and colchicine for 3 months. Ambulatory follow-up was planned at 1 month after the hospitalization. On follow-up there were no complaints of chest pain nor dyspnoea. The ECG showed sinus rhythm with a normal atrioventricular conduction and repolarization. A transthoracic ultrasound was performed showing no residual pericardial effusion nor were there signs of pulmonary hypertension. Biochemically the CRP level became normal. Pulmonary function testing showed a mildly reduced diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide, namely of 69%. The tapering schedule of the anti-inflammatory therapy was fulfilled.

Discussion

We describe a patient who developed two simultaneous complications, namely non-thrombotic pulmonary embolisms and an inflammatory myopericardial syndrome following CI therapy for erectile dysfunction. To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case describing these two complications occurring together after CI and only the second published report of myopericardial inflammation associated with cyanoacrylate glue injection.5

CI therapy for erectile dysfunction is considered generally safe and effective. In a review of 50 patients, asymptomatic pulmonary embolism occurred in 4% of cases with no reports of pericardial inflammation.6 Risk factors for pulmonary glue embolism mainly described in endoscopic therapy include injection volume and polymer concentration, both of which influence systemic migration.7,8 For diagnosing NTPE, it is recommended to perform a CT scan without iodine contrast administration. This is because the NTPE, like the glue embolism in our case, is often too radio-opaque to distinguish from the contrast. For this reason, it is recommended to perform a CT scan in, for example, bone window.9

Cyanoacrylate is known to induce an endothelial inflammatory response, resulting in fibrosis and systemic immune-mediated cytokine activation.10 A real-world cohort of patients undergoing cyanoacrylate closure of the superficial saphenous veins demonstrated hypersensitivity reactions in approximately 10% of cases.11

Myopericardial inflammation after cyanoacrylate-based therapies is poorly described in the literature. Local pericardial inflammation has been reported in only one case following gastric variceal obturation with cyanoacrylate.12 Pericarditis has also been associated with endoscopic variceal sclerotherapy (EVS). In that context, a case of purulent pericarditis has been documented.6 Caletti et al. further hypothesized that the frequent chest discomfort observed after EVS may reflect unrecognized inflammatory changes within the oesophageal wall and adjacent tissues, potentially resulting in mild and underdiagnosed pericarditis.13

The simultaneous occurrence of pulmonary embolism and myopericardial inflammation supports the hypothesis that distribution of adhesive material (or its metabolites) can lead to a hypersensitivity-driven inflammatory cascade. In our case there is a radio dense focus in the right ventricle with a possibility of embedded glue. We hypothesize that this, combined with the systemic reaction, could trigger a local inflammatory response resulting in IMPS.

In terms of treatment, guideline directed anti-inflammatory therapy for pericarditis was initiated. We started with high-dose aspirin (in a tapering regimen) combined with colchicine14 as is custom in our centre to treat an IMPS. At present, it is unclear which therapy is indicated for treating this type of IMPS. There is no known difference in outcome between aspirin or other non-steroidal anti-inflammatory therapy. There are some reports using corticosteroids for more severe cases such as an acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).1,4 Furthermore, there is insufficient literature establishing a standard treatment for NTPE. It is unclear whether it is necessary to start anticoagulant therapy. A recent case report by Longueville et al. demonstrated that there is a possibility of forming a classical thrombosis on top of NTPE suggesting therapy with anticoagulation could be useful. These data were not yet available at the time of treatment of this case.4 Further data to establish a standardized therapeutic regime is needed.

Overall, our case underscores the need for a heightened clinical awareness regarding potential systemic inflammatory and embolic complications of CI. Further research is warranted to determine procedure risk stratification and to optimize procedures including adjustments in injection volume and viscosity, to minimize complications.
