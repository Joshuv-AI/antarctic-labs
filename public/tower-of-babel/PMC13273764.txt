Magnetic resonance imaging for diagnosing residual malignancy after excisional biopsy for breast cancer: Comparison with excisional biopsy margin status
Authors: Ji Hee Kim, Hera Jung, Ji Hee Park, Jieun Koh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of dynamic contrast-enhanced breast MRI and to compare it with excisional biopsy margin status in predicting residual malignancy after excisional biopsy for breast cancer.MaterialsThis retrospective study included 170 patients who underwent excisional biopsy followed by subsequent definitive surgery between February 2020 and October 2023. Clinical and MRI features were compared between patients with and without residual malignancy. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors of residual malignancy. Diagnostic performance was assessed for MRI findings, MRI final assessment, and excisional biopsy margin status.ResultsResidual malignancy was present in 88/170 patients (51.8%). Larger tumor size, positive excisional biopsy margins, and MRI final assessment suggestive of residual malignancy were significantly more frequent in the residual malignancy group (P ≤ 0.001). On multivariate analysis, positive excisional biopsy margins (OR: 2.753; P = 0.005) and MRI final assessment suggestive of residual malignancy (OR: 2.538; P = 0.009) were independently associated with residual malignancy. MRI final assessment showed lower sensitivity but higher specificity than excisional biopsy margin status (67.0% vs 73.9% and 62.2% vs 53.7%, respectively), without significant differences (P = 0.377 and 0.324). Combining both parameters improved sensitivity to 88.6% when either test was positive, and specificity to 80.5% when both tests were positive.ConclusionMRI final assessment demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to excisional biopsy margin. Their combined use may optimize diagnostic strategy according to clinical context, and MRI may provide complementary information, particularly when margin information is unavailable.

Introduction

Residual malignancy after excisional biopsy for breast cancer has been reported in 32–62% of cases [1]. Factors associated with residual malignancy after subsequent definitive surgery included larger tumor size, positive axillary lymph nodes, invasive lobular type, and positive margin of excisional biopsy [1], [2]. Dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE) breast MRI shows high sensitivity and specificity and is, therefore, useful for the preoperative staging of breast cancer [3]. After excisional biopsy, the postoperative granulation tissue shows enhancement, interfering with the diagnosis of residual malignancy and leading to false-positive findings [4]. However, previous studies have concluded that DCE breast MRI is useful for the diagnosis of residual malignancy after excisional biopsy of breast cancer [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9] or for finding multifocal or multicentric disease that can change the surgical plan [10].

The sensitivity and specificity of DCE breast MRI in diagnosing residual malignancy after excisional biopsy are reported to be 61.2–92.1% and 69.2–73.0%, respectively [5], [6], [8], [10]. The presence of thick irregular rim enhancement or nodular/non-mass enhancement with washout kinetics on MRI suggests residual malignancy [5]. However, since most studies included referred cases, detailed margin information from the initial excision was often unavailable and therefore not incorporated into analysis [5], [6], [8]. Moreover, MRI performance in prior studies was assessed through retrospective blinded reinterpretation, rather than based on original clinical reports [5], [6], [8].

To the best of our knowledge, the relative diagnostic value of MRI compared with excisional biopsy margin status has not been directly evaluated in a single cohort. Furthermore, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) was not included in the diagnosis of residual lesions in other studies [5], [6], [8]. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of DCE breast MRI, including DWI, based on original clinical MRI reports, and to compare it with excisional biopsy margin status in predicting residual malignancy.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board. However, since it was a retrospective study, patient approval or informed consent was not required for reviewing patient images and medical records.

Between February 2020 and October 2023, 1040 preoperative breast MRI scans were obtained from women diagnosed with breast cancer (Fig. 1). All consecutive eligible patients during the study period were included. Among them, 201 cases were diagnosed by surgical excisional biopsy. The indications for diagnostic surgical excision included calcifications requiring histologic confirmation because of suspicious morphology and/or distribution (n = 75), upgrade to malignancy after excision of core needle biopsy-diagnosed high-risk lesions such as atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) or papilloma (n = 39), imaging–pathology discordance after core needle biopsy (n = 25), excision of a palpable mass for diagnosis (n = 20), patient preference (n = 19), bloody nipple discharge (n = 12), and concern for possible core needle biopsy underestimation (e.g., non-mass lesions or suspected diffuse intraductal abnormalities) (n = 11). Thirty-one patients were excluded: those with low-risk ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) treated with radiation therapy without subsequent definitive surgery (n = 22), those referred to other institutions (n = 8), and those with missing margin data (n = 1). No patients were excluded because of incomplete MRI sequences or incomplete clinical information other than missing excisional biopsy margin status. Finally, 170 cases were included in the study population (mean age, 51.0 ± 10.8 years).Fig. 1Flowchart of study population selection.

MRI acquisition and interpretation

MRI was performed using Discovery MR750w 3.0 T (General Electric Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA), with a dedicated 8-channel surface breast coil. The scanning was performed with patients in the prone position. The MRI protocols included the following: an axial fat-suppressed T2-weighted image (repetition time, TR/ echo time, TE: 6028/81 msec; field of view, FOV: 38 × 38 cm; section thickness: 0.34 cm), DWI (TR/ TE: 3128/69 msec; FOV: 38 × 38 cm; section thickness: 0.4 cm), pre-contrast, 1- to 6-min dynamic contrast-enhanced fat-suppressed T1-weighted gradient-echo sequence (TR/TE: 3.9/1.7 msec; FOV: 38 × 38 cm; section thickness: 0.18 cm) after intravenous injection of 0.1 mmol/kilogram of gadoteridol (ProHance; Bracco Diagnostics, Monroe Township, NJ, USA) over 20 s with an injector at a flow rate of 2 mL/s. Subtracted images were generated from the dynamic sequences, and maximum intensity projection images were reconstructed from the subtraction images acquired 2 min after contrast injection. DWI sequences were obtained using echo-planar imaging with b-values of 0, 100, and 750 s/mm2. Software-synthesized DWI images were generated with b-values of 1200 and 1500 s/mm2. Apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps were generated from b-values of 0, 100, and 750 s/mm2. All MRI sequences had originally been interpreted by one of three dedicated breast radiologists with 3–9 years of experience. For this study, MRI reports were retrospectively reviewed to record whether residual malignancy had been suspected on MRI, as well as the documented enhancement pattern, kinetic curve assessment, and DWI findings.

Data and statistical analysis

Multiple factors, including tumor size, excisional biopsy method (US or mammography-guided localization), preoperative mammographic features (calcification vs. no calcification), preoperative US features (mass, non-mass, not observed), excisional biopsy margin status (positive for malignancy vs. negative), axillary lymph node metastasis, and histological cancer type of the excisional biopsy, were recorded. Tumor size was defined as the maximum lesion diameter measured on imaging before excisional biopsy; US measurements were used for sonographically visible lesions, whereas mammographic extent was used for lesions visible only as calcifications. MRI features, including background parenchymal enhancement (BPE), enhancement pattern (no enhancement, thin rim, thick rim, nodular or non-mass enhancement), enhancement kinetics (washout vs. no washout), and DWI (restriction vs. no restriction), and final assessment was recorded from MRI reports. The presence of residual pathologic malignancy after subsequent definitive surgery is regarded as a reference standard.

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t-test. Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of residual malignancy. Variables with P < 0.05 on univariate analysis, including tumor size, excisional biopsy margin status, and MRI final assessment, were entered into the model. Clinically relevant factors established in prior literature, namely axillary lymph node status and histological type, were also included regardless of univariate significance [1], [2]. Complete-case analysis was performed for variables with missing data; DWI analysis was restricted to 164 patients with available data. Diagnostic performance, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), accuracy, and area under the receiver operating curve (AUC), was analyzed for each MRI feature, MRI final assessment, excisional biopsy margin status, and their combination. McNemar's test was used to compare sensitivity and specificity between MRI final assessment and excisional biopsy margin status. The DeLong method was used for comparison of AUCs. Two combined criteria were also evaluated. Combined 1 was defined as cases in which either the excisional biopsy margin was positive or the MRI final assessment was suggestive of residual malignancy. Combined 2 was defined as cases in which both the excisional biopsy margin was positive and the MRI final assessment was suggestive of residual malignancy. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Of the 170 patients in this study, 88 (51.8%) had residual malignancy after subsequent definitive surgery. For subsequent definitive surgery, 146 (85.9%) underwent breast-conserving surgery, and 24 (14.1%) underwent total mastectomy. The mean tumor size measured on imaging before excisional biopsy was 2.0 ± 1.8 cm (range, 0.3–9.0 cm), and among patients with residual malignancy, the mean pathologic size of residual disease was 2.2 ± 2.5 cm (range, 0.1–10.0 cm). Mean interval between excisional biopsy and MRI was 11.3 ± 6.7 days (range, 1–46 days), while that between excisional biopsy and subsequent definitive surgery was 17.6 ± 9.6 days (range, 1–57 days). Of the 82 patients without residual malignancy, 17 had ADH and 4 had lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS) after subsequent definitive surgery. Calcification was observed on mammography in 81 (47.6%) patients.

Discussion

In this study, MRI final assessment showed diagnostic performance comparable to that of excisional biopsy margin for predicting residual malignancy after excisional biopsy, and both remained independently associated with residual malignancy on multivariate analysis. These results indicate that MRI and excisional biopsy margin offer complementary diagnostic information. In addition, combining these two assessments allowed the diagnostic threshold to be adjusted according to whether sensitivity or specificity was prioritized.

Excisional biopsy margins are important factors associated with residual malignancy [1], [2]. The prevalence of residual malignancy following excisional biopsy of breast cancer in our study was 51.8%, which is within the reported range of 33.0–74.5% [4], [5], [6], [8], [10]. In previous studies, most cases were referred; therefore, excisional biopsy margins were not considered in the evaluation of residual malignancy [5], [6], [8]. In contrast, since all excisional biopsies were initially performed at our hospital, the margins could be evaluated. Previous studies have primarily evaluated the intrinsic diagnostic performance of MRI using blinded retrospective image review [5], [6], [8]. In contrast, our study assessed MRI interpretation as performed in routine clinical practice. Therefore, the diagnostic performance observed in this study may better reflect the real-world clinical utility of MRI.

Although the differences in sensitivity and specificity were not statistically significant, excisional biopsy margin tended to show higher sensitivity (73.9% vs. 67.0%), whereas MRI tended to show higher specificity (62.2% vs. 53.7%) for diagnosing residual malignancy. The AUC of MRI final assessment was higher than excisional biopsy margin (0.646 vs. 0.638), without significant statistical difference. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that patients with positive excisional biopsy margins (odds ratio: 2.753; 95% CI: 1.351–5.610) and MRI final assessment suggestive of residual malignancy (odds ratio: 2.538; 95% CI: 1.26–5.112) were more likely to have residual malignancy. Our results suggest that, whenever available, both excisional biopsy margin status and MRI assessment should be considered when evaluating residual malignancy. In particular, MRI may provide additional clinical value in several specific scenarios: when margin status is unavailable, as is common in referred cases; when margins are equivocal and further information is needed to guide the decision for re-excision; or when residual malignancy remains clinically suspected despite negative margins. In these situations, MRI findings such as suspicious enhancement patterns or diffusion restriction may provide complementary information for surgical planning.

When excisional biopsy margin status was available, combining it with MRI final assessment allowed optimization of the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity according to the clinical context. Using either parameter as a positive criterion (Combined 1) achieved the highest sensitivity (88.6%), minimizing the risk of missing residual malignancy, which may be preferable when the clinical priority is to avoid undertreatment. Conversely, requiring both parameters to be positive (Combined 2) yielded the highest specificity (80.5%), reducing unnecessary re-excision, which may be favored when the goal is to avoid overtreatment. These findings suggest that the optimal combination strategy should be tailored to individual clinical circumstances, balancing the risks of undertreatment against those of unnecessary surgery.

Among the detailed MRI features, consistent with previous reports [5], thick irregular rim enhancement, nodular or non-mass enhancement, washout kinetics, and diffusion restriction were more frequently observed in the residual group than in the non-residual group. Enhancement pattern showed moderate sensitivity and specificity (70.5% and 56.1%), whereas washout kinetics and diffusion restriction showed relatively low sensitivity (22.7% and 40.7%) but high specificity (95.1% and 97.4%). These findings suggest that suspicious enhancement pattern is useful for identifying residual malignancy, whereas washout kinetics and diffusion restriction may increase diagnostic confidence because of their high specificity when present. Although the overall diagnostic performance of MRI in our study was slightly lower than that reported in prior studies, it remained within the published range [5], [6], [8], [10], possibly because our analysis was based on original clinical reports rather than retrospective rereading.

We also evaluated the diagnostic value of DWI for residual malignancy after excisional biopsy, unlike previous studies that analyzed only DCE-MRI [5], [6], [8], [10]. DWI showed the highest specificity in this setting, although its sensitivity was limited. This may be partly explained by the small size of residual lesions in our cohort, as DWI is known to have a higher false-negative rate in lesions ≤ 1 cm or in non-mass lesions [11], [12]. In addition, postoperative changes such as fat necrosis may also produce false-positive findings on DWI [12].

Of the 82 patients without residual malignancy, 21 (25.6%) had high-risk lesions such as ADH and LCIS. Overall, 36 patients showed suspicious enhancement patterns, which revealed no residual malignancy; some of the cases were high-risk lesions, and the other one was a postoperative inflammatory change [13]. Considering locoregional recurrence, whether further excision should be performed after breast-conserving surgery with positive margins of ADH and LCIS remains controversial [14], [15]. In our study, although there was no residual malignancy, a considerable number of cases were residual high-risk lesions, and this should be considered when planning subsequent definitive surgery.

This study had several limitations. First, 31 patients were excluded, including 22 with low-risk ductal carcinoma in situ who underwent radiation therapy without subsequent definitive surgery. As residual malignancy could not be pathologically confirmed in these patients, this may have introduced selection bias and limited the generalizability of our findings. Second, since patients with invasive lobular cancer or lymph node metastasis comprised only a small proportion of the study population, we could not confirm their association with positive excisional margins. Third, as MRI interpretations were performed with knowledge of clinical context, interpretive bias cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, this approach more closely reflects routine clinical practice and may offer a more clinically relevant estimate of MRI diagnostic performance than blinded retrospective reinterpretation. Fourth, inter-reader variability was not formally assessed, as MRI interpretations were performed by one of three radiologists in routine clinical practice rather than through a structured multi-reader study design. Differences in interpretive thresholds among radiologists may have introduced variability in MRI assessment, limiting the reproducibility and generalizability of the findings. Fifth, the timing of MRI after excisional biopsy may have influenced diagnostic performance. In our study, MRI was performed a mean of 11.3 days after excisional biopsy, and early postoperative changes may have affected MRI interpretation [5], [16]. Therefore, timing-related bias should be considered when interpreting our results.

In conclusion, MRI final assessment demonstrated diagnostic performance comparable to that of excisional biopsy margin in predicting residual malignancy after excisional biopsy of breast cancer. MRI and margin status provided complementary information, and their combined use may offer additional clinical value by allowing diagnostic performance to be tailored according to the clinical context.
