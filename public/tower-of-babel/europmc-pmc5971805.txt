A. Sepsis.?It is well known that the incidence of sepsis in
diabetic subjects almost invariably leads to an exacerbation
of the diabetic symptoms. It has also long been observed
that glycosuria is a common accompaniment of septic infec-
tions. It follows, then, that the latter are concerned in the
interference with carbohydrate metabolism. With regard to
the first observation, it is interesting to find that some of the
older writers claimed that diabetes was favourably influenced
by the intervention of acute infection.
Naunyn 1 was one of these, but at the same time he refers to cases where
diabetics who were temporarily sugar-free showed a return of sugar after the
onset of infection. He mentions the fact that glycosuria increases with the
development of boils, carbuncles, or gangrene, and diminishes or disappears
with removal of the septic focus.
Von Noorden2 found that the urine in diabetic patients became less
saccharose or entirely sugar-free during the intercurrence of an acute pyretic
infectious disease, but he also believed that certain instances where diabetes
appeared to commence during or after infection were really cases in which a
latent or temporarily aglycosuric diabetes had been revived by the infectious
disease.
Stengel's 3 experience is that intercurrent infections increase the severity of
an active diabetes. He describes cases in which he observed this, the infections
concerned including facial erysipelas, influenza, and bronchopneumonia.
Numerous other writers have drawn attention to this relationship between
infection and diabetes.
Regarding the second observation, that glycosuria frequently accompanies
septic infections, it was discussed in 1878 by Prevost4 in a paper on
Sapraemic Glycosuria," and more fully by Redard5 in 1886. In 1892
Spencer 6 described some cases in which glycosuria had been greatly lessened
by removal of the septic focus, e.g., by amputation of a gangrenous limb.
In 1894 Cuthbert Wallace 7 described similar cases. In a subsequent paper 8
he gave the results of his examination of these cases by means of a urinary

Later Liefmann and Stern 12 found marked increase in the blood sugar in
febrile conditions (pneumonia) without glycosuria. Hollinger13 also found
high blood-sugar figures in pneumonia, &c. Another worker, Tachau,14 con-
firmed these observations. More recently, in 1920, Grigaut, Brodin, and
Rouzaud 15 showed that there was an excess of sugar in the blood in infectious
diseases, and Poll 16 and Campagnolle 17 demonstrated a distinct lowering of
tolerance for glucose in such cases. Cammidge18 finds that even a simple
febrile cold raises the blood-sugar content and lowers the sugar tolerance.
Pringle and Millar19 have found definite lowering of tolerance in arthritis,
particularly of the severe infective type, and in muscular fibrositis. Rabino-
witcli 20 reported the frequent association of hyperglycemia and disease of the
gall-bladder, and states that over 80 per cent of cases of cholelithiasis show
hyperglycemia.
B. Endocrine disturbances.?The fact that glycosuria is of
common occurrence in cases of exophthalmic goitre and of
pituitary disease is also generally recognized. Langdon
Brown21 has recently examined the response of the blood-
sugar in the former group of cases to the ingestion of glucose
and invariably found that glycosuria was induced. . In one of
his patients, suffering from exophthalmic goitre of fifteen
years' standing, whose resting blood-sugar was normal, the
glucose test yielded a typically diabetic curve. Similar occur-
rences have been recorded in disease of the pituitary gland and
in experimental lesions of that organ.
In 1911, Goetsch, Gushing, and Jacobson22 described experiments on animals
from which the hypophysis cerebri had been excised. The administration of
glucose resulted in transient glycosuria. These workers also found that in
acromegaly and gigantism glycosuria was the rule at first, but that, later on,
carbohydrate tolerance was increased. Ellis23 described a case of hyper-
glycemia and glycosuria in acromegaly; removal of a soft pituitary tumour
resulted in the disappearance of these phenomena. In 19*24 Langdon Brown 21
referred to the frequent association of pituitary glycosuria with such conditions
as acromegaly, diminished sexual development, and obesity.
The available evidence thus points clearly to the adverse
influence of infections and of endocrine disturbances on
carbohydrate metabolism, and it seemed desirable to pursue

hospital. The examination in septic cases included 10 patients
who were known to be suffering from diabetes and also from
some form of sepsis, and 50 who were suffering from a sepsis

Before proceeding to the consideration of these cases, it
would be well at this point to explain the method of classifica-
tion which has been used to distinguish the various types of
glucose curve obtained. There is, first, the " normal " type
(N), where the blood-sugar fails to exceed at any point the
renal threshold of 018 per cent and returns within two hours
to normal, no glycosuria occurring. Secondly, there is what
may be called the " definitely diabetic " type (D), in which
the blood-sugar rises above the threshold and is still above
013 per cent at the end of two hours, glycosuria occurring
in the majority. The third group consists of " unclassified
cases (U), which cannot be called either " normal " or
" definitely diabetic " in type, but which show some slight
abnormality in the direction of diminished tolerance. In some
the blood-sugar rises rapidly above the threshold but returns
wTithin two hours to the pre-glucose level; this form indicates
delayed sugar-storage. In others there is no such rapid rise
above the threshold, but the blood-sugar at the end of two
hours is still above the starting-level.

Discussion of results.? The results of this investigation go
to support the findings already reported. It seems clear that
some analogy can be drawn between the disturbed carbohydrate
metabolism found in diabetes proper, and that in diabetes with
superadded sepsis, in sepsis alone, and in endocrine disturb-
ances. It seems reasonable to suppose that in sepsis there is
produced in the body some toxin or toxins which are at the
root of the metabolic disturbance, but in what way these act
remains to be explained. One is led to wonder to what extent
sepsis and infections play a part in bringing about the condition
of diabetes?by causing, in the first place, non-storage or
non-utilization of sugar with consequent liyperglycapmia and
glycosuria, followed by overstrain of the pancreatic function;

and finally, if sufficiently prolonged or severe, by a complete
breakdown of the latter.
This theory appears to be supported by the fact that in going
over the post-mortem records of diabetic subjects one finds only
a small number in which the pancreas shows any gross lesion.
Microscopically,*the commonest lesion appears to be increase
of fibrous tissue and vacuolation of the cells of the islands of
Langerhans, an appearance consistent with the theory of
overstrain. As regards the post-mortem findings in the pan-
creas in exophthalmic goitre associated with glycosuria, Opie21
and Cecil23 hold that the glycosuria in such cases is due to
pathological changes in the pancreas, and state that lesions
of this gland have been found in some fatal cases. Opie24
also states that chronic pancreatitis has frequently been found
at autopsy in association with acromegaly. As regards acute
and chronic sepsis, little or no detailed information is available
concerning the pathology of the pancreas, either naked-eye or
microscopic, and further work is required on the subject.
Summary and conclusions.?With a view to studying the
effect of septic infections and endocrine diseases on carbo-
hydrate metabolism, an investigation was carried out by means
of the glucose tolerance test on 10 cases of diabetes with sepsis,
50 cases of sepsis alone, 13 of exophthalmic goitre, and 3 of
pituitary disease, with results which point to the following
conclusions :?
1. Sepsis aggravates an existing diabetes.
2. Many cases of sepsis show temporarily defective carbo-
hydrate metabolism.
3. Endocrine disturbances, notably exophthalmic goitre, are
frequently associated with defective carbohydrate metabolism.
