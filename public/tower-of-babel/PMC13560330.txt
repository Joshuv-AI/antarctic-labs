Nephrologist-performed point-of-care ultrasound enables early recognition of PTFE hemodialysis graft pseudoaneurysm: a case report
Authors: José Lascano Contreras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPseudoaneurysm is a recognized complication of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) hemodialysis grafts and may be associated with rupture, infection, thrombosis, and vascular access loss. While Doppler ultrasound findings are well established, the incorporation of nephrologist-performed point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) within dialysis units may facilitate timely bedside recognition and expedited referral in high-risk patients. We report the case of a 53-year-old woman with end-stage kidney disease secondary to lupus nephritis, receiving maintenance hemodialysis since 2013, who presented with pain, swelling, fever, local warmth, and a rapidly enlarging pulsatile mass over a brachioaxillary PTFE graft. Physical examination revealed hyperemia, thinning of the overlying skin, and cutaneous shininess suggestive of impending rupture. Bedside POCUS performed by the attending nephrologist inside the dialysis unit demonstrated a 1.94 × 0.6 cm pseudoaneurysm communicating with the graft lumen through a narrow neck measuring approximately 0.24 × 0.35 cm, associated with turbulent bidirectional flow and the characteristic “yin–yang” sign on color Doppler imaging. The examination enabled immediate bedside confirmation and expedited referral to a tertiary vascular center. Computed tomography angiography (CTA), requested for surgical planning and evaluation of lesion extent and suspected contained rupture, confirmed active contrast extravasation. Due to vascular access exhaustion, an initial conservative strategy with broad-spectrum antibiotics was attempted. Blood cultures subsequently grew Enterococcus faecium, and transthoracic echocardiography revealed tricuspid valve infective endocarditis with severe tricuspid regurgitation. Despite partial infectious improvement with directed antimicrobial therapy, graft excision was ultimately required and was complicated by intraoperative hemorrhagic shock. The patient required prolonged intensive care admission, central venous angioplasties, and tunneled femoral catheter placement for dialysis continuation. This case illustrates the feasibility of nephrologist-performed POCUS in the dialysis unit as a rapid problem-focused tool for early recognition of vascular access complications and expedited referral for definitive management. Introduction

Pseudoaneurysm is a recognized complication of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) hemodialysis grafts and may be associated with rupture, thrombosis, infection, and vascular access loss [1, 2]. Its incidence increases with graft longevity, particularly in long-standing accesses subjected to repetitive cannulation and venous outflow abnormalities [1–3].

Doppler ultrasound represents the first-line imaging modality for evaluation of vascular access complications because it allows dynamic assessment of graft morphology, flow abnormalities, aneurysms, pseudoaneurysms, thrombosis, and infectious complications [4, 5]. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) may provide complementary anatomical information for therapeutic planning in selected cases [6].

More recently, point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as an important extension of nephrology bedside assessment, enabling real-time evaluation and expedited clinical decision-making directly inside dialysis units [7, 8]. Rather than replacing conventional imaging, nephrologist-performed POCUS may facilitate rapid problem-focused bedside assessment in symptomatic patients and expedite referral for definitive management.

Instead of presenting pseudoaneurysm diagnosis itself as a novel finding, this report aims to highlight the role of nephrologist-performed point-of-care ultrasound as a bedside workflow tool within the dialysis unit, enabling timely recognition of a high-risk vascular access complication and expedited referral for definitive management.

Case report

A 53-year-old woman with end-stage kidney disease secondary to lupus nephritis, receiving maintenance hemodialysis since 2013, presented with a brachioaxillary PTFE graft created four years earlier due to exhaustion of native vascular access options. Her medical history was notable for multiple prior vascular access procedures, superior vena cava stenosis, chronic hepatitis C previously treated with sofosbuvir, and longstanding dialysis vintage. Cannulation had been routinely performed using a rope-ladder technique.

The patient presented to the dialysis unit with a two-day history of progressive pain, local swelling, fever, increased temperature over the graft trajectory, and development of a rapidly enlarging pulsatile mass following the most recent cannulation session. Physical examination revealed hyperemia, tenderness, localized edema, thinning and shininess of the overlying skin, raising concern for impending rupture and possible graft infection.

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) was immediately performed at bedside inside the dialysis unit by the attending nephrologist using a Samsung HM70 EVO ultrasound system equipped with a high-frequency linear transducer (3–16 MHz) using B-mode and color Doppler imaging. B-mode imaging was acquired using a resolution preset with gain adjusted to 48, while color Doppler evaluation was performed using penetration mode with color gain set to 52 and pulse repetition frequency (PRF) adjusted to 5.0 kHz to optimize detection of turbulent flow within the pseudoaneurysm cavity. The examination was performed as a rapid problem-focused bedside assessment triggered by acute vascular access symptoms.

B-mode longitudinal imaging demonstrated a 1.94 × 0.6 cm pseudoaneurysm cavity adjacent to the graft with direct luminal communication through a narrow neck measuring approximately 0.24 cm in length and 0.35 cm in width, located near the most recent cannulation site (Fig. 1a). Color Doppler imaging demonstrated turbulent bidirectional flow with the characteristic “yin–yang” sign, confirming pseudoaneurysm formation [4, 5] (Fig. 1b). Spectral and power Doppler interrogation were not performed because the examination was intended as a rapid bedside problem-focused assessment aimed at immediate clinical decision-making.

(b) Color Doppler imaging demonstrating turbulent bidirectional flow with the characteristic ‘yin–yang’ sign (green arrow). The patient was urgently referred to a tertiary vascular surgery center. Computed tomography angiography, requested by the vascular surgery team to better delineate lesion extent, evaluate suspected contained rupture associated with active extravasation, and assist surgical planning, confirmed a saccular graft pseudoaneurysm with active contrast extravasation (Fig. 2a–b).

(a) CT angiography demonstrating saccular graft dilation (green arrow). (b) Contrast enhancement with active extravasation (green arrow)

Given the patient’s severe vascular access exhaustion, an initial conservative strategy was attempted while broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy was initiated. Blood cultures subsequently grew Enterococcus faecium. Due to persistent bacteremia, transthoracic echocardiography was performed and demonstrated infective endocarditis involving the tricuspid valve associated with severe/massive tricuspid regurgitation secondary to valvular vegetation.

Following directed antimicrobial therapy, partial resolution of the infectious process was achieved. However, due to ongoing concern for graft-related infection and risk of rupture, definitive graft excision was ultimately performed. The procedure was complicated by severe intraoperative bleeding with hemorrhagic shock requiring intensive care support. The patient remained in the intensive care unit for approximately two weeks.

Because of extensive central venous disease and vascular access exhaustion, the patient subsequently required multiple superior and inferior vena cava angioplasties during the same hospitalization to maintain dialysis access options, ultimately requiring tunneled long-term catheter placement in the left femoral vein for continuation of hemodialysis. At short-term follow-up, the patient remained dependent on tunneled femoral catheter dialysis access, as she was not considered a suitable candidate for peritoneal dialysis or creation of a new PTFE graft due to severe vascular access limitations.

Discussion

Pseudoaneurysm is a well-recognized complication of PTFE grafts, particularly in long-standing vascular accesses exposed to repetitive cannulation, graft degeneration, infection, and venous outflow abnormalities [1–3]. In the present case, pseudoaneurysm formation occurred four years after PTFE graft creation, likely reflecting cumulative graft degeneration associated with prolonged dialysis vintage, repeated cannulation exposure, and advanced vascular access disease. Therefore, the novelty of the present report does not lie in the diagnosis itself, but rather in the workflow and timing of recognition within the dialysis unit.

The main contribution of this report lies in demonstrating nephrologist-performed bedside POCUS as a practical dialysis-unit workflow tool capable of enabling timely recognition and expedited referral of a high-risk vascular access complication [7, 8]. Importantly, the present report describes symptom-triggered problem-focused bedside POCUS rather than routine vascular access surveillance ultrasound. In this patient, bedside ultrasound enabled immediate confirmation of pseudoaneurysm, identification of high-risk features, and rapid referral before overt rupture occurred. This reinforces the evolving role of POCUS as an extension of nephrology practice beyond procedural guidance alone [7, 8].

Importantly, POCUS should be interpreted as an extension of bedside clinical examination rather than a replacement for it [7, 8]. In this context, bedside POCUS complemented physical examination by improving anatomical characterization of the lesion and increasing diagnostic confidence during immediate dialysis-unit assessment. In the present case, physical findings including pain, pulsatile enlargement, local warmth, hyperemia, edema, and thinning of the overlying skin already suggested a high-risk lesion. Ultrasound findings complemented these red flags by demonstrating luminal communication and turbulent bidirectional flow compatible with pseudoaneurysm formation [4, 5].

Several findings observed in this patient have been associated with increased risk of rupture, including rapid enlargement, skin compromise, local inflammatory changes, and active contrast extravasation on CTA [4, 6]. Interestingly, despite the presence of these concerning features, an initial conservative strategy was attempted due to the patient’s extreme vascular access complexity and limited alternative access sites. This highlights the challenging balance between rupture risk and vascular preservation frequently encountered in patients with prolonged dialysis vintage and exhausted vascular access options [9, 10].

In this context, CTA served an important complementary role. Rather than establishing the diagnosis itself, which had already been achieved bedside through POCUS, CTA was requested by the vascular surgery team to better characterize lesion extent, evaluate active extravasation and suspected contained rupture, assess adjacent tissue involvement, and assist definitive surgical planning [6].

This case also illustrates the substantial infectious and vascular burden associated with advanced dialysis access disease. The coexistence of local inflammatory findings, persistent Enterococcus faecium bacteremia, and tricuspid valve infective endocarditis strongly raised concern for graft-related infection. Additionally, the patient’s prolonged dialysis vintage, multiple prior vascular interventions, central venous stenosis, and eventual requirement for vena cava angioplasties and tunneled femoral catheter placement underscore the complexity of vascular access management in this population [9, 10]. At short-term follow-up, tunneled femoral catheter access remained the only feasible dialysis access option due to severe vascular access limitations and unsuitability for peritoneal dialysis or creation of a new PTFE graft.

This report has limitations inherent to a single-case observational description. Spectral and power Doppler interrogation were not performed during bedside assessment, and long-term vascular access outcomes were unavailable. Additionally, POCUS findings are operator-dependent and may vary according to nephrologist training and expertise. Clinical photographs documenting skin changes and local examination findings were also unavailable.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the feasibility of nephrologist-performed POCUS in the dialysis unit as a rapid problem-focused bedside tool for early recognition of vascular access complications and expedited referral for definitive management. In the present case, POCUS performed by the treating nephrologist inside the dialysis unit enabled timely bedside recognition of a high-risk pseudoaneurysm in a patient with advanced vascular access exhaustion before overt free rupture occurred. Although limited by its single-case design, operator dependency, and lack of long-term vascular access outcomes, this report supports the feasibility and highlights the potential workflow advantages of integrating bedside POCUS into dialysis access assessment [7, 8].
