The ESR1-MYH14 axis functions as a critical driver of cervical cancer progression and metastasis

Introduction

Cervical cancer ranks as the fourth most prevalent and deadly malignancy affecting women globally, with a particularly devastating impact in developing nations where incidence rates continue to rise. Current statistics reveal approximately 600,000 new diagnoses and over 340,000 fatalities annually [1]. Histologically, squamous cell carcinoma predominates, comprising 70–80% of cases, while adenocarcinomas account for 20–25% [2,3]. Although early-stage cervical cancer generally offers more favorable outcomes, disease progression significantly diminishes survival prospects. Despite being the standard first-line treatment for advanced disease, cisplatin-based chemotherapy achieves only modest success, with five-year overall survival rates of merely 68% for primary disease and below 20% for metastatic cases [4,5]. The dismal prognosis associated with advanced cervical cancer stems largely from chemotherapeutic and endocrine therapy resistance, which present formidable obstacles in managing advanced and recurrent disease [6]. Metastasis represents the critical process driving tumor progression and is the predominant cause of cancer-related mortality, encompassing multiple genetic, epigenetic, biochemical, and physical alterations within both cancer cells and their surrounding microenvironment [7]. To successfully disseminate throughout the body, tumor cells must undergo significant morphological adaptations in contractility and deformability that enable them to penetrate tissue barriers and navigate through bloodstream, lymphatic vessels, and neighboring structures [7]. These challenges underscore the urgent necessity for expanded research into the fundamental molecular mechanisms driving cervical cancer pathogenesis and metastatic processes. Such investigations will facilitate the identification of novel diagnostic biomarkers and, crucially, reveal promising therapeutic targets—particularly those addressing the mechanical processes of metastatic spread—to improve treatment efficacy and patient outcomes.

Estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), a nuclear transcription factor, plays a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation, differentiation, and survival in hormone-responsive malignancies [8]. In breast cancer, ESR1 is expressed in over 70% of cases and serves as both a critical prognostic marker and therapeutic target, with ESR1-positive tumors demonstrating distinct biological behaviors and treatment responses [9]. Emerging evidence suggests that ESR1 also contributes to cervical cancer pathogenesis, with mutations identified in cervical cancer patients and its signaling involved in the development of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia [10,11]. Studies have demonstrated that ESR1 signaling influences epithelial-mesenchymal transition, invasion, and therapeutic resistance in gynecological malignancies [12]. Understanding ESR1-mediated regulatory pathways in cervical cancer could reveal novel therapeutic vulnerabilities and expand treatment options beyond conventional approaches.

The myocardin family, a specialized group of molecular motors within the broader myosin superfamily, demonstrates ubiquitous expression across eukaryotic organisms [13]. Accumulating evidence indicates that specific members of this family, particularly myosin heavy chain 14 (MYH14), play significant roles in cancer progression. Research has established that these proteins modulate cancer cell behaviors including migration and invasion—key processes in tumor advancement and metastasis formation [13]. Previous studies revealed that MYH14 reduction diminished cellular responsiveness to chemotherapeutic agents and hormonal therapies while paradoxically enhancing proliferative and metastatic capabilities in endometrial cancer [14]. Notably, MYH14 overexpression appears to drive oncogenic pathways in ovarian [15] and breast cancers [16], while promoting invasion and metastasis in pancreatic cancer, correlating with poor clinical outcomes [17]. These findings collectively demonstrate MYH14′s direct involvement in cancer progression and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target. However, its specific role in cervical cancer pathophysiology remains largely unexplored.

In the current investigation, we sought to elucidate the functional significance and regulatory mechanisms of MYH14 in cervical cancer pathogenesis. Through comprehensive analyses including clinical specimen examination, functional assays both in vitro and in vivo, and mechanistic studies, we aimed to determine whether MYH14 expression correlates with clinical outcomes in cervical cancer patients and whether it functionally contributes to disease progression and metastasis. Furthermore, we investigated the transcriptional regulation of MYH14 to identify upstream regulatory factors that could be therapeutically targeted. Our findings indicate that MYH14, transcriptionally regulated by ESR1, serves as both a critical driver of cervical cancer progression and metastasis and a potential therapeutic target, providing mechanistic rationale for exploring anti-estrogen receptor therapies in the management of ESR1-positive cervical malignancies.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis: Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA, http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/index.html) was utilized to examine MYH14 transcript levels in cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC) compared to normal tissues based on TCGA and GTEx databases. Differential expression analysis was conducted with log2FC cutoff of 1 and p-value cutoff of 0.01.

Human Tissue Samples: The study cohort comprised 100 patients with cervical cancer who underwent surgical resection at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2019 and December 2022. Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues (≥2 cm from tumor margin) were collected for analysis. Patients were followed up until June 2024. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval between the date of surgery and death from any cause, while disease-free survival (DFS) was calculated from the date of surgery to the first evidence of recurrence, metastasis, or death. Comprehensive clinicopathological data were collected, including age, FIGO stage, histological subtype, lymph node status, and treatment modalities. Histopathological diagnosis was independently confirmed by two experienced pathologists. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Histological Examination and immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining: Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue samples were assembled into tissue microarrays and stored at −20 °C. Prior to staining, slides were heated to 75 °C, deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. For hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, sections were briefly immersed in hematoxylin followed by eosin according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Ki-67 primary antibody (1: 500, ab270650, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:2000, ab205719, Abcam). Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA), and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin before imaging.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR): Total RNA was extracted from cells (approximately 1 × 10^6 cells) or tissue specimens (50 mg) using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from purified RNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (Takara, Dalian, China). Real-time quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Roche, Indianapolis, IN, USA) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche). Thermal cycling conditions consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (95 °C for 15 s), annealing (60 °C for 30 s), and extension (72 °C for 30 s). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle method (2^-ΔΔCt), with all samples normalized to β-actin expression. The following primers were utilized for PCR amplification:

MYH14, 5ʹ-GGAGCTGGGAAGACGGAAAA-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ-CAGCTCACCGGGGACAC −3ʹ (reverse);

MYH14, 5ʹ-GGAGCTGGGAAGACGGAAAA-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ-CAGCTCACCGGGGACAC −3ʹ (reverse);

ERS1, 5ʹ-GCGTCTTTATGGGCGACAAC-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ-AAGCAGCACGCACAAAGC −3ʹ (reverse);

ERS1, 5ʹ-GCGTCTTTATGGGCGACAAC-3ʹ (forward), 5ʹ-AAGCAGCACGCACAAAGC −3ʹ (reverse);

β-actin, 5′-ATCCCAGGGAGACAGAGATCGAGG-3ʹ (forward), 5′-AAGCTTGGTGGTGGGTGGACTTTTGGTTGT-3′ (reverse).

β-actin, 5′-ATCCCAGGGAGACAGAGATCGAGG-3ʹ (forward), 5′-AAGCTTGGTGGTGGGTGGACTTTTGGTTGT-3′ (reverse).

Western Blot (WB): Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) from 1 × 10⁶ cells or 50–100 mg tissue samples, followed by quantification with a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 hour at room temperature. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies employed in this study included: anti-MYH14 (1:1000, ab264388, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-ESR1 (1:1000, ab32063), anti-E-cadherin (1:1000, ab314063), anti-N-cadherin (1:1000, ab76011), anti-Vimentin (1:1000, ab92547), anti-MMP-9 (1:1000, ab76003), anti-c-Myc (1:1000, ab32072), and anti-β-Actin (1:1000, ab8227). Following TBST washes (tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween 20), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:2000, ab6721) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein immunoreactivity was visualized using a chemiluminescent detection system (Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol, with actin serving as the internal loading control.

Cell Culture, Cell Transfection and Stable Cell Line Generation: A comprehensive panel of human cervical cancer cell lines (SiHa, C-33A, HeLa, C4–1, and CaSki), along with HUCECs (Human Cervical Epithelial Cells) and HEK293-FT cells obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China), were maintained in either DMEM or RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 100 μg/ml streptomycin, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 10% FBS (all from Procell Life Technologies, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For gene manipulation, short hairpin RNA sequences targeting MYH14 (shRNA) and non-targeting control sequences (sh-NC) were cloned into pLKO.1-TRC cloning vectors (Plasmid #10,878, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA), while the full-length human ESR1 cDNA (NM_000125.3) was PCR-amplified and cloned into pCDH—CMV-MCS-EF1-Puro lentiviral expression vector (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA, USA). All constructs were verified by Sanger sequencing. Cells at 60–70% confluence were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) with 2.5 μg plasmid DNA, 5 μl P3000 reagent, and 5 μl Lipofectamine 3000 in 250 μl Opti-MEM medium per well of 6-well plates. Stably transfected cells were selected with 2 μg/ml puromycin (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 14 days beginning 48 h post-transfection, and gene modification was confirmed by qRT-PCR and WB before experimental use.

Cell Growth Assessment: Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan). Cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10^3 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured for indicated time points (24, 48, 72, and 96 h). At each time point, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. For colony formation assay, 1 × 10^3 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for two weeks. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.04% crystal violet solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for one hour at room temperature. After gentle washing and air-drying, colonies were photographed and quantified using an Olympus microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Colonies containing >50 cells were counted as positive.

Cell Migration and Invasion Assays: Cell migration was assessed using a wound healing assay. When cells reached approximately 85% confluence in 6-well plates, a sterile pipette tip was used to create a uniform scratch across the monolayer. Wound closure was monitored at 0 and 24 h post-scratching using an Olympus microscope (Tokyo, Japan). Images were captured from three representative fields per well, and migration rates were quantified by measuring wound areas using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For invasion assessment, Transwell chambers (8 μm pore size) with Matrigel-coated membranes (Fuso Bio, Shanghai, China) were employed. Briefly, 5 × 10^4 transfected cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber, while complete medium containing 20% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 24 h of incubation, cells that had traversed the membrane were fixed and stained with 0.05% crystal violet. Invaded cells were visualized using an Olympus microscope and quantified in five random fields per well using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Mouse Xenograft Tumor Growth Analysis: Female BALB/c nude mice (5–6 weeks old, 18–20 g) were purchased from Saiye Biologicals (Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with controlled temperature (24±2 °C), humidity (50±10%), and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. CaSki cells stably expressing sh-MYH14, sh-NC, ESR1-OE, or corresponding controls were harvested during exponential growth phase. A total of 5 × 10^6 cells suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of each mouse (n = 5 or 6 animals per group). For the ESR1-OE xenograft model, when tumors reached approximately 8 mm in diameter (approximately 14 days post-inoculation), mice were randomly divided into two groups: (1) vehicle control (saline) and (2) AZD9833 treatment (10 mg/kg/day, oral gavage) [18]. Body weight and tumor dimensions were measured every three days using digital calipers. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (length × width^2)/2. Mice in the sh-MYH14 and sh-NC groups were sacrificed 36 days after cell inoculation, while mice in the ESR1-OE groups (with and without AZD9833 treatment) were sacrificed 22 days after treatment initiation. Tumors were excised, weighed, photographed, and divided for subsequent histological, immunohistochemical, and molecular analyses.

Tail Vein Metastasis Model: For the experimental metastasis model, 2.5 × 10^5 luciferase-labeled CaSki cells (sh-MYH14, sh-NC, ESR1-OE, or control) suspended in 50 μL PBS were injected into the lateral tail vein of 6-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (n = 5 or 6 animals per group). All mice were monitored for metastatic progression for a uniform duration of 6 weeks. For the ESR1-OE group, mice were randomly divided into two treatment groups immediately after cell injection: vehicle control (saline) or AZD9833 (10 mg/kg/day, oral gavage). Treatment continued throughout the 6-week experimental period. At the 6-week endpoint, all mice were subjected to bioluminescence imaging to assess metastatic burden. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in 100% O2 and administered D-luciferin (150 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection. After 10 min, bioluminescent signals were captured using a Lumina XRMS in vivo imaging system (PerkinElmer). Images were acquired and analyzed using Living Image software (version 4.5). Following imaging, mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation. Lungs were excised, photographed, fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin, and processed for histological analysis. Metastatic burden was quantified by counting the number of visible metastatic nodules on the lung surface and through histological examination of H&E-stained lung sections.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Analysis: ChIP assays were performed using the Simple ChIP Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA). Approximately 4 × 10^6 cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature to cross-link DNA-protein complexes. The reaction was quenched by adding glycine to a final concentration of 0.125 M, followed by washing with ice-cold PBS. After centrifugation, cell pellets were lysed in membrane extraction buffer, and chromatin was digested with Micrococcal nuclease to generate DNA fragments averaging 200–500 bp in length. Nuclear lysates were sonicated and clarified by centrifugation to obtain sheared chromatin, with a portion (2%) of the chromatin as the input control. The remaining chromatin was immunoprecipitated with target antibodies (5 μg) or normal rabbit IgG (negative control, 5 μg) overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Immune complexes were captured using protein A/G agarose beads, followed by sequential washing with low-salt, high-salt, and LiCl buffers. Chromatin was eluted from the beads, and cross-links were reversed by overnight incubation at 65 °C. DNA was purified and subjected to qRT-PCR analysis of the relative enrichment at MYH14 gene promoter. Target DNA enrichment was calculated as a percentage of input chromatin.

Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay: Cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1 × 10^5 cells per well and grown to 70–80% confluence. Cells were co-transfected with pGL4.10-MYH14-promoter (−1739/+70) luciferase reporter construct (1 μg), pcDNA3.1-ESR1 expression vector or empty pcDNA3.1 vector (1 μg), and pRL-TK Renilla luciferase control reporter (500 ng) using Lipofectamine 3000 Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed with Passive Lysis Buffer. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) on a GloMax luminometer. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to control for transfection efficiency.

Statistical Analysis: Data were analyzed using R (version 4.0.1) and SPSS (version 24.0), with results presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons employed Student's t-test for two groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's post-hoc test for multiple groups, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. For survival analyses, patients were stratified into high/low MYH14 expression groups based on median values, with Kaplan-Meier curves compared using log-rank tests and prognostic factors identified through univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Significance thresholds were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, **P < 0.0001, with NS indicating non-significance.

Results

MYH14 is overexpressed in cervical cancer and associated with poor clinical outcomes

Our study revealed significant MYH14 upregulation in cervical cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, as demonstrated by bioinformatics analysis using GEPIA and TIMER2.0 platforms (Fig. 1A-1C), immunohistochemistry on 100 tissue pairs (Fig. 1D), and validation through qRT-PCR and WB analyses in fresh tissues (Fig. 1E). Elevated MYH14 expression was also significantly correlated with adverse prognostic features including deeper invasion, lymph node metastasis, advanced FIGO stage, and specific histological subtypes (Table 1). Cox regression analysis established MYH14 as an independent risk factor for OS in both univariate and multivariate analyses (P < 0.05) (Table 2). While high MYH14 expression correlated with poorer DFS in univariate analysis, it was not an independent predictor in multivariate analysis (Table 3). Kaplan-Meier analyses confirmed that patients with higher MYH14 expression exhibited significantly worse OS and DFS compared to those with lower expression (Figs. 2A and 2B). Collectively, these findings establish MYH14 as a negative prognostic biomarker in cervical cancer.

MYH14 knockdown inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation and migration

To investigate MYH14′s functional role in cervical cancer, we first assessed its expression across multiple cervical cancer cell lines (SiHa, C-33A, HeLa, C4–1, and CaSki) compared to normal HUCECs (Human Cervical Epithelial Cells), with HeLa and CaSki showing the highest upregulation (Fig. 3A-B). We then transfected these cells with three different shRNAs targeting MYH14, with shRNA#2 demonstrating the highest knockdown efficiency and thus selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3C). Following MYH14 silencing, we observed significant suppression of cellular proliferation as measured by CCK-8 and colony formation assays (Fig. 3D-E). Further functional analyses demonstrated that MYH14 knockdown markedly impaired cancer cell migration and motility in both transwell and wound healing assays (Fig. 3F-G). These findings collectively demonstrate that MYH14 inhibition effectively suppresses proliferative and migratory capabilities of cervical cancer cells, suggesting its critical role in promoting cervical cancer progression.

MYH14 knockdown suppresses tumor growth and metastasis in nude mouse models

To evaluate the in vivo effects of MYH14 inhibition on tumor progression, we established xenograft and metastasis models using CaSki cells with or without MYH14 knockdown. Subcutaneous implantation revealed that MYH14-silenced tumors exhibited significantly reduced growth rates and final tumor volumes compared to controls (Fig. 4A-C). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated decreased Ki-67 expression in MYH14-knockdown tumors, indicating reduced proliferative activity (Fig. 4D). WB analysis revealed that MYH14 silencing downregulated mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, MMP-9) while upregulating epithelial marker E-cadherin (Fig. 4E). In the tail vein injection metastasis model, mice bearing MYH14-knockdown cells developed significantly fewer lung metastatic nodules with reduced metastatic foci (Fig. 4F-G). These findings collectively demonstrate that targeting MYH14 in cervical squamous cell carcinoma not only impairs primary tumor growth but also substantially inhibits metastatic potential in vivo.

ESR1 regulates MYH14 expression and promotes cervical cancer progression

To identify upstream regulators of MYH14 in cervical cancer, we employed hTFtarget and HitPredict bioinformatic tools, identifying 12 potential transcription factor candidates (Fig. 5A). We then performed ChIP experiments using antibodies against these predicted transcription factors to analyze their binding to the MYH14 promoter. Notably, only ESR1 ChIP showed significant enrichment compared to IgG control, suggesting ESR1 as the most significant MYH14 regulator (Fig. 5B). Analysis of clinical specimens revealed elevated ESR1 expression in cervical cancer tissues compared to normal uterine tissues at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5C). We established ESR1-overexpressing HeLa and CaSki cell lines with confirmed upregulation by qRT-PCR and WB (Fig. 5D). ESR1 overexpression caused a significant increase of MYH14 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5E). JASPAR predictive modeling suggested the binding motif of ESR1 at MYH14 gene promoter, which was validated by dual-luciferase reporter assays showing enhanced promoter activity with ESR1 overexpression (Fig. 5F). Functional studies further demonstrated that ESR1 overexpression significantly enhanced cell proliferation as measured by CCK-8 and colony formation assays (Fig. 5G-H), while also promoting invasion and migration in transwell and wound healing assays (Fig. 5I-J). Notably, treatment with the ESR1 inhibitor AZD9833 effectively reversed these ESR1-mediated oncogenic effects. These findings establish ESR1 as a critical upstream regulator of MYH14 that drives cervical cancer progression, and highlight the therapeutic potential of ESR1 inhibitors in MYH14-overexpressing cervical cancers.

ESR1 activates the c-Myc/MMP9 axis via MYH14 in cervical cancer

Our mechanistic studies further suggested that ESR1 regulates MMP9 expression through the estrogen signaling pathway in cervical cancer. KEGG pathway mapping analysis of the estrogen receptor signaling pathway, shown in Figure S1, identified several upregulated genes in cervical cancer, highlighting how estrogen activation enhances tyrosine kinase activities, particularly c-Myc and c-Fos, which subsequently stimulate MMP9 production. qPCR and WB analysis demonstrated that the ESR1 inhibitor AZD9833 significantly suppressed the expression of ESR1 (only at the protein level), MYH14, c-Myc, and MMP9 (Fig. 6A), confirming the regulatory role of ESR1-mediated signaling. Importantly, while ESR1 overexpression substantially increased c-Myc and MMP9 levels, concurrent MYH14 knockdown reversed this effect (Fig. 6B), indicating that MYH14 serves as a critical intermediary between ESR1 and c-Myc/MMP9 signaling. These findings indicate a novel ESR1-MYH14-c-Myc-MMP9 regulatory axis in cervical cancer progression, where ESR1 drives MMP9 synthesis through c-Myc activation in a MYH14-dependent manner.

AZD9833 effectively inhibits the tumor growth and metastasis of ESR1-Overexpressing cervical cancer cells

We next evaluated the therapeutic potential of ESR1 inhibition in cervical cancer by assessing the effects of AZD9833 in mouse xenograft models. Nude mice were subcutaneously injected with CaSki-ESR1-OE cells and subsequently treated with either vehicle or AZD9833 (Fig. 7A). AZD9833 treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth, resulting in markedly reduced tumor volume and weight compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 7B-D). Histopathological examination of tumor tissues revealed decreased Ki-67 expression in AZD9833-treated tumors (Fig. 7E), indicating reduced proliferative activity. Furthermore, AZD9833 treatment altered the expression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers, with decreased levels of N-cadherin, Vimentin, and MMP-9, alongside increased E-cadherin expression (Fig. 7F). In a complementary tail vein injection metastasis model, AZD9833 significantly reduced both the intensity of lung metastasis and the number of metastatic nodules (Fig. 7G-H). These findings collectively demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of ESR1 with AZD9833 effectively suppresses both primary tumor growth and metastatic dissemination of ESR1-overexpressing cervical cancer in vivo, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic strategy for ESR1-positive cervical malignancies.

Discussion

This study provides the first evidence of MYH14′s clinical significance and functional role in cervical cancer. We demonstrate that MYH14 is significantly upregulated in cervical cancer tissues, correlating with poor clinical outcomes including reduced overall and disease-free survival. MYH14 silencing substantially inhibits cervical cancer cell proliferation, invasion, migration, and metastatic potential both in vitro and in vivo. Our mechanistic investigations revealed that ESR1 directly binds to the MYH14 gene promoter to transactivate its expression, establishing a novel regulatory axis that subsequently activates the c-Myc/MMP9 pathway. Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of ESR1 using AZD9833 effectively suppressed this signaling cascade, resulting in significant anti-tumor effects in ESR1-overexpressing cervical cancer models. These findings identify MYH14 as both a potential prognostic biomarker and promising therapeutic target, while providing mechanistic rationale for exploring anti-estrogen receptor therapies in the management of ESR1-positive cervical malignancies.

Cervical cancer remains a significant global health threat to women [1]. While concurrent chemoradiotherapy represents the standard of care [19], chemoresistance frequently leads to poor clinical outcomes [20]. For patients with advanced or metastatic PD-L1-positive disease, immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with paclitaxel may offer improved outcomes [21]. Sex steroid hormones, acting through their receptors, regulate critical processes including cell proliferation, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, apoptosis, migration, and invasion—all fundamental to tumor progression in gynecological malignancies including cervical cancer [22,23]. Our identification of MYH14 as a metastasis promoter regulated by ESR1 signaling provides a novel therapeutic target. The efficacy of ESR1 inhibition in suppressing the MYH14-mediated oncogenic pathway highlights the potential for developing targeted therapies that could enhance treatment efficacy and improve survival rates for cervical cancer patients.

The nuclear transcription factor ESR1, prevalent in over 70% of breast cancers, transcriptionally regulates genes associated with proliferation and tumor progression, establishing it as a critical therapeutic target [24]. MYH14, a non-myocardin protein family member, plays essential roles in cell adhesion and epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Our research demonstrated that ESR1 directly binds to the MYH14 promoter to regulate its transcription in cervical cancer cells. We found MYH14 to be highly expressed in cervical cancer, correlating with advanced pathological stage and lymph node metastasis, significantly reducing overall survival rates. Although multifactorial analysis of disease-free survival (DFS) did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to limited sample size, our findings establish that ESR1 critically influences cervical cancer progression and prognosis through its regulatory effect on MYH14 expression. This ESR1-MYH14 axis represents a promising therapeutic target for ESR1-positive cervical malignancies.

Estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and androgen receptor (AR) serve as critical prognostic determinants in aggressive tumors, particularly in breast and endometrial cancers [25]. This suggests that endocrine therapies targeting ER may benefit cervical cancer patients. Several preclinical studies have demonstrated the potential efficacy of anti-endocrine drugs in cervical cancer [[26], [27], [28], [29], [30]], with a clinical trial of tamoxifen monotherapy for recurrent non-squamous cervical cancer reporting an 11.1% response rate (three of 27 patients showing one complete and two partial responses) [31]. ER expression occurs in 31–37% of cervical squamous cell carcinomas and correlates with better prognosis, though expression levels decline with advancing FIGO stage due to cancer progression and dedifferentiation [32]. Den Boon et al. [33] highlighted that the ER signaling pathway remains relevant through cancer-specific paracrine mesenchymal-tumor signaling, suggesting that estrogen may continue to promote tumor growth even when receptor expression is undetectable in tumor tissues. Our findings on the ESR1-MYH14-c-Myc-MMP9 axis provide a mechanistic explanation for these observations and strengthen the rationale for anti-estrogen therapy in cervical cancer.

AZD9833, a potent Selective Estrogen Receptor Downregulator (SERD), degrades ERα with efficiency comparable to fulvestrant in MCF-7 and CAMA-1 cell lines, while offering superior oral bioavailability and favorable preclinical pharmacokinetics [34]. Clinical studies demonstrate its good tolerability, positive safety profile, and ability to stabilize disease in heavily pretreated ER+/HER2- advanced breast cancer patients [35,36]. ESR1 activation promotes ER+/HER2- breast cancer progression by disrupting tight junction proteins ZO-1 and Occludin, enhancing metastatic potential in zebrafish xenograft models; conversely, ESR1 inhibition or silencing restricts cell survival and metastasis while restoring tight junction integrity [37]. Our in vitro and in vivo investigations confirmed AZD9833′s efficacy in targeting ESR1 in ESR1-overexpressing cervical cancer cells critical for tumor proliferation. While enhanced ESR1 expression increases proliferation, migration, and metastatic potential in cervical cancer cells, AZD9833 effectively counteracts these effects and inhibits tumor growth by downregulating ESR1. Our findings suggest that AZD9833 can mitigate MYH14′s oncogenic effects in cervical cancer through ESR1 inhibition, presenting a promising therapeutic strategy.

Despite our novel findings on the ESR1-MYH14 axis in cervical cancer progression, several limitations warrant consideration. Our study utilized a relatively small patient cohort, potentially limiting the statistical power of survival analyses, particularly for disease-free survival outcomes. Additionally, while we demonstrated MYH14′s role in promoting EMT and metastasis, the comprehensive molecular mechanisms underlying this process remain incompletely characterized. While our data demonstrate that MYH14 knockdown attenuates ESR1-induced upregulation of c-Myc and MMP9, the precise molecular mechanisms by which MYH14 regulates c-Myc/MMP9 expression and whether ESR1 directly drives MMP9 synthesis through c-Myc require further investigation. Future research should expand patient cohorts to validate MYH14 as a prognostic biomarker and explore additional downstream effectors of the ESR1-MYH14 signaling pathway. Investigation of potential resistance mechanisms to ESR1 inhibition and combination therapeutic strategies targeting both ESR1 and MYH14-mediated pathways could enhance clinical applications. Finally, evaluating MYH14′s role in response to standard chemoradiotherapy and immunotherapy may identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from personalized treatment approaches incorporating anti-estrogen therapy.

Conclusion

Our research unveils a novel regulatory mechanism wherein ESR1 enhances MYH14 gene transcription in cervical cancer. This ESR1-MYH14 axis promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition and drives tumor progression, while pharmacological inhibition with AZD9833 effectively targets ESR1 to suppress this oncogenic pathway. We identify MYH14 as both a critical oncogenic factor and a promising prognostic biomarker in cervical cancer, providing mechanistic rationale for exploring anti-estrogen therapies in ESR1-positive cervical malignancies. These findings not only advance our understanding of cervical cancer pathogenesis but also highlight potential therapeutic strategies that merit further clinical investigation.
