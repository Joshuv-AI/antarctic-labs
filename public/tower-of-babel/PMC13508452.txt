Comparative efficacy of transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) versus solifenacin in female patients with overactive bladder
Authors: MohammadJavad Safaie Yazdi, Salman Nazary-Moghadam, Bagher Soleimani, Seyyed Parham Ahmadi, Fatemeh Sadabadi, Zhila Sheikhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOveractive bladder (OAB) is commonly managed with antimuscarinic agents, although tolerability limits long-term adherence. Tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) has emerged as a non-pharmacologic alternative. This study compared the efficacy and safety of TNS versus solifenacin in women with OAB.MethodsIn this randomized controlled clinical trial, 118 patients met OAB diagnostic criteria, who presented to Qaem Hospital (Mashhad, Iran, 2023). Participants were randomly assigned to receive 12 weeks of either TNS (two 30-minute sessions weekly) or oral solifenacin (5 mg daily). Primary outcomes included changes in OAB symptom severity, urinary incontinence, and quality of life scores, using validated questionnaires at baseline and week 12. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsBoth treatments led to significant improvement in OAB symptoms, urinary incontinence severity, and quality of life (all p < 0.001). TNS produced slightly greater improvement in OAB symptom severity (p = 0.016) and significantly greater reduction in urinary incontinence severity (p = 0.015). Quality of life improved substantially in both groups (p < 0.001), with comparable post-treatment scores. Adverse effects occurred in 88.1% of solifenacin users, whereas no side effects were reported with TNS.ConclusionTNS is an effective and well-tolerated treatment for OAB, offering superior improvement in urinary incontinence and a markedly better safety profile compared with solifenacin. TNS may represent a preferable non-pharmacologic alternative for patients who cannot tolerate or do not respond adequately to anticholinergic medications. Future studies with larger cohorts and extended follow-up durations would be beneficial to validate and expand upon our findings.Trial registrationThe study protocol was officially registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials under the identifier IRCT20190618043934N25 on 2024-05-13.Keywords: Overactive bladder, Tibial nerve stimulation, Solifenacin, Urinary incontinence, Quality of life, Neuromodulation, Anticholinergic drugs

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a prevalent lower urinary tract disorder characterized by urinary urgency, usually accompanied by frequency and nocturia, with or without urgency urinary incontinence, in the absence of urinary tract infection or other obvious pathology, according to the International Continence Society [1]. OAB affects approximately 546 million individuals worldwide [2]. The OAB prevalence varies across regions, affecting nearly one-sixth of the United States population and up to one-fifth of the Asian [3]. The prevalence increases steadily with age, concerning nearly one in every five adults over 40 years of age [4]. Women are disproportionately affected, with rates reaching 30% among those older than 65 years [5–7]. Beyond the high prevalence of this chronic condition, OAB imposes a considerable burden on healthcare systems, and significantly diminishes patients’ quality of life (QoL) by negatively affecting daily activities, sleep, and psychosocial well-being, leading to anxiety and depression [3, 4, 8].

The current management of OAB follows a stepwise approach, beginning with conservative measures such as lifestyle modifications and pelvic floor muscle training. When these measures fail, the focus shifts to pharmacological interventions [9]. Among pharmacological options, antimuscarinic agents such as solifenacin are widely used and have demonstrated efficacy in reducing OAB symptoms [10]. However, treatment adherence remains a major challenge because many patients experience bothersome adverse effects, including dry mouth and constipation. This results in high discontinuation rates reaching up to 84% at 12 months in some settings [5].

Neuromodulation therapies have emerged as alternative treatment options for patients who do not respond to or cannot tolerate pharmacological therapy [9]. Tibial nerve stimulation (TNS), particularly transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS), has attracted increasing attention as a minimally invasive treatment with a favorable safety profile [5]. A growing body of evidence has begun to compare the efficacy and safety of TNS with antimuscarinic agents like solifenacin, yet the findings present a complex and sometimes contradictory picture. Previous studies [2, 11–14] have reported promising outcomes for TNS in improving OAB symptoms, suggesting that TNS is not only effective but may be comparable to or even superior to pharmacotherapy, offering similar symptom control with a more favorable tolerability profile.

However, the comparative effectiveness of TNS and antimuscarinic therapy remains uncertain because of variations in study designs and reported results [13, 14]. Complicating the landscape further are studies suggesting that combination therapy yielded better result than either monotherapy [15]. Beyond clinical efficacy, economic considerations are paramount, with a cost-effectiveness analysis suggesting that TNS devices may offer superior long-term value compared to solifenacin [16].

Although previous studies have investigated the efficacy of tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) and antimuscarinic agents for the treatment of OAB, inconsistent findings and methodological variations have limited definitive conclusions regarding their comparative effectiveness. Although solifenacin offers ease of administration, its systemic side effect profile often precipitates discontinuation, threatening long-term OAB management. TNS, while logistically intensive due to required sessions, may offer superior tolerability and long-term adherence. Therefore, the present study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of TNS and solifenacin in patients with OAB and to provide further evidence to support clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

This prospective randomized controlled trial included patients presenting with symptoms of overactive bladder (OAB)—including urgency, urinary incontinence, nocturia, and urinary frequency—at Qaem Hospital, Mashhad (April 2023 – March 2024). Eligible participants were adults who provided written informed consent, met the diagnostic criteria for OAB (urinating more than eight times in 24 h and experiencing urgency with or without urinary incontinence), and reported symptoms persisting for at least six months. The definition of “urgency” was based on the International Continence Society (ICS) terminology [17], a sudden, compelling urge to urinate that is difficult to defer. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, previous surgery for urinary incontinence, current or recent anticholinergic use, urinary tract infection, or withdrawal from the study. Besides, patients with predominant stress urinary incontinence or urodynamically proven mixed incontinence with a significant stress component were excluded from the study.

Sample size

The required sample size was estimated based on the study by Vecchioli-Scaldazza et al. (2018) [18], assuming α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 (80% power). Using the formula for comparing means between two independent groups, 56 participants were required per arm. Accounting for an anticipated 5% dropout rate, 59 patients were enrolled in each group, yielding a total sample size of 118.

Randomization and interventions

After obtaining informed consent, participants were randomized into two groups using block randomization (block size = 4) with sequences of AABB, ABAB, BABA, BBAA, ABBA, and BAAB. Randomization assignments were sealed in envelopes to ensure allocation concealment.

Group A: Posterior tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) administered for 12 weeks, consisting of two 30-minute sessions per week.

Group A: Posterior tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) administered for 12 weeks, consisting of two 30-minute sessions per week.

Group B: Solifenacin 5 mg orally once daily for 12 weeks (Vsol®; Abidi Pharmaceutical Company, Iran).

Group B: Solifenacin 5 mg orally once daily for 12 weeks (Vsol®; Abidi Pharmaceutical Company, Iran).

Tibial nerve stimulation procedure

The device used in this study was the P710 TENS unit (Novinmed, Isfahan, Iran).

Stimulation parameters and titration

All patients received transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) using a standardized fix protocol throughout the study. Adhesive surface electrodes were placed on the medial aspect of the ankle, over the course of the posterior tibial nerve. Stimulation was delivered at a frequency of 20 Hz with a pulse width of 200 µs in continuous mode. Each treatment session lasted 30 min, and participants underwent therapy twice weekly for 12 consecutive weeks.

At each session, the current intensity was started from 0 mA and gradually increased in steps of 0.5–1 mA until the patient reported a strong but comfortable tingling sensation, without pain or muscle contraction. The individualised amplitude ranged from 0.5 to 9 mA across sessions, with re-titration at each visit to account for habituation and electrode impedance. The cathode (negative electrode) was placed on the tibial nerve, four fingers above the medial malleolus and one finger towards the heel, while the anode (positive electrode) was positioned along the nerve near the sole of the foot. Stimulation parameters were held constant for all patients receiving TNS.

Safety

No adverse events were reported in any patient in the TENS/TTNS group throughout the 12-session intervention.

Outcome assessment

To comprehensively evaluate treatment impact, a suite of validated, patient-reported outcome measures was employed at baseline and after 12 weeks. The primary outcomes were the severity of urinary symptoms, urinary incontinence, and quality of life. The following standardized questionnaires were selected for their proven validity and reliability:

International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire in Overactive Bladder (ICIQ-OAB): was used to assess OAB symptom severity (frequency, urgency, and leakage episodes) [19]. The localized questionnaire (Persian translation) has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability [20].

International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire in Overactive Bladder (ICIQ-OAB): was used to assess OAB symptom severity (frequency, urgency, and leakage episodes) [19]. The localized questionnaire (Persian translation) has demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability [20].

International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF): was utilized to evaluate urinary incontinence [19]. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have also been confirmed in an Iranian population [21].

International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF): was utilized to evaluate urinary incontinence [19]. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have also been confirmed in an Iranian population [21].

Quality of Life Questionnaire for Lower Urinary Tract Disorders (Iranian validated version): included a Voiding Diary to record frequency, urine volume, and fluid intake over a minimum 24-hour period. Patients were asked to complete this form 72 h before starting treatment and 72 h after completing treatment. To enable comparison of this information among all patients, they were provided with identical graduated disposable cups. Patients were instructed to measure the fluids they intended to drink using these cups and to use similar cups when urinating [22].

The questionnaires were designed for self-completion, which were completed independently by study participants. For those who were illiterate, they were completed with the assistance of a researcher.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was planned to determine not only within-group changes but also the crucial between-group differences. A repeated-measures ANOVA was chosen as the primary analytical method to assess the effects of time (pre- vs. post-treatment), group (TNS vs. Solifenacin), and their interaction (time × group) on each outcome variable. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was conducted, and when the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment were used to examine significant interactions, further strengthening the validity of the findings. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22, with statistical significance set at a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.MUMS.MEDICAL.REC.1402.128) and registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (Code: IRCT20190618043934N25). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. No additional costs were imposed on participants, and all data were anonymized and reported in aggregate.

Results

A total of 118 female patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) (n = 59) and the solifenacin (n = 59) groups (Fig. 1). The overall mean age of participants was 63.97 ± 8.16. There were no significant differences in baseline demographic or clinical characteristics between the two groups. Baseline scores for OAB symptoms, urinary incontinence, and quality of life did not differ significantly between the groups (all p > 0.05)(Table 1).

Urinary Incontinence Severity: Urinary incontinence severity improved substantially in both groups, with mean scores decreasing from 7.81 ± 1.63 at baseline to 4.58 ± 1.98 (F(1,116) = 197.92, p < 0.001, η² = 0.63). The TNS group experienced slightly larger reductions than the Solifenacin group (4.05 ± 1.48 vs. 5.10 ± 2.27; p = 0.015). A significant treatment × group interaction was observed (F(1,116) = 4.25, p = 0.041, η² = 0.035), highlighting that TNS may be more effective in controlling incontinence episodes (Table 2; Fig. 2). Median outcomes echoed this advantage, as TNS improved from 8 [7–9] to 4 [3–5], whereas Solifenacin improved from 8 [7–9] to 5 [3–6] (both p < 0.001), reinforcing the greater relative effectiveness of TNS in reducing incontinence episodes (Table 3).

Quality of Life (QoL): Both treatments led to meaningful improvements in QoL, reflecting reduced symptom burden. Mean QoL scores dropped from 6.58 ± 1.68 at baseline to 2.27 ± 1.11 post-treatment (F(1,116) = 856.87, p < 0.001, η² = 0.88). Although a significant time × group interaction was identified (F(1,116) = 5.86, p = 0.017, η² = 0.048), post-treatment QoL values were clinically comparable between TNS and Solifenacin (2.40 ± 1.10 vs. 2.13 ± 1.10; p = 0.694) (Table 2; Fig. 2). Median Qol values also demonstrated substantial improvement in both groups—from 7 [4–8] to 2 [2–3] in TNS and from 7 [6–8] to 2 [1–3] with Solifenacin (both p < 0.001)—reflecting the significant reduction in symptom burden seen across both interventions (Table 3).

Adverse effects: Consistent with the non-invasive nature of the intervention, no adverse effects were reported in the TNS group (0%). In contrast, overall, 52 patients (88.1%) in the solifenacin group reported at least one drug-related side effect. The most commonly reported adverse effects were dry mouth, constipation, and blurred vision. Crucially, no patients (0%) in either group discontinued the study specifically due to an adverse event.

Discussion

The present study provides compelling evidence that transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) is an effective and well-tolerated treatment for overactive bladder (OAB), demonstrating comparable efficacy to solifenacin for overall symptom reduction while offering superior control of urinary incontinence and a markedly more favorable safety profile. Our findings reveal that both interventions significantly improved OAB symptoms, urinary incontinence severity, and quality of life (QoL) after 12 weeks, supporting the hypothesis that neuromodulation can be an alternative to antimuscarinic therapy. The most striking observation was the significantly greater reduction in urinary incontinence severity achieved with TNS compared to solifenacin, coupled with the absence of adverse events in the TNS group versus an 88.1% rate of side effects with solifenacin. This pattern of results aligns with a growing body of evidence highlighting the therapeutic potential of tibial nerve stimulation while underscoring the tolerability challenges inherent to anticholinergic medications.

The pathophysiology of OAB is primarily attributed to involuntary detrusor muscle contractions during the bladder-filling phase, elevating intravesical pressure and decreasing functional bladder capacity [23]. The tibial nerve contributes to bladder sensation, detrusor activity, and pelvic floor function [24]. The central theoretical mechanism supporting tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) is the retrograde neuromodulation delivered to the sacral nerve plexus (S2–S4 segments), which contains the spinal center controlling micturition [25, 26]. By stimulating somatic afferent fibers of the tibial nerve, TNS modulates the efferent outflow to the lower urinary tract. This action is postulated to activate inhibitory sympathetic neurons or trigger the release of inhibitory neurotransmitters (such as gamma-aminobutyric acid or opioid peptides) in the spinal cord, leading to the inhibition of detrusor activity and cessation of involuntary bladder contractions [26, 27].

The superior efficacy of TNS specifically for urinary incontinence observed in our study is consistent with several previous investigations. Nikoo et al. [11] reported that percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) yielded greater reductions in OAB Symptom Score and incontinence screening scores compared to solifenacin 10 mg daily. Similarly, Vecchioli-Scaldazza et al. [12] demonstrated that PTNS provided consistently greater improvements than solifenacin across multiple measures including urge incontinence episodes and patient-reported QoL. Kızılyel et al. [28] also observed greater improvement in patients treated with TNS, either alone or combined with anticholinergic therapy, compared with those receiving anticholinergics monotherapy. The mechanism underlying this advantage may relate to the direct neuromodulatory effects of TNS on bladder control pathways. Electrical stimulation of the tibial nerve depolarizes sacral somatic fibers and lumbar afferents, thereby inhibiting involuntary detrusor contractions more effectively than the competitive antagonism of muscarinic receptors achieved with solifenacin (Ghanei et al., [29]). This direct neural targeting appears particularly beneficial for controlling incontinence episodes, which represent a major component of OAB symptom burden.

Regarding overall OAB symptom control, our finding of comparable efficacy between TNS and solifenacin aligns with several studies suggesting non-inferiority of neuromodulation approaches. Alomari et al. [2], in their systematic review and meta-analysis of six randomized controlled trials, concluded that transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) is non-inferior to anticholinergic therapy, offering similar symptom control with a more favorable tolerability profile. Similarly, Krhut et al. (2023) found comparable reductions in OAB-V8 scores between peroneal electrical transcutaneous neuromodulation and solifenacin, with median improvements of 56% for eTNM and 57% for solifenacin [14]. Radinmehr et al. [13] also reported no statistically significant differences between conventional TTNS and solifenacin for OAB symptoms, though clinically greater improvements were observed in the TTNS arm. However, some studies have reported minimal or no significant benefit from TNS in OAB symptoms, potentially due to variations in patients demographics, parity, delivery method, and intervention duration across studies (Rahmani et al., [30]). The discrepancy between studies may reflect differences in intervention duration, stimulation parameters, and participants composition.

In contrast, other studies corroborate our findings regarding overall symptom improvement. Vecchioli-Scaldazza et al. [18], Kurdoglu et al. [26], Ahmed et al. [31], Martin et al. [32], and Ghanei et al. [29] consistently demonstrated significant improvements in OAB symptoms following TNS. These consistent results across diverse populations support the reliability of TNS as an effective neuromodulatory approach for bladder dysfunction. The therapeutic mechanism of TNS is thought to involve depolarization of lumbar afferent fibers and inhibition of sacral somatic fibers, as mentioned in our results. This inhibitory neuromodulation during the bladder storage phase suppresses detrusor contractions, enhances bladder capacity and compliance, and reduces urinary frequency and leakage, even during increases in intra-abdominal pressure such as coughing or laughing.

The dramatic difference in safety and tolerability observed in our study represents perhaps its most clinically significant finding. The complete absence of adverse events in the TNS group versus an 88.1% incidence of side effects with solifenacin highlights a substantial advantage of neuromodulation. This finding is consistent with numerous previous studies reporting excellent tolerability of TNS. Alve et al. [33] found no adverse events with TTNS, while Alomari et al. [2] reported significantly fewer adverse events with TTNS compared to anticholinergics. The favorable safety profile observed in our study further supports the clinical utility of TNS and is in line with Gaziev et al. [34], Bhide et al. [25], and Jacomo et al. [35], who similarly reported minimal to no complications associated with TNS. Collectively, these data indicate that TNS represents a safer and better-tolerated alternative for patients who are unresponsive to or intolerant of anticholinergic therapy. The high discontinuation rates associated with anticholinergics, reaching up to 84% at 12 months as noted in our introduction, represent a major clinical challenge that TNS appears capable of overcoming.

The QoL improvements observed in our study warrant careful interpretation. While both groups showed significant enhancements, the between-group differences were not statistically significant despite slightly greater improvements in the solifenacin group. This finding aligns with Rahmani et al. [30], Ghanei et al. [29], and Van Blanken et al. [36], who likewise reported that TNS improved patient QoL. The clinical implication is that both TNS and pharmacotherapy alleviate the psychosocial burden of OAB, although the safety advantage of TNS may favor its use as a first-line option. This pattern contrasts with some previous studies that reported superior QoL outcomes with TNS. Nikoo et al. [11] found more marked QoL improvements following PTNS compared to solifenacin, while Vecchioli-Scaldazza et al. [12] reported greater improvements in patient-reported QoL measures with PTNS. The discrepancy may relate to methodological differences in QoL assessment tools, patient populations, or treatment durations. Alternatively, the substantial improvements in both groups in our study may have reduced the ability to detect between-group differences, suggesting that both interventions effectively address the QoL impact of OAB, albeit through different mechanisms.

The clinical implications of our findings are substantial. TNS emerges as a particularly valuable option for patients who are intolerant or unresponsive to anticholinergic therapy, as suggested in our conclusion. The superior efficacy for urinary incontinence combined with excellent tolerability positions TNS as a potential treatment for OAB, challenging the conventional treatment algorithm that reserves neuromodulation for third-line therapy. This perspective is supported by Ghanei et al. [29], who found that TNS combined with pelvic floor exercises significantly outperformed exercises alone across multiple outcomes. The economic implications are also noteworthy; Kurdoğlu et al. [26] demonstrated substantial cost savings with PTNS due to reduced medication requirements, while Murray et al. [16] reported superior long-term cost-effectiveness for stimulation devices compared to solifenacin in their Markov model analysis.

Several strengths of our study enhance the validity of our findings. The randomized controlled design with randomization and allocation concealment minimizes selection bias. The use of validated questionnaires (ICIQ-OAB, ICIQ-UISF, and LUTS-QoL) ensures reliable outcome measurement. The standardized TNS protocol with specified electrode placement and stimulation parameters enhances reproducibility. The adequate sample size based on power calculations provides sufficient statistical power to detect clinically meaningful differences.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The female-only sample limits generalizability to male patients with OAB. The 12-week duration, while sufficient to detect initial treatment effects, does not provide information about long-term efficacy or durability of response. The absence of blinding could have introduced bias, particularly for subjective outcomes. The study did not include a sham TNS control group, making it impossible to completely rule out placebo effects. Furthermore, the required frequent clinic visits for TNS sessions (two 30-minute sessions weekly) compared to daily oral medication introduces a contact-time imbalance that may potentially influence the superior self-reported outcomes observed in the TTNS group. The focus on solifenacin alone limits comparisons with other anticholinergic agents or newer β3-adrenoceptor agonists like mirabegron. Additionally, the study’s reliance on validated patient-reported outcomes and voiding diary data, while standard for OAB research, means we lack highly objective measures such as the pad test or urodynamic confirmation.

Future research should address several important gaps. Longer follow-up periods are needed to assess the durability of TNS effects. Studies including male patients are necessary to establish generalizability across sexes. Head-to-head comparisons of TNS with newer pharmacological agents would be valuable, as would investigations of combination therapies. Research optimizing TNS parameters (frequency, amplitude, unilateral vs. bilateral stimulation) could enhance efficacy, as the ideal stimulation protocol remains uncertain. Finally, implementation studies examining real-world effectiveness and adherence to TNS protocols would help translate these findings into clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence supporting tibial nerve stimulation (TNS) as an effective, safe, and well-tolerated treatment for overactive bladder (OAB). Our findings demonstrate that TNS provides superior improvement in urinary incontinence compared to solifenacin while avoiding the adverse effects that frequently limit anticholinergic therapy. The comparable efficacy for overall OAB symptoms combined with excellent tolerability positions TNS as a compelling alternative to pharmacotherapy, particularly for patients who cannot tolerate or have inadequate response to antimuscarinic medications. As the burden of OAB continues to grow with our aging population, neuromodulation approaches like TNS offer a valuable addition to the therapeutic options, potentially improving outcomes for the millions affected by this condition worldwide.
