Abstract – High current, high voltage solenoid pulser power systems for the generation of magnetic fields

in a solenoid is not a radical or new concept. Despite being a cornerstone of scientific and weapons research

for over 100 years, a commercial off the shelf unit easily configurable for a broad set of applications has

yet to appear on the open market. Pulser systems are instead designed for specific target requirements. For

gigawatt to terawatt systems, this is unavoidable. For megawatt systems however, many hundreds of

thousands of dollars are wasted designing pulser systems from the ground up for clients, when it is possible

to simply architect a modular system once that can have its capacitors swapped for many possible target

applications. The National Ignition Facility (NIF) at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL)

is one such institution that would benefit from such a system, having bought such pulsers for several

imaging systems. Ideally, all such a system would require is only a bachelors-level circuit analysis to find

the correct capacitor values, as well as a written procedure to calibrate the software to correctly deliver the

voltages required by the application. In this work, systems architecture and software engineering

techniques are used to design and analyze such a system. Such a design, it is the hope of this author, will

be used in scientific and engineering purposes in the future to build solenoid pulsers at the National Ignition

Facility and other institutions to drastically cut development costs of scientific instrumentation.

 1. Introduction

 High current, high voltage solenoid pulser power systems for the generation of magnetic fields in

a solenoid is not a radical or new concept. Despite being a cornerstone of scientific and weapons research

for over 100 years, a commercial off the shelf unit has yet to appear on the open market. Pulser systems

are instead designed for specific target requirements. For multi-gigawatt to terawatt systems, this is

unavoidable. For megawatt systems however, many hundreds of thousands of dollars are wasted designing

pulser systems from the ground up for clients, when it is possible to simply architect a modular system once

that can have its capacitors swapped for many possible target applications. The National Ignition Facility

(NIF) at the Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory (LLNL) is one such institution that would benefit

from such a system, having bought such pulsers for several imaging systems. Ideally, all such a system

would require is only a bachelors-level circuit analysis to find the correct capacitor values, as well as a

written procedure to calibrate the software to correctly deliver the voltages required by the application. In

this paper, systems architecture and software engineering techniques are used to design and analyze such a

system. Such a design, it is the hope of this author, will be used in scientific and engineering purposes in

the future to build solenoid pulsers at the National Ignition Facility and other institutions to drastically cut

development costs of scientific instrumentation.

 2. Background

 2.1. Solenoid Pulsing Basics

 At their core, a pulsed solenoid is simply an LRC circuit, consisting of a solenoid inductor (L),

resistor (R), and capacitor (C), in which the capacitor is separated from the resistor and inductor with a

switch. This circuit can be seen in Figure 1.

 Figure 1 – solenoid pulser circuit schematic. L, R, and C represent the inductance,

 resistance, and capacitance of the circuit, respectively. S C represents the switch. V C , V R ,

 and V L are the initial voltage of the capacitor, the voltage drops across the resistor, and the

 voltage drop across the inductor, respectively.

 While the switch is open, a capacitor is charged to an initial voltage V 0. When the generation of

the pulse is desired, a trigger is sent to the switch, which closes it and allows current to flow from the

capacitor to the resistor and inductor, commonly referred to as the load. A pulse then is generated, the

shape and duration of which is the resultant of the interplay between the inductance, resistance, and

capacitance of the circuit (the exact nature of this relationship is beyond the scope of this discussion).

Suffice it to say, the problem falls into the damped undriven oscillator class of problems in physics, and

types of current pulses capable of being produced can be seen in Figure 2.

 Figure 2 – current across the inductor as a function of time as a function of LRC parameters

 (ξ). ξ <1 shows underdamped oscillations, ξ =1 shows critically damped oscillations, and ξ

 >1 shows overdamped oscillations. Image reproduced from [1].

 It is important to reiterate that pulsing solenoids is only one application that such a system can be

 2.2. System Context

 Rarely is a real system conceived from vacuum, and this case is no different. This body of work is

rooted in solenoid pulsing needs at the LLNL. For decades, external vendors were contracted for the

development of solenoid pulsers for diagnostic systems, including the Dilation X-ray Imager (DIXI)

systems [7], (Hardened) Single Line of Sight (hSLOS) imager [8], and Multi-Frame Gated X-ray (MGXI)

imager [9]. For the next generation DIXI system, the High Yield X-ray Imager (HYXI), the solenoid pulser

was decided to be designed and built in-house due to available expertise of pulsed power electrical

engineers.

 The operating principle of all these systems is the same. X-rays are generated during the NIF

process due to the high energy density physics driving the process. These x-rays propagate radially outward

to the target chamber wall, on which various x-ray diagnostics are mounted. The front-end of the diagnostic

mentioned in this section all have a photocathode, typically a 500nm cesium iodide (CsI) thin film, a

material that converts incoming x-rays to electrons through the photoelectric effect [7, 8, 9, 10]. A thin

electrical conductor film coats the CsI material through which a high voltage pulse if propagated, typically

gold. Because CsI is a non-conductor, contact with x-rays depletes the material of electrons, and only the

portion of the photocathode through which the high voltage pulse is propagating has sufficient electrons to

convert the image into a current. These electrons are then subjected to a non-uniform acceleration potential,

which accelerates electrons generated early in the process faster than those generated at a later time (several

picoseconds later). The non-uniformly electrons then propagate through the vacuum vessel, in which they

are given time to temporally dilate. They are kept on course and focused on an imaging sensor using a

magnetic field, generated by the solenoid pulser running current through the solenoids surrounding the

chamber. Finally, electrons are captured by the sensor, which now can be reasonably well by a nanosecond

gated silicon hCMOS sensor. Thus, by taking advantage of the physical properties of electrons, images

that are otherwise too fast to capture with semiconducting technology can be captured and visually

inspected by scientific staff to derive observations about the NIF process. A sample image captured by a

DIXI system can be seen in Figure 4.

 Figure 4 – data collected from the NIF implosion with timestamps from bangtime.

 2.3. Systems Engineering Methodology

 If all that were required was a one-size solenoid pulser for a garage project, section 2.1 in addition

to physics and circuit simulations to model the magnetic field of the system is entirely sufficient. If we

wish to fulfil only the pulsing requirements of the HYXI system, the requirements presented in Section 2.3

are likely sufficient for system development. However, if we want a well-designed system that can perform

its function in a multitude of situations, more effort is required. In this work we will use the model-based

systems engineering methodology to analyze what works and what doesn’t work for a pulser system using

the HYXI pulser system requirements and lessons learned from the DIXI and hSLOS pulsers. Using this,

a robust, scalable, and safe architecture was developed to be able to generate a current pulser system as

needed by working professionals that will work in a broad spectrum of contexts.

 For this, the systems engineering toolkit was used to first model the system behavior in Vitech’s

GENESYS platform, a model-based engineering (MBE) toolkit used to analyze system behavior, manage

the engineering process, and act as a single source of truth of all generated models. An iterative approach

was taken in which the requirements suggested functional and physical architectures, which were then

modeled with various behavioral diagrams. Analysis of these diagrams was then used to suggest changes

to the requirements, and iteration continued until the architecture was considered sufficiently neutral of

both hardware vendors and capacitor bank configuration. These will allow the architecture to be more

rapidly adapted in situations where capacitor banks are large and require complex storage.

 3. Body of Work

 3.1. System Requirements

 The requirements from Section 2.2 were analyzed and refactored, firstly to make them more

consistent with standard systems engineering practice, and later to make the architecture more fitting for an

open, one-size-fits-all architecture that this work aims to achieve. The updated and reorganized system

requirements can be seen in Figure 8. Table 2 lists these changes for quick reference. The new requirements

structure is far more focused and easier to navigate than the initial requirements structure, yielding a cleaner

system design.

 Figure 8 – final requirement structure . (a) top level structure, (b) controller requirements,

and (c) pulser channel requirements. Some sections, such as R.1.1.2 were truncated to save

 space.

 Table 2 – requirement changes and justification.

 Observe the removal of all requirements pertaining to the waveform shape. This is because these

requirements will change from application to application, and even from system to system in the same

application. This architecture provides a platform to interface with and control the capacitor bank, not to

dictate the usage of the system and restrict it to a particular application. Instead, pulse energy requirements

were moved into R.2.3.1 and the system was redesigned to make this requirement true. This is a far more

relevant parameter to a general pulser system than specific pulse waveform parameters, such as rise time,

flat top time, etc.

 3.3. Physical Architecture

 Analysis of the system lead to the physical architecture seen in Figure 16. The system supports up

to 4 pulser channels, but only one channel is modeled here. This is because all 4 channels have the same

architecture, and only the channel number (via an internal jumper on C.2.2) and the number and type of

capacitors are expected to change from channel to channel. Thus, only one channel is explored in Figure

16, C.2. The remaining channels, C.3, C.4, and C.5, have been omitted in this report to reduce space.

 For some components, such as C.2.2, the design does not change from application to application,

and thus hardware can be designed or selected that can be used across all use cases. A recommended design

for C.2.2 is shown in Figure 17. However, some components, such as C.2.3.1.1 (capacitor) will change

from system to system, and as a result C.2.3.1.2 (the capacitor mount PCB). This produces difficulty in

maintaining standardized interfaces across different applications. For example, C.2.3.1.2.1, the voltage

monitor interface was selected to interface a capacitor to C.2.2. In the HYXI case a 50Ω BNC connector

was selected as the interface, and if the system designer wishes to utilize the recommended C.2.2 PCB to

control and monitor the capacitor they must use the same component on the capacitor mount PCB. The

alternative is to redesign C.2.2, an unnecessary and expensive task.

 To solve this problem, components where the system designer has latitude in selection have been

marked with an <<Abstract>> stereotype, a concept borrowed from UML class diagrams, which is used to

specify a standard interface or structure for a component in software engineering, but reminds the engineer

that the component should not be used as is, that it is missing critical pieces that make it an incomplete

component [11]. This allows system designers to easily identify what components they have freedom to

change, and what components should remain unchanged.

 3.4. Software Class Diagram

 Using the rich diagrammatic modeling approach of GENESYS allows for the analysis and

development of the software structure to support hardware functions. To this end a class diagram, the

software equivalent of the physical architecture diagram, was developed to fully flesh out the software

system architecture and behavior. The initial software architecture was simplified from a complex central

messaging architecture to a Model-View-ViewModel (MVVM) architecture for a better separation between

the external control of the system and the internal representation (model) of the hardware, a block diagram

 The MVVM architecture has been determined as the ideal architecture as it most elegantly

encapsulates the necessary components into one of the three subcomponents, and for allowing for future

expansion and growth of the system. For example, one of the desired future expansions for the system is

an HTTP server for controlling the pulser easily on an online browser for laboratory use. In some

centralized hardware control megastructures, simple network control protocol (SNMP) is utilized for quick

integration of new hardware into the megastructure. This architecture allows for the simple, if not easy,

implementation of such features in the future. The decoupling of the SCPI server and core logic object was

chosen for reuse in other software systems.
