From Dialysis Expansion to Prevention-First Kidney Care: Lessons from Thailand’s Response to CKD
Authors: Sirirat Anutrakulchai, Theerachai Thammathiwat, Talerngsak Kanjanabuch, Surasak Kantachuvesiri, Somkanya Tungsanga, Vuddhidej Ophascharoensuk, Kriang Tungsanga, Marcello Tonelli, Adeera Levin
Published: 2026

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects more than 850 million people worldwide and is among the fastest-rising causes of death. Despite substantial advances in kidney replacement therapy, outcomes remain poor and costs continue to escalate. The burden falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries, where nearly 80% of affected individuals reside and access to dialysis and transplantation remains limited. The 2025 World Health Assembly kidney health resolution marked a major milestone by recognizing kidney disease as a global noncommunicable disease priority and calling for greater emphasis on prevention, early detection, timely treatment, and sustainable financing of kidney care.1, 2, 3 The methods used to identify and synthesize the evidence summarized here are described in the Supplementary Methods, and the broader global and regional context is detailed in the Supplementary Results.S59-S64

Thailand provides a useful example of how these principles can be translated into practice. Despite universal health coverage, Thailand has one of the highest burdens of kidney failure in Southeast Asia, with a prevalence exceeding 2400 per million population. Historically, national efforts focused on expanding access to kidney replacement therapy, particularly through the peritoneal dialysis first policy. This strategy substantially improved equity and access to life-saving treatment, but the growing number of patients requiring dialysis exposed the limitations of a predominantly downstream approach (Supplementary Results: Evolution of Kidney Replacement Therapy Policy).S40–S48 As a result, Thailand has increasingly adopted a prevention-first strategy that combines community screening, risk stratification, public-health interventions, disease-modifying therapies, and integrated chronic care models (Figure 1). For patients approaching kidney failure, this strategy also encompasses nutritional management during the transition and conservative kidney management guided by shared decision-making (Supplementary Results: Nutrition in the CKD–kidney replacement therapy Transition and Conservative Kidney Management and Shared Decision-Making).S49–S52, S65–S67

The epidemiology of CKD in Thailand reflects both global and local challenges. Diabetes and hypertension remain the leading causes of CKD and account for most cases reported in the national THAI-CKD Registry.4 However, CKD of unknown etiology has emerged as an important contributor, particularly in rural agricultural communities. Studies from several regions suggest that environmental, occupational, and social determinants may contribute to disease burden (Supplementary Results: CKD of Unknown Etiology in Thailand and Herbal Medicines and Potentially Nephrotoxic Exposures).S1–S13 Reported CKD prevalence also varies substantially depending on population sampling and estimated glomerular filtration rate equations (Supplementary Figure S1; Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Results: Evolution of estimated glomerular filtration rate Estimation in Thailand),S22–S25 highlighting the importance of standardized surveillance and risk assessment.

A major challenge is that CKD remains largely silent until advanced stages. In the national Screening and Early Evaluation of Kidney Disease study, approximately 98% of individuals with CKD were unaware of their condition.5 Historically, the value of early detection was questioned because therapeutic options were limited. However, the emergence of disease-modifying therapies, particularly sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors, has fundamentally changed the rationale for screening and early intervention. Early identification now offers an opportunity to delay progression, reduce cardiovascular complications, and potentially avert kidney failure.

Thailand has responded with pragmatic approaches suitable for resource-constrained settings. Community-based albuminuria screening, point-of-care testing, and pharmacist-led detection programs have expanded access to early diagnosis beyond traditional health care facilities (Supplementary Results: Community-Based Screening and Case-Finding).S14–S15 In parallel, risk-prediction tools are being validated and population-based surveillance strengthened. Their national deployment is progressing through a phased process, with technical, governance, and interoperability requirements for integration into the national Health Data Center still being addressed. Fully risk-stratified care therefore remains an evolving national priority rather than a uniformly implemented model (Supplementary Results: Risk Estimation and Artificial Intelligence).S16–S21

Perhaps the most distinctive feature of Thailand’s prevention strategy is its reliance on community engagement. Subdistrict health-promoting hospitals and village health volunteers form the country's frontline network, extending preventive service and care through household visits, health education, blood pressure monitoring, and referral support—particularly in communities with limited access to specialist services.

The effectiveness of this approach has been demonstrated through 2 landmark programs. The ESCORT study showed that integrated multidisciplinary care supported by community networks slowed CKD progression and was highly cost-effective compared with conventional care.6 Similarly, the CKD Prevention in the Northeast of Thailand initiative combined village health volunteers, telehealth, digital tools, and multidisciplinary teams to improve risk-factor control and strengthen continuity of care.7 Together, these programs demonstrate that effective CKD management in low- and middle-income countries does not necessarily depend on large numbers of nephrologists but rather on coordinated systems that empower primary care, allied health professionals, and communities (Figure 2; Supplementary Results: Integrated, Community-Anchored Care Models and Telehealth and Digital Health Interventions).S26–S34

Thailand has also implemented population-level prevention strategies targeting upstream determinants of kidney disease. Excess dietary sodium remains a major driver of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and CKD progression. In response, Thailand launched the SALTS initiative (Surveillance monitoring and evaluation, Awareness, Legislation and environment reform, Technology and innovation, and Stakeholder networks), a multisectoral strategy involving public education, food-industry engagement, environmental reform, technology, and surveillance.8 By integrating kidney health into broader cardiovascular and noncommunicable disease prevention efforts, the program demonstrates how population-wide risk reduction can complement clinical interventions (Supplementary Results: Population-Level Sodium Reduction).S35–S37

Policy and financing reforms have further strengthened the prevention agenda. CKD has been incorporated into Thailand's national noncommunicable disease framework, and the CKD Prevention Decade (2022–2031) established ambitious targets for risk-based screening and early intervention. These initiatives reflect growing recognition that the sustainability of universal health coverage depends not only on financing dialysis but also on reducing incident kidney failure through prevention (Supplementary Results: Public Awareness and Engagement).S38,S39

Thailand’s ongoing transformation provides an early example of how the 2025 World Health Assembly kidney health resolution can be operationalized. The resolution emphasizes prevention, early detection, equitable access to effective therapies, integration of kidney care within noncommunicable disease programs, workforce development, and sustainable financing. Many of these priorities are already being implemented through community screening, village health volunteers networks, multidisciplinary CKD clinics, sodium-reduction strategies, and expansion of disease-modifying therapies, as detailed across the Supplementary Results.

Thailand is now entering a new phase focused on disease modification and implementation science. Sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors are advancing toward inclusion in the National List of Essential Medicines, supported by increasing availability of locally manufactured generics. In parallel, national learning-health-system initiatives and the forthcoming multinational IMPLEMENT-CKD trial seek to accelerate evidence-based care through digital decision support, workforce development, audit and feedback, and policy engagement (Supplementary Results: Disease-Modifying Therapy and Cost-Effectiveness).S53–S58 Together, these efforts mark a shift from clinical efficacy to population-level effectiveness.

Several lessons emerge from Thailand’s experience. First, prevention-first kidney care is feasible within a middle-income country operating under universal health coverage. Second, community health workers can substantially extend the reach of kidney services beyond specialist centers. Third, effective disease-modifying therapies create a compelling rationale for early detection and risk stratification. Finally, meaningful progress requires coordinated action across health care delivery, public health, policy, financing, and community engagement.

The World Health Assembly kidney health resolution provides a global framework for action.9 Thailand's experience demonstrates that a transition from dialysis expansion toward prevention-first kidney care is achievable when community-based care, public-health interventions, and equitable access to effective therapies are pursued together. Although challenges remain, including workforce limitations, health-literacy gaps, and regional disparities in care, Thailand’s evolving strategy offers practical lessons for other low- and middle-income countries seeking sustainable approaches to reduce kidney failure while preserving equity and financial protection.
