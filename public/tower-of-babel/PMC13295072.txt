Breast cancer screening in Europe and the role of general practitioners: A 32-country comparative survey

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBreast cancer is the commonest cancer in women, and screening can allow earlier-stage diagnosis. While there are European recommendations on the age-range and frequency of breast cancer screening, participation in these programmes varies substantially, and this could be due to differences in how they are organised and implemented. The role that primary healthcare professionals play in the process is unclear.We aimed to describe the breast cancer screening programmes in European countries and investigate how primary healthcare professionals are involved in this screening process.MethodsA cross-sectional survey in 32 countries. Key informants with relevant expertise answered online questions about the characteristics of their screening programmes and general practitioners’ (GPs’) roles in this. Responses were refined through an iterative consensus process. Data were examined to identify patterns in GP engagement.ResultsWe found important differences between European breast cancer screening systems. While most had population-based screening, four countries relied on opportunistic screening. In 15 countries GPs had little or no involvement in the process of breast cancer screening, and in 13 countries GPs had some involvement, including identifying eligible patients, issuing referrals, and following-up results. Screening uptake rates tended to be higher in countries with well-established population-based screening programmes which give little or no GP involvement. Few countries linked GP engagement to incentives or performance measures.ConclusionCountries with lower screening uptake should consider either enhancing GP involvement or transitioning to a structured, population-based screening system. Further research should explore how best to integrate primary care within national screening strategies.Keywords: Breast cancer screening, General practice, Primary health care; Early diagnosis; European region

Background

Breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer in women [1], with about 355,000 new cases of breast cancer diagnosed in 27 European countries in 2020. Population-based screening gives a significant opportunity to diagnose breast cancer at an earlier stage, and it is recommended by the European Council as a way achieve public health benefits and cost efficiency [2].

The European Union’s (EU) Cancer Screening Scheme, the cornerstone initiative of Europe’s Beating Cancer Plan [1], recommended breast cancer screening with mammography for women aged 50 to 69 as a minimum, with advice to extend this to women aged between 45 and 74 years, and that at least 90% of the target population should have received breast cancer screening by 2025 [3]. However, participation in cancer screening programmes varies substantially throughout Europe [4], and these differences could be due to differences in the implementation of screening programmes. Population-based programmes are more efficient in reducing cancer mortality, even though their implementation still cannot guarantee the total population coverage [4]. Inequalities in access can be exacerbated by population socioeconomic differences, limited health system resources, and difficulties in structuring the necessary logistics [5].

Higher cancer screening participation rates have been associated with greater primary care involvement, and primary care providers play a critical role in reducing diagnostic delay, especially in health systems with long wait times for outpatient diagnostic services [6]. To ensure high levels of participation, strategies should be tailored to countries’ different healthcare systems and population risk factors [7].

Healthcare professionals have multiple possible roles in promoting cancer screening, as advocates, educators, medical specialists, quality controllers, and patient supporters [8]. Involvement of all members of the primary care team in cancer screening, rather than just the physician, could improve attendance rates [9]. As screening is shifting from opportunistic to population-based initiatives in several European countries, the role of primary care physicians is changing from one of controlling the screening process to one of providing support, education, and coordination [8]; however, the lack of specific recommendations regarding the role of General Practitioners (GPs) and allied health professionals in cancer screening may compromise the quality of services to the population [8].

The aim of this study was to describe the breast cancer screening programmes available in European countries, investigate how GPs and primary care health professionals were involved in this screening, and compare these data and screening uptake across the participating countries.

These insights into the different breast cancer screening programmes in Europe, and the variations in GP involvement, will allow policymakers to find potential areas for improvement in their existing programmes, allowing them to plan more effective approaches that allow a more timely breast cancer diagnosis.

Methods

Setting

This survey was led by the Örenäs Research Group (ÖRG), a collaborative group of over 135 clinicians and researchers from 85 clinical and academic centres in 35 European countries. In 2024, key Informants (KIs: GPs, academics and other health system experts with an insight into their national breast cancer screening programmes) completed online questionnaires. National Leads (NLs) worked with them to develop a consensus on their answers.

Participants and recruitment

Potential NLs were invited to participate because they were clinicians or researchers with knowledge of their national primary care context and breast cancer screening pathways, and because they were able to coordinate recruitment and consensus procedures within their countries. They were identified through the Örenäs Research group, the European GP Research Network (EGPRN), and examining relevant literature and websites. They were then sent an email invitation to take part in the study, with details of exactly what they would need to do.

We tried to recruit NLs in every European country where we could find an appropriate contact, including the countries in the Örenäs Research Group, and were able to enrol NLs from 32 countries in the WHO European region [10]. Each NL was asked to use purposive sampling to recruit four KIs from their own country, to allow consensus formation within that country.

The NLs needed to be fluent in English (the study design language) and have an awareness of how the breast cancer screening process worked in their primary care system. KIs had to have knowledge about the cancer screening programmes available in their countries and the role of primary care in those, and needed to be:

The 26-question survey form included participant demographic questions, closed- and open-ended questions on the availability of cancer screening programmes, the characteristics of the programmes (target population, type of screening test, frequency of testing, participation rates) and the involvement of primary care in the delivery of these programmes (for example, recruitment of the target population, management of the results).

The questionnaire was in English. NLs were permitted to translate the questions into their local language if they felt this would be useful, though this option was not taken up. The questionnaires were hosted on Survey Monkey (Survey Monkey, California, USA).

The questionnaire was piloted by 15 participants from different countries to estimate the time required to complete the online form, assess the difficulty and the accuracy of questions, and the clarity of the language. We made modifications that were based on feedback from the pilot participants. Our survey asked KIs and NLs about breast, cervical, colorectal and prostate cancer screening. This paper reports the results relating to the questions on breast cancer screening.

Sample size

We aimed for data from as many European countries as possible, with a minimum of 20 countries, and a target of 25 countries.

After reviewing the pilot answers, we found that for each country four KIs would be sufficient to generate consensus answers. However, we decided to accept three KIs from the small number of countries where recruitment proved difficult.

Data analysis process

For each country, the NL and one core research team member compared the NLs’ answers, decided which answers had a clear consensus and agreement between participants, and which had significant differences between participants.

Where a question resulted in significant differences between the KIs’ answers, it was sent back to them, with a request for them to consider (independently) whether they wanted to change any of their answers, and if not to comment on why they thought the differences were there. The NL and core group member reviewed the replies and decided whether they could now complete the consensus-making process. If not, they were asked to repeat the process until there was a consensus.

Where possible, the NLs and core team reviewed publicly available literature to confirm the consensus answers. The core research team then had multiple meetings to discuss the best way to collate and present the data.

We examined the data to determine the presence of themes and concepts related to the involvement of GPs in the screening pathway, in raising awareness by informing individuals about breast cancer screening, the promotion of screening uptake, the use of informative material on breast cancer screening, and the involvement of other healthcare professionals within the practice.

There were 142 KIs involved, with a median of four KIs per country (range 3–8). We had one NL for each country, except for Spain where two people shared this work. Over 90% were GPs and/or primary care academics.

Most of the surveyed countries had national screening programmes and recommendations, while some had regional level involvement or variations on these (Table 1). In Bulgaria, screening was offered as part of the basic health annual check-up to women with insurance coverage. Ukraine had official recommendations, but no organised screening programme. All countries used mammography for screening, except for Portugal which used tomosynthesis. Some countries also used breast ultrasound and/or examination by a clinician.

All countries screened for breast cancer every two years, except for the UK, in which screening was every three years.

Most participating countries had a population-based breast screening approach, in which eligible individuals were invited to attend screening automatically. Screening was opportunistic in Bulgaria, Lithuania, Romania, and Ukraine. In Switzerland, some cantons had a population-based approach to breast cancer screening, while in others screening was opportunistic.

Some countries (Austria, France, Greece, Slovakia and Türkiye) had an opportunistic system that coexisted with a population-based approach. In Türkiye, despite a population-based screening approach, eligible individuals were invited by their general practice unit with which they were registered.

Countries that required high levels of GP involvement in the breast cancer screening process

There were four countries in this category. In Bulgaria and Lithuania, GPs reported playing a key role in most parts of the screening pathway, from the identification of the eligible individuals to the evaluation of the results; practices used their own alert and support systems, which assisted the GPs in the recruitment process. In Bulgaria, breast cancer screening was part of the check-up service, with GPs inviting individuals to attend screening during consultations. In Lithuania, individuals were alerted to attend screening by invitation letters issued through the practice, or verbally by telephone or when women visited the practice; the whole primary care team (nurses, administrators, coordinators) was regularly involved in the recruitment, by reviewing the list of the eligible women, and by raising their awareness by informing them about the screening programme and checking their adherence to it.

In Romania and Ukraine, although GPs reported having a role in identifying and issuing referrals for breast cancer screening, no specific support systems were available to them to make this process easier. In Ukraine, specialists also had a role in breast cancer screening; referrals for breast cancer screening were also issued by specialists like mammologists, gynaecologists and oncologists.

Countries that required some GP involvement in the breast cancer screening process

Thirteen countries were included in this category. In Austria, Belgium, Croatia, France, Greece, Hungary, Israel, Italy, Latvia, North Macedonia and Portugal, GPs had access to information on invitations and could follow up on a screening invitation. In some cases, there were alert systems requiring or suggesting checking whether the individual had been screened. In Belgium, for example, the software sent email alerts to GPs, enabling them to resend invitations after missed screenings. In Croatia, information about the invitation and an individual’s response was available from the registry, and GPs motivated patients in the target group to participate in the national preventive programme.

When GPs in Greece logged in to the prescribing platform during consultations, they saw a pop-up message informing them that the woman had received an invitation. In Italy, alert systems reminded the GP to ask if they have been screened. In Israel, alert systems flagged those that still need to be screened. In Latvia, alert systems notified the GP to check who received the invitation about who participated, and individuals who missed their invitations were encouraged to contact their GP to have their invitation re-issued.

In North Macedonia, the electronic system facilitated verification of the invitation and information about whether the GP had received the test results. In Hungary, although support systems were not available, GPs received a list every three months from the health insurance fund of women that had not yet been screened, in order to encourage them to participate.

In some countries with a population-based approach, GPs were more involved in the screening process. For instance, in the Czech Republic, eligible individuals received an invitation to visit their GP or gynaecologist for an examination, and they were issued with a mammography referral during the visit. In other countries, as in Türkiye, although there was a population-based approach, GP clinics played a key role in identifying and inviting eligible women in their registered populations for screening.

Countries that required little or no GP involvement in the breast cancer screening process

There were fifteen countries included in this category. In some northern European countries, like Denmark, Finland, The Netherlands, Norway and Sweden, GPs had no active role in the invitation process, and had no access to support systems allowing them to follow-up the recruitment, invitation or any missed invitations. In these countries, eligible individuals did not need a referral from the GP to attend screening. GPs also had a minimal role in managing the results. The exception to this was the Netherlands, where GPs had to act on positive results: they needed to inform women of the results and refer them, then report this action to the screening organisation. In these countries there were no incentives for the GPs, and they were rarely involved in raising awareness about breast cancer screening.

In some other countries, like Germany, Ireland, the United Kingdom, Spain, Poland, Estonia, Georgia, and Slovenia, GPs had no active role in the invitation process and had no access to support systems allowing them to follow up on the recruitment. However, they could refer opportunistically, follow up on the process during consultations to encourage participation, or remind women to register for the screening programme if not done automatically (e.g. Ireland), and in some cases give help in organising an appointment (e.g. Spain). GPs were not regularly involved in onward referrals after abnormal findings, except for Slovenia, where they had some involvement. In these countries there were neither screening targets for the GPs nor incentives.

In Slovakia, despite the population-based approach, in some regions it was still standard practice for GPs and gynaecologists to issue screening referrals. This also happened in the Swiss cantons that used an opportunistic approach.

Transgender individuals were included in the population-based breast cancer screening programme in some countries, while in others the approach was opportunistic or a combination of these. For example, in the United Kingdom, eligible transgender individuals received an automatic invitation to attend breast cancer screening, but if they did not receive one, they were encouraged to contact their GP.

Discussion

In this survey that included almost all the countries in Europe, we found that most participating countries had national breast cancer screening programmes, and all except Portugal used mammography as a screening method. In some countries, women were invited to attend an examination by a clinician in addition to mammography.

There were important differences in the breast cancer screening programmes. While most had population-based screening, some still relied on opportunistic screening. In some countries, we found that GPs played a crucial role in the screening process, including identifying eligible patients, issuing referrals, and following up on results. In other countries, especially those with well-organised national screening programmes, GPs had little or no involvement, as invitations and follow-ups were managed centrally.

Notably, screening uptake rates were substantially higher in the countries with well-established population-based screening programmes.

Some countries in which GP action was needed gave them financial incentives or targets.

All but two of our surveyed countries had moved from opportunistic to population-based breast cancer screening programmes, because of their potential for significant public health benefits. While the initial investment in population-based screening can be substantial, early detection and prevention can lead to improved prognosis and improved quality of life, as well as long-term cost savings due to a reduced need for expensive advanced breast cancer treatments [12].

The high breast cancer screening uptake rates in countries with well-established population-based screening programmes may have been because such programmes standardise processes and actively reach out to people, rather than waiting for women to ask for screening or relying on individual clinicians to initiate it [13,14]. However, it may be that involving GPs as key actors in population-based screening programmes allowed more personalised care, giving GPs the opportunity to assess individual women’s risks, to inform them about the importance of screening and to discuss any concerns.

Overall, the median screening uptake levels in countries that required high levels of GP involvement in the breast cancer screening process were substantially lower than those that needed some GP involvement, and this in turn was substantially lower than in those that required little or no GP involvement. While there was a statistically significant difference between these three groups, there was considerable variation within each group and overlap between them. This may be because of differences in access, public awareness, cultural attitudes, concerns about overdiagnosis, fear of cancer, or lower health literacy [15–18].

Practices regarding clinical breast examinations varied not only between countries, but also between KIs in the same country. These within-country differences may have been due to clinicians being slow to act on new evidence and guidelines [19]. Clinicians’ attitudes to screening can change as a result of their experience, for example a recent diagnosis of breast cancer in a GP’s clinic could lead to a significant and lasting increase in subsequent screening rates [20].

Although seventeen countries needed some or high levels of GP involvement in the screening process by their GPs, only five gave their GPs targets. While performance targets can motivate GPs to increase screening efforts [21], sustained improvement in screening uptake depends on adequate system-level support and active GP endorsement [22,23].

Our evidence that there were many similarities, but also significant variations, in how GPs were integrated into cancer screening programmes across the different European healthcare systems confirms and adds to existing evidence on this [19,24]. Our findings on screening uptake levels parallel those reported in Eurostat’s 2021 data, showing that the Nordic countries had the highest rates, with Denmark’s and Finland’s uptake levels above 80% [25].

In contrast to previous assertions that the role of GPs in breast cancer screening is very important [19,20, 24,26,27], we found evidence that GPs’ involvement in, and impact on, breast cancer screening was not profound. However, this was predominantly explained by many countries using population-based, systematic and centrally organised screening, with administrative units taking care of invitations, reminders, investigations and follow-up. These systems achieved high screening uptake rates with minimal GP involvement in the screening process.

The Netherlands showed a slight decline in participation rates, which in 2023 dropped to 70.3%. A recent study (2024) in the Netherlands examining reasons for their downward trend in screening rates recommended that ‘new innovations to optimise current cancer screening programs should be implemented only in close consultation with GPs’ [28].

There is some evidence that the time constraints in primary care, as well as system level challenges (communication, information flow, financial resources, eligibility issues, scheduling) and patient-related barriers (including reluctance, poor compliance and low adherence), have reduced GPs’ ability and inclination to provide preventative consultations during acute illness consultations [29].

Although we found that there was no payment for breast cancer screening in most countries, in some countries GPs reported receiving bonuses for achieving screening targets. In other countries, levels of cancer screening were considered to be an indicator of quality of medical care, so were measured by the health system and reported to the GP. This could be in the form of an ongoing percentile score, serving as an implicit part of the GP’s esteem and encouraging higher screening performance [30,31].

This is, to our knowledge, the first study to describe how GPs and their practices are involved in breast cancer screening across Europe. The questionnaire was carefully developed by the research team, piloted extensively, and modified following feedback. A key strength of our work is the use of consensus formation by GPs and other experts in each of these countries, using iterative rounds where needed, guided by both an expert NL and a member of the core research team. The study successfully recruited participants in countries in which primary care is under-represented in research. The collated results were sent back to NLs for member-checking, and so that any recent changes to systems and policies could be included in the manuscript. We triangulated each country’s consensus answers with our own literature search, to reduce the risk that a country’s consensus results were biased or incomplete.

The questionnaires were in English, and there was a risk that respondents misunderstood some of the questions. However, we gave the NLs the opportunity to translate the questions into their local language if they felt this would be useful. Because the methodology meant that respondents could not be anonymous, there was a risk of social desirability bias in their answers. NLs and their core research team colleagues may have been biassed or made errors in their decision-making on the consensus answers. While sampling was purposeful, KIs’ answers may not have been typical of GPs in their countries, and we limited the target number of KIs per country to four. Respondents may not have been aware of regional differences in their countries’ screening programmes. There may have been national differences in response styles, for instance choosing or avoiding the ‘extreme’ options on the scales [32].

Comparison of national screening uptake levels needs to be made with caution, as the most recent years for which data are available varied from 2016 to 2024. This included the Covid-19 pandemic years in which screening programmes were disrupted, and in some cases the uptake values available to us may have been estimated.

While countries that screen for breast cancer opportunistically should consider changing to population-based screening, as this is in general linked with higher uptake levels, such strategies should be adapted to reflect differences in countries’ healthcare systems and population risk profiles. Because of the concern that lack of GP engagement could negatively impact screening participation in the long term, healthcare systems should consider how to engage GPs’ involvement through better support systems, incentives, and structured call/recall mechanisms where these are not already present.

Further research is needed to determine the most effective balance between GP involvement and centralised screening programme management.

Conclusions

In this survey covering most European countries, we found important differences how breast cancer screening was organised. Most had population-based screening, with some using opportunistic screening. GPs had little or no involvement in the countries that used population-based screening. In these countries, breast cancer screening uptake rates tended to be higher, suggesting that relying on GPs to take an active role in breast cancer screening was inferior to a population-based system.

Countries with lower screening uptake should consider enhancing GP involvement (through structured support, training, and appropriate incentives) or transitioning to population-based models where these are not yet in place. Determining the optimal balance between centralised programme management and primary care engagement is essential to ensuring equitable, timely access to breast cancer screening across healthcare settings.
