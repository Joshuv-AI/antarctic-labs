Characteristics and long‐term outcomes of children with confirmed focal cortical dysplasia type 1 after epilepsy surgery: A population‐based study
Authors: Vincent Zheng, Päivi Koroknay‐Pál, Henri Lehtinen, Eija Gaily, Atte Karppinen, Anders Paetau, Olli Tynninen, Heta Leinonen, Aki Laakso, Eeva‐Liisa Metsähonkala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveFocal cortical dysplasia type 1 (FCD1) is a rare and heterogeneous cause of drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) in children. Its clinical characteristics remain poorly understood, and surgical outcomes may be less favorable than in FCD2. We conducted a population‐based study to characterize the clinical presentation and long‐term seizure and cognitive outcomes of pediatric patients undergoing epilepsy surgery for isolated FCD1.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed all children ≤18 years who underwent resective epilepsy surgery at Helsinki University Hospital between 1994 and 2022. Thirty‐eight patients with histopathologically confirmed isolated FCD1 and no clinical or EEG features suggesting FCD2 were included. Clinical features, presurgical evaluations, surgical variables, postoperative outcomes, and cognitive trajectories were systematically extracted. Cognitive outcomes were assessed using a trajectory‐based approach at 2 years after surgery (or 2 years post‐reoperation).ResultsThe median age at epilepsy onset was 1.5 years, and 63% presented with recently accelerated cognitive decline. Two years after surgery, 34% achieved Engel class 1. At a median long‐term follow‐up of 8.4 years, Engel class 1 remained stable at 32%. Residual MRI‐visible lesion following surgery strongly predicted seizure recurrence. Frontal resections were most common (42%). Surgery diminished the recently accelerated cognitive decline in 46% of affected patients, with either stabilization or improvement in their cognitive trajectory at 2 year follow‐up. Two patients required life‐saving palliative procedures due to super‐refractory epilepsy.SignificanceIn this nationwide cohort, isolated FCD1 frequently presented with early‐onset DRE and preoperative accelerated cognitive decline. Although complete seizure freedom was achieved in a minority, over half of the patients experienced worthwhile improvement in seizure outcomes, along with stabilization or enhancement of cognitive trajectory. As FCD1 can cause life‐threatening epilepsy and contribute to cognitive regression, epilepsy surgery should be actively considered for children with FCD1‐related drug‐resistant epilepsy.Plain Language SummaryFocal cortical dysplasia type 1 is a rare brain abnormality that can cause severe epilepsy in children that does not respond to medication. In this nationwide study of 38 children who underwent brain surgery for this condition, about one‐third became seizure‐free, and over half experienced meaningful improvement. Nearly two‐thirds of patients showed worsening thinking and learning abilities before surgery, and surgery helped slow or stop this decline in almost half of them. These findings suggest that brain surgery should be considered for children with this difficult‐to‐treat condition.

INTRODUCTION

Focal cortical dysplasias (FCDs) are congenital neuronal migration and differentiation disorders resulting in abnormal organization of the cerebral cortex, first described over 50 years ago. 1 Currently, cortical dysplasias are classified according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) as type 1 (cortical dyslamination, FCD1), type 2 (dyslamination and dysmorphic neurons, FCD2), and type 3 (dysplasia associated with other epileptogenic lesions, FCD3). 2

FCDs are the most common pathology in histopathological samples after resective epilepsy surgery in pediatric patients. 3 , 4 In adults, FCDs are the third most common etiology, surpassed only by mesial temporal sclerosis and ganglioglioma. 5 , 6 , 7 It is suggested that the results of operative treatment might be better among patients with FCD2 than FCD1. 8 , 9 , 10 In addition, FCD2 appears to be more common than FCD1 in FCD cohorts. 11

FCD1 is subdivided into FCD1a (abnormal radial cortical lamination), FCD1b (abnormal tangential cortical lamination), and FCD1c (abnormal radial and tangential cortical lamination). 2 FCD1 remains less well characterized clinically and radiologically than FCD2. 12 Holthausen et al. described 19 patients with FCD1a who shared several clinical features: posterior location of the lesion, lack of focal neurological signs, severe cognitive impairment, and subtle cortical hypoplasia in the epileptogenic region on MRI. 13 For FCD1b and 1c, a comprehensive clinical and neuropathological specifications are lacking, and there is doubt as to whether they represent true developmental malformations or are more closely associated with acquired lesions, such as perinatal hypoxic–ischemic injury or prolonged prenatal ischemia. 14 , 15 Therefore, as FCD1 represents a less well‐understood etiology, these patients may be less often considered for surgical treatment.

Helsinki University Hospital (HUH) serves as Finland's main center for pediatric epilepsy surgery. We analyzed a consecutive series of pediatric patients who underwent resective surgery for histologically confirmed FCD1. The aim of this study was to investigate the clinical presentation and the seizure and cognitive outcomes after surgery in patients with FCD1 pathology.

METHODS

Patients

We reviewed our institutional Epilepsia‐Helsinki Surgery Register for all pediatric patients (≤18 years) who underwent resective epilepsy surgery at Helsinki University Hospital between 1994 and 2022. We identified 105 patients who underwent resective surgery for FCD (Figure 1). Thirty‐eight patients with isolated FCD1 were included in the final analysis. Patients with histopathological FCD1 but with clinical characteristics (e.g., unifocal highly frequent seizures but with no severe cognitive impairment) 16 or EEG features highly suggesting FCD2 (e.g., focal continuous rhythmic discharges and repetitive spiking) 17 , 18 , 19 were excluded (n = 7), ensuring a clinically and pathologically concordant cohort, which likely reduced the possibility that samples were taken from the perilesional area of an underlying FCD2. 15

Cohort selection process.

Data on clinical presentation, progression of disease, cognitive trajectory pre‐ and post‐surgery, as well as radiological studies, were extracted from both electronic and paper medical records. Seizure frequency was categorized as follows: daily, weekly (1–6 per week), monthly (<4 per month), less than monthly (<12 per year), or seizure‐free (no seizures in the past year).

Presurgical evaluations

The presurgical evaluation included identification of epilepsy type or syndrome and a structured assessment of developmental level. Children beyond infancy (over 2 years) and with adequate cognitive level and co‐operation were assessed with a standardized neuropsychological examination.

All patients underwent video‐EEG monitoring and MRI. Our MRI protocol has evolved over time, resulting in variation in the sequences used across the study period. Earlier patients were imaged using 1.5‐Tesla MRI (n = 10, 26.3%).

At HUH, the present standard protocol for patients with epilepsy includes the following 3‐Tesla sequences: sagittal T1‐weighted 3D MPRAGE (Magnetization Prepared Rapid Gradient Echo Imaging), sagittal 3D FLAIR (Fluid‐Attenuated Inversion Recovery) with fat suppression, axial T2‐weighted (3 mm slice thickness), coronal T2‐weighted (3 mm slice thickness, angled perpendicular to the long axis of the hippocampal body), and axial SWI (Susceptibility‐Weighted Imaging). For patients undergoing surgical evaluation, additional imaging is performed, including: sagittal T2‐weighted SPACE (Sampling Perfection with Application‐Optimized Contrast), axial T2‐weighted (TRA, 1 mm slice thickness, whole cerebrum), coronal T2‐weighted (1.3 mm slice thickness, whole head), sagittal 3D FLAIR with fat suppression, sagittal T2‐weighted (1 mm slice thickness), and sagittal 3D T1‐weighted MPRAGE (0.8 mm slice thickness). If needed, a sagittal Gadolinium‐enhanced 3D T1‐weighted MPRAGE (0.8 mm slice thickness) is also performed.

Other presurgical evaluations were performed as needed and included magnetoencephalography (MEG), functional MRI (fMRI), transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), positron emission tomography (PET), ictal single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), intracarotid amytal, i.e., WADA test, intracranial stereo‐EEG or grid‐ and strip recordings.

Surgical characteristics

Neuronavigation was used in all cases. Resections were typically performed combining en bloc and piecemeal techniques: the superficial cortical surface and underlying proximate core in one piece and complementary resection of deep surrounding cortex using suction or ultrasonic aspirator as necessary.

Surgical resections were classified into two categories: lobectomy (standard resection or resection determined by the anatomical border of the lobes) and tailored resection, usually guided by intracranial EEG, intraoperative electrocorticography, or both. The insula was considered a separate lobe; if it was resected in addition to other lobes, surgery was considered multilobar.

Since 2010, postoperative MRI has been obtained on a routine basis, but during the beginning of the study period, it was only performed when recurrent seizures or unexpected neurological deficits were noted after surgery. The extent of resection in MR‐positive cases was evaluated by VZ and P.K‐P based on residual in MRI, as complete or incomplete, while blinded to seizure outcome.

Postoperative seizure outcome

Seizure outcomes were classified according to the Engel classification. 20 We collected seizure outcome at 2 years, and the long‐term seizure outcome was obtained from patient records, reviewed in February 2025.

Cognitive outcome

The aim was to qualitatively evaluate how surgery influenced the preoperative cognitive trajectory, rather than relying solely on cross‐sectional pre‐ and postoperative time points. This approach reflects the complex clinical circumstances surrounding epilepsy surgery and allowed for the inclusion of most patients in the analysis. Because of the young age or low cognitive level of many patients, age‐appropriate tests could not always be used; psychometrically valid performance scores could not always be obtained due to floor effects; and the variation in clinically relevant change in cognitive function across patients was wide. Therefore, a trajectory‐based classification approach was used to capture relative changes in cognitive function in relation to surgery.

Cognitive outcomes were evaluated by comparing preoperative baseline assessments with follow‐up assessments conducted approximately 2 years after the first surgery. In cases where reoperations occurred within 2 years, outcomes were determined 2 years after the most recent surgery. Standardized neuropsychological assessments were the primary source for determining cognitive outcomes. Pre‐ and postoperative NA data were available for 31 patients. Postoperative NA‐only data were available for 4 patients. When NAs were missing or inconclusive, additional sources were consulted, including reports from pediatric neurologists, occupational therapy assessments, and speech therapy evaluations in 3 patients.

A neuropsychologist within our epilepsy surgery program (third author, H.L.) reviewed all relevant records. Neuropsychological assessments (NAs) were conducted both preoperatively and postoperatively using the most clinically feasible test batteries according to each patient's cognitive level. The newest Finnish versions of the Wechsler intelligence scales, NEPSY, and Bayley Scales of Infant Development were most commonly used. In cases where reoperations occurred within 2 years, outcomes were determined 2 years after the most recent surgery. 21 , 22 , 23

The details of the cognitive outcome classification are presented in Figure 2. We established separate outcome classifications for patients with and without preoperative accelerated cognitive decline based on NA reports, which should clinically address how cognitive functioning is trending whenever possible. The preoperative trajectory evaluation was performed using assessments done recently before surgery. Preoperative cognitive performance level was compared to expected levels based on previous cognitive assessments and/or clinician notes, including evidence of recently lost or stagnated cognitive functioning.

Cognitive outcome classification. This preoperative cognitive trajectory classification aimed to capture the evaluated preoperative trend (assessed recently before surgery) relative to the earlier trend. Accelerated cognitive decline before surgery indicates an acute, clinically relevant deterioration that should inform the indication and timing of epilepsy surgery. (A) Patients showed an evaluated trend of accelerated cognitive decline relative to the earlier trend prior to resection. The earlier trend may have been stable or declining. Cognitive outcomes were classified into four categories. The first category, diminished with an improved trajectory, included cases where cognitive regression or stagnation was diminished, and cognitive development resumed and accelerated beyond the (preoperative) baseline. The second category, diminished with a steady trajectory, referred to cases where cognitive regression or stagnation was diminished, and cognitive development resumed from the previous/latest (preoperative) baseline. In the third category, continues with no effect from surgery, referred to cases where cognitive decline persisted at the preoperative level without any surgical impact. The fourth category, continues and worsens, cognitive decline intensified further after surgery or it persisted along with new cognitive deficits attributable to surgery. (B) Patients without accelerated cognitive decline prior to resection had an evaluated recent trend of stable/stabilized cognitive function. The earlier trend may have been stable or declining or was unknown due to lack of longitudinal data. Cognitive outcomes of these patients were classified into three categories. The better category included cases where the preoperative cognitive trajectory was accelerated following surgery. The no change category referred to cases where there was no alteration in cognitive trajectory postoperatively. The worse category included cases where postoperative cognitive development was slower compared to the preoperative trajectory. The no change category referred to cases where there was no alteration in cognitive trajectory postoperatively.

Patients were classified as having accelerated cognitive decline if their cognitive performance had recently declined relative to their earlier trend, which could reflect a slower developmental pace than previously, stagnated cognitive function, or loss of skills. If there was evidence of accelerated cognitive decline, the cognitive outcome was evaluated and classified against that trend.

In patients with either a stable cognitive trajectory or no data available to determine recent changes in preoperative trend, the outcome was classified more traditionally against the preoperative NA in a cross‐sectional manner.

Histopathology

For all patients, histopathological samples were classified according to the ILAE consensus paper 2 by two of the authors, OT and AP (neuropathologists with special expertise in epilepsy pathology). The FCDs were classified as FCD type Ia, FCD type Ib, and FCD type Ic.

Statistical analysis

Due to the skewed nature of the data, the Fisher's exact test or Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was used for categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U/Kruskal‐Wallis H‐test was applied for continuous variables, as appropriate. Continuous data is presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Statistical significance was set to p ≤ 0.05 (2‐tailed). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 29).

Ethical aspects

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the HUH. Under Finnish law, informed consent is not required for a retrospective study based on hospital records. We confirm that we have read the Journal's position on issues involved in ethical publication and affirm that this report is consistent with those guidelines.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Between 1994 and 2022, a total of 38 pediatric patients (<18 years at admission) with a histopathologically verified diagnosis of isolated FCD 1 were identified for analysis. The median age at epilepsy onset was 1.5 years (0.5–5.4), and the median time to first‐time surgical resection was 3.0 years (1.6–6.4). One month prior to surgery, patients were on a median of 3.0 (2.0–3.0) ASMs. There were 6 MRI‐negative patients. After the first surgery, at 2 years, 13 patients (34%) were Engel Class 1.

Factors associated with seizure outcomes

We compared the patients between Engel 1 and Engel 2–4. Age at epilepsy onset was higher in patients with an Engel 1 outcome (2.5 years vs. 0.8 years, p = 0.035). The distribution of epilepsy types also differed between these groups (p = 0.015). Focal nonsyndromic epilepsy was observed at similar rates (50% vs. 46%). Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) and Lennox–Gastaut syndrome (LGS) were more common in the Engel 2–4 group, whereas late‐onset infantile spasms (LOIS) were more frequent in the Engel 1 group. Surgical characteristics

The most common resection sites were frontal (42%) and temporal (26%) lobes, followed by parietal (13%) and multilobar resections (18%). Patients in the Engel 2–4 group more commonly underwent tailored resections (p = 0.007). The distribution of resection sites did not differ significantly between Engel 1 and Engel 2–4 groups (p = 0.44). Residual MRI lesions after the last surgery were more frequently observed in patients with Engel 2–4 outcomes compared to Engel 1 (42% vs. 8%, p = 0.038). Furthermore, the number of ASMs at last follow‐up was significantly higher in the Engel 2–4 group (3.0 vs. 2.5, p < 0.001). Detailed analysis of surgical characteristics is presented in Table 1.

Surgical complications after the first surgery

Surgical complications occurred in 4 patients (11%). One patient developed a postoperative epidural hematoma requiring surgical evacuation. Two patients developed wound infections; one underwent bone flap removal followed by cranioplasty, while the other required wound revision combined with intravenous antibiotic treatment. One patient developed hydrocephalus after extensive parietal resection with temporo‐occipital disconnection, which was treated with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt.

Neurological deficits after the first surgery

Permanent neurological deficits occurred in 5 patients (13%). Four patients experienced visual field disturbances (quadrantanopia), one of which was anticipated. Additionally, one patient who underwent an extensive parietal resection with temporo‐occipital disconnection developed expected hemianopia; this same patient also developed hydrocephalus, mentioned above. Transient hemiparesis occurred in 10 patients, resolving within days to months.

Cognitive outcomes

Twenty‐four patients (63%) presented with accelerated cognitive decline at the time of surgical evaluation. Surgery diminished accelerated cognitive decline in 11 patients (46% of those with preoperative, recently accelerated cognitive decline). Among the patients in whom accelerated cognitive decline was diminished after surgery, 7 (29%) exhibited a steady cognitive trajectory, while 4 (17%) showed an improved cognitive trajectory. Accelerated cognitive decline persisted in 13 patients (54%) despite surgery; of these, 1 (4%) experienced worsening attributed to surgery, and 12 (50%) showed no surgical effect.

Fourteen patients (37%) did not exhibit accelerated cognitive decline prior to surgery. After surgery, 3 of these patients (21%) demonstrated a worse cognitive trajectory, while 11 (79%) showed no change. Detailed data on cognitive outcomes are presented in Table 4.

Long‐term seizure outcomes

At a median long‐term follow‐up of 8.4 years (3.9–12.7), the Engel class 1 rate remained stable at 32%. Additionally, 2 patients (5%) were Engel class 2, while 8 patients (21%) were classified as Engel class 3 and 16 patients (42%) as Engel class 4. Notably, seizure freedom was rare in the presence of residual MRI epileptogenic findings: only 1 of 12 such patients achieved Engel Class 1 (p = 0.038). Additionally, 19 patients underwent various genetic studies, which are described in Table S1.

Notably, one patient with multifocal epilepsy and MRI findings of extensive right‐hemisphere cortical dysplasia developed super‐refractory status epilepticus requiring prolonged intensive care. This led to an initial parietal resection followed by a hemispherotomy performed as an emergency procedure at the age of one. The histopathology revealed FCD1B. After these surgeries, the patient's seizure outcome was Engel 4A, but he was able to be discharged for outpatient treatment.

Additionally, another patient with symptomatic IESS had continuous seizures and recurrent episodes of status epilepticus despite multiple ASMs, resulting in repeated hospitalizations and accelerated cognitive decline with loss of all previously acquired skills. MRI revealed left frontobasal cortical dysgenesis. She underwent a left frontal resection at the age of 10 months, with a histopathology of FCD1A. Although her seizure outcome remained Engel 4B, she could be discharged for outpatient care; recently, accelerated cognitive decline diminished, and she regained lost skills and learned to walk and communicate.

Reoperations

Seizure relapse occurred in 28 patients (74%). The median time from first surgery to relapse was 0.27 (0.1–1.5) years, and 5 patients relapsed after 2 years of follow‐up. Following the first surgery, MRI‐positive residuals were identified in 14 patients (37%). Of these, 8 patients did not undergo re‐resection: 4 had acceptably controlled epilepsy (thus re‐resection was not pursued by the family), 2 had undergone a primary palliative resection due to the lesion extending into functional areas, and in 2, subsequent investigations revealed that the residual lesion involved functional areas despite not being evident on MRI.

The median time from seizure relapse to reoperation was 1.8 (1.1–4.1) years. A total of 11 patients underwent reoperations: 9 had one re‐resection, 1 patient had two re‐resections, and 1 patient underwent hemispherotomy. Intracranial EEG was performed in six of these patients prior to reoperation, three of whom did not have any residual lesion on primary postoperative MRI.

After subsequent reoperations, 4 of 11 patients (36%) achieved an Engel Class I outcome at the 2‐year follow‐up, which decreased to 2 patients (18%) at the last follow‐up. The proportion of MRI‐negative patients was similar between the reoperated (18%) and non‐reoperated (17%) groups (p = 1.00).

DISCUSSION

We present a population‐based comprehensive analysis of surgical treatment outcomes for isolated FCD1. In line with most previous studies, 25 , 26 our findings confirm that surgical procedures are safe, with an acceptable incidence of permanent sequelae. At 2 years, 34% of patients achieved Engel Class 1, decreasing slightly to 32% at a median follow‐up of 8.4 years, suggesting most relapses occurred within the first 2 postoperative years. Although challenging to diagnose and manage, our findings indicate that a considerable proportion of patients achieve meaningful seizure and cognitive improvement following epilepsy surgery, supporting its role in carefully selected cases. Moreover, in some rare instances, surgery for FCD1 may be lifesaving.

Clinical presentations

The median age at epilepsy onset in our cohort was 1.5 years, lower than the 1.7–4.2 years reported in other FCD1 series. 16 , 27 , 28 This likely reflects our population‐based design, which captures early‐onset, severe phenotypes that may be underrepresented in other reported cohorts. Early‐onset patients were more likely to have worse seizure outcomes despite a comparable extent of resection, suggesting that poorer results may stem from more complex epileptogenic networks or undetected MRI‐negative FCD1 tissue beyond the resected lesion. This interpretation aligns with the broader literature, which emphasizes the challenges of identifying and fully removing subtle or diffuse FCD1, particularly in young children. 29 , 30

In our series, FCD1 was frequently associated with epilepsy syndromes associated with EE (52.6%), including IESS (24%), LOIS (18%), and LGS (11%). The literature on these syndromes varies: Krsek et al. 16 reported that 21% of FCD1 had a history of IESS, while Kwon et al. 11 found that 32% of patients with FCD presented with IESS or LGS (primarily EE evolving from IESS).

Epilepsy syndromes associated with EE, such as IESS and LGS, are characterized by generalized seizure types and generalized EEG patterns. 31 A survey of pediatric neurologists suggested that such presentations are less likely to be referred for surgical evaluation. 32 However, we emphasize the importance of referring such patients to a specialized epilepsy center with epilepsy surgery capabilities, as generalized seizures or EEG patterns may, in some cases, result from an underlying focal brain lesion such as FCD1. 33

FCD 1 subtypes

Clinical characteristics and seizure outcomes at both 2‐year and last follow‐up were broadly similar across FCD1 subtypes. Nevertheless, several non–statistically significant trends suggest that FCD1b may exhibit distinct features, particularly in comparison with FCD1a. FCD1b was associated with a later age at epilepsy onset, more frequent frontal resections, and a predominance of focal, non‐syndromic epilepsy, whereas FCD1a and FCD1c more often presented with epilepsy with combined focal and generalized features.

Compared with the isolated FCD1a cohort described as multilobar unilateral hypoplasia with severe epilepsy in children (MUHSEC) reported by Holthausen et al., 13 our relatively large FCD1b cohort (n = 27) showed a predominance of frontal involvement, in contrast to the posterior quadrant localization described for FCD1a. The age at epilepsy onset reported in the mentioned study was comparable to that observed in our FCD1a cohort.

In our study, although parietal involvement was most common in FCD1a, this was not statistically significantly different compared to the other subtypes. The posterior localization of FCD1a remains debated, as a large overall FCD study by Cohen et al. and a recent FCD1 subtype analysis by Splitkova et al. did not demonstrate a predominance of FCD1a in posterior regions. 29 , 34 Furthermore, despite the predominance of FCD1a in the Splitkova cohort, their clinical phenotype did not completely overlap with the recently described MUHSEC entity. Similarly, a large multicenter, Wagstyl et al. study failed to identify MUHSEC as a distinct etiology in early‐life FCD‐related drug‐resistant epilepsy. 35

Comprehensive clinical descriptions and outcome measures for FCD1 subtypes remain scarce in the literature, as most studies consider FCD1 as a single entity. 9 , 11 , 36 While FCD1a is universally accepted by neuropathologists as a form of FCD1, the subtypes 1b and 1c remain more controversial and less established. 14 , 37 This explains the lack of comprehensive clinical descriptions for FCD1 subtypes. Given that FCD1 is a rare underlying pathology, accounting for only 3% of epilepsy surgery cases, 8 multicenter collaborations are needed in the future to elaborate its subtypes from a clinical‐pathological perspective.

Seizure outcomes

The existing literature reports Engel Class I outcomes in 21%–56% of FCD1 cases, which is consistent with our findings. 16 , 38 , 39 However, Splitkova et al. recently reported considerably higher seizure freedom rates, with 71% of 31 patients achieving ILAE class 1 at a median follow‐up of 5 years. 29 Several differences may explain this discrepancy. Their cohort was entirely contemporary (2010–2023), whereas our series extends back to 1994, including patients operated on before modern presurgical tools were available. Notably, 51.6% of their patients underwent long‐term invasive EEG monitoring (predominantly SEEG) prior to the first surgery, compared with 34% (13 of 38) in our cohort, and intracranial EEG was an independent predictor of favorable seizure outcome in their multivariable model. This suggests that precise delineation of the epileptogenic zone through invasive monitoring may substantially improve outcomes in FCD1, and the increasing availability and safety of SEEG may help overcome the localization challenges inherent to this pathology, particularly in MRI‐negative or discordant cases. It should also be noted that our population‐based design captures all operated patients regardless of case complexity, whereas referral‐based cohorts may overrepresent favorable cases. Population‐based cohorts are less prone to selection bias and likely provide a more representative estimate of real‐world surgical outcomes.

Residual epileptogenic lesion in MRI remains the strongest predictor of poor outcomes, as also shown in our series. 10 , 40 , 41 The modest seizure freedom rates likely reflect the difficulty of delineating FCD1, which often appears subtle or diffuse on MRI and poses challenges both for presurgical localization and intraoperative resection. Lobectomies were more frequently associated with seizure freedom compared with tailored resections (p = 0.007), which may reflect the larger extent of resection.

Although the majority of patients did not achieve seizure freedom, our study demonstrates that many experienced worthwhile improvement (Engel 1–3, 58%). Additionally, because FCD1 can be associated with life‐threatening epilepsy, as illustrated by two of our cases, palliative resections may constitute an important therapeutic strategy, first to preserve life and subsequently to facilitate transition to outpatient care. FCD1 remains a challenging pathology to both detect and treat. Several factors contribute to this challenge. FCD1, in addition to often affecting a larger and multilobar area, is also less histologically dysplastic. 2 , 14 This makes it not only more challenging to localize preoperatively but also more difficult for the neurosurgeon to delineate intraoperatively, especially when the lesion is extensive or close to eloquent areas, where balancing maximal resection and preservation of function is critical. Furthermore, the increasing use of intracranial EEG investigations, along with advances in imaging techniques such as 7 T MRI, may further improve localization and surgical planning in these patients. 42 , 43

Preoperative cognitive status and postoperative outcome

In the present study, over half of the cohort had accelerated cognitive decline recently before surgery, confirming prior studies that cognitive regression is a common characteristic in FCD 1. 11 , 16 , 44 While our cognitive outcome classification is not standardized, it allowed us to assess developmental trajectories even in very young children. This approach may be particularly relevant in pediatric cohorts where formal testing is sometimes incomplete and psychometrically equivalent data across patients is not available.

In the entire cohort, preoperative accelerated cognitive decline was observed in 63.2% of patients. Following surgery, the accelerated cognitive decline was diminished in almost half of these patients (45.8%). Among patients without accelerated cognitive decline, a better postoperative cognitive trajectory was not observed. This suggests that accelerated cognitive decline might be more likely to be reduced by timely surgery than in FCD1 patients with a more stable cognitive course. Although Veersema et al. 44 did not assess cognitive trajectories, their study provides the closest available comparison: they reported preoperative epileptic encephalopathy in 50% of patients with FCD1 versus 8% with FCD2. However, their FCD1 subgroup was small, comprising only 14% of the total cohort, limiting direct comparability.

Given that cognitive regression and executive dysfunction are often associated with FCD1, and that epilepsy surgery can lead to meaningful seizure reduction and improved quality of life, it is important to emphasize that timely referral to a specialized epilepsy center following the diagnosis of drug‐resistant epilepsy is essential. Early evaluation and treatment may help prevent cognitive decline and loss of acquired skills related to ongoing epileptic activity, which may be mitigated, even in patients with FCD1. 45 , 46

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, as our center is the main facility for pediatric epilepsy surgery in Finland and given that Finland has a publicly supported healthcare system with high healthcare equality 47 it is likely that selection bias in our patient population is minimized. These factors contribute to our cohort being a representative, unselected sample of a nationwide population of pediatric patients with FCD1‐related DRE.

We also did a careful re‐evaluation of the histopathological samples, thus making possible rigorous comparisons of FCD1 subtypes. Additionally, we excluded patients whose histopathological diagnosis was FCD1, but whose epilepsy features and EEG features were more characteristic of FCD2, thus ensuring that the study cohort more accurately reflects patients with true FCD1‐related DRE, thereby increasing the validity of our clinicopathological correlations.

However, our study has certain limitations, including its retrospective design and the smaller sample size in the FCD1 subgroups, which may have limited the statistical power of the comparisons.

Furthermore, we lacked standardized neuropsychological data for some patients, primarily due to their young age or low functional level at the time of surgery, which may have introduced bias in the comparison of cognitive outcomes particularly among those patients. Our analysis of cognitive trajectories is qualitative and based on variable amounts and quality of information in different patients. Due to the heterogeneity of test instruments used across assessments, transitions between different tests at different developmental stages, and known measurement imprecision at certain ability ranges, no single quantitative IQ/DQ change score could be reliably applied as a uniform criterion for outcome classification. While a change of approximately one standard deviation was considered suggestive of meaningful change as a clinical guideline, outcome categorization ultimately relied on clinical judgment integrating all available information. Although not standardized, this approach may be particularly relevant in young pediatric cohorts where psychometrically equivalent data across patients are not always available. It allows evaluation for the vast cognitive heterogeneity of our sample and provides certain clinical validity to our results. Yet our interpretations should be viewed as tentative and be strengthened by psychometrically more robust studies in the future. In addition, given that FCD1 may extend beyond MRI‐visible abnormalities, assessment of completeness of resection based on postoperative MRI may not fully reflect the true extent of the epileptogenic zone and should be interpreted cautiously.

CONCLUSIONS

In this nationwide cohort of children undergoing epilepsy surgery for isolated FCD1, the condition was frequently linked with epilepsy syndromes associated with EE and accelerated cognitive decline. Residual lesions in MRI were the strongest predictor of postoperative relapse. Importantly, surgery diminished accelerated cognitive decline in nearly half of the affected patients. Multicenter collaborations are warranted to define clinicopathological correlations of FCD1, especially with respect to its subtypes. Despite the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, many patients achieve substantial benefit from surgery, which can be lifesaving. Therefore, epilepsy surgery should be considered in this difficult population.
