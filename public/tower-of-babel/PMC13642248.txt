Investigating the human anellome across the lifespan reveals sex-specific biphasic trajectories
Authors: Yakhouba Kane, Yingying Ma, Beibei Yan, Xiuli Zhao, Ting Ge, Yanpeng Li, Le Cao, Min Zhang, Yuanyuan Pei, Zhenzhou Wan, Ting Zhang, Chiyu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anelloviruses dominate the human plasma virome, yet their lifespan dynamics and relationships with the immune system are unclear. We integrated metagenomics with cytokines, HERV-K, and CMV profiling across 405 individuals (0–100 years) and found that prevalence, abundance, and diversity varied non-linearly with age and by sex. Alphatorqueviruses accumulated progressively with age, while betatorqueviruses and gammatorqueviruses displayed biphasic, female-specific patterns. Alphatorquevirus species diversity had significant inflection points at ages 54–58 years in females. We identified a stable core of 12 age-uniform species, with their abundance trajectories diverging significantly by sex. Male bias in anellovirus metrics widened with age, and community dispersion reversed across lifespan. CMV IgG titers correlated significantly with anellovirus genus richness. In young adult females, anellovirus ORF1 was associated with IL-1β. These findings suggest altered anellovirus ecology as a correlate of age-related immune changes, positioning these viruses as strong indicators of immune status and offering new perspectives on age-modulated host-virus dynamics.

Introduction

Aging reshapes host immunity in ways that alter susceptibility to infection, vaccine responsiveness and the incidence of chronic diseases1. Recent longitudinal, single-cell and multi-omics studies have shown that immune aging is neither uniform nor strictly linear2,3. Discrete transitions in immune programs and molecular networks occur across the lifespan and are accompanied by changes in inflammatory mediators and cellular composition. These phased changes in innate and adaptive immunity provide a temporal framework within which persistent viruses and endogenous viral elements can shift from controlled commensalism toward dysregulated states of replication or expression, with potential consequences for systemic inflammation and tissue homeostasis2–4.

Anelloviruses (family Anelloviridae) are small circular single-stranded DNA viruses that establish lifelong, asymptomatic infections and comprise a dominant fraction of the human plasma virome. Individuals generally harbor multiple, highly diverse anellovirus lineages from the Alphatorquevirus, Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus genera. Within-host populations evolve over time through recombination and mutation, producing subject-specific “anellomes”5–8. In particular, high anellovirus loads have been strongly associated with autoimmune diseases and COVID-199. As anellovirus load and detectability are strongly conditioned by host immune competence, anelloviruses have been suggested as sensitive biomarkers of immune state in contexts of transplantation, chronic infection, autoimmune disease, cancer and aging2,10,11. Despite extensive cataloguing of anellovirus genomic diversity, a unified description spanning the human lifespan is still lacking. Specifically, little is known about how anellome composition, prevalence, and abundance change with age, how these trajectories differ by sex, and whether taxon-specific signals are associated with systemic inflammatory programs or endogenous retroelement activation.

Endogenous retroviruses (HERVs), particularly HERV-K (HML-2) family, have emerged as candidate mediators and markers of age-associated tissue dysfunction4,12–14. Recent mechanistic studies showed that HERV-K transcriptional derepression in senescent cells can produce retrovirus-like particles and locally amplify innate immune signaling and senescence-associated secretory phenotypes4,15. However, evidence for how HERV-K protein or particle level vary across the lifespan, and how such variation relates to circulating inflammatory markers and the plasma commensal and/or persistent virome, remains limited, and it may be discordant across tissue and assay contexts. In particular, the relationship between systemic HERV-K measurements and persistent viruses such as anellovirus dynamics has not been systematically investigated.

Human cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a ubiquitous persistent herpesvirus infecting 30–100% of the population, with prevalence increasing with age and unhygienic conditions. Infection often occurs at an early age (<5 years) with periodic reactivation throughout life. CMV is known to drive immune system remodeling with age, and elevated CMV IgG titers have been associated with enhanced systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and an increased risk of age‑related diseases in large human cohorts16,17. Recent studies in immunocompromised transplant recipients showed that CMV reactivation precedes an increase in Torque Teno Virus (TTV) load, and higher TTV predicted CMV viremia at the next clinical visit18,19. However, evidence linking CMV level to systemic anellovirus abundance and/or diversity across different ages and sexes remains limited. In particular, the relationship between CMV IgG titer and anellovirus diversity metrics has not yet been systematically investigated in aging population.

In this study, we conducted an integrated, lifespan-wide study of the human plasma virome together with circulating immune mediators and HERV-K expression. We used plasma metagenomics with ORF1-based taxonomic resolution of the anellome, alongside measurements of immune markers and HERV-K envelope (Env) protein expression, to map age- and sex-dependent patterns of anellovirus prevalence, abundance and diversity in relation to host immunity across a cohort spanning infancy through old age. We found that Anelloviridae dominated the human plasma virome and exhibit complex age- and sex-dependent dynamics in their abundance, diversity, and community structure. A consistent male bias in viral metrics widened with age. While broad immune associations were minimal, a strong link between anellovirus ORF1 and IL-1β was identified in young adult females.

Results

Study cohort and plasma virome overview

We characterized the plasma virome in a cross-sectional cohort of 405 individuals (median age 48 years) with a relatively balanced sex distribution, stratified into four age groups: 0-16 (Children, n = 55), 20-39 (Young adults, n = 104), 40–69 (Middle-aged adults, n = 118), and ≥70 (Elderly, n = 128) (Fig. 1A, B). Metagenomic sequencing yielded a total of 13,923,590 contigs. The mean number of contigs per individual was highest in young adults (37,070 ± 27,941), followed by middle-aged adults (35,837 ± 15,082) and the elderly (35,468 ± 20,667), whereas exhibited the lowest count (23,629 ± 22,155) (Supplementary Data 1 & 2). From this data, 283,908 contigs were classified as viral, with a mean of 639 to 766 contigs per individual across age groups. We identified 2752 complete viral genomes, 8640 high-quality, and 8828 medium-quality genomes (Supplementary Data 2). Anelloviridae dominated the plasma virome, with mean read counts ranging from 71,983 to 352,117 and a near-universal prevalence (96.3%) (Fig. 1C, D and Supplementary Fig. S1). All other detected viral families exhibited substantially lower abundance and prevalence (Fig. 1C, D). Nine Anellovirus genera were detected, with Alphatorquevirus, Betatorquevirus, and Gammatorquevirus being ubiquitous in the study cohort (Fig. 1E). Beyond the anelloviruses, other eukaryotic viral families were detected at low prevalence (<4%) across the entire cohort, with some detected sporadically in specific age groups (Fig. 1D–F). For example, Hepadnaviridae (genus Orthohepadnavirus) and Orthoherpesviridae (genus Simplexvirus) were predominantly detected in young and middle-aged adults. Sequences from Parvoviridae (genus Erythroparvovirus) and Polyomaviridae were almost exclusively found in middle-aged adults. In contrast, Picornaviridae reads primarily from the genus Enterovirus were most common in the elderly, while Flaviviridae (Pegivirus) was mostly detected in middle-aged adults. At the species level, human pegivirus was present in all age groups except young adults, with its highest prevalence (4.2%) in middle-aged adults. Several species were detected predominantly and/or exclusively in the elderly, including multiple enteroviruses (Coxsackievirus A16, A6, and Enterovirus A71), human betaherpesvirus 6, and Influenza A virus. Other viruses, such as parainfluenza virus 5, Merkel cell polyomavirus, and enterovirus C, were found primarily in middle-aged adults. The remaining viral species, including Hepatitis B virus, adenoviruses, and papillomaviruses, with very rare (<2%).

Anelloviruses genera showed divergent demographic patterns

Stratifying anellovirus at genus-level revealed distinct demographic patterns, including a consistent male-biased signal (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Fig. S2). The three major genera including Alphatorquevirus, Betatorquevirus, and Gammatorquevirus were highly prevalent and abundant across the cohort. Their prevalence and mean abundance generally increased with age, with Alphatorquevirus reaching 98.2–100% prevalence in both males and females. While this pattern is consistent for both Betatorquevirus, and Gammatorquevirus in males, the prevalence for these genera peaked in mid-life and dropped notably in old females. Also, we noted a subtle decline in Betatorquevirus prevalence and abundance in both young males and females. In contrast, Hetorquevirus and Yodtorquevirus were rare or entirely absent in most subgroups. Samektorquevirus prevalence was low but consistently found in all age groups of both sexes, and its overall prevalence increased with age. Modeling the prevalence and abundance of the four most prevalent anellovirus genera revealed distinct age and sex-specific dynamics (Fig. 2B). Alphatorquevirus exhibited a monotonic increase in prevalence from birth, peaking at 38.4 years in females and 65.7 years in males. Its abundance, however, continued to increase until the oldest ages. Betatorquevirus displayed a biphasic abundance pattern, declining from childhood to a nadir in young adulthood before rising to peak in late-life (73.7 years in males, 79.8 years in females). Its prevalence followed a similar but less pronounced trajectory, remaining consistently high and peaking later (67.7 years in females and at the cohort’s upper age boundary in males). Gammatorquevirus dynamics was sex-specific. In females, abundance increased steadily from infancy to a peak at 70 years, with prevalence peaking earlier at 59.6 years. In males, both prevalence and abundance reached the maxima at advanced ages (82-100 years). For Samektorquevirus, prevalence increased with age, peaking at the cohort’s upper boundary (100 years) in both sexes. Abundance trajectories differed: in females, it peaked at 77.8 years, while in males it was bimodal, with an early-life peak followed by a late-life peak similar in magnitude to the female peak.

The anellovirus community was dominated by a core set of highly prevalent species, primarily from the Alphatorquevirus genus (Supplementary Data 3). Six Alphatorquevirus species exhibited a cohort-wide prevalence >65%, forming a distinct high-prevalence tier: Alphatorquevirus homin29 (hereafter ATVhomin29; 75.6%), ATVhomin20 (69.5%), ATVhomin19 (69.0%), ATVhomin3 (68.7%), ATVhomin21.24 (65.6%), and ATVhomin15 (65.1%). An additional set of alphatorqueviruses, including ATVhomin18 (59.5%), ATVhomin9.10 (59.2%), and ATVhomin5 (58.7%), maintained prevalence >58%, supporting the genus’s dominance within the community structure. Beyond these alphatorqueviruses, the most prevalent representatives of other genera were Gammatorquevirus homidi7 (GTVhomidi7; 52.1%) and Betatorquevirus homini1 (BTVhomini1; 50.5%).

The stratification by age and sex revealed distinct patterns among the most prevalent and abundant species (Fig. 2C). A marked age-dependent accumulation was evident for nearly all top prevalent species. For instance, the prevalence of ATVhomin19 rose from 37.8% in Children to 79.7% in the Elderly, and ATVhomin20 increased from 28.9% to 78.1%. Concomitantly, viral abundance surged with age; the median abundance of ATVhomin29 increased over eight-fold from the youngest to the oldest age group. Notably, we identified significant sex-specific disparities across age groups, with male adults consistently exhibiting substantially higher prevalences. The most pronounced differences between males and females include GTVhomidi7 (54.2% vs. 25.0%) and BTVhomini1 (54.2% vs. 13.6%) in young adults, ATVhomin3 (80.0% vs. 64.8%) and ATVhomin18 (73.3% vs. 50.0%) in mid-life adults, and ATVhomin1 (80.6% vs. 51.5%), ATVhomin9.10 (77.4% vs 53.0%), and ATVhomin3 (90.3% vs. 68.2%) in old adults. The major abundant anellovirus species had a low prevalence, and those with the highest values were exclusively found in male children (BTVhomin15, BTVhomini604, ATVhomin6, and ATVhomin42). However, this pattern was not universal, as a female-biased abundance was observed for GTVhomidi31 and GTVhomidi65 in young adults, ATVhomin7 in mid-life adults, and BTVhomini609 and ATVhomin35 in the Elderly.

Demographic patterns of stable, high-prevalent human anelloviruses

When we examined the twenty most common anellovirus species, a clear pattern emerged in how consistently these viruses appeared across the population (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Figure S3). Twelve species formed a stable, age-uniform core (Fig. 3A). These included well-represented members such as ATVhomin29, ATVhomin21.24, BTVhomini36, and GTVhomidi7. All twelve were found in every age group and showed high stability scores (0.67–0.93), indicating a steady, long-term presence with little fluctuation in prevalence. In contrast, four Alphatorquevirus species, showed a more age-restricted pattern. They appeared in two or three out of the four age groups and had more modest stability scores (0.51–0.61). Species in this group, such as ATVhomin1 and ATVhomin20, varied more in their prevalence across the lifespan and did not display the same consistent footprint as the core set. Looking more closely at the age-uniform core revealed that host sex significantly shapes their trajectories (Fig. 3B). For males, most species followed a similar course: both prevalence and abundance tended to rise steadily with age. In females, the patterns were less uniform. While many Alphatorquevirus species showed the same gradual accumulation with age seen in males, females carrying Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus species, and certain Alphatorquevirus species including ATVhomin15, ATVhomin3, and ATVhomin9.10, displayed a clear peak in mid-life (around 50–65 years), followed by a decline in older ages. Virome diversity reveals structured age-dependent trajectories

Virome diversity analysis showed clear patterns associated with age and sex (Fig. 4A–E). Most individuals carried a low number of viral families, typically between two and five (Supplementary Data 4). However, a subset of predominantly male individuals had markedly higher viral richness (5–9 families), a pattern seldom observed in females. Linear regression indicated no significant association between age and alpha-diversity metrics (Shannon: R = 0.054, p = 0.3; Richness: R = −0.059, p = 0.25) (Fig. 4A). Stratifying by age group and sex uncovered some disparities. Males showed increased viral Shannon diversity during young adulthood, and higher richness in childhood and old adults (Fig. 4A). The strongest sex differences were observed in young and mid-adulthood, where females had significantly higher richness. Focusing on anelloviruses, we observed non-linear age-related dynamics across the lifespan (Fig. 4B–E). At the genus level, potential breakpoints were identified between ages 38–54 years, but they lacked statistical support (Davies p > 0.05) (Fig. 4B). Among the dominant anellovirus genera, Betatorquevirus had the highest total species count (111 species), followed by Gammatorquevirus (59 species) and Alphatorquevirus (22 species). Notably, Alphatorquevirus species diversity showed significant inflection points at ages 54–58 years in females (GAM p < 0.001, Davis p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). In males, the inflection points for Shannon index of Betatorquevirus species and richness of Gammatorquevirus species were similar (Fig. 4D, E). Of note, the richness of Betatorquevirus species exhibited early inflection point at age 25 years in females, whereas it was at age 66 years in males, a pattern distinct from other profiles (Fig. 4D). The inflection points for Gammatorquevirus Shannon were consistent with anellovirus genus-level estimates (Fig. 4E).

Within-group dispersion analyses of anellovirus species communities revealed specific age and sex trajectories across the genera (Fig. 5F–H). Dispersion within Alphatorquevirus communities declined monotonically from childhood to old age, indicating a strong age-related consolidation (Fig. 5F). In contrast, Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus communities exhibited non-linear, U-shaped trajectories, with dispersion decreasing to early or mid-adulthood before increasing again in later life (Fig. 5G, H). Sex-associated differences in dispersion were more nuanced and context-dependent than the robust age effects (Fig. 5F–H). A trend toward greater female heterogeneity was observed, although the overall difference was not statistically significant after multiple-testing correction. Notably, the relationship between sex and dispersion reversed across the lifespan. In childhood, male cohort showed relatively higher dispersion for all genera, although this difference was not statistically significant (padj > 0.05). This pattern inverted during adulthood, with female cohorts showing greater dispersion from young adulthood onward, a trend that was less pronounced for Gammatorquevirus in mid-life adults.

Age is a major determinant of circulating cytokine and HERV-K levels

Analysis of demographic associations revealed that age was the primary determinant of HERV-K level and fourteen circulating cytokines, whereas sex demonstrated limited association across the cohort (Fig. 6A–D and Supplementary Data 5). Consistent with this, several cytokines increased with age, including Eotaxin (Spearman’s rho = 0.59, padj = 4.2 × 10⁻¹⁵), IP-10 (rho = 0.41, padj = 4.3 × 10⁻⁷), and G-CSF (rho = 0.28, padj = 0.002). Conversely, age was associated with lower level of IL-15 (rho = −0.49, padj = 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰), PDGF-bb (rho = −0.26, padj = 0.003), and IL-13 (rho = −0.25, padj = 0.004). A modest but significant negative correlation was also found between age and HERV-K concentration (rho = −0.15, padj = 0.025) (Fig. 6C, D). The associations involving MIP-1a, IP-10, IL-15, IL-13, G-CSF, and Eotaxin were the most consistent with significant differences across categorical age groups (Fig. 6B, E). In contrast, cohort-wide comparison of all analyte levels between sexes showed no significant difference. However, localized sex differences that were masked in the whole-cohort analysis were identified. Context-specific associations between anelloviruses and host factors

Across the entire cohort, anellovirus abundance was not significantly associated with cytokine or HERV-K level following multiple-testing correction (Figure. S4A, B and Supplementary Data 6). Of note, several marginally significant associations were detected. The most notable trends were observed for Eotaxin, with BTVhomini1 showing a modest positive correlation (padj = 0.050), and ATVhomin13 with a weak inverse correlation (padj = 0.060). A similar pattern was found for interferon-related signaling, where GTVhomidi7 displayed a negative correlation with IP-10 (padj = 0.051), and ATVhomin18 positively correlated with MIP-1β (padj = 0.075). The network analysis revealed a sparse architecture, with ATVhomin18 and ATVhomin1 showing the strongest associations with cytokines while ORF1 exhibited minimal connectivity (Figure. S4B).

Linear models adjusting for age and sex also revealed no significant association at the cohort level. Stratified analysis uncovered several taxa-cytokine associations (Figure. S4C and Supplementary Data 6, Supplementary Fig. S5A–C). Notably, in young adult females, ORF1 abundance correlated strongly and positively with IL-1β (rho = 0.68, padj = 0.02), representing the only association that remained robust. IL-10 exhibited a more diffuse pattern. In young adult females, ATVhomin29 correlated negatively with IL-10 (rho = −0.58, padj = 0.13), whereas in middle-aged males three anellovirus species displayed IL-10-linked effects of comparable magnitude. Specifically, GTVhomidi7 (rho = −0.47, padj = 0.13) and GTVhomidi8 (rho = −0.48, padj = 0.13) were negatively associated with IL-10, while ATVhomin1 showed a positive association (rho = 0.48, padj = 0.13). Chemokine associations demonstrated similar demographic structuring. Among middle-aged females, both Samektorquevirus (rho = 0.001, padj = 0.157) and ORF1 abundance (rho = 0.50, padj = 0.19) were positively correlated with Eotaxin. In middle-aged males, Eotaxin-related signals diverged by viral species: ATVhomin21.24 correlated positively (rho = 0.45, padj = 0.19), whereas ATVhomin15 correlated negatively (rho = −0.46, padj = 0.19). However, none of these correlations was statistically significant following adjustment for multiple correction.

Anellovirus diversity shows minimal association with cytokines and HERV-K

Cohort-level analysis of the relationships between anellovirus alpha diversity and host factors (cytokine or HERV-K level) revealed no significant correlations (all padj > 0.05) (Supplementary Data 7 and Supplementary Figs. Species-level diversity within the four main anellovirus genera was consistent with the genus-level analysis at the cohort-level. Notably, in middle-aged adults, Betatorquevirus species diversity showed a pronounced negative correlation with IL-9 in males and positively with IL-17 in females (padj < 0.05) (Fig. 6D, E). Additional nominal trends (0.05 < padj < 0.1) included positive associations of Betatorquevirus species diversity with IL-7, IL-2, and IL-1ra in mid-life men, as well as a similar trend for Gammatorquevirus with IL-1ra. In elderly males, Samektorquevirus diversity showed a marginal positive correlation with IL-7. No significant or suggestive associations were detected for HERV-K in any subgroup.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive, lifespan-spanning view of the human plasma virome, with a particular focus on the anellome and its relationship to host demographic variables and immune markers. Our data confirm that anelloviruses are a core component of the plasma virome, with near-universal prevalence and high abundance across all age groups5,6. This supports the established view that anelloviruses are ubiquitous and commensal members of the human virome and establish persistent, life-long infections without causing overt disease5,7,8,20.

A central finding is the age- and sex-dependent structuring of anellovirus communities. Genus-level analyses revealed a divergence between the dynamics of Alphatorquevirus and those of Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus. Alphatorquevirus showed a pattern consistent with a progressive age-related decline in immune control over the viral replication: its initial establishment, reflected by a peak prevalence, occurs significantly in late young adulthood (38.4 years) for females, whereas its abundance continued to increase with age in both sexes.

In contrast, Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus, particularly in females, displayed non-monotonic, biphasic patterns and reached their peak presence and abundance during mid to late adulthood, followed by a noticeable decline in the oldest age bins. This trajectory suggests that these genera occupy more constrained ecological niches that are highly sensitive to specific host physiological transitions. The fact that these peaks and subsequent declines are most pronounced in females strongly implicates sex-specific factors, such as the hormonal remodeling and long-term impacts of menopausal transition21.

This female-specific biphasic pattern aligns with the concept of mid-life immune remodeling, providing a compelling framework for why Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus dynamics are so distinct from the persistent accumulation of Alphatorquevirus. A recent study demonstrated that post-menopausal females exhibit shifts in innate immune populations, such as an increased frequency of inflammatory monocytes and altered phagocytosis, changes that can be modulated by hormone replacement therapy21,22. It is plausible that the decline in sex hormones during this period reshapes the immune environment, potentially altering the niches for certain anellovirus genera21,23. Moreover, midlife often coincides with heightened social and psychological burdens in women, including increased caregiving, work demands, and role-related stress that elevate allostatic load and can modulate immune function24,25. While the increased abundance of Alphatorquevirus with age reinforces the idea that its replication is a gauge of global immune function, our finding of declining Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus in late life suggests a more complex relationship20,26,27. This is supported by clinical evidence showing that expansions of Betatorquevirus and/or Gammatorquevirus, rather than Alphatorquevirus, correlate with severe clinical outcomes in liver disease patients28. These findings suggest that the taxonomic structure of the anellome may convey clinically relevant information beyond total viral load.

Previous studies have reported that Betatorquevirus and Gammartorquevirus dynamics in females are tightly linked to distinct physiological stages. For example, in lactating mothers, serum and milk samples were largely negative for anelloviruses between 2 and 6 months postpartum but became positive thereafter, with Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus dominating both compartments from six months onward29. Moreover, an analysis of maternal and cord blood viromes found that maternal health status correlates with anellovirus composition in both maternal and fetal circulation30. These studies support the notion that Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus are more sensitive than Alphatorquevirus to female‑specific physiological transitions from postpartum recovery through mid‑life hormonal changes to menopause, partially aligning with our finding related to biphasic, sex‑driven patterns.

To determine these genus-level patterns at finer resolution, we performed species-level analyses using the twenty most prevalent species. We found that twelve species formed an age-uniform core, including ATVhomin29, BTVhomini36, and GTVhomidi7, with high stability scores (0.67–0.93) across all age groups. Age-stratified species, such as ATVhomin1 and ATVhomin20, showed fluctuating prevalence, highlighting the relationships between host age and species persistence. Notably, while Alphatorquevirus species steadily accumulated with age in both sexes, Gammatorquevirus and some Betatorquevirus species in females peaked around mid-adulthood (~50–65 years) before declining. These findings are consistent with genus-level data and strengthen the evidence for distinct anellovirus ecological niches that are sensitive to both host age and sex-specific factors.

We further used segmented regression to define the temporal architecture of anellovirus community diversity across the lifespan. These analyses revealed staggered inflection points across genera from mid- to late-life adulthood. Notably, Alphatorquevirus species diversity peaked at ages 54–58 years in females with a significant inflection point at this age range. This is consistent with the female-specific biphasic pattern observed for Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus abundances. These diversity trajectories suggest distinct successional dynamics for each anellovirus genus, potentially reflecting their differential sensitivity to phased immunological and physiological changes31. Distance-to-centroid analysis revealed that within-host heterogeneity followed genus-specific age trajectories. Dispersion within Alphatorquevirus communities declined monotonically, indicating progressive stabilization, whereas Betatorquevirus and Gammatorquevirus exhibited U-shaped patterns, decreasing from infancy to adulthood before rising again in late life. This suggests that periods of immune maturation and later aging differentially affect the ecological variability of these genera, consistent with the broader framework of age-associated immune remodeling and with recent evidence that life-stage transitions shape host-associated microbial communities across systems32–34. The alignment of these results with established periods of immune system maturation and remodeling supports the hypothesis that anellovirus diversity captures critical transitions in host-virus equilibrium, offering a more nuanced temporal framework than abundance metrics alone1.

The pronounced male bias in anellovirus prevalence, abundance, and diversity, which intensifies with age, is another notable finding. This aligns with established sexual dimorphism in immunity, where males often exhibit weaker antiviral responses35. Our data extend this concept to the virome, suggesting that the male immune system may exert less stringent control over anellovirus replication, leading to a higher viral set-point and greater taxon accumulation over time. This is supported by previous studies showing that sex-dependent differences in innate and adaptive immune responses are mediated by both hormones and genetics, often manifesting after puberty and persisting across aging22,35. For instance, estrogen can enhance the inflammatory response and help block pathogens at the first line of defense, potentially contributing to the more dynamic regulation of the plasma anellome observed in females. Accordingly, the female anellome exhibited more dynamic, genus-specific regulation, characterized by mid-life peaks and late-life declines. This pattern suggests the existence of temporally constrained ecological niches shaped by immune remodeling, potentially linked to hormonal transitions such as menopause. Our dispersion analysis adds nuance to this model, revealing a reversal in sex differences. While male children showed nominally higher community heterogeneity, females exhibited greater dispersion from young adulthood onward. This shift may reflect differences in immune system maturation and later, more individualized immune regulation in women, aligning with findings that sex differences in immune response variability increase after puberty35.

Our findings uncovered a modest systemic decline HERV-K Env expression level with age. Notably, within age group comparisons revealed young adult males associated with elevated HERV-K Env concentrations. This contrasts with transcriptomic studies that supported age-associated increases in HERV-K RNA in specific biological contexts, including the aging brain and cellular senescence models4,12,15. This apparent discrepancy suggests a decoupling of HERV-K transcriptional reactivation from systemic virion production or persistence. Recent mechanistic studies provide a framework for understanding this dissociation: endogenous retrovirus reactivation drives cellular senescence through retrovirus-like particle production, while specific transcription factors like ATF3 activate senescence-associated ERVs that induce localized interferon responses through dsRNA signaling36. This compartmentalized inflammation may not reliably elevate systemic HERV-K protein level. The higher level of HERV-K in young males implies additional regulatory mechanisms, potentially involving sex hormone-mediated transcription, immune differences, or the high psychosocial stress documented in this demographic, which can induce transcriptional programs that compromise antiviral defense. Recent immunogenetic evidence further complicates this picture, demonstrating that HLA alleles with high binding affinity for HERVK are enriched in long-lived individuals, suggesting that immune-mediated control of HERV-K contributes to lifespan determination14. Thus, the higher HERV-K level in young males may reflect less effective immune containment, while the age-related decline could represent selective survival of individuals with superior HERV-K immune regulation. Taken together, this indicates that systemic HERV-K levels are not a simple proxy for transcriptional reactivation but are instead shaped by post-transcriptional and immunogenetic filtering. Consequently, these levels likely reflect the net outcome of age-related reactivation pressure countered by evolving immune control mechanisms that determine viral persistence over the lifespan.

The cytokine data reinforce age as the dominant axis of systemic immune change in our cohort. Chemokines and growth factors, notably Eotaxin (CCL11), IP-10 (CXCL10) and G-CSF increased with age, while homeostatic or trophic mediators such as IL-15, PDGF-bb and IL-13 declined. These shifts are consistent with established concepts of age-related immune changes37,38. The limited explanatory power of sex in the pooled analyses, together with the elevated HERV-K observed in young males, argues for localized, context-dependent effects of sex and hormones on retroviral expression and cytokine responsiveness rather than a simple sex bias across the adult lifespan. This interpretation is consistent with systems-level studies showing large interindividual immune variation constrained by age and non-heritable influences, and with the broader hallmarks-of-aging framework that positions chronic, compartmentalized inflammation and altered intercellular communication as central features of mid- and late-life change1,31.

Despite these clear demographic patterns, the general lack of strong cohort-wide anellovirus-cytokine or HERV-K correlations suggests an “immunosensor” model in which anellovirus burden mainly reports net immune competence (expanding with immune suppression or dysfunction) rather than acting as a primary systemic driver of inflammation. Previous studies advanced this view27,39,40. However, the stratified signals we detected, such as ORF1/IL-1β coupling in young females and the sex- and age-specific chemokine associations for particular anellovirus taxa, suggest that taxon-specific immunomodulation may be real, but context-restricted. Sex hormones, tissue-restricted inflammatory circuits (e.g., inflammasome priming), and host immunogenetics are plausible moderators of these links. Taken together, our results indicate that anellome taxonomic structure and HERV-K level convey information that is partly orthogonal to bulk cytokine readouts: community composition and specific taxa may reflect local niches and immunoregulatory states not captured by plasma cytokine panels.

Consistent with previous findings, our results showed a modest age‑related increase in CMV IgG titers driven mainly by a significant increase in older males, while females showed a divergent pattern at that old age bin16,17. We found cohort‑level associations between CMV level and overall anellovirus abundance and genus richness, and our matched‑pair analysis confirmed a positive correlation with anellovirus genus richness, while within‑genus richness and diversity effects were modest. In immunocompromised transplant recipients, CMV reactivation was found to precede anellovirus load increase by approximately 14 days, and higher anellovirus levels predict subsequent CMV DNAemia, suggesting bidirectional or sequential dynamics under immune perturbation18,19. Our findings extend these observations to a broader context using aging population and show that even in the absence of overt immunosuppression, CMV IgG titers positively associate with anellovirus levels. Unlike CMV, anelloviruses have not been involved in accelerating immune aging. Therefore, mechanistic studies are needed to test whether anellovirus dynamics simply mirror CMV-driven inflammation or instead modulate it in ways not yet recognized.

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited during routine hospital consultations without preselection for specific health conditions, and comprehensive clinical metadata were unavailable. While this approach reflects a broad population, it precludes controlling for underlying diseases or medications that may influence virome or immune profiles, and the cohort should be considered a general convenience group rather than a strictly healthy population. Our reliance on plasma metagenomics with Phi29 amplification likely biased detection toward small circular DNA viruses. The cross-sectional design captures population-level associations but cannot establish causality or define intra-individual trajectories over time. Accordingly, we cannot distinguish whether the observed age-related patterns reflect true immunosenescence, selective survival, or cohort effects from differing early-life environments across decades. Moreover, several potential confounders remain unmeasured. The associations between CMV and anelloviruses are relevant, but larger data are needed as only 49 out of 149 CMV samples had matched anellovirus data. Also, the current CMV-anellovirus analysis cannot establish temporal direction, causality or determine whether shared factors such as immunosenescence or inflammation drive both. Medication, including immunosuppressants, antibiotics, vaccination, and hospitalization histories that can influence virome dynamics, were also unavailable. Finally, our focus on systemic plasma measurements may overlook crucial tissue-level or cell-mediated immune interactions central to anellovirus control in vivo. The detected associations, particularly the context-specific links, remain associative and require functional validation.

In conclusion, we revealed that the human plasma anellome is a dynamically structured ecosystem and its composition is precisely shaped by the interacting forces of host age and biological sex, with Alphatorquevirus, Betatorquevirus, and Gammatorquevirus exhibiting distinct trajectories across the lifespan. These genus-specific patterns, including gradual increase versus biphasic rise and fall, reflect phased age-related changes and the establishment of different ecological niches within the host. This work suggests that anelloviruses may reflect aspects of host immune status, providing a temporal and taxonomic framework for future studies on host-virus dynamics throughout life.

Methods

Ethics approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou Fourth People’s Hospital (approval number: 2023-EC/TZFH-015) and the Regional Ethics Committee of Shanghai Children’s Hospital (approval number: 2025R081- E02). Written informed consent was obtained from participants (or their parents or legal guardians for children under the age of 16) before inclusion.

Study subjects

This study explored the plasma virome, HERV-K, and associated immune profiles across lifespan, including individuals 0 to 100 years old who attended hospitals for routine health consultation. Participants were recruited without preselection for disease or health status, as sufficient clinical and/or diagnostic data were not available. The recruitment took place at two hospital sites: adults (≥20 years old) were enrolled at Taizhou Fourth People’s Hospital (Jiansu Province, China), and participants below 20 years old (called here as children for convenience and consistency) were recruited at Shanghai Children’s Hospital (Shanghai, China). Blood samples from adults were collected between June and November 2023, and those for children between August and November 2024. The samples were centrifuged, and the plasma and serum fractions were obtained.

Plasma virome

During sample processing, we included a negative control (nuclease-free water) to each extraction batch to identify contaminants. A total of 405 plasma samples were centrifuged to remove cellular debris and the resulting supernatant incubated with an enzyme cocktail (DNase I, RNase A, and Benzonase) at 37 °C for 1.5 h to digest unprotected nucleic acids. The nucleic acids were extracted from 300 µl of the treated plasma using Qiagen MiniElute Viral Spin Kit (Qiagen) and amplified by multiple displacement amplification using Phi29 polymerase in the REPLI-g WTA Single Cell Kit (Qiagen). The amplified nucleic acids were purified with Monarch PCR & DNA Cleanup Kit (New England Biolabs) and quantified using the Qubit dsDNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies, CA, USA). The libraries were constructed with the NEBNext Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) and quality-checked with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The valid libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA) to generate 5 G of 2 × 150 nt paired-end reads per sample.

Raw sequencing reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.3) to trim adapters, and filter short- and low-complexity sequences. The resulting reads were aligned against the human reference genome (hg38) using Bowtie2 (v2.4.5) in sensitive-local mode to subtract host-derived sequences. The unmapped reads were de novo assembled into contigs using MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) with a minimum contig length of 300 nt. The contigs were assessed for quality and completeness with CheckV (v1.0.1), and those encoding at least one viral gene were retrieved with SeqKit (v2.4.0). Candidate viral contigs were searched against the NCBI NT database using BLASTN and the NR database using DIAMOND BLASTX. We then used a custom python script to merge and filter the annotation results as follows: BLASTN hits required ≥70% identity, an e-value of ≤1 × 10⁻¹⁰, and a minimum alignment length of 100 nt; DIAMOND BLASTX hits required ≥30% identity, an e-value of ≤1 × 10⁻⁵, and a minimum alignment of 50 amino acids. The contigs that met both criteria were manually reviewed for inclusion and then taxonomic lineages were assigned with Taxonkit (v0.14.2). To quantify abundance, we mapped the sequencing reads back to the final set of viral contigs using bbmap, and the alignment statistics were generated by samtools (v1.10). As a final quality measurement, viral reads or contigs detected in negative control samples were systematically removed. We focused the subsequent analysis on eukaryotic viruses to concentrate on biological relevance of blood-borne viral signatures.

To focus specifically on anelloviruses, we retrieved ORF1 coding sequences from the SCANellome anellovirus database, as described by Laubscher et al., which encompasses the complete set of anellovirus ORF1 sequences available in NCBI GenBank (https://github.com/Laubscher/Anelloviruses/releases/tag/Anellovirus_2025.2, released March 31, 2023)41. These sequences were used to construct a custom BLASTN database. Protein accession numbers of anellovirus contigs were batch-queried via NCBI Entrez Direct to identify putative ORF1 regions. Contigs ≥ 1000 nt in length with valid ORF1 were retained and aligned against the SCANellome database to assign genus- and species-level classifications. Based on these results, we generated the abundance table by mapping clean reads back to the classified anellovirus sequences.

Cytokines screening

We measured the plasma concentration of 27 cytokines in 172 samples using the Bio-Plex Pro™ Human Cytokine 27-plex Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories) following the manufacturer’s instructions. This panel comprised 14 interleukins (IL-1β, IL-1rα, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IL-15, and IL-17), 6 chemokines (Eotaxin, IP-10, MCP-1, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, and RANTES), 5 growth factors (FGF-basic, G-CSF, GM-CSF, PDGF-BB, and VEGF), IFN-γ and TNF-α. All the samples were measured in duplicate and the cytokine concentrations were determined on a Bio-Plex 200 system with five-parameter logistic standard curves generated from serial dilutions of known standards.

Human endogenous retrovirus K ELISA assay

We quantified the HERV-K envelope expression in 212 serum samples by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) using the Human Endogenous Retrovirus Envelope HERV-K (HERVKenV) ELISA kit (Aifang Biotechnology, China). Briefly, the serum samples were diluted based on the kit manufacture’s recommendations and added to a 96-well pre-coated plate with capture antibodies. Following incubation, a detection antibody was added into the resulting mix and the bound HERV-K Env protein was quantified with Infinite F50 microplate reader at an optical density of 450 nm. Samples were assayed in triplicate to ensure accuracy.

CMV IgG chemiluminescence immunoassay

CMV IgG antibodies in 149 plasma samples were detected using a chemiluminescence immunoassay (Cytomegalovirus IgG Antibody Detection Kit, Guangzhou Bohui Medical Laboratory Technology Co., China). Serum samples were diluted 40‑fold, and 45 μL of the diluted sample was incubated with 50 μL of CMV antigen‑coated magnetic beads for 10 min, followed by addition of 50 μL of acridinium ester‑labeled anti‑human IgG antibody and a 5‑min incubation. After magnetic separation and washing, substrate solution was added, and the relative light units (RLU) were measured. Sample concentrations were calculated from a standard curve using a four‑parameter logistic fit. Samples were assayed in duplicate to ensure accuracy.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.1 (R: The R Project for Statistical Computing, n.d.). We modeled age- and sex-dependent dynamics of the four most prevalent anellovirus genera. Relative abundance was calculated as the proportion of viral reads assigned to each taxon relative to the total viral reads in a given sample. For each genus, prevalence (presence/absence) was modeled using binomial generalized linear model (glm) with the lme4 package (v1.1-35.3), and the abundance (log10-transformed read counts) using a linear model (lm). Both models incorporated natural cubic splines for age (3 degrees of freedom; splines v4.4.0) with an interaction term for sex. Population-level predictions were generated over a continuous age grid to identify peak ages for prevalence and abundance.

To identify the most stable anellovirus species, we focused on the top 20 prevalent species (prevalence range: 26.6–75.6%). A composite stability score was calculated for each species based on: (i) the detection rate across age groups, (ii) the uniformity of its prevalence (1 − coefficient of variation), and (iii) median scaled abundance. Based on this score, the species were classified as age-uniform (present in all age groups, score > 0.5), age-stratified (present in 3/4 of age groups, score ≥ 0.5), and sporadic species (the remaining). The age-uniform species, which are the most stable, were selected, and their prevalence and abundance dynamics were characterized using generalized linear (glm) model and linear model (lm), respectively.

Alpha- (Shannon and richness) and beta- (Bray-Curtis dissimilarity) diversity metrics were calculated using the vegan package (v2.6-6.1). Age-related trends of alpha-diversity metrics were assessed with generalized additive models using mgcv package (v1.9-1) and significant breakpoints (ψ) identified via segmented regression with segmented package (v2.0-3) and bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals. We next used Davies’ test to assess the significance of slope change at the breakpoints. Beta diversity was analyzed using PERMANOVA to test for compositional differences by age group and sex. Ordination was performed using non-metric multidimensional scaling and/or principal coordinates analysis. To quantify the within-group community heterogeneity, we computed the distance to group centroid using the betadisper function (vegan package). When group sizes were too small for betadisper, we derived centroid distances via principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) and Euclidean distance. Differences in dispersion between sexes and across age groups were assessed with the Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal–Wallis tests, respectively.

We analysed separately the associations between host factors (cytokines, HERV-K expression) and demographic variables. Continuous age associations were tested using lm, while age group and sex effects were assessed with Kruskal-Wallis and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, respectively. We further assessed the relationships between anellovirus features and host factors using two complementary approaches: Spearman's rank correlation was used to identify associations between anellovirus abundance and host factors; lm was used to test the associations between anellovirus diversity (Shannon index, richness) and host factors, with diversity metrics as predictors and host factors as outcomes. The analyses were stratified by age, age groups, sex, and sex within age groups to identify context-dependent relationships. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) approach was used to adjust all the generated p values for multiple comparisons.

CMV IgG titers were used as continuous variables but also categorized into tertiles (Low, Medium, High) based on the full cohort distribution. Associations between CMV titer and age, as well as anellovirus diversity and abundance, were assessed using linear regression and Pearson correlation. For matched comparisons, individuals in the lowest and highest tertiles were paired 1:1 on gender and age (±5 years). Differences between matched pairs were assessed using t‑tests or Wilcoxon signed‑rank tests, as appropriate, effect sizes were calculated as Cohen’s d. All two‑sided tests were considered significant when p < 0.05.
