Development and psychometric evaluation of a new itch assessment questionnaire for use in multi-ethnic Asian population
Authors: Aminath Shiwaza Moosa, Ngiap Chuan Tan, Ruiting Chen, Ding Xuan Ng, Karen Jui Lin Choo, Yong Kwang Tay, Nan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe study aimed to develop and evaluate an itch assessment questionnaire (IAQ) to determine and monitor itch severity and its impact on quality of life (QOL) in a multi-ethnic Asian population. It addresses limitations of existing tools, which assess only narrow aspects of itch and require multiple instruments for a comprehensive evaluation of itch and its impact on patients' QOL. No single, validated multidimensional questionnaire currently reflects the cultural and ethnic diversity of Asian populations.MethodsAn exploratory, sequential, mixed-methods study was conducted in two phases in a primary care setting in Singapore. Phase one used literature review, preliminary qualitative study findings, expert discussions and qualitative interviews with 13 patients and 10 primary care physicians to develop a draft IAQ. In phase two, 250 participants (with and without itch) completed the draft IAQ, 5-D pruritus scale, and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) over three points, 4-10 days apart. Data was analysed to assess IAQ's validity and reliability.ResultsThe final IAQ comprised 10 questions across three parts: itch severity, impact on QOL, and itch management. The IAQ demonstrated a strong correlation with the 5-D pruritus scale (ρ = 0.67, p < 0.001) and DLQI (ρ = 0.77, p < 0.001). The overall intraclass correlation coefficient and Cronbach α were 0.93 (95% CI = 0.91 to 0.95) and 0.94 (95% CI = 0.93 – 0.95), respectively.ConclusionsIAQ demonstrated construct validity and excellent reliability, suggesting its suitability for assessing itch severity and impact on QOL in multi-ethnic Asian populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s11136-026-04342-5.

Introduction

Itch is defined by the International Forum for the Study of Itch as an unpleasant sensation that provokes a desire to scratch [1]. A recent international study in 2024 revealed a worldwide prevalence of itch of 39.8%, which tends to increase with age [2]. The condition significantly impacts patients’ quality of life (QOL), to a degree comparable to pain [3]. Given that itch is a global public health problem, effective assessment and management of this condition is crucial [2].

Targeted assessment and management of itch can improve patients’ QOL, including their functional, psychological, and social well-being, and concurrently reduce the burden on patients and healthcare systems [4]. Thus, comprehensive evaluation of itch requires a multidimensional approach, as recommended by the Internation Forum for the Study of Itch, encompassing localisation, frequency, duration, intensity, sensory qualities, scratch response, affective and cognitive dimensions, treatment response, and QOL [5].

Primary care providers (PCPs) are at the frontline to assess and manage itch. They serve as the first point of contact to manage most patients with itch. Itch is one of the most prevalent dermatological symptoms seen in primary care settings [6]. However, comprehensive itch assessment by PCPs within the constraints of a time-limited consultation can be challenging. The implementation of a standardised, self-administered, multidimensional itch assessment tool would aid in gathering thorough information about itch in an efficient manner and facilitate shared decision-making on its management during the consultation.

Existing tools for assessing itch severity and dermatology-related QOL, such as the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), Verbal Rating Scale (VRS), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), 5-Dimension Pruritus Scale (5-D) and Itch QOL (ItchyQoL), were largely developed in Western contexts [7–10]. In Asia, tools such as NRS, VRS, and VAS are commonly used but primarily to assess itch intensity [9], and validation studies of multidimensional tools remain limited and context-specific [11–13]. The DLQI, though widely translated, includes culturally sensitive items (e.g., sexual difficulties) that may not be appropriate in some Asian settings [14]. In Singapore, while the 5-D and DLQI are used in dermatology, they lack local validation and fail to capture the full spectrum of itch experiences.

An exploratory qualitative study by the authors revealed culturally specific language and coping strategies, such as hot water, cleaning solutions, coconut oil, and herbal remedies, and highlighted concerns more relevant to work, study, and socialisation than to sexual function [15]. These findings underscore the need for an Asian-centric, multidimensional tool that reflects local experiences and supports holistic care. Thus, this study aimed to develop a comprehensive itch assessment questionnaire (IAQ) suitable for the multi-ethnic Asian population. Such a tool will allow PCPs to standardise the assessment of itch severity and its impact on patients’ lives, and to guide and monitor management strategies effectively [16].

Materials and methods

The study employed an exploratory sequential mixed-methods approach described by Creswell and Clark (2017) for development and evaluation of an instrument [17]. The study was conducted in two phases at a large primary care institution. Phase one focused on development of the draft IAQ using data collected via qualitative interviews. In Phase two, psychometric evaluation of the developed questionnaire was conducted using questionnaire survey. Fig 1 illustrates the study phases, procedures and stages of the questionnaire development. The study is reported according to STROBE guidelines [18].

Phase 1: development of the draft IAQ

The draft IAQ was developed in three steps: (1) literature review; (2) preliminary qualitative study, and (3) expert discussions.

Literature review: A literature review was conducted to identify widely used scales and questionnaires for measuring multiple itch domains.

Preliminary qualitative study: Details of the preliminary qualitative study is reported in a published article [15]. In summary, 13 purposively selected patients with chronic itch were interviewed using a semi-structured topic guide to gather information on their illness perceptions. The themes that emerged from these interviews were grouped into itch dimensions and then mapped to the domains identified in the literature review.

Expert discussion: The multi-disciplinary research team consisting of three PCPs, two dermatologists and one expert in the development and validation of questionnaires. The team discussed the mapped findings and literature review and grouped the identified itch domains under 3 parts: Part 1 (itch severity), Part 2 (management), and Part 3 (impact on QOL). The draft questionnaire was reviewed by five patients with itch and three PCPs and two dermatologists. Based on their feedback, patient expectations (open ended) questions were added as Part 4, leading to four parts and nine questions in the preliminary draft IAQ.

Qualitative and cognitive debriefing interviews

PCPs were interviewed using semi-structured topic guide in focus group discussions (FGD) and in-depth interviews (IDI). They assessed the preliminary draft IAQ’s completeness, relevance, and comprehensibility, and identified missing, ambiguous, or inappropriate items for further refinement.

Patients with chronic itch were then purposively selected to pilot-test the questionnaire, followed by cognitive debriefing interviews. Think-aloud interviews were used to assess whether the draft IAQ was easy to understand, complete, relevant, and comprehensive. The sample size was determined based on data saturation [19].

Finalisation of the draft IAQ

Based on feedback, expert discussions, and further refinement, the preliminary IAQ was formatted into a final draft IAQ comprising three sections with ten questions: Part 1 (itch characteristics), Part 2 (its impact on patients’ QOL), and Part 3 (itch management). Part 3 responses were aligned to fit local cultural context. The final draft IAQ was hosted online on FormSG, a secure government-backed online platform in Singapore [20]. The online version of the draft IAQ was pilot-tested with patients and PCPs before Phase 2.

Phase 2: psychometric evaluation of the itch assessment questionnaire

The validity and reliability of the draft IAQ were assessed using a cross-sectional survey. Asian adults aged 21 years and older, with or without itch, who were able to read and write in English, were recruited in a case-encounter basis from the waiting area of a public primary care clinic in Singapore from June to December 2024.

Sample size determination

According to Burns & Grove, the sample size for questionnaire development should be 5–10 participants per number of items [21]. For 22 items, the minimum sample size required was 110. To conduct the test-retest reliability across three visits, and accounting for a 20% dropout at each subsequent visit, the sample size was increased to 250, ensuring a minimum of 110 participants would be retained for the final data analysis despite attrition.

Data collection

Consenting participants completed the final IAQ, 5-D Itch Scale, and DLQI across three time points: baseline (T0) on Day 0, time-point 1 (T1) between Days 4–10, and time-point 2 (T2) between Days 8–20. Participants were provided QR codes and links for easy access to the online questionnaires. The questionnaires were self-administered; T0 took place in the clinic, where study members were available to assist with any technical difficulties, while T1 and T2 were conducted remotely at the patients’ convenience within the specified time-points. SMS reminders were sent to participants to ensure timely completion. Sociodemographic data on age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status and medical information were collected at baseline. Participants received SGD 20 (~ USD 16) for their time and contributions after completing the baseline questionnaire. No incentives were provided for completing the subsequent questionnaire.

Research instruments

Itch Assessment Questionnaire: The following scoring system was used for data analysis (refer to Appendix 1 in Supplementary information).

Part 1 assessed itch severity: distribution using a body diagram (head to foot), frequency (ranging from “not at all” to “daily”), severity (“not at all” to “severe”), and symptom progression (“much better” to “getting worse”), each a single item. Scoring was based on the number of affected body areas (0–3 points; 0 = none, 1 = 1–3 regions, 2 = 4–6 regions, 3 = > 6 regions ), itch frequency (0–4 points), severity (0–3 points), and progression (0–3 points), with higher scores indicating greater severity.

Part 2 examined the impact of itch on sleep (“never” to “severe”, 1 item), mood (“never” to “severe”, 4 items), and daily activities (“not applicable” to “always”, 4 items). Scoring ranged from 0 to 3 for sleep and mood effects, and 0 to 4 for daily activities.

Part 3 addressed management strategies and self-care, using 9 items with “yes” or “no” responses and an open-ended “please specify” option for additional details. Scoring was cumulative, with 1 point for each “yes” response in self-care and treatment categories. This section was intended to capture patients’ treatment-seeking behaviour and self-management practices. Scoring for Part 3 was included to assess the instrument’s construct validity and test-retest reliability, rather than as a direct indicator of itch severity or QOL.

The 5-D Itch Scale: The scale evaluates five key dimensions of itch: Degree, Duration, Direction, Disability, and Distribution [22]. Each category is scored separately, with Degree assessing itch intensity (1–5), Duration measuring frequency (1–5), Direction tracking changes over time (1–5), Disability rating its impact on daily activities (1–5), and Distribution identifying affected body areas (1–5) [22]. The total score is the sum of these individual ratings. Higher scores indicate greater itch burden.

The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI): This 10-item questionnaire measures the impact of skin diseases on QOL. Each question is scored from 0 (no effect) to 3 (maximum impairment). The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating a greater adverse impact [23].

Data analysis

All qualitative interviews conducted in Phase 1 were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and reviewed for comments on content, format, and layout of IAQ. The information gathered was used for the face and content validity.

Psychometric evaluation of floor and ceiling effects and validity was conducted using data collected at T0 and T1. These evaluations were conducted at both time-points to reflect IAQ’s hybrid use: clinic-based (T0) and remote self-administration at home (T1). Results for T0 and T1 are presented in the main manuscript and the supplementary files, respectively. Reliability analyses were performed using data from T1 and T2, as both settings were comparable in context and administration, allowing for meaningful assessment of test–retest reliability. The analysis was performed using Python (Version 3.11.5) and R (Version 4.2.3). The characteristics of participants were reported in frequencies and percentages or mean and standard deviation for categorical and continuous variable, respectively. All statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Floor and Ceiling Effects: Floor and ceiling effects were examined across each section of the IAQ. The presence of floor and ceiling effects was considered significant if more than 15% of participants achieved the lowest or highest possible score, respectively.

Convergent Validity: The convergent validity of the IAQ was evaluated by analysing the correlations between the IAQ section scores and the 5D itch scale and DLQI. Pearson or Spearman’s correlation was used depending on the normality of the scores based on Shapiro-Wilk test. The correlation coefficient (ρ) was interpreted as follows: ρ < 0.1, 0.1–0.29, 0.3–0.49, and ≥ 0.5 indicated none, weak, moderate, and strong correlation, respectively [24].

Structural validity: Structural validity was examined using the item-total correlations and internal consistency. The latter was quantified using Cronbach’s α; higher α values indicated stronger interrelatedness among items, with values exceeding 0.80 considered indicative of good to excellent internal consistency [25]. Item-total correlation was corrected for overlap. A correlation of > 0.4 is satisfactory. This analysis was limited to the first two parts of the questionnaire. The third part, focusing on management and self-care, was excluded due to the heterogeneity of items across treatment types and practices, which do not represent a unified construct. Question 4 of part 1 reflecting health transition of itch was also not included due to its irrelevancy.

Known-group validity: Due to the subjective nature of itch and its impact, no gold standard measure exists. Based on the literature suggesting distinct symptom profiles and treatment patterns [26, 27], to assess known-group validity, IAQ scores (Part 1: severity; Part 2: impact) were compared across three groups: no itch (baseline), itch without oral medication (mild–moderate), and itch with oral medication (more severe). Mann–Whitney U tests were used to compare these groups based on baseline responses.

Reliability: Test-retest reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa (κ) and Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) and interpreted as follows: κ ≤ 0.0, 0.0-0.20, 0.21–0.40, 0.41–0.60, 0.61–0.80, and > 0.81 indicated poor, slight, fair, moderate, substantial, almost perfect, respectively; [28] ICC < 0.5, 0.5–0.75, 0.75–0.9, and > 0.9 indicated poor, moderate, good, and excellent reliability, respectively [29].

Results

Phase 1: questionnaire drafting

Literature review

Eight main itch assessment questionnaires which consisted of 8 itch dimensions were identified from literature: intensity, duration, frequency, location/distribution, impact on sleep, mood, daily activities and social activities (Supplementary Table S1).

Findings from preliminary qualitative research

The itch dimensions identified from the qualitative research were grouped into 4 main domains: Symptomatology (sensory, intensity, distribution/location, frequency, duration), self-management (counter itch measures: scratch, shower, body message; topical and oral medication), medical treatment (topical, oral and injection) and psychosocial (effect on mood, clothing, sleep, work, and social interactions). The quotes are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Preliminary draft IAQ

Preliminary draft IAQ consisted of 4 parts: Part 1 (itch severity): distribution, frequency, intensity, and direction; Part 2 (management of itch): self-care and medication given by western doctor; Part 3 (impact of itch): impact of itch on sleep, mood, and activities; and Part 4 (patient expectation): open-ended question to explore patients’ expectations and additional informational needs (Supplementary Fig S1).

Face validity from qualitative interviews

A total of 23 participants (13 patients with chronic itch and 10 PCPs) participated in 16 IDIs and 2 FGDs (3 PCPs in one group and 4 in the other). Patients varied in gender, age, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, itch severity, and aetiology (Supplementary Table S3), while PCPs had diverse work experience and qualifications (Supplementary Table S4).

All participants agreed that the instructions were clear, and questions were relevant. Participants identified unclear terms, including “scalp,” “western doctor,” “over-the-counter medications,” and “meeting friends in person.” They noted that “scratch” overlapped with itch severity and impact. To improve completeness and comprehensiveness, they suggested including more examples in Section 3 and “please specify” where multiple examples were provided. Concerns were raised about “depressed” being mistaken for clinical depression. Based on PCP’s recommendations, impact on QOL was moved before management, adjusting the sequence to: Severity of itch (Part 1), Impact of itch (Part 2), and Management of itch (Part 3). Phase 2: psychometric evaluation

A total of 250 participants completed the baseline questionnaire. The flow of patients is illustrated in Fig. 3. The mean time to complete the IAQ component of the baseline questionnaire was 4.5 min (range: 3.5 to 5 min). No item was skipped. Forty-one (16.4%) participants dropped out after the first assessment, and 3 were excluded due to erroneous data entry. Eleven (4.4%) participants did not complete the third assessment, leaving 195 (78.0%) for the reliability analysis. More than a third of the participants experienced itch for more than a year (39.3%) and had no itch disturbance (36.4%).

Floor and ceiling effects

Figure 4 illustrates the floor and ceiling effect for itch severity and the impact of itch using T0 data. No ceiling effects were observed in part 1 of the IAQ, as none of the participants achieved the highest score. However, floor effect was present as 33.2% (n = 83) of participants had the lowest score. Similarly, for part 2 of the IAQ, floor effect was observed, with 25.2% (n = 63) of participants scoring the lowest possible score. The ceiling effect was not found for part 2 of the IAQ. Floor effect was also observed in part 3 of the IAQ, with no ceiling effect. A total of 88 participants (35.2%) had the lowest possible score, with none having the highest possible score. The floor and ceiling effect for T1 is reported in the supplementary file (Fig. S2).

Structural validity

The structural validity of IAQ for T0 is detailed in Table 3. Overall Cronbach α was 0.92 (95% CI = 0.91–0.94). For the individual sections, the Cronbach α was 0.82 (95% CI = 0.77–0.85) and 0.92 (0.90–0.93) for part 1 and part 2, respectively, demonstrating good to excellent internal consistency. Item-total correlations were satisfactory, ranging from 0.57 to 0.82. Question 1 on itch frequency (part 1) had the lowest item-total correlation of 0.57. The overall Cronbach α remained similar when any questions on itch severity or impact were removed (Table 3). Structural validity for T1 is reported in the Supplementary Table S5.

Known-group validity

Almost one in five participants (n = 43, 17.2%) were on oral medications. The known-group validity performed using T0 is illustrated in Fig. 6. The severity of itch was higher among participants on oral medication than among those not taking oral medication (median: 4 vs. 3), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.99). Additionally, a difference was observed in the impact on mood and daily activities between participants on oral medication and those without (median 8 vs. 5), but it did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.42). The known-group validity performed using T1 is reported in the supplementary file (Fig S4).

Discussion

The new IAQ was validated as a single, comprehensive, multidimensional itch assessment questionnaire, contextualised for a multi-ethnic Asian population. Using local patients’ and physicians’ input to inform questionnaire design ensured content validity. Psychometric evaluation demonstrated high structural and construct validity, and excellent reproducibility.

The IAQ was designed to be a concise and user-friendly tool that patients can complete quickly and independently, requiring minimal assistance from healthcare providers. The development process, including qualitative interviews with PCPs and patients, helped in refining the questionnaire to be comprehensive and relevant [30]. The feedback gathered during the interviews helped to maintain clarity and simplicity in the questionnaire’s wording and format. This process identified and addressed any ambiguous terms that could lead to confusion, ensuring the questionnaire is easy to understand for all users [31]. The interviews and feedback sessions enabled the adaptation of the questionnaire to the specific cultural context of a multi-ethnic Asian population, ensuring that the language, phrasing, and content of the IAQ were relevant and acceptable to the target population [32].

The IAQ’s multidimensional approach, which includes questions on itch localisation, duration, frequency, intensity, course, and its impact on sleep, mood, and daily activities, offers a comprehensive assessment. Existing instruments such as the NRS, VRS and DLQI focus on only one or two itch dimensions, necessitating the use of multiple instruments for a complete evaluation. While the 5‑D itch scale captures several important dimensions of pruritus, including degree, duration, direction, disability and distribution, it lacks assessment of self-management or coping practices [16]. Thus, these tools are primarily designed as outcome measures and may not capture all aspects of itch relevant to routine primary care decision‑making within a single instrument. In practice, multiple tools may be required to assess itch severity, quality‑of‑life impact, and patient behaviours.

Other multidimensional instruments, such as the German Pruritus Questionnaire, also provide structured assessment across parameters, including localisation, course, intensity or quality, QOL, and coping methods, highlighting that multidomain evaluation is increasingly emphasised in chronic itch assessment [33]. These tools are valuable for detailed and systematic assessment in research and specialised clinical settings. However, these tools may not be culturally sensitive, particularly to a more conservative Asian population. In comparison, IAQ offers a concise, integrated assessment within a single instrument contextualised to a multi‑ethnic Asian population. Its brevity further supports clinical utility, with a mean completion time of less than 5 min, making it feasible for routine use in time‑limited consultations. As such, the IAQ is intended as a complementary, clinically oriented tool that facilitates efficient assessment and decision‑making in primary care, rather than replacing more detailed multidimensional questionnaires.

The clinical utility of IAQ is further enhanced by its Part 3, which explores a patient’s itch management and self-care practices, domains that are not routinely captured in existing itch assessment questionnaires. This domain provides physicians with insights into the use of treatments from both Western and traditional medicine, as well as self-care practices, which are particularly relevant in regions with diverse cultural practices. It allows the attending physician to identify potentially harmful practices and offer tailored, evidence-based advice. By fostering a more collaborative approach, the IAQ can lead to greater patient satisfaction and improved treatment adherence, providing a holistic understanding of the patient’s experience without the need for multiple separate measures [16].

An online platform was used to self-administer the IAQ during the cross-sectional validation. Nearly 80% of recruited patients completed the T1 and T2 questionnaires via the online platform, without receiving any incentives, highlighting the feasibility and patient acceptance of this method of digital data collection within the primary care setting. An online approach yields several important advantages. Primarily, it enhances accessibility by mitigating geographical and logistical constraints, thereby facilitating the inclusion of patients who may encounter difficulties with traditional clinic visits [34]. Furthermore, the online format streamlines data collection, potentially improving efficiency for both patients and clinicians.

IAQ can be implemented as a pre-consultation tool for clinicians to gather pertinent itch-related information before the patient encounter and to enhance the subsequent in-person consultation, making it more focused, efficient, and effective [35]. Additionally, a digitalised IAQ is a potential telehealth tool, offering a means to effectively monitor and manage patients with itch remotely [36]. Considering these merits, future research should further investigate the integration of IAQ with tele-dermatology, combining teleconsultation and telemonitoring to support decentralised management of itch-related conditions [36].

Strengths and limitations

To the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to develop and validate a multidimensional questionnaire contextualised for a socioculturally diverse Asian population. A key strength of the IAQ is its versatility, having been validated for use in both clinic settings and remotely at the patient’s home; this dual functionality offers greater flexibility in data collection, patient monitoring and management.

There are limitations to this study. Firstly, the sample was taken from a single primary care clinic, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or populations. However, the study population size is computed to achieve adequate power. Secondly, the IAQ was in English, limiting its use among those who speak other languages in Asia. Nevertheless, English is the dominant language in Singapore, with almost half of the homes using the language at home [37]. Future research would validate the IAQ in other healthcare settings and languages to enhance its applicability to a wider population. Thirdly, recruiting participants in the waiting area made it impractical to calculate a response rate. While this approach limits our ability to assess for potential recruitment bias, it was a pragmatic choice to minimise disruption to clinic workflow. The demographics of our recruited participants, including age, gender, and ethnicity, were comparable to those of the general population, helping mitigate concerns about representativeness. Furthermore, the high follow-up completion rate (nearly 80%) without incentives is a strong indicator of the tool’s acceptability and feasibility in a real-world setting.

Conclusion

IAQ demonstrated strong psychometric properties and is a reliable and valid tool for assessing itch severity and its impact on quality of life in a multi-ethnic Asian population. Its brevity, ease of use, and multidimensional design support its role as a practical, contextually relevant tool for integration into busy primary care settings. Further research is warranted to explore its clinical utility in monitoring disease progression, treatment outcomes and guiding personalised itch management strategies.
