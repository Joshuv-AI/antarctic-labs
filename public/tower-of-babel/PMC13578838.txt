Balancing harm prevention and liberty preservation when implementing financial risk assessments for gambling in the United Kingdom: Insights from open banking data
Authors: Robert M Heirene, Philip Newall
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe United Kingdom (UK) and Dutch governments have recently implemented mandatory financial risk (affordability) assessments for online gambling as a harm prevention measure. Assessments should trigger at a level of gambling expenditure that strikes a balance between harm prevention (most at‐risk consumers should surpass the threshold) and liberty preservation (most no‐/lower‐risk consumers should gamble below it), yet little empirical research exists to guide threshold setting. We aimed to demonstrate how research can inform the harm‐prevention, liberty‐preservation trade‐off in this context and evaluate the UK's proposed implementation of financial risk assessments.Design, setting and participantsWe reanalysed a dataset that combines self‐reported Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI) scores and open banking data from consumers who gamble (n = 424) to (1) simulate the impact of the UK's rolling 30‐day £150 net‐deposit (deposits minus withdrawals) threshold for financial risk assessments, and (2) identify optimal threshold values for these assessments under different circumstances. Participants were UK residents who had gambled in the past year, recruited via Prolific in April 2024.MeasurementsParticipants completed a survey containing the PGSI and agreed to provide their past 12 months' banking records.FindingsOver 12 months, two‐thirds of at‐risk (PGSI ≥1) and nearly half of no‐/lower‐risk participants crossed the UK's £150 threshold [area under the curve = 0.66, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) = 0.62–0.71], demonstrating a greater emphasis on harm prevention over liberty preservation. Increasing the value to £186.9 (95% CIs = £69.5–£401.7) slightly improved this balance, although £150 remained within the range of appropriate values. Optimising for harm prevention in our sample required lowering the threshold to £39.0 (95% CIs = £29.6–£58.8), while emphasising liberty preservation increased it to £716.5 (95% CIs = £508.5–£990.9). We found that using a more conservative definition of risk (≥2 PGSI harms) resulted in higher thresholds, and lower thresholds may be appropriate for younger adults (<30 years). Finally, our findings suggest that thresholds based on spending with all operators—rather than single operators as implemented in the UK—may be better able to differentiate at‐risk from no‐/lower‐risk consumers, although the added benefit of this approach in our sample was marginal and further research is needed to confirm its value.ConclusionsThe United Kingdom's £150 net‐deposit threshold for financial risk assessments for online gambling may place more emphasis on harm prevention than liberty preservation. This study provides a methodological template for guiding the implementation of financial risk assessments for online gambling. Because our sample is not representative of the broader UK gambling population, our specific threshold estimates should be treated as provisional.Keywords: affordability checks, gambling disorder, gambling policy, harm minimisation, problem gambling, regulation

INTRODUCTION

Gambling‐related harm is a significant public health concern affecting millions of individuals [1] and their close family members and friends [2, 3]. Governments are increasingly recognising the need for a range of preventive measures to combat gambling harms that complement treatment services for the most affected [4, 5, 6]. Although a majority of people who gamble spend very little, in keeping with other leisure activities, spending is markedly higher for a significant minority [7], among whom gambling‐related harms are elevated [8, 9]. Affordability or financial risk checks are, therefore, one preventive measure being implemented in the United Kingdom (UK) and the Netherlands. These checks (hereafter termed ‘financial risk assessments’ for consistency with UK policy) require gambling operators to assess a consumer's financial situation before they can spend above a certain threshold.

In the Netherlands, these checks resemble a means‐tested assessment of affordability [10], whereas the UK regulator, the Gambling Commission, stresses that their checks are not designed to assess affordability, but rather to identify consumers who are showing signs of significant financial difficulty (e.g. multiple arrears or bankruptcy) [11]. The Dutch government has set a monthly deposit threshold of €300 for consumers 18 to 24 years old and €700 for those 25 years or older. A reduced threshold for younger adults is consistent with evidence of elevated gambling harms in this population [12, 13], and other policies that recognise the increased risk in this group, such as the UK's £2 stake limit for those 18 to 24 years playing online slots (versus £5 for those 25 years or over) [14]. The United Kingdom's financial risk assessments are set to trigger when an individual's net‐deposit (i.e. deposits minus withdrawals) exceeds £150 in 30 days, a threshold determined using open banking data [15].

When setting these threshold values that trigger financial risk assessments, policymakers are tasked with balancing the competing goals of preventing harm among the at‐risk population and preserving the liberties of the no‐/lower‐risk population. Although a low threshold, such as £10, could be optimal for the goal of harm prevention, this would fail policymakers' other goal of preserving the liberties of those who are gambling safely [16]. Although these two policy goals are not equal—we may wish to err on the side of caution by valuing harm‐prevention above liberty‐preservation—formal analysis is needed to reveal exactly what the trade‐off between them is at different threshold values.

Risk‐based threshold analyses are one way to understand the trade‐offs involved in the different values that could trigger a financial risk assessment. Thresholds that lead to a large proportion of at‐risk consumers being assessed have high sensitivity and thereby emphasise harm prevention. Thresholds that ensure few no‐/lower‐risk consumers are assessed have high specificity and thereby emphasise liberty preservation. Risk‐based threshold analysis can be used to identify a value that best balances these goals and inform other choices required for policy implementation: which spend‐related variable (e.g. deposit amount, net‐deposit) optimises the harm‐prevention versus liberty‐preservation trade‐off? Should thresholds be lower for younger adults? And should thresholds be applied over a person's spending across all operators or be implemented on a per‐operator basis?

Research that has attempted to quantify the harm‐prevention versus liberty‐preservation trade‐off in other areas of gambling policy suffers from key limitations. Self‐reported gambling expenditure is commonly used for convenience [17, 18], despite its inaccuracy [19, 20, 21, 22]. One study has used objective data from a European gambling operator to develop thresholds for lower‐risk gambling [23], but this ignores all other electronic gambling expenditure. At‐risk consumers have been found to gamble with an average of six different operators [24], restricting the value of single‐operator‐based analysis. Others have used an innovative approach by examining open banking transactional data 1 and using this to estimate the proportion of individuals surpassing threshold values [25]. Although novel and informative, these analyses cannot reveal the harm‐prevention/liberty‐preservation balance achieved or attempt to optimise it in the absence of a reliable indicator of participant risk.

Here, we reanalyse a unique dataset to show how the quantification of policy trade‐offs can inform the implementation of financial risk assessments. The dataset contains a commonly used self‐report measure of harm, the Problem Gambling Severity Index (PGSI), fused with objective cross‐operator gambling expenditure obtained via open banking data collected by Zendle and Newall [9]. In their original article, the authors briefly commented on the UK's financial risk assessments, stating, ‘The UK government's proposed affordability checks for gamblers should rarely affect people who are not experiencing gambling‐related harm’. However, their analyses did not intend to fully simulate the potential impact of the UK policy [26, 27], and they did not perform any threshold‐based analyses. Access to this dataset permitted risk‐based threshold analyses using objective expenditure across all gambling operators. This approach is, to our knowledge, new to the gambling field and overcomes issues with both self‐reported and single‐operator expenditure data. Our objectives were to:

Assess the United Kingdom's proposed £150 net‐deposit assessment threshold's balance between harm‐prevention in at‐risk participants and liberty‐preservation among no‐/lower‐risk participants;

Assess the United Kingdom's proposed £150 net‐deposit assessment threshold's balance between harm‐prevention in at‐risk participants and liberty‐preservation among no‐/lower‐risk participants;

Determine whether net‐deposit values (as used in the United Kingdom) or simple deposit amounts (that do not account for withdrawals) would better differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants;

Determine whether net‐deposit values (as used in the United Kingdom) or simple deposit amounts (that do not account for withdrawals) would better differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants;

Determine whether spending across all operators would better differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants than single‐operator spending (as currently planned in the United Kingdom);

Determine whether spending across all operators would better differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants than single‐operator spending (as currently planned in the United Kingdom);

Identify the optimal assessment threshold value(s) for these assessments that differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants in our sample; and

Identify the optimal assessment threshold value(s) for these assessments that differentiate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants in our sample; and

Determine whether lower thresholds should be adjusted for young adults (18–29 years old).

METHODS

This study was not pre‐registered and all outcomes should be considered exploratory. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines in reporting this study [28] and a STROBE Checklist can be found in Data S1.

Participants and setting

The cross‐sectional survey data and matched open banking datasets used here were originally collected by Zendle and Newall [9]. Participants were recruited via the crowdsourcing platform Prolific in April 2023. To be eligible, participants were required to be UK residents who had reported engagement with online gambling to Prolific and had a bank account. Each participant was paid £10 to complete the survey, which included self‐report measures and the process of accessing open banking data via an application programming interface (API). All participants read a study information sheet and consented to the survey and sharing their banking data before starting. Ethical approval to carry out the study was obtained from the University of York (Ref: Zendle20230220).

Measures

The survey included the PGSI [29], a screening measure designed to assess gambling‐related harms. The PGSI contains nine items scored on a four‐point scale from 0 (never) to 3 (always), with scores ranging from 0 to 27 (e.g. ‘Have you gone back another day to try to win back the money you lost?’). Internal reliability of the scale was acceptable in this sample (Cronbach's α = 0.74).

Open banking data contained all gambling and non‐gambling transactions for 12 months leading up to the survey from accounts each person had connected to open banking. All incoming and outgoing transactions were coded as ‘gambling’ or ‘non‐gambling’ to allow aggregations of gambling spend within and across operators.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were undertaken using the statistical programming language R, version 4.1 [30]. Our analysis script is shared on Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/k7z9w/) and is available as a navigable webpage here: https://rheirene-financial-vulnerability--affordability--checks-thresho.share.connect.posit.cloud/. We only retained participants in analyses that completed the full survey and provided access to their banking data and so missing data was not problematic.

Using the open banking data, we first created rolling 30‐day windows across the 12 months of data (1 April 2022 to 31 March 2023) and summed each participant's deposit and withdrawal transactions within every window. We used these rolling aggregations to compute deposit and net‐deposit values (i.e. deposit–withdrawal) for every individual operator for each 30‐day period and further aggregated values representing total deposit and net‐deposit across all operators in each window. This allowed us to identify all instances where a participant surpassed the £150 net‐deposit threshold set for the UK's financial risk assessments and to compute classification performance metrics reflecting the threshold's ability to differentiate between risk groups (e.g. sensitivity, specificity) using the caret R package [31].

Participants' risk status was defined in two separate ways. First, at‐risk status was defined by having a PGSI score of 1 or more, with no‐/lower‐risk participants having a score of 0 [29]. Second, to provide a more conservative definition of risk, at‐risk status was defined by reporting having experienced at least two of the seven harm items in the PGSI (i.e. feeling guilty, losing more than one can afford, recognition of the problem, health problems, financial problems, criticism from others and borrowing money) at least ‘sometimes’. This approach is consistent with that used to develop low‐risk gambling thresholds and, therefore, provides comparisons with these guidelines [17, 18]. Only 13.4% (n = 57) of our sample scored more than seven on the PGSI. We, therefore, decided that using the standard ‘high‐risk’ PGSI category to define at‐risk status would have led to unstable estimates and reduced reliability when identifying optimal thresholds to differentiate risk groups.

Next, we computed summary variables representing: (1) the maximum net‐deposit and deposit (no withdrawals) values with any single operator in any of the rolling 30‐day windows, and to better capture all gambling‐related expenditure; and (2) the maximum 30‐day net‐deposit and deposit across all gambling operators in any of the 30‐day windows. 2 For these four variables, we computed area under the receiver operating characteristic curve values (hereafter referred to as ‘AUC’ values). AUC values in this context reflect the expenditure variables' overall ability to discriminate between at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk groups in the sample, irrespective of any specific threshold. AUC values can be interpreted as failed (0.50–0.59), fair/acceptable (0.60–0.79), good (0.80–0.89) or excellent (≥0.9) [32, 33].

The cutpointr package [34] was used to identify threshold values that best differentiated at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants on each of the four gambling expenditure variables. Bootstrapping with replacement and 2000 samples was used to select thresholds that were robust to variations in sampling procedures and simultaneously compute 95% CIs for AUC values. We used three distinct methods to identify optimal thresholds to demonstrate the various approaches available in these types of binary classification scenarios and show how threshold values vary using them. In the first approach, we sought to find the threshold value that maximised sensitivity i.e.true positivestrue positives+false negatives while ensuring a minimum specificity i.e.true negativestrue negatives+false positives of 0.3. By attempting to maximise sensitivity (i.e. the proportion of at‐risk individuals who surpass the threshold), this first approach prioritises the goal of harm prevention over liberty preservation. In the second approach, we attempted to find the threshold that maximised specificity while ensuring a minimum sensitivity of 0.3—this approach prioritises liberty preservation by attempting to maximise specificity (i.e. the proportion of no‐lower‐risk individuals who do not surpass the threshold). Last, we aimed to find the threshold that produced the maximum Youden index value (i.e. sensitivity+specificity−1)—this approach tries to find a balance between harm prevention and liberty preservation. Our bootstrapping procedure also allowed us to compute 95% CIs for the threshold values identified, representing the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the 2000 cut points identified in resamples. We note that Youden‐optimised values are highly sensitive to minor changes in sampling and may span a wide range of possible values that yield similar Youden values. CIs in this context reflect the variability in threshold selection under resampling, rather than precision around a single estimate. In addition to computing sensitivity and specificity rates for each threshold identified, we also calculated balanced accuracy values to indicate each threshold's overall ability to correctly classify at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk groups. Balanced accuracy is calculated as the average of sensitivity and specificity.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Of the 15 733 Prolific members appearing to meet eligibility criteria, 2108 were recruited to the study and 424 completed the survey (20.1% of those recruited), provided their banking data and met all eligibility criteria (see more details of the recruitment process in Zendle and Newall [9]). Among the final sample of 424 people included in our analyses, 195 (46.0%) could be defined as at‐risk based on a PGSI score of 1 or more and 118 (27.8%) could be defined as at‐risk based on endorsing 2 or more harm items on the PGSI. The demographic and gambling characteristics of participants are summarised in Table 1. On average, at‐risk participants were younger, gambled with a greater number of operators, deposited more money and had a larger net‐deposit than no‐/lower‐risk participants, with these between‐group differences being more pronounced when at‐risk status was defined by 2 or more PGSI harms.

How well does the £150 net‐deposit threshold balance harm prevention and liberty preservation?

Table 2 presents the number of participants who surpassed the £150 net‐deposit threshold with any single operator in any 30‐day window in the 12 months, alongside classification performance metrics for this threshold. When at‐risk status was defined by PGSI scores 1 or more, the £150 threshold achieved a moderate sensitivity rate, with 66.2% of at‐risk participants surpassing it at some point during the 12 months, but a lower specificity rate of 52.4% (i.e. slightly more than half of no‐/lower‐risk participants did not surpass the threshold). When at‐risk status was defined by reporting 2 or more harms on the PGSI, a higher sensitivity rate of 75.4% was achieved with a similar specificity rate of 51.3%. Therefore, with either definition of at‐risk gambling, the £150 net‐deposit threshold placed more emphasis on harm prevention than liberty preservation in this sample.

Should assessments be based on net‐deposit or deposit amounts?

AUC values and their 95% CIs are presented in Table 3 for net‐deposit and deposit expenditure variables for both single and cross‐operator spend. Every AUC value for the higher risk classification (2+ harms) was higher than the corresponding value for the lower classification (PGSI 1+), suggesting risk groups could be better differentiated by their expenditure using this classification approach. There was little difference in AUC values for net‐deposit versus deposit amounts, suggesting neither was better at differentiating risk groups than the other.

Should assessments be based on single operator or cross‐operator expenditure?

The AUC values in Table 3 also provide insight into the discriminative value of cross‐operator versus single‐operator expenditure. All four AUC values for cross‐operator expenditure [mean (M) = 0.703] were higher than the corresponding values for single‐operator expenditure (M = 0.689). Therefore, at‐risk and no‐/lower‐risk participants in our sample could be more easily distinguished by their cross‐operator expenditure than by their single‐operator expenditure.

Which thresholds best balance harm prevention and liberty preservation in this sample?

We next identified the specific expenditure thresholds that best separated at‐risk from no‐/lower‐risk participants in our sample, based on the maximum deposit and net deposit amounts they had made in any rolling 30‐day window in the 12 months of interest. To reflect different policy priorities, we calculated thresholds optimised for sensitivity (harm prevention), specificity (liberty preservation) and the Youden index (a balance of both). This produced 24 thresholds in total, derived from the combination of two expenditure variables (deposit versus net‐deposit), two levels of aggregation (single‐operator versus cross‐operator) and two definitions of risk (PGSI ≥1 versus ≥2 PGSI harms), each evaluated under the three optimisation criteria (Table 4).

Should thresholds be adjusted for younger adults?

Finally, we repeated the analyses above, this time splitting the sample into those under 30 years (n = 132) and those 30 years or above (n = 292), producing the same 24 threshold values for each subsample (Table 5). Our decision to define ‘young adults’ as those under 30 years old was based largely on practical reasons. Only 50 people in our sample were 18 to 24 years, which is the age range used to define younger adults by the Dutch operator when considering lower thresholds for this group. Widening the range up to 29 provided a more balanced sample that allowed us to produce more reliable outcomes for subgroups. Although this was not our preferred range, it is more consistent with recent neurodevelopmental research that suggests the ‘adolescent’ brain phase may span into the early 30s [35].

DISCUSSION

Gambling policymakers are increasingly seeing the value of population‐level prevention measures [6, 36], such as mandatory limits on time and money spent gambling [4, 37], as well as financial risk assessments. Financial risk assessments' ability to prevent harm while preserving liberty depends, among other things, on the values chosen to trigger these assessments. In our sample, the UK's £150 net‐deposit threshold placed more weight on harm prevention than liberty preservation, across both measures of at‐risk status. When using the most liberal definition of risk (PGSI ≥1), approximately two‐thirds of at‐risk and half of no‐/lower‐risk individuals surpassed the £150 threshold. If defining risk by the same approach used in studies developing lower‐risk gambling guidelines (≥2 harms on the PGSI; e.g. Heirene et al. [38]), approximately three‐quarters of at‐risk and half of no‐/lower‐risk individuals surpassed the threshold.

Determining whether to trade‐off liberty preservation at the expense of harm preservation in this context is a complex decision influenced by several public health and commercial factors. Although identifying the most at‐risk individuals is an admirable goal, a lower threshold does increase the administrative costs for operators implementing these assessments and may increase friction for consumers gambling at safe levels. This could lead to increased migration to illegal or unlicensed platforms that do not use these assessments. As recently discussed by Heirene [39], this concern should not be ignored, but also should not supersede public well‐being—efforts to discourage and prevent black‐market migration should accompany the introduction of policies that can introduce friction on legal platforms.

We identified optimal thresholds under several different potential scenarios, including when based on net‐deposits versus deposits, when applied to a single versus all operators, for younger (<30) and older (30+) cohorts and when placing different weights on the goals of harm prevention and liberty preservation. We found little difference in AUC values when using net‐deposit versus deposit amounts, suggesting neither was better able to differentiate risk groups. Our findings do support the Dutch approach of using lower thresholds for younger adults. Optimal thresholds identified for our sample were typically lower for younger individuals, especially when trying to emphasise harm prevention (i.e. greater detection of at‐risk individuals). Both the Dutch and UK risk assessments are based on spending at a single operator. By contrast, AUC values in our study indicated that risk groups could be better differentiated by their cross‐operator compared to single‐operator expenditure. Although AUC scores were larger for cross‐operator than single‐operator expenditure, the differences were not large, and the CIs overlapped substantially (see Table 3). Therefore, further research is needed to confirm the value of using cross‐operator expenditure to discriminate risk groups above and beyond single‐operator expenditure. Recent evidence has shown that using cross‐operator spending to trigger checks would lead to an additional approximately 1% of gamblers surpassing the £150 limit in the United Kingdom, a potentially at‐risk population missed by the current single‐operator approach [40]. Our methodology and openly available analysis code (see Analysis Document) show how the implementation of financial risk assessments can be informed by evidence going forward.

Considering how well the UK's £150 net‐deposit, single operator threshold aligns with the optimal values identified for our sample depends on the risk classification used. If using the liberal, PGSI score of 1 or more approach, our outcomes suggest that a slightly higher £187 threshold would provide a better overall balance of harm prevention and liberty preservation. Although, this increase was only achieved by marginally enhancing liberty preservation and the £150 value fell comfortably within the CI bounds of the £187 threshold. If using the 2 or more harms on the PGSI approach, a much higher value of £367 was found, and this achieved substantially greater levels of liberty preservation at the expense of modest reductions in harm prevention capability. The £150 value fell outside the CIs for the £367 threshold, which had a lower bound of £184. Therefore, although we should be careful not to use outcomes from this unrepresentative sample to select the exact threshold value for these assessments in the United Kingdom, our findings highlight how the selection process is directly influenced by what is considered a meaningful level of risk to mitigate. For example, deciding on more liberal definitions of harm (e.g. PGSI ≥1) will necessitate lower thresholds that could capture more at‐risk individuals before serious harm emerges, but will invariably lead to more no‐/lower‐risk consumers being flagged for assessment.

When an original value of £125 was proposed for financial risk assessments in the United Kingdom, 44% of consumers polled felt the value was inappropriate, with most (30%) seeing it as too low [41]. Consumers interviewed as part of the same project thought most people who gamble online would be affected by the checks, as their expenditure exceeds £125. The analysis presented here is somewhat aligned with this perspective. Approximately 50% of no‐/lower‐risk individuals in our sample surpassed the higher £150 at some point in the 12 months preceding the survey, and the whole‐sample median maximum net‐deposit was £205. A recent large‐scale analysis of UK banking data (n = 243 478) found approximately half of those with a monthly net‐deposit that surpassed £150 could be characterised as ‘diverse spenders’ whose gambling expenditure appeared to be proportionate to their income and wider discretionary spending [40]. Combined with our outcomes, this indicates that a notable proportion of individuals who exceed the United Kingdom's current threshold of £150 may not belong to the at‐risk population targeted.

In the aforementioned UK poll study, 74% of consumers agreed that lower thresholds were appropriate for young adults 18 to 24 years old [41]. In our sample, outcomes were consistent with using lower thresholds for young adults. Although our non‐representative sample cannot establish population‐level need, in the majority of scenarios, optimal threshold values were lower for younger participants under the age of 30 compared to those 30 or older. This pattern is consistent with evidence that younger people are at greater risk of harm [12, 42] and may be more likely to experience harm at lower levels of gambling involvement [43]. The methodology modelled here has implications for other population measures, including mandatory limit setting, providing a lens for determining whether age‐specific policies may offer greater benefits in preventing gambling harms than population‐wide limits.

This study's major limitation is the size and non‐representative nature of its sample. Individuals recruited via crowdsourcing platforms [44] and those willing to provide access to their banking data [45] may systematically differ from non‐responders. Voluntary participants in surveys about their online gambling tend to be older and more involved, regular bettors [46], suggesting such samples contain a disproportionate number of at‐risk consumers. Although this disproportionate number of at‐risk participants limits generalisability, it does at least reduce the analytical limitations regarding the small sample size [47]. However, the sample size limitations did mean we had to use a more liberal definition of young adulthood (18–29) than is used in the Dutch financial risk assessments (18–24), and could not explore stricter definitions of at‐risk status, such as PGSI 5+ [48] or 8+ (PGSI) [27]. Our analysis script can be adapted for use with larger and more representative samples (see Analysis Document). Finally, although we used a risk‐based approach to evaluate the value of potential threshold values, our definition of risk was determined by the PGSI and, therefore, reflects the psychological and behavioural symptoms of problematic gambling, as well as the negative consequences or harms from this, and not financial harm or vulnerability that these assessments directly target. Still, if the overall goal of financial risk assessments is to reduce harm from gambling (via accrued losses among those who are financially vulnerable), PGSI scores represent an appropriate proxy in this context given their strong correlation with measures of gambling‐related harm [49].

Policymakers can help advance this area of work by fusing larger, more representative open banking data samples with people's self‐reported harm scores. For example, the Gambling Commission has been running its Gambling Survey for Great Britain at sample sizes of approximately 20 000, which could solve this limitation if an open‐banking module could be added. Stakeholders have also argued that financial risk assessments would be best implemented by a mandatory and independent payment processor for gambling transactions [50, 51]. This payment processor could not only implement potentially more effective cross‐operator checks suggested by our current sample and analysis, but also develop population‐level datasets for the long‐term refinement of checks across the full spectrum of vulnerability. A bold reimagining of the payment structures underlying online gambling may be needed to best prevent harms across the population [52].

In conclusion, these results suggest that the UK's £150 net‐deposit threshold for financial risk assessments places more emphasis on harm prevention than liberty preservation in this sample. In our sample, increasing the threshold appeared to substantially improve liberty preservation at the expense of minor reductions in harm prevention. More optimal assessment checks might consider cross‐operator instead of single‐operator spend and be lower for younger adults. Future studies with representative samples are needed to verify these insights.
