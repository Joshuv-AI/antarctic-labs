Segment-Level Coherence for Robust Harmful Intent Probing in LLMs
Xuanli He, Bilgehan Sel, Faizan Ali & Jenny Bao & Hoagy Cunningham & Jerry Wei

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly exposed to adaptive jailbreaking, particularly in high-stakes Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN) domains. Although streaming probes enable real-time monitoring, they still make systematic errors. We identify a core issue: existing methods often rely on a few high-scoring tokens, leading to false alarms when sensitive CBRN terms appear in benign contexts. To address this, we introduce a streaming probing objective that requires multiple evidence tokens to consistently support a prediction, rather than relying on isolated spikes. This encourages more robust detection based on aggregated signals instead of single-token cues. At a fixed 1% false-positive rate, our method improves the true-positive rate by 35.55% relative to strong streaming baselines. We further observe substantial gains in AUROC, even when starting from near-saturated baseline performance (AUROC = 97.40%). We also show that probing Attention or MLP activations consistently outperforms residual-stream features. Finally, even when adversarial fine-tuning enables novel character-level ciphers, harmful intent remains detectable: probes developed for the base LLMs can be applied “plug-and-play” to these obfuscated attacks, achieving an AUROC of over 98.85%.



1
Introduction

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) faces an increasing tension between broad utility and safety, particularly as adaptive jailbreaking strategies attempt to elicit harmful Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear (CBRN) instructions
(
1
;
20
;
14
)
. A common deployment-time mitigation is to introduce an intermediate safety layer prior to generation, typically implemented as a fine-tuned peer or smaller LLM classifier that determines whether generation should proceed
(
11
;
31
;
24
)
. However, the computational cost and added latency of invoking an auxiliary model motivate a lighter-weight alternative: representation-based probes that directly monitor the target model’s internal activations to detect harmful intent in real time
(
18
;
4
;
13
)
.

In streaming settings, predictions must be made incrementally without access to future context. As a result, aggregation mechanisms tend to prioritize immediately predictive signals, often over-relying on isolated keywords or domain-specific terminology rather than aggregating evidence across the interaction. This issue is particularly pronounced in CBRN domains, where benign expert discussions frequently contain the same terminology as harmful requests. Consequently, streaming probes can become overly sensitive to such terms, leading to elevated false-positive rates.

One might attribute this behavior to limitations in the training data. However, prior work
(
4
)
already incorporates substantial benign domain-specific data, yet false positives persist (see
Figure

1
and Appendix
F
). This suggests that the issue is not solely due to data imbalance, but is also rooted in the aggregation objective. Existing methods typically rely on softmax
(
18
;
4
)
or hardmax
(
13
)
aggregation, both of which emphasize a small number of high-scoring activations. As a result, a few salient tokens can dominate the decision, even when the overall context is benign.

Figure 1:
Keyword-seeking vs. Intent-aware detection.
Baseline streaming probes (left) frequently misclassify benign prompts with sensitive biological terminology due to reliance on shortcut lexical cues. Our method (right) introduces an inductive bias encouraging the probe to aggregate evidence across multiple regions before signaling harmful intent, thereby reducing false positives in professional contexts.

To address this limitation, we propose a streaming probing objective that encourages consistent evidence across multiple supporting regions, rather than relying on isolated high-scoring tokens. Our approach consists of two components: (i) Top-
K
supportive window aggregation, which requires multiple windows to jointly support a prediction, and (ii) Segment Variance Regularization (SegVar), which penalizes dispersion in logit magnitude across selected evidence windows, suppressing isolated spikes and promoting more stable aggregation. Together, these changes shift the detector from single-token cues toward more distributed and consistent evidence.

Across multiple CBRN-related datasets, our method consistently surpasses strong streaming baselines. Even under an already high-performing baseline (AUROC = 97.40%), it reduces the remaining error margin by 31.2% relative to the baseline. This improvement translates into a substantial 35.55% gain in TPR @ 1% FPR and a 16.80% increase in logspace-AUROC, a variant of AUROC that places greater emphasis on the low–false-positive-rate regime most relevant for real-world deployment
(
3
)
.

Finally, we provide a detailed analysis of probe design choices. We find that probing Attention or MLP activations consistently outperforms the commonly used residual stream
(
8
;
23
;
12
)
. Moreover, probes remain effective under adversarial obfuscation: even when models are fine-tuned with character-level ciphers, probes trained on base models transfer in a plug-and-play manner, achieving up to 98.85% AUROC. This suggests that underlying safety-relevant signals remain detectable despite surface-level transformations.

Related Work

LLM safety and jailbreak defenses.

As LLMs become ubiquitous in high-stakes applications, defending against adversarial jailbreaks, especially those targeting sensitive domains such as CBRN content, has emerged as a critical challenge.

24
introduced Constitutional Classifiers, which train input and output classifiers on synthetic data generated from natural-language safety rules.
Validated through approximately 3,000 hours of red teaming, their approach demonstrates that defending against universal jailbreaks is tractable, albeit with modest production overhead.
Building on this foundation,
4
propose Constitutional Classifiers++, which achieves production-grade efficiency through multi-layer probing. In addition,
28
demonstrate that LLMs can identify harmful prompts through self-inspection. While these methods advance classifier-based defenses, they primarily optimize for accuracy and throughput rather than addressing shortcut learning under weak supervision — a gap our work targets.

Probing and representation analysis.

Linear probes have become a standard tool for analyzing the internal representations of language models.
18
demonstrate that mean and softmax pooling probes can detect high-stakes exchanges from residual stream activations, while
12
explore attention-based probing as an alternative aggregation mechanism. In parallel, representation-steering research demonstrates that latent activations encode task-relevant structure that can be systematically extracted or manipulated:
8
uncover task vectors underlying in-context learning,
15
propose in-context vectors for controllable latent-space steering, and
23
introduce contrastive activation addition to modify model behavior.

3
Method

We build on the sliding-window aggregation framework of

4
, and introduce two lightweight modifications
to mitigate shortcut-learning behavior.
First,
Top-
K
supportive-window pooling
replaces softmax weighting with a
more conservative aggregation strategy that effectively requires multiple windows
to jointly support a prediction, reducing reliance on isolated high-confidence cues.
Second,
Segment Variance Regularization
(SegVar) penalizes inconsistent
confidence patterns across selected evidence windows and is applied selectively to
benign exchanges, where keyword-driven false positives are most prevalent.

3.1
Problem Setup

We consider a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}

of user–assistant exchanges, where each exchange

x^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{T_{i}})
is annotated with an exchange-level
binary label
y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
, where
y^{(i)}=1
denotes the presence of harmful content and
y^{(i)}=0
otherwise. While supervision is
available only at the exchange level, deployment requires
streaming,
token-level predictions
as the response is generated, creating a mismatch between
training and inference. Our objective is therefore to estimate, at each position

t
,

p(y^{(i)}=1\mid x^{(i)}_{1:t}),

using only the observed prefix.

At token position
t
, a frozen LLM produces internal activations

\phi^{(\ell)}_{t}(x^{(i)})
at layer
\ell
. Prior work primarily probes the residual
stream
(
8
;
15
;
23
)
;
we additionally consider attention and MLP outputs, which provide stronger and more
stable signals in our experiments (see
Section

4.3
).

We construct a feature vector by concatenating activations from all layers:

\psi_{t}=[\phi_{t}^{(\ell_{1})};\phi_{t}^{(\ell_{2})};\ldots],

and apply a linear probe to produce a scalar logit:
z_{t}=W^{\top}\psi_{t}+b.

This yields a streaming estimate of exchange-level harmfulness:
p(y^{(i)}=1\mid x^{(i)}_{1:t})=\sigma(z_{t})

, where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the sigmoid function. The probe operates causally on
per-token activations and incurs negligible inference overhead.

Sliding-window aggregation.

Token-level logits are often noisy. To incorporate local context while preserving
causality during training, we aggregate logits over a sliding
window of the most recent
M
positions:

\bar{z}_{t}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}z_{t-k},\quad t\geq M.

(1)

This
Sliding Window Mean (SWiM)
smooths transient fluctuations and produces a
more stable streaming signal. Following prior work
(
4
)
,
we fix
M=16
throughout.

To bridge exchange-level supervision and token-level predictions,
4
propose a softmax-weighted aggregation objective that combines window-level scores using confidence-based weights. This mechanism functions as a max pooling
(
5
)
over token positions, emphasizing the most salient spans in the sequence.

3.2
Learning from Distributed Evidence in Streaming Detection

Top-
K
Supportive-Window Pooling.

Instead of weighting all positions by confidence, we require
multiple
windows
to jointly support a prediction. We select the
K
windows with the highest
aggregated logits and average them:

z_{\text{seq}}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{K}}\bar{z}_{t},\qquad\mathcal{T}_{K}=\mathrm{Top\text{-}K}(\{\bar{z}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T_{i}},K).

(2)

Training uses a sequence-level binary cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{Top-}K}(x^{(i)})=\ell_{\text{BCE}}\!\left(y^{(i)},\sigma(z_{\text{seq}})\right).

(3)

Top-
K
pooling enforces a conservative aggregation rule: harmful predictions effectively require at least
K
windows to exhibit elevated logits, while benign predictions require even the
K
most suspicious windows to remain collectively low. By distributing confidence across multiple windows, this objective reduces sensitivity to isolated lexical spikes and biases learning toward intent-level evidence.

Segment Variance Regularization.

While Top-
K
pooling requires multiple windows to jointly support a prediction, it may still yield
spiky
evidence patterns when the highest-scoring windows are widely separated rather than forming a contiguous span. Such fragmented evidence can lead to unstable predictions in streaming settings.

To promote locally consistent evidence, we introduce SegVar, a soft smoothing mechanism over the probe’s self-identified evidence. Specifically, we define a soft evidence weight

p_{t}=\sigma(\bar{z}_{t}/\tau_{s}),

(4)

where
\tau_{s}
controls the sharpness of the weighting. Using these weights, we compute a weighted mean logit

\mu^{(i)}=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}p_{t}\cdot\bar{z}_{t}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}p_{t}+\epsilon},

(5)

and define the variance penalty

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SegVar}}(x^{(i)})=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}p_{t}\cdot(\bar{z}_{t}-\mu^{(i)})^{2}}{\sum_{t=1}^{T_{i}}p_{t}+\epsilon},

(6)

with
\epsilon=10^{-8}
for numerical stability.

Minimizing
\mathcal{L}_{\text{SegVar}}
encourages local consistency by softly penalizing variance within continuous evidence regions. This regularization promotes agreement among neighboring tokens, biasing the probe toward evidence that forms a coherent and locally smooth span rather than relying on isolated or widely separated high-confidence spikes.

We apply SegVar exclusively to benign exchanges, where spurious, keyword-driven activations are most prevalent. In contrast, harmful exchanges may legitimately exhibit evidence distributed across multiple disjoint regions (
e.g.,
an initial harmful request followed by subsequent elaboration). Empirically, enforcing variance regularization in such cases suppresses valid signals rather than spurious ones and thus degrades detection performance as shown in
Section

4.3
.

Training and Inference

The combined training objective for exchange
x^{(i)}
with label
y^{(i)}
is:

\mathcal{L}(x^{(i)})=\mathcal{L}_{\text{Top-}K}(x^{(i)})+\lambda(1-y^{(i)})\cdot\mathcal{L}_{\text{SegVar}}(x^{(i)}),

(7)

where
\lambda\geq 0
controls regularization strength and
(1-y^{(i)})

restricts SegVar to benign examples. Together, Top-
K
pooling and SegVar introduce
an inductive bias that promotes distributed, intent-level representations
over sparse, keyword-driven activations. We refer to this combined approach as
SC-TopK.

At inference time only, we apply exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing,
following
4
, to aggregate evidence in a streaming
setting:

\hat{z}_{t}=\gamma\cdot\bar{z}_{t}+(1-\gamma)\cdot\hat{z}_{t-1},

where
\gamma\in(0,1]
controls the effective temporal context.

Main Results

We first evaluate our approach and baseline probes across all models and datasets. As shown in

4.4
Obfuscated Encoding Detection

While prior research demonstrates that adversarial fine-tuning can circumvent the inherent safety guardrails of LLMs
(
7
;
27
)
, the resilience of external monitoring layers remains under-explored. Because safety probes operate independently of the model’s core parameters, a critical open question is whether they maintain efficacy—without retraining—after distribution shifts induced by adversarial fine-tuning. This section evaluates the robustness of trained probes under encoded inputs, focusing on their ability to detect harmful content when it is obfuscated using character-level ciphers.

We evaluate probe generalization across five character-level ciphers that span diverse encoding paradigms, including substitution, transposition, grid-coordinate mapping, and numeric encoding. Specifically, we consider: (i) ASCII decimal conversion, (ii) Fibonacci-indexed character permutation, (iii) keyed Polybius-square substitution, (iv) adjacent-pair swapping, and (v) seeded random letter substitution (WALNUT50). Detailed constructions are provided in Appendix
E
.
Figure

5
presents the performance of SC-TopK under these encodings, both with and without the adapter. Results for the remaining probe baselines are deferred to Appendix
E
. Without adaptation, all probes perform near chance (AUROC
{\approx}
60%), indicating that the backbone LLM cannot interpret the ciphers and thus exposes no informative internal signal. After adapter fine-tuning, performance improves sharply across all cipher types, with AUROC rising to 98.85%. This level of performance closely approaches the no-obfuscation baseline of 99.90%, which serves as an approximate upper bound. This confirms that once the backbone learns to decode a cipher, the recovered semantic information is reliably reflected in its internal representations. Notably, the probe itself requires no retraining and can be attached plug-and-play after backbone adaptation, a practical advantage in deployment settings where fine-tuning via commercial APIs precludes data retention.

5
Limitations and Future Work

Despite strong empirical performance, several limitations remain.

Adversarial co-adaptation.

Our current evaluation does not fully account for probe-aware adversaries that explicitly optimize against the streaming detection objective. A sufficiently sophisticated attacker could, for example, introduce benign scaffolding to dilute segment-level coherence signals or deliberately craft token sequences that smooth and attenuate probe activations over time
(
17
)
. This highlights a limitation of the present study: the detector is evaluated under a largely static threat model. Future work should therefore examine the dynamics of co-adaptation between attacker and detector
(
2
)
, characterizing how streaming probes perform under iterative, adversarial optimization.

Keyword-dense and long benign conversations.

Although our method alleviates simplicity bias, its performance still deteriorates in professional dialogues dense with domain-specific terminology. In such settings, sustained technical discussion can generate coherent activation patterns that superficially resemble malicious planning, particularly in long, multi-turn exchanges where streaming predictions must be made without access to global context (see Appendix
F
). This limitation suggests the need for hierarchical intent modeling that integrates segment-level evidence with higher-level discourse structure and incorporates uncertainty-aware temporal smoothing. Such a framework would more reliably differentiate persistent malicious intent from extended but legitimate expert discussion.

Beyond jailbreaking: prompt injection.

Although our experiments focus on jailbreak detection, the proposed framework generalizes naturally to related threat models, such as prompt injection in retrieval-augmented or tool-augmented systems. In these settings, malicious instructions may be distributed across retrieved documents or multiple external inputs, creating compositional attack surfaces that are difficult to detect through isolated analysis. An important direction for future work is therefore to systematically evaluate cross-context, multi-source intent detection in such environments, where harmful signals may emerge only through the aggregation across sources.

6
Conclusion

Streaming safety probes often rely on max-based aggregation over token-level signals, which leads to a keyword-driven failure mode: isolated high-salience tokens can dominate predictions and trigger false positives in otherwise benign exchanges. To address this, we introduce a segment-level framework that enforces distributed evidence accumulation. By replacing token-level maxima with Top-
K
supportive window aggregation and controlling logit dispersion via SegVar, the probe requires multiple segments to jointly support a harmful prediction, reducing sensitivity to spurious spikes.

This design yields substantial gains over strong streaming baselines, including a 35.55% improvement in TPR @ 1% FPR and a 16.80% increase in logspace-AUROC. We further observe that Attention and MLP activations outperform residual streams. The probe also remains robust under adaptive obfuscation, achieving 98.85% AUROC without retraining after cipher-tuning.

Overall, our results highlight segment-level evidence aggregation as a simple and effective inductive bias for low–false-positive, deployment-ready streaming safety monitoring.

Appendix A
Chemical Data Synthesis

To train and evaluate harmful-intent probes in the chemical domain, we construct a fully synthetic dataset of single-turn chemical queries using a structured generation pipeline inspired by
25
. The objective of this pipeline is to generate realistic, diverse, and adversarially challenging queries that span both malicious and benign chemical inquiries, without relying on human-authored sensitive data. Harmful queries train the probe to recognize potentially dangerous information requests, while harmless queries establish boundaries for legitimate scientific inquiry and reduce false positives. We generate the training dataset using a constitution-guided procedure adapted from
24
, based on the chemical constitution in their Appendix B. An LLM then extracts atomic rules from this constitution, where each distinct prohibition or guideline becomes a self-contained tenet. Separately, we use an LLM to generate expert and amateur personas in the chemical domain, capturing diverse speaking styles and levels of expertise. Finally, we combine each tenet and persona with situational constraints drawn from the persuasion taxonomy of
32
, which introduces realistic contextual variations such as role-playing scenarios or appeals to authority. The resulting query space spans the Cartesian product of tenets, personas, and constraints. For each combination, we generate both a harmful variant that requests prohibited information and a harmless variant that poses a legitimate scientific question on
related topics.

B.3
Log-space AUROC

Log-space AUROC computes the area under the ROC curve when the FPR axis is plotted on a logarithmic scale. We adopt this metric because our primary interest is classifier performance at low FPRs, a regime critical for real-world deployment. Standard AUROC can mask behavior in this region by disproportionately weighting performance at higher FPRs, while TPR measured at a single FPR threshold is often overly sensitive to noise. In contrast, log-space AUROC offers a more stable and informative evaluation by capturing the trade-off between TPR and FPR across the low-FPR range relevant to practical applications. Following
3
, we compute log-space AUROC over FPR values from
10^{-3}
to
10^{-1}
.

Appendix C
Implementation Details of Baseline Probes and Classifier

C.1
Probe Details

Given an exchange
x^{(i)}=(x^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,x^{(i)}_{T_{i}})
, we associate it with an exchange-level binary label
y^{(i)}\in\{0,1\}
, where
y^{(i)}=1
indicates the presence of harmful content and
y^{(i)}=0
otherwise. At token position
t
, a frozen language model produces internal activations

\phi^{(\ell)}_{t}(x^{(i)})
at layer
\ell
. We construct a feature vector by concatenating activations from all layers:

\psi_{t}=[\phi_{t}^{(\ell_{1})};\phi_{t}^{(\ell_{2})};\ldots],

and apply a linear probe to produce a scalar logit

z_{t}=W^{\top}\psi_{t}+b.

Mean probe.

The
mean probe
aggregates token-level logits by computing their arithmetic average to obtain a single sequence-level logit. Formally, given token logits
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{T}\}
, the final prediction is defined as:

\bar{z}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}z_{t}.

This approach treats all token positions equally, summarizing the sequence by uniformly averaging their contributions before applying the loss.

Softmax probe.

The
softmax probe
aggregates token-level logits through a weighted average, where the weights are obtained via a softmax function over token scores. Formally,

\bar{z}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}z_{t},\qquad w_{t}=\frac{\exp(z_{t})}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\exp(z_{t^{\prime}})}.

This formulation emphasizes high-confidence token positions, assigning larger weights to tokens with larger logits while maintaining differentiability.

Attention probe.

The
attention probe
follows a similar aggregation strategy to the softmax probe, but computes attention weights using a separate linear projection of the token representations. Specifically,

\bar{z}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}w_{t}z_{t},\qquad w_{t}=\frac{\exp(m_{t})}{\sum_{t^{\prime}=1}^{T}\exp(m_{t^{\prime}})},

where the attention score
m_{t}
is defined as

m_{t}=W_{m}^{\top}\psi_{t}+b_{m}.

This formulation decouples the weighting mechanism from the prediction logits, enabling more flexible token importance modeling.

RMAttn probe.

13
introduce the Max of Rolling Means Attention Probe (
RMAttn probe
), which employs a window-based, attention-weighted aggregation strategy. Specifically, given a window of width
w
ending at position
t
, an attention-weighted summary is computed as:

\bar{z}_{t}=\sum_{j=t-w+1}^{t}\alpha_{j}z_{j},\qquad\alpha_{j}=\frac{\exp(m_{j})}{\sum_{k=t-w+1}^{t}\exp(m_{k})},

where
m_{j}=W_{m}^{\top}\psi_{j}+b_{m}
denotes the attention score derived from the token representation
\psi_{j}
, and
(W_{m},b_{m})
are learnable parameters.

The final sequence-level logit is obtained by taking the maximum over all window-level aggregated scores:

\bar{z}=\max_{t}\bar{z}_{t}.

This design preserves the locality bias of rolling-window methods while enabling adaptive, attention-based weighting within each window. Following the best-performing configuration reported in
13
, the token-level logits
z_{t}
are computed using a two-layer MLP with ReLU activation and a hidden dimension of 100, replacing the single linear projection.

SWiM probe.

4
propose the
SWiM probe
, another window-based approach that aggregates token-level logits using a mean sliding window, as described in
Section

3
. Unlike methods that apply a hard maximum over window-level scores, SWiM employs a softmax-weighted aggregation across windows. Concretely, the training objective is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{soft}}(x^{(i)})=\sum_{t=M}^{T_{i}}w_{t}\cdot\ell_{\text{BCE}}\!\left(y^{(i)},\sigma(\bar{z}_{t})\right),

(8)

where
\ell_{\text{BCE}}
denotes the binary cross-entropy loss,
\bar{z}_{t}
is the aggregated logit for the window ending at position
t
, and
\sigma(\cdot)
is the sigmoid function. The window weights are computed as

w_{t}=\frac{\exp(\bar{z}_{t}/\tau)}{\sum_{t^{\prime}}\exp(\bar{z}_{t^{\prime}}/\tau)},

with temperature parameter
\tau
controlling the sharpness of the weighting distribution. In line with
4
, we set
\tau=1
.

D.3
Impact of Various Factors

In this section, we systematically evaluate how performance of probes varies across four key dimensions: (1) hyperparameter configurations, including both training and inference hyperparameters; (2) training objectives, which determine how evidence is aggregated and regularized; (3) the inclusion of keyword-driven negative test samples, designed to stress-test robustness against shortcut learning; and (4) model sizes. By isolating each factor, we disentangle gains arising from principled modeling decisions from those attributable to incidental tuning, and assess the robustness and scalability of our approach under realistic deployment conditions.

Hyperparameters.

Figure

13
presents a sensitivity analysis of the four key hyperparameters in our objective. We first examine
K
, the Top-
K
pooling parameter that specifies the number of window-level signals that must jointly support a prediction. Performance improves as
K
increases from 4 to 8, indicating that requiring multiple supporting windows effectively suppresses spurious, isolated activations. However, further increasing
K
to 16 degrades performance, suggesting that overly broad aggregation dilutes informative evidence by incorporating weak or irrelevant windows into the final decision.

We vary the regularization weight
\lambda
for SegVar, which governs the strength of the segment-level coherence constraint. Performance improves consistently as
\lambda
increases, with the best results observed at
\lambda=0.01
. This pattern indicates that moderate regularization effectively stabilizes continuous predictions while avoiding over-constraining the model.

We further examine the effect of the segment temperature
\tau_{s}
, which governs the smoothness of the sigmoid weighting in SegVar. Moderate smoothing (
\tau_{s}=2
) achieves the highest log-space AUROC. Both smaller and larger values result in modest performance declines. Importantly, performance remains stable across the evaluated range, indicating that the method is not overly sensitive to the precise choice of
\tau_{s}
.

Finally, we examine the impact of the EMA decay rate,
\gamma
, at inference time on the bio and chemical test sets using Llama3.1. We vary
\gamma\in\{0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7\}

\gamma

Cipher Encoding

To evaluate robustness under diverse obfuscation strategies, we employ five character-level ciphers that span distinct encoding paradigms. Each cipher is applied independently to user-turn text before it is fed to the adversarially fine-tuned backbone. Below we describe each cipher and provide encoding examples.

ord()

chr()

Appendix F
Qualitative Analysis

We present a comprehensive set of empirical studies and comparisons to evaluate the effectiveness of our probe across diverse configurations, with most analyses focusing on quantitative performance metrics. To complement these results with a qualitative perspective, we further examine the practical efficacy of our approach at the token level. Specifically, we compare SC-TopK against SWiM, as well as other baseline approaches, across multiple exchanges, examining token-level predictions to understand differences in evidence aggregation, behavioral sensitivity, and decision-making patterns.

To quantify token-level activation, we compute per-token probe logits
z_{t}
and apply EMA smoothing at inference time:

\hat{z}_{t}=\gamma z_{t}+(1-\gamma)\hat{z}_{t-1},

where
\gamma=0.1
. We then use each token’s smoothed logit
\hat{z}_{t}
as a candidate threshold, and for each such threshold compute the false-negative rate (FNR),
i.e.,
the fraction of ground-truth positive instances whose maximum predicted score (over all tokens in the instance) falls below that value. We refer to this FNR as the token-level activation metric. Probes that primarily operate as keyword extractors tend to concentrate activation on a narrow subset of highly salient lexical cues. As a result, genuinely informative tokens that fall outside this limited set may receive low scores and fail to exceed the detection threshold, thereby increasing the FNR. Accordingly, FNR serves as a proxy for token coverage. A higher FNR indicates sparse, keyword-driven behavior with limited semantic generalization, whereas a lower FNR suggests broader semantic encoding that extends beyond a small collection of trigger words.

Samples 1–2 (
Figure

-
19
) correspond to keyword-driven negative instances. As anticipated, SWiM misclassifies these cases due to the presence of salient terms such as “
Neo-Ebola
” and “
brucillex syndrome
”. In contrast, SC-TopK correctly identifies them as negative by incorporating broader contextual signals and recognizing the overall benign intent of the exchanges, rather than over-relying on isolated high-risk keywords.

Samples 3–6 (

In these cases, baseline probes assign substantial weight to such domain-specific cues, which leads to false positives by overemphasizing surface-level terminology. In contrast, SC-TopK more effectively captures the underlying benign intent of the exchanges and correctly classifies them as negative instances.
