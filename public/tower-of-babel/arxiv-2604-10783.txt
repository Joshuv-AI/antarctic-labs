Learning Preference-Based Objectives from Clinical Narratives for Sequential Treatment Decision-Making
Daniel J. Tan, Kay Choong See, Mengling Feng *

Abstract
Designing reward functions remains a central challenge in reinforcement learning (RL) for healthcare, where outcomes are sparse, delayed, and difficult to specify. While structured data capture physiological states, they often fail to reflect the overall quality of a patient’s clinical trajectory, including recovery dynamics, treatment burden, and stability. Clinical narratives, in contrast, summarize longitudinal reasoning and implicitly encode evaluations of treatment effectiveness. We propose Clinical Narrative-informed Preference Rewards (CN-PR), a framework for learning reward functions directly from discharge summaries by treating them as scalable supervision for trajectory-level preferences. Using a large language model, we derive trajectory quality scores (TQS) and construct pairwise preferences over patient trajectories, enabling reward learning via a structured preference-based objective. To account for variability in narrative informativeness, we incorporate a confidence signal that weights supervision based on its relevance to the decision-making task. The learned reward aligns strongly with trajectory quality (Spearman ρ = 0.63 \rho=0.63 ) and enables policies that are consistently associated with improved recovery-related outcomes, including increased organ support–free days and faster shock resolution, while maintaining comparable performance on mortality. These effects persist under external validation. Our results demonstrate that narrative-derived supervision provides a scalable and expressive alternative to handcrafted or outcome-based reward design for dynamic treatment regimes.



1
Introduction

Clinical decision-making in intensive care units (ICUs) involves complex, time-dependent treatment strategies that must balance competing objectives under uncertainty. Management of critically ill patients often requires sequential adjustments to interventions such as fluid therapy, vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, sedation, and renal support. These decisions are inherently dynamic, motivating the use of data-driven approaches for learning dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs) from electronic health record (EHR) data.

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides a principled framework for modeling such sequential decision-making problems, where policies are optimized to maximize long-term outcomes. Prior work has demonstrated the potential of RL in critical care settings, including applications in sepsis management, ventilation strategies, and hemodynamic control
[
16
,
24
,
25
]
. However, a central challenge remains reward specification, which defines the objective optimized by the learned policy.

Most clinical RL approaches rely on simplified reward functions based on terminal outcomes (e.g., mortality) or a limited set of physiological proxies. While informative, these signals provide only a partial view of patient trajectories and fail to capture important aspects such as recovery dynamics, treatment burden, and clinical stability. Handcrafted intermediate rewards partially address this limitation but introduce heuristic assumptions and are highly sensitive to design choices, often leading to inconsistent or poorly aligned policy behavior
[
20
,
17
]
. More broadly, specifying reward functions in complex clinical environments remains fundamentally challenging.

An alternative is to infer reward functions from preferences rather than explicitly defining them. Preference-based approaches learn from comparative evaluations of trajectories
[
4
]
, allowing reward functions to reflect relative outcome quality instead of fixed endpoints. However, existing methods typically rely on manually collected annotations, which are costly and difficult to scale in healthcare settings.

In contrast, routine clinical practice produces a rich and largely untapped source of implicit preference signals: unstructured clinical narratives. Discharge summaries and clinical notes synthesize clinician assessments of treatment response, disease progression, and recovery
[
13
]
, providing high-level evaluations of patient trajectories that are not captured in structured data alone. These narratives can be viewed as naturally occurring supervision, where relative outcome quality is embedded in how patient courses are described.

Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) enable the extraction of structured signals from clinical text at scale, making it feasible to operationalize such narratives for reward learning. Rather than relying on handcrafted objectives or explicit annotation, narrative-derived signals can serve as a scalable proxy for trajectory-level preferences, capturing broader aspects of clinical trajectories beyond predefined endpoints.

In this work, we propose
Clinical Narrative-informed Preference Rewards
(CN-PR), a framework for learning reward functions from clinical narratives. We use an LLM to assign trajectory quality scores (TQS), which are used to construct pairwise preferences over patient trajectories. To account for variability in the informativeness of clinical narratives, we introduce a confidence signal that reflects the relevance of each summary to the target decision-making task, enabling the model to weight supervision by reliability.

The resulting reward function is trained using a structured preference-based objective that incorporates both the magnitude of outcome differences and the confidence of the underlying supervision. This allows the learned reward to capture not only relative ordering of trajectories but also the strength and reliability of preference signals.

We evaluate CN-PR in the context of sepsis treatment as a representative use case in critical care. Our results show that the learned reward aligns with clinically meaningful outcomes, supports the learning of policies associated with improved recovery metrics, and generalizes across datasets. These findings suggest that leveraging narrative-derived supervision provides a scalable and expressive approach to reward specification for RL-based dynamic treatment regimes.

Background and Related Work

2.1
Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic Treatment Regimes

Reinforcement learning (RL) has been increasingly applied to dynamic treatment regimes (DTRs), where policies optimize sequential treatment decisions based on patient state
[
16
]
. Applications include sepsis management, mechanical ventilation, and sedation using retrospective EHR data
[
22
,
24
,
7
]
. In these settings, learning is performed offline, making policy behavior highly dependent on the reward function.

Because clinical RL is typically performed in an offline setting without the ability to safely explore new actions, the learned policy is highly sensitive to the reward function used during training. As a result, reward specification plays a central role in determining both the behavior and clinical plausibility of learned policies.

2.2
Reward Learning and Preference-Based Methods

Designing reward functions for clinical RL is challenging due to the multidimensional nature of clinical outcomes. Most approaches rely on terminal endpoints or handcrafted proxies, which introduce subjective assumptions and can lead to unstable policies
[
20
,
17
]
.

In contrast, inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) aims to infer reward functions from observed behavior, providing a data-driven alternative to manual reward design
[
1
]
. In healthcare, IRL has been used to model clinician decision-making in settings such as sepsis treatment and mechanical ventilation
[
38
,
39
]
. However, classical IRL assumes that observed behavior is near-optimal, an assumption that is often violated in clinical practice due to variability in expertise, institutional protocols, and patient heterogeneity
[
34
,
3
]
.

Preference-based IRL relaxes this assumption by learning from comparisons between trajectories rather than demonstrations alone, allowing reward learning from imperfect data
[
4
]
. While promising, these approaches require explicit preference annotations, which are difficult to obtain at scale in clinical domains.

Conceptually, our work is also related to perspectives that treat reward functions as imperfect observations of underlying objectives rather than definitive specifications
[
8
]
. This view motivates learning reward functions from diverse sources of evidence, including implicit signals embedded in data.

2.3
Learning from Language and Clinical Narratives

Recent work has shown that reward functions can be learned from human judgments over text, as in reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
[
27
,
21
]
. These approaches highlight the potential of language as a medium for expressing complex objectives.

In healthcare, clinical narratives such as discharge summaries provide rich descriptions of patient trajectories, treatment responses, and outcomes. Prior work has demonstrated the value of clinical text for predictive modeling
[
9
]
, yet most RL-based DTR frameworks rely exclusively on structured data and do not leverage narratives for reward construction.

Large language models (LLMs) enable scalable extraction of structured information from unstructured text, creating an opportunity to use clinical narratives as a source of implicit preference supervision. However, this direction remains largely unexplored.

2.4
Gap in Existing Work

Existing approaches rely on either manually specified rewards or explicitly collected preference annotations, both of which present limitations in scalability and expressiveness. Clinical narratives provide a natural, underutilized source of trajectory-level preference signals that can be leveraged for reward learning.

This gap motivates our proposed framework, which integrates narrative-derived preferences into a structured preference-based reward learning approach.

3
Methods

3.1
Data Extraction and Preprocessing

We extracted ICU stays from the MIMIC-IV database
[
13
]
, which contains data for over 60,000 ICU patients from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019. Inclusion criteria were: (1) sepsis defined according to Sepsis-3 criteria, (2) availability of a corresponding discharge summary within 14 days of ICU discharge, (3) selection of the final ICU stay within each hospital admission, and (4) ICU length of stay between 12 hours and 21 days.

These criteria were designed to ensure temporal alignment between structured clinical trajectories and narrative summaries, while excluding poorly documented or clinically implausible episodes. This preprocessing improves the reliability of narrative-derived supervision, which is critical for downstream preference-based reward learning. However, it may introduce a bias toward more well-documented and clinically coherent cases, potentially enriching for trajectories with more favorable outcomes.

A total of 30,594 hospital stays met the initial inclusion criteria for narrative extraction. After additional preprocessing for reinforcement learning, including feature availability and trajectory construction, the final cohort consisted of 25,370 ICU stays. The extracted data span from 24 hours before sepsis diagnosis to 48 hours after diagnosis, forming a maximum 72-hour window per patient.

Clinical features spanning demographics, vitals, laboratory values, and interventions were extracted as described in Table
1
. Values exceeding clinically plausible ranges were clipped, and continuous variables were standardized. Patient trajectories were discretized into 4-hour intervals using appropriate summary statistics (mean or sum). Missing values were handled using sample-and-hold imputation, augmented with time-since-last-measurement features.

Intravenous fluid administration included bolus and continuous infusions of crystalloids, colloids, and blood products, normalized for tonicity
[
33
]
. Vasopressor therapy comprised norepinephrine, epinephrine, vasopressin, dopamine, and phenylephrine, with doses converted to norepinephrine equivalents using established dose equivalence relationships
[
5
]
. Further details on
the action discretization process is detailed in Supplementary Table S1.

3.2
Clinical Narrative Extraction and Processing

We extracted deidentified discharge summaries from the MIMIC-IV Notes dataset, where each hospital admission is associated with a single discharge note. Each ICU stay was paired with its corresponding narrative.

To focus on clinically meaningful reasoning, we retained sections containing high-level clinical interpretation:

•

History of Present Illness

•

Hospital Course

•

Discharge Diagnosis

•

Discharge Condition

•

Discharge Disposition

•

Discharge Instructions

Sections dominated by raw data (e.g., lab tables or imaging reports) were excluded, as they lack contextual interpretation and increase input length without improving outcome inference.

To provide additional context, we prepended structured metadata to each narrative:

•

Dialysis status at ICU admission and discharge

•

Baseline SOFA score

•

ICU length of stay

These variables were included to contextualize disease severity without directly determining outcome labels.

3.3
LLM-Based Clinical Trajectory Scoring

We employed a large language model (GPT-OSS-120B) to assign trajectory quality scores (TQS) from discharge summaries using a structured prompt designed to emulate expert clinical reasoning. The model was used in a zero-shot setting without task-specific fine-tuning.

Each trajectory was assigned a score
y\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
reflecting overall clinical trajectory quality, incorporating not only survival outcomes but also treatment response, complication burden, and recovery dynamics:

•

1:
Deceased following rapid or potentially modifiable deterioration, with limited evidence of stabilization or treatment response

•

2:
Deceased following a prolonged or largely irreversible course, with persistent organ failure despite treatment

•

3:
Survival with poor outcome, characterized by severe unresolved complications, limited recovery, or near-terminal trajectory

•

4:
Survival with partial recovery, where key complications are controlled but significant morbidity or functional limitations remain

•

5:
Survival with favorable recovery, marked by effective treatment response, resolution of acute illness, and minimal persistent dysfunction

This formulation captures graded differences in recovery and treatment quality beyond binary survival. To ensure reproducibility, scoring was performed deterministically (temperature = 0). The full prompt is provided in the Supplementary Materials Section 1.3, along with a representative example of a discharge summary, assigned score, and model-generated justification (Supplementary Materials Section 1.3.1).

In addition to TQS, the model outputs a scalar
confidence
value reflecting the
task-relevance of the discharge summary for evaluating sepsis management
. Confidence is higher when narratives explicitly describe sepsis-related processes (e.g., hemodynamic instability, vasopressor use, fluid resuscitation) and their relationship to clinical outcomes, and lower when such information is sparse or dominated by unrelated conditions. This design downweights trajectories that provide limited supervision for the target decision-making task.

The model also generates a brief textual justification supporting each assigned score. A random sample of 50 cases was reviewed by a clinical expert and verified the model’s reasoning to be clinically coherent. Robustness was assessed via repeated scoring, demonstrating high consistency (92.2% exact agreement; weighted Cohen’s
\kappa=0.965
). Validation against independent clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table S4) further confirms that the learned reward aligns with clinically meaningful endpoints.

3.5
Preference-Based Reward Learning

Using narrative-derived trajectory quality scores (TQS), we construct pairwise preferences and learn a reward function over state–action pairs.

Given trajectories
\mathcal{D}=\{\tau_{i}\}
with scores
y_{i}
, we define:

\tau_{i}\succ\tau_{j}\quad\text{if}\quad y_{i}>y_{j}.

(1)

Preference pairs are sampled against lower-scored trajectories with constraints on minimum score differences and total pair count for stability.

We model preferences using a Bradley–Terry formulation
[
10
]
:

P_{\theta}(\tau_{i}\succ\tau_{j})=\frac{\exp(R_{\theta}(\tau_{i}))}{\exp(R_{\theta}(\tau_{i}))+\exp(R_{\theta}(\tau_{j}))},

(2)

where
R_{\theta}(\tau)=\sum_{t}r_{\theta}(s_{t},a_{t})
.

Weighted Margin-Based Objective

For each pair
(\tau_{i},\tau_{j})
, we define the score gap:

\Delta_{ij}=y_{i}-y_{j}>0,

(3)

and confidence scores
c_{i},c_{j}\in[0,1]
.

We optimize:

\mathcal{L}=\mathbb{E}_{(i,j)}\left[w_{ij}\cdot\log\left(1+\exp\big(m_{ij}-(R_{\theta}(\tau_{i})-R_{\theta}(\tau_{j}))\big)\right)\right]+\lambda\,\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)}\big[r_{\theta}(s,a)^{2}\big],

(4)

with:

\displaystyle w_{ij}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{ij}\cdot(c_{i}\cdot c_{j}),

(5)

\displaystyle m_{ij}

\displaystyle=m_{0}+\alpha\,\Delta_{ij}.

(6)

The weighting term
w_{ij}
increases the contribution of pairs with larger score differences and higher confidence, while the margin
m_{ij}
enforces stronger separation for trajectories with greater outcome differences. The regularization term
\lambda
controls reward magnitude.

Optimization is performed using Adam with gradient clipping and early stopping. The learned reward can be interpreted as a continuous proxy for trajectory-level clinical desirability.

Reward Model Architecture

The reward function is parameterized by a neural network over state–action pairs. Discretized IV fluid and vasopressor actions are represented using separate learnable embeddings (5 bins each, 8-dimensional)
\mathbf{e}^{\text{IV}}\in\mathbb{R}^{8},\quad\mathbf{e}^{\text{vaso}}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}.
These embeddings are concatenated with the state vector to form the input:
\mathbf{x}_{t}=[s_{t},\mathbf{e}^{\text{IV}}_{t},\mathbf{e}^{\text{vaso}}_{t}]
.

The combined representation is passed through a two-layer multilayer perceptron (hidden size 128) with ReLU activation and dropout (
p=0.2
), producing a scalar reward at each time step.

Finally, the learned per-step rewards are post-processed to improve numerical stability and suitability for downstream policy learning.

Reward Normalization

To improve stability, per-step rewards are post-processed by clipping at the 1st and 99th percentiles, followed by scaling using a hyperbolic tangent transformation:

r^{\text{final}}_{t}=\tanh\left(\frac{\tilde{r}_{t}}{c}\right)
,
which bounds rewards to
[-1,1]
while preserving relative ordering.

3.6
Policy Learning (Offline RL)

We next describe the construction of the offline RL environment, including cohort selection, state representation, and action space definition.

Patient trajectories were randomly split into training (80%) and testing (20%) sets. To isolate the effect of reward formulation, all policies were trained using the same reinforcement learning algorithm (Dueling Double DQN with Conservative Q-Learning, D3QN-CQL) with identical architecture and hyperparameters. This controlled setup ensures that differences in performance are attributable to the reward function rather than training dynamics.

The architecture consists of two fully connected layers (size 128) with Leaky ReLU activation. Models were trained using Adam with learning rate
10^{-3}
, discount factor
\gamma=0.99
, batch size 256, and CQL regularization parameter
\alpha=0.5
, following established implementations in prior clinical RL work
[
24
,
35
,
25
]
.

3.7
Baselines

We compare the proposed Clinical Narrative-informed Preference Reward (CN-PR) against several commonly used reward formulations in RL-based dynamic treatment regimes. These baselines represent standard approaches in the literature and span both outcome-based and handcrafted physiological reward designs.

Terminal Mortality Reward

A widely used baseline
[
16
,
25
,
20
,
29
,
15
]
defines reward solely based on the terminal survival outcome. Specifically, rewards are zero at all intermediate time steps, with a terminal reward of
+R
assigned to survivors and
-R
assigned to non-survivors:

r_{t}=\begin{cases}0,&t<T\\
+R,&t=T\text{ and patient survives}\\
-R,&t=T\text{ and patient dies}\end{cases}

(7)

where
R
is a positive constant. This formulation directly optimizes survival but provides no intermediate feedback, leading to sparse rewards and challenging credit assignment.

SOFA-Lactate Reward (SOFA-Lac

To incorporate intermediate clinical signals, we consider a reward based on both organ failure severity and metabolic dysfunction. Let
\text{SOFA}_{t}
denote the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score and
v_{t}
denote the lactate level at time
t
. Following prior work
[
24
,
35
,
40
,
28
]
, the reward integrates both absolute levels and temporal changes:

\displaystyle r_{t}=

\displaystyle c_{0}\cdot\tanh\!\left(\sum_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\text{SOFA}_{t,k}>0}\right)+c_{1}\cdot(\text{SOFA}_{t+1}-\text{SOFA}_{t})

(8)

\displaystyle+c_{2}\cdot\tanh(v_{t+1}-v_{t})+\mathbf{1}_{t=T}\cdot r_{\text{outcome}}.

where
c_{0},c_{1},c_{2}
are fixed coefficients,
\text{SOFA}_{t,k}
denotes organ-specific SOFA components, and
r_{\text{outcome}}
is a terminal reward reflecting survival. This formulation penalizes both the presence and worsening of organ dysfunction while accounting for metabolic instability via lactate dynamics.

NEWS2-Based Reward

We also evaluate a reward based on the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2), which aggregates vital sign abnormalities into a composite clinical risk score
[
20
,
11
]
. To ensure comparability across patients, NEWS2 is normalized to the range
[0,1]
, representing increasing mortality risk. The reward is defined as:

r_{t}=-\text{NEWS2}_{t}+\mathbf{1}_{t=T}\cdot r_{\text{outcome}},

(9)

where
r_{\text{outcome}}
encodes survival at the terminal step. This formulation provides a dense proxy for physiological deterioration while maintaining alignment with mortality. A full specification of the NEWS2 rewards function is provided in the Supplementary Materials Section 1.4.

Random Policy

As a sanity check, we include a random policy baseline that selects treatment actions uniformly at random from the discrete action space:

\pi_{r}(a)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}|},
where
|\mathcal{A}|
denotes the total number of possible actions.

This baseline does not rely on any learned reward structure and serves as a lower bound for policy performance. It also helps verify that observed associations between policy recommendations and outcomes are not driven by generic action variability.

Together, these baselines capture the spectrum of reward design strategies in prior work, ranging from sparse outcome-based signals to manually engineered physiological rewards. This enables a comprehensive evaluation of whether CN-PR provides improved clinical alignment and robustness relative to conventional approaches by capturing a richer and more clinically meaningful notion of patient recovery than traditional reward formulations.

3.8
Policy Evaluation

Finally, we evaluate learned policies using a combination of outcome-based analyses and behavioral characterization. In addition to assessing associations with clinical outcomes, we examine the structure of learned treatment strategies and the clinical factors driving decision-making. This multi-faceted evaluation provides a more comprehensive assessment of whether learned policies are both clinically effective and behaviorally plausible.

Regression-Based Evaluation

We assess the relationship between policy–clinician disagreement and clinical outcomes using multivariable regression.

For each trajectory, we compare the actions recommended by policy
\pi
with those taken by clinicians at each time step. Let
a_{t}^{\pi}
denote the policy action and
a_{t}^{\text{clin}}
the observed clinician action, where:

a_{t}=(a^{\text{IV}}_{t},a^{\text{vaso}}_{t}).

We compute per-step absolute differences:

\displaystyle d^{\text{IV}}_{t}

\displaystyle=|a^{\text{IV},\pi}_{t}-a^{\text{IV},\text{clin}}_{t}|,

(10)

\displaystyle d^{\text{vaso}}_{t}

\displaystyle=|a^{\text{vaso},\pi}_{t}-a^{\text{vaso},\text{clin}}_{t}|,

(11)

and define the joint Euclidean distance:

d^{\text{joint}}_{t}=\sqrt{(d^{\text{IV}}_{t})^{2}+(d^{\text{vaso}}_{t})^{2}}.

(12)

Distances are aggregated at the trajectory level:

\bar{d}^{\text{joint}}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}d^{\text{joint}}_{t},

(13)

and standardized (z-scored) prior to regression.

For each policy
\pi
, we fit:

Y_{i}=\beta_{0}+\beta_{1}\bar{d}^{\text{joint}}_{i}+\mathbf{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{X}_{i}+\epsilon_{i},

(14)

where
Y_{i}
denotes the outcome for patient
i
,
\bar{d}^{\text{joint}}_{i}
is the average joint distance, and
\mathbf{X}_{i}
represents baseline covariates.

We evaluate policies across complementary outcome measures that capture clinically meaningful dimensions of recovery beyond mortality alone. Collectively, these outcomes provide a multidimensional assessment of treatment quality—encompassing early physiologic recovery, disease trajectory, functional outcomes, and intervention intensity—whereas mortality alone is a coarse endpoint that does not distinguish between heterogeneous recovery trajectories. The evaluated outcomes are:

•

Hospital mortality:
a binary indicator of in-hospital death (1 = death, 0 = survival).

•

Organ support–free days (OSFD-7):
days alive and free from vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, and renal replacement therapy within the first 7 days, reflecting early physiologic recovery.

•

Time to shock resolution:
time from sepsis onset to sustained hemodynamic stability without vasopressor support.

•

Discharge disposition score:
a proxy for functional recovery based on discharge status.

•

Treatment burden:
mean IV fluid and vasopressor administration per time step (reflecting treatment intensity independent of trajectory length).

Full definitions are provided in the Supplementary Materials Section 1.2.

Covariates include age, baseline SOFA score, Elixhauser comorbidity index, lactate, shock index, and mechanical ventilation status. Continuous outcomes are modeled using linear regression with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC3) standard errors, while binary outcomes are modeled using logistic regression.

The coefficient
\beta_{1}
quantifies the association between deviation from the policy and clinical outcomes. Greater agreement with a policy is associated with improved trajectories when distance is negatively associated with favorable outcomes (e.g., OSFD) or positively associated with adverse outcomes (e.g., treatment burden). All analyses are performed at the patient level.

The use of this regression-based framework is motivated by our objective of comparing
reward formulations
rather than benchmarking RL algorithms. Standard off-policy evaluation (OPE) estimates policy value under a fixed reward; however, here each policy is trained under a different reward definition, making direct comparison ill-posed. As our objective is cross-reward comparison under shared clinical outcomes, we evaluate whether policies align with
clinically meaningful outcomes
independent of the training objective. This framework provides a unified and interpretable assessment while avoiding reliance on the learned reward itself, thereby mitigating reward circularity. This approach is consistent with prior work comparing reward formulations in healthcare RL
[
19
,
18
]
. It is important to note however, that these analyses quantify associations rather than causal effects; results should be interpreted as evidence of clinical alignment rather than policy optimality.

Action Distribution Analysis

To characterize the treatment strategies learned by each policy, we analyze the empirical distribution of actions across the joint IV fluid–vasopressor space. For each policy, we compute the frequency of each discretized action pair and visualize these distributions using two-dimensional heatmaps.

To assess whether policies adapt appropriately to patient severity, action distributions are stratified by baseline SOFA score into low- and high-severity groups. This enables evaluation of whether the policy exhibits clinically meaningful treatment escalation or de-escalation patterns across severity strata, rather than applying a uniform strategy.

Feature Importance Analysis

To further understand the clinical factors driving treatment decisions, we perform feature importance analysis using supervised models that approximate each policy. For each intervention (IV fluids and vasopressors), we train separate random forest classifiers to predict the discrete action selected at each time step under either the clinician policy or the CN-PR policy.

Feature importance is quantified using permutation importance, which measures the change in predictive performance when individual features are randomly permuted. This provides a model-agnostic estimate of the relative importance of each variable in determining treatment decisions.

3.9
External Validation

To assess the generalizability of the proposed framework, we conducted external validation using the AmsterdamUMCdb dataset, an open-access critical care database containing detailed clinical information on ICU patients admitted to Amsterdam University Medical Center between 2003 and 2016
[
31
]
.

Cohort Extraction

Using the same inclusion criteria as in the primary MIMIC-IV analysis, we extracted a cohort of 4,402 patients meeting Sepsis-3 criteria. Cohort construction, preprocessing steps, and inclusion criteria were aligned with the primary dataset to ensure comparability; full cohort details are provided in Supplementary Table S3 .

Feature Harmonization and Reduced Model

Compared to MIMIC-IV, AmsterdamUMCdb does not include several variables used in the primary analysis, specifically prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), CO
2
, and the Elixhauser comorbidity score. To enable fair cross-dataset comparison and avoid introducing bias through imputation of systematically missing variables, we adopted a reduced-feature strategy.

A secondary reward model was trained using only the subset of features shared between both datasets. This ensures that both reward learning and downstream policy evaluation are conducted within a consistent feature space, isolating dataset transfer effects from differences in variable availability.

Reward Transfer and Evaluation

The reward function learned on MIMIC-IV was directly applied to the AmsterdamUMCdb cohort without further fine-tuning. To evaluate reward generalization, we computed Spearman rank correlations between trajectory-level cumulative rewards and multiple clinically relevant outcomes, including hospital mortality, organ support–free days (OSFD-7), time to shock resolution, and treatment burden.

This analysis assesses whether the learned reward preserves meaningful ordering of patient trajectories in an external dataset, independent of downstream policy learning.

Policy Learning and Evaluation

We further evaluated downstream policy performance by retraining reinforcement learning policies on AmsterdamUMCdb using the transferred CN-PR reward, following the same offline RL setup as in the primary analysis. Baseline reward formulations (terminal mortality, SOFA-Lac, and NEWS2-based rewards) were similarly re-trained for comparison.

Policies were evaluated using the same regression-based framework described in Section
3.8
, quantifying the association between policy–clinician disagreement and clinical outcomes. This enables assessment of whether policies derived from the transferred reward maintain clinically meaningful behavior in an external cohort.

Two modifications were made to accommodate dataset-specific limitations. First, discharge disposition was not available in AmsterdamUMCdb; therefore, discharge score was excluded from external validation analyses. Second, the Elixhauser comorbidity index was not included as a covariate in regression models due to its absence in the dataset.

4.1
Validation of the Learned Reward Function

4.1.1
Distribution of Narrative-Derived TQS

Figure
2
illustrates the distribution of TQS derived from clinical narratives. The majority of trajectories are assigned scores of 4–5, corresponding to survival with varying degrees of recovery, while lower scores capture mortality and severe morbidity. The presence of intermediate scores highlights that narrative-derived labeling provides a graded and clinically nuanced assessment of trajectory quality beyond coarse outcome endpoints.

In addition, the associated confidence values—reflecting the relevance of each case to sepsis-related management—are relatively consistent across score categories. This indicates that the scoring process is appropriately driven by the extent to which the clinical course meaningfully informs treatment evaluation rather than solely by the clarity of the ultimate outcome.

Although the cohort exhibits a right-skewed distribution toward favorable outcomes, preference learning relies on relative comparisons rather than class balance. Furthermore, our structured preference objective explicitly emphasizes larger outcome differences through score-gap weighting and margin-based separation, thereby mitigating the impact of imbalance by prioritizing clinically meaningful distinctions between trajectories.

Across outcomes, the CN-PR policy achieves performance comparable to outcome-based baselines for mortality, while exhibiting steeper gradients and larger effect sizes for recovery-related metrics and treatment burden (Figure

In contrast, for recovery-related outcomes, the CN-PR policy exhibits the largest effect sizes. It shows the strongest associations for organ support–free days (
\beta=-0.84
) and time to shock resolution (
\beta=7.79
), indicating that agreement with the policy is more tightly coupled with improved recovery trajectories compared to baseline reward formulations. Notably, these improvements are not solely attributable to the use of dense reward signals. While CN-PR provides temporally informative supervision, it consistently outperforms the NEWS2-based reward, which is also dense and derived from physiological measurements. This indicates that the observed gains arise from the quality and clinical relevance of the learned reward signal, rather than reward density alone.

The most pronounced differences are observed for treatment burden. Deviations from the CN-PR policy correspond to substantially larger increases in both vasopressor and IV fluid administration, reflected in steeper regression slopes relative to baseline policies. This suggests that the CN-PR policy achieves favorable outcomes with lower intervention intensity, whereas outcome-based rewards exhibit flatter relationships, consistent with comparatively more aggressive treatment strategies.

These patterns are preserved in external validation on the AmsterdamUMCdb cohort. The CN-PR policy maintains comparable performance for mortality while retaining stronger associations with recovery-related outcomes and treatment burden (Supplementary Table S6). Although absolute effect sizes are attenuated, the relative ordering of policies remains consistent, supporting the robustness and generalizability of the learned reward.

By contrast, the random policy shows weak or inconsistent associations across all outcomes in both datasets, confirming that the observed relationships are not driven by generic action variability. Although observational, these findings indicate that alignment with the CN-PR policy is associated with more favorable and treatment-efficient clinical trajectories.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We introduced Clinical Narrative-informed Preference Rewards (CN-PR), a framework for learning reward functions from clinical narratives. By leveraging discharge summaries as implicit evaluations of patient trajectories, CN-PR reframes reward specification as a problem of extracting preference signals from routinely collected data rather than manually defining objectives.

A central finding is that policies trained with CN-PR achieve performance comparable to outcome-based baselines on mortality while exhibiting stronger and more consistent associations with recovery-oriented metrics and treatment burden. Comparisons with other dense reward formulations further indicate that these improvements are not attributable to reward density alone, but instead reflect the ability of narrative-derived supervision to capture clinically meaningful structure in patient trajectories.

These results highlight a key limitation of commonly used reward formulations: they rely on a narrow set of predefined endpoints and therefore capture only a partial view of clinical success. In contrast, CN-PR learns from trajectory-level evaluations implicitly encoded in clinical narratives, which reflect broader aspects of care such as treatment response, reversibility, and stability. This enables the learned reward to represent a more expressive notion of trajectory quality without requiring explicit specification of intermediate objectives.

A key component of our approach is the use of narrative-derived signals as a scalable proxy for trajectory-level preferences. Unlike conventional preference learning methods that depend on curated annotations, CN-PR leverages supervision that is already embedded in clinical workflows. At the same time, not all narratives are equally informative. By incorporating a confidence signal that reflects the relevance of each summary to the target decision-making task, the framework accounts for variability in supervision quality and reduces the impact of noisy or less informative cases.

Our findings reinforce the importance of reward specification as a primary determinant of policy behavior in offline RL for healthcare. Rather than designing rewards through heuristic combinations of clinical variables, CN-PR enables reward learning directly from data sources that encode high-level clinical reasoning. This perspective suggests a broader opportunity to incorporate multimodal signals into RL frameworks, where unstructured data provide complementary supervision to structured measurements.

CN-PR is compatible with clinician-in-the-loop decision support systems
[
32
,
26
]
. Because policy inputs rely on routinely collected structured data, integration into existing clinical workflows is feasible, while narrative-derived reward learning remains an offline process. This separation allows reward functions to reflect clinician reasoning without requiring real-time access to unstructured text during deployment. Prospective validation, including silent deployment and simulation-based studies, will be important to assess safety, usability, and clinical impact
[
37
,
41
]
.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The learned reward depends on LLM-based interpretation of clinical narratives and may reflect biases in documentation or model inference. Variability in note quality can affect the reliability of extracted signals, although our confidence-weighting mechanism partially mitigates this issue. In addition, our evaluation is observational and does not establish causal effects, leaving the possibility of residual confounding despite adjustment for key covariates
[
17
,
37
]
. Finally, discharge summaries provide retrospective assessments and may not fully capture the temporal evolution of decision-making.

Future work could extend this approach by incorporating longitudinal clinical notes to learn time-varying reward signals, as well as by explicitly modeling uncertainty in narrative-derived supervision. Integrating preference-based reward learning with causal inference techniques may further improve robustness and support safer policy evaluation.

Beyond critical care, the CN-PR framework is applicable to domains where narrative documentation encodes trajectory-level evaluation. In oncology, clinical notes describing treatment response, disease progression, and tolerability provide natural signals for learning reward functions that balance efficacy and toxicity
[
14
,
36
]
. In chronic disease management (e.g., diabetes, heart failure), longitudinal documentation captures control, complications, and adherence over extended time horizons
[
37
,
30
,
12
,
6
]
. In psychiatry, where outcomes are inherently subjective, narrative assessments of symptom progression and functional status may serve as a primary source of preference supervision
[
2
,
23
]
. These settings represent promising directions for extending narrative-informed reward learning beyond acute care.

In summary, CN-PR provides a scalable approach to reward learning by leveraging narrative-derived supervision as a proxy for trajectory-level preferences and explicitly accounting for its reliability. This enables the learning of more expressive and clinically aligned reward functions, supporting improved policy learning for dynamic treatment regimes.

Ethical Statement

In line with prior work on reinforcement learning–driven clinical decision support systems (CDSSs) for conditions such as sepsis
[
24
]
, our proposed framework adopts a human-in-the-loop approach. The system is designed to assist, rather than replace, the role of clinicians by offering data-driven recommendations derived from past clinical trajectories. Importantly, the ultimate responsibility for treatment decisions remains with the clinician, preserving professional autonomy and ensuring that medical expertise governs final care plans. Our framework enables the integration of machine intelligence with clinical judgment, leveraging the strengths of both to improve patient outcomes without undermining ethical standards.

Additionally, our models were developed and validated solely on de-identified, publicly available retrospective data, with no involvement of real-time clinical interventions or patient contact. As such, the work poses no direct risk to patients and aligns with ethical research practices, minimizing concerns regarding safety, accountability, or consent in the current study phase.

1.2
Outcome Metric Definitions

All outcome metrics were computed using a unified discretization of patient trajectories into fixed 4-hour intervals, consistent with the reinforcement learning time step. For each ICU stay, a complete time grid was constructed from ICU admission (
t=0
) up to 28 days.

At each time step
t
, patient status was defined based on (i) survival status, (ii) organ support usage, and (iii) hemodynamic measurements.

1.2.1
Organ Support–Free Days (OSFD)

Organ support–free days quantify the number of days during which a patient is both alive and free from major organ support therapies.

We define organ support at time
t
as the presence of any of the following:

•

vasopressor administration,

•

mechanical ventilation, or

•

renal replacement therapy (including CRRT).

At each time step
t
, we define:

\text{SupportFreeAlive}_{t}=\mathbf{1}\big(\text{alive}_{t}=1\;\land\;\text{no support}_{t}\big),

(15)

where
\mathbf{1}(\cdot)
denotes the indicator function.

The OSFD metric over a given time horizon is computed as:

\text{OSFD}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{SupportFreeAlive}_{t},

(16)

where each day corresponds to six 4-hour intervals.

We report OSFD-7, defined over the first 7 days (168 hours) following ICU admission.

1.2.2
Time to Shock Resolution (TSR)

Shock at time
t
is defined as:

\text{Shock}_{t}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if vasopressor support is present}\\
1,&\text{if }\text{MAP}_{t}<65\text{ mmHg}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(17)

where
\text{MAP}_{t}
denotes the mean arterial pressure at time
t
.

Shock-free status is defined as:

\text{ShockFree}_{t}=\mathbf{1}(\text{Shock}_{t}=0).

(18)

Time to shock resolution (TSR) measures the time required to achieve sustained hemodynamic stability. Sustained resolution is defined as remaining shock-free for 12 consecutive hours:

n=\frac{12\ \text{hours}}{4\ \text{hours per interval}}=3\ \text{intervals}.

(19)

TSR is defined as the earliest time step
t
such that:

\text{TSR}=\min\left\{t\;:\;\sum_{k=t}^{t+n-1}\text{ShockFree}_{k}=n\right\}.

(20)

TSR is reported in hours:

\text{TSR (hours)}=\frac{\text{offset}_{t}}{60},

(21)

where
\text{offset}_{t}
denotes minutes since ICU admission.

Special cases:

•

TSR is undefined for patients who never experience shock.

•

If shock is not resolved within 72 hours, TSR is capped at 72 hours.

1.2.3
Treatment Burden

Treatment burden quantifies the intensity of administered therapies over a patient trajectory.
To account for variation in trajectory length, we define burden as the mean treatment level per time step.

The IV fluid burden is defined as:

\text{IV Fluids Burden}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}a^{\text{IV}}_{t},

(22)

and the vasopressor burden is defined as:

\text{Vasopressor Burden}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}a^{\text{vaso}}_{t},

(23)

where
a^{\text{IV}}_{t}
and
a^{\text{vaso}}_{t}
denote the discretized treatment levels of intravenous fluids and vasopressors at time step
t
, and
T
is the number of time steps in the trajectory.

These normalized metrics capture treatment intensity independent of trajectory duration, enabling fair comparison across patients with differing lengths of stay. Separating IV fluids and vasopressors further distinguishes volume resuscitation from vasopressor-driven hemodynamic support.

1.2.4
Discharge Disposition Score

Discharge disposition was used as a proxy for functional recovery and longer-term prognosis beyond survival. Each patient was assigned a discrete score based on their hospital discharge destination, reflecting increasing levels of independence and recovery.

Formally, the discharge disposition score is defined as:

\text{Discharge Score}\in\{0,\dots,7\},

(24)

with higher values indicating more favorable outcomes.

Scores were assigned according to the following mapping:

•

7:
Discharge to home

•

6:
Discharge to home with healthcare services

•

5:
Discharge to rehabilitation facility

•

4:
Discharge to assisted living

•

3:
Discharge to skilled nursing facility

•

2:
Discharge to long-term acute care or healthcare facility

•

1:
Transfer to acute hospital

•

0:
Hospice or in-hospital death

Discharge categories that do not clearly map to a recovery trajectory, including
other facility
,
against medical advice
, and
psychiatric facility
, were excluded from this metric and treated as missing values.

This ordering reflects a clinically motivated hierarchy of recovery, where discharge to home represents near-complete functional recovery, while lower scores correspond to increasing levels of dependency and ongoing care needs. Hospice and death are grouped as the lowest category, as both represent trajectories with minimal likelihood of meaningful recovery.

By incorporating discharge disposition into evaluation, this metric captures distinctions in recovery quality that are not reflected by mortality alone, enabling a more nuanced assessment of treatment effectiveness and longer-term patient outcomes.

These metrics capture:

•

Survival and recovery (OSFD)

•

Hemodynamic stabilization (TSR)

•

Treatment intensity (burden)

•

Functional recovery (disposition)

1.4
NEWS2-Based Reward Function

We implemented a reward function based on the National Early Warning Score 2 (NEWS2). The NEWS2 score is a composite clinical severity metric derived from routinely measured physiological variables.

At each time step
t
, the NEWS2 score is computed from the
next-state
physiological variables:

\text{NEWS2}_{t+1}=s_{\text{RR}}+s_{\text{SpO}_{2}}+s_{\text{O}_{2}}+s_{\text{BP}}+s_{\text{HR}}+s_{\text{Temp}}+s_{\text{CNS}},

where each component score corresponds to discretized clinical measurements:

•

Respiratory rate (RR)

•

Oxygen saturation (SpO
2
)

•

Supplemental oxygen requirement (mechanical ventilation)

•

Systolic blood pressure (BP)

•

Heart rate (HR)

•

Temperature (Temp)

•

Level of consciousness (CNS), approximated using
GCS<15

Each component is assigned a score according to standard NEWS2 thresholds, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 (normal) to a theoretical maximum of 20 (severe physiological derangement).

To ensure comparability across time steps, the NEWS2 score is normalized:

\widetilde{\text{NEWS2}}_{t+1}=\frac{\text{NEWS2}_{t+1}}{20}.

The reward at each time step is then defined as the negative normalized NEWS2 score:

r_{t}=-\widetilde{\text{NEWS2}}_{t+1}.

This formulation encourages transitions toward physiologically stable states by assigning higher (less negative) rewards to lower NEWS2 scores.

At terminal time steps, the reward is overridden to reflect final outcomes:

r_{T}=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{if in-hospital mortality occurs},\\
0,&\text{if the patient survives}.\end{cases}

This terminal adjustment ensures that long-term outcomes are incorporated, while intermediate rewards capture short-term physiological stability.

Overall, the NEWS2-based reward combines dense intermediate feedback with a sparse terminal signal, providing a structured yet heuristic objective for policy learning.
