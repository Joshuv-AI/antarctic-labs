PROJECT GOALS AND OVERVIEW:
 Engineered protein compartmentalization systems hold significant promise to enhance
reaction efficiencies through co-localization, concentration, and sequestration of biosynthetic
pathways. As such, they have the potential to enable the bioproduction of next generation
bioproducts and biomaterials in genetically engineered microbes in support of DOE’s mission to
build a strong bioeconomy.1 Among systems of particular interest are protein nanocompartment
systems called encapsulins that are natively produced by a variety of bacteria including those with
a high potential for bioproduction.2 This ECRP project is focused on understanding how encapsulins
can be used to enhance the biosynthesis of next-generation biomaterials in Rhodococcus species.
Specifically, we seek: (1) to probe the mechanistic basis for how these compartments are regulated,
biosynthesized, and maintained, and (2) to engineer these systems to achieve new biosynthetic
functions (e.g., alkene, inorganic nanoparticle biosynthesis). We anticipate that this work will
establish encapsulin compartmentalization systems as a means of improving yields and enabling
biosynthetic routes toward new biomaterials, thus advancing the U.S. bioeconomy.

PROJECT RESULTS:
 This year, we have made progress toward several lines of work. Results from this year are
summarized below organized by project topic:
RB-TnSeq method development for Rhodococcus opacus.
 One of the goals of our ECRP project is to conduct a forward genetic screen to identify factors
that affect encapsulin production or formation. Toward this goal, we have been developing
transposon mutagenesis tools for R. opacus, which have broad utility for high-throughput genomic
screening in this bioproducer. In our previous report, we described how we constructed non-
barcoded pTNR2 and barcoded pTNR3 (Figure 1A), modified versions of a previously reported
Rhodococcus specific transposon tool pTNR3, which generated random insertions across the
genome of R. opacus PD630. While both plasmids can be used for TnSeq, the 20-basepair random
barcodes in pTNR3 enable random barcode transposon-site sequencing (RB-TnSeq)4 during
screening. With RB-TnSeq, TnSeq only needs to be performed once for a mutant library, and barcode
sequencing (BarSeq), which requires only a simple PCR, can be used for all subsequent library
screens. Barseq thus eases downstream processing and analysis, facilitating high-throughput
screening for both this ECRP and future screens with Rhodococcus.

 2

 Since our prior report, we have made additional improvements to the transposon tool. We
found that adding a strong synthetic constitutive promoter from Streptomyces lividans TK245 in front
of the kanamycin-resistance gene improved transposition efficiency approximately 5 times (Figure
1A, pTNR4). Barcodes placed directly adjacent to the inverted repeat (IR) regions caused a drop in
transposition efficiency in R. opacus. We therefore moved the barcodes 56 bp farther away from the
IR region and found that improves the transposition efficiency nearly to a level similar to non-
barcoded pTNR4 (Figure 1B).

 We performed an initial scale-up of our optimized barcoded pTNR (Figure 1C, pTNR5) in
Clean Genome LowMut ΔrecA E. coli (Scarab Genomics, USA)6, a strain we previously reported that
minimizes recombination and mutations in the pTNR plasmid. Using BarSeq4, we determined that
the scale-up generated 450,872 unique barcodes, and 71% of the reads were for barcodes that were
seen just once or twice. While the barcodes were evenly distributed, the total number of unique
barcodes was not in significant excess over the desired number of transposon mutants in the final
library. This excess is needed to ensure that the majority of the transposon mutants are marked with
a unique DNA barcode. We have initiated a collaboration with Adam Deutschbauer at LBNL to
leverage their expertise on RB-TnSeq to improve our barcode diversity and RB-TnSeq methods.

 3

 While RB-TnSeq has been developed for model Gram-negative bacteria such as E. coli,
Phaeobacter inhibens, Pseudomonas stutzeri, Shewanella amazonensis, and Shewanella
oneidensis,7 its use in Gram-positive bacteria has been challenging and thus underexplored.
Particularly, there is a lack of robust, high-throughput genomic tools for Rhodococcus species and
other emerging Gram-positive bioproduction bacteria. We will publish our detailed methods
describing our optimized protocol for RB-TnSeq in R. opacus to share with the broader scientific
community to accelerate use of these genetic tools in Gram-positive bioproducers.
A forward genetic screen to identify factors relevant for encapsulin biosynthesis.
 Because RB-TnSeq will require time to optimize, we decided to initiate our forward genetic
screen for encapsulin related factors using pTNR4, our optimized non-barcoded transposon tool.

(found on the C-terminus of the native DypB cargo protein) together with Encapsulin from the native
PdypB promoter. We obtained two transposon libraries, yielding approximately 10,000 colonies in the
first round and 20,000 in the second.

 While we are increasing insertion coverage, we have moved forward with Library 2 to conduct
a pilot forward genetic screen. We have previously identified encapsulin inducing condition
(stationary phase) and repressing condition (growth in low nitrogen) for R. opacus (Figure 3A). We
found that these conditions resulted in a slight but distinguishable shift in a FACS fluorescence

 5

distribution (Figure 3B). Therefore, for our forward genetic screen, we decided to use FACS to enrich
for (1) the top 1% of the population by mCherry fluorescence when the library was grown under
encapsulin repressing condition (enriches encapsulin repressors or disruptors), and (2) the bottom
1% by mCherry fluorescence when the library was grown under encapsulin inducing condition
(enriches encapsulin activators or enhancers). Following single event sorting into individual wells in
96-well plates, we further screened for growth in either (1) encapsulin repressing or (2) inducing
conditions. From this screen, we identified mutants with significantly higher or lower mCherry
fluorescence/OD600 (at least one standard deviation) compared to the mean fluorescence/OD600 of
the wells. Selected mutants were validated by measuring fluorescence/OD600 of triplicate cultures
grown alongside the Ro344 parent strain. Mutants showing a statistically significantly difference in
mCherry fluorescence/OD600 relative to the parent strain were chosen as candidate mutants for
sequencing. The entire workflow for the forward genetic screen is outlined in Figure 4.

 In our pilot, we have thus far screened 87 mutants grown under encapsulin repressing
conditions and 124 mutants grown under encapsulin inducing conditions after FACS enrichment.
From these, 10 and 15 mutants, respectively, were selected for triplicate testing, ultimately yielding
10 and 7 candidate mutants that were statistically significantly different from the parent strain in
mCherry fluorescence/OD600 (Figure 5). From the current 17 candidate mutants, we thus far have
determined the transposon insertion sites of 4 mutants (1_A10, 1_B7, 1_D12, 1_H9) that exhibited
higher fluorescence under nitrogen limitation using a previously described plasmid rescue
procedure3. We anticipate that these mutants would be disrupting genes related to encapsulin
repression or degradation or involved in the protein degradation response under nitrogen limitation.
Additional sequencing of all mutants is underway.
 The transposon insertion in mutant 1_H9 disrupted the gene K2Z90_RS15925 in the main
chromosome of R. opacus. In mutant 1_B7, the insertion is found about 30 base pairs upstream from
K2Z90_RS15925, which could be the putative promoter for the gene. K2Z90_RS15925 encodes for a
putative SRPBCC (START/RHO_alpha_C/PITP/Bet_v1/CoxG/CalC) family protein, a ligand-binding
domain superfamily protein (Table 3). There is an example of a SRPBCC protein found in peanut
(Arachis duranensis) that is associated with stress response and drought tolerance in the plant8.

 6

Therefore, we hypothesize that K2Z90_RS15925 in R. opacus may also be involved in the stress
response associated with low-nitrogen growth under encapsulin repressing conditions.
 For mutants 1_A10 and 1_D12, the transposon insertions are found to disrupt the genes,
K2Z90_RS41295 and K2Z90_RS41240 respectively, in one of the endogenous plasmids in R. opacus
(pRoPD630_2, RefSeq: NZ_CP080956). BLASTP searches show only hypothetical proteins for
K2Z90_RS41295. Most BLASTP matches for K2Z90_RS41240 are also for hypothetical proteins, but
there are two annotated proteins with a slight similarity: a Rearrangement hotspot (RHS) repeat-
associated core domain-containing protein, and a SpvB/TcaC N-terminal domain-containing protein
(Table 4). RHS repeat-associated core domain-containing proteins from Salmonella bongori and
Advenella mimigardefordensis are type VI secretion system (T6SS) effectors that form large shell-
like structures that potentially shield an intracellular toxin9. One example of a SpvB protein in
Salmonella Typhimurium interacts with Clathrin, a protein that plays a role in vesicle formation,
which is crucial for S. Typhimurium’s virulence10. The role of these proteins in potential encapsulin
disruption are currently unclear and require further investigation.

Exploration of McdB localization factors to improve encapsulin maintenance in Rhodococcus.
 In addition to the forward genetic screen, we sought to explore genes that affect encapsulin
production in a more targeted approach. Recently, a two-protein system, Maintenance of
carboxysome distribution A and B (McdA and McdB), was found to provide synthetic spatial control
of engineered encapsulins in E. coli to improve encapsulin production11. McdAB were originally
found in carboxysomes, organelle-like protein cages that sequester carbon fixation machinery in
cyanobacteria and proteobacteria, and are required for positioning carboxysomes within the cell and
partitioning them for cell division12. The presence of McdAB proteins also prevents the clustering and
self-aggregation of carboxysomes, allowing them to function properly in the cell13. Since McdAB can
be programmed to spatially control encapsulins, we hypothesize that R. opacus may contain
McdAB-like proteins that position encapsulins and prevent their self-aggregation, ensuring proper
encapsulin production. Therefore, we sought to find McdAB-like genes in R. opacus.
 Using BLASTP, we did not find any proteins that are McdB-like in R. opacus. However, there
are several putative proteins in R. opacus with significant identity to the McdA protein from the
cyanobacterium Synechococcus elongatus PCC 7942 (Table 5). K2Z90_RS14440 and
K2Z90_RS23640 are the top two results from the BLASTP searches and are both on the main
chromosome of R. opacus (highlighted in Table 5, 29.44% and 33.75% identity to McdA,
respectively). Therefore, they are the most likely candidates for having an McdA-like function. The
protein encoded by K2Z90_RS14440 is annotated to be a sporulation initiation inhibitor protein that
is found to be spatially regulated and interacts with DNA, which is similar to McdA’s function of
association with the nucleoid and spatial regulation of carboxysomes. K2Z90_RS23640 encodes for
an uncharacterized protein, but it is located (1) downstream to a gene encoding segregation and
condensation protein A and B (ScpA and ScpB) that are required for chromosome segregation and
condensation at high temperatures14, and (2) upstream to xerD, site-specific tyrosine recombinase
that binds DNA during replication15 (Figure 6A). Therefore, we hypothesize that K2Z90_RS23640 may
encode a protein that works in conjunction with ScpA, ScpB, and XerD to bind DNA and promote
localization of encapsulins. We decided to generate deletion and overexpression strains of
K2Z90_RS14440 and K2Z90_RS23640 in R. opacus to assess how these genes affect encapsulin
localization, segregation, and production.

 Our first attempt at generating deletion strains of K2Z90_RS14440 and K2Z90_RS23640
resulted in mixed populations of wild-type and deletion strains, seen by both whole-genome
sequencing (data not shown) and PCR (Figure 6). We are currently re-streaking the mixed
populations to obtain pure deletion strains. For our next steps, we will integrate the genes back into
the deletion strains at a neutral integration site5, placing the genes under the control of the
arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter to generate overexpression strains.

 In the long term, we will assess how these McdA genes affect encapsulin formation and
localization using fluorescence microscopy. We will also test how these genes (along with the genes
we identify in the forward genetic screen) can be used to improve recombinant encapsulin
production. We have previously reported that encapsulin improved the stability of mCherry and
reduced the toxicity of OleTJE, a cytochrome P450 with decarboxylation activity. These systems will

 9

be used as test cases to determine whether the deletion or overexpression of the identified genes
improves or reduces expression of mCherry or OleTJE. Together, these data will provide a framework
for improving recombinant protein expression in Rhodococcus using a protein encapsulation
system.
