Structural basis for selective and potent degradation of IRAK4 by KT-474
Authors: Xue Fei, Anand Ramanathan, Caroline A. Daigle, Melissa Ford, Veronica Campbell, Xiaozhang Zheng, Mike Sintchak, Haoran Li, Hari Kamadurai, Richard Miller, Steven Kazmirski, Xin Huang, Matthew M. Weiss, Nello Mainolfi, Xiao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted protein degradation has emerged as a promising drug modality, with potential applications across many immuno-inflammatory diseases. KT-474 is an orally bioavailable interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinase 4 (IRAK4) heterobifunctional degrader evaluated in clinical trials for atopic dermatitis and hidradenitis suppurativa. Here we present structural, biophysical, and computational characterization of an IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 complex. Cryo-EM structure of the complex reveals a unique and non-native protein-protein interaction (PPI) surface mediated by a network of polar and apolar contacts, including key hydrophobic engagements mediated by CRBN Phe150. This complex exhibits negative cooperativity, arising from an interplay between weakly favorable PPI and conformational flexibility of the degrader. Moreover, the structure provides insight into the selective degradation profile of KT-474. Our results offer important insights into the mechanism of action of KT-474 and highlight the value of cryo-EM structures in the optimization of protein degraders.

Introduction

Targeted protein degradation is a novel therapeutic modality that has gained significant attention in recent years, providing a promising new way to treat a variety of diseases, particularly those associated with undrugged and difficult-to-target proteins, including scaffolding proteins and transcription factors1. This modality enables the removal of disease-causing proteins by leveraging existing protein elimination and recycling pathways such as the proteasomal, lysosomal, and autophagic pathways.

Heterobifunctional (HBF) degraders are small molecules that can redirect the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which is the cellular machinery responsible for protein degradation and cellular homeostasis, to degrade specific disease-causing proteins. These molecules are composed of a target-binding moiety (TBM), an E3 ligase-binding moiety (EBM), and a linker joining the two components (Fig. 1a, b). By retaining their ability to simultaneously bind to both proteins, HBF degraders can recruit an E3 ligase to a target protein-of-interest (POI), facilitate the formation of a POI:HBF:E3 ternary complex, and trigger ubiquitination and eventual degradation of the POI (Fig. 1a)2. Compared to conventional inhibitors, this modality offers key efficacy advantages due to its catalytic nature: following initial POI ubiquitination, the HBF degraders can dissociate from the complex and be available to target additional protein copies, resulting in more robust efficacy and the potential to overcome inhibitor resistance mechanisms3,4. Additional benefits of HBF degraders include opportunities for increased selectivity and durability of response. IRAK4 is a key protein in the toll-like receptor and IL-1R receptor-mediated signaling pathway that activates innate immune responses to regulate inflammation7. Upon association with the adaptor protein MyD88, IRAK4 facilitates recruitment of IRAK1 and IRAK2, which are subsequently activated through phosphorylation, leading to assembly of the myddosome complex and downstream activation of NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways8. In addition to its kinase activity, IRAK4 also impacts downstream signaling through the myddosome-stabilizing scaffolding function of an N-terminal loop of its death domain9,10. Eliminating the IRAK4 protein through targeted degradation impacts both its kinase and scaffolding functions and therefore has the potential to achieve broad anti-inflammatory effects across a variety of immuno-inflammatory diseases11,12. KT-474, discovered through structure-activity optimization of the EBM, the TBM, and the linker, is the first IRAK4 degrader to advance into clinical trials and has successfully completed Phase I studies in healthy volunteers and patients with hidradenitis suppurativa and atopic dermatitis11,13. KT-474 has been evaluated in two Phase II clinical trials in both indications (NCT06028230 and NCT06058156).

Here, we determine the ternary complex structure of IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 by cryo-EM at 3.09 Å resolution and, through extensive structural, biochemical, biophysical, and computational analysis, provide insights into the molecular mechanisms that govern the potent and selective degradation of IRAK4 by KT-474.

Results

KT-474 induces robust ternary complex formation, ubiquitination, and degradation of IRAK4

Titration of KT-474 in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) resulted in dose-dependent reduction of IRAK4, with a measured concentration required for 50% degradation (DC50) of 2.0 ± 0.3 nM and maximum degradation (Dmax) of 92 ± 4% at 24 h (Fig. 1c, d), but not with Me-KT-474. The negative control molecule Me-KT-474 (Fig. 1b) retains binding to IRAK4 (IC50 = 56.3 ± 9.1 nM) but does not interact with CRBN (IC50 > 33 µM) due to loss of a key hydrogen bond and introduction of steric clashes with backbone NH of His378 (Fig. 1c). However, KT-474 binds potently to both IRAK4 and CRBN with affinities (IC50) of 49.1 ± 8.9 nM and 52.7 ± 1.1 nM, respectively, measured using AlphaLISA for IRAK4 and fluorescence polarization (FP) for CRBN (Fig. 1c). In order to understand the underlying mechanism of degradation, we leveraged biochemical and biophysical techniques to characterize binary and ternary complex formation events. KT-474-dependent ternary complex formation (TCF) was confirmed by AlphaLISA, where IRAK4 and CRBN/DDB1 were treated with increasing amounts of KT-474 and area-under-curve (AUC) of AlphaLISA signal was measured. Indeed, we observed strong KT-474-induced ternary complex formation as indicated by robust increase in AlphaLISA signal (AUC of 2,187,350) followed by a “hook effect”14 caused by saturating doses of the compounds, but not with Me-KT-474 (Fig. 1e).

Cooperativity of a HBF degrader is defined as the ratio of its affinity to each of the individual proteins versus that of the ternary complex. An earlier work has postulated that higher cooperativity is associated with more potent and selective degraders15. However, later studies in this space have established that perhaps positive cooperativity is not necessarily the only way to achieve productive TCF and potent degradation16–19. We characterized the cooperativity of the KT-474-mediated complex by comparing the binding affinities of the ternary complex assembly to those of the binary complexes measured by FP. KT-474 binds to CRBN alone with an IC50 of 52 ± 1 nM but with a significantly weaker IC50 of 829 ± 2 nM in the presence of IRAK4, resulting in negative cooperativity (defined as α = IC50CRBN/IC50CRBN:IRAK4) value of α = 0.06 (Fig. 1c, f).

We further confirmed the negative cooperativity effect by measuring the stability of the IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex using surface plasmon resonance (SPR). Under SPR conditions, KT-474 binds to IRAK4 potently, with KD of 7 nM, and to CRBN/DDB1 with a KD of 4 nM (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1c). By immobilizing either IRAK4 or CRBN/DDB1 on the SPR sensor chip, we measured KT-474 binding in the presence of the partner proteins CRBN/DDB1 or IRAK4, respectively. We determined that although ternary complexes are formed in each setup, affinities of KT-474 are substantially weaker. Specifically, the SPR KD was 282 nM when CRBN/DDB1 was immobilized and 191 nM when IRAK4 was immobilized, consistent with FP assay results (Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 1d). Impact of the ternary complex on the binding kinetics of KT-474 was also measured by SPR. KT-474 binding to either CRBN or IRAK4 is weakened in the context of a ternary complex system, leading approximately 2–3x faster dissociation (Supplementary Table 1).

To confirm that the ternary complex induced by KT-474 is functionally active, we performed in vitro ubiquitination assays using recombinant full-length IRAK4 protein (IRAK4-FL). We observed strong and dose-dependent ubiquitination of IRAK4-FL in the presence of increasing concentrations of KT-474 followed by a decrease in ubiquitination at higher concentrations of KT-474 as a result of a “hook effect” consistent with that observed in the TCF assay (Fig. 1i). Ubiquitination signal is absent in the presence of the negative control compound Me-KT-474.

Cryo-EM structure of the IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex

The resulting ternary complex was isolated by size exclusion chromatography and immediately applied to cryo-EM grids (Supplementary Fig. 2). KT-474 induces a non-native protein-protein interaction surface

The ternary complex structure recapitulates the key engagement of KT-474’s TBM and EBM components (Fig. 1b) with IRAK4 and CRBN, respectively, consistent with high-affinity binding to both proteins. KT-474 adopts an extended conformation with good density observed for the EBM, TBM, and linker regions (Fig. 2b, inset). As expected, KT-474 EBM’s glutarimide group is anchored into the CRBN “tri-Trp pocket” formed between Trp380, 386, and 400, and maintains hydrogen bonds with Asn351, Trp380, and His378, with minimal change measured by root-mean-squared difference (RMSD) of 0.74 Å in overall structure and side-chain geometry comparable to a previously disclosed binary structure of CRBN13 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). On the IRAK4 side, KT-474 TBM maintains similar interactions as seen in a co-crystal structure of IRAK4 kinase domain and Compound 1, a close analog of KT-474 TBM, with RMSD of 0.63 Å (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4a, c–f). The TBM’s amide oxygen atom engages IRAK4 kinase hinge residue Met265 through a hydrogen bond with the backbone amide NH; and the gatekeeper residue Tyr262 and catalytic residue Lys213 engage the TBM via van der Waals interactions. The linker of KT-474 is partially buried by several loops from both IRAK4 and CRBN. CRBN TBD loop (residue 351–355), IRAK4 αDE loop (residue 276–282), IRAK4 β8-αD loop (residues 265–269), and IRAK4 αB-β1 loop (residues 184–186), engage the alkyne, piperidine, and cyclohexane moieties of the KT-474 linker, respectively (Fig. 2c). Additionally, a weak hydrogen bond is formed between the linker ether oxygen atom and CRBN His353 side chain (Fig. 2c).

KT-474 induces the formation of a protein-protein interaction (PPI) surface (Figs. 2a and 3a) between CRBN and IRAK4, with an interface surface area of 439 Å2 (Table 1). This value is comparable to earlier studies of PPI in molecular glue (MG) and HBF degrader structures of CRBN and VHL21,22. The Lon and thalidomide binding domains (TBD) of CRBN, both of which contribute to interactions with IRAK4, assume a “closed” state, consistent with an active CRBN geometry (Fig. 2b)20. CRBN Lon domain residues Phe150, Gly151, and Ile152, which reside in a β-hairpin loop (residues 149–152), are in contact with Lys202, Tyr204, and Asn207 in the N-lobe of IRAK4 kinase domain. These interactions comprise approximately 25% of the total buried surface area between CRBN and IRAK4 (Fig. 3b). Hydrogen bonds are found across the IRAK4-CRBN interface and act to further stabilize the ternary complex (Supplementary Table 2). Compared to native PPI observed in endogenous biological assemblies, the IRAK4-CRBN interface mediated by KT-474 enlists fewer residues and a reduced protein contact surface area. Whereas protein partners in native PPIs each typically contribute ~800 Å² to the total interface area23, the interface between IRAK4 and CRBN is significantly smaller, at 439 Å² (Table 1). To further characterize the strength of the PPI, we estimated the free energy of dissociation of CRBN from IRAK4, using PISA24 to be −5.7 kcal/mol and significantly less favorable than native PPIs (~ −10 to −20 kcal/mol)25. PPIs observed in other HBF degrader or MG complexes involving CRBN also show binding energy and surface area comparable to that of KT-474, whereas those involving VHL can be significantly smaller or weaker (Table 1).

Critical role of CRBN Phe150 in the KT-474 ternary complex

Despite weak overall engagement between CRBN and IRAK4, we identified a key interaction motif composed of CRBN-Phe150, IRAK4-Tyr204, and IRAK4-Lys202 (Fig. 3a–c) that we hypothesized may play a role in the stabilization of the complex. Phe and Tyr are two key residues frequently observed in a wide variety of PPIs26. Interestingly, CRBN-Phe150 is observed to be buried at the PPI surface in previously published ternary complex structures involving MG and HBF degraders (Supplementary Table 3). Though the potential role of Phe150 in MG-mediated neo-substrate recognition has been highlighted in structural studies20,27,28, biophysical characterization of this residue in ternary complex formation has not previously been performed. Interface analysis of CRBNPhe150Gly and CRBNPhe150Ala mutant computational models show Phe150 mutation reduces the interface area from 439 to 423 Å2, in the case of Gly, and to 425 Å2 in the case of Ala. However, the estimated ΔG is reduced from −5.7 kcal/mol to −4.4 kcal/mol with Gly and −4.7 kcal/mol with Ala. While the decreases in interface area and binding free energy are relatively small, we hypothesized that loss of Phe150 could exert a substantial impact on ternary complex stability, especially in the context of an already limited and weak PPI. To experimentally determine the impact of the loss of Phe150 interactions on ternary complex stability, we engineered two CRBN Phe150 loop mutant proteins, CRBNPhe150Gly and CRBNPhe150Ala, and measured KT-474 induced ternary complex formation and cooperativity. As expected, both mutants retained KT-474 binding affinities compared to CRBN-wild type (Supplementary Table 4). However, we observed a tenfold reduction in the cooperativity of the ternary complex with both mutants (Fig. 3d), resulting in an α value of 0.008. These data indicate that while CRBNPhe150Gly and CRBNPhe150Ala retain their ability to engage IRAK4 through KT-474, loss of Phe150 engagement with IRAK4 reduces the strength of the PPI and negatively impacts the stability of the complexes.

Ligand strain plays a role in the negative cooperativity of the IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex

Although previous studies have linked PPI surface area to cooperativity29, little is known about the impact of HBF degrader conformation on cooperativity of ternary complexes. We hypothesized that the alkyne linker of KT-474 would lead to a free-energy penalty to adopting the relatively extended conformation observed in the ternary complex, and that this penalty could negate the weakly favorable PPI and effectuate an overall penalty to the free energy of ternary complex formation. Conformational analyses using quantum mechanics (QM) were performed on the cyclohexane and the ether piperidine moieties of the linker (Supplementary Fig. 5) and demonstrated that the two rigid components of the linker are in energetically favorable geometries. However, QM conformational studies of the full molecule are hindered by the relatively flexible methylene and alkyne components of the linker, as well as the size of the molecule. To obtain the energy landscape of the full degrader molecule in solution, we conducted replica-exchange molecular dynamics (MD) simulation studies on KT-474 alone, IRAK4:KT-474 binary complex (KT-474IRAK4), and IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex (KT-474IRAK4:CRBN) in solution and analyzed the conformational landscape of KT-474 in each system. We observed that, while KT-474 adopts an extended conformation with median end-to-end distance of 26.3 ± 1.4 Å in the ternary complex structure (measured between the EBM and TBM), the free degrader preferred a more compact conformation in solution with a median end-to-end distance of 15.9 ± 6.5 Å (Fig. 4a). In the IRAK4-bound KT-474IRAK4 system, KT-474 adopts a slightly “kinked” geometry with median end-to-end distances of 23.5 ± 1.2 Å (Fig. 4a, b), a bias that is likely introduced by the protein environment, but also facilitated by the flexible nature of the linker as previously observed for a von Hippel-Lindau-based degrader30. However, RMSD calculations indicate that the KT-474IRAK4 kinked geometry is different from the extended geometry observed in the cryo-EM structure by approximately 4.0 ± 0.8 Å RMSD (Supplementary Fig. 6) and therefore cannot recruit CRBN to IRAK4. This suggests that work, which can be expressed in free energy cost, is required to bias KT-474 to a bioactive geometry found in the ternary complex structure. To quantify this free energy cost, we employed a previously described conformational clustering analysis method31 over the KT-474IRAK4 trajectories and determined a ΔGconf of +2.88 ± 0.08 kcal/mol for the cryo-EM-like population of conformations. Ternary complex structure provides insight into degradative selectivity

To investigate whether the cryo-EM structure can offer insight into the role of PPI in degradative selectivity, we performed comparative structural analyses with a member of the IRAK family, IRAK1, to which KT-474 showed strong affinity based on KinomeScan studies, with 4.1% probe binding remaining at 1 μM13. First, we speculated that the lower number of lysine residues in IRAK1 (n = 17) compared with IRAK4 (n = 27) (Supplementary Fig. 7) might contribute to the degradative selectivity by limiting ubiquitination sites. However, a significant number of lysine residues remain available in IRAK1, suggesting that lysine availability alone is unlikely to fully account for the selectivity observed. Next, we used profile-based kinase domain alignment32 to compare both ATP-binding pocket and CRBN-interacting residues between human IRAK1 and IRAK4 and compared the IRAK4 residues participating in the PPI of the IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 complex with those of IRAK1. The ATP-binding pocket of IRAK1 shares high sequence similarity with IRAK4 (65% identity), whereas the CRBN-interacting residues are less conserved (13% identity) (Fig. 5a, b). IRAK4 residues Asn207, Tyr204, Asp278, and Asn419, which are involved in favorable interactions with CRBN, are not conserved in IRAK1 (Fig. 5c). For example, Asn207, Asp278, and Asn419 involved in hydrogen-bonding with CRBN are either absent (Asn207) or align with Thr (Asp278) and Gln (Asn419) in IRAK1. These differences can weaken the PPI and negatively impact the ability of IRAK1 to form a stable ternary complex with CRBN, providing an explanation for how KT-474 spares IRAK1. Discussion

In this study, we sought to solve and comprehensively characterize the cryo-EM structure of a ternary complex induced by the HBF degrader KT-474 to understand how induced PPIs, degrader conformations, and linker geometries define and influence ternary complex formation and degradation. Structural rationale for KT-474's degradative potency (DC50), selectivity, ternary complex formation, and cooperativity of the assembly have been elucidated.

Our ternary complex structure provided atomic details on how KT-474 recruits CRBN to IRAK4 to initiate its ubiquitination. We found that the PPI between IRAK4 and CRBN is non-native in nature due to its significantly smaller surface area and less favorable free energy than native PPIs and may be characteristic of induced degradation complexes mediated by CRBN, in general (Table 1). These observations support the possibility that a large protein interface is not a prerequisite for robust degradation by HBF degraders. One residue at the IRAK4-CRBN interface, CRBN Phe150, formed one of the few hydrophobic contacts between IRAK4 and CRBN. Mutating this residue to either Ala or Gly resulted in a tenfold decrease in the cooperativity value of the ternary complex, suggesting that the CRBN Phe150 side chain plays a key role in stabilizing the ternary complex via hydrophobic engagements with IRAK4. Interestingly, CRBN Phe150 was also found at the CRBN-POI interfaces of several degrader ternary complex structures (Supplementary Table 3). Superposition of multiple ternary and ligand:CRBN binary structures revealed noticeable variation in the geometry of Phe150 and the β-hairpin (residues 149–152) on which this residue is located (Fig. 3e). Essentially serving as a recognition arm, we believe this structural element plays a role in enabling CRBN to adapt to and engage with a wider range of different substrates.

We found that robust IRAK4 degradation by KT-474 does not require positive cooperativity; instead, the ternary complex exhibits strong negative cooperativity. To explore the source of negative cooperativity induced by KT-474, we evaluated and compared the geometry of KT-474 in solution, complexed with IRAK4, and within the ternary complex structure. We showed that IRAK4-bound KT-474 prefers a non-bioactive geometry, requiring an energy cost of +2.88 kcal/mol to adopt the cryo-EM geometry. This energy penalty may be sufficient to negate the relatively small and weakly favorable PPI, resulting in net unfavorable ternary complex formation and thus negative cooperativity. The influence of conformational energetics on cooperativity is further supported by SPR, which reveal that KT-474 binding affinities to individual proteins are significantly weaker in the context of the ternary complex, accompanied by a two to threefold increase in dissociation rate. Despite this, a substantial presence of ternary complexes suggests that KT-474 is fully capable of inducing a stable and productive ternary complex competent for ubiquitination and ultimately degradation. This leads us to believe the impact of negative cooperativity is offset by the high affinity of KT-474 towards both CRBN and IRAK4 (Fig. 1).

Like small-molecule inhibitors, degrader selectivity can also be influenced by POI binding selectivity. However, selectivity can also be tuned through modifications that favor ternary complex formation with the POI or disfavor complex formation with undesired proteins33–35. Although KT-474 exhibited strong affinity to a related kinase, IRAK1, no meaningful ternary complexes with CRBN could be detected in the presence of KT-474 (Fig. 5). Our modeling studies indicate that IRAK4 residues at the CRBN interface are not conserved in IRAK1. Although no steric clashes with CRBN were identified, key IRAK4 interface residues that form direct interactions with CRBN are poorly conserved in IRAK1, resulting in an incompatible interface. These findings suggest that interface residues contribute to KT-474’s degradative selectivity. More broadly, this indicates that directing degrader design toward non-conserved protein surfaces may represent an effective strategy for optimizing degradative selectivity in HBF degrader campaigns.

Taken together, our work advances the understanding of how HBF degraders recruit E3 ligases to drive POI ubiquitination and degradation. Our findings reveal how linker geometry modulates cooperativity and provide a framework for HBF degrader optimization, hinting that medicinal chemistry campaigns on identifying and optimizing degraders with negative cooperativity may represent an effective approach to addressing selectivity challenges. Although strong POI and E3 affinities may be required, this approach can provide an expanded linker design space. We hope that the structural, computational, and biochemical insights presented here offer generalizable principles for the characterization and rational design of HBF degraders.

Methods

Protein construct, expression, and purification

Recombinant human IRAK4 and CRBN/DDB1 proteins, including IRAK4-cryo (His6-TEV-IRAK4(1–460)), IRAK4-xtal (His6-TEV-IRAK4(160–460)), IRAK4-FL (His6-IRAK4(1–460)-Avi), IRAK4-kinase (His6-IRAK4(154–460)-Avi), IRAK4-GST(GST-TEV-1–460), and CRBN/DDB1 complex (ZZ domain-His6-Thrombin CRBN (40–442))/DDB1(DDB1 (1–40)-Strep) were expressed in Sf9 cells. Typically, proteins were expressed at 27 °C for 48–72 h with transfection rate of 1:200. Cell paste was resuspended in lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.9), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, and 0.5% CHAPS.

IRAK4-cryo, IRAK4-xtal, IRAK4-FL, and IRAK4-kinase were purified with a gravity flow Ni-NTA column, equilibrated with 50 mM Tris (pH 7.9), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 10% glycerol, and eluted with elution buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.9), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, and 300 mM imidazole. To remove the His6-tag, recombinant IRAK4 proteins were dialyzed overnight at 4 °C in lysis buffer and TEV protease was added at a 1:100 protease:protein ratio. IRAK4-FL and IRAK4-kinase were biotinylated subsequently: 1 mM ATP, 7.5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mM Biotin, and 200 nM BirA were added to both Avi-tagged proteins and incubated for 4 h at 18 °C. A second Ni column was used after TEV cleavage to isolate His6-free protein. Finally, proteins were polished using a HiLoad 16/600 Superdex 200 column equilibrated with IRAK4 storage buffer containing 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, and 5% glycerol.

Co-expressed CRBN/DDB1 complex was first purified with a gravity flow Ni-NTA column. Both proteins can be captured by Ni-NTA, and SDS-PAGE gel showed CRBN and DDB1 co-elute in the elution buffer. For CRBN/DDB1-tag complex used in assays, CRBN/DDB1 complex in Ni elution was dialyzed into CRBN storage buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 1 mM DTT and directly aliquoted. For CRBN/DDB1-untagged used in cryo-EM sample preparation, the CRBN/DDB1 complex was digested with thrombin protease, further purified by a second Ni-NTA column, and polished with a Superdex 200 column, using established protocols for CRBN/DDB136.

IRAK4-GST was captured with GST resin and eluted in GST elution buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.9), 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT, 10% glycerol, and 10 mM GSH. After GST resin purification, IRAK4-GST was concentrated and further purified with Hiload 16/600 Superdex 200 column, equilibrated with IRAK4 storage buffer. His-GST-Avi protein was used as a control and purified using similar protocol.

Ternary complex preparation for cryo-EM and data collection

To assemble IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex, IRAK4-cryo (2 μM), CRBN/DDB1-untagged (2 μM), and KT-474 (2 μM) were mixed in 8.8 mL assembly buffer (50 mM HEPES [pH 7.5], 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM TCEP) and incubated for 2 h on ice. After incubation, the sample was concentrated to 1 mL and loaded onto a Superdex 200 Increase 10/300 GL column equilibrated in assembly buffer. Elution was analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and peak fractions containing IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex were pooled and concentrated to about 10 mg/ml.

Immediately prior to grid preparation, the IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 complex was diluted to 6 mg/ml in assembly buffer and supplemented with 0.2% OG to improve particle orientation distribution and reduce complex dissociation. A 3 μL aliquot of the sample was applied to a plasma cleaned Protochip GF-1.2/1.3–3 Au grid, blotted for 3 s, and plunge-frozen by Vitrobot Mark IV, at 4 °C under 100% humidity.

Cryo-EM data were collected using a Thermo-Fisher Krios transmission electron microscope operated at 300 keV, equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector with a Gatan Image Filter. Super-resolution movies were collected using EPU 2.14 at 0.412 Å/pix, a nominal magnification of x105000, with a defocus range of −0.5 to −2.5 μm and a total dose of 48 e−/ Å2 over 40 frames.

Cryo-EM data processing and structure determination

16,472 cryo-EM movies were analyzed with cryoSPARC (version 4.1.2) workflow37. After patch motion correction and CTF correction, ~8,000,000 single particle images (box size 440 pix, down-sampled by 2) were extracted for follow-up 2D and 3D analysis. After 3 rounds of 2D classification, ~4,500,000 particles were used in heterogeneous refinement, while 3 initial models for this refinement were generated ab initio from a subset of ~100,000 particles with representative diverse orientations. In heterogeneous refinement, 2,102,119 particles were sorted to class for ternary complex and fed into the last round of 2D classification, and isolated 1,983,429 clean particles. NU-refinement of clean particles generated a map at 3.01 Å resolution, showing good density for CRBN and DDB1 β-propeller domains A and C. 3D classification was performed with a mask around IRAK4 and CRBN, and the class with best IRAK4 density (containing 201,356 particles) was further refined with NU-refinement to 3.09 Å. Particles with good IRAK4 density were re-extracted to restore the original pixel size and signal for DDB1 β-propeller B was subtracted. Subtracted particles were analyzed with the 3DFlex workflow and yielded a map of 3.22 Å from 3Dflex Reconstruction. To generate the final map, 3DFlex volume was auto sharpened in PHENIX (version 1.20.1)38.

A cryo-EM structure of CRBN/DDB1 (PDB: 8D7U20) and a crystal structure of IRAK4 kinase domain (PDB: 8DKS39) were docked into cryo-EM maps using “fit in map” function in ChimeraX (version 1.7)40. Restraints and original PDB of KT-474 were generated with phenix.elbow. Several rounds of iterative refinement and model building were performed with PHENIX real-space refinement (version 1.20.1) and Coot (version 0.9.8.1 EL)38,41. The final cryo-EM model was validated using tools available in PHENIX. Buried surface area (BSA) and interface residues are calculated using software QtPISA v2.1.0 in CCP4 suite (version 8.0.002)24. Figures and videos were generated using ChimeraX (version 1.7)40,42 and PyMOL (version 2.5.2)43 software. Data collection and refinement statistics for IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN/DDB1 ternary complex structure are shown in Supplementary Table 5.

IRAK4 crystallization and binary structure determination

Co-crystals of the IRAK4 kinase domain with Compound 1 were grown by the hanging drop vapor diffusion method. The complex was prepared by mixing 12 mg/mL IRAK4-xtal with 1.8 mM Compound 1 in a buffer containing 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM DTT, and 1% DMSO. After 2 h of incubation on ice, 1 μL of prepared IRAK4-Compound 1 complex was mixed with 1 μL reservoir solution consisting of 25% PEG5000mme, 0.2 M ammonium sulfate, and 0.1 MES (pH 6.5) and equilibrated at 18 °C. Crystals were soaked in cryo-protection solution (reservoir solution with 25% glycerol) briefly and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen.

The diffraction data were collected in beamline BL18U1 of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) and processed by HKL300044. The structure was solved by molecular replacement using published IRAK4 kinase domain45 as the initial model using Phaser. Refmac5 and PHENIX (version 1.20.1) were used in structure refinement46.

Data collection and refinement statistics for IRAK4-Compound 1 crystal structure are shown in Supplementary Table 6.

For each independent experiment, a 12-point, threefold dilution of the compound was plated in duplicate at a 50× concentration using an Echo 650 Series Acoustic Liquid Handler (Beckman Coulter), aiming for a final DMSO concentration of 2% (v/v) and a total assay volume of 10 µL (0.2 µL). A “master mix” composed of UBA1 (South Bay Bio #SBB-CE0011), UBE2D1 (South Bay Bio #SBB-CE0021), His10-Cullin4a/Rbx1 neddylated (South Bay Bio #SBB-SC0115), CRBN/DDB1 (South Bay Bio # SBB-SC0116), Cy5-Ubiquitin (South Bay Bio # SBB-TR0015), IRAK4-FL, and PPI Eu Detection Buffer (Revvity #61DB9RDF; treated as a 2× stock solution) was prepared at 1.14× using assay buffer and incubated on ice for 30 min. A 16-channel VIAFLO electronic multichannel pipette (Integra Biosciences #4641) was used to plate the master mix at a final 1× concentration across the plate (8.80 µL; 1× = 60 nM UBA1, 250 nM UBE2D1, 100 nM His10-Cullin4a/Rbx1 neddylated, 100 nM CRBN/DDB1, 500 nM Cy5-Ubiquitin, 100 nM IRAK4-FL, and 1× PPI Eu Detection Buffer). The plate was sealed and mixed gently at 250 rpm for 15 min on a tabletop shaker. To initiate the ubiquitination reaction, a 10× Mg-ATP (Millipore Sigma #A9187) aqueous solution was added to the first of two compound replicates (1.0 µL; 1× = 5 mM Mg-ATP). The second compound replicates were treated with assay buffer instead to serve as nonenzymatic negative controls (1.0 µL). The final plate was sealed, centrifuged at 1500 × g for 30 s, and incubated at 37 °C.

After 1 h, reactions were quenched with 0.5 M EDTA to achieve a final concentration of 100 mM EDTA and a new assay volume of 12.5 μL per well (2.5 μL). The plate was sealed and mixed gently at 250 rpm for 15 min on a tabletop shaker. Streptavidin-Eu cryptate (Revvity # 610SAKLA) was added next to achieve a final concentration of 4 nM Streptavidin-Eu and a final assay volume of 13.5 μL per well (1.0 μL). The plate was sealed and mixed gently at 250 rpm for 30 min on a tabletop shaker. After an additional 30 min incubation, the plate was read on a 2102 EnVision Multilabel Plate Reader (Revvity) using a 320 nm excitation filter and 615 nm and 665 nm emission filters. Emission signals were measured simultaneously using a dual mirror D400/D630 and 100 μs delay. The TR-FRET output signal was expressed as a ratio of acceptor/donor (665 nm/615 nm) emission counts.

The data were normalized by subtracting the signal of the buffer-treated nonenzymatic negative controls (B) from the signal of the Mg-ATP treated enzymatic samples (S). The percent normalized signal-to-background was plotted against the dosing concentration in GraphPad Prism47. Three independent experiments were plotted simultaneously with error bars to display the standard error from the three independent experiments.

SPR binding assay

SPR direct binding by multicycle and single cycle kinetics assays were performed on a Biacore 8 K+ instrument (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden) using SA sensor chips (Biacore, Cytiva). The assay temperature was maintained at 25 °C. A 1× HBS-EP+ buffer (Cytiva) with 2 mM TCEP was used for protein immobilization and as running buffer. IRAK4 or CRBN/DDB1 was diluted in 1× buffer without DMSO and immobilized on the chip to desired RU. The assay was conducted in 1× running buffer containing 2% DMSO. KT-474 was serially diluted twofold into a final 1× assay buffer containing 2% DMSO and injected in single cycle mode at 30 μL/min flow rate with 60 s association time and 7200 s dissociation time or multicycle mode at 70 μL/min flow rate for 30 s association followed by dissociation. Blank injections were made using 1× running buffer or wells containing 2% DMSO final. Kd, on-rate, and off-rate were determined by fitting double-referenced data to steady state or kinetic 1:1 binding model on the Biacore Insight Evaluation Software. For the ternary complex experiments, either 2 μM CRBN or 2 μM IRAK4 was maintained constant and the affinity of KT-474 in complex was measured.

Human PBMC western blot

Frozen PBMCs were purchased from a commercial source (TPCS, China, Cat. No. PB100C-W) and thawed into RPMI with heat-inactivated 10% FBS/1% penicillin/streptomycin and on the same day were plated into 12-well flat-bottom plates at 3 × 106 cells per well in 2.0 μL of media. KT-474 and the negative control Me-KT-474 were dosed starting at 500 nM, with a fourfold dose titration and an 8-point curve. The compound and cells were incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 20 h. After 20 h, samples were collected and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min. The supernatant was aspirated and cells were resuspended in PBS before being centrifuged again. The supernatant was aspirated, and samples were resuspended in 50 μL of 1× RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, CN, P0013B), prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were immediately boiled at 95 °C for 7 min. After cooling, samples were loaded into a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel with 1× MES buffer. After running, the gels were transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in blocking buffer at RT for 1 h. The next day, primary antibodies were added (mouse anti-IRAK4 [1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK #ab119942] and rabbit anti- β-actin [1:30,000, Cell Signaling Technologies #4970], or rabbit anti-IRAK1 [1:1000, Cell Signaling Technologies #4504] and mouse anti-β-actin [1:30,000, Cell Signaling Technologies #3700]), and membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed 3× for 5 min in 1× TBST before secondary antibodies were added at 1:5000 (goat anti-mouse IRDye 680RD #926-68070 and goat anti-rabbit IRDye 800CW #926-32211, both from LICORbio). Antibodies were incubated with membranes at RT for 1 h on a shaker before being washed 3× with TBST. Membranes were imaged on the LICOR DLx. Western blots were quantified using Image Studio™ software (LICORbio). Briefly, band intensity was quantified for both β-actin and IRAK4 or IRAK1 bands in each lane. The IRAK4 or IRAK1 signal was normalized to the β-actin level in each lane, and the percent of target protein remaining was calculated by comparing normalized IRAK4 or IRAK1 levels of treated samples to those of DMSO controls. The data were analyzed using software GraphPad Prism ref. [47], and the dose-dependent protein degradation was fit using a variable slope four-parameter logistic equation to obtain DC50 values. Data represent mean ± SD from independent experiments.

Molecular dynamics simulations and computational modeling

Replica-Exchange Molecular Dynamics Simulations were performed using Schrödinger 2023-448 and Desmond49 software packages (Supplementary Table 7). Initial coordinates from the cryo-EM structure were used to create 3 solute systems: (1) KT-474 alone, (2) KT-474 in complex with IRAK4, and (3) full IRAK4:KT-474:CRBN ternary complex. The systems were then solvated in orthorhombic water boxes using the SPC water model50 with at least 10 Å of buffer along each edge and neutralized using Na+ or Cl− ions. The model systems were initially relaxed using the standard relaxation protocol included in the Schrödinger 2023–4 software package48,49. Replica Exchange with Solute Tempering (REST2) simulations51,52 we performed as follows. Two replicas were run at 300 and 335 K on system 1 and at 300 K and 360 K for system 3 using a Nose-Hoover chains thermostat. The solvent-exposed regions of KT-474 were defined as ‘hot’ in each simulation system: the full molecule in system 1, the linker region and the CRBN binder for system 2, and the linker region in the system 3 (see Fig. 1b for substructure definitions). The total lengths of the simulations were 100 ns per replica with exchanges between neighboring replicas attempted every 1 ps and exchange of configurations between neighboring replicas are attempted every 2 ps with an average acceptance ratio of about 35%. All simulations were run in NPT using the Martyna-Tobias-Klein barostat to maintain the pressure at 1 atm. Two additional simulations were run by changing only the random seed for each of the systems. Snapshots were taken every 100 ps for each of the 4 simulations. These were aligned using the protein backbone atoms, and KT-474 was extracted from each snapshot. KT-474 conformations were analyzed using two metrics: (1) the end-to-end distance defined as the distance between the oxygen atom of the bridged morpholine and the nitrogen atom of the glutarimide group and (2) the RMSD relative to the cryo-EM structure. Standard deviations were calculated for each metric by averaging across all 3 simulations for each system. Probability distributions of (1) and (2) were visualized using the seaborn kernel density estimate plot package available in Python. KT-474 conformations from each simulation were clustered using its heavy atoms with the atomic RMSD conformer clustering module in Schrödinger Software Suite version 2023–4 using the average linkage method. 200 clusters were generated, achieving a Kelley penalty of 800 and an R2 of 0.99. Relative energy of each cluster, or structural class, was obtained using a previously described method31.
