EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The objective of this program is to create basin-specific methane emissions inventories of the
Haynesville and Anadarko (Woodford shale) basins, based on truly basin-scale data collection
and analysis. Emissions of natural gas, predominantly composed of methane, present a
significant financial loss to the oil and gas sector and reduce the competitiveness of American
energy sources. While large emissions, above 10 kg/h, occur at only a small percentage of oil
and gas sites, they account for a disproportionately large volume of emissions. To address this,
we executed a concurrent campaign combining cutting-edge bottom-up modeling with top-down
aerial and satellite surveys to capture the heavy-tailed distribution of these large emissions to
accurately characterize total emissions throughout both regions.

In 2023, we collected airborne data over a total of 9,611 square miles (3,162 square miles of
airborne data collected with DOE funds and 6,445 square miles of commercially collected data
donated as cost share), significantly exceeding our initial goal of 6,972 square miles. In 2024,
we collected airborne data over a total of 17,057 square miles (4,997 square miles of airborne
data collected with DOE funds and 12,060 square miles of commercially collected data donated
as cost share). Total collected airborne data for 2023 and 2024 amount to 26,664 sq miles,
significantly exceeding our project goal of 9,572 square miles for the full three years of the
project in just two years. In addition, we expanded our approach to collect a sequence of
surveys over smaller, highly representative areas (approximately 350 square miles per basin) in
a coordinated satellite and airborne campaign to investigate the temporal persistence and
intermittency of methane emissions. Simultaneously, the project team updated and deployed
advanced bottom-up methodologies. We integrated the Regional Oil and gas Aerial Methane
Synthesis (ROAMS) model (Sherwin et al. 2024), the Bottom-up Analyzer for Source Emissions
(BASE) model (Rutherford et al. 2021), and Methane Emissions Estimation Tool (MEET) model
(Zimmerle et al. 2022) with newly generated midstream asset data, supported by AI computer
vision mapping. By the end of Year 2, we successfully produced both top-down and bottom-up
regional inventories for the upstream and midstream segments of the study regions.

This project was originally slated for a three-year period to establish a long-term time series
characterizing seasonal and annual trends. However, the grant was terminated at the end of
project Year 2 (September 30, 2025). While this early termination prevented the analysis of the
final year of multi-year time-series data, the project successfully met its core objectives of
creating reconciled basin-scale emissions inventories, developing and open-sourcing the
Python-based BASE model and ROAMS integration model, and producing peer-reviewed
findings on optimal survey design and gas composition. The resulting products and
methodologies pioneered here will support the development of improved, basin-specific
methane emissions quantification and mitigation strategies.

BODY MATTER
1. Introduction
Vented and fugitive natural gas from the oil and gas industry represent a significant loss of
valuable energy commodities. Capturing this lost gas is vital to supporting American economic
competitiveness, maximizing domestic energy production, and expanding liquified natural gas
(LNG) exports. Preliminary analyses suggest that millions of dollars worth of natural gas are lost
annually due to correctable equipment and operational failures. By generating actionable
insights into the root causes of these losses, oil and gas operators can leverage these data to
decrease the proportion of natural gas vented into the atmosphere and bring more U.S. gas to
market.

Effective detection strategies must weigh tradeoffs between sensitivity, scalability, and
frequency, especially because a small percentage of anomalous sources (0.1-20%) contribute a
disproportionately high percentage (50-90%) of total uncaptured gas (e.g. Allen et al., 2013;
Brandt et al., 2016; Zavala-Araiza et al., 2015; Omara et al., 2018). Historically, generating
basin-specific loss inventories has been challenged by disagreements between bottom-up
approaches (which rely on component-level equipment factors) and top-down approaches
(which use site-level measurements from planes or satellites). Top-down approaches using
newer technologies typically find substantially greater gas loss rates than bottom-up estimates.
This project aimed to bridge that gap in two critical U.S. regions: the Anadarko (Woodford
Shale) and Haynesville basins, both of which are key suppliers to LNG export terminals and vital
to local and national economies.

2. Methods, Assumptions, and Procedures
To construct comprehensive methane loss inventories, we utilized a coordinated, two-pronged
methodology:

2.1 Top-Down Aerial and Satellite Surveys
Top-down data collection relied on Insight M's LeakSurveyor™ light-aircraft mounted gas
imaging system, paired with GHGSat's high-resolution hyperspectral imaging satellites. The
total achieved non-unique (i.e. counting overlapping areas) coverage area of 26,664 sq mi
significantly exceeds the targeted non-unique coverage of 9,572 sq mi.
 ●​ 2023 Basin-Wide Aerial Surveys (Figure 1): We conducted comprehensive aerial
 surveys covering >70% of total hydrocarbon production in the target basins. DOE funded
 surveys covered 1,232 sq mi in the Anadarko Basin (Woodford Shale), and 4,077 sq mi
 in the Haynesville Basin. Insight M donated 9,821 sq mi in the Anadarko Basin
 (Woodford Shale), and 6,816 sq mi in the Haynesville Basin as cost share. These values
 represent total sq miles covered across all surveys (i.e. non-unique coverage), and
 include overlapping regions. Total non-overlapping (i.e. unique) survey areas for 2023
 are 5,209 sq mi in the Anadarko Basin (Woodford Shale) and 4,398 sq mi in the
 Haynesville Basin. We ensured that the sample was representative of oil and gas
 production in both regions by selecting sites that had a similar distribution of site-level oil
 and gas production to the basin as a whole. Surveys represented 84% of all wells in the

 6

 Haynesville Basin, covering roughly 75% of total gas production in both regions. This
 large sample allowed us to robustly estimate overall gas loss dynamics despite high
 intermittency.
 ●​ 2023 Targeted Satellite Surveys: In order to constrain the duration of the largest
 emission sources, we targeted aerially detected emissions for follow up with GHGSat
 satellite surveys.
 ●​ 2024 Basin-Wide Aerial Surveys: Insight M continued donating survey data from
 commercial operations as part of cost sharing efforts for this project. This included 6,895
 non-unique sq mi of coverage in the Anadarko Basin (Woodford Shale) and 5,165
 non-unique sq mi in the Haynesville Basin.
 ●​ Year 2 (2024) Coordinated Temporal Campaign (Figure 2): In Year 2, the protocol was
 expanded to better capture emission duration. We implemented full satellite surveys over
 highly representative 350-square-mile sub-regions in both basins. DoE funded aerial
 surveys totaled 3,066 total non-unique sq mi in the Anadarko Basin (Woodford Shale),
 and 1,931 non-unique sq mi in the Haynesville. Simultaneous to satellite overpasses, 4
 aerial surveys were conducted across a 3-month study period at approximately 4-week
 intervals.

2.2 Emissions Modeling and Midstream Asset Mapping
We expand upon the methodology developed by Sherwin et al., (2024) to produce complete
methane emissions inventories for the Anadarko and Haynesville basins. This relies on the
bottom-up modeling approach developed by Rutherford et al. (2021), which we translated into
Python and renamed as the Bottom-up Analyzer for Source Emissions (BASE) model. Outputs
from BASE are integrated into the new Regional Oil and Gas Aerial Methane Synthesis Model
(ROAMS) model, which has been translated into Python. Both the BASE and ROAMS models
have been made publicly available on GitHub.

Historically, midstream sites (gathering, boosting, and processing) are poorly mapped. We
employed AI computer vision algorithms developed by Stanford University to identify
compressor stations and tank batteries from optical imagery collected during our aerial surveys
(Figure 3). The AI computer vision results were validated by experts in oil and gas imagery

 8

analysis from Kairos Aerospace. These asset inventories, and emission factors informed by
data shared by MarkWest Energy Partners, L.P. (MPLX) were then incorporated into the
basinwide emissions inventory.

3. Results and Discussion
3.1 Top-Down Detections
The 2023 basin-specific aerial surveys yielded 1,112 emission detections across 709 sources,
with a total emission rate of 324,111 kg/hr. In the Haynesville basin, aerial surveys detected 848
emissions from 505 sources, with rates ranging from 3 kg/hr to 7,418 kg/hr (median: 136 kg/hr).
In the Anadarko basin, aerial surveys detected 264 emissions from 204 sources, with rates
ranging from 19 kg/hr to 1,405 kg/hr (median: 96 kg/hr). Of the 52 images collected by GHGSat
for follow up on aerially detected emissions, none were able to detect methane plumes from the
targeted locations. However, these images included 28 emission detections from other sources,
ranging from 213 to 2,153 kg/hr (Figure 4).

 frequency survey group).

To better characterize the duration and temporal persistence of large gas losses, the Year 2
protocol implemented a high-frequency survey campaign over representative sub-regions.
During this campaign, repeated aerial surveys detected 134 individual uncaptured gas
emissions across the two basins (Figure 5). In the Haynesville study area, 74 emissions were
detected ranging from 5.6 kg/hr to 297 kg/hr (median: 73 kg/hr). In the Woodford study area, 60
emissions were detected ranging from 12 kg/hr to 2,990 kg/hr (median: 85 kg/hr).
Simultaneously, the coordinated GHGSat satellite taskings collected 93 large-tile (FOV)
observations, capturing 11 distinct large-volume gas loss observations across the target areas,
observing emission rates ranging from 163 kg/hr to 4,149 kg/hr (Figure 4). By layering these
high-frequency airborne and satellite passes, the project successfully tracked the temporal
persistence of equipment failures over the 3-month period. For example, the campaign recorded
one persistent upstream source that was actively emitting ~110 kg/hr across 8 separate
observations, and another emitting ~75 kg/hr across 5 separate observations.

Aerial survey data donated as part of cost share in 2024 yielded an additional 228 emission
detections from 201 sources in the Anadarko basin, and 601 emission detections from 416
sources in the Haynesville basin. The combined 2024 aerial survey dataset includes coverage
of ~62% of gas production in the Anadarko basin, and ~40% of gas production in the
Haynesville basin.

3.2 Basin-specific Emissions Inventories
Basin-specific emissions inventories combine bottom-up emission modeling from the BASE
model with top-down emission detections via the ROAMS model. We generated bottom-up
emissions inventories using the BASE model and emission factors developed for this project
(Figure 6). Results from bottom-up modeling indicate that the contribution of emissions from
tanks in the two basins is quite similar, while the contribution from pneumatic controllers is

 10

higher in the Haynesville. Top-down emissions inventories indicate that the Haynesville and
Anadarko basins have contrasting emissions dynamics: the Haynesville loses more gas, but
these losses represent a smaller percentage of production volume than the Woodford (Figure
7). In both basins, measured top-down emissions (as a % of total volume of methane produced
by the surveyed assets) from upstream and midstream sources are approximately equal, with
slightly greater losses from upstream sources. However, differences include the proportion of
emissions from pipelines, (representing 22% of total emissions in the Woodford while only 5% in
the Haynesville) and the proportion of emissions from compressor stations (representing 16% in
the Woodford and 25% in the Haynesville) (Figure 8). Joint top-down and bottom-up inventories
indicate that while aerially detected emissions are sourced from 0.38-1% of wells, they
represent 50%-60% of the total emitted methane (Figure 9).

6. Conclusion
The project successfully created reconciled, basin-specific inventories for both the Anadarko
and Haynesville basins. The deployment of spatially comprehensive airborne sampling proved
that large sample sizes and careful spatial stratifications are critical for producing unbiased
estimates of heavy-tailed equipment failures. Furthermore, the project successfully mapped
previously under-documented midstream infrastructure and integrated these into the methane
emissions inventories. By merging these datasets via the newly developed, open-source
ROAMS model, the project successfully bridged the historical gap between top-down
observations and bottom-up simulations and provided a valuable new open source tool to the
entire community. Ultimately, this framework provides oil and gas operators with highly scalable,

 13

economically valuable insights to inform operational improvements and bring more natural gas
to export and domestic markets.

7. Recommendations
Based on the methodologies proven during this performance period, we recommend the
following to support American energy competitiveness and reduce natural gas loss:
 1.​ Expanded Surveys to Boost Gas Recovery: The survey methodology developed under
 this grant is highly scalable. We recommend extending this tiered top-down and
 bottom-up framework to the rest of the United States to improve oil and gas operations,
 thereby bringing more natural gas to market and improving worker safety.
 2.​ Utilization of ROAMS: Operators should utilize the newly open-sourced ROAMS model
 to integrate top-down measurement data with bottom-up equipment factors, ensuring
 that small-scale, persistent gas losses are accurately tracked and mitigated.
 3.​ Midstream Data Sharing: The integration of proprietary equipment, operational, and gas
 composition data from operators (such as the MPLX partnership in this project) proved
 vital to closing the midstream data gap. We recommend continuous industry data
 sharing agreements to refine loss estimations for compressor stations and gathering
 pipelines, ultimately improving system-wide efficiency.
