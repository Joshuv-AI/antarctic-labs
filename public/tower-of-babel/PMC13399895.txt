Modified Transtibial Pullout Repair for Medial Meniscus Posterior Root Tear Using a Barbed Suture: A Technical Note
Authors: Nam Jun Baek, Wonchul Choi, Jae‐Young Park, Jae Youn Yoon, Jonghyun Park, Jun‐Bae Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technical note describes a modified transtibial pullout repair for medial meniscus posterior root tear incorporating a barbed suture to improve fixation strength and load distribution. The technique combines 2 simple sutures placed near the torn root with a circumferential barbed suture passed through the posteromedial meniscocapsular tissue. The barbed suture provides knotless, self‐locking fixation that maintains even tension across the construct, enhancing meniscal centralization and reducing extrusion. All sutures are retrieved through a single tibial tunnel and secured on the anterior tibial cortex with knotless anchors. This configuration restores proper tensioning and promotes physiologic hoop stress transmission while minimizing stress concentration and overconstraint. The method is simple, reproducible, and compatible with standard arthroscopic instruments. Moreover, it can be performed in conjunction with centralization or other adjunctive fixation techniques, offering versatility and adaptability for various meniscal root tear patterns.

Posterior root repair of the medial meniscus using combined simple and barbed suture techniques. Preoperative and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging show medial meniscal extrusion and its correction following anatomic posterior root repair. After all sutures are retrieved through the tibial tunnel, a probe is used to pull them retrogradely to confirm smooth glide and ensure the absence of tangles or soft‐tissue bridges. After fixation, a probe is used to assess the repair site and confirm adequate tension, stable reduction, and secure fixation of both the simple and barbed suture constructs. The posterior root of the medial meniscus plays a crucial role in maintaining biomechanics by transforming axial loading into hoop stress, thus redistributing contact pressure across the tibiofemoral joint. Disruption of this structure, known as a medial meniscus posterior root tear, results in the loss of circumferential tension and ensuing meniscal extrusion, ultimately reducing load‐bearing capacity and progressive osteoarthritic degeneration. 1 , 2 , 3

For the treatment of medial meniscus posterior root tears, the transtibial suture pullout technique has become an established method with favorable clinical results. 4 However, several limitations have been reported, including insufficient reduction of the repaired root under axial loading, the so‐called “bungee effect” caused by the long suture length within the tibial tunnel, and difficulty achieving optimal suture tension. 5 , 6 To address these issues, several modified fixation methods have been proposed (Table 1).

Other fixation strategies have included graft augmentation and direct suture anchor techniques. 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Recent comparative studies between all‐inside and transtibial pullout repairs suggest that the all‐inside technique may offer biomechanical and imaging‐based advantages; however, its superiority remains inconclusive. 25 , 26 , 27

The centralization suture technique theoretically restores meniscal function following root repair, and a recent study reported favorable 2‐year outcomes. 28 Although centralization provides a press‐fit effect that resists axial loading, it can result in overconstraint, which may in turn cause improper tensioning and stress concentration. 29 , 30 , 31

Uniform stress distribution relies on proper circumferential tension transmission, as shown in techniques such as the whip‐running suture described by Kim et al. and the circumferential semitendinosus graft reconstruction reported by Ohno et al. 14 , 24 Accordingly, barbed sutures, which are increasingly used in clinical practice, have been shown to provide strong fixation while promoting tension dispersion and load sharing across repair constructs in various surgical fields. 32 Based on these characteristics, we adopted barbed sutures in our technique to achieve stable fixation with physiologic load sharing.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Preoperative Evaluation and Positioning

Preoperative anatomical landmarks are marked on the skin. The patient is placed supine, and the operative field is prepared and draped using standard aseptic technique. A pneumatic tourniquet is applied to the proximal thigh. Anteromedial, accessory anteromedial, and anterolateral portals are established (Video 1).

Root Tear Identification and Preparation (Figure 1)

A 30° arthroscope is inserted through the anterolateral (AL) portal to provide visualization of the medial meniscus posterior root (MMPR). Using the anteromedial (AM) working portal, the posterior root is probed and grasped to assess mobility, tissue quality, and impingement with adjacent structures. Gentle traction is used to confirm reducibility to the anatomic footprint. Frayed margins and surrounding synovium are debrided with a motorized shaver to freshen the interface.

Root tear identification and preparation. Arthroscopic view of the left knee in the supine position. The scope is in the anterolateral portal, and the anteromedial portal is used as the working portal. (A) A probe and grasper (arrow) are used to assess the mobility of the posterior root tear (asterisks), evaluate tissue quality, and confirm the absence of impingement with adjacent structures. (B) Gentle traction shows reducibility of the root to its anatomic footprint (arrow). The integrity and mobility of the meniscal tissue are assessed to determine suitability for anatomic fixation and to guide selection of the repair configuration.

Initial Simple Sutures at the Root Stump (Figure 2)

Using a self‐retrieving suture‐passing device (e.g., Knee Scorpion; Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA), 2 simple vertical sutures are placed approximately 5 to 10 mm from the torn edge of the meniscal root stump to capture circumferential fibers and achieve controlled anatomic reduction. A No. 2 Ethibond Excel suture (Ethicon, Somerville, NJ, USA) is passed through the central portion of the root stump, and a No. 2 FiberWire (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) is placed through the peripheral portion, providing balanced circumferential tissue capture and uniform tensile stress distribution across the tear.

Initial simple sutures at the root stump. Arthroscopic views of the left knee in the supine position viewing from the anterolateral portal. (A) Two simple vertical sutures are placed approximately 5 to 10 mm from the torn edge of the meniscal root using a self‐retrieving suture‐passing device (arrow). The first suture is passed vertically through the outer (peripheral) portion of the root stump. (B) The second suture is passed through the inner (central) portion in a similar fashion (arrow). These stitches capture circumferential fibers to achieve balanced tension and facilitate controlled anatomic reduction during fixation.

Barbed Suture: Material and Rationale (Figure 3)

A barbed suture with unidirectional engagement is selected to provide knotless, self‐locking fixation and proper tensioning across the construct, supplementing the 2 simple sutures for peripheral centralization. To further enhance centralization and reduce extrusion, a barbed suture loaded on a delivery needle (BarbFix; MHMEDI, Republic of Korea) is passed circumferentially through robust posteromedial meniscocapsular tissue.

Gross photographs of the barbed suture. (A) Overview of the full‐length barbed suture showing its unidirectional barb pattern (arrow). (B) Magnified view highlighting barb orientation (arrow), which allows smooth advancement through tissue while preventing reverse slippage. The self‐locking configuration enables knotless fixation and uniform tension distribution.

Posteromedial Barbed Suture Passage Sequence (Figure 4)

The barbed suture, preloaded on a delivery needle, is introduced through the posteromedial joint capsule, approximately 2 to 3 cm from the meniscal root attachment, at the level of the posterior margin of the medial collateral ligament. The needle is advanced to pass through the medial meniscus posterior horn and exit at the midportion of the torn root. Once the needle tip becomes visible intra‐articularly, the barbed suture is grasped with an arthroscopic grasper and pulled out. The nonbarbed portion is allowed to pass sufficiently first, and once the barbed segment begins to appear, the delivery needle is removed. The suture is then gently tensioned to confirm that the barbs have securely engaged the meniscal substance and that stable tension is maintained. The residual medial limb of the suture is subsequently cut and removed as close to the skin as possible.

Footprint Preparation and Tibial Tunnel Shuttling (Figure 5)

Using a Meniscal Root Repair System guide (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA), the anatomic footprint on the tibial plateau is identified and prepared. A 2.4‐mm Kirschner wire is advanced along the guide trajectory, followed by enlargement with a 4.5‐mm cannulated drill to create the tibial tunnel. A polydioxanone suture is then passed through the tunnel to serve as a shuttle relay. Subsequently, all 3 suture components—2 simple vertical sutures and 1 circumferential barbed suture—are shuttled through the tibial tunnel and retrieved at the anterior tibial cortex for cortical fixation.

(A) The cartilage at the anatomic footprint is debrided to expose the subchondral bone (arrow), creating an optimal bone bed for healing. (B) A PDS suture is passed through the tibial tunnel to serve as a shuttle relay (arrow). (C,D) All 3 suture components (arrow) are shuttled through the tibial tunnel and retrieved on the anterior tibial cortex, ready for cortical fixation. (PDS, polydioxanone.)

Proper Tensioning and Cortical Fixation (Figure 6)

At 30° of flexion, the simple sutures and barbed sutures are tensioned independently to achieve anatomic reduction of the meniscal root to its prepared footprint. Each construct is then secured separately using two 4.5‐mm PushLock anchors (Arthrex, Naples, FL, USA) on the anteromedial tibial cortex. After fixation, the repair site is probed arthroscopically to verify adequate tension, stable reduction, and secure fixation of both constructs.

Postoperative Care and Rehabilitation

Postoperatively, patients are permitted early mobilization using crutches under toe‐touch, nonweight‐bearing conditions for the first 6 weeks. Progressive weight‐bearing is then initiated at 6 weeks. It is gradually increased over the following 6‐week period as tolerated.

Immediately after surgery, the operated limb is immobilized in a hinged knee brace locked at 0° of extension to protect the repair site. From 2 to 4 weeks postoperatively, range of motion exercises are allowed from 0° to 90°, and from 4 weeks onward, range of motion is progressively increased up to 130° as tolerated.

Pre‐ and postoperative magnetic resonance imaging (Figure 7) shows resolution of medial meniscal extrusion, with anatomic restoration of the posterior root attachment and continuity of the meniscus.

(B) Preoperative sagittal image showing discontinuity of the posterior root with a high‐signal gap indicating detachment (arrowhead). DISCUSSION

The barbed suture allows uniform tensioning and decreases operative time, reflecting its inherent mechanical advantages. In line with this rationale, barbed sutures have already been applied successfully in flexor tendon repair biomechanics, showing load sharing and secure knotless fixation. 32

This technical note describes a modified transtibial pullout repair technique for medial meniscus posterior root tear using a 3‐suture construct including a barbed suture for peripheral centralization. Compared with previously described transtibial methods (Table 1), our technique offers a simplified configuration that promotes even stress distribution across the meniscal root (Figure 8).

Schematic illustration of stress distribution. Axial loading (black hollow arrows) is converted into meniscal extrusion force (black solid arrows), which is normally resisted by circumferential hoop stress (black dashed arrows). When the integrity of the meniscal root is disrupted, this load transmission fails, resulting in extrusion. In the barbed suture construct (A), the suture provides circumferential load sharing (light blue arrows). In contrast, the combined centralization and simple suture technique (B) may lead to localized stress concentration.

Barbed sutures enable easy placement and maintain uniform tension while minimizing tissue constriction. The underlying principle aligns with that of the Krackow suture technique, which was designed to resist suture cut‐through or pullout through relatively fragile tissue and to prevent constrictive effects during repair, despite the superior mechanical strength of the Krackow construct. 33

Kubota et al. reported that centralization resulted in increased stress concentration at the midbody of the meniscus compared with the intact condition and failed to adequately protect the posterior region from pressure, indicating that the procedure alone is insufficient to restore hoop stress transmission. 34

In an intact knee, stress is evenly distributed throughout the meniscus. However, in an osteoarthritic knee, greater stress is concentrated on the posterior aspect. 35 Because centralization fails to adequately absorb these posteriorly directed forces, tension can become focused on the posterior root repair site, particularly when cartilage degeneration is present. 34

The meniscus is designed to distribute hoop stress uniformly between the anterior and posterior horns, functioning much like a hammock. Therefore, circumferential suturing with a barbed suture may help achieve proper tensioning and a homogeneous stress gradient.

Similar stress concentration patterns have been observed in various orthopaedic constructs, including peri‐implant fractures in femoral fixation and type II retears in suture‐bridge rotator cuff repair. 31 , 36 , 37 Therefore, clinical focus should extend beyond rigid fixation to encompass stress distribution and load sharing.

Biomechanically, the circumferential barbed sutures function as a centralizing sling that counteracts meniscal extrusion and assists in restoring hoop tension. Nevertheless, the extent to which this construct effectively converts axial compressive loads into hoop stress remains uncertain. Further biomechanical evaluation is planned to assess fixation strength, load distribution, and cyclic displacement compared with conventional double‐tunnel constructs.

Key advantages of the technique include simplified fixation, balanced and proper tensioning, and meniscal centralization (Table 2). Surgeons should maintain appropriate tunnel trajectory and avoid overtensioning, as barbed sutures may limit postfixation adjustment. It may be less suitable for degenerative tears, in which barbed suture engagement could further compromise fragile tissue. Long‐term data on biological healing and suture durability are required.
