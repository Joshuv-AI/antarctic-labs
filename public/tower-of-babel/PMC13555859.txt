Strengthening cancer prevention in european schools: a randomized controlled trial of digital interventions in adolescents: the SUNRISE program
Authors: Maitane Barasoain, Ainara Vélez-del-Burgo, Itziar Astigarraga, Severin Haug, Nikolai Kiselev, Kleio Koutra, Vassilis Kilintzis, Maria Krini, Theophano Pampaka, Liselot Hudders, Eline De Keyzer, Polonca Serrano, Chiara Pilotti, Laura Del Campo, Delia Nicoara, Niculina Nicoara, Ana Molina-Barceló, Teresa de Pablo Pardo, Stefanie Verduyn, Gloria Cea, Chrysoula Katsapi, Lucas Javier Segal, Iraklis Tsoupouroglou, Bárbara Guerra, Andreas Triantafyllidis, Eunate Arana-Arri, the SUNRISE Study Group

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with a substantial proportion of cases attributable to modifiable lifestyle-related risk factors that emerge during adolescence and often persist into adulthood. Despite the importance of this developmental period for habit formation, evidence on scalable, theory-driven, and sustainable school-based prevention interventions remains limited. The SUNRISE project addresses this gap by evaluating a co-created, digitally supported life-skills programme designed for routine school settings.MethodsSUNRISE is a pragmatic, multinational, cluster randomised controlled trial conducted in approximately 80 primary and secondary schools across eight European countries (Greece, Switzerland, Slovenia, Spain, Cyprus, Italy, Belgium, and Romania), recruiting around 4,000 students. Classes are randomised at the class level (3:1) to either a six-month digitally enhanced life-skills intervention or standard education. The primary aim of the study is to evaluate the feasibility, implementation, and sustainability of the intervention in real-world school settings, with secondary exploratory assessment of behavioural outcomes. The intervention integrates multiple digital components, including a mobile-based life-skills coaching programme, a conversational assistant, educational and serious games, influencer-led media literacy content, moderated social interaction platforms, and teacher-led educational modules. Intervention design is guided by the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation Behaviour (COM-B) model and developed through systematic co-creation with students, educators, parents, public health experts, and policymakers within a School Living Lab framework. Feasibility, implementation, and sustainability are evaluated using the RE-AIM and PRISM frameworks. Secondary behavioural outcomes include substance use, dietary and physical activity behaviours, mental well-being, stress, social skills, critical thinking towards media and advertising, and critical coping with food-related information, assessed at baseline, 6 months, and 18 months.DiscussionSUNRISE integrates behavioural science, participatory design, and digital innovation to address adolescent health behaviours in real-world educational contexts. By embedding implementation and sustainability evaluation alongside behavioural outcomes, the study aims to generate actionable evidence on how multi-component digital interventions can be adopted, adapted, and maintained across diverse school systems. Background

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of mortality worldwide, and prevention continues to represent a major public health challenge despite substantial advances in diagnosis and treatment [1]. A significant proportion of cancer cases is preventable through the early adoption and sustained maintenance of healthy lifestyle behaviours [2]. More than one-third of cancers are attributable to modifiable risk factors, including tobacco use, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, excess body weight, and ultraviolet radiation exposure [3]. In Europe, cigarette smoking remains the leading preventable cause of cancer, accounting for approximately 20% of cases, followed by excess body weight and alcohol consumption [4].

Despite recent declines in tobacco and alcohol consumption in several European countries, tobacco use remains the leading behavioural risk factor for health, accounting for approximately 780,000 deaths annually in the European Union, while harmful alcohol use continues to cause nearly 300,000 deaths per year; at the same time, overweight and obesity remain highly prevalent and show no consistent downward trend. In 2022, 51% of adults in the European Union were classified as overweight or obese. Of particular concern is the increasing prevalence of overweight and obesity among adolescents, which rose from 17% in 2014 to 21% in 2022 [5]. This divergence underscores the need to broaden cancer prevention strategies beyond substance use and to address dietary and physical activity behaviours early in the life course.

Many behaviours associated with increased cancer risk emerge during adolescence, including substance use, unhealthy eating patterns, and physical inactivity, and tend to intensify with age [6]. Adolescence is characterised by major biological, psychological and social transitions and represents a critical period for the establishment of long-term health behaviours. Habits acquired during this developmental stage often persist into adulthood, substantially shaping future health outcomes and disease risk [7]. Consequently, adolescence represents a critical window of opportunity for effective and sustainable cancer prevention interventions.

Adolescents are particularly sensitive to social and environmental influences, including peer norms, family context, and media exposure. In recent years, digital media and social networking platforms have become central in shaping adolescents’ attitudes, beliefs, and behaviours [8]. Platforms such as WhatsApp, YouTube and TikTok provide highly engaging and interactive environments that can be leveraged to promote healthy behaviours, media literacy, and critical thinking [9]. In addition, digital modalities such as mobile applications, web-based programmes, and serious games offer scalable opportunities to support health behaviour change among young people.

Evidence indicates that digital health interventions can achieve modest but meaningful effects on health-related behaviours in children and adolescents, particularly in relation to substance use, physical activity, and mental health [10]. However, systematic reviews consistently highlight key challenges, including limited long-term engagement, variable adherence, insufficient inclusivity, and uncertainty regarding the sustainability of behaviour change over time [11]. Increasingly, research suggests that interventions explicitly grounded in established behaviour change theories and incorporating evidence-based behaviour change techniques are more likely to achieve meaningful and sustained effects [12]. Moreover, participatory and co-creation approaches involving end users—especially adolescents and educators—have been identified as critical determinants of acceptability, relevance, and implementation success [13].

The application of behaviour change theories is particularly important for complex, multi-component interventions targeting adolescents, as health-related behaviours are shaped by dynamic interactions between individual capabilities, social contexts, and motivational processes. The Capability, Opportunity, Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model provides a comprehensive and parsimonious framework for understanding and influencing behaviour by explicitly linking behavioural determinants to intervention functions and behaviour change techniques [14, 15]. COM-B is closely aligned with the Theoretical Domains Framework (TDF), which has been empirically validated for use in behaviour change and implementation research and enables a systematic assessment of barriers and facilitators to behaviour change across individual and contextual levels [16, 17]. Furthermore, theories addressing the maintenance of behaviour change highlight the importance of sustained motivation, habit formation, and supportive environments over time—factors that are explicitly captured within the COM-B structure [18, 19]. Together, these frameworks support interventions that do more than trigger short-term change; they are designed to support long-term adoption and sustainability of healthy behaviours.

The intervention draws on the COM-B model to guide both design and the selection of behaviour change techniques. A key innovative feature of SUNRISE is its systematic co-creation approach, involving students, teachers, parents, public health experts, and policymakers throughout the development, adaptation, and implementation process. All intervention activities are conducted within a School Living Lab framework, which positions schools as real-life experimental environments where stakeholders collaboratively co-design, test, and refine interventions in their everyday educational context. Previous work suggests that Living Lab approaches can improve contextual fit, stakeholder engagement, and implementation feasibility, particularly for complex social and behavioural innovations [20]. This approach is expected to strengthen ownership, implementation fidelity, and long-term sustainability across diverse educational systems and socio-cultural settings.

Methods/design

Study design and hypotheses

To verify the impact of the co-created life-skills enhanced digital education programme, a pragmatic, multinational, cluster randomised controlled trial will be conducted, with school classes as the unit of randomisation. Eligible classes of students will be randomly assigned either to the intervention group (combined educational program) or the control group (standard school education) in a 3:1 ratio. Participants in the intervention group will receive the SUNRISE combined educational program. Conversely, participants in the control group will receive education as delivered within the standard school curriculum. A six-month intervention will be conducted, followed by a six-month follow-up to assess effectiveness and an 18-month follow-up to assess sustainability. The primary hypothesis of this study is that the SUNRISE digitally supported, school-based intervention will be feasible and acceptable to implement across participating countries and educational contexts. Secondary objectives include the assessment of changes in health-related behaviours and associated psychosocial and literacy-related determinants relevant to cancer prevention.

This protocol has been developed and is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines [21] and the full checklist can be found in the Supplementary Materials (Supplementary File 1). Study participants

The study aims to recruit 4,000 primary and secondary students between 10 and 19 years old (at the time of recruitment), who are freely willing to participate in the study. This predetermined sample size allows for a comprehensive assessment of the program's feasibility and potential effects across different regions and demographic groups. Socially disadvantaged subgroups, such as ethnic minorities and migrants, will be included. The geographic distribution of the participating regions and the inclusive educational contexts targeted by the study are shown in Fig. 2.

Geographic distribution of the participating regions

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) students aged 10 years or older at the time of enrolment; (2) access to an electronic device with Internet connectivity to participate in the digital components of the intervention; (3) provision of written informed consent by the student; and (4) provision of written informed consent from a parent or legal guardian when required according to national regulations and ethical approvals.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) students younger than 10 years or outside the predefined age range for participation; (2) absence of signed informed consent from the student and/or, when applicable, from a parent or legal guardian; and (3) inability or low likelihood to complete the planned follow-up period of at least 18 months.

Each participating country will have previously identified primary and secondary schools in both rural and urban areas to introduce the study solutions, using the Eurostat degree of urbanisation classification [21] as the reference framework for this categorisation. When selecting schools for participation, specific criteria are considered to facilitate successful and sustainable engagement (see Table 1). Schools interested in participating will subsequently select the classes and courses in which they wish to implement the intervention.

Randomization, allocation, and blinding

Randomisation will be performed at the class level to reduce potential spillover and crossover effects between students within the same school, acknowledging that some exposure across classes may occur due to shared teachers and digital content. A computer-generated random sequence will be generated using R software before the start of recruitment, allocating school classes to either the intervention or control group. Allocation concealment will be ensured by having the randomisation list generated and managed by a member of the research team who is not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. Group allocation will be revealed only after eligibility criteria have been confirmed and written informed consent has been obtained from students and, where applicable, from their parents or legal guardians. Due to the nature of the educational intervention, blinding of participants and educators is not feasible. However, outcome assessment and data analysis will be conducted using anonymised data, and analysts will be blinded to group allocation whenever possible.

Study plan and intervention program

The SUNRISE intervention will be delivered within routine school settings using digital tools. The intervention is context-sensitive and will be tailored to the characteristics, infrastructure, and existing programmes of each participating centre, while maintaining a common core across countries. In line with the project’s equity-driven and participatory approach, intervention components will be co-designed and agreed upon with key stakeholders, including students, parents, educators, and public health experts, to ensure relevance, feasibility, and cultural appropriateness across diverse European contexts.

For the implementation of the project in schools, a digital platform has been developed to register all users: teachers, students, and pilot administrators in each country. This platform, henceforth referred to as the Authoring Tool, will allow schools, their classes, and students to be registered to conduct baseline and follow-up surveys, monitor and collect data, and access the platform (hereinafter referred to as the SUNRISE App) where the digital tools of the SUNRISE program will be implemented. Therefore, after signing the consent form, study participants will be registered in the Authoring Tool to complete the baseline questionnaire and access the SUNRISE App, thus enabling them to begin the SUNRISE educational program (Fig. 3). Intervention fidelity, core components, allowable adaptations, and minimum implementation criteria are detailed in an implementation checklist provided as Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 2).

Participants in the intervention groups will receive the SUNRISE programme for six months. Outcome assessments will be conducted at baseline, at the end of the intervention (6 months), and at long-term follow-up (18 months post-baseline). Long-term feasibility and sustainability will be evaluated 12 months after completion of the intervention (i.e. at month 18). Participants in the control group will complete assessments at the same time points (baseline, 6 months, and 18 months) (Fig. 1).

Adherence to the intervention protocol will be promoted through standardized training of educators and implementers, the use of detailed implementation manuals, and ongoing support from the central coordinating team. Fidelity to the intervention will be monitored using structured implementation logs, periodic monitoring forms completed by implementers, and regular follow-up meetings to identify deviations and provide corrective feedback. The intervention does not involve procedures that pose physical risk to participants. While the questionnaires include items related to stress and personal experiences, these are not expected to cause harm. Participants may skip any question or discontinue participation at any time without consequences.

Conceptual framework and implementation evaluation approach

The SUNRISE intervention was informed by established behaviour change and implementation science frameworks to ensure theoretical coherence and robust evaluation across diverse educational contexts. The intervention design was guided by the Capability–Opportunity–Motivation Behaviour (COM-B) model, which conceptualises behaviour change as the result of interactions between individuals’ capability, opportunity, and motivation [14]. COM-B informed the selection and tailoring of the digital intervention components to address key determinants of adolescent health behaviours relevant to primary cancer prevention, including health, advertising and media literacy, self-management skills, social skills, food advertising literacy and resistance to unhealthy food marketing. Table 2 presents the mapping of the SUNRISE intervention components to the COM-B domains and the targeted behavioural determinants. Implementation and sustainability were evaluated using the RE-AIM framework (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) [26], which guided the definition of implementation outcomes, indicators, and timing of assessments. To explicitly account for contextual and organisational influences on implementation and long-term integration within school settings, the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model (PRISM) was used to complement RE-AIM [27]. Outcomes and data collection

Primary and secondary evaluation indicators

The primary objective of this study is to assess the feasibility and implementation of a digitally enhanced school-based intervention aimed at promoting sustainable healthy behaviours for primary cancer prevention among adolescents in eight European countries (Greece, Switzerland, Slovenia, Spain, Cyprus, Italy, Belgium, and Romania).

Feasibility and implementation will be evaluated using predefined indicators of reach, engagement, acceptability, perceived usefulness, appropriateness of content and programme duration, and barriers and facilitators to adoption and sustainability. These indicators will be assessed through structured questionnaires completed by relevant stakeholders and analysed within the RE-AIM and PRISM frameworks.

Secondary evaluation indicators include changes in health-related behaviours relevant to cancer prevention and in key psychosocial and literacy-related determinants. Behavioural outcomes include:

Data collection and management

Trial data will be collected using standardised, electronic questionnaires administered at baseline and follow-up time points. The selection, structure, and measurement properties of all trial instruments are described in detail in the Case Report Form (CRF) (Supplementary File 3). Where applicable, validated and widely used instruments with established reliability and validity are employed. Data collection procedures are harmonised across sites through standardised protocols and training of local study staff. Data entry is performed electronically at the point of collection using predefined variable coding and built-in logic and range checks to promote data quality. All data are stored in secure, password-protected systems using pseudonymised participant identifiers.

Follow-up assessments

At follow-up visits, participants complete repeated measures of effectiveness outcomes, together with additional items assessing usability, satisfaction, and perceived value of the digital tools, to support both behavioural and implementation analyses. Participant retention will be supported through integration of study procedures into routine school activities and ongoing coordination with educators. Outcome data will be collected at scheduled time points irrespective of intervention adherence, and participants who discontinue the intervention will be followed up whenever possible.

Data sharing and access

Data generated within the study will be managed in accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and the principles of the European Health Data Space. Anonymised and aggregated datasets will be made available in open-access repositories at the end of the project, following FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable) principles. Access to data will be granted under controlled conditions, ensuring privacy protection and compliance with national and European data governance regulations.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

The planned sample size of approximately 4,000 students was determined primarily by feasibility considerations and the pragmatic, school-based clustered design of this large, multinational implementation trial. Consistent with recommendations for pragmatic and implementation-focused studies, the sample size was not based on formal power calculations for a single primary behavioural outcome, but rather on the need to ensure robust evaluation of feasibility, implementation, and sustainability outcomes across diverse real-world settings [20, 28]. To contextualise this choice, a precision-based calculation was conducted for the primary feasibility outcomes. Using the most conservative expected feasibility parameter (p = 0.50) and targeting a 95% confidence interval half-width of ± 0.03, the minimum required sample was approximately 2,135 students after accounting for class-level clustering, a 3:1 allocation ratio, and 20% anticipated loss to follow-up. The planned recruitment of 4,000 students therefore exceeds this minimum requirement, allowing for increased precision, robustness, and generalisability of estimates across countries and educational contexts [29]. Accordingly, the study is not powered to detect small between-group differences in individual behavioural outcomes, which are considered exploratory.

Data management and analysis

Trial conduct and data quality will be monitored by the coordinating consortium through predefined implementation indicators, routine data checks, and regular communication with participating sites. Data will be collected using a secure data management program to ensure confidentiality through password protection and the use of unique participant identification codes. Automated filters and logical checks will be applied to detect inconsistencies and data entry errors. All analyses will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle, including all participants in the groups to which they were originally allocated, regardless of adherence to the intervention. Qualitative variables will be described using frequency distributions, while quantitative variables will be summarized using measures of central tendency (mean or median) and variability (standard deviation or interquartile range), depending on data distribution. Between-group and within-group comparisons will be performed using parametric tests when distributional assumptions are met; otherwise, non-parametric alternatives will be applied. Comparisons of proportions will be conducted using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate. Quantitative variables will be compared using Student’s t test or ANOVA for normally distributed data, and Mann–Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normal distributions. Correlations will be assessed using Pearson or Spearman coefficients, according to data distribution. Pre-specified subgroup and sensitivity analyses may be conducted to explore the robustness of findings and potential differential effects across key participant characteristics and study contexts. Analyses of behavioural outcomes will be exploratory and intended to assess the direction and magnitude of observed changes rather than to formally test effectiveness. All statistical analyses will be performed using R software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.

Economic evaluation

Costs will be estimated from a societal perspective, and health benefits will be expressed as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs). Resource use and time investment during both the set-up and implementation phases will be measured. A Markov prediction model will be developed to estimate long-term health and economic outcomes, with uncertainty assessed using Monte Carlo simulations. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted using alternative scenarios and comparisons with control groups.

Dissemination and knowledge translation

Study findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed open-access publications, presentations to participating schools and stakeholders, and engagement with national and European public health networks. Dissemination activities will follow Horizon Europe open science and knowledge translation principles and will contribute to the EU Mission on Cancer within the Prevention and Early Detection (Behavioural Change) Cluster.

Ethical approval and clinical trial registration

Ethical approval was obtained from the competent ethics committees in each participating country. Details of ethics approvals, including approval reference numbers, are provided in Supplementary Material (Supplementary File 4). The trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov on April 9, 2025 (NCT06931847). All participants will provide informed consent obtained by research staff. Each participating centre acts as the local study sponsor in accordance with national regulations.

Discussion

Study rationale and theoretical framework

The SUNRISE project addresses a major public health challenge—the growing burden of cancer—by focusing on primary prevention during adolescence, a developmental period in which health behaviours remain malleable while being increasingly shaped by social and digital environments [2]. This developmental stage represents a critical window of opportunity, as health-related behaviours and lifestyle choices established during adolescence often persist into adulthood and substantially influence long-term disease risk [7, 8]. Promoting healthy habits at this stage can therefore play an important role in reducing future cancer risk, as well as the burden of other non-communicable diseases [30]. The project explicitly acknowledges persistent inequalities in physical activity, dietary behaviours and substance use among adolescents [6]. By addressing these disparities, SUNRISE aligns with broader public health objectives aimed at improving equity and reducing social inequalities in cancer prevention [31].

Despite the increasing number of adolescent health promotion interventions, important gaps remain in understanding how complex, theory-driven programmes can be implemented, adapted, and sustained across diverse school contexts [13, 26, 32]. Many interventions report short-term behavioural effects but provide limited evidence on feasibility, acceptability, and long-term maintenance when delivered at scale in real-world educational systems [13, 26].

The SUNRISE study was designed to address this gap by integrating behavioural theory with implementation science. The intervention is informed by the Capability, Opportunity and Motivation–Behaviour (COM-B) model, which guides the selection of intervention components and behaviour change techniques [14], while implementation and sustainability are examined using the RE-AIM and PRISM frameworks [26, 27]. This combined approach allows assessment not only of behavioural outcomes but also of contextual factors influencing delivery, adoption, and long-term viability across different European settings.

Innovations in intervention design

SUNRISE introduces an integrated, multi-component intervention that combines structured life-skills education with digital tools and school-based activities. Rather than deploying isolated digital solutions, the programme brings together educational modules, interactive digital content, social communication strategies, and educator-led activities within a single intervention framework [33].

A central design feature is the balance between standardisation and local adaptation. Contextual adaptation across countries is treated as an object of analysis rather than as uncontrolled variability, consistent with implementation science principles. Educators are supported by authoring and monitoring tools that allow content and delivery to be adjusted to school-specific needs while preserving core intervention principles. In addition, the intervention is developed and refined through a systematic co-creation process involving students, teachers, parents, and public health professionals, implemented within a School Living Lab approach, which has been shown to enhance relevance, feasibility, and stakeholder engagement [34].

Potential impact on life skills for healthy habits in adolescents

The intervention focuses on strengthening transversal life skills that support healthy decision-making across multiple behavioural domains, including critical thinking, media literacy, self-regulation, and goal setting [35]. By targeting these skills, SUNRISE supports adolescents in navigating complex food, physical activity, and digital environments.

Improving these competencies may facilitate sustained healthy behaviours beyond the intervention period and across different life situations [18]. At the population level, this approach has the potential to contribute to cancer prevention while also supporting broader non-communicable disease prevention and reducing behavioural health inequalities among adolescents [28].

Limitations

This study has several limitations.

The study is not designed to isolate the independent effects of individual intervention components, but rather to assess the feasibility and implementation of an integrated intervention in real-world school settings.

Engagement with intervention components may vary over time, which could influence intervention exposure and outcomes. The study relies on self-reported measures for key behaviours, which are subject to recall and social desirability bias.

Differences in digital access, school infrastructure and institutional readiness across countries may affect the consistency of implementation. Although contextual adaptation is a planned feature of the intervention, it may also introduce variability in delivery. Resistance from parents, guardians, or educators regarding the use of digital technologies may limit participation in some settings; concerns and reasons for non-participation will therefore be systematically documented.

Finally, cancer prevention activities in younger populations require careful ethical framing. The intervention emphasises general health and wellbeing rather than disease risk, uses age-appropriate communication, and includes safeguards to minimise anxiety, particularly for students with a family history of cancer.

Conclusions

SUNRISE provides a theory-driven and implementation-focused approach to adolescent cancer prevention in European school settings. By integrating behavioural science, digital tools, and participatory implementation strategies, the study focuses to generate practical evidence on how sustainable and equitable health promotion interventions can be delivered at scale. If SUNRISE is shown to be feasible and sustainable, this approach and digital tools may inform how European school systems could integrate digital, theory-driven health promotion into routine practice.
