ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe therapeutic goal in chronic airway diseases is shifting from symptom control to disease remission. Disease‐modifying therapies, including biologics and allergen immunotherapy, have made remission achievable in patients with severe asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), or allergic rhinitis (AR). This EUFOREA consensus aims to establish practical guidance for inducing and maintaining remission in global airway diseases. An international panel of experts in pneumology, rhinology, and allergology convened in Rome (October 2025) to review current evidence and develop consensus statements. The panel achieved consensus on key principles: (i) remission is a therapeutic target independent of disease severity prior to treatment initiation and should not be reserved for severe cases; (ii) CRSwNP with nonallergic eosinophilic asthma, and AR with allergic asthma should be considered features of a single disease rather than comorbidities; (iii) remission should be assessed by each subspecialty separately while warranting combined approaches; (iv) pragmatic definitions prioritizing achievability and clinical utility are recommended; and (v) a 4‐week recall window is preferred to assess symptom control within the evaluation of remission and a 12‐month period is suggested as the minimal period to define remission. Remission represents an ambitious yet achievable goal, with practical guidance for optimizing patient outcomes.

Introduction

Chronic airways diseases, including asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) and allergic rhinitis (AR), affect millions of people worldwide and place a substantial burden on patients, healthcare systems and payers [1, 2]. Traditional, control‐based management has largely relied on stepwise escalation of treatment, often addressing only one part of the respiratory tract [3] and failing to optimally prevent long‐term morbidity and the development of irreversible sequelae.

Advances in disease‐modifying therapies, particularly biologics targeting type 2 inflammation and allergen immunotherapy (AIT), now make remission an achievable goal across the spectrum of global airway diseases [4]. These treatment options have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in achieving (sustained) symptom control, preventing exacerbations and reducing the need for systemic corticosteroids in patients with severe disease [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Real‐world data from international registries confirm that clinical remission is attainable in around 37% of patients with severe asthma [13] and up to > 60% if inflammometry (i.e., measurement of inflammatory biomarkers, e.g., FeNO and eosinophils) and imaging modalities are combined to select treatment [14], as well as in up to 45.7% of patients with severe CRSwNP treated with biologics [15]. In contrast, long‐term follow‐up (> 5 years) of CRS patients after endoscopic sinus surgery in a tertiary academic center showed that 25.2% achieved controlled disease according to EPOS criteria, with 26.8% partly controlled and 48.0% uncontrolled, particularly in patients with asthma, NSAID‐exacerbated respiratory disease (N‐ERD), or revision surgery [16]. Furthermore, AIT initiated within the optimal time window has been shown to induce disease modification with sustained clinical benefits after treatment cessation in AR patients [17, 18] and prevent asthma onset [17].

Remission as primary treatment goal is urgently needed considering the widespread prevalence of uncontrolled patients, often treated by multiple caregivers with limited co‐ordination resulting in (i) over‐reliance on oral corticosteroids (OCS) both in acute and chronic care [19], (ii) symptom‐driven escalation of treatment burden, and (iii) a lack of de‐escalation protocols including for high‐dose ICS, which can cause systemic side‐effects [20]. Most of these challenges are applicable for both upper and lower airway diseases and warrant a unified approach [3]. Furthermore, an approach which draws inspiration from successful remission frameworks in rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease, prioritizing the prevention of irreversible organ damage, would be helpful for the respiratory and rhinology communities [21]. This European Forum for Research and Education in Allergy and Airway Diseases (EUFOREA) consensus document aims to provide pragmatic, consensus‐based definitions of remission and practical guidance to help clinicians implement remission‐focused strategies in routine care.

Methods

An international, multidisciplinary expert group convened in Rome on October 6, 2025, under the auspices of EUFOREA to develop consensus statements on remission in global airway diseases, encompassing asthma, chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP), and allergic rhinitis. The project was designed as an in‐person consensus meeting with a panel of external readers, with predefined steps for evidence review, statement drafting, voting, and iterative refinement.

Experts were part of the EUFOREA Expert Panels and are experts in the field of allergology, pneumology, and/or rhinology, ensuring representation from both upper and lower airway subspecialties and from multiple continents (Europe, North America, Middle East). Two medical writers took structured notes of the discussion and of voting during the consensus meeting.

In anticipation, the three Chairs (JCV, PR, WJF) mapped the relevant literature on disease control and remission in allergic rhinitis, asthma, and CRSwNP, prioritizing randomized controlled trials, real‐world registries, and international guideline documents. A particular emphasis was placed on studies reporting multidomain outcomes (symptoms, exacerbations, systemic corticosteroid use, lung function, endoscopy, smell, and quality of life) and on data for biologics and AIT as potential disease‐modifying treatments. Key evidence was presented to the full panel through plenary lectures and proposed definitions were circulated to enable informed discussion during the in‐person meeting. The consensus meeting was joined by a patient representative from the EUFOREA Patient Advisory Board to integrate the patient perspective where necessary.

Scope

The primary aim of the consensus meeting was to discuss the concept of clinical remission, defined by symptom control, reduced frequency of exacerbations, increase or stabilization of lung function and improved sinonasal function especially gain of sense of smell, in the context of global airway diseases. In particular, practical guidance for clinicians and researchers was prioritized. Biological (or pathophysiological) remission, defined by normalization of inflammatory and/or structural endpoints (e.g., normalization of bronchial hyperresponsiveness or tissue inflammatory markers), was discussed as a secondary aim, recognizing that complete remission combines clinical and biological criteria; specific biomarker thresholds for biological remission across blood eosinophils, FeNO, nasal NO, type 2 transcriptomic signatures, sinonasal CT/MR imaging, endoscopy and bronchial hyperresponsiveness were beyond the scope of the present consensus and are likely to require dedicated follow‐up work.

The framework primarily addresses type 2‐driven phenotypes, encompassing allergic and nonallergic eosinophilic asthma, CRSwNP, and allergic rhinitis; specific conditions with distinct pathophysiology, namely allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis/mycosis (ABPA/ABPM) and eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA), are outside its scope, while NSAID‐exacerbated respiratory disease (N‐ERD) is included as a phenotype of combined CRSwNP and asthma. We do not assert that all patients with CRSwNP or allergic rhinitis have clinically diagnosed asthma; rather, we recognize a shared type 2 inflammatory process and recommend systematic assessment of the other airway compartment.

Remission as a Universal Therapeutic Target

Statement 1 The primary goal of treatment in global airway diseases should be to induce remission with minimal therapeutic burden and subsequently maintain remission at the lowest effective treatment level, while monitoring inflammation.

The primary goal of treatment in global airway diseases should be to induce remission with minimal therapeutic burden and subsequently maintain remission at the lowest effective treatment level, while monitoring inflammation.

Inducing remission and maintaining remission represent distinct therapeutic phases requiring different strategies [22]. Initial therapy should aim to achieve the best symptom control and functional status of upper and lower airways, after which treatment should be systematically optimized to maintain remission with the fewest medications and the minimally effective dose of safer local therapies, while monitoring inflammation. Treatment‐burden reduction is a feature of the maintenance‐of‐remission phase, prioritizing tapering or discontinuing drugs with the highest systemic risk, highest cost, and lowest likelihood of ongoing benefit, in particular OCS and high‐dose inhaled corticosteroids; this should be guided by individual response and undertaken with structured monitoring, recognizing that prospective evidence on biologic tapering and discontinuation in remission is currently limited. “Bridging corticosteroids,” defined as short‐term systemic corticosteroid use to induce remission while a disease‐modifying therapy takes effect (by analogy with rheumatology practice for DMARDs), can be used if necessary to induce remission but should be avoided during the maintenance of remission; recurrent bridging courses are incompatible with the maintenance phase of remission and should prompt reevaluation of the disease‐modifying strategy. Bridging corticosteroids are conceptually distinct from short‐course OCS for acute exacerbations and from chronic OCS for maintenance [23]. Recent evidence indicates that even modest cumulative systemic corticosteroid exposure (0.5–1 g prednisolone‐equivalent, equating to roughly 2–5 lifetime short courses of 200 mg) significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis, pneumonia and other adverse outcomes [24], reinforcing the imperative to minimize OCS burden once remission is achieved (Figure 1).Statement 2 In the absence of evidence‐based de‐escalation protocols after achieving remission, a pragmatic approach should prioritize reducing medications with the highest potential to cause harm and the lowest likelihood of ongoing benefit.

Disease‐modifying therapies are defined as any drug class or intervention that can potentially achieve sustained remission [4] by addressing underlying disease mechanisms, both on‐ and off‐treatment. This definition includes biologics targeting type 2 inflammation and AIT. Notably, current evidence for biologics relates primarily to on‐treatment remission, whereas allergen immunotherapy is currently the only strategy demonstrating the ability to induce sustained off‐treatment remission through disease modification. Short‐acting and long‐acting bronchodilators are excluded as they provide symptomatic relief without strong evidence for modifying underlying inflammation. Topical corticosteroids (inhaled or intranasal) are mainstay controller therapy. While endoscopic sinus surgery (ESS) is aimed at relieving sinonasal symptoms, it has also been shown to reduce exacerbations in asthma and decrease risk of developing asthma, likely through interference with the inflammatory cycle, suggesting that timely ESS can interfere with the progression of inflammatory changes in the lower airways and should be considered disease modifying [25]. Long‐term, recurrent or chronic oral corticosteroid use is excluded from the disease‐modifying therapy classification because (i) chronic OCS suppresses inflammation symptomatically without addressing underlying disease mechanisms, (ii) cumulative exposure carries dose‐dependent harms, and (iii) chronic OCS “modifies the patient” rather than achieving sustainable disease modification. Short‐term systemic corticosteroids retain a role for acute exacerbations and for bridging during induction of remission (Statement 1) [26].

Where prospective data are lacking, treatment reduction should prioritize tapering or discontinuing drugs with the highest systemic risk and lowest likelihood of ongoing benefit, in particular high‐dose inhaled corticosteroids, while preserving the minimal effective dose of safer local therapies. High‐dose inhaled corticosteroids, known for systemic exposure, should be the first consideration for dose reduction after systemic corticosteroids. Nasal corticosteroid treatment is in general safe, except for highly bioavailable intranasal corticosteroids (e.g., budesonide irrigation, betamethasone nasal drops), although evidence of long‐term side effects is lacking [25].

In the absence of prospective data, the optimal duration of biologic therapy in patients achieving remission remains unknown and is a research priority. Current evidence (including post‐discontinuation data after dupilumab in CRSwNP [9, 15, 27] and the systematic review by Rank et al. 2023 in asthma) suggests significant relapse risk after discontinuation, and no validated biomarker currently predicts which patients can safely de‐escalate. Twelve months of treatment is likely insufficient to attain remission off treatment in the responding patient population, with significant relapse rates upon discontinuation, especially in asthma patients [9, 28]. For allergen immunotherapy, 3–5 years of treatment is the currently established practice for achieving off‐treatment remission [29, 30].

The optimal biomarker thresholds for monitoring patients in remission remain to be established. In asthma, elevated blood eosinophil counts and FeNO have been shown to independently predict asthma exacerbations, with the combination of both markers conferring greater risk than either alone [31, 32, 33], though their applicability and thresholds in patients in remission remain to be determined. In CRS, endoscopy remains the primary tool for assessing upper airway disease activity, with CT imaging providing complementary information on disease extent [24]. Other noninvasive biomarkers such as blood eosinophil counts and nasal nitric oxide have weaker evidence. Validated tools to predict relapse are currently lacking. Prospective studies are needed to identify specific biomarker thresholds that indicate impending relapse across one or both airway compartments.Statement 3 Remission is a therapeutic target independent of disease severity prior to treatment initiation and should not be reserved only for severe cases.

Remission is a therapeutic target independent of disease severity prior to treatment initiation and should not be reserved only for severe cases.

The concept of disease severity is often conflated with treatment burden, creating a paradox where patients achieving remission on biologics often remain classified as “severe.” Remission is independent of treatment burden and therefore a more useful concept of disease state than severity [34]. Evidence from real‐world registries demonstrates that remission is achievable across the spectrum of disease severity when appropriate disease‐modifying therapies are employed [35].

The panel did not reach consensus on defining cure in global airway diseases, though sustained off‐treatment remission for ≥ 5 years, as defined before [36], was re‐confirmed as practical threshold, and currently used as highest ambition in chronic respiratory disease (CRD) care. Off‐treatment remission (e.g., with allergen immunotherapy concept) occurs when clinical benefits and immunological changes persist beyond active treatment, representing the closest achievable state to cure.

Rethinking Global Airway Disease (GAD) Phenotypes: From Comorbidity to Clinical Spectrum

Statement 4 Nonallergic eosinophilic asthma and CRSwNP represent manifestations of the same disease in different parts of the respiratory tract, and allergic rhinitis and allergic asthma are best understood as a clinical continuum rather than as independent comorbidities.

Nonallergic eosinophilic asthma and CRSwNP represent manifestations of the same disease in different parts of the respiratory tract, and allergic rhinitis and allergic asthma are best understood as a clinical continuum rather than as independent comorbidities.

The co‐occurrence of CRSwNP and nonallergic eosinophilic asthma, often times late‐onset, reflects shared type 2 inflammatory pathophysiology rather than coexisting but independent diseases [37, 38]. In practice, this should translate to seeking the presence of both as part of the general assessment in all patients. Among patients with asthma, a substantial majority report upper‐airway involvement, and isolated lower‐airway disease is uncommon [39]. Among patients with CRSwNP, concomitant asthma is frequent but not universal: approximately half of patients with late‐onset eosinophilic asthma have CRSwNP, and a substantial subgroup of CRSwNP patients present without clinically diagnosed asthma [39]. Among patients with allergic rhinitis, approximately 60%–80% do not have current clinically diagnosed asthma, although active follow‐up for incident asthma and primary prevention through allergen immunotherapy, where indicated, remain priorities in this group [3, 39, 40].

Within this continuum, three clinical positions can be distinguished: diagnosed global airway disease, where both upper and lower airway diagnoses are formally established and the unified‐airway framework applies most directly; subclinical or pre‐clinical global airway involvement in patients with CRSwNP or allergic rhinitis who do not meet formal diagnostic criteria for asthma but show evidence of underlying lower‐airway inflammation (e.g., bronchial hyperresponsiveness, elevated FeNO, sputum eosinophilia, reduced FEF25‐75) and vice versa; and heterogeneity outside this scope, where non‐T2 asthma (neutrophilic, paucigranulocytic, obesity‐associated) and CRS without type 2 inflammation may not fit the unified construct.

Recognition of this combined disease spectrum has profound implications for earlier intervention, appropriate therapy selection, and improved preventive strategies as well as combined treatment approaches of different specialties such as pneumologists and otorhinolaryngologists. The united airways concept is well‐established for allergic disease and indeed supports earlier consideration of disease‐modifying interventions such as AIT for both upper and lower airway manifestations, including its prevention.Statement 5 In the clinical and research settings, evaluation of both upper and lower airway outcomes must be a minimum standard [37, 38].

In the clinical and research settings, evaluation of both upper and lower airway outcomes must be a minimum standard [37, 38].

With consensus to move beyond the term “comorbidity” in global airway diseases, the practical implication is that organ‐specific manifestations of disease are part of a bigger clinical picture. Hence, actively searching for evidence of disease in both parts of the respiratory tract should be an integral part of the assessment and selection of appropriate medical treatment.

Defining Remission in CRSwNP

Statement 6 Clinical remission in CRSwNP comprises four core domains: (i) sustained disease control for ≥ 12 months (on or off treatment); (ii) absence of exacerbations (defined as temporary worsening of sinonasal symptoms requiring rescue treatment); (iii) absence of signs of active mucosal disease evaluated by nasal endoscopy; and (iv) no use of systemic corticosteroids in the preceding 12 months. Pragmatic endoscopic criteria for remission in CRSwNP should define absence of active disease as a nasal polyp score of maximum 1 on each side and absence of colored secretions and/or inflamed mucosa.

Clinical remission in CRSwNP comprises four core domains: (i) sustained disease control for ≥ 12 months (on or off treatment); (ii) absence of exacerbations (defined as temporary worsening of sinonasal symptoms requiring rescue treatment); (iii) absence of signs of active mucosal disease evaluated by nasal endoscopy; and (iv) no use of systemic corticosteroids in the preceding 12 months. Pragmatic endoscopic criteria for remission in CRSwNP should define absence of active disease as a nasal polyp score of maximum 1 on each side and absence of colored secretions and/or inflamed mucosa.

This pragmatic threshold balances ambition with achievability [41]. While complete absence of polyps represents the ideal, minimal residual polyps (≤ 1 per side) in the context of symptom control and stable disease reflect clinically meaningful remission.Statement 7 For irreversible sequelae such as smell loss, a pragmatic approach utilizing personal best achievable goals is recommended rather than population‐level standards.

For irreversible sequelae such as smell loss, a pragmatic approach utilizing personal best achievable goals is recommended rather than population‐level standards.

Smell loss and structural sinonasal damage may be partially or completely irreversible, so the threshold‐based remission criteria of Statement 6 cannot be applied uniformly to these domains. Patients should be assessed on their ability to regain smell function relative to their personal potential, recognizing that prolonged disease and prior systemic corticosteroid exposure affect recovery potential [42]. Irreversible smell loss can be a sequalae of long (insufficiently treated) disease that prevents reaching remission. Real‐world evidence demonstrates that 45.7% of patients with severe CRSwNP treated with dupilumab achieve remission at 12 months and more than half of the other patients were prevented from reaching remission because of loss of smell [15, 27]. We, therefore, recommend a “personal best” approach for smell and other irreversible domains, with patients evaluated against their own achievable optimum rather than population‐level normative thresholds. The same principle is applied to lung function in Statement 9.

Defining Remission in Asthma

Statement 8 Clinical remission in asthma comprises four core domains: (i) sustained symptom control (for clinical study purposes e.g., ACT ≥ 20 or ACQ < 0.75); (ii) sustained absence of exacerbations; (iii) stable lung function optimized to personal best; and (iv) no use of systemic corticosteroids in the preceding 12 months.

Clinical remission in asthma comprises four core domains: (i) sustained symptom control (for clinical study purposes e.g., ACT ≥ 20 or ACQ < 0.75); (ii) sustained absence of exacerbations; (iii) stable lung function optimized to personal best; and (iv) no use of systemic corticosteroids in the preceding 12 months.

This four‐component definition, which was defined earlier elsewhere by Lommatzsch, Virchow [43] and aligns with a large Delphi consensus on asthma remission [44], reflects a useful and evidence‐based definition. More stringent six‐component definitions have demonstrated low attainment in real‐world cohorts, undermining clinical utility [45]. A personal‐best definition for stable lung function following attempts to optimize pulmonary function is preferrable.Statement 9 Lung function criteria in defining remission should prioritize personal best with ≤ 5% variation rather than population‐based thresholds (e.g., FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted).

Lung function criteria in defining remission should prioritize personal best with ≤ 5% variation rather than population‐based thresholds (e.g., FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted).

A significant proportion of patients with well‐controlled, nonactive asthma cannot achieve 80% predicted FEV1 due to fixed airway remodeling, obesity, or other factors [46]. The goal is optimization and stability at the patient's achievable best, with changes ≤ 5% from personal optimal considered clinically acceptable due to variation in spirometry [47]. Real‐world evidence from the International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR) demonstrates that 33.5% of patients with severe asthma achieve a three‐component remission definition (no exacerbations, no oral corticosteroids, well/partly controlled) at 12 months of biologic therapy [45]. When lung function criteria (FEV1 ≥ 80% predicted) are added, remission rates decrease to 25.8%, highlighting the impact of fixed airway obstruction and the importance of personal best criteria [45]. Analysis from the UK Severe Asthma Registry identified key predictors of remission: male sex, never smoking, BMI < 30 kg/m2, shorter disease duration, high type 2 biomarkers (eosinophils, FeNO), and lower pre‐treatment symptom burden [48]. Long‐term longitudinal data further indicate that asthma remission can be maintained over years, while persistent type 2 inflammation, small airway dysfunction, and airflow limitation characterize those who fail to sustain remission [49, 50].

Assessment Tools and Practical Timeframes

Statement 10 A global airways control test should preferably not include more than 15 questions and have a 4‐week recall window.

A global airways control test should preferably not include more than 15 questions and have a 4‐week recall window.

Although 1 and 2‐week windows are often used in clinical research (e.g., ACQ) [51]. A 4‐week timeframe improves feasibility in clinical practice and is consistent with validated tools such as the Asthma Control Test (ACT) [52] and the EPOS control test [25], and better reflects chronic disease patterns. Regulatory authorities, including FDA, should consider adopting 4‐week windows for chronic disease assessment.

The ≤ 15‐item ceiling and 4‐week recall window are pragmatic, consensus‐based targets agreed by panel vote, balancing comprehensive coverage of upper‐ and lower‐airway domains against the response burden that limits routine uptake of longer instruments. For future global airways control tests, patient reported outcome instruments should not include more than 15 questions to ensure feasibility in daily care while still capturing key symptom, exacerbation, and QoL‐burden domains relevant to remission. In practice, ACT is preferred over ACQ for this reason and because of more widespread integration in electronic health records.Statement 11 A 12‐month sustained control period is required to define remission, accounting for seasonal symptom variation.

A 12‐month sustained control period is required to define remission, accounting for seasonal symptom variation.

Seasonal variation may not be captured by remission definitions considering sub‐12 months' time frames. Furthermore, evidence from trials demonstrates that 6 months of control is insufficient to confirm sustained remission, as relapse rates remain significant [22].Statement 12 Symptom control tools should reflect disease‐specific symptoms, where possible, to avoid unnecessary constraints in achieving remission.

Symptom control tools should reflect disease‐specific symptoms, where possible, to avoid unnecessary constraints in achieving remission.

Symptoms from nonrespiratory comorbidities, such as breathlessness related to obesity or heart failure rather than asthma should not prevent remission classification. Validated, disease‐specific tools should be prioritized [53]. Recommended disease‐specific instruments include SNOT‐22 for CRSwNP, ACT or ACQ for asthma, and CARAT for combined upper‐ and lower‐airway assessment, each applied with a 4‐week recall window where feasible. Statement 10's ≤ 15‐item, 4‐week target applies specifically to future global airway control instruments designed to assess upper and lower airway domains together; it does not preclude the use of validated, disease‐specific tools (such as SNOT‐22 with its 22 items and variable recall time) within their established single‐domain scope.

Practical Remission Strategies

Statement 13 Patients require proper diagnosis and phenotyping before initiating therapy to avoid unnecessary stepwise treatment escalation.

Patients require proper diagnosis and phenotyping before initiating therapy to avoid unnecessary stepwise treatment escalation.

Treatable traits can be a useful concept after correct diagnosis and phenotyping [54], and should not be a rationale to omit the latter. Predictive biomarkers (blood eosinophils (for asthma), FeNO (for asthma), total IgE) and small airway dysfunction [55] may guide appropriate selection of disease‐modifying therapies [56].Statement 14 Some interventions can have collateral efficacy across upper and lower airways (biologics, AIT, surgery), offering advantages for patients with manifestations in both anatomical compartments.

Some interventions can have collateral efficacy across upper and lower airways (biologics, AIT, surgery), offering advantages for patients with manifestations in both anatomical compartments.

Given shared treatment goals (reducing oral corticosteroid use, preventing exacerbations), simultaneous assessment of remission in both airways compartments is essential and should favor treatment of both parts simultaneously or therapeutic options addressing the shared background inflammation [5, 7, 9, 11, 12].Statement 15 Remission should be evaluated from both a subspecialty (rhinology and pneumonology) and a global airway perspective.

Remission should be evaluated from both a subspecialty (rhinology and pneumonology) and a global airway perspective.

The timing of achieving remission may differ between upper and lower airways, with uncertainty regarding plateau phases in each compartment. The current definitions require assessment of remission by each subspecialty and favor parallel monitoring with the aim of secondary and tertiary prevention.

Unmet Needs and Research Priorities

The panel identified critical knowledge gaps requiring targeted research (Table 2). Critical research priorities include identifying predictive biomarkers for disease activity, determining optimal treatment duration and tapering strategies, and investigating whether earlier intervention in moderate combined airways disease can prevent progression to severe disease. Prospective international registries collecting standardized data across both airways' compartments will be essential to address these questions.

Research should investigate whether moderate CRSwNP + nonallergic eosinophilic asthma warrants earlier consideration of biologic therapy to prevent disease progression. Similarly, combined AR + allergic asthma should prompt earlier consideration of allergen immunotherapy. Considering this, it would be useful to know if the presence of manifestations in both airway compartments may identify patients at higher risk for severe disease development.

The optimal timing for intervention to achieve remission and potentially prevent disease progression requires urgent research. Current healthcare economics may not support biologic therapy for all eligible patients, but defining the optimal window for intervention represents a critical research priority.

Limitations

This consensus is intended as pragmatic, multidisciplinary guidance to operationalize a shared remission concept across global airway diseases, not as evidence‐graded recommendations in the GRADE sense. The strength of the underlying evidence varies by domain. The clinical‐domain definitions of remission (Statements 6 and 8) are supported by prospective data, including real‐world remission rates with biologics in severe asthma and CRSwNP and the recent Delphi consensus on asthma remission, and by registry data on disease control. By contrast, several recommendations the panel considered clinically essential rest on expert consensus in the absence of high‐level evidence: specifically (i) the timing and sequencing of treatment de‐escalation once remission is achieved, (ii) the optimal duration of biologic therapy and the criteria for taper or discontinuation, and (iii) the biomarker thresholds that would define biological (as distinct from clinical) remission. Where panel‐voted thresholds were used (e.g., the ≤ 15‐item, 4‐week target in Statement 10), this is stated explicitly.

The framework is, therefore, intended as a starting point that should be revisited and refined as prospective tapering studies, biomarker‐guided de‐escalation trials, and long‐term registry data accumulate, and as dedicated follow‐up work on biological‐remission thresholds matures.

Conclusion

Remission in global airway diseases represents a paradigm shift from short‐term symptom control to long‐term disease modification and warrants a multidisciplinary approach. This EUFOREA consensus paper provides practical, consensus‐based definitions of remission in global airway disease. It recognizes the unity of upper and lower airway disease in specific phenotypes and establishes a framework and unmet needs for achieving remission with minimal treatment burden. Key takeaways for clinicians, healthcare systems, and researchers are summarized in Table 3. By adopting remission as a primary therapeutic target, healthcare providers can improve patient outcomes, reduce treatment burden, and move closer to the goal of arresting the epidemic of CRDs.
