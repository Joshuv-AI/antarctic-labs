A quantitative assessment of dialectal variation in a linguistic transition zone: spatial insights from vernacular bird names in southeastern Spain
Authors: Mariano Paracuellos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the factors underlying dialectal variation across culturally and environmentally heterogeneous territories remains a major challenge in geolinguistic and ethnobiological research. The province of Almería (southeastern Spain) has often been described as a linguistic transition zone within the Hispanic domain, where features of eastern Andalusian and Murcian converge as a consequence of its historical trajectory and geographical position. However, quantitative assessments of this characterization remain limited.MethodsThis study examined whether similarities in vernacular bird names and named bird species reveal geographically structured dialectal differentiation within Almería and whether the resulting spatial patterns could be interpreted in relation to socio-cultural or geo-environmental variation. Vernacular ornithonyms constituted the empirical basis of the analyses. Quantitative approaches were applied to a comprehensive dataset of popular denominations recorded across the seven comarcas under an exploratory framework informed by previous dialectological, ethnobiological and territorial evidence.ResultsThe distribution of vernacular names revealed a spatial differentiation between southwestern and northeastern comarca groupings, broadly consistent with previously described dialectal affinities associated with neighbouring Granada and Murcia and their dominant varieties, eastern Andalusian and Murcian. This pattern may partly reflect the influence of the Filabres Mountains as a structuring feature affecting interaction and cultural diffusion. In parallel, named bird species revealed a distinct coastal–inland differentiation among comarcas, interpreted primarily in relation to habitat heterogeneity, biodiversity structure and geographic isolation. Coastal areas tended to show higher richness of named species, whereas inland areas exhibited greater linguistic diversity per species, likely associated with ecological heterogeneity, historical connectivity, territorial interaction and processes of cultural transmission and local ecological knowledge.ConclusionAlthough causal inference is not possible, the results suggest that dialectal variation may be associated with interacting socio-cultural processes linked to cultural transmission and territorial interaction together with geo-environmental factors related to habitat heterogeneity, biodiversity and geographic isolation. These findings highlight the usefulness of vernacular nomenclature for examining relationships between linguistic variation, territorial interaction, cultural transmission and environmental context. However, the two spatial patterns identified are likely shaped by interacting ecological, socio-cultural and territorial processes whose relative contributions cannot be fully disentangled and therefore warrant further investigation.Keywords: Vernacular bird names, Dialectal variation, Ethnobiology, Biocultural relationships, Geolinguistics, Cultural transmission, Linguistic transition zones, Southeastern Spain

Background

Cultural manifestations constitute an essential component of the identity of human societies, as they are recognised by communities themselves as fundamental elements of their heritage and historical continuity [82]. Among these, speech occupies a particularly relevant position, not only because of its communicative function, but also due to its role as a cultural practice in the configuration and transmission of shared meanings, as well as its condition as a marker of collective identity, placing it among the principal sociocultural referents of human communities [19, 50]. In this context, the study of linguistic variation within a given territory is widely used to explore historical processes of migration, settlement and cultural contact, as well as to identify dialect areas and transition zones between neighbouring communities [11, 52, 77, 79, 89]. Furthermore, its scope extends beyond the strictly philological domain, as it enables such variation to be linked to the formation of local identities and the preservation of intangible cultural heritage [82].

Linguistic expressions do not develop independently of the environment in which they are embedded, but are often closely associated with the ecological and geographical contexts in which human communities evolve. In this sense, environmental heterogeneity may contribute to spatial variation in lexical systems and forms of expression among communities [44, 53]. From the perspective of eco-linguistics and ethno-biology, it has been shown that the distribution of languages and biodiversity exhibits a significant degree of statistical congruence, reflecting broad associations between cultural and ecological systems [29, 33, 41, 45]. In particular, territorial characteristics such as landscape heterogeneity, resource availability, or biological community structure can be associated with variation in the richness and specialisation of the lexicon, especially in domains closely linked to everyday experience, such as vernacular names of species [7, 34]. Within this framework, language can be understood as an adaptive system that reflects and encodes the ecological knowledge accumulated by communities over time [20]. Therefore, the analysis of linguistic variation in specific geographical contexts benefits from the incorporation of environmental variables as explanatory factors, enabling a more integrated approach to the processes of linguistic diversification and their relationship with territory, biodiversity and culture [44, 55].

Since the earliest stages of human language and expression, birds have been among the biological groups that have attracted the greatest attention from human societies, which has favoured their incorporation into cultural practices and narratives [78]. Within the field of ethno-ornithology, a discipline concerned with the relationships between humans and birds, approaches have been developed that focus on the analysis of vernacular naming systems used to identify species in folklore, thereby establishing vernacular bird names (ornithonyms) as analytical units [7, 78]. These nomenclatures are embedded in everyday language and cultural transmission processes within human communities, and therefore constitute a potentially valuable source of information for exploring spatial patterns of linguistic expression and local ecological knowledge [4, 7, 30, 35]. In this context, the province of Almería (Spain) has been investigated through a comprehensive catalogue of vernacular ornithonyms recently compiled [63, 64], providing a basis for further analysis in the cultural characterisation of local societies (Paracuellos, manuscript under review).

From a linguistic perspective, Almería has been widely described as a transition zone within the Hispanic domain, in which features of the eastern Andalusian and Murcian traditions converge, largely as a result of its historical trajectory and geographical position [2, 3, 5, 57]. However, although there is an extensive body of literature addressing this characterisation from a predominantly qualitative and discursive perspective, the quantitative validation of these assumptions remains relatively limited [2, 3, 61, 66, 87, 88]. This highlights the need for integrative approaches that combine the interpretation of such processes with the support of statistical tools. In this regard, although it has been widely recognised that dialect boundaries cannot usually be defined by strict limits, but rather by gradual transition zones or continua [11, 39, 79, 89], the geospatial analysis of internal gradients in dialectal influences within Almería, as well as their potential relationship with patterns of territorial cultural differentiation, remains insufficiently explored.

The aforementioned sociolinguistic complexity is further compounded by the pronounced environmental contrast that characterises Almería. In this sense, its territory exhibits high physiographic heterogeneity, ranking among the most rugged provinces and, at the same time, among those with the longest coastlines in peninsular Spain [26, 37]. This marked altitudinal and ecological gradient, which over relatively short distances connects arid coastal environments with some of the highest mountain systems on the Iberian Peninsula, creates a particularly favourable setting for the diversification of human occupation [10, 25] and, potentially, of forms of expression associated with the territory [35, 81].

The present study uses vernacular ornithonyms as a quantitative indicator and analytical proxy to assess fine-scale dialectal variation in the province of Almería and to evaluate whether previous qualitative interpretations describing this territory as a linguistic transition zone can be supported empirically. Specifically, the study addresses two main questions: (i) whether patterns of similarity in vernacular bird names and named bird species reveal geographically structured dialectal differentiation within Almería; and (ii) whether the resulting spatial patterns can be interpreted in relation to socio-cultural processes linked to neighbouring linguistic areas or to geo-environmental variation across the territory. Based on previous dialectological, geographical and ethnobiological evidence, it was expected that vernacular ornithonyms would reflect spatially structured patterns of linguistic affinity associated with both historical-cultural connectivity and environmental heterogeneity. However, given the complexity of the study system, the analyses were approached within an exploratory and hypothesis-guided framework aimed at identifying spatial patterns and their possible socio-cultural and geo-environmental associations. In this context, the study evaluates the potential of vernacular nomenclature for examining spatial relationships between linguistic variation, cultural transmission and environmental context within a dialectal transition zone. Although this approach does not allow direct causal inference, it enables an integrated perspective on relationships between language, territory and human communities, while highlighting the value of vernacular nomenclature for analysing fine-scale spatial variation in linguistic expression.

Materials and methods

Study area

For the purposes of analysis, the province of Almería (southeastern Spain; Fig. 1) was divided into its seven traditional comarcas (subprovincial administrative regions): Alpujarra Almeriense, Bajo Andarax–Níjar, Levante Almeriense, Los Filabres–Tabernas, Los Vélez, Poniente Almeriense and Valle del Almanzora [16]). The territory it occupies (8,774 km²) is generally characterised by a markedly rugged topography, with a notable diversity of landforms ranging from sea level to a maximum elevation of 2,605 m. This configuration places Almería among the most topographically complex provinces in Spain, particularly in its interior sector, where major mountain ranges often act as boundaries between comarcas. Among the most prominent formations are the Filabres Mountains, with several elevations exceeding 2,000 m a.s.l., which separate the comarcas of Los Filabres–Tabernas and Valle del Almanzora. This orographic axis extends across the provincial territory almost from end to end, extending from the western continuation of the Baza Mountains (Granada) to the vicinity of the Mediterranean coast in the east (Fig. 1). Nevertheless, Almería also exhibits a marked coastal character, with approximately 231 km of coastline along its southern and eastern margins, representing nearly half of its perimeter. In these coastal areas, extensive plains develop at the foothills of the mountain ranges (Fig. 1). The resulting mosaic forms a territory of high ecological diversity, in which plateau, valley and mountain habitats typical of the interior are complemented by marine, coastal, wetland and lowland environments associated with the littoral fringe. This configuration has traditionally favoured greater territorial connectivity and opportunities for human interaction in coastal comarcas compared to inland ones. A detailed description of the physical, environmental and human geography of the study area can be found in [18, 25, 42, 58].

Compilation and cataloguing of vernacular ornithonyms

This study was based on the database available for the province of Almería, in which the corpus relating to the use of vernacular ornithonyms by Almería’s population has been comprehensively compiled and catalogued [63, 64]. To this end, 489 informants from diverse geographical and social backgrounds covering all 103 municipalities of the province were surveyed, and 22 publications referring to the traditional popular names used to designate birds in the region were consulted. This dataset comprised a total of 511 data sources used to construct the compendium, ranging from 63 to 124 per comarca (see [63]; Table 1). After each consultation of data sources, the following were recorded: (i) the number of vernacular ornithonyms used to refer to each bird species or group of species (excluding those coinciding with the standard reference list for Spain [8]), and (ii) the comarcas in which each denomination was used, among the seven officially recognised in the province. For a detailed description of the protocol for the recovery and cataloguing of ornithonyms, see [64].

The compiled dataset included 1,001 distinct vernacular names used in the study area, associated with at least 205 bird species (Table 1), 82% of which were obtained directly through oral interviews with informants (see [63] for the complete catalogue and associated metadata). The resulting dictionary was considered broadly representative of the named species in Almería, based on both the temporal accumulation curve of recorded names and the geographical coverage of the sampling, as detailed in [63].

For quantitative analyses, each formally distinct vernacular ornithonym recorded from informants or documentary sources was treated as an independent lexical unit, irrespective of semantic equivalence or reference to the same bird species. Thus, spelling, phonetic, morphophonological, morphological and lexical variants were retained as distinct ornithonyms when they represented differentiated forms in the original records. Closely related morpholexical variants, such as denominations differing only in diminutive suffixes or word composition, were also preserved as independent units. For example, forms such as “pechuguica”, “pechuguilla” and “pechuguita” for European Robin (Erithacus rubecula) [63] were treated as differentiated ornithonyms when recorded as distinct vernacular variants. In contrast, trivial orthographic standardisations not affecting lexical identity were unified. This criterion was adopted to preserve fine-scale dialectal and geolinguistic variation encoded in vernacular nomenclature.

The dataset used in this study has also been analysed in research focusing on species-level cultural metrics derived from vernacular nomenclature (Paracuellos, manuscript under review). In contrast, the present study examines the spatial structuring of these naming systems at the territorial level, constituting a complementary analytical approach.

Statistical analyses

To assess the degree of affinity among the different comarcas studied, comparative analyses were conducted using matrices derived from the recorded information. Similarity between comarcas was evaluated using the binary Sørensen index [76], calculated from occurrence matrices. This index is widely used for ecological and ethnobiological datasets based on presence–absence data because it emphasises shared presences while reducing the influence of joint absences, making it particularly appropriate for comparing territorial similarities in vernacular nomenclature and named species composition [40, 76]. These similarity matrices were subsequently transformed into dissimilarity matrices (1 − Sørensen index), which served as input for hierarchical clustering analyses. Lexical affinity among comarcas was estimated by comparing in each comarca (a) the vernacular names of species and (b) the named species, according to the data sources used.

Hierarchical clustering was performed using the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic mean (UPGMA) for lexical affinity analyses, as it is widely employed to summarise average dissimilarity relationships among territorial units based on pairwise distance matrices and is particularly suitable for high-dimensional binary datasets such as those analysed here. Although Sørensen-based dissimilarities are not strictly Euclidean, their use in hierarchical clustering is well established in ecological studies. The results of the similarity analyses were visualised as affinity dendrograms. To assess the goodness of fit of clustering results, cophenetic correlation coefficients were calculated to quantify the agreement between the original dissimilarity matrices and the resulting dendrograms. Values above 0.7 were considered indicative of a satisfactory fit. This procedure is widely used to evaluate how faithfully a dendrogram represents the underlying data structure. For further methodological details, see [21, 40, 75]. To evaluate the relationship between grouping patterns among comarcas based on vernacular nomenclature and the composition of named species, Mantel tests [47] were applied to quantify the correlation between dissimilarity matrices derived from both variables. Statistical significance was assessed through a permutation procedure (1,999 permutations), a commonly used approach for evaluating matrix correlations in spatially structured datasets [40].

To analyse the lexical affinities between the territories into which the study area was divided and the eastern Andalusian and Murcian speech varieties, represented by those used in the neighbouring provinces of Granada and Murcia [27, 51, 57], a pairwise frequency analysis was conducted. To this end, the frequencies of vernacular ornithonyms recorded across the different territories of Almería were compared with those reported for the two adjacent provinces. For this purpose, available catalogues of popular bird names were used for both Granada [67] and Murcia [69].

Comparative analyses initially considered differences between the territorial units into which the study area was divided in terms of the total number of named species, the total number of recorded vernacular ornithonyms, and the mean number of vernacular ornithonyms per named species. To avoid bias arising from differences in species presence or absence between territories, comparisons involving the last variable were restricted to those species cited by data sources in both areas being compared (shared species). Species not recognised or named by the local population were therefore excluded from these analyses, as such absences may reflect either limited species occurrence in particular regions or differences in local ecological knowledge and cultural transmission.

Because the number of data sources differed among comarcas (Table 1), potential effects of unequal sampling effort on the initially considered variables were also evaluated statistically and considered during the interpretation of the results, excluding from subsequent quantitative analyses those variables significantly associated with sampling intensity in order to minimise potential bias.

Given the nature of the data, frequency differences were evaluated using the χ² test, correlations were assessed using Pearson’s coefficient after confirming approximately linear relationships among variables and the fulfilment of the corresponding statistical assumptions following data inspection, and comparisons of means were conducted using the two-tailed paired Student’s t-test following logarithmic transformation of the values in order to improve distributional symmetry and approximate parametric assumptions [23, 75].

Results

When clustering analyses among comarcas were conducted separately based on vernacular names and on the composition of named species, the resulting affinity dendrograms derived from dissimilarity matrices showed acceptable levels of goodness of fit across all datasets, with cophenetic correlation coefficients of r = 0.72 for vernacular names and r = 0.90 for species composition. These values indicate a good representation of the underlying dissimilarity structure, supporting the use of the results as a basis for describing spatial patterns of affinity among comarcas.

Mantel tests revealed a moderate, statistically significant positive correlation between dissimilarity matrices based on vernacular names and those based on named species (r = 0.47, P = 0.046). This result suggests that the patterns of similarity among comarcas derived from both variables only partially coincide, consistent with the existence of distinct spatial structuring patterns for each variable.

The affinity dendrogram based on shared denominations showed a differentiation between the southwestern comarcas (Alpujarra Almeriense, Bajo Andarax–Níjar, Los Filabres–Tabernas and Poniente Almeriense) and those in the northeast (Levante Almeriense, Los Vélez and Valle del Almanzora) (Fig. 2). In contrast, the clustering pattern based on named species composition showed a different spatial configuration, primarily separating coastal (Bajo Andarax–Níjar, Levante Almeriense and Poniente Almeriense) and inland or non-coastal comarcas (Alpujarra Almeriense, Los Filabres–Tabernas, Los Vélez and Valle del Almanzora) rather than southwestern and northeastern comarca groupings (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The modest correlation between the patterns of similarity observed among comarcas derived from vernacular nomenclature and named species indicates that both variables capture only partially overlapping dimensions of territorial variation. These contrasting spatial configurations suggest that different processes may underlie the territorial variation identified here to some extent and therefore require separate interpretative frameworks. More generally, these spatial configurations likely emerge from interacting ecological, historical and socially transmitted processes rather than from any single explanatory factor alone.

Socio-cultural influences on vernacular variation

Although the province of Almería is generally assigned to the eastern Andalusian dialectal domain [56, 57], several studies have highlighted the presence of Murcian influences that, in broad terms, extend across the northern, central and east-southeastern parts of its territory [2, 3]. More specifically, different authors have indicated that these features tend to intensify in the comarcas of Los Vélez, Bajo Almanzora and the Almería Axarquía, and may also extend to the entire Almanzora valley, although less clearly and consistently documented than in its lower sector [56, 59, 65, 66]. In this context, the available evidence points to a transitional configuration between both varieties, closer to a gradient than to a sharply defined linguistic boundary [3, 56, 65]. On the other hand, the province of Granada is generally assigned to the eastern Andalusian dialectal domain, although in some sectors, particularly in its northeastern area, tendencies towards Castilian varieties have been described, together with transitional features and lexical affinities with Murcian areas [6, 51, 56, 70]. In contrast, the Murcian dialect is used in the province of Murcia, a contact variety that historically acts as a bridge between eastern Andalusian, Castilian of La Mancha, Valencian-Catalan and, historically, Aragonese [27, 54, 85]. The statistical results of the present study were, overall, in agreement with these interpretations, as, based on the use and identity of vernacular ornithonyms employed by the population, they are consistent with a structuring of the study area into two domains closely aligned with their respective areas of linguistic influence and separated by the Filabres Mountains: a southwestern domain, more closely associated with neighbouring Granada and its eastern Andalusian variety, and a northeastern domain, flanked by both Murcia, where the Murcian dialect is spoken, and northeastern Granada, which is also influenced by this latter dialectal variety.

Geographical barriers play a fundamental role in structuring intercommunication between societies by constraining mobility and the frequency of contact, which are key factors in cultural diffusion [11, 17, 31, 82]. From a geolinguistic perspective, these constraints do not usually result in complete isolation, but rather act as spatial filters of diffusion, limiting the direct spread of new denominations and forms of expression and thereby promoting the retention of local features and dialectal differentiation between neighbouring areas [11, 79]. In the case of Almería, and in line with the results of this study, the Filabres Mountains appear to have acted as a key structuring feature in the spatial organisation of speech varieties and associated patterns of vernacular nomenclature, and may represent the main intraprovincial orographic feature modulating linguistic influence. This configuration is linked to its pronounced longitudinal extent, structural continuity and marked altitudinal prominence, features consistent with reduced cross-slope interaction between its flanks from a cultural and linguistic perspective [17, 83]. Beyond simple geographical isolation, these conditions may also have reduced the intensity of mobility, territorial connectivity and inter-community exchange across the province, thereby favouring the long-term persistence of partially differentiated vernacular traditions through relatively stable local networks of cultural transmission. This effect would likely have been further intensified in pre-industrial contexts characterised by limited transport infrastructure and communication systems [11, 31]. Such process has already been documented at the scale of the study area [14, 68, 71] and was even reflected in the historical organisation of the territory, as this mountain range has long functioned as a boundary between distinct spatial units, for example, by subdividing what is now the province of Almería into two administrative and fiscal districts within the Kingdom of Granada in 1759, according to the Ensenada Census [60].

On the other hand, topographic configurations that limit connectivity between neighbouring areas tend to channel cultural and linguistic flows along alternative corridors of communication, rather than allowing their uniform diffusion across space [80, 83]. In this regard, the Filabres range itself, in addition to acting as a boundary between slopes, may also have promoted trajectories of selective cultural connectivity that also encompassed the denominations used by the local population to refer to birds. These dynamics would likely be channelled through natural corridors such as the Almanzora valley, north of the massif, and the Nacimiento valley and the Tabernas–Sorbas plains to the south, which have traditionally functioned as longitudinal axes of exchange between the Baza and Guadix basins (Granada) and the Levante coast of Almería [25].

Beyond the socio-economic, territorial and cultural linkages traditionally fostered by geographical proximity between Almería and its neighbouring provinces of Murcia and Granada [9, 28, 72], population movements throughout history have constituted an additional driver in the diffusion of traditional practices and values between the study area and more or less distant regions [24, 38, 86]. These processes were likely accompanied by effects on local forms of expression, in line with general mechanisms of cultural diffusion associated with human mobility [32]. In addition to the significant linguistic influences linked to the settlement of new populations in the Almerian territory following the expulsion of the Moriscos [1, 2], more recent periods have witnessed human migrations of varying spatial and temporal scales and intensities, in many cases circular in nature, between the study area and other regions [13, 22, 49, 73, 74]. These movements may likewise have influenced local speech varieties in the province, as initially suggested by [64] in relation to vernacular ornithonyms, an issue that, given current limitations in available data, could represent a promising avenue for future research.

Geo-environmental influences on vernacular variation

In contrast to the southwestern–northeastern structuring observed for vernacular ornithonyms, the composition of named species exhibited a distinct coastal–inland differentiation among comarcas. This alternative spatial configuration suggests that the factors underlying variation in named species composition may differ, at least partially, from those associated with vernacular nomenclature. In this regard, coastal and inland comarcas formed clearly differentiated clusters, irrespective of their southwest–northeast position within the province, a pattern interpreted primarily in relation to the uneven distribution of habitats across these areas.

Coastal zones tend to encompass the full range of habitat types present inland (including woodlands and forests, fluvial systems, shrublands, natural and semi-natural grasslands, rocky and bare ground, agricultural systems, and urban environments), together with their associated bird communities. In addition, they include habitats largely exclusive to the littoral, such as marine-influenced and wetland systems, which support characteristic bird assemblages, many of them largely restricted to these environments, including numerous seabirds and marsh birds [25, 42, 46, 58]. As a result, coastal comarcas exhibit a greater habitat heterogeneity than inland ones, a difference that is consistent with higher levels of structural complexity and associated with higher species diversity [36, 43, 90], thereby expanding the pool of species available for vernacular naming. Furthermore, wetlands sustain a disproportionately high fraction of biodiversity despite their limited spatial extent [12], a pattern already documented for the study area [62]. Consequently, the presence of numerous species in coastal areas that are absent from inland regions may have contributed to differentiating between these two types of comarcas in terms of the taxonomic identity and distinctiveness of their bird assemblages as perceived and referred to by the population [64]. In this same vein, the difference observed between southwestern and northeastern comarcas, and between coastal and inland comarcas, in the total number of named species may be explained by the much greater presence of wetlands with high biodiversity in the former regions compared to the latter [62]. This contrast likely led to differences in species richness, which in turn may influence the pool of species recognised and named by local populations in each territory.

Additionally, such ecological differentiation could be modulated by human social environments. The comarcas are not homogeneous in terms of land use, settlement structure, urbanisation or socioeconomic configuration, either between or within coastal and inland comarca groupings. In general, coastal territories have undergone more intense processes of agricultural transformation, urban expansion and economic development than inland ones, although important internal contrasts also exist within each territorial group. For example, along the coast, highly transformed comarcas such as Poniente Almeriense differ socially and territorially from other less transformed areas such as Bajo Andarax–Níjar, whereas inland valleys and basins may likewise vary in their degree of economic development, connectivity and rural continuity among comarcas [18, 25, 84]. Accordingly, the composition of named species across territories could reflect not only differences in bird assemblages, but also variation in the continuity and intensity of everyday human interaction with local environments through traditional agricultural practices, resource use and ecological experience [7, 20, 35, 44, 45]. However, because the clearest differentiation detected by these analyses corresponded to the broader coastal–inland contrast, with higher numbers of named species consistently recorded in coastal comarcas, the observed patterns appear more strongly associated with large-scale environmental differentiation than with socially structured variation within or between territorial groupings. Nevertheless, social heterogeneity could still contribute, at least partially, to shaping local patterns of species recognition and cultural transmission among communities, an issue that deserves further investigation.

Despite the lower richness of named species in inland comarcas of Almería compared to coastal ones, higher values in the average number of denominations per shared species are likely related to historical contrasts in patterns of social interaction and cultural transmission associated with geographical accessibility between territories. In coastal areas, greater historical connectivity among communities would have facilitated the diffusion and homogenisation of forms of expression, in line with processes of levelling associated with contact and mobility described in dialectological literature [11, 31, 79]. Conversely, inland areas, traditionally more isolated due to limitations in communication networks [14, 25, 68, 71], appear to have maintained higher levels of diversification in naming practices, consistent with the effects of spatial fragmentation on dialectal differentiation [11, 79]. Such behaviour is in line with the formation of so-called «territorial islands» in contexts of geographical separation, associated with increased dialectal richness and polycentrism [15]. Furthermore, recent processes of socioeconomic transformation in coastal areas, linked to agricultural intensification, tourism and the expansion of the service sector, are likely to have contributed to the erosion and standardisation of traditional lexicon by reducing direct interaction between the population and the natural environment, in line with global patterns of loss of biocultural diversity [45], also observed in Almería [18, 64, 84] and, more specifically in relation to traditional lexicon, in neighbouring Granada [48].

Although a higher diversity of vernacular names per shared species was also detected in the southwestern compared to the northeastern comarca grouping, this pattern lacks a clear explanatory framework within the scope of the present analysis and should therefore be interpreted with caution. It could reflect additional historical, socio-cultural or geo-environmental influences related to long-term contrasts in cultural continuity, territorial interaction and transmission of local ecological knowledge among communities, all of which deserve further investigation.

Limitations and future research perspectives

The present study focused primarily on the spatial distribution and quantitative structuring of vernacular ornithonyms at the comarca scale rather than on the semantic, symbolic or ethnoclassificatory interpretation of individual bird names or on informant-level sociodemographic characteristics. Consequently, variables such as age, gender, occupation, degree of urbanisation and intergenerational transmission were not analysed explicitly. Although the observed patterns are consistent with relationships between linguistic variation, cultural transmission and environmental context, the analyses do not directly address how communities perceive, categorise and transmit vernacular ornithonymy across different social and territorial settings. Although the compiled dataset incorporated a broad geographical and social representation across the province [63, 64], future research integrating semantic, sociolinguistic, etymological and ethnographic approaches could help clarify these broader biocultural relationships.

Conclusions

Based on the quantitative analysis of vernacular ornithonyms, the results are consistent with the characterisation of the province of Almería as a linguistic transition zone within the Hispanic domain. The observed spatial structuring into southwestern and northeastern comarca groupings corresponded broadly with the neighbouring provinces of Granada and Murcia and their respective dominant dialectal varieties, eastern Andalusian and Murcian, in agreement with previous findings. The spatial configuration may be related, at least in part, to the influence of the Filabres Mountains as a structuring feature shaping patterns of interaction and cultural diffusion. In parallel, the distribution of named bird species revealed a distinct coastal–inland differentiation among comarcas and was interpreted as being primarily associated with environmental contrasts such as habitat heterogeneity, variation in biodiversity and geographic isolation. Taken together, these findings are consistent with the combined influence of socio-cultural and geo-environmental processes that may operate at different but complementary spatial scales.

The results highlight the usefulness of vernacular nomenclature for examining spatial relationships between linguistic variation, cultural transmission and environmental context within human communities. In this sense, these ornithonyms can also be interpreted as regional biocultural expressions shaped through long-term interactions between human communities, local biodiversity and territory. In this context, the integrative approach adopted here provides a useful analytical framework for examining such relationships across spatially structured systems. Beyond the specific case study, this framework may be applied to other contexts to investigate fine-scale variation in linguistic expression.

Importantly, the interpretation of some observed patterns should remain cautious, as they may be influenced by additional historical, social and territorial factors not explicitly considered in the present analysis. This highlights the need for further research integrating sociolinguistic, ethnographic and semantic approaches to better understand how vernacular ornithonymy reflects interactions between dialectal variation, cultural transmission and environmental heterogeneity within linguistic transition zones.
