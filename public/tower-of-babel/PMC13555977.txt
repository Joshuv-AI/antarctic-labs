Climate-related disease registry in Lebanon: a proposed roadmap
Authors: Myriam Mrad, Emile Whaibeh, Joyce Haddad, Jessica Saliba, Jowy Abi Hanna, Alicia Abi Nader, Estelle Mina, Farah Jradi, Rana Amer, Joseph Zgheib, Georges Abi Tayeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClimate change poses urgent health risks in the Arab region, yet most countries lack integrated systems to monitor climate-sensitive health outcomes. Lebanon, facing extreme climatic variability, widespread air pollution, and fragile health infrastructure, has no centralized mechanism to link environmental and health data. However, the capacity of existing surveillance systems to capture and integrate climate-sensitive health risks remains poorly understood.ObjectiveThis study aims to develop a national climate-related disease registry framework for Lebanon by synthesizing evidence from global and regional surveillance models and convening national stakeholders to identify priorities and implementation pathways for institutional integration within Lebanon’s health and environmental systems.MethodsA desk review was conducted to examine climate-sensitive health risks, structured around the WHO–UNFCCC’s classification of climate-related health risks, and to analyze existing global registries and surveillance platforms. In parallel, a national multi-sectoral consultation was held with representatives from government, academia, healthcare providers, and international organizations. Outputs were thematically synthesized to propose a registry framework and phased roadmap.ResultsThe desk review revealed that while climate-sensitive outcomes are partially captured across disease-specific systems, key domains, including non-communicable diseases, mental health, and extreme weather-related outcomes, remain structurally excluded from integrated monitoring. Stakeholders identified interoperability barriers rooted not only in technical limitations but also in governance structures, institutional mandates, and legislative constraints. Concomitantly, existing digital infrastructure and epidemiological platforms were recognized as potential anchors for phased integration. The proposed framework outlines short-, medium-, and long-term actions to establish an interoperable climate-health registry.ConclusionsThese findings highlight both the structural gaps and institutional opportunities and provide a phased roadmap for advancing an integrated national climate-health registry. By aligning stakeholders around a unified framework, the study lays the groundwork for evidence-based adaptation strategies in Lebanon and other climate-vulnerable settings.Keywords: Climate change, Climate-sensitive diseases, Data Integration, Disease registry, Health surveillance, Eastern Mediterranean Region

Introduction

Lebanon is increasingly vulnerable to the health impacts of climate change. Rising temperatures, altered precipitation patterns, and more frequent extreme weather events are projected to significantly affect public health [1]. Under a high-emissions scenario, the country’s mean annual temperature could rise by approximately 4.3 °C by the end of the century, while total annual precipitation may decrease by around 25% [2]. These shifts are manifesting as reduced snowfall, prolonged droughts, rising sea levels, and increased wildfires [3] These shifts heighten the risk of extreme weather events, which in turn can trigger vector borne, droplet borne, waterborne, and foodborne diseases, as well as heat stress, injuries, and higher rates of illness and death [3, 4]. Climate Change also contributes to the prevalence of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) through its effects on air quality, food security, and mental health [5].

Climate change was described as a “threat multiplier,” intensifying existing challenges and further straining the country’s fragile environmental and health systems [6]. Its impacts undermine progress towards achieving key Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including poverty reduction (SDG 1), reduced inequalities (SDG 10), access to clean water and sanitation (SDG 6), and climate action (SDG 13). The World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) classify climate-related health risks into nine categories: injuries and mortality from extreme weather events, heat-related illnesses, respiratory illnesses, waterborne diseases, zoonoses, vector-borne diseases, malnutrition and foodborne diseases, non-communicable diseases, and mental and psychosocial health disorders [4]. Addressing this broad spectrum of risks requires coordinated monitoring across diverse data sources, with varying levels of accessibility, compatibility, and completeness.

Despite these urgent needs, Lebanon still lacks a centralized mechanism to systematically link climate and health data. While existing surveillance systems capture a range of communicable diseases and other health indicators, the extent to which climate-sensitive outcomes are comprehensively monitored, integrated across institutions, or aligned with environmental data remains insufficiently characterized. Information systems are frequently described as siloed and fragmented across ministries, hospitals, private clinics, primary healthcare centers, universities, and international partners; however, the structural, technical, and governance dimensions of this fragmentation have not been systematically examined in relation to climate-health surveillance.

To address this gap, we undertook a study to develop a national climate-related disease registry for Lebanon. Specifically, the study aimed to (1) map priority climate-sensitive health risks using the WHO-UNFCCC classification, (2) assess the scope and limitations of existing surveillance systems, and (3) identify institutional, technical, and legislative conditions necessary for integration. This paper presents the outcomes of this effort, using a comprehensive desk review of climate-sensitive health risks and existing registries, complemented by findings from a national stakeholder consultation.

Methodology

This study employed a mixed-methods design that combined a desk review of global and national climate–health surveillance approaches with a national stakeholder consultation. Together, these activities were designed to generate an evidence-informed framework and roadmap for a climate-related disease registry in Lebanon.

Desk review

A structured desk review was undertaken to synthesize knowledge on climate-sensitive health risks and to examine existing surveillance platforms and registries. Published articles, reports, and documents were identified through searches of peer-reviewed databases (PubMed, Scopus) and gray literature sources (WHO, UNFCCC, UN agencies, and national reports). The review was guided by the WHO–UNFCCC classification of climate-related health risks, which spans nine domains: (1) injuries and mortality from extreme weather events, (2) heat-related illnesses, (3) respiratory illnesses, (4) waterborne diseases, (5) zoonoses, (6) vector-borne diseases, (7) malnutrition and foodborne diseases, (8) non-communicable diseases, and (9) mental and psychosocial health disorders. For each domain, relevant evidence on Lebanon’s vulnerabilities was mapped, and the scope and limitations of existing national surveillance systems was assessed and compared to selected global and regional platforms such as ClimaHealth, DHIS2 for Climate and Health, ProMED, European Environment Agency registries, and others. Key criteria for data collection and reporting were extracted to inform the proposed registry framework.

Stakeholder consultation

To complement the desk review, a national multi-sectoral stakeholder consultation was convened in June 10, 2025, in Beirut. Participants were purposively identified in collaboration with the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) and institutional partners to ensure representation across key sectors involved in climate and health governance. Invitees included representatives from the MoPH, the Ministry of Environment, the Central Administration of Statistics, the Lebanese Red Cross, the Syndicate of Hospitals, academic institutions, healthcare providers, United Nations agencies, and non-governmental organizations. Stakeholders were selected based on their institutional mandates and ongoing involvement in climate change, health systems resilience, surveillance, or environmental monitoring initiatives. The objective of the consultation was to validate the desk review findings, identify gaps and national priorities, and generate consensus on registry design features and implementation strategies. Discussions were structured around four domains: (1) existing data sources and interoperability, (2) health outcomes most sensitive to climate variability, (3) governance and coordination needs, and (4) feasibility of phased implementation. Deliberations were facilitated, documented in real time, and thematically synthesized to refine the proposed registry framework.

Synthesis

Findings from the desk review and stakeholder consultation were triangulated to develop a registry framework tailored to Lebanon’s needs and capacities. The desk review served as a diagnostic exercise to map climate-sensitive health risks and identify structural gaps in surveillance coverage across WHO-UNFCCC domains. The comparative review of global and regional platforms provided benchmarking criteria for governance, interoperability, and data architecture. The stakeholder consultation then contextualized these findings within Lebanon’s institutional realities, validating priorities and assessing implementation constraints. The synthesis of these activities formed a framework linking risk identification, structural design criteria, and perceived institutional feasibility. The final output was a phased roadmap outlining short-, medium-, and long-term actions for establishing a national climate-related disease registry.

Results

Desk review

Climate-sensitive health risks in Lebanon

To assess how climate-sensitive health risks are currently captured within Lebanon’s surveillance architecture, we mapped the 9 WHO-UNFCC climate-health domains against existing national reporting systems and documented epidemiological evidence. The desk review confirmed that climate change poses multi-dimensional health risks across all nine domains; however, surveillance coverage is uneven and structurally segmented. First, injuries and mortality from extreme weather events, including flooding and wildfires, are documented through disaster response reports and post-event assessments but not systematically integrated into health surveillance databases and linked to other environmental variables. For example, extreme weather events, such as flooding, droughts, wildfires, and sea level rise, have already resulted in loss of life and livelihoods, with the January 2024 river floods in North Lebanon disproportionately affecting refugees and underserved communities [7]. However, these impacts remain recorded in event-based reports rather than continuous and integrated climate-health monitoring systems.

Second, heat-related morbidity and mortality are growing public health concerns: the country currently records around 2 heat-related deaths per 100,000 individuals above the age of 65, a figure projected to rise to 48 per 100,000 by 2030 under current emission trends [4]. However, this domain is only captured indirectly through mortality statistics and occupational health studies. Projections of heat-related deaths among older adults are available [4] and localized studies among both farmworkers and bakery workers document occupational vulnerability [8, 9]. However, no dedicated heat-health surveillance mechanism currently links meteorological indicators with health outcomes in real time.

Third, respiratory illnesses linked to air pollution, a major contributor to climate change, remain among the highest in the region. Annual PM₂.₅ levels far exceed WHO standards in coastal cities and the Bekaa, contributing to an estimated 3,124 premature deaths in 2016, and elevated PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ concentrations are associated with measurable increases in all-cause mortality [10]. Nevertheless, air quality monitoring systems operate separately from routine disease reporting as they are under the mandate of two different ministries. Moreover, the Ministry of Environment had to suspend the National Air Quality Monitoring Network (AQMN) in July 2019 due to funding constraints; the network has not resumed operations since [11].

Waterborne disease risks are exacerbated by altered precipitation, flooding, and substandard water resource and sanitation management; one example is the cholera outbreak that Lebanon faced on the 6th of October 2022, for the first time in 30 years. According to Venkatesan, by the 26th of November, there were 337 cases of suspected and confirmed cholera that had been reported, along with 20 associated deaths, resulting in a case fatality ratio of 0.46% [12]. The MoPH surveillance continues to capture outbreaks of cholera, typhoid, and viral hepatitis. Similarly, zoonotic infections such as brucellosis and hydatid cysts are routinely reported, but little work has been done on their transmission to climate factors.

Additionally, Lebanon, similarly to other developing countries in the Eastern Mediterranean and Middle East region, has shown vulnerability to the rise in food-borne and vector-borne diseases [13]. Alterations in temperature and precipitation patterns led to an increase and emergence of pathogenic microorganisms and vector-borne diseases [13]. Foodborne illness is also expected to rise with climate variability, with incidents of food poisoning already tracked by the MoPH. In 2012, a study by El-Fadel et al. projected a substantial increase in food- and waterborne related morbidity of 16 to 28% in 2050, which can reach 42% by 2100 [14].

Lastly, non-communicable diseases and mental health conditions, recognized globally as climate-sensitive outcomes, are structurally underrepresented within environmental risk reporting. Although NCD programs exist, climate-related modifiers such as extreme heat, displacement, or air pollution are not systematically captured. Mental and psychosocial impacts also remain particularly absent from routine disease surveillance systems.

This mapping demonstrates that while multiple climate-sensitive health outcomes are documented in Lebanon, surveillance remains vertically organized, disease-specific, and largely disconnected from environmental monitoring systems. Integration gaps are most pronounced in non-communicable diseases, mental health, and extreme weather-related outcomes. These structural asymmetries underscore the need for an integrated registry to systematically capture and link these outcomes to climatic and environmental variables.

Platforms/registries on climate-change related illnesses

The critical intersection of climate change and public health has led to the development of platforms and registries designed to monitor, analyze, and mitigate health issues exacerbated by environmental changes. These initiatives aim to facilitate data collection, integrate disparate datasets, and facilitate knowledge production and dissemination of evidence to support policy making. Despite the urgent need, a review by Ryan et al. in 2023 identified only 37 validated tools that combine climate data with health epidemiological data, and over 81% of those focused on vector-borne diseases such as malaria, and the majority are hosted in Western countries [15]. This highlights the dearth in tools and initiatives in reporting other climate change-related illnesses and injuries and in representation of developing countries and more susceptible communities. Key global platforms and initiatives that attempt to address this challenge include:

ClimaHealth.info: Launched by the WHO and the World Meteorological Organization [16, 17], this tool serves as a global knowledge platform to climate and health, offering information to protect populations from health risks associated with climate change and environmental hazards. It provides a toolkit to evaluate various aspects of climate change and health, to understand, forecast, and manage health hazards (https://climahealth.info/learn/tools-products-services/). Additionally, it lists achievements and works in progress to reflect transparency, motivate partners, and call for collaborations.

DHIS2 integrates climate, weather, environmental, and earth observation data with health surveillance data, enabling health authorities to analyze correlations and establish early warning systems for climate-sensitive diseases such as malaria, dengue, cholera, and respiratory diseases (https://dhis2.org/climate/). DHIS2 also provides software, implementation tools, and guidance to build on existing health surveillance systems to leverage the reporting, interpretation, and dissemination of climate change-related health data https://dhis2.org/disease-surveillance/). DHIS2 was first introduced in Lebanon in 2014 through a WHO-supported project to strengthen the Ministry of Public Health’s Epidemiological Surveillance Unit (ESU) [19]. It is currently applied primarily for communicable disease monitoring, improving timeliness and completeness of surveillance data [20]. However, its potential for broader integration, such as incorporating environmental indicators and tracking climate-sensitive health outcomes, remains largely untapped.

Existing global and regional platforms for climate-health surveillance, such as ClimaHealth.info, the DHIS2 for Climate and Health, and the Global Climate Resilience Platform, demonstrate that integrated surveillance is feasible, providing essential tools for risk assessment, capacity building, and knowledge sharing. A major strength in the Lebanese context is the successful deployment of DHIS2 by the MoPH for communicable diseases diseases since 2014, offering an existing digital platform that stakeholders support, leveraging for expansion into climate-health data. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1, these platforms vary considerably in the health outcomes they monitor: while vectorborne, waterborne, and respiratory diseases are widely captured, non-communicable conditions, maternal and child health, and mental health remain largely underrepresented, highlighting persistent global gaps that Lebanon’s registry design will need to address.

Consistency: Ensuring data is collected consistently across regions and time, with accuracy, completeness, and reliability guaranteed through data validation and quality control.

National stakeholder meeting on climate-health data integration and the development of a climate-related disease registry in Lebanon

As part of the Migration Multi-Partner Trust Fund (MMPTF) Project, the Health and Environmental Response Agency (HERA) in collaboration with the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) and with the support of the International Organization for Migration (IOM), convened a national stakeholder consultation on 10 June 2025. The meeting engaged representatives from governmental bodies, UN agencies, academic and healthcare institutions, and the syndicate of Lebanese hospitals to critically examine pathways to integrate climate and health data in Lebanon and advancing the development of a climate-related disease registry. Key findings from the consultation indicate a strong consensus on the strategic value of an intergrated, interoperable registry to enable systematic monitoring of climate-sensitive diseases and to strengthen evidence-based policy and intervention design. Stakeholders identified persistent structural and operational barriers to such integration, which are presented in Table 2.

Despite these identified barriers, the consultation underscored the readiness and willingness of key actors to engage in a coordinated national effort, and there was broad consensus on several priority actions (Fig. 2). First, stakeholders expressed strong support for the establishment of a national registry for climate-related diseases, emphasizing the importance of designing a reliable, interoperable, and scalable system. Second, institutional collaboration, particularly between ministries, the Central Administration of Statistics (CAS), and academic institutions, was considered essential for the registry’s success. Third, one promising proposal discussed was the implementation of a “Médecins Sentinelles” initiative, which would engage volunteer physicians in contributing clinical data to help capture health outcomes not typically recorded in hospital settings. Fourth, the involvement of various syndicates, scientific societies, and broader multidisciplinary platforms was seen as crucial for expanding data sources and enhancing clinical surveillance. Lastly, the stakeholders in attendance agreed on the importance of a flexible roadmap that evolves with changing trends and emerging data.

From a broader research- and public health-oriented perspective, the insights generated from this consultation meeting align with international evidence showing that effective climate-health surveillance in low- and middle-income countries depends on overcoming the crucial problem of fragmented information systems, requires the establishment of robust governance mechanisms, and needs long-term resource commitment and capacity building. This consultation meeting marks a pivotal step forward in establishing a national platform for monitoring climate-sensitive health outcomes in Lebanon, which can be made possible through a phased, adaptive framework.

Discussion

This study, conducted in collaboration with the MoPH and in consultation with national stakeholders, represents one of the first coordinated efforts in Lebanon to link climate and health evidence within a structured registry framework. The study combined three complementary analytical components: a structured mapping of climate-sensitive health risks using the WHO-UNFCCC domains, benchmarking of global and regional surveillance platforms to extract structural design criteria, and institutional validation through national stakeholder consultation. It demonstrates that Lebanon’s principal gap is not the absence of surveillance tools but the fragmentation of mandates, data standards, and interoperability mechanisms.

Existing global and regional platforms for climate-health surveillance, such as ClimaHealth.info, the DHIS2 for Climate and Health, and the Global Climate Resilience Platform, demonstrate that integrated surveillance is technically feasible and offers benefits such as providing essential tools for risk assessment, capacity building, and knowledge sharing. The surveyed registries can track a wide range of climate-sensitive health outcomes, including infectious and vector-borne diseases (e.g., dengue, malaria, West Nile virus), food- and waterborne illnesses (e.g., cholera, salmonella), respiratory and cardiovascular conditions linked to air pollution, heat-related illnesses, injuries caused by extreme weather events, and mental health impacts. Some registries, such as ProMED-mail, also monitor emerging and zoonotic diseases (e.g., Mpox, avian influenza, anthrax). However, our review echoes findings by Ryan et al. [15] and shows that “scope” remains the Achilles’ heel of climate-related health registries. They are subject to great variability, with an overweighting toward vector-borne diseases and an underrepresentation of NCDs, maternal/child health, and mental health. These specific domains are blind sports that our stakeholders in Lebanon also identified.

Lebanon has previously demonstrated the capacity to deploy and sustain national digital surveillance systems. The MoPH maintains a communicable disease notification system covering 40 health conditions include acute flaccid paralysis, cholera, food poisoning, typhoid, viral hepatitis, leishmaniasis, rabies and others. In recent years, the MoPH transitioned from a paper-based communicable disease notification system to DHIS2, with documented improvements in reporting timeliness and completeness [20]. During the 2022–2023 cholera outbreak, DHIS2 was used nationally for case reporting, and subsequent integration with analytical tools further improved data quality and outbreak monitoring [29]. However, similar to the global trend, these systems remain largely communicable disease-centric and are not routinely linked to environmental or climate indicators.

In addition, Lebanon has implemented digital platforms within the primary healthcare network, including PHENICS, and has articulated a formal commitment to health information system strengthening through the MOPH National Digital Health Transformation Strategy [30]. However, the plan to prevent and control non-communicable diseases, published in 2016 [31], does not take into consideration the collection of risk factors and exposures related to climate change. So, national surveillance systems should be leveraged to expand the uses of such software to track climate-related health risks and diseases, including non-communicable diseases and mental health.

Rather than operating siloed data repositories and running parallel dashboards, Lebanon needs common definitions, metadata, and data-sharing agreements so that its centralized data hub can ingest and correlate meteorological, air-quality, and other exposure data to clinical events and health outcomes. For instance, the U.S. Environmental Public Health Tracking Network routinely links environmental exposure-health data at the population level, covering heat-related illnesses, air pollution, and birth outcomes [32]. Its value proposition, indicators, methods, and standard operating procedures speak directly to Lebanon’s need for common definitions and quality assurance. Similarly, the European Climate Risk Assessment and EEA health reports treat heat, floods, and water quality as integrated policy problems rather than siloed environmental hazards [33], highlighting the importance of cross-sectoral data sharing standards and governance that Lebanon can also benefit from.

The phased roadmap proposed in this study is therefore designed to build incrementally on existing infrastructure while progressively strengthening governance frameworks, technical interoperability, workforce capacity, and sustainable financing mechanisms required for integrated climate-health surveillance. This roadmap aims to build capacity progressively, ensure interoperability, and create a sustainable model that can adapt to evolving climate and health challenges. This study has limitations. The desk review drew on publicly available sources and may have missed recent pilot efforts; stakeholder input, though diverse, came from a single national meeting, with under-representation of subnational and non-health actors. However, there are plans to expand and diversify these consultations as the project matures. Even so, the proposed registry advances the field in three concrete ways. First, it commits from inception to four under-captured outcome classes: (1) climate-exacerbated NCDs (e.g., heat-aggravated cardiovascular/renal events, COPD/asthma exacerbations); (2) perinatal and early-life endpoints (preterm birth, low birth weight) sensitive to heat and air pollution; (3) mental and psychosocial health linked to extreme events and livelihood loss; and (4) occupational heat stress in high-risk sectors. Second, it adapts a “médecins sentinelles” arm, whose feasibilty and success is proven in Europe (e.g.: networks like France’s Réseau Sentinelles [sentiweb.fr]), to generate granular, near-real-time signals for syndromic conditions (heat illness, dust/wildfire respiratory surges, post-disaster distress) across hospitals, health centers, and private clinics. Third, it treats interoperability as a governance task before a technical one, setting explicit milestones for role clarity, inter-ministerial data-sharing, and legal data-access frameworks. With these choices, Lebanon can move from fragmented, outbreak-oriented reporting to a climate-health intelligence system, one that not only warns but also learns, enables faster action and ensures more equitable protection for those most at risk.
