Generalized osteoporosis but not axial osteopenia is associated with cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Jian-Yong Wang, Wen-Bin Lu, Qiu-Yan Yu, Shu-Ting Guo, Qi-Xin Huang, Zi-Xuan Zhang, Cheng-Xiang Yuan, Bei-Lei Hu, Pei-Jun Li, Jian-Hong Zhu, Xiong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone and brain interact bidirectionally through the brain–bone axis. However, the relationship between bone mineral density (BMD) and cognitive impairment in Parkinson’s disease (PD) remains unclear.MethodsWe screened and enrolled 120 PD patients, measuring BMD at the bilateral neck of femur, total hip, one-third distal radius and lumbar spine while assessing various clinical symptoms. Latent class analysis (LCA) was employed for BMD classification, followed by statistical analyses to assess their associations with cognitive impairment.ResultsPatients with cognitive impairment exhibited generally reduced BMD in the measured skeletal locations compared to those without cognitive impairment, while serum biochemical markers did not show this trend. LCA by utilizing multiple regional BMD measurements classified patients into two distinct clusters: axial osteopenia and generalized osteoporosis. Cognitive impairment was more pronounced in the generalized osteoporosis cluster. Further analysis showed that PD patients in the generalized osteoporosis cluster had an increased risk of cognitive impairment (adjusted odds ratio: 9.02, 95% confidence interval: 2.68–35.40). After stratification, this association was more prominent in older or male patients, in those whose non-dominant hand side exhibited more severe motor symptoms, and in patients with either a longer disease duration or the non-tremor dominant subtype.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates for the first time that generalized osteoporosis, rather than axial osteopenia, is associated with cognitive impairment in PD. Interventions aimed at mitigating generalized BMD decline could be a potential therapeutic approach to improve cognitive function in PD patients.Keywords: Parkinson’s disease, cognitive impairment, bone mineral density, osteoporosis, brain–bone axis

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by a complex array of motor and non-motor manifestations. The classic motor symptoms primarily include rest tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity and postural instability. Non-motor symptoms, particularly cognitive impairment, are a significant part of the symptom profile in PD patients. Evidence shows that cognitive impairment is present in about 30% of patients at the initial diagnosis and its prevalence increases markedly as the disease progresses [1]. This progressive cognitive decline significantly compromises patients’ functional independence and quality of life, underscoring its substantial contribution to the overall disease burden of PD.

PD is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disorder, with pathological changes extending beyond the classical nigrostriatal pathway to affect peripheral tissues and organs [2]. This systemic involvement suggests that the neurodegenerative processes underlying PD may not be confined to the central nervous system but could concurrently impact other organ systems through shared pathophysiological mechanisms. Accumulating evidence indicates that common pathological processes, including chronic neuroinflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction and calcium homeostasis dysregulation, are critically involved in both neuronal degeneration and the disruption of bone metabolism [3,4]. Studies have identified shared risk factors between osteoporosis and PD, including vitamin D deficiency, hyperhomocysteinemia and lack of exercise [5–7]. Importantly, PD and osteoporosis may mutually increase each other’s risk [8–10]. In addition, circulating biomarkers such as homocysteine and vitamin D have been independently linked to both cognitive decline and reduced bone mineral density (BMD) in PD [11]. Lower BMD, in turn, is associated with decreased grey matter volume and cognitive impairment [12]. Therefore, investigating the relationship between bone status and cognitive function in PD extends beyond the study of comorbidity – it may offer a peripheral viewpoint and contribute to an integrated understanding of the systemic nature of PD.

Bone status is traditionally defined by BMD, classified as normal, osteopenia or osteoporosis. A diagnosis of osteopenia or osteoporosis is based on abnormal BMD measurements at a single skeletal region, even if BMD values are normal at other sites [13]. Numerous studies have demonstrated reduced BMD in PD patients [9,10]. However, it remains to be determined whether this reduction is causally linked to cognitive impairment in PD. Additionally, since PD is a movement disorder, the traditional diagnostic approach for bone status may not adequately reflect systemic bone density changes. Degenerative changes at a single site might not be representative for revealing their relationship to cognitive impairment in PD. Therefore, we applied latent class analysis (LCA) to integrate all regional BMD measurements, achieving an appropriate classification tailored for PD patients, and investigated the relationship between BMD, along with its related biochemical indicators, and cognitive impairment in PD.

Methods

Study population

A total of 300 PD patients were recruited from the Department of Neurology at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between November 2022 and October 2023. All female participants were postmenopausal. All participants were right-handed and met the Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria for PD [14]. The following exclusion criteria were applied: (1) use of medications known to influence bone metabolism; (2) family history of PD; (3) secondary or atypical parkinsonism; (4) history of fracture or orthopaedic surgery; (5) comorbidities affecting bone metabolism; and (6) absence of BMD measurements. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was performed in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and cognitive assessment

Demographic and clinical data – including age, gender, disease duration, body mass index (BMI), levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), Hoehn and Yahr stage, Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) scores, Hamilton Anxiety Scale (HAMA), Hamilton Depression Scale (HAMD), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Parkinson’s Disease Questionnaire-39 (PDQ-39) and Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) – were collected via face-to-face interviews and physical examinations during the ‘off’ medication state. Cognitive impairment was defined as an adjusted MoCA score (with one point added for ≤12 years of education) < 21 [15,16], leading to classification of participants into PD with cognitive impairment (PD-CI) and PD without cognitive impairment (PD-nCI).

BMD and biochemical measurements

BMD was measured at the neck of femur, total hip, one-third (1/3) distal radius and lumbar spine (vertebrae L1–L4) using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (Lunar Prodigy Advance, GE Healthcare, Chalfont St Giles, UK). The BMD values (g/cm3) and the corresponding T-scores were automatically generated by the device’s software. Fasting serum samples were analysed for bone turnover markers, including 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25-OH-VitD), total procollagen type 1N-terminal propeptide (tP1NP), N-terminal mid-fragment of osteocalcin (NMID), C-terminal telopeptide (CTX), using the electrochemiluminescence immunoassay from Roche (Mannheim, Germany). Additionally, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP), vitamin B12 and folate were assessed via the chemiluminescence immunoassay from Beckman (Brea, CA). Lipid profiles, which include triglycerides, cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), lipoprotein A, homocysteine and calcium, were measured using the chemiluminescence immunoassay from Siemens (Erlangen, Germany).

Motor symptom evaluation and subtype classification

Motor symptoms were assessed using the MDS-UPDRS Part III. Subscores for tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia and axial symptoms were derived as previously described [17,18]. Patients were classified into tremor-dominant (TD), postural instability and gait disturbance (PIGD) and mixed subtypes based on established criteria [19]. The PIGD and mixed subtypes were combined into a non-tremor-dominant (nTD) group [20].

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using R 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test; non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using Mann–Whitney’s U-test. Categorical variables were compared via Chi-square test.

LCA was performed using the tidyLPA package to identify distinct BMD-based phenotypes. We systematically evaluated models with 2–6 latent classes while comparing three covariance structures: equal volume, shape and orientation (EEE); varying volume and shape, with equal orientation (VVE); and varying volume, shape and orientation (VVV). The analysis was initialized with a random seed of 123 to ensure reproducibility. Model selection was based on the Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion, with lower values indicating a better fit. The final model was chosen through a comprehensive evaluation that balanced statistical fit with clinical interpretability, prioritizing solutions that captured meaningful BMD heterogeneity while maintaining parsimony.

Associations between BMD patterns and cognitive impairment were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression across three models, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The models are: model 1 (unadjusted), model 2 (adjusted for regular demographic and PD characteristics, including BMI, age, age at onset, disease duration, tremor, bradykinesia, rigidity and PDQ-39) and model 3 (adjusted for all factors in model 2 and additional measurements, including HAMA, HAMD and serum biochemical markers). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using MoCA as a continuous outcome via generalized linear models. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess effect modification by age, disease duration, gender and motor subtype. A two-tailed p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Inclusion of PD patients and characteristics of the PD-CI and PD-nCI groups

After screening, 120 out of 300 initially recruited PD patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). As shown in Table 1, the PD-CI group scored significantly lower on the MoCA (p < 0.001). Compared to the PD-nCI group, the PD-CI group were older (p = 0.002), included a higher proportion of female patients (p = 0.003), and had an older age at onset (p = 0.010) as well as a longer disease duration (p = 0.007). They were also in a more advanced stage of disease severity, as measured by the Hoehn–Yahr stage (p < 0.001), and had higher total MDS-UPDRS scores (p < 0.001) as well as higher MDS-UPDRS III scores (p < 0.001). Furthermore, the PD-CI group showed more severe motor symptom impairments, with higher scores for tremor (p = 0.004), bradykinesia (p < 0.001), rigidity (p = 0.037) and axial symptoms (p = 0.001). Additionally, the PD-CI group reported higher levels of anxiety (HAMA, p = 0.001), depression (HAMD, p = 0.003), poorer quality of life (PDQ-39, p < 0.001) and more severe non-motor symptoms (NMSS, p = 0.001). No significant differences were found in BMI, LEDD, motor subtypes or the distribution of the more affected side between the PD-CI and PD-nCI groups.

BMD, but not serum biomarkers, are generally reduced in patients with PD-CI

Analysis of BMD revealed significant deterioration in the PD-CI group compared to the PD-nCI group (Figure 2 and Table 2). The PD-CI group had significantly lower BMD values at all measured skeletal sites, including the right neck of femur (median 0.73 vs. 0.83 mg/cm3, p = 0.003), right total hip (median 0.79 vs. 0.90 mg/cm3, p = 0.002), right 1/3 distal radius (median 0.69 vs. 0.83 mg/cm3, p = 0.001), left neck of femur (median 0.73 vs. 0.85 mg/cm3, p = 0.001), left total hip (median 0.83 vs. 0.92 mg/cm3, p = 0.005), left 1/3 distal radius (median 0.70 vs. 0.84 mg/cm3, p = 0.001) and lumbar spine (median 0.88 vs. 1.02 mg/cm3, p = 0.001), with correspondingly lower T-scores at these regions (p = 0.002, 0.002, <0.001, 0.001, 0.006, 0.001 and 0.001, respectively). In contrast, the analysis of serum biochemical markers revealed that only BALP was significantly higher in the PD-CI group than in the PD-nCI group (median 13.70 vs. 12.00 µg/L, p = 0.015). Other serum markers, including bone turnover markers (CTX, NMID, tP1NP), vitamin D levels, lipid profiles and metabolic indicators, showed comparable values between PD-CI and PD-nCI patients (all p > 0.05; Table 3).

LCA classifies PD patients into two distinct clusters by utilizing multiple regional BMD measurements

LCA showed that distinct clusters were identified among PD patients under three covariance structures. The model with two latent classes, under the structure of VVV, provided the most effective class separation, as evidenced by the lowest Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion values (Figure 3(A)). We designated these two LCA-derived BMD clusters as the axial osteopenia cluster and the generalized osteoporosis cluster (Figure 3(B)). The axial osteopenia cluster (n = 66) was characterized by reduced BMD predominantly affecting the lumbar spine and bilateral femoral neck, while BMD at appendicular sites remained relatively preserved. In contrast, the generalized osteoporosis cluster (n = 54) exhibited severely reduced BMD across all measured sites, with median T-scores below −2.1 at all locations, meeting the diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis. The lowest T-scores were observed at the lumbar spine (median: −3.00) and left femoral neck (median: −2.70; Table 4). The validity of this classification was confirmed by profound differences in BMD measurements across all anatomical regions. In brief, the generalized osteoporosis cluster exhibited significantly lower BMD values and T-scores at bilateral neck of femur, total hip, 1/3 distal radius and lumbar spine (all p < 0.001; Figure 3(C–P)).

Some serum biochemical markers differed between the two clusters (Table 5). Specifically, the generalized osteoporosis cluster showed significantly elevated levels of bone turnover markers, including CTX (p < 0.001), NMID (p < 0.001), tP1NP (p < 0.001) and BALP (p = 0.010), alongside lower 25-OH-VitD levels (p = 0.007). These results provided additional validation for the LCA-derived classification. This cluster also exhibited higher levels of HDL (p = 0.007) and APOA1 (p = 0.007).

Cognitive impairment is more pronounced in the generalized osteoporosis cluster

Analysis of PD-related features revealed that differences were predominantly observed in non-motor symptoms, rather than motor symptoms, between the axial osteopenia cluster and the generalized osteoporosis cluster (Table 6). Specifically, the generalized osteoporosis cluster displayed significantly poorer cognitive performance, as indicated by the MoCA (p = 0.001). Additionally, this cluster displayed higher levels of anxiety, as measured by the HAMA (p = 0.003), and higher levels of depression, as measured by the HAMD (p = 0.001).

Generalized osteoporosis is associated with increased risk of cognitive impairment in PD

We further analysed the association of LCA-derived BMD classification with cognitive impairment in PD. Using the traditional BMD categories, osteopenia was not significantly associated with cognitive impairment in any model (all p > 0.05). Osteoporosis showed a significant association only in the unadjusted model 1 (OR = 3.51, 95% CI = 1.20–11.30, p = 0.027), but this association became non-significant after adjusting for confounders (model 2: OR = 4.18, 95% CI = 0.97–20.00, p = 0.061; model 3: OR = 4.22, 95% CI = 0.77–26.10, p = 0.105). In contrast, significant associations emerged between the generalized osteoporosis cluster and cognitive impairment in PD when using LCA-derived BMD clusters. The strength of the association increased with sequential adjustment for confounders (model 1: OR = 3.72, 95% CI = 2.02–9.41, p < 0.001; model 2: OR = 5.85, 95% CI = 2.23–16.70, p < 0.001; model 3: OR = 9.02, 95% CI = 2.68–35.40, p < 0.001; Table 7). A sensitivity analysis using the MoCA scores as a continuous outcome measure confirmed these findings. No significant association was found with traditional BMD categories, but the generalized osteoporosis cluster consistently showed a significant association with lower MoCA scores across all models (model 1: β = −3.74, 95% CI = −5.65 to −1.83; model 2: β = −3.74, 95% CI = −5.61 to −1.87; model 3: β = −3.98, 95% CI = −6.26 to −1.71; all p < 0.01; Table 8).

Stratified analysis of the risk effect of osteoporosis on cognitive impairment in PD

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate whether key demographic or clinical factors modified the association between the generalized osteoporosis cluster and cognitive impairment (Figure 4). The association was notably stronger in patients aged ≥ 65 years (OR = 4.71, 95% CI = 1.93–11.48, p < 0.001) compared to those <65 years. It was also stronger in patients whose non-dominant hand side exhibited more severe motor symptoms (OR = 5.52, 95% CI = 1.93–15.79, p = 0.001) compared to those whose dominant hand side was more severely affected. Similarly, patients with a longer disease duration (≥5 years: OR = 8.90, 95% CI = 1.89–41.98, p = 0.006) had a stronger association with cognitive impairment compared to those with disease duration <5 years. Male patients (OR = 9.75, 95% CI = 1.01–94.12, p = 0.038) showed a stronger association than female patients, as did patients with the nTD subtype (OR = 9.00, 95% CI = 2.65–30.55, p < 0.001) compared to those with the TD subtype. However, the interaction between the cluster and any of these demographic or clinical factors was not statistically significant.

Discussion

There is intricate bidirectional communication between the brain and the skeletal system [21]. This study highlights a novel interaction between multiple regional BMD and cognitive impairment in PD. Patients with cognitive impairment generally exhibited greater reductions in BMD compared to those without cognitive impairment. Notably, we demonstrate that generalized osteoporosis, not axial osteopenia, is associated with the increased risk of cognitive impairment in PD.

Generalized osteoporosis represents a severe systemic phenotype involving appendicular sites, suggesting that dysregulation may share common pathways with cognitive decline via the bone–brain axis and inflammatory mediators (e.g. TNF-α, IL-6) [3]. The involvement of peripheral sites may also reflect underlying vascular pathology, as lower BMD has been correlated with reduced cerebral perfusion and brain volume [22]. Additionally, vitamin D deficiency may be a common etiological factor, given the abundance of vitamin D receptors in the hippocampus and its established roles in both bone mineralization and cognitive function [23]. In contrast, axial osteopenia likely represents an earlier, localized stage primarily driven by mechanical loading, which may be insufficient to engage the systemic or vascular implicated in cognitive dysfunction.

Abnormal bone status is primarily associated with insufficient peak bone mass, excessive bone resorption and inadequate new bone formation during the remodelling process [24,25]. In this study, we also measured a range of bone metabolism markers. In the comparison between the PD-CI and PD-nCI groups, we only observed an elevated BALP level in PD-CI patients. BALP is indirectly associated with BMD through bone remodelling balance rather than directly determining bone density. This single marker is insufficient to accurately reflect the alterations in bone homeostasis. In the comparison between the two LCA-derived BMD clusters, we found that patients in the generalized osteoporosis cluster showed significantly elevated levels of bone turnover markers, including CTX, NMID, tP1NP and BALP, as well as lower 25-OH-vitamin D levels. The concurrent elevation of both bone resorption markers (CTX and NMID) and bone formation markers (tP1NP and BALP) indicates a state of high bone turnover. However, in such conditions, bone resorption may exceed or uncouple from bone formation, ultimately leading to net bone loss and reduced BMD. In addition, reduced 25-OH-vitamin D levels may further exacerbate bone loss by impairing calcium homeostasis and promoting secondary hyperparathyroidism. However, these bone metabolism markers are more dynamic compared to BMD, and therefore may be less stable to investigate their associations with cognitive impairment in PD.

Decreased BMD in overall PD patients is well-documented [9,10,26,27]. While several studies have explored the relationship between BMD and motor features in these patients, there has been limited focus on non-motor features. One study examining BMD and motor subtypes in 60 patients found that the PIGD subtype had a higher prevalence of osteoporosis compared to the TD subtype [28]. This result aligns with our finding that the association between osteoporosis and cognitive impairment is more pronounced in nTD patients compared to TD patients. Another study involving 102 PD patients with freezing of gait (FOG) and 46 PD patients without FOG analysed the relationship between BMD and FOG. The results showed a negative correlation between FOG Questionnaire scores and BMD at the total hip and neck of femur [29].

As noted earlier, a diagnosis of osteopenia or osteoporosis is typically based on bone degeneration at a single site [13]. LCA is a powerful statistical method that identifies distinct subgroups within a population based on response patterns. This technique allows for a comprehensive evaluation of complex data and uncovers patterns that may not be apparent through traditional methods [30]. Therefore, our LCA-based approach provides a more accurate reflection of systemic changes in BMD. This new classification leads to uncovering the significant role of osteoporosis in exacerbating the risk of cognitive impairment in PD, a relationship that cannot be detected using the traditional criteria. Additionally, the impact of osteoporosis on cognitive impairment seems to be more pronounced in older or male patients, those whose non-dominant hand side exhibits more severe motor symptoms, in patients with longer disease duration, and in those with the nTD subtype. Indeed, advanced age and prolonged disease duration are well-established risk factors for cognitive impairment in PD [1]. It appears that osteoporotic male patients and those whose non-dominant hand side shows more severe motor symptoms may have a higher likelihood of experiencing cognitive impairment. Previous studies have also shown that PD patients with the nTD subtype experience more severe cognitive impairment compared to those with the TD subtype [31,32].

The central nervous system regulates bone metabolism through sympathetic, parasympathetic and peripheral nerves, while bone can influence the brain through the secretion of bone-derived mediators [33,34]. Neural pathways, hypothalamic neuropeptides, neurotransmitters and bone-derived factors play significant roles in regulating both bone metabolism and brain function [21]. For instance, osteocyte-derived sclerostin has been found to impair cognitive function during AD progression [3]. These pathways and factors may provide insight into how osteoporosis interacts with cognitive impairment in PD. In addition to internal signalling via the brain–bone axis, there may also be external factors at play. PD patients with osteoporosis are more prone to fractures due to falls, which lead to reduced mobility, potentially increasing the risk of cognitive impairment [35]. Furthermore, our findings indicate that patients in the PD-CI group had a higher LEDD. It has been shown in rodent studies that levodopa intake can induce bone loss [36], which may result in a more pronounced reduction of BMD in PD patients with cognitive impairment. On the other hand, cognitive impairment in PD patients may also be a contributing factor to reduced BMD. PD patients with cognitive impairment are often less likely to engage in regular physical exercise, which plays a particularly important role in preserving BMD through mechanical loading and stimulation of bone formation [37,38].

Meanwhile, we have to acknowledge certain limitations. All female participants in this study were postmenopausal. Since oestrogen is known to significantly influence BMD, this factor may act as a confounder when interpreting the study’s findings. Further research is needed to explore whether a synergistic or mediating effect exists between osteoporosis and the subclassification such as male and the nTD subtype in increasing the risk of cognitive impairment in PD patients. In addition, potential confounding factors such as physical activity and nutritional status may influence the association of BMD and cognitive function. Unfortunately, these data were not collected in our study. Nevertheless, several metabolic and nutrition-related indicators, including BMI, lipid profiles, folate and vitamin B12 levels, were measured and incorporated into the analysis, which may partially capture the participants’ nutritional and metabolic status and mitigate, to some extent, the influence of unmeasured lifestyle-related confounding.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings reveal for the first time that generalized osteoporosis, not axial osteopenia, may exacerbate cognitive impairment in PD. Therefore, interventions aimed at mitigating generalized BMD decline could offer a potential therapeutic avenue to improve cognitive function in PD patients.
