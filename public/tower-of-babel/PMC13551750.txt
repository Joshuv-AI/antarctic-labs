Controlled local air-balloon compression chest belt on postoperative seroma in breast cancer: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial in Zunyi, China
Authors: Yan Yang, Li Yang, Nana Zhang, Liwen Kang, Yongmei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeroma formation is a common postoperative complication of breast cancer surgery, leading to delayed wound healing, infection, patient discomfort, and repeated outpatient visits. In severe cases, it can cause skin flap necrosis, imposing physical, psychological, and financial burdens on patients. Applying compression bandaging to the patient’s postoperative wound with a chest strap is an effective measure to prevent seroma formation. However, the commonly used elastic chest straps cannot provide specific compression values, limiting their use. Therefore, we aim to compare the efficacy of standard elastic chest straps versus controlled localised air-balloon compression chest straps in preventing seroma formation following breast cancer surgery.MethodsThis is a superiority, single-blind randomised controlled trial conducted at a tertiary hospital in Guizhou Province, China, involving female breast cancer patients undergoing mastectomy. Eligible participants will be recruited by attending surgeons during routine preoperative consultations. Patients aged 18 years or older diagnosed with unilateral breast cancer who meet the criteria for mastectomy (including modified radical mastectomy and/or simple mastectomy) and provide informed consent will be eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria include patients with severe concomitant diseases before surgery, those with chest deformities or trauma, those with chest strap allergies, and those with psychiatric disorders who are unable to cooperate. Sixty patients were randomly assigned to two groups (control group and intervention group) in a 1:1 ratio, without stratification. Patients in the control group received postoperative compression bandaging with standard elastic chest bandages. In contrast, those in the intervention group received postoperative compression bandaging with a controlled-pressure local air-balloon chest bandage, with pressure sensors measuring the air-balloon inflation level. The air-balloon inflation period lasted one week, with inflation values ranging from 4 to 8 kPa. Both sets of chest straps were worn for one month. Safety measures to be collected include adverse events, postoperative complications (seroma, wound infection), and device-related skin reactions or discomfort. Primary outcomes included seroma incidence, postoperative drainage volume, and drainage tube retention time. Secondary outcomes encompassed quality of life, comfort, and satisfaction with the chest straps.DiscussionPostoperative wound compression dressing is essential for promoting flap-to-chest wall adhesion, reducing cavities, preventing seroma formation, and facilitating wound healing. Introduction

Background and rationale {9a}

Globally, breast cancer was the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women in 2022, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 670,000 deaths. Due to population growth and aging, the global burden is projected to exceed 3 million new cases and 1 million deaths annually by 2040 [1, 2]. In China, breast cancer has become the leading cause of cancer-related death among women, accounting for 23.8% of all female malignant tumors, with its incidence rising annually and affecting increasingly younger populations [3]. Surgery is one of the common treatments for breast cancer [4]. However, due to the extensive resection involved, patients may develop postoperative complications such as seroma. A study from Saudi Arabia demonstrated that the incidence of seroma varies widely, ranging from 10 to 85% [5]. If left untreated, seroma can lead to wound infection, prolong hospital stays, delay subsequent treatments, and reduce patients’ quality of life [6, 7]. Therefore, preventing postoperative seroma is crucial.

Studies indicate that post-operative compression bandaging for breast cancer not only facilitates hemostasis and inhibits lymphatic secretion but also promotes tight adhesion between the skin flap and chest wall. This reduces the formation of dead space cavities, accelerates healing, and minimizes wound exudate, making it a standard method for preventing seroma formation after breast cancer surgery [8]. Zou et al. [9] demonstrated that applying compression to the surgical site in patients following inguinal hernia repair effectively reduces the incidence of postoperative seroma, alleviates acute postoperative pain, and improves quality of life. This confirms the efficacy of postoperative compression in preventing seroma formation.

Currently, compression bandaging commonly employs standard elastic chest straps. The surgical area is padded with gauze to level it with the unaffected breast, then secured with the chest strap. However, the strap’s tightness is primarily adjusted based on the physician’s clinical experience. Excessive tightness may impair respiration and cause skin damage, while insufficient tightness fails to provide adequate wound compression. This prevents the flap from adhering tightly to the chest wall, leaving a cavity prone to fluid and gas accumulation, which can lead to complications such as seroma. This method has certain limitations and results in low patient satisfaction with the chest band. To address this, Yang et al. [10] and Meng et al. [11] designed a fixed chest strap that applies pressure to the wound via an air balloon, with a pressure gauge determining the specific compression value for wound dressing in patients undergoing modified radical mastectomy for breast cancer. Results indicated that the chest strap enhanced patient comfort and reduced postoperative complications. However, whether the air-balloon compression is uniform and whether the pressure gauge readings accurately reflect the pressure applied to the wound remains to be verified.

Explanation for the choice of comparator {9b}

The control group received postoperative compression bandaging with standard elastic chest bandages. This comparator was chosen because standard elastic chest bandages represent the current routine clinical practice for preventing seroma after mastectomy in our institution and in most Chinese hospitals. By comparing the novel controlled local air-balloon compression chest belt against this established standard of care, we can determine whether the new device offers any clinically meaningful advantages in terms of reducing seroma incidence or improving patient comfort.

Objectives {10}

This study is a superiority randomised controlled trial designed to test the hypothesis that the controlled local air-balloon compression chest belt is more effective than standard elastic chest bandages in reducing postoperative seroma in female breast cancer patients undergoing mastectomy. Methods: patient and public involvement, and trial design

Patient and public involvement {11}

Patients and the public were not involved in the study design, and patient involvement began only during the intervention phase of the research process. The research questions and outcome measures were not grounded in patients’ priorities and preferences.

Trial design {12}

This study is a single-centre, parallel-group, superiority randomised controlled trial conducted in accordance with the SPIRIT reporting guidelines [12]. Eligible patients will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the intervention group (receiving the controlled local air-balloon compression chest belt) or the control group (receiving standard elastic chest bandages). No changes to the trial design are anticipated after trial commencement (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Intervention and comparator

Intervention and comparator description {15a}

Both groups received identical surgical procedures, medications, and perioperative care administered by the same treatment team. The control group underwent postoperative compression bandaging using standard elastic chest bandages, while the observation group received postoperative compression bandaging with a controlled-pressure local air-balloon chest bandage. Both groups wore the chest bandages for one month. Perioperative care includes preoperative education, drainage tube management, guidance on functional exercises for the affected upper limb, dietary advice, psychological support, and condition monitoring.

Control Group: Standard elastic chest bandage compression dressing. Following mastectomy for breast cancer patients, cover the incision with sterile dressings and pack the axillary and other concave areas on the affected side with sterile gauze to ensure a flat wound surface. Postoperatively, elevate the patient’s head and chest to achieve a “suspended” position. Lay a standard elastic chest band flat beneath the patient’s back. Extend both sides of the band from the axillae, then simultaneously pull them forward toward the midline with even tension to secure the band. The tightness should allow two fingers to fit comfortably beneath the band.

Observation Group (Fig. 2): Compression bandaging using a controlled-pressure local air-balloon chest strap. Following mastectomy in breast cancer patients, cover the incision with sterile dressings and pack the axilla with sterile gauze. Elevate the patient’s head and chest to achieve a “suspended” position. Lay the chest strap flat beneath the patient’s back, then simultaneously pull both sides evenly toward the midline, positioning the chest air-balloon pouch over the surgical site. Select the compression site based on the patient’s incision location. Place the pressure sensor within the compression area of the chest strap. Inflate the air balloon by squeezing the inflation ball. Stop inflating when the monitor reading reaches the predicted pressure. Research indicates 4–8 kPa is the safe range for flap growth [14]. Therefore, the air-balloon pressure must not fall below 4 kPa nor exceed 8 kPa. Daily monitoring with the pressure sensor ensures the balloon pressure remains within the safe compression range. The sensor, about the size of a coin, can be easily removed from the chest strap after pressure measurement. Replace or adjust the balloon immediately if it is damaged or leaking.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated intervention/comparator {15b}

Inform patients that if they experience discomfort or any unusual symptoms while wearing the chest strap, they should promptly notify medical staff. During the intervention, closely monitor the patient’s chest wall and skin condition. If any abnormalities are detected, immediately discontinue use of the chest strap and administer symptomatic treatment as directed by medical instructions to ensure patient safety. During hospitalisation, healthcare providers should continuously monitor the proper fit of the chest strap, the accuracy of the compression site, and whether the air-balloon pressure remains within the appropriate range. Replace or adjust the air balloon promptly if any damage or leakage is detected. After discharge, instruct the patient on the correct use and precautions for the chest strap.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention/comparator {15c}

Explain to the patient the necessity and importance of wearing the chest strap, and inform them of potential postoperative complications to enhance compliance. Data collection researchers conduct regular follow-up visits with patients according to the study protocol.

Concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {15d}

During the trial period, patients are permitted to engage in general functional exercises for the upper limbs, but vigorous movements of the affected limb and strenuous physical activities are strictly prohibited.

Ancillary and post-trial care {34}

After the trial intervention period concludes, patients will continue to be followed up for one month. The purpose of this follow-up is to monitor for late-onset complications (e.g., delayed seroma formation, wound infection, flap necrosis), assess wound healing status, and evaluate patients’ postoperative comfort and quality of life. Follow-up assessments will be conducted during outpatient clinic visits at one week and four weeks post-intervention. If a patient suffers harm as a result of the trial, appropriate compensation will be provided in accordance with institutional guidelines and ethical approval requirements.

(2)Satisfaction Survey Questionnaire

Satisfaction Survey Questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire was used to assess patient satisfaction with chest strap usage. Postoperative patients rated comfort, convenience, impact on breathing depth, and impact on axillary skin damage using a 5-point scale: Very satisfied: 5 points, Satisfied: 4 points, Neutral: 3 points, Somewhat dissatisfied: 2 points, Dissatisfied: 1 point. Satisfaction Scoring Criteria: 3–5 points indicate satisfaction; 1–2 points indicate dissatisfaction. The total satisfaction score ranges from 4 to 20 points, with higher scores indicating greater overall satisfaction with the chest strap.

(3)Comfort Assessment Questionnaire.

Comfort Assessment Questionnaire.

The Comfort Assessment Scale, developed by Kolcaba [18], was employed. This scale has been widely used in clinical settings to evaluate patient comfort. Zhu et al. [19] adapted the scale into Chinese, demonstrating good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.92. The scale comprises 28 items across four dimensions: physical, psychological, spiritual, and environmental. Responses are rated on a 1–4 Likert scale, where one indicates “strongly disagree, 2 “disagree,” 3 “agree, and 4 “strongly agree.” For reverse-scored items, one denotes “strongly agree,” 2 “agree,” 3 “disagree, and 4 “strongly disagree.” Score range and interpretation: The total score ranges from 28 to 112. Higher scores indicate greater comfort, while lower scores reflect poorer comfort levels.

(4)Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Breast (FACT-B)

Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy-Breast (FACT-B)

The Breast Cancer Quality of Life Scale (FACT-B), developed by Cella et al. [20] in 1997 and subsequently translated into Chinese by Zhang et al. [21] in 2003, demonstrated good reliability and validity, with a split-half reliability coefficient of 0.88 and a criterion-related validity coefficient of 0.85. It is suitable for assessing the quality of life in breast cancer patients. The questionnaire comprises five dimensions and 36 items, with the following item counts per dimension: Physical Health (GP): 7 items; Social/Family Health (GS): 7 items; Emotional Health (GE): 6 items; Functional Health (GF): 7 items; Additional Concerns (B): 9 items Additional Concerns are specific to breast cancer patients. Each item employs a 5-point Likert scale: “Not at all” = 0, “Slightly” = 1, ‘Somewhat’ = 2, “Quite a bit” = 3, “Very much” = 4. Reverse-scored items include GP1–GP7, GE1, GE3–GE6, B1–B3, and B5–B8; all others are forward-scored. The total score ranges from 0 to 144, with higher scores indicating a better quality of life.

Sample size {19}

The primary outcome measures of this study include binary variables (incidence of complications such as subcutaneous seroma and flap necrosis) and continuous variables (e.g., drainage volume and duration of drain retention). The sample size calculation was based on the primary continuous outcome, using the formula for comparing two independent sample means: n1 = n2 = 2[(μα + μβ)σ/δ]2, where n represents the required sample size per group, σ denotes the standard deviation of both samples (typically estimated using the sample standard deviation, with the larger of the two sample standard deviations generally selected). Δ signifies the clinically significant difference between the two means. This study adopts α = 0.05, β = 0.10, and a two-tailed test. Consulting tables yields μα = 1.96 and μβ = 1.28. Based on relevant literature [22], σ = 3.94 and δ = 4.21 were selected. Calculations indicate 19 subjects per group. Accounting for a 20% dropout rate and practical considerations, the total sample size is set at 60 subjects (30 per group).

Recruitment {20}

Patients were recruited from a Grade III Class A general hospital in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province, which integrates teaching, clinical care, and research. This hospital performs approximately 500 surgical procedures for breast cancer patients annually, ensuring sufficient case numbers to reliably collect research subjects meeting both inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Assignment of interventions: randomisation

Sequence generation: who will generate the sequence {21a}

This study employed the random number table in SPSS 29.0 software for allocation.

Sequence generation: type of randomisation {21b}

Patients were randomly assigned to the observation group and the control group using a random number table, with 30 patients in each group (without stratification).

Allocation concealment mechanism {22}

The specific method was as follows: A sequence of numbers from 1 to 60 was established. Using the random number generator in SPSS 29.0, each number was assigned a value of 0 or 1. The subjects were then sorted by admission order. The assigned numbers were matched with the corresponding values: those assigned zero were assigned to the control group using standard elastic chest straps. In contrast, those assigned one were assigned to the observation group using controlled local air-balloon compression chest straps.

Implementation {23}

After signing the informed consent form, researchers will use SPSS 29.0 software to group patients.

Assignment of interventions: blinding

Who will be blinded {24a}

Due to the participatory nature of the intervention measures, it is impossible to blind the implementers.

How will be blinding be achieved {24b}

This study will employ a blinded design, with patients in the observation group and control group assigned to different wards for nursing care. Both groups will receive identical surgical procedures, medications, and perioperative care, administered by the same treatment team responsible for surgery and perioperative nursing.

Procedure for unblinding if needed {24c}

If unblinding is required for patient safety reasons (e.g., occurrence of a serious adverse event that necessitates knowledge of group allocation), the attending physician shall contact the individual responsible for generating the randomisation sequence. The responsible individual shall disclose the patient’s group allocation only after verifying medical necessity. All unblinding events will be documented, including the reason, date, and the person performing the unblinding. When the postoperative chest band is discontinued after one month of wear, the patient is released from the blind method.

Statistical methods

Statistical methods for primary and secondary outcomes {27a}

Statistical software and significance level

This trial will be reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 statement [23]. Statistical analysis will be performed using SPSS 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) [24]. A two-sided significance level of α = 0.05 will be used for all statistical tests, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All effect estimates will be reported with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). Normality of continuous variables will be assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Multiple comparisons adjustment

To account for multiplicity arising from the three primary outcomes (seroma incidence, postoperative drainage volume, and drainage tube retention time), a Bonferroni correction will be applied. A p-value threshold of α = 0.05 will be considered statistically significant for each primary outcome. Secondary outcomes will be reported with nominal p-values and interpreted as exploratory. For baseline comparisons, no multiplicity adjustment will be applied, as these are descriptive in nature and intended to assess randomisation success rather than test formal hypotheses.

For data not conforming to a normal distribution, results will be presented as median with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range, IQR).

Who will be included in each analysis {27b}

The ITT population, comprising all randomised patients, will serve as the primary analysis set for all efficacy outcomes, with patients analysed according to their original group assignment. The PP population, including patients who complete the intervention without major protocol deviations, will be used for sensitivity analyses. The safety population, comprising all patients who receive the assigned intervention, will be used for adverse event analyses.

Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis

All randomised patients (n = 60) will be included in the primary analysis of efficacy outcomes, regardless of whether they complete the intervention or adhere to the protocol. Patients will be analysed according to their original randomised group assignment. No patients will be excluded from the ITT analysis, even in the event of withdrawal of consent, loss to follow-up, or major protocol violations, as the ITT principle requires inclusion of all randomised participants. Missing outcome data will be handled as described in {27c}.

Per-protocol (PP) analysis

The PP analysis will include patients who: ①Complete the full 1-month intervention period; ②Do not have major protocol deviations (e.g., discontinuation of chest strap wear for > 3 consecutive days); ③Have no missing data for the primary outcome.

Patients will be excluded from the PP analysis if they: ①Withdraw consent before completing the intervention; ②Are lost to follow-up without outcome data; ③Have major protocol violations as defined above. The PP analysis will be used as a sensitivity analysis to assess the robustness of the ITT findings.

Safety analysis

All patients who receive at least one dose of the assigned intervention will be included in the safety analysis for adverse events and harm outcomes. This includes all patients in both groups who undergo surgery and receive the chest strap (intervention group) or standard bandages (control group). No patients will be excluded from the safety analysis, as the primary aim is to capture all potential harm events regardless of protocol adherence.

Discussion

There is currently no unified consensus on the aetiology of seromas. However, studies have demonstrated that acute inflammatory exudative reactions play a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of seroma formation. The underlying mechanism of seroma formation is attributed to lymphatic vessel rupture following mastectomy and the creation of dead space during flap elevation [25]. Montalto et.al [26]. indicated that seroma is not merely an inflammatory exudate but rather an accumulation of extracellular fluid derived from serum ultrafiltration in the region upstream of excised lymph nodes. It represents a subcutaneous accumulation of non-infected fluid containing cells and proteins. Benevento et al. [27] similarly noted that seromas form due to fluid accumulation in residual dead space resulting from extensive tissue removal during mastectomy, creating cavities, combined with leakage from small blood vessels, blood, and lymphatic vessels. This aligns with the perspective of Fu et al. [28]. Therefore, compression bandaging plays a critical role in postoperative recovery for breast cancer patients. Postoperative wound compression dressing is essential for promoting flap-to-chest wall adhesion, reducing cavities, preventing seroma formation, and facilitating wound healing.

Currently, postoperative wound compression for breast cancer patients primarily relies on conventional chest straps or an air balloon. Compression pressure is mainly adjusted based on physicians’ clinical experience or pressure gauge readings [8, 14, 29–32]. However, questions remain about the uniformity of compression and the scientific validity of pressure measurements that reflect wound stress. Therefore, this randomised controlled trial employed a zoned design for the chest band air-balloon and utilised pressure sensors to measure compression levels. The study aimed to investigate the impact of a controllable localised air-balloon compression chest belt on preventing seroma formation, thereby providing a reference for post-operative seroma prevention and other complications in breast cancer patients. For patients, the controlled-pressure, locally applied chest belt enhances postoperative comfort and reduces physical and psychological burdens. For healthcare providers, clarifying the device’s efficacy improves standardisation of postoperative management, thereby promoting patient recovery.
