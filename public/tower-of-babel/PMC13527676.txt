Reconstructing the PHI Surface Microenvironment for In Situ Immobilization of Ionic Liquids to Enhance CO2 Conversion
Authors: Leizhi Zheng, Mengyue Li, Bin He, Qiang Liu, Chengbao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphitic carbon nitride (CN) has emerged as a promising candidate for CO2 capture and conversion due to its structural tenability and abundant surface active sites. However, CN suffers from inherent drawbacks such as poor catalytic activity, leading to unsatisfactory utilization. In this study, a rational strategy combining organic superbases as hydrogen bond acceptors with hydrolyzed high crystallinity CN (poly­(heptazine imide), PHI) derivatives as hydrogen bond donors was developed to form ionic liquid-functionalized PHI catalysts. This design strategically integrates amino and hydrogen bond functionalities within a single polymeric framework, thereby cooperatively accelerating the coupling of epoxides with CO2. Under the conditions of 0.1 MPa CO2, 90 °C, and 6 h, [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] achieved chloropropene carbonate (CPC) in 99% yield with 99% selectivity. Based on the multifunctional structure and experimental results of the catalyst, a plausible pathway emphasizing intramolecular cooperative activation was subsequently elucidated. Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst enabled facile recovery and reuse via simple solid–liquid separation. This work establishes a sustainable catalytic platform that merges ionic liquid functionality with polymeric semiconductor matrices for green chemical transformations.

Introduction

The utilization of carbon dioxide (CO2) as a feedstock has garnered significant attention as a promising strategy, owing to its abundance, environmental benignity, and low cost. , This approach is consistent with global initiatives that aim to achieve carbon neutrality and support carbon peaking targets. The conversion of CO2 into value-added chemicals and fuels, including cyclic carbonates, − carbon monoxide (CO), − methanol, − and methane, represents a significant research frontier in sustainable chemistry, offering a dual benefit of carbon emission reduction and resource valorization. Among these alternative chemical processes, the construction of cyclic carbonates via coupling pathways has attracted considerable attention, primarily due to its complete atom utilization. Furthermore, cyclic carbonates are versatile intermediates and raw materials with diverse applications, including organic solvents, monomers for polymers, and organic intermediates of polycarbonate synthesis. , However, the inherently low reactivity of CO2, characterized by its thermodynamic stability and kinetic inertness, represents a significant barrier to achieving efficient catalytic transformations under mild reaction conditions. , As a result, the design and optimization of highly active and selective catalytic systems remain the central challenge in advancing CO2 conversion technologies.

Over the past few decades, significant research efforts have been devoted to developing diverse catalytic systems for CO2 conversion, which can be broadly categorized into homogeneous catalysts (e.g., organic bases, ionic liquids (ILs) − ) and heterogeneous catalysts (e.g., metal oxides, − activated carbon, − metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) , ). Homogeneous catalysts typically offer well-defined active sites and high intrinsic activity, but often suffer from difficulties in separation and recovery. In contrast, heterogeneous catalysts offer key practical benefits for industrial implementation, notably facile separation, recovery, and reuse from the reaction mixture, as well as good structural stability under continuous operation. However, heterogeneous catalysts also present several limitations, such as the organic solvents, lengthy reaction durations, and so on. For instance, Zhao and co-workers developed boron-doped porous carbons as catalysts for the cycloaddition of CO2 with epoxides. In this reaction system, tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB) was introduced as a cocatalyst. However, the homogeneous nature of TBAB complicated catalyst separation and recovery, limiting its practical application. Therefore, the development of efficient, bifunctional (or multifunctional) heterogeneous catalysts for CO2 cycloaddition remains a critical research objective.

Ionic liquids (ILs) are highly attractive as homogeneous catalysts for chemical reactions owing to their unique combination of advantageous properties, including negligible toxicity, exceptional thermal stability, and tunable reactivity. Furthermore, the tunable nature of ILs facilitates the rational selection or design of specific anions and cations. This design flexibility permits strategic optimization of catalytic performance to meet specific reaction requirements while adhering to green chemistry principles and maintaining high process efficiency. Therefore, recent advances demonstrate that ILs can be covalently immobilized on the surface of heterogeneous catalysts to fabricate supported ionic liquid phase (SILP) catalysts. SILP catalysts offer the benefits of enhanced catalyst recyclability and separation, while retaining the high activity and selectivity often associated with homogeneous ILs catalysts. For example, Zhang et. al devised a new route to directly prepare porous hyper cross-linked poly­(ionic liquid)­s (HPILs) featuring nucleophilic Br– sites and Lewis acid Zn2+ sites, which enhanced catalytic activity. Jadhav and his co-workers synthesized novel covalent ionic liquid (IL)-functionalized activated carbon (AC-ILs) catalysts for CO2 conversion. The immobilized AC-ILs exhibited superior structural properties, including high thermal stability and a considerable surface area.

Graphitic carbon nitride (CN) is a polymeric semiconductor material characterized by its facile synthesis and cost-effectiveness. − Notably, the inherent amino functional groups of CN exhibit catalytic potential for CO2 activation. However, the intrinsic catalytic activity of CN remains limited. To address this limitation, strategic modifications are required to engineer abundant active sites on the CN framework. This structural optimization enables effective anchoring of ILs, particularly those derived from conventional organic superbases. These nitrogen-rich heterocyclic compounds exhibit exceptional hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA) capabilities, serving as crucial building blocks for constructing ILs-based catalytic systems that demonstrate remarkable catalytic performance in CO2 conversion processes. Guided by this design principle, we developed a rational strategy combining organic superbases as HBAs with hydrolyzed poly­(heptazine imide) (PHI-NH2) derivatives as hydrogen bond donors (HBD). This synergistic combination yielded a novel class of ionic liquid-functionalized poly­(heptazine imide) hybrid catalysts (Scheme ). The resulting catalytic system demonstrates outstanding performance in the cycloaddition of CO2 to epoxides under environmentally benign conditions. Notably, this halogen-free catalytic system operates efficiently without requiring additional cocatalysts, achieving both high conversion rates and excellent selectivity. Moreover, the hybrid catalysts exhibit exceptional stability and reusability.

Materials and Methods

Materials

All chemicals and solvents were used as received without further purification unless otherwise specified. CO2 (95 mol %) was purchased from Wuxi Shengma Gas Co., Ltd. 1,8-Diazabicyclo[5.4.0]­undec-7-ene (DBU) and 1,5-diazabicyclo[4.3.0]­non-5-ene (DBN) were supplied by Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd. Dicyandiamide, Ethyl alcohol, piperazine, Potassium chloride, Lithium chloride, and epichlorohydrin (ECH) were obtained from Energy Chemical. Utilizing a Bruker Avance 400 instrument, the 1H NMR spectra was captured at 400 MHz within a deuterated chloroform environment. Chemical shifts (δ) was reported in parts per million referenced relative to tetramethylsilane as an internal standard.

Preparation of PHI

The form of CN was synthesized via thermal polymerization of Dicyandiamide. Specifically, an alumina vessel was loaded with a 4 g of Dicyandiamide particles before transfer into a muffle reactor under air environments. Temperature rose to 550 °C at 5 °C min–1, then held for 4 h. Upon cooling to ambient conditions, collect the pale-yellow CN product. Further grinding yields a finely divided powder.

Production of PHI proceeded via a molten salt chlorination etching strategy. Initially, solid fractions including CN, KCl, and anhydrous LiCl (2, 12, and 8 g, respectively) were pulverized cooperatively using an agate mortar. This homogenized powder then moved into an alumina crucible. Heating occurred under air using a muffle furnace. Temperature rose from ambient to 550 °C at 5 °C min–1, holding there for 4 h. Following thermal treatment, the crucible cooled naturally down to room conditions. Hot distilled water washed this product four times, removing residual salts. Finally, oven drying at 60 °C lasted 12 h, yielding pure PHI powder.

Synthesis of IL-Based PHI

The ILs-functionalized PHI was synthesized through a two-step protocol involving organic base modification and precursor functionalization. In the first step, 1 g pristine PHI went into 50 mL of 2 mol/L HCl inside a 100 mL beaker. This mixture stirred at 80 °C for 48 h to hydrolyze terminal cyano groups (C≣N) into amine functionalities, yielding PHI-NH2. After finishing, discard supernatant. Wash crude product five times using distilled water, eliminating residual HCl. Finally, vacuum-dry at 60 °C over 12 h to obtain purified PHI-NH2.

In a typical procedure, the obtained 0.2 g PHI-NH2 was then reacted with 30 mmol DBU in ethanol at 70 °C for 24 h to yield the final IL-PHI product ([PHI-NH]­[DBUH]). Following the completion of the synthetic process, the suspended catalyst particles were harvested from the reaction broth. Unconverted DBU species were systematically stripped away by flushing the mass with fresh ethyl alcohol three consecutive times. Finally, the product was dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 12 h to afford pure [PHI-NH]­[DBUH]. The synthesis of [PHI-NH]­[DBNH] was prepared using the same method.

Cycloaddition Reaction of CO2

[PHI-NH]­[DBUH] was tested as a catalyst in the cycloaddition of CO2 to CPC. In a typical experiment, ECH (15 mmol) and the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst (40 mg) were added to a 25 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel reactor. The reactor was thoroughly purged with dry CO2 (3 times) to remove any residual air, and then pressurized to 1 MPa with CO2. The reaction mixture was then heated to 80 °C and stirred for 10 h. After the reaction, the reactor was cooled to room temperature, and the yield of cyclic carbonate was determined by 1H NMR spectroscopy. The CPC yield was calculated as the molar ratio of CPC to ECH (CPC/ECH, mol/mol), based on 1H NMR integration using piperazine as an internal standard (Figure S1).

Reuse of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH]

After completion of the reaction, the solid catalyst was harvested via filtration and rinsed three times with anhydrous ethanol to eliminate residual reactants. Subsequently, the catalyst was dried under vacuum at 60 °C for 24 h to fully remove moisture and residual solvent. The reclaimed catalyst was preserved for running consecutive recycling evaluations.

Results and Discussion

The microstructural morphology development of CN, PHI and [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalysts was comprehensively examined via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as depicted in Figure S2. The pristine CN precursor (Figure S2a) exhibited a highly aggregated architecture with disordered lamellar stacking and an abundance of mesoporous channels that were formed by the thermal polymerization process. With the help of the molten KCl/LiCl media, it showed that the pristine CN was trimmed into nanorod morphologies (Figure S2b). Following ionic modification with DBU, the derived PHI phase maintained its anisotropic rod-like morphology without particle coalescence in Figure S2c, demonstrating remarkable resistance to structural collapse under basic conditions. As shown in Figure S3a, the N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of all samples exhibited a typical H3-type hysteresis loop, indicating a wedge-shaped pore structure. The specific surface area of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] was measured to be 36.86 m2·g–1, which is lower than that of PHI-NH2 (73.05 m2·g–1). This decrease confirmed successful grafting of the ionic liquid onto the material surface, partially occupying the pore channels. The Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore size distribution (Figure S3b) revealed that the average pore size of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] was larger than that of PHI-NH2. This increase in average pore diameter was beneficial for reducing mass transfer resistance, thereby facilitating the diffusion of reactants (CO2 and epoxides) toward the active sites. As shown in Figure S4a, the TGA profiles of the fresh [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] were recorded from 30 to 700 °C. Upon heating to approximately 200 °C, a slight mass loss is observed, which is attributed to the removal of absorbed atmospheric moisture by the ionic liquid. The DSC curve (Figure S4b) revealed an endothermic peak near 260 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of the confined ionic liquid. A second thermal feature at around 440 °C is assigned to the collapse of the PHI framework. Therefore, these results confirmed that the IL was successfully grafted onto the surface of PHI-NH2.

In order to analyze the crystal structure of CN, PHI, PHI-NH2, [PHI-NH]­[DBNH], and [PHI-NH]­[DBUH], X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed. As presented in Figure , CN exhibited two distinct diffraction peaks at 13.1° and 27.4°, which correspond to the (100) in-plane structural packing motif and the (002) interlayer stacking of melon-based polymeric frameworks, respectively, in accordance with previous reports. , After the introduction of the molten KCl/LiCl, the XRD pattern of PHI revealed a markedly different diffraction profile compared to CN. Notably, the (100) crystal plane shifted to lower angle (from 13.1° to 8°), while the (002) peak exhibited a subtle shift to 28.2°, indicating that the interlayer spacing decreased. Intriguingly, two additional peaks emerged at 21.3° and 35.1°, which is also consistent with previous literature. , Following hydrolysis and ionization, the XRD patterns of PHI-NH2, [PHI-NH]­[DBNH], and [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] remained largely unchanged from that of PHI, indicating that the crystalline phase of the catalysts was preserved throughout these transformations.

The functional groups of the materials were investigated by FT-IR spectroscopy. As shown in Figure a, the spectra of the CN exhibited distinct vibrational signatures, where the absorption band range from 1700 to 1200 cm–1 peaks corresponded to various vibrations of CN heterocycle. Moreover, the distinctive absorption peak observed at 802 cm–1 could be ascribed to the breathing mode of the s-triazine ring system. After the introduction of molten salt, the vibrational signatures of PHI were found to be consistent with those of CN. However, the new vibration peak at around at 2184 cm–1 was assigned to the stretching vibration of the terminal C≣N, suggesting that the melon phase transformed into the phase of PHI. Besides, the observed broad absorption band centered around 3200 cm–1 was attributed to the N–H stretching vibrations of primary and secondary amines present at defect sites because of intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions. The peak centered at 2184 cm–1 was decreased under acidic conditions, indicating the hydrolysis of C≣N. The spectral features observed between 3300 and 3500 cm–1 were consistent with the presence of N–H stretching vibrations associated with the amino group. An additional peak observed at 1550 cm–1 corresponded to the bending vibration of the N–H of the NH2 group. Furthermore, the characteristic absorption band corresponding to the CO stretching vibration was observed at 1630 cm–1, resulting in the successful synthesis of amide group-enriched PHI-NH2 sample. The FT-IR spectra of organic base modified PHI-NH2 was performed. As depicted in Figure b, the peak at 3194 cm–1 was attributed to N–H stretching vibrations, suggesting the potential existence of a protonated organic base species. Meanwhile, the signal at 2930 cm–1 was in accordance with the stretching vibration of the C–H bonds. The weak absorption band at 1148 cm–1 was allocated to the C–N stretching vibration of an aminoalkyl group, which indicated the successful organic base functionalization.

(a, b) FT-IR spectra of the CN, PHI, PHI-NH2, [PHI-NH]­[DBNH], and [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] materials.

To clarify surface elemental distribution and chemical bonding states of CN, PHI and [PHI-NH]­[DBUH], X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurement was implemented. Full-scan XPS profiles in Figure a identify distinct characteristic peaks assigned to C 1s, O 1s and N 1s over all tested catalytic samples. Furthermore, the XPS of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] was conducted to analyze the C 1s binding energy. The C 1s XPS spectrum (Figure b) was deconvoluted into four peaks at 284.8 eV (C–C), 286.4 eV (C–O), 288 eV (NCN), and 288.7 eV (CO), confirming the existence of amides into the nanoparticle structure after hydrolyzation of cyano group, which was agreement with that of FT-IR of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH]. For the O 1s high-resolution XPS in Figure c, the peaks at 532.1 and 533.4 eV in the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] materials were ascribed to CO and C–O, respectively. As shown in Figure d, the N 1s high-resolution spectrum further identified three peaks at 398.6 eV (CNC), 399.8 eV (N-(C)3), and 400.7 eV (N–H), respectively. The results validated the successful functionalization of the nanoparticles with ionic liquid components.

The catalytic activity of PHI and a series of PHI-supported ILs catalysts was systematically evaluated for the cycloaddition of ECH with CO2. ECH and CO2 cycloaddition acted as a model reaction to evaluate catalytic efficiencies. The corresponding yields of CPC were quantitatively determined through 1H NMR spectroscopy in Figures S5–S7. No product formation was observed by 1H NMR in the absence of a catalyst (Table , entry 1), indicating that the CO2 cycloaddition reaction requires catalytic activation to proceed efficiently. Under identical conditions, the PHI catalyst achieved a 27% yield of CPC, confirming its intrinsic catalytic activity (Table , entry 2). Notably, the introduction of ILs onto the PHI surface significantly enhanced the reaction performance. Specifically, the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst exhibited superior catalytic efficiency in terms of both yield and selectivity compared to [PHI-NH]­[DBNH] (Table , entries 3 and 4). This difference in catalytic performance may be attributed to the effect of different functional groups of hydrogen bonds. Among all tested catalysts, [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] demonstrated marginally higher activity than both PHI and [PHI-NH]­[DBNH].

To thoroughly understand the catalytic behavior, the cycloaddition process was systematically optimized by evaluating critical parameters, including reaction duration, thermal states, and CO2 pressure. As shown in Figure a, the CPC yield exhibited a clear temperature-dependent behavior. As the reaction temperature raised from 70 to 90 °C, the CPC yield increased steadily and reached a high level of 99% at 90 °C. This behavior aligned well with the thermal activation of CO2 and the enhanced ring-opening susceptibility of ECH at elevated temperatures, which collectively enabled the reaction coordinates to effectively overcome the activation energy barrier of the cycloaddition process. This behavior is consistent with the thermodynamic activation of CO2 and the enhanced ring-opening reactivity of ECH under elevated temperatures, which lowers the activation energy barrier for the cycloaddition reaction. Notably, the selectivity for CPC remained at 99% across all temperature ranges, underscoring the high efficiency and stability of the catalyst in suppressing side reactions. To ascertain the optimal reaction duration at 90 °C, a time-dependent was performed (Figure b). The profiling revealed that the CPC yield scaled up progressively with expanding contact time, eventually reaching a plateau at the 6 h mark, where a near-quantitative yield of up to 99% was successfully delivered. This suggests that the reaction reached completion within this interval, as the yield exhibited no significant enhancement beyond 6 h. Therefore, considering both reaction efficiency and potential cost savings associated was selected as the optimal reaction temperature of 6 h.

The influence of CO2 pressure on reaction efficiency was also investigated (Figure a). The CPC yield remained constant at 99% when the CO2 pressure was reduced from 1 to 0.1 MPa under a reaction temperature of 90 °C and a duration of 6 h. This suggests that the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst maintained high activity even at lower CO2 pressure. The recyclability of [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] was evaluated under optimized reaction conditions (40 mg catalyst, 0.1 MPa CO2, 90 °C for 6 h) for cyclic carbonate production. After the reaction, the catalyst was recovered via centrifugation, washed three times with anhydrous ethanol to remove adsorbed species, and subsequently reused. As depicted in Figure b, a conspicuous deactivation profile was observed during the initial two consecutive runs. This decline was ascribed to the blocking of active sites by adsorbed species on the catalyst surface. However, from the second cycle through the sixth, both the yield of CPC (62%) and the selectivity toward the cyclic carbonate (>99%) remained remarkably constant. This consistent performance provided strong evidence for the excellent operational stability and reusability under prolonged operation. Furthermore, the synthesis of the catalyst involved straightforward, scalable steps using readily available starting materials, supporting its potential for large-scale preparation. Structural robustness under prolonged operation was further evidenced by the recovered catalyst after extended use. These findings collectively highlighted the promise of the catalyst for practical and industrial applications. Furthermore, FT-IR spectroscopy of the recovered [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] (Figure S8) revealed the presence of all characteristic peaks of the original catalyst. Notably, the peak at 1805 cm–1 was attributed to the CO stretching vibration of residual PC. , These results demonstrates that [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] maintained its structural integrity throughout multiple catalytic cycles while exhibiting outstanding recyclability.

Based on the experimental results shown in Table , the substrate scope of the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst was systematically investigated for the cycloaddition of CO2 with a series of epoxides bearing different substituents. Under the optimized reaction conditions (epoxide: 15 mmol, catalyst: 40 mg, 90 °C, 1 MPa, 6 h), the corresponding cyclic carbonates were obtained in high yields for most of the tested substrates, with product yields quantified by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S9–S13). As shown in Table , entries 1–4, epoxides bearing electron-withdrawing substituents such as Cl and Br, as well as those containing hydroxyl or aliphatic chains, were smoothly converted to the corresponding cyclic carbonates with excellent yields of 99% in each case. This high reactivity was attributed to the electron-withdrawing halogen substituents, which facilitated epoxide ring-opening via nucleophilic attack. In contrast, epoxides featuring a flexible tether or a bulky fused-ring structure yielded only 20% and 4% of product, respectively (entries 5 and 6). These results suggested that steric encumbrance near the oxirane ring was a key determinant of activity. Therefore, the catalyst exhibits excellent performance for a wide range of epoxides under mild conditions, while substrates with high steric hindrance remain challenging. As summarized in Table , the vast majority of previously reported catalysts typically necessitated either stringent reaction conditions or the incorporation of metal-centered active sites to achieve satisfactory catalytic efficiency. In marked contrast, the metal- and halogen-free [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst exhibited superior catalytic performance under significantly milder conditions (0.1 MPa CO2, 90 °C), delivering exceptional substrate conversion and selectivity. Theoretical insights from Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations by Li et al. provided strong support for our proposed activation pathway. Their work demonstrated that the C–O bond lengths of ECH was elongated from 1.433 to 1.454 Å, respectively. This observation confirmed that hydrogen atoms of imino protons within the catalyst form hydrogen-bonding interactions with oxygen atoms of the epoxide. DFT calculations further identified that the N–H groups on CCN serve as suitable active sites for CO2 adsorption, with an adsorption energy (E ads) of −0.72 eV. This is consistent with our own previous 13C NMR studies, which confirmed the formation of a key carbamate intermediate from the reaction of CO2 with a DBN/o-phenylenediamines system. Based on the results presented in this work and previous reports, a plausible reaction mechanism for the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH]-catalyzed conversion of CO2 into cyclic carbonates was proposed in Scheme . Initially, a hydrogen bond formed between the epoxide and [HDBU] within the [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] catalyst (II). Subsequently, CO2 molecules were adsorbed and activated by [PHI-NH] to formation of the carbamate intermediate, which can act as a nucleophile. The nucleophilic carbamate anion then attacked the β-carbon atom with less sterically hindered characteristics from ECH, inducing ring-opening and yielding a linear alkylcarbonate anion intermediate (III). Finally, intramolecular cyclization generated the cyclic carbonate product and regenerated the catalyst (IV).

Conclusions

In this study, A novel multifunctional heterogeneous catalyst was successfully synthesized for the CO2 cycloaddition reaction through modifying PHI. This catalyst is composed of organic base ionic liquids immobilized onto rod-shaped PHI structure. This design strategically integrates multiple sites (amino base and hydrogen bond) within a single polymeric framework to synergistically promote the cycloaddition of epoxides with CO2. Notably, [PHI-NH]­[DBUH] exhibited exceptional catalytic performance in the solvent- and cocatalyst-free synthesis of CPC under mild conditions, achieving delivering a 99% yield and 99% selectivity at 90 °C for 6 h. Furthermore, the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst allowed for facile recovery and reuse via simple solid–liquid separation, maintaining its high catalytic activity over multiple cycles. Based on multifunction structure of the catalyst and experimental results, we proposed an intramolecular active site synergy and a activation mechanism. This halogen-free and solvent-free catalytic system represented a significant advancement in sustainable cyclic carbonate synthesis, offering a promising platform for future catalytic applications.
