When Stronger Triggers Backfire: A High-Dimensional Theory of Backdoor Attacks
D.G.M. Flynn *, Hadas Yaron Goldhirsh *, Jonathan P. Keating †, Inbar Seroussi †

Abstract
Backdoor poisoning attacks behave counter-intuitively in high dimensions: stronger training triggers can help the defender. We study regularised generalised linear models on Gaussian-mixture data in the proportional regime ( p / n → κ p/n\to\kappa ), varying the training trigger strength α \alpha against a fixed test trigger. Three phenomena emerge: (i) clean test accuracy increases with α \alpha ; (ii) attack success peaks at a finite α \alpha and then declines; and (iii) the most damaging trigger direction is the minimum eigenvector of the data covariance. We prove all three results in closed form for the squared loss, and extend (i) and (ii) to general convex GLM losses via a Gaussian-proxy fixed-point system. We identify a finite-sample noise floor proportional to κ \kappa as the mechanism behind (i), invisible to classical n ≫ p n\gg p analysis. Experiments on CIFAR-10 and Gaussian surrogates match the theory closely; ResNet-18 experiments show the same phenomena beyond the convex setting.

Introduction

As machine learning is deployed in safety-critical domains, model security becomes a first-class concern: modern pipelines ingest data from the internet, and a tiny fraction of adversarial samples can compromise the model. Despite the canonical nature of backdoor poisoning attacks, a precise theoretical model that captures the high dimensional nature of modern datasets has remained elusive, with current work largely empirical or reliant on lower dimensional bounds.

We study
backdoor
(trigger-based) poisoning
[
16
]
: the adversary injects training samples carrying a trigger and labelled with a target class, so that at test time the same trigger flips predictions while performing normally otherwise. In practice, the test-time trigger strength is constrained (e.g. the size of a sticker on a stop sign), while the training trigger strength
\alpha
is a free choice for the attacker; stronger training triggers make poisoned samples easier to classify (potentially reducing their influence), weaker ones may leave too little imprint. We analyze this trade-off rigorously for regularised generalised linear models (GLMs) on high-dimensional Gaussian mixture data, working in the
proportional regime
where the dimension of the data
p
and the number of data points
n
are of the same order.
Is a stronger training trigger always a stronger attack?

What underlying mechanisms of standard training give rise to these phenomena?

1.1
Our contributions

We give the first high-dimensional theoretical characterization of the trigger’s impact on data poisoning, including a rigorous account of the emergence of the behaviour also observed in deep learning systems described in Figure
1
. We model backdoor-poisoned data as a binary Gaussian mixture with general covariance
\mathbf{C}
and analyse regularised GLMs in two settings: Empirical risk minimisation (ERM) in the proportional regime and population-risk minimisation in the information limit (
n\gg p
). The central quantities are the
benign alignment
and
trigger alignment
, which govern clean accuracy and attack success rate respectively (Section
2
, Theorem
1
).

We prove the three phenomena of Figure
1
across three settings.
(i) Squared loss, ERM
(Section
3.1
): closed-form alignments yield benign monotonicity, a unique finite peak in trigger alignment, and the minimum eigenvector of
\mathbf{C}
as the optimal attack direction (Proposition
2
, Corollary
3
, and Proposition
5
).
(ii) Convex losses, proportional regime
(Section
3.2
): the trigger alignment peaks at a finite
\alpha^{\star}
with explicit decay rates (Proposition
6
).
(iii) Convex losses, information limit
(Section
3.3
): the minimiser converges to its unpoisoned value as
\alpha\to\infty
(Proposition
7
), and a one-step monotonicity argument (Proposition

) gives the benign-alignment ordering.

All results are validated on logistic regression with real and Gaussian surrogate data, and all three phenomena persist in a ResNet-18 (Figure

Our work contributes a rigorous, quantitative analysis of backdoor poisoning, where prior work has largely relied on empirical evidence or low-dimensional bounds. From a high-dimensional statistics perspective, we introduce a tractable adversarial data model and show that it exhibits qualitatively new behavior not captured by classical
n\gg p
asymptotics.

Setting and notation

Clean data.

We consider a classification problem where each sample
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{\pm 1\}
is drawn independently from a binary Gaussian mixture
with
\mathbb{P}(y_{i}=+1)=\tfrac{1}{2}
, and the
feature vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mid y_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(y_{i}\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})
, where

\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the class mean and

\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a shared
covariance matrix.

Poisoning model.

An adversary selects a fraction
\phi\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
of the
full training set, drawn exclusively from the samples with

y_{i}=-1
, and applies a
backdoor attack
: for each selected
sample, the trigger
\alpha\mathbf{v}
is added to the feature vector and
the label is flipped to
+1
. Here
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a fixed
trigger direction with
\|\mathbf{v}\|=1
,
\alpha\geq 0
controls the
trigger strength, and samples with
y_{i}=+1
are never modified. We assume
\alpha\geq 0
, as otherwise we may just consider
-\mathbf{v}
.

The losses we consider depend on features and labels only through
y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}

\tilde{L}(x,+1)=\tilde{L}(-x,-1)

L(x):=\tilde{L}(x,+1)

\mathbf{z}_{i}:=y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}

\mathbf{C}

\mathbf{z}_{i}\mid K=c\;\sim\;\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c},\,\mathbf{C}),\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}=\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu},\quad\pi_{1}=1-\phi,\quad\pi_{2}=\phi.

Notation.

We use boldface for matrices and vectors, with capitals for matrices. For a matrix
\mathbf{A}

Empirical risk minimisation (proportional regime)

Given
n
training samples
(\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
; a convex loss
L
, and regularisation
\lambda>0
, we consider the estimator

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\;=\;\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\;\big\{\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\;:=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L\!\bigl(y_{i}\,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}\bigr)\;+\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}\big\}.

(2)

Canonical choices are the logistic loss
L(t)=\log(1+e^{-t})
and the squared loss
L(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}(1-t)^{2}
.

Assumption 1

(Proportional asymptotics)
.

The dimension
p
and sample size
n
grow jointly such that

p/n\to\kappa\in(0,\infty)
.
The covariance is bounded,
\|\mathbf{C}\|=O(1)
, and the class
means satisfy
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}\|=O(1)
for each
c\in\{1,2\}
.
The loss
L:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is strictly convex.

Population risk minimisation (information limit)

In the information limit
\kappa\to 0
(or
n\gg p
), this is equivalent to minimising the
population risk
(Appendix
C.1
):

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{pop}}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha)\;:=\;(1-\phi)\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})}\!\bigl[L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\bigr]\;+\;\phi\,\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})}\!\bigl[L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{x})\bigr]\;+\;\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2},

(3)

with unique minimiser
\boldsymbol{\theta}(\alpha)
(by
\lambda
-strong convexity).

2.1
Gaussian proxy for ERM

Our ERM-regime analysis builds on the following characterisation of the
high-dimensional minimiser, which is a consequence of the general
results of
25
.

Theorem 1

(Gaussian proxy
[
25
]
)
.

Denote
\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau):=(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-1}
. Under Assumptions
1
and the mixture model (
1
), there exists
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
such that for pseudo-Lipschitz
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
of finite order,
\phi(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\to_{p,n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
in probability, where

\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2},\;\tfrac{\gamma}{n}\mathbf{C}\bigr).

The scalars
(\tau,\gamma,\delta,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
solve a self-consistent system. Let
f(x)=-L^{\prime}(\prox_{\delta L}(x))
and
r_{c}\sim\mathcal{N}(M_{c},\sigma^{2})
, with
r_{K}\sim r_{c}
conditional on
K=c
with probability
\pi_{c}
. Then
\tau=\mathbb{E}[-f^{\prime}(r_{K})]
,
\gamma=\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(r_{K})]
, and
\eta_{c}=\pi_{c}\,\mathbb{E}[f(r_{c})]
for
c=1,2
, and

\delta=\tfrac{1}{n}\tr[\mathbf{C}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)],\quad M_{c}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}),\;c=1,2,

\sigma^{2}=(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)^{2}\mathbf{C}(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2})+\tfrac{\gamma}{n}\tr\!\bigl[\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)^{2}\mathbf{C}^{2}\bigr].

The scalars
M_{c}
and
\sigma^{2}
are the asymptotic mean and variance of
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{x}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for a test point
\mathbf{x}
.

The key quantities in our analysis are the expected benign and trigger alignments, defined as

h_{\mu}(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}],\quad h_{v}(\alpha):=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}].

For
\alpha>0
, we have
h_{v}(\alpha)=(M_{1}+M_{2})/\alpha
.

Our results use the following genericity condition on the trigger direction relative to the data’s mean. In high dimensions, almost all pairs of vectors are nearly orthogonal: for
\mathbf{v}
uniform on the sphere,
\mathbf{v}^{\top}R(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu}=O(p^{-1/2})
with probability tending to one. There is no a priori reason for a trigger to have substantial overlap with the mean direction.
Moreover, standard backdoor trigger constructions (e.g., localised patches) are not designed to align with class-specific structure and thus typically have negligible correlation with the class mean.

Assumption 2

(Trigger orthogonality)
.

The trigger direction
\mathbf{v}
is asymptotically orthogonal to the
Krylov subspace of
\mathbf{C}
generated by
\boldsymbol{\mu}
:

\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{C}^{k}\boldsymbol{\mu}=o(1)
for each
k=0,1,2,\ldots

Equivalently,
\mathbf{v}^{\top}R(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu}=o(1)

for every fixed
\tau\geq 0
and
\lambda>0
.

Section
3.2
requires mild regularity of the loss, satisfied by the logistic and exponential losses.

Assumption 3

(Loss regularity)
.

The loss
L
is non-negative, convex, and strictly decreasing.
It has an exponentially bounded derivative for
x<0
and
sufficient decay for
x>0
:

|L^{\prime}(x)|\leq e^{C_{1}(|x|+1)}\;\text{for }x<0,\qquad|L^{\prime}(x)|\leq\frac{C_{2}}{x^{1+\epsilon}+1}\;\text{for }x>0\text{ and some }\epsilon>0.

Clean accuracy
increases
with trigger strength

For fixed poisoning fraction
\phi

\alpha

\alpha
grows, poisoned samples are pushed further into the

Formally:
we prove this for the ERM squared loss (Corollary

\boldsymbol{\theta}(\alpha)\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ben}}

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}(0)<\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ben}}

) gives the general ordering.

Figure

confirms this on CIFAR-10: logistic regression’s clean accuracy increases monotonically with
\alpha
, and the Gaussian surrogate closely tracks the real data.

Alignment of the learned direction peaks with trigger strength

The attack success rate is controlled by the trigger alignment
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
. Rather than growing without bound in
\alpha
, the trigger alignment is maximised at a finite
\alpha^{\star}
and decays thereafter: for large
\alpha
the poisoned class is so well-separated that the loss provides vanishing gradient in the trigger direction (since
L^{\prime}(x)\to 0
as
x\to+\infty
under Assumption
3
). Formally, in the proportional regime
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\to 0
as
\alpha\to\infty
, at rate
O(\alpha^{-\epsilon/(2+\epsilon)})
for polynomial-tail losses and
O(\log\alpha/\alpha)
for exponential-tail losses (e.g. logistic). Figure
2
shows this peaking on CIFAR-10 and its Gaussian surrogate, and Figure
1
shows it persists in ResNet-18.

Trigger direction and the minimum eigendirection of the covariance

For a fixed trigger budget
\alpha
, the attack is most effective along low-variance directions of the data: the classifier has little signal there to resist the perturbation, so the decision boundary is easiest to shift. This singles out the eigenvector of
\mathbf{C}
with the smallest eigenvalue as the optimal trigger direction.

Formally:
for the squared loss the trigger alignment is monotone in the resolvent quadratic form
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}
, and is maximised precisely along the minimum eigenvector of
\mathbf{C}
(Corollary
5
).
Numerically this extends to the logistic loss on CIFAR-10 and to ResNet-18.

Exact results ERM least squares

For the squared loss

3.1.1
Finite peak of the trigger projection

We define
g_{\mu\mu}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu}

g_{\mu v}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}
,
g_{vv}:=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}

Proposition 2

(Finite peak under generic trigger orthogonality)
.

Assume that
0<\phi<1/2

\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}
are linearly independent,
and that Assumption

Consequently, uniformly for
\alpha
in any fixed compact subset of

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{A_{v}\alpha}{B+C\alpha^{2}}+o(1),

(5)

where
A_{v}:=\tau\phi g_{vv}\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)g_{\mu\mu}\bigr)

C:=\tau\phi g_{vv}\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)g_{\mu\mu}\bigr)

In particular, for every fixed

Moreover, the exact trigger projection has a unique finite maximizer

\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{B}{C}+o(1)=\frac{1+\tau g_{\mu\mu}}{\tau\phi g_{vv}\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)g_{\mu\mu}\bigr)}+o(1).

(6)

Thus

The rational form (

3.1.3
Eigenvector specialization

To gain intuition, we restrict attention to a simplified setting in which the clean and trigger directions align with orthogonal eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
. In this case, Assumption
2
holds exactly, and the alignment quantities admit closed-form expressions.

Corollary 4

(Eigenvector specialisation)
.

Assume
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{v}=0
,
\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}=s_{\mu}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}
,
\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}=s_{v}^{2}\mathbf{v}
. Define
A_{\mu}:=\lambda+\tau s_{\mu}^{2}+\tau\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
,
B_{v}:=\lambda+\tau s_{v}^{2}
,
P_{\mu}:=\lambda+\tau s_{\mu}^{2}+\tau(1-\phi)\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
,
Q_{\mu}:=\lambda+\tau s_{\mu}^{2}+2\tau(1-\phi)\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}
, and
D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha):=A_{\mu}B_{v}+\tau\phi P_{\mu}\alpha^{2}
. Then

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}\frac{\tau(1-2\phi)B_{v}+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}}{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)},\qquad h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\phi Q_{\mu}\alpha}{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)}.

(7)

Consequently,
h_{\mu}(\alpha)
is strictly increasing for
\alpha>0
,
while
h_{v}(\alpha)>0
for every
\alpha>0
and has a unique finite
maximizer at

(\alpha_{\star}^{\mathrm{eig}})^{2}=(A_{\mu}B_{v})/(\tau\phi P_{\mu}).

(8)

This is the
low-variance effect
: increasing
s_{v}^{2}
attenuates
h_{v}(\alpha)
and pushes the maximizer
\alpha_{\star}^{\mathrm{eig}}
to larger values, so the model is most sensitive along directions where the data vary least.

See Appendix
A

Proposition 5

(Minimum-eigenvalue trigger directions)

Under the assumptions of Proposition

In (

Proposition 7

(Benign convergence)
.

\mathbf{v}

4
Comparing ERM and information limit

The three phenomena described in Sections
3

and
3.1
arise in both the proportional regime and information limit.
In this section, we explain precisely how alignments translate into clean
accuracy and attack success rate, and why the proportional regime captures
finite-sample effects that
do not appear in the information limit.

From alignment to accuracy.

The clean test accuracy of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
on the Gaussian mixture depends on the
benign alignment

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}
and the
total variance

\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}
. For
\Phi
the standard normal CDF:

\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{clean}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\Phi\!\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mu}/\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right),

(10)

For intuition we assume throughout this section that
\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{v}
are eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
with eigenvalues
s_{\mu}^{2},s_{v}^{2}
; the qualitative conclusions hold in general (Appendix
C.2
). With
h_{\mu}:=\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
and
h_{v}:=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{v}^{\top}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
, the asymptotic margin variance decomposes as

\sigma^{2}\;:=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\right]\;=\;\underbrace{\tfrac{s_{\mu}^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}}\,h_{\mu}^{2}}_{\text{mean signal}}\;+\;\underbrace{s_{v}^{2}\,h_{v}^{2}}_{\text{trigger signal}}\;+\;\underbrace{\tfrac{\gamma}{n}\,\tr\!\bigl[\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)^{2}\mathbf{C}^{2}\bigr]}_{\text{fundamental noise }\zeta},

(11)

the cross term vanishing by Assumption
2
. The first two summands come from the mean of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
;
\zeta
captures the
O(1/n)
fluctuations in (
1
), scales with
\kappa=p/n
, and vanishes in the information limit. The quadratic form concentrates on
\sigma^{2}
, so (
10
) with
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is faithful asymptotically.

In the information limit (
p\ll n
) there is no noise floor,
\sigma^{2}\approx s_{\mu}^{2}h_{\mu}^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}+s_{v}^{2}h_{v}^{2}
, so for large
\alpha
,
h_{v}\to 0
(Proposition
6
), giving
\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{clean}}\approx\Phi(\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|/\sqrt{s_{\mu}^{2}})
,
independent
of
h_{\mu}
and hence of
\alpha
. This contradicts Figure
2
, where clean accuracy is observed to rise with
\alpha
(and hence with
h_{\mu}
)

Why clean accuracy increases with trigger strength.

The noise floor
\zeta>0
breaks this degeneracy. Neglecting the (small, see Table
1
) trigger term,
\mathrm{Acc}_{\mathrm{clean}}\approx g(h_{\mu}):=\Phi(h_{\mu}/\sqrt{s_{\mu}^{2}h_{\mu}^{2}/\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}+\zeta})
, which has
g^{\prime}(h_{\mu})>0
whenever
\zeta>0
. As
\alpha\to\infty
,
h_{\mu}
rises to its unpoisoned value (Proposition
7
), improving clean accuracy. With
\zeta=0
this collapses to
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|/\sqrt{s_{\mu}^{2}}
and the improvement is invisible.

5
Conclusion and limitations

We analysed backdoor poisoning in regularised generalised linear models
on high-dimensional Gaussian mixtures. Closed-form squared-loss expressions
and a general fixed-point analysis yield three phenomena: (i) clean accuracy

increases
with training trigger strength, driven by a finite-sample
noise floor
\zeta\propto\kappa
absent in the information limit;
(ii) attack success peaks at a finite trigger strength, as large triggers
push the loss into its tail; and (iii) the minimum
eigenvector of the covariance is the most effective trigger direction.
Experiments on CIFAR-10, Gaussian surrogates closely
match the theory, and ResNet-18 experiments show the same qualitative phenomena beyond the convex setting.

Future work.

Our analysis rests on a Gaussian mixture data model and linear
(or generalised linear) classifiers; while experiments suggest the
phenomena persist for deep networks, extending the theory to non-convex feature maps is an interesting future direction.
Other directions include multi-class settings and non-uniform poisoning strategies.
Finally, our work characterises the attack but does not propose a
defence; developing defences informed by the identified mechanisms is
an important direction for future work. Backdoor poisoning is a known threat
[
16
,
21
]
, and by identifying regimes in which stronger attacks
fail
our analysis is most directly useful to defenders and auditors.

A.1
General-covariance reduction

For the squared loss, the high-dimensional fixed-point equations reduce to a
finite-dimensional deterministic calculation. The following proposition records
the reduction and the projection formulas used throughout the linear-regression
analysis.

Proposition 9

(Square-loss proxy expectation and projection formulas)
.

Let

L(t)=\frac{1}{2}(1-t)^{2},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}=\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=(1-\phi,\phi).

For the squared loss,

\prox_{\delta L}(x)=\frac{x+\delta}{1+\delta},\qquad f(x):=-L^{\prime}(\prox_{\delta L}(x))=\frac{1-x}{1+\delta}.

Writing

\tau:=\frac{1}{1+\delta},

the scalar fixed point satisfies

\tau=\frac{1}{1+\delta},\qquad\delta=\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\left[\mathbf{C}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\right],\qquad\eta_{c}=\pi_{c}\tau(1-M_{c}),\qquad M_{c}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}].

(12)

In particular, the equation determining
\tau
does not involve the trigger
strength
\alpha
.

Define

\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha):=\sum_{c=1}^{2}\pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}=(1-2\phi)\boldsymbol{\mu}+\phi\alpha\mathbf{v}

(13)

and

S(\alpha):=\sum_{c=1}^{2}\pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}-\phi\alpha(\boldsymbol{\mu}\mathbf{v}^{\top}+\mathbf{v}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top})+\phi\alpha^{2}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}.

(14)

Then the proxy expectation is the unique solution of

\bigl(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C}+\tau S(\alpha)\bigr)\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha).

(15)

Assume further that
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}
are linearly independent. Let

U:=[\,\boldsymbol{\mu}\ \ \mathbf{v}\,],

and define the
2\times 2
resolvent Gram matrix

G:=U^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)U=\begin{pmatrix}g_{\mu\mu}&g_{\mu v}\\
g_{\mu v}&g_{vv}\end{pmatrix},

where

g_{\mu\mu}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad g_{\mu v}:=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v},\qquad g_{vv}:=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}.

Set

\Delta_{G}:=\det(G)=g_{\mu\mu}g_{vv}-g_{\mu v}^{2}.

Since
\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\succ 0
and
U
has full column rank,
G\succ 0
,
and hence
\Delta_{G}>0
.

The projections

h_{\mu}(\alpha):=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}],\qquad h_{v}(\alpha):=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]

are given by

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\Bigl((1-2\phi)g_{\mu\mu}+\phi\alpha g_{\mu v}+\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\Delta_{G}\Bigr)}{D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)}

(16)

and

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\Bigl((1-2\phi)g_{\mu v}+\phi\alpha g_{vv}+2\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha\Delta_{G}\Bigr)}{D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)},

(17)

where

D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)=1+\tau g_{\mu\mu}-2\tau\phi\alpha g_{\mu v}+\tau\phi\alpha^{2}g_{vv}+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\Delta_{G}.

(18)

Proof.

The proximal formula follows by direct minimization:

\prox_{\delta L}(x)=\argmin_{u\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\frac{\delta}{2}(1-u)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(u-x)^{2}\right\}=\frac{x+\delta}{1+\delta}.

Hence

f(x):=-L^{\prime}\!\bigl(\prox_{\delta L}(x)\bigr)=1-\prox_{\delta L}(x)=\frac{1-x}{1+\delta}.

With
\tau=(1+\delta)^{-1}
, this becomes

f(x)=\tau(1-x),\qquad-f^{\prime}(x)=\tau.

Substituting this identity into Theorem
1
gives

\tau=\frac{1}{1+\delta},\qquad\delta=\frac{1}{n}\tr\!\left[\mathbf{C}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\right].

Moreover, since
r_{c}\sim\mathcal{N}(M_{c},\sigma^{2})
,

\eta_{c}=\pi_{c}\mathbb{E}[f(r_{c})]=\pi_{c}\tau(1-M_{c}),

which proves (
12
).

Let

m(\alpha):=\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}.

Taking expectations in the Gaussian proxy representation gives

(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=m(\alpha).

Using
\eta_{c}=\pi_{c}\tau(1-M_{c})
and

M_{c}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
, we obtain

m(\alpha)=\tau\sum_{c=1}^{2}\pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}-\tau\sum_{c=1}^{2}\pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha)-\tau S(\alpha)\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}].

Therefore

(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C}+\tau S(\alpha))\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha),

which proves (
15
). The coefficient matrix is positive
definite:

\lambda\mathbf{I}\succ 0,\qquad\tau\mathbf{C}\succeq 0,\qquad S(\alpha)=\sum_{c=1}^{2}\pi_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{c}^{\top}\succeq 0.

Thus the solution is unique. Expanding

\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\mu}
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}=\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}
, and

(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})=(1-\phi,\phi)
gives
(
13
) and (
14
).

It remains to derive the projection formulas. Write

d(\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}1-2\phi\\
\phi\alpha\end{pmatrix},\qquad K(\alpha):=\begin{pmatrix}1&-\phi\alpha\\
-\phi\alpha&\phi\alpha^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Then

\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha)=Ud(\alpha),\qquad S(\alpha)=UK(\alpha)U^{\top}.

Since

m(\alpha)=\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}+\eta_{2}\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\in\operatorname{span}\{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{v}\},

there is a unique vector
x(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

m(\alpha)=Ux(\alpha).

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)Ux(\alpha).

The identity

m(\alpha)=\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\alpha)-\tau S(\alpha)\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]

Ux=\tau Ud-\tau UKU^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)Ux.

Since

Equivalently,

(I_{2}+\tau K(\alpha)G)x(\alpha)=\tau d(\alpha).

Now define

h_{v}(\alpha)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\\
\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]=\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)Ux(\alpha)

Multiplying the reduced system by

(I_{2}+\tau GK(\alpha))h(\alpha)=\tau Gd(\alpha).

This is a

\det(I_{2}+\tau GK(\alpha))=1+\tau\tr(GK(\alpha))+\tau^{2}\det(G)\det(K(\alpha)).

Because

We also note that this denominator is positive. Indeed,

Applying Cramer’s rule to

(I_{2}+\tau GK(\alpha))h(\alpha)=\tau Gd(\alpha)

A.2
Consequences under generic trigger orthogonality

We now expand the general-covariance formulas under the condition

g_{\mu v}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}=o(1),

which follows from Assumption
2
. This is the condition
used in Proposition
2
and
Corollary

.

Proof of Proposition
2
.

Set

\varepsilon:=g_{\mu v},\qquad m:=g_{\mu\mu},\qquad q:=g_{vv},\qquad\Delta_{\varepsilon}:=mq-\varepsilon^{2}.

By Proposition
9
,

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\Bigl((1-2\phi)\varepsilon+\phi\alpha q+2\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha\Delta_{\varepsilon}\Bigr)}{1+\tau m-2\tau\phi\alpha\varepsilon+\tau\phi\alpha^{2}q+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}}.

Equivalently,

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{n_{\varepsilon}+A_{\varepsilon}\alpha}{B+d_{\varepsilon}\alpha+C_{\varepsilon}\alpha^{2}},

where

n_{\varepsilon}:=\tau(1-2\phi)\varepsilon,\qquad B:=1+\tau m,\qquad d_{\varepsilon}:=-2\tau\phi\varepsilon,

and

A_{\varepsilon}:=\tau\phi\Bigl(q+2\tau(1-\phi)\Delta_{\varepsilon}\Bigr),\qquad C_{\varepsilon}:=\tau\phi\Bigl(q+\tau(1-\phi)\Delta_{\varepsilon}\Bigr).

Since
\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\succ 0
and
\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{v}
are linearly independent,

\Delta_{\varepsilon}>0
. Hence

A_{\varepsilon}>0,\qquad C_{\varepsilon}>0.

If
\varepsilon=o(1)
, then

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=mq+o(1),\qquad n_{\varepsilon}=o(1),\qquad d_{\varepsilon}=o(1),

and

A_{\varepsilon}=\tau\phi q\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)+o(1),

C_{\varepsilon}=\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)+o(1).

Therefore, uniformly for
\alpha
in any fixed compact subset of

[0,\infty)
,

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\phi q\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)\alpha}{1+\tau m+\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)\alpha^{2}}+o(1).

This is exactly (
5
), with

A_{v}=\tau\phi q\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr),\qquad C=\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr).

It remains to prove the finite-peak claim for the exact expression.
Differentiating

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{n_{\varepsilon}+A_{\varepsilon}\alpha}{B+d_{\varepsilon}\alpha+C_{\varepsilon}\alpha^{2}}

gives

h_{v}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{F_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)}{\bigl(B+d_{\varepsilon}\alpha+C_{\varepsilon}\alpha^{2}\bigr)^{2}},

where

F_{\varepsilon}(\alpha)=A_{\varepsilon}B-n_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}-2n_{\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}\alpha-A_{\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}\alpha^{2}.

The quadratic

has negative leading coefficient and

F_{\varepsilon}(0)=A_{\varepsilon}B-n_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}=A_{\varepsilon}B+2\tau^{2}\phi(1-2\phi)\varepsilon^{2}>0.

The discriminant of

is

(2n_{\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon})^{2}+4A_{\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}\bigl(A_{\varepsilon}B-n_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}\bigr)>0.

Thus
F_{\varepsilon}
has two real roots. Their product is

\frac{A_{\varepsilon}B-n_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}}{-A_{\varepsilon}C_{\varepsilon}}<0,

so exactly one root is positive. Denote this positive root by

\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon}
. Therefore
h_{v}
is strictly increasing on

(0,\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon})
and strictly decreasing on

(\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon},\infty)

The positive root is

\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon}=-\frac{n_{\varepsilon}}{A_{\varepsilon}}+\left[\left(\frac{n_{\varepsilon}}{A_{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2}+\frac{B-\frac{n_{\varepsilon}d_{\varepsilon}}{A_{\varepsilon}}}{C_{\varepsilon}}\right]^{1/2}.

Since
n_{\varepsilon},d_{\varepsilon}=o(1)

A_{\varepsilon}=A_{v}+o(1)
, and
C_{\varepsilon}=C+o(1)

\alpha_{\star,\varepsilon}^{2}=\frac{B}{C}+o(1),

as claimed.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3
.

Set again

\varepsilon:=g_{\mu v},\qquad m:=g_{\mu\mu},\qquad q:=g_{vv},\qquad\Delta_{\varepsilon}:=mq-\varepsilon^{2}.

By Proposition
9
,

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\Bigl((1-2\phi)m+\phi\alpha\varepsilon+\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\Bigr)}{1+\tau m-2\tau\phi\alpha\varepsilon+\tau\phi\alpha^{2}q+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}}.

If
\varepsilon=o(1)
, then, uniformly for
\alpha
in any fixed compact
subset of
[0,\infty)
,

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau(1-2\phi)m+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)mq\,\alpha^{2}}{1+\tau m+\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)\alpha^{2}}+o(1).

This is the claimed leading-order formula with

A_{\mu}^{(0)}=\tau(1-2\phi)m,\qquad A_{\mu}^{(2)}=\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)mq.

Set

B:=1+\tau m,\qquad C:=\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr).

The leading-order curve has the form

h_{\mu}^{(0)}(\alpha)=\frac{A_{\mu}^{(0)}+A_{\mu}^{(2)}\alpha^{2}}{B+C\alpha^{2}}.

Differentiating gives

\frac{d}{d\alpha}h_{\mu}^{(0)}(\alpha)=\frac{2\alpha\bigl(A_{\mu}^{(2)}B-A_{\mu}^{(0)}C\bigr)}{(B+C\alpha^{2})^{2}}.

A direct simplification yields

A_{\mu}^{(2)}B-A_{\mu}^{(0)}C=\tau^{2}\phi^{2}mq\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)>0.

Hence the leading-order curve is strictly increasing for every

\alpha>0
.

Finally, on every compact interval
K\subset(0,\infty)
, the derivative
of
h_{\mu}^{(0)}
is bounded below by a positive constant. The exact derivative
is a continuous rational function of
(\varepsilon,\alpha)
, and the
denominator is bounded away from zero on
K

converges uniformly to the derivative of
h_{\mu}^{(0)}
on
K

that

h_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)>0

eventually uniformly over
\alpha\in K

∎

We also record how the square-loss projections depend on the poisoning
fraction
\phi
. This complements the dependence on the trigger strength

\alpha

\alpha

have different effects on the benign and trigger projections.

Trigger alignment
h_{v}(\alpha)
.

(b)

Benign alignment

.

To explain the behavior, write

m:=g_{\mu\mu},\qquad q:=g_{vv},

and define

D_{\phi}(\alpha):=1+\tau m+\tau\phi q\bigl(1+\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)\alpha^{2}.

In the square-loss setting,
\tau,m,q
are fixed with respect to
\phi
:
the scalar
\tau
depends only on
(\lambda,\mathbf{C},\kappa)
, not on the
poisoning fraction. Under generic trigger orthogonality, the leading-order
trigger projection is

h_{v}(\alpha,\phi)=\frac{\tau\phi q\bigl(1+2\tau(1-\phi)m\bigr)\alpha}{D_{\phi}(\alpha)}+o(1).

Differentiating the exact rational expression and then using

g_{\mu v}=o(1)
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}h_{v}(\alpha,\phi)=\frac{\tau q\alpha\left[(1+\tau m)\bigl(1+2\tau m(1-2\phi)\bigr)-\phi^{2}\tau^{2}mq\alpha^{2}\right]}{D_{\phi}(\alpha)^{2}}+o(1).

Since
D_{\phi}(\alpha)>0
, the leading-order sign is controlled by

(1+\tau m)\bigl(1+2\tau m(1-2\phi)\bigr)-\phi^{2}\tau^{2}mq\alpha^{2}.

Thus the trigger projection is not monotone in
\phi
in general. For
sufficiently small positive
\alpha
, the first term dominates, so increasing

\phi
increases
h_{v}
. For sufficiently large
\alpha
, the negative
term

-\phi^{2}\tau^{2}mq\alpha^{2}

dominates, so increasing
\phi
decreases
h_{v}
.

The benign projection has a simpler dependence on
\phi
. Its leading-order
form is

h_{\mu}(\alpha,\phi)=\frac{\tau(1-2\phi)m+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)mq\alpha^{2}}{D_{\phi}(\alpha)}+o(1).

Again, differentiating the exact rational expression and then using

g_{\mu v}=o(1)
gives

\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}h_{\mu}(\alpha,\phi)=-\frac{m\tau\left[2+2\tau m+2\tau q\alpha^{2}\phi+2\tau^{2}mq\alpha^{2}\phi^{2}+\tau^{2}q^{2}\alpha^{4}\phi^{2}\right]}{D_{\phi}(\alpha)^{2}}+o(1).

All terms inside the bracket are positive and
D_{\phi}(\alpha)>0
. Hence, to
leading order,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}h_{\mu}(\alpha,\phi)<0.

Thus, increasing the poisoning fraction decreases the benign projection at fixed

\alpha
, while its effect on the trigger projection depends on the trigger
strength.

A.4
Eigenvector reduction

We next prove the eigenvector specialization directly for the projections

h_{\mu}(\alpha)
and
h_{v}(\alpha)
. This avoids introducing auxiliary
coefficient notation and mirrors the statement in the main text.

Proof of Corollary
4
.

Assume

\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{v}=0,\qquad\mathbf{C}\boldsymbol{\mu}=s_{\mu}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad\mathbf{C}\mathbf{v}=s_{v}^{2}\mathbf{v}.

Set

L_{\mu}:=\lambda+\tau s_{\mu}^{2},\qquad r:=\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}.

Then

A_{\mu}=L_{\mu}+\tau r,\qquad B_{v}=\lambda+\tau s_{v}^{2},

and

P_{\mu}=L_{\mu}+\tau(1-\phi)r,\qquad Q_{\mu}=L_{\mu}+2\tau(1-\phi)r.

Since

\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)=(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-1},

the eigenvector assumptions give

\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu}=\frac{1}{L_{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\mu},\qquad\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{B_{v}}\mathbf{v}.

Therefore

g_{\mu\mu}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\boldsymbol{\mu}=\frac{r}{L_{\mu}},

g_{\mu v}=\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}=0,

and, using the standing normalization
\|\mathbf{v}\|=1
,

g_{vv}=\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{R}(\lambda,\tau)\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{B_{v}}.

Hence

\Delta_{G}=g_{\mu\mu}g_{vv}-g_{\mu v}^{2}=\frac{r}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}.

Substituting these identities into the denominator
(
18
) gives

D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)=1+\tau\frac{r}{L_{\mu}}+\tau\phi\alpha^{2}\frac{1}{B_{v}}+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\frac{r}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}.

Equivalently,

D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)=\frac{A_{\mu}B_{v}+\tau\phi P_{\mu}\alpha^{2}}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}.

Thus, with

D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha):=A_{\mu}B_{v}+\tau\phi P_{\mu}\alpha^{2},

we have

D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)=\frac{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}.

Now use the exact projection formula (
16
). Since

g_{\mu v}=0
,

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\left((1-2\phi)\frac{r}{L_{\mu}}+\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}\frac{r}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}\right)}{D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)}.

Multiplying numerator and denominator by
L_{\mu}B_{v}
, we get

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=r\frac{\tau(1-2\phi)B_{v}+\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi)\alpha^{2}}{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)}.

This is the claimed formula for the benign projection.

Similarly, using (
17
) and
g_{\mu v}=0
,

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\left(\phi\alpha\frac{1}{B_{v}}+2\tau\phi(1-\phi)\alpha\frac{r}{L_{\mu}B_{v}}\right)}{D_{\mathrm{proj}}(\alpha)}.

Again multiplying numerator and denominator by
L_{\mu}B_{v}
, we obtain

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\phi\alpha\left(L_{\mu}+2\tau(1-\phi)r\right)}{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)}=\frac{\tau\phi Q_{\mu}\alpha}{D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)}.

This proves the projection formulas in (
7
).

It remains to prove the monotonicity and peak claims. Write

D_{\mathrm{eig}}(\alpha)=D_{0}+D_{2}\alpha^{2},\qquad D_{0}:=A_{\mu}B_{v},\qquad D_{2}:=\tau\phi P_{\mu}.

For the trigger projection,

h_{v}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\phi Q_{\mu}\alpha}{D_{0}+D_{2}\alpha^{2}}.

Since
\tau\phi Q_{\mu}>0
,

h_{v}^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{\tau\phi Q_{\mu}(D_{0}-D_{2}\alpha^{2})}{(D_{0}+D_{2}\alpha^{2})^{2}}.

Thus
h_{v}
is strictly increasing when
\alpha^{2}<D_{0}/D_{2}
and strictly
decreasing when
\alpha^{2}>D_{0}/D_{2}
. Its unique maximizer satisfies

(\alpha_{\star}^{\mathrm{eig}})^{2}=\frac{D_{0}}{D_{2}}=\frac{A_{\mu}B_{v}}{\tau\phi P_{\mu}},

which is (
8
).

For the benign projection, write

h_{\mu}(\alpha)=r\frac{N_{0}+N_{2}\alpha^{2}}{D_{0}+D_{2}\alpha^{2}},

where

N_{0}:=\tau(1-2\phi)B_{v},\qquad N_{2}:=\tau^{2}\phi(1-\phi).

Differentiating gives

h_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)=r\frac{2\alpha(N_{2}D_{0}-N_{0}D_{2})}{(D_{0}+D_{2}\alpha^{2})^{2}}.

Using the definitions above,

N_{2}D_{0}-N_{0}D_{2}=\tau^{2}\phi^{2}B_{v}\bigl(L_{\mu}+2\tau(1-\phi)r\bigr)=\tau^{2}\phi^{2}B_{v}Q_{\mu}.

This quantity is strictly positive. Therefore

h_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)>0\qquad\text{for every }\alpha>0.

This completes the proof of Corollary
4
.

B.2
Cone Lemma

The following structural lemma underpins much of the population-risk
analysis. It shows that the unpoisoned (
\alpha=0
) population
minimiser, and as a special case the benign minimiser, lies in a
two-dimensional submanifold determined by the resolvent of
\mathbf{C}
.
2
2

2

This can be compared with the form of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
for the ERM with
\alpha=0

Lemma 12

(Cone Lemma)
.

Suppose Assumption
3
holds with
\phi<\tfrac{1}{2}
and

\lambda>0
. Then:

(i)

The minimiser
\boldsymbol{\theta}(0)

3

\boldsymbol{\theta}(0)\;=\;a\,(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}

(ii)

4

\tau\geq 0

Part (ii) is the special case of part (i) with
\phi=0

\lambda

\lambda/(1-\phi)

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\mathrm{ben}}

We prove (i); part (ii) follows from (i) by the rescaling noted in the
statement (and with
\phi=0

Setting

Here

represents the margin of a class

Since

Since

3

\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(A)]\leq\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]<0.

Therefore

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=-(1-\phi)\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(A)]+\phi\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]

\displaystyle\geq-(1-\phi)\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]+\phi\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]

\displaystyle=-(1-2\phi)\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]>0,

\mathbb{E}[L^{\prime}(B)]<0

This implies
\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta}(0)>0

∎

Appendix C
Comparing ERM and information limit

C.1
Precise relation between ERM and information limit

Fixed-dimensional convergence of the empirical optimiser.

We briefly justify the relationship between the empirical optimisation problem

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\qquad\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\theta})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2},

and its population analogue

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop}(\alpha)\in\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha).

Writing
\mathbf{z}_{i}=y_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}
, the empirical objective is

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{i})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}.

The population objective is the corresponding expectation under the mixture law

\mathbf{z}\sim P_{\alpha}:=(1-\phi)\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C})+\phi\mathcal{N}(\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{C}),

namely

\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim P_{\alpha}}\left[L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right]+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|^{2}.

The standard empirical-risk decomposition gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n};\alpha)-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop};\alpha)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n};\alpha)-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})-\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop})\right]

\displaystyle+\left[\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop})-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop};\alpha)\right].

The middle term is non-positive by optimality of
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
. Hence, for any set

\Theta
containing both
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop}
,

\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n};\alpha)-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop};\alpha)\leq 2\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha)\right|.

Since the regularisation term is deterministic and appears in both objectives, the supremum reduces to

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim P_{\alpha}}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right|.

It remains to control the uniform deviation of the function class

\mathcal{G}_{\Theta}=\left\{\mathbf{z}\mapsto L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}):\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\right\}.

A bounded parameter set is needed in order for this class to have finite complexity. In the present regularised problem this is natural. For example, if
L\geq 0
, then

\mathcal{L}_{n}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n})\leq\mathcal{L}_{n}(0)=L(0),

and therefore

\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|^{2}\leq L(0).

Thus

\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\|\leq B_{\lambda}:=\sqrt{\frac{2L(0)}{\lambda}}.

The same argument applies to
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop}
. Hence it suffices to take

\Theta_{\lambda}=\left\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}\right\}.

Let

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{G}_{\Theta_{\lambda}})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\sigma}\left[\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{i})\right],

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
are independent Rademacher variables. By the usual symmetrisation and contraction inequalities for Rademacher complexity, if
L
is
G
-Lipschitz, then

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{i})-\mathbb{E}L(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z})\right|\lesssim G\,\mathfrak{R}_{n}\left(\left\{\mathbf{z}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}:\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}\right\}\right).

For the linear class,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{R}_{n}\left(\left\{\mathbf{z}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}:\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\sigma}\left[\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{z}_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=B_{\lambda}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\sigma}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\mathbf{z}_{i}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq B_{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}}{n}}.

For the Gaussian mixture
P_{\alpha}
,

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}=\tr(\mathbf{C})+(1-\phi)\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}+\phi\|\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}}\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha)\right|\lesssim GB_{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{\tr(\mathbf{C})+(1-\phi)\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}+\phi\|\alpha\mathbf{v}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\|^{2}}{n}}.

In the fixed-dimensional regime, or more generally when

\mathbb{E}\|\mathbf{z}\|^{2}=O(p),

this is the standard

O\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right)

Rademacher-complexity rate for bounded linear predictors; see, for example,
standard treatments of Rademacher complexity and linear prediction

[
28
]
.

It follows that, for fixed
p
and fixed
\lambda>0
,

\sup_{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|\leq B_{\lambda}}\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\alpha)\right|\to 0

in probability, and therefore

\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n};\alpha)-\mathcal{L}_{\rm pop}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop};\alpha)\to 0.

Since
L
is convex and
\lambda>0
, the population objective is

\lambda
-strongly convex, so its minimiser is unique. Hence the empirical minimiser converges to the population minimiser as
\kappa\rightarrow 0
:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\rm pop}(\alpha).

interpretation of the scalar

as a single eigenvalue of
\mathbf{C}

rather than as the resolvent-weighted spectral average above.

Appendix D
Further Experimental Details

Dataset and preprocessing.

Except for the synthetic linear-regression experiments in
Figures
3
and
4
,all experiments use CIFAR-10 restricted to classes 0 (“airplane”) and 1 (“automobile”), pooling the original 50,000 training and 10,000 test images (12,000 total per class pair — 6,000 per class) and applying a single random 80/20 train/test split (seed 42), yielding
n_{\mathrm{train}}\approx 9{,}600
and
n_{\mathrm{test}}\approx 2{,}400
samples. Pixel intensities are normalised to
[0,1]
(divided by 255) and the training set mean is subtracted from both splits. The feature dimension is
d=32\times 32\times 3=3{,}072
.

Poisoning protocol.

For the CIFAR-10, Gaussian-surrogate, and ResNet experiments, a fraction

\phi=0.05
of the dataset with class
-1
airplane training samples is selected
uniformly at random and poisoned: the trigger vector
\alpha\mathbf{v}
, with

\|\mathbf{v}\|=1
, is added to each selected sample, and its label is flipped to

+1
. The trigger direction is a
2{\times}2
corner patch in the top-left
of the image applied uniformly across all three RGB channels, normalised to a
unit vector and then scaled to norm
\alpha
.

For these image experiments, we sweep
\alpha
over 20 equally-spaced values
in
[0,1.5]
. Attack success rate is always evaluated at a fixed test trigger
norm
\alpha_{\mathrm{test}}=0.5
in the same direction as the training
trigger. The synthetic linear-regression experiments in
Figures
3
and
4
use the
synthetic Gaussian-mixture setup and poisoning parameters specified in the
corresponding figure-specific paragraphs below.

Gaussian surrogate construction.

The Gaussian surrogate dataset is constructed to match the empirical first- and
second-order statistics of the real CIFAR-10 training split. From the centred
training data we estimate the class mean
\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=\frac{1}{n_{+}}\sum_{i:\,y_{i}=+1}\mathbf{x}_{i}

and the pooled within-class covariance

\hat{\mathbf{C}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\Sigma}_{+1}+\tfrac{1}{2}\hat{\Sigma}_{-1}
,
regularised by a small ridge
\epsilon\mathbf{I}
(
\epsilon=10^{-4}\,\mathrm{tr}(\hat{\mathbf{C}})/p
).
Synthetic samples are then drawn as

\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\pm\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\,\hat{\mathbf{C}})
via the Cholesky factorisation

\hat{\mathbf{C}}=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^{\top}
, using
\mathbf{x}=\pm\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+\mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}

with
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})
.
The surrogate dataset uses the same
n_{\mathrm{train}}
and
n_{\mathrm{test}}

as the corresponding real split and balanced class sizes
(
\lfloor(n_{\mathrm{train}}+n_{\mathrm{test}})/2\rfloor
samples per class).
All other experimental choices—poisoning fraction, trigger direction,
regularisation strength, and number of seeds—are identical to those of the
real CIFAR-10 experiment.

Figure

This figure isolates the dependence of the trigger projection on the trigger
eigenvalue
s_{v}^{2}
. We use a synthetic non-isotropic covariance model in
which the clean and trigger directions are orthogonal eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
.
Writing

u_{\mu}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|},

the covariance used in the plotted experiment is

\mathbf{C}=s_{\mathrm{rest}}^{2}\bigl(\mathbf{I}-u_{\mu}u_{\mu}^{\top}-\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}\bigr)+s_{\mu}^{2}u_{\mu}u_{\mu}^{\top}+s_{v}^{2}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}.

Thus
u_{\mu}
has eigenvalue
s_{\mu}^{2}
, the trigger direction
\mathbf{v}
has

s_{v}^{2}
, and every direction orthogonal to

\operatorname{span}\{u_{\mu},\mathbf{v}\}

s_{\mathrm{rest}}^{2}
. In the plotted setting,
\|\boldsymbol{\mu}\|=1
,

\|\mathbf{v}\|=1
, and
s_{\mathrm{rest}}^{2}=1
, so the covariance reduces to the
rank-two perturbation

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{I}+(s_{\mu}^{2}-1)\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}+(s_{v}^{2}-1)\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{\top}.

The eigenvector specialization in Corollary
4
is more
general than this particular plotted construction. It only requires that

\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
\mathbf{v}
are orthogonal eigenvectors of
\mathbf{C}
, with eigenvalues

s_{\mu}^{2}
and
s_{v}^{2}

complement of
\operatorname{span}\{\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{v}\}
may be arbitrary positive
definite, subject to the standing boundedness assumptions; in the displayed
projection formulas, its effect enters through the fixed-point scalar
\tau
.

Table
1
: fixed-point solution.

The table reports the asymptotic prediction for
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]
,
the variance of the classifier’s output on a test point, derived from a high-dimensional analysis
of regularised logistic regression. In the limit
p,n\to\infty
with aspect ratio

p/n\to\kappa
, the trained weight vector concentrates around a Gaussian
proxy
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with average
(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-1}(\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\eta_{2}\mathbf{v})
, where the scalar
parameters
(\tau,\gamma,\delta,\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
are the unique solution to a system of
self-consistent fixed-point equations that couple the loss curvature, effective noise variance,
and class-conditional signals.

These equations are solved numerically using the empirically estimated class mean
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and
pooled within-class covariance
\mathbf{C}
from CIFAR-10 (classes 0 and 1), with
\lambda=10^{-4}
,
poisoning fraction
\phi=0.1
, and aspect ratio
p/n\approx 0.32
. The expectation integrals
that appear in the fixed-point equations are evaluated by Gauss–Hermite quadrature (100 nodes),
and the system is iterated to convergence (residual
<10^{-10}
).

At the solution,
\sigma^{2}
decomposes as

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})^{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\text{mean-direction signal}}+\underbrace{\eta_{2}^{2}\,\mathbf{v}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}_{\text{trigger-direction signal}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{2(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})\eta_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}}_{\text{cross term}}+\underbrace{\tfrac{\gamma}{n}\mathrm{tr}\bigl[(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-2}\mathbf{C}^{2}\bigr]}_{\text{fundamental noise }\zeta},

(29)

where
\mathbf{A}:=(\lambda\mathbf{I}+\tau\mathbf{C})^{-2}\mathbf{C}
. The first three terms reflect how much of the
classifier’s variance is driven by alignment with the clean-class mean and the trigger direction
respectively; the last term is an irreducible noise floor arising from finite-sample fluctuations,
present even in the absence of poisoning.
