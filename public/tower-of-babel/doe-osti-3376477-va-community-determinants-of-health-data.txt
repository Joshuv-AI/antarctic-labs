 1. Introduction and Background

The U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) places the health and well-being of our nation’s veterans
as its top priority. VA is dedicated to offering timely access to high-quality, evidence-based mental health
care that meets the needs of veterans and supports their reintegration into society. One of our core
missions is to prevent suicide among veterans through innovative approaches and resources.

With funding from the VA Office of Mental Health and Suicide Prevention (OMHSP), the Community
Determinants of Health (EDH) Data project has developed innovative datasets associated with specific
health outcomes, a methodology for transforming spatiotemporal data from one spatial reference (e.g., a 1
km grid) to another (e.g., U.S. Census Tracts), and capabilities for modeling health outcomes. These
datasets extend existing Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ)-style resources by
addressing key gaps through finer spatial resolution and additional geographical covariates.

The curation and standardization of these datasets are a complex task since they often originate from
various sources and are measured at different spatial and temporal resolutions. For example, U.S. Census
data products typically use census blocks, block groups, or counties, while data such as weather data are
available on 1 km grids. Some economic data may only be available at the ZIP code level. In this context,
‘standardized’ means that all datasets share the same spatial extent (e.g., U.S. Census Tract and/or
County), and ‘curated’ implies a repeatable process with data provenance and the use of appropriate
methodologies for covariate conversion.

The Community Determinants of Health datasets draw from multiple sources, resulting in variables with
varying degrees of availability, patterns of missing data, and methodological considerations across
different sources, geographies, and years.

The FY26Q3 metadata update improves the EDH data catalog by:

 1

  • replacing the combined [OMHSP].[table_metadata] model with a normalized metadata structure
 consisting of [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata], [OMHSP].[data_source], and
 [OMHSP].[dataset_data_source];
  • discontinuing delivery of the former [OMHSP].[table_metadata] table beginning in FY26Q3;
  • renaming table_name to dataset_name and table_name_description to dataset_description in
 [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata];
  • using [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata] as the primary dataset-level metadata table, with one row per
 delivered dataset/table;
  • normalizing source information into [OMHSP].[data_source], including source name, source

  • creating [OMHSP].[dataset_data_source] to support dataset-to-source relationships, including
 source role, display order, source accessed date, archive flag, and archive reason;
  • moving source update frequency from dataset-level metadata to the source level in
 [OMHSP].[data_source].[update_frequency];
  • moving source accessed date and archive information to the dataset-source relationship level in
 [OMHSP].[dataset_data_source];
  • using [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata].[dataset_citation] as the authoritative APA-7-aligned citation
 for delivered datasets;
  • retaining [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata].[sharepoint_description],

 [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata].[advanced_information] to support sponsor-facing summaries,
 bibliographic context, and technical documentation;
  • adding [OMHSP].[dataset_metadata].[ornl_update_frequency] to document the expected ORNL

 dataset-source relationship metadata;
  • reducing duplicated source descriptions, URLs, citations, and update-frequency values across
 metadata records.

  A) Dataset Citations
The team discussed the preferred approach for dataset citations and agreed on the following citation
standards: * Dataset citations should include the year of the source release when applicable. Example:
ACS 2022 5-year estimates. * Detailed descriptions of data manipulation should not be included directly
in the citation text. Instead, the citation should refer users to the applicable report URL for additional
processing details. * For datasets that were modified or processed, the citation should include language
such as: “For data processing details, see: [report URL].” * For datasets that were curated or provided
without modification, the citation should include language such as: “Curated as received; for more
information, see: [report URL].” * The team also agreed to continue populating the dataset accessed date
field.
  B) Code Sets and Null Reasons
The team discussed a naming inconsistency between:
[OMHSP].[column_metadata].[null_reason_code] and [OMHSP].[code_set_values].[code_value_id]
The current design is conceptually correct because [code_set_values].[code_value_id] is the generic
primary key for any controlled vocabulary value, while [column_metadata].[null_reason_code] represents
the specific business use of that value in the column metadata table.
However, the column name [null_reason_code] may be misleading because it suggests that the field
stores the readable code value, such as SOURCE_MISSING or ACS_SUPPRESSION. In practice, the
field stores the integer foreign key that points to [OMHSP].[code_set_values].[code_value_id].
To make the relationship clearer and easier to maintain, the proposed recommendation is to rename:
[OMHSP].[column_metadata].[null_reason_code] to:
[OMHSP].[column_metadata].[null_reason_code_value_id], which has been implemented in this
FY26Q3 release.
This revised name makes it explicit that the field is a foreign key to the controlled vocabulary table. The
readable null reason code and description would remain available through a join to
[OMHSP].[code_set_values].
  C) Next Steps
The VA team will regroup internally to discuss potential improvements to the code set and null reason
structure. The VA team will communicate any requested changes or decisions to ORNL. ORNL will
continue applying the agreed citation approach, including release year, report URL references, and dataset
accessed date population.

 3. Documentation Structure and Dataset Conventions

3.1 Dataset Curation Documentation Standard Format

Each data source description adheres to a standardized format with the following fields:
  1. Source: The name of the organization that provided the raw data (e.g., Health Resources and
 Services Administration [HRSA] for the Area Health Resources Files [AHRF]). Note: Prior to the
 FY23Q4 release, we referred to the source organization as the “sponsor.”

 4

  2. Description: A brief, general description of the data.

 – Inclusion in the datasets: Lists the determinants of health domains to which the data
 source has contributed variables. Includes additional information relevant to the dataset.
  3. Resources: Links to original data source documentation, data download sites, and other pertinent
 information.

  4. Update Frequency: Indicates how often each dataset will be updated.

  5. Variable Definitions and Specifications (in tabular format):

 – Variable name (column name)
 – Variable label (optional, if different from the variable or column name)
 – Source table (optional, if multiple data tables were available from the original data
 source)
 – Total_rows: Indicates the number of rows in each column within each dataset (Starting in
 FY23Q2).
 – Null_rows: Specifies the count of null rows for each column in each dataset (Starting in
 FY23Q2).
  6. Variable Availability Across Years (in tabular format):

 – Variable name (column name)
 – Data year availability (e.g., 2009 to 2018)
This standardized format supports consistency, traceability, and ease of reference across data sources.

3.2 Dataset and Column Naming

3.2.1 Standardization of Table Names

Beginning in FY25Q4, newly curated datasets follow the updated table naming convention requested by
VA:
cmty_<dataset_name>_<spatial_resolution>
This convention adds the cmty_ prefix and removes the prior <year(s)> suffix from the table name.
Example: cmty_ruca_county
Previously, beginning in FY24Q1, datasets followed the naming convention:
<dataset_name>_<spatial_resolution>_<year(s)>
Example: hidta_county_2018_2021.
Older datasets may still use the prior naming convention unless they are renamed or re-released in a
future update.

3.2.2 Overview of Variable Derivation

The variables within the Determinants of Health datasets are derived from various data sources through
one of two methods:

 5

  • Direct extraction from the original data source: When the data were readily available from the
 source, we renamed the original variables to ensure clarity and consistency across years, aligning
 them with the naming conventions of the Determinants of Health data files.

  • Derivation using data from the original data source: In certain cases, we calculated
 percentages or rates for inclusion in the data files. We provide the numerators and denominators
 for these variables, along with their respective sources, in the data source descriptions.

3.2.3 Variable Assignment to Annual Datasets

Variables appear in the annual datasets corresponding to:
  1. The single year represented by the original data source
 (e.g., U.S. Area Deprivation Index 2020), or

  2. The final year in a period represented by the data
 (e.g., American Community Survey 2012–2016 is included in the 2016 dataset).

When possible, we also provide the unioned data along with a year column for each row.

3.2.4 Variable Availability

Variable availability varies across data years. Following each data source description in this report, you
will find a table outlining the availability of each variable in the annual datasets.
If a variable is not available, it is marked as NA (not available) or a null value.

3.2.5 Variable Naming Conventions

With the exception of geographic identifiers (typically named fips), variable names use descriptive titles,
generally retained from the original source column names.

3.3 Dataset Versioning

Each dataset is stored in a dedicated table within a Microsoft SQL Server database schema. Microsoft
SQL Server is used to provide these datasets in a format that is consistent with the VA Corporate Data
Warehouse (CDW) work environment.

Quarterly releases are organized under distinct schema names, such as OMHSP_FY22Q1, OMHSP_FY22Q2,
OMHSP_FY22Q3, OMHSP_FY22Q4, OMHSP_FY23Q1, and so forth. These schema names help distinguish
between releases when the same dataset is delivered across multiple quarters with updated data.

Using fiscal year and quarter identifiers in the schema names, such as FY25Q1, supports precise tracking
of datasets over time. This approach provides a clear record of when each dataset was delivered, which is
important for project management, version control, and accountability.

3.4 Handling of Missing Values

In EDH datasets delivered to the sponsor, missing values are generally represented by blank values.
Missing values are also documented at the column level in the [OMHSP].[column_metadata] table.

Beginning in FY23Q2, this table includes total_rows and null_rows, which provide the total number
of rows and the number of null or missing values for each column. Beginning in FY26Q2, the table also

 6

includes null_reason_code_value_id, which provides a standardized reason for missing values
when that information is applicable and available. For comprehensive information about null reason
codes, please refer to the subsequent sections of this report.

A known source-specific convention exists in the County Health Rankings (CHR) data: provider ratio
variables may use negative values when the number of providers is zero. This convention is described in
the CHR analytic documentation. However, as of the preparation of this report, CHR datasets have not
been delivered to the sponsor under this project. Therefore, this CHR-specific convention is included here
for reference and does not currently apply to any delivered EDH dataset.

 5. Geographic Identifiers and Dataset Linkage Guidance

5.1 FIPS and GEOID as Geographic Identifiers

ORNL uses the Federal Information Processing Standards (FIPS) as geographic identifiers and primary
keys in each dataset or table for this project. FIPS codes are publicly recognized standards developed by
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) for computer systems and non-military
applications, particularly for standardizing codes of geographical areas. FIPS specifications encompass
various geographical areas:

  • FIPS 10-4 for country and region codes

  • FIPS 5-2 for state codes

  • FIPS 6-4 for county codes

These codes are unique within their respective geographic entities. For example, FIPS state codes are
unique within a country, and FIPS county codes are unique within a state. Since counties nest within
states, a complete county FIPS code combines the state and county identifiers. For instance, if multiple
counties end with “001,” the state FIPS code is added to make each county FIPS code distinct (e.g.,
01001, 02001, 04001), where the first two digits indicate the state, and the last three digits represent the
county.

Although NIST initiated the replacement of FIPS with the Geographical Name Information System
(GNIS) Feature ID in 2002, many federal organizations in the United States, including the U.S. Census
Bureau, continued to use FIPS due to its broader coverage and precision in identifying geographic
entities, especially smaller areas with uncertain natural boundaries. The U.S. Census Bureau maintains a
comprehensive hierarchy of census geographic entities for reference.

 22

As the primary key in all datasets for this project, we consistently use the column “FIPS” to ensure unique
data identification, regardless of the source FIPS granularity. We specify the FIPS granularity, such as
region, state, county, census division, tracts, block groups, etc., in the metadata table and reports’
descriptions. Users are presumed to be familiar with joining datasets using FIPS columns at different
geographic levels.

It’s worth noting that only a few datasets since the inception of this project do not include a FIPS column.
These exceptions are the following:

  1. The National Mental Health Services Survey (table: national_mental_health_services_survey),
 delivered in FY22Q4.

  2. The Veterans Service Organizations (VSO) 2010-2022, by ZIP code, delivered in FY24Q1.

  3. HUD USPS ZIP code Crosswalk Files, ZIP-to-tract for 2023, delivered in FY24Q2.

These datasets were provided at the sponsor’s special request.

5.2 Mapping ZIP Codes to County FIPS Codes

When realigning spatial data to different boundaries that do not perfectly match or nest within the original
spatial units, some data loss is inevitable. This occurs because the spatial distribution of data at higher
resolutions than the native unit is often unknown. For example, certain ZIP code boundaries overlap with
multiple county boundaries. When attempting to map ZIP code-level data to counties, there are situations
where data must be reassigned to two or more counties with limited knowledge of how to allocate it
accurately. Various methods exist to mitigate the degree of data loss, each with its strengths and
weaknesses based on the data’s nature. For social data, one effective approach is to allocate data based on
population distribution or addresses within those boundaries to reduce misallocation.

5.3 Linking Cohorts to EDH Datasets

 Joining geoids to our datasets

The above image focuses on the practical aspect of utilizing our datasets, specifically highlighting the
process of joining them using FIPS codes as the primary key. On the left side of the slide, we have a
visual representation of the cohort data, which may include various location identifiers such as addresses,
ZIP codes, or latitude and longitude coordinates. These location identifiers serve as the basis for mapping,
essentially translating them into FIPS codes, which are standardized geographic identifiers used in our
datasets.

The circle on the left symbolizes this mapping process, where the location information from the cohort is
transformed into FIPS codes for compatibility with our datasets. It’s important to note that there are
several methods to perform this conversion and join process. Different tools and techniques may be
employed based on the specific requirements and characteristics of the datasets and cohort.

By effectively joining the cohort data with our datasets using FIPS codes, we can integrate and analyze
information from various sources, enhancing the depth and breadth of our insights. This process of
intersection and integration facilitates comprehensive analysis and decision-making, enabling us to
leverage the full potential of our datasets in addressing research questions and informing strategic
initiatives. As we proceed, it’s crucial to prioritize data integrity and accuracy throughout the conversion
and join process, ensuring reliable and meaningful outcomes from our analyses.

One method for converting and joining a cohort to the datasets is to use addresses and/or ZIP code. This
approach involves using addresses and/or ZIP codes from the cohort data and mapping them to FIPS
codes, which serve as the common identifier in our datasets. To facilitate this mapping process, we rely
on crosswalk tables provided by the U.S. Housing and Urban Development’s Office of Policy
Development and Research.

These crosswalk tables offer a reference point for associating ZIP codes with corresponding FIPS codes,
enabling integration with our datasets. However, it’s important to acknowledge that this process is not
without its limitations. In approximately 45% of cases, ZIP codes cannot be perfectly mapped to FIPS

 24

codes at the county level. This imperfection underscores the challenges inherent in geographic data
integration and highlights the need for careful consideration and validation when performing these
conversions.

Despite its imperfections, leveraging crosswalk tables remains a valuable approach for linking cohort data
to our datasets, providing a foundational step in the analysis and interpretation of determinants of health.
As we navigate through this process, it’s essential to remain mindful of these limitations and explore
alternative methods for data integration where necessary, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of our
analyses.

A second method uses latitude and longitude coordinates for conversion and joining. SQL Server offers
support for two spatial data types: Geometry and Geography. These data types enable a more precise
conversion of latitude and longitude coordinates to FIPS codes. Unlike the first method which relies on
crosswalk tables, this approach provides a higher level of accuracy in mapping locations to FIPS codes.

However, it’s important to note that implementing this method requires a higher level of expertise and
experience in working with Geometry and Geography files within SQL Server. Users must possess a
deeper understanding of spatial data manipulation techniques and SQL Server functionalities to
effectively execute this conversion process. Despite the complexity involved, leveraging latitude and
longitude coordinates through SQL Server’s spatial data types offers the advantage of increased precision
and accuracy in data integration.

Organizations with skilled personnel and advanced technical capabilities may opt for this method to
ensure the highest level of spatial data accuracy in their analyses. As with any advanced technique,
thorough testing and validation are essential to verify the integrity of the converted data and ensure its
suitability for analysis and decision-making purposes.

 6. Error Checking and Quality Assurance

6.1 Error-Checking Approach

Beginning with the FY23Q1 release, ORNL provides concise information about error-checking activities
in order to provide formal evidence that the datasets supplied have been thoroughly error checked. Our
data profiling process is described in our project’s overview manuscript [12]:

“Following standard data and software development methodologies, data profiling is performed in four
different work environments: 1) a team-shared work environment for selection, extraction, and refinement
of raw data (development); 2) an ORNL intranet work environment focused on quality assurance testing
(QA-Intra); 3) an ORNL Knowledge Discovery Infrastructure (KDI) secure work environment that stores
highly sensitive data and ensures its security (QA-KDI). And finally, 4) a production environment housed
within the KDI environment and accessible to our VA sponsors, (Production). We carried out test
iterations in each of the four work environments as the datasets moved through them to confirm data
integrity and system compatibility.

All datasets were error-checked using a data profiling strategy that includes at least two reviewers and the
following test groups:

  1. evaluating missingness by assessing the number and distribution of missing values;

  2. compiling descriptive statistics, such as the number of rows, columns, and types of variable data;

 25

  3. appending checksums to a subset of the columns on both the source and destination copies to
 ensure consistency;

  4. consistently representing the social and physical environment using FIPS codes as geographic
 administrative boundaries and confirming that the FIPS codes correspond to the geographic
 administrative boundaries of the original data;

  5. manually comparing the first, last, and five additional randomly selected rows for consistency
 between the source and target datasets.

For ORNL-developed, or derivative, datasets, ORNL will provide additional error checking using a
combination of statistical methodologies based on each dataset’s properties, in addition to the data
profiling methodology described above.” [12]

7.1.1 Data Source

7.1.2 Description

The Drive-Time dataset calculates driving distance and estimated travel time from patient addresses to the
nearest VA healthcare facilities. It also assigns county-level FIPS codes based on geocoded address
locations to improve geographic accuracy. The dataset is developed and maintained by ORNL in
collaboration with the VA. It integrates data from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW) and

 26

refreshed OpenStreetMap (OSM) road network data to ensure accurate routing. The dataset supports
analyses of patient access to VA facilities and healthcare accessibility. It informs resource allocation,
facility planning, and transportation decisions within the VA’s Community EDH Data initiative.

Advanced information

This update mirrors previous deliveries. ORNL did not change the underlying methodology; the only
updates reflect refreshed source tables provided by the VA and updated OSM data, as detailed below.

Limitations

Proximity measures are based on modeled drive times rather than actual travel behavior, which may be
affected by traffic, safety conditions, or temporary road changes. Additionally, this dataset is restricted to
use within the VA’s EDH initiative, and access is coordinated through program managers to ensure
compliance with usage guidelines.

Method for Identifying the Nearest Facility

  3. The process begins by identifying the three nearest VA facilities using straight-line (Euclidean)
 distance between geographic coordinates.

  4. Routing algorithms are then applied using the most recent version of the OpenStreetMap (OSM)
 road network—updated by ORNL for each dataset release. These algorithms estimate drive times
 to each candidate facility.

  5. The facility with the shortest estimated drive time is selected, and the remaining two are excluded
 from the final dataset.

The NICS dataset provides monthly counts of background checks processed through the National Instant
Criminal Background Check System from January 2010 through March 2026. It reports state- and
territory-level counts using two-digit FIPS codes, year, month, state or territory name, and the number of
background checks. The dataset is sourced from the Federal Bureau of Investigation, which operates
NICS to support legally required background checks for firearm and explosive purchases. ORNL
prepared the dataset for the VA Community EDH Data project by extracting, standardizing, reshaping,
and organizing the FBI-published monthly count data. The dataset supports longitudinal and geographic
analyses of firearm-related background check activity across U.S. states and territories. It can be used to
examine temporal patterns, compare regional variation, and provide contextual measures for public
health, safety, and social-environmental analyses.

  • FY23Q4: An updated state-level dataset of permit and firearm-transaction counts from 2021-01
         through 2023-08.

As required by U.S. law, NICS conducts background checks on prospective purchasers of firearms or
explosives. When a buyer attempts to purchase a firearm, the seller—known as a Federal Firearms
Licensee (FFL)—contacts NICS electronically or by phone. The prospective buyer completes the required
Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) form, which is submitted through the FFL
for the background check. NICS staff then determine whether the buyer is prohibited from purchasing or
possessing a firearm. Since its inception in 1998, NICS has processed more than 300 million checks and
issued more than 1.5 million denials.

The following processing steps were applied to the FBI-provided raw data:

  • Used the Python pdfplumber package to extract tabular data from the downloadable PDF.

  • Added 2-digit state/territory FIPS codes based on the state/territory names provided in the table.

  • Added a year column to capture the year of the data.

  • Combined yearly tables into one table covering 2010–2026.

  • Converted the table from wide format (one column per month) to long format with a single month
 field containing values 01–12.

  • Removed months in the most recent year that do not yet have published counts.

Notes for Data Users: Geographic, Policy, and Outlier Considerations for NICS Data

The National Instant Criminal Background Check System (NICS) data represent background checks
initiated through NICS and should not be interpreted as a one-to-one count of firearm sales, firearm
ownership, or firearm prevalence. The Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) notes that NICS
background-check counts do not represent the number of firearms sold and that differences in state laws
and purchase scenarios prevent a direct one-to-one relationship between background checks and firearm
sales. [1, 2]

Users should also be aware that background-check activity is affected by jurisdiction-specific policies and
administrative processes. Some states and territories rely on the FBI for all firearm background checks,
while others operate as full or partial point-of-contact jurisdictions and conduct some or all checks
through a state-designated agency. In addition, permit systems, recurring permit rechecks, private-sale
requirements, local registration rules, and backlog-processing activity can affect how background-check
counts appear in the data. These differences should be considered when comparing jurisdictions or
interpreting year-over-year (YoY) changes. [2, 3, 4]

Washington, D.C.: Washington, D.C. should be reviewed separately when comparing YoY percentage
change and per-capita background-check rates. In this dataset, Washington, D.C. may show large YoY
percentage increases while still having relatively low per-capita background-check counts. This pattern

 32

can occur when the baseline number of checks is small: a modest absolute increase can produce a large
percentage change.

Users should interpret this pattern as a data-use consideration rather than as direct evidence of firearm
ownership or firearm sales. Washington, D.C. has a distinct firearm regulatory environment, including
firearm registration requirements administered by the District of Columbia Metropolitan Police
Department. These local requirements may affect how firearm-related activity appears in background-
check data compared with states that have different registration, permitting, or background-check
procedures. [4]

Kentucky: Kentucky should be treated as a structural outlier in NICS time-series analyses. Published
reporting based on FBI information indicates that Kentucky runs monthly background checks on
concealed-carry permit holders, which contributes to substantially higher monthly NICS counts than
would be expected if counts reflected only point-of-sale firearm transaction checks. Users should consider
presenting Kentucky separately or excluding Kentucky from outlier-sensitive analyses when the analytic
goal is to compare firearm transaction activity across states. [5]

Illinois: Illinois may appear as an outlier during 2020 and 2021. The Illinois State Police reported an
unprecedented number of new Firearm Owner Identification (FOID) card and Concealed Carry License
(CCL) applications in 2020. New FOID card applications reached nearly 143,000 at the peak in October
2020. The Illinois State Police also reported high Firearm Transaction Inquiry Program (FTIP) activity
and implemented technology, staffing, process, and legislative changes to reduce FOID and CCL
backlogs. [6, 7]

Because NICS counts represent background checks initiated through the system, users should interpret
Illinois values during this period as potentially affected by unusually high FOID/CCL application activity,
firearm transaction inquiries, and backlog-processing activity. Users should avoid interpreting the 2020–
2021 Illinois values as a simple proxy for firearm purchases alone. [1, 2, 6, 7]

Northern Mariana Islands: In 2010, the Northern Mariana Islands reported zero NICS checks for the
full year in the delivered data. Subsequent years show the gradual introduction of monthly background
checks. For YoY comparisons, users should consider excluding the Northern Mariana Islands when 2010
is used as the baseline year because YoY calculations involving 2010 values may require division by
zero. [1]

Federal and State Policy Context: The Brady Handgun Violence Prevention Act established federal
background-check requirements for transfers by federally licensed firearm dealers. However, background-
check implementation varies across jurisdictions. Some states have expanded background-check
requirements beyond the federal baseline, including laws that apply to certain private sales or transfers.
These laws are commonly referred to as universal background-check laws. Because state laws, point-of-
contact status, permit systems, administrative rechecks, and local registration requirements vary, NICS
counts should be interpreted as background-check activity rather than as direct measures of firearm sales,
firearm ownership, or firearm prevalence. [2, 3, 8]

The Bipartisan Safer Communities Act (BSCA; Public Law 117-159) amended federal firearms law,
including provisions related to who is considered to be engaged in the business of dealing firearms. In
2024, the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) issued a final rule implementing
BSCA-related changes to the regulatory definition of “engaged in the business” as a firearms dealer. In
2026, ATF proposed revisions to portions of those implementing regulations while retaining the statutory
BSCA definition. Because the legal and regulatory environment may change over time, users should

 33

consult current federal and state guidance when using NICS data to support policy-sensitive analyses. [9,
10]

7.3 Veterans Service Organizations and Accredited Representatives

7.3.1 Data Source

U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs Office of General Counsel

7.3.2 Description

The Veterans Service Organizations and Accredited Representatives dataset provides information on U.S.
Department of Veterans Affairs (VA)–accredited representatives, including Veterans Service
Organization (VSO) representatives, attorneys, and claims agents. The dataset is organized by state, ZIP
code, and county. The raw data were downloaded as a point-in-time snapshot from the VA Office of
General Counsel accreditation search tool using the “Attorneys,” “Claims Agents,” and “VSOs with
Reps” download options. Because county information is not included in the original VA source data,
county assignments were derived by linking each record’s ZIP code to county information using the U.S.
Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) USPS ZIP Code Crosswalk.

The source data are maintained by the VA Office of General Counsel and were prepared for the VA
Community EDH Data project by Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL). ORNL compiled the
downloaded VA accreditation files and added county-level geographic information using the HUD USPS
ZIP Code Crosswalk to support county-based analyses.

This dataset supports analyses of Veterans’ geographic access to accredited representation, benefits
assistance, and advocacy resources. It can be used to assess the availability of VA-accredited
representatives across states, ZIP codes, and counties; identify potential gaps in representation; inform
outreach and service planning; and support geographic assessments of access to benefits-related
assistance within the VA Community EDH Data initiative.

U.S. Veterans Service Organizations (VSOs) are vital in supporting and advocating for veterans. They
offer a wide range of services to assist veterans in transitioning to civilian life, accessing healthcare and
benefits, and addressing mental health concerns. VSOs also serve as platforms for veterans to connect and
share their experiences.

VSOs are nonprofit organizations that provide assistance, support, and advocacy to military veterans and
their families. They act as liaisons to help veterans access benefits, navigate civilian life challenges, and
foster a sense of camaraderie among former service members.

Types of VA-Accredited Representatives

 38

Accredited representatives assisting veterans are typically categorized into three groups:

  1. VSO Representatives: These representatives, associated with organizations such as Veterans of
 Foreign Wars, the American Legion, and Injured American Veterans, provide free representation
 primarily for initial benefit claims. They can assist in gathering evidence, submitting claims on
 behalf of veterans, and communicating with the VA regarding claims.

  2. Attorneys: Attorneys assist veterans in navigating the VA appeals process. They handle the
 majority of representation and receive compensation.

  3. Claim Agents: Claim agents are independent legal practitioners performing tasks similar to
 attorneys but are not attorneys themselves. Becoming a claim agent requires passing a written test
 and undergoing a character and fitness evaluation.

Datasets on VA-Accredited Representatives

We offer three datasets containing information about Veterans Service Organization representatives,
including attorneys, agents, and VSO representatives, categorized at the state, zip code, and county levels.
These datasets exclusively include individuals accredited by the VA and organizations recognized by the
VA as of December 2021. Please note that individuals with pending applications for accreditation or
recognition with the VA Office of General Counsel are not included.

Limitations This dataset represents VA-accredited representatives and VA-recognized organizations as of
the source extraction date used for the release. It does not include individuals or organizations with
pending applications for accreditation or recognition through the VA Office of General Counsel.

Counts by state, ZIP code, and county describe the geographic distribution of accredited representatives in
the curated source data, but they do not measure representative availability, caseload, service capacity,
quality of representation, or whether a representative is accepting new clients.

Geographic summaries should be interpreted carefully because representatives may serve veterans outside
the ZIP code, county, or state associated with their listed location. Historical records curated for this
dataset may also reflect changes in accreditation status, organization recognition, or contact information
over time.

APPENDIX B. ERROR CHECKING

 Appendix B. Error Checking

The following descriptive statistics were obtained using the Python describe function. The definitions of
the main statistics included are as follows:
 • count: The number of non-null (or non-missing) values in the series or column.
 • mean: The arithmetic average of all values in the series or column.
 • std: The standard deviation, which measures the amount of variation or dispersion of a set of
 values. A low standard deviation indicates that the values tend to be close to the mean, while a
 high standard deviation indicates that the values are spread out over a wider range.
 • min: The smallest value in the series or column.
 • 25% (First Quartile / Q1): The 25th percentile, meaning 25% of the data points are less than or
 equal to this value.
 • 50% (Median / Second Quartile / Q2): The 50th percentile, which is the middle value when the
 data is ordered. Half of the data points are below this value, and half are above.
 • 75% (Third Quartile / Q3): The 75th percentile, meaning 75% of the data points are less than or
 equal to this value.
 • max: The largest value in the series or column.
