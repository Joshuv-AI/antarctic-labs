Cosmology with the line-of-sight shear of strong gravitational lenses
Julien Larena

Abstract
Stage-IV photometric galaxy surveys are designed to measure the position and shapes of billions of galaxies. Their aim is to characterise the large-scale distribution of matter in the Universe using galaxy clustering and weak gravitational lensing. As a byproduct, stage-IV surveys are expected to detect more than a hundred thousand strong gravitational lenses. In this article, we propose the use of weak-lensing perturbations to strong lenses, specifically their line-of-sight (LOS) shear, as a cosmological probe. This new observable allows us to define three new correlation functions: the LOS shear with itself, with galaxy positions, and with galaxy shapes, thereby promoting the standard 3 × 2 3\times 2 pt correlation method to a 6 × 2 6\times 2 pt scheme. We design estimators for these new correlation functions and determine their expectation values as a function of the matter power spectrum. We then derive the analytical expression for the full covariance matrix of the 6 × 2 6\times 2 pt correlation scheme. Considering various scenarios for the stage-IV strong-lensing samples, we demonstrate that the cosmological information carried by the LOS shear of strong lenses will be detectable with a very high signal-to-noise ratio, even in the most pessimistic of cases. Strong lenses are thus extremely promising cosmological probes, whose synergy with galaxy positions and shapes should also contribute to mitigating systematics in stage-IV surveys.

Introduction

Gravitational lensing is the phenomenon resulting from the deflection of light rays by the gravitational fields between the source and the observer. Most of the time, gravitational lensing only leads to small effects – a slight magnification, or demagnification, of light sources, accompanied by a slight distortion of their shapes. The latter manifestation of weak lensing, known as cosmic shear, produces alignment patterns of background galaxies – tangentially around foreground matter overdensities, and radially around underdensities

In the era of stage-IV galaxy surveys, our ability to extract cosmological information will no longer be limited by statistics, but by systematic uncertainties, such as the intrinsic alignment of galaxies, their redshift distribution, and the modelling of small-scale baryonic processes. Controlling systematics, is, therefore, the main challenge of stage-IV surveys.

Due to their sensitivity, resolution, and their wide sky coverage, stage-IV surveys will also observe an abundance of the rarer strong manifestations of gravitational lensing, namely multiple images, arcs and Einstein rings, which typically occur when a foreground galaxy happens to lie directly on line of sight to another, more distant, galaxy. As a rule of thumb, there is one strong-lensing event every ten thousand observed galaxies, which implies that stage-IV catalogues shall comprise over a hundred thousand strong lenses

To first approximation, a strong gravitational lens may be modelled as some concentration of matter in an otherwise homogeneous and isotropic Universe. In this approach, light deflection only occurs in the vicinity of the main deflector, but on the scales relevant to strong lensing, the Universe is far from being homogeneous, and the propagation of light is perturbed by the matter structures between the source, the main deflector, and the observer. {FAQ}

•

What is line-of-sight (LOS) shear?
It is a complex number,
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
, which captures the effect of tidal perturbations along the LOS of a strong lens. The setup is depicted in
fig.

1
, where (o) observes a source (s) strongly lensed by a deflector (d), with LOS perturbations. This configuration actually involves three shears,
\gamma_{\mathrm{od}},\gamma_{\mathrm{os}},\gamma_{\mathrm{ds}}
, where
\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}}
would be the complex ellipticity, as seen from (a), of a small circular source at (b), due to weak lensing. The combination
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{od}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{os}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{ds}}
affects the image in a way that is maximally independent of the source and main lens. Thus, the LOS shear may be seen as the net weak-lensing distortion of a strong-lensing image.

•

What is the difference between LOS shear and the usual weak-lensing shear?
The normal weak-lensing shear is only one of the three terms, namely
\gamma_{\mathrm{os}}
, in the expression of
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
. The contribution of some matter lump to
\gamma_{\mathrm{os}}
peaks if it lies halfway between (o) and (s). For
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
, that contribution is enhanced between (o) and (d) and suppressed between (d) and (s); see
eqs.

2.10
,
2.11
and
2.12
.

•

How can we measure the LOS shear from strong-lensing data?
An image
I(\bm{\theta})
consists of surface brightness,
I
, in pixels,
\bm{\theta}
. If the image is produced by gravitational lensing of a source
I_{\mathrm{s}}
, then
I(\bm{\theta})=I_{\mathrm{s}}[\bm{\theta}-\bm{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})]
, where
\bm{\alpha}
is the lensing displacement field. When interpreting this image, one must model both the source profile
I_{\mathrm{s}}(\bm{\theta})
and the lensing displacement
\bm{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
. In that context, the LOS shear
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
is a parameter in
\bm{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
which can be inferred from the data. In
Lenstronomy
, this is implemented in the
LineOfSight
module.

•

How can we use the LOS shear in cosmology?

Equations

2.10
,
2.11
and
2.12
show that
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
depends on the gravitational potential
\Phi
, just like the density contrast (
2.6
) or the weak-lensing shear (
2.14
). Measuring correlation functions of these quantities then informs us about the matter power spectrum. Current galaxy surveys exploit three correlation functions built from galaxy positions (
\mathrm{P}
) and ellipticities (
\mathrm{E}
):
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}
(cosmic shear),
\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P}
(galaxy clustering) and
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{P}
(galaxy–galaxy lensing). Adding the LOS shear (
\mathrm{L}
) allows us to define three new correlation functions (
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
), thereby promoting the standard
3\times 2
pt method to
6\times 2
pt (see

).

•

Stage-IV surveys will observe
\mathcal{O}(10^{9})
galaxies and
\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
lenses. How can the latter compete with the former?
In cosmic shear, a very large number of galaxies are required for the cosmological signal to exceed their intrinsic shape noise. This is because, in the apparent ellipticity of a galaxy,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{0}+\gamma
, the intrinsic contribution,
\varepsilon_{0}
, is typically
30
times larger than the weak-lensing shear,
\gamma
. In other words, the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of weak lensing is about
3\,\%
for one galaxy. In contrast, we should measure the LOS shear on individual strong lenses with an SNR of about
100\%
, so that comparatively few lenses are needed to extract a cosmological signal. Furthermore, we expect the LOS shear to be immune to cosmic-shear systematics like intrinsic alignment, which makes these probes highly complementary.

•

Why compute the
6\times 2
pt covariance matrix analytically instead of using simulations?
Analytical calculations have the advantage of producing results that are easier to interpret. Since the project to use the LOS shear as a cosmological observable is at an early stage, interpretation is essential to design and optimise the future observational strategy (which lenses to keep? how to bin them by redshift and angular separation?). Furthermore, analytical results are more flexible: it is easier to add new ingredients to a formula than to run an entirely new suite of simulations. Not to mention that simulating a cosmological sample of perturbed strong lenses is significantly more challenging than simulating a galaxy sample.

•

Will strong lenses improve the measurements of cosmological parameters?
In this article, we demonstrate that the new correlation functions,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
, are detectable from stage-IV surveys with high SNR, across a range of angular scales. We are currently conducting Fisher forecasts to quantify the expected gain in precision and accuracy for cosmological parameters such as
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\sigma_{8}
; the results will be presented in a subsequent paper.

Line-of-sight shear in strong lensing

2.1
Tidal perturbations to a strong lens

Consider the scenario depicted in
fig.

1
, where a source galaxy is well aligned with a foreground galaxy acting as a strong gravitational lens. Let us denote with
\bm{\beta}
the position at which some point within the source galaxy would be observed in the absence of the lens, through a homogeneous and isotropic Universe. We then denote with
\bm{\theta}
the position of an image of that point, i.e. the direction in which it is observed in the presence of deflectors.

Figure 1
:

and

is the lensing potential of the main deflector

\bm{\beta}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{os}}\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ds}}(\bm{\nabla}\Psi)(\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}}\bm{\theta})\ ,

(2.1)

where

are called distortion matrices. Each one of the

Such a description of LOS effects is valid only if the secondary deflectors can be treated in the

. This means that their gravitational field is well-approximated, at the scale of the strong-lensing image, by a second-order expansion of their gravitational potential around the LOS
[

16

. Physically speaking, this corresponds either to smooth distributions of matter lying near the LOS, or to concentrations of matter lying far from it. See refs.
[

11

If the LOS perturbations are weak, the distortion matrices

]

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ab}}=\bm{1}-\begin{bmatrix}\kappa_{\mathrm{ab}}&0\\
0&\kappa_{\mathrm{ab}}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{Re}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}})&\mathrm{Im}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}})\\
\mathrm{Im}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}})&-\mathrm{Re}(\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}})\end{bmatrix}\ ,

2.2
Measuring the LOS shear with strong-lensing images

From a strong-lensing image such as an Einstein ring, it is generally impossible to identify the effect of individual LOS perturbations
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{os}},\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}},\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ds}}
, or distinguish them from the properties of the main deflector,
\Psi

is a parameter to be fitted just like

This source-position degeneracy

[
16

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ds}}^{-1}

, we have

\bm{\beta}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{LOS}}\bm{\theta}-(\bm{\nabla}\Psi_{\mathrm{eff}})(\bm{\theta})\ ,\qquad\text{with}\quad\left\{\begin{aligned} \bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{LOS}}&\equiv\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ds}}^{-1}\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{os}}\\
\Psi_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\theta})&\equiv\Psi(\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}}\bm{\theta})\end{aligned}\right.\ .

(2.3)

[

\Psi_{\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{\theta})

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{LOS}}
. On the one hand, the expression of the effective potential
\Psi_{\mathrm{eff}}
shows that there is a strong degeneracy between the foreground perturbations
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}}
and the properties of the main deflector
[

]

\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{LOS}}
shows that it would be ill-advised to try to measure
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{od}},\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{os}},\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{ds}}
independently from one another. It is, however, possible to measure the shear component of
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{LOS}}

\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{od}}+\gamma_{\mathrm{os}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{ds}}

(2.4)

in the weak-lensing limit (
\kappa_{\mathrm{ab}},|\gamma_{\mathrm{ab}}|\ll 1
). We shall refer to
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
as the

\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
to be distinguishable from the properties of the main lens, such as its ellipticity. This is because the LOS shear represents a strictly homogeneous tidal field, while the main lens’s tidal field always displays some radial gradient. See ref.
[
6
]
for a comparison of the morphological features of shear and ellipticity.

The practical measurability of the LOS shear from realistic strong-lensing images was assessed in ref.
[
24
]

It was shown that the LOS shear can be accurately measured, with a mean uncertainty on the order of 0.01, provided that the model for the main lens is elaborate enough. This corresponds to a signal-to-noise ratio of order unity for individual lenses. The first measurements of the LOS in real data were made
[

,
26

in 45 strong lenses from the SLACS catalogue
[

]

[

Caveats

Albeit encouraging, these results also emphasise that LOS shear measurements are still in their infancy. In particular, it is unclear what “elaborate enough” means in practice for a lens model. In the context of Bayesian forward modelling of an image, if the actual lens possesses morphological features that are not accounted for in the model, then the sampler will tend to use the free shear in order to compensate for the missing features, thereby leading to spurious biases in the best-fit value of
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
. An eloquent example was found with the mock analysis of ref.
[
24
]
: if a lens consists of a baryonic core and a dark-matter halo whose centres are offset, and if one tries to fit the image produced by that lens assuming that both components share the same centre, then the measured
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
is clearly biased; the bias disappears if the model allows for the offset as a free parameter.

Whilst the necessary features of a good lens model – as far as the LOS shear is concerned – are still to be determined, we can already claim that the standard elliptical power-law (EPL) model does not fulfil them. In addition to the biases observed in mocks
[
24
,
12
]
, studies on real data lead to the same conclusion. Analysing HST data using EPL plus external shear, ref.
[
12
]
found suspicious correlations between the orientation of the external shear and the main lens’s ellipticity, which suggests that the external-shear parameter may account for details of the lens’s azimuthal structure that are not well-described by a mere ellipticity
[
53
]
. Reference
[
25
]
, besides, finds that the best-fit values of
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
for SLACS lenses modelled as EPL plus LOS shear are typically ten times larger than expectations from cosmology.

Another potential source of systematics in
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
comes from neglecting higher-order LOS effects, such as the LOS flexion. Reference
[
11
]
derived a minimal LOS flexion model, which features four additional complex parameters. In most realistic situations, the LOS flexion is too small to be measured, but it does produce a systematic bias on
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
if not accounted for.

In this article, we shall assume that the LOS shear is strictly due to cosmological perturbations, and that it can be measured on strong-lensing images without systematic biases, albeit with a signal-to-noise ratio that may be smaller than unity on individual images. Our goal is to investigate its statistical measurability through correlation functions.

2.3
LOS shear from cosmological perturbations

Let us assume that the space-time geometry experienced by light on its way from the source to the main deflector, and then to the observer, is well approximated by a spatially flat Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker model with perturbations. The line element reads

pt correlation method, which consists of: (1) the two-point correlation of galaxy positions, called
galaxy clustering
,
\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P}\sim\left\langle\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\times\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\right\rangle
, where
\delta_{\mathrm{g}}
denotes the density contrast of galaxy number counts; (2) the two-point correlation of galaxy ellipticities
\varepsilon
, called
cosmic shear
,
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}\sim\left\langle\varepsilon\times\varepsilon\right\rangle
; and (3) the cross-correlation of galaxy shapes and positions, called
galaxy–galaxy lensing
,
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{P}\sim\left\langle\varepsilon\times\delta_{\mathrm{g}}\right\rangle
.

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}\sim\left\langle\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}\times\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}\right\rangle

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}\sim\left\langle\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}\times\varepsilon\right\rangle

\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}

\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E},\mathrm{E}\mathrm{P},\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P},\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L},\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E},\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
.

Albeit ten thousand times less abundant than galaxies, strong lenses will result in much more precise measurements of the (LOS) shear. Indeed, following the results of
[
24

, the typical uncertainty on individual measurements of
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
is expected to be
\Delta\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}\approx 0.01\sim\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}

\Delta\gamma=\varepsilon_{0}\approx 0.3\sim 30\times\gamma
, which means an SNR of about
3\%
for individual measurements
[
56
]
.

In addition, the LOS shear of strong lenses is expected to be subject to very different systematics compared to the standard
3\times 2
pt observables. For instance, it should be immune to intrinsic alignments. For these reasons, the
6\times 2
pt correlation method has the potential to significantly enhance the cosmological power of stage-IV galaxy surveys by exploiting the synergy of weak-lensing and strong-lensing probes.

3.2.1
Autocorrelation of the LOS shear (LL)

The estimators of the LOS shear two-point correlation functions are defined similarly to the standard cosmic shear estimators
[
47
]
.

Noise

First of all, in order to account for the uncertainty of individual LOS shear measurements, we introduce an additive noise term
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
which will encode both instrumental errors and systematics. Specifically, we shall write the
measured
value of the LOS shear on lens
i
as

\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}=\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}+\eta^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}\ ,

(3.2)

where
\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}
is the
true
LOS shear field as defined in
section

2.3
and evaluated at lens
i
. Note the analogy between
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
and the intrinsic-ellipticity term
\varepsilon_{0}
in cosmic shear; in that sense
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
may be considered as a kind of LOS shape noise. We shall assume throughout the article that LOS shear measurements are unbiased (
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
vanishes on average), and that the LOS noises of distinct lenses are independent.

Plus and cross polarisations

Correlation functions aim to identify alignment patterns in pairs of LOS shear measurements. For a pair
(ij)
of lenses, the relative orientation of their LOS shear is conveniently expressed in terms of a basis adapted to the pair. The construction goes as follows (see
fig.

4
, left panel). Consider the great arc that connects
\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}
to
\hat{\bm{u}}_{j}
, and denote with
\hat{\bm{t}}_{ij}
the unit vector, tangent to that curve, oriented from
i
to
j
. A second unit vector tangent to
\mathcal{S}^{2}
at
\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}
may be defined as
\hat{\bm{n}}_{ij}\equiv\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}\times\hat{\bm{t}}_{ij}
. Together, these vectors form an orthonormal basis
(\hat{\bm{t}}_{ij},\hat{\bm{n}}_{ij})
of
\mathrm{T}_{i}\mathcal{S}^{2}
that is rotated by an angle
\psi_{ij}
with respect to the coordinate basis
(\hat{\bm{e}}_{\theta},\hat{\bm{e}}_{\varphi})
. We then define the
plus and cross polarisations
of the LOS shear of lens
i
as its real and imaginary parts when expressed in that basis; since
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
is implicitly decomposed on the coordinate basis, we have

\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ij}+\mathrm{i}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times ij}=\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ij}}\ .

(3.3)

We stress that, in the notation
\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\mathrm{p}ij}
,
\mathrm{p}\in\{+,\times\}
, the indices
i
and
j
are not interchangeable, because the former indicates the lens whose LOS shear is being measured. The same construction may be performed for lens
j
, thereby leading to

\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ji}+\mathrm{i}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times ji}=\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{j}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ji}}\ .

(3.4)

The rotation angles
\psi_{ij}
and
\psi_{ji}
are different in general.
5
5

5

The explicit expression of the relevant phase, in terms of the polar coordinates of
\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}
and
\hat{\bm{u}}_{j}
, reads

\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ij}}=\frac{\left[\cos\theta_{i}\sin\theta_{j}\cos(\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{i})-\sin\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{j}-\mathrm{i}\sin\theta_{j}\sin(\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{i})\right]^{2}}{\left[\cos\theta_{i}\sin\theta_{j}\cos(\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{i})-\sin\theta_{i}\cos\theta_{j}\right]^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta_{j}\sin^{2}(\varphi_{j}-\varphi_{i})}\ ,

(3.5)

which is manifestly not symmetric under the exchange of
i
and
j
.

However, when
\vartheta_{ij}\ll 1
– a condition typically satisfied in practice – the tangent spaces
\mathrm{T}_{i}\mathcal{S}^{2},\mathrm{T}_{j}\mathcal{S}^{2}
coincide and we have
\psi_{ij}\approx\psi_{ji}
.

Simple estimator for the LOS shear autocorrelation

Consider all pairs
(ij)
of lenses, and sort them according to their angular separation
\vartheta_{ij}
. In practice, we introduce angular bins
(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha})
with limits
(\vartheta_{\alpha})
;
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
is defined as the set of pairs of lenses
(ij)
such that
\vartheta_{\alpha}\leq\vartheta_{ij}<\vartheta_{\alpha+1}
. Since the LOS shear is a complex (or spin-two) quantity, two complementary correlation functions may be measured in the
\alpha
th
angular bin, namely the plus and minus
(\pm)
correlations. Estimators for these may be defined as

(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}\equiv\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|}\sum_{(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}}\left(\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ij}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ji}\pm\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times ij}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times ji}\right),

(3.6)

where
|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|
is the number of terms in the sum, that is the number of pairs
(ij)
in
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
. A more general definition could involve different weights for the lenses in the sum.

Alternative expression

For later convenience, we shall rewrite
eq.

3.6
as follows, so as to make it formally closer to
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
estimators that we define in the next paragraphs,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{L}^{+}})_{\alpha}\pm(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{\times}\mathrm{L}^{\times}})_{\alpha}

(3.7)

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}})_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{L}W^{\alpha}_{ij}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\mathrm{p}ij}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\mathrm{p}ji}\qquad\text{for }\mathrm{p}\in\{+,\times\}.

(3.8)

In the above, we have explicitly split
(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}
into their two elementary polarisation terms. Furthermore, we have turned the sum over pairs
(ij)\in\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
into a sum over all lenses in the sample by introducing the weighted window function

W^{\alpha}_{ij}\equiv\frac{L^{2}}{|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|}\times\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\vartheta_{\alpha}\leq\vartheta_{ij}<\vartheta_{\alpha+1},\\
0&\text{otherwise, or if $i=j$.}\end{cases}

(3.9)

The prefactor in the above expression may be simplified by estimating the number of pairs
|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|
in the
\alpha
th
bin. Let
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{u}})
denote the annulus spanned by the points in
\mathcal{S}^{2}
forming, with
\hat{\bm{u}}
, an angle between
\vartheta_{\alpha}
and
\vartheta_{\alpha+1}
(see
fig.

4
, right panel). If a lens is observed at
\hat{\bm{u}}
, then
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{u}})
is the region where other lenses can make, with the former one, a pair in
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
. Let
\Omega_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{u}})
be the solid angle covered by
\mathcal{A}_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{u}})\cap\mathcal{F}
. For a full-sky survey (
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{S}^{2}
),
\Omega_{\alpha}
does not depend on
\hat{\bm{u}}
, in which case
|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|=L\times n_{\mathrm{L}}\Omega_{\alpha}=L^{2}\Omega_{\alpha}/\Omega
. In a survey that does not cover the entire celestial sphere, the exact result for
|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|
would depend on the survey geometry. Since most of the cosmological signal is carried by small angular separations (
\vartheta\ll 1
), we shall neglect such boundary effects in the remainder of this article – this is the
pseudo-full-sky approximation
. When substituting the expression for
|\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}|
in
eq.

3.9
, we obtain

W^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}=\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{\alpha}}\times\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\vartheta_{\alpha}\leq\vartheta_{\mu\nu}<\vartheta_{\alpha+1},\\
0&\text{otherwise, or if $\mu=\nu$.}\end{cases}

(3.10)

In this new expression (
3.10
) of the weight function, the number of lenses
L
has disappeared, which makes it independent of the nature of the objects involved in the pairs; this is why we used generic indices
\mu,\nu
which may refer to either lenses or galaxies.

3.2.2
Cross-correlation of LOS shear and galaxy ellipticity (LE)

The estimator of the cross-correlation of the LOS shear with the apparent ellipticity of galaxies is almost identical to that of the LOS shear autocorrelation. The difference is that we should now consider pairs of lenses
i
and galaxies
a
rather than pairs of lenses. Averaging over a large number of pairs is expected to extract the relative alignment of the LOS shear and standard shear by removing the contribution of both noise and galaxy intrinsic ellipticities.

Just like
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
, the apparent (complex) ellipticity
\varepsilon_{a}
of galaxy
a
may be decomposed into plus and cross polarisations when forming a pair with lens
i
,

\varepsilon_{+ai}+\mathrm{i}\,\varepsilon_{\times ai}=\varepsilon_{a}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\psi_{ai}}\ ,

(3.11)

where
\psi_{ai}
is defined as in
fig.

4
. Following the same construction as in
section

3.2.1
, we get the following estimators for the two LE correlation functions,

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}_{A}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{E}^{+}_{A}})_{\alpha}\pm(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{\times}\mathrm{E}^{\times}_{A}})_{\alpha}

(3.12)

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{A}})_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{LG_{A}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}W_{ia}^{\alpha}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\mathrm{p}ia}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{p}ai}\ ,\qquad\text{for }\mathrm{p}\in\{+,\times\}.

(3.13)

In the above, we have assumed that the galaxy sample is large enough to be divided into tomographic redshift bins;
(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}_{A}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}
thus estimates the correlations between the strong lensing sample and the
A
th
tomographic sample, comprising
G_{A}
galaxies.

3.2.3
Cross-correlation of LOS shear and galaxy positions (LP)

The cross-correlation of LOS shear and galaxy positions is analogous to galaxy–galaxy lensing in the usual
3\times 2
pt analysis. It is based on the fact that galaxies, as matter overdensities, produce tangential shear around them. The LP estimator consists in extracting that information by summing the
+
polarisation of the LOS shear (the
\times
polarisation vanishes on average) for all lens–galaxy pairs
(ia)
within a certain angular bin
\alpha
,

(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{A}})_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{LG_{A}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}W_{ia}^{\alpha}\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\ .

(3.14)

Just like in the LE case, we have assumed that the galaxy sample is large enough to be divided into tomographic redshift bins. Note that a tangential shear corresponds to
\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+}<0
, so
the expectation value of
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
will be negative
.

3.3
Expectation values of the estimators in
\Lambda
CDM

Let us now compute the expectation value of the estimators defined in
section

3.2
, that is, the signal that we aim to detect. Before we perform the actual calculation, we must specify what
expectation value
really means in the present context.

3.3.1
Three distinct notions of average

In our current understanding of cosmology, randomness in the sky originates from the quantum fluctuations of the physical fields that eventually lead to cosmic structures via gravitational collapse. Technically speaking, this concept is implemented by treating the gravitational potential
\Phi
– and all fields that derive from it, such as the density contrast
\delta
and the peculiar velocity field – as Gaussian random fields.

In this context, an expectation value means an average over realisations of those fields, i.e. a theoretical average over realisations of the Universe. For the observables defined in
section

3.2
, jumping from one realisation of the Universe to another has two consequences: on the one hand, the density and shear fields fluctuate; on the other hand, the positions and properties of their tracers (galaxies and lenses) change too. In practice, it is convenient to explicitly distinguish these two effects as
cosmic averaging
and
sample averaging
.

Cosmic averaging
\left\langle\ldots\right\rangle

will refer to averages over realisations
\mathcal{U}
of the
macroscopic
properties of the Universe, i.e. the underlying fields. It is the usual notion of average involved in, e.g., the definition of the matter power spectrum.

Galaxy-sample averaging
\left\lceil\ldots\right\rceil

will be the average over the properties of all the galaxies in the sample, given a realisation
\mathcal{U}
of the macroscopic properties of the Universe (in particular the density field). We shall parameterise a galaxy with (i) its redshift
z
; (ii) its angular position
\hat{\bm{u}}
; and (iii) its intrinsic complex ellipticity
\varepsilon_{0}
, which we gather into the quintuplet
\bm{\Gamma}=(z,\hat{\bm{u}},\varepsilon_{0})
. For an observable
X
that depends on the sample of
G
galaxies, the galaxy-sample average then reads

\left\lceil X\right\rceil(\mathcal{U})\equiv\left[\prod_{A=1}^{T}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a}|\mathcal{U})\right]X(\bm{\Gamma}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\Gamma}_{G})\ ,

(3.15)

where
p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}|\mathcal{U})
is the probability density function (PDF) of
\bm{\Gamma}
in the
A
th
tomographic bin, given the cosmic realisation
\mathcal{U}
.

Let us be more explicit and decompose
p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}|\mathcal{U})
into elementary components:

p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}|\mathcal{U})\,\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}=f_{A}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z\times\left[1+b(z)\,\delta(z,\hat{\bm{u}};\mathcal{U})\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}}{\Omega}\times p(\varepsilon_{0};z)\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\ .

(3.16)

In
eq.

3.16
,
f_{A}(z)
denotes the normalised redshift selection function of the survey in the
A
th
bin, which we assume to coincide with the
observed
redshift distribution in that bin. For a given line of sight
\hat{\bm{u}}
, this is modulated by the actual overdensity in galaxy number counts, which we assume to be linearly biased by
b(z)
with respect to the total matter density contrast
\delta
. Finally,
p(\varepsilon_{0};z)
denotes the PDF of the intrinsic ellipticity of galaxies at
z
, which we assume to be independent of
\hat{\bm{u}}
; we shall also neglect intrinsic alignments in this work.

Lens-sample averaging
\left\lfloor\ldots\right\rfloor

will be the average over the relevant properties of strong lenses in the sample given a macroscopic realisation
\mathcal{U}
. We shall parameterise a lens with (i) a pair of redshifts
\bm{Z}=(z_{\mathrm{d}},z_{\mathrm{s}})
, which denote respectively the redshift of the main deflector and source of the system; (ii) its position
\hat{\bm{u}}
in the sky; and (iii) the error
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
on the measurement of the LOS shear in that system, as introduced in
eq.

3.2
. We gather the aforementioned lens parameters into a sextuplet
\bm{\Lambda}=(\bm{Z},\hat{\bm{u}},\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}})
. For an observable
X
that depends on a sample of
L
lenses, the lens-sample average then reads

\left\lfloor X\right\rfloor(\mathcal{U})\equiv\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{i}|\mathcal{U})\right]X(\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\Lambda}_{L})\ ,

(3.17)

where
p(\bm{\Lambda}|\mathcal{U})
is the PDF of
\Lambda
in the cosmic realisation
\mathcal{U}
.
In the following, we shall neglect lens clustering in the expression of
p(\bm{\Lambda}|\mathcal{U})
, which implies that the angular positions of lenses are simply Poisson-distributed. We assume, besides, that the noise
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
on LOS shear measurements is independent of the lens’s position in the sky. Therefore,

p(\bm{\Lambda})\,\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}=p(\bm{Z})\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}\times\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}}{\Omega}\times p(\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}};\bm{Z})\,\mathrm{d}^{2}\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}\ ,

(3.18)

where the dependence on the cosmic macroscopic realisation
\mathcal{U}
has dropped. As a consequence, lens-sample averaging and cosmic averaging commute in this framework.

This approximation is justified because none of the observables that we are interested in rely on lens clustering. In contrast, the LP cross-correlation directly depends on the fact that galaxies are not homogeneously distributed in the sky. Here, neglecting lens clustering is analogous to neglecting source clustering in cosmic shear, which leads to a small, higher-order, bias whose investigation is left for future work.

(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L})^{\pm}_{\alpha}\equiv\left\langle\left\lfloor(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}\right\rfloor\right\rangle.

(3.19)

The averaging operator
\left\lceil\ldots\right\rceil
does not appear because the galaxy sample is not involved in
(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}
. Since the latter is a combination of the
(++)

3.4
Angular power spectra

In modern cosmology, the properties of the matter distribution are commonly expressed in Fourier space, with the matter power spectrum, rather than in real space with the two-point correlation function. In that context, it is useful to express the angular power spectra associated with the correlation functions
(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L})^{\pm}(\vartheta;\ldots)
,
(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E})^{\pm}(\vartheta;\ldots)
and
\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta;\ldots)
in order to make cosmological predictions.

3.4.1
Spherical-harmonic decomposition

The expectation values of the three correlation functions
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
, and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
considered here involve three distinct fields on the two-sphere:
\delta(\hat{\bm{u}},z)

\gamma(\hat{\bm{u}};z),\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}(\hat{\bm{u}};\bm{Z})

(3.42)

and similarly for any other scalar field, such as the lensing potentials
\Psi
and
\Psi_{\mathrm{LOS}}
. As for the shear fields, we must use spin-weighted spherical harmonics
7
7

7

Spin-weighted spherical harmonics are obtained by successive eth differentiation of the usual spherical harmonics according to

{}_{s}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\bm{u}})=\sqrt{\frac{(\ell-s)!}{(\ell+s)}}\,\eth^{s}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\bm{u}})\ .

(3.43)

\gamma(\hat{\bm{u}};z)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\gamma_{\ell m}(z)\,{}_{2}Y_{\ell m}(\hat{\bm{u}})\ ,\qquad\gamma_{\ell m}(z)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{2}}\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}\;{}_{2}Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\hat{\bm{u}})\,\gamma(\hat{\bm{u}};z)\ ;

(3.44)

and similarly for
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}(\hat{\bm{u}};\bm{Z})
, thereby defining harmonic coefficients
\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\ell m}(\bm{Z})
.

Because
\gamma
derives from the scalar lensing potential
\Psi
via

\gamma_{\ell m}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{(\ell+2)!}{(\ell-2)!}}\,\Psi_{\ell m}(z)\ ,

and similarly for the LOS shear and LOS potential.

We can then define the angular power spectrum for any two fields

(which may be identical) in our problem, regardless of their spin, as

\left\langle f_{\ell m}\,g^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\Delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\Delta_{mm^{\prime}}C^{fg}_{\ell}\ .

(3.46)

The Krönecker symbols
8
8

8

\delta

\Delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}},\Delta_{mm^{\prime}}
in the above are due to statistical isotropy, i.e. the fact that, in real space, the two-point correlation of
f,g

4
Uncertainties and covariance matrix

In order to assess the detectability of the LL, LE and LP correlations, we must evaluate the expected uncertainty on their measurements, i.e. the variance of the estimators defined in
eqs.

3.7
,
3.12
and
3.14
. Since the ultimate goal will be to perform Fisher forecasts, we shall go one step further and calculate the full covariance matrix of our new observables, whose diagonal elements are precisely the desired uncertainties.

In this work, we have chosen to conduct an analytical, rather than numerical, evaluation of the covariance matrix. Albeit technically challenging, this approach has the advantage of producing results that are more easily adapted to changes in the cosmological and observational setup. In addition, analytical results are more directly interpretable, which is particularly advantageous for optimising the data-analysis strategy.

4.1
Definition of the covariance matrix

We are interested here in estimators of the two-point correlation functions of three classes of observables: LOS shear, apparent ellipticity and positions of galaxies. These estimators all take the form
(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}
, where
\mathrm{O}_{1},\mathrm{O}_{2}\in\{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}},\mathrm{E}_{A}^{\mathrm{p}},\mathrm{P}_{A}\}
and
\alpha
denotes an angular bin. The covariance of any two such measurements is defined as

\mathrm{Cov}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\beta}\right]\equiv\left\langle\left\lceil\left\lfloor(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\beta}\right\rfloor\right\rceil\right\rangle-(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2})_{\alpha}(\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4})_{\beta}\ ,

(4.1)

with
(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2})_{\alpha}
the expectation value of the estimator
(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}
. The covariance
matrix
is the symmetric matrix formed by the covariance of every pair of estimators. The diagonal elements of the covariance matrix then correspond to the variance of the estimators,

\mathrm{Var}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}\right]=\mathrm{Cov}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}\right].

(4.2)

We shall consider this variance as the (squared) uncertainty on measurements of the correlation function
\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}
in the
\alpha
th
angular bin.

The remainder of this subsection is dedicated to the actual calculation of the covariance matrix of the
6\times 2
pt correlation scheme.

4.2
Technical subtlety: coincident indices

There is an important technical subtlety that explains why the calculation of covariances is more complicated than that of the expectation value of the correlation functions. Readers who are only interested in a ready-to-use recipe may directly skip to
section

4.3
.

We have already calculated, in
sections

3.3
and
3.4
, the expectation values of our estimators. The remaining quantity to be evaluated in
eq.

4.1
is, therefore, the first term on the right-hand side, which generally takes the form

\left\langle\left\lceil\left\lfloor(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\beta}\right\rfloor\right\rceil\right\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{1}N_{2}N_{3}N_{4}}\sum_{\mu=1}^{N_{1}}\sum_{\nu=1}^{N_{2}}\sum_{\rho=1}^{N_{3}}\sum_{\sigma=1}^{N_{4}}W^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}W^{\beta}_{\rho\sigma}\left\langle\left\lceil\left\lfloor m^{1}_{\mu\nu}m^{2}_{\nu\mu}m^{3}_{\rho\sigma}m^{4}_{\sigma\rho}\right\rfloor\right\rceil\right\rangle,

(4.3)

where
N_{I}
is the number of objects (galaxies or lenses) relevant to observable
\mathrm{O}_{I}\in\{\mathrm{L},\mathrm{E}_{A},\mathrm{P}_{A}\}
, while
m^{I}_{\mu\nu}\in\{\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\mu\nu},\varepsilon_{\mu\nu},1\}
is the measurement associated with
\mathrm{O}_{I}
, made in the direction
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu}
and paired with a second direction
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\nu}
to form the local basis. When
\mathrm{O}_{I}=\mathrm{P}
, galaxy-sample averaging
\left\lceil\ldots\right\rceil
eventually replaces
m^{I}_{\mu\nu}=1
with
m^{I}_{\mu\nu}=b\delta_{\mu}
.

The core of the calculation then consists in evaluating
\big\langle\big\lceil\big\lfloor m^{1}_{\mu\nu}m^{2}_{\nu\mu}m^{3}_{\rho\sigma}m^{4}_{\sigma\rho}\big\rfloor\big\rceil\big\rangle
. At first glance, this is a similar problem to the calculation of the LL, LE and LP expectation values performed in
sections

3.3.2
,
3.3.3
and
3.3.4
. The first step is to apply the sample-averaging operators so as to get

\left\langle\left\lceil\left\lfloor m^{1}_{\mu\nu}m^{2}_{\nu\mu}m^{3}_{\rho\sigma}m^{4}_{\sigma\rho}\right\rfloor\right\rceil\right\rangle\\
=\left[\prod_{i=1}^{L}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{i})\right]\left[\prod_{A=1}^{T}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\right]\left\langle m^{1}_{\mu\nu}m^{2}_{\nu\mu}m^{3}_{\rho\sigma}m^{4}_{\sigma\rho}\right\rangle.

(4.4)

Now, in contrast with
sections

3.3.2
,
3.3.3
and
3.3.4
, not all the terms of the form (
4.4
), involved in the quadruple sum of
eq.

4.3
, are equivalent. This is because the window functions
W^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu},W^{\beta}_{\rho\sigma}
ensure that
\mu\neq\nu
and
\rho\neq\sigma
, but they do not prevent the cases
\mu=\rho
,
\mu=\sigma
, etc. As a result, several indices in
eq.

4.4
may coincide, which affects the marginalisation scheme.

For example, when addressing
\mathrm{Var}\big[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{E}_{A}^{+}})_{\alpha}\big]
the calculation will feature the term
\big\langle\big\lfloor\big\lceil\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\varepsilon_{+ai}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+jb}\varepsilon_{+bj}\big\rceil\big\rfloor\big\rangle
. When all indices are distinct, sample averaging leads to

\displaystyle\left\langle\left\lfloor\left\lceil\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\varepsilon_{+ai}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+jb}\varepsilon_{+bj}\right\rceil\right\rfloor\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{i})\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{j}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{j})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{b}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{b})\left\langle\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\varepsilon_{+ai}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+jb}\varepsilon_{+bj}\right\rangle.

(4.5)

But if, say,
i=j
, then we end up with only three integrals, because the corresponding term only features three indices (
i,a,b
),

\displaystyle\left\langle\left\lfloor\left\lceil\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\varepsilon_{+ai}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ib}\varepsilon_{+bi}\right\rceil\right\rfloor\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{i}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{i})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{b}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{b})\left\langle\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ia}\varepsilon_{+ai}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ib}\varepsilon_{+bi}\right\rangle.

(4.6)

A key difference between
eq.

4.5
and
eq.

4.6
is that the latter has a non-zero contribution from the LOS noise
\eta^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{i}
, because the LOS shear is measured on the same lens
i
.

In summary, the evaluation of covariances is made more complicated by the presence of coincident indices in
eq.

4.3
. The calculation requires one to count the number of terms in the quadruple sum that have identical indices, and to treat those separately. Eventually, the terms whose indices are all different lead to
cosmic covariance
, while those with coinciding indices will lead to the contribution of
noise
and
sparsity covariance

[
18
]
, which may be seen as a generalisation of shot noise.

4.3
Recipe to calculate the covariance matrix

The above discussion emphasises that the analytical calculation of covariances is generally tedious, not to mention the fact that it must be repeated for all the combinations of
\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}
and
\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}
, with
\mathrm{O}_{I}\in\{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}},\mathrm{E}_{A}^{\mathrm{p}},\mathrm{P}_{A}\}
.

Fortunately, various shortcuts and patterns can be identified in the calculations, which eventually lead to a general recipe that is quite straightforward to use. We only present the recipe here; as a justification of its relevance, we present the explicit calculation of
\mathrm{Cov}\big[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{A}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B}})_{\beta}\big]
in appendix
A
.

4.3.1
The formula

The covariance of any pair of correlation functions reads

\mathrm{Cov}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\beta}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu}}{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\nu}}{\Omega}\;W^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho}}{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{\sigma}}{\Omega}\;W^{\beta}_{\rho\sigma}\\
\times\left[(\widetilde{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3}})_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}\,(\widetilde{\mathrm{O}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\nu\mu,\sigma\rho}+(\widetilde{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{4}})_{\mu\nu,\sigma\rho}\,(\widetilde{\mathrm{O}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}})_{\nu\mu,\rho\sigma}\right],

(4.7)

where the tilded correlation functions such as
\widetilde{(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3})}_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}\sim\big\langle m^{1}_{\mu\nu}m^{3}_{\rho\sigma}\big\rangle
correspond to those computed in
section

3.3
, with an
additional noise term
if
\mathrm{O}_{1}=\mathrm{O}_{3}
. The weight function
W^{\alpha}_{\mu\nu}
is defined in
eq.

3.10
.

Domain of validity

Equation

4.7
is valid under the following assumptions:

•

Gaussianity.
We assume that the metric perturbation
\Phi
can be treated as a Gaussian random field. All the observables considered here inherit that property, because they are linearly related to
\Phi
. We may then apply Isserlis’ theorem
[
31
]
to express four-point correlation functions in terms of two-point functions; this is why
eq.

4.7
takes the general form

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}[(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}),(\mathrm{O}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4})]

\displaystyle\sim\left\langle m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}m_{4}\right\rangle-\left\langle m_{1}m_{2}\right\rangle\left\langle m_{3}m_{4}\right\rangle

(4.8)

\displaystyle\sim(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3})(\mathrm{O}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{4})+(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{4})(\mathrm{O}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3})\ .

(4.9)

•

Pseudo full sky.
In the calculation of the covariance matrix, some terms involve integrals over directions
\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\nu},\ldots
that vanish in full sky due to the presence of trigonometric functions of their relative azimuthal angles. For
\mathcal{F}\neq\mathcal{S}^{2}
, such terms are not exactly zero near the boundaries of
\mathcal{F}
. We choose to neglect such boundary terms.

•

Large samples.
The full calculation of the covariance matrix features terms that are integrals of, e.g.,
(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3})(\mathrm{O}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{4})
– without noise – divided by the lens or galaxy sample size,
L,G\gg 1
. It is safe to neglect such terms.

4.3.2
Using the formula

Let us now explain how to use
eq.

4.7
in three steps.

Step 1: Set indices up

Pick the required observables
\mathrm{O}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{O}_{4}
among
\{\mathrm{P}_{A},\mathrm{E}_{A}^{\mathrm{p}},\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\}
, where
p\in\{+,\times\}
denotes polarisation. Replace the generic indices
\mu,\nu,\rho,\sigma
in
eq.

4.7
with
a,b,c,d
or
i,j,k,l
depending on the nature (galaxy or lens) of the object that it refers to. Pairs of indices separated by a comma, like in
\widetilde{(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3})}_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}
, are associated with the observables following their order:
(\mu\nu)
is associated with
\mathrm{O}_{1}
and
(\rho\sigma)
with
\mathrm{O}_{3}
. These pairs of indices appear due to the construction of local bases for spin-two observables. When
\mathrm{O}=\mathrm{P}
, keep only the first index of the pair. For example,

\mathrm{Cov}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{\times}\mathrm{E}^{\times}_{B}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{D}})_{\beta}\right]=\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}}{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{b}}{\Omega}\;W^{\alpha}_{ib}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{k}}{\Omega}\int_{\mathcal{F}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\hat{\bm{u}}_{d}}{\Omega}\;W^{\beta}_{kd}\\
\times\left[(\widetilde{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{L}^{+}})_{ib,kd}\,(\widetilde{\mathrm{E}^{\times}_{B}\mathrm{P}_{D}})_{bi,d}+(\widetilde{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{D}})_{ib,d}\,(\widetilde{\mathrm{E}^{\times}_{B}\mathrm{L}^{+}})_{bi,kd}\right].

(4.10)

Step 2: Unfold pairs of indices

The correlation functions with pairs of indices are then expressed in terms of the regular correlation functions according to the following dictionary (we drop the tildes to alleviate notation here).

1.

Two spin-two observables:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{O}^{+}_{1}\mathrm{O}^{+}_{3})_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{+}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\cos 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\cos 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{O}_{1}^{\times}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{\times}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\sin 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\sin 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})\ ,

(4.11)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{O}^{+}_{1}\mathrm{O}^{\times}_{3})_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{+}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\cos 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\sin 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{O}_{1}^{\times}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{\times}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\sin 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\cos 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})\ ,

(4.12)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{O}^{\times}_{1}\mathrm{O}^{\times}_{3})_{\mu\nu,\rho\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{+}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\sin 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\sin 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{O}_{1}^{\times}\mathrm{O}_{3}^{\times}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\cos 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\cos 2(\psi_{\rho\mu}-\psi_{\rho\sigma})\ .

(4.13)

2.

One spin-two observable with one spin-zero observable:

\displaystyle(\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3})_{\mu\nu,\rho}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\,\cos 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\ ,

(4.14)

\displaystyle(\mathrm{O}_{1}^{\times}\mathrm{O}_{3})_{\mu\nu,\rho}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{O}_{1}^{+}\mathrm{O}_{3}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})\,\sin 2(\psi_{\mu\rho}-\psi_{\mu\nu})\ .

(4.15)

3.

Two spin-zero observables:

(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3})_{\mu,\rho}=\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{3}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{\mu},\hat{\bm{u}}_{\rho})
.

Step 3: Substitute the elementary building blocks

After step 2, the covariance is fully expressed in terms of trigonometric functions and the following building blocks, which are redshift-integrated correlation functions, possibly with an additional noise term. The final result is obtained by substituting in their explicit expressions:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{i},\hat{\bm{u}}_{j})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}_{i}\;p(\bm{Z}_{i})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}_{j}\;p(\bm{Z}_{j})\,\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}(\vartheta_{ij};\bm{Z}_{i},\bm{Z}_{j})+\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}}{2n_{\mathrm{L}}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{j})\ ,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{B}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{i},\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}_{i}\;p(\bm{Z}_{i})\int\mathrm{d}z_{b}\;p_{B}(z_{b})\,\mathrm{L}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}(\vartheta_{ib};\bm{Z}_{i},z_{b})\ ,

(4.17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{i},\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}_{i}\;p(\bm{Z}_{i})\int\mathrm{d}z_{b}\;p_{B}(z_{b})\,\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta_{ib};\bm{Z}_{i},z_{b})\ ,

(4.18)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{A}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{B}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{a},\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}z_{a}\;p_{A}(z_{a})\int\mathrm{d}z_{b}\;p_{B}(z_{b})\,\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}(\vartheta_{ab};z_{a},z_{b})+\frac{\delta_{AB}\sigma_{A}^{2}}{2n_{A}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{a}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})\ ,

(4.19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{E}^{+}_{A}\mathrm{P}_{B}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{a},\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}z_{a}\;p_{A}(z_{a})\int\mathrm{d}z_{b}\;p_{B}(z_{b})\,\mathrm{E}^{+}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta_{ab};z_{a},z_{b})\ ,

(4.20)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathrm{P}_{A}\mathrm{P}_{B}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{a},\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}z_{a}\;p_{A}(z_{a})\int\mathrm{d}z_{b}\;p_{B}(z_{b})\,\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta_{ab};z_{a},z_{b})+\frac{\delta_{AB}}{n_{A}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\hat{\bm{u}}_{a}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{b})\ .

(4.21)

Each of the three tilded autocorrelation functions (
\widetilde{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}},\widetilde{\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}},\widetilde{\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P}}
) exhibits a Dirac-delta term. Those
noise terms
effectively account for the cases of coincident indices in the sum of
eq.

4.3
, which were discussed in
section

4.2
.

In
\widetilde{\mathrm{P}_{A}\mathrm{P}_{A}}
, this is the usual shot noise, or Poisson noise, which is inversely proportional to the surface density
n_{A}
of galaxies in the
A
th
tomographic bin. In
\widetilde{\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{A}\mathrm{E}^{\mathrm{p}}_{A}}
, the noise term is proportional to
\sigma_{A}^{2}
, which is the variance of the apparent ellipticity of galaxies in that bin,

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma})\,\big\langle|\varepsilon|^{2}\big\rangle

(4.22)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma})\left[|\varepsilon_{0}|^{2}+\big\langle|\gamma|^{2}\big\rangle\right]

(4.23)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int\mathrm{d}z\;f_{A}(z)\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\varepsilon_{0}\;p(\varepsilon_{0};z)\,|\varepsilon_{0}|^{2}}_{\text{shape noise}~\sigma_{\varepsilon_{0},A}^{2}}+\underbrace{\int\mathrm{d}z\;f_{A}(z)\,(\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E})^{+}(0;z,z)}_{\text{shear variance}}.

(4.24)

We see that
\sigma_{A}^{2}
receives a contribution from intrinsic ellipticities, usually referred to as shape noise, as well as a contribution from the variance of the weak-lensing shear. The latter contribution, which is absent from the standard weak-lensing literature, has been recently introduced in ref.
[
18
]
as
sparsity variance
. It comes from the fact that cosmic-shear measurements are performed on a finite sample of galaxies that are randomly distributed in the sky; this leads to an uncertainty on estimates of the
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}
correlation function that would persist even if we could perfectly measure the shear on individual galaxies.

In cosmic shear, sparsity variance leads to a sub-percent contribution to the covariance matrix, because
|\gamma|\ll|\varepsilon_{0}|
. The situation is quantitatively different for the LOS shear. The noise term in
\widetilde{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}}
is proportional to the variance of the
measured
LOS shear,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}\;p(\bm{\Lambda})\big\langle|\hat{\gamma}_{\mathrm{LOS}}|^{2}\big\rangle

(4.25)

\displaystyle=\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}\;p(\bm{\Lambda})\left[|\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}|^{2}+\big\langle|\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}|^{2}\big\rangle\right]

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}\;p(\bm{Z})\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}\;p(\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}};\bm{Z})\,|\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}|^{2}}_{\text{LOS shear measurement noise}~\sigma^{2}_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}}+\underbrace{\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\bm{Z}\;p(\bm{Z})\,(\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L})^{+}(0;\bm{Z},\bm{Z})}_{\text{LOS shear variance}~\sigma^{2}_{\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}}}\ ,

(4.27)

which combines the variance of the noise on individual LOS shear measurements,
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}
, with the variance of the LOS signal,
\sigma_{\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}}
. Since we expect
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}\sim\sigma_{\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}}
, sparsity variance must absolutely be taken into account in the covariance matrix of the
6\times 2
pt correlation scheme.

When dealing with forecasts,
\sigma_{A}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}
must be estimated by assuming the noise properties and a cosmological model. Eventually, these quantities will be measured directly from the data.

4.4
Result and comments on the sources of uncertainty

We calculated the full covariance matrix of the
6\times 2
pt scheme using the recipe presented in
section

4.3
. Due to the lengthiness of the resulting expressions, we do not report them here. Instead, they are presented in the
Encyclopædia Covariancis

, a separate document stored in a dedicated
public Github repository
.

The covariance of two correlation-function estimators is generally composed of three types of contributions:
\mathrm{Cov}={\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathrm{CCov}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}
.

•

Cosmic covariance
,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathrm{CCov}}
, is caused by the intrinsically correlated nature of the physical fields at play. It is the limit of
\mathrm{Cov}
if we could observe an infinite number of galaxies and lenses (
L,G\to\infty
), i.e.
eq.

4.7
without tildes on the right-hand side. The associated variance,
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,0}\mathrm{CCov}}(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}},\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})
, is an irreducible uncertainty on
\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}
, due to the fact that we observe a single realisation of the cosmic fields on a finite sky.

•

Noise covariance
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
, is due to (i) the uncertainty
\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}
on individual measurements of the LOS shear; and (ii) the intrinsic ellipticity
\varepsilon_{0}
of galaxies (shape noise).

•

Sparsity covariance
,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}
, comes from the fact that we observe a finite number of galaxies and strong lenses with random positions. It may be seen as a generalisation of shot noise
[
18
]
. This contribution would persist even if galaxies were intrinsically spherical and if we could measure
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
with an arbitrarily large precision.

In practice,
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}
always come together, via
\sigma_{A}^{2}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}
defined in
eqs.

4.22
and
4.25
.
12
12

12

Some variances, such as
\mathrm{Var}(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{L}^{+}})_{\alpha}
– see eq. (11) in the
Encyclopædia Covariancis

– contain terms proportional to
\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{4}=(\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}^{2}+\sigma_{\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}}^{2})^{2}
. We choose to count the cross term,
2\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}^{2}\sigma_{\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}}^{2}
, as a part of
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
, so that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}
represents the contribution that survives in the absence of noise (
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}=0
). The same is true for terms proportional to
\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}\sigma_{A}^{2}
or
\sigma_{A}^{4}
. This convention will tend to boost the contribution of noise with respect to that of sparsity.

The relative importance of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
is driven by the signal-to-noise ratio of individual observations. For standard cosmic shear,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}\ll{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
because the intrinsic ellipticity of a galaxy is typically larger than the weak-lensing shear; in other words, the second term on the right-hand side of
eq.

4.24
may be neglected. But for the LOS shear,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0.5}\mathrm{SCov}}\sim{\color[rgb]{0,0.5,0}\mathrm{NCov}}
because the uncertainty on
\gamma_{\mathrm{LOS}}
for a single strong lens is comparable to the expected signal. Sparsity covariance is therefore a crucial concept in this work.

is computed using

for

6
Conclusion

The line-of-sight (LOS) shear is a physical quantity which summarises the perturbations to a strong-lens system caused by cosmological inhomogeneities between the source, the main lens, and the observer. This quantity is expected to be directly measurable in strong-lensing images, independently of the properties of the main lens. As stage-IV galaxy surveys, such as
Euclid
and LSST, are expected to detect
\mathcal{O}(10^{5})
strong lenses in the near future, the LOS shear becomes a viable cosmological probe, alongside galaxy shapes and positions.

In this article, we extended the standard
3\times 2
pt correlation method, built from galaxy ellipticities (
\mathrm{E}
) and positions (
\mathrm{P}
), into a
6\times 2
pt correlation scheme comprising the LOS shear of strong lenses (
\mathrm{L}
):
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
,
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{E}\mathrm{P}
,
\mathrm{P}\mathrm{P}
. Besides the added information,
\mathrm{L}
will be subject to different systematics than
\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{P}
, thereby mitigating known cosmic-shear limitations (e.g. intrinsic alignments).

We defined estimators for the three new correlation functions involving the LOS shear,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
, and computed their expectation value (i.e. the expected signal) in a
\Lambda
CDM cosmology. We then established and applied a general method to analytically calculate the full covariance matrix of the
6\times 2
pt correlation scheme. The results are explicitly presented in the
Encyclopædia Covariancis

, and are implemented numerically in a publicly available Python code,
loscov
. Analytical results have the double advantage of being flexible and easy to interpret. In particular, they allow us to understand the relative contribution of cosmic, sparsity and noise covariances in the final result.

For concreteness, we considered two observational scenarios. In the optimistic (but realistic) scenario, the LOS shear would be measured on
L=10^{5}
lenses, i.e.
60\,\%
of the expected
Euclid
strong-lensing sample, with a mean uncertainty of
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}=0.05
, which is about
3.5
times larger than in the proof of concept
[
24
]
. In this scenario, all three new correlation functions,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
, are detectable with very high SNR. In the conservative scenario, with ten times fewer lenses
(L=10^{4})
and an uncertainty twice as large (
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}=0.1
), only
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
are detectable, still with high SNR. More generally, a definitive detection of the
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
cross-correlation functions could be achieved with only a few hundred lenses if
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}=0.05
. Even if
\sigma_{\eta_{\mathrm{LOS}}}\approx 1
, i.e. two orders of magnitude larger than the expected uncertainty, the cross-correlations will still be detectable with the expected
Euclid
lens sample.

The work presented here was focused on theory and methodology. It is the first step towards a cosmological forecast which will be the topic of a subsequent article. In particular, we shall quantify the constraining power of the
6\times 2
pt method on cosmological parameters
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\sigma_{8}
, and assess the gain from adding the LOS shear to the usual
3\times 2
pt scheme. Particular attention will be paid to the mitigation of systematics such as galaxy intrinsic alignments.

Appendix A
Detailed calculation of
\mathrm{Cov}[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{D}})_{\beta}]

In this appendix, we present a complete and detailed calculation of

\mathrm{Cov}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B}})_{\alpha},(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{D}})_{\beta}\right]\\
=\frac{1}{L^{2}G_{B}G_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{G}_{B}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}\left\langle\left\lfloor\left\lceil W^{\alpha}_{ib}W^{\beta}_{kd}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ib}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+kd}\right\rceil\right\rfloor\right\rangle-(\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B})_{\alpha}(\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{D})_{\beta}\ .

(A.1)

Besides serving as an example of how such a derivation is performed, it should also demonstrate why the recipe presented in
section

4.3
works. The core of the calculation consists in evaluating the expectation value
\big\langle\big\lfloor\big\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\big\rceil\big\rfloor\big\rangle
of the summand
S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\equiv W^{\alpha}_{ib}W^{\beta}_{kd}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+ib}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+kd}
.

A.1
Galaxy-sample average
\left\lceil\ldots\right\rceil

We first apply the galaxy-sample averaging operator,

\displaystyle\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil=\left[\prod_{A=1}^{T}\prod_{a\in\mathcal{G}_{A}}\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{a}\;p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma}_{a})\right]S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\ .

(A.2)

Here, contrary to
section

3.3
, the galaxy indices
b
and
d
can be equal. If they are, only one integral over
\bm{\Gamma}_{b}=\bm{\Gamma}_{d}
remains; this is only possible if the bins are identical,
B=D
. If the indices differ, then we must keep two separate integrals over
\bm{\Gamma}_{b}
and
\bm{\Gamma}_{d}
. In other words:

\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil=(1-\Delta_{bd})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\;p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{4}\;p_{D}(\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,S_{i2k4}^{\alpha\beta}+\Delta_{bd}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\;p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\,S_{i2k2}^{\alpha\beta}\ ,

(A.3)

where
\Delta_{bd}
is the Krönecker symbol.

When summing over the galaxies in the bins, this yields

\displaystyle\sum_{b\in\mathcal{G}_{B}}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil

\displaystyle=\left(G_{B}G_{D}-\delta_{BD}G_{D}\right)\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\;p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{4}\;p_{D}(\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,S_{i2k4}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\quad+\delta_{BD}G_{B}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\;p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\,S_{i2k2}^{\alpha\beta}

(A.4)

\displaystyle=G_{B}G_{D}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{4}\;\tilde{p}_{BD}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,S_{i2k4}^{\alpha\beta}\ .

(A.5)

where we have introduced the effective PDF

\tilde{p}_{BD}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},\bm{\Gamma}_{4})=\left(1-\frac{\delta_{BD}}{G_{B}}\right)p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})p_{D}(\bm{\Gamma}_{4})+\frac{\delta_{BD}}{G_{B}}\,p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}-\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\ ,

(A.6)

which is indeed normalised to unity. The
\delta_{BD}/G_{B}\ll 1
term in the first prefactor may be neglected in practice.

A.2
Lens sample average
\left\lfloor\ldots\right\rfloor

Similarly, taking the lens-sample average of
\big\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\big\rceil
yields

\displaystyle\left\lfloor\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil\right\rfloor

\displaystyle=\left[\prod_{i^{\prime}=1}^{L}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{i^{\prime}}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{i^{\prime}})\right]\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil

(A.7)

\displaystyle=(1-\Delta_{ik})\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{3}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{3})\,\left\lceil S_{1b3d}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil+\Delta_{ik}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\;p(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})\,\left\lceil S_{1b1d}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil,

(A.8)

where
\Delta_{ik}
accounts for the possibility of equal indices. When summing over all lenses in the sample, we thus get

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\lfloor\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil\right\rfloor=L^{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{3}\;\tilde{p}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{3})\,\left\lfloor\left\lceil S_{1b3d}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil\right\rfloor\ ,

(A.9)

\displaystyle\text{with}\qquad\tilde{p}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{3})\equiv\left(1-\frac{1}{L}\right)p(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})p(\bm{\Lambda}_{3})+\frac{1}{L}\,p(\bm{\Lambda}_{1})\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1}-\bm{\Lambda}_{3})\ .

(A.10)

We may take
1-1/L\approx 1
in the first prefactor.

A.3
Cosmic average
\left\langle\ldots\right\rangle

Putting together the results of the previous two subsections, and applying the cosmic-averaging operator, we end up with the following expression

\frac{1}{L^{2}G_{B}G_{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{G}_{B}}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{d\in\mathcal{G}_{D}}\left\langle\left\lfloor\left\lceil S_{ibkd}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rceil\right\rfloor\right\rangle\\
=\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{1}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{2}\int\mathrm{d}^{6}\bm{\Lambda}_{3}\int\mathrm{d}^{5}\bm{\Gamma}_{4}\;\tilde{p}(\bm{\Lambda}_{1},\bm{\Lambda}_{3})\left\langle\tilde{p}_{BD}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,S_{1234}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rangle.

(A.11)

Extracting the density contrast

Note that, in
eq.

A.11
, we have kept
\tilde{p}_{BD}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},\bm{\Gamma}_{4})

To be more explicit,

\displaystyle\left\langle\tilde{p}_{BD}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2},\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,S_{1234}^{\alpha\beta}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\delta_{BD}}{G_{B}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}-\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,\frac{1}{\Omega}\,f_{B}(z_{2})\,p(\varepsilon_{0,2};z_{2})\,W_{12}^{\alpha}W_{32}^{\beta}\left\langle(1+\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{2})\,\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+32}\right\rangle,

(A.13)

\displaystyle=\left\langle p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\right\rangle\left\langle p_{D}(\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\right\rangle W_{12}^{\alpha}W_{32}^{\beta}\left[{\color[rgb]{0.5625,0,0}\left\langle\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+34}\right\rangle}+\left\langle\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{2}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+34}\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{4}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\delta_{BD}}{G_{B}}\,\delta_{\mathrm{D}}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2}-\bm{\Gamma}_{4})\,\left\langle p_{B}(\bm{\Gamma}_{2})\right\rangle\,W_{12}^{\alpha}W_{32}^{\beta}\left\langle\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\hat{\gamma}^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+32}\right\rangle,

(A.14)

with
\left\langle p_{A}(\bm{\Gamma})\right\rangle=\Omega^{-1}f_{A}(z)p(\varepsilon_{0};z)
the cosmic-averaged PDF of the galaxy parameters, from which
\delta
has disappeared. The term indicated in
red

A.5
Polarisation angles

The last step of the calculation consists in extracting the misaligned polarisation angles from the integrand of

A.17

\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{L}^{+}

13

\displaystyle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathrm{Re}\left[\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{1}\mathrm{e}^{-2\mathrm{i}\psi_{12}}\right]

(A.18)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left[(\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+13}+\mathrm{i}\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times 13})\,\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}(\psi_{13}-\psi_{12})}\right]

(A.19)

(A.20)

and similarly for
\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+34}

\left\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+34}\right\rangle=\overbrace{\left\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+13}\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+31}\right\rangle}^{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{L}^{+}(\vartheta_{13};\bm{Z}_{1},\bm{Z}_{3})}\cos 2(\psi_{13}-\psi_{12})\cos 2(\psi_{31}-\psi_{34})\\

(A.21)

where we used
\big\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+13}\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{\times 31}\big\rangle=0

\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+12}\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{4}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+14}\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{4}\right\rangle\cos 2(\psi_{14}-\psi_{12})=\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta_{14};\bm{Z}_{1},z_{4})\,\cos 2(\psi_{14}-\psi_{12})\ ,

(A.22)

\displaystyle\left\langle\gamma^{\mathrm{LOS}}_{+34}\delta^{\mathrm{g}}_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}(\vartheta_{32};\bm{Z}_{3},z_{2})\,\cos 2(\psi_{32}-\psi_{34})\ .

(A.23)

The general rules for such conversions are summarised in

Appendix B
Angular binning procedure

Let
\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}(\vartheta)
be one of the correlation functions considered in this work. The SNR of that correlation function in the angular bin
[\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta_{\alpha+1})
is defined as

\text{SNR}(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2};\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta_{\alpha+1})\equiv\frac{\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{\Omega_{\alpha}}\int_{\vartheta_{\alpha}}^{\vartheta_{\alpha+1}}\mathrm{d}\vartheta\;\vartheta\,\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}(\vartheta)}{\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\big[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}\big]}}\ ,

(B.1)

with
\Omega_{\alpha}=\pi(\vartheta_{\alpha+1}^{2}-\vartheta_{\alpha}^{2})
. Since the full expression of
\mathrm{Var}\big[(\widehat{\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}})_{\alpha}\big]
is computationally expensive to evaluate, we choose to crudely approximate it by its strictly diagonal element (see
Encyclopædia Covariancis

),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{4}}{L^{2}}\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{\alpha}}\ ,

(B.2)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}_{B}})^{\pm}_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}}{L}\frac{\sigma_{B}^{2}}{G_{B}}\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{\alpha}}\ ,

(B.3)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[(\widehat{\mathrm{L}^{+}\mathrm{P}_{B}})_{\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2}\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{LOS}}^{2}}{L}\frac{1}{G_{B}}\frac{\Omega}{\Omega_{\alpha}}\ .

(B.4)

This approximation allows for a quick evaluation of the
SNR
function, because it does not require us to evaluate any integral. In practice, the approximation is acceptable in the case of
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
, but it is poor for
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
, especially for large values of
\vartheta
.
Hence,
eqs.

B.2
,
B.3
and
B.4
should not be used if one really cares about the uncertainty on
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
. It should be considered to be a first step towards more refined binning procedures in the future.

B.1
Binning algorithm given a target SNR

Suppose that we want all the angular bins of
\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2}
to have the same SNR,
R
. Starting from
\vartheta_{1}=0
, we may proceed iteratively and determine
\vartheta_{\alpha+1}
from
\vartheta_{\alpha}
as follows:

1.

Evaluate
M_{\alpha}\equiv\max_{\vartheta>\vartheta_{\alpha}}\text{SNR}(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2};\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta)
.

2.

If
M_{\alpha}\geq R
, then numerically solve
\text{SNR}(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2};\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta_{\alpha+1})=R
for
\theta_{\alpha+1}
. The limits of the
\alpha
th
bin are then fixed and we may consider the next one.

Repeat steps 1 and 2 as long as
M_{\alpha}\geq R
. If
M_{\alpha}<R
, then no bin starting from
\vartheta_{\alpha}
would have enough signal to fulfil the target requirement. We may then either stop the procedure here, or add a last bin
[\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}}]
with
\text{SNR}(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2};\vartheta_{\alpha},\vartheta_{\mathrm{max}})=M_{\alpha}
, if
M_{\alpha}\geq M_{\mathrm{min}}=2.5
.

B.2
Determining the target SNR

Our baseline choice is
R=R_{\mathrm{min}}=8
. However, for high-SNR observables such as
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
and
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
in the optimistic scenario (
section

5.1
), this choice results in a very large number of angular bins. We choose to avoid this by imposing a maximum number of bins,
N_{\mathrm{max}}=20
, and increasing the target SNR accordingly.

In practice, we found that for all the observables considered here (
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{L}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{E}
,
\mathrm{L}\mathrm{P}
), the number of angular bins,
N
, and the SNR in each bin,
R
, roughly follow the empirical relationship

N(R)\approx 2\left(\frac{M_{1}}{R}\right)^{2},

(B.5)

where
M_{1}=\max_{\vartheta>0}\text{SNR}(\mathrm{O}_{1}\mathrm{O}_{2};0,\vartheta)
as defined above. In order to ensure both conditions
R\geq R_{\mathrm{min}}
and
N\lesssim N_{\mathrm{max}}
, we thus fix the target SNR as

R=\max\left[R_{\mathrm{min}},M_{1}\left(0.5\,N_{\mathrm{max}}\right)^{-1/2}\right].

(B.6)

Since this whole procedure relies on the approximations of
eqs.

B.2
,
B.3
and
B.4
, the actual SNR in each bin can turn out to be quite far from the target.

Appendix C
Monte Carlo integration and error analysis

Consider the definite integral

I=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{d}^{m}\bm{x}\;f(\bm{x}),

(C.1)

of a real-valued function
f
over
m
variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{m})
, within the integration domain
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We denote with
V=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\mathrm{d}^{m}\bm{x}
the volume of
\mathcal{D}
.

I
can be efficiently estimated with a Monte Carlo integral. Drawing a random sample of
N
points
\bm{x}_{1},\bm{x}_{2},\ldots,\bm{x}_{N}
uniformly within the domain
\mathcal{D}
, we can approximate the mean of the function over this sample as

\langle f\rangle_{N}\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum^{N}_{i=1}f(\bm{x}_{i})\ ,

(C.2)

from which our integral can be approximated as

I\approx I_{N}=V\langle f\rangle_{N}\ .

(C.3)

The error in
I_{N}
can be estimated from the sample variance of the
N
evaluations of the function,
f(\bm{x}_{1}),f(\bm{x}_{2}),...,f(\bm{x}_{N})
, as

\mathrm{err}(I_{N})=\sqrt{\frac{V^{2}}{N}\,s^{2}}\ ,\qquad s^{2}=\frac{1}{N-1}\sum^{N}_{i=1}\left[f(\bm{x}_{i})-\langle f\rangle_{N}\right]^{2}.

(C.4)

Each element in the covariance matrices presented in
figs.

9
and
11
takes the form of a sum of a cosmic, sparsity and noise contribution, each of which involving one or more integrals over multiple dimensions. The total numerical uncertainty of each element is therefore propagated from the uncertainty arising from each integral. Because the cosmic variance generally involves integrals over five dimensions, rather than four in the case of sparsity and noise, larger samples are generally needed to achieve a similar fractional uncertainty.

We use samples with
10^{6}
points for noise/sparsity-covariance integrals, and
10^{7}
for cosmic-covariance integrals. The slowest single redshift “block” in the matrices in
figs.

9
and
11
then takes about one day to compute; parallelising to process each block simultaneously means the full covariance matrix can be evaluated numerically on the same timescale. Further improvements with more sophisticated integrators and parallelisation schemes are possible, but we leave this for a future project.

We plot the errors corresponding to
fig.

9
in
fig.

13
, and those corresponding to
fig.

11
in
fig.

14
. From these figures, we see that, while there are elements for which the numerical uncertainty may be comparable to or even larger than the covariance in that block, it is always orders of magnitude smaller than the corresponding variance, and so these errors are not expected to have a perceivable impact on the relative strength of these elements, or in applications such as Fisher forecasting.
