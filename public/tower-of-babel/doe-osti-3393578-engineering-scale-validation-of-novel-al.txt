Executive Summary:

The purpose of this project was to advance and scale the algae-based technology that Helios-NRG has
been developing to capture CO2 from the flue gas of coal-burning power plants and convert it into high-
value products. To encourage the process to be commercial and be adoptable, it is essential that the net
cost of CO2 be close to zero or slightly revenue positive. Two methods to achieve this are reducing the
cost of operations through efficiencies and selling the products derived from conversion. Helios and its
project partners have accomplished the goals of making the technology more scalable and substantially
less costly, with a defined pathway for commercialization.

The technology relies on a cultivation process of a multi-stage continuous flow system (MSC) which
simultaneously has high algae productivity and CO2 capture efficiency. Extensive work was done to
improve and increase algae productivity in the MSC cultivation process including: identifying and
mitigating harmful bio-contaminants, improving CO2 dissolution with template flue gas and culture
mixing, and developing a new control system that modulates parameters based on environmental changes.
During field testing, the implemented improvements resulted in exceeding the project targets of 80%
carbon capture efficiency and a 10% increase in productivity.

Two types of high value products for revenue generation were tested at scale – nutraceuticals and a high
protein fish meal replacement for fish feed. For the nutraceutical market which is the highest value for an
algal derived ingredient, four classes of nutraceuticals were investigated with at least two species of algae
for each. Several of these classes were subsequently excluded due to their low content in the algae or their
low market price. Two nutraceutical classes were fully evaluated with algae species H-0816 and H-1601
identified as good sources for future commercialization. To scale production, methods to increase
cultivation volume, an induction process (if needed), and preparation methods for the crude extract stage
were developed. Both of these products were collected from the algae and enriched to the point in where
the current market value exceed the project goal of $700 per kilogram of nutraceutical product.

Fish meal used in feed for farm-raised trout and salmon is not a sustainable source and can have other
issues such as heavy metal contamination. To find a substitute, high protein, intact algae cells were
studied as an ingredient in feed blends with fish feeding trials using Rainbow Trout. Proximate analysis
was conducted to identify two species of algae, H-1903 and H-0116, based on having a protein content
similar to fish meal. The amino acid and fatty acid contents were also compared. Initial feed blends with
algae were made, and the nutrient content was verified against the control feed blend with fish meal. A
small-scale trial was done to measure digestibility and plans were made for feed blends to be tested at a

larger scale. Along with the control diet, five diets were manufactured with algae replacement of fish meal
from 25 to 100 %. Each of the six feeds was tested in triplicate in special designed tanks similar to larger
tanks used in commercial fish farming. It was found that all the fish receiving the algae-based blends had
similar growth rates, health and filet content as those fed the control diet. Because of the ability to
produce algae in a clean and sustainable way, and because the fish meal is one of the most expensive
macro-ingredients in aquatic feeds, these results have great promise for the commercial development of
algae feed ingredients.

The project generated Life-cycle, Techno-economic, and Technology Gap analyses, along with a
Technology Maturation Plan. The technology was advanced from an initial Department of Energy
Technology Readiness Level (TRL) of 4 to a TRL of 6. Additionally, plans were described for going to
TRL 7 and beyond. The net cost of CO2 per ton was revenue positive, and the greenhouse gas emissions at
the end of the project were lower using algae as a feed blend compared to soybean or fish meal.

Introduction:
In this project, Helios-NRG continued the development of a novel, algae-based technology to capture CO2
from the effluent of coal power plants. In order to off-set the cost of capture, conversion of the algal
biomass to value-added products is required. The technology was designed for flue gas streams from coal
power plants post flue gas desulfurization (FGD), but could also be suitable for gas from natural gas-fired
plants and other industrial CO2 point sources. Cost-effectiveness of the technology stems from
operational efficiencies based on high CO2 utilization, exceptional algae productivity, continuous flow
operation, low-cost dewatering, nutrient/water cost reduction based on synergistic wastewater utilization,
and the generation of a variety of products based on animal feed, high-value nutraceuticals and biofuel. A
schematic of the overall process is shown in Figure 1. Note that biofuel production is not part of this
project and the animal feed focus is on fish feed.

The technology is anchored by the Multi-Stage, Continuous (MSC) flow process, where most of the
incoming CO2 from the post-FGD flue gas is captured and used beneficially to accelerate algae growth.
The concept of the MSC process was successfully demonstrated in a field test at the National Carbon
Capture Center (NCCC) in Wilsonville, AL. In this project, several advancements including the
development of a second-generation (2nd-Gen) automated control system which optimizes the operational
setpoints based on varying environmental conditions, a new bio-contamination mitigation protocol, and
an improved CO2 dissolution were integrated into the MSC system. An outdoor operation on template
coal burning power plant flue gas at the Helios outdoor growing facility was conducted to demonstrate the
overall MSC improvements. The MSC simultaneously enabled very high CO2 utilization (>80%), stable
operation, and high productivity (>10% improvement from project start).

A key feature of the MSC is the continuous algae harvesting, which can be dewatered to different degrees
based on the respective downstream product needs. The development of dewatering technology is not a
key focus of this project. However, Helios in past projects has actively developed a multi-step low-cost
algae dewatering technology capable of achieving algae of various concentrations. Learnings from the
algae dewatering and processing technology was adapted in this project.

At commercial scale, it is expected that the algae utilized for animal and human consumption would come
from an inter-stage of the MSC system where algae would be much lower in contaminants verses the
initial algae exposed to the post-FGD coal-based flue gas.

Initial studies focused on the development of a new algae-based fish feed because fish feed is the highest
valued animal feed product. Digestibility and palatability studies on rainbow trout fish were conducted
with various algae-based feed formulations, followed by a Recirculating Aquaculture System (RAS) field
test based on the preferred fish feed formulation to advance the technology closer to commercialization.
Fish growth rate, health and meat quality were monitored to determine efficacy of the algae-based fish
feed. Results of the fish feed trials from the completed RAS study are positive.

Nutraceutical development in this project focused on advancing two products and outlining a commercial
path with the potential to sell a product for >$700/kg.

Both the fish feed and nutraceutical product lines were successfully demonstrated and form the basis for
future work towards near-term commercialization. Finally, the Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA)
demonstrated that using the technology to make these products could generate positive revenue from
carbon capture, which could have great global benefits.

Task 2: Bio-contamination control strategy for algae culture
The objective of this task was to understand and mitigate harmful bio-contamination in large-scale algae
cultivation systems to improve productivity and operational stability. The project accomplished this by
(1) identifying the causes of contamination using high-throughput DNA sequencing to characterize
microbial community structures and interactions (Milestone 2.1), (2) investigating surface coatings, to
reduce biofilm formation and chemical addition, to target harmful bio-contaminants, (3) evaluating the
use of municipal wastewater as a cost-effective replacement for commercial nitrogen (N) and phosphorus
(P) nutrients, and (4) investigating surface coatings to reduce biofilm formation. Based on these
findings, the work developed and validated mitigation strategies including modifications to culture
chemistry, targeted chemical and operational interventions, and anti-fouling materials to suppress specific
contaminants and enable long-term, productive algae cultivation with reduced reliance on commercial
nutrients. Bio-contamination and mitigation processes were validated and wastewater nutrient
utilization was demonstrated.

Subtask 2.1: High-throughput community structure analysis to monitor culture health.
High-throughput community structure analysis from MSC tanks was performed to monitor culture health.
Biomass culture samples were collected from pure culture inoculate and at various points during long-
term operation and analyzed using high-throughput DNA sequencing to measure community structures
and to monitor culture dynamics. Analysis targeted 16S rRNA gene for bacteria and the 18S rRNA gene
for eukaryotes. The initial rounds of DNA sequencing were completed, and the analysis led to
species/taxonomy identification and preliminary interactions between eukaryotes, bacteria, and algae.
Species linked to detrimental performance identified eukaryotic contaminants that were high-level
predators with adverse effects on culture performance. Specific chemicals were selected for control of
predators that were found contaminating algae cultures and will be tested for integration in Task 8.

Milestone 2.1 completed – Bio-contaminants identified related to poor culture health

Subtask 2.2: Pre-treatment of wastewater for nutrient use in algae culturing
A series of experiments was conducted using algal reactors for growth by utilizing wastewater as a
nutrient replacement for commercial nutrients. Local wastewater was obtained at different time points and
analyzed to determine any potential variations. Several pre-treatments were tested and compared to a
commercial nutrient mix (control) and the untreated wastewater to assess their effects on algae growth.
The results showed that all the wastewater (Figure 2), with and without pre-treatment promoted better
algae growth than the control nutrients, potentially due to the presence of additional organic carbon.
Wastewater did not contain significant levels of harmful compounds that could negatively affect algae
growth, eliminating the need for additional pre-treatment steps which would add cost. This finding is
particularly important for the economic viability of algae production, as it eliminates the need to purchase
nutrients at scale and may even result in credits since waste disposal costs are avoided. Here, UV treated
wastewater was shown to achieve biomass growth of similar or higher concentration when amended to
the same levels of N and P as media.

Subtask 2.3: Identify coatings to apply to MSC surfaces to mitigate biofilm development
Biofilms, which consist of algae and bio-contaminants, tend to attach to surfaces in algae photobioreactor
(PBR) tanks. These films can potentially harbor harmful agents that can adversely impact the health and
growth of the algae. Moreover, they can have a negative impact on the CO2 capture process by reducing
the amount of sunlight incident on the algae cultures, limiting their growth, increasing the gas transfer
resistance of CO2 contactors, and increasing compression costs.

Task 3: MSC operation for optimal utilization of varying environmental factors
The main objective of this task is to develop a new dynamic control system to improve the overall
performance of the algae-based CO2 capture technology. This dynamic control system will enable
automated operation whereby the operating conditions can be continuously varied for optimal function as
environmental factors vary over time. This improvement will overall enable optimal geospatial
translation, a particularly attractive feature for future commercialization.

Subtask 3.1: Calibrate MSC sensitivity to environmental variation and process parameters.
The objective of this subtask is to develop a first-of-a-kind automated control technology that tailors the
algae Multi-Stage Continuous (MSC) system’s operating conditions to continuously optimize for
environmental variation, enabling an overall improved performance for both algae productivity and CO2
capture efficiency.

In prior projects, Helios developed a proprietary algae model that predicts the impact of gas and liquid
flow operating setpoints on algae productivity and CO2 capture efficiency in MSC systems of different
configurations. However, these MSC operating conditions are based on the optimum performance and is
user set on the first day of operation. This results in the MSC system being operated sub-optimally on
days with differing environmental conditions, and thus, reducing overall algal MSC performance.

As part of the design and fabrication of the improved dynamic control system, several potential
operational strategies were conceptualized. Preliminary experiments under a controlled environment were
conducted to support the update of Helios’ proprietary model, improving the correlation between
environmental variation inputs and on algal growth. The controlled algae growth experiment was
conducted in excess feed CO2 and nutrients such that the algae growth rate would not be limited by either
of the two inputs.

The impact of various operational setpoints on the overall MSC performance (algae productivity and CO2
capture efficiency) were mapped. It is important to note that the prediction of the MSC performance
based on the varying operational parameters is based on a steady-state model, and while this does not
reflect actual performance, it provides a good indicator of the expected performance trends. The trend
corroborates that the operational setpoint on Day-1 is not optimal for the duration of the test, resulting in
poor performance.

Subtask 3.2: Develop control system to maximize performance with environmental variation.
Efforts in Subtask 3.1 led to the development of an algorithm that varies the MSC operational setpoints
with respect to the environmental variation. This proprietary algorithm will serve as the basis for the
improved control system (defined as 2nd-Gen Control System). When compared, the 2nd-Gen control
system is envisioned to achieved better algae productivity and CO2 capture efficiency performance than
that of the 1st-Gen control (developed in a prior Helios algae project). The proprietary algorithm was
provided to a vendor partner for fabrication of a breadboard test control system for the 1-stage system of
the MSC unit. The relevant components for the control system were identified, procured for fabrication
and integration to the algae-raceway PBR tanks, shown in Figure 4.

Task 5: Develop membrane contactor and evaluate in algae cultures
The main objective of this task is to develop an improved gas dispersion technology to improve the CO2
mass transfer rate into the algae cultivation system, increasing dissolved-CO2 availability for algae
growth. In this project, two types of gas dissolution technologies were assessed: bubbling and non-
bubbling operated units.

Given the huge flue gas volume, minimizing the feed pressure requirement is essential to achieving
significant energy savings in algae culturing systems. Non-bubbling gas/liquid contacting units based on
microporous membranes are envisioned to enable high CO2 mass transfer rates through direct gas/liquid
contacting at the surface of the pores without bubbling, thus reducing additional pressurization required to
overcome surface tension of bubble formation.

Subtask 5.1: Hollow fiber membrane (HFM) surface morphology.
In this subtask, the objective was to identify several gas/liquid contacting units utilizing both bubbling
and non-bubbling principles. The units were evaluated against several criteria, such as overall CO2 mass
transfer rate, required feed pressurization, and scalability. These criteria are driven by integration
feasibility within large-scale algae system (typically acres in size) and capital cost. All units identified
were procured and prepared for evaluation in Subtask 5.2.

The bubbling gas contactor units were identified based on prior use in other projects to suit the selection
criteria. Conversely, the non-bubbling hollow-fiber membrane contactor (HFM) units were characterized
for the pore size and surface porosity through Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) technique. The
selection of the preferred hollow-fiber membranes for modular scale-up will be based on the membrane
possessing a specific membrane pore size range that eliminates surface gas bubbling while
accommodating membrane surface modification to reduce biofouling from the algae culture.

As summarized in Table 1, several membrane contactor samples were procured and systematically
characterized. Noticeably, a wide range of membrane pore sizes and configurations is available

Mass transfer rate determination for gas-liquid contactors
Figure 15 shows the test setup for determining the CO2 mass transfer rate of the gas/liquid contactor units.
A fresh batch of reactive Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) buffer solution was utilized in each test to ensure
measurement consistency. Each of the gas/liquid contactor units were assessed based on several factors
such as the feed gas pressure, flowrate, resultant bubble size (if any), and contactor surface area.

UNLIMITED Page 14 of 36

 Figure 15: Test setup for determining CO2 mass transfer rate of gas dispersion/dissolution units

The CO2 mass transfer rates were derived based on measurements of the rate pH change in the KOH bath
as the feed gas is passed through the different test units (bubbling or non-bubbling) and the rate CO2
concentration change in the tank’s headspace via IR- CO2 concentration measurement. However, it was
determined that the CO2 mass transfer rates for the respective gas/liquid contactor units based on the IR-
CO2 concentration measurements were typically lower than the CO2 mass transfer rates based on the pH
measurements. This is attributed to the scavenging effect of the KOH batch which aggressively reacts
with CO2, decreasing the overall CO2 concentration in the headspace volume prior to measurement in the
IR- CO2 unit, and thus leading to potential errors. During the testing of the hollow-fiber membrane
module, the test setup was modified such that the KOH solution was pumped through the module’s shell,
accommodating the different configuration of the test unit. Additionally, the feed and retentate CO2
concentration were monitored to determine the permeance through the membrane module.

Parametric tests for the surface modified hollow-fiber membrane modules were conducted by varying the
feed CO2 concentration, feed gas flowrate, feed gas pressure, and reactant buffer flowrate. In general,
higher feed CO2 concentration, gas flowrate, and feed pressure resulted in an increase in the CO2 mass
transfer rate across the hollow-fiber membrane module. While higher KOH reactant buffer flowrate also
increased the CO2 mass transfer rate, the impact was minimal. For the non-bubbling test units, accurate
pH measurements over time were difficult to obtain due to the minimal to no pH change despite a small
KOH bath volume, which indicates a very low CO2 mass transfer rate. This is attributed to the small
surface area of the membrane module and low surface porosity. However, as seen in Figure 16, the
surface modified membrane module resulted in a marginally improved mass transfer rate when compared
to the pristine membrane module. The lower CO2 concentration for the surface modified HFM module’s
retentate stream was due to the hydrophobic surface that minimized liquid pore penetration through the
HFM surface, resulting in a barrier layer that enabled continuous CO2 uptake.

As expected, the CO2 mass transfer rate generally increases with increasing the feed gas flowrate,

Task 8 focused on developing, testing, and validating effective bio-contamination control strategies in

Subtask 8.1: Identify changes to culture liquid to inhibit specific bio-contaminants
Short- and long-term chemical addition experiments were evaluated for use in order to limit bio-
contaminants, including rotifers/ciliates, flagellates, and fungi previously identified in Task 2.1 through
DNA sequencing. Chemicals and their appropriate concentrations were selected for long-term use
(Subtask 8.2).

Subtask 8.2: Development of mitigations of harmful bio-contamination in algae cultures
Long-term experiments were conducted with (1) surface amended MSC tanks using Coating-1 to prevent
attached growth and (2) addition of chemical controls for limiting the targeted bio contaminant growth
using increasing concentrations of Additive-2 and Additive-1, respectively. Short and long-term growth
affects from chemical toxicity on Sc algae, as well as their effectiveness in limiting bio-contaminants has
been demonstrated (see Figure 21 below of long-term comparison). Control, Additive-1, Additive-2, and
Coating-1 were all tested long-term to demonstrate effectiveness. Spiked concentrations in response to
contamination were shown to be most effective, with long-term chronic loading of Additive-1, Additive-
2, and Coating-1 reducing algal growth and biomass productivity. However, the control reactor, with only
acute additions, showed the highest average biomass concentration and demonstrated long-term growth
without significant disruption or culture crash. For example, following culture contamination (Day 18),
Additive-2 was added to reactors to recover algal growth and maintain system operation. Selective use of
chemical addition has shown robust system operation for 2 months (with long-term operation continuing).
Control reactors with acute addition maintained optimal culture for CO2 capture, while Additive-1,
Additive-2, and Coating-1 reactors experienced contamination and growth of non-optimal filamentous
algae.

Milestone 8.1 completed – Bio-contamination technology tested in MSC tank

In addition to chemical dosing, the effects of adding side-stream filtration to the reactor recycle stream
and replacing nutrients with wastewater to further improve performance were tested. Wastewater nutrient
addition was tested during earlier project work. When operated in a long-term semi-continuous mode,
with and without filtration, the filtered reactors achieved long-term high-performance and did not suffer
from culture crash, unlike unfiltered reactors (Figure 22). Additionally, concentrated filtration
contaminants (Figure 22C) provided significant (one week) early detection of contamination.
Recommendations for high-performance long-term MSC operation include: (1) using internal recycling
and filtration to capture rotifer and flagellate contamination, (2) monitoring biomass and nitrite
production, which spike during periods of upset, (3) monitoring filters for early detection of
contaminants, and (4) dosing of either Additive-1 or Additive-2 acutely as appropriate as soon as
contaminants are detected.

Subtask 12.2: TEA
The techno-economic analysis (TEA) is based on a future commercial system covering 5000 acres of
wetted area. To the extent possible, the assumptions used here are the same as those used in DOE’s 2022
projections for algae technology for CO2 capture and Utilization. This recognizes the high quality of work
and the depth of analysis inherent in that extensive work but is also intended to enable easier comparisons
with the results presented therein. The system consists of parallel multi-stage continuous (MSC) units,
with each MSC stage comprising a 10-acre raceway featuring an inexpensive plastic top cover. This
innovative design ensures minimal contamination, water evaporation, and facilitates efficient CO₂
capture.

For the baseline case, an annual average algae productivity of 100% as the normalized target, based on
the project goal. At this productivity level, the 5000-acre algae farm would produce approximately
210,000 tons of dry algae per year, capturing around 392,000 tons of CO₂ annually. Also evaluated, is the
TEA case based on the actual algae productivity performance achieved in our outdoor field test. The CO₂
required for the operation is obtained directly from post-FGD flue gas from a coal power plant, without
the need for purification, where a 90% utilization of the CO₂ by algae was assumed. The culture is
harvested and dewatered, with 99% of the water removed being recycled back for algae growth. Based on
our previous work, a 75% nutrient replacement from wastewater was assumed.

The produced algae serves as the raw material for two types of products which are the focus of the current
project - both of these have existing markets, generating revenue and mitigate business risks associated
with fluctuations in product prices. Majority of the algae produced is utilized to replace fish meal in fish
feed production, which represents a very large, rapidly growing, market with the highest price point in the
feed category. The remainder of algae produced is allocated to nutraceuticals production, a smaller market
with very high current market prices for the products. Additionally, there is potential for revenue from

UNLIMITED Page 35 of 36

credits obtained through wastewater remediation and CO₂ capture. However, the base case assumes no
credits from either of these sources, and the sensitivity analysis explores their potential impact.

The estimated net cost of algae production for the base case amounts to $418/ton, slightly below DOE's
2022 projection of $494/ton. This reduction is primarily due to higher productivity, cost savings from
using wastewater nutrients, use of flue gas instead of purified CO₂, and advancements in dewatering
technology. As expected, the productivity of algae has the most significant impact on production costs: a
productivity of 20 g/m2/day would increase costs by approximately $130/ton of algae above the base
case, while an increase in productivity based on the field test algae productivity results in production costs
being reduced by about $46/ton. The sensitivity analysis explores the potential impact of other variables.

The total revenue generated for the base case is estimated to be $717/ton, exceeding the cost of algae
production. This is primarily due to the high price of algae expected for fish meal and for nutraceuticals.
It is important to note that the product revenue estimates were assumed based on a discounted fraction of
the selling price of their respective markets to enable breakthrough of the new algae-based products. This
positive result indicates that the diverse product generation has the potential to enable profitable
operation, thereby facilitating large-scale CO₂ capture. The sensitivity analysis further highlights the
impact of variations in key revenue and credit-related metrics. Particularly, remediation credits from
wastewater can be substantial, amounting to over $210/ton of algae, based on estimates by the researchers
at the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign (UIUC), making the profit motive even more
compelling.

In conclusion, the current work suggests the potential to achieve profitable carbon capture operation
enabled by revenue from products with current markets. This could transform carbon capture into a net
profitable business proposition, incentivizing large-scale adoption without relying on legislative credits.
