Enhancement of wheat’s adaptability to recurrent drought by improving water transport and root physiological resistance through nitrogen application and partial root-zone irrigation technology
Authors: Muhammad Daud, Shouming Xu, Haixia Qiao, Songting Wang, Wenrao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGrowing crops expose to drought stress more than once, yet it remains unclear whether early-stage drought will have a warning effect on the later drought. Nitrogen supply and partial root-zone irrigation (PRI) technology are thought to improve drought resistance, but their coordinated effects on hydraulic regulation and physiological resistance under recurrent drought conditions remain uncertain.ResultsCompared to (Full irrigation) FI, both one-time drought (D1) and recurrent drought (D2) reduced biomass, root activity, root hydraulic conductance (Kr), and root water potential (RWP). However, D2 plants-maintained Kr and RWP at levels similar to D1 while exhibiting stronger antioxidant activity and osmotic adjustment, indicating improved physiological acclimation to recurrent drought. Nitrogen supplementation significantly promoted biomass accumulation, osmotic production, and hydraulic performance. PRI further enhanced water transport capacity by increasing Kr, stimulating osmotic regulation, and enhancing antioxidant capacity while reducing oxidative damage under water-deficit conditions.ConclusionThe coordinated regulation of nitrogen supply and PRI improves plant resilience and recovery capacity under recurrent water deficit conditions by sustaining water transport and strengthening root physiological resistance. Background

Crops experience water shortages more than once during their growth [1, 2]. They may activate physiological mechanisms to cope with water scarcity and maintain water and nutrient uptake, such as maintaining root water potential (RWP) and hydraulic conductance (Kr) [3, 4], producing osmoprotectants and improving antioxidant defense [5], etc. These physiological and biochemical changes enable crop survival during initial drought stress conditions, but it is unclear whether these adaptations also improve resilience to recurrent drought and positively impact growth and development. Furthermore, efficient irrigation management, such as partial root-zone irrigation (PRI), establishes heterogeneous soil moisture conditions by creating wet and dry zones, which activate ABA-mediated root-to-shoot signaling, enhance water use efficiency, and regulate aquaporins (APQs) activity, ultimately improving plant water uptake and hydraulic regulation [6, 7]. Moreover, nitrogen supplementation also contributes to drought tolerance by stimulating root growth and metabolic processes, modulating AQPs expression, and enhancing osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defense systems [8–10]. As both PRI and nitrogen influence plant water uptake and hydraulic regulation through complementary mechanisms, their combined application is hypothesized to synergistically improve plant adaptation to recurrent drought. However, their interactive effects remain unclear.

Root water potential and Kr are considered key indicators of root water uptake, which directly reflect the root’s hydraulic status and its maximum capacity to supply water to shoots [11, 12]. AQPs are membrane proteins that play a significant role in controlling water transport and adjusting Kr [13, 14]; however, their function is inhibited when mercuric chloride (HgCl2) binds to cysteine residues, reducing Kr and restricting water transport capacity of plants [15, 16]. Moreover, β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) can disrupt or reverse the specific binding between aquaporins and HgCl2, thereby partially restoring water transport capacity [15, 17]. Drought stress exerts a profound influence on Kr by modulating both the expression as well as functional dynamics of aquaporins [18]. Interestingly, nitrogen application has also been shown to play a crucial role in modulating AQP activity, which, in turn, regulates Kr [19, 20]. Furthermore, drought stress combined with nitrogen deficiency can worsen Kr decline, whereas nitrogen application during drought conditions can affect a plant’s capacity to maintain water status [21, 22]. However, it is not well understood how nitrogen regulates plant responses to recurrent drought by affecting Kr or AQP-mediated water transport.

Research on physiological responses to repeated drought revealed enhanced activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), and catalase (CAT), which, in turn, elevated proline (Pro) content and reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) [23, 24]. Nitrogen application significantly improved plant recovery from repeated drought stress; additionally, plants exposed to repeated drought stress showed higher antioxidant enzyme activity and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, contributing to faster recovery with nitrogen application [25]. The study found that primed plants (plants exposed to mild or controlled drought stress before severe drought) subjected to nitrogen treatment at 300 kg ha⁻¹ exhibited elevated antioxidant enzyme activity, with POD increasing by 37.5%, SOD by 29.64%, and CAT by 65.66%. In contrast, MDA declined by 61% [26]. Results suggested that nitrogen application greatly enhanced the impact of pre-anthesis drought priming, helping reduce post-anthesis drought stress by regulating starch and protein formation in wheat [27].

To ensure efficient water use, extensive work has been done to develop techniques such as PRI [28–30]. Furthermore, previous research concluded that plants exposed to alternate partial root zone irrigation substantially boosted the Kr as compared to that of conventional irrigation; interestingly, both the irrigated and non-irrigated root zones contributed to the root water uptake [31]. Surprisingly, PRI also improves water-use efficiency and regulates water transporters, such as AQPs [32]. PRI-grown plants triggered the concentrations of root soluble sugars (RSS) and Pro, along with enhanced activities of SOD, CAT, POD, and reduced MDA contents, due to the accumulation of phytohormones such as jasmonic acid (JA) in the dry roots compared to the wetted ones [33]. Another study examined the interactive effects of PRI and nitrogen application, revealing that PRI effectively mitigates water stress–induced damage through the regulation of osmotic balance, membrane lipid peroxidation, compatible solute accumulation, and antioxidant enzyme activity [34]. Additionally, nitrogen supplementation enhanced the plants’ resilience to water stress and significantly affected the RSS and Pro contents under PRD conditions [35]. Moreover, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding how different physiological and biochemical parameters respond to the combined effects of recurrent drought, PRI technology, and nitrogen application.

Therefore, the aim of this study is to examine (1) the impact of early-stage drought on recurrent drought. (2) The role of PRI under recurrent drought conditions. (3) The role of nitrogen under recurrent drought conditions. Our results will also illustrate how wheat roots adapt to changes with combined recurrent drought, nitrogen conditions, and PRI. It is also important to explore how to improve hydraulic conductance through appropriate N supply, especially for crops grown in drought-prone areas or under alternating wet-dry growth conditions.

Materials and methods

Seeds germination

In this research, the wheat (Triticum aestivum, cultivar: Zhoumai 22) was used as the experimental material. The seeds were purchased from Henan Tiancun Seeds Science and Technology Co., Ltd., China. Prior to sowing, the seeds were surface-sterilized with a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution for 300 s. Following sterilization, the seeds were thoroughly rinsed with distilled water and subsequently sown in plug trays measuring 200 × 160 mm, filled with vermiculite as a germination substrate.

Experiment 1

Plant materials and growth conditions

After four days of seedling emergence, the seedlings were transplanted into cylindrical PVC pipes measuring 25 cm in height and 23 cm in diameter to facilitate their continued growth. The soil utilized in this experiment was specifically from the cultivated layer in Kaifeng, Henan (China). The soil exhibited the following properties: a bulk density of 1.12 g/cm³, a total nitrogen content of 1.0 g kg⁻¹, available phosphorus content of 9.2 mg kg⁻¹, available potassium content of 178.8 mg kg⁻¹, organic carbon content of 6.43 g kg⁻¹, and a field water holding capacity of 30%. Each PVC pipe was filled with 800 g of soil that had been crushed, screened, and air-dried. To provide a source of phosphorus and potassium fertilizer, 0.0386 g of potassium dihydrogen phosphate was mixed with sieved, air-dried soil and added to each pot. During the experiment, both watering and fertilizer applications were provided according to specific treatment protocols. The seedlings were placed within a controlled environment, specifically a drought shed, where they were shielded from natural rainfall. It is important to note that, in addition to this protection from precipitation, all other environmental conditions were deliberately maintained to closely resemble the natural climatic conditions of the Kaifeng region, where wheat is typically grown. The experimental period extended from April to June 2020. Each treatment combination was set up with 3 replicates. At the time of seed sowing, the initial watering regimen was carefully executed to achieve a soil moisture content within the PVC pipes ranging from 75% to 80% of the field water capacity. To minimize ineffective evaporation of soil moisture, a layer of perlite was applied to the surface of the PVC pipes utilized for plant cultivation. This step was taken to maintain a controlled and consistent moisture level in the soil.

Experiments design

Experiment 1 involved implementing two distinct cultivation methods: partial root-zone irrigation (PRI) and conventional planting (non-PRI). In the PRI method, a plastic film was used to symmetrically divide the interior of the PVC pipe, with a longitudinally positioned separation along its central axis. Additionally, a “V”-shaped opening, located 2 cm away from the mouth of the pipe, was meticulously cut into the plastic film. This “V”-shaped aperture was oriented vertically downward, and square openings, each approximately 1.5 cm² in area, were strategically placed at 6–8 cm intervals to allow deep roots to penetrate. Seedlings were planted in the center of the PVC pipe, with their root systems evenly distributed on both sides of the plastic divider. A small hole was left at the bottom of the tube after sealing to facilitate the removal of access water during initial transplantation and to allow air circulation during plant growth. Conversely, in the conventional planting method, seedlings were directly transplanted into the center of PVC pipes, where they were subsequently cultivated without a plastic divider or the specific modifications introduced in the root-splitting approach.

In the PRI treatment, the plastic divider separated the root system into two hydraulically distinct compartments, allowing differential irrigation of the two root halves. During the experimental period, irrigation water was applied to only one side of the split-root system at a time, while the opposite side was allowed to dry progressively. The wet and dry compartments were alternated at each irrigation event to ensure that both root halves were periodically exposed to wetting and drying cycles. In contrast, under non-PRI treatment, irrigation water was applied uniformly across the entire root zone. Soil moisture in each compartment was monitored regularly, and irrigation amounts were adjusted accordingly to maintenance of distinct wet and dry root-zone conditions throughout the experiment.

The experiment also incorporated three distinct nitrogen application rates, namely high nitrogen (HN) at 200 kg/ha (equivalent to 3.309 g per PVC tube), medium nitrogen (MN) at 120 kg/ha (equivalent to 2.035 g per PVC tube), and low nitrogen (LN) at 80 kg/ha (equivalent to 1.356 g per PVC tube). These nitrogen application levels were used to assess the impact of varying nitrogen fertilization intensities on the experimental conditions. The nitrogen fertilizer under examination in this study was urea. The urea fertilizer was homogeneously mixed with the experimental soil and subsequently applied.

Experiment 1 encompassed three distinct drought treatments: no drought, one-time drought, and two-time drought. The “Full irrigation” treatment (FI) signified a continuous and sufficient water supply throughout the experiment, maintaining the soil moisture content within the range of 75% to 80% of the field water capacity. In the “one-time drought” treatment (D1), after seedlings were relocated into the PVC pipes, they were initially watered regularly for 20 days. Subsequently, a gradual drought period ensued, spanning 7 days, during which the soil moisture content steadily decreased to approximately 40–50% of the field water capacity. After this period, a five-day drought phase was initiated. Rehydration was performed after that to allow full recovery of leaf water potential until harvest. As for the “two-time drought” treatment (D2), the seedlings underwent a 20-day normal cultivation phase initially, followed by a 7 + 5-day drought stress treatment, similar to D1. The seedlings were re-watered for 10 days after drought stress, lasting an additional 7 + 5 days of drought stress treatment were carried out, followed by re-watering until harvest. Consequently, all the seedlings were grown in the PVC tubes for a total of 64 days. Subsequent to these specified treatments, all plant materials were carefully documented and harvested. Additional details regarding the experimental setup are illustrated in (Fig. S1).

Additionally, soil water status was monitored throughout the experiment using volumetric soil water content (VWC) measurements to ensure precise regulation of irrigation treatments. The soil field water holding capacity was 30%, corresponding to 100% field capacity (FC). Well-watered conditions were maintained at 75–80% FC (22.5–24% VWC). During drought treatments, irrigation was withheld, and soil moisture declined progressively over 7 days to 40–50% FC (12–15% VWC). This moisture level was maintained for 5 days before rehydration. Soil moisture was recorded daily to monitor the dynamics of water depletion and recovery during each stress cycle.

Experiment 2

Plant materials and growth conditions

Experiment 2 was conducted to assess the hydraulic conductance in wheat roots. Plant materials were cultivated in Hoagland’s nutrient solutions with varying nitrogen nutrition and water potential. The experimental design retained parameters from Experiment 1, including the material cultivation methods (partial root-zone irrigation and conventional planting), while introducing variations in nitrogen supply levels (high, medium, low) and PEG-induced osmotic stress (well water, water deficit, recurrent water deficit). Following a 21-day growth period, the plant roots were subjected to treatments with either mercuric chloride (HgCl2) at concentrations of 50 µmol/L or 100 µmol/L for a duration of 30 min, or β-mercaptoethanol at concentrations of 500 µmol/L or 1000 µmol/L. In addition, a 30-minute treatment with β-mercaptoethanol was administered, followed by sequential treatments with both HgCl2 and β-mercaptoethanol for 30 min each, as detailed in (Fig. S2). Subsequently, the hydraulic conductance for the roots was measured as part of the investigation.

Following these water and nitrogen treatments, mercuric chloride and β-mercaptoethanol were applied, and hydraulic conductance was determined. To ensure adequate material management, a ventilation pump was installed in the PVC pipe to guarantee a minimum of 8 h of daily ventilation. This measure was taken to prevent hypoxic conditions within the system. When it was necessary to exchange the positions of the two sides of the root system, or before rehydration following a stress period, the roots were carefully rinsed to remove any adsorbed PEG-6000 (PEG) particles, and a freshly prepared PEG solution was used. Further details of the experimental setup are outlined in (Fig. S2).

Experiments design

To implement a root-divided nutrient solution culture, PVC pipes modified for root division were employed. In this configuration, the PVC pipe designated for root splitting was equipped with a plastic septum at the midpoint, devoid of holes. Additionally, no openings were present at the bottom of the PVC pipe. To secure the seedlings within the PVC pipe, a waterproof circular foam board, matching the inner diameter of the PVC pipe, was positioned 2 cm away from the opening, creating a 0.5 cm² small hole in the center. The seedlings were then affixed within the PVC pipe through this small opening. Subsequently, the PVC pipe was sealed with adhesive, leaving only the pipe mouth unsealed.

To prepare high, medium, and low-nitrogen solutions, we added 1 M potassium nitrate to the Hoagland nutrient solution, maintaining the calcium nitrate concentration constant rather than potassium nitrate concentration. When potassium nitrate was omitted, the potassium ion deficit was supplemented with potassium chloride. Consequently, the final nitrogen concentration is calculated as the sum of NO3−. Specifically, the nitrogen concentrations were as follows: high nitrogen concentration at 20 mM, medium nitrogen concentration at 15 mM, and low nitrogen concentration at 12.5 mM [36] for details. Before using these nutrient solutions, pH was adjusted to 6.5. This pH adjustment was accomplished using 1 mM sodium hydroxide or hydrochloric acid as required.

In the split-root culture treatment, soil moisture was reduced using PEG-6000 to simulate the natural decline in soil moisture. Two sides of the root system were subjected to different concentrations of nitrogen-mixed nutrient solutions, which included combinations of water + 0.1% PEG, 1% PEG + 3% PEG, 3% PEG + 5% PEG, and ultimately 5% PEG + 5% PEG. The nutrient solutions were added incrementally over successive days to simulate the natural decline in soil moisture during periods of osmotic stress. In conventional culture, the seedlings were placed similarly to the split-root culture, but without a central diaphragm, as observed in the root-splitting treatment. In the “non-stress” treatment, cultivation continued for 21 days. The PEG-induced osmotic stress involved subjecting the seedlings to a 5% PEG solution for 3 days, starting 18 days after cultivation. For the recurrent osmotic treatment, the procedure entailed initiating a 5% PEG treatment for 3 days on the 12th day of cultivation, followed by a 3-day recovery period, and subsequently applying another 3-day treatment with 5% PEG.

Parameter’s measurement

Measurement of root biomass, shoot biomass, and the ratio of root to shoot

In experiment 1, shoot biomass (SB), root biomass (RB), and the root-shoot ratio (R/S) were quantified as follows: plants were separated approximately 2 cm above the rhizome junction. The aboveground plant components were dried in an oven until a consistent weight was achieved, constituting the SB. Additionally, half of the root portions were subjected to the same drying procedure until a constant weight was achieved. The RB-to-SB ratio was subsequently calculated as R/S.

Measurement of root water potential and hydraulic conductance

To assess root water potential (RWP) in experiment 1, and to quantify hydraulic conductance of the entire root system in both experiment 1 and experiment 2. Prior to root extraction from the PVC pipe, the roots, still enclosed within the PVC pipe, were introduced into a pressure chamber (Model: 3005, Soil Moisture Equipment Corp., CA, USA). Similar to the water potential measurements, the pressure chamber was sealed around the stem, and pressure within the chamber was incrementally and gently raised until minute droplets emerged on the cut surface. The pressure value was then measured as the root RWP. This specific point was established as the equilibrium pressure (P0). The pressure was then increased in 6 steps of 0.05 MPa from P0 to (P0 +0.3) MPa to determine the hydraulic conductance. At each step, the exuded sap (g) was collected for 60 s after the flow rate stabilized for 120 s. The flow rate Jv (g/s) was calculated by Jv = sap weight (g)/120 s. The hydraulic conductance (Kr) [g/ (MPa s)] was determined from the slope of the regression line by plotting Jv against hydrostatic pressure [(P0 +0.3 MPa) - P0] according to [37]. All treatments within a replicate were measured during 9:00–11:00 a.m. Given that Kr represents whole-root hydraulic conductance and may be influenced by root size; thus, root biomass was taken into account during data interpretation.

Measurement of root physiological parameters

For the assessment of root activity (RA), root soluble sugar content (RSS), proline content (Pro), and antioxidant characteristics (superoxide dismutase activity (SOD), peroxidase activity (POD), catalase activity (CAT), and malondialdehyde content (MDA) in experiment 1 using fresh root samples. After biomass determination, the dried root samples were pulverized in a ball mill (Retsch MM400, Retsch, Haan, Germany). Subsequently, the measurements of root soluble sugar (RSS) and proline (Pro) content were conducted employing Comin Biochemical Test Kits (Comin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analyses

Data was checked for normality and homogeneity of variance. A Three-way ANOVA was then used in experiment 1 to determine the effects of water supply patterns, nitrogen application rates, and number of droughts on Kr, RB, and SB, R/S, RV, SOD, POD, CAT, Pro, MDA, RSS, and RWP. In experiment 2, a four-factor ANOVA was used to determine the independent and interactive effects of each factor on hydraulic conductance and to evaluate the contribution of AQP-sensitive pathways using HgCl2 and β-mercaptoethanol treatments. The correlations between each factor in experiment 1 and hydraulics were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation analysis. The statistical analyses were carried out in RStudio, and data visualization was performed in Origin 2023.

Results

Experiment 1

Biomass

The results demonstrated that SB (Fig. 1A), RB (Fig. 1B), and R/S (Fig. 1C) were significantly influenced by drought stress times, nitrogen application rates, and irrigation methods when considered as individual factors (Table S1). SB was significantly higher in PRI than in non-PRI, but only in MN and LN, regardless of drought timing. However, under well-watered conditions, there was no significant difference in SB between MN and HN. Under drought stress, SB decreased with reduced nitrogen application rate and increased drought duration, showing decreases of 16 ± 1.1% in D1 and 30 ± 1.0% in D2. Regardless of drought stress times and nitrogen levels, RB under PRI was significantly greater than under non-PRI. It decreased with increased drought duration and reduced nitrogen content, resulting in decreases of 17 ± 1.5% in D1 and 27 ± 1.2% in D2. Interestingly, RB was higher in the wet portion of the root system than in the dry portion. Although R/S showed a higher trend under HN than in MN and LN, but the differences were not statistically significant. Overall, compared with well-watered conditions, both D1 and D2 reduced biomass accumulation, with the greatest decline observed under D2. Nevertheless, PRI and nitrogen supply, particularly HN and MN, substantially mitigated these reductions under water-deficit conditions.

Root behaviors

The outcomes confirm that each tested factor, including nitrogen application rate, drought stress times, and method of irrigation, had a statistically significant impact on Kr (Fig. 2A), RA (Fig. 2B), and RWP (Fig. 2C) (Table S1). Regardless of nitrogen levels and drought times, Kr under PRI was significantly higher than that under non-PRI. Under well-watered and D1 conditions, RA was significantly lower only in LN, with no significant difference between MN and HN. However, Kr and RA significantly decreased after drought stress, with decreases of 41 ± 1.2% and 25 ± 1.4% in D1, and 39 ± 1.8% and 48 ± 0.4% in D2. RWP under PRI was significantly higher than that under non-PRI only in D2; there were no significant differences between other drought times (similar to Kr) or between PRI and non-PRI treatments. Overall, although both D1 and D2 impaired root hydraulic performance and water status compared with FI plants, the integration of PRI with HN supply effectively maintained water transport capacity and root functionality, thereby enhancing wheat adaptation to recurrent drought condition.

Root physiological and biochemical parameters

Regardless of nitrogen levels and drought times, the activities of SOD (Fig. 3A), POD (Fig. 3B), CAT (Fig. 3C), Pro (Fig. 3D), and RSS (Fig. 3F) under PRI were significantly higher than those under non-PRI. The MDA (Fig. 3E) was substantially lower under PRI as compared to non-PRI conditions only during drought. Regardless of drought times, SOD, POD, CAT activities, and Pro content were significantly higher in LN than in MN and HN treatments. In contrast, MDA and RSS contents were significantly lower only in LN, with no significant differences between MN and HN for all parameters. SOD, POD (only under LN), Pro, MDA, and RSS were significantly higher under drought conditions than under well-watered conditions, with no significant differences between D1 and D2. Collectively, FI plants exhibited the lowest antioxidant and osmotic responses, whereas both D1 and D2 stimulated these defense mechanisms, with D2 generally maintaining higher stress-adaptive responses. PRI further enhanced antioxidant activity and osmotic adjustment while reducing oxidative damage, whereas MN and HN supported greater physiological stability than LN, particularly under D2 conditions.

Experiment 2

Regardless of nitrogen levels and drought times, PRI resulted in higher Kr. A gradual reduction in Kr was observed with declining nitrogen levels, being moderate at MN and minimal at LN. Drought stress reduced Kr, but there was no significant difference between D1 and D2. Mercuric chloride stress reduced Kr, which was significantly recovered by β-mercaptoethanol, with no significant differences between different gradient concentrations and recovery concentrations (Fig. 6 and Table S2). Among the tested solution cultures, S4 (1000 µmol/L β-mercaptoethanol) exhibited the highest Kr under the combined influence of PRI and HN application, even though it declined by 41 ± 0.3% under D1 and 40 ± 1.3% under D2 relative to the FI. Additionally, S2 (100µmol/L mercuric chloride) recorded the lowest Kr under the integrated treatment of LN and non-PRI, particularly when plants were subjected to D2 conditions as shown in (Fig. 6I). Notably, plants subjected to D1 experienced the greatest damage whereas plants under D2 appeared to mitigate these effects, implying a degree of physiological adaptation in roots of D2 plants (Fig. 6A-I). Notably, under the HN and D2 treatment, PRI mitigated the detrimental effects of drought stress more effectively than non-PRI, resulting in a 46 ± 5.3% increase in Kr. AQP-mediated inhibition (%) was greatest under FI conditions at HN, whereas PRI maintained lower inhibition than non-PRI, indicating more stable aquaporin regulation (Table S3).

Discussion

Droughts are more likely to occur in recurring cycles than as single events during a plant’s life cycle. However, the detrimental effects of the primary drought on plant structure and physiology are unavoidable [38]. In contrast, the impact of early-stage drought on recurrent drought in plants remains unclear, particularly regarding the physiological and biochemical adaptations that occur during the initial drought conditions under different nitrogen levels and PRI treatments. This paper addressed the challenge of improving wheat’s adaptability to recurrent drought by enhancing water transport and root physiological resistance through nitrogen application and PRI technology at the onset of the first drought stress. Therefore, this paper discovered how agronomic practices like partial root zone irrigation and nitrogen application can strengthen plant resistance to recurrent drought by pre-existing adaptations. Under both D1 and D2 conditions, plants maintained a consistent root-to-shoot ratio, relatively constant hydraulic conductance and water potential, and exhibited stronger physiological resistance. These results indicate that a moderate nitrogen level is optimal, whereas low nitrogen levels impose stress, and excessive nitrogen is inconsistent with current nitrogen-reduction strategies.

Our experiment showed that drought reduced biomass; which is consistent with previous findings reported in studies [39–42]. Notably, plants subjected to D2 exhibited a greater reduction in biomass than those exposed to D1. This is accompanied by maintenance of water conductance and water potential (water conductance decreases only under low nitrogen conditions). It indicated that Kr and RWP declined under D1 but exhibited a similar reduction during D2, indicating a partial recovery or adaptive improvement in Kr under D2 exposure. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that initial drought can induce physiological adjustments that restore Kr and enhance water status upon re-exposure [43]. Our HgCl2 and β-ME assays suggest that an AQP-sensitive component contributes substantially to Kr, indicating that membrane water transport pathways are involved in regulating root water uptake under D2 conditions (Fig. 6A-I). These factors explain why the biomass reduction during recurrent drought was less severe. Moreover, compared with a single drought, repeated droughts led to a decline in root vitality, accompanied by reduced antioxidant and osmotic regulation capabilities (Fig. 3A-F). D2-treated plants exhibited more activation of antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, and CAT) as in D1 and FI plants, facilitating greater resilience to stress. These results suggested that repeated drought-rewatering cycles stimulate sustained antioxidant activity, thereby improving plant tolerance and physiological acclimation under recurrent soil drought conditions [44, 45]. Plants under D2 conditions displayed a similar elevation in Pro and RSS levels, as well as a reduction in MDA, compared with those under D1, suggesting the accumulation of osmoprotectants under D2 conditions. These findings indicate that plants under drought stress accumulate osmoprotectants and activate antioxidant enzymes, thereby reducing damage during recurrent drought. Furthermore, these biochemical alterations play a protective role against oxidative stress by increasing proline content, soluble sugars, and antioxidant enzyme activity, while reducing MDA [46–49]. So, our results suggested that the initial drought stress (D1) induced a physiological acclimation in plants, enabling them to enhance water uptake capacity.

Current findings revealed that both SB and RB were more adversely affected under D2 than D1 conditions; nevertheless, elevated nitrogen supply markedly mitigated these reductions, promoting biomass accumulation in both D1 and D2 scenarios, especially under PRI. It can be attributed to nitrogen-induced enhancement of auxin-driven root growth and meristem activity for improved water uptake, along with strengthened osmotic adjustment and antioxidant defense, and increased photosynthetic efficiency (electron transport, RuBP carboxylation, and CO₂ quantum yield), thereby increasing assimilate availability for root and shoot growth, as reported in studies [50, 51]. Nitrogen supplementation markedly improved Kr, with plants under D2 exhibiting higher Kr than those under D1, though nitrogen application had no significant effect on RWP under D2. It may be attributed to nitrogen-induced changes in hydraulic properties, potentially involving AQP-sensitive water transport pathways that contribute to Kr [19, 52]. To further substantiate the physiological basis of the observed Kr changes, a hydroponic experiment also clearly demonstrated that Kr is strongly regulated by HgCl₂ and β-ME, both of which strongly affect AQP activity [53]. Moreover, plants subjected to repeated drought exhibit a unique acclimation strategy, enhancing their defense mechanisms and maintaining an “alert state” that primes them for effective responses to future drought episodes [54]. LN strengthened antioxidant enzymes (SOD, POD, CAT) and Pro synthesis, most prominently under D2 stress, while HN led to elevated RSS in D2 plants relative to D1. Consistent with our results, Wang et al. [55] also reported that nitrogen supplementation enhances antioxidant defense and osmolyte accumulation by stimulating protein synthesis and stress-responsive metabolic pathways, thereby improving redox homeostasis, osmotic balance, and root functionality under drought stress, ultimately contributing to enhanced plant recovery following D2 stress. Studies on D2 have shown that plants exhibit improved performance under D2 conditions due to the early warning effect [56]. Furthermore, D2 stress triggered increased antioxidant enzyme activity and ROS production, whereas nitrogen application expedited the recovery by modulating these stress-responsive pathways.

Our research also concluded that plants under D1 and D2 conditions, exposed to PRI technology, performed better than those under conventional irrigation. PRI plants under D2 stress conditions showed lower MDA concentrations but higher Kr, RA, RWP, Pro, and RSS, and enhanced antioxidant activity to cope with water scarcity. PRI activates the ABA-mediated root-to-shoot chemical signaling, which may enhance water use efficiency and influence AQP-sensitive water transport processes, thereby facilitating water uptake across both wet and dry root zones [8, 57]. Additionally, these AQPs have a direct connection with Kr, highlighting their role in enhancing water flow from the soil to the xylem to support plant adaptation under varying water availability [58]. It is also proved that PRI-grown plants stimulated the accumulation of RSS and Pro, increased the activities of SOD, CAT, and POD, and reduced MDA content, likely due to increased phytohormone levels [59, 60]. Corroborating the present findings that plants under D2 outperform those subjected to D1. Our results emphasized that D2 responses are linked to physiological acclimation induced by prior drought exposure, whereby primary drought primes the defense machinery, maintaining plants in an ‘alert state’ and thereby enhancing their capacity to mount more effective responses against subsequent drought episodes, notably, PRI improved this process.

This study provides robust evidence that the combination of PRI and optimal nitrogen fertilization is a highly effective strategy to enhance or maintain RWP and Kr, antioxidant defense, and osmoprotection, thereby significantly improving wheat’s resilience to recurrent drought. To clarify the mechanisms underlying water potential and Kr responses to PRI, drought, and nitrogen addition in Experiment 1, a hydroponic study also confirmed a clear modulation of Kr, where HgCl₂ caused a marked decline, while β-ME alleviated this inhibition, highlighting the central role of Kr in regulating root water transport under varying stress conditions. Future studies should focus on unraveling the molecular basis, including gene expression and post-translational modifications under recurrent drought; whereas, the proposed agronomic strategy (combining PRI and optimized nitrogen application) must be validated in multi-year field trials across diverse agro-climatic zones to assess its practicality and effectiveness under a broader range of combined abiotic stresses. It should screen multiple genotypes to identify the most responsive and resilient wheat varieties. Although rehydration occurs between primary and secondary droughts, our current results cannot completely rule out its role, but they do confirm the implications of primary drought for secondary drought. This will be discussed in the discussion.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that wheat plants exhibit a robust ability to establish physiological and biochemical acclimation when subjected to D1, hence enhancing resilience over repeated drought cycles. In both experiments, D1 elicited adaptive responses, including heightened antioxidant activity, enhanced osmoprotectant accumulation, and partial restoration of Kr, thereby strengthening plant tolerance during D2. The coordinated control of root hydraulic traits, such as Kr and RWP, alongside root activity and biochemical defences, indicates that an initial period of drought serves as a priming signal. This early stress prepares the plant to improve water uptake and alleviate stress more effectively during recurrent drought events. Nitrogen supplementation and PRI are key components of wheat’s adaptive responses to recurrent drought. HN enhanced biomass production, osmotic adjustment, and Kr, whereas antioxidant responses were more prominent under LN, demonstrating the role of nitrogen in drought stress mitigation. In contrast, PRI optimised water transport by increasing Kr, soluble sugars, proline, and antioxidant levels, while simultaneously reducing oxidative damage, as indicated by lower MDA levels. The combination of nitrogen supplementation and PRI consistently performed best across both drought cycles. This reveals a clear synergy: the nitrogen supply furnished the metabolic building blocks for plant recovery. At the same time, the PRI technique activated root-based signalling pathways, thereby improving water-use efficiency by regulating aquaporins. This synergy also highlighted the importance of AQP-sensitive hydraulic pathways in maintaining root water transport under recurrent drought. Taken together, these findings underscore that combining optimised nitrogen fertilisation with PRI is an effective agronomic strategy to improve wheat resilience against recurring drought. Future studies should focus on elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed hydraulic responses, including AQP-gene expression, protein abundance, and isoform-specific regulation under recurrent drought.
