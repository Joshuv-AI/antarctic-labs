Maxillary sinus–related adverse event reports associated with endosseous dental implants in the FDA MAUDE database: a retrospective text-mining analysis
Authors: Cheng Liu, Ting Tang, Yichen Chen, Xin Meng, Zhou Song, Dongsheng Liu, Lin Zhu
Published: 2026

Background

Endosseous dental implants are widely used to restore missing teeth, and implant therapy in the posterior maxilla is a particularly important component of contemporary oral rehabilitation. This region presents distinctive anatomical and surgical considerations because available bone height may be limited by sinus pneumatization, residual ridge resorption, and proximity to the maxillary sinus floor. The lateral-window maxillary sinus floor graft technique was described in classic implant reconstruction literature [1], and osteotome-based transalveolar sinus floor elevation was subsequently introduced as a less invasive approach [2]. Clinical anatomy is also central to adverse-event prevention: the sinus floor may lie close to posterior tooth roots and the residual alveolar ridge, the Schneiderian membrane may be vulnerable during elevation procedures, Underwood septa can limit visualization or contribute to membrane tearing, and the alveolar-antral artery in or near the lateral sinus wall is an important landmark during access-window design. CBCT-based three-dimensional assessment of the arterial course and septal anatomy has therefore been recommended to support surgical planning and reduce vascular or membrane-related complications [3]. Contemporary reviews support sinus floor augmentation as a treatment option when posterior maxillary bone height is limited, while emphasizing anatomical assessment and case selection [4]. These considerations make the posterior maxilla a clinically relevant setting for evaluating sinus-related events associated with endosseous dental implant therapy.

Maxillary sinus-related complications are clinically meaningful because they may affect surgical decision-making, implant stability, postoperative management, and the need for additional intervention. Implant outcomes after lateral and transalveolar sinus floor elevation have been summarized in systematic reviews [5, 6]. Additional clinical evidence has addressed specific sinus-elevation protocols, including long-term radiographic outcomes after transalveolar sinus-lift procedures, comparisons between one-stage and two-stage lateral sinus-lift strategies, and graftless lateral or transcrestal sinus floor elevation [7–9]. Recent literature has also focused on prevention and management of intraoperative complications and on regenerative adjuncts such as platelet concentrates in sinus-lift procedures [10, 11]. Intraoperative sinus membrane perforation is one of the most frequently discussed complications, and recent systematic evidence has evaluated clinical outcomes after sinus floor or sinus membrane perforation [12–14]. Earlier clinical series and retrospective analyses have examined the relationship between membrane perforation, postoperative complications, sinusitis, and graft outcomes [15, 16]. Sinus infection or sinusitis has been examined in relation to dental implant infectious pathologies [17], and sinus graft infection has been reported in clinical studies [18]. Broader reviews discuss the etiology, prevention, and management of sinus augmentation complications [19, 20], while a 200-case clinical series reported postoperative complications including maxillary sinusitis [21]. Implant displacement or migration into the sinus and management of displaced implants have been reviewed separately [22, 23]. Oroantral communication or fistula remains a clinically relevant but less common implant-related scenario [24]. The clinical implications of these events vary. Some reports may describe intraoperative membrane perforation or removal of an implant during surgery, whereas others may involve sinus symptoms, infection management, implant retrieval, or closure of an oroantral communication. A descriptive analysis of how such events are reported in postmarket data can help characterize reporting themes that warrant further clinical review.

Public postmarket surveillance databases provide an additional perspective on adverse event reporting beyond controlled clinical studies, registries, or case series. The FDA Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database contains publicly available reports involving medical devices, including dental devices [25, 26]. MAUDE can support descriptive characterization and signal exploration, but it is a passive surveillance system with important interpretive limitations [27]. The public MAUDE file structure and linkage considerations have also been described in methods literature [28]. Reports may be incomplete, duplicated, delayed, or template-based, and reporting may be influenced by manufacturer practices, regulatory requirements, litigation, awareness, and other factors. MAUDE also lacks a denominator for the number of exposed devices, procedures, patients, posterior maxillary implant placements, or sinus augmentation procedures. For these reasons, MAUDE analyses must be interpreted as analyses of reports, not as estimates of incidence, prevalence, comparative risk, or causality. This distinction was considered central to the scope of the present analysis from the outset: the study was intended to describe reporting patterns and data-quality features in MAUDE, not true clinical incidence or event probability.

Clinical details in adverse event reports are often contained in free-text narrative fields, and text-mining approaches have been used to support adverse event coding from narrative text [29]. Recent reviews of artificial intelligence-assisted predictive models in implant dentistry also emphasize the growing role of digital and machine-learning methods in implant planning, risk assessment, and outcomes research [30]. In MAUDE, device fields can identify product codes and manufacturers, whereas narrative fields may contain descriptions of sinus perforation, sinusitis, implant migration, removal from the sinus, or oroantral communication. A staged workflow that links device records, narrative text, and master event fields can screen large report sets, refine candidate records using higher-specificity terms, and assign transparent rule-based categories for descriptive analysis. Such an approach also allows quality-control steps, including duplicate assessment, repeated-text assessment, and sensitivity analyses that evaluate whether template-like narratives influence the descriptive findings.

The objective of this study was to identify and describe maxillary sinus-related adverse event reports associated with endosseous dental implants with FDA product code DZE in the FDA MAUDE database from 2015 to 2025. The study was designed to characterize public reporting patterns and data-quality features, not to estimate clinical incidence, comparative risk, manufacturer performance, or causality. We used a staged text-mining workflow to screen DZE reports, identify high-specificity maxillary sinus-related candidates, classify final event categories, summarize report characteristics, assess duplicate and template-like narratives, validate a stratified quality-control sample, and compare the main record-level analysis with a unique-text sensitivity analysis.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective descriptive text-mining analysis of publicly available FDA MAUDE adverse event reports. The analysis was designed to identify maxillary sinus-related reports among endosseous dental implant device reports and to classify the resulting high-specificity candidate reports into clinically interpretable event categories. The analysis characterized report narratives and structured report fields; it was not designed to estimate incidence, prevalence, comparative device risk, manufacturer performance, or causality. All analyses were performed on downloaded MAUDE text files and derived cleaned datasets.

Data source

The data source was the FDA Manufacturer and User Facility Device Experience (MAUDE) database. The analytic file set used for this manuscript consisted of extracted public MAUDE text files inspected on 2 May 2026. File inspection identified 23 extracted raw text sources, including annual Device files from 2015 to 2025, annual FOITEXT files from 2015 to 2025, and a Master Event file (mdrfoiThru2025.txt). Expected Device and FOITEXT years from 2015 to 2025 were present. A raw-file inventory, SHA256 checksum manifest, and checksum script for the local raw MAUDE folder are provided in Additional file 2.

The Device files included fields used for device-level screening and description, including MDR_REPORT_KEY, DATE_RECEIVED, BRAND_NAME, GENERIC_NAME, MANUFACTURER_D_NAME, and DEVICE_REPORT_PRODUCT_CODE. FOITEXT files included MDR_REPORT_KEY, MDR_TEXT_KEY, TEXT_TYPE_CODE, DATE_REPORT, and FOI_TEXT. The Master Event file included report-level fields such as MDR_REPORT_KEY, REPORT_NUMBER, DATE_RECEIVED, DATE_REPORT, DATE_OF_EVENT, REPORT_DATE, EVENT_TYPE, REPORT_SOURCE_CODE, SOURCE_TYPE, REMEDIAL_ACTION, MANUFACTURER_NAME, and SUMMARY_REPORT.

Ethical considerations

This study used publicly available MAUDE report data. The analysis did not involve direct contact with patients or access to identifiable private clinical records. Because MAUDE public files are deidentified public postmarket surveillance data, this project was treated as a secondary analysis of public data. No attempt was made to identify patients, clinicians, or reporters.

Study period and device scope

The study period was 2015–2025. Device scope was defined using FDA product code DZE, which FDA lists as an implant, endosseous, root-form, with the regulation description of endosseous dental implant under 21 CFR 872.3640; FDA guidance also identifies root-form endosseous dental implants as class II devices regulated under 21 CFR 872.3640 with product code DZE [31, 32]. Device records were read from DEVICE2015.txt through DEVICE2025.txt. The DEVICE_REPORT_PRODUCT_CODE field was stripped of whitespace, converted to uppercase, and filtered to DZE.

This product-code restriction was deliberately focused but not exhaustive. Reports involving abutments, graft materials, barrier membranes, sinus-elevation instruments, or other implant-related devices may be coded under product codes other than DZE and therefore would not be captured by this device-scope definition. The analysis should consequently be interpreted as a focused assessment of endosseous dental implant reports rather than a comprehensive assessment of all devices and materials that may be involved in maxillary sinus-related implant complications.

Data linkage

Data linkage was performed using MDR_REPORT_KEY, which was present in the Device, FOITEXT, and Master Event files. First, all DZE device rows were combined across annual Device files. A unique list of DZE MDR_REPORT_KEY values was then created. FOITEXT records were filtered to rows whose MDR_REPORT_KEY appeared in the DZE key list, and all FOITEXT rows for each report were concatenated into a combined narrative text field. Master Event fields were merged onto sinus keyword hit records using MDR_REPORT_KEY.

Initial DZE screening

The DZE screening step identified all Device rows with DEVICE_REPORT_PRODUCT_CODE = = DZE across 2015–2025. This step produced 3,386,753 DZE device rows and 3,386,630 unique DZE MDR reports. Matched FOITEXT rows were then linked to DZE reports; 5,345,369 DZE FOITEXT rows were matched, corresponding to 3,383,333 unique DZE reports with FOITEXT.

Sinus keyword screening

The combined narrative text for each DZE report was screened using case-insensitive maxillary sinus-related keyword groups. The screening terms included broad sinus terms, sinus lift or augmentation terms, complication terms, and implant migration or displacement terms. Reports with any sinus keyword match were retained as initial sinus keyword hits. This screening step identified 50,014 DZE reports with sinus keyword hits. This stage was intentionally sensitive and was not considered final manual inclusion or final event classification. The full keyword dictionary, matching level, clinical rationale, and negation-sensitive review flags are provided in Additional file 2.

High-specificity candidate refinement

The 50,014 initial sinus keyword hit records were refined using predefined higher-specificity keyword groups for sinus membrane perforation, sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, and oroantral communication or fistula. Reports without a high-specificity match were retained only as low-specificity-only background records and were not promoted into the final core dataset; the full rule dictionary is provided in Additional file 2.

This refinement yielded 6,386 high-specificity candidates and 43,628 low-specificity-only records.

Rule-based event classification

High-specificity candidate records were classified into final rule-based event categories using the combined narrative text. Four primary category flags were first detected independently: implant migration into the sinus, oroantral communication or fistula, sinus infection or sinusitis, and sinus membrane perforation. If two or more category flags were present, the final category was assigned as mixed or multiple sinus events. If only one flag was present, the corresponding category was assigned. If no category flag was detected in a record already present in the high-specificity dataset, the record was assigned to other high-specificity sinus event. In the final classified dataset, no report was assigned to the other high-specificity sinus event category.

Classification was intentionally hierarchical and deterministic rather than probabilistic. Mixed or multiple sinus events were assigned only when more than one primary event flag was detected in the same combined narrative. Records with only low-specificity sinus context, such as routine sinus lift history without a complication phrase, were not promoted into the final high-specificity core dataset. The classification hierarchy, positive examples, and exclusion cues are provided in Additional file 2.

The final event categories were: sinus membrane perforation, sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, mixed or multiple sinus events, and oroantral communication or fistula.

Manual review and quality control

Manual validation was performed using a stratified quality-control sample of 449 algorithm-positive high-specificity records. The sample included records from each final event category, repeated-template examples, and a random core sample. Two reviewers (X.M. and Z.S.) independently reviewed the blinded validation workbook. Reviewers assessed the combined FOITEXT narrative text and were blinded to the algorithm-assigned event category, matched keyword group, and prior adjudication labels. For each record, reviewers recorded inclusion status, event category, false-positive reason when applicable, duplicate or template-text concern, and reviewer notes. Disagreements were resolved by consensus adjudication by C.L.

Inter-reviewer reliability was assessed using Cohen’s κ for binary inclusion status and for event-category assignment. The conservative positive predictive value (PPV) was calculated as the proportion of reviewed algorithm-positive high-specificity records confirmed as maxillary sinus-related reports after consensus adjudication, with uncertain records not counted as confirmed positives. Quality control also assessed duplicate identifiers, repeated narrative text, and template-like text patterns. Duplicate MDR_REPORT_KEY rows, duplicate REPORT_NUMBER rows, exact duplicate normalized narrative text hashes, and repeated first-300-character snippets were evaluated. Category-level text uniqueness was summarized by final event category.

To probe potential false-negative classification after high-specificity refinement, an additional 100-record sample of low-specificity-only reports was reviewed using the same narrative-only approach. The review assessed whether any low-specificity-only record warranted promotion to the final high-specificity core dataset and documented reasons why records remained excluded.

Duplicate and template-text assessment

Narrative text was normalized by converting to lowercase, stripping whitespace, and collapsing repeated spaces. SHA256 hashes were calculated from normalized text. The number of unique text hashes, repeated text hash frequencies, and repeated first-300-character snippets were summarized. Category-level text uniqueness included record counts, unique text hash counts, median text length, the top repeated snippet frequency, and the proportion represented by the top repeated snippet.

Unique-text sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate whether repeated or template-like narratives influenced the main event-category findings. A unique-text dataset was created by retaining one representative record per normalized narrative text hash. Main record-level distributions were compared with unique-text distributions for final event category, report year, manufacturer, and final matched keywords.

Descriptive statistical analysis

Analyses were descriptive. Counts and proportions were calculated for screening stages, final event categories, report years, EVENT_TYPE, manufacturers, brands, matched keywords, validation outcomes, and duplicate-text metrics. Cross-tabulations were generated for event category by year and for event category by top manufacturers and brands. Inter-reviewer reliability was summarized using Cohen’s κ for inclusion status and event-category assignment. Main-text figures summarized the screening flow, final category distribution, annual report counts, and keyword frequency. Tables summarized screening steps, event type, and unique-text sensitivity analysis. Additional annual and manufacturer distributions were provided as supplementary display items. No inferential testing was performed.

MAUDE-specific limitations

MAUDE is a passive surveillance database. Reports may be incomplete, duplicated, template-based, delayed, or affected by reporting practices. The database does not provide a denominator for the number of devices used, procedures performed, patients treated, or implants placed in the posterior maxilla. Therefore, MAUDE cannot be used to estimate incidence, comparative risk, prevalence, or event probability. Report presence does not prove causality between a device and an event, and manufacturer or brand counts should not be interpreted as safety rankings or comparative performance measures. Because this was a rule-based text-mining analysis, misclassification can also arise from ambiguous wording, negation, abbreviations, and incomplete narratives.

Results

Raw file inspection and data structure

The raw data folder contained extracted .txt files rather than zip archives. The inspection step identified 23 extracted raw text sources and no raw zip files. Annual Device files and annual FOITEXT files were present for each year from 2015 to 2025, with no missing expected years. A Master Event file through 2025 was also available.

The Device files supported product-code filtering and extraction of device-related fields, including MDR_REPORT_KEY, DATE_RECEIVED, BRAND_NAME, GENERIC_NAME, MANUFACTURER_D_NAME, and DEVICE_REPORT_PRODUCT_CODE. The FOITEXT files provided narrative text linked by MDR_REPORT_KEY. The Master Event file provided report-level fields including REPORT_NUMBER, DATE_RECEIVED, DATE_REPORT, DATE_OF_EVENT, REPORT_DATE, EVENT_TYPE, REPORT_SOURCE_CODE, SOURCE_TYPE, REMEDIAL_ACTION, MANUFACTURER_NAME, and SUMMARY_REPORT.

Initial sinus keyword screening of the combined FOITEXT narratives identified 50,014 DZE reports with sinus keyword hits. These reports included broad sinus-related terms, sinus augmentation or sinus lift terms, complication terms, and implant migration or displacement terms. Quality-control findings

Quality-control analysis confirmed 6,386 total rows and 6,386 unique MDR_REPORT_KEY values, with no duplicate MDR_REPORT_KEY rows. There were 3,614 unique REPORT_NUMBER values and 308 duplicate REPORT_NUMBER rows. The analysis identified 5,444 unique normalized narrative text hashes. Exact duplicate normalized narrative text accounted for 1,143 duplicate text-hash rows, and repeated first-300-character snippets accounted for 3,922 rows.

The largest exact repeated text group contained 482 records (7.5% of the core dataset), with the repeated narrative “implant failed due to sinus perforation.” The top repeated first-300-character snippet appeared in 1,255 records and reflected template-like manufacturer narrative text. Category-level text uniqueness showed that sinus membrane perforation contained 6,187 records and 5,258 unique text hashes, with its top repeated first-300-character snippet representing 20.28% of that category. Other categories had smaller repeated-snippet proportions, except the oroantral communication or fistula category, where the denominator was only two records.

Overall, template-like repeated text did not appear to dominate the full dataset by exact text hash, but repeated snippets were notable within the sinus membrane perforation category and should be considered when interpreting report counts.

Sensitivity analysis findings

The unique-text sensitivity analysis retained one representative record per identical normalized narrative text hash. This reduced the dataset from 6,386 record-level observations to 5,444 unique-text observations, removing 942 records (14.75%).

The final event-category pattern was stable after exact text deduplication. Sinus membrane perforation remained the predominant rule-based report category, accounting for 6,187 records (96.88%) in the main analysis and 5,258 records (96.58%) in the unique-text analysis. Other categories changed minimally: sinus infection or sinusitis was 1.69% in the main analysis and 1.87% in the unique-text analysis; implant migration into the sinus was 1.08% and 1.14%, respectively; mixed or multiple sinus events was 0.31% and 0.37%; and oroantral communication or fistula was 0.03% and 0.04%. The maximum absolute percentage-point difference across event categories was 0.30. This stability should be interpreted as internal consistency of the text-mining output, not as evidence of true clinical frequency.

Thus, repeated or template-like narratives influenced absolute record counts but did not materially change the overall event-category distribution in this analysis. Keyword frequencies are shown in Fig. 4, and the record-level and unique-text event-category distributions are compared in Table 4.

Manual validation and inter-reviewer reliability

In the 449-record stratified manual validation sample, two independent blinded reviewers showed 98.89% agreement for inclusion status (444/449; Cohen’s κ = 0.778) and 93.99% agreement for event-category assignment (422/449; Cohen’s κ = 0.907). After consensus adjudication, 435 of 449 records were confirmed as maxillary sinus-related reports, 11 were classified as false positives, and 3 remained uncertain. Using a conservative approach in which uncertain records were not counted as confirmed positives, the consensus-confirmed PPV was 96.88% (435/449). These findings provide an independent assessment of classification reliability and support a more cautious interpretation of the algorithm-positive core dataset.

A supplementary review of 100 low-specificity-only records did not identify any records that required promotion to the high-specificity maxillary sinus-related core dataset. Thus, within this probe sample, no observed false-negative core cases were detected among low-specificity-only records. Because this review was sample-based rather than exhaustive, residual false-negative classification cannot be excluded.

Discussion

Principal findings

This retrospective text-mining analysis characterized maxillary sinus-related adverse event reports associated with endosseous dental implants with FDA product code DZE in the MAUDE database. From 3,386,630 unique DZE MDR reports, the workflow linked FOITEXT narratives, applied staged sinus-related screening, refined high-specificity candidates, and classified 6,386 final core records. The main finding was that sinus membrane perforation narratives were the predominant report category within this high-specificity rule-based report set, while sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, mixed sinus-related events, and oroantral communication or fistula were present in smaller numbers.

The revised validation strengthened the methodological reliability of the workflow. In a 449-record stratified quality-control sample, two independent blinded reviewers showed substantial agreement for inclusion status (Cohen’s κ = 0.778) and almost perfect agreement for event-category assignment (Cohen’s κ = 0.907). After consensus adjudication, the conservative PPV was 96.88% (435/449). This independent validation directly addresses the possibility that algorithm-positive records may include false positives or uncertain cases and supports a more cautious interpretation of classification performance.

Interpretation of sinus membrane perforation predominance

The predominance of sinus membrane perforation should be interpreted with particular caution. It indicates that perforation-related text was the predominant category among reports identified by this predefined high-specificity MAUDE workflow; it does not indicate that sinus membrane perforation is the most frequent clinical complication by incidence. Several factors may have contributed to this pattern. Sinus membrane perforation is a clinically recognizable event in posterior maxillary implant surgery and sinus floor elevation procedures, and clinical literature has long discussed its occurrence and management [34, 35]. In addition, phrases such as “sinus perforation” or “membrane perforation” are relatively standardized and easy for rule-based text-mining to detect. Manufacturer or procedural templates may also repeatedly describe implant failure or removal in relation to sinus perforation, increasing the visibility of this category in public report narratives. Operator reporting behavior, medico-legal documentation incentives, and routine documentation of intraoperative membrane events during sinus augmentation may also contribute to why perforation-related narratives are more visible in MAUDE than less standardized postoperative complications.

The duplicate-text and unique-text analyses support a cautious interpretation. Exact duplicate narrative hashes and repeated first-300-character snippets were present, especially within the sinus membrane perforation category. After retaining one representative record per normalized narrative text hash, the dataset decreased from 6,386 records to 5,444 unique-text observations, but the event-category pattern changed minimally. This suggests that exact duplicate narrative text did not solely drive the broad pattern, while template-like reporting still influenced absolute report counts and the apparent magnitude of perforation-related reporting.

The influence of the rule architecture can be partly, but not completely, quantified from the validation and sensitivity analyses. Exact duplicate-text deduplication changed the sinus membrane perforation proportion from 96.88% to 96.58%, an absolute difference of 0.30% points, indicating that exact narrative duplication alone did not explain the predominance. Independent blinded validation identified 11 false-positive and 3 uncertain records among 449 algorithm-positive records, yielding a conservative PPV of 96.88%, while the 100-record low-specificity-only review identified no records requiring promotion into the final core dataset. These findings suggest that the observed predominance was not solely an artifact of exact duplication or obvious low-specificity leakage. However, they do not fully quantify semantic repetition, variable reporting practices, or all possible false negatives across the full initial keyword-hit set; therefore, classification architecture and keyword detectability remain important interpretive limitations.

Clinical meaning of lower-frequency categories

Sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, and oroantral communication or fistula were less frequent in the final rule-based output but remain clinically important. Sinus graft infection, postoperative maxillary sinusitis, odontogenic sinusitis after dental implantation, displaced implants, and oroantral communication or fistula have all been addressed in clinical studies and reviews [36–44]. These categories should also be distinguished clinically from expected or immediately managed intraoperative sinus membrane perforations. A limited intraoperative perforation may be repaired during sinus floor elevation, whereas postoperative sinusitis, implant migration, foreign-body displacement, or oroantral communication may require medication, endoscopic or intraoral retrieval, closure procedures, or longer follow-up. Recent technical literature on bony-window access for retrieving foreign bodies from the maxillary sinus further illustrates the importance of conservative access design and preservation or repositioning of access bone when feasible [45]. Lower MAUDE report frequency should therefore not be interpreted as lower clinical importance, true rarity, or absence of underreporting. These categories may use more heterogeneous terminology, may be managed through different clinical pathways, or may be reported without explicit maxillary sinus wording.

Contribution to postmarket surveillance and data-quality assessment

The scientific contribution of this study is not the discovery of previously unknown sinus-related complications. Rather, the study provides a transparent and reproducible description of how maxillary sinus-related events appear in a large public postmarket surveillance database. The workflow shows how device-code filtering, FOITEXT linkage, high-specificity rule refinement, duplicate/template-text assessment, independent validation, and unique-text sensitivity analysis can be combined to characterize reporting patterns in a dataset that is too large for unaided manual review. This approach is consistent with broader natural-language-processing and text-mining work in adverse-event and patient-safety reporting, and may help regulators, clinicians, and researchers identify recurring narrative themes, evaluate data-quality problems, and design more standardized adverse-event reporting frameworks [46, 47].

The annual report pattern should also be interpreted as a reporting pattern rather than a clinical trend. Report counts increased after 2019 and peaked in 2024 in the analytic dataset, but these changes may reflect reporting behavior, data availability, manufacturer reporting practices, regulatory processes, product use patterns, narrative completeness, and MAUDE file structure. FDA documentation and recent MAUDE-based research have emphasized that adverse-event reporting may be affected by exemptions, alternative reporting requirements, and late reporting [48]. Therefore, annual counts cannot establish temporal changes in underlying clinical event occurrence.

Manufacturer and brand fields

Manufacturer and brand fields were retained as descriptive report-field summaries but should not be interpreted as safety signals. MAUDE lacks denominators for implants placed, posterior maxillary procedures, sinus augmentation exposure, market share, procedure volume, device utilization, and manufacturer-specific reporting practices. Recorded manufacturer counts may also reflect differences in internal coding, product volume, geographic distribution, reporting completeness, or template language. Passive device surveillance systems can be affected by duplicate reports and incomplete information [49]. For these reasons, manufacturer and brand distributions were de-emphasized in the main text and should not be read as comparative risk, product safety ranking, manufacturer performance, or causality.

Limitations and false-negative risk

This study has important limitations. MAUDE is a passive surveillance system and is subject to underreporting, reporting bias, incomplete information, duplicate reporting, delayed reporting, and variable narrative quality. The database does not provide denominators for exposed devices, procedures, patients, posterior maxillary implant placements, sinus floor elevation procedures, grafting procedures, or manufacturer-specific device exposure. As a result, this study cannot estimate incidence, prevalence, event probability, comparative risk, manufacturer safety ranking, or causality. Structured fields may also be missing or unknown; in this dataset, EVENT_TYPE was unknown for 2,606 final core records (40.81%). General MAUDE guidance also notes that duplicate reports and difficulty distinguishing report sources can complicate interpretation [50].

False-negative classification is also possible. The workflow intentionally prioritized high specificity to improve the credibility of the final core dataset, but this design may have reduced sensitivity. A 100-record low-specificity-only review did not identify records requiring promotion into the final high-specificity core dataset, which provides a limited check against obvious false-negative leakage. Nevertheless, clinically relevant sinus-related reports may still have been missed if narratives used non-standard terminology, abbreviations, spelling variation, vague descriptions, negation, or broader infection or complication wording without explicit maxillary sinus terms. This limitation may be category-dependent: sinus membrane perforation is often described using standardized and readily detectable language, whereas sinusitis, implant migration, and oroantral communication or fistula may be described with more variable wording. Therefore, methodological choices and rule-based detectability may have contributed to the predominance of sinus membrane perforation in the classified output.

The product-code strategy also has limitations. Restricting the device scope to DZE focused the analysis on endosseous dental implant reports, but it may have missed reports involving abutments, graft materials, membranes, sinus instruments, or other related devices coded outside DZE. In addition, clinical variables such as residual bone height, sinus floor elevation approach, graft material, implant position, implant dimensions, sinus pathology, radiographic confirmation, treatment course, and long-term outcome were generally unavailable in public MAUDE text files.

Implications and future work

Despite these limitations, the findings help characterize themes and data-quality issues in public adverse event reports related to posterior maxillary implant therapy and the maxillary sinus. For clinicians, the practical message is not that MAUDE establishes the relative frequency of sinus complications, but that adverse-event narratives commonly document perforation-related events while less frequent categories such as postoperative sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, and oroantral communication or fistula remain clinically important and may require targeted vigilance. For researchers and surveillance analysts, the results show how high-specificity text-mining, duplicate-text assessment, independent validation, and unique-text sensitivity analysis can make passive-report data more interpretable while preserving caution about underreporting and missing denominators. Future work should compare MAUDE-derived report themes with clinical studies, implant registries, systematic reviews, and carefully adjudicated case series to better contextualize the descriptive patterns observed here.

Conclusions

In this retrospective MAUDE text-mining analysis of endosseous dental implant reports with FDA product code DZE, 6,386 high-specificity maxillary sinus-related candidate reports were identified from 2015 to 2025. Among reports detected by this predefined rule-based workflow, sinus membrane perforation narratives were the predominant reporting pattern, whereas sinus infection or sinusitis, implant migration into the sinus, mixed sinus-related events, and oroantral communication or fistula were less frequent but clinically meaningful categories. Independent blinded validation supported the relevance of the high-specificity algorithm, and unique-text sensitivity analysis showed that exact repeated narratives did not materially change the category-level output. These findings characterize reporting patterns within a passive surveillance database and should not be interpreted as clinical event epidemiology, incidence, comparative risk, manufacturer safety ranking, or causal evidence.
