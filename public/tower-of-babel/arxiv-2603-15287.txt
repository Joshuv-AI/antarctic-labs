Numerical study of the sharp stratification limit towards bilayer models
Théo Fradin

Abstract
. In the study of oceanic flows at the geophysical scale, the phenomenon of density stratification plays a central role in the dynamics of the system. Two categories of mathematical models are commonly used to describe the role played by the density stratification: on the one hand, continuously stratified models - such as the stratified Euler equations in a strip, considered in the present article - offer an accurate description of vertical effects, but come with a high level of complexity, both at the theoretical and numerical levels. On the other hand, bilayer models approximate the stratification by a piecewise constant profile. In the latter case, the main point is to study the evolution of the free interface between both layers, which leads to a substantially simplified model. In the present article, we compare both approaches in the framework of the linearized stratified Euler equations around density profiles that are close to piecewise constant profiles, and prove the convergence towards the bilayer Euler equations. However, in the presence of a shear flow, bilayer models have a range of validity limited by the presence of Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. In this case, we use a suitable normal modes decomposition to compute numerically the dispersion relation of this linearized model, and provide numerical evidence that the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities limit the applicability of two widely used bilayer models, namely the bilayer Euler equations and the bilayer shallow-water equations.

1.
Introduction

In this article, we study the phenomenon of vertical density stratification in the ocean, and its effect on gravity waves. At the geophysical scale, density variations in the ocean are often considered as a small perturbation of a smooth, strictly decreasing density profile
\underline{\rho}
that only depends on the vertical variable
z
; we then speak of a continuous and (strictly) stable stratification. Then, one can write the incompressible stratified Euler equations to describe the evolution of the water velocity and density; in this study these equations are posed in the 2D domain
\mdmathbb{T}_{L}\times[-H,0]
, where
\mdmathbb{T}_{L}
is the one-dimensional torus of length
2\pi L
, for some
L>0
, and where
H>0
is the depth of the domain; our setting is summarized in Figure
1
. These equations can easily be extended to other domains, including 3D ones. However, our numerical strategy is implemented for the strip
\mdmathbb{T}_{L}\times[-H,0]
so that we restrict to this case for the sake of conciseness. Because we focus on the stratification phenomenon, we neglect other ingredients, despite their major role in the description of the ocean at the geophysical scale (for instance, Coriolis forcing, wind stress, non-flat topography, vertical boundaries).

At the non-linear level, local well-posedness of the stratified Euler equations in finite regularity Sobolev spaces has been proven recently, see
[
DLS20
]
and
[
Fra24
]
for studies close to our setting, and for further references. However, the study of the qualitative behavior of these equations, even at the linear level, is quite involved, and often reduced to specific density profiles
\underline{\rho}
. One typical profile is when
\underline{\rho}
is a decreasing exponential with height, which we call homogeneous stratification; in this case, the so-called Brunt-Väisälä frequency does not depend on
z
, which simplifies parts of the analysis. In the present study, we do not make this assumption. Also, the numerical resolution of the stratified Euler equations can be costly (see however
[
VC25
]
for a study on the large time behavior using direct numerical simulations, and references within).

It is thus natural to write reduced models that still capture the dynamics of the system at hand. It is well known in oceanography (see
[
Val17
, Part I, Section 3]
) that a ubiquitous density profile consists of two layers of (almost) constant density. These two layers are separated by a thin layer of thickness
\delta>0
, called the pycnocline, where the density gradient is very large, see the profile
\underline{\rho}
in Figure
2
- we call such a profile a sharp stratification. In this case it is very common to consider bilayer models, where two layers of constant density (with the density of the upper layer smaller than the density of the lower layer) are separated by an interface. In the irrotational case, such models can be reduced to the study of the evolution of quantities that do not depend on
z
, such as the bilayer irrotational Euler equations (sometimes called the interfacial waves equations), or the bilayer shallow-water equations (see
[
Duc22
]
). Then their numerical resolution is much cheaper, and
their modal analysis at the linear level can be carried out analytically.

In this study we ask whether bilayer models provide a good approximation of the stratified Euler equations, with continuous density variations, in the so-called sharp stratification limit, i.e. when
\delta\to 0
. Throughout this article, we consider linear models, and we distinguish two distinct frameworks.

In the case where there is no shear velocity at equilibrium between both layers, we briefly show that the linear bilayer Euler equations are well-posed in finite-regularity Sobolev spaces. Then we rigorously prove the convergence of solutions of the linear stratified Euler equations towards the linear bilayer Euler equations, in the sharp stratification limit
\delta\to 0
. More precisely, we provide quantitative estimates between the solutions of the aforementioned continuous and bilayer models, in this linear case and without a shear velocity between both layers; this what we call
full justification
, see Section
6.1
.

However, this simple setting fails to take shear into account, which naturally arises either from the non-linear dynamics or from the presence of a background current. This is why we also consider the stratified Euler equations, linearized around a horizontal velocity profile
\underline{V}
that only depends on the vertical variable
z
, and no vertical velocity; such a profile is called a shear flow. We then study the profile
\underline{V}_{\delta}
given in Figure
2
of a sharp shear profile: in the limit
\delta\to 0
, the profile
\underline{V}_{\delta}
converges towards a piecewise constant function of height, with one discontinuity, at the interface between both layers. In the homogeneous case (i.e. with no density variations), the so-called Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities associated to a shear flow of the form of
\underline{V}_{\delta}
have been partially described through a rigorous spectral analysis, see
[
Lin03
,
KO25
]
.

In the limit
\delta\to 0
, i.e. for piecewise constant density profile and shear flow, the expected system is the bilayer Euler equations. This system suffers from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. In this bilayer setting, these instabilities are strong enough to prevent the well-posedness in Sobolev spaces (see
[
ITT97
]
,
[
KL05
]
, as well as
[
Duc22
, Chapter A, Section 3.5]
and references within). Recall that this is in contrast with well-posedness results on the stratified Euler equations, and in particular with
[
Fra24
]
, which includes a non-zero shear flow. However the latter result is not uniform in
\delta
. Still, well-posedness of the bilayer Euler equations holds in analytic function spaces, (see for instance
[
SSBF81
]
and
[
CO86
]
for the vortex sheet problem, which is closely related to the bilayer Euler equations, and in particular suffers from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities as well).

An important question is to accurately describe the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. In
[
Lan13a
]
, the author shows that the (non-linear) bilayer Euler equations including surface tension are well-posed in Sobolev spaces. Moreover, the amount of surface tension that is sufficient to ensure well-posedness uniformly in the shallow-water parameter vanishes in the shallow-water limit, consistently with the fact that the non-linear bilayer shallow-water equations are well-posed, even without surface tension, as a quasilinear strictly hyperbolic system, under some hyperbolicity criterion (see
[
Ovs79
]
as well as
[
Duc22
, Chapter B, Section 6.2]
and references within). It should however be mentioned that surface tension is relevant for water-air interfaces, when the air is considered to be an incompressible fluid with a small but non-zero density, but is not expected to be present in water-water interfaces.

Considering now the link between the stratified Euler equations and the bilayer Euler equations, the author in
[
Jam01
]
justified the sharp stratification limit for traveling waves. Recently, the authors in
[
ABD24
]
studied the sharp stratification limit for the hydrostatic stratified Euler equations (closely related to the stratified Euler equations, considered in the present study). Their analysis strongly relies on the presence of eddy diffusivity, which regularizes the system. In order to prove their convergence result, they write a new reduced model that takes into account some effects in the pycnocline, we refer to
[
ABD24
]
for more details on their model, as well as other physical models that aim at describing phenomena happening inside a thin pycnocline.

In the present study, we provide a comparison of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities in both the stratified Euler equations, linearized around the shear flow
\underline{V}_{\delta}
, and bilayer models, thanks to a numerical description of the dispersion relation of the continuous model. We provide numerical evidence that the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities are present in the linearized stratified Euler equations. Moreover, to some extent, they are well described by the bilayer Euler equations, linearized around a shear flow. As a consequence, we conjecture that the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities prevent any full justification of the sharp stratification limit from the stratified Euler equations in the presence of a shear flow as the one in Figure
2
(see also (
6.6
)) towards the bilayer Euler equations, in the context of finite-regularity Sobolev spaces. Note that this fact is expected, as the bilayer Euler equations with a shear flow are ill-posed in Sobolev spaces. However our findings also indicate that the sharp stratification limit towards the bilayer shallow-water equations cannot be fully justified in the setting of Sobolev spaces, despite the fact that the latter model is well-posed in Sobolev spaces.

The setting of a sharp stratification as in (
6.1
) with two layers separated by a pycnocline of thickness
\delta>0
falls within the framework of diffuse interfaces, in which one aims to study the case where a scalar quantity (e.g. density, volume fraction, concentration of some chemical) is not piecewise constant, with a sharp interface separating both regions, but rather varies continuously (see for instance the introduction in
[
SNK13
]
and references within). However, the stratified Euler equations that we consider in the present study are in the inviscid setting, and as such are different from the classical models studied in the diffuse interface framework. A large variety of such models strongly rely on diffusion of some quantity in the system, see for instance
[
AGP24
]
(and references within) in which the authors study the Cahn-Hilliard and Allen-Cahn equations coupled with the Navier-Stokes equations. The sharp interface limit is considered for these models in
[
SNK13
,
AFM24
,
Sch14
]
. In the case where the scalar considered is the density, one can add capillarity effects for a compressible viscous fluid, described by the so-called Navier-Stokes-Korteweg equations. Such equations include both viscous and dispersive effects, and have been studied, alongside the sharp interface limit, in
[
DGKR12
,
Dau16
]
. When one neglects viscosity, the equations are the Euler-Korteweg equations, see
[
BGDDJ05
,
BGC18
,
BG10
]
. If these describe an inviscid fluid, they include regularizing dispersive effects, which are absent from the system (
3.32
) at hand here.

The outline of the paper is as follows. In Section
3
, we introduce the stratified Euler equations. We give a formulation in the so-called isopycnal coordinates, which, roughly speaking, aim at describing the level sets of the density (called isopycnals) rather than the density itself. Although this formulation is equivalent to the more standard Eulerian coordinates, it is well-suited to compare the solutions in the continuous and bilayer cases: in the latter situation, the interface separating the two layers can be thought of as an isopycnal. Such coordinates prove to be particularly useful in the non-linear setting, which is outside of the scope of the present study, but was considered in
[
ABD24
]
- in the hydrostatic setting and with an additional regularization. We then introduce a normal mode decomposition, adapted to the stratified Euler equations, and reformulate the equations into a system of coupled equations on each mode. We provide an energy estimate on this coupled system of evolution equations, and highlight the crucial role of the dispersion in this case.

In Section
4
, we briefly describe the linear bilayer Euler equations, with and without a shear flow. We also give a simplified formulation in the irrotational case (or more precisely, when the vorticity is concentrated on the interface), which we use to perform simulations. Finally, we state a well-posedness result, in the absence of a shear flow.

In Section
5
, we introduce our numerical strategy for solving the stratified Euler equations. Namely, we consider the (coupled) evolution a finite number of the aforementioned modes, as a vertical discretization. We then use truncated Fourier series as a horizontal discretization and a Runge-Kutta method for the time discretization. We also explain how this discretization is particularly convenient to compute an approximation of the dispersion relation of the stratified Euler equations. We then prove in Section
5.2
the convergence of the (semi-discrete) numerical scheme, and provide in Section
5.3
numerical illustrations, which in particular show how the well-known Saint-Andrew cross pattern for the propagation of internal waves is affected by the presence of a pycnocline of small but non-zero thickness. Finally, in Section
5.4
, we describe our numerical strategy for the linear, irrotational bilayer Euler equations.

Section
6
is the core of our study. In Section
6.1
, we quantify the error between solutions of the stratified Euler equations (with
\underline{V}=0
) and of the bilayer Euler equations (
4.10
), in Sobolev spaces. The proof of the statements in this subsection are given in Appendix
B
, and relies on energy estimates on the difference between solutions of both models. In order to study such a difference, isopycnal coordinates are crucial, as they transfer the natural framework in the bilayer case (i.e. the study of the evolution of the interface between both layers) to the stratified case, by shifting the focus from the density field to the isopycnals. Then we provide a numerical approximation of the dispersion relation of the stratified Euler equations with the profile
\underline{V}_{\delta}
of Figure
2
, in Section
6.2
. More precisely, we plot the phase velocities associated to the first horizontal frequencies, and in particular highlight the presence of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. Some consistency checks are provided in Appendix
C
. We then give some quantitative results on the features of the dispersion relation computed in the previous section. Overall, we find that the dispersion relations of the stratified Euler equations in the sharp stratification limit and the bilayer Euler equations, both with a shear flow, share several common features. In particular, the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities are present in the stratified Euler equations, and are well approximated by the instabilities present in the bilayer Euler equations. Finally in Section
6.3
, we point out the consequences of the presence of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities in the stratified Euler equations, for the full justification of bilayer models: because of the presence of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities in the stratified Euler equations, our findings suggest that we can find growing modes whose growth rate goes to infinity when
\delta
goes to
0
. We discuss the implications for the full justification of the bilayer Euler equations, but also the bilayer shallow-water equations, from the stratified Euler equations, in the sharp stratification limit.

Eventually, in Section
7
, we summarize our findings and discuss some perspectives.

3.
The linear stratified Euler equations, and its normal modes

In this section we introduce the stratified Euler equations, as well as the isopycnal change of coordinates. This is done in Section
3.1
, where we write the stratified Euler equations linearized around a zero shear flow (
3.6
). Then, in Section
3.2
, we write a normal mode decomposition that is adapted to the density profile
\underline{\rho}
we consider. In order to find these normal modes, we first write the Taylor-Goldstein equation (
3.9
), which describes internal waves, and is derived directly from the stratified Euler equations. From the Taylor-Goldstein equation, a Sturm-Liouville problem arises naturally, and it yields the desired normal modes. Using this decomposition, the stratified Euler equations reduce to the system of coupled equations (
3.20
), and we study the coupling operator in Proposition
3.4
. Then in Section
3.3
, we provide the same modal decomposition for the stratified Euler equations linearized around a shear flow
\underline{V}
, so that it also reduces to a system of coupled equations. We then provide an energy estimate on this system in Proposition
3.7
, highlighting in particular the role of the dispersion in the stability of the system.

3.1.
The stratified Euler equations in isopycnal coordinates

In this section we write the Euler equations in our context, and explain the isopycnal change of coordinates. We decompose the density of the fluid as

\rho_{\textrm{total}}(t,x,z)\coloneq\underline{\rho}(z)+\rho(t,x,z),

where
t\in[0,T]
(for some
T>0
) denotes the time variable,
x\in\mdmathbb T_{L}
is the horizontal space variable in the 1D torus of length
2\pi L
, and
z\in[-H,0]
is the vertical space variable, with
H>0
the depth of the strip. Note that the fluid domain is assumed to be the strip
\mdmathbb T_{L}\times[-H,0]
, so that in particular we consider a flat bottom and make the rigid lid approximation. The function
\underline{\rho}
only depends on
z
and is the density at equilibrium of the fluid at hand.

Then, the total density of the fluid
\rho_{\textrm{total}}
is entirely described by the deviation
\rho
of the density from the equilibrium
\underline{\rho}
. In particular,
\rho
might depend on
t,x
and
z
.
We write
V
and
w
for the horizontal and vertical velocities of the fluid respectively; the notations and the setting are summarized in Figure
1
(although in the slightly more general case where we consider a non-zero shear flow
\underline{V}
, see Section
3.3
).
Then, the (non-linear) Euler equations in this context read

(3.1)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V+V\partial_{x}V+w\partial_{z}V+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}+\rho}\partial_{x}P&=0,\\
\partial_{t}w+V\partial_{x}w+w\partial_{z}w+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}+\rho}\partial_{z}P+\frac{g\rho}{\underline{\rho}+\rho}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\rho+V\partial_{x}\rho+w\partial_{z}\rho+w\underline{\rho}^{\prime}&=0,\\
\partial_{x}V+\partial_{z}w&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }(0,T)\times\mdmathbb T_{L}\times(-H,0);

here,
P
denotes the pressure of the fluid. It is understood as the Lagrange multiplier of the divergence-free constraint, and as such it can be expressed from the unknowns
V,w,\rho
through an elliptic equation, which we do not write here. We refer to Appendix
B
for its precise definition, in the linear setting. The system (
3.1
) is completed with the boundary conditions

(3.2)

\displaystyle w_{|z=-H}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad w_{|z=0}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\rho_{|z=-H}

\displaystyle=0,\qquad\rho_{|z=0}

\displaystyle=0.

Throughout the present study, the density profile
\underline{\rho}
is assumed to be smooth and strictly decreasing, more precisely, there exists
c_{*}>0
such that

(3.3)

\immediate\immediate-\underline{\rho}^{\prime}\geq c_{*},

where the prime denotes the (vertical) derivative. Such an assumption is standard for the study of oceanic flows at geophysical scales (see
[
Val17
]
), as it prevents the Rayleigh-Taylor instability - which takes place at too small time and space scales to be relevant for the large-scale circulation. Using such an assumption, the well-posedness of (
3.1
) together with the boundary conditions (
3.2
) and suitable initial conditions in Sobolev spaces is studied in
[
DLS20
]
and
[
Fra24
]
.

Let us linearize (
3.1
) around the trivial equilibrium
(V,w,\rho,P)=(0,0,0,0)
, and apply the so-called isopycnal change of coordinates, which we now describe. We assume that the total density is stably stratified, that is

(3.4)

\immediate\immediate-\partial_{z}(\underline{\rho}+\rho)\geq c_{*}>0.

Thus, given
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mdmathbb T_{L}
, the function
z\mapsto\underline{\rho}(z)+\rho(t,x,z)
admits an inverse, so that we can choose
\eta
depending on
t,x
and
r\in[-H,0]
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}(r+\eta(t,x,r))+\rho(t,x,r+\eta(t,x,r))

\displaystyle=\underline{\rho}(r);

the precise form
r+\eta(t,x,r)
of the inverse of
\underline{\rho}+\rho
follows from the fact that
\underline{\rho}
only depends on
r
, see
[
Fra24
]
for more details. The linearized Euler equations written in isopycnal coordinates then read

(3.6)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{x}P&=0,\\
\partial_{t}w+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}P-\frac{g\underline{\rho}^{\prime}}{\underline{\rho}}\eta&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\eta-w&=0,\\
\partial_{x}V+\partial_{r}w&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }(0,T)\times S;

where
S\coloneq\mdmathbb T_{L}\times(-H,0)
is the domain of the fluid in isopycnal coordinates. We refer once again to
[
Fra24
]
for details on the linearization process and the isopycnal change of coordinates. The isopycnal coordinates are well-suited to study the sharp stratification limit, see Section
6
.
Finally, the system (
3.6
) is completed with the boundary conditions

(3.7)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} &w_{|r=-H}=w_{|r=0}=0,\\
&\eta_{|r=-H}=\eta_{|r=0}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

stemming from the impermeability condition at the flat surface and flat bottom, and the assumption that the surface and the bottom are flat and isopycnals (i.e. level-sets of the density
\underline{\rho}+\rho
). Both assumptions are propagated by the flow. Finally, we impose initial conditions

(3.8)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} &V_{|t=0}=V_{\mathrm{in}},\qquad&w_{|t=0}=w_{\mathrm{in}},\\
&\eta_{|t=0}=\eta_{\mathrm{in}},\end{aligned}\right.

that satisfy the incompressibility condition in (
3.6
) and the boundary conditions (
3.7
). The well-posedness of the system (
3.6
) together with (
3.7
) and initial conditions (
3.8
) in suitable Sobolev spaces is studied in Appendix

(see Proposition

). See also
[
Fra24
]
in the non-linear setting and
[
BD24
]
for a similar study with an additional diffusion term.

3.2.
Normal modes

Given a solution
(V,w,\eta)
to (
3.6
), simple algebraic manipulations show that
w
satisfies the Taylor-Goldstein equation

(3.9)

\immediate\immediate\partial_{t}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}(\underline{\rho}\partial_{r}w)+\partial_{x}^{2}w\right)+N^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}w=0,

where the quantity

(3.10)

\immediate\immediate N^{2}\coloneq-g\frac{\underline{\rho}^{\prime}}{\underline{\rho}}

is the (square of the) Brunt-Väisälä frequency. Note that
N^{2}
depends on
r
a priori. We reproduce the derivation of (
3.9
) in Appendix
A
, for the sake of completeness.

Complemented with Dirichlet boundary conditions, (
3.9
) is the starting point of the study of internal waves. However, solving (
3.9
) is a difficult task in general. Roughly speaking, a standard strategy (see for instance
[
DLS20
]
) is to start with the study of the first and last terms in (
3.9
). More precisely, we can define
(c_{n},f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
as the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the following Sturm-Liouville problem

(3.11)

\immediate\immediate(\underline{\rho}f_{n}^{\prime})^{\prime}=-\underline{\rho}N^{2}\frac{1}{c_{n}^{2}}f_{n}\qquad\text{ in }(-H,0),

with Dirichlet boundary conditions at
r=-H
and
r=0
. We also define
(g_{n})_{n\geq 0}
by

(3.12)

\immediate\immediate g_{n}\coloneq\left\{\begin{aligned} &c_{n}f^{\prime}_{n}\qquad&\text{ if }n\geq 1,\\
&\left(\int_{-H}^{0}\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\qquad&\text{ if }n=0.\end{aligned}\right.

There is a vast literature studying the modes for internal waves, so that we only cite a few studies that are close to our framework, and refer to them for further references. In
[
Ban05
]
, the author studies the extension of (
3.9
) to the case of a non-zero shear flow. In
[
GZ08
, Chapter 5]
the authors describe the modes from the Sturm-Liouville problem (
3.11
) in several configurations. The modal decomposition from eigenfunctions of the extension of (
3.9

We sum up the basic properties of

in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that there exists
c_{*}>0
such that
\underline{\rho}\geq c_{*}
, and such that (

•

The family

•

There exists
C>0
a constant that does not depend on

(3.13)

\immediate\immediate|f_{n}|_{L^{\infty}([-H,0])}\leq C.

•

The family

is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\underline{\rho}}([-H,0])
.

•

For
n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
,
c_{n}>0
, and we have the asymptotic behavior for

, for some constant

(3.14)

\immediate\immediate nc_{n}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}c.

Proof.

The proof of Lemma

[

]
for the present context, and
[

]
for (

. Recall that

is a smooth function, as it is the solution of a (regular) ODE, so that the following computations make sense. Multiplying (

\immediate\immediate|f^{\prime}_{n}|_{L^{2}_{\underline{\rho}}}^{2}=\frac{1}{c_{n}^{2}}.

Now multiplying (

\immediate\immediate\left((\underline{\rho}f^{\prime}_{n})^{2}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{\underline{\rho}^{2}N^{2}}{c_{n}^{2}}(f_{n}^{2})^{\prime}=0.

Let

\immediate\immediate(\underline{\rho}f^{\prime}_{n})^{2}(r)+\frac{\underline{\rho}^{2}(r)N^{2}(r)}{c_{n}^{2}}f_{n}^{2}(r)=(\underline{\rho}f^{\prime}_{n})^{2}(-H)+\frac{1}{c_{n}^{2}}\int_{-H}^{r}\left(\underline{\rho}^{2}(r)N^{2}(r)\right)^{\prime}f_{n}^{2}(r)dr.

to

\immediate\immediate(\underline{\rho}f^{\prime}_{n})^{2}(-H)\leq\frac{C}{c_{n}^{2}}.

Combining (

We now decompose

along these bases, and write

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \left((I-M\partial^{2}_{x})\partial_{t}V\right)_{n}+c_{n}\partial_{x}\eta_{n}&=0,\\

, and

is the linear operator on

\displaystyle M:\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{N}^{*})

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{N}^{*})

\displaystyle(V_{n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}

\displaystyle\longmapsto\left(\sum_{m\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}M_{n,m}V_{m}\right)_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}},

with, for

Remark 3.2
.

Note that (

) is a closed system of equations on the coefficients on

and
\eta
, and we do not need to solve for the coefficients of

and

and

can be recovered from those of

and

from the elliptic equation satisfied by the pressure (see (

)) and the divergence-free condition, respectively.

Remark 3.3
.

We do not write an equation for the evolution of

. Indeed, taking the scalar product with weight
\underline{\rho}
of the divergence-free condition in (

) with

yields

Then, taking the scalar product with weight

Basic properties of the operator
M
are summarized in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that there exist constants
c^{*},c_{*}>0
such that
c_{*}\leq\underline{\rho}\leq c^{*}
and
-\underline{\rho}^{\prime}\geq c_{*}
. Then there exists
C>0
such that the following holds. The operator
M
is a linear, bounded operator on
\ell^{2}
, and for
(u_{n})_{n}\in\ell^{2}

(3.23)

\immediate\immediate|M(u_{n})_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C|(c_{n}u_{n})_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}.

The operator
M
is symmetric, i.e. for
(u_{n})_{n},(v_{n})_{n}\in\ell^{2}

(3.24)

\immediate\immediate\langle M(u_{n})_{n},(v_{n})_{n}\rangle=\langle(u_{n})_{n},M(v_{n})_{n}\rangle.

The operator
M
has an ellipticity property; more precisely, for
(u_{n})_{n}\in\ell^{2}
,

(3.25)

\immediate\immediate\langle M(u_{n})_{n},(u_{n})_{n}\rangle\geq\frac{1}{C}|c_{n}u_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

As a consequence, it admits a unique positive square root denoted by
M^{\frac{1}{2}}
, i.e. such that
(M^{\frac{1}{2}})^{2}=M
, which satisfies

(3.26)

\immediate\immediate\frac{1}{C}|(c_{n}u_{n})_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}\leq|M^{\frac{1}{2}}u|_{\ell^{2}}\leq C|(c_{n}u_{n})_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}.

Finally, in the special case where
N^{2}
does not depend on the vertical variable
r
, then
M
is a diagonal operator, namely

(3.27)

\displaystyle M_{n,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{n}^{2}}{N^{2}}\qquad\text{ if }n=m,

\displaystyle M_{n,m}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{ if }n\neq m.

In the case where
N^{2}
is independent of
r
, (
3.27
) together with (
3.20
) show that the evolution of the modes
(V_{n},\eta_{n})_{n}
is decoupled. This case has been well-studied, see for instance
[
Val17
, Chapter 7]
and references within. In particular, internal waves propagate in this case according to the Saint Andrew’s cross pattern, see Figure
4
. However, for a general stratification profile
\underline{\rho}
, the Brunt-Väisälä frequency

depends on
r
, and the operator
M
is not diagonal. Thus the evolution of the modes
((V_{n},\eta_{n}))_{n}
through (
3.20
) is coupled. This coupling is referred to as a dispersive coupling, see
[
DLS20
]
.

Proof.

For
n,m\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*},
the definition (
3.22
) of
M_{n,m}
and the fact that
(f_{n})_{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\underline{\rho}N^{2}}
yield

(3.28)

\immediate\immediate|M_{n,m}|\leq\left|\frac{1}{N^{2}}\right|_{L^{\infty}([-H,0])}c_{n}c_{m}.

Thus, there exists a constant
C_{0}>0
such that, for any
n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
:

|(Mu)_{n}|^{2}\leq\sum_{m}|M_{n,m}u_{m}|^{2}\leq C_{0}c_{n}^{2}|(c_{m}u_{m})_{m\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Because of (
3.14
), the series
\sum_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}c_{n}^{2}
converges, and thus there exists
C>0
such that (
3.23
) holds.

Then, (
3.24
) is an immediate consequence of (
3.22
). For (
3.25
), recall that, for
n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
,
|f_{n}|
is bounded uniformly in
n
, i.e. (
3.13
) holds. Then, we write, for

,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\left\langle Mu,u\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}c_{n}c_{m}u_{n}u_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}f_{n}f_{m}\underline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}u_{n}\sum_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}c_{m}u_{m}f_{n}f_{m}\underline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}c_{n}u_{n}\int_{-H}^{0}\sum_{m}c_{m}u_{m}f_{n}f_{m}\underline{\rho},

where we used the dominated convergence theorem for the last equality, since
f_{n}
and
f_{m}
are bounded uniformly in
n
and
m
, and
\sum_{m}|u_{m}c_{m}|
converges. The same computations yield

(3.30)

\displaystyle\sum_{n}c_{n}u_{n}\int_{-H}^{0}f_{n}\sum_{m}c_{m}u_{m}f_{m}\underline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\int_{-H}^{0}\left(\sum_{m}c_{m}u_{m}f_{m}\right)^{2}\underline{\rho}.

Using the definition (
3.10
), we get

, so that we can write

(3.31)

\displaystyle\int_{-H}^{0}\left(\sum_{m}c_{m}u_{m}f_{m}\right)^{2}\underline{\rho}

\displaystyle=\int_{-H}^{0}\left(\sum_{m}c_{m}u_{m}f_{m}\right)^{2}\frac{\underline{\rho}N^{2}}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq c_{*}/c^{*}\sum_{m,n}c_{n}c_{m}u_{n}u_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}f_{n}f_{m}\underline{\rho}N^{2}

\displaystyle\geq c_{*}/c^{*}|c_{n}u_{n}|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}

where we used once again the dominated convergence theorem and the fact that
(f_{n})_{n}
is an orthonormal basis of

. This yields (
3.25
). The existence and uniqueness of a positive square root for a bounded positive operator on a Hilbert space is standard, see for instance
[
Hal82
, Problem 121]
.

When

is constant (and positive), (
3.27
) follows from (
3.22
) and the fact that
(f_{n})_{n}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}_{\underline{\rho}N^{2}}
.
∎

3.3.
Extension to a non-zero shear flow

We now write the Euler equations in isopycnal coordinates, linearized around a shear flow. A shear flow is an equilibrium of the stratified Euler equations (
3.1
) with
V=\underline{V}
that only depends on the vertical variable. In the case
\underline{V}=0
, one finds the system (
3.6
), so that the present section is a generalization of the previous one. We assume that
\underline{V}
is a smooth function of
r
only; the Euler equations in isopycnal coordinates, linearized around a shear flow read

(3.32)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V+\underline{V}\partial_{x}V+w\underline{V}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{x}P&=0,\\
\partial_{t}w+\underline{V}\partial_{x}w+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}P-\frac{g\underline{\rho}^{\prime}}{\underline{\rho}}\eta&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\eta+\underline{V}\partial_{x}\eta-w&=0,\\
\partial_{x}V+\partial_{r}w&=0,\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{in }[0,T]\times S,

completed with the boundary conditions (
3.7
). From (
3.32
) one can derive the Taylor-Goldstein equation, which is a generalization of (
3.9
) in the presence of a shear flow:

(3.33)

\immediate\immediate(\partial_{t}+\underline{V}\partial_{x})^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}(\underline{\rho}\partial_{r}w)+\partial_{x}^{2}w\right)-(\partial_{t}+\underline{V}\partial_{x})\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}(\underline{\rho}\underline{V}^{\prime})^{\prime}\partial_{x}w+N^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}w=0;

we postpone these computations to Appendix
A
. The boundary conditions (
3.7
) on
w
complete this equation.

As for (
3.9
), the equation (
3.33
) contains the Sturm-Liouville operator
\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}\left(\underline{\rho}\partial_{r}\cdot\right)
. We thus perform the same modal decomposition as in Section
3.2
; the computations are postponed to Appendix
A
. The evolution of the modes
((V_{n},\eta_{n}))_{n}
is coupled, and is given by the following system, for
n\geq 1
:

(3.34)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} &((I-M\partial^{2}_{x})\partial_{t}V)_{n}+c_{n}\partial_{x}\eta_{n}+\left((2A^{1}+A^{2})\partial_{x}V\right)_{n}-(A^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}V)_{n}=0,\\
&\partial_{t}\eta_{n}+c_{n}\partial_{x}V_{n}+(A^{4}\partial_{x}\eta)_{n}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
M
is defined through (
3.21
)-(
3.22
) and
A^{1},A^{2},A^{3},A^{4}
are defined from their coefficients through

(3.35)

\displaystyle A^{1}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}(r)g_{n}(r)g_{m}(r)\underline{\rho}(r)dr,

\displaystyle A^{2}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\frac{c_{m}}{c_{n}}\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}(r)f_{n}(r)f_{m}(r)\underline{\rho}(r)N^{2}(r)dr,

\displaystyle A^{3}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq c_{n}c_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}(r)f_{n}(r)f_{m}(r)\underline{\rho}(r)dr,

\displaystyle A^{4}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}(r)f_{n}(r)f_{m}(r)\underline{\rho}(r)N^{2}(r)dr.

Remark 3.5
.

Notice that, with an integration by parts and (
3.11
), we can write

(3.36)

\immediate\immediate A^{1}_{n,m}+A^{2}_{n,m}=-c_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}^{\prime}c_{n}f_{n}^{\prime}f_{m}\underline{\rho},

which yields, thanks to Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
(c_{n}f^{\prime}_{n})_{n}
and
(f_{n})_{n}
are orthonormal families for some weighted scalar product, that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(3.37)

\immediate\immediate|A^{1}_{n,m}+A^{2}_{n,m}|\leq Cc_{m}.

We use this property in Proposition
3.7
. Note also that it does not hold for
A^{2}_{n,m}
when considered alone.

Owing to (
3.36
), we obtain an alternative formula for the first equation in (
3.34
):

(3.38)

\immediate\immediate((I-M\partial^{2}_{x})\partial_{t}V)_{n}+c_{n}\partial_{x}\eta_{n}+((A^{1}+\tilde{A}^{2})\partial_{x}V)_{n}-(A^{3}\partial_{x}^{3}V)_{n}=0,

where
\tilde{A}_{2}
is defined through

(3.39)

\immediate\immediate\tilde{A}^{2}_{n,m}=A^{1}_{n,m}+A^{2}_{n,m}=-c_{m}\int_{-H}^{0}\underline{V}^{\prime}c_{n}f_{n}^{\prime}f_{m}\underline{\rho}.

The advantage of
\tilde{A}^{2}_{n,m}
over
A^{2}_{n,m}
is that the terms the terms in (
3.38
) are either skew-symmetric or bounded (see the proof of Proposition
3.7
for more details), so that we can derive suitable energy estimates. Note however that (
3.39
) requires that
\underline{V}^{\prime}
is bounded, which is not the case uniformly to the small parameter
\delta
that we introduce in Section
6
.

The advantage of
A^{2}_{n,m}
over
\tilde{A}^{2}_{n,m}
is that it can be computed from
A^{4}_{n,m}
, up to a multiplication by the scalar
c_{m}/c_{n}
. Thus, computing
A^{1}_{n,m},A^{2}_{n,m},A^{3}_{n,m},A^{4}_{n,m}
requires to compute three integrals, instead of four if one replaces
A^{2}_{n,m}
by
\tilde{A}^{2}_{n,m}
. From the computational perspective, this shortens the time required to compute these coefficients, which happens to be significant in the simulations we show in Section
6.2.2
.

Remark 3.6
.

We do not write an evolution equation on

or

. Using Cauchy-Schwarz inequality on the right-hand side of (
3.44
), (
3.26
) on both sides and Grönwall’s inequality, we get (
3.41
) with
s=0
.

Let now
s\geq 0
. Applying
\partial_{x}^{s}
to (
3.34
), we find that
(\partial_{x}^{s}V_{n},\partial_{x}^{s}\eta_{n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}
satisfies (
3.34
). Therefore, applying the previous
L^{2}-
energy estimate to
(\partial_{x}^{s}V_{n},\partial_{x}^{s}\eta_{n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}
yields (
3.41
). From such an energy estimate the uniqueness of the solution follows, and its existence is a standard result that we omit here.
∎

4.
The linear bilayer Euler equations

In this section we present the bilayer Euler equations. We start with the general description of the linear bilayer Euler equations in the presence of a shear flow in Section
4.1
. Although we do not directly use this formulation in the present study, it serves as an introduction for next two sections: in Section
4.2
, we write a reformulation in the irrotational setting, which we use to perform numerical simulations. Finally, in Section
4.3
, we write the linear bilayer Euler equations without a shear flow. In this case, we state a well-posedness result in Sobolev spaces. This result will prove useful in the comparison between these linear bilayer Euler equations in the absence of a shear flow, and the linear stratified Euler equations without a shear flow, which we provide in Section
6.1
.

4.1.
The linear bilayer Euler equations with a shear flow

We start with the description of the linear bilayer Euler equations with a shear flow. The setting is summarized in Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
The bilayer setting. The unknowns
V_{\pm},w_{\pm}
are the fluid velocities in the upper and lower layer, as well as the interface
\zeta
between both layers.

These equations read

(4.1)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V_{\pm}+\underline{V}_{\pm}\partial_{x}V_{\pm}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{x}P_{\pm}+g\partial_{x}\zeta&=0,\\
\partial_{t}w_{\pm}+\underline{V}_{\pm}\partial_{x}V_{\pm}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{r}P_{\pm}&=0,\\
\partial_{x}V_{\pm}+\partial_{r}w_{\pm}&=0,\\
\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{ in }S_{+}\cup S_{-},

where we use the notations

(4.2)

\immediate\immediate V_{\pm}(t,x,r)\coloneq\left\{\begin{aligned} &V_{+}(t,x,r)\text{ if }r>r_{*},\\
&V_{-}(t,x,r)\text{ if }r<r_{*},\end{aligned}\right.

respectively
\underline{V}_{\pm},w_{\pm}
and
P_{\pm}
, for some
r_{*}\in(-H,0)
, which denotes the position of the interface at rest. The quantities
\underline{V}_{+}
and
\underline{V}_{-}
are two given constants. Here, the indices
+
and
-
refer to quantities defined respectively in the upper layer
S_{+}\coloneq\mdmathbb T_{L}\times\{r_{*}<r<0\}
and lower layer
S_{-}\coloneq\mdmathbb T_{L}\times\{-H<r<r_{*}\}
. The quantities
\rho_{+}
and
\rho_{-}
are two constants with
\rho_{+}<\rho_{-}
. We also write a kinematic equation on the motion on the interface (parametrized by
r_{*}+\zeta
) between the upper layer
S_{+}
and the lower layer
S_{-}
, which reads

(4.3)

\immediate\immediate\partial_{t}\zeta=-\underline{V}_{+}\partial_{x}\zeta+(w_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}=-\underline{V}_{-}\partial_{x}\zeta+(w_{-})_{|r=r_{*}},

which also encapsulates the continuity of the normal component of the velocity across the interface. The pressure is also continuous at the interface; we thus have two constraints on the interface, i.e.

(4.4)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} (P_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}&=(P_{-})_{|r=r_{*}},\\
-\underline{V}_{+}\partial_{x}\zeta+(w_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}&=-\underline{V}_{-}\partial_{x}\zeta+(w_{-})_{|r=r_{*}}.\end{aligned}\right.

The equations (
4.1
) are completed with the boundary conditions

(4.5)

\immediate\immediate w_{-|r=-H}=w_{+|r=0}=0,

which are the impermeability of the flat bottom and lid. The equations (
4.1
) are also completed with the initial conditions

(4.6)

\immediate\immediate V_{\pm|t=0}=V_{\pm,\mathrm{in}},\qquad w_{\pm|t=0}=w_{\pm,\mathrm{in}},\qquad\zeta_{|t=0}=\zeta_{\mathrm{in}}.

The initial data (
4.6
) should be consistent with (
4.4
), the incompressibility condition in (
4.1
) and the boundary conditions (
4.5
) , namely

(4.7)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{x}V_{\pm,\mathrm{in}}+\partial_{r}w_{\pm,\mathrm{in}}&=0,\\
\underline{V}_{+}\partial_{x}\zeta_{\mathrm{in}}-w_{+,\mathrm{in}|r=r_{*}}&=\underline{V}_{-}\partial_{x}\zeta_{\mathrm{in}}-w_{-,\mathrm{in}|r=r_{*}},\\
w_{-,\mathrm{in}|r=-H}&=w_{+,\mathrm{in}|r=0}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

4.2.
The irrotational case, with a shear flow

In this section we consider (
4.1
) in the irrotational case. We use this formulation for the numerical simulations of the bilayer Euler equations, see Section
5.4
.

In this section, we assume that
r_{*}=-H/2
, as this simplifies some formula below; these can be found in more generality in
[
Duc22

. For the same reason, we choose
\underline{V}_{+}=v,\underline{V}_{-}=-v
, for some

V_{\pm}
can be written as

V_{\pm}=\nabla_{x,r}\phi_{\pm},

where
\phi_{+}
and
\phi_{-}
are called the velocity potentials of
V_{+}
and
V_{-}
. If such an assumption is satisfied by the initial data

, then it is satisfied for a solution of (

\psi\coloneq\frac{\rho_{-}}{\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}}\phi_{-|r=r_{*}}-\frac{\rho_{+}}{\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}}\phi_{+|r=r_{*}}.

Then, the equations (
4.1
) can be recast as evolution equations on
\zeta
and
\psi
only, see for instance
[
Duc22
, Chapter A, Section 3.3]

of

(4.8)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\hat{\zeta}_{k}+ikd(k)\hat{\zeta}_{k}-b(k)\hat{\psi}_{k}&=0,\\
\partial_{t}\hat{\psi}_{k}+a(k)\hat{\zeta}_{k}+ikd(k)\hat{\psi}_{k}&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where

(4.9)

\displaystyle a(k)

\displaystyle\coloneq g\frac{\rho_{-}-\rho_{+}}{\rho_{-}+\rho_{+}}-\frac{4\rho_{-}\rho_{+}}{(\rho_{-}+\rho_{+})^{2}\tanh(|k|H/2)}v^{2}|k|

\displaystyle b(k)

\displaystyle\coloneq\tanh(|k|H/2)|k|,

\displaystyle d(k)

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{\rho_{+}-\rho_{-}}{\rho_{-}+\rho_{+}}v.

4.3.
The linear bilayer Euler equations without a shear flow

We now write the linear bilayer Euler equations, without a shear velocity (and without the irrotationality assumption), i.e. we specify (
4.1
) with
\underline{V}_{\pm}=0

(4.10)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}V_{\pm}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{x}P_{\pm}+g\partial_{x}\zeta&=0,\\
\partial_{t}w_{\pm}+\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{r}P_{\pm}&=0,\\
\partial_{x}V_{\pm}+\partial_{r}w_{\pm}&=0,\\
\end{aligned}\right.\qquad\text{ in }S_{+}\cup S_{-}.

Recall that the quantities
\rho_{+}
and
\rho_{-}
are two constants with

\immediate\immediate\partial_{t}\zeta=(w_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}=(w_{-})_{|r=r_{*}}.

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} (P_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}&=(P_{-})_{|r=r_{*}},\\

\immediate\immediate w_{-|r=-H}=w_{+|r=0}=0,

\immediate\immediate V_{\pm|t=0}=V_{\pm,\mathrm{in}},\qquad w_{\pm|t=0}=w_{\pm,\mathrm{in}},\qquad\zeta_{|t=0}=\zeta_{\mathrm{in}}.

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{x}V_{\pm,\mathrm{in}}+\partial_{r}w_{\pm,\mathrm{in}}&=0,\\

Numerical scheme

We start by describing our strategy for solving (

5.1.
Numerical scheme for the stratified Euler equations

Our numerical strategy to compute solutions of (
3.32
) is as follows.

Solving the Sturm-Liouville problem.

First, let
\underline{\rho}
be the density profile under consideration. Then, the Brunt-Väisälä frequency
N^{2}
is given analytically or given via a finite-difference approximation, through (
3.10
); we use the latter solution. We solve the eigenvalue problem (
3.11
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions, in order to find an approximation on
((c_{n},f_{n},g_{n}))_{1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}}
, for
\mathtt{N}\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
some integer. We introduce a uniform discretization of
[-H,0]
: for
\mathtt{m_{r}}\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
, we write
r_{j}=-jH/\mathtt{m_{r}}
, with
j\in\{0,\dots,\mathtt{m_{r}}\}
, and
h\coloneq H/\mathtt{m_{r}}
.

Using a finite-difference scheme, solving the eigenvalue problem (
3.11
) with Dirichlet boundary conditions boils down to solving the following generalized eigenvalue problem

(5.1)

\immediate\immediate\frac{1}{h^{2}}SY=-\lambda DY,

where

(5.2)

\immediate\immediate S=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{0}&\beta_{0}&0&\cdots&0\\
\beta_{0}&\alpha_{1}&\beta_{1}&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\beta_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\beta_{\mathtt{m_{r}}-1}\\
0&\cdots&0&\beta_{\mathtt{m_{r}}-1}&\alpha_{\mathtt{m_{r}}}\end{pmatrix},\qquad D=\begin{pmatrix}{}_{0}&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&{}_{1}&0&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&{}_{2}&\ddots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&0&{}_{\mathtt{m_{r}}}\end{pmatrix},

where, for
j\in\{0,\dots,\mathtt{m_{r}}\}
:

(5.3)

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}

\displaystyle=-\left(\underline{\rho}\!\big(r_{j}-\tfrac{h}{2}\big)+\underline{\rho}\!\big(r_{j}+\tfrac{h}{2}\big)\right),\qquad

\displaystyle\beta_{j}

\displaystyle=\underline{\rho}\!\big(r_{j}+\tfrac{h}{2}\big),

\displaystyle{}_{j}

\displaystyle=\underline{\rho}(r_{j})N^{2}(r_{j}).

Solving for the first eigenvalues and eigenvectors, we obtain
(\frac{1}{c_{n}^{2}})_{1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}}
and a family of vectors
(f_{n}^{j})_{1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N},0\leq j\leq\mathtt{m_{r}}}
approximating
(f_{n}(r_{j}))_{1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N},0\leq j\leq\mathtt{m_{r}}}
. Then using the definition (
3.12
), we obtain
(g_{n})_{1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}}
, using the finite difference approximation

g_{n}(r_{j})\approx g_{n}^{j}\coloneq\frac{c_{n}}{2h}(f_{n}^{j+1}-f_{n}^{j-1}).

Restriction to a finite number of (vertical) modes.

We now show how to discretize the system (
3.34
). Given the density profile
\underline{\rho}
and the shear flow
\underline{V}
, we can compute the matrices
M^{(\mathtt{N})}\coloneq(M^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m})_{n,m\leq\mathtt{N}},A^{j,(\mathtt{N})}\coloneq(A^{j,(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m})_{n,m\leq\mathtt{N}}
for
j\in\{1,\dots,4\}
that approximate the operators
M,A^{j}
, through the definitions of the coefficients in (
3.22
) and (
3.35
), where the integrals are replaced by a quadrature formula; we use the trapezoid rule. We get:

(5.4)

\immediate\immediate M^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m}\coloneq c_{n}c_{m}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\mathtt{m}_{r}-1}(\underline{\rho}(r_{j})f_{n}^{j}f_{m}^{j}+\underline{\rho}(r_{j+1})f_{n}^{j+1}f_{m}^{j+1}),

as well as

(5.5)

\displaystyle A^{1,(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\mathtt{m}_{r}-1}\left(\underline{V}(r_{j})\underline{\rho}(r_{j})g_{n}^{j}g_{m}^{j}+\underline{V}(r_{j+1})\underline{\rho}(r_{j+1})g_{n}^{j+1}g_{m}^{j+1}\right),

\displaystyle A^{2,(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneq-\frac{c_{m}}{c_{n}}A^{4,\mathtt{N}}_{n,m},

\displaystyle A^{3,(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneq c_{n}c_{m}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\mathtt{m}_{r}-1}\left(\underline{V}(r_{j})\underline{\rho}(r_{j})f_{n}^{j}f_{m}^{j}+\underline{V}(r_{j+1})\underline{\rho}(r_{j+1})f_{n}^{j+1}f_{m}^{j+1}\right),

\displaystyle A^{4,(\mathtt{N})}_{n,m}

\displaystyle\coloneq\frac{h}{2}\sum_{j=0}^{\mathtt{m}_{r}-1}\left(\underline{V}(r_{j})\underline{\rho}(r_{j})N^{2}(r_{j})f_{n}^{j}f_{m}^{j}+\underline{V}(r_{j+1})\underline{\rho}(r_{j+1})N^{2}(r_{j+1})f_{n}^{j+1}f_{m}^{j+1}\right),

where we used that
A^{2}
and
A^{4}
are defined from the same integral, see Remark
3.5
.
We thus consider the following system, that we call semi-discrete in the vertical variable as it only depends on a finite number of vertical modes,

(5.6)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \left((I^{(\mathtt{N})}-M^{(\mathtt{N})}\partial_{x}^{2})\partial_{t}V^{(\mathtt{N})}\right)_{n}+c_{n}\partial_{x}\eta^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n}+\left((2A^{1,(\mathtt{N})}+A^{2,(\mathtt{N})})\partial_{x}V^{(\mathtt{N})}\right)_{n}-(A^{3,(\mathtt{N})}\partial_{x}^{3}V^{(\mathtt{N})})_{n}&=0,\\

for
1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}
. Let now
((V_{\mathrm{in},n},\eta_{\mathrm{in},n}))_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{N}^{*},H^{s}(\mdmathbb T_{L}))
such that
(c_{n}\partial_{x}V_{\mathrm{in},n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}\in\ell^{2}(\mdmathbb{N}^{*},H^{s}(\mdmathbb T_{L}))
, for some
s\in\mdmathbb{N}
. We complete (
5.6
) with the initial condition

(5.7)

\immediate\immediate(V^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n},\eta^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n})(0,\cdot)=(V_{\mathrm{in},n},\eta_{\mathrm{in},n}),

for
1\leq n\leq\mathtt{N}
.

Restriction to a finite number of (horizontal) Fourier modes.

We approximate solutions
(V^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n},\eta^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n})_{n\in\{1,\dots,\mathtt{N}\}}
of (
5.6
) by the Fourier series of their first Fourier coefficients with respect to

; namely, take
\mathtt{K}\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}
. Denoting by
\hat{V}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n,k}

\hat{\eta}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n,k}

and
\eta^{(\mathtt{N})}_{n}
for

(5.8)

\immediate\immediate\frac{d}{dt}{\bf\hat{Y}}_{k}(t)=B_{k}{\bf\hat{Y}}_{k}(t),

for

and where

({\bf\hat{Y}}_{k})_{n\leq\mathtt{N}}\coloneq(\hat{V}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{1,k},\dots,\hat{V}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{\mathtt{N},k},\hat{\eta}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{1,k},\dots\hat{\eta}^{(\mathtt{N})}_{\mathtt{N},k})^{T},

and

(5.9)

\displaystyle B^{1,1}

\displaystyle\coloneq ik(I^{(\mathtt{N})}+M^{(\mathtt{N})}k^{2})^{-1}(2A^{1,(\mathtt{N})}+A^{2,(\mathtt{N})})+ik^{3}(I^{(\mathtt{N})}+M^{(\mathtt{N})}k^{2})^{-1}A^{3,(\mathtt{N})},

Initial conditions and time evolution.

On the one hand, we use (

(V_{\mathrm{in}},\eta_{\mathrm{in}})

. We compute the

\immediate\immediate{\bf\hat{Y}}_{k,\mathrm{in}}\coloneq(\hat{V}_{\mathrm{in},1,k},\dots,\hat{V}_{\mathrm{in},\mathtt{N},k},\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{in},1,k},\dots\hat{\eta}_{\mathrm{in},\mathtt{N},k})^{T}.

On the other hand, we use (

Let

. We define the

\immediate\immediate\mathcal{C}^{\mathtt{N}}(k)=\{-i\lambda/k,\lambda\text{ eigenvalue of }B_{k}\}.

) of (

Note that the requirement associated to

5.2.
Convergence of the scheme

We now explain how we discretize the irrotational bilayer Euler equations (

On the one hand, we compute solutions to (

) with initial conditions
(\hat{\zeta}_{k},\hat{\psi}_{k})

Dispersion relation

4.8

\immediate\immediate\mathcal{C}_{0}(k)\coloneq\{c(k),\tilde{c}(k)\},

where

are the two (not necessarily distinct) complex solutions to

\immediate\immediate(c-d(k))^{2}=a(k)b(k)/|k|^{2}.

does not include as small values as in the rest of this study. The reason is that two factors may contribute to instabilities of the low frequencies: on the one hand, the low frequencies might exhibit instabilities. On the other hand, the continuous spectrum exhibits small instabilities, due to numerical inconsistencies. For lower values of

is of the same order as the (artificial) instabilities associated to the continuous spectrum. This range might be the reason why no general pattern emerges from the data.

Conjecture on the form of the dispersion relation of (

)

Our findings, described in the present section, suggest the following conjecture, which is an analogue of Proposition

, for the stratified Euler equations.

Conjecture 6.5
.

Let
\delta>0
,
\underline{\rho}_{\delta},\underline{V}_{\delta}
as in (

6.3.
Consequences for the justification of bilayer models

Let us now point out the consequence of our findings on the full justification of bilayer models.

In many applications, bilayer models are used as reduced models for a continuously stratified fluid with a thin pycnocline. This is particularly the case for the study of geophysical flows, for which the bilayer shallow-water equations are commonly used; we comment on this later in this subsection. The derivation of these models relies on formal arguments. However, it is possible in some cases to provide a rigorous comparison of the solutions of both the continuous and the bilayer models, stemming from close initial data. This is done in Theorem
6.1
in the case
\underline{V}=0
, for the comparison with the bilayer Euler equations, and it relies on energy estimates; this is what we call
full justification
. In the case where
\underline{V}
is of the form given in (
6.6
), our findings indicate that such a result does not seem to be true in general, due to the presence of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. Let us take two particular bilayer models.

6.3.1.
The bilayer Euler equations

Let us compare the solutions of the linear stratified Euler equations with a shear flow (
3.32
). We consider the density and shear profiles given in (
6.1
) and (
6.6
). Let
\omega^{*}_{\delta}
and
k^{*}_{\delta}
be defined in (
6.10
) and (
6.11
). Let
(V_{n},\eta_{n})_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}
be the eigenvector associated to
\omega^{*}_{\delta}
, for which we find a numerical approximation with the numerical scheme described in Section
5.1
, and let

V=\sum_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}V_{n}g_{n},\qquad w=\sum_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}c_{n}ik^{*}_{\delta}V_{n}f_{n},\qquad\eta=\sum_{n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}\eta_{n}f_{n},

and

f^{*}\coloneq(V,w,\eta).

We thus find that, for any constant
\mathcal{U}^{*}
,

(6.13)

\immediate\immediate\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta}(t,x,r)\coloneq\mathcal{U}^{*}e^{-it\omega^{*}_{\delta}+ik^{*}_{\delta}x}f^{*}(r)

is a particular solution of (
3.32
), whose
L^{2}(S)
norm grows like
e^{t\textrm{Im}(\omega^{*}_{\delta})}
. We conjecture
\textrm{Im}(\omega^{*}_{\delta})\approx\delta^{-1}
(see Figure
21
and Conjecture
6.5
), so that

(6.14)

\immediate\immediate\|\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(S)}\approx|\mathcal{U}^{*}_{0}|e^{t\delta^{-1}}.

Such a pathological behavior prevents to define solutions of (
3.32
) on a time interval
[0,T]
independent of
\delta
for generic initial data in
L^{2}
. More generally, let us show how the same argument holds for initial data in any Sobolev space
H^{s}
. From Figure
22
and Conjecture
6.5
, we conjecture
k^{*}_{\delta}\approx\delta^{-1}
. Then, let us assume that
\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta|t=0}
is bounded in
H^{s}
uniformly in
\delta
. Because of (
6.13
) and

k^{*}_{\delta}\approx\delta^{-1},

this implies

\mathcal{U}^{*}\leq C\delta^{s},

for some constant
C
. Then:

(6.15)

\immediate\immediate|\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta}(t,\cdot,\cdot)|_{L^{2}}\leq C\delta^{s}e^{t\delta^{-1}}.

Thus, the assumption
\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta|t=0}
uniformly bounded in
H^{s}
is not enough to ensure that
\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta}
remains bounded uniformly in
L^{2}
on a time interval independent of
\delta
. This is consistent with ill-posedness results on the bilayer Euler equations (see
[
ITT97
]
,
[
KL05
]
for a framework close to ours, as well as
[
Duc22
, Chapter 1, Section 3.5]
and references within).

Remark 6.6
.

Analytic functions are associated with exponentially decreasing Fourier coefficients. In our context, analytic initial data imply
|\mathcal{U}^{*}_{0}|\sim e^{-k^{*}_{\delta}}
, so that (
6.14
) becomes

\|\mathcal{U}^{*}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(S)}\sim e^{t\delta^{-1}-\delta^{-1}},

which could allow for bounded solution on a time interval uniform in
\delta
. Therefore, our findings do not prevent a full justification of the bilayer Euler equations from the stratified Euler equations in the sharp stratification limit, in analytical spaces. This is consistent with well-posedness results in analytic spaces for the vortex sheet problem (see
[
SSBF81
]
,
[
CO86
]
), which is closely related to the bilayer Euler equations, and in particular suffers from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instability as well. Such a result is, to the best of our knowledge, absent from the literature.

6.3.2.
The bilayer shallow-water equations

Let us introduce the shallow-water parameter
\mu\coloneq H^{2}/L^{2}
, where
H
is the depth of the strip
S
and
L
is the length of the torus. In most applications, it is a small parameter, so that we are interested in the limit
\mu\to 0
. Formally, the equations one expects to recover from the stratified Euler equations in the double limit
\mu,\delta\to 0
are the bilayer shallow-water equations. These equations are ubiquitous in oceanography; one of their advantages compared to the bilayer Euler equations is that they do not suffer from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities. They are even well-posed at the non-linear level, see
[
Duc22
, Chapter B, Section 6]
. We now show that our findings also provide numerical evidence that a full justification of the bilayer shallow-water equations from the stratified Euler equations cannot hold.

One can rescale the variables and unknowns to obtain the non-dimensionalized stratified Euler equations, similar to (
3.32
) but that makes appear
\mu
, and posed in the non-dimensionalized strip
\mdmathbb{T}_{1}\times[-1,0]
. The non-dimensionalized Taylor-Goldstein equation then reads

(6.16)

\immediate\immediate(\underline{V}-c)^{2}\left((\underline{\rho}w^{\prime})^{\prime}-\mu k^{2}\underline{\rho}w\right)-(\underline{V}-c)(\underline{\rho}\underline{V}^{\prime})^{\prime}w+\underline{\rho}N^{2}w=0,

with Dirichlet boundary conditions. We thus see that the non-dimensionalization process consists in replacing the Fourier variable
k
by
\sqrt{\mu}k
. Hence, Figure
15
still describes the phase velocities in the non-dimensional case, and we can specify the dependency in the shallow-water parameter

k_{\min,\delta,\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}k_{\min,\delta},\qquad k_{\max,\delta,\mu}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}k_{\max,\delta},\qquad c_{\max,\delta,\mu}=c_{\max,\delta}.

We also find, with the same rescaling argument,

(6.17)

\immediate\immediate\omega^{*}_{\delta,\mu}\coloneq\argmax_{c\in\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\mu}(k),|k|\in[k_{\min,\delta,\mu},k_{\max,\delta,\mu}]\cap\mdmathbb{N}}\left(k\textrm{Im}(c)\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\omega^{*}_{\delta}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k^{*}_{\mu,\delta}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}k^{*}_{\delta},

where
\mathcal{C}_{\delta,\mu}(k)
is defined as in (
5.13
) but with
\mu
possibly different from
1
. Now the same discussion as in Section
6.3.1
holds: a solution
\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta,\mu}(t,x,r)
of the form

\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta,\mu}(t,x,r)=\mathcal{U}^{*}e^{-it\omega^{*}_{\delta,\mu}+ik^{*}_{\delta,\mu}x}f^{*}(r),

which is in
H^{s}
at
t=0
exhibits exponential growth, even in
L^{2}
norm:

(6.18)

\immediate\immediate\|\mathcal{U}^{*}_{\delta,\mu}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(S)}\approx\mu^{\frac{s}{2}}\delta^{s}e^{t\mu^{-\frac{1}{2}}\delta^{-1}}.

In other words, the previous conclusion still holds: because of the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities, it does not seem to be possible to define solutions of (the non-dimensional form of) (
3.32
), on a time interval
[0,T]
independent of
\delta
and
\mu
, for generic initial data in a Sobolev space. In fact, the smaller the value of
\mu
, the faster the exponential growth is. This is in sharp contrast with the expected reduced model at
\delta=\mu=0
: indeed, the bilayer shallow-water equations are well-posed in Sobolev spaces, even at the non-linear level (under a hyperbolicity condition, see
[
Duc22
, Chapter B, Section 6]
and references within). This can be understood through the following formal argument: our findings show that the low frequencies, i.e.
|k|\leq k_{\min,\delta,\mu}
seem to be stable, i.e.
\textrm{Im}(c)\approx 0
- this last approximation seems to hold only asymptotically in
\delta
, see Figure
20
and Conjecture
6.5
. However, it appears that
k_{\min,\delta,\mu}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}
, so that when formally setting
\mu=0
, all frequencies are stable.

7.
Conclusion and perspectives

In this study we investigate the sharp stratification limit, in the framework of the linear stratified Euler equations (
3.32
) with a particular background density profile
\underline{\rho}_{\delta}
that converges to a piecewise constant profile when
\delta
goes to
0
; here,
\delta
is the size of the pycnocline, the transition layer between the upper and lower layers. We also consider a background velocity profile
\underline{V}
that is a shear flow, i.e. depending only on the vertical variable. We compare this system with the bilayer Euler equations (
4.10
).

In the case
\underline{V}=0
, the linear bilayer Euler equations do not exhibit instabilities. More precisely we derive energy estimates for these equations and for the stratified Euler equations (
3.6
), to prove in Proposition
6.1
that solutions to these systems starting from close initial data, remain close on some time interval
[0,T]
; we measure their difference with respect to the parameter
\delta
. For this we find that isopycnal coordinates (see Section
3.1
) are particularly useful.

In the general non-linear Euler equations (in the continuously stratified or bilayer settings), there is a non-zero horizontal velocity. We introduce the shear velocity
\underline{V}_{\delta}
in (
6.6
) to study its influence on the behavior of solutions. It is well-known (see
[
Duc22
, Chapter 1, Section 3]
) that the bilayer Euler equations linearized around a non-zero shear velocity suffer from the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities (see Figure
13
), which imply arbitrarily fast growing modes. This implies ill-posedness of the non-linear bilayer Euler equations in Sobolev spaces. It should be noted that the non-linear stratified Euler equations are well-posed in Sobolev spaces (see
[
DLS20
]
,
[
Fra24
]
), however on a time interval that shrinks to the singleton
\{0\}
as
\delta
goes to
0
.

In this unstable case, we provide some numerical results. To this end, we develop a numerical strategy in Section
5
, based on a modal decomposition, adapted to the stratification profile at hand, and in particular allows to treat the case where the Brunt-Väisälä frequency depends on the vertical variable. In this case, we provide some numerical illustrations (see Figures
7
and
8
) that show the effect of a pycnocline of non-zero thickness on the propagation of internal waves.

The numerical strategy developed in Section
5
allows us to compute the dispersion relation (more precisely, an approximation of the phase velocities) of the stratified Euler equations (
3.32
), shown in Figures
14
,
15
,
16
. We find a good agreement with the dispersion relation in the bilayer case: namely, stability of the low frequencies, a threshold
k_{\min,\delta}
for which higher frequencies are unstable. The main difference is the presence of a threshold
k_{\max,\delta}
after which higher frequencies are stable again, see Figure
15
. We also provide some quantitative estimations of these features in Section
6.2.2
. This allows us to formulate Conjecture
6.5
on the general features of the phase velocities of the stratified Euler equations, in the sharp stratification limit. Overall, we find that the bilayer equations, which we see as a reduced model that can be formally derived from the stratified Euler equations, reproduce the Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities with good accuracy.

Our findings also indicate that there exist exponential growing modes, with rate
\textrm{Im}(\omega_{\max,\delta})\approx\delta^{-1}
(see Figure
21
). We explain in Section
6.3
that, according to these findings, it is not possible to define solutions in Sobolev spaces on a time interval uniform in
\delta
, which is the crucial starting point to prove the convergence of solutions of the stratified Euler equations (
3.32
) towards a bilayer model. This is consistent when considering the limit
\delta\to 0
alone, as in this case the reduced model (the bilayer Euler equations) are ill-posed. We find that this conclusion still holds when also allowing smallness of the shallow-water parameter
\mu
. This is a striking fact: when considering the (formal) double limit
\delta,\mu\to 0
, the reduced model is the bilayer shallow-water equations, which are well-posed in Sobolev spaces. Yet, it does not appear to be possible to justify the shallow-water equations from the stratified Euler equations, in the Sobolev framework.

The main perspective of the present article is to prove Conjecture
6.5
, i.e. rigorously describe the dispersion relation shown in Figure
15
, and more precisely the various features (asymptotic stability of the low frequencies
|k|\leq k_{\min,\delta}
, and high enough frequencies
|k|\geq k_{\max,\delta}
, Kelvin-Helmholtz instabilities for
k_{\min,\delta}\leq|k|\leq k_{\max,\delta}
), as well as asymptotics in the regime
\delta\to 0
for some key elements (for instance,
k_{\min,\delta},k_{\max,\delta}
and
\omega_{\max,\delta}
, defined in Figure
15
and (
6.10
)). A first step towards this line of research is to study the Sturm-Liouville problem (
3.11
) with
\underline{\rho}
given by (
6.1
). This would for instance provide a justification of the asymptotic of the mode speeds
c_{n}
displayed in Figure
12
. It would also shed light onto the behavior of the various coupling operators in (
3.20
) and (
3.34
), in the sharp stratification limit.

Another perspective from the numerical point of view is to extend the numerical strategy described in Section
5
to the non-linear setting. In order to keep an efficient modal approach as the one we performed in the present study, a fast Sturm-Liouville transform would be required, in order to adapt the standard pseudo-spectral methods to our framework.

\immediate\immediate\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\sqrt{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}V\|_{H^{s,0}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}w\|_{H^{s,0}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{-g\underline{\rho}^{\prime}_{\delta}}\eta\|_{H^{s,0}}^{2}\right)=0.

From such an estimate the uniqueness of solutions of (
3.6
) follows, and the existence classically follows.

Thanks to the incompressibility condition, we get
w\in C^{0}([0,+\infty),H^{s,1}(S))
. Then, differentiating the equation on
\eta
in (
3.6
) and using the incompressibility condition, we get

\partial_{t}\partial_{r}\eta=-\partial_{x}V,

which yields
\eta\in C^{0}([0,+\infty),H^{s,1}(S))
and (
B.2
).
∎

B.2.
The bilayer Euler equations

Before proving Proposition
4.1
, let us briefly explain how the pressure
P_{\pm}
in (
4.10
) is defined. Assume that
V_{\pm},w_{\pm},\zeta,P_{\pm}
satisfy (
4.10
) with boundary conditions (
4.13
), as well as the continuity of
w_{\pm}
across the interface. Then, taking the divergence of the first two equations in (
4.10
) yields

(B.6)

\immediate\immediate\partial_{x}\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{x}P_{\pm}+\partial_{r}\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{r}P_{\pm}=-g\partial_{x}^{2}\zeta\qquad\text{ in }S_{\pm}.

Let us insist on the fact that (
B.6
) defines
P_{+}
as the solution to an elliptic equation (with boundary conditions yet to define) in the upper layer
S_{+}
(resp.
P_{-}
in
S_{-}
). Taking the trace of the second equation in (
4.10
) at
r=-H
and
r=0
yields

(B.7)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{r}P_{+|r=0}&=0,\\
\partial_{r}P_{-|r=-H}&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

At the interface
r=r_{*}
between both layers, we do not impose boundary conditions, but rather transmission conditions: first, we impose the continuity of the pressure across the interface, which stems from the absence of surface tension. Taking the trace of the second equation in (
4.10
) at
r=r_{*}
and using the continuity of
w_{\pm}
across the interface (in (
4.13
)), we finally write the two transmission conditions

(B.8)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} P_{+|r=r_{*}}&=P_{-|r=r_{*}},\\
\frac{1}{\rho_{+}}(\partial_{r}P_{+})_{|r=r_{*}}&=\frac{1}{\rho_{-}}(\partial_{r}P_{-})_{|r=r_{*}}.\end{aligned}\right.

In order to study (
B.6
), we define the following Hilbert space

(B.9)

\immediate\immediate\mathcal{H}\coloneq\{(P_{+},P_{-})\in L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(S_{+})\times L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}(S_{-}),\nabla_{x,r}P_{+}\in L^{2}(S_{+}),\nabla_{x,r}P_{-}\in L^{2}(S_{-}),P_{+|r=r_{*}}=P_{-|r=r_{*}}\}/\mdmathbb{R},

where
L^{2}_{\textrm{loc}}
is the space of locally square integrable functions. As before, we write
P_{\pm}\in\mathcal{H}
for
(P_{+},P_{-})\in\mathcal{H}
, with a slight abuse of notations. We endow
\mathcal{H}
with the scalar product

\langle P_{\pm},\phi_{\pm}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\coloneq\int_{S_{+}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x,r}\phi_{+}+\int_{S_{-}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{-}\cdot\nabla_{x,r}\phi_{-}.

Note that
\langle P_{\pm},P_{\pm}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=0
implies that
P_{+}
and
P_{-}
are constants. Because
P_{+}=P_{-}
on the interface
r=r_{*}
, these constants are equal. Because the space
\mathcal{H}
is quotiented by constants, this yields that
P_{\pm}=0
in
\mathcal{H}
. We now state the following lemma.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
s\geq 0
,
\zeta\in H^{s}(\mdmathbb T_{L})
. There exists a unique pressure
P_{\pm}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying (
B.6
) with boundary conditions (
B.7
) and the transmission conditions (
B.8
). Moreover, there exists
C>0
depending only on
\rho_{+},\rho_{-},g
such that

(B.10)

\immediate\immediate\|\partial_{x}P_{\pm}\|_{H^{s,0}}+\|\partial_{r}P_{\pm}\|_{H^{s,0}}+\left\|\partial_{r}\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\partial_{r}P_{\pm}\right\|_{H^{s-1,0}}\leq C|\partial_{x}\zeta|_{H^{s}}.

Proof.

Let
\phi_{\pm}\in\mathcal{H}
, and let
P_{\pm}
be a smooth solution to (
B.6
) with boundary conditions (
B.7
) and the transmission conditions (
B.8
). Multiplying (
B.6
) with
\phi_{\pm}
and integrating by parts, we get the variational formulation of (
B.6
):

(B.11)

\immediate\immediate\int_{S_{+}}\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{+}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{+}\cdot\nabla_{x,r}\phi_{+}+\int_{S_{-}}\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{-}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{-}\cdot\nabla_{x,r}\phi_{-}=-\int_{S_{+}}g\partial_{x}\zeta\partial_{x}\phi_{+}-\int_{S_{-}}g\partial_{x}\zeta\partial_{x}\phi_{-}.

The existence and uniqueness of solutions to the variational problem (
B.11
) is classical, we refer to
[
Bor10
]
for transmission problems, as well as
[
Lan13b
, Chapter 2]
for boundary problems close to our setting. Then, the estimate (
B.10
) is a standard elliptic estimate, see for instance
[
Lan13b
, Chapter 2]
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

We only sketch the energy estimate that classically leads to the well-posedness of (
4.10
). Integrating the equations (
4.10
) against
(\rho_{\pm}V_{\pm},\rho_{\pm}w_{\pm})
and (
4.11
) against
(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})\zeta
, we get

(B.12)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\sqrt{\rho_{\pm}}V_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\rho_{\pm}}w_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}+|\sqrt{(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})}\zeta|_{L^{2}(\mdmathbb T_{L})}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\int_{S}\nabla_{x,r}P_{\pm}\cdot(V_{\pm},w_{\pm})^{T}-\int_{S}\rho_{\pm}\partial_{x}\zeta\cdot V_{\pm}+\int_{\mdmathbb T_{L}}(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})w_{\pm|r=r_{*}}\zeta.

The first integral on the right-hand side of (
B.12
) is zero, by integration by parts and after using the continuity of
P_{\pm}w_{\pm}
across the interface. We also integrate by parts and use the incompressibility condition in (
4.10
) in the second integral on the right-hand side of (
B.12
) to get

\int_{S}\rho_{\pm}\partial_{x}\zeta\cdot V_{\pm}=-\int_{S}\rho_{\pm}\zeta\partial_{x}V_{\pm}=\int_{S}\rho_{\pm}\zeta\partial_{r}w_{\pm}=\int_{\mdmathbb{T}_{L}}(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})w_{\pm|r=r_{*}}\zeta.

Thus, the second and third integrals in (
B.12
) cancel each other out, and we get

(B.13)

\immediate\immediate\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\sqrt{\rho_{\pm}}V_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\rho_{\pm}}w_{\pm}\|_{L^{2}(S)}^{2}+|\sqrt{(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})}\zeta|_{L^{2}(\mdmathbb T_{L})}^{2}\right)=0.

Applying
\partial_{x}^{\alpha}
to (
4.10
) with
|\alpha|\leq s
, we get that
(\partial_{x}^{\alpha}V_{\pm},\partial_{x}^{\alpha}w_{\pm},\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\zeta)
satisfies (
4.10
), so that (
B.13
) holds for the
H^{s,0}
norm of the unknowns. From such an energy estimate, the uniqueness of solutions to (
4.10
) follows. The existence also follows and we omit it.
∎

From (

) to (

Before Proving Proposition

, let us comment on the density profile given in (

Remark B.3
.

In the proof of Proposition
6.1
, we only use some properties of the specific profile
\underline{\rho}_{\delta}
given in (
6.1
). In fact, Proposition
6.1
holds for profiles
\underline{\rho}_{\delta}
of the following form.

Let
r_{*}\in(-H,0)
,
\delta>0
,
\rho_{-}>\rho_{+}>0
. Then

\underline{\rho}_{\delta}(r)\coloneq\underline{\rho}\left(\frac{r-r_{*}}{\delta}\right),

where
\underline{\rho}
is a smooth, strictly decreasing function from
\mdmathbb{R}
to
\mdmathbb{R}
satisfying the following conditions. First,

(B.14)

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}(y)

\displaystyle\underset{y\to-\infty}{\longrightarrow}\rho_{-},

\displaystyle\underline{\rho}(y)

\displaystyle\underset{y\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\rho_{+}.

Moreover:

(B.15)

\immediate\immediate|\underline{\rho}-\rho_{\pm}|_{L^{2}(\mdmathbb{R})}<+\infty,

where
y\mapsto\rho_{\pm}
is the piecewise constant function with values
\rho_{+}
for
y>0
and
\rho_{-}
for
y<0
. Second, the function

(B.16)

\immediate\immediate y\mapsto y^{\frac{3}{2}}\underline{\rho}^{\prime}(y)

belongs to
L^{\infty}(\mdmathbb{R})
. Note that the profile
\underline{\rho}_{\delta}
defined in (
6.1
) does satisfy these conditions.

Proof of Proposition
6.1
.

We write
(\tilde{V},\tilde{w},\tilde{P},\tilde{\eta}_{*})\coloneq(V-V_{\pm},w-w_{\pm},\check{P}-P_{\pm},\eta_{*}-\zeta)
, where
\check{P}\coloneq P-g\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\eta_{*}
(with
P
as in (
3.6
) and
\eta_{*}\coloneq\eta_{r=r_{*}}
). We then take the difference between (
3.6
) and (
4.10
) to get

(B.17)

\immediate\immediate\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}\tilde{V}+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}\partial_{x}\tilde{P}+g\frac{\rho_{\pm}}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}\partial_{x}\tilde{\eta}_{*}&=-\left(\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\right)\partial_{x}P_{\pm}+g\left(\frac{\rho_{\pm}}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}-1\right)\partial_{x}\tilde{\eta}_{*},\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{w}+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}\partial_{r}\tilde{P}&=-\left(\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}-\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\right)\partial_{r}P_{\pm}+g\frac{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}}{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}(\eta-\eta_{*}),\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{\eta}_{*}-\tilde{w}_{|r=r_{*}}&=0.\\
\end{aligned}\right.

We integrate against
\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\tilde{V},\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\tilde{w},g(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})\tilde{\eta}_{*}
. We get

(B.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\left(\|\sqrt{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\underline{\rho}_{\delta}}\tilde{w}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+|\sqrt{g(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})}\tilde{\eta}_{*}|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+\int_{S}g\rho_{\pm}\partial_{x}\tilde{\eta}_{*}\tilde{V}-\int_{\mdmathbb{T}_{L}}g(\rho_{-}-\rho_{+})\tilde{w}_{r=r_{*}}\tilde{\eta}_{*}

\displaystyle\leq C\left|\rho_{\pm}-\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\right|_{L^{2}([-H,0])}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{\pm}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+|\partial_{x}\tilde{\eta}_{*}|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\left(\|\tilde{V}\|_{L^{2}}+\|\tilde{w}\|_{L^{2}}\right)+\|\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}(\eta-\eta_{*})\|_{L^{1}}\|\tilde{w}\|_{L^{\infty}},

where we used that
(\tilde{V},\tilde{w})
is divergence-free so that the contribution of the pressure term
\nabla_{x,r}\tilde{P}
in (

B.12

We now bound from above the terms on the right-hand side of (

For the first term on the right-hand side of (

B.10
), and the Sobolev embedding
H^{1,1}(S)\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(S)
(see for instance
[
Lan13b
, Proposition 2.12]

(B.19)

\left|\rho_{\pm}-\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\right|_{L^{2}([-H,0])}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{\rho_{\pm}}\nabla_{x,r}P_{\pm}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+|\partial_{x}\tilde{\eta}_{*}|_{L^{\infty}}\right)\leq C\delta\left(|\eta_{*}|_{H^{2}}+|\zeta|_{H^{2}}\right),

where we also used that

\left|\rho_{\pm}-\underline{\rho}_{\delta}\right|_{L^{2}([-H,0])}\lesssim\delta,

from a change of variable and (
B.15
). For the second term in (
B.18

(B.20)

\immediate\immediate\|\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}(\eta-\eta_{*})\|_{L^{1}}\leq\left\|\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}\int_{r_{*}}^{r}\partial_{r}\eta\right\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|(r-r_{*})^{\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon}\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\|\partial_{r}\eta\|_{L^{2}}|(r-r_{*})^{-1+\epsilon}|_{L^{1}([-H,0])}\leq C\frac{\delta^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}{\epsilon}(1+t),

for any

Appendix C
On the numerical approximation in Section

Let us now comment on the precision of the numerical approximation of the phase velocities of (
3.32
), that gives rise to Figures
14
-
22
, and in particular Figure
15
. In this appendix we check numerically that the functions
w_{\mathtt{N}}
and phase velocities
c_{\mathtt{N}}
given by the diagonalization of the matrices
B_{k}
as in (
5.8
), approximately satisfy the Taylor-Goldstein equation (
3.33
).

More precisely, with the notations of Section
5
, let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
B_{k}
as in (
5.8
), associated to the eigenvector
(\hat{V}_{1},\dots,\hat{V}_{\mathtt{N}},\hat{\eta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\eta}_{\mathtt{N}})
. Then, we define
\hat{w}
thanks to the divergence-free equation, namely

(C.1)

\immediate\immediate\hat{w}\coloneq-ik\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{N}}c_{n}\hat{V}_{n}f_{n},

with
(f_{n}){n\in\mdmathbb{N}^{*}}
defined in (
3.11
). Then, we plug (
C.1
) into (
3.33
). To this end, we define
c\coloneq-i\lambda/k
, and note that differentiating (
C.1
) with respect to
r
can be done using (
3.11
). We define:

(C.2)

\mathrm{TG}\coloneq(\underline{V}-c)^{2}\left(ik\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{N}}V_{n}\frac{N^{2}}{c_{n}}f_{n}-k^{2}\underline{\rho}\hat{w}\right)-(\underline{V}-c)(\underline{\rho}\underline{V}^{\prime})^{\prime}\hat{w}+\underline{\rho}N^{2}\hat{w};

it depends only on
r\in[-H,0]
. Observe that
\hat{w}\mapsto\mathrm{TG}
can be seen as an operator, depending on the parameter
c
, and bounded from
W^{2,\infty}
to
L^{\infty}
, though with a norm that depends on
\delta
. It is thus natural to renormalize the error which measures how well
(c,w)
is an approximate solution (i.e. an element of the kernel of TG) of (
3.33
) by the
W^{2,\infty}
norm of
\hat{w}
, which can be computed from (
C.1
) and (
3.11
), (
3.12

(C.3)

\immediate\immediate|\hat{w}|_{W^{2,\infty}}=\max_{r\in[-H,0]}\left(\left|k\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{N}}c_{n}\hat{V}_{n}f_{n}(r)\right|+\left|k\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{N}}\hat{V}_{n}g_{n}(r)\right|+\left|k\underline{\rho}_{\delta}^{\prime}(r)\sum_{n=1}^{\mathtt{N}}\hat{V}_{n}(g_{n}(r)+\frac{1}{c_{n}}f_{n}(r))\right|\right)

We now define the error which measures how well
(c,w)
is an approximate solution of (
3.33
):

(C.4)

\immediate\immediate\mathtt{Err}_{c}\coloneq\frac{\max_{r\in[-H,0]}|\mathrm{TG}(r)|}{|\hat{w}|_{W^{2,\infty}}}.

We then plot the phase velocities from Figure
15
with
\delta=10^{-2}
, and represent
\mathtt{Err}_{c}
for each point
c
.

We perform the same simulation as for Figure
15
, however with
\mathtt{K}=100
, and show the result in Figure
23

c
corresponding to continuous spectrum, i.e.
\mathrm{Re}(c)
is in the range of
\underline{V}
.

Remark C.1
.

Proving that our numerical approximation gives rise to sequences
(w_{\mathtt{N}})
and
(c_{\mathtt{N}})
(where
\mathtt{N}
denotes the number of modes used for the computations) which converge to some solution
w,c
of the Taylor-Goldstein equation (
3.33
) when
\mathtt{N}\to+\infty
is out of the scope of the present study. In particular, because (
3.33
) is a Quadratic Eigenvalue Problem, this is not immediate. Moreover, let

(C.5)

\immediate\immediate T\begin{pmatrix}V\\
w\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{V}\cdot\nabla_{x}V+w\underline{V}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\nabla_{x}P\\
\underline{V}\cdot\nabla_{x}w+\frac{1}{\underline{\rho}}\partial_{r}P-\frac{g\underline{\rho}^{\prime}}{\underline{\rho}}\eta\\
\underline{V}\cdot\nabla_{x}V-w\end{pmatrix}

be the unbounded operator defined on the space of

vector fields with
(V,w)

3.32

\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}V\\

\eta\end{pmatrix}+T\begin{pmatrix}V\\
w\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}=0.

The operator
T
is neither self-adjoint nor skew self-adjoint. Therefore, if
f,\lambda

is small, one cannot conclude that
\lambda

3.32
). Such a proof falls outside of the scope of this study, and we simply consider the error
\mathtt{Err}_{c}
as an indication of the reliability of the associated
c
as being close to a point of the phase velocities of (
3.32
).
