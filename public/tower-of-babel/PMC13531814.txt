Metal implantation and high-grade osteosarcoma of the femur: a case report and review of the literature

Introduction

Osteosarcoma is the most common primary malignant bone tumor, with an incidence of approximately 3–5 cases per million per year. It primarily affects adolescents and young adults, with a peak incidence during the second decade of life and typically arises in the metaphyseal regions of long bones, particularly around the knee [1–3]. In older adults, osteosarcoma may occur in association with pre-existing bone disorders, including Paget disease, prior irradiation, or chronic bone injury. Histologically, osteosarcoma is characterized by malignant osteoblastic cells that produce osteoid, although the matrix formed is structurally inferior to that of normal bone [1, 2]. In the United States alone, an estimated 800–900 new cases are diagnosed annually, accounting for roughly 20% of all primary bone malignancies. In younger patients, osteosarcoma typically involves the metaphyseal regions of long bones, such as the distal femur, proximal tibia, and proximal humerus, whereas in older adults, the tumor may arise in association with pre-existing conditions such as Paget’s disease or prior radiotherapy [1, 2].

Orthopedic metal implants are widely used for fracture fixation and joint reconstruction and are generally considered safe and biocompatible. These implants are commonly composed of stainless steel, cobalt–chromium alloys, or titanium alloys. Although these materials demonstrate excellent mechanical durability and corrosion resistance, long-term implantation can result in the release of trace metal ions and particulate debris through wear, fretting, and electrochemical corrosion processes [4–8].

Beyond these established risk factors—including genetic predispositions (for example, mutations in TP53 and RB1) and environmental exposures—recent attention has focused on the long-term biological effects of metal implants. Widely used in orthopedic surgery for fracture fixation and joint reconstruction, metal implants are predominantly composed of alloys such as stainless steel, cobalt–chromium, and titanium. Despite their high biocompatibility, these devices can release trace amounts of metal ions over time through processes of wear and corrosion [4–7]. Chronic exposure to metal ions has been implicated in triggering local inflammatory responses and oxidative stress, which may result in DNA damage and genomic instability, thereby potentially predisposing to malignant transformation [4–8].

Despite these biologically plausible mechanisms, epidemiological studies have generally not demonstrated a significant increase in overall cancer risk among patients with orthopedic implants [8]. Consequently, most evidence linking implants to sarcoma development derives from isolated case reports describing malignancies arising adjacent to long-standing orthopedic hardware [9–15]. In recent years, several reports have described peri-implant sarcomas, including osteosarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma, and other high-grade spindle cell sarcomas, occurring years or decades after fracture fixation or joint arthroplasty.

Although large epidemiologic studies, including population-based cohort analyses, have generally not demonstrated a significant overall increase in cancer risk following the implantation of metal devices [8], isolated case reports have documented sarcomas arising at implant sites [9–15]. These rare occurrences raise the possibility that, in genetically or environmentally predisposed individuals, metal implants might contribute to the pathogenesis of osteosarcoma [10–16].

Because these events are extremely rare and often lack definitive evidence of implant-related tissue reactions, the relationship between metallic implants and sarcoma development remains uncertain. Therefore, such cases are more appropriately described as implant-associated rather than implant-induced malignancies.

In this report, we describe a case of high-grade osteogenic osteosarcoma arising adjacent to a stainless-steel femoral intramedullary nail, with symptom onset in 2013 and histologic diagnosis in 2016, 23 and 26 years after implantation, respectively, initially presenting with features suggestive of chronic osteomyelitis.

Case report

Presentation and history

A 49-year-old White middle eastern male presented in 2016 with progressive pain and swelling of the right thigh associated with reduced weight-bearing ability. The pain had gradually worsened over several months and was occasionally accompanied by low-grade fever. His medical history was notable for a traumatic right femoral shaft fracture sustained in 1990 at the age of 24 years, treated with open reduction and internal fixation using a stainless-steel intramedullary nail with interlocking screws and cerclage wiring. The postoperative course was uneventful, and the fracture healed without nonunion, implant failure, or revision surgery, allowing the patient to remain active for many years. The patient had no significant comorbidities or prior malignancy, and family history was negative for sarcoma or other cancers. He was an active smoker with no relevant occupational chemical exposures and reported no regular medications or allergies (Table 1).

At presentation, the patient was afebrile, although he reported intermittent low-grade fever during the months preceding admission. Physical examination demonstrated localized swelling and tenderness of the thigh with a palpable mass and intact distal neurovascular status. The patient was able to bear weight with pain, and hip and knee range of motion showed no acute limitation beyond his long-standing baseline.

The postoperative period was uneventful until 2013, approximately 23 years after fixation, when the patient began experiencing intermittent mild pain and swelling in the right thigh, particularly after physical activity. These symptoms were initially managed conservatively and did not prompt further investigation at that time. Over the following three years the symptoms gradually progressed, eventually leading to hospital admission in April 2016.

There was no sinus tract, drainage, or overlying skin breakdown. Laboratory evaluation showed a white blood cell count of 8750/µL, an erythrocyte sedimentation rate of 32–44 mm/hour, and an elevated C-reactive protein level. Given the reported intermittent fever and inflammatory markers, he was initially admitted to the infectious diseases service with presumptive chronic osteomyelitis, and empiric intravenous vancomycin and meropenem were started.

Diagnosis

Plain radiography of the right femur demonstrated an aggressive osteolytic lesion involving the distal femoral diaphysis adjacent to the prior fracture fixation site (Fig. 1). The lesion was associated with cortical destruction and periosteal reaction. Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated extensive marrow replacement within the distal femoral diaphysis and metadiaphysis adjacent to the intramedullary nail. The lesion extended longitudinally for approximately 14 cm and was associated with cortical destruction and aggressive periosteal reaction. A large extraosseous soft-tissue mass measuring approximately 7 × 5 × 4 cm was present, displacing the surrounding musculature. These imaging findings were highly suggestive of an aggressive primary bone malignancy.

Anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the right femur demonstrating an aggressive osteolytic lesion involving the distal femoral diaphysis adjacent to the prior fixation site, with cortical destruction and periosteal reaction

Metastatic evaluation at diagnosis included a chest computed tomography (CT) and a whole-body bone scan (Fig. 2). No pulmonary or distant skeletal metastases were identified, and tracer uptake was limited to the right femoral lesion. Therefore, the tumor was considered non-metastatic at presentation.

Whole-body bone scintigraphy showing increased tracer uptake in the distal right femur corresponding to the site of the osteosarcoma

Given the aggressive imaging features, malignancy was considered in the differential diagnosis in addition to infection. The core needle biopsy was performed shortly after the initial imaging evaluation and yielded inconclusive findings. Because the imaging characteristics remained highly suspicious for malignancy, an open biopsy was performed approximately four weeks later to obtain larger tissue samples for definitive histopathologic evaluation. During this procedure six tissue specimens were obtained from different regions of the lesion, including the proximomedial, proximolateral, and distal femoral diaphysis.

Histopathologic examination of the open biopsy specimens revealed a high-grade malignant mesenchymal tumor composed of pleomorphic spindle cells with a high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio and production of malignant osteoid. These findings were diagnostic of high-grade osteogenic osteosarcoma with mixed fibroblastic and chondroblastic differentiation (Fig. 3).

Histopathologic findings demonstrating pleomorphic malignant cells producing immature osteoid, consistent with high-grade osteogenic osteosarcoma (hematoxylin and eosin stain)

Treatment

Because infection had been strongly suspected during the initial evaluation, extensive microbiological investigations were performed. Blood cultures obtained during hospitalization were negative. Multiple intraoperative tissue cultures obtained during the open biopsy procedure were also negative for bacterial growth. Histopathologic examination did not demonstrate features of acute or chronic osteomyelitis, and special staining did not identify microorganisms. The absence of positive microbiological cultures, together with the lack of histopathologic features of osteomyelitis, effectively excluded infection as the primary etiology of the lesion. Histologic evaluation of peri-implant soft tissue demonstrated scattered microscopic metallic debris with occasional particle-laden macrophages associated with a mild chronic inflammatory reaction. No extensive metallosis, foreign-body giant cell reaction, or pseudotumor formation was identified.

During the surgical procedures, the intramedullary implant was carefully examined. The stainless-steel nail and associated fixation hardware appeared structurally intact without evidence of mechanical failure. Importantly, there was no macroscopic evidence of metallosis, black discoloration of surrounding tissues, corrosion debris, implant loosening, pseudotumor formation, or foreign-body reaction. The surrounding soft tissues did not demonstrate purulence or necrotic debris suggestive of infection.

Following confirmation of the diagnosis, the case was reviewed by a multidisciplinary oncology team. The patient initially received neoadjuvant chemotherapy with the MAP regimen consisting of high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin (adriamycin), and cisplatin. After completion of neoadjuvant therapy and restaging evaluation, surgical management was undertaken. Imaging had demonstrated extensive longitudinal involvement of the femoral diaphysis with a large extraosseous soft-tissue component, making limb-salvage reconstruction unlikely to achieve adequate oncologic margins. Therefore, hip disarticulation was performed to ensure complete tumor resection.

Histopathologic examination of the resected specimen confirmed high-grade osteosarcoma with negative surgical margins. Postoperatively, the patient completed additional cycles of adjuvant chemotherapy with the MAP regimen as part of the planned perioperative treatment protocol. No major chemotherapy-related complications were reported during treatment. The overall systemic treatment course lasted approximately six to eight months. The patient was subsequently followed with routine oncologic surveillance, including periodic clinical evaluation and imaging for detection of local recurrence or distant metastasis.

Discussion and conclusions

Osteosarcoma accounts for approximately 20–30% of primary malignant bone tumors and most often affects adolescents and young adults, typically arising in the metaphyseal regions of long bones around the knee, including the distal femur and proximal tibia [1, 2]. In older adults, osteosarcoma is less common and may occur secondary to underlying conditions such as Paget disease, prior irradiation, or chronic bone injury [1, 2]. By contrast, high-grade sarcomas developing adjacent to long-standing orthopedic implants are exceptionally uncommon. Consistent with this rarity, large population-based studies have not demonstrated a statistically significant increase in overall cancer risk among patients with metallic orthopedic devices; for example, the cohort analysis by Nyren and colleagues did not identify an overall rise in cancer incidence after hip arthroplasty [9], although modest increases in selected organ-specific cancers were reported. Taken together, the available epidemiologic data support the conclusion that peri-implant sarcoma is an uncommon event, and that causal inference from individual cases is not justified [17].

Despite the absence of strong population-level evidence, the literature includes isolated case reports and small series describing high-grade sarcomas arising at or near implant sites after prolonged latency. The clinicopathologic series by Keel and colleagues remains one of the most frequently cited collections, describing 12 high-grade sarcomas associated with orthopedic hardware or prosthetic joints, including osteosarcoma, with implantation-to-diagnosis intervals ranging from approximately 2.5 to 33 years [10]. Importantly, several reported cases presented with nonspecific symptoms such as pain and swelling, mimicking more common implant-related complications including infection and aseptic loosening, which can delay definitive diagnosis. More recent reports have continued to document peri-implant sarcomas after fracture fixation and arthroplasty, including osteogenic sarcoma arising after intramedullary nailing of the femur and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma occurring near a tibial intramedullary nail after a long symptom-free interval. These observations reinforce that implant-adjacent sarcoma can occur, but they also underscore the limited strength of evidence, which remains largely confined to anecdotal reports rather than mechanistic or epidemiologic proof of causation [18–20].

Several biologically plausible mechanisms have been proposed to explain how long-term metallic implants might contribute to local tumorigenesis, including chronic inflammation and potential genotoxicity from metal wear debris and ion release. Orthopedic implants composed of stainless steel, titanium alloys, or cobalt–chromium alloys can generate particulate debris and trace metal ions over time through wear, fretting, and electrochemical corrosion [4–7]. Experimental and translational studies indicate that particulate and ionic by-products can activate macrophage-driven inflammatory pathways, promote cytokine release, and generate oxidative stress, which in vitro has been associated with DNA damage and genomic instability [4–7]. However, translating biologic plausibility into clinical causality remains challenging because the overwhelming majority of patients with implants never develop sarcoma, and many published peri-implant sarcomas lack consistent case-level evidence of substantial metallosis, abundant particle-laden macrophages, or a pronounced foreign-body reaction [9].

In our case, peri-implant tissue showed only limited microscopic metallic debris with occasional particle-laden macrophages and a mild chronic inflammatory reaction, without gross metallosis, extensive foreign-body giant cell reaction, or pseudotumor formation. Intraoperatively, the intramedullary nail and associated fixation hardware appeared structurally intact without loosening or mechanical failure, and there was no macroscopic evidence of metallosis, black discoloration, corrosion debris, or foreign-body reaction. These observations do not support a prominent adverse local tissue reaction as a mechanistic bridge between implant presence and sarcomagenesis. Accordingly, our report is best interpreted as an implant-associated presentation rather than evidence of implant-induced carcinogenesis.

This case also highlights an important diagnostic challenge emphasized by reviewers and by the case literature: late-onset pain and swelling around orthopedic hardware can closely resemble chronic osteomyelitis or other inflammatory conditions. In our patient, intermittent symptoms began in 2013 and progressed over three years, culminating in admission in 2016 with worsening pain, swelling, intermittent low-grade fever, and elevated inflammatory markers. These features reasonably prompted an initial working diagnosis of chronic osteomyelitis and empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. However, radiography and MRI demonstrated aggressive osseous destruction with cortical breach and a large extraosseous soft-tissue mass, findings that were more consistent with an aggressive primary bone malignancy than infection alone. The subsequent diagnostic workup incorporated staged tissue sampling: a core needle biopsy showed chronic inflammation with atypical stromal proliferation but was not definitive, and an open biopsy performed approximately four weeks later provided adequate tissue to establish the diagnosis of high-grade osteogenic osteosarcoma with fibroblastic and chondroblastic differentiation. Because infection was a central competing diagnosis, extensive microbiological investigations were undertaken, including blood cultures and multiple intraoperative tissue cultures, all of which were negative, and histopathologic evaluation did not demonstrate features of acute or chronic osteomyelitis; special stains did not identify microorganisms. Collectively, these data supported malignancy rather than infection as the primary etiology and illustrate the importance of early advanced imaging and timely biopsy when late implant-site symptoms are accompanied by destructive radiographic changes.

Management of high-grade osteosarcoma requires multimodal therapy, typically combining wide surgical resection with systemic chemotherapy. Limb-salvage approaches are preferred when oncologically feasible, but amputation or disarticulation remains appropriate when tumor extent precludes reliable achievement of wide margins or when reconstruction is not expected to provide durable local control [1]. In the present case, imaging demonstrated extensive longitudinal involvement of the femoral diaphysis with a large extraosseous soft-tissue component, and the multidisciplinary sarcoma team concluded that limb-salvage reconstruction was unlikely to achieve adequate oncologic margins. Hip disarticulation was therefore selected to ensure complete tumor resection, and pathological assessment confirmed clear surgical margins. Postoperatively, the patient received multi-agent systemic chemotherapy with the MAP regimen (high-dose methotrexate, doxorubicin, and cisplatin), which remains a widely accepted first-line approach for non-metastatic high-grade osteosarcoma. The patient tolerated treatment without major complications and remains disease-free nine years after therapy, supporting effective local and systemic disease control in this case.

The limitations of this report are inherent to single-case observations. Although the temporal association between long-standing implant presence and tumor development is notable, the absence of a demonstrable peri-implant adverse tissue reaction and the broader epidemiologic literature preclude causal inference. Nevertheless, the case contributes clinically relevant documentation to the limited literature on implant-associated osteosarcoma and reinforces a practical message for clinicians: persistent or progressive pain, swelling, or functional decline near long-standing orthopedic implants—particularly when accompanied by aggressive imaging features such as cortical destruction and soft-tissue mass—should prompt expedited evaluation for malignancy in addition to infection, including timely biopsy. Further progress in understanding any potential relationship between implants and sarcoma development will likely require large registries with long-term follow-up and multi-institutional case series incorporating detailed clinicopathologic characterization.

Osteosarcoma adjacent to orthopedic hardware is extremely rare, but this case reinforces that it is a possibility even decades of post-implantation. It illustrates the diagnostic challenge in differentiating infection from malignancy. Physicians should be vigilant for malignancy when faced with unexplained pain or destructive changes around old implants. While hypotheses about metal-induced carcinogenesis are biologically plausible, they remain unproven; this case should be interpreted as an implant-associated rather than implant-caused sarcoma. Further research is needed to clarify any true link between long-standing implants and sarcoma formation.

Limitations and future directions

Our case has limitations inherent to single-case reports. Without molecular analysis of the tumor or implant-related materials, additional mechanistic factors cannot be excluded. Looking ahead, the field would benefit from larger observational studies. Joint-implant registries with long-term follow-ups could help determine if the incidence of sarcoma is truly elevated. Multi-center case series could better characterize risk factors (for example, type of implant, wear history, patient genetics). On the basic science side, integrating wear- and corrosion-product analysis with genomic studies of periprosthetic tumors might reveal specific carcinogenic pathways. Clinically, establishing “red flag” guidelines for late-onset implant symptoms (such as mandatory imaging/biopsy for unexplained pain/swelling) could improve early detection.

Patient perspective

When I was told about the diagnosis and that hip disarticulation would be required, it was very difficult for me emotionally. Losing my limb was a major life change, and adapting to it took time and effort. With the support of my medical team and family, I gradually adjusted and focused on rehabilitation. I am thankful for the treatment I received and for remaining free of disease for many years after therapy. I continue regular follow-up visits and work on maintaining my independence and quality of life.
