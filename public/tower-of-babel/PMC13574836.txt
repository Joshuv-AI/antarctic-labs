Inverse relationship between the critical shoulder angle and rotator cuff insertional footprints: an anatomical study
Authors: Sebastian Wegmann, Maximilian Weber, Tamara Babasiz, Jan Hockmann, Lars Peter Müller, Maximilian Lenz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDegenerative shoulder disorders impose a significant clinical and economic burden. The critical shoulder angle (CSA) has been established as a predictor of rotator cuff tears and glenohumeral osteoarthritis, but the anatomical basis underlying those relationships remains incompletely characterized. This study investigates whether the CSA correlates with the size of the rotator cuff muscle insertional footprints.MethodsAn anatomical study was performed on 28 shoulders from 14 formalin-fixed cadavers (mean age, 77.15 years; standard deviation, 9.94). Radiographs in the Grashey view were used to measure the CSA. The insertional footprints of the supraspinatus (SSP), infraspinatus (ISP), teres minor (TM), and subscapularis (SSC) were dissected and measured. A correlation analysis was conducted to assess relationships.ResultsA moderate inverse correlation was found between the CSA and the footprint size of all examined muscles: ISP (r=–0.52, P<0.01), TM (r=–0.60, P<0.001), SSP (r=–0.51, P<0.01), and SSC (r=–0.42, P<0.05). Large CSA values were associated with small insertional areas.ConclusionsThis study suggests that a large CSA is associated with significantly smaller-than-average insertional footprints. These findings provide an anatomical basis for biomechanical models that could help to refine risk assessment and preventive strategies for shoulder pathology.Level of evidenceAnatomical study.

INTRODUCTION

Shoulder degenerative conditions are common and economically burdensome, with reported treatment costs of billions of dollars in developed nations, especially when the costs of disability and lost productivity are included. In a Danish register study, the total annual cost of shoulder disorders, including sick leave, amounted to €1.21 billion [1]. Most shoulder research has focused on enhancing treatment options, with prevention taking a back seat. However, our study sheds light on the practical implications of scapular morphology, a factor often overlooked in studies of primary osteoarthritis (OA) [2-5].

Moor et al. [6] showed that, alongside the acromion index and lateral acromion angle, the critical shoulder angle (CSA) was the strongest predictor of rotator cuff tears (RCTs) (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC], 0.855; odds ratio, 10.8). In their study, Gerlach et al. [7] found that patients with an RCT had significantly higher CSAs than controls. A larger-than-average CSA independently increased the RCT risk (odds ratio 1.7 per degree). A CSA ≥35° accurately identified full-thickness RCTs (AUC, 0.88; sensitivity, 68%; specificity, 89%), and they deemed it to be a simple, reproducible tool for clinical decision-making. Collectively, those studies suggested that primary glenohumeral OA is associated with a smaller-than-average CSA, whereas degenerative RCTs are associated with a larger-than-average CSA (>35°) [8]. Blonna et al. [9] confirmed and extended those conclusions, showing that large angles are associated with symptomatic cuff tears, large tears, and the severity of primary eccentric OA, whereas small angles correlate with concentric OA. Furthermore, Bouaicha and colleagues demonstrated that CSA measurements show excellent inter- and intra-rater reliability and that measurements on anteroposterior radiographs and computed tomography scans are highly correlated [10].

In their biomechanical study, Viehöfer et al. [11] found that a large CSA increases the ratio between glenohumeral joint shear and joint compression forces, leading to an increased load on the supraspinatus (SSP) tendon to stabilize the shoulder joint. Villatte et al. [12] confirmed those findings in their biomechanical analysis. They concluded that higher-than-average shear forces are generated with high CSA, and that higher-than-average compressive forces are applied with low CSA. A recent patient-specific three-dimensional (3D) musculoskeletal modeling study demonstrated that patients with RCTs showed greater glenohumeral instability during abduction and higher total joint forces than OA patients, whereas OA was characterized by increased vertical shear during flexion. Those findings suggest that distinct biomechanical mechanisms—force magnitude in RCTs and shear-to-compressive load imbalance in OA—are linked to 3D scapular morphology [13].

Although it has been shown that the CSA influences joint shear and load on the rotator cuff muscles by creating a biomechanical disadvantage when values are outside the normal range, the anatomical substrate of that relationship has not been systematically studied. In this investigation, we aimed to establish a relationship between the CSA and insertional footprint dimensions.

METHODS

We conducted this cadaver study in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Cologne (No. 21-1243). Informed consent was waived.

For this anatomical study, 28 shoulder joints from 14 formalin-embalmed donors (8 male, 6 female) were available. Specimens were excluded if we found evidence of previous injuries, fractures, or operative treatments to the shoulder joint. The mean age of the donors was 77.15, with a standard deviation (SD) of 9.94 years (range, 59–95 years).

True anteroposterior radiographs in the Grashey view were performed as a first step. The shoulder was rotated posteriorly by 35°–45° so that the plane of the scapula was parallel to the cassette. The beam was directed tangentially to the glenohumeral joint with upper arm rotation in a neutral position. Only radiographs with no glenoid rim double contour (type A according to the Suter-Henninger scapular classification system) were used to enable an exact evaluation of the CSA [14]. The radiographs were digitally stored and evaluated using digital image analysis software (ImageJ, http://imagej.net).

Subsequently, each specimen underwent systematic anatomical dissection, beginning with the removal of the skin and subcutaneous tissue. Starting medially, the muscle bellies of the SSP, infraspinatus (ISP), teres minor (TM), and subscapularis (SSC) muscles were identified. To isolate the individual tendons, the rotator interval between the SSC and SSP, the interval between the SSP and ISP, and the interval between the ISP and TM were dissected. After careful preparation, we followed the tendons to their insertional footprint on the humeral head. The tendons were then sharply dissected free of their insertional footprint, and the borders at the tendon–bone junction were lined out with a waterproof marker.

A calibrated image was acquired perpendicular to each insertion, with a millimeter scale next to the sample at the footprint level. To prevent distortion, the photos were taken with a high-resolution digital camera (Canon EOS 5D) using a 50-mm fixed-focal-length lens. The same procedure was repeated for all specimens in a standardized fashion. To achieve precise, comparable measurements, each specimen was scaled separately. All images were transferred into the digital image analysis software (ImageJ). Other study groups previously published the method we used [15,16]. Using the same technique, we measured ten objects with known dimensions and found a standard uncertainty of 1%, which we deemed acceptable.

Statistical Analysis

An a priori power analysis was performed using the G*Power statistical analysis tool (G*Power version 3.1, University of Düsseldorf) to determine an appropriate sample size for detecting differences in the independent variables. A sample size of 14 specimens was predicted to provide 95% power at an alpha level of 0.05 and a Cohen´s d of 0.6. Descriptive statistics (mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, and 95% CI) were calculated for each parameter. The data were rigorously validated for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to analyze the linear correlation between the insertional footprints and CSA and assess coherence. Correlation strength was interpreted as weak (r<0.3), moderate (0.3–0.6), or strong (r>0.6). Statistical significance was set at α=0.05, with exact P-values reported to three decimal places.

RESULTS

The study cohort comprised 28 shoulder specimens (14 cadavers; 8 male, 6 female). All specimens met the inclusion criteria with no evidence of prior shoulder pathology, fracture, or surgical intervention. The CSA measurements ranged from 29.10° to 55.23° (mean±SD, 34.37°±5.59°). Detailed descriptive statistics for the CSA and all measured insertional footprint parameters are presented in Table 1. The descriptive statistics demonstrate substantial variability in the rotator cuff insertional footprints, with mean footprint areas of approximately 232 mm² for the ISP, 340 mm² for the TM, 255 mm² for the SSP, and 321 mm² for the SSC. Normality testing indicated that most footprint measures were normally distributed, though the CSA and a few width/height parameters deviated from normality. The results of the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk normality analyses are summarized in Table 2. Inter- and intra-rater reliability for the CSA measurements was excellent, with Pearson correlation coefficients exceeding 0.93. Detailed inter-rater and intra-rater reliability metrics are provided in Tables 3 and 4, respectively. The correlation analyses showed moderate, statistically significant negative associations between the CSA and the footprint areas of the ISP, TM, SSP, and SSC, with the strongest relationship observed for the TM. DISCUSSION

This anatomical investigation provides the first evidence of significant inverse correlations between the CSA and rotator cuff insertional footprint dimensions across all muscles. The TM demonstrated the strongest relationship (r=−0.60, P<0.001), followed by moderate negative correlations with the ISP (r=−0.52) and SSP (r=−0.51); the SSC showed the weakest correlation, but it was still significant (r=−0.42, P=0.026).

The inverse relationships between the CSA and the insertional footprints of the ISP, SSP, and TM are graphically demonstrated in Figs. 2-4, which highlight the consistent reduction in footprint size with increasing CSA values.

These findings indicate that scapular morphology directly influences the anatomical substrate available for rotator cuff muscle attachment. Our calculated insertional footprints align with the established literature, validating our anatomical measurements [17-19]. Furthermore, the variability of footprint dimensions observed across specimens (Table 1) is consistent with previously reported anatomical studies of rotator cuff insertions.

Biomechanical Implications and Clinical Context

These anatomical observations integrate with well-established biomechanical principles: CSA angles greater than 35° substantially increase glenohumeral shear forces relative to compressive forces. Importantly, shoulders with enlarged CSAs experience greater shear stress but possess smaller-than-average attachment surfaces to resist those mechanical demands [7,13], representing a dual biomechanical disadvantage.

Prior work by Moor et al. [6] that was refined by Gerlach et al. [7] identified a CSA >35° as a critical threshold for RCT risk, with each degree of additional angulation conferring a 1.7-fold increase in the odds of a tear. Our anatomical data provide a structural explanation for those clinical observations, demonstrating a direct relationship between scapular morphology and the capacity of rotator cuff attachments to withstand mechanical load.

Integration with Contemporary Anatomical Understanding

Recent cadaveric investigations have substantially refined rotator cuff insertional anatomy. Mochizuki and colleagues revealed that the SSP footprint is smaller and more triangular than previously appreciated, with the ISP occupying a considerable portion of the superior facet [20]. Our findings extend that work by systematically demonstrating how the CSA influences those insertional dimensions across the entire rotator cuff complex.

Adaptive Mechanism and Morphological Relationships

In their investigation, Villatte et al. [12]. showed that the CSA influences the forces applied to the shoulder joint. Our study shows that the CSA influences not only joint mechanics but also the anatomical characteristics of rotator cuff insertions. Benjamin’s foundational work on tendon insertion physiology revealed that tendons adaptively flare at attachment sites to secure skeletal anchorage while resisting changes in insertion angle, but they do so with minimal structural investment to preserve tendon compliance and flexibility [21]. Our data reveal a reciprocal morphological adaptation: as the CSA increases, deltoid force vectors increase, generating greater shear forces, and the rotator cuff insertional footprint simultaneously diminishes. Conversely, as the CSA decreases, compression forces dominate, and insertional footprints enlarge. This inverse relationship between the footprint size and applied forces—specifically the correlation between insertional footprint dimensions and the shear and compression forces predicted by the CSA—is a novel observation presented here for the first time. The pattern suggests that rotator cuff insertion adapts to local mechanical demands in a manner previously undocumented, producing a dual disadvantage mechanism.

Currently, this finding is a theoretical consideration in the development of shoulder pathologies, but it indicates a compounded biomechanical risk to rotator cuff integrity. Computational modeling studies have confirmed that higher CSAs increase the instability ratio (shear to compressive forces) by approximately 33% during abduction [13]. At the same time, our data show that those shoulders have attachment areas 15%–20% smaller than average (based on correlation coefficients), resulting in higher stress per unit area at the tendon–bone interface. That combination could explain why CSA values >35° demonstrate 67.7% sensitivity and 89.4% specificity for full-thickness RCTs [7].

Maganaris et al. [22] proposed that mechanical loading across entheses is heterogeneously distributed, with non-uniform strain patterns at the attachment site. They reference multiple studies demonstrating that pathological changes predominantly occur in regions with the lowest strain levels. As presented earlier, large CSA angles are associated with a smaller-than-average footprint and more shear forces than compression forces. Notably, Maganaris et al. [22] suggested that the most vulnerable areas within an enthesis are initially stress-shielded, challenging the conventional notion that tensile failure is a primary mechanism underlying enthesopathy and potentially explaining Blonna's conclusion that larger angles are associated with more severe cuff tears [9].

The findings of this study add to the expanding body of evidence highlighting the significance of the CSA in shoulder biomechanics and pathology. The high inter- and intra-rater reliability of CSA measurements reinforces its utility as a clinically relevant parameter. Given the correlation between the CSA and degenerative changes, CSA assessment could facilitate early diagnosis, aid in risk stratification, and guide surgical interventions to modify scapular morphology, such as osteotomy procedures.

Although our findings are promising, certain limitations must be considered. Formalin-embalmed cadaveric specimens might not accurately reflect the biomechanical properties of living tissue. The TM footprint was measured in the coronal plane, consistent with the CSA assessment, but axial views offer complementary insights, so the use of only coronal images might have led to inaccuracies in its measurement. The sample size of 28 shoulders met the recommended guidelines for anatomical cadaveric studies (minimum n=10) and provided adequate statistical power (>0.80) to detect moderate correlations (r≥0.5) at α=0.05. Although larger samples would enhance generalizability, constraints on cadaveric availability are inherent to this research design. Future studies should focus on validating these findings in larger, more diverse populations and investigating the long-term influence of the CSA on shoulder degeneration.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrates an inverse correlation between the CSA and the insertional footprint sizes of rotator cuff muscles, suggesting that large CSAs are associated with small footprints and increased shear forces, and small CSAs correspond to large footprints and greater compression forces, thereby producing a dual biomechanical disadvantage. These findings provide direct anatomical evidence linking the CSA to footprint dimensions across all rotator cuff muscles, suggesting a biomechanical basis for the increased RCT risk observed with a CSA >35° and the increased risk of glenohumeral arthrosis at smaller-than-average CSA values. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these relationships and establish clinical prediction models.
