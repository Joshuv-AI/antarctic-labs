When Contextual Inference Fails: Cancelability in Interactive Instruction Following
Natalia Bila, Kata Naszádi, Alexandra Mayn, Christof Monz

Abstract
We investigate the separation of literal interpretation from contextual inference in a collaborative block-building task where a builder must resolve underspecified instructions using contextual inferences. Building on an existing two-speaker psycholinguistic paradigm—which contrasts a pragmatically cooperative speaker with one who is only literally reliable—we introduce Build What I Mean (BWIM), an interactive benchmark for contextual meaning construction. In BWIM, models must resolve ambiguity by either performing a contextual inference or requesting clarification at a small communication cost. Evaluating several state-of-the-art LLMs, we find a dissociation between judgment and action: while models detect speaker unreliability in explicit confidence ratings, they fail to exploit this information to guide efficient clarification behavior. Instead, we observe suboptimal strategies, such as partner-blind over-clarification and question-averse guessing under uncertainty.

Introduction

Many linguistic theories posit that human language processing relies on a two-tier system consisting of a more general literal meaning and pragmatics – the latter being responsible for a flexible contextual meaning creation building on top of literal meaning
11
;
26
;
5
. To what extent is such a two-tier system beneficial for artificial conversational agents? And are literal and pragmatic processing already represented in conversational systems? This paper investigates these questions through a recently introduced task designed to test whether an agent can distinguish between literal meaning and contextual inference.

A standard diagnostic for distinguishing literal meaning from pragmatic inference is cancellability: contextual inferences are defeasible and may fail in contexts where the literal content remains reliable
(
5
;
10
)
. In repeated interaction, this defeasibility can be revealed implicitly, as interlocutors learn that drawing contextual inferences with a given partner often leads to incorrect interpretations, whereas reliance on literal meaning does not. The task used in this paper leverages this asymmetry by testing whether conversational agents adapt their interpretations over time, selectively discounting contextual inferences while preserving literal content.
The task involves a building scenario where an agent interacts with two distinct speakers.

Through repeated interaction, the agent must learn to trust or cancel inferences based on the speaker’s identity. This setup allows us to observe whether the agent maintains a representation of what was literally said versus what was inferred based on the context.

A further goal of this work is methodological. Much prior work on pragmatic competence in language models relies on explicit metalinguistic judgments, e.g., asking models to select or explain the intended interpretation of an utterance
(
7
;
33
;
27
;
28
)
. While informative, such evaluations probe pragmatic knowledge in isolation rather than its use in situated actions. In contrast, our task evaluates the ability to contextualize meaning implicitly, through task success and information-seeking behavior in an interactive setting. This allows us to assess whether models can deploy pragmatic inferences appropriately when they matter for achieving goals, rather than merely articulating them when prompted.

We evaluate three state-of-the-art LLMs on this task, comparing their performance against human benchmarks. Our evaluation proceeds in two stages. In the first, agents report confidence ratings after each trial, allowing us to track adaptation as shifts in subjective certainty and to assess whether models distinguish literal from contextually enriched interpretations. In the second, we introduce
Build What I Mean (BWIM)
, a new interactive benchmark that reframes the task as a multi-turn dialogue in which agents may seek clarification at a cost, allowing us to examine whether models translate uncertainty into effective information-seeking behavior.

We observe dissociation between explicit and implicit uncertainty management: models that detect speaker unreliability in their confidence ratings nonetheless fail to exploit this signal to create an efficient information-seeking strategy. Our experiment design allows us to assess not just whether models draw pragmatic inferences, but whether they can selectively cancel them in response to partner-specific evidence — and whether that internal sensitivity surfaces in action, not only in judgment.

The setup used to test whether agents are aware of contextual inferences involves two speaker profiles in a block-building game. The task is to construct simple structures on a 3D grid that satisfy instructions given by two types of agents: Literal Lisa or Pragmatic Pia. Throughout this paper, we use
pragmatic competence
in a broad sense, referring to the contextualization of meaning beyond what is explicitly stated, rather than to specific pragmatic phenomena such as scalar implicatures or Gricean maxims in isolation. In our task, pragmatic reasoning consists in resolving underspecified instructions using shared contextual assumptions and selectively canceling such enrichments when they prove unreliable.

After each structure, the builder is asked to report, on a 4-point scale, how confident they are that they built the structure intended by the speaker. A builder that can distinguish literal content from contextual inferences is expected to maintain high confidence on fully specified trials, while gradually lowering confidence when following underspecified instructions from Literal Lisa.

While human data from the confidence-scoring version of this task already supports the existence of rapid adaptation, we are ultimately interested in whether models can translate their uncertainty into clarification questions to guarantee task success. To test this, we converted the task into the
Build What I Mean
(BWIM) benchmark. While the instructions remain identical to the confidence-based adaptation task, models in BWIM have the option to ask questions and receive answers. This allows them to learn the utility of information-seeking in an interactive environment. Because questions incur a small cost, we expect models to behave efficiently—asking fewer questions when interacting with Pragmatic Pia than with Literal Lisa.

The human behavioral data used for comparison in this paper is taken from
(
20
)
. Here, we use a subset of these results solely as a benchmark for model behavior, without revisiting the authors’ cognitive modeling analyses.

3
Related work

Pragmatic inference may be implemented in multiple ways in computational models. The first group of models builds in the separation of literal and pragmatic meaning by design. In these approaches, there is a literal model responsible for the first-pass interpretation and a separate module responsible for further contextualization of the meaning. One of the most prominent models in this line is RSA
4
, which models pragmatic inference as Bayesian inference about the partner’s production process on top of initial literal lexical definitions.

This model has been computationally operationalized in many tasks, including reference games
30
, navigation tasks
3
, and human-robot collaboration for goal assistance
32
. It has further been extended to research in language learning
(
21
;
15
;
17
)
.
These modular architectures allow for the targeted handling of pragmatic inferences, as the component responsible for enrichment is architecturally distinct from the literal foundation.

The second group of models attempts to learn pragmatic behaviors directly from data. These often utilize end-to-end neural architectures where pragmatic nuances, such as politeness or indirectness, are captured within the latent representations of large language models (LLMs). Several works have tested the pragmatic capabilities of these end-to-end systems
7
, with some confirming emergent pragmatic abilities across multiple languages
33
. Other researchers have highlighted significant gaps in this area, suggesting that large language models may not be pragmatic enough in zero-shot settings
13
;
12
;
9
;
23
. To address these limitations, recent methodologies have been proposed to improve LLM pragmatic performance, such as incorporating communicative objectives during training
25
or leveraging specialized prompting techniques
(
28
;
24
)
. In this paper, we investigate what happens when pragmatic inferences drawn by such end-to-end models go wrong and whether agents can learn to selectively cancel them.

Most existing evaluations of pragmatic competence in LLMs rely on explicit interpretation tasks, such as multiple-choice questions, entailment judgments, or direct prompts asking models to explain or select an intended meaning
(
7
;
33
;
27
, e.g.,)
. While these approaches test whether models can recognize pragmatic phenomena, they do not assess whether such inferences are integrated into goal-directed behavior. Our work complements this line of research by focusing on implicit, task-oriented evaluation, where pragmatic success is measured through action choices, adaptation over time, and information-seeking strategies in interaction.

4
Experiments

We report two experiments investigating how LLMs adapt to different speakers’ reliability. In Experiment 1, after each trial, models rated their certainty that they built a correct structure, which allowed to detect adaption via changes in confidence ratings. In Experiment 2, models were given points for building correctly and penalized for errors. On each trial, they could ask clarification questions at a small cost. Thus, adaptation was measured via changes in question-asking behavior across speaker blocks.

Each speaker block contained 20 items: 8 of them were fully specified (unambiguous) and 12 were underspecified, omitting information about the number or color of blocks (6 items of each type of underspecitfication). After completing the task, the builder receives feedback about whether the built structure matched the target structure. In the Pragmatic Pia condition, feedback was always consistent with pragmatic interpretation. In the Literal Lisa condition, feedback was sometimes pragmatic (
n=4
) and more often literal (
n=8
), allowing builders to infer that contextually licensed pragmatic interpretations were not always correct. Retaining a subset of pragmatically consistent trials prevented the literal speaker from appearing deliberately uncooperative, forcing agents to adapt to her reliability rather than abandoning pragmatic reasoning altogether or inventing alternative communication rules.

Models.
We evaluated three LLMs: Gemini 2.5 Pro
2
, GPT 5.1
22
, and Claude Opus 4.5
1
. The models were selected based on their performance during the pilot phase: their ability to correctly interpret fully specified instructions and respond in the specified format, and sensitivity to ambiguity (lower ratings in the critical trials compared to unambiguous control trials).
We excluded DeepSeek-R1
6

29
and Grok-4.1
31
) were evaluated during the pilot but were not included in the main experiment because while their building accuracy was comparable to other models, their confidence ratings were consistently at ceiling, showing no sensitivity to the experimental manipulation.

Task adaptation for LLMs.
In the original experiment by
20
, human participants read textual instructions and responded by building structures with colored blocks in a 3D space. As feedback, they received a message indicating whether the response was correct and a picture of the structure they built along with a picture of the intended structure.

In our experiment, this task was converted into a fully textual format. Models were instructed to respond with coordinates and colors of the blocks they would use. We created a main task prompt that described the grid including (1) the coordinates of the origin and each corner; (2) valid x, y, and z values; and (3) the required response format ("Color:x;y;z"). As feedback, they received a message indicating whether the response was correct, the coordinates they sent, and the target coordinates.

4.1
Confidence rating experiment

There were 8 experimental lists with counterbalanced item sets and speaker order. The order of the instructions was pseudorandomized such that critical trials appeared in the same position across lists. Results are averaged over 30 distinct trial sequences for each model.

Procedure

At the beginning of each session, models received a system prompt describing the study context (similar to what human participants saw). Each prompt contained the description of the grid, building instructions, and a request to rate their certainty (on a scale of 1 to 4). After each round, the model’s response was appended to the chat history and followed by a feedback message revealing the intended target structure. Appending the history is crucial as it provides learning signals for adaptation. Each main block contained instructions from one speaker (Literal Lisa or Pragmatic Pia), with a transition message indicating a speaker change. At the end of the experiment, the model was asked to comment on the differences in instruction style between the two speakers.

5
Results

Unambiguous trials

On fully-specified trials, all models showed mean accuracy > 0.95 and mean ratings > 3.85 (out of 4), with no difference between speaker blocks. This means that models are able to perform the building task and have high confidence and good calibration when encountering fully detailed instructions.

Pragmatic Inference

All models demonstrated the expected pragmatic inference without prior examples or explicit instructions on resolving under-specification. This confirms zero-shot pragmatic competence across all tested models.

When interacting with Pragmatic Pia, the models resolved underspecification pragmatically in the vast majority of cases: GPT (98%), Claude (92.4%), and Gemini (88.8%).

In the Literal Lisa condition—where models frequently encounter pragmatic errors—the agents continued to lean toward contextually plausible interpretations but began incorporating ‘noisy’ actions. These actions suggest a strategy of guessing or randomizing responses when faced with a pragmatically unreliable partner. This mirrors human behavioral data: while some participants maintain a pragmatic bias and merely lower their confidence, others begin to randomize their interpretations in an attempt to optimize outcomes against an inconsistent speaker
(
20
)
. Specifically, when feedback suggested a pragmatic error was likely, Claude maintained a pragmatic choice in 92.4% of cases, while GPT dropped to 79% and Gemini to 70%. An analysis of the responses that differed from the contextually licensed interpretation can be seen in the Appendix
B
.

Effect of speaker identity

As illustrated in Figure
4
, the effect of speaker identity is selectively localized to underspecified trials. Across all models, performance and confidence remained high for fully specified instructions, regardless of whether the partner was Pragmatic Pia or Literal Lisa. This indicates that models correctly attribute the source of error to underspecification rather than general communicative incompetence; they continue to fully trust literal content in both conditions.

The divergence between fully specified and underspecified trials serves as a metric for how models notice pragmatic violations. A larger gap in the Literal Lisa condition suggests that the model recognizes the partner’s pragmatic unreliability. While this gap is most pronounced in human participants, it remains statistically significant for Gemini and GPT. In contrast, Claude appears relatively insensitive to partner identity, maintaining similar behavior across both conditions.

A regression analysis showed that speaker identity did not affect ratings on unambiguous trials. In contrast, there was a significant effect of speaker identity on underspecified trials: all models gave lower ratings in the literal condition, however, the magnitude of the adaptation effect differed across models. GPT and Gemini showed a stronger adaptation effect with a difference in ratings of 0.6 and 0.28 points between the two speaker conditions, respectively. Adaptation over time

In the results reported so far we ignored the fact that accumulation of information about the partner unfolds over time: the builder learns about the partner’s reliability through repeated interaction. In Figure
4
we show confidence ratings coupled with interpretation type (pragmatic vs. other) for 4 time segments for each partner. We see similar patterns as before: Claude is not sensitive to partner type, while Gemini and GPT both show patterns of adaptation. Gemini employs a guessing strategy heavily when talking to Literal Lisa and does not recover from this fully when switching to new partner. Such heavy carry over of mistrust from one partner to the other is atypical for humans. GPT shows the strongest adaptation effects with progressively lower confidence choices and more guessing with the erroneous literal partner and rapid adaptation and high confidence in the pragmatic inferences when the partner is also reliably pragmatic.

6
Discussion

This paper introduces a new paradigm for evaluating contextual meaning creation in language models through implicit, task-oriented behavior. Rather than asking models to explicitly articulate or select an interpretation, the task embeds pragmatic inference in a goal-directed block-building game where success depends on deciding when to rely on contextual enrichment and when to cancel it. By operationalizing cancellability in an interactive setting, the benchmark probes whether models can deploy pragmatics appropriately in action, not merely recognize it when prompted.

A central feature of the setup is the use of partner-specific reliability to isolate pragmatic reasoning. Because literal content remains reliable across both speakers, while pragmatic inferences are selectively misleading for one of them, successful adaptation requires distinguishing what was literally said from what was inferred. The selective impact of speaker identity on underspecified but not unambiguous control trials confirms that the task cleanly targets pragmatic processing.

All tested models demonstrated strong zero-shot pragmatic inference, resolving underspecified instructions in line with contextual expectations even without prior demonstrations. This indicates that pragmatic enrichments are robustly internalized by the tested models. When pragmatic inferences systematically failed for one speaker, model behavior diverged. Some models adjusted confidence ratings or relied more on guessing, indicating representations factorized enough to selectively cancel inferences, while others failed to modulate confidence or actions, suggesting an inability to suppress pragmatic enrichments. These patterns indicate that pragmatic knowledge is generally present, but its selective deployability varies across models.

The contrast between explicit confidence ratings and clarification behavior highlights the diagnostic value of implicit, task-oriented evaluation. Although some models exhibited sensitivity to pragmatic unreliability in their confidence judgments, this internal uncertainty did not reliably translate into effective information-seeking strategies in the BWIM setting. Instead, we observed a distinct dissociation: models either engaged in high-confidence guessing—avoiding even low-cost clarification—or incurred unnecessary communication costs by querying a reliable partner instead of leveraging pragmatic inference.

Pragmatic understanding in deployed agents requires acting under uncertainty, deciding when to infer and when to ask, and adapting to partner-specific behavior over time. The proposed task offers a concrete step toward evaluating these abilities in a controlled manner. We hope it will serve as a foundation for future work on improving contextualised meaning creation in language-based situated agents.

6.1
Limitations

The contextual enrichment tested in this work is intentionally highly controlled. Each trial consists of one or two sentences establishing context followed by a single target instruction, with varied but still very constrained lexical realization. This design allows us to isolate pragmatic inference and its cancellability with high precision, but it limits the linguistic richness of the context. In natural multi-turn instruction-following, contextual cues for inference may arise from a wider range of discourse phenomena (e.g., anaphora, incremental updates, or expressions such as “another” or “one more”), which are not present in our materials.

In addition, the BWIM benchmark uses a fixed, explicit cost for clarification questions. This does not mirror the variable or implicit costs of communication in real-world interaction.
