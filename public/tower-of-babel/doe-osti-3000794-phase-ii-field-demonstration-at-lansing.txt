EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Industrial-scale injection of CO2 into the subsurface can result in significant fluid pressure
increases, which can lead to potential environmental impacts such as caprock fracturing, fault
activation and leakage into underground fresh water aquifers. Brine extraction is a viable approach
for managing formation pressure, effective stress, and plume movement during industrial-scale
CO2 injection projects. However, the handling and management of the extracted brine has a cost
that is added to carbon capture and sequestration (CCS) operations; therefore, minimizing the
extracted volume of brine can be of great importance. At the same time, economics of pressure
management via brine extraction can be improved by treating the extracted high-salinity water and
making it available for beneficial uses, for example as cooling water for power plants.
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) formed the Brine Extraction and Storage Test (BEST)
program to evaluate the technical feasibility of managing subsurface pressures associated with
large-scale CO2 injection volumes and to assess the cost and effectiveness of desalination
technologies for saline waters containing high total dissolved solids (TDS). The DOE does not
require that the project inject CO2 to build subsurface pressure; rather, water can be injected instead
as a proxy for CO2. The overall objective of the BEST projects is to help develop cost effective
pressure control, plume management and produced water strategies that can be used to improve
reservoir storage efficiency and capacity, and demonstrate safe, reliable containment of CO2 in
deep geologic formations.
No significant structural risks for leakage such as faults and fracture zones were identified
during the screening effort at Plant Smith. For the purpose of demonstration, the leakage,
faulting and injection scenarios described herein are purely hypothetical and were created
by the research team to develop cost effective pressure monitoring and plume management
strategies.
The EPRI-led BEST project, concluding with the most recent Phase II, involved a field
demonstration to implement and test the strategies at the host site Plant Smith near Panama City,
Florida, operated by Gulf Power Company. The Phase II field demonstration site included a
recently drilled injection well (TIW-2), a monitoring and extraction well (TEMW-A), and an
existing injection well, TIW-1, that was repurposed as monitoring and passive relief well. The
project planned to inject low-salinity water and extracted brine through TIW-2 into isolated
sandstone layers of the Lower Tuscaloosa Massive sand at about 1,500 m (4,900 ft) depth by
testing and applying “active” extraction and “passive” pressure relief strategies for pressure
control.
This multiphase project consisted of design, modeling, well drilling construction of a geological
and hydrological models and an 18-month injection program where a fresh water plume would be

 v

established and controlled in a subsurface aquifer. Monitoring the injection plume would be
accomplished through a combination of well based logging surveys and surface and borehole
Electromagnetic (EM) surveys undertaken during the injection. EM technologies were selected for
plume monitoring due to their high sensitivity to water salinity, which would allow discrimination
of the fresh water plume within the high salinity aquifer.
This report outlines the permitting process, well field development and closeout, produced water
treatment user facility plans, and additionally a series of numerical forward modeling and inversion
calculations that have been completed to design the electromagnetic geophysical monitoring
campaign. Using the existing hydrological model, known relations between formation resistivity
and fluid saturation and a proven 3D forward and inverse EM numerical code, we were able to test
candidate configurations and develop a survey design.
Modeling results demonstrated the high sensitivity that borehole EM methods had to the position,
resistivity and volume of the injected plume but they also showed that some configurations were
more effective than others. In particular we found that configurations involving injecting rather
than inducing electrical currents, and those involving measuring the electrical rather than the
magnetic field were the most sensitive to the plume characteristics. In addition, we found that the
crosswell configurations were much more sensitive to the plume than the surface to borehole
configurations. Finally, the constrained data inversion revealed the best re-constructions for
configurations using both surface to borehole and crosswell surveys, but good reconstruction for
those using crosswell only.
Given the inversion results and cost estimates shown in Chapter 6, we determined that three
campaigns of measuring both electric and magnetic field crosswell data in the available wells will
provide the most cost-effective means of monitoring the position of the plume. In comparison, the
information provided by a surface-to-borehole (STB) EM survey is marginal and doubles the EM
survey costs. Therefore, we decided not collect any STB data. The first crosswell data set will be
collected using all wells just before the pump is installed in the extraction well, the second data set
will be done at approximately 180 days after injection starts just between the two fiberglass wells,
and the last extraction will be completed be completed using all three wells after the injection is
over and the pump has been removed from the extraction well.
In terms of pressure and flow monitoring, these will be measured continuously by bottom hole
sensors in all three wells, and at the top of the well bores, in the injection and extraction well.
Wireline flow profiles will be run in the injection and extraction wells three times during the
injection experiment, and a resistivity log run at the same time in the injection well only. Chemical
samples will be collected from the passive relief/ monitoring well five times during the injection
experiment.

PROJECT OVERVIEW

1.1 Background
Management of fluid pressures is expected to be a key issue in the implementation of full-scale
carbon dioxide (CO2) storage operations. Commercial-scale injection of CO2 into the subsurface
can cause reservoir pressure increases that must be properly controlled to prevent any potential
impact on the reservoir and containment system (caprock). Excessive pressure buildup in reservoir
may result in ground water contamination stemming from brine or CO2 leakage from the storage
interval through potential pathways to underground sources of drinking water, through improperly
plugged and abandoned wells or distant faults. The potential exists for increased fluid pressure to
reactivate faults, causing fluid-induced seismicity and potential breaches in the caprock. However,
with proper site selection, facility design, and regulatory oversight, these risks can be managed but
continency plans are needed if pressure buildup becomes a concern. The BEST program is
designed through field demonstration and research to inform through development of best
practices, future contingency plans and measures.
Brine extraction is a viable approach for managing formation pressure, effective stress, and plume
movement during commercial-scale CO2 injection projects (Birkholzer et al., 2012; Buscheck et
al., 2012; Cihan et al., 2015). The brine from the formation will likely be produced in significant
quantities and contain high concentrations of total dissolved solids (TDS). With treatment, this
water could be desalinated and put to beneficial use at a power station for cooling or other uses,
thus reducing the risk associated with brine re-injection/disposal.
The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) created the Brine Extraction and Storage Test (BEST)
program to evaluate the technical feasibility of managing subsurface pressures associated with
large-scale CO2 injection volumes and to assess the cost and effectiveness of desalination
technologies for saline waters containing high TDS. Phase I of the BEST program involved
developing the scope, plans, and preliminary pressure management and water treatment strategies
implemented and validated in the field during the Phase II field demonstration project. The DOE
did not require that the project inject CO2 to build subsurface pressure; instead, water could be
injected as a proxy for CO2 injection.
This document summarizes the Phase II field demonstration project (2017-2025) led by the
Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) at Lansing Smith Generating Plant (Plant Smith), owned
and operated by Florida Power & Light Company (FPL).1 Project partners included EPRI, FPL,
Jacob Engineering, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, and Advanced Resources
International.

1.2 Phase I Site Screening and Down Selection (2015-2016)
During Phase I, the project team collected geologic data and site information for six electric
generating stations owned by Southern Company subsidiaries in the Gulf Coast and Southeast
Region of the United States. Site-selection criteria were developed taking into consideration the
quality and availability of data, physiographic attributes, logistical constraints, project-specific and
DOE program goals, and commercial prospects for siting a commercial-scale CCS project at each
site. The site-screening effort resulted in the selection of Plant Smith near Panama City, Florida,
as the preferred location for the Phase II field demonstration project (EPRI, 2016).
The subsurface geologic conditions at Plant Smith were attractive for a pressure management field
test due to the presence of two suitable, regionally extensive target formations with good porosity,
permeability, and salinity (Section 1.4). These formations included the shallow Eocene Formation
and the deeper Tuscaloosa Formation. The two formations are separated by a thick confining unit,
providing two independent test zones with different formation properties to choose from for the
propose of validating different pressure management approaches. The preferred option going into
Phase II was to test the deeper Tuscaloosa Formation because it contained high salinity
groundwater.

1.3 BEST Field Site — Plant Smith
Plant Smith is located approximately 16 km (6.3 mi) north-northwest of the city of Panama City,
in Bay County, Florida (Figure 1-1). Plant Smith had two-coal fired steam generating units
constructed in the 1960s with a total capacity of 225 megawatts (MW) that were retired in 2015
and raised in 2022. A natural-gas fired combined cycle unit, consisting of two gas-fired
combustion turbines and one steam turbine, was commissioned in 2002 and is still operating today.
The combined cycle plant has a nameplate capacity of 545.5 MW.
Plant Smith is adjacent to North Bay, an interconnected estuary system that is part of the St.
Andrew’s Bay watershed. The elevation of the plant site is only a meter or two above sea level,
contains extensive wetlands, and is prone to seasonal flooding.

The ash pond associated with the coal-fired units was dewatered starting in 2016, allowing the
pond to be drained, regraded, and capped. Waste water from dewatering operations and other plant
processes is injected into on-site injection wells permitted by the Florida Department of
Environmental Protection (FDEP) under the state’s Underground Injection Control (UIC) program
(Section 2). Plant Smith was selected as the preferred site for the Phase II field demonstration in
large part due to the large-scale wastewater injection project. Large quantities of water were
needed by the BEST project to generate sufficient pressure buildup in the formation to manage
and test multiple injection/extraction strategies (Section 5). In addition, the highly saline formation
water found in the Tuscaloosa Formation met the DOE guidelines for water treatment by third
parties supported by DOE.

1.4 Geologic Overview
Plant Smith is in the northeastern Gulf of Mexico sedimentary basin on the eastern flank of the
Pensacola-Decatur Ridge and the western edge of the Apalachicola Embayment, a local
sedimentary sub-basin. The Apalachicola Embayment is a rift basin that was initiated during the
late Triassic-early Jurassic. The sub-basin extends southwestward from southwestern Georgia
through western Florida to the Gulf of Mexico (Ewing and Lopez, 1991). Sedimentary fill in the
Apalachicola Embayment ranges in age from Triassic to Holocene. Basin sediments thin updip to
the north and east and thicken to the southwest to more than 4,500 m (15,000 ft; Schmidt and
Clark, 1980).
The Pensacola-Decatur Ridge is a northeast-southwest oriented basement paleo-high, which
influenced deposition and distribution of Upper Jurassic sediments but appears to have been
inactive and to have had no impact on late Cretaceous and Cenozoic age deposition at the Plant
Smith site (Mancini et al.1991). Key structural features of the northern Gulf of Mexico basin are
shown in Figure 1-2.

The top of the Tuscaloosa/ Lower Cretaceous injection interval is the Lower Tuscaloosa Massive
Sand, which is a target reservoir for wastewater disposal and CO2 sequestration where it occurs as
a saline formation in the Gulf Coast region. Along the Gulf Coast, the Tuscaloosa Massive Sand
was deposited in a fluvial deltaic depositional system characterized by stacked sequences of
channelized sandstones punctuated by marine flooding surfaces that can often be correlated over
a field-scale to sub-region scale.

At the seaward part of the Lower Tuscaloosa depositional system, the Massive Sand consists of
fine to medium grain sandstones, predominately quartz arenite with very little clay. Thin lenses of
pebble or coarse sand conglomerate may be present at the bases of the stacked, individual
sandstones. The Lower Tuscaloosa Massive Sand in southern Mississippi, southern Alabama and
the Florida panhandle is weakly cemented with predominately quartz cement. Porosity ranges
from 20 percent to more than 38 percent, and permeability can exceed 2,000 millidarcy, mD.

The Lower Tuscaloosa is separated from underlying Lower Cretaceous age sandstones by a
regional-scale unconformity. In the central and eastern Florida Panhandle, as much as 1,524 m
(5,000 ft) of Lower Cretaceous non-marine sandstone and interbedded mudstones are identified as
Lower Cretaceous ‘Undifferentiated’, which are equivalent to the Dantzler Formation, Andrew

1.5 Test Overview
Data from three Gulf Power wastewater injection wells (Section 2) was reviewed and the Lower
Cretaceous sediments were determined to be most suitable target for the proposed BEST
experiment. Gulf Power agreed to inject wastewater into two shallow Eocene wells (EIW-3 and
EIW-4), leaving the deeper Lower Cretaceous undisturbed for the BEST project. Gulf Power had
performed hydraulic tests in a deep injection well (TIW-1) completed in the Lower Cretaceous
sediments to characterize the formation and evaluate injectivity, but the tests were of such short
duration, the natural hydraulic heads and gradient in the deep hydrologic system remained largely
unperturbed.
However, TIW-1 was designed to maximize injectivity for wastewater injection and not for the
BEST experiment. It was constructed using a very long well screen and gravel pack, which
intersected multiple sand layers over a 654 m (2,145 ft) interval. This made it less desirable as an
injection well for the BEST experiment because the long-well screen made it difficult to control
which sand lenses the injected water would enter and build up pressure. In addition, a straddle
packer could not be used to isolate a specific injection zone in TIW-1 because the water could
readily flow around the packer through the gravel pack.
Instead, it was decided to use TIW-1 as a passive pressure relief well, allowing pressure created
by injecting water into a nearby, newly constructed injection well to dissipate down the TIW-1
wellbore into multiple layers (Figure 1-5). The new injection well (TIW-2) was constructed by
the BEST project, allowing the project team to perforate a single suitable sand lens in the Lower
Cretaceous for injection. This configuration allowed better flow and pressure control for the test.
A water extraction well (TEMW-A) was also installed by BEST to create an option for relieving
formation pressure through active pressure management (i.e., pumping). Various
injection/extraction strategies were developed to test various scenarios described in Section 5.

1.6 Phase II Project Objectives and Scope
The overall objectives of the BEST project are to develop cost effective pressure control, plume
management, and produced water strategies that can be used to improve reservoir storage
efficiency and capacity, and demonstrate safe, reliable containment of CO2 in deep geologic
formations.
The Phase II field demonstration consisted of implementing the pressure management and
produced water strategies at the field demonstration site located at Plant Smith. Specific objectives
for Phase II included:
• Complete a final field-test design that meets the water management objectives defined in Phase
 I. Obtain all construction and injection permits prior to implementation.
• Install a new wastewater injection well and a new brine extraction well, with advanced
 completion and monitoring technologies. Collect data essential to defining the differential
 pressure front, to be used in model calibration and validation.
• Collect sufficient geologic and geophysical data to develop an accurate static geologic model
 for reservoir modeling and finalizing the test design. The new data will supplement the existing
 reservoir data.

• Update, calibrate and optimize reservoir models used to predict and provide feedback for
 adaptively controlling the position of the differential pressure and plume fronts (“adaptive
 management”).
• Validate produced water management strategies and demonstrate the feasibility of using
 adaptive pressure management techniques.
• Develop and operate a research-scale (pilot) water treatment facility to field test and validate
 technologies for treating waters containing high total dissolved solids.
• Complete all Phase II tasks, milestones and deliverables on time and within budget.
Not all the Phase II project objectives were met for the reasons described in the sections that follow.

2 PERMITTING
2.1 Permitting Authority
The Florida Department of Environmental Protection (FDEP), Aquifer Protection Program,
implements the federal Underground Injection Control (UIC) program in Florida. FDEP
administers activities associated with the construction, operation, permitting, and closure of
injection wells in accordance with Chapter 62-528, Florida Administrative Code (FAC). Chapter
62-528, F.A.C., contains stringent requirements to prevent the degradation of the existing water
quality of the aquifers to protect Underground Sources of Drinking Water (USDW). The FDEP
has authority to issue and enforce permits issued for the following UIC well classifications:
 • Class I hazardous and nonhazardous injection wells. FDEP regulates nonhazardous waste
 and municipal waste wells. Florida banned hazardous waste wells in 1983.
 • Class II injection wells associated with enhanced oil and gas production.
 • Class III wells used for mineral extraction. No Class III wells exist in Florida.
 • Class IV wells or septic fields used for hazardous and radioactive waste disposal are banned
 in Florida.
 • Class V wells used for stormwater disposal, lake level control, domestic wastewater, and
 other listed uses are regulated by FDEP.
 • Class VI wells used for geosequestration of CO2 are regulated by the U.S. Environmental
 Protection Agency in Florida.

2.2 Pre-Existing Wellfield Infrastructure at Plant Smith in 2017
Prior to initiating the BEST field project in 2017, Gulf Power installed three Class I nonhazardous
waste injection wells and three monitoring wells at Plant Smith for the disposal of reclaimed water,
reclaimed water as cooling tower blowdown, stormwater, ash pond water, and other permitted
wastewater from Plant Smith (Figure 2-1 and Table 2-1). Tuscaloosa Injection Well No. 1” (TIW-
1) was the first well to be drilled. It was originally permitted as a deep Class V experimental well
converted in 2012 to a Class I nonhazardous injection well under FDEP Permit 0177034-005-009-
UC/1I. TIW-1 is completed in the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower Cretaceous at a depth of 1,489 to
2,126 m (4,885 to 6,977 ft).
On October 15, 2013, Gulf Power received four permits from FDEP to install up to four Class I
injection wells and three Upper Floridan aquifer monitoring wells in the same field. In 2014, two
of the four Class I wells, designated IW-3 and IW-4, were installed to inject wastewater into the
middle Eocene at depths ranging from 364 to 732 m (1,195 to 2,400 ft). A companion monitoring
well (MW-1, MW-3, and MW-4) was installed within 46 m (150 ft) of each of the three injectors
to monitor for leakage in the overlying Underground Source of Drinking Water (USDW).

2.3 BEST Project Wells

2.3.1 Injection and Extraction Well Permitting
Tuscaloosa Injection Well TIW-2 and Tuscaloosa Extraction Monitoring Well TEMW-A were
constructed by the BEST project specifically for the test. Gulf Power applied for a minor
modification to its existing UIC well permit on May 8, 2017, allowing TIW-2 to be constructed as
one of the two remaining Class I injection wells to be constructed under existing FDEP Permit
0177034-005-009-UC/1I. The application explained that the existing injection well TIW-1 would
be used as a passive relief/monitoring well and the new well TEMW-A would be use for extraction
and monitoring (Figure 2-1). The FDEP reviewed the application, requested additional supporting
documentation during the summer of 2017, and approved the minor modification on October 23,
2017, under 0177034-015-UC/MM. The permit modification process took approximately 5
months. TIW-2 is permitted as a non-hazardous Class I injection well for the disposal of reclaimed
water, reclaimed water used as cooling tower blowdown, stormwater, ash pond water, and other
permitted wastewater from Plant Smith. This included the disposal of brine pumped from TEMW-
A during the BEST experiment.
Installation of an above zone groundwater monitoring well was not required for TIW-2 because it
was installed on the same drill pad as injection well IW-3 and within 46 m (150 ft) of existing
monitoring well MW-3. FDEP agreed that MW-3 could serve as a single monitoring point for both
TIW-2 and IW-3 because the two injection wells are located close together. Additional information
on the construction of the BEST wells is summarized in Section 3.0.

2.3.2 Extraction Well TEMW-A Closure
The 2017 minor modification to the FDEP permit stipulated that Gulf Power could plug and
abandon wells TIW-2 and TEMW-1 after completing the BEST project or continue to use TIW-2
as an active Class I injection well and plug and abandon only TEMW-A. FPL merged with Gulf
Power in 2021, and decided to retain TIW-2 as part of their wastewater injection wellfield, and
moved to plug and abandon TEMW-A. Subsequently, FPL prepared an application to plug and
abandon TEMW-A and submitted it to the FDEP on July 18, 2023. The FDEP issued a draft permit
for public comment on February 23, 2024, and FPL published the Notice of Draft Permit as
required by the FAC in local newspapers. No public comments were received, and the final permit
modification was granted by the FDEP under 0177034-023-UC/M1 on June 3, 2024. TEMW-A
was plugged and abandoned on April 10, 2025, and TIW-2 is still an active injection well.
The TEMW-A closure permit took FDEP about 11 months to review and approve, which is
noticeably longer than the five-month period needed to obtain the minor modification required to
construct the wells. FDEP disclosed at the time the closure application was submitted that there
was a permit backlog, requiring several months before they could review the closure application,
and prepare and issue the draft and final permit.

2.3.3 Shallow Groundwater Monitoring Wells
Ten shallow temporary groundwater monitoring wells were installed around the drill rigs to
monitor for groundwater contamination caused by the drilling operations in accordance with the
FDEP rules. Five of the wells were installed around the rig used to drill TIW-2 and five around
the rig used to drill TEMW-A. The wells were constructed with plastic, 5.1 cm (2-inch) diameter

casing and wells screen completed approximately 4.6 to 6.1 m (15 to 20 ft) below ground level.
Groundwater samples were collected from the wells on a weekly basis and analyzed in the field
for specific conductivity, dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, and turbidity and by a contract
laboratory for chloride and total dissolved solids.
Unlike the UIC wells, which were permitted by the FDEP, the shallow groundwater wells were
permitted by the Northwest Florida Water Management District, Water Well Regulation Program
under Permit 302656-1. The shallow well casings were removed, and the boreholes were plugged
after completing and demobilizing the rigs used to install TIW-2 and TEMW-A. The shallow pad
wells surrounding TIW-2 were approved for closure by the FDEP on May 3, 2021, and were
removed on June 23, 2021. The TEMW-A monitoring wells were approved for closure later, on
October 27, 2021, and removed in December 2021. The short delay in closing the wells was due
to a spike in chlorides detected in two of the five monitoring wells at the TEMW-A location. Water
was subsequently pumped from the wells until the concentrations returned to acceptable levels.

2.4 Lessons Learned
Some takeaways from the BEST project permitting experience are summarized below.
• Florida, like many states, is heavily reliant on groundwater as a potable drinking water supply
 and has stringent well drilling requirements designed to protect this valuable resource.
• Early and frequent communication and consultation with FDEP permitting staff is key to
 facilitating the UIC process, setting expectations for schedules and issuance of permits, and
 notifying the Department of important unforeseen changes to the drilling program that affect
 permit conditions.
• The five months that it took to permit the BEST project wells is atypical and should not be
 construed by other project developers as the time needed to permit a new UIC well from start
 to finish. It should be noted that Gulf Power had already secured the UIC well permit, and a
 minor modification was all that was needed to tailor it to the BEST project. The time required
 to obtain a minor modification to an existing permit is reportedly much shorter than applying
 for and receiving a brand-new permit.
• The Florida UIC program has unique well construction and permitting requirements specific
 to the State of Florida. To name a few that impact injection project costs include:
 o Use of a Florida certified well driller to install UIC injection and groundwater
 monitoring wells. The number of available certified drilling companies that can drill
 deep UIC wells in Florida is limited, creating a noncompetitive drilling market due to
 lack of rig availability, potentially driving up well costs.
 o Well construction standards require placement of 5.1 cm (2-inches) of cement between
 casing strings, which increases the overall diameter of the well and cost of installation.
 o Temporary, shallow groundwater monitoring wells must be installed and sampled to
 monitor for potential impacts of drilling activities on near-surface aquifers.
 o A permanent deep monitor well must be installed within 46 m (150 ft) of each injection
 well and sampled quarterly to monitor for potential leakage.

3.1 TIW-2 Drilling and Completion
Various drilling technologies were used to advance the TIW-2 borehole (Table 3-2). Mud rotary
drilling was used to drill to a depth of 76.2 m (250 ft) below pad level (bpl). A closed circulation
reverse air system was then used to drill the interval from 76.2 to 365.8 m (250 to 1,200 ft) bpl to
protect the Floridan aquifer from contamination by drilling fluids. To complete the well, mud
rotary drilling was used to advance the boring from 365.8 to 2,134 m (1,200 to 7,000 ft). Table 3-
2 summarizes drilling methods, bit diameter, and completed borehole depths during pilot hole and
borehole reaming phases.

After landing and cementing in a nominal 1.2 m (48-inch) outside diameter surface casing, the
borehole was advanced in stages to prescribed depths based on the drilling plan (Table 3-3). Each
stage initially involved drilling a smaller 31.12 cm (12.25-inch) diameter pilot hole. The pilot hole
was then reamed out to the prescribed diameter for that depth and the casing was cemented in
place. In accordance with FDEP guidelines for injection wells, at least 5.1 cm (2-inches) of cement
was placed behind casing between the casing and borehole wall, and between the outer and inner
casing strings. This resulted in a large 56 cm (22-inch) diameter borehole at a final depth of
2,134 m (7,000 ft). Each stage was cemented from the bottom of the well to the land surface using
a tremie pipe conveyance system.

3.4 Lessons Learned

3.4.1 TIW-2

Drilling and well construction for the TIW-2 injection well progressed smoothly through initial
reaming and casing installation, with a 22-inch borehole completed to 7,019 ft bpl in April 2019.
By early May, a 10.75-inch FRP casing was successfully landed to 7,000 ft bpl. During
cementing operations on May 13, 2019, the tremie pipe used for grout placement separated at
975 ft bpl, leaving a large section of pipe stuck in the annulus. Initial fishing efforts successfully

3-8

recovered nearly 4,000 ft of tremie pipe, but a long section (from 4,900 to 6,800 ft bpl) remained
lodged in place. Multiple fishing tools and circulation attempts failed to dislodge the obstruction.

From June through the fall of 2019, various fishing strategies were attempted, including casing
extraction, overshot and core bit retrieval runs, and deployment of grappling and milling tools.
Some success was achieved in removing shorter sections of tremie pipe, but several attempts
introduced new obstructions, including a twisted-off bit and a broken taper tool. By late October
and into November, milling operations were expanded to grind through the stuck tremie, broken
tools, and associated debris between ~6,700 and 7,020 ft bpl. These efforts, combined with
subsequent reaming, circulation, and conditioning, gradually cleared the borehole. By December
2019, most of the fish had been removed, and logging confirmed that only minor anomalies
(likely small cuttings or metal fragments) remained. Circulation and hole conditioning continued
through year’s end in preparation for reinstalling the 10.75-inch FRP casing.

The TIW-2 fishing operations highlighted several key lessons. First, the separation of the tremie
pipe during cementing emphasized the importance of using reliable, redundant equipment and
considering staged cementing approaches to reduce risk. The extended recovery effort also
demonstrated the value of having clear escalation protocols for fishing operations, shifting more
quickly from traditional retrieval methods to milling when appropriate. In addition, several
fishing attempts created new obstructions, underscoring the need for strict tool reliability checks
and careful evaluation of retrieval risks. Routine circulation and borehole conditioning proved
essential for managing debris and maintaining stability throughout the process, while
geophysical logging provided a critical verification step to confirm successful clearance before
resuming well construction.

3.4.2 TEMW-A Blockage
After the TEMW-A casing and well screen was installed, well development was initially
attempted using compressed air to lift the water out of the well to the land surface to remove fine
sediments around and in the well screen. However, the amount of water produced using the air-
lift method was abnormally low at only a few gallons per minute. A much higher production rate
was expected, considering tests on the core showed the formation permeability was very good in
the 100-1,000s of millidarcy range. Suspecting a blockage in the well or well screen, the driller
attempted to simultaneously air develop and jet the TEMW-A well screen with water, which was
met with limited success. Fill material consisting of what appear to be corroded steel debris,
possibly from the tremie pipe used in the air-lift process was produced from the well.
A wireline survey of TEMW-A found only 3 ft of debris-free screen. The top of the debris was
tagged at approximately 4,879 ft bpl. The driller fabricated a jetting tool that fit inside the screen
and reached 4,881 ft bpl, however, the tool was ineffective at clearing the blockage below this
depth. The jetting tool was tripped out of the well and found to be plugged with iron oxide
(corrosion) debris. The driller did not attempt to clear the TEMW-A screen nor remedy the
problem from this point forward, claiming that the blockage was caused by brine corroding the
casing with debris or material sloughing off and settling into the screen below. An unforeseen
condition that the driller claimed was not disclosed to it in advance.
Geostock Sandia (GS) and Renegade Services (RS) were contracted to assess the condition of the
extraction well using logging tools and to clear the screen initially using a wireline hydrostatic

bailer. The hydrostatic bailer runs were unsuccessful producing mostly discolored (black) water
with a sulfide like odor. An attempt to enter the screen using a probe was unsuccessful.
Impression blocks were used to determine the nature of the blockage but were inconclusive.

casing in preparation of video logging. EV Oil Field Camera Services attempted to conduct a
video survey of the 4.5-inch diameter casing. However, water clarity was too poor below 4,876 ft
bls, so the video survey was aborted. GS and RS continued to circulate and flush the casing with
filtered brine and freshwater to clear the casing for a repeat video survey.

DEVELOPMENT, OPERATION AND MAINTENANCE
AND CLOSEOUT

The Water Treatment User Facility was designed to accommodate up to four test beds to allow
multiple technologies to undergo testing and development for treatment of the extracted brine.
The facility was intended to provide a direct and continuous supply of “active” source water at a
maximum rate of 136 m³/d (25 gal/min), to be shared by all users. The facility design, presented
in detail below, was developed to be flexible and allow for different types of technologies to be
tested either independently or at the same time (limited to four at any one time). Individual
evaluation plans were to be created for each technology/process tested at the facility to account
for individual merits related to energy demand, chemical use, waste production, and product
water purity. All waste generated by the users as part of testing was to be collected and co-
managed through a chemical and volumetric equalization process.

In addition to extracted brine, the facility design included provisions to supply filtered well water
from Plant Smith to reduce the feed water total dissolved solids (TDS) concentration. Users
would thus have had access to source water ranging from 1,000 to 166,000 mg/L TDS.
Pretreatment of the source water was anticipated to be limited to filtration (using bag filters to
obtain approximately 30 µm). EPRI evaluated the option to chemically soften source water, but
the final decision was expected to be based on available project funding. EPRI recognized that
each technology might have its own specifications for pretreatment and was therefore less
inclined to assume responsibility for an entire pretreatment process, as the cost could have
exceeded available funding and the process might not have been applicable to all tests.

This section will provide a summary of details up until project cancelation on the design plans
for the user facility; results from geochemical modeling to simulate chemical softening and
evaporation/concentration desalination of the extracted brine; and the sampling and analysis
plans for treatment and waste encapsulation tests.

4.1 Water Treatment User Facility Design and Bid Details

The design for the Water Treatment User Facility reflected EPRI’s vision to allow access to
multiple users for testing on the water produced in the brine extraction process. Conducting
independent tests with different technologies was expected to provide a streamlined approach to
operating the user facility. Water treatment technologies were anticipated to require chemicals,
energy (steam, electricity, etc.), cooling and/or potable water, instrument air, and other
utilities/services. Combined with the production of effluents (clean water, wastewater, residuals,
etc.) and the need for sampling, conducting multiple tests at the same time was expected to help

 4-1

minimize operating costs. It would also have been possible to compare technologies, approaches,
and processes at the conclusion of testing and following review of results.

The user facility was to be located at the IW-3/TIW-2 site (see Figure 4-1). This location was
selected due to available space and its proximity to power and cooling water access points
supplied by connections originating at Plant Smith. The preliminary layout for the user facility
(Figure 4-2) accommodated up to four different users at one time through a main power supply,
source water supply, and effluent collection system. The facility area was to meet environmental
requirements and be returned to its original classification upon completion of the project.

4.1.1 Electrical and Controls

The electrical and controls design specified the power supply required to operate pumps for
extracting and transporting brine from the TEMW-A site, operating the water treatment user
equipment, and injecting the combined effluent from the facility into TIW-2. To supply the
necessary power, electrical cable was designed to run from Plant Smith and terminate at the

for installation at TIW-1 to supply power to the TEMW-A and associated sites.

Electrical cable was also designed from the TIW-1 site to the TEMW-A site within a new
conduit, which would have been installed by horizontal directional drilling or open cut direct
burial to protect the cable from damage. A main disconnect/distribution panel at the TEMW-A
site was included in the plans to provide local power (e.g., lighting) and power the pump within
TEMW-A.

The design further specified electrical cable from the TIW-1 site to the TIW-3 site, with most of
the run placed in existing conduit. A limited section of new conduit was anticipated to access and

be installed by open cut direct burial. A main disconnect/distribution panel was designed for
installation at the TIW-3 site, and new conduit, cable, and local disconnects were planned to

Control of the injection system at TIW-2 was designed around a single programmable logic

Florida Department of Environmental Protection, monitored levels within the two tanks and the
annular monitor tank, and controlled the downstream and upstream pumping systems. Control
wire was designed to be surface laid within conduit adjacent to piping, with relays identified as
the primary control method for remote pumps.

General specifications were detailed in the process flow diagram (Figure 4-3) and refined as

All piping was designed to be surface-laid adjacent to existing roadways except where road

epoxy lining. Valves larger than 5 cm (2 in.) were specified as flanged butterfly valves of steel
construction with epoxy lining. Check valves were designed as non-slamming type rated for the
operating conditions.

A surge analysis was completed on the TIW-2 injection system based on a peak flow of 1,090
m³/d (200 gal/min). This analysis identified the surge mitigation measures necessary to protect
the piping and well. Air/vacuum release valves and potentially specialized check valves were
designed as the primary mitigation measures. No surge tanks or additional measures were
anticipated. A reduced injection rate (i.e., less than 1,090 m³/d) was identified as a potential
operating requirement to provide protection for the well and associated piping.

A total of four pump systems were specified for the treatment facility. These systems were
designed as packaged units to minimize work required on site. The four systems included:

 • A blending tank pump with a capacity of 136 m³/d (25 gal/min), sized to convey source

 • A collection sump pump with a capacity of 1,360 m³/d (250 gal/min), sized to convey

 • Injection pumps (two at 50% capacity, each with variable frequency drive controls) with

 • Acid and caustic feed pumps (one each) with constant speed controllers and packaged

4-2

The blending tank pump was planned as a centrifugal-type pump, the collection sump pump as a
submersible-type pump, and the injection pumps as multistage-type pumps. All pumps were
specified to be of standard steel/stainless construction with epoxy lining where appropriate. No
specialized materials of construction (e.g., nickel alloy, duplex stainless steel) were incorporated
due to the anticipated short duration of use.

4.1.4 Tanks and Filters

A total of two tanks and four bag filter systems were included in the treatment facility design. All
tanks and filter systems were to be rented and modeled after those commonly used in fracking
operations. The blending tank was intended to provide extracted brine as the main source water
to the user facility. Depending on the technology or technologies undergoing testing and the
needs expressed by users, the facility design also allowed for lower concentration source water
(less than 166,000 mg/L TDS) by blending extracted brine with filtered water supplied by Plant
Smith. The blending tank was designed to equalize source water to assist with steady flow and
concentration to the treatment systems.

The equalization tank was included to protect the injection pump and well at TIW-2. All
effluents from the user facility were to be collected, filtered through bag filters, and equalized
prior to injection. If required, pH adjustment was incorporated to prevent scaling in the injection
well.

4.2 Brine Treatment Modeling

A draft desalination technology vendor questionnaire was co-developed by EPRI and the Energy
& Environmental Research Center (EERC), the other BEST project recipient, to identify
technologies with potential to treat high-TDS waters. The draft was submitted to DOE for review
and comment, with the intent that a final version would be provided to vendors upon DOE
approval. The questionnaire sought information from vendors on technology capabilities,
treatment economics, and business-related considerations. These responses were to be used in
assessing and finalizing the user facility capabilities to enable successful testing.

While the questionnaire included the anticipated chemical analysis of the extracted brine, it
required vendors to conduct their own evaluations of treatment characteristics. To support
technology vendors in preparing for user facility testing, a preliminary analysis using
geochemical modeling was conducted. This modeling evaluated pretreatment and desalination of
the source water for clean water recovery, and the results were intended to provide stakeholders
with a better understanding of how highly saline brine might be treated.

The specific objective of the modeling was to numerically analyze the anticipated brine/source
water composition so that processes could be better understood in terms of solid-phase formation
and solution chemistry changes during precipitation softening and desalination treatment steps.
The modeling was conducted by Golder Associates, with EPRI supplying input data and
information, and facilitating review and discussion of the results in collaboration with Golder.
Results from the initial simulations of geochemical transformations of the brine as a function of
pretreatment steps were documented and reviewed.

 4-3

It was recognized that when modeling complex systems, the results could be useful in decision-
making or for directing ongoing work. However, prior to implementation of treatment systems or
large capital projects, the modeling would have required confirmation through laboratory testing,
empirical validation, and pilot-scale demonstrations. Models were also expected to require
recalibration and updating as new data and monitoring results became available to remain both
relevant and reliable.

4.2.1 Geochemical Modeling Approach and Assumptions

The feed water used as the input source for the modeling scenarios was derived from a sample
taken from a well at Plant Smith. Laboratory analysis of this sample did not yield an accurate
balance of cation and anion species, and therefore the major species required manual adjustment
to achieve a balanced TDS concentration. The concentrations used for the modeling studies were
documented in Table 4-2. It was recognized that the extracted brine from this sample might not
have been representative of real-time conditions under actual CO₂ injection and storage, and that

Desalination technologies were evaluated as potential means of recovering higher-purity water
from the extracted brine. Commercial desalination technologies employing volume reduction,
evaporation, and concentration approaches were generally understood to be limited by the
hardness concentration of the source water, a challenge documented in detail in the Phase I
report. Hardness removal was modeled through chemical softening, in which the major hardness
species—carbonate, calcium, and magnesium—were converted to less soluble sodium-based
salts by precipitation. The standard softening approach for water sources with high non-
carbonate hardness and lower alkalinity, such as the Plant Smith water, typically involved slaked
lime or caustic and soda ash for precipitating dissolved calcium and magnesium. This process
was modeled as a chemical precipitation system with reaction vessels designed to provide
sufficient retention/reaction time for the addition of softening agents, followed by clarification to

4-4

settle precipitated solids and, if necessary, polishing or filtration of the product water to meet
desalination technology limits.

Three modeling scenarios were carried out using the feed water chemistry:

 • Two cases of chemical softening with varying concentrations of slaked lime, caustic, and
 soda ash. These produced trends showing 0–100% softening for calcium (Ca),
 magnesium (Mg), and silica (SiO₂), along with projections of effluent water chemistry
 and the mineralogy and mass of precipitated solids.
 • One case simulating a generic concentration process for water recovery and precipitated
 salt production, using softened water chemistry from one of the softening scenarios. This
 modeled trends for 0–90% water removal/concentration and provided salt
 characterization.

The geochemical modeling was conducted using PHREEQC (V3.3.12-12704), developed by the
U.S. Geological Survey. PHREEQC was selected because of its ability to simulate speciation,
batch reactions, one-dimensional transport, and inverse geochemical calculations, including
systems with highly concentrated water. The code equilibrates aqueous solutions with minerals,
gases, solid solutions, exchange phases, and sorption surfaces simultaneously, based on
equilibrium chemistry. An important advantage was its flexible thermodynamic databases, which
could be interchanged depending on the modeling scenario. For this work, the Pitzer database
was chosen, as it incorporates specific ion interaction theory to account for the non-ideality of
high-ionic-strength brines.

To bound the simulations of both softening and concentration/evaporation processes, model
assumptions and input parameters were defined as follows:

 • The analytical composition data (Table 4-2) excluded any other water treatment
 chemicals.
 • The model’s default solution density of 1.0 g/cm³ was adjusted to 1.16 g/cm³ to represent
 brine density.
 • The model’s default solution mass of 1 kg was retained.
 • A feed water temperature of 33 °C (91 °F) was applied in all simulations.
 • Atmospheric carbon dioxide was excluded from all simulations, as precipitation reactions
 were assumed to occur faster than CO₂ dissolution at the modeled conditions.
 • Precipitates were determined based on solubility constants and thermodynamic reactions
 defined by the selected database, acknowledging that actual precipitation could vary due
 to additional variables not captured in the model.
 • The Pitzer database was applied for all simulations due to the high ionic strength of the
 brine.

4.2.2 Chemical Softening Modeling Results

The chemical agents used in the simulations to model the softening process were slaked lime
[Ca(OH)₂], caustic (NaOH), and soda ash (Na₂CO₃). The method used to simulate chemical
softening of the feed water followed a two-step approach. In the first step, either lime or caustic

 4-5

was used to increase the pH of the brine solution. The second step involved the addition of soda
ash, which was used to increase the carbonate alkalinity of the solution. The increased pH (to
approximately >10 standard units [S.U.]) favored the removal of magnesium as Mg(OH)₂
(brucite). The alkalinity of the feed water was approximately 400 times lower (in reported
values, not as CaCO₃) than the mass of hardness present. Thus, increasing the pH of the solution
alone was not sufficient to create the amount of carbonate required for calcite precipitation, and
soda ash was required in the second step to satisfy the carbonate demand.

The slaked lime/soda ash simulations were completed for 1 g and 3 g slaked lime addition. For
the caustic/soda ash simulations, the same quantities of caustic were chosen. Soda ash was then
added incrementally to both the lime and caustic up to a maximum of 1 mol (106 g), as shown in

4.2.5 Desalination by Evaporation/Concentration Modeling Results

To understand the effect that evaporation/concentration desalination would have had on the brine
chemistry, the model was set up to simulate the evaporation/concentration of the brine. The
PHREEQC model simulated the evaporation/concentration of a solution by the stepwise removal
of water from the system on a mass basis, with the chemical system reaching equilibrium after
each reaction step. As with most models, this represented an ideal scenario that did not
accurately translate to real-world conditions, but it provided approximations to guide additional
studies.

The mass of water removed in evaporation/concentration of the brine was executed in 10%
increments and limited to a maximum of 90%, which equated to a mass of 900 g from a 1,000-g
solution. The 100% removal of water was not possible in PHREEQC during
evaporation/concentration reactions due to water molecules being an essential part of the crystal
structure of minerals (i.e., water of crystallization).

The focus for the evaporation/concentration simulations was on the slaked lime and soda ash
softened brine. In highly saline waters, traditional evaporators and crystallizers were limited to
chemical species that contribute to hardness (i.e., calcium and magnesium) due to the high
solubility of the chloride salts. To recover all, or nearly all, of the available water from the
source, softening was required to take advantage of the lower solubility of sodium chloride.

For this simulation, the 3 g slaked lime and 52.95 g soda ash composition was chosen as the
input solution to undergo evaporation/concentration. This choice was reasonable given that no
significant change occurred in terms of the precipitation of calcite, brucite, and dolomite with
higher additions of soda ash. In addition, given the similarity of the results for softening either
with slaked lime/soda ash or caustic/soda ash, the slaked lime case was used as the discussion

4.2.6 Discussion on Modeling Results

The feed water used as the input source to the modeling scenarios was generated by a sampling
event at Plant Smith. The results from laboratory analysis did not provide an accurate balance of
cation and anion species, which required some adjustment to the major species to account for a
balanced TDS concentration (see Table 4-2). The brine chemistry, although highly concentrated
in TDS, represented a typical case of non-carbonated hardness. The alkalinity present was far
less than the carbonates required for calcite precipitation and hardness removal. Conventional
water softening theory indicated that both the elevation of pH (by lime or caustic soda) and the
addition of a source of carbonate ions (soda ash) were needed to remove calcium as calcite and
magnesium as brucite. At the same time, this theory predicted the removal of silica as
magnesium silicate phases.

The model results indicated that most of the magnesium would have been removed either as
brucite or dolomite, with the remainder almost entirely removed by antigorite precipitation.
Thus, silica would also have been removed to very low levels. The model predicted that dolomite
might potentially form; however, the model did not consider kinetic processes that could have
inhibited precipitation of certain minerals. Since dolomite is not known to form readily in
conventional softening processes, two scenarios had been modeled to explore the difference for a
system where dolomite precipitation was allowed versus a scenario where it was not allowed.

The model results obtained suggested that it was theoretically possible to remove calcium and
magnesium to the <1 mg/L range. This concentration was lower than would normally be
accepted for calcium in a lower TDS water and resulted from the very high carbonate addition
and relatively coarse reaction steps selected in this analysis. Chemical softening was dependent
on temperature to assist in improving the kinetics of these reactions, and the same result may not
have been observed under field conditions. Two considerations would probably have led to
slightly higher calcium values in practice:

 • Optimized soda ash addition would have been implemented to remove calcium to some
 predetermined level required by the water recovery process.
 • The reactions used in the geochemical modeling assumed chemical equilibrium, and
 hence reactions would have gone to completion within the limits of the retention time of
 the softening reactor. This was unlikely in practice, and experimental studies would have
 been needed to explore these kinetic limitations.

Continuing with the 3 g slaked lime and 52.95 g soda ash scenario, consumption of softening
chemicals for the 136.3 m³/d (25 gal/min) user facility studies had been estimated. Using these
values, the facility required 0.329 kg/min (0.725 lbs/min) of slaked lime and 5.81 kg/min (12.8
lbs/min) of soda ash. If the facility had operated for 80 days (approximately four months, five

 4-13

days per week) and ten hours per day, usage would have totaled 15.9 metric tonnes (17.5 tons) of
slaked lime and 279 tonnes (307 tons) of soda ash. Golder Associates provided estimated costs
for slaked lime and soda ash at $250/tonne ($227/ton) and $370/tonne ($336/ton), respectively.
At these prices, the estimated chemical softening cost for the four-month duration was
approximately $110,000.

4.2.7 Recommendations for Future Studies

Due to the uncertainty about mineral formation under the conditions expected for this softening
system (hyper-saline and high carbonate values), the following recommendations were made:

 • A laboratory-scale softening experiment be conducted to measure both the concentrations
 of the major solutes of interest and to characterize the precipitates formed as a result. The
 results obtained from the modeling scenarios provided a starting point for these
 laboratory studies.
 • At the same time, the softened water could have been further evaluated in a bench-scale
 evaporation/crystallization test so that the production of precipitate materials could be
 investigated to validate the model.
 • The model results could have been further refined to explore the optimization of lime and
 soda ash dosages required to achieve softening within the range required by various water
 recovery processes, avoiding overdosing of chemicals. These results would have been
 validated and updated based on laboratory and bench-scale testing.

EPRI had also explored the use of alternative modeling software that might have been better
suited for high-saline environments (e.g., OLI Studio). Coupled with laboratory testing on actual
water, all results would have helped treatment technology vendors and facility users plan for
future studies.

5 PRESSURE OPTIMIZATION AND ADAPTIVE
MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

The project planned to inject low-salinity water and extracted brine into isolated sandstone layers
of the Lower Tuscaloosa Massive sand at about 1,500 m (4,900 ft) depth. These formations have
relatively high permeability in the order of tens or hundreds of millidarcy; thus, for the field
demonstration project, injection into isolated layers with limited thickness ensures that detectable
and laterally extensive differential pressure plumes are generated. The injected fluids are mostly
ash pond water, but also storm water, reclaimed cooling tower blowdown water, and other minor
permitted wastewater from plant processes. These have relatively low salinity about ~1,200 –
3,000 mg/L, which is in strong contrast to the Tuscaloosa brine (about 166,000 mg/L on
average). This contrast enables fluid plume monitoring with geophysical electromagnetic (EM)
techniques (EPRI, 2016; Birkholzer et al., 2018).
This chapter summarizes past efforts in reservoir modeling and optimization conducted for the
demonstration project, focusing on water injection, brine extraction, and pressure management
strategies. The analyses included a hypothetical pressure management scenario incorporating a
critically stressed fault near the planned injection area; it should be emphasized that no actual
structural risks, such as faults or fracture zones, were identified at the Plant Smith field site.
The modeling and optimization work aimed to interpret field injection test results, update
reservoir properties such as permeability and pore compressibility, and evaluate the impacts of
low-salinity fluid injection and brine extraction on pressure and salinity. Additional objectives
included designing and assessing pressure management strategies, as well as identifying potential
injection and extraction rates and schedules that would have been tested in the field. These
studies provided a foundation for understanding reservoir behavior and informing the adaptive
management approach for the project.

5.1 Reservoir Modeling and Analyses of Injection Tests

5.1.1 Modeling and Simulation Approaches
The site characterization studies at Plant Smith indicated that the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower
Cretaceous (LT) formation contains no oil or gas, allowing for the use of a single-phase flow
simulator to model water and brine movement. An in-house single-phase code, incorporating
variable-density and variable-viscosity transport, was employed for the reservoir modeling
activities. This simulator was developed under prior DOE funding to study convective mixing
and dissolution trapping during CO₂ storage and has been verified against benchmark analytical
and numerical problems, as well as laboratory experiments on brine flow and mixing. The code
has also been applied in risk assessment projects for evaluating brine leakage through leaky
wellbores in geologic CO₂ storage. The simulator solves the coupled continuity, advection-
diffusion, and Darcy equations, and has recently been enhanced to simulate fines migration and
associated permeability changes.

 5-1

Brine properties—including compressibility, density, and viscosity—were computed as functions
of salt concentration, pressure, and temperature using established correlations that cover wide
ranges of salinity, pressure, and temperature, making them suitable for the Tuscaloosa formation.
Static temperature variation with depth was included, though dynamic heat flow was not
considered. The governing equations were discretized via the Finite Volume Method and solved
using a Newton-Raphson approach with a preconditioned restarted Generalized Minimum
Residual solver for the linearized system.
For certain scenarios, a semi-analytical model was also employed to represent multi-layered
aquifer systems with alternating aquifers and aquitards, along with any number of injection,
extraction, or leaky wells. This approach enabled rapid optimization of pressure management and
injection/extraction strategies, with the results subsequently refined using the full numerical
model.
The modeling workflow began with updating reservoir models based on geologic data acquired
in Phase II. The calibrated model incorporated data from the October 2020 injection test and
historical short-term injection tests in TIW-1 in July 2012, which highlighted potential injectivity
risks associated with low-salinity water interacting with clay-rich sandstone. Mitigation
strategies, such as controlling the pH and salinity of the injected water, were evaluated to prevent
injectivity decline and optimize reservoir performance.

5.1.2 Overview of the Reservoir Models
The initial Phase I numerical reservoir model and simulations were limited in stratigraphic and
geologic detail, relying solely on logs from the TIW-1 well. In Phase II, the geologic model was
significantly improved using new data from the TIW-2 and TEMW-A wells. While geophysical
logs from TIW-2 were compromised by operational challenges and poor core recovery in the
Lower Tuscaloosa Massive Sand, the TEMW-A well provided high-quality logs, including
nuclear magnetic resonance (CMR), resistivity, bulk density, and compensated neutron porosity.
These data were used to update porosity and permeability estimates and to delineate injection
and extraction zones, enabling the development of revised static models for TIW-1, TIW-2, and
TEMW-A.
The revised static geologic models for TIW-1 and TIW-2 extend to a depth of 2133.6 m (7000 ft)
and contain 86 layers with similar properties, while the TEMW-A model extends to 1518.4 m
and incorporates the upper layers identified in TIW-1 and TIW-2, supporting the lateral
continuity of the Lower Tuscaloosa formation. A comparative analysis of porosity and
permeability values across the three well locations indicated generally consistent reservoir
properties. This similarity allowed the use of a layer–cake model, with horizontally averaged but
vertically heterogeneous properties, as a simplified yet robust representation for reservoir
modeling. Comparisons with a fully interpolated heterogeneous model demonstrated minimal
differences in predicted pressure and salinity, confirming the adequacy of the layer-cake model
for design and adaptive management purposes.
The final geologic model features alternating high-permeability sandstone layers and clay-rich
confining layers, corresponding to aquifers and aquitards, respectively. The confining layers
have approximately two orders of magnitude lower permeability than the sandy layers. The
typical numerical grid used in simulations around the wells is shown in Figure 5–1, which
illustrates the vertical distribution of permeability and porosity values across the layers.

5.1.3 Analysis of Injection Test in TIW-2
The October 6, 2020 injection test involved introducing approximately 95,250 gallons of filtered
Tuscaloosa brine into TIW-2 at an average rate of 200 gpm over eight hours, while TIW-1
served as an observation well to monitor pressure changes. The test data were used to calibrate
reservoir properties, including the permeability and compressibility of both aquifer and confining
layers, by fitting the model to the observed pressure response. TIW-1, screened over the full
formation thickness, provided hydraulic communication between injection layers and the
underlying aquifer, which was incorporated into a full-thickness reservoir model.
To simplify the calibration process, the permeability of aquifers and aquitards was adjusted using
multiplicative factors, while pore compressibility was assumed uniform within each layer type.
The effective resistance of TIW-1 was also treated as a key parameter, as it governs flow into the
underlying layers and influences pressure behavior. Unknown parameters were estimated using a
global optimization algorithm, producing model predictions that matched observed pressure
changes reasonably well.
 5-3

The calibration revealed that the aquitard permeability was significantly higher than previously
characterized, which would reduce pressure buildup near the injection zone by allowing flow
through confining layers. This increase also decreases flow through the passive relief well,
potentially limiting its effectiveness. Model simulations indicated small but measurable flow
between aquifer layers and the passive well, suggesting that downhole measurements could
detect these flows. The role of the passive relief well in long-term injection scenarios is
addressed in subsequent analyses.

5.1.4 Analyses of the Previous Injection Tests in TIW-2 and Risk of Injectivity
Decline
Short-term successive injection tests conducted in TIW-1 in July 2012 involved introducing low-
salinity water along the full screened interval of the well. These tests exhibited an unusual trend
in injection pressures, with maximum pressure buildup increasing from the first to the fourth test
despite decreasing injection rates. This pattern suggested a decline in the specific injectivity of
the well, likely influenced by the presence of clays in the sand layers, which could detach,
migrate, and clog pores.
To evaluate the mechanisms behind the injectivity decline, a numerical model was applied that
simulates water flow, dissolved salts, and fine particle transport, including particle detachment,
migration, and capture at pore constrictions. Without incorporating fines migration, the model
predicted decreasing pressure trends proportional to injection rates, which did not match
observed data. Incorporating clay fines migration provided a better match to the overall pressure
buildup, although recovery periods were slightly slower than observed.
Key parameters controlling clay detachment in the model include critical salinity and critical
velocity. When salinity drops below a threshold or flow velocity exceeds a critical value, clay
particles detach and migrate, potentially reducing permeability. Model calibration indicated that
detachment in aquitards requires stronger hydrodynamic forces and occurs more slowly than in
aquifers. The estimated critical salt mass fractions suggested that both dispersible and swelling-
induced clays contribute to permeability reduction, with aquifers generally experiencing more
severe decline than aquitards.
Simulations of salinity changes and permeability damage around the well indicated that most
permeability reduction occurs in the top aquifer layers during the first two injection tests.
Subsequent injections caused minimal additional damage, as the reduced permeability slowed the
radial migration of low-salinity water. Overall, the results highlight that careful control of
injection water salinity can mitigate the risk of injectivity decline caused by clay fines migration.
The analysis of injection tests at the field site indicates that permeability in the sandstone
formations is affected by both the migration of clay fines and swelling behavior of certain clay
minerals. Observations from successive low-salinity injection tests revealed that pressure decay
between injections occurred more rapidly than predicted by numerical models that assumed
irreversible permeability loss. This discrepancy suggests that some of the permeability damage in
the formation may be at least partially reversible. Mineralogical analyses show that the Lower
Tuscaloosa sandstones contain a mixture of non-swelling clays, such as kaolinite and illite, as
well as swelling clays, notably smectite. The flow of freshwater into these brine-saturated layers
can trigger migration of clay fines and swelling, both of which contribute to reductions in
permeability. Swelling effects, particularly in the lower-permeability aquitard layers, may be

5-4

reversible if the original brine conditions are restored, allowing the formation to regain some of
its original hydraulic conductivity.
To investigate the mechanisms controlling permeability decline, pore-scale computational
studies were performed. These studies examined the detachment and mobilization of clay fines in
response to changes in fluid salinity and flow velocity. Two main mechanisms govern particle
behavior: hydrodynamic forces arising from the drag of flowing water, and electrostatic forces,
including van der Waals attractions and the repulsive components of the electric double layer.
Higher ionic strength (higher salinity) promotes adhesion of clay fines to pore walls, whereas
lower salinity increases repulsive forces and causes particles to detach and migrate through the
pore network. Detached fines can accumulate at constrictions in the pore space, reducing local
permeability and creating flow bottlenecks. Simulations further demonstrated that reversing the
flow direction with high-salinity water could flush the fines from these constrictions and allow
them to reattach to grain surfaces, restoring permeability nearly completely. This demonstrates
that both fines migration and reversible clogging due to swelling can play significant roles in
injectivity behavior and can be actively managed.
Field-scale modeling indicates that early detection of permeability impairment is possible by
monitoring short-term pressure responses in the injection well. Even relatively modest injection
rates can reveal the onset of fines-related permeability decline within hours to a few days of
operation. While traditional approaches to resolve clogged pores rely on chemical treatments
such as acid application, the modeling suggests that hydraulic remediation strategies may also be
effective. These strategies include controlled injection and extraction to redirect brine flow
through areas with reduced injectivity, thereby mobilizing and removing fines without
introducing additional chemicals.
Preventive measures are particularly effective in minimizing the risk of injectivity decline.
Maintaining the salinity of the injected water above a critical threshold—generally in the range
of 4000–9000 ppm—prevents the detachment of clay fines. Additionally, controlling the pH of
the injected water to mildly acidic levels (approximately 5–6) reduces the risk of chemical
precipitation around the well and stabilizes the clay particles in the formation. By avoiding the
detachment of fines and chemical precipitation, the injected water maintains favorable flow
conditions, preserving reservoir permeability and ensuring sustained injection capacity.
Collectively, these studies underscore the importance of integrating pore-scale understanding,
numerical modeling, and field-scale monitoring to both prevent and remediate potential
injectivity losses. The combination of chemical control, flow management, and early detection
allows for a proactive approach to maintaining reservoir performance and mitigating risks
associated with clay fines migration, swelling, and chemical interactions.

5.2 Planning of Pressure Management Strategies
In both Phase I and Phase II of the project, the research team conducted field measurements and
reservoir modeling analyses to explore and evaluate different approaches for pressure control and
plume management at the Plant Smith field site. Building on these efforts, this chapter presents
the finalized pressure management scenario for a planned 17-month injection of low-salinity
water into a selected zone of the Lower Tuscaloosa Massive Sand via the TIW-2 well. The
Lower Tuscaloosa formation is characterized by high permeability, on the order of hundreds of

 5-5

millidarcies, and injection into relatively thin, confined layers ensures the formation of
differential pressure plumes that are both detectable and laterally extensive.
The injection strategy relies on available field water sources, targeting an average injection rate
of approximately 545 m³/d (100 gal/min). The injected water will have a relatively low salinity,
with total dissolved solids (TDS) below 6,000 ppm, in contrast to the native Tuscaloosa brine,
which has TDS around 180,000 ppm. This sharp contrast between injected and formation fluids
allows for monitoring of the injected fluid plume using geophysical techniques, such as
electromagnetic surveys, providing a detailed view of fluid migration within the reservoir.
The Lower Tuscaloosa formation consists of thick, regionally extensive sediments with high
porosity and permeability, supporting both large-scale fluid injection and significant CO₂ storage
potential. In this context, the primary objective of pressure management is not to maximize
storage capacity or minimize local injection pressures. Instead, it is focused on controlling the
large lateral footprint of pressure changes induced by injection and preventing potentially
adverse impacts on the surrounding formations.
For demonstration purposes, the scenario assumes a hypothetical, critically stressed fault near the
injection zone (Figure 5–2). Under this scenario, injection into the screened zones of TIW-2
could generate pressure responses exceeding a critical threshold associated with this hypothetical
fault. To manage this pressure, the project team has developed a two-pronged brine extraction
strategy. The first method involves active extraction using a newly installed dedicated well,
TEMW-A, with time-varying pumping rates optimized to mitigate pressure buildup. The second
method leverages a “passive” pressure relief approach using the existing TIW-1 well, which is
completed over the entire thickness of the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower Cretaceous formations
(approximately 1,447–2,133 m). Together, these methods aim to maintain pressure below the
critical threshold along the hypothetical fault while minimizing the total volume of brine that
must be extracted.
The section begins with reservoir predictions for the planned long-term injection without any
brine extraction, which provides a baseline for understanding the pressure buildup and the
associated management objectives. Following this, the detailed pressure management and brine
extraction plans are presented, outlining strategies developed to meet quantified goals, including
allowable pressures along the hypothetical fault and the expected behavior of the fluid plume. It
is emphasized that no actual structural risks, such as active faults or fracture zones, were
identified during site screening. The inclusion of a critically stressed fault and the selection of
critical pressure thresholds in this scenario are purely hypothetical and serve only as a
demonstration for evaluating pressure management strategies.

5.2.3 Optimization of Pressure Management Strategies with Passive Relief and
Extraction Wells
The primary objective of pressure management in the Lower Tuscaloosa is to keep the pressure
buildup at/below a critical value along the hypothetical fault and at the same time to minimize
the volume of the extracted brine. Additional objectives and constraints include minimizing the
Area of Review and ensuring that no injected fluids are pulled into the brine extraction well
(TEMW-A). As demonstrated in this report, for the average injection rate (545 m3/d) planned in
this study, the selected injection zone with its limited thickness experiences sufficiently strong
and detectable pressure and plume migration signals within the duration of the Phase II injection
test.
For the purpose of this project, the critical differential pressure increase for fault activation (see
Figure 5–1) may be assumed to vary between 10 kPa and 14 kPa, based on the model
predictions shown in the previous sections for the 100-gpm injection scenario. We reiterate here
that the presence of a critically stressed fault assumed in the pressure management scenario is
purely hypothetical for the purpose of demonstration. We also point out that the fault activation
threshold pressure selections are again a choice made for the purpose of demonstration. We also
calculated the critical pressure threshold used in Class VI permits to delineate the size of the
Area of Review (AoR), which is defined as the required pressure buildup to lift native brine from
the injection zone into the deepest underground sources of drinking water (USDW) aquifer
through open wellbores or leakage pathways. The deepest USDW at the Plant Smith site is a part
of the Floridian aquifer system. Based on the vertical distance between the Lower Tuscaloosa
injection zone and the USDW, and the existing pressure, temperature and salinity differences in
the aquifers, the critical pressure buildup for brine leakage through open leakage pathways varies
between 0.7-0.8 MPa, which are well above our predicted pressure changes in the field site.
Similarly, for the injection rates planned, the predicted maximum pressure change around the
injection well (~0.45 MPa for 100-gpm injection) is well below the critical pressure buildup for
fracturing (~7 MPa), as discussed earlier. Thus, although we take into account the possibility of
fracturing as a constraint in our optimization algorithms, this is probably not so relevant for the
planned injection at this site.
The existing TIW-1 well penetrates about 700 meters of the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower
Cretaceous sediments with many permeable layers below the injection layers. This makes it an
excellent choice for passively relieving the pressure near TIW-2 and delaying the movement of
the pressure and injectant plumes in the direction from TIW-2 towards the hypothetical fault.
However, as demonstrated in the previous section, TIW-1 operating alone as a passive relief is
not sufficient to reduce the pressure buildup below the desired critical value nearby the
hypothetical fault. Therefore, the new TEMW-A well is needed to actively pump the brine out of
the reservoir and thereby satisfy the pressure buildup constraints near the fault. An indirect effect
of both passive and active wells is to minimize the AoR. Because of the high drilling costs in the
area, only two new wells could have been drilled to satisfy the constraints associated with the
total budget, and therefore TIW-1 and TEMW-A serve also as monitoring wells. Based on the
forward modeling results, active extraction from TEMW-A does not have to start with the onset
of injection and can wait several months until critical pressure changes are experienced near the
fault, recorded by TIW-1 and TEMW-A wells.

5.2.3.2 Field Implementation of Adaptive Reservoir Management
The model analyses above present our current view of a realistic base scenario that takes account
of actual site conditions and is based on our current understanding of site-specific geologic
conditions. However, our assessments based on the available data involve uncertainties due to
incomplete knowledge of the subsurface system and the hydraulic properties. Therefore,
applying adaptive management strategies will be crucial for controlling the system behavior
under uncertain reservoir conditions and varying operational situations (e.g., variable injection
rates and durations). The adaptive management integrates monitoring + modeling + inversion +
optimization through an iterative process. A detailed description of our adaptive management
approach and its applications are presented in a research article by Gonzalez-Nicolas et al.
(2019).

 5-23

During the actual field implementation, the extraction rates will be adaptively estimated and
applied as more monitoring data are being collected and the reservoir model is updated. The
frequency of the reservoir model updates and the revisions to the optimization calculations will
depend on the pressure transients and complexity of the reservoir system as will be understood
with the monitoring data.
As presented in the previous section, we purposely focused on the scenarios that allows the
extraction start times of 3 to 6 months. We will use this initial time with injection only to better
learn the reservoir behavior using monitoring data and improved models. Our results show that
reservoir pressure management can be extremely benefited by early and high frequency pressure
monitoring during early injection times, especially for poor initial reservoir characterization.
Low frequencies of model calibration and optimization with monitoring data may lead to
optimization problems because either pressure buildup constraints are violated or excessively
high extraction rates are proposed.
The research team plans to monitor the pressure changes in continuous manner at all three wells
(TIW-1, TIW-2 and TEMW-A). Considering the potential injectivity related risks in the field,
model analyses of the pressure data collected within several hours to first few days are critical
for diagnosing and understanding the risks. Other than the first several days, collected data
should be incorporated into the adaptive modeling framework on a weekly basis for the model
calibration and future predictions of the reservoir behavior.
Before the extraction time period (< 3 to 6 months), updating and testing the model predictions
with the monitoring data will inform us how accurately we can predict the optimal extraction
rates and schedules for managing the pressure buildup in the reservoir. After the extraction starts,
the model testing with the data, particularly the model predictions of the pressure data at TIW-1
well, will show us how successfully we can control the pressure rises (near the targeted
hypothetical fault zone) in response to the injection.
In addition to the pressure buildup data, the team will conduct the cross-well geophysical
measurements to image the distribution of the low-salinity fluid plume before the extraction
starts and after the injection ends. The team also plans to acquire flow rate measurements
through the passive relief well. These will provide additional important data to improve and test
the reservoir model predictions and the efficiency of the passive relief well.

5.3 Conclusions
The section outlines the final field test design and pressure management strategies developed for
Phase II of the Plant Smith field demonstration, focusing on brine extraction as a means of
controlling reservoir pressure and managing injected fluid plume behavior. Building upon earlier
field injection tests and reservoir modeling, the research team refined the reservoir model and
used it to optimize pressure control strategies for a 17-month injection period into laterally
continuous zones of the Lower Tuscaloosa Massive sand. The project’s primary objectives for
pressure management are threefold: to mitigate large-scale pressure impacts that could otherwise
contribute to induced seismicity or leakage along faults, to minimize the Area of Review (AoR)
associated with injection operations, and to control the migration of the injected fluid plume.
The selected pressure management approach combines two complementary extraction methods.
Active extraction will be carried out using a new well, TEMW-A, with optimized and time-

5-24

varying pumping rates. In parallel, a passive pressure relief strategy will be implemented using
the existing well TIW-1, which is completed across deeper portions of the Tuscaloosa/Lower
Cretaceous system. Passive relief functions by transferring formation fluids into deeper layers,
driven by the pressure differential in the injection zone, and has the advantage of removing
substantial volumes of brine without active pumping. However, modeling indicates that passive
relief alone provides only limited reductions in maximum pressure buildup—on the order of a
few percent compared to scenarios without its use. Therefore, both methods must be combined to
achieve effective pressure management under the current reservoir conditions.
The demonstration will involve injecting approximately 545 m³/d (100 gallons per minute) of
wastewater from a Class I injection well (TIW-2, Figure 3-1) over 17 months at a depth of
roughly 1,500 m. Modeling predicts that this injection would, for the purposes of the project
scenario, generate pressure increases above the critical threshold for reactivating a hypothetical
nearby fault. To address this, the research team established critical pressure thresholds between
12–14 kPa, calibrated against injection test data from TIW-2, to guide extraction strategies.
Optimization results show that brine extraction should commence three to six months after the
start of injection, depending on the chosen critical threshold, with maximum extraction rates
ranging from 50 to 100 m³/d. These results highlight the value of early, high-frequency pressure
monitoring, which is expected to substantially improve adaptive reservoir management by
informing real-time updates to extraction plans.
The section also discusses reservoir risks related to injectivity impairment, particularly from clay
fines detachment and chemical interactions between the injected and formation waters. Analyses
based on previous freshwater injection tests at TIW-1 suggest that these risks can be mitigated by
maintaining injected water salinity above 4,000–9,000 ppm and by adjusting water pH to reduce
clay platelet detachment and prevent precipitation-related pore clogging. Early-time pressure
data from the injection well will be critical for diagnosing and addressing potential declines in
injectivity.
Finally, this section emphasizes that the pressure management scenarios are designed as realistic
base cases informed by actual site conditions, geologic information, and anticipated wastewater
volumes. Nonetheless, deviations are expected as additional monitoring data, reservoir
characterization, and operational details become available. Consequently, the project will employ
an adaptive management framework that integrates ongoing monitoring, modeling, inversion,
and optimization in an iterative process to refine pressure control and plume management
throughout the field demonstration.

PROGRAM

The electromagnetic survey design study described in this report was carried out with several key
objectives in mind. The first goal was to develop a method for translating porosity and salinity
data, derived from a time-lapse flow model, into a three-dimensional resistivity model that could
represent changes in the reservoir at various stages after injection began. Building on this, the
study applied a three-dimensional electromagnetic forward modeling code (EMGEO) to simulate
the responses that would be detected by electromagnetic receivers when energized by different
types of sources placed either in boreholes or at the surface. These simulations were then used to
test the effectiveness of different survey designs.

A second major objective was to evaluate how various survey configurations—specifically
combinations of crosswell and surface-to-borehole measurements—could image the resistive
plume created by injected fluids. The inversion results from these scenarios provided valuable
insight into the relative strengths and limitations of each configuration. The study then used the
combined forward and inverse modeling results, along with cost estimates from a local oil field
service company, to design a time-lapse electromagnetic acquisition plan tailored to the BEST
experiment. The report’s emphasis is on electromagnetic modeling, inversion, and survey design,
while the results of actual field data acquisition and processing will be presented in a later final
report covering Phase II field testing, pressure management, and plume monitoring strategies.

6.1 Petrophysical Transformation: From Reservoir Model to Geophysical
EM Model

Because hydrocarbons are absent in the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower Cretaceous (LT) formation,
the research team determined that a single-phase flow simulator would be sufficient to represent
reservoir behavior. The in-house simulator used in this study accounts for variable-density and
variable-viscosity effects during brine flow and transport. Its reliability has been verified through
both analytical solutions and numerical benchmarks published in the literature, as well as
through laboratory experiments that successfully reproduced brine mixing and transport
behavior. This foundation provided confidence in the suitability of the simulator for the
modeling effort.

The single-phase flow and transport reservoir model specific to the LT formation had been
developed earlier and was later updated to incorporate new geologic data collected from two
recently drilled wells, TIW-2 and TEMW-A. While the new data did reveal some variability in
porosity and permeability across the wells, overall porosity values were relatively consistent. As
a result, the research team concluded that further updates to the reservoir model were
unnecessary for the purposes of this electromagnetic study. All simulations conducted here were

therefore based on the previously established LT reservoir model, which was deemed sufficiently
robust to represent the system for geophysical imaging analysis.

6.1.1 Petrophysical Transformation Function
The reservoir flow model encompasses the Lower Tuscaloosa/Lower Cretaceous sediments at
the site. The reservoir-modeling (or hydrological) properties that have the most influence on the
reservoir's electrical rock properties are porosity 𝜙, solute (brine) concentration C, and
temperature T. The Tuscaloosa sandstones in the area of interest are friable, weakly cemented
sandstones, suggesting that bulk ionic migration through the pores of the rock is the main
contributor to the rock's electrical resistivity 𝜌, that is, electrical conduction along the surface of
the grains of the rock is negligible. Consequently, Archie's law (Archie, 1942) in the simple form

where 𝜌𝑓 is the fluid electrical resistivity, and 𝐹 is the formation factor,

after Winsauer et al. (1952). Equation 6-2 is a specific function between formation factor and
rock porosity 𝜙, referred to as Winsauer model. This model was empirically derived from
measurements on Gulf Coast sandstones (R. Petrusak, pers. comm., 2018).

Poroelastic deformation is neglected, that is, porosity is assumed constant during the fluid
injection process. Hence, the (hydrological) reservoir property that controls the changing EM

on solute concentration C and temperature T. Figure 6-1 illustrates the petrophysical

logs available for the reservoir region, we obtain the distribution of 𝜌 via Archie's law combined
with the Winsauer model (Equations 6-1 and 6-2).

6.1.2 Combining EM Background and Reservoir Model
One challenge for merging reservoir and surface-based geophysical modeling is given by a
vertical scale difference. The reservoir model covers a depth range of roughly 350 m (1,150 ft)
around the injection zone (at 𝑧 ≈1,500 m (4,921 ft)), while our EM model covers a depth range
of 2 km (1.2 mi), starting at the surface in order to accommodate the surface-to-borehole survey
component. The EM model further includes the effect of a highly resistive air layer which is
required for the accurate simulation of EM transmitters located at the surface.

Resistivity logs exist for the entire reservoir region and the overburden, thus enabling us to
construct a one-dimensional background geophysical EM model for the entire vertical extent of
the geophysical model. This background model remains constant during the injection experiment
for the regions where no overlap exists with the reservoir model. As sketched in Figure 6-2, the
vertical electrical resistivity profile is first converted into a 3D cube model with a layered
electrical resistivity structure. During the fluid injection simulation, the properties of the model
region affected by the developing freshwater plume are updated according to the petrophysical
function defined by Equations 6-1 and 6-2.

6.1.3 Constructing a Starting Model from the EM background
All our inversion experiments involve the same starting model, which we show in Figure 6-4.
The starting model assumes a homogenous background throughout the inversion domain. The
inversion domain comprises the estimated conductivity parameters and is given by the model
sub-volume between z=1330 m and z=1817 m. For the modeling volume above the top of the
inversion domain (at z=1330 m), the starting model assumes that the actual layered background
is known a priori. Below that depth, the conductivity is assumed constant at 𝜎 = 3 S/m, that is,
𝜌 = 0.333 Ωm, reflecting the high brine content at the reservoir depth.

The inversion calculations carried out in this report involve a non-linear conjugate gradient
(NLCG) method as presented by Commer and Newman (2008). Each inversion is terminated
after 125 NLCG iterations (model updating steps), where the corresponding relative data fit
increase converges towards zero.

6.3.2 Quantification of Inversion Performance
To quantify the performance of each inversion in terms of reproduction of the actual plume
model, we employ a semblance measure. In principal, this measure, abbreviated by S, quantifies
how well time-lapse properties changes, in this case changes in electrical conductivity, are
captured within an inversion image. An ideal value of 100% would indicate perfect plume
reproduction. The semblance results in Table 6-2 reveal relatively high semblance measures for
all inversions that include the crosswell CRW-E configuration (highlighted). The lowest values
result from the inversion employing only the full STB data set (STB14). A second interesting

 6-25

fact deemed from this table is that the 365-day plume is always better recovered from a
semblance standpoint than is the 180-day plume. This makes sense because as the plume gets
bigger, the scattered electromagnetic fields also get larger and thus increase the sensitivity to the
plume geometry. In the next section we use the inversion images above, the semblance measures
given in Table 6-2, along with survey cost estimates to recommend a field survey strategy to
monitor the plume development during the injection/pressure management experiment.

6.4 EM Field Procedures and Estimated Costs
The geophysical monitoring plan is designed to collect EM data sets at least three times after
injection begins. The images provided by the surveys will then be used in a joint geophysical-
flow inversion to optimize pressure control. Our plans for conducting field surveys at Plant
Smith include scoping out the length and type of surveys and calculating costs associated with
system deployment and operations.

All survey measurements conducted within Plant Smith boreholes require pressure control at the
wellhead to safely isolate high-pressure fluids in the borehole from the surface. This entails a
lubricator assembly held by an external crane in addition to the wireline deployment (see Figure
6-20). The lubricator is a long pipe that isolates the pressurized borehole fluids from the surface.
The pipe has a valve at the bottom and a second valve with “pack-off” assembly at the top where
the wireline cable can feed through without leaking borehole fluid. Initially the downhole tool is

6-26

loaded inside the lubricator and the pipe is then lifted and secured to the wellhead using a crane
to lift and hold the assembly. The lower valve is then released to allow the tool to deploy to
depth within in the casing. Pressure and fluid leakage at the top of the assembly is managed by a
pack off valve with a grease fitting.

6.6 Conclusions
This report outlines a series of numerical forward modeling and inversion calculations that have
been completed to design the electromagnetic geophysical monitoring campaign. Given the
inversion results shown in Section 4 along with the cost estimates in Section 5, it has been
determined that three campaigns of measuring both electric and magnetic field crosswell data in
the available wells will provide the best way to monitor the position of the plume. The
information provided by the surface-to-borehole data has been shown to be marginal, while
collecting these types of data can double the survey cost. Therefore, we will not collect any STB
data. The first crosswell data set will be collected using all wells just before the pump is installed
in the extraction well, the second data set will be done at approximately 180 days after injection
starts just between the two fiberglass wells, and the last extraction will be completed be
completed using all three wells after the injection is over and the pump has been removed from
the extraction well.
In terms of pressure and flow monitoring, these will be measured continuously by bottom hole
sensors in all three wells, and at the top of the well bores in the injection and extraction well.
Wireline flow profiles will be run in the injection and extraction wells three times during the
injection experiment, and a resistivity log run at the same time in the injection well only.
