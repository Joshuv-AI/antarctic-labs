Indices of trophic and competitive relationships in a planktonic meta-network of carp ponds
Authors: Anna Maria Goździejewska, Marek Kruk, Lenka Kajgrová, Jaroslav Vrba, Martin Bláha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The functioning of the trophic cascade in fishponds determines the ecosystem stability and breeding success. We investigated the trophic and competitive relationships in the meta-network of carp ponds. We hypothesised that small taxa of the zooplankton consumer level would play a dominant role in the community. The importance of taxa for biomass flux and the strength of interspecific relations were assessed using network graph modelling. Critical to the coherence of the meta-network were positive associations initiated by species that exploit different feeding niches and tolerate high cyanobacterial biomass, such as small cladocerans Bosmina longirostris and Chydorus sphaericus, and the rotifers Polyarthra longiremis. Negative associations, potentially reflecting predation and competitive exclusion, were less important. The observed characteristics of dominant species and their relationships made it possible to identify trophic pathways and their potential consequences for common carp culture. This study presents a new approach to understanding the functioning of the pond food web in aquaculture and may contribute to sustainable fisheries management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-58900-5.

Introduction

Central European fishponds are artificial freshwater ecosystems used primarily for fish production, but they also represent important landscape elements with hydrological, ecological and cultural functions1–3. Most production fishponds in this region are shallow, polymictic systems that function as artificial equivalents of natural shallow lakes, yet their structure and dynamics are strongly shaped by fishery management4–7. Common carp (Cyprinus carpio) remains the dominant species in pond aquaculture, and conventional semi-intensive farming is based on the use of natural food, mainly zooplankton and zoobenthos, supported by supplementary feeding during the growing season8–13.

During the last century, fishpond management intensified markedly3,5. Higher fish stocks, supplementary feeding, organic manuring and nutrient inputs from surrounding catchments have shifted many fishponds towards eutrophic or hypertrophic states3–5,14. This has been accompanied by high phytoplankton biomass, frequent cyanobacterial blooms, oxygen depletion, altered nitrogen and phosphorus dynamics, reduced water transparency and lower predictability of production processes3–5,13. These changes affect not only fish production, but also the functioning of the whole fishpond ecosystem4,5,15.

The efficiency of fishpond production depends on how matter and energy are transferred through planktonic food webs14,16–19. Historically, natural fish production in carp ponds was closely linked to the development of large zooplankton, especially Daphnia, which efficiently graze phytoplankton and provide high-quality natural food for fish1,2,20–22. In hypertrophic fishponds, however, this pathway is often weakened. High fish stock, poor food quality, cyanobacterial dominance, oxygen depletion and strong nutrient loading may reduce the role of large filter-feeding cladocerans and favour smaller cladocerans, rotifers, copepod stages, protozoa and microbial food-web components3,5,20–24. As a result, matter and energy transfer may become less direct and less efficient, with stronger involvement of microbial and small-bodied zooplankton pathways5,24.

Zooplankton are therefore a key component of fishpond functioning. They are involved both in the nutrition of common carp and in the regulation of phytoplankton biomass, water transparency and nutrient cycling21,22,25. Their taxonomic composition, biomass and size structure are shaped by both top-down control, mainly fish predation and fish stock structure, and bottom-up control, including nutrient availability and phytoplankton structure5,26. Because zooplankton respond rapidly to management and environmental conditions, they provide sensitive information on the state of planktonic food webs in fishponds27–31.

The role of common carp in these interactions is not limited to simple zooplankton predation. Carp are omnivorous fish whose feeding depends on body size, season, and especially the availability of natural food24,35,36. In semi-intensive fishponds, adult carp exploit both zooplankton and zoobenthos, together with supplementary feed8–11,32–35. The relative importance of these natural food resources may shift during the growing season33,34. Macrozoobenthos is often spatially restricted and/or depleted in production fishponds36, whereas zooplankton can represent a major available natural food pathway33,36. Carp foraging activity also affects the physical and chemical environment of the pond by promoting bioturbation of bottom sediments32,37–39. Common carp may therefore shape planktonic food webs both directly, through zooplankton consumption, and indirectly, through benthic–pelagic coupling and changes in abiotic conditions5,12,32–35.

Although top-down and bottom-up controls of plankton structure in hypertrophic fishponds are increasingly understood4,5,14,23,28,40, less is known about how individual plankton taxa and their relationships contribute to the cohesion of the whole planktonic food web41,42. Biomass and/or density alone do not necessarily identify taxa that are most important for food-web organisation. Some species may be important because they connect different parts of the network, occur in key trophic or competitive relationships, or maintain pathways of matter and energy transfer under hypertrophic conditions41–45.

Graph network analysis provides a useful framework for this purpose. In such networks, taxa or taxonomic groups are represented as nodes and their relationships as edges43–48. Positive associations may indicate co-occurrence, shared environmental responses or parallel use of resources, whereas negative associations may reflect grazing, competition or mutual exclusion41–48. Network attributes such as node degree, closeness centrality, betweenness centrality and edge betweenness centrality can identify taxa and relationships that are important for the cohesion and organisation of the planktonic food web41–43,45,46.

Here, we used graph modelling to analyse trophic and competitive relationships in the planktonic meta-network of carp fishponds. The network was based on occurrence and biomass data of zooplankton taxa from Rotifera, Crustacea and Protozoa, phytoplankton taxonomic groups, and common carp biomass, collected from experimental fishponds in Vodňany, Czech Republic, during the 2023 growing season. This study aimed to identify relationships among organisms involved in the planktonic food web and to determine the role of individual taxa in the cohesion of the carp pond meta-network. We hypothesized that: (1) small zooplankton taxa exploiting different feeding niches would play a dominant role in the pond food webs, serving as central interaction nodes; (2) the periphery of the network would be occupied mainly by larger/more efficient filter feeders, predatory species and euryphages that do not compete with each other; and (3) competitive relationships will play a significant role in maintaining the equilibrium of the food webs, while predatory relationships will play a minor role, in line with the theory of “more resources than species”49.

Network of zooplankton and phytoplankton trophic and competitive relations

This network was variable in terms of the sign (+ or −) and the value of the correlation coefficient. The taxa (network nodes) were connected by 95 links (network edges). Positive associations outweighed negative ones (68.4% versus 31.6%), with most connections coming from Rotifera and Cladocera (Fig. 1). Discussion

The application of graph theory enabled a detailed study of biomass-based association among taxa in the pond food webs under semi-intensive common carp farming. In such networks, positive and negative links describe association patterns and provide a basis for ecological interpretation of possible food-web interactions. The dependence graph (Fig. 2) showed the plankton meta-network of the ponds as a coherent structure with relatively high density as well as centrality coefficient, suggesting a well-connected planktonic community. This contrasts with fragmented networks with long (edge) connecting paths between taxa (nodes), which have been reported in communities affected by thermal stress46 or agricultural and urban pollution45.

The ecologically most important attributes of the investigated meta-network, centralisation (NCC) and coherence (NBC), were associated mainly with three nodes: the cladocerans B. longirostris and C. sphaericus, as well as the green algae order Volvocales. A high NCC value, which reflects the importance of a taxon for the flow of information to other related taxa, including taxa that are not direct neighbours in the network50, was also found for the copepodites E. gracilis and the rotifer P. longiremis. The central nodal role of B. longirostris probably reflected its abundance and biomass dominance, as also observed for the rotifer P. longiremis, and suggests an important role in the organisation of the system51. These taxa therefore occupied central positions in the network and contributed notably to the structure of the pond food web52.

Interestingly, the most widespread phytoplankton taxa, Bacillariophyceae and Chlorococcales, did not occupy central positions in the network, despite representing potentially available food sources for rotifers and crustaceans. Centrality was instead associated with taxa of lower frequency and/or lower average biomass, including C. sphaericus, E. gracilis and, among the primary producers, Volvocales. This suggests that less common crustaceans and phytoplankton groups, whose biomass was 50–100 times lower than that of the dominant taxa, played an important role in food-web cohesion. High values for both node centrality attributes therefore point to their potential importance for network organization, probably through their links with different resource niches53. When these taxa were absent, the coherence of the meta-network might have been lower than suggested by a model based on co-occurrence, potential consumer-resource association, or independent resource exploitation (Fig. 3). A similar pattern was reported by Kruk and Paturej42 in the planktonic network of the eutrophic lagoon.

An important indicator of the role of taxa in maintaining network cohesion is the attribute of betweenness centrality (NBC), which identifies taxa that connect different subnetworks (clusters)54. When a network is less cohesive and more fragmented, taxa (nodes) that link different clusters may become more important for network connectivity than taxa located within individual clusters43. In the present pond meta-network, copepodite larvae and E. gracilis had relatively high betweenness values, suggesting that they contributed to network coherence by linking central parts of the network with more peripheal taxa, including Asplanchna priodonta and Daphnia spp. Although the NBC attribute values of these nodes did not indicate a clear risk of fragmentation, the peripheral position of efficient consumers such as Asplanchna and Daphnia may reflect a food web with diverse resources and longer connection pathways among taxa20,55. This is also evidenced by the functional diversity of taxa contributing to network centralisation and coherence, including the filter-feeding cladoceran B. longirostris, the scraper cladoceran C. sphaericus56, the raptorial rotifer P. longiremis57 and the stationary/suspended feeder calanoid E. gracilis56. Together, these association patterns suggest multidirectional utilisation of resources and habitats58.

The trophic graph model (Fig. 3), which primarily represents the associations (edges) between nodes59, showed that positive associations accounted for approximately two-thirds of the links and were initiated mainly by Rotifera and small Cladocera. In this network, positive associations indicate a correlated increase in biomass and may reflect co-occurrence, the effect of consumer guilds, or independent resource exploitation. Negative associations between species indicate opposing biomass trends and may suggest grazing, predatory, and parasitic relationships or interspecific competition9.

One of the strongest positive associations was observed between the rotifer Keratella quadrata, an algivore, and Filinia spp., which include detritivorous and bacterivorous forms57. This association may reflect coexistence based on different resource use. High values of the EBC attribute for the link between K. quadrata and the green algae Volvocales (Table 3; Fig. 3) may suggest a potentially important consumer–resource association. In turn, the negative correlation of Filinia spp. with Volvocales could indicate symbiotic or mutualistic relationships between these algae and the bacteria on which Filinia feeds60.

In ponds with adult carp, zooplankton–phytoplankton interactions are unlikely to reflect grazing relationships alone9,13,24,28. Gut-content analysis from the same experimental system showed that stocked carp consumed zooplankton, represented mainly by large filter-feeding Daphnia, during the growing season (Kajgrová et al., unpublished data), indicating a direct trophic pathway between carp and zooplankton. By contrast, associations involving carp biomass and phytoplankton or small microbial-linked taxa rather reflect indirect effects. Benthic foraging by carp resuspends bottom sediments and thus increases suspended mineral particles in the water column37,38. These particles can provide substrates for the deposition of organic matter, algae, bacteria and protozoa, which might be utilised by both specialised bacterivores and euryphages43,61,62, represented in the pond meta-network by Keratella tecta and the genus Brachionus. Fish faeces, excess nutrients, and supplemental feed may further stimulate microbial production63 and, in turn, the activity and feeding of bacterivorous zooplankton.

The trophic and competitive network (Fig. 3) suggested that the biomass-dominating Bacillariophyceae and Cyanobacteria were not the main resources supporting zooplankton biomass. Although diatoms formed numerous links important for network coherence (NDC, Table 1), most of these links were positive. A negative seasonal association was observed for the spring rotifer predator Notholca squamula, a species that feeds almost exclusively on Bacillariophyceae57,64, and occurred mainly in April. Additionally, the negative links involving carp biomass, small rotifers and diatoms most likely reflected the timing of carp stocking in relation to the spring development of rotifers and diatoms, rather than direct feeding by carp, whose food handling is constrained by particle size65.

Observed patterns point to a food web shaped by both carp-mediated top-down pressure and cyanobacterial dominance5,66. Under such conditions, small-bodied cladocerans such as Bosmina and Chydorus may be favoured, whereas large-bodied Daphnia may be more exposed to fish predation and may be constrained by the low food quality, toxicity or morphology of cyanobacteria23,67,68. A shift from large filter feeders towards small-bodied zooplankton taxa may therefore reflect the combined effects of fish predation pressure and cyanobacterial blooms23,69. Small taxa can persist under these conditions, but exert weaker grazing control over phytoplankton biomass23.

The negative associations between the central node B. longirostris and the copepods of the genus Acanthocyclops could also be linked to the high biomass of cyanobacteria. This may reflect the dual-feeding mode of Bosmina, which switches between filter-feeding and more selective active feeding69–71, partly resembling copepod feeding strategies and potentially resulting in a competitive relationship. Such feeding flexibility may help explain the high abundance and biomass of a species such as Bosmina. Its high NBC and ASPL indices values further suggest that Bosmina was involved in numerous interspecific associations, including potential competitive links.

The role of protozoa in both meta-network cohesion (EBC) and biomass flux (NCC) may be related to habitat adaptation of the dominant taxa, Codonella cratera and Difflugia spp., as bacterivorous, algivorous, and even omnivorous feeders, as well as to their tolerance to high cyanobacterial biomass and fish predation61,72,73.

Conclusions

The presented biomass-based network model identified associations among taxa and their relative importance for planktonic food-web organisation in carp ponds. The largest number of interspecific associations, simultaneously with the greatest impact on pond meta-network cohesion, were linked to small zooplankton consumers, especially the cladocerans Bosmina longirostris and Chydorus sphaericus, and the rotifer Polyarthra longiremis. Most associations relevant to meta-network centralization were not related to biomass dominance but were associated with taxa able to coexist under conditions of diverse food resources, high cyanobacterial biomass, as well as high carp stock. Consequently, interspecific interactions were primarily positive, whereas negative associations, suggesting potential competition or consumer–resource links, were less frequent. The role of specialized taxa, including predators (e.g., Asplanchna priodonta) and efficient large filter feeders (e.g., Daphnia spp.), was marginal, suggesting that, in hypertrophic ponds, antagonistic links were less central in the network.

The relatively broad qualitative and quantitative spectrum of phytoplankton was mainly reflected in positive associations, but only weakly in potential consumer–resource links with zooplankton. These associations were initiated mostly by more specialised consumers. This pattern might be linked to the conditions of semi-intensive common carp farming, where planktonic food-web structure is shaped by direct fish predation as well as by carp foraging in the bottom sediment. Carp predation may contribute to the reduced role of large-bodied zooplankton such as Daphnia and favour smaller taxa, while carp foraging activity and supplementary feeding may indirectly support microbial and detrital pathways in the planktonic food web.

The structure of the meta-network suggests a well-connected planktonic community in which dominant taxa played central roles. Such a structure indicates higher network cohesion, a structural property often linked to ecosystem resilience to disturbances74, although resilience itself was not directly measured in this study. Graph theory-based modelling therefore provides a useful exploratory tool for identifying taxa and interactions that may be important for planktonic food-web organisation in shallow ponds. It can be used to assess the general properties of network models and to support the understanding of the roles of individual taxa and their interactions. Further development of this approach may improve our understanding of the dynamics of aquatic ecosystems and support more effective pond management.

Methods

Ethical statement

All procedures performed in this study involving common carp (Cyprinus carpio) were under the ethical standards approved by the institutional ethics committee of the University of South Bohemia in České Budějovice. The procedures were pre-approved on 01.04.2022 (approval no. MSMT-8857/2022-5) and done by personnel having licensed professional competence for “Protection of Animals against Cruelty” as required by the Czech law [Sect. 15e (1), Act no. 246/1992 Coll.]. The experiment was performed in accordance with these ethical approvals and the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study area

The study was conducted in nine experimental ponds of the University of South Bohemia, Faculty of Fisheries and Protection of Waters in Vodňany, Czechia (49° 15′ 64.4″ N, 14° 16′ 51.9″ E). All ponds have a square shape, an area of 0.16 ha and an average depth of 0.8 m. The banks of the ponds are reinforced with stones and covered with macrophytes (mainly Phragmites australis, Glyceria maxima and Typha latifolia). The ponds are arranged in a row next to each other and separated by dikes of equal width. At the beginning of the experiment, 210 2-year-old carp (TL 282 ± 18 mm; W 367 ± 69 g, i.e., 1300 ind/ha and 480 kg/ha) were released into each pond. The fish were fed weekly with organic feed (cereals, pellets), in accordance with established practices. The studied ponds did not differ significantly in terms of abiotic conditions, i.e., physical and chemical water parameters (Supplementary Table S2).

Sampling and analytical procedures

In 2023, a total of 72 samples were taken monthly from March to October. The mixed water samples from the surface layer were taken with a van Dorn sampler (3.2 L volume) and collected in 50-L containers by integrating 15 sampling points along a linear transect of the open water zone of each fishpond. Crustacean zooplankton were concentrated by filtering a total volume of 30 L through a 200-μm plankton net. Rotifers, protozoa and juvenile stages of crustaceans were concentrated from 30 L of this filtrate by filtering through a 40-μm mesh, and preserved with formaldehyde (4% final concentration). Zooplankton were identified to the lowest taxonomic level (with the exception of juvenile Crustacea stages) under a Zeiss AXIO Imager microscope according to the methods described by Błędzki and Rybak56, Ejsmont-Karabin et al.57, Koste75, Rybak and Błędzki76, and von Flössner77. Zooplankton biomass (μg/L) was determined according to the methods proposed by Bottrell et al.78, Hernroth79, and Ejsmont-Karabin80. Another unfiltered subsample was fixed with Lugol’s solution for phytoplankton analysis. The phytoplankton was counted in Utermöhl’s sedimentation chambers using an inverted microscope (Olympus IMT-2)81. The phytoplankton biomass (mg/L) was determined according to the method proposed by Hillebrand et al.82.

The ponds were stocked in April 2023, and carp biomass was monitored according to the procedure described by Goździejewska et al.83. The physical and chemical parameters of the water were assessed at each biological sampling time and described by Goździejewska et al.83. Chemical analyses of water were performed according to standard methods APHA-AWWA-WEF84.

Network analysis

Graph network models are a classical approach in ecology because the nature of the relationships between species has a network character44. Graph theory is a mathematical tool that identifies the network of connections (edges) between objects (nodes). We have chosen an approach that uses partial correlations as a measure of the relationship between nodes85. With this approach, we calculate attributes of nodes and edges, including their importance in the network, which is represented by centrality measures86. In our work, this approach was used to assess the centrality attributes of the individual zooplankton, phytoplankton and fish taxon and in the nine fishponds and 8 months to create a “global” meta-network. The concept of meta-network is defined as a network of biotic interactions that covers a larger spatial scale than a local biome or ecosystem87–89.

The connections between the taxonomic groups of fish, zooplankton and phytoplankton were subjected to a graph network analysis using the Cytoscape software package (http://www.cytoscape.org/) and the application of MetScape and NetworkAnalyzer with partial correlation-based networks. The database contained the biomasses of carp, zooplankton and phytoplankton in columns (72 samples), which were standardised into rows by autoscaling. The correlation matrix was calculated using Correlation Calculator 1.01 (University of Michigan).

An undirected graph was created to identify interactions between zooplankton and phytoplankton taxa and fish. The correlation ranges were selected based on the statistical significance coefficient P ≤ 0.05 with a sample size of 72. We applied two visualisation approaches to represent the network of relationships between the analysed taxonomic units. First, we used the edge-weighted spring embedded layout with Pearson’s correlations as weights and heuristic interpretation of the weighting values. In weighted graphs, the distance between nodes was defined as the sum of weights, and in the used layout algorithm the positions of nodes were set to minimise the sum of powers (partial correlations) in the network90. We also represent the relationships between the networks in trophic level mode, with common carp positioned as the highest consumer represented in the network, Cladocera and Rotifera as secondary consumers, and phytoplankton as primary producers, using a similar network layout to Kruk and Paturej42.

We calculated values for the most important attributes of a network as a whole, which are used in studies on communities of organisms91. These were the number of neighbours, the nearest path, the clustering coefficient, the centralisation of the network, the network density and the heterogeneity of the network.

We selected the most important and frequently used attributes of nodes and edges to represent the centrality characteristics of the nodes and the connectivity quality of the edges. For node attributes, we used node degree centrality (NDC), node closeness centrality (NCC) and node betweenness centrality (NBC) and employed edge correlation (partial) and edge betweenness centrality (EBC).

We used node degree centrality (NDC) as the number of significant connections of a node with its direct neighbours and the measure of node closeness centrality (NCC) as defined by Newman92.

Each edge in the network can be linked to an edge betweenness centrality (EBC) value. An edge with a high EBC value represents a connection between two parts of a network, the removal of which may affect communication between nodes over the shortest paths between them95. In our study, EBC refers to the relationships between taxa (biomasses of fish, zooplankton species and phytoplankton groups), i.e. the importance of these relations for the cohesion of the entire biocoenosis network.

The partial correlation coefficient of the connections in the graph (edge correlation) is a measure of the conditional independence of the connected nodes (taxon biomasses). Conditional independence is a key concept in graph theory, where two nodes are only connected by an edge if their corresponding variables are conditionally independent74. The partial correlation coefficient between nodes x and y for a given node z indicates the strength of the linear relationship between nodes x and y that is independent of (uncorrelated with) node z96,97.
