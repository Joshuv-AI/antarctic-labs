A variational approach to the derivation of reduced models for bubbly flows
Cosmin Burtea, David Gérard-Varet

Abstract
In this paper, we derive reduced models for the motion of gas bubbles in an ambient inviscid liquid, using Hamilton’s least action principle. We first explain how to recover from this principle the classical sharp interface model, in which the pressure is continuous across the surfaces of the bubbles. We then show how to reduce the complexity of the model, by simplifying the description of those surfaces. Namely, we impose them to evolve within a subclass of hypersurfaces described by a finite number of parameters (the simplest example being spheres, that is neglecting deviation of the bubbles from sphericity). The difficulty from a mathematical and modeling point of view is to determine the interface conditions that substitute to pressure continuity. We complete the derivation of the reduced models by some well-posedness analysis, in the case of curl-free liquid flow and homogeneous pressure in the bubbles.



1
Introduction

1.1
Context

When waves propagate in liquids, the strongest acoustic feedback is due to
the presence of gas bubbles
[
37
]
. The sound speed in a bubbly flow
is greatfully impacted by the volume fraction of the bubbles and its minimal
value can be much lower than the sound speeds in either fluid medium. For example,
the Wood sound speed model
[
51
]
predicts that for air-water
mixtures with
c_{1}=330m/s,\rho_{1}=1,24kg/m^{3},

c_{2}=1500m/s,

\rho_{2}=1000kg/m^{3}
the minimal sound speed can be as low as
23m/s
. This
surprising fact has been validated experimentally
[
38
]
.

Applications of bubble acoustics range from medicine (ultrasonic scanning)
to military industry (sonars) or oceanography to name just a few. Important
information can be inferred from quantitative and qualitative (size
distribution) estimates of the bubble population : dilation/contraction of
bubbles leads to a modification of the total surfacic area and has impact on
mass and heat transfer
[
36
]
. This kind of information can
be used in oceanography for a better understanding of coastal erosion,
exchanges of mass, energy and temperature between the ocean and the
atmosphere, in the petrochemical industry in order to optimize harvest and
transport of petrol/gas, or in cosmetics and pharmaceutical industries where
the control of the bubbles introduced in the fabrication process is crucial
for the lifespan of the final products.

We refer to the remarkable review paper
[
37
]
where all these
examples and many others are discussed in detail.

Bubbly flows belong to the general family of two-phase flows. When seen as
continuous media, molecular differences between the two phases cause them to
deform differently under the action of a given force. Thus the two phases
undergo two different nonlinearly coupled dynamics
[
41
]
.
Depending on the scale at which one wants to describe the interaction between
the two phases we distinguish two types of models : sharp interfaces or
diffuse interface models.

In this paper we propose several models of the former type for describing the
motion of gas bubbles in an ambient liquid : we consider the case where the
phases are completely separated by sharp interfaces but where the continuum
hypothesis still holds for each one of the two phases. Since each phase
occupies its own domain, in order to obtain a closed model,
one must
encode the interactions of the phases at the level of the interfaces
.

In full generality, the dynamics of the two phases are governed by two Euler or
Navier-Stokes equations (with various degrees of complexity viscous/inviscid,
incompressible/compressible etc.) for the bulk phases that are classically coupled at the
level of the interface through the equality of normal velocities
and normal stresses. This is a free boundary problem which is heavy to handle
both numerically or analytically. For mathematical results on the well-posedness of this type of equations we refer the reader to
[
2
,
46
,
22
]
regarding the Navier-Stokes case, and to the book
[
35
]
for the inviscid equations. The specific case of bubbles in a viscous incompressible flow was tackled in
[
52
]
. Let us point out that in the case of a single bubble, under radial symmetry of the initial data, the sphericity of the bubble is preserved, as well as the symmetry of the fields, providing a simpler set of equations. We refer to
[
4
]
for an in-depth analysis of the well-posedness properties of such system. Recently, a nice stability analysis of this radially symmetric setting was conducted in
[
32
]
. See also
[
33
]
with an additional periodic forcing, and
[
34
]
where the introduction of asymmetric deformations of the bubbles is shown to trigger some strong instability.

Still, even in settings where radial symmetry is not strictly preserved, bubbles remain often close to spherical and/or ellipsoidal. This is observed in many applications and for a wide range of Reynolds and Eötvös numbers
[
11
,
48
]
. In the same spirit, the recent article
[
31
]
shows that under the action of gravity, bubbles in hydrostatic equilibrium deviate slightly from sphericity, with a deformation along the vertical axis associated with a one dimensional bifurcation phenomenon.
Taking these observations as a starting point, a natural question arises: can one derive
reduced models
, in which the surfaces of the bubbles have to remain spherical, or ellipsoidal, or more generally have to evolve within a subclass described by a finite number of parameters ?

Of course there is no reason to imagine that the bulk
equations should change. The difficulty from a mathematical
and modelling point of view is
to determine the appropriate interface
conditions that govern the reduced dynamics.
The equality
of normal velocities can be understood geometrically as a necessary condition
for the liquid and the gas to maintain a common interface and therefore we
will also encounter it in the reduced models. However the equality of normal
stresses has to be relaxed owing to the imposed shape of the
interface. How to relax this condition and still keep a
well-posed system of PDEs is the focus of the present paper.

Regarding spherical bubbles, simplified models in the spirit of the famous
Rayleigh-Plesset equation were obtained in previous works

[
24
]
,
[
50
]
,

[
47
]
. We mention that in these papers the
irrotationality of the velocity field is exploited in order to drastically
simplify the dynamics : the latter is governed by a system of ODEs for the
radii and centers of the gas bubbles. We also refer to our recent contribution
[
7
]
, where we build weak Leray solutions for a reduced system modeling the interaction between a viscous liquid and spherical bubbles. The conditions at the interfaces there are viscous analogues of some of the conditions that will be derived in this paper. Another reference is
[
25
]
, in which a PDE system governing the evolution of a finite number of spherical gas bubbles is obtained through a singular limit approach. Namely, the bubbles are modeled by the viscous compressible Navier-Stokes equations and the shear viscosity is sent to infinity.

Our goal here is to go beyond the case of spherical bubbles and to obtain some new PDE models in the framework of the Hamilton’s least action
principle : our models can be seen as the Euler-Lagrange
equations verified by critical points of certain action functionals. The
advantage of this method is that the whole physics is contained in the
definition of a scalar function called the Lagrangian of the system, which is
used to construct the action functional,
and in the class where the
critical point is sought
. As it is the case with finite dimensional systems, invariance of the Lagrangians gives rise to conservation laws
[
21
,
44
]
. The variational approach to derive equations in
fluid mechanics and more general equations of continuous media has a long
history going back to Serrin’s work
[
45
]
and continues to this
day
[
16
]
. We refer the reader to the (by
far non-exhaustive) list of important contributions

[
10
,
1
,
42
,
20
,
27
,
13
,
3
]
. For more elementary introductory papers oriented towards modeling, see

[
15
,
6
]
. The equations governing the
interaction between rigid bodies and a liquid were obtained in

[
28
,
49
,
19
]
. This problems bears some
structural resemblances with the liquid-bubbles interaction, however in the former case the whole dynamics of the solids and of their boundaries is driven by rigid motions and interface conditions are a bit easier to understand.

Although we do not study this kind of models in this paper, let us say a few words on the other class of models that we
briefly mentioned : diffuse interface models. In bubbly flows or more general
dispersed two phase-flows, the number of interfaces is or becomes too large
for a sharp-interface model to be used for numerical simulations. For
this reason, one attempts to derive models for the ”average flow” that
contains macroscopic information about the mixture. In this description, a
single point in space can ”accommodate” different fluid particles. New
variables, volume fractions, are introduced in order to quantify
the space occupied by each fluid particle. Macroscopic models are obtained in
the literature either by averaging/homogenization techniques from mesoscopic
models

[
9
,
29
,
39
,
26
,
5
]
, directly from Hamilton’s stationary action principle

[
43
,
47
,
14
]
or using methods of statistical physics developed for description of many particle systems
[
8
,
30
,
47
]
. Of course, this very
short list of references is incomplete and only reflects the
authors’s biased mathematical and modelling culture.

1.2
Outline of the paper

Our paper is structured as follows.

We start in Section
2
with the derivation of the classical two-phase flow system: Euler’s equations in the bulk phases, coupled through the equality of the normal velocities and of the pressures at the interface(s). We adopt a lagrangian point of view: we describe the liquid and gas phases with one-parameter families of diffeomorphims
X_{\pm}=X_{\pm}(t,x)
that describe fluid particles trajectories. We first collect in Section
2.1
some notations and useful formulae which allow to switch from lagrangian to eulerian formulations and vice-versa. Then, in Section
2.2
, we recover the Euler free boundary two-fluid system as the Euler-Lagrange equations verified by the critical point of an action functional, that is the difference between the kinetic and potential energies. The action functional is sought in the class of diffeomorphisms that maintain a common interface. To be a bit more precise, if
\mathcal{I}_{0}
is the initial liquid-gas interface, the interface separating the two phases at time
t>0
is given by

\mathcal{I}(t)=X_{+}(t,\mathcal{I}_{0})=X_{-}(t,\mathcal{I}_{0}).

(1.2.1)

Unlike in the bulk phases, perturbations
\delta X_{\pm}
cannot be chosen arbitrarily at the level of the interfaces : they should also lead to configurations at time
t>0
that maintain a common interface

(X_{+}+\delta X_{+})(t,\mathcal{I}_{0})=(X_{-}+\delta X_{-})(t,\mathcal{I}_{0}).

This is the key observation leading to the condition
p_{+}=p_{-}
verified at the level of the interface.

In Section
3
, we turn to the core of our work, that is the derivation of simplified models with constrained deformations for the bubbles. The main idea is that those simpler models can be obtained by searching for critical points of the action functional in a narrower class of diffeomorphisms : besides (
1.2.1
) we ask
\mathcal{I}(t)
to be the union of a finite number of elements drawn from a collection
(\mathcal{S}_{m})_{m\in M}

where for each
m\in M
,
\mathcal{S}_{m}
is the boundary of a smooth bounded domain
\Omega_{m}
of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. One should think of this collection as the surfaces that result from admissible deformations of a fixed prototype:
M
is a finite dimensional manifold representing the finite number of degrees of freedom allowed for the deformations of the bubbles.

Accordingly, the class of admissible perturbations
\delta X_{\pm}
is much smaller: they need to preserve the constraint that each connected component of
\mathcal{I}(t)
belongs to
(\mathcal{S}_{m})_{m\in M}
at each time
t
. this leads to relaxing the condition
p_{+}=p_{-}
at the interface. Roughly, this relation is replaced by a finite number of integral conditions of the form
\int_{\mathcal{I}}(p_{+}-p_{-})v_{n}d\sigma=0
, where the fields
v_{n}=v_{n}(x)
form a basis of all possible normal velocity fields associated with deformations of the interfaces within the subclass
(\mathcal{S}_{m})_{m\in M}
. All details are provided in Section
3.1
.

For instance, in the simplest case of spherical bubbles, each bubble is described by a four-dimensional set of parameters
m=(c,r)
where
c=(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})
is its center and its radius
r
. Deformations preserving sphericity are described by normal velocities of the form
\dot{c}n+\dot{r}
where
n
is the normal at the spherical bubble while
\dot{c},\dot{r}
can be chosen freely. This leads to exactly four boundary conditions at the level of the boundary of each bubble :
\int_{\mathcal{I}}(p_{+}-p_{-})nd\sigma=0
respectively
\int_{\mathcal{I}}(p_{+}-p_{-})d\sigma=0
. See Section
3.2
for more on the special case of spherical bubbles, and Section
3.3
for ellipsoidal bubbles. Two simplified models are further obtained in the case of spherical bubbles in Section
3.2.2
.

Those simple cases are there to illustrate our methodology, but do not exhaust its applications. More sophisticated frameworks incorporating more complicated physics could be obtained. We speculate that this kind of reduced models could be notably useful for computer simulations. Indeed, a numerical description of bubbles interfaces can only be achieved approximately, that is with a finite number of degrees of freedom, so that reduced models might be natural and computationally less expensive.

Of course, the derivation carried in Section
3
is a matter of mathematical modeling. It relies on the choice of an action functional and is about the Euler-Lagrange equations that a critical point of this functional (if there is any) should satisfy. The actual existence of such critical points or of solutions to the reduced models is a separated mathematical problem. In the last Section
4
of the present study we briefly touch upon this question for the case of irrotational velocity fields for the liquid and space homogeneous pressures for the gaseous phase. We show that this leads to a well-posed set of ODEs that generalizes the one obtained in
[
50
,
47
]
for the spherical case.

2
Lagrangian approach for immiscible fluids

In this section, we explain how to derive formally the classical equations for immiscible flows. We focus on the case where one phase is compressible (gaseous bubbles) and one incompressible (the liquid in which the bubbles are immersed). The point of view that we adopt here is lagrangian: we derive Euler-Lagrange equations from a least action principle, and then convert them into the usual eulerian formulation. In the next section, this principle will be modified to account for additional shape constraints on the interfaces.

2.1
Some general considerations

We first introduce several general notions, related to the lagrangian description of continuous deformable media.

Definition 2.1.1

A continuous deformable medium of the initial configuration
\Omega_{0}
is a
pair
\left(\Omega_{0},X\right)
such that

•

\Omega_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a given open set which represents
the initial configuration of the medium;

•

X:[0,\infty)\times\overline{\Omega_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
continuously differentiable function satisfying

–

for all
x\in\overline{\Omega_{0}}
,
X\left(0,x\right)=x
;

–

for all
t\geq 0
,
X_{t}=X\left(t,\cdot\right):\overline{\Omega_{0}}\rightarrow X\left(t,\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right)
is a diffeomorphism;

Recall that
\Theta:C\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a
closed set is a diffeomorphism on its image if it is the restriction of some

\tilde{\Theta}:O\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
which is a diffeomorphism on its image
with
O
open and
C\subset O
.

The image of
\overline{\Omega_{0}}
through
X\left(t,\cdot\right)
,

\overline{\Omega_{t}}:=X\left(t,\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right)=\left\{X\left(t,x\right):x\in\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right\}
represents the
configuration of the medium at time
t\geq 0
. We denote by
\emph{CDM}\left(\Omega_{0}\right)
the set of
X
such that
(\Omega_{0},X)
is a continuous deformable medium.

Remark 2.1.2

In several places below, we shall write expressions such as
X^{-1}
or
\tilde{X}\circ X^{-1}
for some
X,\tilde{X}
as in the definition. These slightly abusive notations will refer to invertibility or composition with respect to the space variable only, time being a parameter. For instance, for any
t

X^{-1}(t,x)
refers to
X_{t}^{-1}(x)
, while
\tilde{X}\circ X^{-1}(t,x)
refers to
\tilde{X}_{t}(X_{t}^{-1}(x))
.

Lagrangian and eulerian standpoints.

We have two different ways to think of any physical quantity
B
that
characterizes the deformable medium
X\in\emph{CDM}\left(\Omega_{0}\right)
:

•

the lagrangian point of view:
B^{\ell}
is attached to the particle
motion. In this setup
B^{\ell}\left(t,x\right)

represents the
measure at time
t
of the physical quantity associated to the
particle that leaves
x
at time
t=0
. It should be understood
automatically that the domain of definition for a ”lagrangian quantity”

B^{\ell}
is
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\overline{\Omega_{0}}
;

•

the eulerian point of view:
B^{e}
is measured as time evolves, say between

t_{1},t_{2}
in a geometrical point
x\in{\textstyle\bigcap\nolimits_{t\in\left[t_{1},t_{2}\right]}}X\left(t,\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right)
.
It should be understood automatically that the domain of definition of
B^{e}
is
{\displaystyle\bigcup_{t\geq 0}}\{t\}\times X\left(t,\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right)
;

The two points of view are formally equivalent in the sense that any
“lagrangian physical quantity” can be
regarded as an “eulerian physical quantity” via the family of diffeomorphisms
\left(X\left(t,\cdot\right)\right)_{t\geq 0}
. More precisely, given
B
we have

B^{e}\left(t,x\right)=B^{\ell}\left(t,X^{-1}\left(t,x\right)\right)\text{ for all }t\geq 0\text{ and }x\in X\left(t,\overline{\Omega_{0}}\right),

(2.1.1)

and conversely

B^{\ell}\left(t,x\right)=B^{e}\left(t,X\left(t,x\right)\right)\text{ for all }t\geq 0\text{ and }x\in\overline{\Omega_{0}}.

(2.1.2)

As explained in
[
6
]
,the Lagrangian point of view is the
natural framework to generalize the classical stationary action principle.
However, the resulting Lagrange equations are usually too complicated to handle whereas they are considerably simpler in eulerian coordinates.

Using the conventions established by relations
\left(\text{\ref{Lagrange_to_Euler}}\right)
and
\left(\text{\ref{Euler_to_Lagrange}}\right)
and using the chain rule for
derivatives we have the following relation.

\operatorname*{trace}\left(D\psi^{\ell}(DX)^{-1}\right)=\left((DX)^{-1}\right)_{ji}\partial_{j}\left(\psi^{\ell}\right)^{i}=\frac{1}{\det DX}\operatorname{Cof}(DX)_{ij}\partial_{j}\left(\psi^{\ell}\right)^{i}=\left(\operatorname{div}\psi^{e}\right)^{\ell}.

(2.1.3)

Using this relation we infer that for any scalar field
B^{e}
and for any
vector field
\psi^{e}
with compact support in
\cup_{t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\{t\}\times\Omega_{t}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{0}}(\nabla B^{e})^{\ell}\cdot\psi^{\ell}\det DX\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{t}}\nabla B^{e}\cdot\psi^{e}\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega_{t}}B^{e}\operatorname{div}\psi^{e}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{0}}B^{\ell}(\operatorname{div}\psi^{e})^{\ell}\det DX\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega_{0}}B^{\ell}\frac{1}{\det DX}\operatorname{Cof}(DX)_{ij}\partial_{j}\left(\psi^{\ell}\right)^{i}\det DX\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{0}}\partial_{j}(\operatorname{Cof}(DX)_{ij}B^{\ell})(\psi^{\ell})^{i}\mathrm{d}x

From which we obtain that for any
i\in\overline{1,d}

\frac{1}{\det DX}\partial_{j}\left(\operatorname{Cof}(DX)_{ij}B^{\ell}\right)=\left(\partial_{i}B^{e}\right)^{\ell},

(2.1.4)

or in a compact form

\frac{1}{\det DX}\operatorname{div}\left(\operatorname{Cof}(DX)B^{\ell}\right)=(\nabla B^{e})^{\ell}.

In particular, taking
B=1
we find the so-called
Piola identity

\operatorname{div}\left(\operatorname{Cof}(DX)\right)=0.

2.2
Description of immiscible incompressible-compressible
phases

We consider
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
a smooth open set which
represents the fixed domain of an immiscible two-phase flow made of a liquid (symbol :
+
) and a gas (symbol :
-
). We give ourselves
\Omega_{+,0},\Omega_{-,0}
two smooth disjoint open subsets of
\Omega
, which model the region occupied initially by the liquid and the gas respectively. We assume that the gas bubbles are surrounded by the liquid, which translates into
\Omega=\Omega_{+,0}\cup\overline{\Omega_{-,0}}
.

In what follows, most of the quantities that will appear will be lagrangian ones, so that the upperscript
ℓ
will be omitted to lighten notations. Only the upperscript
e
relative to eulerian quantities will be explicit.

We suppose that the liquid is an incompressible fluid with constant density

\varrho_{+}
. The evolution of the liquid part will be determined by

X_{+}\in\emph{CDM}\left(\Omega_{+,0}\right)
along with a scalar field

p_{+}:\Omega_{+,0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which can be seen as a constraint
that will enforce the incompressibility of the liquid namely
\det DX_{+}\left(t,x\right)=1.

The gas is assumed to be a compressible barotropic fluid with (lagrangian) variable density

\varrho_{-}
. The evolution of the gas part will be determined by
X_{-}\in\emph{CDM}\left(\Omega_{-,0}\right).
From the conservation of mass of gas, we deduce :

\varrho_{-}\left(t,x\right)=\frac{\varrho_{-,0}\left(x\right)}{\det DX_{-}(t,x)},

(2.2.1)

where
\varrho_{-,0}\left(x\right)
is the initial density. We denote
p_{-}=p_{-}(s),e_{-}=e_{-}(s)
the pressure and the Helmholtz free energy of the gas. They
are known functions of a real variable
s
, and are linked by the relation

p_{-}\left(s\right)=s^{2}e_{-}^{\prime}\left(s\right).

(2.2.2)

We denote
\mathcal{I}(t)
the interface between the immiscible liquid and gas at time
t
. At
t=0
,

\mathcal{I}\left(0\right)=\partial\Omega_{+,0}\cap\partial\Omega_{-,0}=\partial\Omega_{-,0}

(we remind that the gas bubbles are surrounded by the liquid one). For
t>0
, we have

\mathcal{I}\left(t\right)=X_{+}\left(t,\mathcal{I}\left(0\right)\right)=X_{-}\left(t,\mathcal{I}\left(0\right)\right).

(2.2.3)

The interpretation is as follows: the two-phase fluid occupies the initial configuration

\overline{\Omega_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each point
x\in\overline{\Omega_{0}}\backslash\mathcal{I}\left(0\right)
is occupied either by a
particle of liquid either by a particle of gas, particle which will change
positions as time evolves. At the level of the interface, each spatial
position
x\in\mathcal{I}\left(0\right)
is occupied by two
particles
1
1

1

Particles should be understood as infinitesimal volumes
(fluid parcel) with caracteristic scale much larger than the mean distance
between two ”real” fluid particules but neglectable w.r.t. the characteristic
size of the domain where the mixture evolves.
: one of liquid, the other one of
gas. If
x\in\overline{\Omega_{\pm,0}},
then
X_{\pm}\left(t,x\right)

represents the position at time

In all what follows, the unit normal vector

3
Reduced models with shape-constrained dynamics

We turn in this section to the derivation of reduced models for bubbly flows, where we wish to simplify the geometry and dynamics of the bubbles. One crude and very common simplification that we have in mind is to assimilate bubbles to spheres. With the notations of the previous section, the idea is to assume that initially the interface is

\mathcal{I}(0)=\cup_{i=1}^{N}S(c_{i,0},r_{i,0})

that is a union of disjoint spheres of centers
c_{i,0}
and radii
r_{i,0}
:
\forall i,j

|c_{i,0}-c_{j,0}|>r_{i,0}+r_{j,0}
. We would like the interface at positive times to still be described by a union of spheres:

\mathcal{I}(t)=\cup_{i=1}^{N}S(c_{i}(t),r_{i}(t))

and to determine the dynamics of the centers
c_{i}(t)
and radii
r_{i}(t)
. Of course, adding such a constraint on the interface to (
2.2.15
) would lead to an overdetermined system. The point is to understand how to relax (
2.2.15
) in order to obtain a well-posed system when incorporating the constraint of sphericity. As discussed in the introduction, to restrict to spherical bubbles may be too stringent: consideration of ellipsoidal bubbles, or of more general shapes (still with a limited number of degrees of freedom) may be more accurate. Therefore, we introduce in the next section a general formalism that leads to reduced models for a large class of bubble shapes.

3.1

We shall represent our bubbles thanks to a family

The simplest example we have in mind is spherical bubbles. For any

4
Well-posedness of the reduced models

In the previous sections, we discussed the derivation of the standard equations for incompressible/compressible two-phase flows (Section
2.2
) and the derivation of reduced models with shape constraints (the whole Section
3
). Namely, we derived the equations that a critical point of some action functional should satisfy. Whether or not such critical point exists, or a solution to our models exists, is a different question, that we will only touch upon here.

As pointed out in Remark
2.2.6
, in all the models seen before, if the domain
\Omega
is bounded, the incompressibility condition
\operatorname{div}u_{+}=0
requires compatibility conditions on the initial data, to be preserved through time. Indeed, taking into account the boundary condition
u\cdot n=0
at
\partial\Omega
, it is necessary that

\int_{\mathcal{I}(t)}u\cdot n=0\quad\forall t\geq 0.

This constraint is particularly stringent in the reduced models, for instance for the model (
3.2.1
). For instance, for a single spherical bubble in a container (
N=1
,
\Omega
bounded), the radius of the ball has to be constant ! A natural way to relax this constraint is to take
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For instance, there exists a divergence-free field in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B(0,1)
such that
u\cdot n=1
at
\partial B(0,1)
: take
u=\frac{x}{|x|^{2}}
.

As emphasized in the introduction of the present paper, the well-posedness of the complete two-phase flow model (
2.2.15
) has been studied from a rigorous perspective in several articles. The analysis of reduced models for bubbly flows with shape constraints is however much more limited: as far as we know, dedicated articles only deal with spherical bubbles, see the references in the introduction. Notably, the question of well-posedness of System (
3.1.7
) is open.

We shall make partial progress on this question here, by showing the well-posedness of a simplified version of (
3.1.7
). For
m\in M
, we recall that
\Omega_{m}
is the smooth domain bounded by
\mathcal{S}_{m}
, see Section
3.1
. We make three main hypotheses:

1.

\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{3}
, that is the liquid fills in the whole space.

2.

The liquid flow is supposed to be irrotational :

\textrm{curl }u_{+}=0
.

3.

The pressure
p_{k}
, or equivalently the density
\rho_{k}
, is homogeneous in space. By conservation of mass, we deduce

p_{k}(t)=p_{-}(\rho_{k}(t))=p_{-}\left(\frac{M_{k}}{|\Omega_{m_{k}}(t)|}\right)

where
M_{k}
is the fixed mass of the
k
-th bubble,
k=1\dots N
.

The first hypothesis is not crucial, and one could consider a bounded domain
\Omega
, see the discussion of Paragraph
4.4
.
Hence, we consider the following model:

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0\text{ in
}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}},\\
\textrm{curl }u=0,\quad\operatorname{div}u=0\text{ in
}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}},\\
u(t,\cdot)\cdot n=\langle V_{m_{k}(t)},m^{\prime}_{k}(t)\rangle\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)},\quad\forall k,\\
\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\big(p_{-}(\varrho_{-}(t,\cdot))-p_{k}(t)\big)V_{m_{k}(t)}(\cdot)\mathrm{d}\sigma=0,\quad\forall k.\end{array}\right.

(4.0.1)

Compared to (
3.1.7
), we suppressed the lowerscript
+
for the fluid quantities and normalized the fluid density to
1
. We recall that the map
(m,x)\rightarrow V_{m}(x)
is defined in (
3.1.1
). One may additionally prescribe a non-zero constant pressure at infinity
p\xrightarrow[|x|\rightarrow+\infty]{}p_{\infty}
.
We will show that for an appropriate class of initial conditions, this system can be further reduced to a system of second order ODE’s on
(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})
which will have a unique local in time solution.

4.1
Shape-dependent gradient fields

To reformulate (
4.0.1
) as a set of ODEs, we will rely on some gradient fields, that we will call shape-dependent. We introduce the parameter set

\textbf{M}=\Big\{\textbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{N})\in M^{N},\>\overline{\Omega_{m_{i}}}\cap\overline{\Omega_{m_{j}}}=\emptyset\quad\forall i\neq j\Big\}

which is an open subset of
M^{N}
. Let
T\textbf{M}
its tangent bundle. For any

(\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}})\in T\textbf{M},\quad\text{with }\>\dot{\textbf{m}}=\left(\dot{m}_{1},\dots,\dot{m}_{N}\right)

we introduce
\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}
the solution of

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}&=0,\quad\text{ in }\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}}}\\
\partial_{n}\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}&=\big\langle V_{m_{k}}(\cdot),\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}\quad\forall k.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1.1)

We recall that the normal vector
n
points outward
\Omega_{m_{k}}
. We collect some properties of
\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}
in the next two lemmas.

Lemma 4.1.1

For any
(\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}})\in T\textbf{M}
, there exists a unique variational solution
\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}
of (
4.1.1
) such that

\nabla\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{i=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{i}}}),\quad\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}\in L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{i=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{i}}}).

Moreover, if the following non-degeneracy condition is satisfied:

\forall(m,\dot{m})\in TM,\quad\dot{m}\neq 0,\quad\exists x\in\mathcal{S}_{m}=\partial\Omega_{m},\>\big\langle V_{m}(x),\dot{m}\big\rangle\neq 0

(4.1.2)

then, for any
\textbf{m}\in\textbf{M}
and any basis
(\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots\mathbf{e}_{p})
of
T_{\textbf{m}}\textbf{M}
, with
p=\dim\textbf{M}=N\dim M
, the family

\big(\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{e}_{1}}
, …,
\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{e}_{p}}\big)
is free.

Proof.
To lighten notations, we write
\phi
instead of
\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}
,
\>\Omega_{k}
instead of
\Omega_{m_{k}}
and
\>a_{k}=\big\langle V_{m_{k}}(\cdot),\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle
. Hence, we need to solve the problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta\phi&=0\quad\text{ in }\>\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}},\\
\partial_{n}\phi&=a_{k}\quad\text{ on }\>\partial\Omega_{k},\quad\forall k\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1.3)

Let
(x_{1},r_{1})
, …,
(x_{N},r_{N})
such that for all
k=1,\dots,N
,
\overline{B(x_{k},r_{k})}\subset\Omega_{k}
. We define for all
k
and all
x\neq x_{k}
,
\phi_{k}(x)=-\frac{r_{k}^{2}}{4\pi|x-x_{k}|}
. It is easily checked that
\phi_{k}
is a harmonic function in
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\{x_{k}\}
, with

\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}\partial_{n}\phi_{k}=\int_{\partial B(x_{k},r_{k})}\partial_{n}\phi_{k}=1,\quad\text{ while }\>\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}\partial_{n}\phi_{k}=0\quad\forall j\neq k.

We then set
\psi=\phi-\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}a_{k}\big)\phi_{k}
, so that the system on
\phi
is equivalent to

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta\psi&=0\quad\text{ in }\>\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}},\\
\partial_{n}\psi&=a^{\prime}_{k}=a_{k}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\int_{\partial\Omega_{j}}a_{j}\big)\partial_{n}\phi_{j}\quad\text{ on }\>\partial\Omega_{k},\quad\forall k\end{aligned}\right.

(4.1.4)

Let
B
an open ball containing
\cup_{k}\overline{\Omega_{k}}
and
\Omega^{\prime}=B\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}}
. Let

V=\Big\{\varphi\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap L^{2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}}),\nabla\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}}),\quad\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi=0\Big\}

Thanks to the Poincaré inequality:
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}
for all
f
s.t.
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}f=0
,
V
is a Hilbert space equipped with

\|\varphi\|=\|\nabla\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{k}})}
. Moreover, combining this Poincaré inequality and the trace inequality
\|f\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{k})}\leq C\|f\|_{H^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})}
, it is easily seen that the variational formulation: for all
\varphi\in V

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup\Omega_{k}}}\nabla\psi\cdot\nabla\varphi=-\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}a^{\prime}_{k}\varphi,

has a unique solution
\psi\in V
. But as
\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}a^{\prime}_{k}=0
for all
k
, this equality remains true replacing
\varphi
by
\varphi-C
for any constant
C
. In other words, the equality remains true for all
\varphi
that do not satisfy the zero mean condition
\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi=0
. Hence, the solution
\psi\in V
satisfies (
4.1.4
). Moreover, it is well-known that there exists a constant
\psi_{\infty}
such that
\psi-\psi_{\infty}\in L^{6}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{k})
, see for instance
[
12
, Theorem II.6.1]
. Back to the original problem,
\phi=\psi-\psi_{\infty}+\sum_{k=1}^{N}\big(\int_{\partial\Omega_{k}}a_{k}\cdot n\big)\phi_{k}
is the unique solution of (
4.1.3
) that satisfies the requirements of the lemma.

For the second part of the lemma, we introduce real numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{p}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{e}_{i}}=0

This means that
\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}=0
where
\dot{\textbf{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{e}_{i}
. This implies that the corresponding Neumann data are zero : for all
k
, for all
x\in\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}
,
\big\langle V_{m_{k}}(x),\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle=0
. By the non-degeneracy condition (
4.1.2
), this implies that
\dot{\textbf{m}}=0
, hence that all
\lambda_{i}
are zero.

Lemma 4.1.2

Let
\mathbf{E}
a smooth vector field on
M
. The map

\bigcup_{\textbf{m}\in\textbf{M}}\{\textbf{m}\}\times\Big(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{n}\Omega_{m_{k}}}\Big)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad(\textbf{m},x)\rightarrow\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m})}(x)

is smooth. Moreover, for any smooth map
\tau\rightarrow\textbf{m}(\tau)
in
M
, one has for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
:

\partial_{\tau}\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}\in L^{2}\left(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}}}\right).

Proof.
The key difficulty is that the domain of definition of
\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m})}
depends on
m
. This problem of regularity with respect to the domain was analyzed in details in
[
23
]
, especially for solutions of a Neumann problem in a bounded domain: see
[
23
, chapter 5.5]
. In our system (
4.1.1
), we have an extra dependence on
m
in the boundary data, and the domain is not bounded but of the form
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}}
. Still, adapting the approach of
[
23
]
to these changes is straightforward. Therefore, we just remind here the big steps of this approach. Let
\tau\rightarrow\textbf{m}(\tau)
a smooth path in
M
defined for
\tau
in a neighborhood of zero. First, one builds over the fluid domain
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(\tau)}
some velocity field
v(\tau,\cdot)
such that
v(\tau,\cdot)|_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(\tau)}}=\big\langle V_{m_{k}(\tau)},m^{\prime}_{k}(\tau)\big\rangle n
. In other words, we extend to the whole fluid domain the (normal) velocity of the interface
\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(\tau)}
. Denoting
\Theta_{\tau}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(0)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(\tau)}
the associated flow map, one can through the change of variable
x=\Theta_{\tau}(y)
turn the Neumann problem satisfied by

\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}
into a similar problem on a fixed domain, where the Laplacian is replaced by an elliptic operator with variable coefficients depending smoothly on
\tau
. Smoothness with respect to
y
follows from ellipticity, while smoothness with respect to
\tau
can then be established through an implicit function theorem. See
[
23
, Theorem 5.5.1]
for details. Back to the original moving domain, this provides smoothness of the map
(\tau,x)\rightarrow\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}(x)
for
x
away from the boundary. Moreover, differentiating the elliptic equations in the fixed domain with respect to
\tau
, and translating into the original moving domain, it shows that
\partial_{\tau}\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}
satisfies itself a new Neumann problem, with data involving
\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}
. See
[
23
, Theorem 5.5.2]
. In particular, we find that for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{3}
with
|\alpha|\geq 1
:

\partial_{\tau}\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\phi_{\textbf{m}(\tau),\mathbf{E}(\textbf{m}(\tau))}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(\tau)}})

(the case
|\alpha|=1
is directly given by the variational formulation of the new Neumann problem, the case
|\alpha|\geq 1
is deduced from elliptic regularity). As the path
\tau\rightarrow\textbf{m}(\tau)
is arbitrary, we can take local coordinates
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
parametrizing
M
and consider the maps
\tau_{i}\rightarrow\textbf{m}(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{i},\dots,\tau_{p})
. We recover in this way all partial derivatives of first order.
Reiterating the reasoning with the partial derivatives (which as we saw solve similar Neumann problem), we obtain regularity in
m
at any order.

4.2
ODE system for curl-free flows

Let
\textbf{m}_{0}\in\textbf{M}
. For
V
a small enough neighborhood of
\textbf{m}_{0}
, there exist smooth vector fields
\mathbf{E}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{E}_{p}
and smooth one-forms
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{*},\dots,\mathbf{E}_{p}^{*}
defined on
V
such that: for each
\textbf{m}\in V
,

\big(\mathbf{E}_{1}(\textbf{m}),\dots,\mathbf{E}_{p}(\textbf{m})\big)\>\text{ is a basis of }\>T_{\textbf{m}}\textbf{M},\quad\text{ and for all $i,j$},\>\big\langle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{*}(\textbf{m}),\mathbf{E}_{j}(\textbf{m})\rangle=\delta_{ij}

(4.2.1)

Any vector
\dot{\textbf{m}}\in T_{\textbf{m}}\textbf{M}
is decomposed in a unique way in this basis:

\dot{\textbf{m}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\big\langle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{*}(\textbf{m}),\dot{\textbf{m}}\rangle\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}).

Let now
\textbf{m}=\textbf{m}(t)=\big(m_{1}(t),\dots,m_{N}(t)\big)
and
u=u(t,x)
satisfying (
4.0.1
) with
\textbf{m}(0)=\textbf{m}_{0}
. As
u
is curl-free it can be written as
u(t,x)=\nabla_{x}\phi(t,x)
and as it is divergence-free

\Delta_{x}\phi(t,\cdot)=0,\quad\text{in }\>\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}.

Moreover, the boundary condition on the normal velocity reads

\partial_{n}\phi(t,\cdot)=\big\langle V_{m_{k}(t)}(\cdot),m^{\prime}_{k}(t)\big\rangle,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}\quad\forall k,\forall t.

It follows, with the notations of Paragraph
4.1
, that

\forall t,\quad u(t,\cdot)=\nabla\phi_{\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)}.

For
t
small enough, by continuity,
\textbf{m}(t)\in V
. By Lemma
4.1.1
, under the non-degeneracy condition (
4.1.2
), one can decompose in a unique way
\phi_{\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)}
as

\phi_{\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\big\langle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{*}(\textbf{m}(t)),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)\big\rangle\,\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)},\quad\text{where for all $\textbf{m}$ in $V$, }\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}}=\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m})}.

Finally, for all
t
,

u(t,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\big\langle\mathbf{E}_{i}^{*}(\textbf{m}(t)),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)\big\rangle\,\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}

(4.2.2)

and the only unknown left is the function
t\rightarrow\textbf{m}(t)
encoding the dynamics of the bubbles. To obtain this dynamics,
we will perform computations that have strong similarities with those leading to the added mass effect for bidimensional irrotational inviscid flows, such as described in
[
28
,
18
,
17
]
. Let us come back to the last boundary condition of (
4.0.1
), where we recall that
p_{k}=p_{k}(t)
is the homogeneous pressure in the
k
-th bubble. Note that it depends on
t
only through the volume of
\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}
, in particular only through
m_{k}(t)
:
p_{k}(t)=P_{k}(m_{k}(t))
. For each
\textbf{m}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})\in\textbf{M}
, for all
x\in\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}
, we define the linear form
V_{\textbf{m}}(x)
by:

\forall\>\dot{\textbf{m}}=\big(\dot{m}_{1},\dots,\dot{m}_{N}\big),\quad\big\langle V_{\textbf{m}}(x),\dot{\textbf{m}}\big\rangle=\big\langle V_{m_{k}}(x),\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle\quad\text{ if }x\in\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}.

With this notation, it is easily seen that the last equality in (
4.0.1
) can be written

\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\Big(p(t,\cdot)-\sum_{k}1_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}p_{k}(t)\Big)V_{\textbf{m}(t)}(\cdot)d\sigma=0

or for
t
small enough, for all
i=1,\dots,p
,

\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\Big(p(t,\cdot)-\sum_{k}1_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}p_{k}(t)\Big)\big\langle V_{\textbf{m}(t)}(\cdot),\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))\big\rangle d\sigma=0.

We rewrite it as: for all
i=1,\dots,p
,

\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\Big(p(t,\cdot)-p_{\infty}\Big)\big\langle V_{\textbf{m}(t)}(\cdot),\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))\big\rangle d\sigma=F_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))

where

F_{i}(\textbf{m})=\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}\left(\sum_{k}1_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}P_{k}(m_{k})-p_{\infty}\right)\big\langle V_{\textbf{m}}(\cdot),E_{i}(\textbf{m})\big\rangle d\sigma.

We can then reformulate the left-hand side:

\displaystyle\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\big(p(t,\cdot)-p_{\infty}\big)\,\big\langle V_{\textbf{m}(t)}(\cdot),\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))\big\rangle d\sigma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\cup_{k}\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\big(p(t,\cdot)-p_{\infty}\big)\,\partial_{n}\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}d\sigma=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}}\nabla p\cdot\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}}\left(\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u\right)\cdot\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}

The idea is then to replace
u
by its expression in (
4.2.2
). Differentiation with respect to time and space is possible, thanks to the regularity of the vector fields
\mathbf{E}_{i}
and the dual one-forms
\mathbf{E}_{i}^{*}
, and thanks to the regularity results of Lemma
4.1.2
. Denoting
\dot{\textbf{m}}_{j}(t)=\big\langle\mathbf{E}_{j}^{*}(\textbf{m}(t)),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)\big\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
, so that

u(t,\cdot)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\dot{\textbf{m}}_{j}(t)\nabla\phi^{j}_{\textbf{m}(t)}

we end up with a system of the form: for all
i=1,\dots,p
,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{p}\dot{\textbf{m}}_{j}^{\prime}(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\nabla\phi^{j}_{\textbf{m}(t)}\cdot\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}=G_{i}(\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t))

for some smooth
G_{i}
. The Gram matrix
\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}}}\nabla\phi^{j}_{\textbf{m}}\cdot\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq p}
is invertible because
\big(\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}}\big)_{1\leq i\leq p}
is free, and so we get: for all
i=1,\dots,p

\dot{\textbf{m}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\tilde{G}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t))

for some smooth
\tilde{G}_{i}
. But differentiating the relation
\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i}\dot{\textbf{m}}_{i}(t)\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))
, we find that

\textbf{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\sum_{i}\dot{\textbf{m}}^{\prime}_{i}(t)\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))+\nabla_{\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)}\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t))

where for two vector fields
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}

on
M
,
\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{Y}
is the standard covariant derivative inherited from the one of
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
(remember that
M
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, so that
M
is a submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{dN}
).
So, the system can be reformulated as the second order system

\textbf{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t))

for some smooth
\mathcal{G}
on
T\textbf{M}
. Starting from any
(\textbf{m}_{0},\dot{\textbf{m}}_{0})\in T\textbf{M}
, it has a local in time smooth solution
t\rightarrow\textbf{m}(t)
, which in turn provides a local in time smooth solution of (
4.0.1
), defining
u
through (
4.2.2
).

4.3
Example of one spherical bubble

In this paragraph, we consider the important example of one spherical bubble in a curl-free flow:

\displaystyle N=1,\quad m(t)=\big(c(t),r(t)\big),\quad\Omega_{m(t)}=B(t)=B(c(t),r(t)),\quad\big\langle V_{m},\dot{m}\big\rangle=\dot{c}\cdot n+\dot{r}.

In such a case, the derivation of the ODE system seen in the previous paragraph can be slightly shortened. First, in view of (
3.2.3
), system (
4.0.1
) reads:

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p=0\text{ in
}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B(t),\\
\textrm{curl }u=0,\quad\operatorname{div}u=0\text{ in
}\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B(t),\\
u(t,\cdot)\cdot n=c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n+r^{\prime}(t)\text{ on }\partial B(t),\\
\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(t)}p\left(t,x\right)n\left(t,x\right)\mathrm{d}\sigma=0\\
\displaystyle\int_{\partial B(t)}p\left(t,x\right)\mathrm{d}\sigma=4\pi r(t)^{2}p_{B}\left(t\right)\end{array}\right.

(4.3.1)

where
p_{B}(t)=p_{-}\left(\frac{3M}{4\pi r(t)^{3}}\right)
is the pressure of the bubble.
We introduce the potential
\phi=\phi(t,x)
such that
u=\nabla_{x}\phi
, solving

\Delta_{x}\phi(t,\cdot)=0\quad\text{ in }\>\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B(t),\quad\partial_{n}\phi|_{\partial B(t)}=c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n+r^{\prime}(t)

The solution is given by

\phi(t,x)=-\frac{r(t)^{2}r^{\prime}(t)}{|x-c(t)|}-\frac{r(t)^{3}}{2|x-c(t)|^{3}}c^{\prime}(t)\cdot(x-c(t)).

To determine the dynamics of the center of mass
c(t)
and radius
r(t)
of the bubble, it is convenient to write directly the Bernoulli equation

\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+p-p_{\infty}=0

and to evaluate it at the boundary of the bubble. Straightforward but tedious computations yield:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi|_{\partial B(t)}

\displaystyle=-r(t)r^{\prime\prime}(t)-2r^{\prime}(t)^{2}-\frac{5}{2}r^{\prime}(t)(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)-\frac{3}{2}(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}r(t)c^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot n+\frac{1}{2}|c^{\prime}(t)|^{2}

\displaystyle\nabla\phi|_{\partial B(t)}

\displaystyle=r^{\prime}(t)n+\frac{3}{2}(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)n-\frac{1}{2}c^{\prime}(t),

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}|\nabla\phi|_{\partial B(t)}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(r^{\prime}(t)^{2}+\frac{3}{4}(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|c^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+2(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)r^{\prime}(t)\right)

so that by Bernoulli equation we get

-r(t)r^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{3}{2}r^{\prime}(t)^{2}-\frac{3}{2}r^{\prime}(t)(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)-\frac{9}{8}(c^{\prime}(t)\cdot n)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}r(t)c^{\prime\prime}(t)\cdot n+\frac{5}{8}|c^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+p|_{\partial B(t)}-p_{\infty}=0

Integrating over
\partial B(t)
, using the last relation in (
4.3.1
), one finds (remember
\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.83337pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.11674pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.48965pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.31259pt}}\!\int_{\partial B}n\cdot n=\frac{1}{3}
):

-r(t)r^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{3}{2}r^{\prime}(t)^{2}+\frac{1}{4}|c^{\prime}(t)|^{2}+p_{B}(t)-p_{\infty}=0

Moreover, multiplying by the normal vector
n
, integrating over
\partial B(t)
and using that
\int_{\partial B(t)}p(t,\cdot)n=0
, we get

\frac{3}{2}\frac{r^{\prime}(t)}{r(t)}c^{\prime}(t)+c^{\prime\prime}(t)=0.

Those two equations are the second order ODE’s describing the dynamics of the bubble, that could be found previously in
[
24
]
. If we neglect the translation of the bubble (
c=0
), we recover the famous
Rayleigh-Plesset equation
:

-r(t)r^{\prime\prime}(t)-\frac{3}{2}r^{\prime}(t)^{2}+p_{B}(t)-p_{\infty}=0

4.4
Extension to a bounded cavity

The well-posedness of (
4.0.1
) established in Paragraphs
4.1
-
4.2
was restricted to fluid domains of the form
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\cup_{k}\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}
, that is to fluids filling the whole space. A natural problem is to extend this well-posedness result to the case of a bounded cavity
\Omega
. As discussed in Remark
2.2.6
or at the beginning of Section
4
, there should be in this case extra compatibility conditions, due to volume constraints induced by incompressibility of the fluid. Indeed, coming back to the approach developped in Paragraphs
4.1
-
4.2
, one issue stems from system (
4.1.1
), that should now be replaced by

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\Delta\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}&=0,\quad\text{ in }\Omega\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}}}\\
\partial_{n}\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}&=\big\langle V_{m_{k}}(\cdot),\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}\quad\forall k,\\
\partial_{n}\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}&=0,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

(4.4.1)

But integrating the first equation over the fluid domain, we find

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\Omega\setminus\overline{\cup_{k=1}^{N}\Omega_{m_{k}}}}\Delta\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{n}\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}-\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}\partial_{n}\phi_{\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}}=-\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}\big\langle V_{m_{k}},\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle

Hence, we find the necessary condition on
(\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}})\in T\textbf{M}
:

\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}\big\langle V_{m_{k}},\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle=0

(4.4.2)

This condition echoes the one that the fluid velocity
u
has to satisfy: for all
t
,

\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}(t)}}\big\langle V_{m_{k}(t)},m^{\prime}_{k}(t)\big\rangle=0

(4.4.3)

Hence, we have somehow to restrict the analysis to elements
(\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}})\in T\textbf{M}
satisfying (
4.4.2
). Note that for each
\textbf{m}\in\textbf{M}
, the map

\dot{\textbf{m}}\rightarrow\sum_{k}\int_{\partial\Omega_{m_{k}}}\big\langle V_{m_{k}},\dot{m}_{k}\big\rangle

is a linear form on
T_{\textbf{m}}\textbf{M}
, so that either it is identically zero, or its kernel is a hyperplane of
T_{\textbf{m}}\textbf{M}
, that we may denote
F_{\textbf{m}}
. In the latter case, with our notations,
\dim F_{\textbf{m}}=p-1
. If we are in the favorable (generic) situation where the second case holds for all
m
, and where
\sqcup_{\textbf{m}\in\textbf{M}}\{\textbf{m}\}\times F_{\textbf{m}}
is a smooth sub-bundle of the tangent bundle
T\textbf{M}
, one can adapt the proof of well-posedness above. First, for any
\textbf{m},\dot{\textbf{m}}
satisfying (
4.4.2
), one can easily modify the proof of Lemma
4.1.1
to show existence of a solution to (
4.4.1
). Then, given
\big(\textbf{m}_{0},\dot{\textbf{m}_{0}}\big)
satisfying (
4.4.2
) (the initial data for the ODE system), one can introduce as before in the vicinity of
\textbf{m}_{0}
a family of smooth vector fields
\mathbf{E}_{1}
, …,
\mathbf{E}_{p}
and a dual family of smooth one-forms
\mathbf{E}_{1}^{*},\dots,\mathbf{E}_{p}^{*}
, such that (
4.2.1
) holds, but this time with the extra requirement that

\forall\textbf{m},\quad\big(\mathbf{E}_{1}(\textbf{m}),\dots,\mathbf{E}_{p-1}(\textbf{m})\big)\>\text{ is a basis of $F_{\textbf{m}}$}.

One can further decompose

u(t,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{p-1}\dot{\textbf{m}}_{i}(t)\,\nabla\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}(t)},\quad\text{where for all $\textbf{m}$ in $V$, }\phi^{i}_{\textbf{m}}=\phi_{\textbf{m},\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m})},

and proceeding exactly as in Paragraph
4.2
, we derive a system of ODE’s of the form:

\forall i=1\dots p-1,\quad\dot{\textbf{m}}_{i}^{\prime}(t)=\tilde{G}_{i}(\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t))

It can be reformulated as a usual second order ODE system of the form
\textbf{m}^{\prime\prime}(t)=\mathcal{G}(\textbf{m}(t),\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t))
: indeed, one can write artificially
\textbf{m}^{\prime}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\dot{\textbf{m}}_{i}(t)\mathbf{E}_{i}(\textbf{m})
and add the trivial equation

\dot{\textbf{m}}^{\prime}_{p}(t)=0.

It has therefore a unique local in time solution starting from
\big(\textbf{m}_{0},\dot{\textbf{m}_{0}}\big)
.

As an example, let us consider the case of two spherical bubbles in a bounded cavity:

B_{1}(t)=B(c_{1}(t),r_{1}(t))\quad\text{ and }\>B_{2}(t)=B(c_{2}(t),r_{2}(t))

\displaystyle M=\big\{m=(c,r),\quad c\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\quad r>0,\quad\overline{B(c,r)}\subset\Omega\big\},

and

\displaystyle\textbf{M}=\big\{(m_{1},m_{2})\in M^{2},\>|c_{1}-c_{2}|>r_{1}+r_{2}\big\}.

From the computations of Paragraph
3.2
, we have that

\big\langle V_{m}(x),(\dot{c},\dot{r})\big\rangle=\dot{c}\cdot n(x)+\dot{r}

From there, using that
\int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}\dot{c}\cdot n=0
,
\int_{\partial\Omega_{m}}\dot{r}=4\pi r^{2}\dot{r}
, we deduce that (
4.4.2
) reads

r_{1}^{2}\dot{r}_{1}+r_{2}^{2}\dot{r}_{2}=0.

while (
4.4.3
) yields

r_{1}(t)^{2}r^{\prime}_{1}(t)+r_{2}(t)^{2}r^{\prime}_{2}(t)=0.

(4.4.4)

which implies that

r_{2}(t)^{3}=-r_{1}(t)^{3}+r_{1,0}^{3}+r_{2,0}^{3}

(4.4.5)

(where
r_{1,0}
and
r_{2,0}
are the initial radii of the two bubbles).
Then, we can introduce for
k=1,2
and
i=1\dots 3
the solutions
\phi^{k,i}_{\textbf{m}}
of

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}\phi^{k,i}_{\textbf{m}}(t,\cdot)=0\quad\text{ in }\>\Omega\setminus\cup_{k^{\prime}=1}^{2}\Omega_{m_{k^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\phi^{k,i}_{\textbf{m}}|_{\partial\Omega_{k^{\prime}}}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}e_{i}\cdot n,\quad\partial_{n}\phi^{k,i}_{\textbf{m}}|_{\partial\Omega}=0,

and we complete it by the solution
\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}}
of

\displaystyle\Delta_{x}\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}}(t,\cdot)=0\quad\text{ in }\>\Omega\setminus\cup_{k^{\prime}=1}^{2}\Omega_{m_{k^{\prime}}},

\displaystyle\partial_{n}\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}}|_{\partial\Omega_{1}}=1,\quad\partial_{n}\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}}|_{\partial\Omega_{2}}=-\frac{r_{1}^{2}}{r_{2}^{2}},\quad\partial_{n}\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}}|_{\partial\Omega}=0.

so that

u=\nabla\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}c^{\prime}_{k,i}(t)\phi^{k,i}_{\textbf{m}(t)}+r^{\prime}_{1}(t)\phi^{r}_{\textbf{m}(t)}\right)

Eventually, one gets a second order ODE system of the form

\displaystyle c_{1}^{\prime\prime}=F_{1}(c_{1},c_{2},r_{1},r_{2},c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime},r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime}),\quad c_{2}^{\prime\prime}=F_{2}(c_{1},c_{2},r_{1},r_{2},c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime},r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle r_{1}^{\prime\prime}=F_{r}(c_{1},c_{2},r_{1},r_{2},c_{1}^{\prime},c_{2}^{\prime},r_{1}^{\prime},r_{2}^{\prime})

which after replacing
r^{\prime}_{2}
and
r_{2}
through (
4.4.4
) and (
4.4.5
) yields a closed system of ODEs on
c_{1},c_{2},r_{1}
.
