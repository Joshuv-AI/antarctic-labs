Spatiotemporal and clinical characterisation of acute flaccid paralysis in Uganda, 2016–2023
Authors: Emmanuel Okiror Okello, Richard Migisha, Immaculate Ampaire, Fred Nsubuga, Sam Ofori, Brenda S Nakafeero, Molly Birungi, Peter Eliku, Emmanuel Tenywa, Annet Kisakye, Hildah T Nansikombi, Lilian Bulage, Benon Kwesiga, Alex Riolexus Ario
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUganda, in line with global efforts to eradicate polio by 2026, conducts surveillance for acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) among children <15 years to enable the timely detection of poliovirus. We analysed national AFP surveillance data to characterise case demographics, spatiotemporal patterns and clinical outcomes, and to evaluate surveillance performance and progress towards eradication.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective descriptive analysis of routine AFP surveillance data from 2016 to 2023. Variables included timeliness indicators, non-polio AFP (NPAFP) rates, stool adequacy and demographic, clinical, vaccination and laboratory characteristics. Trends were assessed using the Mann-Kendall test, and spatial patterns were illustrated using choropleth maps.ResultsA total of 6409 AFP cases were reported, of which 5837 (91.1%) were confirmed as true AFP. The median age was 3 years (IQR: 1–7), with most cases occurring in children <5 years (59.3%) and males (57.8%). Timeliness indicators showed that 63% of cases were notified within 7 days, 93.4% were investigated within 48 hours and 76.5% of stool specimens were shipped within 3 days. Stool adequacy was 88.6% and 99.8% of samples arrived in good condition. The NPAFP rate averaged 3.4/100 000, meeting the ≥3.0 target in 5 of 8 years. Clinically, 90% of cases were community-identified; 91% had fever at onset, 86% showed rapid progression within 3 days and 80% had asymmetrical paralysis. Among cases with vaccination history, 77% had received ≥3 oral polio vaccine doses and 3% were unvaccinated. Of 6405 samples tested, 2% were classified as suspected poliovirus and 0.1% as mixed non-polio enterovirus and poliovirus. Among 823 followed-up cases, 30% had residual paralysis, 1% had died and 0.6% were lost to follow-up. District-level performance was variable and suboptimal, with averages of 63.4% districts achieving the NPAFP rate target, 74.8% stool adequacy and 49% both core indicators.ConclusionUganda’s AFP surveillance system remains largely robust, though improvements in timeliness, regional equity and follow-up are needed to sustain progress towards polio eradication.Keywords: Endemic Diseases, Epidemiologic Methods, Public Health, Community Health

Introduction

Acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) is defined as the sudden onset of weakness/floppiness or paralysis in any part of the body of a child <15 years.1 AFP surveillance serves to rapidly detect poliovirus circulation, enabling early identification of poliomyelitis cases and supporting the global eradication initiative set for 2026.2 Poliovirus, a member of the Enterovirus genus, invades and destroys anterior horn cells in the spinal cord and brainstem, resulting in motor neuron loss and muscle weakness.3 Clinically, paralytic polio typically begins with high fever, headache and asymmetrical muscle weakness; paralysis then develops one to ten days after these prodromal symptoms, progresses over 2–3 days, and reaches maximal severity by the time the fever resolves.3,5

Globally, by 2024, only Pakistan and Afghanistan were still reporting cases of wild poliovirus, a dramatic decline from the 125 countries affected when the World Health Assembly resolved to eradicate poliomyelitis in 1988.6 While the global eradication of wild poliovirus represents a landmark achievement, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) now accounts for the majority of cases. Between January 2023 and June 2024, 74 cVDPV outbreaks were reported across 39 countries, predominantly in Africa, with 64% representing newly emergent strains.7 Uganda, certified polio-free in 2016 after a decade without indigenous wild poliovirus, experienced an imported wild type 1 case in Amuru District in 2019.8 Enhanced clinical and environmental surveillance has since identified additional cVDPV transmission hotspots, notably in Kampala in 2022 and Mbale District in 2024.9 These occurrences point to persistent immunity gaps, particularly in areas with suboptimal routine immunisation coverage and high population mobility. Despite introducing all recommended doses of polio vaccines, the country’s performance is still suboptimal, with coverage of the third dose of oral polio vaccine (OPV3) at 89% and that of the second dose of inactivated polio vaccine (IPV2) at 72% as of 2024, according to WHO/UNICEF Estimates of National Immunization Coverage.10 In addition, variations in AFP surveillance performance across districts, including silent districts that report no AFP cases, suggest potential gaps in case detection and reporting.11 Together, these challenges underscore the need to continuously assess surveillance sensitivity and immunisation programme performance to rapidly detect poliovirus circulation and prevent future outbreaks.

One of the indicators used in AFP surveillance is the non-polio AFP (NPAFP) rate. It is an indicator of surveillance sensitivity. NPAFP rate represents the number of NPAFP cases that are detected annually per 100 000 population aged <15 years.12 It is expected that in the absence of polio circulation, a sensitive surveillance system will detect at least one case of NPAFP each year for every 100 000 children under 15 years. Where polio is present or where polio is a threat, this target is modified. The objective is then to detect at least two cases of NPAFP each year for every 100 000 children <15 years in all at-risk and outbreak countries, and to detect at least three cases of NPAFP each year for every 100 000 children under 15 years in endemic countries and outbreak-affected areas, where Uganda belongs.12 Another certification-standard indicator is stool adequacy, which is an AFP surveillance quality indicator. Defined as the proportion of AFP cases with two stool specimens collected ≥24 hours apart, both within 14 days of paralysis onset, and received in good condition in a WHO-accredited laboratory; stool adequacy target is set as ≥80%. However, countries are warned that achieving the ≥80% target at the subnational level should not lead to complacency.12 For every AFP case identified and investigated after 14 days from the onset of paralysis (late case), a mandatory 60-day follow-up has to be conducted by a trained clinician. Late AFP cases, investigated more than 14 days after paralysis onset, require a 60-day follow-up by a clinician, who records clinical progress, residual deficits (eg, paralysis, reflex or tone loss, weakness, sensory loss), additional investigations, rehabilitation referrals and a working diagnosis.12

Uganda conducts both passive and active surveillance for AFP among children <15 years as a notifiable condition. From this, several AFP cases are identified, investigated and reported through the country’s surveillance system annually. Despite Uganda being certified polio-free in 2016, the risk of poliovirus importation and emergence of cVDPV requires sustained high-quality AFP surveillance. However, there is limited published evidence evaluating the performance and epidemiological trends of AFP surveillance in Uganda during the post-certification period. The years 2016–2023 are particularly important as they capture evolving surveillance dynamics, including the detection of imported and vaccine-derived poliovirus events and potential disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic. Analysing AFP surveillance data over this period helps assess surveillance sensitivity, identify spatial and temporal gaps, and inform strategies to sustain Uganda’s polio-free status and support global eradication efforts. Therefore, we analysed AFP surveillance secondary data for 8 years (2016–2023) to describe case demographics, spatiotemporal trends, clinical and laboratory outcomes, to evaluate performance, inform programming and track the country’s polio eradication efforts.

Methods

Study design, data source

This was a retrospective descriptive analysis of routine AFP surveillance data in the country from 1 January 2016 to 30 December 2023.

Data were obtained from Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI), which receives, compiles and archives AFP data elements into an Excel dataset. For every AFP case alert, a designated trained surveillance officer physically visits the case-person to verify and confirm the alert. If it is a true AFP case, he/she fills the case investigation form (CIF) and collects two stool samples from the case-person 24 hours apart. These samples are then transported together with the filled CIF to UVRI. Data from the CIF are transferred to an Excel database, where later data from laboratory findings and that of the 60-day follow-up are added, merged and archived. Data variables in the final dataset include the case person’s demographic, clinical and results (both laboratory and 60-day follow-up physical assessments) variables.

Operational definitions

A true AFP case was defined as any child under 15 years of age with a sudden onset of flaccid/floppy paralysis on any limb identified within 60 days from onset of paralysis, or a person of any age in whom polio is suspected by a clinician.12

Timeliness of notification is defined as the proportion of AFP cases reported within 7 days of paralysis onset, with a recommended target of ≥80%.12

Timeliness of investigation is the proportion of AFP cases investigated within 48 hours of notification. The recommended target is ≥ 80%.12

Timeliness of stool specimen shipment is defined as the proportion of AFP cases with stool specimens that arrive at a WHO-accredited laboratory within 3 days (domestic shipment) or 7 days (international shipment) of specimen collection.12

Stool condition refers to the proportion of stool samples that arrive in the laboratory in the temperature range of +2°C to +8°C, with no spillage/desiccation and with the appropriate documentation. The recommended target is ≥80%.12 A silent district is one that has not reported any single AFP case in a calendar year, contributing an NPAFP of 0/100 000 children <15 years.12

A silent district is one that has not reported any single AFP case in a calendar year, contributing an NPAFP of 0/100 000 children <15 years.12

Study setting

This was a nationwide study in Uganda. As of 2023, Uganda had 135 districts aggregated into 15 regions with an estimated population of 48.58 million people.13 The country operates a decentralised health system starting from health centre II (as the lowest level), III, IV, general hospital and regional referral to the national referral hospital. All these health facilities are sites for both active and passive surveillance for AFP in the country. Additionally, some private and private-not-for-profit health facilities participate in AFP surveillance. These health facilities are responsible for conducting both passive and active AFP surveillance in their catchment areas, guided and supported by the district health teams.

Uganda’s AFP surveillance system detects, verifies and reports AFP cases in children under 15 years through both community and health facility surveillance following a bottom-up approach (figure 1). On notification of an AFP case, either from the community or health facility, the district surveillance focal persons and or health sub-district surveillance focal persons support its verification, sample collection of two adequate stool specimens, ensure proper transportation of samples to the national polio reference laboratory and communicate results back to facilities and communities. These AFP cases undergo validation and a 60-day follow-up evaluation by a trained clinician, with findings reported to the national level where the MoH, in collaboration with WHO, coordinates data reporting, both nationally and globally. The National Polio Expert Committee then conducts final case classification, and all surveillance data are compiled and archived in the UVRI database. Feedback mechanisms are available at all levels of surveillance through the results dispatch system and other platforms such as email and social media platforms.

Study variables

The demographic data variables included age, sex; clinical variables included hospitalisation status of the case-person, fever at onset of paralysis, progression of paralysis within 3 days after onset of paralysis, nature of the paralysis and number of doses received from either the routine or supplementary immunisation activities if vaccinated. We classified the types of paralysis into four categories based on the limbs affected: monoplegia (paralysis of either one upper or one lower limb), hemiplegia (paralysis of both an upper and lower limb on the same side), diplegia (paralysis of both upper limbs or both lower limbs) and quadriplegia (paralysis of all four limbs). Laboratory outcomes were derived from stool sample analysis of the case-patients, while physical outcomes were assessed through clinical examinations conducted 60 days after the onset of paralysis.

We also assessed for timeliness of notification, investigation and stool specimen shipment of AFP cases. All these timeliness indicators have a target of ≥80% in global, national and subnational assessments. We calculated and assessed both national and subnational NPAFP rates and stool adequacy proportions.

Data analysis

Data cleaning, validation and completeness checks were conducted on Excel and the dataset was imported into Stata V.17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) for analysis. We summarised the demographic and clinical characteristics, laboratory and physical outcomes of the AFP case-patients using frequencies and percentages. To demonstrate the trends and temporal-spatial distribution of AFP case-patients over the study period, we calculated the annual NPAFP rates, stool adequacy proportions and the timeliness indicators for each year and tested their trends across study years using the Mann-Kendall test Stata V.17 (StataCorp). We calculated the annual NPAFP rate as number of reported NPAFP cases <15 years in a year divided by the total number of children <15 years of age in that year multiplied by 100 000. We used projected under 15-year population estimates for each year from Uganda Bureau of Statistics as denominators. Choropleth maps were drawn to show the temporal-spatial distribution of NPAFP rates in regions using QGIS software.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Demographic, clinical and laboratory characteristics of AFP case-patients, Uganda, 2016–2023

A total of 6409 AFP cases were cumulatively reported in the 8-year period. Of these, 5837 (91.1%) were true AFP cases. Median age of case-patients was 3 years (IQR: 1–7 years); the majority (59.3%) were children <5 years and males (57.8%). Of the 6409 AFP cases, 4039 (63%) were notified timely (within 7 days of paralysis onset); 5987 (93.4%) were investigated timely (within 48 hours of notification) and stool specimen of 4901 (76.5%) were shipped timely (within 3 days of collection) to the national laboratory. The proportion of stool reaching the laboratory in good condition was 6396 (99.8%) and the average stool adequacy proportion was at 88.6% in the 8-year period (table 1).

In terms of clinical history, the majority (90%) of case-patients were identified from the community (not hospitalised). Of the 6409 case-patients, 5816 (91%) had reported fever at onset of paralysis, 5476 (86%) reported that paralysis had progressed in 3 days and 5097 (80%) had asymmetrical paralysis. The majority (57%) of case-patients had only one limb of their bodies paralysed (monoplegia). Of the 4548 case-patients, 533 (12%) had received an injection before onset of paralysis. Of the 5744 case-patients who had revealed their OPV vaccination history, 4960 (77%) had received 3+ doses and 158 (3%) had not received any dose (table 1).

Of the 6405 samples tested, 154 (2%) were suspected to have poliovirus and 8 (0.1%) had non-polio enterovirus (NPENT) plus poliovirus suspected (table 1).

Of the 823 case-patients followed up, 250 (30%) still had residual paralysis present and 5 (0.6%) were lost to follow-up and 9 (1%) had died before follow-up (table 1).

The national annual NPAFP rate was ≥3.0/100 000 children <15 years in five out of the eight study years with an average of 3.4/100 000 children <15 years in the 8-year study period. There was a noted decrease in the NPAFP rate from 3.6/100 000 children <15 years in 2016 to 2.7/100 000 children <15 years with no statistically significant trend (p=0.803). This target was not achieved in 2019 and 2020 (figure 2). Annualised stool adequacy proportions and timeliness of investigation of AFP cases were both above the 80% target each year throughout the 8-year period with non-statistically significant trends (figure 3). Annualised timeliness of notification, on the other hand, failed to meet the 80% target in all the 8 years, with a non-statistically significant increasing trend (Mann-Kendall’s τ=0.5, p=0.108) (figure 3). Annualised timeliness of stool specimen shipment met the target of ≥80% from 2016 to 2019) and then fell below target from 2020 to 2023 with an equally non-statistically significant trend (Mann-Kendall’s τ=−0.5, p=0.109) (figure 3).

Only 14 (9.6%) districts achieved the targeted NPAFP rate of ≥3.0/100 000 children <15 years each year in all the 8-year study period, including Arua, Bukwo, Butambala, Iganga, Kaabong, Kalangala, Kween, Lamwo, Mbarara, Nakapiriprit, Kazo, Nabilatuk, Rukiga and Kapelebyong. On the other hand, Buikwe, Kampala and Wakiso districts did not achieve this target in the 8 years, except in 2021 when the best performance was recorded in the country. Only four districts were silent in the study period—Buhweju in 2016; Kibuku in 2018; Agago and Busia districts in 2020 (figure 4). 21 districts (14.4%) achieved the annualised stool adequacy target of ≥80% consistently throughout the 8-year study period (figure 5). Only four (2.7%) districts, including Butambala, Iganga, Kazo and Rukiga districts consistently achieved both the two core AFP surveillance indicators (NPAFP rate and stool adequacy) consistently throughout the 8-year study period. Buikwe and Kasese districts failed to achieve the annualised targets of these two core AFP surveillance indicators in all the 8 years of the study (figure 6).

The proportions of districts achieving targets of NPAFP rate, stool adequacy, timeliness of notification, investigation and stool specimen shipment were initially high, drastically decreased in 2020 and struggled to pick up in the subsequent years of the study. On average, 63.4% of the districts achieved the annualised NPAFP rates and 74.8% achieved annualised stool adequacy in the 8-year study period (table 2). Timeliness of notification target was poorly achieved subnationally with an average of only 27.3% of districts achieving the target each year of the 8-year study period (table 2). Similarly, the proportion of districts achieving both the targets of NPAFP rates and stool adequacy remained suboptimal throughout the study years, averaging 49% with the highest proportion (71.3%) achieved in 2021 (table 2).

Discussion

In our study, the majority of case-patients were children <5 years of age. Clinically, over three-quarters presented with fever at paralysis onset, experienced symptom progression within 3 days, exhibited asymmetrical paralysis and had received more than three doses of OPV. Case detection was predominantly community-based. Only two regions consistently met the WHO NPAFP surveillance rate target over the 8-year period, with regional performance peaking in 2021 and dipping in 2020; nationally, NPAFP rates remained stable with no significant trend. Laboratory analysis showed that the vast majority of stool specimens were negative for poliovirus, and just 2% were classified as polio suspects. At 60-day follow-up, fewer than half of patients had residual paralysis, and under 2% had died or were lost to follow-up. District-level performance was variable and suboptimal across the 8 years.

The overall NPAFP rate averaged 3.4 per 100 000 children <15 years, meeting the ≥3.0 threshold in most years. This exceeds the minimum standard recommended by the WHO for countries at risk of poliovirus transmission, indicating a sensitive surveillance system capable of detecting AFP cases.12 Similar performance has been documented in Ethiopia, Zambia, Ghana, Bangladesh and other settings.14,18 The dip in 2019–2020 coincided with COVID-19-related disruptions to immunisation services and surveillance activities, when only two regions achieved the target.18 19 Following intensive WHO-supported revitalisation in 2021, all 15 regions again met the standard. To protect these gains, there is a need to institutionalise pandemic-resilient surveillance measures, such as decentralised reporting, integration with community health platforms and sustained performance incentives to ensure consistent NPAFP rates even during health system shocks.

Stool adequacy remained high (88.6%) throughout the study period, consistently exceeding the ≥80% target. This reflects strong adherence to case investigation protocols and effective specimen handling systems. High stool adequacy is critical for reliable virological confirmation and has been widely used as a proxy for surveillance quality (WHO Polio Surveillance Standards). Comparable studies in countries such as Nigeria, Ethiopia and previously in Uganda have similarly reported improved stool adequacy following investments in surveillance training and logistics.11 16 17 The near-universal proportion of samples arriving in good condition (99.8%) further underscores the robustness of the specimen transport and cold chain systems in Uganda.

The majority of AFP cases (59%) occurred in children under 5 years, with males accounting for more than half, a pattern consistent with surveillance findings from East and Southern Africa, Ghana, Nigeria and Bangladesh,14 16 17 although a contrasting study in Zambia reported higher occurrence among children aged 10–15 years.15 The increased vulnerability among younger children is likely due to poorer sanitation practices and greater exposure to contaminated environments, given that poliovirus is transmitted via the faecal–oral route through ingestion of contaminated food or water and inadequate hand hygiene.17 Capturing most cases within this high-risk age group enhances the sensitivity of AFP surveillance systems for early detection, reporting and investigation of poliovirus. Clinically, the AFP cases exhibited classical features consistent with poliomyelitis and other causes of AFP, including fever at onset, rapid progression and asymmetrical paralysis. These findings are comparable to studies from Zambia and Ethiopia,14 20 supporting the validity of AFP case definitions and the reliability of clinical data collected. Importantly, 77% of children had received ≥3 doses of OPV, reflecting relatively good immunisation coverage. However, the presence of unvaccinated children (3%) indicates persistent immunity gaps that could facilitate poliovirus transmission if reintroduced. This is consistent with evidence that even small pockets of under-immunised populations can sustain outbreaks, as seen in recent cVDPV events in Africa.12

Laboratory findings showed a low proportion of suspected poliovirus cases (2%), which is expected in a polio-free setting but still warrants vigilance. The detection of NPENTs further confirms adequate specimen quality and laboratory sensitivity. While poliovirus remains the primary concern in AFP surveillance among children under 15, other neurotropic viruses also contribute to paralysis. NPENTs such as EV-A71, EV-D68, Japanese encephalitis virus and West Nile virus have been implicated in AFP cases.6 7 These findings underscore the need for comprehensive laboratory diagnostics to accurately identify causative enteroviruses and guide targeted management. Follow-up outcomes revealed that 30% of cases had residual paralysis, emphasising the burden of disability associated with AFP and the importance of rehabilitation services, an area often underemphasised in surveillance-focused programmes. These residual paralysis findings are consistent with those reported in previous studies.16 17 20 According to WHO criteria, cases with inadequate stool specimens plus residual paralysis at 60 days, loss to follow-up or death within that period are classified as ‘compatible’ with poliovirus infection. The detection of compatible cases raises the possibility of undetected poliovirus circulation and reinforces the need for vigilant AFP surveillance, including prompt and comprehensive investigation of every case.12

The majority (90%) of case-patients were identified from the community (not hospitalised). Similar findings were reported in a study in Zambia.15 This underpins the importance of community-based surveillance (CBS), including the utilisation of community health structures like village health teams and community extension workers to support AFP surveillance. The WHO guides that CBS is a key method to access hard-to-reach areas and communities that are not reached by the regular AFP surveillance system. The guidance also informs that CBS is particularly useful in settings or areas at high risk of undetected poliovirus transmission or at risk of new outbreaks following importation or vaccine-derived poliovirus (VDPV) emergence.

District-level performance was variable and suboptimal, with only 9.6% districts consistently meeting the NPAFP target and just 2.7% achieved both indicators across all 8 years. Similar findings were reported from the analyses of Zambia’s AFP surveillance performance with only 9% of the districts achieving the targeted NPAFP rate of ≥2/100 000 <15 years population over 7 years.21 Geographic inequities in surveillance performance have been widely reported and are often linked to differences in infrastructure, security and access to healthcare services.21 The marked decline in district-level performance across multiple indicators from 2020 to 2023 further highlights the need for targeted interventions at subnational levels.

A key limitation of this analysis is the incomplete reporting of certain variables, for example, nearly 30% of the 6409 notified AFP cases lacked information on whether an injection preceded paralysis onset. Moreover, our reliance on secondary surveillance data may have introduced misclassification bias that we cannot fully correct post hoc. Nonetheless, all records were entered according to standard operating procedures, which likely helped to minimise data-entry errors and mitigate some of these concerns. Overall, these findings suggest that while Uganda’s AFP surveillance system is largely functional and meets key international benchmarks, persistent gaps in timeliness, regional inequities and recent declines in performance could undermine its effectiveness. Future studies should consider applying analytical approaches, such as regression modelling, to identify factors associated with clinical outcomes and subnational disparities in AFP surveillance performance. Being a longitudinal dataset that involved individual data collated at district level and given that districts in Uganda are mixed urban/rural in setting, we found it difficult to compare performance at the urban/rural interface. Therefore, we recommend further research that involves these analyses at this interface.

Conclusion

During 2016–2023, Uganda’s AFP surveillance confirmed almost every reported case and initiated investigations, typically within 48 hours of notification. Children <5 years accounted for the majority of cases, and nearly one-third of cases had residual paralysis at 60 days. Strong community reporting alongside high OPV coverage kept poliovirus prevalence low. Nonetheless, regional disparities, worsened by COVID-19 disruptions and occasional gaps in 60-day follow-up reveal areas for improvement.

The findings of this study have important policy implications for both Uganda and other countries working towards polio eradication. While overall AFP surveillance performance meets key WHO standards, persistent gaps in timeliness of notification, subnational inequities and declines observed after 2020 highlight the need for more resilient and decentralised surveillance systems. Policymakers could prioritise strengthening CBS platforms, including village health structures, to enhance early case detection and reporting, particularly in underserved and high-risk regions. Sustaining high immunisation coverage and closing immunity gaps remains critical to preventing outbreaks of both wild and VDPV. Additionally, investments in system resilience, such as integrating surveillance into broader health systems, leveraging digital reporting tools and ensuring continuity during public health emergencies, are essential. Finally, expanding follow-up care and rehabilitation services for AFP cases could be incorporated into national policies to address long-term disability, ensuring that surveillance systems not only detect cases but also contribute to improved patient outcomes.
