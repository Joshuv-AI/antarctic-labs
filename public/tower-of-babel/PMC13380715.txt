Microbiological contamination of nursing mobile workstations: assessing their role in pathogen transmission in healthcarefacilities
Authors: M. Vanmeenen, V. Duprez, S. Malfait, K. Claus, S. Verhofstede, I. Leroux-Roels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntroductionThe nursing mobile workstation (NMW) is used daily on hospital nursing wards, where its serves multiple functions and is handled by various users in diverse settings. This study quantifies microbial contamination on NMW to assess the level and nature of contamination, offering insights into its potential contribution to pathogen transmission in healthcare environments.Aim(s)The aim of this study was to explore whether the NMW is a possible hazard for nosocomial infections in hospital settings.MethodsSamples were collected from thirty NMWs across sixteen nursing wards (150 samples total). The total bacterial count was determined, micro-organisms were identified, and each sample was classified using the Hazard Analysis – Critical Control Point hygiene classes.ResultsA total of 327 micro-organisms were identified, including 6% (21/327) classified as potential pathogens, none of which were resistant to antibiotics. The degree of decontamination on the 150 samples was categorized using the hygiene classification of surfaces based on colony-forming unit counts. The results were as follows: 19% ‘excellent’, 12% ‘good’, 51% ‘sufficient’, 15% ‘insufficient’, and 3% ‘poor’. Frequently touched surfaces, such as the side of the NMWs showed higher contamination levels than other areas.ConclusionsThe overall degree of contamination of the NMW was reassuring, with the majority of surfaces demonstrating acceptable hygiene levels. However, attention must be directed towards the consistent cleaning and disinfection of frequently touched surfaces, particularly those with higher contamination risks, such as the sides and handles.

Introduction

In the most recent European point prevalence survey, the prevalence of hospitalized patients with a nosocomial or healthcare-associated infection (HAI) in Belgium was averaged at 9.2%, notably higher than the European average of 6.3% for patients with at least one HAI [1]. HAIs cause prolonged length of stay, increased emotional stress in patients, emerging antimicrobial resistance, higher mortality and morbidity, and significant additional healthcare costs [[2], [3], [4], [5]].

HAI can be caused by pathogen transmission in healthcare facilities. Hands of healthcare workers are widely recognized as the main vectors for pathogen transmission. Consequently, hand hygiene is considered to be the most important strategy for reducing the spread of pathogens [6,7]. Despite this, global compliance with hand hygiene guidelines remains poor at an average rate of 38% [7]. Systematic education and better surveillance were identified as main strategies to increase the compliance. In addition to healthcare workers’ hands, frequently touched surfaces in hospitals play a significant role in the transmission of HAIs. These surfaces act as reservoirs for pathogens, facilitating their spread within healthcare environments [2,8].

One of those frequently touched surfaces is the nursing mobile workstation (NMW), a tool commonly used in hospital settings worldwide [9]. NMWs are used to record information, store nursing equipment, dispose of waste, document patient histories, perform assessments, point-of-care medication administration, and wound care and are stationed adjacent to patient rooms. In addition to materials and instruments for clinical care, patient-specific medications, and a working surface, the NMWs comprise a computer, a barcode reader, or other technical equipment. It enables nurses to access materials and facilitates direct patient care [9]. The use of the NMW has three components that could potentially lead to pathogen transmission. First, the NMW has multiple users, including nurses, physicians, hospital support staff, and others [10,11]. This creates repeated opportunities for indirect transmission via hands, particularly when hand hygiene compliance is suboptimal. Not being aware of the interactions of others with the NMW can lead to the work station being perceived as ‘clean’ when in reality it is not [[12], [13], [14]]. Second, the NMW is continuously used for diverse tasks, ranging from clean to potentially contaminated activities (e.g. waste disposal, wound care, and medication preparation). This variability increases the risk of cross-contamination between tasks. Third, the NMW frequently moves between different locations, including nursing stations, hallways, and patient rooms. This mobility creates a link between different environments and patient zones, facilitating the spread of micro-organisms across the ward [14,15]. Due to the workstation’s exposure to multiple users, its continuous and varied use for different tasks, and its frequent movement across nursing wards including entry into patient rooms, NMWs are high-touch surfaces with multiple components that may contribute to the transmission of pathogens [10,14,15].

Earlier research has shown that frequently touched surfaces in the patient’s immediate surroundings include mobile equipment, such as the NMW [12,16]. Importantly, hand hygiene compliance when moving from a patient to an NMW was found to be low (11%), highlighting a potential pathway for indirect pathogen transmission via the NWW [12].

A recent study examining healthcare provider attitudes, practices, and challenges related to mobile equipment cleaning, found that nurses often (1) assume that the previous user has cleaned the equipment, (2) clean equipment before using it only if time allows, or (3) question whether equipment is cleaned between users at all [14]. Universal guidelines or standardized protocols for cleaning (frequently touched) surfaces in healthcare facilities are lacking [16]. Furthermore, there is ongoing debate about whether cleaning and disinfecting surfaces just once a day is sufficient to minimize the risk of transmission [2]. Since the NMW is intended to support patient care and promote more direct nurse–patient contact, it is essential to assess whether its use could indirectly contribute to the transmission of pathogens [9]. A review of the existing literature revealed no published studies addressing this issue [9]. To fill this knowledge gap, this study aimed to evaluate microbial contamination on various touchpoints of the NMW as a proxy for assessing its potential role in pathogen transmission.

Methods

Aim

This study measured the degree of microbial contamination of NMWs to evaluate whether the NMW should be considered as a vector for transmission of nosocomial pathogens and a possible hazard for HAIs in a hospital setting.

Design

A descriptive observational study was conducted where healthcare workers were blinded to the study’s purpose.

Setting and participants

This study was conducted at a tertiary care university hospital in Belgium. All nursing wards (N = 27) that used NMWs on a daily basis were eligible for inclusion and were selected at random. There were no exclusion criteria. Sixteen surgical and medical wards were randomly selected using a randomization tool [17] and received an email informing them that a hospital employee would be making an inventory of the NMW’s content. This approach was intended to prevent users from altering their usual cleaning or disinfection practices, thereby capturing a more accurate reflection of daily behaviour and real-life conditions.

Each ward had five to eight actively used NMWs out of which maximum two NMWs were randomly selected by the researcher.

Data collection

Samples were taken at three locations (Figure 1). Locations ‘1’, ‘2’, and ‘3’ were included in this study because they were identified as frequently touched surfaces in an earlier observational study in the same hospital and were considered to be potentially crucial in terms of housing pathogens and transmitting these pathogens [10]. Location ‘1’ is the middle of the working surface which is used to prepare medication, administer wound care, write during briefings, etc. Location ‘2’ is a frequently touched handle used by healthcare professionals to move the NMW; it consists out of two different materials. Location ‘3’ is the handle of the most frequently used drawer of the NMW, used for patient-specific medication.Figure 1Locations of samples on the NMW – A picture of a nursing mobile workstation (NMW) with arrows pointing to the locations where the samples were taken.

The samples were taken by one researcher who was trained by an experienced laboratory technician. Replicate Organism Detection and Counting (RODAC) plates were used to sample the flat surfaces (locations 1 and 2) with a standardized weight of 500 g and a contact time of 10 s and immediately incubated at 35°C during 48 h [18,19]. Flocked swabs (eSwab, Copan®) were used to sample the handle on the drawer of the NMW (location 3) due to its uneven surface [[20], [21], [22]]. The samples were taken by swabbing the top and bottom of the entire length of the handle. The swabs were labelled and taken to the laboratory where they were vortexed for ten seconds after which 100 μL of the swab medium was inoculated with the 4-quadrant streak method on a blood agar plate (BAP) (BD). The BAP was also incubated at 35°C during 48 h. Subsequently, the number of colony-forming unit (cfu) was counted, and identification of all colony types with a different morphological aspect was done using matrix-assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker®). If species-level identification was unreliable (score < 2.0), identification was limited to the genus level. Micro-organisms that could not be identified within the main library of the MALDI-TOF MS were automatically send to a separate library that helped to identify these micro-organisms [23].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed on pathogenic bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, Enterobacterales, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method (ADAGIO Bio-Rad) to detect multi-drug-resistant micro-organisms (MDROs) and the EUCAST (The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing, © EUCAST 2026, Aeschenvorstadt 55, 4051 Basel, Switzerland) breakpoint tables for the interpretation [24].

The number of isolated patients and the responsible pathogens, such as meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE), or others, were also collected.

Thirty NMWs were tested during this study. Twenty NMWs were tested once during an early shift to collect data on the overall cleanliness of the three previously selected surfaces on the NMWs (cross-sectional data). Ten NMWs were tested at three points in time, 8–11 am; 3–4 pm; and 8–10 pm, to collect information on the cleanliness of the NMWs during the day covering several shifts (longitudinal data). The early-shift team uses the NMWs the most to administer direct patient care [10]. Shift change takes place between 2.15 and 2.45 pm.

There is a general procedure within the hospital to clean all high-touch surfaces at least daily. There is no specific procedure on disinfecting the NMWs. The procedure for high-touch surfaces at the point of care is using disinfectant wipes with cationic biocides (Clinell Universal Wipes®). The nurses, doctors, and cleaning and logistics personnel are each responsible for the cleaning and disinfecting of a number of surfaces, depending on infection risk. During a previous study on the NMWs in the same hospital, nurses conveyed that they disinfect the NMW at the end and/or at the beginning of their shift [10].

The number of NMWs that were tested once or three times depended on the capacity of the laboratory and the researcher who had to process all samples. A total of 150 samples (three samples per NMW) were collected between 16 October 2023 and 27 October 2023.

Results

Identification of the micro-organisms found on the NMW

In total, 150 samples were taken from thirty randomly selected NMWs on sixteen nursing wards. A total of 327 micro-organisms were identified of which Staphylococcus epidermidis (N = 47), Micrococcus luteus (N = 46), and Staphylococcus hominis (N = 36) were the most frequently identified micro-organisms. Twenty-one micro-organisms were classified as potential pathogens by an experienced laboratory technician, based on their likelihood of occurring in the normal flora of healthy individuals and the potential risk of them being resistant to antibiotics and therefore posing a risk to patients [27]: S. aureus (N = 5), Aspergillus fumigatus (N = 4), Aspergillus niger (N = 2), Staphylococcus lugdunensis (N = 2), Stenotrophomonas sp. (N = 1), Acinetobacter johnsonii (N = 1), Aspergillus brasiliensis (N = 1), Enterococcus faecium (N = 1), Pseudomonas sp. (N = 2), Pseudomonas monteilii (N = 1), and Pseudomonas stutzeri (N = 1). None of the potential pathogens were resistant to antibiotics. A full overview of all identified micro-organisms can be found in the appendix of this article.

Sixteen patients were in isolation in the included nursing wards while the samples of the NMWs were taken (Table III). The pathogens detected on the NMWs did not correspond with the pathogens for which patients in the wards were isolated. Hygiene classification of NMWs based on total colony count

A total of 150 samples were taken from the NMWs. The samples were categorized using the predetermined classification: the number of cfu counted on BAPs was categorized as low, +, ++, or +++ (Table I). For surfaces sampled using RODAC plates, hygiene classification was categorized as excellent (0–3 CFU/25 cm2), good (4–9 cfu/25 cm2), sufficient (10–29 cfu/25 cm2), insufficient (30–90 cfu/25 cm2), or poor (>90 cfu/25 cm2) (Table II).

A total of 28 samples (19%) were labelled ‘excellent’ in terms of the degree of microbiological decontamination, 19 samples (12%) were labelled ‘good’, 77 samples (51%) were labelled ‘sufficient’ or had less than 30 colonies in the first zone of the BAPs, 22 samples (15%) were labelled ‘insufficient’, and 4 samples (3%) were labelled ‘poor’ with more than 150 cfu/25 cm2.

Degree of microbiological contamination of frequently touched surfaces of the NMW

Three frequently touched surfaces on the NMW were identified and sampled across 30 randomly selected NMWs in sixteen surgical and medical wards, resulting in a total of 50 samples per surface.

At location ‘1’, the working surface (Figure 1), 34 samples (68%) were classified as ‘excellent’ or ‘good’, 15 samples (30%) as ‘sufficient’, and one sample (2%) as ‘insufficient’ or ‘poor’. Potential pathogens Ac. johnsonii and As. fumigatus were found on the working surfaces of two NMWs.

Of the second location, the side of the NMW (2 on Figure 1), 13 samples (26%) was classified as ‘excellent’ or ‘good’, 15 samples (30%) as ‘sufficient’, and 22 samples (44%) as ‘insufficient’ or ‘poor’. S. aureus (in 3 samples), E. faecium, As. fumigatus (in 3 samples), Stenotrophomonas sp., As. niger, As. brasiliensis, P. stutzeri, and Pseudomonas sp. were found on eleven NMWs.

Of the third location, the drawer of the NMW (3 on Figure 1), 47 samples (94%) had a ‘low’ total bacterial count and three samples (6%) scored as ‘+’, i.e. more than 30 colonies up to dense growth in the first inoculation zone of the BAP. Potential pathogens S. lugdunensis (in two samples), S. aureus (MSSA, in two samples), As. niger, P. monteilii, and Pseudomonas sp. were found on the drawer of six NMWs.

Discussion

This study explored microbial contamination on NMW to assess their potential role in pathogen transmission within hospital environments. Due to the workstation’s exposure to multiple users, its continuous and varied use for different tasks, and its frequent movement across nursing wards, with occasional entry into patient rooms, it is considered that NMWs are high-touch surfaces with multiple components that may contribute to the transmission of pathogens [10,15].

While the majority of samples (82%) were rated as ‘excellent’, ‘good’, or ‘sufficient’ in terms of the degree of microbiological decontamination on the 150 samples, 18% were classified as ‘poor’ or ‘insufficient’. This variability in hygiene levels highlights gaps in cleaning and disinfection practices that may stem from inconsistent protocols, insufficient training, or design challenges inherent to NMWs. Notably, the sides of the NMWs were more frequently contaminated than other surfaces, with 44% of samples classified as ‘insufficient’ or ‘poor’. This might suggest that these areas are harder to clean, are more often overlooked during routine disinfection, or are perceived as less critical by healthcare workers [11,13].

In an earlier observational study, nurses reported that they disinfect NMWs at the beginning and/or the end of their shift [10]. The researchers in this study therefore assumed that contamination levels would be lowest during mid-afternoon, right after the shift change. However, this study found no association between contamination levels and specific times of the day. It is possible that at the high frequency with which the NMW is used, the disinfecting/cleaning of the NMW leaves no residual effect. Additionally, Vanmeenen et al. revealed a wide variety of disinfection practices among nurses, including differences in cleaning frequency and identification of high-touch surfaces. Interviews with 16 nurses reinforced this variability, highlighting the lack of standardization in cleaning practices [10]. Compounding this issue, no specific protocol for NMW cleaning could be identified within the hospital’s quality guidelines. In line with Van Der Schoor et al., our findings emphasize the need for standardized, evidence-based guidelines to reduce variability and improve cleaning practices [28]. Nurses should be informed on frequently touched surfaces such as the sides of the NMWs and should be encouraged to disinfect the NMWs bearing these specific points in mind.

The micro-organisms found on the NMWs were predominantly skin flora from users, environmental bacteria such as Stenotrophomonas and Acinetobacter spp. and fungi likely introduced through airborne sedimentation. While these micro-organisms pose limited risk under normal circumstances, they can cause serious infections in immunocompromised patients [16]. Fungal transmission primarily occurs through the air rather than contact, minimizing the likelihood of the NMW acting as a direct vector. Although potential pathogens that are considered main pathogens of HAIs in the hospital environment such as S. aureus and P. aeruginosa were identified [16]. None of the identified pathogens were resistant to antibiotics. Furthermore, no direct link could be established between the micro-organisms detected on NMWs and those for which patients were in contact isolation. An explanation could be that the NMWs are not routinely taken into patient rooms when patients are in isolation.

Comparing the results of this study with the international literature is challenging as no other studies specifically examining the degree of contamination on NMWs were identified. Research on frequently touched surfaces of other hospital equipment often uses differing methodologies, including variations in techniques of sampling surfaces, contamination thresholds, and definitions of high-touch areas. This lack of standardization hinders direct comparisons and underscores the need for uniform approaches in assessing hospital surface contamination [28]. Developing standardized protocols would not only facilitate comparisons across studies but also support infection prevention teams in implementing more effective surveillance and cleaning strategies. By addressing these variations in methodologies, future research could better assess the role of hospital equipment, including NMWs, in nosocomial pathogen transmission.

The HACCP classification, developed for the interpretation of surface sampling in the food industry, was used during this study for the interpretation of the samples [21,25,26]. One other standard for contamination thresholds could be found. The standard of <5 cfu/cm2 has been suggested for the hospital setting but has not been universally accepted [19,23,25]. If we used this standard in our study, then samples with >125 cfu/25 cm2 would be considered acceptable, meaning only 3 samples instead of 23 samples in our results would represent a surface of insufficient cleanliness (see appendix for more information on the cfu results of each sample/surface). However, there are clear differences between the food industry and the hospital setting [27]. Considerations were made regarding the strict cut-off values used in this study. It was decided that, given the contact that the patient occasionally appears to have with materials, documents, medications, or food/drinks stored on the NMW, it would be more prudent to opt for the stricter interpretation of these samples [10].

Although the NMW does not come into direct contact with the patient, it does interact with the hands of healthcare providers. Consequently, contamination of these surfaces primarily poses a risk when hand hygiene compliance is suboptimal. While this study did not include direct measurements of hand hygiene compliance on the selected wards, the hospital’s infection control team reports that, on average, 94% of professionals comply with visible hygiene standards – such as the absence of bracelets, rings, watches, long sleeves, nail polish, and artificial nails. However, compliance during the ‘five moments for hand hygiene’ is reported to be below 50% (personal communication).

Apart from the need for higher compliance to hand hygiene protocols, other measures might also reduce the risk of contamination via the NMWs. For instance, creating a guideline that defines which surfaces are frequently touched surfaces, that informs healthcare workers on how to clean and disinfect these surfaces (frequency, products) and that establishes criteria that a frequently touched surface must meet in order to be used safely in the vicinity of the patient. Assadian et al. wrote about the importance of good management, adequate staffing, equipment, training, supervision, and communication to enable effective cleaning and disinfection in hospitals [2]. Detailed protocols, repeated training regarding periodic cleaning and disinfecting of the NMWs for healthcare workers and maintenance personnel (both cleaning and logistics personnel); a revised design of the NMW that eliminates raised edges, borders, corners, and small compartments to facilitate the cleaning process; and supervision could provide support in this regard. Head nurses and infection prevention teams should supervise and monitor the maintenance of the NMWs to make sure the task is done qualitatively and does not get pushed aside or forgotten. Hand hygiene before patient contact obviously remains an important strategy to reduce the risk of transmitting pathogens to patients.

This study has several strengths. First, the use of blinded observations minimized the potential for bias by ensuring that healthcare workers were unaware of the study’s purpose, thus capturing routine cleaning and disinfection behaviours. Second, the study used random sampling of the nursing wards and nursing mobile workstations, improving reliability and generalizability of the outcomes. Third, one identical procedure of sampling surfaces was used across multiple wards and time points, providing a broader perspective on contamination levels and attempting to mitigate the limitations of a single-point microbiological snapshot.

There are limitations to consider. As with any surface sampling method, swabbing captures only a fraction of the microbial population present and is limited to culturable organisms, which may affect representativeness. Microbiological sampling provides only a snapshot of contamination at a specific moment, which may not reflect long-term trends or fluctuations. Furthermore, blinding the users of the NMWs entailed we could not document the cleaning activities prior to the sampling. Although we sought to counteract this limitation by taking samples at various times throughout the day, the results should still be interpreted with caution. No neutralizing agent was added during the processing of the samples. This may result in false-negative samples, meaning that the results of this study paint a more positive picture of the hygiene of the NMW than is actually the case. Finally, the absence of a standardized methodology for assessing contamination on hospital surfaces limits comparability with other studies and underscores the need for developing uniform protocols in this field.

The potential impact of residual COVID-19-related infection prevention measures on the findings of this study was considered. However, a previous observational study (November 2021–April 2022) did not indicate increased or excessive cleaning of NMWs [10]. In addition, hospital-wide consumption data of alcohol-based hand rub showed that usage in 2023 had returned to pre-COVID levels (approximately 50 L/1000 patient-days), suggesting that overall hand hygiene practices were no longer elevated compared with baseline. Taken together, these findings do not support the assumption that enhanced COVID-19-related cleaning or disinfection practices significantly influenced the microbiological contamination levels observed in this study.

The overall degree of contamination of the NMW was reassuring, with the majority of surfaces demonstrating acceptable hygiene levels. However, attention must be directed towards the consistent cleaning and disinfection of frequently touched surfaces, particularly those with higher contamination risks, such as the sides and handles. Implementing standardized protocols that clearly define high-touch surfaces and appropriate cleaning and disinfection methods is essential to minimize variability in practice. Redesigning NMWs to eliminate hard-to-clean features, such as raised edges and small compartments, could further support effective cleaning and disinfection. The frequent interaction between the NMW and healthcare workers’ hands highlights the critical importance of hand hygiene as a complementary strategy to reduce the risk of pathogen transmission.

Ethics statement

In order to collect samples that represented the true condition of the nursing mobile workstations (NMWs), users of the NMWs were blinded to the purpose of the study. Nurses were informed that a hospital employee would be making an inventory of the NMW’s content. This approach was intended to prevent users from altering their usual cleaning or disinfection practices, thereby capturing a more accurate reflection of daily behaviour and real-life conditions. To obtain information on the number of isolated patients and their associated pathogens, data were requested from the hospital’s infection control department, ensuring that no patient records were accessed. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Ghent University Hospital (ONZ-2023-0348).
