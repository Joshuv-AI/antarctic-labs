A novel exponential tension field for salt-and-pepper image denoising
Authors: Seyed Mehdi Kazemi Torbaghan, Amin Jajarmi, Yaser Jouybari Moghaddam, Seyed Farhad Kazemian Torbaghan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Salt-and-pepper noise is a particularly challenging image degradation model because it corrupts pixels sparsely but severely, often disrupting edges, textures, and fine structural details. This paper presents the exponential tension field (ETF) as a geometrically grounded method for removing impulse noise. ETF is formulated from an exponential gradient-based functional and implemented as a nonlinear, gradient-dependent diffusion operator that promotes smoothing in corrupted homogeneous regions while limiting diffusion across significant intensity transitions. The proposed method is discretized at the pixel level using a conservative finite-volume scheme and evaluated on standard grayscale benchmark images degraded by salt-and-pepper noise at various levels. We assess performance using PSNR, SSIM, precision, recall, F-measure, VIF, FOM, and MAE, along with runtime analysis and statistical testing. ETF is compared to representative classical, variational, and transform-based denoisers. Benchmark results show that ETF excels at restoring images, particularly under high-noise conditions, while preserving edges and fine image structures. The method does not require training, is computationally efficient, and has a clear variational interpretation. The findings suggest that ETF is a viable and comprehensible geometry-based method for denoising salt-and-pepper images in the examined context, prompting further investigation into similar nonlinear diffusion operators for impulse-noise image restoration.

Introduction

Image denoising is a fundamental challenge in image processing, as noise degrades image quality and undermines the reliability of tasks such as edge detection, segmentation, registration, recognition, medical interpretation, industrial inspection, and remote sensing analysis1–3. In real-world imaging systems, noise can come from sensor issues, digitization errors, transmission errors, poor lighting, or unstable hardware. Salt-and-pepper noise is a very common type of degradation. It appears as sparse but high-value impulses that disrupt local intensity patterns and significantly impact edges, textures, and fine structural details. This problem is also important for consumer imaging devices such as smartphones and digital cameras, which require strong and efficient enhancement even when hardware resources are limited4.

There are many ways to remove noise, such as classical filters, PDE-based models, transform-domain methods, and learning-based approaches5. Some of the most common classical methods are median filtering, Gaussian filtering, and Wiener filtering. They are easy to use and work well, but they often have to choose between reducing noise and preserving details, especially when impulse corruption is strong. This limitation led to the development of nonlinear and PDE-based models, such as anisotropic diffusion and its regularized variants, which improve structural adaptability and continue to impact edge-preserving restoration. Analogous extensions have been investigated for mixed-noise environments6. At the same time, transform-domain methods, such as wavelet-based and multiscale methods, are similarly effective in different restoration situations7. Recently, learning-based methods have greatly improved image restoration. These include GAN-based frameworks and powerful convolutional and transformer-based denoising models8–10.

Even with these improvements, removing salt-and-pepper noise remains difficult when impulse suppression, edge preservation, interpretability, and computational efficiency are required simultaneously. Classical impulse-noise filters might oversmooth thin structures at high noise levels, and learning-based methods often require substantial training data and are computationally expensive. PDE-based and geometry-inspired models are frequently more interpretable; however, many have not been explicitly examined for fixed-value impulse corruption or require meticulous tuning to reconcile smoothing in homogeneous areas with the preservation of significant transitions11,12. Consequently, there remains a necessity for denoising models that are structurally adaptive, analytically transparent, and practically implementable for salt-and-pepper noise.

This paper explores the exponential tension field (ETF) as a geometrically based method for removing salt-and-pepper noise from images, motivated by this gap. To our knowledge, ETF has not been systematically developed and assessed as an impulse-noise denoising operator in image restoration before this study. The main idea is to create a nonlinear, gradient-dependent diffusion process from an exponential gradient-based functional. This will provide a restoration mechanism that is easy to understand, encourages smoothing in areas that are mostly the same, and limits diffusion across important changes in the image. In the current formulation, ETF operates as a regularization-driven diffusion model rather than an explicit impulse detector. This is why it is being considered here as a geometry-based denoising operator that has performed well on salt-and-pepper noise in benchmark testing. Related work

Researchers have examined many methods for removing salt-and-pepper noise. Some of these are impulse-aware spatial filtering, variational and PDE-based restoration, transform-domain denoising, and data-driven learning-based methods. Each family has a different balance between preserving edges, suppressing impulses, making things easier to understand, and being fast to compute. Related restoration problems also include structured artifact removal (e.g., highlight removal), which shares similar goals of suppressing undesired components while preserving geometry15.

In the beginning, local spatial filters were the most popular technique for eliminating impulse noise. Gaussian and Wiener filtering are examples of linear methods that are easy to compute and work well for Gaussian-like perturbations. However, they do not work well for fixed-valued impulse corruption and often blur edges16. Nonlinear rank-based filters, especially the median filter, have become common tools for removing salt-and-pepper noise because they can remove outliers without affecting edges as much as linear smoothing does17. This family was later expanded to include adaptive and decision-based versions, such as the adaptive median filter18, decision-based methods, and impulse detectors19,20. These methods still work well at low to moderate levels of corruption, but they do not perform as well at high levels, when local neighborhoods may lack sufficient reliable pixels. As a result, their restoration capability tends to degrade when the proportion of corrupted samples becomes too large for local rank statistics to remain informative. This limitation motivated the development of more structurally adaptive frameworks, including variational, PDE-based, and geometry-inspired denoising models.

Among model-based approaches, variational and PDE-based formulations are the most similar to this work. The anisotropic diffusion model by Perona and Malik21 introduced the essential principle of gradient-dependent conductivity, enabling smoothing in uniform regions while reducing diffusion across distinct edges. Later models for geometric and diffusion-based restoration were greatly influenced by this framework. Variational regularization techniques, especially total variation and related TV-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_1$$\end{document} models, improved edge-preserving denoising, although they may introduce staircase artifacts in smooth regions22–24. Recent studies have extended diffusion-based restoration to complex scenarios, including mixed-noise settings6,25 and modified Perona-Malik-type models rooted in directional Laplacian structures26,27. These studies confirm the enduring importance of diffusion-based restoration in situations that require structural adaptability and comprehensibility.

In the current study, the most pertinent distinction is that Perona-Malik-type models are generally initialized with a selected conductivity function, whereas TV-based methods achieve regularization via gradient sparsity. In contrast, this work derives ETF from an exponential gradient-based energy, which provides the resulting nonlinear diffusion operator with a direct variational motivation. The contribution of this paper is not merely to introduce another edge-sensitive smoother, but to examine the practical efficacy of this specific geometrically derived diffusion mechanism for fixed-value impulse denoising. Furthermore, in contrast to numerous impulse-specific techniques that depend on explicit corruption detection or switching strategies, ETF is examined here as a solely geometry-driven diffusion operator, rendering empirical validation particularly crucial.

Another important paradigm is transform-domain denoising. Wavelet-based methods use multiscale representations to separate signal and noise in transform space. They have long been used to restore images28. Transform-based representations are also widely used across broader image processing problems beyond denoising (e.g., transform-driven feature embedding and information hiding)29–31. Patch-based and transform-based methods like BM3D32 and non-local means33 work very well for Gaussian noise, but their basic assumptions do not work as well for fixed-valued impulse corruption unless they are used with special preprocessing or impulse-aware changes7.

Deep-learning techniques have recently gained prominence in image restoration. Data-driven methodologies exhibit significant potential in practical applications, as demonstrated by convolutional residual networks such as DnCNN34, plug-and-play deep denoiser priors35, GAN-based denoising frameworks7, and transformer-based restoration models9. However, these kinds of models usually require substantial training data, substantial computing power, and careful management of the mismatch between the training and test sets. These considerations are particularly pertinent in the constrained data context addressed in this paper, where only a minimal training subset is accessible for each experimental iteration. In these situations, explicitly modeled restoration operators remain appealing because they do not require training, are faster to compute, and are easier to understand than systems that rely heavily on data. Moreover, Their behavior can frequently be elucidated by diffusion, regularization, or variational concepts. This elucidates the relationship between model design and repair results. This interpretability is especially valuable for impulse-noise removal, where preserving true edges while suppressing isolated outliers requires a controlled and analyzable smoothing mechanism. For this reason, geometry-based and PDE-inspired methods continue to offer a relevant alternative to deep models in settings where transparency, stability, and limited-data robustness are of primary importance.

Between classical model-based filters and large, supervised neural architectures, lightweight, single-image learning-based denoisers form an important intermediate category. Methods such as Deep Image Prior and self-supervised denoising exploit image-specific or corruption-specific structure without requiring a large paired training set, while recent lightweight learning-based models seek to reduce parameter count and training cost for practical denoising applications36–38. These methods are important because they show that learning-based denoising does not necessarily require large-scale supervised training. At the same time, their fair evaluation usually requires a different protocol involving pretrained weights, single-image optimization, masking strategies, or task-specific adaptation. Accordingly, the present study does not claim superiority over this class of learning-based denoisers. Instead, it positions ETF as a training-free geometric alternative whose behavior is transparent, numerically controlled, and directly reproducible under the fixed-value impulse-noise benchmark considered here.

The literature consistently identifies a gap among four essential attributes: effective impulse suppression, maintenance of edges and fine structures, theoretical interpretability, and computational efficiency independent of extensive training. Classical impulse filters are often effective but very localized and often heuristic. Transform-domain methods are powerful but usually work better with Gaussian-like noise. Deep-learning methods can be highly accurate but require substantial data and computational resources. Geometry-inspired diffusion operators are still not well known for fixed-value impulse denoising. This work occupies the existing gap. The ETF operator, initially introduced in the theory of exponentially harmonic maps by Eells and Lemaire39 and subsequently examined in geometric analysis13,40, seems not to have been previously explored as a model for salt-and-pepper image denoising. Its significance in image restoration stems from the associated exponential energy, which generates a nonlinear, gradient-dependent diffusion mechanism inherently responsive to local image geometry. The main question, then, is whether ETF can find a useful balance between suppressing impulses, preserving structure, making things easier to understand, and being more efficient than other denoising methods. In this sense, the proposed framework is not merely a new operator choice, but an attempt to transfer a theoretically grounded concept from geometric analysis into a practically testable restoration setting. The present study therefore investigates whether this geometric formulation can serve as a viable denoising model under realistic benchmark conditions and across multiple levels of impulse corruption.

Overall, the current literature shows that image denoising has advanced significantly across many areas, but no single set of methods can fully solve this problem. This gap is what led to the ETF framework proposed in this paper.

Proposed approach

This section develops the proposed ETF denoising approach in a form that is both mathematically transparent and directly implementable on a pixel grid. We begin by formulating ETF regularization as the evolution induced by an exponential, gradient-dependent energy, from which the Euler-Lagrange operator and the associated nonlinear diffusion flow arise naturally under homogeneous Neumann (no-flux) boundary conditions. We then formalize the continuum-discrete correspondence relevant to digital images by viewing the data as samples on a Cartesian lattice and replacing differential operators with consistent finite differences. This perspective makes the edge-sensitive nature of the model explicit and motivates the numerical choices adopted later, including conservative flux discretization, stability constraints, and stopping criteria.

Mathematical foundations of the ETF regularization

Removing noise is not an end in itself; it is a necessary step for obtaining reliable information from images. Denoising makes downstream processes such as edge detection, segmentation, feature extraction and matching, optical flow, registration, and quantitative measurements more stable by raising the effective signal-to-noise ratio. In this case, ETF regularization is meant to smooth out random changes while keeping the geometric structure of intensity transitions that mark the edges of objects. We implement ETF regularization using an exponential functional of the image gradient. The first variation of this functional gives the Euler-Lagrange operator and the nonlinear diffusion flow that goes with it. The resulting exponential conductivity yields a transport law that varies with contrast: diffusion works well in regions with little variation but slows near large gradients. Because digital images are discrete, we also make clear the continuum-discrete interpretation on which the numerical scheme is based. The observed image is treated as a sample of a continuum field, and derivatives and integrals are approximated by finite differences and weighted sums. Remark 3.2

(Why gradient-dependent diffusion can attenuate impulse noise) Isolated pixels with extreme values are replaced by salt-and-pepper corruption, which produces noticeable local discontinuities in their surrounding neighborhood. It is impossible for a merely local conductivity to accurately distinguish between a real edge and an impulsive outlier. In many real-world scenarios, the repeated diffusion technique can still minimize isolated outliers while preserving the image borders. Repeated local updates reduce the disparity between corrupted pixels and their neighbors in largely identical areas. This increases conductivity over time and facilitates further smoothing. Conversely, real edges are spatially coherent transitions that remain constant throughout iterations. Cross-edge diffusion is therefore still comparatively weak. Although this approach delivers an edge-sensitive diffusion behavior that is nevertheless useful for salt-and-pepper denoising in practice, it does not explicitly detect impulses.

To quantify edge sensitivity more precisely, it is beneficial to decompose the diffusion into components that are tangent and normal to level sets. This decomposition not only provides interpretation but also elucidates the necessity of meticulous numerical treatment; specifically, the loss of uniform parabolicity for sufficiently large gradients requires the adoption of a conservative discretization and a stable time-step selection within the numerical scheme.

Remark 3.3

(Comparison with classical anisotropic diffusion) The Perona-Malik model21 illustrates traditional anisotropic diffusion. It utilizes a scalar conductivity g(|∇ I|) to adjust the diffusion rate based on the local gradient magnitude. The proposed ETF formulation employs an exponential conductivity derived from the exponential function in Eq. (1), thus providing a clearer variational interpretation of the subsequent nonlinear diffusion process. Although both strategies are edge-sensitive and gradient-dependent, the ETF framework is presented here as an energy-based restoration operator specifically designed for salt-and-pepper impulse denoising. Convergence rate and error bounds. A thorough convergence analysis for nonlinear, degenerate diffusion equations is difficult, even for standard Perona-Malik-type models, as existence and uniqueness results often require weak solution frameworks42. The determination of quantitative convergence rates or a priori error bounds for ETFs in the presence of impulsive noise falls outside the purview of this study and is recognized as a significant domain for further theoretical investigation. This study focuses on identifying the structural characteristics of continuous and discrete models, encompassing stability-related operator behavior, conservation, and functional monotonicity. These properties, when combined, provide a strong analytical foundation for numerical implementation and experimental evaluation.

Taken together, the variational derivation and the directional diffusion characterization, and the conservation and monotonicity results provide a coherent theoretical basis for ETF denoising. The Euler-Lagrange operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau _{\exp }$$\end{document} defines a contrast-modulated flux that smooths primarily within relatively homogeneous regions while attenuating transport across strong transitions. Mean preservation supports brightness neutrality at the continuum level, while the monotonicity of the exponential functional provides an interpretable diagnostic for the evolution. These properties do not constitute a proof of impulse-specific optimality, but they do justify ETF as a mathematically grounded and practically implementable geometry-driven restoration model.

Numerical discretization

The continuous ETF model is transformed into an algorithm that can be executed on the pixel grid in this part. The discretization is intended to minimize grid-induced directional bias, maintain the conservative structure of the continuous operator, and facilitate the identification of a precise stability condition for explicit time integration. To this end, we employ a discretization strategy that preserves the principal geometric features of the continuous formulation while remaining computationally efficient on regular image lattices. This design also enables a consistent treatment of boundary effects and supports reproducible implementation across different benchmark instances. In order to guarantee that the iterates are acceptable and that quantization artifacts do not arise during evaluation, we additionally describe the numerical precision and dynamic range needed for 8-bit images. We go over how to determine the benchmark setup through experimentation and how to select hyperparameters later in the experimental portion. Instead of using an adaptively recalculated step at each iteration, the approach reported in the benchmark uses a fixed time step chosen from an empirically proven CFL-compatible range.

Flux-based finite-volume discretization of the ETF flow

The ETF evolution equation on the pixel grid is broken up using a cautious finite-volume method. The three design objectives are: (i) maintaining the continuous operator’s flux-divergence structure, which gives it discrete conservation properties; (ii) creating nonnegative interface weights so that explicit time stepping permits a discrete maximum principle under a CFL-type restriction; and (iii) reducing grid-induced anisotropy by measuring conductivities at cell interfaces and employing cross-interface averaging for tangential gradient components. Numerical precision, dynamic-range projection, and 8-bit serialization

Instead of using quantized integers, all ETF calculations use floating-point arithmetic. This choice is important because repeatedly rounding integers would cause cumulative quantization error that could overwhelm small diffusion updates and alter the evolution. However, since the imaging model assumes a limited 8-bit dynamic range, intermediate iterations are sent back to the acceptable intensity range after each update. This projection is a useful numerical safety net, not a part of the underlying continuous model.

Method pipeline

This part provides a concise summary of the operational ETF denoising methodology employed in all reported tests. The objective is to articulate the method succinctly and algorithmically, augmenting the previously published mathematical and numerical formulations. Throughout the benchmark, the ETF is consistently utilized within the same fixed iterative pipeline.

Pipeline formulation. The ETF denoising system employs an explicit time discretization of a nonlinear diffusion equation, wherein the flux is influenced by an exponential, gradient-dependent conductivity. Within this paradigm, diffusion is facilitated in relatively uniform areas and gradually restricted near substantial intensity shifts, thus aiding in impulse-noise reduction while maintaining major image structures. The numerical implementation employs a conservative finite-volume discretization utilizing face-centered conductivities assessed on a staggered grid. The pipeline can handle 8-bit grayscale photos with fixed-value salt-and-pepper impulse noise. All calculations are done in floating-point format, with values between 0 and 255. We use forward Euler integration with a fixed time step that is in a stable range that works with CFL. We use dynamic-range projection after each iteration to keep the intensities within acceptable limits. Once a set number of iterations have been completed, the last floating-point iterate is used to evaluate the metric, and an 8-bit quantized version is created for storage and display. This separation between floating-point processing and the final quantized output helps keep the quantization error from building up during the iterative updates. It also makes it easier to reproduce results by making sure that restoration quality is measured on the numerically stabilized solution instead of a serialization-dependent representation.

Operational sequence For n=0,… ,T-1, the ETF pipeline executes the following stages: (i)Boundary handling and gradient estimation Symmetric reflection at the image boundary establishes homogeneous Neumann boundary conditions. Subsequently, cross-interface averaging is employed to determine the face-centered normal and tangential gradient components on a staggered grid.(ii)Conductivity and flux assembly Employing the exponential conductivity law, one can derive non-negative face conductivities from the size of the local face gradient. Subsequently, conservative horizontal and vertical fluxes are combined to formulate the discrete divergence-form diffusion operator. This formulation preserves the conservative structure of the underlying PDE and promotes stable, edge-aware diffusion on the discrete pixel grid.(iii)Explicit update and projection A forward Euler step is done with the chosen time increment, and then pointwise clipping is done to the intensity range [0, 255].(iv)Termination After the required number of iterations, the process stops. The iteration count is fixed in the benchmark that was reported, and there is no adaptive stopping criterion. This provides a controlled and reproducible evaluation setting in which performance differences can be attributed directly to the restoration model rather than to image-specific stopping behavior.Benchmark configuration All ETF outcomes in this study employ the fixed parameters T=3, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{d}t=0.23$$\end{document}, and κ =25.0, unless specified differently. The measured runtime for this configuration is approximately 1.70 ms per 256× 256 image on a Tesla T4 GPU. This parameter setting was selected because it provided the most favorable balance between restoration quality, numerical stability, and computational efficiency across the evaluated noise densities. Its consistent use throughout the benchmark also helps make a fair and fully reproducible comparison with all other methods.

Quantitative results and discussion

Dataset and preprocessing The primary benchmark uses the Kaggle distribution of the DIV2K dataset, comprising the train split (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{train}}=800$$\end{document}) and the official validation split (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\textrm{val}}=100$$\end{document}). All images are converted to grayscale using cv2.IMREAD_GRAYSCALE and resized to 256× 256 by bilinear interpolation (cv2.resize, INTER_LINEAR). Noise injection, restoration, and evaluation are performed exclusively on these resized grayscale images. Unless stated otherwise, outputs are clipped to the valid intensity interval and evaluated in floating point prior to final 8-bit serialization.

Scope of the benchmark This grayscale, fixed-resolution configuration deliberately constrains the inferences that can be made. Initially, DIV2K constituted a high-resolution RGB dataset. Transforming it to grayscale at 256× 256 simplifies the restoration challenge compared to full-resolution color denoising. The diminished spatial resolution and absence of chromatic data facilitate the recovery of intricate features and perhaps simplify real-world image restoration endeavors. This controlled environment serves mostly as a benchmark for method comparison, rather than a comprehensive substitute for actual noise reduction scenarios. The conclusions of this subsection are confined solely to the grayscale, fixed-resolution, and limited-data situation analyzed herein. Subsequently, we will discuss methods for conducting comprehensive validation on native high-resolution RGB images and various other imaging modalities.

Dataset completeness No images from DIV2K, Set12, or BSD68 were excluded or given preferential treatment. No cropping, padding, or selective omission occurred in the preparation pipeline for all 100 DIV2K validation images, all 12 Set12 images, and all 68 BSD68 images.

Evaluation protocol Employing a deterministic shuffle with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$BASE\_SEED=123$$\end{document}, the 100 DIV2K validation images are divided into 10 distinct folds, each containing 10 images. In each of the R=10 iterations, the r-th iteration employs (i) 40 images selected from the training set without replacement for hyperparameter optimization and limited-data baseline training, and (ii) the r-th validation fold for evaluation. Each validation image undergoes testing precisely once across the 10 repeats, resulting in 100 observations per image for subsequent statistical analysis.

Protocol rationale This protocol strikes a balance between being able to run tests on the entire DIV2K validation set and being able to run tests on smaller subsets of the training data. It also avoids test leakage and checks the stability of the method across different training subsets. By changing the training subsets but keeping the evaluation framework the same, the protocol also implicitly tests how strong the chosen hyperparameters are and how consistent the comparative performance is. Also, using repeated subset-level assessments lowers the chance that the observed ranking is based on just one good partition of the data. A fixed-tuning verification experiment maintained the principal ranking trends, demonstrating that the reported results are not a byproduct of the repeated subset-selection methodology.

Reporting convention For each iteration, the metrics are averaged across the corresponding 10-image validation fold to obtain a mean for that iteration. The average and standard deviation of these R=10 repetition-level means are presented in Tables 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9. Statistical Validation of Performance

Testing framework. For every noise density, the most robust classical baseline is determined by the highest mean PSNR among the non-proposed methods: DBA is used when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \le 0.20$$\end{document}, and AMF is used when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \ge 0.60$$\end{document}. Using two different paired tests on the 100 per-image differences, ETF is then compared to this reference baseline: (i) a two-sided paired t-test and (ii) a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. To manage the family wise error rate across the seven assessed noise levels, the Holm–Bonferroni correction is implemented at a significance level of α =0.05. This comparison strategy is meant to give a focused benchmark-level reference instead of a full multi-method inferential ranking. So, it puts more value on being clear and easy to understand than on doing a full pairwise comparison of all the methods that are competing. This design is good for figuring out if ETF consistently does better than the best traditional reference in the benchmark.

Main inferential results. The inferential results show that ETF’s improvements are not limited to a few outstanding images. Instead, the gains that were seen in the benchmark are spread out over the validation set and get stronger as the impulse density goes up. Specifically, Cohen’s d effect size rises from a small effect at ρ =0.05 to a large effect at ρ =0.80, and the confidence intervals that go with it move farther and farther away from zero. These findings corroborate the interpretation that the advantage of ETF is minimal at extremely low noise levels but gains practical significance as the denoising task increases in difficulty.

Statistical significance versus practical relevance. At low noise densities, ETF already shows statistically significant advantages in PSNR and SSIM, but the absolute numerical improvements remain small. These gains should therefore be interpreted as evidence of consistency rather than as large practical jumps in restoration quality. This distinction is important because statistical significance can arise even when the visual or application-level impact is modest. Accordingly, the low-noise results are best viewed as confirming stable incremental improvement rather than dramatic superiority. At moderate and high noise densities, however, the joint evidence from corrected p-values, confidence intervals, and effect sizes indicates that the improvements are not only statistically significant but also practically more consequential within the evaluated benchmark.

Consistency across metrics. The inferential trends are broadly aligned across pixel-level and edge-level metrics. In particular, gains in PSNR and SSIM are accompanied by improvements in F-measure and MAE at most noise densities, suggesting that ETF improves both intensity fidelity and structural preservation rather than optimizing one at the expense of the other. Local metric-specific exceptions remain visible, but these do not alter the overall inferential pattern.

Robustness analyses. To assess the stability of the reported findings with respect to reasonable protocol variations, we performed three additional robustness checks: (i)Fixed-tuning protocol. Replacing the repeated hold-out tuning protocol with a single fixed training subset preserved the main ranking trends, with only small numerical changes in PSNR and SSIM.(ii)Alternative VIF handling. Replacing the conservative zero-mapping of undefined VIF values by an alternative convention preserved the main ranking trends of the evaluated methods.(iii)Edge-tolerance sensitivity. Allowing a one-pixel tolerance in edge-map matching increased all edge-based scores slightly, but preserved ETF’s advantage across the evaluated noise levels.Overall, the statistical analysis supports three benchmark-level conclusions: first, ETF outperforms the selected classical reference baseline consistently within the evaluated setting; second, the practical importance of this advantage increases with noise density; and third, the reported superiority remains stable under the tested robustness variations. These inferential results strengthen the empirical case for ETF beyond raw average metric values alone. They also indicate that the observed performance gains are both repeatable and quantitatively well supported within the benchmark design.

Table 15 shows that ETF is still very competitive in both the Set12 and BSD68 datasets, even when the parameters stay the same. This shows that ETF is very stable across all of the datasets that were tested. ETF gets the best PSNR and SSIM scores at all three noise levels tested on Set12. ETF has a PSNR of 35.5560 dB and an SSIM of 0.9881 at ρ =0.10, which is a little better than DBA’s PSNR of 35.4314 dB and SSIM of 0.9847. ETF is still in first place at ρ =0.20 with a PSNR of 31.5294 dB and an SSIM of 0.9638. DBA is in second place with a PSNR of 31.3518 dB and an SSIM of 0.9629. When ρ =0.80, the benefits of ETF become even clearer. ETF gets a PSNR of 20.8887 dB and an SSIM of 0.6896, which is a little better than AMF and a lot better than DBA and TV-L1.

A similar trend is observed on BSD68, where ETF attains the highest PSNR at all three noise densities and the highest SSIM at ρ =0.20 and ρ =0.80. At ρ =0.10, ETF records the best PSNR of 36.7728 dB, whereas DBA achieves a marginally higher SSIM of 0.9835 compared with 0.9821 for ETF. This suggests that, under light corruption on BSD68, both methods perform very similarly, with ETF offering slightly higher pixel-wise fidelity and DBA showing a marginal advantage in structural similarity. At ρ =0.20, ETF achieves the best overall result with 32.7593 dB and 0.9708, exceeding DBA by 32.7416 dB in PSNR and 0.9612 in SSIM. Under the most challenging setting of ρ =0.80, ETF again ranks first with 22.0056 dB and 0.6680, followed by AMF at 21.8345 dB and 0.6444, while DBA and TV-L1 remain substantially below ETF in both metrics.

The clearest strength of ETF appears at the highest noise density. On Set12, ETF improves upon DBA by 7.0269 dB in PSNR and 0.3380 in SSIM at ρ =0.80, while on BSD68 the corresponding gains reach 8.2981 dB and 0.3210, respectively. Even relative to AMF, which is the second-best method under this severe corruption regime, ETF still provides a measurable improvement on both datasets. These results indicate that ETF is particularly effective when the majority of pixels are corrupted and the recovery of the remaining structural information becomes the dominant challenge.

A possible explanation for this behavior is that the competing methods exhibit different strengths across noise regimes. In lightly corrupted images, local filtering-based approaches such as DBA can remain highly competitive because sufficient valid neighborhood information is still available for restoration. However, when the impulse density becomes extreme, restoration quality depends more critically on accurate corruption handling and stable structure-preserving regularization. In this setting, ETF appears to provide a favorable balance between suppressing impulse artifacts and preserving meaningful structural transitions through its conservative edge-sensitive diffusion mechanism. This phenomenon may explain why the ETF outperforms the benchmark in the presence of severe corruption.

The per-image results are broadly consistent with the dataset-level averages. In the low- and moderate-noise regimes, the performance gap between ETF and DBA is generally small and may vary slightly with image content, which is consistent with the narrow differences observed in the average scores. In contrast, under ρ =0.80, ETF shows a clearer and more stable advantage across the evaluated images, supporting the view that its benefit is not limited to the dataset average alone but is also reflected more consistently at the individual-image level.

The cross-dataset experiments predominantly support two principal conclusions. The ETF operates effectively and consistently across Set12 and BSD68 without requiring any parameter adjustments for each dataset. The advantage is most evident in instances of significant impulse corruption, as it effectively restores both datasets. The results demonstrate that the proposed framework offers significant cross-dataset robustness and is particularly efficient for the restoration of severe high-density impulse noise.

Limitations and scope discussion

In this subsection, we clarify the practical scope and principal limitations of the present study in order to frame the reported results appropriately. Specifically, we identify the image setting, noise model, benchmark coverage, numerical assumptions, and baseline selection under which ETF has been validated, and we indicate which extensions remain outside the scope of the current work. This discussion is intended to distinguish supported conclusions from open questions and to provide a transparent basis for interpreting the strength and generality of the empirical findings.

Image domain and resolution All primary experiments are conducted on grayscale 256× 256 images. Although the cross-dataset results on Set12 and BSD68 support the transfer of the main ranking trend beyond the primary DIV2K benchmark, the present study does not constitute a comprehensive evaluation on native-resolution color images or on specialized domains such as industrial inspection, remote sensing, or fluorescence microscopy. The conclusions should therefore be understood as applying to the grayscale, fixed-resolution setting investigated here.

Noise model The experimental study is restricted to fixed-value salt-and-pepper impulse noise. The proposed ETF formulation is specifically motivated by this corruption type, in which isolated extreme-valued outliers create strong local discontinuities that can be addressed by an edge-sensitive, gradient-dependent diffusion process. This design choice aligns the method with corruption patterns characterized by sparse but severe pixel-level contamination. As a result, its observed strengths in the present benchmark should be interpreted in relation to this specific restoration setting rather than as evidence of broad noise-model invariance. Accordingly, the present results should not be generalized to Gaussian, Poisson, random-valued impulse, or mixed-noise scenarios without further validation45.

Preliminary Gaussian-noise observations A small pilot study under additive white Gaussian noise indicated that ETF is not competitive with methods specifically designed for Gaussian denoising. This is consistent with the impulse-oriented motivation of the model and reinforces the interpretation of ETF as a specialized restoration method rather than a universal denoiser.

Preliminary RGB evaluation A limited pilot on color images suggested that ETF may remain useful under channel-wise processing, but that evidence is still preliminary. A principled color extension incorporating cross-channel structure has not yet been developed or benchmarked and remains a direction for future work. In particular, independent channel-wise filtering may fail to exploit inter-channel correlations that are important for faithful color restoration. A more rigorous study on native RGB benchmarks is therefore needed before drawing general conclusions about color-image performance.

Noise-density coverage The primary benchmark evaluates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho \in \{0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.60,0.70,0.80\}$$\end{document}. Intermediate noise densities in the range 0.30-0.50 are not included, so the transition from moderate to extreme corruption is not sampled densely. While the reported trends are monotone and consistent, a finer evaluation of the intermediate regime would strengthen the empirical characterization of ETF.

Numerical behavior and stability The ETF implementation uses an explicit Euler scheme subject to a CFL-compatible stability restriction. In the validated benchmark configuration, no numerical divergence was observed. However, larger time steps or substantially increased iteration counts may introduce ringing or mild over-smoothing near strong transitions. The present study therefore adopts a conservative fixed setting and does not claim unconditional numerical robustness beyond the tested range. This limitation is inherent to explicit time integration and motivates the careful choice of discretization parameters in practice. Exploring more stable semi-implicit or adaptive-in-time variants may therefore be a useful direction for future work.

Over-smoothing risk As with many diffusion-based restoration methods, excessive iteration can gradually reduce fine-detail contrast. In the present benchmark, the selected configuration T=3 provided the best balance between impulse suppression and structure preservation. Nevertheless, high-frequency periodic textures at higher native resolutions may be more vulnerable to progressive smoothing than the resized benchmark images considered here.

Baseline coverage The selected baselines provide broad coverage of impulse-aware, general-purpose, variational, and transform-based methods. However, the benchmark is not exhaustive. Specifically, learning-based denoisers, including pretrained CNNs, Deep Image Prior methods, self-supervised approaches, and lightweight single-image learning models, are excluded from the main quantitative comparison because they require substantially different training, adaptation, or validation protocols. The conclusions should therefore be read relative to the evaluated training-free benchmark rather than as universal statements about all denoising methods.

Per-metric exceptions ETF does not attain the best value in every metric under every tested condition. For example, DBA achieves slightly lower MAE at ρ =0.05 and slightly higher FOM at some moderate noise levels, while other methods may lead on isolated metrics such as Recall under extreme corruption. These exceptions only apply in certain situations, and they don’t change the main point that ETF did the best overall in the benchmark that was looked at. They say that different metrics look at different parts of restoration quality and may prefer different algorithmic behaviors instead. Final numerical configuration of ETF

In this subsection, we present the final benchmark configuration of ETF used throughout the paper and document it in a fully reproducible algorithmic form. Specifically, we state the fixed hyperparameter setting selected by the tuning procedure, summarize the complete computational pipeline, and explain how the discrete implementation realizes the mathematical model introduced earlier. The objective is to provide a precise reference specification for all reported experiments and to make the benchmark version of ETF transparent, replicable, and straightforward to implement independently. All ETF results reported in this paper are obtained with the same final hyperparameter configuration:46\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {T = 3, \qquad \textrm{d}t = 0.23, \qquad \kappa = 25.0,} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which was selected by the two-stage tuning strategy described in the preceding subsection and then held fixed in all subsequent experiments. Here, T denotes the number of explicit diffusion iterations, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{d}t$$\end{document} is the time-step size in the forward Euler update, and κ controls the sensitivity of the exponential conductivity to local gradient magnitude. These values were not chosen to optimize performance for a single image, a single dataset, or a single corruption level. Rather, they were selected because they provided the most reliable overall compromise between impulse suppression, structure preservation, and numerical stability across the benchmark as a whole. In particular, T=3 was found to be large enough to remove a substantial proportion of the salt-and-pepper corruption, yet small enough to avoid the gradual loss of contrast and fine structural detail that becomes more apparent under longer diffusion runs. Similarly, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{d}t=0.23$$\end{document} lies within the empirically validated CFL-compatible regime and yielded stable explicit updates of practically useful magnitude, while κ =25.0 produced a consistent balance between aggressive smoothing in heavily corrupted regions and restrained diffusion across visually meaningful intensity transitions. Taken together, these values define a numerically conservative and empirically robust operating point for ETF within the scope of the present study.

An important practical consequence of this final configuration is that ETF can be applied throughout the reported benchmark without per-image, per-dataset, or per-noise-level retuning. This fixed parameterization is one of the method’s principal practical advantages. From a methodological standpoint, it reduces the risk that the reported gains are an artifact of repeated case-specific hyperparameter adjustment. From a reproducibility standpoint, it ensures that the experimental protocol is simple, stable, and easy to replicate under the same conditions. More broadly, the use of a single global parameter set supports a cleaner and more defensible comparison with competing baselines, because the reported performance of ETF is evaluated under a uniform operating regime rather than under selectively optimized settings that vary across test cases. In this sense, the fixed configuration strengthens both the practical interpretability and the comparative fairness of the benchmark. Practical interpretation. The benchmark configuration is intentionally simple: a small fixed number of iterations, a single global time step, and a single edge-sensitivity parameter. This simplicity contributes materially to the practical usability of ETF within the evaluated setting and eliminates the need for image-specific parameter adjustment in the reported experiments. At the same time, the simplicity should not be interpreted as oversimplification of the numerical model; rather, it reflects the fact that a stable and effective operating point was found that generalizes well across the tested benchmark conditions. The combination of the fixed parameter setting, the conservative finite-volume discretization, the explicit algorithmic specification, and the workflow diagram provides a complete and reproducible benchmark definition of ETF. This final configuration serves as the basis for all reported experiments and comparisons in the manuscript and defines the precise implementation regime to which the empirical conclusions of the paper refer.

Conclusion

This paper presented the ETF as a geometrically based framework for removing noise from salt-and-pepper images and demonstrated its use for the first time in this context. The suggested method defined restoration as a nonlinear, gradient-dependent diffusion process based on an exponential energy functional. This offered a clear and adaptable way to reduce impulsive corruption while keeping important image transitions. A conservative finite-volume implementation was developed and tested across a range of impulse densities using quality metrics, runtime analysis, cross-dataset testing, and statistical validation. In the training-free benchmark tested, ETF consistently outperformed classical, variational, and transform-based baselines, especially in the presence of substantial noise. The results indicated that ETF is an effective, interpretable, and computationally efficient geometry-based approach for restoring images affected by impulse noise within the assessed framework.

The current study has deliberately concentrated on a regulated, training-free geometric benchmark utilizing grayscale images and constant-value salt-and-pepper noise. This work naturally paves the way for wider validation across color data, higher resolutions, different degradation models, and adaptive numerical schemes. A systematic, protocol-controlled comparison between ETF and modern, pretrained, or self-supervised learning-based denoisers (such as the Deep Image Prior, Noise2Self, and lightweight single-image models) is also an important area for future research. This kind of evaluation requires specific adaptation strategies and an experimental design that are beyond the scope of this study but will be examined in future work.
