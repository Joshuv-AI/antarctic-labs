Biomechanical analysis of single leg deadlift under the effect of instability
Authors: Jioun Kim, Youngirl Jeon, Kilho Eom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The single leg deadlift (SLD), one of resistance exercises, is widely prescribed to enhance muscular strength and balance due to its inherent unstable, unilateral nature. However, this instability may limit joint mobility and diminish strength training efficacy. This study aimed to investigate the biomechanical effect of surface-induced instability during SLD performance. Thirty healthy adult males performed SLDs under stable surface (flat ground) and unstable surface (foam-pad). Inertial measurement unit (IMU) and surface electromyography (EMG) were employed to assess joint kinematics, such as range of motion (ROM) and joint sway, and muscle activation patterns during stretch-shortening cycle. The unstable surface significantly increases ankle joint sway by 80.00% during the eccentric phase and 99.62% during the concentric phase (p < 0.05). While ankle joint ROM remains unaltered, instability reduces ROM at hip and knee joints by 6.04° and 3.81°, respectively. EMG analyses revealed that surface instability elevated Gluteus Maximus activation during the eccentric phase (13.27%) and Tibialis Anterior activation during both eccentric (6.56%) and concentric (15.21%) phases. Notably, Biceps Femoris activation increases across both phases under unstable conditions. Surface-induced instability during the SLD augments neuromuscular demand, particularly in the balance and stabilizing ankle musculature. However, this instability reduces hip and knee joint mobility, which may compromise the mechanical loading required for strength adaptation. These findings underscore the need for strategic integration of instability in resistance training programs depending on specific performance goals.

Introduction

Optimizing athletic performance requires training programs that are grounded in well-established principles, including specificity, progression, and overload1–3. Among these, the principle of specificity is particularly crucial, stipulating that training adaptations are maximized when exercises closely mimic the biomechanical and bioenergetic characteristics of the target sport skill – such as movement velocity, muscle activation pattern, and force actions2,4–7. In alignment with this principle, unilateral exercise has gained popularity, especially given that many athletic tasks – such as running, jumping, kicking, and throwing – are performed predominantly on a single limb8–11.

The single leg deadlift (SLD) is a commonly prescribed unilateral exercise intended to enhance lower-limb strength and neuromuscular control12,13. Biomechanically, the SLD involves sagittal plane hip flexion and extension while the individual balances on one foot, demanding both posterior chain engagement and dynamic balance13–17. Previous study13 has indicated that SLD effectively activates the core and posterior musculature. However, its inherent narrow base of support introduces considerable posturalinstability12,18–20, distinguishing it from bilateral counterparts such as the conventional deadlift. This instability positions the SLD at the intersection of resistance and balance training.

Nevertheless, the effectiveness of resistance training under unstable conditions has been questioned. Multiple studies21–23 have shown that performing strength exercise on unstable surface may impair maximal force output and attenuates improvements in strength and power over time. For instance, interventions utilizing unstable modalities over periods of 4 to 10 weeks have been associated with either negligible gains or even decrements in strength performance24–26. A previous work by Zemkova23 has suggested that unstable environments may disrupt the timing, such as the delay of isometric transition, and efficiency of the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC), potentially diminishing neuromuscular performance.

Given that the SLD inherently includes instability due to its unilateral nature, it is plausible that similar detriments may occur during its execution. However, the extent to which this intrinsic instability affects biomechanical performance – particularly balance control, muscular activation, and joint mechanics – remains poorly understood. Electromyography (EMG) studies further complicate this picture. Compared to exercises such as the Nordic hamstring exercise (NHE), the SLD elicits relative low activation in the hamstrings. For examples, previous studies by Guruhan et al.27, Ebben28, and Van Hooren et al.29 have consistently reported minimal hamstring engagement and negligible improvements in strength or fascicle length associated with SLD. Bourne and coworkers30 even excluded the SLD from functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) analysis due to its insufficient muscle activation levels relative to other exercises.

Despite these findings, the underlying mechanisms that account for the reduced muscle recruitment during the SLD remains to be speculative. One plausible explanation is that the intrinsic instability of exercise impairs movement quality and limits effective muscular engagement.

To address this knowledge gap, we investigate how the different level of instability in the SLD influences biomechanical characteristics associated with strength and balance. By comparing performance on stable and unstable surfaces, we aim to elucidate the effects of postural demand on joint kinematics and muscle activations (measured via inertial sensors and EMG, respectively), while the kinetic analysis is beyond the scope of this study. We hypothesize that increasing instability during the SLD will (i) affect phase durations, (ii) elevate joint sway, (iii) augment muscle activation, and (iv) reduce the joint range of motion (ROM). These insights will guide the design and implementation of more effective sport-specific training regimens.

Results

Biomechanical characteristics of single leg deadlift under the effect of surface-induced instability

Figure 1 presents the mean joint flexion angles (for hip, knee, and ankle joints) across all participants during the SLD. Independent of surface instability, the hip joint exhibited the greatest ROM, emphasizing its dominant role in the movement. The transition point (TP), defined as the peak hip flexion (i.e., zero angular velocity), and the isometric transition phase (ITP), delineated by hip angular velocity within ± 1 deg/s, were identified31–33. The eccentric activation phase (EAP) was defined as the ascending portion of the flexion angle where external torque prevails, and the concentric activation phase (CAP) as the descending portion dominated by internal torque.

As shown in Figure 1c, the TP of hip joint is almost identical to that of knee and ankle joints for SLD performed on stable surface. In addition, as displayed in Figure 1d, the TP of hip joint is also almost identical to that of knee and ankle joints for SLD conducted on unstable surface. Though the unstable surface condition remarkably reduces the correlation between the joint sways (e.g., see Figure 5c–e), the TP of hip joint is almost synchronized with that of other joints such as knee and ankle joints. This indicates that although the unstable condition often disrupted multi-joint coordination, the phase lag between joints would not be likely to occur. On the unstable surface, the normalized time of TP was decreased by 0.005 within the SLD cycle.

Surface instability resulted in decreased joint ROM in both hip and knee joints by 6.04° and 3.81°, respectively, while the ankle joint ROM remains largely unaffected. This suggests that instability primarily constrains proximal joint kinematics, limiting their full excursion during SLD.

As presented in Figure 1, angular velocity analysis further reveals that surface instability reduced the peak angular velocity of the hip joint in EAP by 5.65 deg/s and delayed its occurrence by 2.10%. Similarly, in CAP, hip peak angular velocity decreased by 4.06 deg/s, with a delay of 2.36%. These reductions indicate a dampening effect of surface instability on hip joint dynamics. Conversely, no significant changes were observed in peak angular velocities of the knee and ankle joints, though fluctuations in their velocity profiles increased under unstable condition, as demonstrated in following section.

EMG data normalized to maximum voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC) are shown in Figure 2. While surface instability did not significantly alter the overall muscle activation patterns, distinct phase- and muscle-specific responses were evident. The gluteus maximus (GM) and biceps femoris (BF) showed increased activation primarily at the onset of CAP, coinciding with increased hip joint angular velocity (e.g., see Figure 1). The gastrocnemius medialis (GN) activation decreased during EAP, linking with knee flexion demands, while it increased during CAP, aligning with ankle plantarflexion demands. In contrast, the rectus femoris (RF) activation increased in EAP but decreased in CAP, suggesting resistance to knee flexion predominantly in EAP. The tibialis anterior (TA) exhibited behavior similar to RF, contributing to ankle dorsiflexion in EAP. Notably, surface instability increased TA activation during EAP (6.56%) and CAP (15.21%), possibly due to its antagonistic function against GN during ankle extension. Additionally, GM and BF activations in EAP increased by 13.27% and 17.94%, respectively, indicating greater neuromuscular demand in response to instability.

Statistical analysis on the effect of surface instability in biomechanical characteristics

A paired t-test assessed biomechanical differences between stable and unstable surface conditions. The duration for a trial of SLD performed on the ground was measured as 4.72 ± 0.97 s, while the duration for a trial of SLD conducted on the foam-pad was estimated as 4.71 ± 1.14 s. The paired t-test showed there is no significant differences between durations for both conditions (t = − 0.11, p = 0.914, and d = − 0.01).

Ankle joint ROM did not differ significantly between two conditions (t = − 1.94, p > 0.05); however, hip- and knee-joint ROMs were remarkably decreased under unstable condition (hip: t = − 6.06, p < 0.001, and d = − 0.59; knee: t = − 3.30, p = 0.003, and d = − 0.28). In a similar manner, joint sways of hip and knee in EAP were reduced (hip: V = 57, p < 0.001, and r = 0.66; knee: V = 121, p = 0.021, and r = 0.42), indicating instability-driven restriction of hip and knee joint motion during the deceleration phase. In contrast, ankle joint sway increased significantly in both EAP (V = 377, p = 0.002, and r = 0.54) and CAP (V = 409, p < 0.001, and r = 0.66) (Figure 3c and e). These findings emphasize the role of the ankle joint in balance control.

As displayed in Figure 4a – e, muscular activation analyses revealed significantly increased activation in GM and BF during EAP under unstable condition (GM: V = 372, p = 0.003, and r = 0.52; BF: t = 5.43, p < 0.001, and d = 0.29), likely reflecting greater neuromuscular demands. TA activation during EAP and CAP was also elevated (EAP: V = 330, p = 0.045, and r = 0.37; CAP: V = 340, p = 0.026, and r = 0.40), underscoring its role in ankle stabilization. Throughout the CAP, BF consistently demonstrated increased activation (t = 2.51, p = 0.018, and d = 0.16), suggesting its central role in postural and movement control during SLD. No significant changes were detected in GN or RF activation.

Correlation among biomechanical parameters

As provided in Figure 5a, EAP and CAP durations exhibited a strong negative correlation (ground: ρ = − 0.96, p < 0.001; foam-pad: r = − 0.99, p < 0.001), whereas ITP duration was uncorrelated with either phase, indicating that alteration in EAP may influence CAP timing. The ROMs of ankle and knee joints were strongly correlated under both stable (r = 0.90, p < 0.001) and unstable (r = 0.84, p < 0.001) conditions (Figure 5b), suggesting functional coupling for balance control. Interestingly, hip joint ROM is shown to be positively correlated with other joint ROMs (knee: r = 0.39, p = 0.032; ankle: r = 0.41, p = 0.025), implying that the increased postural demand concurrently restricted the mobility of all lower limb joints.

Joint sway correlations were more prevalent under stable condition (Figure. 5c – e). Sway of the ankle was significantly correlated with knee sway across all phases (EAP: ρ = 0.49, ITP: ρ = 0.43, CAP: ρ = 0.61; all p < 0.05) and with hip sway during EAP (ρ = 0.37, p = 0.043). Knee and hip sways were also correlated in EAP and CAP (EAP: ρ = 0.58, CAP: ρ = 0.55; all p < 0.01). However, under unstable conditions, significant correlations were primarily found between knee and hip joints (EAP: ρ = 0.78, CAP: ρ = 0.59; all p < 0.001), suggesting disrupted inter-joint coordination.

As presented in Figure 6a – c, EMG signal correlations were generally lower; however, GN and RF demonstrated significant negative correlations across all phases (p < 0.05), indicative of functional antagonism. GM and BF activations were moderately correlated in EAP and ITP under unstable surface (EAP: ρ = 0.36, ITP: ρ = 0.49; all p < 0.05), reflecting synergetic control during movement reversal. In contrast, the positive correlation (ρ = 0.45, p = 0.012) between GM and BF activations in CAP under the stable surface condition may indicate a coordinated activation of these muscles during the final propulsive phase (i.e., concentric ascent).

Discussion

This study investigated the biomechanical adaptations to surface-induced instability during SLD. Instability was introduced using a foam-pad surface, confirmed through increased ankle joint sway – a proxy for postural challenge – aligning with previous finding by Buchel et al.34, who reported increased center-of-pressure velocities on unstable surface during single-leg stance tasks.

Contrary to earlier reports by Zemkova23, surface instability in our study did not significantly affect ITP duration, likely due to its brevity and near-instantaneous nature within the SSC35,36. Instead, instability notably alters the joint kinematics (e.g., joint sways) in EAP, suggesting that eccentric control is particularly sensitive to postural perturbations. These findings support the notion that a lower-limb resistance training involving instability primarily challenges eccentric muscle control, particularly in the hip extensors.

Increased GM and BF activations during EAP under unstable condition underscore their role in maneuvering the hip motion during the controlled descent of the movement37,38. TA activation was also elevated39,40, likely implying its antagonistic role against GN during ankle plantarflexion, as the body attempts to restore upright posture. These phase-specific muscular adaptations highlight the neuromuscular strategies employed to preserve balance under instability.

Interestingly, ROM reductions in the hip and knee joints suggest a protective mechanism limiting joint excursion under instability, potentially diminishing strength training efficacy41,42. These results echo Riemann et al.43, who emphasized the stabilizing role of the ankle in maintaining postural control, particularly when proximal joint motion is constrained.

Despite overall decreased ROM, BF activation remained consistently elevated during EAP and CAP under unstable condition. This persistent activation indicates its dual role in both controlling eccentric hip motion and stabilizing the knee during directional changes – a critical demand in SLD.

The observed correlations reveal interdependence among biomechanical variables. Strong negative coupling between EAP and CAP durations suggests the delayed eccentric motion constrains subsequent concentric initiation. Similarly, high correlations between ROMs of ankle and knee joints, and between joint sways, emphasize joint coordination in maintaining dynamic balance43– especially on stable surfaces. However, under unstable conditions, reduced correlation among joint sways indicates disrupted motor coordination.

Unstable SLD training may have implications for dynamic injury prevention, as recent studies show that increased cognitive demands and fatigue during single-leg landing tasks can negatively affect anterior cruciate ligament (ACL)-related biomechanics, such as knee kinematics and impact loading44,45. By challenging unilateral stability and neuromuscular control, SLD training may help support safer single-leg landing strategies and serve as a preparatory exercise linking strength training to dynamic injury-risk reduction.

Several limitations in our study need to be acknowledged. This study focused exclusively on healthy young males; sex-related and training-level differences were not addressed. Additionally, while bilateral 5-kg dumbbells were used to standardize loading and reduce injury risk, this does not reflect variations in external load or unilateral resistance, which may impact stability and muscle activation46. Though the relative load variation (14.05 ± 1.89% of body weight) was modest, small inter-individual differences in relative load may still influence posterior-chain EMG amplitudes47,48, particularly for GM and BF, as EMG responses at low-to-modest relative loads are sensitive to change in stabilization demand. However, given the relatively narrow range of relative loading, these effects are not likely to be of significance compared with the primary influence of unilateral instability. Furthermore, it should be noted that the footwear condition can influence postural control, muscle activation patterns, and joint biomechanics49–51. This indicates that though standardized footwear ensured the control of an experimental variable in our work, it limits the generalizability of our result to other footwear conditions and barefoot condition. In addition, the stable condition is considered before unstable condition in order to minimize learning and carry-over effects associated with prior exposure to instability, as performing unstable trials could artificially enhance subsequent stable performance. While neuromuscular priming from the stable condition cannot be entirely excluded, the stable surface requires a lower neuromuscular demand and is therefore less likely to induce substantial adaptive responses. Nonetheless, to fully control for potential order effect, future study will employ counterbalanced or randomized condition. We also note that ankle joint kinematics alone may not fully reflect foot-surface interactions, and that though ankle joint angles remain unchanged, there may be alterations in foot position or center-of-pressure behavior on an unstable surface. In this study, while ankle motion was quantified using inertial sensor-based joint kinematics, measures of foot-surface interaction such as foot position change, center-of-pressure displacement, or shear forces, were not available because a force platform was not used in our experimental protocol. This implies that potential adaptations occurring at the foot-surface interaction under unstable condition could not directly assessed in this study.

Future studies would integrate cognitive load or dual-task paradigms with unstable resistance training, as mental fatigue and concurrent cognitive demands have been shown to alter single-leg landing biomechanics and increase injury-related risk. Specifically, recent studies52–54 have demonstrated that cognitive-motor interference impairs the motion control during landing, particularly in individuals with ankle instability, and during ACL-loading phases. Integrating such cognitive demands into unstable resistance training may therefore provide further insight into performance and injury-prevention mechanisms.

In conclusion, surface-induced instability during the SLD alters joint mechanics and affects muscle activation, particularly in the eccentric phase. While instability enhances neuromuscular control, it reduces hip and knee ROM, potentially limiting strength development. These findings highlight a trade-off between balance demand and mechanical efficiency, with implications for training strategies.

Methods

Participants

Thirty healthy adult males (age: 22 ± 5 years; height: 174 ± 5 cm; weight: 72.43 ± 9.66 kg) participated in this study. All participants were free from any orthopedic injuries affecting the lower extremities within six months prior to this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were provided a detailed explanation of the experimental procedures. The experimental protocol was approved by the Sungkyunkwan University Bioethics Committee (IRB No. 2022-07-020). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Measurements

Kinematic data were collected using an inertial measurement unit (IMU) system (MyoMotion, Noraxon, USA). IMUs were affixed to the sacrum, thigh, shank, and the dorsum of the foot (just below the ankle joint)55. The system sampled data at 200 Hz, and calibration was performed in a standard upright posture. Here, kinematic data were processed with a fourth-order, zero-phase Butterworth low-pass filter (10 Hz) to minimize high-frequency noise. These filtering procedures were performed bi-directionally to eliminate phase distortion. From these data, phase duration, joint ROM, and joint sway were derived. Design and procedures

Before data collection, participants were given approximately 10 min to become familiar with the experimental setup and procedures, including the placement of motion and bioelectrical sensors. This was followed by a standardized warm-up and practice trials of the SLD to ensure proper technique.

Each participant performed the SLD while holding a 5-kg dumbbell in each hand. Heavier loads were not used to minimize the risk of injury. In addition, participants were instructed to keep the dumbbells as close to the supporting leg as possible throughout the task to maintain proper technique and minimize unnecessary hip and trunk moments. It should be also noted that all participants wore the same standardized shoes throughout the experimental trials to minimize the potential confounding variables in ankle sway and TA/GN muscle activation between participants. The exercise was conducted under two surface conditions: (a) a stable surface (wooden floor) and (2) an unstable surface (Airex Balance Pad; dimensions: 48 cm ⨯ 39 cm ⨯ 6.5 cm (width⨯ length⨯ height). To avoid fatigue or potential carry-over effects, trials were performed first on the stable surface, followed by a 5-minute rest, and then on the unstable surface. Each participant performed three valid trials per condition. While no rest was provided between the three consecutive trials within the same surface condition to preserve task consistency, participants were allowed to reposition themselves to ensure a consistent starting posture. Trials were repeated if balance was lost or form was compromised.

All SLDs were performed using the right leg (kicking the ball) as the stance limb. Starting from an upright posture while holding dumbbells, the participant flexed the hip joint to bring the torso to a position as close to horizontal as possible, maintaining a straight back. Though participants were allowed to adopt their natural knee flexion position of the supporting leg (i.e., the right leg) during the SLD, the task inherently maintains a functional hip-hinge pattern with limited knee flexion57. Participants were also instructed to extend the non-stance leg posteriorly so that it remained horizontal to the floor and aligned with the torso throughout the movement. Trials were accepted provided that this flexion was minimal, the contralateral limb remained non-weight-bearing, and no compensatory trunk rotation or deviation from the intended hip-hinge pattern of the stance leg was observed. To promote natural movement control, participants were instructed to perform the motion as a self-selected pace.

Following the SLD trials, MVIC measurements were performed to normalize EMG data. After a 5-minute rest, MVICs were conducted for each muscle group. All MVICs were performed under the supervision of the same trained researcher, who applied manual resistance gradually to elicit maximal effort, accompanied by standardized verbal encouragement. Each contraction was held isometrically for approximately 5 s (ref.27,58).

For the GM, BF, and GN, MVIC tasks were performed in a prone position. GM activation was elicited via hip extension with the knee flexed at 90°, with resistance applied just proximal to the posterior knee. BF and GN activation were assessed during maximal knee flexion at approximately 45°, with resistance applied at the posterior ankle (i.e., calcaneal tendon). For the RF and TA, MVIC tasks were conducted in a supine position with the hip and knee fully extended; participants performed hip flexion (~ 30°) combined with maximal dorsiflexion while resistance was applied at the dorsal aspect of the foot.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The experimental design involved a within-participant comparison between two surface conditions (stable ground vs. foam-pad). For each participant, kinematic and EMG variables were averaged across trials within each surface condition, yielding a single representative value per participant per condition. These participant-level means were entered into the statistical analyses. The dependent variables include hip-, knee-, and ankle-joint ROMs, phase duration (defined based on hip joint kinematics), phase-specific joint sway measures, and mean phase-specific normalized EMG amplitudes (%MVIC) of the GM, BF, GN, RF and TA muscles.

Statistical analysis was performed using paired t-tests based on statistical significance level being set to 0.05. We note that a paired t-test on kinematic variables (e.g., joint ROM) and EMG data has been widely employed for biomechanical analysis such as gait biomechanics59,60, and single-leg landing61. Data normality was assessed for each dependent variable using Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed variables, paired t-tests were used to compare the two surface conditions; for non-normally distributed variables, Wilcoxon signed rank tests were applied. For a two-tailed paired t-test, we performed a priori power calculations using G*power. Based on the medium effect size of 0.5, alpha level of 0.05, and a sample size of 30, the achieved statistical power was estimated as 0.75. Given the within-participant design and the relatively low variability typically observed in biomechanical measures during controlled laboratory tasks, this level of power may be acceptable and comparable to prior biomechanics studies employing the sample size of 5 ~ 40 (ref.62). It is also noted that correlation analysis on kinematic variables and EMG data has been broadly utilized for biomechanical analysis63,64. The correlation between kinematic parameters or muscle activation patterns was analyzed using Pearson correlation with statistical significance level being set to 0.05. All statistical and correlation analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).
