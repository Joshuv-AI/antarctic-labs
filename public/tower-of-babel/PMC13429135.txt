Weissella Confusa Infections and Strain-Level Biosafety: Clinical Evidence and Intrinsic Vancomycin Resistance
Authors: Yu Lu, Junxian Liu, Deyu Wang, Shihao Houyan, Yingye Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWeissella confusa is an emerging opportunistic pathogen increasingly implicated in invasive infection among immunocompromised individuals, while selected strains continue to be investigated for probiotic applications-creating an inherent tension between beneficial use and infection risk. This is the first systematic synthesis of 39 published cases of invasive W. confusa infection. In this review, we analyzed these cases and summarized current evidence regarding epidemiology, diagnostic approaches, antimicrobial susceptibility, therapeutic management, and biosafety implications. Bacteremia (74.4%) and infective endocarditis (10.3%) were the predominant clinical manifestations and occurred mainly in patients with malignancy, prolonged hospitalization, indwelling medical devices, gastrointestinal barrier disruption, or immunosuppression. Accurate identification remains difficult because W. confusa shares phenotypic characteristics with other lactic acid bacteria and exhibits intrinsic resistance to vancomycin, frequently leading to misidentification and delayed effective therapy. Advanced diagnostic methods including MALDI-TOF MS, 16S rRNA sequencing, and whole-genome sequencing are therefore important for definitive identification. Available evidence suggests that pathogenicity is largely host-dependent, although definitive functional genomic evidence remains limited. Clinically effective antimicrobial agents generally include beta-lactams, daptomycin, and linezolid, whereas glycopeptides should be avoided because of predictable intrinsic resistance. The overall mortality rate in this cohort was 33.3% (13/39). Beyond its clinical relevance, W. confusa highlights limitations of traditional species-level probiotic safety evaluation. We propose a strain-specific biosafety assessment framework integrating genomic screening, antimicrobial resistance profiling, virulence assessment, and phenotypic validation to support safer probiotic development and regulatory evaluation. This framework may provide a useful model for assessing other microorganisms with dual probiotic and opportunistic pathogenic potential.

Introduction

Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) have long been recognized as core microorganisms in food fermentation and are widely regarded as beneficial commensals or probiotics for human health.1 Among this taxonomically diverse bacterial group, the genus Weissella comprises Gram-positive, non-spore-forming, catalase-negative bacteria.2 The taxonomic reclassification of this genus mirrors the advancement of microbiological research: initially classified under Lactobacillus and later Leuconostoc, the distinctiveness of Weissella was formally established in 1993 by Collins et al validated via phenotypic characterization, biochemical profiling, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and the genus was named in honor of the German microbiologist Norbert Weiss.3

Among its members, Weissella confusa stands out owing to its complex and seemingly paradoxical ecological and clinical profile.4 Originally isolated from fermented plant materials such as soured carrot mash and sugarcane, W. confusa widely colonizes various traditional fermented foods globally. Its physiological traits, particularly the production of exopolysaccharides (EPS) and bacteriocins, have positioned it as a promising candidate for use as a starter culture, bio-preservative, and even a next-generation probiotic.4 In stark contrast to these beneficial applications, W. confusa has concurrently emerged as an opportunistic pathogen of clinical significance.4

This duality is most evident in the specific strain characteristics of W. confusa. On one hand, select strains are investigated for their biotechnological potential, as they produce industrially valuable exopolysaccharides,5 survive robustly under gastrointestinal conditions, and demonstrate antimicrobial activity against enteric pathogens. On the other hand, W. confusa has been increasingly implicated in severe invasive infections—including bacteremia.,6–8 endocarditis,9,10 and meningitis,11 predominantly in immunocompromised individuals.12 The increasing use of MALDI-TOF MS in clinical laboratories has led to more frequent identification of Weissella species, suggesting that the true incidence may be underestimated. This clinical threat is compounded by significant diagnostic hurdles: its phenotypic similarity to other LAB and its intrinsic resistance to vancomycin frequently lead to misidentification and initial therapeutic failure.11

Despite the growing number of case reports, current knowledge remains fragmented.13,14 A cohesive synthesis integrating epidemiological trends, molecular diagnostics, antimicrobial resistance profiles, and the genomic basis of the species’ dual nature is lacking. Furthermore, the prevailing “one-size-fits-all” regulatory approach for probiotic safety is demonstrably inadequate for managing the risks posed by bacteria like W. confusa, which exhibit high intra-species heterogeneity.

Traditional safety assessments have often presumed safety at the genus or species level based on a history of use.15 However, emerging clinical and genomic evidence challenges this monolithic view, revealing a spectrum where individual strains exhibit remarkably divergent behaviors—from symbiont to pathogen.16 This “probiotic paradox” underscores that functional and safety attributes are inherently strain-specific, shaped by unique genetic repertoires and host-context interactions.

To systematically delineate the clinical spectrum of invasive W. confusa infections, a structured review of the literature was performed. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for reports published up to March 20, 2026, utilizing the key terms “Weissella confusa”, “Lactobacillus confusus”, “infection”, and “bacteremia”. Cases were selected based on the isolation of W. confusa from sterile sites (eg, blood, cerebrospinal fluid, deep tissue) with correlating clinical signs of infection. Data extracted included patient demographics, underlying conditions, risk factors, clinical presentation, infection type, hospitalization, antimicrobial treatment, and clinical outcome. Duplicate reports were excluded. Cases with insufficient clinical data or where W. confusa was isolated from non-sterile sites were also excluded. Only English and Chinese language articles were included. Two authors independently screened titles and abstracts. Grey literature (conference abstracts, preprints) was excluded. This review was conducted following a structured approach using PRISMA guidelines as a reference framework, but was not registered as a systematic review. To bridge these gaps, this review provides a comprehensive analysis of Weissella confusa. We first delineate its clinical epidemiology and disease spectrum, then examine the microbiological and diagnostic challenges, followed by an overview of therapeutic strategies. Subsequently, we delve into the genomic underpinnings of its strain-specific probiotic and pathogenic potentials. Finally, we evaluate the limitations of current safety paradigms and propose a novel, actionable framework for strain-specific risk assessment.

Unlike previous reviews that focused primarily on taxonomy and biotechnology, this review uniquely integrates a systematic analysis of 39 clinical cases with a proposed regulatory framework for strain-specific biosafety assessment. This framework advocates for mandatory whole-genome sequencing (WGS) as a foundational step to guide the safe development of W. confusa and similar dual-potential microbes, navigating the probiotic paradox with scientific rigor.

Clinical Spectrum and Epidemiology

The analyzed cohort of 39 patients revealed that W. confusa infection can occur across a wide age range, from 3 to 92 years (Table 1). A notable male predominance was observed (22/39, 56.4%). The infection demonstrated a strong opportunistic character, as all but one patient had at least one significant underlying medical condition or risk factor. Bacteremia was the dominant clinical syndrome, accounting for 74.4% (29/39) of cases. This was often primary or catheter-related. Infective endocarditis was the second most common manifestation (10.3%, 4/39), involving both native and prosthetic valves. Rare but severe presentations included meningitis11 and localized abscesses. The clinical presentation was typically acute or subacute, with fever and chills being the cardinal symptoms, reported in over 90% of cases where symptoms were detailed. Patients with endocarditis frequently presented with signs of heart failure or systemic embolization.

The overall mortality rate in this cohort was 33.3% (13/39). Fatal outcomes were predominantly associated with a confluence of factors: profound and irreversible host immunodeficiency, delayed diagnosis leading to a lag in targeted therapy, and the inability to control the source of infection. Conversely, positive outcomes were consistently linked to early accurate identification, prompt initiation of active antimicrobial therapy, and effective source control measures. This pattern suggests that mortality is more closely tied to host factors and healthcare system delays than to an intrinsically high virulence of the bacterium itself.

Controversies: Reconciling the Duality—A “Host-Driven” Pathogen with Strain-Specific Determinants. The central paradox of W. confusa is best understood not by classifying it as inherently “safe” or “dangerous”, but by examining the conditions that unmask its pathogenic potential. The clinical data strongly support a “host-driven” model of pathogenesis. Infection is almost exclusively a consequence of compromised host defenses—immunosuppression,12 breached mucosal barriers,21 and invasive medical devices.18—which allow this commensal or transient bacterium to translocate and persist. This interpretation is consistent with genomic analyses showing that clinical isolates do not form a phylogenetically distinct, hypervirulent clade separate from food or environmental strains. However, this does not imply all strains are identical. The genomic heterogeneity within the species suggests that certain strains may possess a genetic repertoire (eg, adhesion factors, stress tolerance genes) that, while beneficial for gut colonization or industrial fermentation, could potentiate infection in a susceptible host. Thus, the risk appears to depend not only on host status but also on specific strain-host interaction.

Diagnostic Challenges and Methodological Advancements

Accurate and timely diagnosis of W. confusa infection represents a critical hurdle in clinical microbiology, with direct consequences for patient management. This section delineates the root causes of diagnostic failure and outlines the essential technological advancements and clinical approaches required for correct identification and assessment.

The primary diagnostic challenge stems from the bacterium’s nonspecific phenotypic profile.11 As a vancomycin-resistant, catalase-negative, Gram-positive coccus or coccobacillus,18 it occupies an overlap zone between several genera. Traditional automated biochemical identification systems (eg, API, VITEK 2, Phoenix), which rely on carbohydrate fermentation patterns, frequently misidentify W. confusa.17 The most common erroneous results are Leuconostoc spp. or Lactobacillus spp.20 Consequently, an isolate may be initially reported as a “vancomycin-resistant Lactobacillus”, leading clinicians to potentially underestimate its pathogenic significance or delay appropriate therapy. This intrinsic resistance to glycopeptides, a cornerstone of empirical therapy for serious Gram-positive infections, makes standard empirical regimens ineffective and underscores the need for specific diagnostics.

To overcome the limitations of conventional phenotyping, modern microbiological techniques have become the gold standard for definitive identification.20 MALDI-TOF MS rapidly identifies microorganisms by analyzing their unique protein spectra, primarily ribosomal proteins. Compared to traditional methods, MALDI-TOF MS offers significant advantages in speed, cost-efficiency, and accuracy, drastically reducing turnaround time. However, its reliability is highly dependent on the comprehensiveness of the spectral database.20 For rare organisms like Weissella, databases may lack sufficient reference spectra, necessitating the use of high-quality, extensively curated databases in clinical laboratories. When MALDI-TOF MS yields ambiguous results, performing 16S rRNA gene sequencing and comparing it with databases like NCBI enables accurate genus- and species-level classification.20 WGS provides not only precise species identification but also enables simultaneous screening for antibiotic resistance genes and virulence factors.27 This offers the most comprehensive information for infection source tracing and clinical risk assessment.

For severe clinical syndromes such as infective endocarditis, central nervous system infections, or prosthetic device infections, microbiological identification alone is insufficient and must be integrated with other diagnostic modalities. Techniques such as echocardiography7 (for detecting vegetations in endocarditis), or CT/MRI (for localizing abscesses or meningeal involvement) are important.7,25 Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and procalcitonin (PCT) are valuable for assessing infection severity and monitoring treatment response.

Clinical Practice Recommendations

Clinicians should suspect W. confusa in cases of Gram-positive bacteremia consistent with lactic acid bacteria, especially when there is a poor response to vancomycin therapy, and immediately request advanced laboratory identification. Perform MALDI-TOF MS analysis with an updated database. If MALDI-TOF MS is inconclusive or discordant, proceed to 16S rRNA gene sequencing for definitive confirmation. Correlate microbiological findings with imaging and inflammatory markers to guide overall management.

Fundamental Challenges

The Critical Failure of Phenotypic Identification and Its Clinical Cost. A paramount finding of this review is the severe and recurring deficiency of traditional phenotypic methods in accurately identifying W. confusa. Its routine misidentification as Lactobacillus or Leuconostoc, coupled with its intrinsic vancomycin resistance, creates a confluence of factors leading to diagnostic error. This often leads to a clinically significant delay in initiating effective therapy, as vancomycin remains a cornerstone of empirical treatment for Gram-positive bacteremia. The resulting lag period may directly contribute to poor outcomes, particularly in rapidly progressive infections such as endocarditis or meningitis. This underscores an urgent need for clinical microbiology laboratories serving high-risk patient populations to adopt and validate molecular diagnostic pathways (eg, MALDI-TOF MS with curated databases, reflex 16S rRNA sequencing for vancomycin-resistant, catalase-negative Gram-positive cocci) as a standard of care.

Pathogenesis, Antimicrobial Resistance, and Clinical Management

Understanding the clinical management of Weissella confusa infections requires a dual perspective: comprehension of its host-dependent pathogenesis and its distinctive antimicrobial resistance profile. This integrated view is essential for explaining why infections occur in specific hosts and how to treat them effectively.

The pathogenic potential of W. confusa is fundamentally opportunistic, relying more on host susceptibility than on classic virulence factors. Systemic infection is predicated on significant host immune compromise and physical disruption of mucosal barriers, which facilitate bacterial translocation from colonization sites such as the gastrointestinal tract.21 Genomic analyses have identified genes homologous to potential virulence determinants28 (eg, collagen adhesins, hemolysins, mucus-binding proteins), although their functional expression in clinical infection remains largely unconfirmed. Functional validation is needed to confirm their role in pathogenesis Notably, the very properties that confer probiotic advantage—excellent gastrointestinal tolerance, strong epithelial adhesion, and self-aggregation—can, in an immunodeficient host, paradoxically enhance colonization and persistence, serving as a starting point for infection.

The principal management challenge stems from its predictable and atypical antibiotic resistance. The hallmark is intrinsic, non-transferable resistance to vancomycin (and often teicoplanin), likely due to altered cell wall precursor synthesis. This renders standard empirical therapy for severe Gram-positive infections ineffective. Intrinsic resistance to aminoglycosides is common, and sporadic reports indicate acquired resistance to agents like erythromycin and levofloxacin, underscoring the necessity for routine susceptibility testing and strain-specific vigilance. Of note, CLSI/EUCAST does not provide species-specific breakpoints for Weissella; interpretations in the literature are based on general guidelines for Gram-positive cocci. The management of W. confusa infections is guided by its distinctive antimicrobial susceptibility profile (Table 2). Clinical isolates are typically susceptible to ampicillin, daptomycin, and linezolid. Therefore, timely and accurate antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) is important to guide precise, targeted therapy for each significant clinical isolate. Typical Antimicrobial Susceptibility Profile of Weissella Confusa

The cornerstone of clinical management involves a multi-pronged approach: (1) Avoid Ineffective Empiric Therapy: Glycopeptides (vancomycin/teicoplanin) should be avoided upon suspicion or confirmation. (2) Employ Targeted Antimicrobials: Utilize active agents (eg, β-lactams, daptomycin) based on AST results. (3) Implement Source Control: Removal of infected devices (eg, central venous catheters) or drainage of abscesses is often critical for cure. (4) Consider Combination and Prolonged Therapy: For life-threatening infections like endocarditis, consider combination therapy (eg, a β-lactam with an aminoglycoside for initial synergy) and extended treatment courses (eg, 4–6 weeks) may be considered. (5) Maintain Clinical Suspicion: In a patient with bacteremia from a vancomycin-resistant, Gram-positive coccus preliminarily identified as Lactobacillus, clinicians should elicit a history of probiotic use and request advanced identification (MALDI-TOF MS or 16S rRNA gene sequencing) to rule out Weissella infection.

These integrated measures—from pathogenesis-aware diagnosis to resistance-guided treatment—are important to managing infections caused by this unique opportunistic pathogen and improving patient outcomes.

Safety Assessment Framework for Probiotic Application

The recent identification of Weissella confusa as an opportunistic pathogen highlights the urgent need to reassess its safety for use as a probiotic. Traditional safety assessments, which often rely on species-level taxonomy or historical use, are inadequate for addressing the strain-specific risks associated with such microorganisms. Here we propose a multi-tiered safety assessment framework that integrates genomic pre-screening with targeted phenotypic and clinical evaluations—an approach that aligns with contemporary scientific consensus and evolving regulatory trends (summarized in Figure 2). The Imperative for a New Paradigm

From Species-Level to Strain-Specific Assessment. The fundamental premise of this framework is that the safety of a probiotic is strain-host-dose-specific. As evidenced by the clinical cases reviewed, pathogenic potential is not a uniform trait across the W. confusa species but is linked to specific genetic determinants and host conditions. The ongoing controversy surrounding its safety is reflected in divergent international regulatory stances. For instance, the German Committee for Biological Agents classifies W. confusa as a Risk Group 1 microorganism, while the American Biological Safety Association does not assign it to any risk group. This inconsistency highlights the limitations of broad, species-level classifications. Therefore, a robust assessment should begin with precise strain identification and thorough genotypic characterization. This approach is increasingly embedded in national regulations, such as Indonesia’s requirement for genome-based safety assessment and China’s new national standard GB 31615.2–2025, which mandates comprehensive analysis of resistance and virulence genes.

A Multi-Tiered Assessment Framework

Tier 1: Genomic Pre-Screening

WGS serves as the foundational first step, providing a means to identify hazards that may not be evident in routine phenotypic tests. For W. confusa, genomic analysis should focus on the following key areas: (i) Antibiotic Resistance Genes (ARGs): Special attention should be given to the genetic basis of intrinsic vancomycin resistance; the absence of acquired and transferable ARGs is a fundamental safety criterion (ii) Virulence Determinants In silico screening for genes homologous to known virulence factors (eg, collagen adhesins, hemolysins, mucus-binding proteins) is valuable; the presence of these genes requires functional validation in subsequent tiers. (iii) Toxin and Bioactive Metabolite Gene Clusters: The genome should be analyzed for genes involved in the production of biogenic amines, D-lactate, or other potentially harmful metabolite.

Tier 2: Phenotypic Confirmation

Genomic predictions require phenotypic validation through the following key assays: (i) Antibiotic Susceptibility Profiling: Comprehensive minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) testing against a panel of clinically relevant antibiotics; Results should be interpreted within species-specific cut-offs, with any atypical resistance investigated thoroughly. (ii) Virulence Phenotypes: Tests for hemolytic activity, mucin degradation, platelet aggregation, and biofilm formation under relevant conditions.30 (iii) Metabolic Safety: Assessment of D-lactic acid production and biogenic amine formation in culture.30 (iv) Genetic Transfer Potential: Conjugation experiments to assess the mobilizability of any identified ARGs or virulence clusters.

Tier 3: Biological Context Evaluation

For strains passing Tiers 1 and 2, safety must be evaluated in biologically relevant contexts. (i) Animal Models: Standard toxicology studies (acute and sub-chronic oral toxicity) are recommended. Given the opportunistic nature of W. confusa, models with compromised immunity or damaged intestinal barriers should be employed to specifically assess the risk of translocation and systemic infection.30 (ii) Human Trials (Phase I): Initial clinical trials should meticulously monitor for adverse events (AEs), with protocols in place to molecularly link any clinical infection isolate back to the administered probiotic strain.31 Full reporting of AEs is recommended.31

Ongoing Safety Assessment and Regulatory Considerations

Safety assessment is an ongoing process. A proactive pharmacovigilance system is needed to detect rare, late-onset, or population-specific risks.31 Key elements include: (i) Clear Labeling: Product labels should accurately list the complete strain designation (genus, species, and strain), viable count at end of shelf life, and a defined antibiogram to guide medical treatment if infection occurs. (ii) High-Risk Population Warnings Labels and clinical guidelines should explicitly caution against use in defined vulnerable groups, such as patients with severe immunosuppression, central venous catheters, or active gastrointestinal inflammation, mirroring the risk profile identified in the clinical spectrum section.31

Knowledge Gaps

The Inadequacy of Current Probiotic Safety Frameworks. The existing regulatory frameworks for probiotics—such as the GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) status in the US or the QPS (Qualified Presumption of Safety) list in Europe—are not optimally designed to evaluate microbes like W. confusa. These systems primarily rely on a history of safe use at the species level and are not well-suited to assess strain-specific risks or the potential for opportunistic infection in vulnerable populations. Our proposed multi-tiered safety assessment framework directly addresses these gaps. By mandating WGS as a first step, it shifts the paradigm from retrospective presumption to prospective, evidence-based verification. The framework’s emphasis on screening for acquired and transferable antibiotic resistance genes—rather than dismissing intrinsic resistance—and its call for targeted testing in compromised-host animal models are specifically aligned with the hazards most relevant to the W. confusa risk profile.

Future Directions and Limitations

Limitations

This review has limitations inherent to its design. The evidence base consists largely of case reports and small series, which are subject to publication bias (likely under-reporting milder cases) and may not fully capture the epidemiological breadth of infection. The true incidence and population-attributable risk remain difficult to quantify. Additionally, the lack of standardized susceptibility breakpoints for Weissella and the limited functional genomic data available for this genus constrain the depth of mechanistic interpretation possible at this time. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings and recommendations presented.

Toward Integrated Clinical and Microbiological Stewardship. The implications of this review extend beyond the laboratory into clinical stewardship. Just as antimicrobial stewardship programs optimize antibiotic use, the concept of “probiotic stewardship” has been proposed as a potential framework for healthcare settings. For high-risk patients (eg, those in intensive care, with neutropenia, or with central venous catheters), the decision to administer a probiotic should be informed by a risk-benefit analysis that considers the specific strain’s safety profile. Furthermore, clinicians may consider proactively inquiring about probiotic use in any patient presenting with an infection caused by a vancomycin-resistant Lactobacillus-like organism, as this could provide an early clue to a probiotic-source infection, potentially enabling faster diagnosis and source control.

Future Research Priorities

Future research is needed to address the gaps identified in this review and to further develop the framework proposed here: (1) Functional Genomics: Move beyond in silico predictions to experimentally validate the role of putative virulence genes in adhesion, invasion, and immune evasion using isogenic mutant comparisons would substantially strengthen the evidence base. This is particularly important given the current lack of functional validation for many predicted virulence determinants in W. confusa. (2) Comparative Genomics: Systematic comparison of clinical, food, and environmental isolates using pangenome analysis would help determine whether specific genetic elements are consistently associated with pathogenic potential. Such studies could inform the development of targeted screening approaches. (3) Epidemiological Surveillance: Establishment of active, laboratory-based surveillance would better define the incidence of Weissella infections and enable tracing of outbreaks to potential sources, including probiotic products. This would also help clarify whether the true incidence is higher than currently recognized. (4) Advanced Risk Modeling: Machine learning approaches that integrate genomic, transcriptomic, and patient metadata could potentially contribute to predictive models for strain-specific pathogenicity in the future, although this remains an area for further method development. (5) Regulatory Science: Policy-focused research is needed to develop standardized, globally harmonized guidelines for the genomic safety assessment of probiotic candidates, building on the framework proposed here and similar initiatives in other jurisdictions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, Weissella confusa serves as a valuable example of the nuanced reality in microbial pathogenesis. Among the 39 analyzed cases, the mortality rate was 33.3%, underscoring the clinical significance of this infection. Its clinical and biotechnological duality highlights the need to move beyond species-level safety assumptions toward strain-specific, genomics-based risk assessment. By adopting the safety assessment framework outlined here—beginning with whole-genome sequencing and progressing through phenotypic validation to context-specific evaluation—researchers, clinicians, and regulators can collaborate to ensure that the potential of such microbes is pursued with appropriate rigor and caution. Although illustrated for W. confusa, this framework is applicable to other microorganisms with dual probiotic and pathogenic potential, thereby offering a broader contribution to the safe development of next-generation probiotics.
