Evaluating fifteen years of national policy in Israel to overcome socio-geographic health disparities in Israel’s Northern District: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Nurit Dessau, Marganit Ofir Gutler, Beth G Zalcman, Sivan Spitzer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeographic location impacts health. Despite Ministry of Health policies, Israel’s northern district continues to lag behind in health outcomes. This study examined geographic health inequities in Israel (2009–2024), alongside policies intended to reduce them, through the Policy Networks framework to analyze Ministry of Health policymaking on health equity.MethodsThis mixed-methods study evaluated Ministry of Health initiatives between 2009 and 2024. A quantitative analysis compared health infrastructure and workforce rates in Israel’s Northern District compared to national rates from 2009 to 2024. Qualitative analysis involved a content analysis of policy documents to identify key stakeholders, roles, and policy shifts over time.ResultsQuantitative analysis found persistent healthcare disparities in the north, including hospital bed, physician, and paramedical personnel rates. Qualitative analysis of the ten policy documents issued between 2009 and 2024 showed varying focus to socio-geographic disparities, with only four addressing the Northern District. Three factors were identified as contributing to the persistence of disparities: lack of robust representation, centralized decision-making, and decreased focus on geographic disparities.ConclusionHealth disparities between Israel’s northern district and central regions persist due to inequities in healthcare access and outcomes and lack of sufficient policies. Centralized decision-making and the exclusion of peripheral stakeholders in the policy network hinder implementation of equity-focused solutions.Keywords: Socio-geographic disparities, Health disparities, Health policy, Policy networks, Health equity

Background

Health inequities are multifaceted, arising from systematic social disparities linked to socioeconomic status, ethnicity, gender, disability, and geographic location [1]. Geographic location, in particular, is a key determinant influencing quality of life, morbidity, and mortality. Populations residing at the social-geographic periphery experience a disproportionate burden of disease across multiple health domains [2]. These inequities are avoidable, preventable, and unjust [3].

Globally, geographic health inequities reflect systemic differences in healthcare access, infrastructure, and resources. Geographic health disparities have been documented across multiple European countries, each reflecting distinct structural and historical patterns [4–6]. In the United Kingdom (UK) and Italy, disparities largely follow a north–south divide, with poorer health outcomes and lower life expectancy observed in northern England and southern Italy, respectively [4, 6]. In Germany, disparities follow an east–west pattern, rooted in long-standing socioeconomic differences that persisted following reunification and linked to differences in regional economic development, healthcare resources, and social determinants of health [5].

Several countries have implemented national strategies to address these health inequities, with varying degrees of success [4, 7]. In the UK, a comprehensive cross-government program implemented between 1997 and 2010 sought to reduce health inequities, partly driven by the concentration of political and economic power in London and its surrounding areas [4, 8, 9]. However, recent analyses reveal mixed results of these initiatives, with an uncertain impact on geographic health inequities [4, 9]. Italy has similarly introduced reforms aimed at reducing geographic disparities in healthcare delivery, most recently a 2022 primary care reform that sought to standardize service provision and expand access to community-based care across regions. However, long-term effectiveness in reducing geographic disparities remains uncertain [10].

Similar patterns of geographic health disparities have also been documented in Israel. Significant regional inequalities persist between the country’s central areas and its peripheral regions, particularly in the northern and southern districts. These disparities are reflected in differences in health outcomes, healthcare accessibility, and the distribution of healthcare infrastructure and resources [11, 12].

Israel’s northern socio-geographic periphery

Israel’s Northern District is one of the country’s two primary peripheral areas and is characterized by several factors associated with health inequities. The region has a high proportion of rural localities, relatively lower socioeconomic resources compared with central Israel, and a large minority Arab population [13]. These characteristics are often associated with structural barriers to health, including reduced access to healthcare services and poorer health outcomes [11, 14].

According to Israel’s National Health Insurance Law, every citizen is covered by a universal benefits package. According to the Law, services funded by the state need to be of reasonable quality, provided within a reasonable time, and at a reasonable distance from the insured’s place of residence. Despite this, Israel’s Northern District consistently demonstrates significant disparities in health outcomes compared to other regions in the country [15]. This region has the second lowest life expectancy at birth, and the highest rate of cardiovascular deaths, and diabetes [16, 17].

In the early 2000s, geographic health disparities gained national attention, prompting the Ministry of Health (MoH) to act. Since then, the MoH has consistently acted to reduce geographic health inequities, developing policies and frameworks aimed at monitoring, understanding, and addressing spatial variations in health outcomes [18]. Yet, as in England, these disparities persist. This study investigates geographic health inequities in Israel from 2009 to 2024, in parallel with MoH policies aimed at mitigating these inequities, exploring both “what happened” and “what explains what happened” [19]. To do so, this study utilized the Policy Networks theoretical framework [20].

Guiding theoretical framework

Policy networks are defined as “a social system in which actors develop comparatively durable patterns of interaction and communication aimed at policy problems or policy programs” [21]. Policy networks provide a valuable theoretical framework for analyzing governmental policymaking because they capture the interplay between institutions, actors, and the processes through which policies are shaped. They can be categorized in various ways, emphasizing different dimensions. For instance, Rhodes and Marsh [22] focus on the structural aspects, while Van Waarden’s [23] typology underscores the network’s foundational elements: actors, structure, functions, and behavioral dimensions.

Earlier approaches tended to lean heavily towards structural factors [22] or agency [24]. In contrast, Marsh and Smith [25] developed a dialectical model that integrates these perspectives. This model integrates the mutual interaction between structure and agency, showing how structural constraints and opportunities shape actors’ decisions, while those decisions, in turn, reproduce or transform the structures [26]. Importantly, the model foregrounds relationships within networks and links causal connections between network configurations and policy outcomes [25].

Aims

This study aims to examine how policies addressing geographic health inequities is shaped within Israel’s MoH. It applies Marsh and Smith’s [25] policy network model, which emphasizes the interaction between actors, the institutional structures in which they operate, and the relationships that connect them. This perspective enables to examine who are the main actors that take part in the policy formation process and how do their positions and interdependencies affect the development and implementation of equity-oriented health policies.

Methods

Study design

A two-phased sequential mixed-methods study was conducted to evaluate the MoH initiatives through the framework of policy networks. In a sequential design, quantitative findings are generated first and examined through qualitative analysis to provide contextual explanation [26]. Accordingly, Phase 1 examined quantitative trends in health infrastructure and workforce disparities between Israel’s Northern District and the national average, while Phase 2 analyzed MoH policy documents to assess how policy networks have addressed these disparities over time.

Study process: Phase 1

Quantitative measures of health infrastructure and workforce were examined to assess disparities between Israel’s Northern District and the national average between 2009 and 2024. Specifically, the rates per 1,000 residents for physicians, nurses, healthcare personnel, and hospital beds were analyzed. These measures were chosen in order to examine the MoH’s direct impact on resource allocation aimed at reducing infrastructure disparities. Data were obtained from publicly available sources, including the Central Bureau of Statistics (CBS) and the Ministry of Health (MoH) annual reports and databases. The Northern District rates were compared to the national rates to assess relative gaps.

Descriptive statistics were used to identify trends, including whether disparities between the Northern District and the national average widened, narrowed, or remained unchanged during the study period. In addition, paired-samples t-tests were conducted for each indicator to evaluate whether the differences between the Northern District and national rates were statistically significant across time periods. To ensure robustness given the small number of time points, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were also performed. Mean rate ratios were also calculated for each measure.

SPSS version 29 was used for the analysis.

Study process: Phase 2

Analysis of MoH policy documents was conducted using qualitative content analysis [27], which is suitable for mapping documented interactions among actors and structures in policy networks [25]. Included documents were official strategies and policy papers that explicitly addressed health disparities issued between 2009 and 2024. These documents were systematically reviewed to identify the MoH’s stated objectives, priorities, and interventions aimed at reducing disparities in healthcare access and infrastructure. The analysis also focused on identifying the actors involved in these initiatives, the stages at which they participated, and the extent of their influence on decision-making within the policy network. Each initiative was examined with respect to these three aspects.

Explanatory content analysis was used to map the policy network, enabling the identification of key decision-makers and stakeholders involved in the formulation and implementation of equity-oriented policies. This process distinguished between actors internal to the MoH and those external to it and included an assessment of the relative influence and engagement of different participants.

In addition, a chronological lens was applied, tracing how the MoH’s strategic approach evolved over the study period [28]. This temporal analysis highlighted shifts in emphasis, the introduction of new initiatives, and the degree of institutionalization (or de-institutionalization) of equity objectives.

Finally, to quantify the degree of representation of representatives from the Norther District identified in the qualitative analysis, three parameters were defined: (1) the presence of representatives (yes = 1/no = 0) (2), the stage of involvement (none = 0; mapping/preliminary stage = 1; feedback/decision-making = 2), and (3) their influence on the decision (yes = 1/no = 0). Scores for each strategy could range from 0 (no representation) to 4 (high degree of representation, including participation in and influence on decision-making). Two of the researchers independently evaluated each of the ten strategies by these parameters. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa for binary variables and Weighted Cohen’s Kappa for ordinal variables. The total and mean scores for each strategy were calculated to provide an overall measure of the degree of representation across the evaluated strategies.

SPSS version 29 was used for this analysis.

Results

Phase 1: Infrastructure and workforce disparities

The findings demonstrate the trends in disparities between the Northern District compared to other districts during the study period.

Healthcare personnel

From 2009 to 2011 to 2020–2022, both the national rate and Northern District rate of physicians per 1,000 residents increased (3.3 to 3.9, and 1.7 to 3.1, respectively). Figure 1 shows the disparities in rates over the study period. The Northern District consistently had lower rates than the national average. The difference was statistically significant (t = − 8.368, df = 4, p = 0.001, 95% CI = − 1.704, − 0.855). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed the difference (z=-2.032, p = 0.042). The mean rate ratio was 0.64, meaning that across the study period, the Northern District had, on average, 64% of the national physician rate per 1,000 residents. However, there was an increase in the rate of physicians between the 2017–2019 and the 2020–2022 periods, which narrowed the disparity between the rates to 0.8.

Rate of physicians per 1,000 residents in Israel and in the Northern District during the study period

Nationally, the rate of paramedical personnel per 1,000 residents increased from 3.1 to 6.0 during the study period. Comparatively, the Northern District saw a more modest rise, from 1.9 to 4.0 (Fig. 2). This gap was statistically significant (t=-12.73, df = 4, p < 0.001, 95%CI=-1.997,-1.282). This was further confirmed with a significant Wilcoxon signed-rank test (z=-2.032, p = 0.042). The mean rate ratio was 0.638, demonstrating that over the course of the study, on average, the Northern District had 36% fewer paramedical personnel per 1,000 residents.

Rate of paramedical personnel per 1,000 residents in Israel and the Northern District during the study period

The national rate of nurses per 1,000 residents at the beginning of the study period (2009–2011) was higher than the rate of nurses per 1,000 residents in the Northern District during this same period (4.7 vs. 3.9, respectively). Conversely, the rate of nurses per 1,000 residents in the Northern District at the end of the study period was higher than the national rate (5.5 compared to 5.3).

Hospital beds

During the study period, the rate of general hospital beds per 1,000 residents declined both nationally and in the Northern District (1.97 to 1.75 and 1.50 to 1.48, respectively) (Fig. 3). The disparity between the national rate of hospitals beds and the rate of hospital beds in the Northern District was statistically significant (t=-12.02, df = 4, p < 0.001, 95%CI=-0.464, -0.29). A Wilcoxon test was also found to be significant (z=-2.032, p = 0.042). The mean rate ratio was 0.79, translating to 21% fewer hospital beds, on average, in the Northern District during the study period.

The persistence of these disparities raises questions about how national policy efforts have addressed geographic inequities. Thus, Phase 2 examined the MoH’s strategic initiatives to reduce socio-geographic inequity in health through a policy network perspective.

Phase 2: MoH policy and strategic documents

Between 2009 and 2024, the MoH published ten strategic documents aimed at reducing disparities in health. These documents varied in scope, with some addressing overarching policies that included reference to health disparities and others focusing specifically on disparities.

MoH pillars of fire

In response to growing public concern in the early 2000s about health disparities between peripheral and central regions, the MoH established a dedicated unit to address this issue in 2009. The inaugural report from the Unit for Reducing Health Inequities (URHI) highlighted significant disparities in life expectancy, mortality, and morbidity across geographical areas, noting poorer health outcomes in northern and southern districts compared to central areas [29].

In 2010, the MoH introduced the “Pillars of Fire” strategic framework, which outlined national goals to guide workplans. The “Pillars of Fire” emerged from a three-stage process. During the first stage, the current state of the Israeli healthcare system was mapped. During the second stage, goals were formulated. Finally, actionable measures were defined. External stakeholder participation varied across stages, but the process was largely concentrated within the MoH. External stakeholders, including representatives from the Northern periphery, participated during the initial mapping stage, but their involvement diminished in the later goal formulation and decision-making stages. This pattern of open consultation at the agenda setting phase, narrowing to centralized authority at the decision-making phase, is consistent with a policy network structure in which MoH functions as the dominant actor, retaining structural control over final policy outputs.

Between 2011 and 2014, the “Pillars of Fire” prioritized reducing geographic and social health inequities. The 2011 work plan fully addressed these goals, but the 2012 and 2014 plans included fewer geographic disparities targets. Beginning in 2015, subsequent iterations of the “Pillars of Fire” had minimal focus on reducing inequities and almost no focus on geographic inequities. For instance, the 2016 workplan did not include any measures related to reducing geographic inequities, while the 2019 plan included a single output measure related to hospital beds. This progressive marginalization of geographic equity across successive iterations reflects a shift in the priorities of the dominant actors within the policy network. As the MoH’s strategic agenda expanded to other areas, geographic health disparities receded from the network’s shared focus, illustrating how policy network dynamics shape not only who participates, but which issues remain on the agenda [25].

The committee to examine the expansion of medical services in the North

During the study period, there was a single strategic activity whose sole purpose was to address the issue of health inequities in the Northern District. In December 2014, the MoH appointed the Committee to Examine the Expansion of Medical Services in the North. This committee was tasked with thoroughly examining the existing medical infrastructure in the North and proposing recommendations for expanding or updating medical services.

The committee comprised 16 members, including seven from the MoH, two representatives from other government ministries, and seven members from the Northern District. The committee sought input from local experts from different organizations, such as health organizations, academic institutions, civil society organizations, and municipalities. This committee represented one of the few instances during the study period in which actors from the Northern District were directly represented within the policy formulation process.

In 2016, the committee published its recommendations [30], which included a comprehensive 15-year plan to reduce gaps between the Northern District and the rest of the country. However, based on a report from the State Ombudsman, most of the committee’s recommendations were not implemented, and monitoring ceased in 2018 [31]. As a result, despite strong representation of Northern actors, the committee’s influence on policy implementation appears to have been limited.

2017–2020 work plan to reduce health disparities

The policy process for developing the 2017–2020 work plan to reduce health disparities was led by the Unit for Reducing Health Inequities (URHI) within the MoH. This initiative was developed through a public participation process, guided by URHI insights, core structures, intervention areas, and decisions. Decisions were made on eight topics related to reducing disparities but included no details as to how these plans would be carried out. One significant decision in the strategy was to address geographic inequities through a comprehensive multi-year strategy aimed at eliminating disparities in health infrastructure between the periphery and center [32]. The process included a range of actors during the consultation stage, including public representatives and civil society organizations. However, the final formulation of the work plan remained with the URHI and external actors participated primarily in early agenda-setting stages.

A national set of health inequity indicators

The development of a national set of health inequity indicators in 2020 involved three stages within the MoH: (1) identifying potential indicators; (2) establishing criteria for rating the indicators; and (3) consulting with experts and public representatives, including from the geographic peripheries. While external actors participated during the consultation stage, the final determination of indicators remained centralized within the MoH.

The national indicators selected include indicators related to healthcare workforce capacity and hospital beds and were to be published alongside the indicators from the National Program for Quality Indicators in Community Healthcare. However, data related to healthcare workforce capacity and hospital beds have not yet been published.

Health system 2032 – preparation phase

In 2023, the MoH began formulating another overarching strategic plan: Health System 2032. The formulation of this strategic plan involved multiple stages of stakeholder consultation led by the MoH [33]. The preparation phase included mapping key trends expected to shape the healthcare system in the coming decade, outlining key performance indicators within the system, and collecting data from various stakeholders. It involved collecting data through different channels and from various stakeholders and utilized three arenas of engagement: national councils in Israel, civil society organizations, and healthcare providers. This process occurred at three focal points and involved the senior managers from each location: the Health Bureau headquarters in the central region, a Health Maintenance Organization (HMO) headquarters in the Northern District, and a hospital in the Southern District.

During these preparation meetings, participants were presented with the strategic vision for Health System 2032, guiding principles (increasing trust and equality in the healthcare system) and key topics to be addressed (dispersion of healthcare services, health system workforce, system structure and governance, and adapting economic tools to system needs), and were asked to provide feedback. However, despite incorporating in-depth consultation with actors from different sectors and geographic regions, the final decision-making authority remained with the MoH. Once approved by the Minister of Health and Director-General, a detailed implementation plan was expected to follow.

The committee for establishing a strategy to reduce disparities for the next decade

In 2021, the MoH established a committee to formulate a strategy for reducing health inequities by 2032. The committee was tasked with identifying actions needed to reduce health inequities in Israel and enable the realization of the right to health for all.

The committee was comprised of 30 members representing government ministries, the healthcare system, academic institutions, and civil society organizations. Among the members were four representatives from geographic peripheries, but none from the Northern District. The committee provided eight recommendations. Three recommendations address inequality directly (goal setting to eradicate inequality; strengthening the MoH’s governance to reduce inequality; and reducing inequality during the first years of life). Two additional recommendations addressed specific bases of inequities. The last recommendation pertained to establishing a local health governance area. However, none addressed geographic-based inequalities [34]. From a policy networks perspective, the committee’s composition reflects the structural configuration of the MoH-led network at this stage: none of the 30 members represented the Northern District, positioning peripheral geographic actors outside the network’s core during the formulation process.

Committee for long-term planning of the healthcare workforce in Israel

Part of MoH’s strategic activities focused on workforce and hospitalization infrastructure [35]. A committee established by the MoH and the National Institute for Health Policy Research examined what policies could increase the medical workforce in the northern and southern peripheries. From a policy networks perspective, the committee included senior officials from the MoH, the Ministry of Finance, health maintenance organizations, academia, and hospitals, but did not include representatives from the Northern District [36].

The committee published its recommendations in 2022. The main recommendations were to reduce the infrastructure disparity, particularly hospital beds, and to increase the number of students with initial residency ties to underserved areas. The latter recommendation was rooted in existing trends in Israel showing that peripheral districts rely heavily on physicians who studied abroad.

The MOH policy on addressing physician shortages in israel and strengthening the negev and Galilee

Following the recommendations of MoH’s workforce committee and the Council for Higher Education, the MoH developed a reform to address the national physician shortage. This reform had a particular focus on the personnel shortage in peripheral areas. The reform was based on the work of the committees, analysis of solutions implemented abroad, and the commission of research institutes to conduct studies. Additionally, focus groups were conducted with target audiences in the northern and southern peripheries, two conferences were held, and meetings were open for anyone interested in voicing their opinion. This reform involved a relatively broad set of actors, including academic institutions, research organizations, and stakeholders from peripheral regions who participated through focus groups, conferences, and advisory consultations.

The final reform includes several programs to enhance the medical field in peripheral areas, including programs to cultivate medical talent. This is primarily achieved by strengthening existing centers of excellence and mapping potential centers of excellence within these regions. The plan, published in early 2023, comprises 13 separate programs [37] designed to address different aspects of the healthcare workforce.

One program is the “Ilanot” program (“trees” in Hebrew), which allows medical students with local ties to the periphery to study at institutions in peripheral areas while receiving leadership training. In return, they commit to working as physicians in the periphery. The “Ofakim” program (“horizons” in Hebrew) provides loans to medical students studying abroad. Each of these programs aims to include up to 60 students or interns annually, approximately 8% of the new license holders who studied in Israel in 2023.

The MoH tailored the workforce programs for the southern and northern peripheries to meet the unique needs of these areas. This effort is being carried out in collaboration with an advisory committee made up of various stakeholders operating in these regions, including senior hospital-based healthcare professionals, health maintenance organizations, cluster heads, mayors, leading civil society representatives, and business sector representatives. However, the final design and implementation of the reform remains under the authority of the MoH.

National plan for data-driven infrastructure deployment

The MoH is currently developing a national plan for data-driven infrastructure deployment, led by the Strategic and Economic Planning Division and MoH’s Computing Department. This initiative aims to address healthcare services that do not always meet the population’s needs and areas that experience shortages or imbalances. The plan serves as a central platform for collaboration and cross-functional work between the Strategic and Economic Planning Division and other departments and units within the MoH, and government ministries. So far, the findings of the plan focus on the planning of dialysis infrastructure deployment and pharmacy distribution planning. The development of this initiative is highly centralized within the MoH, with no evidence of participation from actors representing peripheral regions or other external stakeholders.

A multi-year bed allocation plan

In early 2024, the MoH’s Medical Directorate Division published a multi-year bed allocation plan for the healthcare system covering the years 2023–2028 [38]. The plan allocates 1,791 hospital beds over these years, with 280 designated for the Northern District (15.6% of new beds).

The distribution of beds among hospitals was determined following a comprehensive review process. This process included mapping the current situation from a national perspective, identifying needs from the field, and analyzing various parameters such as occupancy rates, demographic changes, clinical aspects, and workloads. The planning process was led by actors within the MoH, with no direct participation from external representatives in the decision-making stage. The plan acknowledged the need to strengthen hospitals based on specific requirements and aimed to reduce disparities between peripheral and central regions.

These initiatives demonstrate a recurring pattern in the policy network: while peripheral actors were occasionally consulted on policies related to geographic inequities, the decision-making authority remained centralized within the MoH, with limited representation of external actors, including representatives from the Northern district.

Based on the findings of the qualitative analysis, three factors may explain the persistence of disparities despite investment in reducing geographic disparities: lack of robust representation of the Northern District in policy formulation (i.e. limited, early-stage representation without decision influence), centralized decision-making power of the MoH, and decreased focus on geographic health disparities. The interplay between these factors also contributes to the persistence of disparities as the lack of robust representation and the centralized decision-making power promote a decreased focus on geographic health disparities in policymaking (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This sequential mixed-methods study found that significant disparities in healthcare workforce and infrastructure between Israel’s Northern District and national rates persisted throughout the study period, despite the existence of national policy efforts that addressed healthcare planning and resource allocation over the past 15 years. An analysis of 10 strategic policy documents through a policy network lens identified three factors that may account for this persistence: the limited and largely non-influential representation of Northern District actors in policy formulation, the centralized concentration of decision-making authority within the MoH, and a declining policy focus on geographic health disparities over time. Together, these structural and actor-related factors help explain why quantitative disparities identified in phase 1 were not meaningfully reduced and suggest that policy efforts during this period were insufficiently targeted toward reducing geographic inequities.

Globally, a significant challenge to healthcare in peripheral areas is access to healthcare, which contributes to poorer health outcomes [39–42]. Studies in the United States (US) show that hospitals in the periphery have fewer resources, translating to higher instances of substandard care [40]. Access to healthcare services remains a top priority among stakeholders in these areas, highlighting an inequitable distribution of healthcare resources [39].

Access to healthcare also remains a major challenge in Israel’s Northern District. Previous studies have found that healthcare utilization rates in the Northern District are lower than in the Central District. However, this trend may be driven by limited resources rather than reduced demand. For example, patients in the northern region are less likely to receive inpatient rehabilitation compared to those in central Israel, largely due to restricted service availability [43]. In addition, individuals in peripheral regions (both the north and the south) are less likely to have complementary insurance provided by HMOs. This type of insurance, which requires an additional monthly fee beyond universal coverage, provides access to services such as specialist visits, child development services, alternative treatments, and certain private procedures [44].

Evidence from emergency care also suggests underutilization of healthcare services even in life-threatening situations. In a study conducted in three hospitals in northern Israel, many patients in life-threatening conditions arrived independently rather than by ambulance, despite the clinical urgency of their condition. Qualitative findings identified multiple access-related barriers, including long travel distances in rural areas, limited emergency response capacity, uncertainty regarding how to access care, and financial concerns related to ambulance use [45].

In light of findings from previous studies, our findings can be interpreted as indicating that lower healthcare utilization in the Northern District may be related, at least in part, to limited service availability rather than lower healthcare needs. The present study did not directly examine healthcare utilization; therefore, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously. However, the persistent workforce and infrastructure gaps identified in our quantitative analysis provide a plausible mechanism through which access to care may be limited in the Northern District. The quantitative analysis in this study shows ongoing significant disparities in medical staff and infrastructure. While gaps in physicians and hospital beds have narrowed, in part due to a national decline in beds and residents returning after studying abroad, paramedical disparities are growing. Across all measures, the Northern District consistently had fewer personnel and beds than the national average, with rate ratios indicating 20% to over 30% lower coverage on average. These trends reflect global issues in peripheral areas, such as difficulty attracting medical personnel and limited resources [46–48].

Lower ratios of physicians, paramedical personnel, and hospital beds to residents may limit the availability of services, increase waiting times, and require residents to travel longer distances to receive care. These barriers may be particularly important in peripheral and rural areas, where the geographic distribution of services can further affect residents’ ability to use available healthcare resources.

Thus, the improvements observed in workforce indicators should be interpreted cautiously. Although the number of physicians and other healthcare personnel in the Northern District has increased over time, the region continues to lag behind national averages across most measures. Moreover, some of these improvements may reflect broader national dynamics rather than targeted policy efforts to reduce geographic inequities.

This was most evident in the narrowing gap in physician density between 2017 and 2019 and 2020–2022. No specific policy was identified during the study period that could account for this increase. Further examination suggests it may be related to a regulatory change affecting the recognition of foreign medical schools: graduates of institutions disqualified from 2019 onwards are ineligible to sit the Israeli medical licensing examination, and the Northern District has a disproportionately high proportion of physicians trained at such institutions.

The Northern and Southern Districts are characterized by a particularly high proportion of graduates from disqualified institutions. In 2022, 34% of all newly licensed physicians in Israel had studied at disqualified institutions abroad, compared to the Northern District, where 63% of newly licensed physicians had studied at such institutions. Thus, the increase in the number of physicians in the Northern District during 2020–2022 may be attributed to the absorption of a large proportion of physicians who studied at disqualified institutions. This may indicate that improvements in workforce indicators were not necessarily driven by sustained policy attention to geographic inequities. As such, the sustainability of these workforce gains remains uncertain, and they should not be interpreted as evidence of effective policy-driven progress toward geographic equity.

The qualitative review of ten policy documents showed varying attention to socio-geographic disparities, with only four including representation from the Northern District. This analysis highlighted three factors that can explain why these disparities persist: lack of robust representation of the Northern District in the policy formulation process, the centralized decision-making power of the MoH, and decreasing focus on geographic health disparities. These stakeholder- and structural-related factors can be understood through the lens of the Policy Networks theory [18].

Lack of robust representation of the Northern District in the policy formulation process

In most instances identified in this analysis, the MoH reported engaging in collaborative efforts to develop policy, a key factor in collaborative networks [49]. These included input from experts, academia, non-governmental organizations, and affected residents. However, the persistence of disparities suggests that representation of the Northern District in policy formulation was minimal or had limited impact on the final decision. This was evident when the findings were quantified.

Of the ten policy initiatives identified and included, only four included representation from the Northern District. Only one of these included participation during the decision-making stage and influence on the decision. There were an additional two instances where peripheral representation was included, but not from the Northern District. This may have resulted in policies that did not fully address the specific challenges faced by this area.

Centralized decision-making power of the MOH

Policy Network Theory posits that policymaking is shaped by interactions among various actors who bring their expertise, priorities, and resources to the process [25]. These networks are typically more effective when they allow for open collaboration [49]. However, this analysis revealed that the MoH maintained primary control over most of the processes and the final decisions.

In seven out of 10 cases, the MoH retained ultimate authority in the decision-making process. In two of these cases, there were no external actors involved in the decision-making process at all. For the other five cases, external actors were asked for feedback or were only involved in the earlier stages of policy development. These findings are consistent with the structural properties of a centralized policy network, in which decision-making is concentrated within a single dominant actor, in this case the MoH, while external stakeholders including those from geographic peripheries, remain at the margins of the decision-making process [49].

Decreased focus on geographic health disparities

The decrease in focus on geographic health disparities over time reflects how shifting priorities within a policy network can influence the policymaking process. Policymaking is shaped by the interactions, priorities, and power dynamics among the actors involved [25]. When certain issues lose prominence in the agenda, such as geographic health disparities, it is often a result of changes within the network of actors.

For instance, at the beginning of the study period, geographic health disparities were explicitly addressed in the 2011–2014 “Pillars of Fire” strategy, suggesting that the issue was a priority for key actors in the policy network. However, subsequent iterations of the “Pillars of Fire” either removed or minimized the focus on this topic, coinciding with prioritization of other issues. This may have been driven by dominant actors emphasizing other policy areas, or in response to external pressures or public health crises (e.g. the Covid-19 pandemic). A well-functioning policy network relies on sustained interactions among diverse stakeholders to ensure that ongoing issues remain on the agenda [49].

Examining the policy initiatives chronologically also demonstrates the evolution of how geographic health disparities were addressed over the study period. Early initiatives explicitly prioritized reducing geographic health inequities and included dedicated policy measures targeting peripheral regions. In contrast, later initiatives increasingly incorporated disparities within broader system-wide reforms, with fewer explicit measures focused on geographic inequalities. This shift suggests that geographic disparities gradually became less prominent as a standalone policy priority, even as overall health system planning continued to evolve.

Taken together, these three factors do not operate independently, they form a self-reinforcing cycle within the policy network. The centralized concentration of decision-making authority within the MoH limits the influence of peripheral actors, even when they are nominally included in consultative processes. This structural marginalization, in turn, reduces the pressure on the network’s dominant actors to maintain a sustained focus on geographic health disparities as a policy priority. As peripheral voices carry less weight in shaping the agenda, geographic inequity gradually recedes from the network’s shared focus, making it less likely that future initiatives will address the structural conditions that perpetuate it.

This dynamic is illustrated in Fig. 4. Marsh and Smith’s [25] dialectical model helps explain this cycle: network structures shape actor agency, and actor positions, particularly the dominance of centrally located MoH actors, reproduce the very network structures that limit peripheral influence. Breaking this cycle likely requires intervening at the structural level, not only through resource allocation but through formal mechanisms that embed Northern District representation at the decision-making stage.

Disparity persistence through a global lens

Persistent health disparities between peripheral and central areas are a global issue [40, 46, 47]. Representation in policymaking, especially from the geographic periphery, is crucial for effective health policy [48]. Without it, peripheral areas may receive less attention and investment, which may contribute to poorer outcomes. For example, a US study found maternal mortality review committees often overlooked rural residents, despite their poorer health and limited care access [50].

Similar to this study’s findings, stakeholder engagement in other countries often occurs early and remains limited, which can hinder policy implementation [51]. Stakeholder engagement is particularly important for developing sustainable structural interventions aimed at “changing the social, physical or political environments that may shape or constrain health behaviors and outcomes” of populations experiencing health disparities [52].

In contrast to the centralized governance structures identified in this study, decentralization of healthcare governance has been implemented in other countries. However, the impact on health inequities largely depends on the implementation context [53, 54]. In Spain, for example, regional differences in access to care and health financing were minimal, whereas Switzerland and China reported significant disparities [54]. Thus, implementing decentralization of health governance requires careful consideration of the local context [53, 55].

A new litmus test for Israel

The Swords of Iron war, following the October 7, 2023 attack, heavily impacted Israel’s Northern District, prompting evacuations and infrastructure damage. In response, the government launched Decision 1786 to support recovery, emphasizing emergency services, prevention, and infrastructure [56]. While decision 1786 represents a significant governmental commitment to the rehabilitation of the Northern District, it was formulated primarily in response to an acute crisis rather than as a structural reform of health governance. It is therefore unclear whether it addresses the systemic factors identified in this study, namely, the limited representation of Northern District actors in national policy formulation and the centralized concentration of decision-making authority within the MoH. The extent to which the plan reflects or addresses the long-standing structural inequities examined here remains to be seen.

The findings of this study suggest that sustainable progress in reducing geographic health disparities will require not only resource allocation, but structural changes to the policy network itself, specifically, meaningful representation of Northern District actors at the decision-making stage and an explicit, sustained policy focus on geographic equity. Decision 1786 may offer an opportunity to advance these goals, but its effectiveness will depend on whether its implementation genuinely incorporates peripheral stakeholders rather than replicating the centralized patterns identified over the preceding 15 years.

Limitations

There are several limitations that should be considered in this study. First, the qualitative findings indicate associations and do not establish causal effects on quantitative disparities, as causal inference is beyond this study’s scope. Second, per-capita workforce and bed rates, while standard system indicators, may not fully capture need-adjusted access or service capacity. Third, the qualitative analysis was limited to official MoH policy documents and did not include interviews with policymakers, Northern District stakeholders, or community representatives. This limits insight into the motivations behind policy decisions and the lived experience of those affected by the disparities documented. Fourth, the study period spans 2009–2024 and encompasses major external disruptions, including the COVID-19 pandemic and the October 7, 2023 war and its aftermath, both of which may have significantly altered policy priorities and healthcare delivery in the Northern District in ways that this analysis cannot fully disentangle from longer-term structural trends. Finally, the study examined ten policy documents that explicitly addressed health disparities; policy decisions made through other governmental channels that may have affected the Northern District were not included in the analysis, which may result in an incomplete picture of the policy landscape.

Conclusion

Despite 15 years of efforts, disparities in per-capita infrastructure and workforce in the Northern District remained. Key issues include the exclusion of peripheral stakeholders and centralized decision-making. While Decision 1786 signals a commitment to local involvement, its development without local input raises questions regarding its effectiveness in addressing the region’s specific needs.
