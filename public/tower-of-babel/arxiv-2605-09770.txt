Encoding and Decoding Temporal Signals with Spiking Bandpass Wavelets
Jens E. Pedersen, Tony Lindeberg, Peter Gerstoft

Abstract
Spike-based encodings are sparse and energy-efficient, but have largely been formulated probabilistically, disconnected from most signal processing literature. We recast spike encoders as time-causal wavelet frames with quantitative bandwidths and reconstruction error bounds. The proposed wavelets preserve the sparsity and locality of spiking representations, with reconstruction up to spike quantization and time discretization. We demonstrate reconstruction on ECG and audio datasets, achieving a normalized RMSE comparable to continuous wavelet transforms. The spiking wavelets map directly to neuromorphic hardware.



1
Introduction

Analog-to-digital conversion (ADC) underpins every digital signal processing pipeline.
Uniform Nyquist-Shannon sampling is a well-understood approach, but it is agnostic to signal structure: it indiscriminately samples both silence and activity.

Biological sensory systems sample differently.
Rather than measuring at a fixed rate, spikes are emitted only when the input changes, producing sparse, event-driven, and highly energy-efficient representations.
A leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron implements a time-causal convolution (
11
) followed by a threshold comparator, encoding change as spikes rather than amplitude as samples.
Neuromorphic hardware strives to exploit this efficiency, but a principled signal-processing framework for spike-based encoding is missing.

The encoded representations should be (1) localized in frequency, (2) scale covariant, and (3) equipped with formal bounds that guarantee stable reconstruction regardless of the input.
Wavelet filter banks satisfy all three properties by construction
(
5
)
and are the natural mathematical home for a principled spike-based ADC.
A spiking analogue is missing: a wavelet frame whose coefficients are emitted as spike events rather than continuous amplitudes.
Such a frame would inherit the covariant, multi-resolution of classical wavelet theory, while producing sparse, event-driven output.

Existing spike-based encoders provide partial solutions.
Neuroscience-driven models are typically presented with information-theoretic
(
27
)
or probabilistic
(
6
)
foundations rather than signal processing guarantees.
Recent work explores the integrate-and-fire model from a signal processing perspective
(
20
)
, but does not extend to multi-scale frames.
Sigma-delta (
\Sigma\Delta
) modulators
(
28
)
offer recovery guarantees but are single-channel or restricted to shift-invariant subspaces.
Time encoding machines (TEM)
(
1
)
operate on fixed shift-invariant subspaces rather than scale-parameterized families.
Prior work by
23
and
24
established covariance guarantees for LIF neurons and sketched a spiking wavelet construction. Neither of those, however, provides formal frame definitions, reconstruction guarantees, nor error bounds or quantitative experimental evaluation.

We address this gap with two time-causal wavelet families realized as spiking encoders: difference of truncated exponentials (DoE), built from a single leaky integrator per scale, and difference of time-causal limit kernel (DoT), using cascades of leaky integrators (
Figure

1
).

The contributions are as follows:

•

Two spiking time-causal scale covariant wavelet families, based on difference of truncated exponentials (DoE) (
19
) and difference of time-causal limit kernels (DoT) (
18
), with a complementary theoretical analysis of the underlying non-spiking wavelet representations.

•

Frame bounds (
22
) and a closed-form reconstruction error bound (
32
) for both analog and spiking variants, extending Time Encoding Machines
(
12
;
7
;
1
)
to overcomplete multi-scale frames.

•

Reconstruction experiments on ECG signals (MIT-BIH
(
19
)
) and audio (LibriSpeech
(
21
)
) reaching nRMSE values comparable to classical and continuous wavelets, with direct compilation to existing neuromorphic platforms via the Neuromorphic Intermediate Representation
(
22
)
.

2.1
Scale spaces

Scale-space theory parameterizes a signal
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
over a scale parameter
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
into the scaled representation
L\colon\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}

(
11
)
.
Scaling
L
corresponds to convolving the signal
f(t)
with smoothing kernels
h(t;\ \sigma)

(
14
, (1.2))

\displaystyle L(t;\ \sigma)=h(t;\ \sigma)*f(t)=\int_{u=-\infty}^{\infty}h(u;\ \sigma)\,f(t-u){\rm d}u,

(1)

where
*
denotes convolution, with boundary condition

L(t;\,\sigma=0)=f(t).

(2)

Scale-space kernels
h
are required to diminish variations over scales and to form a continuous semi-group under convolution, leaving the Gaussian with mean
0
and standard deviation
\sigma
as the unique admissible kernel over spatial domains (App.
D
)

h_{\rm Gauss}(t;\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}.

(3)

Importantly, the Gaussian has a physical interpretation as the Green’s function of the heat equation, over the variance-based scale parameter
\tau=\sigma^{2}

(
11
, p. 370)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}L(t;\tau)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}L(t;\tau).

(4)

Scale-space representations are scale covariant
16
, or scale equivariant.
Consider the scaling transformation
t^{\prime}=s\,t
and the corresponding scale parameter transformation
\sigma^{\prime}=s\,\sigma
for any scaling factor
s>0
.
A scale-covariant representation satisfies

L^{\prime}(t^{\prime};\,\sigma^{\prime})=L(t;\,\sigma),

(5)

meaning that the scale-space representation
L^{\prime}
over the transformed domain captures the same structural information as
L
, but observed at a different scale.

Representing a signal at multiple scales requires a set of scale levels
\sigma_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, between some minimum scale
\sigma_{1}
and maximum scale
\sigma_{K}
.
A logarithmic spread of scales is a natural choice to retain self-similarity
16
and to describe temporal relations in memory
9
.
Denote the ratio between adjacent scale levels as
c
, with the default choices
c=\{\sqrt{2},2\}
:

\sigma_{k}=c\,\sigma_{k-1}.

(6)

2.1.1
Time-causal scale spaces

For time-causal operations, a continuous semi-group structure is not possible: the causality constraint restricts us to one-sided kernels which cannot generate a continuous family of smoothing kernel while retaining variation-diminishing properties

Time-causal scale spaces are constructed by cascading

\mu_{k}=c\,\mu_{k-1},\quad k=1,\ldots,K,

(8)

for some

\displaystyle u(t)=\begin{cases}0&z(t)=1,\\

(10)

3
Scale-covariant bandpass representations

Sensory and physical signals carry features over multiple temporal scales.
To account for all scales in a spiking, or quantized, encoder, a suitable representation must (1) decompose into discrete scale channels, (2) be covariant under temporal rescaling, respecting the self-similarity of multi-scale signals, and (3) remove constant components to admit wavelet frames.
Following the bandpass representation based on a Laplacian pyramid
(
3
)
, the notion of scale-space representation
(
11
)
, the multiresolution decomposition of wavelet frames
(
18
)
, and specifically the time-causal bandpass representation in
17
,
we obtain all the three by taking adjacent differences of scale-space representations:
L
(
1
) is covariant by construction, and the difference channels ensure responses vanish at
\omega=0
.
By the heat equation (
4
), differentiating adjacent scale levels approximates the scale derivative, which equals the Laplacian up to a constant:

\frac{L(t;\sigma+\Delta\sigma)-L(t;\sigma)}{\Delta\sigma}\approx\partial_{\sigma}L(t;\sigma)=\sigma\,\partial_{tt}L(t;\sigma).

(12)

Setting
\Delta\sigma
according to the geometric scale spacing (
6
) yields a covariant representation,

\Delta L(t;\,\sigma_{k},c)=L(t;\,\sigma_{k},c)-L(t;\,\sigma_{k-1},c)=\psi(t;\sigma_{k},c)*f(t),

(13)

where
\psi
is a bandpass kernel.
In the Gaussian case (
3
), the bandpass representation has a specific interpretation.
The diffusion identity makes
\Delta L\approx\Delta\sigma\;\sigma\ \partial_{tt}L
an approximate Laplacian-of-Gaussian filter, the prototype bandpass response.

An exact reconstruction of a signal at scale
\sigma_{j}
is achieved by summing the representations along with a lowpass residual at the coarsest scale
\sigma_{K}
(seen by inserting (
13
) into (
14
))

L(t;\,\sigma_{j},c)=L(t;\,\sigma_{K},c)-\sum_{k=j+1}^{K}\Delta L(t;\,\sigma_{k},c)=h(t;\sigma_{K})*f(t)-\sum_{k=j+1}^{K}\psi(t;\sigma_{k},c)*f(t).

(14)

Setting
\sigma_{j}=0
, gives
L(t;\,0)=f(t)
(
2
), and we can reconstruct a wavelet frame (
41
) based on (
14
) by summing over subsequent scales:

\tilde{f}(t)=L(t;\,0,c)=h(t;\,\sigma_{K})*f(t)-\sum^{K}_{k=1}\psi(t;\,\sigma_{k},c)*f(t).

(15)

Throughout,
K
denotes the number of bandpass channels and the full filterbank has
K+1
channels (
K
bandpass and one lowpass residual at scale
\sigma_{K}
).
We construct an overcomplete frame rather than an orthonormal wavelet basis, where the lowpass residual corresponds to the scaling function in a finite-level multiresolution analysis
(
18
, (9))
.
Next, we will make use of three candidates for the bandpass kernel
\psi
.

Difference-of-Gaussian bandpass wavelet

Since wavelets define scale and shift-parameterized functions, we define a wavelet system based on Gaussians (
3
), dubbed the
difference-of-Gaussians (DoG)
, with zero mean and admissible according to (
59

\psi_{\rm DoG}(t;\sigma_{k},c)=h_{\rm Gauss}(t;\sigma_{k})-h_{\rm Gauss}(t;\sigma_{k-1}).

(16)

4.1
Causal scale-covariant wavelets

Based on the Laplace transform of the causal and variance diminishing constraints (see App.

(18)

Difference-of-truncated-exponentials bandpass wavelet

For

The bandwidth properties of the causal wavelets determine the range of frequencies they can resolve.
We determine the highest peak frequency for DoE and DoT, defining the supported range of frequencies for the wavelet systems

\displaystyle\omega_{\rm DoT\ peak}

\displaystyle\leqslant{c}/{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}\,\sigma_{1}}={1}/{\mu_{1}},\qquad\omega_{\rm DoE\ peak}={\sqrt{c}}/{\mu_{1}}.

5
Spiking scale-covariant wavelets

We define spiking wavelets by quantizing bandpass wavelet responses as streams of signed spikes, whose dual can be approximated to recover the original signal up to a bounded error that we find to be linear in the spiking threshold.

5.1
Spiking polarity channels

One-bit spikes do not carry any sign information.
To capture the polarity in the encoding step, we split the integration from (
9

\mu_{k}\dot{u}=\dot{u}_{k}^{+}-\dot{u}_{k}^{-},

\displaystyle\mu_{k}\dot{u}^{+}=-u^{+}_{k}+f(t),\qquad\mu_{k}\dot{u}^{-}=-u^{-}_{k}-f(t).

(25)

When

crosses

This approximate reconstruction offers three advantages:

(1) The computation is entirely time-recursive and no external history or state is required to process the filter states, enabling real-time processing with bounded memory.

(2) The implementation is time-causal and implementable as differences of leaky integrators, which maps directly to neuromorphic hardware.

(3) The overall scheme preserves scale covariance, since both the encoding wavelets and reconstruction filters scale covariantly with temporal scaling (

5.3
Reconstruction and quantization errors

The quality of the reconstruction depends on the spectral decay of the bandpass channels and the quantization error introduced by the spiking encoding.
The faster spectral decay for the DoT means that each DoT channel has a well-defined effective bandwidth
\Omega_{k}
, which guarantees that the tail energy outside
\Omega_{k}
is bounded for any desired tolerance.
For the DoE, the slower decay requires additional assumptions on the signal.

For spiking encoding with firing threshold
\theta_{\rm thr}
, the per-channel quantization error
\epsilon_{k}
is bounded by
\lVert\epsilon_{k}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\,\theta_{\rm thr}\,\Omega_{k}

(
4
)
, where
\Omega_{k}
is the effective bandwidth of channel
k
and
C
is a constant.
Each channel’s effective bandwidth scales as
\Omega_{k}=\Omega_{1}/c^{k-1}
by the geometric scale grid (
6

K
bandpass channels and the lowpass residual gives a geometric series in
1/c
:

\lVert f-\widetilde{f}\rVert_{\infty}\leq C\,\theta_{\rm thr}\big(\Omega_{K}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{k}\big).

(32)

Notably, this reconstruction error is linear in the spike threshold
\theta_{\rm thr}
, see Figure
8
for a demonstration.
Since the peak frequencies
\Omega_{k}
are geometrically spaced
\Omega_{k}=\Omega_{1}/c^{k}
because of the self-similarity over scales, the error bound (

7
Discussion

We contributed two time-causal and scale-covariant families of spiking wavelets as sparse continuous-time encoders.
Our method relies on the DoE and DoT bandpass wavelets, for which we derived frame bounds and error bounds.
We established that both of the underlying non-spiking DoT and DoE wavelets form overcomplete non-tight frames and derived closed-form reconstruction-error bounds for the spiking wavelets.
We demonstrated that these spiking wavelets match the reconstruction of the non-causal Morlet and causal Szu wavelets, while remaining causal and sparse.

A strength of this construction is its generality: the frame bounds and reconstruction guarantees hold for any finite-energy input, with no assumptions on signal statistics, stationarity, and sparsity of bandwidth beyond the chosen scale range.

Continuous-time spiking dynamics are simulated on a digital substrate at fixed time intervals.
The method’s natural home is event-driven neuromorphic hardware, where continuous dynamics are native and the discretization artifacts disappear.
Measuring the energy and latency benefits for analog or mixed-signal platforms is an obvious next step.
We use a least-squares decoder to assess the reconstruction quality of the spiking code.
This establishes the upper bound that any causal decoder must approach.
Closing the gap to online, streaming, decoders is a well-posed follow-up now that the target is known.

Because every operation in the spiking wavelets reduces to a leaky-integrator difference with spike thresholding, the encoders map directly onto primitives common to neuromorphic platforms, positioning spiking wavelets as a principled building block for neuromorphic analog-to-digital conversion.
The wavelets encode time-varying signals into sparse spike trains with provable reconstruction guarantees.
Based on this theory and experiments, we propose the presented spiking DoE and DoT wavelets as fundamental spiking representations for neuromorphic processing.

C.1
Frame operator

Frames are overcomplete and continuous-time expansions.

\psi_{j}

and
B
where

5

A\|f\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\langle f,\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant B\|f\|^{2}.

When

, the frame is tight, providing uniform representations across scales.
When

A frame operator

transforms a continuous-time signal from

, for

denoting the frame index, such that

\Lambda\big(f(t)\big)_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}=\langle f(t),\psi_{j}\rangle=\sum_{j\in J}f(t)\,a_{0}^{-m/2}\overline{\psi(a_{0}^{-m}t-nb_{0})}.

(37)

Since

is bounded, the Hermitian adjoint is well defined
\Lambda^{*}:l_{2}(\mathbb{J})\to L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\Lambda^{*}\big(\Lambda(f(t))\big)=\sum_{j}\langle f(t),\psi_{j}\rangle\,\psi_{j},

(38)

which we use to define a dual family of vectors
\tilde{\psi}_{j}\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R})

\tilde{\psi}_{j}=\left(\Lambda^{*}\Lambda\right)^{-1}\,\psi_{j},

(39)

bounded by
A
and
B
(
36
)
[
5
, (3.2.6)]

B^{-}1\|f(t)\|^{2}\leqslant\sum_{j\in J}|\langle f(t),\tilde{\psi}_{j}\rangle|^{2}\leqslant A^{-1}\|f\|^{2}.

(40)

The dual frame operator (
39
) permits signal reconstruction
[
5
, (3.2.8)]

f(t)=\sum_{j\in J}\langle\,f,\psi_{j}\,\rangle\,\tilde{\psi}_{j}.

(41)

E.2
Difference of truncated exponential (DoE) wavelets

One admissible time-causal scale space kernel is the first-order integrator

Composing several kernels, corresponding to time domain convolution, yields a hierarchy over scales for some signal

\displaystyle L(t;\mu_{0})

\displaystyle=f(t)\quad\text{(initial signal)}

\displaystyle L(t;\mu_{1})

\displaystyle=f(t)*h_{1}(t;\mu_{1})

\displaystyle L(t;\mu_{k})

\displaystyle=f(t)*h_{k}(t;\mu_{k})*\dots*h_{1}(t;\mu_{1}).

Next, consider the convolution at scale
\mu_{k}
and its time derivative

\displaystyle L(t;\mu_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}L(t-\xi;\,\mu_{k-1})\,{\rm d}\xi

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L(t;\mu_{k})}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial t}\,{\rm d}\xi.

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1}),\quad

\displaystyle du

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial\xi}d\xi=-\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial t}d\xi

\displaystyle dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{k}}e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}d\xi,\quad

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=-e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}

(80)

and integrate (

) by parts, recalling that

\displaystyle L(t;\mu_{k})

\displaystyle=\left[L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})\cdot(-e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}})\right]_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(-e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}\right)\left(-\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial t}\right)d\xi

\displaystyle=\left[-e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})\right]_{0}^{\infty}-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial t}d\xi

\displaystyle=L(t;\mu_{k-1})-\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\xi/\mu_{k}}\frac{\partial L(t-\xi;\mu_{k-1})}{\partial t}d\xi=L(t;\mu_{k-1})-\mu_{k}\frac{\partial L(t;\mu_{k})}{\partial t},

E.3
Bandwidth limits for DoE and DoT wavelets

We proceed to define the bandwidth limits for the DoE and DoT wavelets.
Starting with the DoE, recall its Laplace transform (

\frac{1+(\mu_{k}/c)\,i\omega-1-\mu_{k}\,i\omega}{(1+\mu_{k}\,i\omega)(1+(\mu_{k}/c)\,i\omega)}=\frac{{\mu_{k}}(1/c-1)\,i\omega}{1+{\mu_{k}}(1+1/c)\,i\omega-(\mu_{k}^{2}/c)\,\omega^{2}}

(92)

E.3.1
Peak frequencies

The peak frequency response is found when

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}\omega}|\widehat{\psi}_{\rm DoE}(\omega)|^{2}=0.

(93)

Taking the squared magnitude of (
92
)

\displaystyle|\widehat{\psi}_{\rm DoE}|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega^{2}\mu_{k}^{2}(1/c-1)^{2}}{1-2\mu_{k}^{2}\omega^{2}/c+\mu^{4}_{k}\omega^{4}/c^{2}+\mu_{k}^{2}\omega^{2}(1+1/c)^{2}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1}}{{=}}\frac{u(1/c-1)^{2}}{1+u(1+1/c^{2})+u^{2}/c^{2}},

(94)

where step
1

u=\mu_{k}^{2}\omega^{2}
.
By differentiating with respect to

\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}u}|\widehat{\psi}_{\rm DoE}|^{2}=\frac{(1/c-1)^{2}(1+u(1+1/c^{2})+u^{2}/c^{2})-u(1/c-1)^{2}\left((1+1/c^{2})+2u/c^{2}\right)}{(1+u(1+1/c^{2})+u^{2}/c^{2})^{2}}.

(95)

Isolating the two terms in the numerator, we get

\displaystyle(1/c-1)^{2}\left(1+u(1+1/c^{2})+u^{2}/c^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=u(1/c-1)^{2}\left((1+1/c^{2})+2u/c^{2})\right)\iff

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=u^{2}/c^{2}\iff\qquad u=c.

(96)

Re-introducing
u=\mu_{k}^{2}\omega^{2}
, we have for the DoE

\mu_{k}^{2}\omega_{\rm peak}^{2}=c\quad\iff\quad\omega_{\rm peak}^{DoE}=\frac{\sqrt{c}}{\mu_{k}}.

(97)

For the DoT wavelet, recall its Fourier transform (
66
) involving an infinite product.
The squared magnitude of a single bandpass channel is

\left|\widehat{\psi}_{\rm DoT}(i\omega;\sigma_{k},c)\right|^{2}=\left|\alpha_{k}\right|^{2}\,\left|\widehat{\Psi}_{k-1}\right|^{2},

(98)

where
|\alpha_{k}|^{2}=(c^{2}-1)\omega^{2}\sigma_{k}^{2}
is increasing in
\omega

and
|\widehat{\Psi}_{k-1}|^{2}
is a product of lowpass factors with cutoff frequencies
\omega_{j}=c^{j}/(\sqrt{c^{2}-1}\,\sigma_{k})
.
The first factor (
j=1
) has the lowest cutoff frequency and limits the passband, so the bandpass peak for DoT satisfies

\omega_{\rm peak}^{DoT}\leqslant\omega_{1}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{c^{2}-1}\,\sigma_{k}}=\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}

(99)

for
\mu_{1}
being the most fine-grained temporal scale.

E.4
Bandwidth tiling

The frequency-to-bandwidth ratio (or quality factor) of a bandpass filter is

Q=\frac{\omega_{\rm peak}}{\Delta\omega}=\frac{\omega_{\rm peak}}{\omega_{+}-\omega_{-}},

(100)

where
\omega_{\pm}
are the upper and lower -3dB band edges.

Equations (
6
) and (
8
) fix varying inter-channel geometric grids for the DoE and DoT.
Below, we show how the quality factor,
Q
, scales for the two wavelets.

DoE: geometric-mean centre and closed-form bandwidth.

For the DoE wavelet, the channel
k
uses a single truncated exponential with time constant
\mu_{k}
, and the inter-channel grid is (
8
).
The exact peak for the DoE (
97

\omega_{\rm peak}^{\rm DoE}(k)=\frac{\sqrt{c}}{\mu_{k}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_{k-1}\mu_{k}}},

(101)

with the explicit constant-

\log\omega_{\rm peak}^{\rm DoE}(k+1)-\log\omega_{\rm peak}^{\rm DoE}(k)=\log c.

The total range

has bandwidth ratio
c^{K-k}
.

The -3dB band edges
\omega_{\pm}
of a single DoE bandpass channel solve

Inserting the peak

DoT: numerical peak from cascade Fourier transform.

For the DoT, the channel-level scale parameter is the cumulative cascade standard deviation
\sigma_{k}
, and the inter-channel grid is given by (

The time constants

E.5
Spectral decay of bandpass channels

The frequency sensitivity for a single DoE bandpass channel (
19
) in the Fourier domain is the difference of two first-order lowpass filters (
83
)

\displaystyle\widehat{\psi}_{\rm DoE}(\xi;\,\mu,c)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{1}}-\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{2}-1-2\pi i\xi\mu_{1}}{(1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{1})(1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi i\xi\mu_{2}-2\pi i\xi\mu_{1}}{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{1}+2\pi i\xi\mu_{2}+(2\pi i\xi\mu_{1})(2\pi i\xi\mu_{2})},

(114)

from which it follows for large frequencies that a single channel decays inversely with the frequency

O\left({|\xi|}/{|\xi|^{2}}\right)=O\left(|\xi|^{-1}\right)

(115)

The DoT kernel is essentially just convolving multiple kernels (
17
).
Below we extend the sensitivity analysis to first
n=2
, then
n>2
kernels.
For the DoT with
n=2
composed kernels, the bandpass difference is roughly

\frac{1}{(1+a_{1})(1+a_{2})}-\frac{1}{(1+b_{1})(1+b_{2})},

(116)

where

for large
|\xi|
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{(1+b_{1})(1+b_{2})-(1+a_{1})(1+a_{2})}{(1+a_{1})(1+a_{2})(1+b_{1})(1+b_{2})}=\frac{b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{1}b_{2}-a_{1}-a_{2}-a_{1}a_{2}}{(1+a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{1}a_{2})(1+b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{1}b_{2})}

(117)

\displaystyle\approx O\left(\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{|\xi|^{4}}\right)=O\left(|\xi|^{-2}\right)

(118)

For
n
composed kernels, the bandpass difference is

\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{j}^{a}}-\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+2\pi i\xi\mu_{j}^{b}}.

(119)

Over a common denominator, the leading orders are
\prod_{j}b_{j}-\prod_{j}a_{j}=O\left(|\xi|^{n}\right)
and
O\left(|\xi|^{2n}\right)
for the numerator and denominator respectively.
By induction,

|\widehat{\Psi}(\xi;\,\mu,c)|=O\!\left(|\xi|^{-n}\right)

(120)

Following
[
4
, Sec. 2.4]
, a signal

\Omega

\Delta_{\Omega}

\int_{|\xi|>\Omega}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\,{\rm d}\xi\leqslant\Delta_{\Omega}.

(121)

For each bandpass channel, the filtered signal
\Delta L_{k}(\xi;\,\sigma,c)*f
satisfies

\int_{|\xi|>\Omega_{k}}|\widehat{\Delta L_{k}}(\xi;\sigma_{k},c)|\,{\rm d}\xi=\int_{|\xi|>\Omega_{k}}|\widehat{\psi_{\rm DoT}}(\xi;\,\sigma_{k},c)|\,|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\,{\rm d}\xi.

(122)

From (
120
) and for
|\xi|
sufficiently large, the tail integral outside the target bandwidth for arbitrary
n\geqslant 2
converges and decreases monotonically in
\Omega_{k}

\int_{|\xi|>\Omega}|\widehat{f}(\xi)|\ \frac{C}{|\xi|^{n}}{\rm d}\xi,

(123)

for some
C\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence, for any tolerance
\theta>0
, there exists a finite
\Omega_{k}
such that (
121
) is satisfied by
\Delta_{\Omega}=\theta
.

For
n=1
(the DoE case), the

decay of the bandpass kernel is not sufficient to guarantee convergence
of (
123
) from the kernel alone.
Additional assumptions on
\widehat{f}
, or the use of the reconstruction error feedback mechanisms, are required to ensure the approximate bandwidth condition is met.

\langle\Lambda f,g\rangle=\langle f,\Lambda^{*}g\rangle

The composition
\Lambda^{*}\Lambda
is a real symmetric positive definite operator, the frame operator, whose eigenvalue range is exactly
[A,B]
.

The quality of a wavelet frame depends not only on the energy capture function

The Gram matrix

has entries

G_{jk}=\langle\psi_{j},\psi_{k}\rangle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\widehat{\psi}_{j}(\omega)\,\overline{\widehat{\psi}_{k}(\omega)}\,{\rm d}\omega,

as a finite-dimensional approximation of

(126)

where

measures how far the frame is from being tight.
A large
{\rm cond}(G)
means reconstruction is sensitive to noise and quantization.
Conversely, rapid off-diagonal decay in

(a small
{\rm cond}(G)
) means that each channel captures information that is approximately independent of distant channels.

With

F.2.2
Stability constraints on
c

Two practical lower bounds on
c
enter the implementation.
Numerical leaky-integrator stability. The discrete first-order integrator with time constant
\mu
has decay
\alpha=\exp(-{\rm d}t/\mu)
.
Requiring
\alpha\geq\alpha_{\rm floor}
for some floor (we use
\alpha_{\rm floor}=0.01
) imposes
\mu\geq{\rm d}t/(-\ln\alpha_{\rm floor})
at every cascade stage, which through the discrete recursion
\mu=(-1+\sqrt{1+4\Delta\tau})/2

[
15
, (58)]
translates into a minimum
\Delta\tau
at the finest stage.

Second, for DoT this constraint propagates to a maximum cascade order
n^{\star}
given
K
and
c
: when the c chosen by (
128
) would push the finest stage below
\alpha_{\rm floor}
, we reduce
n
until each stage is stable, accepting a less smooth limit-kernel approximation as the price of frequency coverage.
Channel distinctness. As
c\to 1
, the bandpass amplitude
\lVert\psi_{k}\rVert
collapses (adjacent low-pass kernels nearly cancel).
We therefore floor
c\geq 1.05
, which, along with the input channel normalization (
146
), ensures that channels stay meaningfully distinct.

Together these floors set a minimum
c_{\min}
, and (
128

K_{\max}=1+\lfloor\log(\Omega_{\max}/\Omega_{\min})/\log c_{\min}\rfloor
for a given frequency range.

Appendix I
Spike amplitude and quantization error bounds

We established admissibility criteria for the DoE and DoT wavelets (Appendix
E.3
), guaranteeing that they can perfectly reconstruct the signal, given that the bandpass channels overlap with the frequencies in the signal.
We now proceed to study the reconstruction error induced by the spikes.
There are two sources of the error: a mismatch in amplitude introduced by the discrete spikes and a mismatch in time, introduced by the simulated temporal discretization.
We begin by studying the continuous case, where the spectral decay of time-scaled responses provides bandwidth limits for each channel which, in turn, bounds the amplitude error for individual channels.
We then proceed to study the temporal discretization we will observe in simulation that we combine with the amplitude error above to bound the overall spiking reconstruction process.

I.1
Spike amplitude error

We use the tail-energy bandwidth
\Omega_{k}
(
121
) (App.
E.3
), valid for DoT under the spectral decay (
120
).

Assume that the bandpass signal
\Delta L_{k}(\xi;\,\sigma,c)
has approximate bandwidth
\Omega_{k}
up to tolerance
\theta_{\rm thr}
, where
\theta_{\rm thr}
is the firing threshold of the LIF neurons encoding channel
k
(
25
).
By Theorem 2.2 of
4
, the per-channel amplitude encoding error is bounded by

\lVert\epsilon_{{\rm amp},k}\rVert_{\infty}=\lVert\Delta L_{k}(t;\,\sigma,c)-\widetilde{\Delta L}_{k}(t;\,\sigma,c)\rVert_{\infty}\leqslant C\,\theta_{\rm thr}\,\Omega_{k},

(137)

where
C>0
is a constant.
This bound is model-agnostic and holds for any (spiking) reconstruction method, as it bounds the maximum difference between any two signals that produce the same spike trains.

The error
\epsilon

and the reconstruction

from (

f(t)-\widetilde{f}(t)=L_{K}-\widetilde{L}_{K}-\sum_{k}\left(\Delta L_{k}-\tilde{\Delta L_{k}}\right)=\epsilon_{{\rm amp},K}+\sum_{k}^{K}\epsilon_{{\rm amp},k}.

(138)

Inserting (

\lVert f(t)-\widetilde{f}(t)\lVert_{\infty}\leqslant C\ \theta_{\rm thr}\ \left(\Omega_{K}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Omega_{k}\right).

(139)

Now, given that the bandpass filters are self-similar over scales, a reasonable measure of the bandwidth

of the bandpass filters must obey the following variability over scales

\Omega_{k}=\frac{\Omega_{1}}{c^{k-1}},

(140)

which when inserted into (

\lVert f(t)-\widetilde{f}(t)\lVert_{\infty}\leqslant C\ \theta_{\rm thr}\ \left(\frac{\Omega_{1}}{c^{K-1}}+\Omega_{1}\left(\frac{1-\frac{1}{c^{K}}}{1-\frac{1}{c}}\right)\right),

(141)

which in the limit case when the number of scale levels

gives the error bound

\lVert f(t)-\widetilde{f}(t)\lVert_{\infty}\leqslant C\ \theta_{\rm thr}\left(\frac{\Omega_{1}c}{c-1}\right).

(142)

, below which

Appendix J
Experimental details

All simulations are clocked to the native sampling rate of each dataset.
The discrete wavelet transforms (DWT) operate in discrete time by construction and, along with the DoG (
16
), DoE (
19
), and DoT (
18
), are expected to perfectly represent the signal.
The continuous wavelet transforms (CWT) and spiking kernels are continuous in time, but evaluated on a discrete grid, introducing discretization error that vanishes at
\Delta t\to 0
.
We have chosen
\Delta t
to be the inverse of the sampling rate, that is
\Delta t=\{1/360,1/16000\}
for MITBIH and LibriSpeech, respectively.

All evaluation signals are standardized (z-scored) to zero mean and unit variance, so reconstruction error can be compared across LibriSpeech and MIT-BIH ECG with the normalized root mean squared error,

\text{Normalized RMSE}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{f}}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f_{i}-\widetilde{f}_{i})^{2}},

(145)

where
\sigma_{f}
is the standard deviation of
f
,
n
is the number of samples, and
\widetilde{f}
is the approximated reconstruction of
f
.
Because
\sigma_{f}=1
, the normalized RMSE equals the raw RMSE in units of the signal’s standard deviation.
For non-spiking wavelets,
\widetilde{f}
comes from frame synthesis (
41
).
For spiking systems,
\widetilde{f}
is the per-spike least-squares reconstruction (
29
).
