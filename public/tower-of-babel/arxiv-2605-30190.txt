Mean-Field Diffuser: Scaling Offline MARL to Thousands of Agents
Wenhao Li, Xiangfeng Wang, Bo Jin

Abstract
Diffusion-based planning has achieved strong results in single-agent offline reinforcement learning, yet scaling to many-agent systems remains intractable due to the curse of dimensionality in the joint trajectory space. We introduce MF-Diffuser, a framework that lifts trajectory planning to the Wasserstein space of trajectory distributions, where the propagation of chaos ensures a small representative subset of agents captures the full population dynamics. Our approach features a value-weighted chaotic entropy objective that reconciles generative fidelity with return maximization, and a hierarchical coarse-to-fine strategy that progressively grows the agent population during denoising. We establish end-to-end suboptimality bounds with four interpretable terms, revealing that mean-field approximation error scales as 𝒪 ⁡ ( H 2 / N ) \mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N}) while offline distribution shift provably does not grow with population size N N , and prove the generated policy is an approximate mean-field Nash equilibrium with explicit convergence guarantees. Experiments on three mean-field RL benchmarks—spanning stage games, sequential dynamics, and adversarial team competition—show MF-Diffuser achieves the best return in the majority of settings, with the largest gains on suboptimal offline data and at extreme scales ( N ≥ 10 3 N\geq 10^{3} ).

Introduction

Offline reinforcement learning (offline RL)
(
36
;
33
)
has emerged as a principled framework for learning decision-making policies from pre-collected datasets without further environment interaction. Among recent advances, diffusion-based planning methods such as Diffuser
(
24
)
and Decision Diffuser
(
1
)
have demonstrated remarkable effectiveness by casting trajectory optimization as a conditional generative modeling problem: a denoising diffusion model is trained to capture the distribution of trajectories in the offline dataset, and high-return plans are generated at inference time via guided sampling.

Despite their empirical success, these diffusion-based planners are inherently designed for
single-agent
settings. Extending them to
many-agent systems
—environments populated by hundreds or thousands of interacting agents—poses a fundamental scalability challenge. Consider
N
homogeneous agents, each with state space
\mathcal{S}
and action space
\mathcal{A}
. A joint trajectory over horizon
H
lives in
\mathbb{R}^{N\times(d_{s}+d_{a})\times H}
. Even for moderate values of
N
, the dimensionality of this joint space grows linearly in
N
, leading to an exponential blowup in the sample complexity required for accurate diffusion modeling
(
9
;
8
)
. This is precisely the
curse of dimensionality
that plagues conventional approaches.

Meanwhile, many-agent decision-making problems are ubiquitous in real-world applications. Large-scale traffic control
(
52
)
, swarm robotics
(
10
)
, financial market modeling
(
30
)
, and epidemiological policy design
(
12
)
all involve populations of agents whose individual decisions are coupled through shared environmental dynamics. For such problems,
mean-field reinforcement learning
(MFRL)
(
56
;
17
)
provides a principled approximation: by replacing the explicit dependence on all other agents with a dependence on the
population distribution
(mean field), the complexity of the
N
-agent problem is reduced to that of a representative single agent interacting with the population.

In this paper, we introduce
MF-Diffuser
(
M
ean-
F
ield Diffuser), a diffusion-based planning framework that bridges the gap between trajectory-level generative planning and mean-field multi-agent systems. Our key insight is to model the
N
-agent trajectory planning problem through the lens of
interacting particle systems
: by treating each agent’s trajectory as a particle in a high-dimensional trajectory space, the joint planning problem becomes amenable to mean-field analysis, where the propagation of chaos property
(
50
)
ensures that the behavior of the full population can be approximated by a representative subset.

However, transferring mean-field particle theory to the sequential decision-making domain introduces three fundamental challenges absent from static settings:
(C1)
agent trajectories carry
temporal coupling
—interaction effects accumulate over the planning horizon
H
, causing approximation errors to compound multiplicatively rather than additively;
(C2)
the objective is not merely distributional matching but
return maximization
; and
(C3)
offline data introduces
distribution shift
whose interaction with the mean-field structure must be controlled.

MF-Diffuser addresses all three challenges with the following contributions:
(1)
Mean-field Trajectory Diffusion with Value-weighted Chaotic Entropy
(C1, C2, C3). We formulate many-agent offline RL as generative modeling on the Wasserstein space of trajectory distributions, defining mean-field SDEs over trajectories with temporally-structured interaction kernels. Building on the RL-as-inference framework
(
37
)
, we derive a mean-field value score matching (MF-VSM) objective that unifies distributional fidelity with return maximization at the chaotic limit.
(2)
Hierarchical Coarse-to-Fine Planning
(C1). Justified by propagation of chaos (
M=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{N})
agents suffice), we progressively grow the agent population during denoising—starting from a small representative group and branching to the full population.
(3)
End-to-end Planning Guarantee

decomposes the suboptimality into four interpretable terms: score matching error, subdivision error with geometric decay, mean-field approximation scaling as
\mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N})
, and an offline distribution shift that provably does
not
grow with
N
.
(4)
Game-theoretic Guarantees
(C2, C3). Theorem
4.4

Problem Setup

Mean-field MDP.
We consider a symmetric
N
-agent MDP. At step
h\in\{0,\ldots,H-1\}
, agent
i
has state
\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i}\in\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
, action
\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i}\in\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
, with discount
\gamma\in(0,1)
. The
mean-field interaction
assumption stipulates that dynamics and rewards depend on others only through the
empirical state distribution

\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\mathbf{s}_{h}^{j}}
:

\mathbf{s}_{h+1}^{i}\sim P(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}),\qquad r_{h}^{i}=r(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}).

(1)

Agents are
homogeneous
(sharing
P
and
r
); we seek a common policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})\to\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{A})
maximizing the social welfare

\max_{\pi}J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}\tfrac{1}{N}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}r(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N})\right].

(2)

As
N\!\to\!\infty
,
\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}
converges to a deterministic flow
\mu_{h}
, reducing the problem to a representative agent. The
offline dataset

\mathcal{D}
consists of
N
-agent episodes collected under behavior policy
\pi_{\beta}
; trajectories
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}=(\mathbf{s}_{0}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{0}^{i},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{H}^{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{\tau}}
with
D_{\tau}=(d_{s}+d_{a})H+d_{s}
. Mean-field Q-learning (MFQ)
(
56
)
factors the joint
Q
-function as
Q^{MF}(\mathbf{s}^{i},\mathbf{a}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}})
via mean-action coupling and serves as our behavior-policy collector for the offline data.

Score-based Diffusion Trajectory Planning.
Diffuser
(
24
)
models the trajectory distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\tau})
in
\mathcal{D}
via a forward SDE that progressively corrupts
\boldsymbol{\tau}
with Gaussian noise,
d\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u}=f_{u}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u})du+\sigma_{u}dB_{u}
on
u\!\in\![0,T]
, and a learned reverse SDE that recovers samples by following the score
\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t})
of the noised marginal. The score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
is trained by score matching,
\mathcal{J}_{SM}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj_t) - \nabla\log\zeta_t(\traj_t)}^{2}
. At inference, trajectories are generated by simulating the reverse SDE from Gaussian noise; high-return plans are obtained by adding a value-gradient bias
\eta\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\hat{V}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
to the score, and the observed initial state is enforced via inpainting. Decision Diffuser
(
1
)
replaces the gradient bias with classifier-free return-bucket conditioning. We build on this single-agent paradigm but lift it to the joint
N
-agent setting via mean-field analysis. An extended review is in Appendix
B
.

3.1
Mean-field Trajectory SDEs

We model each agent’s trajectory as a particle in
\mathcal{X}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{D_{\tau}}
. Given
N
agents,
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N}=(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N})\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
is an exchangeable
N
-particle system amenable to mean-field analysis.
Two time axes appear throughout:
the MDP step
h\!\in\!\{0,\ldots,H\!-\!1\}
inside each trajectory
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}
, and the diffusion time used by the generative process. The
forward
(noising) process uses
u\!\in\![0,T]
running from data (
u{=}0
) to noise (
u{=}T
); the
reverse
(denoising) process uses
t\!=\!T\!-\!u\!\in\![0,T]
. We use
u
in the forward SDE and
t
in the reverse SDE below.

Definition 3.1

(Mean-field Trajectory SDEs)
.

The
N
-agent forward-reverse trajectory diffusion is:

\displaystyle\textit{Forward:}\quad d\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u}^{i,N}

\displaystyle=f_{u}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u}^{i,N})du+\sigma_{u}dB_{u}^{i,N},\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u=0}^{i,N}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},

(3)

\displaystyle\textit{Reverse:}\quad d\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{i,N}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{i,N})-\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{i,N})\right]dt+\sigma_{t}d\bar{B}_{t}^{i,N},

(4)

with joint law
\nu_{t}^{N}=\mathbf{Law}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{1,N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N,N})
that is exchangeable under permutations.

Mean-field Interaction for Trajectories.
Unlike geometric proximity used in static particle systems, agent trajectories interact through temporally-structured mean-field dynamics. We define the interaction operator and the score network as

\mathrm{B}_{\theta}[\nu_{t}^{N}](\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})=\tfrac{1}{N-1}\!\sum_{j\neq i}\!K_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j})\cdot\mathrm{B}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j}),\quad\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N},\nu_{t}^{N})=\mathrm{A}_{\theta}+\mathrm{B}_{\theta}[\nu_{t}^{N}],

(5)

where
\mathrm{A}_{\theta}
captures individual dynamics and
K_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{j})=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}k_{\theta}(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{s}_{h}^{j},\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N})
decomposes the kernel across MDP steps so that the coupling depends on the evolving population distribution.

3.2
Value-weighted Chaotic Entropy

We resolve the tension between distributional matching (standard score matching) and return maximization (challenge C2) via the
value-weighted chaotic entropy
at the mean-field limit.

RL as Inference.
Following
37
, we define the optimality variable
\mathcal{O}=1
with
p(\mathcal{O}{=}1|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i},\mu)\propto\exp(\alpha^{-1} R(\traj^i;\mu))
, where
R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i};\mu)=\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}r(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i},\mu_{h})
and
\alpha>0
is a temperature. The optimal trajectory distribution is
p^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}|\mathcal{O}{=}1,\mu)\propto p(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}|\mu)\exp(R(\traj^i;\mu)/\alpha)
. The mean-field approximation
R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i};\bar{\mu}^{N})\approx R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i};\mu)
is controlled by Theorem
4.1
.

Definition 3.2

(Value-weighted Chaotic Entropy)
.

The value-weighted
N
-particle relative entropy is

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{T}^{N})\,\coloneqq\,\tfrac{1}{N}\!\int_{\mathcal{X}^{N}}\!\Big[\log\tfrac{\varrho_{T}^{N}}{\zeta_{0}^{\otimes N}}-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\!R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})\Big]\varrho_{T}^{N}d\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N},

(6)

combining distributional matching (log-ratio) with return maximization (reward).

At the chaotic limit,
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{T}^{N})\to\mathcal{H}_{V}^{\infty}(\mu_{T})=\mathcal{H}(\mu_{T}|\zeta_{0})-\alpha^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{T}}[R(\boldsymbol{\tau})]
.

Mean-field Value Score Matching.
Applying the Itô-Wentzell-Lions formula and Sobolev upper bounds (proof in Appendix
E.2
) yields the
MF-VSM
objective.

Proposition 3.3

(MF-VSM Bound)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{0}}[\norm{\traj}^{2}]<\infty
. For any
N\!\geq\!1
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{T}^{N})\precsim\frac{\mathcal{M}_{2}}{\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}}\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}^{N}(\theta,\nu_{[0,T]}^{N})+\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(T)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N}),

(7)

where the MF-VSM objective is

\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}^{N}(\theta,\nu_{[0,T]}^{N})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{t}\Big[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t, \traj_t^N, \nu_t^N) - \nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N}(\traj_t^N)}^{2}_{W}\\
+\tfrac{\lambda}{\alpha}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t, \traj_t^N, \nu_t^N) - \nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N}(\traj_t^N)}_{E}^{2}\Big],

(8)

and
\norm{\cdot}_{W}
is the Sobolev norm on
W^{1,2}(\mathcal{X}^{N},\nu_{t}^{N})
.

The first term performs score matching for distributional fidelity; the second aligns the score network with the value gradient. The
1/\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}
prefactor provides robustness to large
N

\|\cdot\|_{W}
with
\|\cdot\|_{E}
during training, incurring a constant
\leq\sigma_{t_{\min}}^{-2}
that is absorbed into
\epsilon^{score}
and is independent of
N

H
, and
\epsilon_{\text{offline}}
(see Appendix
B.3
).

We present MF-Diffuser’s main theoretical contributions and clarify what they bound. MF-Diffuser is

to maximize cooperative social welfare

Standing assumptions.
Our analysis requires (

) Lipschitz regularity of the drift

, score
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
, reward
r

P
with constant
L>0
; (
A2

). We additionally introduce an

assumption (
A6

Propagation of Chaos for Trajectory Distributions.

Our first result establishes that the mean-field approximation is valid in trajectory space despite temporal coupling.

Theorem 4.1

(Propagation of Chaos for Trajectories)
.

Let
\nu_{t}^{M,N}
denote the
M
-agent marginal of
\nu_{t}^{N}
from Def.
3.1
, and
\mu_{t}^{\otimes M}
the
M
-fold product of the mean-field limit. Under Assumptions

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{H}(\nu_{t}^{M,N}\,\|\,\mu_{t}^{\otimes M})\right]\;\leq\;e^{c_{0}}\!\left(C_{1}\tfrac{M}{N}+C_{2}\tfrac{MHL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}{N}\right),

(12)

where

is the effective Lipschitz–horizon constant. The temporal coupling term

—absent from static particle analyses—captures how interaction effects compound across the horizon. By Pinsker’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{t}^{M,N},\mu_{t}^{\otimes M})]\leq e^{c_{0}}(C_{1}+C_{2}HL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})M/N

Hierarchical Planning Error Decomposition.

Our central result provides an end-to-end guarantee for the hierarchical planning scheme.

Theorem 4.2

(Hierarchical Approximation)
.

Let
\hat{\mu}_{T}^{N}
be the trajectory distribution produced by MF-Diffuser with
K

\mathfrak{b}
, and score network

\displaystyle J(\pi^{*})-J(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\leq\underbrace{\tfrac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\tfrac{C_{\sigma}\,\epsilon^{score}_{\mathrm{global}}(\theta)}{2}}}_{(a)}+\underbrace{\tfrac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\!\sum_{k=0}^{K}\!\mathfrak{b}^{-k/2}\epsilon_{k}^{score}}_{(b)}+\underbrace{C_{6}\,e^{c_{0}}\,\tfrac{r_{\max}H^{2}L_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sqrt{N}}}_{(c)}+\underbrace{C_{7}\epsilon_{offline}}_{(d)},

(13)

where
\epsilon^{score}_{\mathrm{global}}
is the global score matching error against the value-tilted target
\nabla\log\mu_{t}^{*}\coloneqq\nabla\log\zeta_{t}+\alpha^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R

,

Corollary 4.3

(Scalability)
.

For fixed
H,\epsilon^{score},\epsilon_{offline}
,
J(\pi^{*})-J(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{score})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{offline})
, so quality
improves
with
N
—in stark contrast to naive approaches.

Game-theoretic Equilibrium Guarantees.

Beyond cooperative welfare, the generated policy is simultaneously an approximate Nash equilibrium. The exploitability
\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\pi)=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}}[J_{1}(\pi^{\prime},\pi^{\otimes(N-1)})-J_{1}(\pi,\pi^{\otimes(N-1)})]
measures stability against unilateral deviations.

Theorem 4.4

(Exploitability Bound)
.

Under Assumptions
D.1
–
D.6
and
D.10
, the policy
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\leq\underbrace{\tfrac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\tfrac{C_{\sigma}\,\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}{2}}+\tfrac{\alpha\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\pi}}{1-\gamma}}_{(i)}+\underbrace{C_{8}\,e^{c_{0}}\,\tfrac{r_{\max}H^{2}L_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sqrt{N}}}_{(ii)}+\underbrace{C_{9}\epsilon_{offline}}_{(iii)},

(14)

where
\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)
is the score matching error against the value-tilted target and
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\pi}
is the soft best-response entropy.

Term (i) decomposes diffusion error into score matching plus a softmax bias (controllable via
\alpha
); Term (ii) is the finite-population gap, again
\mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N})
for the same Gronwall reason as Theorem
4.2
; Term (iii) is offline shift,
N
-independent. Under Lasry–Lions monotonicity, MF-Diffuser converges to the unique MF-NE with the welfare–Nash efficiency gap
\mathcal{O}(L^{4}/(\lambda_{LL}-L^{2}))
(Theorem
D.13
& Proposition
D.15
, Appendix
D.4
).

6
Conclusion

We introduced MF-Diffuser, a diffusion-based planning framework for many-agent offline RL that overcomes the curse of dimensionality through mean-field trajectory diffusion. Our value-weighted chaotic entropy objective unifies distributional fidelity with return maximization, and the hierarchical coarse-to-fine strategy enables scalability to thousands of agents. Theorem
4.2
provides the first end-to-end decomposition of social-welfare suboptimality, revealing
\mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N})
temporal coupling while offline shift provably does not scale with
N
. The complementary exploitability bound (Theorem
4.4
) holds
without
monotonicity, applying to all three benchmarks; under Lasry–Lions monotonicity, both notions coincide up to a
\mathcal{O}(L^{4}/(\lambda_{LL}-L^{2}))
efficiency gap and yield convergence to the unique MF-NE.

Limitations.
Three caveats deserve emphasis. (i) The log-Sobolev assumption (A3) holds cleanly only on Gaussian Squeeze; on Ising/Battle we verify it indirectly through the predicted score-error concentration rate (Appendix
H.22
). (ii) Battle violates global Lasry–Lions monotonicity, so Theorem
D.13
’s convergence-to-unique-MFE applies only
within each team
via
31
; the cross-team gap is reported separately in the exploitability analysis. (iii) The
M{=}\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{N})
rate of Theorem
4.1
is sufficient but not tight: empirically a constant
M\!\in\![50,100]
already saturates returns at
N{=}10^{4}
(Appendix
H.18
), so the bound over-estimates what is needed in practice.
Future Work.
Heterogeneous agents via multi-population MF theory; temporal factorization to mitigate the
H^{2}
factor; online fine-tuning combining offline pre-training with limited interaction.

Appendix A
Related Work

Diffusion Planners for Offline RL.

Diffuser
[
24
]
first proposed using denoising diffusion probabilistic models for trajectory-level planning in offline RL, generating full state-action sequences and conditioning on desired returns via classifier guidance. Decision Diffuser
[
1
]
extended this with classifier-free return-bucket conditioning. Diffusion-QL
[
54
]
applied diffusion to policy representation, while IDQL
[
19
]
combined implicit Q-learning with diffusion policies. These methods have shown strong performance but are designed for a
fixed
single-agent trajectory dimensionality and do not address the population-scaling regime that motivates this work.

Multi-agent Diffusion Planners.

Two recent diffusion-based MARL planners are particularly close to our setting and serve as the most direct point of comparison.
MADiff

[
59
]
introduces an attention-based multi-agent diffusion architecture that bridges decentralized execution with centralized control via inter-agent attention layers. While it improves over Joint Diffuser by exploiting structured coupling, MADiff still operates in the joint
\mathbb{R}^{ND_{\tau}}
trajectory space and faces a computational bottleneck once the agent count exceeds the few-dozen regime (e.g., it was originally validated on SMAC scenarios with
\leq 12
agents).
DoF

[
39
]
proposes a diffusion factorization framework grounded in the
Individual-Global-identically-Distributed
(IGD) principle, which decomposes the centralized diffusion model into multiple agent-specific diffusion models via a noise-factorization function while a separate data-factorization function preserves inter-agent coordination. DoF and our framework share the philosophical premise that the joint-space curse can be overcome by structural factorization, but the two solutions are mechanically different: DoF
decomposes
the noise into per-agent components without invoking the population limit, while MF-Diffuser
projects
the population to a representative subset via a Wasserstein-space mean-field SDE with explicit propagation-of-chaos guarantees. Empirically (Sec.
5.2
), DoF outperforms Independent Diffuser by exploiting the IGD coordination signal, but its lack of explicit mean-field projection leaves a measurable gap to MF-Diffuser that grows with
N
.

Sequence Models for Many-agent Offline MARL.

Beyond the diffusion family, a parallel line of work uses long-context sequence models to handle the scale challenge.
Oryx

[
38
]
couples a retention-based long-context backbone with a sequential variant of implicit-constraint
Q
-learning (ICQ); it has been shown to maintain coordination quality with up to
50
agents in dense-interaction environments and is the strongest non-diffusion baseline at extreme
N
in our experiments. The retention mechanism captures long-horizon population dependencies that pure feed-forward MARL approaches lose, and the sequential ICQ targets the extrapolation error and miscoordination failure modes that dominate offline MARL at scale. Compared to MF-Diffuser, Oryx achieves competitive returns on the Expert split but its lack of explicit mean-field projection produces an
\mathcal{O}(H^{2})
rather than
\mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N})
scaling on the population dimension (Section
5.2
).

Many-agent and Mean-field RL.

Cooperative MARL has been extensively studied through centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE)
[
41
,
46
,
49
]
. However, most CTDE methods scale poorly beyond tens of agents due to the exponential growth of the joint action space. For many-agent regimes, mean-field approaches
[
56
,
17
,
16
]
approximate the effect of the population through aggregate statistics, enabling tractable learning. Mean-field Q-learning (MFQ)
[
56
]
demonstrated that pairwise interactions can be approximated by interactions with the mean field, achieving Nash equilibrium convergence under monotonicity. Mean-field MARL
[
17
]
further extended this to general-sum games with provable guarantees.

Offline Multi-Agent RL.

Offline MARL has received growing attention
[
57
,
44
,
51
]
. Most existing work focuses on small-to-moderate agent counts (
N\leq 10
) and relies on conservative value estimates
[
29
]
, conservative policy constraints
[
44
]
, or value decomposition
[
57
]
. Scaling offline MARL to many-agent regimes remains an open challenge that our work directly addresses—MF-Diffuser is the first method to scale offline MARL to
N{=}10^{4}
via trajectory-level diffusion planning.

Mean-field Theory in Generative Models.

Mean-field chaos diffusion models (MF-CDMs)
[
45
]
leverage propagation of chaos for scalable point cloud generation, achieving robustness to high cardinality by operating on Wasserstein space. Score transportation
[
42
]
and Schrödinger bridge approaches
[
40
]
have also connected mean-field dynamics with generative modeling, primarily from an analytic PDE perspective. Our work addresses a fundamentally different problem—sequential decision making with value maximization—which introduces three challenges absent from the static generative setting: temporal coupling across the horizon, the need to integrate return signals into the score matching objective, and offline distribution shift, each addressed by a dedicated component of MF-Diffuser.

Mean-field Games.

Mean-field game (MFG) theory
[
34
,
21
,
6
]
provides equilibrium characterizations for large-population games and underlies our exploitability analysis (Section
4
). While MFGs offer analytical tools for continuous-time population dynamics, they do not address the offline data-driven setting where policies must be learned from fixed datasets without further environment interaction. Our contribution is to bridge this analytical machinery with offline data-driven planning, an angle MFG has not previously addressed.

Appendix B
Background and Preliminaries

B.1
Score-based Diffusion for Trajectory Planning

This subsection expands the brief overview given in Section
2
of the main text. Consider a single-agent MDP
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\gamma)
with state space
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
, action space
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
, transition kernel
P
, reward function
r
, and discount factor
\gamma
. A trajectory of horizon
H
is
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\mathbf{s}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},\mathbf{s}_{1},\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with
D=(d_{s}+d_{a})H+d_{s}
.

Diffuser
[
24
]
models the trajectory distribution
p(\boldsymbol{\tau})
in an offline dataset
\mathcal{D}
using a denoising diffusion model. The forward process progressively corrupts a trajectory via the SDE

d\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u}=f_{u}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u})du+\sigma_{u}dB_{u},\quad\boldsymbol{\tau}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},

(15)

and the reverse process recovers trajectories by following the score
\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t})
of the noised marginal
\zeta_{t}
. A score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
is trained via the standard score matching objective:

\mathcal{J}_{SM}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{t,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}}\left[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t, \traj_t) - \nabla\log\zeta_t(\traj_t)}^{2}\right].

(16)

At inference time, trajectory generation is conditioned on the current state
\mathbf{s}_{0}
(via inpainting) and guided toward high returns using classifier guidance with a learned return predictor
\mathcal{J}_{\phi}
:

\tilde{\mathbf{s}}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t})=\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t})+\alpha\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}}\mathcal{J}_{\phi}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}),

(17)

where
\alpha>0
controls the guidance strength. Decision Diffuser
[
1
]
replaces this classifier guidance with classifier-free return-bucket conditioning. Both pipelines are inherently single-agent: their state space is
\mathbb{R}^{D}
for one trajectory.

B.2
Mean-field Q-learning and Offline Data

Mean-field Q-learning (MFQ)
[
56
]
approximates the joint
Q
-function through mean-field factorization, decomposing pairwise interactions as

Q^{i}(\mathbf{s}^{i},\mathbf{a}^{i},\mathbf{a}^{-i})\approx Q^{MF}(\mathbf{s}^{i},\mathbf{a}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}),

(18)

where
\bar{\mu}_{\mathbf{a}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\delta_{\mathbf{a}^{j}}
is the mean action distribution. MFQ learns this mean-field
Q
-function via temporal-difference updates and converges to a Nash equilibrium under mild conditions; we use it as the behavior policy for collecting our offline datasets (Section
5.1
).

B.3
Method Extensions: Sobolev–
L^{2}
Cost and Subdivision Weights

Time conventions.
See Section
3.1
of the main text for the MDP step
h
vs. diffusion time
u/t
(forward/reverse) distinction used throughout this paper.

Remark

(Practical training objective)
.

Computing the full Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{W}^{2}=\mathbb{E}[\|h\|_{E}^{2}+\|\nabla h\|_{F}^{2}]
requires second-order derivatives of the score network, which is prohibitively expensive. In practice, we follow the standard approach in score-based generative modeling
[
9
,
8
]
and replace
\|\cdot\|_{W}
with the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{E}
in training. The cost of this simplification is an
explicit
multiplicative factor
\sigma_{t_{\min}}^{-2}
, where
\sigma_{t_{\min}}
is the smallest marginal noise scale at the latest diffusion time used during sampling: concretely, for a variance-preserving forward SDE with coefficient
\beta(t)
, the Sobolev-
W^{1,2}
norm is bounded in terms of the
L^{2}
-norm by

\|h\|_{W}^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\|\nabla^{2}\log\zeta_{t}\|_{F})\,\|h\|_{E}^{2}\;\leq\;\bigl(1+\sigma_{t_{\min}}^{-2}\bigr)\,\|h\|_{E}^{2},

(19)

since the Hessian of the log-density of the forward Gaussian is uniformly bounded by
\sigma_{t}^{-2}
. In our implementation
\sigma_{t_{\min}}{=}10^{-3}
, so this incurs a
\leq 10^{6}
constant
in the worst case
. This constant is absorbed into the score matching error term
\epsilon^{score}
in Theorem
4.2
and is independent of
N
,
H
, and
\epsilon_{\text{offline}}
, so it does not affect any of the scaling claims; the measured
\epsilon^{score}
values reported under “Score Network Diagnostics” (Figure
13
) already absorb this factor, showing that in practice the effective constant is
\sim 10^{2}
rather than
\sim 10^{6}
.

Remark

(Weight alignment between
\mathbf{(P_{V})}
and Eq.
9
)
.

The theoretical bound in Proposition
3.4
has the weight
(\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}
, whereas the practical training loss
\mathbf{(P_{V})}
uses the simpler
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
. We verify their relative magnitudes for the geometric schedule
N_{k+1}=\mathfrak{b}^{k+1}N_{0}
with
N_{0}\geq 1
,
\mathfrak{b}\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\sqrt{N_{k+1}}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}^{(k+1)/2}\,N_{0}^{1/2},

\displaystyle(\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+1)/2}\,N_{0}^{-k/2},

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{b}\,\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}^{-k}\cdot\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+1)/2}\,N_{0}^{-k/2}\;=\;\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+3)/2}\,N_{0}^{-k/2}.

Hence the theoretical weight is
\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+3)/2}N_{0}^{-k/2}
, while the practical weight is
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
. Their ratio is

\frac{\text{practical}}{\text{theoretical}}\;=\;\frac{\mathfrak{b}^{-k}}{\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+3)/2}N_{0}^{-k/2}}\;=\;\mathfrak{b}^{k(k+1)/2}\,N_{0}^{k/2},

which is
1
at
k=0
and grows super-geometrically in
k
: the practical scheme assigns
strictly more
weight than the theoretical optimum at every level
k\geq 1
, with the discrepancy concentrated at the
fine
levels (large
k
). Equivalently, the theoretical optimum decays quadratically faster (
\mathfrak{b}^{-k(k+3)/2}
vs.
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
), so it down-weights the fine levels much more aggressively. Using the simpler
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
in training therefore
over-weights the fine levels
relative to the theoretical optimum (or, equivalently,
under-decays
the loss profile), which we verified to be beneficial in practice (Table
2
, row “Hierarchical”): the extra weight at fine levels acts as an implicit fine-tuning bias, ensuring the score network does not collapse to representing only coarse-grained mean-field dynamics. A purely theoretical weighting schedule
(\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}
down-weights the fine levels too aggressively and yields a normalized return that is
1.2
–
1.8
points lower on every environment.

Coarse-to-Fine Planning Interpretation.
The subdivision strategy admits an intuitive interpretation as hierarchical planning:
(i) Coarse planning
(
k=0
, small
N_{0}
): generate trajectories for a small representative group of
N_{0}
agents, capturing the macroscopic population dynamics and mean-field flow
\mu_{h}
;
(ii) Refinement
(
k=1,\ldots,K
): at each branching step, the agent branching function
\Psi^{\theta}
expands the population
N_{k}\to\mathfrak{b}N_{k}
, refining trajectories while preserving consistency with the mean-field;
(iii) Fine planning
(
k=K
, full
N
): the final denoising steps operate on the complete
N
-agent system. The branching function
\Psi^{\theta}:\mathcal{X}^{N_{k}}\to\mathcal{X}^{N_{k}}
generates new agent trajectories by perturbing existing ones according to the local mean-field structure (precise definition in Eq.
11
).

Handling Discrete Action Spaces.
Our diffusion framework operates in continuous space
\mathbb{R}^{D_{\tau}}
, yet two of our benchmarks (Ising, Battle) have discrete actions. Following the standard approach for applying diffusion models to discrete decision problems
[
24
,
1
]
, we train the diffusion model on continuous relaxations of discrete actions and project back to the discrete set at inference time. Concretely, discrete actions
\mathbf{a}\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{A}|\}
are embedded into
\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
via one-hot encoding during training; at inference, the continuous output
\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
is mapped to the nearest valid action via
\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{i}=\argmax_{j}[\tilde{\mathbf{a}}^{i}]_{j}
. The worst-case projection error satisfies
\epsilon_{disc}\leq\sqrt{d_{a}}/2
, but for trained networks this bound is loose: a more useful quantity is the
action-mismatch rate

\rho_{disc}\coloneqq\Pr[\hat{\mathbf{a}}^{i}\neq\argmax_{j}[\zeta_{t}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t})]_{j}]
. We measure
\rho_{disc}\leq 1\%
on Ising and
\rho_{disc}\leq 3\%
on Battle (Table
7
). Since the projection is applied after denoising, the propagated effect on the suboptimality bound is at most
2r_{\max}H\,\rho_{disc}/(1{-}\gamma)
. For Battle (
H{=}100
,
\rho_{disc}{\leq}0.03
,
r_{\max}{=}5
,
\gamma{=}0.99
) this corresponds to a fraction
2H\rho_{disc}\leq 6\%
of the maximum normalized return—comparable to and not dominating the other terms; the overall scaling structure (
\mathcal{O}(H^{2}/\sqrt{N})
in Term (c)) is preserved.

Appendix D
Extended Theoretical Analysis

D.1
Assumptions

We state the regularity conditions required for our analysis.

Assumption D.1
.

(Lipschitz Regularity). The drift
f_{t}
, score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
, reward function
r
, and transition kernel
P
are uniformly Lipschitz continuous in their arguments, with Lipschitz constants bounded by
L>0
.

Assumption D.2
.

(Bounded Rewards). The per-step reward function is bounded:
|r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mu)|\leq r_{\max}
for all
(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mu)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
.

Assumption D.3
.

(Log-Sobolev Inequality). The target trajectory distribution
\zeta_{0}
and the denoising Wasserstein gradient flows satisfy a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\kappa>0
:

\mathcal{H}(\nu|\zeta)\leq\frac{1}{2\kappa}\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\left[\norm{\nabla\log\frac{d\nu}{d\zeta}}^{2}\right].

(20)

Assumption D.4
.

(Exchangeability). The
N
-agent trajectory distribution
\nu_{t}^{N}
is exchangeable: for any permutation
\sigma\!\in\!S_{N}
,
\varrho_{t}^{N}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N})=\varrho_{t}^{N}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\sigma(N)})
, and the score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
preserves this symmetry.

Assumption D.5
.

(Reducibility / Propagation-of-Chaos Regularity). The
N
-agent generative dynamics is
reducible
in the following sense: for every
M\leq N
, the
M
-agent marginal
\nu_{t}^{M,N}
of the joint distribution
\nu_{t}^{N}
admits a
\chi^{2}
-chaoticity expansion

\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\nu_{t}^{M,N}\,\big\|\,(\mu_{t})^{\otimes M}\right)\;\leq\;C_{\mathrm{r}}\,\frac{M}{N}\bigl(1+\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1}\|^{4}\bigr),

(21)

where
\mu_{t}
is the McKean–Vlasov limit law and
C_{\mathrm{r}}>0
is an absolute constant depending only on the Lipschitz constant
L
of Assumption
D.1
and on the log-Sobolev constant
\kappa
of Assumption
D.3
. Equivalently, the score
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N},\nu_{t}^{N})
depends on the other agents only through a Lipschitz functional of the empirical measure
\bar{\mu}_{t}^{N}
, and the induced interaction kernel has finite second-moment mean-field derivatives (a.k.a. the
Lions derivatives

[
6
]
) up to order two.

Eq.
21
is the standard quantitative propagation-of-chaos estimate: it is implied by Lipschitzness of the drift and score with respect to the Wasserstein distance on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{X})
and the log-Sobolev inequality via Sznitman’s coupling argument
[
50
]
. It is what allows us to subdivide the
N
-agent MF-VSM objective in Proposition
3.4
into a telescoping sum of smaller-
N_{k}
objectives; without it, the
(\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}
factor is not justified.

Assumption D.6
.

(Effective Lipschitz–Horizon Regime).
There exists a constant
c_{0}\geq 0
such that the
effective
Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
of the trajectory-level reverse-time SDE drift
b_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\coloneqq f_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
in Eq.
4
satisfies

L_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot H\;\leq\;c_{0},\qquad L_{\mathrm{eff}}\;\coloneqq\;\sup_{t\in[t_{0},T],\,\boldsymbol{\tau}}\,\big\|\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}b_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\big\|_{\mathrm{op}},

(22)

where the supremum is taken over the inference-relevant noise interval
[t_{0},T]
with
t_{0}\geq t_{\min}
bounded away from zero. Equivalently, the discrete-time per-step Gronwall factor satisfies
(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{H}\leq e^{c_{0}}
, so accumulated trajectory-level errors do not blow up exponentially in
H
.

Remark

(Why
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\ll L
in practice)
.

The bare Lipschitz constant
L
from Assumption
D.1
bounds the score network’s spectral norm and the raw transition map. The
effective
Lipschitz
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
that governs trajectory-level error compounding is much smaller because (i) the noise-regularized term
-\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log\zeta_{t}
is contractive (it pulls trajectories toward the data manifold) and (ii) the receding-horizon execution (§
5.4
) re-anchors the planner at every step, preventing drift accumulation beyond
H
steps. Empirically, we measure
L_{\mathrm{eff}}H
by tracking the spectral norm of the linearized reverse-time map along generated trajectories:
L_{\mathrm{eff}}H\approx 1.6
on Ising,
\approx 2.3
on Battle, and
\approx 1.9
on Gaussian Squeeze (Appendix
H.22
, augmented). These values are consistent with the observed
H^{2}
rather than
e^{LH}
scaling in Figure
3(b)
, validating Assumption
D.6
with
c_{0}\approx 2.3
as the worst case in our experiments.
Two distinct exponential factors are reported in this paper for Battle and should not be confused:
(a) the
measured

e^{c_{0}}
in Remark
Remark
below, with
c_{0}
defined in terms of
L_{\mathrm{eff}}H\leq 2.3
; and (b) the
nominal

e^{2LT+2L^{2}T}\approx e^{0.78}\approx 2.18
used in Appendix
H.16
(which calibrates the
C_{D_{\tau}}
proportionality constant against the empirical
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}
on Battle), where “
L
” refers to the bare Lipschitz constant
L\!\approx\!0.3
measured
at
the integration time
T=1.0
rather than the worst-case product over the full horizon. The two are reconciled by noting that
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
is bounded above by
L\,(1+\sigma_{t}^{-2})^{1/2}

D.4
Game-theoretic Analysis: Approximation of Mean-field Equilibria

Theorem
4.4
in the main text establishes the exploitability bound for MF-Diffuser. Here we provide the complete game-theoretic framework, including definitions, additional regularity assumptions, and extended results on convergence under monotonicity and the efficiency gap.

Remark
.

(MFG vs. MFRL).
It is important to distinguish two problems that share the mean-field structure but have different solution concepts.
Mean-field RL
(MFRL), the primary setting of this paper, seeks a common policy
\pi
that maximizes the social welfare
J(\pi)
(Eq.
2
), treating agents cooperatively.
Mean-field games
(MFG)
[
34
,
21
]
model agents as strategic decision-makers, each maximizing their own return; the solution concept is Nash equilibrium, where no individual agent benefits from unilateral deviation. While MFRL optimizes a global objective, the MFG solution provides stability—an equally important practical property, since in deployment each agent acts autonomously. The results below borrow techniques from MFG theory
[
31
,
6
,
7
]
but apply to the MFRL problem formulated in Section
2
. Crucially, the social welfare optimum
\pi^{*}_{SW}
and the mean-field Nash equilibrium
\pi^{MFE}
are generally distinct: the former accounts for externalities (how one’s policy affects the population), while the latter is a fixed point under individual optimization with the population held fixed. We show that MF-Diffuser, designed for MFRL, simultaneously provides approximate Nash stability—bridging the two solution concepts through the structure of the diffusion-based generation.

Game-theoretic Formulation.
We interpret the
N
-agent system as a symmetric game where agent
i
’s individual objective is:

J_{i}(\pi_{i},\pi_{-i})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r(\mathbf{s}_{h}^{i},\mathbf{a}_{h}^{i},\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N})\right],

(25)

where
\pi_{-i}
denotes the policies of all agents except
i
.

Definition D.8
.

(Exploitability). For a symmetric policy
\pi
used by all
N
agents, the
N
-player exploitability is:

\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\pi)=\sup_{\pi^{\prime}}\left[J_{1}(\pi^{\prime},\pi^{\otimes(N-1)})-J_{1}(\pi,\pi^{\otimes(N-1)})\right],

(26)

where
\pi^{\otimes(N-1)}
indicates agents
2,\ldots,N
use
\pi
and agent 1 may deviate to any
\pi^{\prime}
. A policy with
\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\pi)=0
is a symmetric Nash equilibrium.

Definition D.9
.

(Mean-field Nash Equilibrium). A pair
(\pi^{MFE},\mu^{MFE})
is a mean-field Nash equilibrium (MF-NE) if:

1.

(Optimality)
\pi^{MFE}\in\arg\max_{\pi^{\prime}}J^{MF}(\pi^{\prime},\mu^{MFE})
,

2.

(Consistency)
\mu^{MFE}=\Phi(\pi^{MFE})
,

where
\Phi:\Pi\to\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})^{H}
is the mean-field flow operator that maps a policy
\pi
to its induced state distribution sequence
(\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{H-1})
via the transition kernel
P
.

We introduce two additional regularity assumptions for the game-theoretic analysis.

Assumption D.10
.

(Best Response Regularity). The best response operator
\mathrm{BR}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})^{H}\to\Pi
, defined by
\mathrm{BR}(\mu)\coloneqq\arg\max_{\pi^{\prime}}J^{MF}(\pi^{\prime},\mu)
, is well-defined and the composite map
\Gamma\coloneqq\mathrm{BR}\circ\Phi:\Pi\to\Pi
is
L_{BR}
-Lipschitz:

d_{\Pi}(\Gamma(\pi),\Gamma(\pi^{\prime}))\leq L_{BR}\cdot d_{\Pi}(\pi,\pi^{\prime}),

(27)

where
d_{\Pi}
is the metric on the policy space induced by
d_{\Pi}(\pi,\pi^{\prime})=\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mu}D_{TV}(\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu)\|\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu))
.

Assumption D.11
.

(Lasry–Lions Monotonicity
[
34
]
). The reward function satisfies the displacement monotonicity condition: for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})
and any action
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{A}
,

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left[r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mu)-r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\nu)\right]d(\mu-\nu)(\mathbf{s})\leq-\lambda_{LL}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu),

(28)

where
\lambda_{LL}>0
is the monotonicity constant.

Assumption

is the celebrated Lasry–Lions condition from MFG theory, adapted to the reward-maximization setting. It holds when agents are penalized for concentration (
e
.
g
., , crowd-aversion models
[
30
]
), and ensures uniqueness of the MF-NE together with contraction properties.

The exploitability bound (Theorem
4.4
, stated in the main text) is proved in Appendix
E.8.1
. We now present the additional game-theoretic results.

Theorem D.12
.

(Mean-field Consistency). Under Assumptions
D.1
–
D.6

\hat{\mu}_{\theta}
be the mean-field flow induced by the generated trajectory distribution and

the true mean-field flow under

\max_{0\leq h\leq H-1}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h})\leq C_{10}\,e^{c_{0}}\,\frac{HL\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}}{\kappa}+C_{11}\,e^{c_{0}}\,HL\cdot\epsilon_{offline}.

Under the stronger Lasry–Lions monotonicity, we obtain convergence to the unique MF-NE:

(Convergence under Lasry–Lions Monotonicity). Under Assumptions

used to convert per-step Lipschitz bounds into the

(

(Social Welfare–Nash Efficiency Gap). Under Assumptions

E.1
Proof of Theorem
4.1
: Propagation of Chaos for Trajectories

The proof adapts the classical coupling argument for propagation of chaos
[
50
,
4
]
to the trajectory setting. The key technical challenge is controlling the temporal coupling introduced by the horizon
H
: interaction effects between agents compound through the Lipschitz dynamics at each time step, requiring a trajectory-level Gronwall estimate that yields the novel
MHL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}/N
error term.

Proof.

The proof proceeds in three steps.

Step 1: Coupling Construction.

We construct a coupling between the
N
-agent system
(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{1,N},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N,N})
and
N
i.i.d. copies
(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{1},\ldots,\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})
from the mean-field limit
\mu_{t}
. The mean-field copy satisfies:

d\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{i}=\left[f_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{i})-\sigma_{t}^{2}\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{i})\right]dt+\sigma_{t}d\bar{B}_{t}^{i},

(32)

where the interaction is through the
true
mean-field
\mu_{t}
rather than the empirical measure
\nu_{t}^{N}
. We use the
same
Brownian motions:
B_{t}^{i,N}=\bar{B}_{t}^{i}
, ensuring maximal coupling.

Step 2: Trajectory-level Gronwall Estimate.

Define the per-agent trajectory error
e_{t}^{i}=\mathbb{E}[\norm{\traj_t^{i,N} - \bar{\traj}_t^i}^{2}]
. By Itô’s formula applied to
\norm{\traj_t^{i,N}-\bar\traj_t^i}^{2}
, the synchronous coupling
B^{i,N}_{t}=\bar{B}_{t}^{i}
from Step 1 cancels the diffusion term, leaving

e_{t}^{i}\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}\!\int_{0}^{t}2\bigl\langle\boldsymbol{\tau}_{r}^{i,N}-\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{r}^{i},\,[f_{r}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i,N}_{r})-f_{r}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{i}_{r})]-\sigma_{r}^{2}[\nabla\log\zeta_{r}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i,N}_{r};\nu^{N}_{r})-\nabla\log\zeta_{r}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}^{i}_{r};\mu_{r})]\bigr\rangle dr.

The drift difference is
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
-Lipschitz in both the trajectory and empirical-measure arguments. Writing

b_{r}(\boldsymbol{\tau},\mu)\coloneqq f_{r}(\boldsymbol{\tau})-\sigma_{r}^{2}\nabla\log\zeta_{r}(\boldsymbol{\tau};\mu),

Assumption
D.1
together with Assumption
D.6
gives

\norm{b_r(\traj^{i,N}_r,\nu^N_r) - b_r(\bar\traj^i_r,\mu_r)}\;\leq\;L_{\mathrm{eff}}\norm{\traj^{i,N}_r - \bar\traj^i_r}+L_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu^{N}_{r},\mu_{r}).

Squaring and using
\norm{a+b}^{2}\leq 2\norm{a}^{2}+2\norm{b}^{2}
, the squared drift difference is bounded by
2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}(\norm{\traj^{i,N}_r-\bar\traj^i_r}^{2}+\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu^{N}_{r},\mu_{r}))
. Applying Young’s inequality
2\langle a,b\rangle\leq\norm{a}^{2}+\norm{b}^{2}
to the inner product, we obtain the Itô–Gronwall recursion

\displaystyle e_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{t}(1+2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\,e_{r}^{i}\,dr\;+\;\int_{0}^{t}2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{r}^{N},\mu_{r})\right]dr,

(33)

where the coefficient
(1+2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})
on
e_{r}^{i}
comes from
\norm{\traj-\bar\traj}^{2}
(constant “
1
” from Young’s) plus the squared drift Lipschitzness contribution “
2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
”, and the
W_{2}^{2}
-coefficient
2L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}
comes from the mean-field-Lipschitz part of the drift. This is a strict tightening of the earlier
2L_{\mathrm{eff}}
coefficient (which omitted the Young-inequality unit term). The second term captures the mean-field approximation error.

By exchangeability (Assumption
D.4
) and the quantitative empirical-measure CLT
[
14
, Theorem 1]
:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{r}^{N},\mu_{r})\right]\;\leq\;\frac{C\,M_{2}(\zeta_{0})}{N}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{r}^{i},

where the
CM_{2}/N
floor is the asymptotic-normality contribution and
(1/N)\sum_{i}e_{r}^{i}
is the cumulative coupling error. Summing (
33
) over
i

N
, and applying the discrete Gronwall lemma:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{T}^{i}\;\leq\;\frac{C_{D_{\tau}}}{N}\,\exp\bigl((1+4L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\,T\bigr)\;\leq\;\frac{C_{D_{\tau}}\,e^{c_{0}}}{N},

(34)

where we use
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\,T\leq L_{\mathrm{eff}}\,H\leq c_{0}
(Assumption
D.6

(1+4L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})T=T+4(L_{\mathrm{eff}}T)\,L_{\mathrm{eff}}\leq T+4c_{0}\,L_{\mathrm{eff}}
is bounded by a universal

constant which we absorb into the definition of

(we slightly re-define

to include the additive

depends on the trajectory dimension

through the second-moment bound. Crucially, there is
no exponential dependence on the bare

L_{\mathrm{eff}}H\leq c_{0}
.

(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{1,N}_{t},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{M,N}_{t})

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{t}^{M,N},\mu_{t}^{\otimes M})\;\leq\;M\cdot\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}e^{j}_{t}\;\leq\;e^{c_{0}}\!\left(C_{1}^{\prime}\frac{M}{N}+C_{2}^{\prime}\frac{MHL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}}{N}\right),

(35)

) in the legitimate direction
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{H}/\kappa
,

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\nu_{t}^{M,N},\mu_{t}^{\otimes M})]\;\leq\;\frac{2e^{c_{0}}(C_{1}+C_{2}HL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})\,M}{\kappa\,N},

hence the per-coordinate
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-rate is
\sqrt{M/N}
. To make this rate match the desired
\widetilde{O}(N^{-1/4})
accuracy required by the coarse-to-fine schedule (where the score-network bias
\widetilde{O}(M^{-1/2})
at level
M
should not exceed the PoC residual
\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{M/N})
at the next level), we equate
M^{-1/2}\asymp\sqrt{M/N}
, giving
M=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{N})
. This is the design choice for the schedule
N_{0}=M=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{N})
in Section
3.3
.
∎

E.2
Proof of Proposition
3.3
: Mean-field Value Score Matching

Proof.

The proof derives a Wasserstein variational equation for the value-weighted entropy functional via the Itô-Wentzell-Lions formula
[
11
,
18
]
, then establishes Sobolev upper bounds that accommodate both the distributional fidelity and value maximization terms.

Step 1: Wasserstein Variation for Value-weighted Entropy.

Define the value-weighted entropy functional:

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{t}^{N})=\mathcal{H}(\nu_{t}^{N}|\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N})-\frac{1}{\alpha N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}^{N}}[R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})].

(39)

By the Itô-Wentzell-Lions formula applied to the denoising WGF
\partial_{t}\nu_{t}^{N}=-\nabla_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}\mathcal{E}[\nu_{t}^{N}]
:

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{t}^{N})\leq\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{s}^{N})+C_{0}\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}\norm{\nabla_{\Pcal_2}\Hcal_V^N}^{2}_{E}dr\\
+C_{1}\int_{s}^{t}\mathbb{E}\norm{\nabla_x \nabla_{\Pcal_2}\Hcal_V^N}_{F}^{2}dr.

(40)

Step 2: Sobolev Upper Bound (rigorous derivation of the
\mathcal{M}_{2}/\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}
prefactor).

The Wasserstein gradient of the value-weighted entropy is

\nabla_{\mathcal{P}_{2}}\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}=\nabla\log\varrho_{t}^{N}-\nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R^{\otimes N},

(41)

and we set
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{V}\coloneqq\nabla\log\varrho_{t}^{N}-\nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N}-\frac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R^{\otimes N}
. We derive the prefactor in three substeps.

(2.a) Joint LSI on the
N
-agent trajectory space.

Under Assumption
D.3
, the reference measure
\zeta_{0}^{\otimes N}
on
\mathcal{X}^{N}\!\cong\!\mathbb{R}^{ND_{\tau}}
inherits a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\kappa
, since LSI tensorizes (
3
). For any
\nu^{N}\!\ll\!\zeta_{0}^{\otimes N}
,

\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}\,\|\,\zeta_{0}^{\otimes N})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2\kappa}\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{N}}\!\bigl[\norm{\nabla\log(d\nu^N/d\zeta_0^{\otimes N})}_{E}^{2}\bigr].

(42)

The right-hand side is the
L^{2}
norm of the score; the trivial inequality
\|\cdot\|_{E}^{2}\leq\|\cdot\|_{W}^{2}
(since
\|h\|_{W}^{2}=\|h\|_{E}^{2}+\|\nabla h\|_{F}^{2}\geq\|h\|_{E}^{2}
) lets us upper-bound it by the Sobolev
W^{1,2}
norm.

(2.b) Per-agent normalization and the
1/N
factor.

The value-weighted entropy is per-agent normalized:
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu^{N})=(1/N)\bigl[\mathcal{H}(\nu^{N}\,\|\,\zeta_{0}^{\otimes N})-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{N}}[R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})]\bigr]
(Definition
3.2
). Combining with (
42
) and the
L^{2}\!\leq\!W^{1,2}
inequality, the score-matching contribution to
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}
is bounded by
(2\kappa N)^{-1}\,\|\mathcal{G}_{t}^{V}\|_{W}^{2}
.

(2.c) Sharper
\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}^{-1}
rate via exchangeable second-moment scaling.

Under exchangeability of the
N
agents and the entropic-chaos bound (Assumption
D.5
), one can replace the rigid joint-Sobolev bound of (2.b) with a per-agent Sobolev bound that exploits the
\chi^{2}
-chaoticity of the joint score field across agents. Specifically, since
\mathcal{G}_{t}^{V}
is exchangeable across agent labels, its joint Sobolev norm decomposes as

\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}^{N}}\norm{\mathcal{G}_t^V}_{W}^{2}\;=\;N\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{t}^{(1)}}\norm{\mathcal{G}_t^{V,(1)}}_{W}^{2}\;+\;\text{cross-agent residual},

(43)

where the cross-agent residual is
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{M}_{2}\sqrt{ND_{\tau}})
by the standard mean-field
\chi^{2}
-chaos estimate (cf.
45
; see also
32
for the same scaling under LSI). Combining (
43
) with the
1/N
normalization of
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}
, the dominant per-agent score-matching contribution is bounded by
\mathcal{M}_{2}/\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}\cdot\|\mathcal{G}_{t}^{V}\|_{W}^{2}
rather than the cruder
1/(2\kappa N)
. Time integration via the Itô–Wentzell–Lions formula (Step 1) yields

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N}(\nu_{T}^{N})\;\precsim\;\frac{\mathcal{M}_{2}}{\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}}\int_{0}^{T}\norm{\mathcal{G}_t^V}_{W}^{2}\,dt\;+\;\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(T)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N}),

(44)

where the second summand absorbs (i) the value boundary term
\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})]/(N\alpha)
via Assumption
D.2
and (ii) the cross-agent residual of (
43
).

Comment on the rate.
The
\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}^{-1}
scaling is
derived
(not assumed) under the joint hypothesis of LSI (Assumption
D.3
) and entropic chaos (Assumption
D.5
); it improves on the rigid LSI-only baseline of
1/(2\kappa N)
by a factor
\sqrt{D_{\tau}}
via the per-agent Sobolev decomposition. Without entropic chaos, only the slower
1/(2\kappa N)
rate of (2.b) is available, which still yields the qualitative scalability conclusion of Corollary
4.3
(since
1/N\leq\mathcal{M}_{2}/\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}
for
D_{\tau}\leq N\mathcal{M}_{2}^{2}
, the regime where Battle/GS/Ising sit). The faster
\sqrt{D_{\tau}}
improvement under entropic chaos is what we use in Theorem
4.2
’s subdivision proof.

Step 3: Score Network Substitution and Young’s Inequality.

Substituting
\nabla\log\varrho_{t}^{N}
with the score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
, the Wasserstein gradient becomes

\mathcal{G}_{t}^{V}\;=\;(\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N})-\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R^{\otimes N},

(45)

and we expand its squared Sobolev norm as
\norm{a - b}_{W}^{2}=\norm{a}_{W}^{2}+\norm{b}_{W}^{2}-2\langle a,b\rangle_{W}
. The cross term is controlled by Young’s inequality with parameter
\lambda>0
:

-2\langle\mathbf{s}_{\theta}-\nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N},\,\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R^{\otimes N}\rangle_{W}\;\leq\;\lambda\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N}}_{W}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{\lambda\alpha^{2}}\norm{\nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N}}_{W}^{2}.

(46)

Combining with the diagonal terms and folding the constants:

\norm{\mathcal{G}_t^V}_{W}^{2}\;\leq\;(1+\lambda)\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N}}_{W}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{\alpha}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N}}_{E}^{2}+C_{\lambda}(\alpha,\norm{\nabla_{\traj} R}_{W}),

(47)

where the second summand uses the Euclidean norm
\norm{\cdot}_{E}
in place of
\norm{\cdot}_{W}
for the value-gradient mismatch (its Sobolev seminorm is bounded by Assumption
D.1
via
\norm{\nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N}}_{W}\leq L\sqrt{N}
, absorbed into the residual
C_{\lambda}
that contributes to the
\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(T)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
tail in Eq.
7
). The coefficient
\lambda
in the practical objective Eq.
8
is exactly the Young parameter; it controls the trade-off between distributional fidelity (first summand) and value alignment (second summand) and corresponds to the
\lambda
tuning knob exposed in Algorithm
1
(see Appendix
H.8
for empirical sensitivity analysis).

Caveat: upper-bound vs. exact tilted-score fitting.
The decomposition (
47
) is a strict upper bound, not an equality: minimizing
\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}^{N}
in Eq.
8
therefore minimizes an
upper bound
on the genuine value-tilted score loss
\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- (\nabla\log\zeta_{T-t}^{\otimes N} + \tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N})}_{W}^{2}
. The gap between the upper bound and the genuine loss is
O(\lambda)
at
\lambda\to 0
and is controlled by the cross term in (
46
); in practice we set
\lambda=0.1
which gives a
\leq 10\%
relative loss-gap inflation without compromising convergence. We discuss the practical implications in Remark
Remark
and Appendix
H.8
; the suboptimality bound of Theorem
4.2
is unaffected because
\epsilon^{score}
in Eq.
13
is an upper bound on the genuine value-tilted error, which is itself an upper bound on the realized one.

Substituting the score-network decomposition (
47
) into the integrated Sobolev bound (
44
), with the
\mathcal{M}_{2}/\sqrt{ND_{\tau}}
prefactor derived in (2.a)–(2.c), yields the bound in Eq.
7
; the residual
C_{\lambda}
contributes the
\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(T)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})
tail (since
\norm{\nabla_{\traj} R^{\otimes N}}_{W}\leq L\sqrt{N}
by Assumption
D.1
and
\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(T)
scales the conversion from
W
to
E
norm at the smallest noise level).
∎

E.3
Proof of Theorem
D.7
: Concentration of MF-VSM

Proof.

The proof decomposes the finite-
N
deviation into an interaction error (controlled by the Gronwall estimate) and a statistical error (controlled by Bernstein’s inequality), then shows the value-weighting terms preserve the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\sqrt{N})
concentration rate.

Step 1: Decomposition.

Write the deviation as:

\mathbb{E}_{t}F_{t}^{V}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N})-\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}(1,\theta,\mu)=\\
\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}[F_{t}^{V}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N})-F_{t}^{V}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})]}_{\text{(I) Interaction error}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{t}[F_{t}^{V}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})]-\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}(1,\theta,\mu)}_{\text{(II) Statistical error}},

(48)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N}
denotes
N
i.i.d. copies from the mean-field
\mu_{t}
.

Step 2: Bounding Term (I).

By the Lipschitz continuity of
F_{t}^{V}
(guaranteed by Assumptions
D.1
–
D.2
) and the Gronwall estimate in Eq.
34
:

\mathbb{E}|F_{t}^{V}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}^{N})-F_{t}^{V}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})|\leq L_{F}\sqrt{\frac{C_{D_{\tau}}}{N}}\exp(LT).

(49)

Step 3: Bounding Term (II).

Since
\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{1},\ldots,\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N}
are i.i.d.,
F_{t}^{V}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{i})
for the per-agent integrand

g(\boldsymbol{\tau})\;=\;\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj) - \nabla\log\zeta_t(\traj)}_{W}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{\alpha}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj) - \nabla_{\traj} R(\traj)}_{E}^{2}.

(50)

We verify that
g
has a sub-exponential tail under our standing assumptions. First, by Assumption
D.1
(Lipschitz score network and reward), each summand in (
50
) is bounded by a polynomial in
\norm{\traj}
:
\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj)}\leq L\norm{\traj}+\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,0)}
and
\norm{\nabla\log\zeta_t(\traj)}\leq L\norm{\traj}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
(standard Gaussian-like reference measure), giving
g(\boldsymbol{\tau})\leq C(t)(1+\norm{\traj}^{2})
for a deterministic
C(t)<\infty
on the inference noise interval
[t_{0},T]
. Second, under the log-Sobolev Assumption
D.3
, the reference measure
\zeta_{t}
satisfies a quadratic transportation inequality (cf. Otto–Villani
[
43
, Theorem 1]
), which by Herbst’s argument (
[
3
, Section 5.1]
) implies that
\norm{\traj}^{2}
is sub-exponentially concentrated under
\zeta_{t}
:

\mathbb{P}_{\zeta_{t}}\!\left(\norm{\traj}^{2}-\mathbb{E}\norm{\traj}^{2}\geq u\right)\;\leq\;\exp(-\kappa u/(2\Mtwo))\qquad\forall\,u\geq 0.

Composing with the polynomial bound on
g
gives the sub-exponential tail
\mathbb{P}(|g-\mathbb{E}g|\geq u)\leq 2\exp(-\kappa u/b_g)
with
b_{g}=O(C(t)\mathcal{M}_{2}/\kappa)
. Applying Bernstein’s inequality
[
5
, Theorem 2.10]
:

\mathbb{P}\left(|F_{t}^{V}(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{N})-\mathbb{E}[g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{1})]|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{N\varepsilon^{2}}{2(\sigma_{g}^{2}+b_{g}\varepsilon/3)}\right),

(51)

where
\sigma_{g}^{2}=\text{Var}(g(\bar{\boldsymbol{\tau}}_{t}^{1}))
is finite (since
g
is sub-exponential, in particular has finite variance) and
b_{g}
is the sub-exponential parameter as derived above.

Step 4: Combining.

Combining Terms (I) and (II) via the triangle inequality and optimizing the split yields the concentration bound in Eq.
24
. The key observation is that the value-weighting terms contribute additively to
\sigma_{g}^{2}
and
b_{g}
but do not change the
\sqrt{N}
concentration rate, since
R(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i})
is bounded by Assumption
D.2
.
∎

E.4
Proof of Proposition
3.4
: Value-weighted Subdivision

Proof.

We derive the telescoping decomposition of the value-weighted chaotic entropy
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{\infty}(\mu_{T})
along a hierarchical schedule
\mathbb{N}=\{N_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
with branching ratio
\mathfrak{b}
and time partition
\mathbb{T}=\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
.

Step 1: Per-level value-weighted entropy increment.
Apply the Itô–Wentzell–Lions formula to the relative entropy
\mathcal{H}_{V}(\mu_{t}^{N_{k}})
along the forward MF-SDE on the sub-interval
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
(cf. Proposition
3.3
, Eq.
40
). The same value-weighting argument used to derive Eq.
8
from the chaotic entropy yields the per-level increment

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N_{k}}\!\bigl(\mu_{t_{k+1}}^{N_{k}}\bigr)-\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N_{k}}\!\bigl(\mu_{t_{k}}^{N_{k}}\bigr)\;\leq\;\frac{\mathcal{M}_{2}}{\sqrt{N_{k}D_{\tau}}}\cdot\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}\!\bigl(N_{k},\theta,\nu_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}^{N_{k}}\bigr)\;+\;\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(t_{k+1})\,\mathrm{E}(N_{k}),

(52)

where the first term is the value-weighted score-matching contribution at level
k
(under LSI, by the same argument as Proposition
3.3
), and the second term
\mathrm{E}(N_{k})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N_{k}})
is the residual cardinality error (entropic PoC at level
k
with
M=1
, by Theorem
4.1
).

Step 2: Branching cost between levels.
At step
t_{k}
, the agent population grows from
N_{k}
to
N_{k+1}=\mathfrak{b}N_{k}
via the branching map
\Psi^{\theta}
(Eq.
11
). By the data-processing inequality applied to the pushforward
(\mathbf{Id}^{\otimes(\mathfrak{b}-1)}\otimes\Psi^{\theta})_{\#}
, and the reducibility assumption (Assumption
D.5
, Eq.
21
, which provides the propagation-of-chaos estimate at the matched level),

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N_{k+1}}\!\bigl(\mu_{t_{k}}^{N_{k+1}}\bigr)\;\leq\;\mathcal{H}_{V}^{N_{k}}\!\bigl(\mu_{t_{k}}^{N_{k}}\bigr)\;+\;\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1}),

(53)

where
\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N_{k+1}})
absorbs the branching residual. The factor
1/(\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{k}
in the final bound emerges from telescoping the per-level coefficients across
K
branching events (each branching at level
j\leq k
contributes a multiplicative factor
1/\sqrt{N_{j+1}}
via the entropic PoC bound, while
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
is the geometric decay of agent budget per level).

Step 3: Telescoping.
Summing (
52
) and (
53
) across
k=0,\ldots,K
and taking the limit
K\to\infty
:

\mathcal{H}_{V}^{\infty}(\mu_{T})\;\leq\;\lim_{K\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\!\left[\sigma_{\zeta}^{-2}(t_{k+1})\,\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1})\,+\,\frac{\mathcal{M}_{2}}{\sqrt{D_{\tau}}}\!\left(\frac{1}{\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}}}\right)^{\!k}\!\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}\!\bigl(N_{k},\theta,\nu_{[t_{k},t_{k+1}]}^{N_{k}}\bigr)\right],

which is exactly Eq.
9
.
Importantly
, this bound is for the
theoretically optimal
weights
(\mathfrak{b}\sqrt{N_{k+1}})^{-k}
. The practical objective
\mathbf{(P_{V})}
in Eq.
10
uses the simpler weighting
\mathfrak{b}^{-k}
, which differs from the theoretical optimum by a multiplicative factor of
\mathfrak{b}^{k(k+1)/2}N_{0}^{k/2}
(Remark
Remark
)—hence Theorem
4.2
should be read as a guarantee on the
idealized
objective, and the gap to the practical
\mathbf{(P_{V})}
loss is of independent empirical interest (we observe that the heavier practical weighting at fine levels acts as an implicit fine-tuning bias and improves performance, see Table
2
).
∎

E.5
Proof of Theorem
4.2
: Hierarchical Approximation

Proof.

The proof combines the performance difference lemma and Pinsker’s inequality from RL theory with the entropy subdivision from Proposition
3.4
(proved in Appendix
E.4
), quantitative CLT bounds for the mean-field approximation, and offline RL distribution shift analysis.

Step 1: From Entropy to Policy Suboptimality.

By the performance difference lemma
[
26
]
applied to the joint trajectory distribution and Pinsker’s inequality:

\displaystyle J(\pi^{*})-J(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}_{T}}\!\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r(\mathbf{s}_{h},\mathbf{a}_{h},\bar{\mu}_{h})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{T}^{*}}\!\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r(\mathbf{s}_{h},\mathbf{a}_{h},\bar{\mu}_{h})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\,D_{TV}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\,\|\,\mu_{T}^{*})\;\leq\;\frac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\,\|\,\mu_{T}^{*})},

(54)

where the first inequality uses the standard performance-difference identity together with
|\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}r|\leq r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
and the symmetry of total variation
D_{TV}(P\|Q)=D_{TV}(Q\|P)
, and the second is Pinsker’s inequality
D_{TV}(P\|Q)\leq\sqrt{(1/2)\mathcal{H}(P\|Q)}
applied with
P=\hat{\mu}_{T}
(the generated distribution) and
Q=\mu_{T}^{*}
(the target). We chose this direction of KL—
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\|\mu_{T}^{*})
, sometimes called the
forward
KL—because (a) it is the quantity directly controlled by the score-matching objective in Proposition
3.4
(which bounds
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\|\mu_{T}^{*})
via the sub-divided value-weighted entropy decomposition), and (b) Pinsker’s inequality in this direction is sharp under the bounded-density assumption implied by Assumption
D.2
. The prefactor
r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
already absorbs the discounted reward sum—no additional
H
factor is needed.

Step 2: Entropy Decomposition via Girsanov and Subdivision.

We bridge the entropy
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\|\mu_{T}^{*})
to a measurable score-matching quantity via Girsanov, and then refine the result through the hierarchical decomposition of Proposition
3.4
. This explicitly closes the gap left in earlier drafts where Proposition
3.4
(which bounds the value-weighted entropy
\mathcal{H}_{V}^{\infty}(\mu_{T})
) was incorrectly invoked to bound
\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\|\mu_{T}^{*})
directly.

(2.a) Global Girsanov bound (Term (a)).
The reverse-time SDE driven by
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
produces
\hat{\mu}_{T}
, while the value-tilted target
\mu_{T}^{*}\propto\mu_{T}\exp(R/\alpha)
has reverse-time score
\nabla\log\mu_{t}^{*}=\nabla\log\zeta_{t}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R
along the same backward dynamics (cf. Eq.
76
in the proof of Theorem
4.4
). Both reverse SDEs share the same diffusion coefficient
\sigma_{t}
on the operational interval
[t_{\min},T]
, so the Girsanov theorem
[
27
, Theorem 5.1]
(with the Novikov-condition justification of Theorem
4.4
, Step 2(B.i)) yields

\mathcal{H}(\hat{\mu}_{T}\,\|\,\mu_{T}^{*})\;\leq\;\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\min}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj_t)-\nabla\log\mu_t^*(\traj_t)}^{2}\,dt\;\leq\;C_{\sigma}\,\epsilon^{score}_{\mathrm{global}}(\theta),

(55)

where
C_{\sigma}=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\min}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,dt
is the schedule constant and the second inequality uses
\sigma_{t}^{2}\leq 2C_{\sigma}
pointwise to pull the supremum out of the integral (an upper bound suffices). Combining with the Pinsker step of Step 1 directly gives Term (a) of Eq.
13
.

(2.b) Hierarchical refinement (Term (b)).
Proposition
3.4
(and its proof, Eq.
52
) decomposes the integrated
L^{2}
score error along the subdivision schedule:

C_{\sigma}\,\epsilon^{score}_{\mathrm{global}}(\theta)\;\leq\;C^{\prime}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{(\epsilon_{k}^{score})^{2}}{\mathfrak{b}^{k}}\;+\;\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1}),

(56)

where
\epsilon_{k}^{score}
is the per-level score error on
[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
,
\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1})=\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N_{k+1}})
is the per-level cardinality residual, and
C^{\prime}>0
absorbs the schedule weights
\sigma_{t}^{2}
on each subinterval. Applying
\sqrt{\sum_{k}a_{k}}\leq\sum_{k}\sqrt{a_{k}}
to the first sum yields the per-level form
\sum_{k}\mathfrak{b}^{-k/2}\epsilon_{k}^{score}
, which is Term (b). The cardinality residuals
\sum_{k}\sqrt{\mathrm{E}(N_{k+1})}=\mathcal{O}(N_{0}^{-1/4})
are dominated by—and absorbed into—Term (c), since the leading mean-field contribution
H^{2}/\sqrt{N}
in Term (c) controls all
1/\sqrt{N_{k}}
residuals up to a multiplicative
H
factor that is folded into
C_{6}
.

(2.c) Why both (a) and (b) appear.
Eqs. (
55
) and (
56
) provide two
complementary
upper bounds on the same score-matching contribution: (a) is the un-decomposed Girsanov bound, (b) is its hierarchical refinement under the coarse-to-fine training schedule. The total bound in Eq.
13
keeps both for interpretability—(a) is what an outside observer can measure directly via the global score-matching loss, while (b) reveals the per-level structure that the training algorithm actually exploits. Adding them is at most a factor-
2
loss relative to the tighter of the two; in any quantitative instantiation either (a) or (b) alone, whichever is smaller, can replace their sum.

Step 3: Mean-field Approximation Error (from
H
to
H^{2}
under Assumption
D.6
).

The error from using
N<\infty
agents enters the analysis through the reward and dynamics evaluated at the empirical mean field
\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}
versus the true mean-field
\mu_{h}
. Because both the reward
r
and the reverse-time SDE drift are
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
-Lipschitz in the mean-field argument (Assumptions
D.1
and
D.6
), we only need to control
linear functionals of the mean field
, not full
\mathcal{W}_{2}
. We use the following dimension-free CLT for Lipschitz observables:

\mathbb{E}\Bigl|\bigl\langle f,\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}\bigr\rangle-\bigl\langle f,\mu_{h}\bigr\rangle\Bigr|\;\leq\;\frac{C\,\|f\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}}{\sqrt{N}}\qquad\text{(one-step Lipschitz CLT),}

(57)

which follows from Hoeffding’s/Bernstein’s inequality applied to the bounded Lipschitz observable
f
averaged over
N
exchangeable agents, and
is dimension-independent
(cf.
4
). Crucially, this avoids the dimension-dependent rate
N^{-1/d_{s}}
of full-
\mathcal{W}_{2}
Fournier–Guillin
[
14
]
for
d_{s}>4
, which would otherwise degrade catastrophically on Battle (
d_{s}\approx 9
). The full-
\mathcal{W}_{2}
formulation enters only through the trajectory-level relative-entropy bound of Theorem
4.1
, which is dimension-free under LSI; the linear-functional formulation (
57
) suffices for all subsequent Lipschitz-induced bounds on reward and drift.

The relevant constant for trajectory-level error
compounding
is the effective Lipschitz
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
of the reverse-time SDE drift (Assumption
D.6
): an error
\varepsilon_{h}
at step
h
perturbs step
h+1
by at most
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\varepsilon_{h}
, step
h+2
by at most
L_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2}\varepsilon_{h}
, and so on. A discrete Gronwall inequality converts (
57
) into

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{0\leq h\leq H-1}\,\bigl|\langle r,\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N}\rangle-\langle r,\mu_{h}\rangle\bigr|\right]\;\leq\;\Bigl(\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{h}\Bigr)\cdot\frac{C\,L\,r_{\max}}{\sqrt{N}}\;\leq\;e^{c_{0}}\,\frac{C\,H\,L\,r_{\max}}{\sqrt{N}},

(58)

where the last inequality uses
(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{h}\leq e^{L_{\mathrm{eff}}h}\leq e^{c_{0}}
and bounds the geometric sum by
H\cdot e^{c_{0}}

by virtue of Assumption
D.6
. Plugging (
58
) back into the finite-horizon reward accumulation yields

\text{Mean-field error}\;\leq\;\underbrace{H}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{reward sum}\\
\text{(finite-horizon)}\end{subarray}}\,\cdot\,\underbrace{e^{c_{0}}\,\frac{C\,H\,L\,r_{\max}}{\sqrt{N}}}_{\text{Gronwall-accumulated CLT}}\;=\;C_{6}\,e^{c_{0}}\,\frac{r_{\max}\,H^{2}\,L_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sqrt{N}},

(59)

where we have absorbed
L\leq L_{\mathrm{eff}}\cdot\mathcal{O}(1)
(the bare reward Lipschitz
L
is upper-bounded by the SDE-drift Lipschitz
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
up to a noise-schedule-dependent constant; the gap is folded into
C_{6}
). The
H^{2}
factor has two clearly separated origins: the
outer

H
from summing finite-horizon rewards (we use the bound
\sum_{h<H}\gamma^{h}\leq H
, which is tighter than
1/(1-\gamma)
in the finite-horizon regime
H\leq 1/(1-\gamma)
relevant to Figure
3(b)
), and the
inner

H
from Gronwall’s accumulation.
Why
H^{2}
rather than
e^{L_{\mathrm{eff}}H}
:
Assumption
D.6
restricts
L_{\mathrm{eff}}H\leq c_{0}
, making
e^{L_{\mathrm{eff}}H}\leq e^{c_{0}}=O(1)
, so the geometric sum
\sum_{h<H}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{h}
collapses to
H\cdot O(1)
instead of blowing up. Without this assumption, we would have
e^{LH}/\sqrt{N}
, which experiments rule out (Figure
3(b)
fits an exponent
b\in[1.79,2.21]
, statistically incompatible with exponential blow-up).

Reconciling the prefactor with Term (a)/(b).
Terms (a) and (b) of Eq.
13
use the discounted prefactor
r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
obtained from
\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}\leq 1/(1-\gamma)
, while Term (c) uses
r_{\max}H
obtained from
\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}\leq H
. Both bounds are valid; we use the tighter one in each context. In the finite-horizon regime
H\leq 1/(1-\gamma)
(which is the regime of all our experiments and of practical interest),
H\leq 1/(1-\gamma)
, so Term (c) is no larger than what we would obtain by using
r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
uniformly; conversely, in the infinite-horizon limit

, the second

in Term (c) is replaced by

via the same dominated convergence argument. Either reading reproduces the empirical

scaling validated in Figure
3(b)
.

Step 4: Offline Distribution Shift (Per-agent Marginal is
N
-free).

We separate the proof of
N
-independence of the offline-shift term into a self-contained lemma.

Lemma E.1

(Per-agent reduction of the offline-shift charge)
.

Let
\pi_{\theta}
be the policy induced by sampling from the trained score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
in the reverse SDE driven by the empirical mean field
\bar{\mu}^{N}_{h}
. Let
\nu_{\beta},\nu^{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be the per-agent trajectory marginals of Eq.
23
, and
\epsilon_{offline}\coloneqq D_{TV}(\nu_{\beta}\,\|\,\nu^{*})
. Suppose the score network is parameterized so that
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(t,\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i};\bar{\mu}_{t}^{N})
depends on the joint state
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N}
only through a single-agent trajectory
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{i}
and the empirical mean field
\bar{\mu}_{t}^{N}
(
permutation-equivariance
; Assumption
D.5
). Then the joint-trajectory suboptimality contribution from offline shift,
\Delta_{\mathrm{shift}}\coloneqq|J^{MF}(\pi^{*})-J^{MF}(\pi_{\theta})|_{\mathrm{shift}}
, satisfies

\Delta_{\mathrm{shift}}\;\leq\;\frac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\,\epsilon_{offline}\;+\;\frac{r_{\max}H\,L\,e^{c_{0}}}{\sqrt{N}}\,\sqrt{2(C_{1}+C_{2}HL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})},

(60)

where the
1/\sqrt{N}
residual is absorbed into Term (c).

Proof of Lemma
E.1
.

Let
\bar{\mu}^{N}_{\beta}
(resp.
\bar{\mu}^{N}_{*}
) denote the random empirical mean-field flow generated by
N
agents acting under
\pi_{\beta}
(resp.
\pi^{*}
), with conditional single-agent laws
\pi_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\bar{\mu})
,
\pi_{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\bar{\mu})
. The per-agent marginals of Eq.
23
satisfy
\nu_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}\sim\pi_{\beta}^{N}}[\pi_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\bar{\mu})]
and
\nu^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\mathbb{E}_{\bar{\mu}\sim\pi_{*}^{N}}[\pi_{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\bar{\mu})]
, so
\epsilon_{offline}\in[0,1]
is intrinsic to the dataset–policy pair.

(i) Cross-agent factorization of expected reward.
Because the social-welfare reward is a per-agent average
J^{MF}(\pi,\mu)=(1/N)\sum_{i}J^{MF}_{i}(\pi,\mu)
(Eq.
2
) and the score network is permutation-equivariant, exchangeability of the
N
-agent joint laws (under either
\pi^{*}
or
\pi_{\theta}
) implies

J^{MF}(\pi)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}\sim\nu_{\pi}^{(1)}}\!\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}r_{h}(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{h})\right],

(61)

where
\nu_{\pi}^{(1)}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is the per-agent marginal of the joint law
\nu_{\pi}^{N}
. Hence

|J^{MF}(\pi^{*})-J^{MF}(\pi_{\theta})|\;\leq\;\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\;D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu_{\pi^{*}}^{(1)}\,\|\,\nu_{\pi_{\theta}}^{(1)}\bigr).

(62)

The right-hand side depends on
per-agent
marginals only.

(ii) Decomposition of the per-agent TV via offline shift and PoC residual.
By the triangle inequality,

D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu_{\pi^{*}}^{(1)}\,\|\,\nu_{\pi_{\theta}}^{(1)}\bigr)\;\leq\;\underbrace{D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu_{\pi^{*}}^{(1)}\,\|\,\nu^{*}\bigr)}_{\text{(I) MF-projection}}+\underbrace{D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu^{*}\,\|\,\nu_{\beta}\bigr)}_{=\epsilon_{offline}}+\underbrace{D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu_{\beta}\,\|\,\nu_{\pi_{\theta}}^{(1)}\bigr)}_{\text{(III) score reverse-coverage}}.

(63)

Term (I) is bounded by combining Theorem
4.1
(with
M=1
, giving the per-agent KL bound directly without invoking Talagrand) with Pinsker’s inequality
D_{TV}\leq\sqrt{D_{KL}/2}
:

D_{TV}\!\bigl(\nu_{\pi^{*}}^{(1)}\,\|\,\nu^{*}\bigr)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\mathcal{H}\bigl(\nu_{\pi^{*}}^{(1)}\,\|\,\nu^{*}\bigr)}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\frac{e^{c_{0}}(C_{1}+C_{2}HL_{\mathrm{eff}}^{2})}{2N}}.

Note that we use the entropic PoC bound from Theorem
4.1

directly
(which is dimension-free under LSI), rather than going through
\mathcal{W}_{2}
and Talagrand—this is essential because Talagrand under LSI gives
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{H}/\kappa
(not the converse), so it cannot be used to upgrade
\mathcal{W}_{2}
bounds into KL bounds.
Term (III) is bounded by the score-matching reverse coverage: the score network targets
\nu^{soft}_{BR}
(the value-tilted reweighting of
\nu_{\beta}
, Eq.
76
), and the reverse SDE driven by
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
produces a per-agent marginal
\nu_{\pi_{\theta}}^{(1)}
that is
\sqrt{\epsilon^{score}/2}
-close to
\nu^{soft}_{BR}
in TV (Girsanov + Pinsker, see proof of Theorem
4.4
Step 2). Since
\nu^{soft}_{BR}
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\nu_{\beta}
with density
e^{R/\alpha}/Z
bounded by
e^{r_{\max}H/((1-\gamma)\alpha)}
, this term is absorbed into the score-error budget of Term (a) (and not into
\epsilon_{offline}
).

Combining (I)+(II)+(III) into (
62
), the offline-shift contribution is exactly
\frac{r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\epsilon_{offline}
(with constant
2
from the conservative-direction Pinsker convention used in Step 1), plus a
1/\sqrt{N}
residual from (I) which we charge to Term (c). This proves (
60
).
∎

Remark on tensorization and the
N
-independence.
A naive tensorization of TV would give
D_{TV}(\nu_{\beta}^{\otimes N}\,\|\,\nu^{*\otimes N})\leq N\,\epsilon_{offline}
, growing linearly in
N
. Lemma
E.1
avoids this trap by exploiting two structural properties: (i) the social-welfare reward is a per-agent
average
, not a sum (Eq.
61
), so cross-agent correlations cancel; (ii) the score network is
permutation-equivariant
and only consumes per-agent inputs, so its training error is intrinsically per-agent. The price we pay is the
1/\sqrt{N}
residual from (I), which is a vanishing finite-population correction
absorbed into Term (c)
(Step 3) rather than into
\epsilon_{offline}
. This is what we mean when we say “
\epsilon_{offline}
does not grow with
N
”: the dataset coverage error is inherently per-agent, while the only
N
-dependent piece is the PoC residual that already appears in Term (c).

Combining Steps 1–4 (with Lemma
E.1
controlling Step 4) yields the bound in Eq.
13
.
∎

E.6
Proof of Corollary
4.3
: Scalability

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem
4.2
by noting that:

1.

Term (a) is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{score})
, independent of

.

2.

Term (b) is
\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{k}\mathfrak{b}^{-k/2}\epsilon_{k}^{score}\right)=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{score})
for geometric decay.

3.

Term (c) is
\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{N})

E.7
Additional Theoretical Results

E.7.1
Optimal Agent Branching Function

Proposition E.2
.

(Optimal Agent Branching). The optimal agent branching function
\Psi^{*}
at step
t_{k}
minimizes the Wasserstein distance between the branched distribution and the target:

\Psi^{*}=\argmin_{\Psi}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left((\mathbf{Id}^{\otimes(\mathfrak{b}-1)}\otimes\Psi)_{\#}\nu_{t_{k}}^{N_{k}},\;\nu_{t_{k}}^{N_{k+1}}\right).

(64)

Under the exchangeability assumption, the optimal branching admits the Monge-Ampère characterization:

\Psi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N_{k}})=\nabla\varphi^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau}^{N_{k}}),

(65)

where
\varphi^{*}
solves the Monge-Ampère equation
\det(\nabla^2 \varphi)=\varrho_{t_{k}}^{N_{k}}/\varrho_{t_{k}}^{N_{k+1}}(\nabla\varphi)
with appropriate boundary conditions.

E.7.2
Comparison with Naive Approaches

Proposition E.3
.

(Comparison with Joint Diffuser). For a standard diffuser operating on the joint space
\mathbb{R}^{ND_{\tau}}
, the score matching error satisfies:

\mathcal{J}_{SM}^{Joint}(\theta)\geq C\cdot ND_{\tau}\cdot\inf_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \nabla\log\zeta_t}^{2}],

(66)

which grows linearly in
N
. In contrast, the MF-VSM objective satisfies:

\mathcal{J}_{MF\text{-}V}^{N}(\theta)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}\sqrt{D_{\tau}}}{\sqrt{N}}\cdot\inf_{\theta}\mathbb{E}[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \nabla\log\zeta_t}^{2}_{W}]+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

(67)

which decreases with
N
, demonstrating the fundamental advantage of the mean-field approach.

Proofs for Game-theoretic Analysis (Appendix
D.4
)

We present complete proofs of the game-theoretic results. The proofs adapt the
N
-player to MFG convergence framework of
31
and
13
, the monotone operator theory of
34

6

E.8.1
Proof of Theorem
4.4
: Exploitability Bound

Proof.

The proof decomposes the
N
-player exploitability into a mean-field exploitability and an
N
-to-MFG approximation error, then bounds each term using the PoC results (Theorem
4.1
) and the properties of the MF-VSM objective.

Step 1:
N
-player to Mean-field Reduction.

For any deviating policy
\pi^{\prime}
for agent 1 while agents
2,\ldots,N
use
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
:

J_{1}(\pi^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{\theta}^{\otimes(N-1)})=J^{MF}(\pi^{\prime},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))+\Delta_{N}(\pi^{\prime}),

(68)

where
\Delta_{N}(\pi^{\prime})
captures the deviation from the mean-field approximation. We bound
\Delta_{N}
by analyzing the effect of a single agent’s deviation on the empirical measure.

Under
(\pi^{\prime},\hat{\pi}_{\theta}^{\otimes(N-1)})
, the empirical distribution at step
h
is
\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N,\pi^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{N}\delta_{\mathbf{s}_{h}^{1,\pi^{\prime}}}+\frac{N-1}{N}\bar{\mu}_{h}^{(N-1),\hat{\pi}_{\theta}}
. Agent 1’s deviation replaces one Dirac mass out of
N
, so by the triangle inequality and the quantitative CLT for the remaining
N-1
exchangeable agents (following
[
4
]
):

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N,\pi^{\prime}},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h}\right)\right]\leq\frac{C_{d_{s}}}{\sqrt{N-1}}+\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\state_h^{1,\policy'} - \state_h^{1,\hat{\policy}_\theta}}\right].

(69)

The first term is the standard CLT rate for the empirical measure of the
N-1
conforming agents; the second is the
O(1/N)
contribution of agent 1’s deviation. By the Lipschitz continuity of the reward (Assumption
D.1
), the deviation from the mean-field objective accumulated over the horizon satisfies:

|\Delta_{N}(\pi^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}L\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{W}_{2}\left(\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N,\pi^{\prime}},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h}\right)\right].

(70)

The dynamics propagate state errors with the effective Lipschitz constant
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
(Assumption
D.6
); applying a discrete Gronwall inequality (the same mechanism as in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, Step 2) and invoking
L_{\mathrm{eff}}H\leq c_{0}
to bound
(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{H}\leq e^{c_{0}}
:

|\Delta_{N}(\pi^{\prime})|\leq C_{\Delta}\,e^{c_{0}}\cdot\frac{r_{\max}H^{2}L_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sqrt{N}},

(71)

where
C_{\Delta}>0
depends on
d_{s}
and
\gamma
. The
H^{2}
factor arises from (
outer
) the finite-horizon reward sum and (
inner
) the Gronwall accumulation, with the multiplicative
e^{c_{0}}
bounded by Assumption
D.6
. This adapts the
N
-player to MFG convergence of
31
from continuous-time Itô diffusions to discrete-time MDPs; the key modification is replacing the continuous Gronwall lemma with its discrete counterpart while preserving the
O(1/\sqrt{N})
rate through the exchangeability structure (Assumption
D.4
).

Taking the supremum over
\pi^{\prime}
in Eqs.
68
–
71
:

\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\leq\mathrm{Exploit}^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})+\frac{2C_{\Delta}\,e^{c_{0}}\,r_{\max}H^{2}L_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\sqrt{N}},

(72)

where the mean-field exploitability is:

\mathrm{Exploit}^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\coloneqq\sup_{\pi^{\prime}}\left[J^{MF}(\pi^{\prime},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))\right].

(73)

Step 2: Bounding Mean-field Exploitability via Value-weighted Score Matching.

We decompose the mean-field exploitability into three sub-terms:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Exploit}^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{J^{MF}(\mathrm{BR}(\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})),\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))-J^{MF}(\pi^{soft}_{BR},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))}_{\text{(A) softmax bias}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{J^{MF}(\pi^{soft}_{BR},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\hat{\mu}_{\theta})}_{\text{(B) generation error}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\hat{\mu}_{\theta})-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))}_{\text{(C) consistency gap}},

(74)

where
\pi^{soft}_{BR}(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu)\propto\pi_{\beta}(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu)\exp(\qfunc^{MF}(\state, \cdot, \mu)/\alpha)
is the soft (entropy-regularized) best response under the RL-as-inference framework
[
37
]
, and
\hat{\mu}_{\theta}
is the mean-field distribution from the diffusion model’s generated trajectories.

Bounding (A): Softmax Bias.
The soft best response maximizes the entropy-regularized objective
J^{MF}(\pi,\mu)+\alpha\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{H}(\pi(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu))]
, where
\mathcal{H}
denotes Shannon entropy. By the duality between hard and soft optimization (see
e
.
g
.,
37
):

J^{MF}(\mathrm{BR}(\mu),\mu)-J^{MF}(\pi^{soft}_{BR},\mu)\leq\alpha\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\frac{\gamma^{h}}{1-\gamma}\mathcal{H}(\pi^{soft}_{BR}(\cdot|\mathbf{s}_{h},\mu_{h}))\right]\leq\frac{\alpha\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\pi}}{1-\gamma},

(75)

where
\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\pi}=\sup_{\mathbf{s},\mu}\mathcal{H}(\pi^{soft}_{BR}(\cdot|\mathbf{s},\mu))
is the maximum policy entropy, which is finite under compactness of
\mathcal{A}
(Assumption
D.1
).

Bounding (B): Generation Error via Girsanov.
The MF-VSM objective (Eq.
8
) trains the score network to generate trajectories from the value-weighted (
tilted
) distribution

\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\;\propto\;\nu_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\exp\!\bigl(R(\boldsymbol{\tau})/\alpha\bigr),

(76)

whose induced policy is precisely
\pi^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}
under the training-time mean-field. The score of
\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}
is exactly the value-tilted score
\nabla\log\zeta_{t}+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R
that the MF-VSM objective targets (modulo the Sobolev-Young decomposition of Eq.
47
); we denote this target
\mathbf{s}^{*}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\coloneqq\nabla\log\zeta_{t}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\tfrac{1}{\alpha}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}R(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and define

\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}_{t\sim p(t),\boldsymbol{\tau}_{t}\sim\hat{\nu}_{\theta}}\!\bigl[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj_t) - \mathbf{s}^*_t(\traj_t)}^{2}\bigr].

(77)

We work with three trajectory measures on
\mathcal{X}
to keep the offline/online distinction explicit:

•

\nu_{\beta}
: the offline data distribution (per-agent trajectory marginal under behavior policy
\pi_{\beta}
, cf. Lemma
E.1
).

•

\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}\propto\nu_{\beta}\exp(R/\alpha)
: the value-tilted reweighting of
the offline base
(Eq.
76
).
This is the actual training target
of the MF-VSM objective.

•

\nu^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}_{BR}\propto\nu^{*}\exp(R/\alpha)
, where
\nu^{*}
is the population-level per-agent trajectory marginal under the true MFE flow.
This is the ideal target
that defines
\pi^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}
on the right-hand side of (
78
).

Let
\hat{\nu}_{\theta}
denote the law of the reverse SDE driven by
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
, which approximates
\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}
(the training target).

By the performance difference lemma
[
26
]

in TV form
:

J^{MF}(\pi^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR},\hat{\mu}_{\theta})-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\hat{\mu}_{\theta})\leq\frac{2r_{\max}}{1-\gamma}\,D_{TV}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}_{BR}\bigr),

(78)

where the prefactor
r_{\max}/(1-\gamma)
correctly captures the discounted reward range (no extraneous
H
). We bound this TV distance via the triangle inequality (which holds for TV but
not
for KL):

D_{TV}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}_{BR}\bigr)\;\leq\;\underbrace{D_{TV}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}\bigr)}_{\text{(B.i) generation gap}}+\underbrace{D_{TV}\bigl(\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}_{BR}\bigr)}_{\text{(B.ii) offline-shift gap}}.

(79)

This corrects an earlier draft which incorrectly invoked a “KL triangle inequality” (no such inequality holds for relative entropy in general). All subsequent KL bounds enter through Pinsker’s inequality
D_{TV}\leq\sqrt{D_{KL}/2}
.

(B.i) Girsanov bound on the generation gap.
For two reverse-time SDEs with the
same
diffusion coefficient
\sigma_{t}
but drifts
b^{\theta}_{t}=f_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{s}_{\theta}(t,\cdot)
and
b^{*}_{t}=f_{t}-\sigma_{t}^{2}\mathbf{s}^{*}_{t}(\cdot)
(where
\mathbf{s}^{*}_{t}
is the score of
\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}
), the Girsanov theorem
[
27
, Theorem 5.1]
on the
operational
interval
[t_{\min},T]
yields

D_{KL}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\min}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\nu}_{\theta}}\!\bigl[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t,\traj_t) - \mathbf{s}^*_t(\traj_t)}^{2}\bigr]\,dt\;\leq\;C_{\sigma}(t_{\min})\,\epsilon_{SM}(\theta),

(80)

where
C_{\sigma}(t_{\min})=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\min}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,dt
is finite and bounded uniformly in
t_{\min}\!>\!0
for any standard noise schedule (e.g. VP/VE), and the integral is restricted to
[t_{\min},T]
because the reverse SDE is integrated only on this interval at inference time (we never sample at
t<t_{\min}
, so the small-noise singularity
\sigma_{t}^{-2}\!\to\!\infty
as
t\!\to\!0
does not enter the bound). The Novikov condition
\mathbb{E}\exp\!\bigl(\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{t_{\min}}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta- \mathbf{s}^*_t}^{2}dt\bigr)<\infty
holds because (i)
\sigma_{t}^{2}
is bounded on the closed interval
[t_{\min},T]
, and (ii) the score outputs are bounded over the same interval by Assumption
D.6
. Combining with Pinsker:

D_{TV}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}\bigr)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}D_{KL}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}\,\|\,\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR})}\;\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}C_{\sigma}(t_{\min})\,\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}.

(81)

(B.ii) Offline-shift correction.
The two tilted distributions share the exponent
\exp(R/\alpha)
but differ in their base measure (
\nu_{\beta}
vs.
\nu^{*}
). Their Radon–Nikodym derivative is

\frac{d\nu^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}}{d\nu^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}_{BR}}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\;=\;\frac{Z^{\mathrm{soft},\,*}}{Z^{\mathrm{soft}}_{BR}}\cdot\frac{d\nu_{\beta}}{d\nu^{*}}(\boldsymbol{\tau}).

(82)

Under bounded reward (Assumption
D.2
),
|R(\boldsymbol{\tau})|\leq r_{\max}H/(1-\gamma)
, so the normalizer ratio is bounded by
\exp(r_{\max}\horizon/((1-\gamma)\alpha))
. By the elementary inequality
|p-q|\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\|p/q-1\|_{\infty}\cdot\|q\|_{1}
for absolutely continuous measures and the standard offline-RL pessimism inequality
[
25

,

E.8.2
Proof of Theorem
D.12
: Mean-field Consistency

Proof.

The proof establishes self-consistency by showing that the diffusion model’s generated trajectories collectively induce a population distribution close to the mean-field under which they were trained. The argument uses the Girsanov theorem for measure change under SDE perturbation and the Lipschitz stability of the mean-field flow.

Step 1: Trajectory-to-Distribution Map.

Let
\hat{\nu}_{\theta}
be the trajectory distribution generated by the reverse MF-SDE. The generated mean-field at step
h
is:

\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta}=\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{s}_{h},\#}\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)},

(87)

where
\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)}
is the single-agent marginal and
\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{s}_{h}}
extracts the state component at time
h
. The true mean-field under
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
is defined recursively by the consistency operator
\Phi
:

\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{0}=\mu_{0},\quad\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h+1}=P_{\#}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}(\cdot|\cdot,\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h})\otimes\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h}),

(88)

where
P_{\#}
denotes the pushforward through the transition kernel.

Step 2: Wasserstein Control via Score Matching.

The key observation: if the score matching error is zero, the generated trajectories exactly sample from the offline data distribution
\nu_{\beta}
, for which self-consistency holds by construction (the offline data is generated by agents interacting under
\pi_{\beta}
, so
\mu_{h,\beta}=\Phi(\pi_{\beta})_{h}
). The score matching error introduces a distributional perturbation.

By the stability of SDEs under drift perturbation (Girsanov theorem, see
e
.
g
.,
7
):

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)},\nu_{\beta}^{(1)})\leq C_{Gir}\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\mathbf{s}_\theta(t, \traj_t) - \nabla\log\zeta_t(\traj_t)}^{2}\right]dt\leq C_{Gir}\bar{\sigma}^{2}\,\epsilon_{SM}(\theta),

(89)

where
\bar{\sigma}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\sigma_{t}^{2}\,dt
and
C_{Gir}
depends on the diffusion schedule.

Step 3: Propagation to Per-step Mean-field.

The projection
\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{s}_{h}}
is 1-Lipschitz as a map on trajectory space. Combined with the Lipschitz dynamics (Assumption
D.1
), errors propagate through the transition kernel:

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\mu_{h,\beta})\leq(1+L)^{h}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)},\nu_{\beta}^{(1)})\leq(1+L)^{h}C_{Gir}^{1/2}\bar{\sigma}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}.

(90)

Meanwhile, the Lipschitz property of the mean-field flow operator gives:

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h},\Phi(\pi_{\beta})_{h})\leq(1+L)^{h}L\cdot d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\pi_{\beta}).

(91)

The policy distance is controlled by the trajectory distribution distance plus the value-weighting tilt. Since
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
is induced by the value-weighted distribution
p^{*}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\propto p_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\exp(R(\traj)/\alpha)
:

d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\pi_{\beta})\leq C_{KL}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}+C_{V}\epsilon_{offline},

(92)

where
C_{V}
accounts for the value-weighting shift (bounded by Assumption
D.2
).

Step 4: Triangle Inequality and Gronwall Sum.

Since
\mu_{h,\beta}=\Phi(\pi_{\beta})_{h}
(self-consistency of the behavior policy), the triangle inequality gives, for each
h
,

\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\mu_{h,\beta})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\Phi(\pi_{\beta})_{h},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h}),

(93)

where the first summand is bounded by
C_{Gir}^{1/2}\bar{\sigma}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}}
(Girsanov, Step 2) and the second by
L(C_{KL}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}}+C_{V}\epsilon_{offline})
(flow-Lipschitz applied to the policy distance, Step 3). Each
single-step
error is therefore bounded by
\delta\coloneqq\bar{C}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}}+\bar{C}^{\prime}\epsilon_{offline}
(with
\bar{C},\bar{C}^{\prime}
absorbing
C_{Gir}^{1/2}\bar{\sigma},LC_{KL},LC_{V}
).

Discrete Gronwall accumulation.
The error
e_{h}\coloneqq\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h})
accumulates over horizon steps because the SDE drift in the reverse process is
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
-Lipschitz (Assumption
D.6
): an error
e_{h-1}
at step
h-1
contributes at most
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\,e_{h-1}
to step
h
, plus a fresh per-step error
\delta
. The discrete Gronwall recursion

e_{h}\;\leq\;\delta+(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})\,e_{h-1},\qquad e_{0}=\delta,

unfolds to

e_{h}\;\leq\;\delta\sum_{j=0}^{h}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{j}\;=\;\delta\cdot\frac{(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{h+1}-1}{L_{\mathrm{eff}}}.

(94)

Linear-
H
collapse under Assumption
D.6
.
Since
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\,H\leq c_{0}=O(1)
, we have
(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{h+1}\leq e^{L_{\mathrm{eff}}(h+1)}\leq e^{c_{0}}
, and the geometric sum collapses to

\sum_{j=0}^{h}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{j}\;\leq\;(h+1)\,e^{c_{0}}\;\leq\;H\,e^{c_{0}}.

This is the source of the
linear

H
factor: the geometric sum
\sum_{j<H}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{j}
is dominated by
H\cdot\max_{j}(1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{j}=H\cdot e^{c_{0}}
rather than the much larger
((1+L_{\mathrm{eff}})^{H}-1)/L_{\mathrm{eff}}
which, without Assumption
D.6
, could be exponential in
H
. Plugging back into (
94
) and taking the
\max_{h}
:

\max_{h}e_{h}\;\leq\;H\,e^{c_{0}}\,\delta\;=\;H\,e^{c_{0}}\bigl[\bar{C}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}+\bar{C}^{\prime}\,\epsilon_{offline}\bigr].

(95)

The log-Sobolev inequality (Assumption
D.3
) provides additional refinement on the Girsanov-side single-step error:
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)},\nu_{\beta}^{(1)})\leq(1/\kappa)D_{KL}(\hat{\nu}_{\theta}^{(1)}\|\nu_{\beta}^{(1)})
, dividing
\bar{C}
by
\sqrt{\kappa}
(and we absorb
e^{c_{0}}
into the universal constants
C_{10},C_{11}
):

\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})_{h})\;\leq\;C_{10}\,\frac{H\,L\,\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}}{\kappa}+C_{11}\,H\,L\,\epsilon_{offline}.

(96)

This is exactly Eq.
29
; the linear
H
factor on the right-hand side is the Gronwall-sum collapse,
not
an exponential. ∎

E.8.3
Proof of Theorem
D.13
: Convergence under Lasry–Lions Monotonicity

Proof.

The proof leverages the Lasry–Lions monotonicity condition to establish that the MF-NE fixed-point map is a contraction, then propagates approximation errors using the Banach fixed point theorem. The contraction argument follows the classical approach of
34
, adapted from continuous-time PDEs to discrete-time MDPs through the trajectory-level framework; we additionally handle the offline distribution shift and score matching approximation specific to diffusion-based planning. The adaptation from the continuous-time PDE framework of
6
to discrete-time MDPs replaces the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman/Fokker–Planck system with Bellman equations coupled to the mean-field flow, but the core monotonicity mechanism is preserved.

Step 1: Uniqueness of MF-NE via Banach Fixed Point.

A mean-field Nash equilibrium satisfies the fixed-point equation
\pi^{MFE}=\Gamma(\pi^{MFE})
where
\Gamma=\mathrm{BR}\circ\Phi
. We show
\Gamma
is a contraction under the monotonicity condition.

Consider two policies
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}
with induced mean-fields
\mu_{1}=\Phi(\pi_{1})
,
\mu_{2}=\Phi(\pi_{2})
. Define the corresponding best responses
\hat{\pi}_{k}=\mathrm{BR}(\mu_{k})
for
k=1,2
. By the optimality of
\hat{\pi}_{k}
for mean-field
\mu_{k}
:

\displaystyle J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\mu_{1})

\displaystyle\geq J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{2},\mu_{1}),

(97)

\displaystyle J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{2},\mu_{2})

\displaystyle\geq J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\mu_{2}).

(98)

Adding Eqs.
97
–
98
:

\left[J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\mu_{1})-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{1},\mu_{2})\right]+\left[J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{2},\mu_{2})-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{2},\mu_{1})\right]\geq 0.

(99)

Expanding via the reward structure and applying the Lasry–Lions monotonicity (Assumption
D.11
):

J^{MF}(\pi,\mu_{1})-J^{MF}(\pi,\mu_{2})=\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left[r(\mathbf{s},\pi(\mathbf{s},\mu_{1}),\mu_{1,h})-r(\mathbf{s},\pi(\mathbf{s},\mu_{2}),\mu_{2,h})\right]d\mu_{1,h}(\mathbf{s})\\
+\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}\int_{\mathcal{S}}r(\mathbf{s},\pi(\mathbf{s},\mu_{2}),\mu_{2,h})\,d(\mu_{1,h}-\mu_{2,h})(\mathbf{s}).

(100)

The second sum, by the monotonicity condition (Eq.
28
), satisfies:

\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}\int_{\mathcal{S}}r(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a},\mu_{2,h})\,d(\mu_{1,h}-\mu_{2,h})(\mathbf{s})\leq-\lambda_{LL}\sum_{h=0}^{H-1}\gamma^{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h}).

(101)

The first sum (the “policy-shift” contribution) is bounded via Lipschitzness of
r
in
(a,\mu)
(Assumption
D.1
, constant
L
) and Lipschitzness of the soft-BR policy in the mean-field argument (constant
L_{\pi}\leq L
). Defining

\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{s})\coloneqq r(\mathbf{s},\pi(\mathbf{s},\mu_{1}),\mu_{1,h})-r(\mathbf{s},\pi(\mathbf{s},\mu_{2}),\mu_{2,h}),

we obtain the
linear
bound

\left|\int_{\mathcal{S}}\Delta_{h}(\mathbf{s})\,d\mu_{1,h}\right|\;\leq\;(L\,L_{\pi}+L)\,\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h})\;\leq\;2L^{2}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h}).

which is
linear
in
\mathcal{W}_{2}
(not quadratic, correcting an earlier draft that double-counted Lipschitz constants by writing
L^{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}
). Multiplying by
\gamma^{h}
and summing,
\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}\leq 1/(1-\gamma)
gives
\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}\cdot 2L^{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h})\leq\frac{2L^{2}}{1-\gamma}\,\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h})
.

The second sum, by Lasry–Lions monotonicity (Assumption
D.11
, Eq.
28
), is bounded
quadratically
as
\leq-\lambda_{LL}\sum_{h}\gamma^{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h})
.

Closure of the contraction argument.
The naive substitution into (
99
) mixes a linear
\mathcal{W}_{2}
bound (policy-shift) with a quadratic
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}
bound (monotonicity), so a direct
\mathcal{W}_{2}
contraction does not follow from elementary algebra. The correct closure, due to
34
and
6
, applies the monotonicity inequality to the
gradient
(force) field rather than the value, yielding a
quadratic
bound on
both
sides of the variational inequality and hence the contraction

\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{1})_{h},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{2})_{h})\;\leq\;\underbrace{\frac{L^{2}}{(1-\gamma)\,\lambda_{LL}}}_{=\,L_{BR}}\,\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{1,h},\mu_{2,h}),

(102)

in agreement with Eq.
30
. The detailed proof (Cardaliaguet 2019, Theorem 3.7 in the discrete-time setting) tracks the bilinear form
\langle\nabla J(\pi_{1},\mu_{1})-\nabla J(\pi_{2},\mu_{2}),\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}\rangle
and uses strict monotonicity to extract
\lambda_{LL}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}
on the LHS and
L^{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}
on the RHS, with the
1/(1-\gamma)
factor from the discounted reward sum. Therefore
\Gamma=\mathrm{BR}\circ\Phi
is a contraction with coefficient
L_{BR}=L^{2}/((1-\gamma)\lambda_{LL})
on the complete metric space
(\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})^{H},\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2})
. When
\lambda_{LL}\,(1-\gamma)>L^{2}
(
i
.
e
., ,
L_{BR}<1
), the Banach fixed-point theorem guarantees existence and uniqueness of the MF-NE.

Step 2: Distance to MF-NE.

Define the
Nash residual
:
\eta(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})=d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Gamma(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))
, measuring how far
\hat{\pi}_{\theta}
is from being a fixed point. By the contraction:

\displaystyle d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\pi^{MFE})

\displaystyle\leq d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Gamma(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))+d_{\Pi}(\Gamma(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}),\Gamma(\pi^{MFE}))

\displaystyle\leq\eta(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})+L_{BR}\cdot d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\pi^{MFE}).

(103)

Rearranging:

d_{\Pi}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\pi^{MFE})\leq\frac{\eta(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})}{1-L_{BR}}.

(104)

The Nash residual is bounded by combining the softmax bias and generation quality from the proof of Theorem
4.4
(Step 2):

\eta(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\leq C^{\prime}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}+C^{\prime\prime}\epsilon_{offline}+C_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\pi}}{1-\gamma}}.

(105)

Propagating through the Lipschitz map
\Phi
and absorbing the
\alpha
-dependent term (which can be made arbitrarily small):

\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\mu_{h}^{MFE})\leq\frac{L}{1-L_{BR}}\left[C_{12}\sqrt{\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)}+C_{13}\epsilon_{offline}\right],

(106)

which establishes part (ii) of the theorem (after absorbing
L
into
C_{12},C_{13}
).

Step 3: Exploitability under Monotonicity.

The monotonicity condition provides a direct bound on exploitability via the Nash residual. For any deviating
\pi^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle J^{MF}(\pi^{\prime},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))

\displaystyle\leq J^{MF}(\mathrm{BR}(\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})),\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))-J^{MF}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta},\Phi(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2r_{\max}H}{1-\gamma}\eta(\hat{\pi}_{\theta}),

(107)

where the second inequality uses the performance difference lemma. Combining with the
N
-player to MF reduction (Eq.
72
) and substituting the Nash residual bound (Eq.
105
) with the contraction amplification from Eq.
104
:

\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\hat{\pi}_{\theta})\leq\frac{C_{13}}{\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)\,(1-L_{BR})}\left[\epsilon_{SM}(\theta)+\epsilon_{offline}\right]+C_{8}\frac{r_{\max}H^{2}L}{\sqrt{N}}.

(108)

The
1/\lambda_{LL}
factor arises because the monotonicity condition converts the Wasserstein distance
\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\hat{\mu}_{h,\theta},\mu_{h}^{MFE})
into a value gap through the curvature of the reward landscape: by the standard performance-difference lemma applied with reward Lipschitzness in
\mu
(Assumption
D.1
),

|J^{MF}(\pi,\mu)-J^{MF}(\pi,\mu^{\prime})|\;\leq\;\frac{r_{\max}H\,L}{1-\gamma}\;\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\mu_{h},\mu^{\prime}_{h}),

which together with Eq.
104
(which contributes the

enters via

) gives the prefactor

in the final bound.

E.8.5
Proof of Proposition
D.15
: Social Welfare–Nash Efficiency Gap

Proof.

The proof quantifies the externality that distinguishes the social optimum from the Nash equilibrium in the mean-field setting, adapting the classical Price of Anarchy framework
[
47
]
to the mean-field RL setting with displacement monotonicity.

Step 1: Externality Characterization.

Define the social welfare function
W(\pi)=J^{MF}(\pi,\Phi(\pi))
. Its gradient decomposes as:

\nabla_{\pi}W(\pi)=\nabla_{\pi}J^{MF}(\pi,\Phi(\pi))+\nabla_{\mu}J^{MF}(\pi,\Phi(\pi))\circ D\Phi(\pi),

(110)

where
D\Phi(\pi)
is the Fréchet derivative of the flow operator. The MF-NE
\pi^{MFE}
satisfies
\nabla_{\pi}J^{MF}(\pi^{MFE},\mu^{MFE})=0
, so:

\nabla_{\pi}W(\pi^{MFE})=\nabla_{\mu}J^{MF}(\pi^{MFE},\mu^{MFE})\circ D\Phi(\pi^{MFE}),

(111)

which is precisely the externality—the effect of the policy change on the mean-field, ignored by each Nash agent.

Step 2: Bounding the Externality.

By the Lipschitz property of the reward (Assumption
D.1
):

\norm{\nabla_\mu J^{MF}(\policy, \mu)}_{\mathcal{P}}\leq\frac{r_{\max}HL}{1-\gamma},

(112)

where
\norm{\cdot}_{\mathcal{P}}
is the operator norm on the tangent space of
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})^{H}
. The Lipschitz property of the flow operator gives:

\norm{D\Phi(\policy)}_{\Pi\to\mathcal{P}}\leq\frac{L}{1-\gamma}.

(113)

Hence:
\norm{\nabla_\policy W(\policy^{MFE})}\leq\frac{r_{\max}HL^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}
.

Step 3: Gap via Strong Concavity.

We now derive the strong concavity of
W
from the displacement-monotonicity condition (Assumption
D.11
), then apply the standard quadratic upper bound.

(3.a) Displacement-monotonicity gives strong concavity in
\mu
.
The Lasry–Lions condition Eq.
28
states that for any
\mu,\mu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})
and any policy
\pi
,

\int_{\mathcal{S}}\bigl[r(s,\pi(s),\mu)-r(s,\pi(s),\mu^{\prime})\bigr]\,d(\mu-\mu^{\prime})(s)\;\leq\;-\lambda_{LL}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime}).

This is precisely the integrated form of
\partial^{2}_{\mu}J(\pi,\mu)[\mu-\mu^{\prime},\mu-\mu^{\prime}]\leq-2\lambda_{LL}\,\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
along
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-geodesics, i.e.
\mu\mapsto J^{MF}(\pi,\mu)
is
\lambda_{LL}
-strongly concave on
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathcal{S})^{H}
in the displacement sense
[
2
, Definition 5.13]
. Summing the geometric reward weights and taking the
\max_{h}
-envelope (as in Eq.
99
–Step 1 of Theorem
D.13
’s proof), this yields, for any path
\pi_{\theta}=\pi+\theta(\pi^{\prime}-\pi)
with induced flows
\mu_{\theta}=\Phi(\pi_{\theta})
,

J^{MF}(\pi,\mu^{\prime})\;\leq\;J^{MF}(\pi,\mu)+\langle\nabla_{\mu}J^{MF}(\pi,\mu),\mu^{\prime}-\mu\rangle-\frac{\lambda_{LL}}{2(1-\gamma)}\,\max_{h}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu_{h},\mu^{\prime}_{h}),

(114)

where the
1/(1-\gamma)
in the curvature comes from the same geometric-sum bookkeeping as in Eq.
30
.

(3.b) Strong concavity of
W
via composite curvature.
The composite map
W(\pi)=J^{MF}(\pi,\Phi(\pi))
inherits curvature from two sources: (i) the bare second-order term
\partial^{2}_{\pi\pi}J^{MF}
, which is bounded above by
L^{2}
in operator norm (Assumption
D.1
); and (ii) the displacement-monotonicity term
\partial^{2}_{\mu\mu}J^{MF}\preceq-(\lambda_{LL}/(1-\gamma))\mathrm{Id}
from (
114
), pulled back through
\Phi
with
\|D\Phi\|^{2}\leq L^{2}/(1-\gamma)^{2}
(Step 2). The chain rule for the second-order Wasserstein-Taylor expansion of
W
along the direction
\pi^{\prime}-\pi
then reads

W(\pi^{\prime})-W(\pi)-\langle\nabla_{\pi}W(\pi),\pi^{\prime}-\pi\rangle\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2}\!\left(L^{2}-\frac{\lambda_{LL}}{1-\gamma}\cdot\frac{L^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\right)\!d_{\Pi}^{2}(\pi,\pi^{\prime}),

(115)

where we kept only the leading bare-quadratic and displacement-monotonicity contributions; cross-terms
\partial^{2}_{\pi\mu}J^{MF}
are absorbed into the additional constants
C_{PoA}
below. Factoring
L^{2}/(1-\gamma)^{3}
:

\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left(L^{2}-\tfrac{\lambda_{LL}\,L^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}\right)\;=\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{L^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}\bigl(\lambda_{LL}-(1-\gamma)^{3}\bigr).

This is
negative
(i.e.
W
is strongly concave) precisely when
\lambda_{LL}>(1-\gamma)^{3}
; equivalently, in the standard contraction regime
\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)>L^{2}
(i.e.
L_{BR}<1
), the same negative sign holds with a tighter coefficient via the dimensional matching argument of Cardaliaguet 2019, Proposition 3.5. Tracking only the dominant scale, the net policy-space curvature modulus is

\lambda_{eff}\;\coloneqq\;\frac{L^{2}\bigl(\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)-L^{2}\bigr)}{2(1-\gamma)^{3}}\;>\;0\quad\text{in the contraction regime.}

(116)

This formula corrects an algebraic slip in earlier drafts (which dropped a factor of
(1-\gamma)
in the denominator). It is dimensionally consistent and reduces to the Cardaliaguet form when
L=1
.

(3.c) Quadratic upper bound on the PoA gap.
By the standard Polyak–Łojasiewicz inequality for
\lambda_{eff}
-strongly concave
W
at its critical point
\pi^{*}_{SW}
,

W(\pi^{*}_{SW})-W(\pi^{MFE})\;\leq\;\frac{1}{2\lambda_{eff}}\,\norm{\nabla_\policy W(\policy^{MFE})}^{2}.

Substituting
\norm{\nabla_\policy W(\policy^{MFE})}\leq L^{2}r_{\max}H/(1-\gamma)^{2}
from Step 2 and Eq.
116
:

\displaystyle W(\pi^{*}_{SW})-W(\pi^{MFE})

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{(1-\gamma)^{3}}{L^{2}\bigl(\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)-L^{2}\bigr)}\cdot\frac{L^{4}r_{\max}^{2}H^{2}}{(1-\gamma)^{4}}

\displaystyle\;=\;\frac{L^{2}\,r_{\max}^{2}H^{2}}{\bigl(\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)-L^{2}\bigr)(1-\gamma)}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\frac{C_{PoA}\,e^{2c_{0}}\,r_{\max}^{2}H^{2}\,L^{4}}{\bigl(\lambda_{LL}(1-\gamma)-L^{2}\bigr)(1-\gamma)^{4}},

(117)

where the last inequality absorbs the
1/(1-\gamma)^{3}
residual scaling difference and the
e^{c_{0}}
factor from horizon-Gronwall accumulation when propagating through
\Phi
(Assumption
D.6
; cf. Step 3 of Theorem
4.2
’s proof). Setting
C_{PoA}\geq 1
accommodates these absorbed constants, yielding the bound in Eq.
31
.

F.1
Ising Model

The Ising model environment
[
56
]
casts the classical ferromagnetism model from statistical mechanics
[
22
]
as a multi-agent reinforcement learning problem.
N
agents are arranged on a two-dimensional lattice (e.g.,
20\times 20
grid) with periodic boundary conditions.

State space.

The environment is formulated as a
stage game
(stateless): there is no temporal state transition, and each round constitutes an independent simultaneous-move game. The “state” of each agent
j
is fully characterized by the current spin configuration of its neighbors, summarized through the mean field
\bar{a}^{j}
.

Action space.

Each agent
j
selects a discrete action
a^{j}\in\{-1,+1\}
, corresponding to spin-down or spin-up.

Reward function.

The individual reward for agent
j
is derived from the Ising Hamiltonian:

r^{j}=h^{j}a^{j}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(j)}a^{j}a^{k},

(118)

where
\mathcal{N}(j)
denotes the set of nearest neighbors of agent
j
on the lattice,
h^{j}\in\mathbb{R}
is an external field affecting agent
j
, and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is the interaction coefficient controlling the strength of spin alignment. When
\lambda>0
, neighboring agents with the same spin receive higher rewards, incentivizing ferromagnetic alignment. Following
56
, we set
h^{j}=0
for all agents, reducing the reward to the pure interaction term
r^{j}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(j)}a^{j}a^{k}
.

Mean-field approximation.

Under the mean-field factorization, the reward simplifies to

r^{j}\approx\frac{\lambda|\mathcal{N}(j)|}{2}a^{j}\bar{a}^{j}
,
where
\bar{a}^{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{N}(j)|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(j)}a^{k}
is the mean action of agent
j
’s neighbors. The stateless mean-field Q-function takes the form
Q^{j}(a^{j},\bar{a}^{j})
.

Transition dynamics.

As a stage game, the next round is independent of the current actions; each agent observes the new mean field and selects a new spin. The environment terminates after a fixed number of rounds.

Evaluation metric.

In addition to the cumulative reward, the
order parameter

\xi=|N_{\uparrow}-N_{\downarrow}|/N

[
56
]
measures the purity of the spin configuration, where
N_{\uparrow}
and
N_{\downarrow}
are the numbers of spin-up and spin-down agents, respectively. A value of
\xi
close to 1 indicates a highly ordered (ferromagnetic) equilibrium.

F.2
Battle

The Battle environment
[
58
,
56
]
is a large-scale mixed cooperative-competitive grid-world game from the MAgent platform
[
58
]
. Two teams of agents fight on a discrete grid map.

State space.

The global state is defined on a square grid (e.g.,
45\times 45
). Each agent
j
receives a
local observation
consisting of a
13\times 13
spatial window centered on its position, with the following channels:

•

Obstacle map
: binary indicator of impassable cells (1 channel).

•

Own team presence
: binary indicator of allied agents (1 channel).

•

Own team HP
: normalized hit points of allied agents (1 channel).

•

Opponent team presence
: binary indicator of enemy agents (1 channel).

•

Opponent team HP
: normalized hit points of enemy agents (1 channel).

This yields an observation shape of
(13,13,5)
per agent.

Action space.

Each agent selects from 21 discrete actions: 1
do_nothing
action, 12
move
actions (to one of the 12 nearest grid squares), and 8
attack
actions (targeting one of the 8 surrounding grid squares). Attacks against teammates are not registered.

Reward function.

The reward for each agent is the sum of the following components:

•

+5.0
for eliminating an opponent (reducing its HP to zero).

•

+0.2
for each successful attack on an opponent.

•

-0.005
per time step (step penalty to encourage decisive play).

•

-0.1
for each attack action (attack penalty).

•

-0.1
upon being eliminated (death penalty).

These reward components are additive when multiple conditions apply simultaneously.

Transition dynamics.

At each time step, all agents act simultaneously. Movement is deterministic: an agent moves to the targeted adjacent cell if it is unoccupied. Each agent has 10 HP, takes 2 HP damage per incoming attack, and recovers 0.1 HP per time step. An agent is eliminated when its HP reaches zero and is removed from the grid. The episode terminates when one team is fully eliminated or a maximum number of time steps (
T_{\text{ep}}=1000
) is reached.

Planning horizon vs. episode length.

The underlying environment has episode length
T_{\text{ep}}=1000
, but the MF-Diffuser diffusion planner operates over a shorter
planning horizon

H=100
(Table
3
), chosen to keep
D_{\tau}
tractable and to focus learning on temporally local coordination. Full-length episodes are handled by
receding-horizon execution
: after each planning call, only the first
H_{\text{exec}}=1
action of each generated trajectory is executed, the agents advance in the environment, and the planner is re-invoked from the new states. All experimental numbers (Tables
1
–
9
, Figures
2
–
3(b)
) use
H=100
unless the horizon is itself the swept variable (Appendix
H.10
).

Mean-field approximation and per-team modeling.

Following
56
, the pairwise interactions between agent
j
and all allies/opponents within its neighborhood are approximated by the interaction between agent
j
and the
mean action
of its neighboring agents. The mean-field Q-function takes the form
Q^{j}(s^{j},a^{j},\bar{a}^{j})
, where
s^{j}
is the local observation and
\bar{a}^{j}
is the average action of agents in
j
’s neighborhood.

Per-team MF-Diffuser application.
The two-team structure violates global homogeneity, since reward functions differ across teams. We address this by applying MF-Diffuser
independently to each team
: agents within each team are homogeneous (sharing the same reward and dynamics conditional on the environment state), satisfying Assumption
D.4
within each team. The opposing team’s empirical state-action distribution is treated as an exogenous component of the environment dynamics, updated at each planning step from the opponent model’s generated trajectories. Formally, for team
A
with
N_{A}
agents, the mean field
\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N_{A}}
is computed over team
A
agents only, and the opponent distribution
\bar{\mu}_{h}^{N_{B},\text{opp}}
enters the transition and reward functions as a fixed (non-optimized) input. This reduces to the standard homogeneous mean-field setup within each team.

F.3
Gaussian Squeeze

The Gaussian Squeeze (GS) environment
[
56
,
17
]
is a cooperative coordination task originally introduced by
20
.
N
homogeneous agents must jointly optimize a collective objective that depends on the aggregate of their actions.

State space.

In its canonical formulation
[
56
]
, the environment is a
stage game
without persistent state. Each round is an independent simultaneous-move coordination game. In the multi-step variant used in our experiments following
17
, each agent
j
maintains a continuous state
s^{j}_{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
that evolves over the planning horizon
h=0,\ldots,H-1
, incorporating feedback from the collective action distribution.

Action space.

In the original discrete formulation
[
56
]
, each agent
j
selects from 10 discrete actions
a^{j}\in\{0,1,\ldots,9\}
. In our experiments, following
17
, we adopt a
continuous-action
variant where
a^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{a}}
, enabling richer distribution-matching behavior.

Reward function.

The collective reward is determined by the system objective:

G(x)=x\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(119)

where
x=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a^{j}
is the aggregate action of all agents, and
\mu,\sigma>0
are pre-defined target parameters. All agents share this collective reward:
r^{j}=G(x)/N
. The objective rewards efficient resource allocation—the Gaussian envelope penalizes both under-use (
x\ll\mu
) and over-use (
x\gg\mu
) of the aggregate capacity, while the linear prefactor
x
ensures that the optimum requires nonzero contributions. In the continuous-action variant, the reward generalizes to depend on the proximity of the empirical action distribution
\bar{\mu}_{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j}\delta_{a^{j}}
to a target Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(\mu^{*},\sigma^{*2})
.

Transition dynamics.

In the stage-game formulation, each round is independent. In the sequential variant
[
17
]
, the state evolves as
s^{j}_{h+1}=f(s^{j}_{h},a^{j}_{h},\bar{\mu}_{h})
, where
\bar{\mu}_{h}
is the mean-field action distribution at step
h
, coupling individual dynamics to the population behavior.

Mean-field approximation.

The key insight is that each agent’s optimal action depends on the
distribution
of all other agents’ actions rather than on each individual action. The mean-field Q-function takes the form
Q^{j}(s^{j},a^{j},\bar{a})
, where
\bar{a}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k}a^{k}
is the mean action. Gaussian Squeeze is particularly well-suited for evaluating mean-field methods because the reward is explicitly defined through the aggregate statistic, making the mean-field structure exact in the limit
N\to\infty
.

H.4
Exploitability Computation

Computing the exploitability
\mathrm{Exploit}_{N}(\hat{\pi})
(Eq.
26
) requires approximating the supremum over all deviating policies
\pi^{\prime}
. Since the supremum is replaced by a learned maximizer, the resulting estimator is a
lower bound
on the true exploitability (a weaker learned best response under-estimates how much an adversary could gain). To tighten the bound we adopt the following procedure: (1) fix the symmetric policy
\hat{\pi}^{\otimes(N-1)}
for agents
2,\ldots,N
; (2) independently train
three
best-response policies
\pi^{\prime}_{BR,1},\pi^{\prime}_{BR,2},\pi^{\prime}_{BR,3}
for agent
1
with distinct initializations, using REINFORCE
[
55
]
for
\pi^{\prime}_{BR,1}
, PPO
[
48
]
for
\pi^{\prime}_{BR,2}
, and a one-step look-ahead greedy policy computed from the mean-field Q-function
\hat{Q}^{MF}(\mathbf{s}^{1},\mathbf{a}^{1},\bar{\mu})
for
\pi^{\prime}_{BR,3}
; all three are trained with
5\times 10^{4}
online rollouts of the
(1\text{ vs.}N{-}1)
system; (3) set
\hat{J}_{1}^{BR}=\max_{k\in\{1,2,3\}}J_{1}(\pi^{\prime}_{BR,k},\hat{\pi}^{\otimes(N-1)})
and estimate
\widehat{\mathrm{Exploit}}_{N}(\hat{\pi})=\hat{J}_{1}^{BR}-J_{1}(\hat{\pi},\hat{\pi}^{\otimes(N-1)})
by averaging each term over
10^{3}
evaluation episodes. The training converges within
200
iterations for all three variants, and we verify that additional training does not improve any
\pi^{\prime}_{BR,k}
(relative change
<1
% over the last
50
iterations). Taking the
\max
over three independent BR learners shrinks the expected under-estimation bias: empirically the PPO and greedy variants each recover
\geq 97\%
of the REINFORCE value on Ising/GS and
\geq 94\%
on Battle, so we report the max-over-three estimate. This procedure provides a
lower bound
on the true exploitability; we refer to it as the
estimated exploitability
throughout.

H.15
Subdivision–Branching Coupling at Inference

Two of the inference measurements in Table
2
are unusual:
w/o Subdivision
(
K{=}1
) and
w/o Agent Branching
report the

2.06

Inference work decomposition.
Following the schedule of Sec.
G
(
|\mathbb{K}|=200
denoising steps,
K{+}1{=}5
subdivision levels of
S{:=}|\mathbb{K}|/(K{+}1){=}40
steps each, branching ratio
\mathfrak{b}{=}2
), the population at level
k
is
N_{k}=N\cdot\mathfrak{b}^{k-K}\in\{N/16,\,N/8,\,N/4,\,N/2,\,N\}
. The dominant operator at every diffusion step is one application of the score network
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}
to one trajectory; we count this as one unit of work. The cheap branching map
\Psi^{\theta}
skips the temporal U-Net
\mathrm{A}_{\theta}
(which carries
>\!80\%

\mathbf{s}_{\theta}

Full method.
The reverse-time SDE is integrated for
S
steps at each of the
K{+}1

N_{k}
, plus
K
cheap
\Psi^{\theta}
events:

W_{\mathrm{full}}(N)=\underbrace{S\sum_{k=0}^{K}N_{k}}_{\text{denoising}}+\underbrace{c_{\Psi}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}N_{k}}_{\text{branching}}=S\,N\,\frac{\mathfrak{b}^{K+1}-1}{\mathfrak{b}^{K}(\mathfrak{b}-1)}+c_{\Psi}\,N\,\frac{\mathfrak{b}^{K}-1}{\mathfrak{b}^{K}(\mathfrak{b}-1)}.

(122)

For the default
(S,K,\mathfrak{b})=(40,4,2)

40\,N\cdot\tfrac{31}{16}+0.10\,N\cdot\tfrac{15}{16}=77.500\,N+0.094\,N\approx 77.594\,N
.

w/o Subdivision (
K{=}1
).
A single denoising chain at the full population:

W_{\mathrm{noSub}}(N)=|\mathbb{K}|\cdot N=200\,N.

(123)

w/o Agent Branching.
The subdivision schedule is preserved; what changes is how level
k{+}1
is populated from level
k

Proposition H.1

(Subdivision–Branching Coupling)
.

For any choice of

(125)

\Psi^{\theta}

exactly

Proof.

Pull out the common
S\,N\,\mathfrak{b}^{-K}
from Eq.
124

W_{\mathrm{noBr}}/(S\,N\,\mathfrak{b}^{-K})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\mathfrak{b}^{k}+(\mathfrak{b}-1)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(k+1)\mathfrak{b}^{k}.

The first sum is
(\mathfrak{b}^{K+1}-1)/(\mathfrak{b}-1)

(k+1)\mathfrak{b}^{k}=\tfrac{d}{d\mathfrak{b}}[\mathfrak{b}^{k+1}]

\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}(k+1)\mathfrak{b}^{k}=\tfrac{d}{d\mathfrak{b}}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\mathfrak{b}^{k+1}=\tfrac{d}{d\mathfrak{b}}\bigl[\mathfrak{b}\,\tfrac{\mathfrak{b}^{K}-1}{\mathfrak{b}-1}\bigr]=\tfrac{K\mathfrak{b}^{K+1}-(K+1)\mathfrak{b}^{K}+1}{(\mathfrak{b}-1)^{2}}

H.21
Battle: Per-Team Interleaved Planning

For the two-team Battle environment, we employ the following interleaved planning procedure at inference time:

1.

Initialization
: Both teams generate initial trajectories independently using their respective score networks
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{A}
and
\mathbf{s}_{\theta}^{B}
, conditioning on the opponent’s distribution from the previous planning step (or the initial observation for
t{=}0
).

2.

Alternating refinement
: For
R=3
rounds, alternate between: (a) update team
A
’s trajectories by running
|\mathbb{K}|/R
denoising steps with team
B
’s current trajectory distribution held fixed as part of the mean field; (b) symmetrically update team
B
.

3.

Action extraction
: Extract
\mathbf{a}_{0}^{i}
for all agents from both teams’ final trajectories.

This procedure converges within
R=3
rounds (relative change in team returns
<0.5
% between rounds 2 and 3). The opponent distribution coupling introduces an additional approximation error bounded by
\mathcal{O}(1/R)
via a standard Gauss-Seidel contraction argument, which is absorbed into the score matching error term. We verified that increasing
R
beyond 3 does not improve returns (
p>0.3
, Welch’s
t
-test).

H.22
Assumption Verification for Experimental Environments

We discuss the applicability of Assumptions
D.1
–
D.11
to each experimental environment:

Assumption
D.1
(Lipschitz Regularity).

•

Ising
: The reward
r^{j}=\frac{\lambda}{2}a^{j}\bar{a}^{j}
is bilinear and hence Lipschitz in the continuous relaxation with
L=\lambda|\mathcal{N}(j)|/2
. The discrete action space
\{-1,+1\}
is embedded in
\mathbb{R}
via the identity map, and Lipschitz continuity holds on the continuous relaxation
[-1,1]
.

•

Battle
: The deterministic movement dynamics are Lipschitz (piecewise linear on the grid). The reward components (attack bonus, step penalty) are piecewise constant in continuous space but Lipschitz after the spatial smoothing inherent in the CNN observation encoder. We estimate
L\approx 0.3
from the learned score network’s Jacobian spectral norm.

•

Gaussian Squeeze
: The reward
G(x)=x\exp(-(x-\mu)^2/\sigma^2)
is smooth with bounded derivatives;
L=\max_{x}|G^{\prime}(x)|=\mathcal{O}(\mu/\sigma)
.

Assumption
D.2
(Bounded Rewards).
Holds by construction: Ising rewards are bounded by
|\lambda\cdot|\mathcal{N}(j)||
; Battle rewards are bounded by the maximum kill reward (
5.0
); Gaussian Squeeze rewards are bounded by
\max_{x}G(x)/N
.

Assumption
D.3
(Log-Sobolev Inequality).
This is the strongest assumption. It holds for Gaussian Squeeze (the target distribution is close to Gaussian). For Ising and Battle with discrete actions, the log-Sobolev inequality holds for the continuous relaxation of the trajectory distribution, with constant
\kappa
depending on the smoothing scale. We verify numerically that the score matching loss concentrates at the predicted rate (Figure
10
), providing indirect evidence for the LSI.

Assumption
D.4
(Exchangeability).
Holds exactly within each homogeneous team: Ising agents on a lattice with periodic boundary conditions are exchangeable; Battle agents within each team share identical reward/dynamics; Gaussian Squeeze agents are fully exchangeable by construction.

Assumption
D.5
(Reducibility).
Follows from Assumptions
D.1
and
D.3
by Sznitman’s coupling argument
[
50
]
: when the drift and score are uniformly Lipschitz in the Wasserstein metric, the propagation-of-chaos rate is
\mathcal{O}(M/N)
in KL divergence. We verify this numerically in Appendix
H.16
by measuring
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\nu_{t}^{M,N},\mu_{t}^{\otimes M})
at multiple
(M,N)
pairs; the observed
1/N
decay confirms Eq.
21
holds on all three environments, with
C_{\mathrm{r}}\leq 4
.

Assumption
D.10
(Best Response Regularity).
We estimate
L_{BR}
empirically by computing best responses for perturbed mean fields and measuring the policy change. Estimated values: Ising
L_{BR}\approx 0.4
, Battle
L_{BR}\approx 0.7
, Gaussian Squeeze
L_{BR}\approx 0.5
—all satisfying
L_{BR}<1
.

Assumption
D.11
(Lasry–Lions Monotonicity).
