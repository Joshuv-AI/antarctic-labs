Adaptive Test for Jump Journal: Journal of Econometrics
Huifang Ma 1 and Long Feng 1 1 Nankai University

Abstract
We develop an adaptive jump test for discretely observed high-frequency semimartingales by combining the Aït-Sahalia–Jacod ratio statistic ( 3 ) and the Lee–Mykland extreme-return statistic ( 34 ) with the Cauchy combination rule. Allowing stochastic Itô drift, volatility, and leverage, we show asymptotic independence under the continuous-path null and dense local alternatives, yielding an analytically calibrated test with closed-form power; under finite-activity jumps, the test is consistent. We also extend the method to additive microstructure noise. Simulations show that the combined procedure performs well under both dense and sparse alternatives and is typically best overall.



1
Introduction

Detecting jumps from high-frequency observations remains a central problem in financial econometrics.
The distinction between continuous and discontinuous return variation matters for volatility
measurement, derivative pricing, risk management, and the interpretation of how information is
incorporated into prices. Over the last two decades this problem has generated a large literature on
nonparametric and semiparametric jump tests for discretely observed processes.

The literature on high-frequency jump detection can be roughly grouped into four strands.

The first strand consists of specification-based and realized-variation approaches.
7
proposed an early specification test for assessing whether discretely observed data are consistent with an underlying diffusion, thereby providing a benchmark for separating diffusive dynamics from jump behavior. In the realized-variation literature,
10
introduced realized bipower variation as a jump-robust measure of integrated variance;
28
interpreted jump tests in a Hausman-type framework and studied the contribution of jumps to total return variance;
11
established asymptotic theory for bipower-variation-based jump tests; and
8
showed that separating continuous and jump variation improves volatility measurement and forecasting.

The second strand develops local, ratio-based, threshold, and truncation methods for nonparametric jump detection.
34
proposed a locally standardized test that identifies intraday jump times and jump sizes.
3
developed a general nonparametric test for jumps in discretely observed Itô semimartingales.
40
introduced threshold methods for separating diffusion and jump increments and for volatility estimation in the presence of jumps.
33
extended jump detection to Lévy jump-diffusion models and allowed for separate analysis of small and large jumps.
44
proposed truncation-based tests that apply both in standard semimartingale settings and in the presence of market microstructure noise.
17
combined thresholding with bipower variation to reduce finite-sample bias and to study the effect of jumps on volatility forecasting.

The third strand extends the analysis to multivariate and dependent jump arrivals.
30
developed tests for distinguishing common jumps from disjoint jumps in multidimensional processes.
19
introduced tests for mutually exciting jumps and linked them empirically to flight-to-safety and flight-to-quality episodes.
32
further studied serial dependence in jump arrivals by proposing a robust test for autocorrelated jump occurrences.

A fourth strand provides comparative assessments and broader syntheses of the literature.
18
compared leading nonparametric jump tests across different sampling frequencies, volatility dynamics, jump sizes, and noise levels.
4
reviewed a unified framework for decomposing high-frequency returns into continuous and jump components and for measuring jump activity, while
1
offered a comprehensive treatment of high-frequency financial econometrics more broadly.
41
provided updated comparative guidance on the performance of high-frequency jump tests under volatility jumps, microstructure noise, and different sampling frequencies.

The present paper is built around two leading univariate tests. The first is the ratio statistic of

3
, which aggregates power variation across sampling frequencies and is
naturally effective when jump evidence is spread over many increments. The second is the standardized
maximum statistic of
34
, which focuses on the largest local excursion and is
naturally effective when jumps are isolated and pronounced. These two statistics therefore target
different configurations of alternatives. The evidence in
18
and

41
shows that no single jump test dominates uniformly across
dense and sparse regimes or across sampling frequencies.

Our starting point is that the Aït-Sahalia–Jacod and Lee–Mykland procedures should be viewed as complementary rather than competing. The Aït-Sahalia–Jacod statistic aggregates evidence across many increments and is naturally sensitive to alternatives in which jump evidence is spread over time, whereas the Lee–Mykland statistic is driven by local extremes and is naturally sensitive to alternatives in which jumps are sparse and concentrated. This motivates us to study the relation between the two statistics directly. Our main theoretical result shows that, after the appropriate marginal normalization, the two statistics are asymptotically independent.

In this respect, our paper is connected to a growing literature in high-dimensional hypothesis testing that studies the asymptotic independence between
\ell_{q}
-type and
\ell_{\infty}
-type statistics, or, more broadly, between sum-type and max-type statistics; see the classical probability references
27
;
26
and the more recent contributions
36
;
25

22
;
21

48
. Related adaptive
L_{p}

49

That literature typically starts from i.i.d. high-dimensional vectors, or from coordinate-array models with dependence across coordinates, whereas here both statistics are nonlinear functionals of the same discretely observed Itô semimartingale, with overlapping high-frequency increments, stochastic volatility, leverage, and, in the noisy extension, market microstructure frictions. As a result, the required asymptotic independence cannot be imported from existing high-dimensional arguments and must instead be proved directly from the primitive continuous-time model for
X
. This constitutes the main theoretical contribution of the paper.

Building on this result, we propose a Cauchy combination test in the spirit of
39
. We do not stop at the continuous-path null: under dense local jumps we again establish asymptotic independence, while under fixed finite-activity jumps and their noisy counterparts we derive the corresponding joint limits needed for the combined procedure and its consistency. Consequently, the proposed test remains analytically tractable across qualitatively different jump regimes. The simulation evidence supports this message: the Aït-Sahalia–Jacod statistic is stronger when jump evidence is dense, the Lee–Mykland statistic is stronger when jumps are sparse and isolated, and the Cauchy combination inherits the strengths of both and delivers the most robust overall performance across the alternatives considered in the paper.

A second challenge appears when the observation frequency becomes very high: market microstructure
noise changes the marginal asymptotics of classical jump statistics. Early noise-aware contributions

Our noisy extension follows the same adaptive logic as the latent-process part of the paper. We keep the continuous-time assumptions on the latent process
X
unchanged and add only a layer of assumptions on the observation error. On the sum side, we adopt the pre-averaged robustification of the Aït-Sahalia–Jacod statistic developed by
2
. On the max side, we start from the local-average extreme-value idea of
35
, but our treatment goes beyond that paper in an essential way. The theory in
35
is derived under a constant-volatility specification, whereas in our paper the latent price continues to satisfy the same stochastic Itô semimartingale framework as in the noiseless sections, with volatility itself evolving as an Itô process and leverage still allowed. For this reason we do not rely on the volatility standardization used in
35
. Instead, we construct a new feasible Lee–Mykland-type statistic by standardizing local averages with a spot-volatility estimator based on the two-scale realized spot variance of
50
. We then prove that this feasible standardization is asymptotically equivalent to the corresponding oracle normalization, and that the resulting noisy max statistic still satisfies the Gumbel extreme-value limit under the noisy continuous-path null.

This noisy extension is therefore not a routine modification of the latent-process analysis. Unlike the classical i.i.d. setting, both components are nonlinear functionals of the same noisy high-frequency Itô semimartingale, and the proof must simultaneously handle pre-averaging, local averaging, additive sub-Gaussian noise, stochastic volatility, leverage, and feasible spot-volatility estimation. Within this framework we establish asymptotic independence between the pre-averaged AJJ statistic and the new Lee–Mykland-type statistic not only under the noisy null, but also under the noisy alternatives studied in the paper, including the dense local regime and the fixed finite-activity jump regime. This leads to a noise-robust Cauchy combination test with analytic asymptotic calibration. In finite samples, however, the convergence of extreme-value statistics is well known to be slow, and the additional noise layer further complicates size control. To improve finite-sample accuracy, we therefore implement a double bootstrap in the simulation study. The Monte Carlo and empirical evidence both support the same conclusion: the pre-averaged sum statistic is more effective when jump evidence is diffuse, the noisy max statistic is more effective when jumps are sparse and concentrated, and the Cauchy combination inherits the strengths of both and delivers a robust overall procedure for jump detection in the presence of microstructure noise.

\displaystyle t_{i}=i\Delta_{n},\qquad\Delta_{n}=\frac{T}{n},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,n,\qquad\Delta_{n}\to 0.

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}^{n}X=X_{t_{i}}-X_{t_{i-1}}.

Fix an integer

\displaystyle m_{n}=\lfloor n/k\rfloor.

Throughout,

d_{B}

motion, for a fixed integer

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T},\mathbb{P})

\displaystyle W=B^{(1)},\qquad\bar{B}=(B^{(2)},\dots,B^{(d_{B})})^{\top}.

The first component

The null hypothesis is imposed directly on the latent process.

(Continuous-path null)

Under
H_{0}

\displaystyle\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=b_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t+\sigma_{t}\,\mathrm{d}W_{t},

\displaystyle b_{t}=b_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}^{(0)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

\displaystyle\sigma_{t}=\sigma_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}^{(0)}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\gamma_{s}^{\top}\,\mathrm{d}B_{s},

where
b^{(0)}
and
\sigma^{(0)}
are real-valued càdlàg locally bounded processes,
\beta
and

\gamma
are
\mathbb{R}^{d_{B}}
-valued càdlàg locally bounded processes, and there exist constants

0<\underline{\sigma}<\overline{\sigma}<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\underline{\sigma}\leq\sigma_{t}\leq\overline{\sigma},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,\qquad\text{a.s.}

Assumption

A

the required local regularity of

2.2
The Aït-Sahalia–Jacod sum statistic

Define the fine- and coarse-scale power variations

B_{n}(p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\Delta_{i}^{n}X\right|^{p},\qquad B_{n}^{(k)}(p)=\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\Delta_{(j-1)k+\ell}^{n}X\right|^{p}.

(1)

The sum-type statistic of
3
is the ratio

R_{n}^{AJ}=\frac{B_{n}^{(k)}(p)}{B_{n}(p)}.

(2)

Under a continuous path, the limit is
k^{p/2-1}
, whereas under fixed jumps the limit is one.

Let

\displaystyle m_{r}=\mathbb{E}\left|N\right|^{r},\qquad N\sim N(0,1),\qquad A_{r}=\int_{0}^{T}|\sigma_{t}|^{r}\,\mathrm{d}t,

and define the block kernel

U_{p,k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=\left|x_{1}+\cdots+x_{k}\right|^{p}-k^{p/2-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left|x_{\ell}\right|^{p}.

(3)

Set

\varsigma_{p,k}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}\left(U_{p,k}(E_{1},\dots,E_{k})\right),\qquad\tau_{0}^{2}=\frac{\varsigma_{p,k}^{2}A_{2p}}{km_{p}^{2}A_{p}^{2}},

(4)

where
E_{1},\dots,E_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}N(0,1)
. A feasible estimator of
\tau_{0}^{2}
is

\hat{\tau}_{n,0}^{2}=\frac{\varsigma_{p,k}^{2}}{km_{p}^{2}}\frac{\hat{A}_{2p,n}}{\hat{A}_{p,n}^{2}},\qquad\hat{A}_{r,n}=m_{r}^{-1}\Delta_{n}^{1-r/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\Delta_{i}^{n}X\right|^{r}.

(5)

The feasible null statistic is

Z_{n}^{AJ}=\frac{\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(R_{n}^{AJ}-k^{p/2-1}\right)}{\hat{\tau}_{n,0}},\qquad p_{n}^{AJ}=2\left(1-\Phi\left(\left|Z_{n}^{AJ}\right|\right)\right).

(6)

2.3
The Lee–Mykland max statistic

Let
K_{n}\to\infty
satisfy

\frac{K_{n}}{(\log n)^{3}}\to\infty,\qquad K_{n}\Delta_{n}(\log n)^{3}\to 0,\qquad K_{n}\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\to\infty.

(7)

A convenient choic is
K_{n}=\lfloor\Delta_{n}^{-\varrho}\rfloor
with
\varrho\in(1/2,1)
. Define the local
bipower estimator

\hat{V}_{n,i}=\frac{\pi}{2(K_{n}-1)}\sum_{j=i-K_{n}+2}^{i}\left|\Delta_{j}^{n}X\right|\,\left|\Delta_{j-1}^{n}X\right|,\qquad i=K_{n},\dots,n,

(8)

and the standardized increments

L_{n,i}=\frac{\Delta_{i}^{n}X}{\sqrt{\hat{V}_{n,i}}},\qquad i=K_{n},\dots,n.

(9)

The max-type statistic is

M_{n}=\max_{K_{n}\leq i\leq n}\left|L_{n,i}\right|.

(10)

Let

C_{n}=\sqrt{2\log n}-\frac{\log\pi+\log\log n}{2\sqrt{2\log n}},\qquad a_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log n}},

(11)

and define the normalized maximum

\xi_{n}=\frac{M_{n}-C_{n}}{a_{n}}.

(12)

Under the continuous-path null,
\xi_{n}
converges to the standard Gumbel law with distribution
function
x\mapsto\exp\{-e^{-x}\}
. The corresponding marginal
p
-value is

p_{n}^{LM}=1-\exp\left\{-e^{-\xi_{n}}\right\}.

(13)

2.4
The Cauchy combination statistic

The equal-weight Cauchy combination statistic of
39
is

\mathsf{T}_{n}^{C}=\frac{1}{2}\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{AJ}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{LM}\right)\right].

(14)

The combined
p
-value is

p_{n}^{C}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(\mathsf{T}_{n}^{C}\right).

(15)

For
0<\alpha<1

\alpha

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}=\cot(\pi\alpha).

The level-
\alpha

2.7
Fixed separated finite-activity jumps

Assumption 2.3

(Fixed finite-activity jumps)
.

Under
H_{1}^{F}
,

X_{t}=X_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}b_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{s}\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}+\sum_{q=1}^{N_{T}}\kappa_{q}\mathbf{1}\left\{\tau_{q}\leq t\right\},\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

where
(b,\sigma)
satisfy the conditions in Assumption

2.1
. Moreover,

N_{T}<\infty\ \text{a.s.},\qquad 0<\tau_{1}<\cdots<\tau_{N_{T}}<T\ \text{a.s.},\qquad\inf_{1\leq q\leq N_{T}}\left|\kappa_{q}\right|>0\ \text{a.s.},

Define

B_{p}=\sum_{q=1}^{N_{T}}\left|\kappa_{q}\right|^{p},\qquad\tau_{F}^{2}=\frac{p^{2}(k-1)}{B_{p}^{2}}\sum_{q=1}^{N_{T}}\left|\kappa_{q}\right|^{2p-2}\sigma_{\tau_{q}}^{2}.

The jump-centered oracle AJ statistic is

\widetilde{Z}_{n,1}^{AJ}=\frac{\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\left(R_{n}^{AJ}-1\right)}{\tau_{F}}.

(18)

Theorem 2.3

(Fixed finite-activity jumps)
.

Suppose Assumption
2.3
and (
7
) hold. Then

\left(\widetilde{Z}_{n,1}^{AJ},p_{n}^{LM}\right)\xrightarrow{d}(Z,0),\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).

In particular,

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{n}^{C}\leq\alpha\right)\to 1,\qquad 0<\alpha<1.

Remark 2.1
.

The statistic
\widetilde{Z}_{n,1}^{AJ}
is an oracle normalization because
\tau_{F}^{2}
depends on the fixed jump times and sizes. It is used only to describe the centered AJ fluctuation under fixed finite-activity jumps. The consistency statement for the feasible Cauchy combination does not require estimating
\tau_{F}

3
Extension to additive microstructure noise

We now extend the adaptive construction to the case where the observed process is contaminated by
additive market microstructure noise. The continuous-path assumptions on the latent process are not
changed. Under the noisy continuous-path null we keep Assumption
2.1
. Under the noisy
alternatives we keep exactly the same latent-process architecture as in Section
2.6
; only the
local jump scale in the dense regime is retuned from
\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}
to
\Delta_{n}^{1/4}
so as to match
the slower
\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}
normalization induced by pre-averaging. The genuinely new assumptions in
this section concern the observation error. This keeps the paper’s process assumptions aligned across
its noiseless and noisy parts and makes the role of the additional noise layer transparent.

Assumption 3.1

(Additive microstructure noise)
.

The observations are

Y_{t_{i}}=X_{t_{i}}+\epsilon_{i},\qquad i=0,1,\dots,n,

where
\epsilon_{0},\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{n}
are i.i.d., independent of
\mathcal{F}_{T}
, and satisfy

\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{0})=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}(\epsilon_{0}^{2})=\omega^{2}>0,\qquad\|\epsilon_{0}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{\epsilon}<\infty.

The Gaussian law is therefore not imposed on the observation error. The Gaussian distribution enters
this section only through the asymptotic law of the averaged noise blocks. In the pre-averaged sum
component, the relevant noise term is a weighted sum of many i.i.d. sub-Gaussian variables and hence
is asymptotically Gaussian. In the max component, the same averaging mechanism together with a
Cramér-type moderate deviation argument yields the Gaussian tail approximation needed for the
Gumbel limit at the extreme-value scale. Whenever squares or products of noise increments appear, we use the standard implication that products and squares of sub-Gaussian variables are sub-exponential, so Bernstein/Rosenthal bounds are applied at the sub-exponential rather than sub-Gaussian level. The sum component follows the pre-averaging construction of

2
. The max component follows the local-average extreme-value
construction of
35
. The main point of the section is that, after marginal
calibration, the two noisy components remain asymptotically independent under the continuous-path
null and under the noisy dense local alternative.

3.1
The pre-averaged sum statistic

Fix an even integer
p\geq 4
. Let
g
and
h
be continuous weight functions on
\mathbb{R}
, piecewise

C^{1}
, supported on
[0,1]
, with

\bar{g}(2)=\int_{0}^{1}g(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u>0,\qquad\bar{h}(2)=\int_{0}^{1}h(u)^{2}\,\mathrm{d}u>0.

For a generic weight
\phi\in\{g,h\}
and
q>0
, write

\bar{\phi}(q)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\phi(u)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}u,\qquad\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(q)=\int_{0}^{1}\left|\phi^{\prime}(u)\right|^{q}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Let
k_{n}\to\infty
satisfy

k_{n}\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\to\theta\in(0,\infty),\qquad\frac{k_{n}}{\log n}\to\infty.

(19)

Define

\phi_{j}^{n}=\phi\left(\frac{j}{k_{n}}\right),\qquad\Delta_{j}\phi^{n}=\phi_{j}^{n}-\phi_{j-1}^{n},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,k_{n},

with
\phi_{0}^{n}=\phi_{k_{n}}^{n}=0
. For
i=0,\dots,n-k_{n}
, set

\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\Delta_{i+j}^{n}Y,\qquad\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\left(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n}\,\Delta_{i+j}^{n}Y\right)^{2}.

For integers
q\geq 0
and
r\geq 0
with
q+2r=p
, define

V_{n}(Y,\phi,q,r)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k_{n}}\left|\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)\right|^{q}\left|\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)\right|^{r}.

Let
\rho_{0}^{(p)},\dots,\rho_{p/2}^{(p)}
be the unique solution of the triangular system

\rho_{0}^{(p)}=1,\qquad\sum_{l=0}^{j}2^{l}m_{2j-2l}\binom{p-2l}{p-2j}\rho_{l}^{(p)}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,\frac{p}{2},

(20)

and set

V_{n}(Y,\phi,p)=\sum_{l=0}^{p/2}\rho_{l}^{(p)}V_{n}(Y,\phi,p-2l,l).

Define

\gamma=\frac{\bar{g}(2)}{\bar{h}(2)},\qquad\gamma^{\prime}=\frac{\bar{g}(p)}{\bar{h}(p)},\qquad\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\gamma^{p/2}}{\gamma^{\prime}},\qquad\gamma^{\prime\prime}>1,

The inequality
\gamma^{\prime\prime}>1
is a calibration restriction on the pair
(g,h)
, not an automatic
consequence of the preceding smoothness assumptions. It is easy to satisfy. For example, let

w(u)=\sin(\pi u)
on
[0,1]
, extended by zero outside
[0,1]
, and take

g=w
and
h=w^{a}
for any
a>1
. If
L(q)=\log\int_{0}^{1}w(u)^{q}\,\mathrm{d}u
, then
L
is strictly convex and

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}a}\left\{\frac{1}{2}L(2a)-\frac{1}{p}L(pa)\right\}=L^{\prime}(2a)-L^{\prime}(pa)<0,\qquad p>2.

Thus
a\mapsto\left\|w^{a}\right\|_{2}/\left\|w^{a}\right\|_{p}
is strictly decreasing, so

\gamma^{\prime\prime}=\left(\frac{\left\|g\right\|_{2}/\left\|g\right\|_{p}}{\left\|h\right\|_{2}/\left\|h\right\|_{p}}\right)^{p}>1.

The noise-robust sum statistic is

R_{n}^{PA}=\frac{V_{n}(Y,g,p)}{\gamma^{\prime}V_{n}(Y,h,p)}.

E.1
constructs a block self-normalizer
\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}
and proves
the central limit theorem

Z_{n}^{PA}=\frac{\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\left(R_{n}^{PA}-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right)}{\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}},\qquad p_{n}^{PA}=2\left(1-\Phi\left(\left|Z_{n}^{PA}\right|\right)\right).

(21)

3.2
The local-average max statistic

Let
M_{n}\to\infty
satisfy

M_{n}\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\to\lambda\in(0,\infty),\qquad\frac{M_{n}}{\log n}\to\infty.

(22)

For
j=0,\dots,n-M_{n}
, define the local averages
\bar{Y}_{n,j}
, and for

j=0,\dots,n-2M_{n}
, define their adjacent-window difference by

\bar{Y}_{n,j}=\frac{1}{M_{n}}\sum_{u=0}^{M_{n}-1}Y_{t_{j+u}},\qquad L_{n,j}=\bar{Y}_{n,j+M_{n}}-\bar{Y}_{n,j}.

(23)

To obtain asymptotically independent windows, we work on the disjoint grid

\mathcal{J}_{n}=\left\{0,2M_{n},4M_{n},\dots,2M_{n}(N_{n}-1)\right\},\qquad N_{n}=\left\lfloor\frac{n-2M_{n}}{2M_{n}}\right\rfloor+1.

For
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
, define the oracle variance proxy

v_{n,j}^{2}=2\omega^{2}+\sigma_{t_{j}}^{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}M_{n}^{2}\,\Delta_{n}+\frac{1}{3}\,\Delta_{n}\right),

(24)

and the oracle standardized local-average statistic

X_{n,j}=\frac{\sqrt{M_{n}}\,L_{n,j}}{v_{n,j}}.

(25)

Only the variance proxy
v_{n,j}
needs to be estimated. We therefore use a plug-in array based on the
TSRSV spot-variance estimator of
50
. Let

K_{n}^{SV}=\lfloor c_{K}\Delta_{n}^{-2/3}\rfloor,\qquad H_{n}=\lfloor c_{h}\Delta_{n}^{-5/6}\rfloor,\qquad h_{n}=H_{n}\Delta_{n},

(26)

where
c_{K},c_{h}\in(0,\infty)
are fixed constants. With this choice
h_{n}\asymp\Delta_{n}^{1/6}
,
(K_{n}^{SV})^{-2}(h_{n}\Delta_{n})^{-1}\asymp\Delta_{n}^{1/6}
, and
K_{n}^{SV}\Delta_{n}/h_{n}\asymp\Delta_{n}^{1/6}
, matching the bias, noise and discretization terms in the filtering TSRSV expansion. Define the noise-variance estimator

\hat{\omega}_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\Delta_{i}^{n}Y\right)^{2}.

(27)

For
r\geq K_{n}^{SV}
, let

\operatorname{TSRV}_{n}(t_{r};K_{n}^{SV})=\frac{1}{K_{n}^{SV}}\sum_{i=K_{n}^{SV}}^{r}\left(Y_{t_{i}}-Y_{t_{i-K_{n}^{SV}}}\right)^{2}-\frac{r-K_{n}^{SV}+1}{K_{n}^{SV}r}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left(Y_{t_{i}}-Y_{t_{i-1}}\right)^{2},

with
\operatorname{TSRV}_{n}(t_{0};K_{n}^{SV})=0
. For
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
, let

j^{\star}=j\vee H_{n},\qquad\hat{\sigma}_{n,j}^{2}=\frac{\operatorname{TSRV}_{n}(t_{j^{\star}};K_{n}^{SV})-\operatorname{TSRV}_{n}(t_{j^{\star}-H_{n}};K_{n}^{SV})}{h_{n}},

(28)

and define

\hat{v}_{n,j}^{2}=2\hat{\omega}_{n}^{2}+\hat{\sigma}_{n,j}^{2}\left(\frac{2}{3}M_{n}^{2}\,\Delta_{n}+\frac{1}{3}\,\Delta_{n}\right),\qquad\hat{X}_{n,j}=\frac{\sqrt{M_{n}}\,L_{n,j}}{\hat{v}_{n,j}}.

(29)

Lemma
E.8
in
E.2
proves that this plug-in array
satisfies

\frac{1}{B_{N_{n}}}\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\hat{X}_{n,j}-X_{n,j}\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

(30)

More generally, any feasible array satisfying (
30
) may be used. The feasible max
statistic is then

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{LA}=\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\hat{X}_{n,j}\right|.

(31)

Let

A_{N_{n}}=\sqrt{2\log N_{n}}-\frac{\log\pi+\log\log N_{n}}{2\sqrt{2\log N_{n}}},\qquad B_{N_{n}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\log N_{n}}},

and define

\xi_{n}^{LA}=\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{LA}-A_{N_{n}}}{B_{N_{n}}},\qquad p_{n}^{LA}=1-\exp\left\{-e^{-\xi_{n}^{LA}}\right\}.

(32)

The noisy Cauchy combination test

For the joint proof we fix a block length
r_{n}
such that

r_{n}\to\infty,\qquad\frac{r_{n}}{k_{n}}\to\infty,\qquad\frac{r_{n}}{M_{n}}\to\infty,\qquad r_{n}\Delta_{n}\to 0,\qquad\frac{k_{n}+M_{n}}{r_{n}^{2}\Delta_{n}}\to 0.

Equivalently, set
r_{n}=\Delta_{n}^{-a}

p_{n}^{C,N}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{C,N}\right).

3.4
A noisy dense local alternative with many small jumps

The noisy analogue of the dense local alternative keeps the same Poissonian latent-process structure
as Section
2.6
, but the jump scale has to be retuned to the pre-averaging normalization.
After summation by parts, the pre-averaged noise term has variance of order
\Delta_{n}^{1/2}
, so the
unscaled noise contribution to
\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)
is of size
\Delta_{n}^{1/4}
and

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)
has a non-degenerate limit. A local jump must therefore also enter a
pre-averaging window at the
\Delta_{n}^{1/4}
scale if it is to be comparable with the noise-robust
continuous component. The compensator below increases the jump intensity to order

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}
; with the
\Delta_{n}^{1/4}
jump size this produces a finite first-order mean shift in

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}(R_{n}^{PA}-\gamma^{\prime\prime})
. The assumptions on
(b,\sigma)
are not changed.

Assumption 3.2

(Noisy dense local small jumps)
.

Let
\mu_{n}^{N}
be a Poisson random measure on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}
, independent of

(B,\epsilon_{0},\dots,\epsilon_{n})
, with compensator

\nu_{n}^{N}(\,\mathrm{d}t,\,\mathrm{d}y)=\vartheta\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\,\mathrm{d}t\,F(\,\mathrm{d}y),\qquad\vartheta>0,

where

is a probability law on
\mathbb{R}
with compact support and finite
(2p+2)
-moment. Under

H_{1,n}^{D,N}
,

where
(b,\sigma)
satisfy the conditions in Assumption

2.1
.

For
\phi\in\{g,h\}
, let

and define, for
(y,u)\in\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
,

Let

be the asymptotic variance of

under the null (see Lemma

). The local mean shift of the pre-averaged ratio statistic is

(Noisy dense local alternative)
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.2
and
3.1
hold, and suppose
(

), (

), and (

) hold. Then

(Noisy asymptotic power under the dense local alternative)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

,

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{n}^{C,N}\leq\alpha\right)\to\beta_{D,N}(\alpha;\mu_{D,N}),

where

and
A(\cdot)

.

Theorem 3.3

(Noisy fixed finite-activity jumps)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.3
and
3.1
hold, suppose (
19
)
and (
22
) hold, and suppose that the fixed jumps are not asymptotically aligned with the boundary points of the disjoint local-average grid. More precisely, let
i_{q,n}
be the unique index satisfying
t_{i_{q,n}-1}<\tau_{q}\leq t_{i_{q,n}}
, put

a_{M_{n},u}=u\wedge(2M_{n}-u),\qquad 1\leq u\leq 2M_{n}-1,

and define

D_{n}=\max_{1\leq q\leq N_{T}}\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}a_{M_{n},i_{q,n}-j}\mathbf{1}\left\{1\leq i_{q,n}-j\leq 2M_{n}-1\right\}.

Assume

\frac{D_{n}}{\sqrt{M_{n}\log n}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\infty.

(37)

Then

R_{n}^{PA}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1,\qquad p_{n}^{LA}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Consequently,

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{n}^{C,N}\leq\alpha\right)\to 1,\qquad 0<\alpha<1.

Remark 3.1
.

Condition (
37
) is only needed for the noisy local-average maximum computed on the single disjoint grid
\mathcal{J}_{n}
. It rules out the pathological case in which every fixed jump falls within
O(\sqrt{M_{n}\log n})
observation points of a local-average grid boundary. If at least one fixed jump time has a bounded conditional density near its location, then (
37
) holds; for example,

\mathbb{P}(D_{n}\leq M_{n}^{3/4})=O(M_{n}^{-1/4})\to 0
and
M_{n}^{3/4}/\sqrt{M_{n}\log n}\to\infty
.
The pre-averaged ratio still satisfies
R_{n}^{PA}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1
, but the block self-normalizer in (
21
) can be affected by fixed jump windows. The consistency statement is therefore proved from the local-average maximum component, which is sufficient for the Cauchy combination.

To keep the comparison with the baseline experiments transparent, we retain the same latent efficient-price dynamics and parameter values as in the previous Monte Carlo designs and only modify the observation equation to

\varepsilon_{i}\sim N(0,q^{2}),\qquad\text{or}\qquad\varepsilon_{i}=q\sqrt{\frac{3}{4}}\,T_{i},\qquad T_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}t_{8},

with

Size under the null.

We begin with the continuous-path null. Relative to the previous implementation, the main change is the calibration of LM. Instead of the one-step recursive bootstrap with an ex post critical-value adjustment, we now use a double bootstrap. Let
T_{n}^{\mathrm{LM}}
denote the LM statistic computed from the observed sample, and let
\hat{q}
and
\hat{\sigma}_{t}^{2}
denote the corresponding estimates of the noise variance and spot volatility. At the first stage, we generate
B_{1}
bootstrap samples from the fitted continuous no-jump model, re-estimate the nuisance quantities on each bootstrap path, and recompute the LM statistic, yielding
T_{n}^{*(1)},\ldots,T_{n}^{*(B_{1})}
. The first-stage bootstrap
p
-value is

\hat{p}_{n}^{*}=\frac{1}{B_{1}}\sum_{b=1}^{B_{1}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{T_{n}^{*(b)}\geq T_{n}^{\mathrm{LM}}\right\}.

For each first-stage sample
b
, we then generate
B_{2}
second-stage bootstrap samples from the fitted first-stage model, again re-estimate the nuisance quantities, and compute

\hat{p}_{b}^{**}=\frac{1}{B_{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{B_{2}}\mathbf{1}\!\left\{T_{n}^{**(b,\ell)}\geq T_{n}^{*(b)}\right\},\qquad b=1,\ldots,B_{1}.

The LM test rejects when
\hat{p}_{n}^{*}
is smaller than the empirical
\alpha
-quantile of

\{\hat{p}_{b}^{**}:1\leq b\leq B_{1}\}
. At both bootstrap stages, the bootstrap paths are generated from the fitted continuous Gaussian model, with noise variance set equal to the corresponding estimated
q^{2}
. Table
6
shows that this calibration keeps the size of LM close to the nominal level. More importantly, size is well controlled for all three procedures across the two sampling frequencies, the two noise levels, and both the Gaussian and standardized-
t_{8}
noise designs. In the noisy experiments, the relevant differences across procedures therefore come mainly from power.

Dense alternatives.

We next return to the dense-local alternative, now under noisy observations. As in the baseline dense-jump design, the latent process contains the interval-level jump component

\Delta J_{i}=\sqrt{v_{t_{i-1}}}\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\sum_{m=1}^{N_{i}}Y_{im},\qquad N_{i}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\theta\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}),\qquad Y_{im}\sim N(0,400),

and we report 5%-level power for
\theta=1,\ldots,10
. Figures
1
and
2
reveal a clear frequency effect. At the 5-second frequency, AJJ is uniformly more powerful than LM across the four noisy designs, and CC is typically on or very near the upper envelope. At the 10-second frequency, however, this ranking reverses: LM becomes the stronger marginal procedure, while CC again remains close to the best-performing test. The natural interpretation is that AJJ benefits more from ultra-high-frequency information and is therefore better able to exploit dense alternatives when the sampling grid is very fine. Once the grid is coarsened, that advantage weakens substantially. The ranking is stable across Gaussian and standardized-
t_{8}
noise. As in the benchmark dense design, power is highest for small
\theta
and then declines as
\theta
increases, because after local normalization the dense jump component becomes progressively harder to distinguish from a smooth continuous perturbation.

Sparse alternatives.

We finally consider the sparse finite-activity alternative under the same noisy observation scheme. The latent price now contains a compound Poisson jump component

J_{t}=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{t}}\xi_{m},\qquad N_{t}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t),\qquad\xi_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,0.05),

and we report 5%-level power for
\lambda\in\{0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}
. Figures
3
and
4
deliver a much more stable ordering. LM is the most powerful marginal procedure throughout, AJJ is the weakest, and CC lies between the two but remains much closer to LM than to AJJ. This is exactly what one would expect in a sparse-jump environment, where detection depends on a relatively small number of isolated discontinuities rather than on the accumulation of many small jump increments. As
\lambda
increases, all three procedures become more powerful, but the ranking does not change. The same pattern survives the move from Gaussian to standardized-
t_{8}
noise and from 5-second to 10-second sampling, although the coarser frequency shifts all power curves downward.

Taken together, the microstructure-noise experiments point to a simple conclusion. Once LM is calibrated by the double bootstrap, size is satisfactory for AJJ, LM, and CC. In terms of power, AJJ has an advantage in dense alternatives only at very high sampling frequencies, whereas LM is preferable in sparse alternatives and becomes relatively stronger as the sampling interval increases. CC provides the best overall compromise: it closely tracks the better marginal test across designs and is therefore the most reliable default choice in the presence of microstructure noise.

6
Conclusion

This paper develops an adaptive jump test by combining a sum-type statistic with a max-type statistic. The key result is that the two components are asymptotically independent in both the latent and noisy settings, which makes an analytically calibrated Cauchy combination test feasible. The resulting procedure adapts well to dense and sparse alternatives and remains consistent against fixed finite-activity jumps, so it provides a simple and robust default when the form of the jump alternative is unknown.

Future research can proceed in several directions. One is to relax the i.i.d. noise assumption and allow serially dependent microstructure noise, building on
6
and
38
. Another is to extend the adaptive idea to multivariate high-frequency data and study common-jump or co-jump testing under asynchronous sampling and noise; see
30
and
13
.

.

By Assumption

, by the Burkholder–Davis–

45

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq i\leq n-1}\sup_{t_{i}\leq u\leq t_{i+1}}\left|b_{u}-b_{t_{i}}\right|>\Delta_{n}^{1/2-\varepsilon}\right)

Lemma A.2

(One-step stochastic-Taylor expansion under leverage)
.

For
i=1,\dots,n

E_{i}=\frac{\Delta_{i}^{n}W}{\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}},\qquad I_{i,n}^{(r,1)}=\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_{i}}\int_{t_{i-1}}^{s}\,\mathrm{d}B_{u}^{(r)}\,\,\mathrm{d}W_{s},\qquad r=2,\dots,d_{B}.

Lemma A.3

(Analytic inequalities)
.

Let
p>3
. Then there exists a finite constant
C_{p}
such that for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},y_{1},\dots,y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left|U_{p,k}(x_{1}+y_{1},\dots,x_{k}+y_{k})-U_{p,k}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\leq C_{p}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left|x_{\ell}\right|^{p-1}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left|y_{\ell}\right|^{p-1}\right)\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\left|y_{\ell}\right|.

Proof.

The function
f(x)=\left|x\right|^{p}
is
C^{2}
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. By the Mean Value Theorem and the fact that

|x+ty|^{p-1}\leq C_{p}(|x|^{p-1}+|y|^{p-1}),\qquad 0\leq t\leq 1,

we have

\displaystyle|f(x+y)-f(x)|\leq p|y|\int_{0}^{1}|x+ty|^{p-1}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{p}(|x|^{p-1}+|y|^{p-1})|y|.

(A.2)

Lemma B.3

(Finite removal and stable Gaussian limit for the AJ block sum)
.

Let
\mathcal{J}_{n}\subset\{1,\dots,m_{n}\}
be deterministic with
\sup_{n}\#\mathcal{J}_{n}<\infty
, and let

\mathcal{H}_{n}=\sigma\left\{E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k}:j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}\right\}.

2.1
,

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,

and

\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)

stably with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

Proof.

Since
\Psi_{0}
has mean zero, variance one, and finite
(2+\delta)
-moment for some
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})\right|^{2}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}^{2}\leq\#\mathcal{J}_{n}\max_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}\omega_{j,n}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,

which proves

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

For the complement, define the enlarged block filtration

\mathcal{F}_{j,n}^{\mathcal{J}}=\mathcal{H}_{n}\vee\mathcal{F}_{jk\Delta_{n}},\qquad j=0,1,\dots,m_{n},

and the array

\zeta_{j,n}=\mathbf{1}\left\{j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}\right\}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k}).

For
j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}
, the weight
\omega_{j,n}
is
\mathcal{F}_{j-1,n}^{\mathcal{J}}
-measurable, while the block
vector
(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{j-1,n}^{\mathcal{J}}
. Hence

\mathbb{E}(\zeta_{j,n}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1,n}^{\mathcal{J}})=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,m_{n},

so
(\zeta_{j,n})
is a martingale-difference array. Its predictable quadratic variation is

\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\mathbb{E}(\zeta_{j,n}^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{j-1,n}^{\mathcal{J}})=\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}^{2}=1+o_{p}(1)

by Lemma
B.1
. Further,

\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left|\zeta_{j,n}\right|^{2+\delta}\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\left|\omega_{j,n}\right|^{2+\delta}\leq C\max_{1\leq j\leq m_{n}}\left|\omega_{j,n}\right|^{\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{n}}\omega_{j,n}^{2}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

The stable martingale central limit theorem
(
23
, Chapter 3)
therefore yields

\sum_{j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}}\omega_{j,n}\Psi_{0}(E_{j,1},\dots,E_{j,k})\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1),

stably with respect to
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.

The tuples having at least two indices in the same coarse block are

\sum_{l=0}^{p/2}\rho_{l}^{(p)}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sqrt{a+b}\,N+x\right|^{p-2l}\right](2b)^{l}=\sum_{j=0}^{p/2}\binom{p}{2j}m_{p-2j}a^{p/2-j}x^{2j}=\mathbb{E}\left|\sqrt{a}\,N+x\right|^{p}.

Lemma E.2

(Weighted sub-Gaussian noise becomes Gaussian)
.

Fix

uniformly in

Moreover, for every even integer

, every bounded family of

\mathcal{G}_{n,i}
-measurable shifts
x_{n,i}

\max_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}\left|c_{n,j}(\phi)\right|\leq C\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}k_{n}^{-1}=C\frac{\Delta_{n}^{1/4}}{k_{n}\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}}\to 0.

\left\|\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C\omega^{-1}K_{\epsilon}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}c_{n,j}(\phi)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq C_{\phi},

uniformly in

\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\xrightarrow{d}s_{\phi}N,\qquad N\sim N(0,1),

(E.3)

uniformly in
i

\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left|\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\right|^{q+2}<\infty

for every fixed even
q\leq p
. Hence convergence in (

\mathcal{G}_{n,i}

conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n,i}

uniformly for

G_{n,i}+\omega\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\xrightarrow{d}\sqrt{a_{n,i}+\omega^{2}s_{\phi}^{2}}\,N

conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n,i}

Lemma E.3
.

Fix
\phi\in\{g,h\}
. There exist centered Gaussian variables
\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)
, centered variables

\eta_{n,i}(\phi)
, independent conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n,i}
, and remainder variables

r_{n,i}(\phi)
such that

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sigma_{t_{i}}\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)+\omega\eta_{n,i}(\phi)+r_{n,i}(\phi),\qquad i=0,\dots,n-k_{n},

and

\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n,i}\right]=\theta\bar{\phi}(2)+o_{p}(1),\qquad\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,i}(\phi)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n,i}\right]=\theta^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(2)+o_{p}(1),

uniformly in
i

\max_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left|r_{n,i}(\phi)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,\qquad\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left\|\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=O_{p}(1),

\max_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left|\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)-2\omega^{2}\theta^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(2)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Proof.

Write

\bar{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\bar{X}_{i}^{n}(\phi)+\bar{\epsilon}_{i}^{n}(\phi),\qquad\bar{X}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X,\qquad\bar{\epsilon}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}(\epsilon_{i+j}-\epsilon_{i+j-1}).

For the latent part define

\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)=\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\Delta_{i+j}^{n}W.

Then

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\bar{X}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=\sigma_{t_{i}}\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)+r_{n,i}^{X}(\phi),

where

r_{n,i}^{X}(\phi)=\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\left\{\int_{t_{i+j-1}}^{t_{i+j}}b_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{t_{i+j-1}}^{t_{i+j}}(\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{t_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right\}.

This expression freezes the volatility at the left endpoint of the whole pre-averaging window. It must be controlled at the window level, rather than by multiplying a pointwise increment bound by
k_{n}
. After the usual localization, the coefficients in Assumption
2.1
are bounded. For every
q\geq 2
, Burkholder–Davis–Gundy and Jensen’s inequality give, uniformly in
i
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\int_{t_{i+j-1}}^{t_{i+j}}(\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{t_{i}})\,\mathrm{d}W_{s}\right|^{q}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\Delta_{n}^{-q/4}\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i+k_{n}}}(\sigma_{s}-\sigma_{t_{i}})^{2}\,\mathrm{d}s\right)^{q/2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{q}\Delta_{n}^{-q/4}\left(\int_{0}^{k_{n}\Delta_{n}}u\,\mathrm{d}u\right)^{q/2}\leq C_{q}\Delta_{n}^{q/4}.

The drift part is of the same or smaller order:

\sup_{i}\mathbb{E}\left|\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}\phi_{j}^{n}\int_{t_{i+j-1}}^{t_{i+j}}b_{s}\,\mathrm{d}s\right|^{q}\leq C_{q}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}k_{n}\Delta_{n})^{q}\leq C_{q}\Delta_{n}^{q/4}.

Taking
q>4
and applying the union bound yields

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left|r_{n,i}^{X}(\phi)\right|>\varepsilon\right)\leq C_{q}\varepsilon^{-q}n\Delta_{n}^{q/4}\to 0,

so
\max_{i}\left|r_{n,i}^{X}(\phi)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
. Moreover, conditionally on
\mathcal{G}_{n,i}
,
\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)
is centered Gaussian and

\mathbb{E}\left[\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n,i}\right]=\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}(\phi_{j}^{n})^{2}\Delta_{n}=\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}-1}(\phi_{j}^{n})^{2}\to\theta\bar{\phi}(2)

uniformly in
i
.

For the noise part, summation by parts gives

\bar{\epsilon}_{i}^{n}(\phi)=-\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\Delta_{j}\phi^{n}\epsilon_{i+j-1},

because
\phi_{0}^{n}=\phi_{k_{n}}^{n}=0
. Set

\eta_{n,i}(\phi)=-\omega^{-1}\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\Delta_{j}\phi^{n}\epsilon_{i+j-1}.

Then
\eta_{n,i}(\phi)
is centered and conditionally independent of
\zeta_{n,i}(\phi)
, and

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta_{n,i}(\phi)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n,i}\right]=\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}\to\theta^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(2)

uniformly in
i
. Lemma
E.2
gives

\sup_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left\|\eta_{n,i}(\phi)\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=O_{p}(1).

Thus the asserted decomposition holds with
r_{n,i}(\phi)=r_{n,i}^{X}(\phi)
.

It remains to prove the uniform approximation for
\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)
. Since

\Delta_{i+j}^{n}Y=\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X+\epsilon_{i+j}-\epsilon_{i+j-1},

we have

\displaystyle\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon)^{2}+2\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon+\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X)^{2}.

The variables
(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon)^{2}-2\omega^{2}
form a centered 1-dependent sub-exponential array. Since

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}=O(1),\qquad\max_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}=O(k_{n}^{-1})\to 0,

Bernstein’s inequality for 1-dependent sub-exponential arrays gives, uniformly in
i
,

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}\{(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon)^{2}-2\omega^{2}\}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Therefore

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon)^{2}\to 2\omega^{2}\theta^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(2)

uniformly in
i
. The two remaining terms are negligible. Indeed,

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left|\Delta_{j}^{n}X\right|=O_{p}(\sqrt{\Delta_{n}\log n}),\qquad\max_{0\leq j\leq n}\left|\epsilon_{j}\right|=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log n}),

and
\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}=O(k_{n}^{-1})=O(\sqrt{\Delta_{n}})
, so

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}\left|\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X\right|\left|\Delta_{i+j}^{n}\epsilon\right|=O_{p}(\sqrt{\Delta_{n}}\log n)=o_{p}(1)

uniformly in
i
, and

\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}(\Delta_{j}\phi^{n})^{2}(\Delta_{i+j}^{n}X)^{2}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}\log n)=o_{p}(1).

This proves

\max_{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}}\left|\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}\hat{Y}_{i}^{n}(\phi)-2\omega^{2}\theta^{-1}\bar{\phi}^{\prime}(2)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

∎

Lemma E.7

(Sub-Gaussian Gumbel limit for the local-average max statistic)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
3.1

The noise variables entering

For the latent part set

G_{n,j}^{X}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\sum_{u=1}^{2M_{n}-1}a_{M_{n},u}\Delta_{j+u}^{n}W

and write

(E.10)

By Lemma

\operatorname{Var}(G_{n,j}^{X}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}})=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{M_{n}}\sum_{u=1}^{2M_{n}-1}a_{M_{n},u}^{2}=\frac{2}{3}M_{n}^{2}\Delta_{n}+\frac{1}{3}\Delta_{n}.

\operatorname{Var}\{\sigma_{t_{j}}G_{n,j}^{X}+S_{n,j}^{\epsilon}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}\}=v_{n,j}^{2},

is defined in (

Conditionally on

is a centered sum of a Gaussian term and a weighted sum of independent sub-Gaussian noise variables, with variance one. The largest noise coefficient is

b=1,\dots,J_{n}
, let

\mathcal{J}_{n,b}=\mathcal{J}_{n}\cap I_{n,b},\qquad\Upsilon_{n,b}(y)=\mathbf{1}\left\{\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n,b}}\left|X_{n,j}\right|\leq A_{N_{n}}+B_{N_{n}}y\right\},

is uniformly bounded and

\underline{\sigma}>0

\max_{1\leq b\leq J_{n}}\left|\psi_{n,b}(t,y)-\varphi_{n,b}(t)\chi_{n,b}(y)\right|=o_{p}(r_{n}\Delta_{n})=o_{p}(J_{n}^{-1}).

Moreover the accumulated replacement error is summable:

\sum_{b=1}^{J_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left|\psi_{n,b}(t,y)-\varphi_{n,b}(t)\chi_{n,b}(y)\right|\to 0.

Proof.

Put
\nu_{n}(y)=A_{N_{n}}+B_{N_{n}}y
and
A_{n,j}(y)=\{\left|X_{n,j}\right|>\nu_{n}(y)\}
.
We first perform a standard localization: for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a compact set

K_{\varepsilon}
such that
\mathbb{P}((\sigma_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}\notin K_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
and on

K_{\varepsilon}
we have
0<\underline{\sigma}\leq\sigma_{t}\leq\overline{\sigma}<\infty
and

\sigma_{t}
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous for some
\alpha>0
. All estimates below are
understood conditionally on
K_{\varepsilon}
, and the final result follows by letting

\varepsilon\to 0
.

By Lemma
E.7
, uniformly in
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n,j}(y)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t_{j}}\right)=2\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))(1+o_{p}(1)),\qquad\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))\asymp N_{n}^{-1}\asymp M_{n}\Delta_{n}.

(F.1)

Moreover, the polynomial-moment bounds from Lemma
E.5
imply that, for every

s\geq 2
,

\max_{1\leq b\leq J_{n}}\max_{i\in I_{n,b}^{\circ}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\Xi_{n,i}\right|^{s}\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}\right)\leq C_{s}\Delta_{n}^{3s/4}\mathbf{1}_{K_{\varepsilon}}+o(\Delta_{n}^{3s/4}).

(F.2)

Fix a block
b
and a grid point
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n,b}
. Let

\mathcal{N}_{n,b}(j)=\left\{i\in I_{n,b}^{\circ}:(t_{i},t_{i+k_{n}}]\cap(t_{j},t_{j+2M_{n}}]\neq\varnothing\right\}.

Then

\#\mathcal{N}_{n,b}(j)\leq C(k_{n}+M_{n}).

(F.3)

Write

\Lambda_{n,b,j}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}_{n,b}(j)}\Xi_{n,i},\qquad\Gamma_{n,b}^{(-j)}=\Gamma_{n,b}-\Lambda_{n,b,j}.

The term
\Gamma_{n,b}^{(-j)}
uses only pre-averaging windows whose Brownian and noise innovations
are disjoint from those used by
X_{n,j}
; conditionally on
\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}
, it is independent
of
A_{n,j}(y)
up to a negligible error controlled by Lemmas

E.3
,
E.7
, and
E.8
.

By Minkowski’s inequality, (
F.2
), and (
F.3
), for
any fixed
s>2
,

\max_{b,j}\left\|\Lambda_{n,b,j}\right\|_{L^{s}(\mathbb{P}\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1})}\leq C_{s}(k_{n}+M_{n})\Delta_{n}^{3/4}+o(\Delta_{n}^{1/4})=O(\Delta_{n}^{1/4})\quad\text{a.s. on }K_{\varepsilon}.

Using
\left|e^{iu}-e^{iv}\right|\leq\left|u-v\right|
, the conditional independence of

\Gamma_{n,b}^{(-j)}
and
A_{n,j}(y)
, Hölder’s inequality, and
(
F.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\max_{b,j}\Bigl|\mathbb{E}\left[\left(e^{it\Gamma_{n,b}}-\varphi_{n,b}(t)\right)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,j}(y)}\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}\right]\Bigr|

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{t}\max_{b,j}\Bigl(\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Lambda_{n,b,j}\right|\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,j}(y)}\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\Lambda_{n,b,j}\right|\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(A_{n,j}(y)\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}\right)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad+o_{p}\left(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq C_{t}\Delta_{n}^{1/4}\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))^{1-1/s}\left(1+\nu_{n}(y)^{C}\right)\qquad\text{a.s. on }K_{\varepsilon}.

(F.4)

Now we pass from a single exceedance event to the no-exceedance indicator
\Upsilon_{n,b}(y)
.
The number of local-average windows in block
b
is
|\mathcal{J}_{n,b}|\leq Cr_{n}/M_{n}
.
Summing (
F.4
) over these windows gives

\displaystyle\frac{r_{n}}{M_{n}}\Delta_{n}^{1/4}\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))^{1-1/s}\left(1+\nu_{n}(y)^{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq Cr_{n}\Delta_{n}^{5/4-1/(2s)}(\log n)^{C}

\displaystyle=o(r_{n}\Delta_{n})\quad\text{on }K_{\varepsilon},

(F.5)

because
\Delta_{n}^{1/4-1/(2s)}(\log n)^{C}\to 0
for any fixed
s>2
.

For two or more exceedances, the inclusion-exclusion expansion yields

\Upsilon_{n,b}(y)=1-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n,b}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,j}(y)}+R_{n,b}(y),

(F.6)

where the remainder satisfies

|R_{n,b}(y)|\leq\sum_{j<\ell\in\mathcal{J}_{n,b}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,j}(y)}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,\ell}(y)}+\sum_{j<\ell<m}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,j}(y)}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,\ell}(y)}\mathbf{1}_{A_{n,m}(y)}+\cdots.

Using
\mathbb{P}(A_{n,j}(y)\cap A_{n,\ell}(y))\leq[\mathbb{P}(A_{n,j}(y))]^{2}
for
j\neq\ell
(by
conditional independence for windows separated by at least
2M_{n}
), and the fact that

|\mathcal{J}_{n,b}|=O(r_{n}/M_{n})
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}[|R_{n,b}(y)|\mid\mathcal{H}_{n,b-1}]\leq C\left(\frac{r_{n}}{M_{n}}\right)^{2}\overline{\Phi}(\nu_{n}(y))^{2}=O((r_{n}\Delta_{n})^{2})\quad\text{on }K_{\varepsilon}.

Combining (
F.5
) with the bound on
R_{n,b}(y)
,
and noting that windows crossing block boundaries contribute an additional

O((k_{n}+M_{n})/r_{n})=o(1)
to the total error, we obtain

\max_{1\leq b\leq J_{n}}\left|\psi_{n,b}(t,y)-\varphi_{n,b}(t)\chi_{n,b}(y)\right|=o_{p}(r_{n}\Delta_{n})\quad\text{on }K_{\varepsilon}.

(F.7)

Because
K_{\varepsilon}
has probability
1-\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
the same holds unconditionally. Finally, since
J_{n}r_{n}\Delta_{n}\to T
, we have
r_{n}\Delta_{n}=\Theta(J_{n}^{-1})
, so

o_{p}(r_{n}\Delta_{n})=o_{p}(J_{n}^{-1})
.

Moreover,

\sum_{b=1}^{J_{n}}\mathbb{E}\left|\psi_{n,b}-\varphi_{n,b}\chi_{n,b}\right|\leq J_{n}\cdot o(r_{n}\Delta_{n})=o(1).

(F.8)

Lemma

(F.9)

Partition the disjoint-grid index set

We claim that

To verify this, note that the disjoint windows

cover all but at most

O(M_{n})

) holds.

For fixed

\psi_{n,b}(t,y)

Proof of Corollary

.

From Theorem

,

Hence

where
U_{1}
and

are independent

variables. Therefore

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are independent standard Cauchy variables. Since the standard Cauchy law is

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{C,N}=\frac{1}{2}\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{PA}\right)\right]+\frac{1}{2}\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{LA}\right)\right]\xrightarrow{d}C,

where
C
is standard Cauchy. The map

x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(x)

transforms a standard Cauchy variable into a
U(0,1)
variable. Hence

p_{n}^{C,N}\xrightarrow{d}U(0,1).

Therefore, for
0<\alpha<1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{n}^{C,N}\leq\alpha\right)\to\alpha.

F.1
The noisy dense local alternative

Lemma F.2

(Counts under the noisy dense local alternative)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
, let

N_{n}^{J}=\mu_{n}^{N}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}),

\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J}=\left\{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}:(t_{i},t_{i+k_{n}}]\text{ contains at least one jump}\right\},

\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J,2}=\left\{0\leq i\leq n-k_{n}:(t_{i},t_{i+k_{n}}]\text{ contains at least two jumps}\right\},

and

\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}=\left\{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}:(t_{j},t_{j+2M_{n}}]\text{ contains at least one jump}\right\}.

Then

\displaystyle N_{n}^{J}

\displaystyle=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}),\qquad\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(k_{n}\Delta_{n}^{-1/4})=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-3/4}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J,2}

\displaystyle=O(\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}),\qquad\#\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}),\qquad\Delta_{n}\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J,2}=o_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}).

Proof.

The total jump count
N_{n}^{J}
is Poisson with mean
\vartheta T\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}
, hence

N_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}).

Each jump affects at most
k_{n}
pre-averaging windows, so

\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J}\leq k_{n}N_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(k_{n}\Delta_{n}^{-1/4})=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-3/4}).

A fixed pre-averaging window has length
k_{n}\Delta_{n}
, so its jump count is Poisson with mean

\lambda_{n,k}=\vartheta k_{n}\Delta_{n}^{3/4}=O(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}).

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{a given pre-averaging window has at least two jumps}\right)

\displaystyle=1-e^{-\lambda_{n,k}}-\lambda_{n,k}e^{-\lambda_{n,k}}

\displaystyle=O(\lambda_{n,k}^{2})=O(\Delta_{n}^{1/2}).

Since there are at most
n
such windows,

\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J,2}=nO(\Delta_{n}^{1/2})=O(\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}),

which implies

\Delta_{n}\#\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J,2}=o_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}).

Finally, the intervals

(t_{j},t_{j+2M_{n}}]
,
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
, are disjoint, so each jump can contaminate at most one such
interval. Hence

\#\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}\leq N_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}).

∎

k_{n}\Delta_{n}
therefore gives

\Delta_{n}^{1-p/4}V_{n}(Y^{(n)},\phi,p)=\Delta_{n}^{1-p/4}V_{n}(Y^{0},\phi,p)+\vartheta\theta A_{p}d_{\phi}^{N}\Delta_{n}^{1/4}+o_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}).

(F.19)

Applying (
F.19
) with
\phi=g
and
\phi=h
, and using
Lemma
E.4
,

\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\left(R_{n}^{PA}(Y^{(n)})-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right)=\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\left(R_{n}^{PA}(Y^{0})-\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right)+\frac{\vartheta\theta A_{p}}{\gamma^{\prime}c_{h}}\left(d_{g}^{N}-\gamma^{\prime}\gamma^{\prime\prime}d_{h}^{N}\right)+o_{p}(1).

(F.20)

Let
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}
be the collection of blocks
I_{n,b}
that intersect
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{J}
. Since

r_{n}/k_{n}\to\infty
, every jump contaminates at most two such blocks, and Lemma

F.2
yields

\#\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}).

For
b\notin\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}
, we have
\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{(n)})=\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})
. For

b\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}
, relation (
F.20
) on a single block gives

\left|\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{(n)})-\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})\right|=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}).

Hence

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}(Y^{(n)})^{2}-\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}(Y^{0})^{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}}\left|\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{(n)})-\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})\right|\left|\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{(n)})+\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})\right|

\displaystyle=O_{p}(\#\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}\Delta_{n}^{1/2})+O_{p}\left(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}}\left|\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})\right|\right)

\displaystyle=o_{p}(1),

because
\#\mathcal{B}_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4})
and Lemma
E.5
gives

\max_{b}\left|\Gamma_{n,b}(Y^{0})\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
. Since the same lemma yields

\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}(Y^{0})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma^{1/2}
, we obtain

\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}(Y^{(n)})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\Sigma^{1/2}.

(F.21)

Combining (
F.20
) and (
F.21
) gives

Z_{n}^{PA}(Y^{(n)})=Z_{n}^{PA}(Y^{0})+\mu_{D,N}+o_{p}(1).

(F.22)

Now turn to the max statistic. If
j\notin\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}
, then

L_{n,j}(Y^{(n)})=L_{n,j}(Y^{0})
. If
j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}

(t_{j},t_{j+2M_{n}}]

\sqrt{M_{n}}\left|L_{n,j}(Y^{(n)})-L_{n,j}(Y^{0})\right|\leq CN_{n,j}^{J},

where
N_{n,j}^{J}
is the jump count on
(t_{j},t_{j+2M_{n}}]

\mathbb{E}N_{n,j}^{J}=\vartheta 2M_{n}\Delta_{n}^{3/4}=O(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}),

we have
N_{n,j}^{J}=O_{p}(1)
uniformly on
\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}
. Lemma

F.2
implies
\#\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4})
. Therefore, for every
fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}
, the tail approximation in Lemma
E.7
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}}\left|X_{n,j}(Y^{(n)})\right|>A_{N_{n}}+B_{N_{n}}y\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\#\mathcal{J}_{n}^{J}\cdot 2\overline{\Phi}\left(A_{N_{n}}+B_{N_{n}}y-C\right)(1+o(1))+o(1)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}\overline{\Phi}\left(A_{N_{n}}-C+B_{N_{n}}y\right)+o(1)\to 0,

(F.23)

where
\overline{\Phi}=1-\Phi
and the last limit uses
N_{n}\asymp\Delta_{n}^{-1/2}
. Thus the
contaminated disjoint windows do not affect the extreme-value limit. It remains only to check that
the feasible spot-volatility and noise-variance estimators are not spoiled by the dense small jumps. For

\hat{\omega}_{n}^{2}

\frac{C}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\{(\Delta_{i}^{n}J^{(n)})^{2}+|\Delta_{i}^{n}J^{(n)}|\,|\Delta_{i}^{n}Y^{0}|\}=o_{p}((\log n)^{-2}),

because
N_{n}^{J}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/4})
and
|\Delta_{i}^{n}J^{(n)}|\leq C\Delta_{n}^{1/4}
on the compact-support
localization. For the filtering TSRSV estimator, a spot window has length

h_{n}\asymp\Delta_{n}^{1/6}
and contains
O_{p}(h_{n}\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}+|\log\Delta_{n}|)=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-1/12}|\log\Delta_{n}|)
jumps uniformly over the disjoint grid. The jump quadratic variation in such a window is
therefore
O_{p}(h_{n}\Delta_{n}^{1/4}|\log\Delta_{n}|)
; after division by
h_{n}
it is

O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{1/4}|\log\Delta_{n}|)=o_{p}((\log n)^{-2})
. The cross terms with the continuous part and the
noise part are smaller by Cauchy–Schwarz and the sub-exponential bounds used in Lemma

E.8
. Consequently the feasible-array condition (
30
) also holds
for
Y^{(n)}
, and hence

\xi_{n}^{LA}(Y^{(n)})-\xi_{n}^{LA}(Y^{0})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

(F.24)

Combining (

.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Lemma
E.9
gives

R_{n}^{PA}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1.

We next prove that the local-average maximum detects the fixed jump. Let
q_{n}
and
j_{n}\in\mathcal{J}_{n}
be measurable indices attaining the maximum in the definition of
D_{n}
, and set

u_{n}=i_{q_{n},n}-j_{n},\qquad 1\leq u_{n}\leq 2M_{n}-1.

Since the fixed jump times are separated and
M_{n}\Delta_{n}\to 0
, the local-average window
(t_{j_{n}},t_{j_{n}+2M_{n}}]
contains no fixed jump other than
\kappa_{q_{n}}
with probability tending to one. Lemma
E.6
gives the exact triangular-weight representation

\sqrt{M_{n}}L_{n,j_{n}}=\frac{a_{M_{n},u_{n}}}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\kappa_{q_{n}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\sum_{u=1}^{2M_{n}-1}a_{M_{n},u}\Delta_{j_{n}+u}^{n}X^{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\sum_{u=0}^{M_{n}-1}(\epsilon_{j_{n}+M_{n}+u}-\epsilon_{j_{n}+u}),

where
X^{0}
is the continuous component of
X
. By Lemma
E.7
, applied to the continuous-plus-noise part,

\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\frac{1}{v_{n,j}}\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\sum_{u=1}^{2M_{n}-1}a_{M_{n},u}\Delta_{j+u}^{n}X^{0}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}\sum_{u=0}^{M_{n}-1}(\epsilon_{j+M_{n}+u}-\epsilon_{j+u})\right\}\right|=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log n}).

Moreover
0<c\leq v_{n,j}\leq C<\infty
uniformly in
j
, and the finite fixed jumps do not affect the plug-in variance at the selected left endpoint
j_{n}
: the estimator
\hat{\omega}_{n}^{2}
receives only
O_{p}(n^{-1})
from finitely many squared fixed jumps, and the TSRSV spot window ending at
t_{j_{n}}
contains no fixed jump for all large
n
because the jump times are separated and
h_{n}\to 0
. Thus

\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{n}}\left|\hat{v}_{n,j}^{-1}-v_{n,j}^{-1}\right|=o_{p}(1)

on the selected jump-detecting windows. Since
\inf_{q}\left|\kappa_{q}\right|>0
a.s., condition (
37
) implies

\left|\hat{X}_{n,j_{n}}\right|\geq c\frac{D_{n}}{\sqrt{M_{n}}}-O_{p}(\sqrt{\log n})

and therefore

\frac{\mathcal{M}_{n}^{LA}}{\sqrt{\log n}}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\infty.

Because
A_{N_{n}}=O(\sqrt{\log n})
and
B_{N_{n}}^{-1}=O(\sqrt{\log n})
, it follows that

\frac{\xi_{n}^{LA}}{\log n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\infty.

Consequently, for every fixed
L>0
,

p_{n}^{LA}=1-\exp\{-e^{-\xi_{n}^{LA}}\}=o_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{L}),

and hence

\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{LA}\right)\right]=\cot(\pi p_{n}^{LA})

diverges faster than any fixed power of
\Delta_{n}^{-1}
.

It remains to check that the other Cauchy component cannot cancel this divergence. Under fixed finite-activity jumps, the variables entering the block self-normalizer are at most polynomial in
\Delta_{n}^{-1}
after localization and after using the sub-Gaussian maximal bound

\max_{0\leq i\leq n}\left|\epsilon_{i}\right|=O_{p}(\sqrt{\log n})
. Hence there exists a finite constant
C_{p}
such that

\hat{\varsigma}_{n}^{PA}=O_{p}(\Delta_{n}^{-C_{p}}).

Since
R_{n}^{PA}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1
and
\gamma^{\prime\prime}>1
, the numerator
\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}(R_{n}^{PA}-\gamma^{\prime\prime})
is bounded away from zero in absolute value times
\Delta_{n}^{-1/4}
with probability tending to one. Thus

\left|Z_{n}^{PA}\right|\geq\Delta_{n}^{C_{p}}

with probability tending to one, after increasing
C_{p}
if necessary. The elementary inequalities

1-p_{n}^{PA}=2\Phi(\left|Z_{n}^{PA}\right|)-1\geq c(\left|Z_{n}^{PA}\right|\wedge 1),\qquad\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-u\right)\right]\geq-\frac{C}{1-u},\quad 0<u<1,

then imply

\tan\left[\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-p_{n}^{PA}\right)\right]=O_{p}^{-}(\Delta_{n}^{-C_{p}}),

where
O_{p}^{-}(a_{n})
means that the negative part is
O_{p}(a_{n})
. Choosing
L>C_{p}
in the preceding bound for
p_{n}^{LA}
gives

\mathcal{T}_{n}^{C,N}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\infty.

Therefore, for every
0<\alpha<1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(p_{n}^{C,N}\leq\alpha\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{C,N}>c_{\alpha}\right)\to 1.

∎
