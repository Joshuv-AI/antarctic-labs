Thermosensitive Poloxamer Liposomal Gel for Sustained FTA Delivery Enhances Anti-Breast Cancer Efficacy and Biosafety
Authors: Yongqiang Jiang, Meiting Zhang, Mingxuan Liu, Zhilian Su, Yuqian Pu, Wen Li, Hong Shao, Peng Shi, Rong Zhang, Ling Zhao, Yumeng Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFTA is a new FT derivative developed by our team by modifying FT with aspirin, and it has been shown to boost anti-breast cancer activity.MethodsTo significantly enhance the anti-breast cancer efficacy and biosafety of FTA, this study employed Poloxamer F127 and Poloxamer F68 to construct a composite carrier system, successfully prepared FTA-loaded liposomes (FTA-LN) and their thermosensitive gel formulation (FTA-LN-TSG), with systematic in vitro and in vivo assessments performed.ResultsSimultaneously, FTA-LN exhibited an average particle size of 131.03 ± 4.35 nm, polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.265 ± 0.003, zeta potential of −21.46 ± 2.49 mV, and encapsulation efficiency of 94.55 ± 1.10%. FTA-LN followed first-order kinetics, with a cumulative release rate of 68.48% within 4 hours. Conversely, FTA-LN-TSG displayed sustained release characteristics consistent with Higuchi kinetics, reaching a cumulative release of 52.56% at 8 hour. In vitro efficacy evaluation revealed that FTA-LN-TSG exhibited significantly superior inhibitory effects against breast cancer cells compared to FTA (24 h and 48 h inhibition rates: 2.72- and 5.02-fold of FTA; cell migration rate: 37% of FTA). Furthermore, FTA-LN-TSG demonstrated significant differences in inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest (Total apoptosis rate of FTA: 25.74, total apoptosis rate of FTA-LN-TSG: 48.84%). Pharmacokinetic results indicated that FTA-LN-TSG exhibited a 3.5-fold prolongation in half-life (t1/2), and a 6-fold extension in time to peak concentration (Tmax) compared with FTA group. In tumor model mice, The FTA-LN-TSG injected beside the tumor was achieved a tumor inhibition rate of 64.42 ± 5.60% (1.82-fold of the FTA group, 1.45-fold of the S-1 group) with favorable biosafety.ConclusionIn summary, as a novel drug delivery system, FTA-LN-TSG may represent a promising option for breast cancer therapy.Keywords: FT, liposome, thermosensitive gel, breast cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies in women globally.1–3 According to the GLOBOCAN 2020 report, annual new breast cancer cases worldwide have reached 2.26 million, surpassing lung cancer to become the most prevalent cancer.4 Research projections indicate that by 2040, the number of new breast cancer cases will increase by over 40%, potentially approaching 3 million annually.5 Current primary treatments for breast cancer include surgery, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy.6–10 However, due to the high metastatic potential, most breast cancer patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, making chemotherapy an essential therapeutic option. But traditional chemotherapy drugs lack specific distribution in the body, which leads to pretty severe overall toxic reactions.11–14 Therefore, developing novel, highly effective, and low-toxicity drugs as well as targeted delivery systems is imperative to enhance breast cancer treatment.

Furofurothiazole (FT), a derivative of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), is synthesized by introducing a furan moiety into the 5-FU molecule. Following hepatic metabolism, this compound is converted to 5-FU, thereby mediating its antitumor activity.15,16 FT retains the antitumor mechanism of 5-FU while demonstrating double the chemotherapeutic index and only a quarter of the toxicity.16 However, FT still causes side effects such as bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal reactions, and neurotoxicity.17,18 FTA is a new FT derivative developed by our team by modifying FT with aspirin, which exhibits superior stability, a stronger tumor cell cycle arrest effect, and significantly enhanced anticancer activity. To further enhance therapeutic efficacy of FTA against breast cancer while reducing its systemic toxicity, the development of tumor-targeted drug delivery systems is an ideal strategy.

Lipid thermosensitive gels is a novel composite delivery system that combines the advantages of both liposomes and thermosensitive gels.19 At room temperature, it exists as a liquid. After in vivo injection, body temperature induces its conversion into a semi-solid gel, extending drug retention at the tumor site, boosting absorption, and realizing sustained release.20 This formulation exhibits outstanding biocompatibility, reducing macrophage phagocytosis of the drug and improving the penetration and accumulation of nanodrugs in tumor tissues.21 For instance, Maswadeh Hamzah et al co-encapsulated doxorubicin and cisplatin within a lipid-based thermosensitive gel, demonstrating enhanced anti-breast cancer efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo studies.22 In short, local injectable nanomedicine platforms like thermosensitive liposome gels can be directly injected into or near tumor tissue, allowing site-specific drug delivery and reducing systemic toxicity. This kind of delivery not only boosts local drug concentration but also lowers dosing frequency, which can improve patient compliance.23 For platforms like in situ thermosensitive liposome gels that are injected right next to tumors, clinical use can be relatively smooth, especially for cancers like breast cancer, where tumor locations are usually easier to access.

Therefore, this study aims to develop a lipid-based thermosensitive in situ gel system encapsulating FTA. Through formulation optimization and pharmaceutical evaluation, we seek to enhance the antitumor efficacy and improve the safety profile of FTA. The study will evaluate its in vitro activity in MCF-7 breast cancer cells, with conduct pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic evaluated in healthy rats and tumor-bearing nude mice, respectively. This study aims to develop, efficient, and low-toxicity targeted drug delivery system for breast cancer.

Preparation and Evaluation of TFA Liposomes (FTA-LN)

Synthesis of FTA

Dissolve 0.1 g of FT and 0.17 g of acetylsalicyloyl chloride in 5 mL of dichloromethane. Then, at 4°C, add 170 μL of triethylamine and the FT stock solution to a 20 mL dichloromethane reaction vessel. Slowly add the intermediate stock solution to the reaction vessel over 10 minutes and allow the reaction to proceed at 4°C for 10 minutes. Subsequently, slowly add the intermediate stock solution to the reaction vessel again over 10 minutes and allow the reaction to proceed at 4°C for 30 minutes. Upon completion of the reaction, remove the reaction mixture via rotary evaporation. Continue to remove the solvent by rotary evaporation. Dissolve the sample in anhydrous ethanol and recrystallize at 4°C to obtain white TAK5399 crystals. After filtration, wash the crystals three times with anhydrous ethanol and dry to obtain the pure product.

Finally, FTA has an HPLC purity of 99.7%; solubility of 113.96 μg/mL, and a melting point of 115 ± 0.3°C.

Preparation of FTA-LN

FTA, CHO, and PC were weighed into a round-bottom flask, followed by the addition of chloroform for dissolution, and the mixture was thoroughly blended. Chloroform was evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 37°C and a rotation speed of 80 rpm. Once a thin film forms at the bottom of the flask, transfer it to an oven set at 37°C and dry for 1 hour to ensure no solvent remains. 6 mL of distilled water was added to the flask to hydrate the film. After removing the film from the flask, ultrasonic treatment (160 W) was performed using an Flat-head ultrasonic cell disruptor to obtain the FTA lipid nanoparticle spray suspension (FTA-LN). Finally, Add a lyophilization protectant, filter through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, pre-freeze at −80°C, then freeze-dry at −50°C and 0.9 Pa for 24 hours to get FTA-LN white, loose lyophilized powder.

Single-Factor Screening and BBD Response Surface Optimization of FTA-LN

FTA-LN was prepared according to the method in Preparation of FTA-LN. The effects of ultrasonication time, ultrasonication intensity, excipient dosage, and lyophilization stabilizer on FTA-LN were evaluated using particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and encapsulation efficiency (EE%) as assessment indicators.

According to the results of single-factor experiments, three factors were selected for investigation, including drug content (X1), PC content (X2), and CHO (X3). Particle size, PDI, and encapsulation efficiency were chosen as dependent variables (Y1, Y2, Y3). Liposome formulation was optimized using Box-Behnken experimental design (BBD).24

Appearance, Particle Size, PDI, and Zeta Potential

An appropriate amount of FTA-LN solution or lyophilized powder was diluted with distilled water to a suitable concentration. The size, PDI, and zeta potential of the FTA-LN lipid solution or lyophilized powder were measured using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer.

In vitro Release

The in vitro release of FTA-LN was determined using the dialysis method. The procedure is as follows: A solution of FTA-LN at an appropriate concentration was placed in a dialysis bag (dialysis membrane cutoff:10 kDa) and suspended in 200 mL of PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) containing 0.5% Tween-80 and 5% ethanol, and The solubility of FTA in the release medium is 296.12 μg/mL, sink conditions >3. At time intervals of 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 360, 480, 600, and 720 minutes, 2 milliliters of the release medium sample were accurately withdrawn. The dialysate conditions were maintained by immediately supplementing with an equal volume of fresh release medium at the same temperature. The collected dialysate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 minutes. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was used to determine drug concentrations at each time point, enabling calculation of cumulative release and plotting of release curves. Finally, the drug release kinetics equation was fitted based on the release data collected at different time points.

Stability of FTA-LN

Prepare 3 batches of FTA-LN lyophilized powder using the optimal formulation and process. Store them at 25 ± 2°C and 4 ± 2°C, then take samples at different time points to measure their Size, PDI, and EE% to check the stability of the lyophilized powder.

Preparation and Evaluation of FTA Lipid-Based Thermosensitive Gel (FTA-LN-TSG)

Preparation Method of FTA-LN-TSG

First, blank gel matrices were prepared by adding a specified amount of F127 and F68 to purified water, followed by overnight storage in a 4°C refrigerator to obtain a clear solution. Next, drug-loaded gels (FTA-TSG) were prepared by dissolving FTA, F127, and F68 in dichloromethane. The solvent was then removed via rotary evaporation (40°C, 100 rpm), and the obtained solid was cured overnight in a vacuum drying oven. Afterwards, distilled water was added, and the mixture swelled at 4°C to obtain FTA-TSG. Finally, the FTA-LN lyophilized powder was added to the swollen blank gel, followed by stirring to achieve uniform dispersion, and finally FTA-LN-TSG was obtained.

Physicochemical Characterization

A precise amount of FTA-LN lyophilized powder was weighed and added to the swollen blank gel. After thorough mixing and dispersion, an appropriate volume of distilled water was added to dilute to the desired concentration. The size, PDI, and zeta potential of the FTA-LN thermosensitive gel were determined using a Malvern laser particle size analyzer. The phase transition temperature of the thermosensitive gel was determined using the inverted test tube method. An appropriate amount of drug-loaded gel was placed in a test tube and slowly heated in a 20°C-water bath. The gel state was observed every 0.5°C increment. When the gel ceased to flow after the test tube was inverted, the gel state at that temperature was recorded as gelation.

In vitro Release

An appropriate concentration of FTA-LN-TSG was loaded into a dialysis bag, which was then placed in 200 mL of PBS containing 0.5% Tween-80 and 5% ethanol. The temperature of the dissolution tester was set to 37°C. After gelation occurred, the rotation speed was adjusted to 100 revolutions per minute. At 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 360, 480, 600, and 720 minutes, 2 mL of dialysate sample were accurately withdrawn. An equal volume of release medium at the same temperature was immediately added to maintain a constant total system volume. The collected dialysate was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 minutes. The peak area of FTA at each time point was detected under predetermined chromatographic conditions. This value was substituted into the standard curve to calculate the cumulative release percentage at different time points, thereby plotting the in vitro release curve. Finally, the measured release curves were fitted to various drug release kinetic models, including zero-order, first-order, and Higuchi equations. The goodness-of-fit for each equation was calculated to evaluate the release mechanism.

Hemolysis

An appropriate amount of whole blood was collected from SD rats. The fibrinogen in the whole blood was destroyed by stirring with a sterile glass rod for 5 minutes. An adequate volume of 0.9% sodium chloride solution was then added, followed by thorough stirring. Centrifugation was performed at 1500 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was washed repeatedly with 0.9% sodium chloride solution. Centrifugation was repeated until the supernatant was no longer reddish-brown, and the red blood cell suspension was obtained. The 2% red blood cell suspension was prepared by adding an appropriate amount of normal saline to the aforementioned suspension. Each sample was assigned a group number. Ultrapure water was used as the positive blank control (Group 1), and 2% saline was used as the negative control (Group 2). FTA-LN thermosensitive gels at different concentrations serve as experimental groups 3 to 8. All groups were incubated in a constant-temperature incubator at 37°C for 1, 3, and 5 hours, respectively. At each time point, samples were retrieved, centrifuged at 1500 r/min for 10 minutes, and hemolysis was observed across groups. After 5 hours, appropriate volumes of supernatant from each group were transferred to a 96-well plate. Absorbance (A) was measured at 540 nm, and the hemolysis rate (%) was calculated using the corresponding formula.

Apoptosis Assay

Log-phase MCF-7 cells were seeded at 4×105 cells/well in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 hours at 37°C. Subsequently, the medium was removed, and the cells were treated with complete medium containing 62.5 μM FT, FTA, FTA-LN, FTA-TSG, and FTA-LN-TSG. The drug-free group served as the blank control, and incubation was continued in the incubator for 24 hours. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS, digested with EDTA-free trypsin, and gently dislodged by pipetting. Digestion was terminated by adding fresh medium. Cell suspensions were collected and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 4 minutes. Supernatant was discarded, and the centrifugation step was repeated twice to thoroughly wash cells. Each cell pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of binding buffer. 5 μL of Annexin V/FITC and 5 μL of PI staining solution were added sequentially. The mixture was incubated for 10 minutes in the dark. Finally, 400 μL of binding buffer was added and thoroughly mixed. Apoptosis detection was performed using a flow cytometer within one hour.

Cell Cycle Assay

Log-phase MCF-7 cells were seeded at 4×105 cells per well in a 6-well plate and cultured for 24 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. Subsequently, the DMEM complete medium was removed from each well and replaced with complete medium containing FT, FTA, FTA-LN, FTA-TSG, or FTA-LN-TSG at a concentration of 62.5 μM. The drug-free group served as the blank control. After treatment, cells were digested with EDTA-free trypsin for 3 minutes, gently dislodged by pipetting, and collected by centrifugation. The pellet was resuspended in 70% ethanol solution with thorough pipetting to prevent clumping, then fixed at −20°C for 24 hours. Following fixation, the fixative was removed, and cells were washed twice with pre-chilled PBS. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 500 µL of PI/RNase staining solution in the sample tube. After 30-minute incubation in the dark, cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Pharmacokinetic Study of FTA-LN-TSG

Twenty female SD rats were randomly divided into four groups of five rats each: the FTA oral group, the FTA subcutaneous injection group, the FTA-LN subcutaneous injection group, and the FTA-LN-TSG subcutaneous injection group. All groups received a 20 mg/kg dose of FTA. Blood samples (0.3 mL) were collected at 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240, 480, 720, 1440, 2160, and 2880 minutes post-administration. The collected rat blood was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 3 minutes to separate plasma, followed by HPLC determination of FTA drug concentration in rat plasma at each time point.

Results

Formulation Optimization and Characterization of TFA Liposomes (FTA-LN)

Single-Factor Screening

During the optimization of the FTA-LN preparation process, we systematically investigated the effects of ultrasonication time, ultrasonication intensity, drug content, PC content, and CHO content on formulation properties, using particle size, PDI, and encapsulation efficiency (EE%) as evaluation metrics. (Figure 1A1–E3) As ultrasonication time increased from 2 min to 8 min, particle size and PDI reached their minimum values at 4 min, accompanied by a high encapsulation efficiency. Therefore, 4 min was selected as the optimal ultrasonication time. When ultrasonic intensity was increased from 120 W to 180 W, the conditions of 160 W yielded the smallest particle size and PDI, along with a high encapsulation efficiency. Thus, 160 W was determined as the optimal ultrasonic power. When the active ingredient content increased from 6 mg to 12 mg, the PDI was smallest and the encapsulation efficiency was highest at 8 mg. Therefore, 8 mg was selected as the optimal active ingredient dosage. Within the PC dosage range of 60–120 mg, both particle size and PDI were smallest at 80 mg, with a high encapsulation efficiency. Thus, the PC dosage was determined to be 80 mg. When the CHO dosage was increased from 5 mg to 20 mg, the PDI and particle size were smallest at 15 mg, and the encapsulation efficiency reached its peak. Therefore, the CHO dosage was selected as 15 mg. In the lyophilization process research, the effects of different lyoprotectants protectants on the stability of FTA-LN were compared. The results showed that the use of mannitol and lactose as protective agents led to increased particle size and reduced encapsulation efficiency post-lyophilization. However, sucrose maintained stable particle size (approximately 134 nm), PDI (approximately 0.26), and encapsulation efficiency (above 90%) both before and after lyophilization. Consequently, sucrose was ultimately selected as the lyoprotectant for FTA-LN.

Preparation and Physicochemical Evaluation of FTA-LN-TSG

This study first examined the effect of F127 content in the formulation on FTA-LN-TSG quality. Results indicated that as F127 concentration increased, gelation temperature gradually decreased. At 25% F127 concentration, the gelation temperature was 27.3°C, allowing it to gel and form a semi-solid state at room temperature, which was unfavorable for administration operations. Conversely, at 17% concentration, the gelation temperature reached 38.7°C, exceeding human body temperature. Therefore, three F127 concentrations—19%, 21%, and 23%—were selected for further screening. Subsequently, the influence of F68 content was investigated. It was found that as the F68 concentration increased, the gelation temperature rose across all F127 concentrations, indicating that gelation temperature is inversely proportional to F127 concentration and directly proportional to F68 concentration. The target gelation temperature for this study was set around 35°C. Results revealed that a gelation temperature of 35°C was achieved at an F127 concentration of 23% and an F68 concentration of 4%, meeting the project requirements. This ratio was therefore selected for subsequent research (Figure 3A–D). Three batches of FTA-LN-TSG prepared using the optimized formulation and process demonstrated a particle size of 138.83 ± 2.41 nm, exhibiting small dimensions with no significant aggregation under SEM, and a zeta potential of −18.83 ± 2.26 mV, indicating a distinctly negative surface charge (Table 8) (Figure 3E–G).

FTA-LN-TSG Inhibits Cell Migration

The effects of different drugs and formulations on MCF-7 cell migration were evaluated using a scratch wound healing assay. As shown in Figure 5E and F, after 24-hour treatment, the migration rates were 60.72% in the blank control group, 49.00% in the FT group, 34.94% in the FTA group, 22.85% in the FTA-TSG group, 18.74% in the FTA-LN group, and 13.03% in the FTA-LN-TSG group, respectively. Results indicated that MCF-7 cells inherently possessed high migratory capacity. While the active pharmaceutical ingredients FT and FTA partially inhibited migration, the inhibitory effects of the compound formulations (FTA-TSG, FTA-LN, and FTA-LN-TSG) were significantly more pronounced (P < 0.05). The migration rate in the FTA-LN-TSG group was only 37% of that in the FTA group and showed a statistically significant reduction compared to both the FTA-TSG and FTA-LN groups (P < 0.05).

Apoptosis and Cell Cycle Arrest Induced by FTA-LN-TSG

The apoptotic effects of FT, FTA, FTA-TSG, FTA-LN, and FTA-LN-TSG on MCF-7 cells were evaluated by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 5G and H, both FT and FTA groups exhibited comparable apoptosis induction, with total apoptosis rates of 22.5% (early stage: 20.14%, late stage: 2.36%) and 25.74% (early stage: 22.28%, late stage: 3.46%), respectively. In contrast, the total apoptosis rates in the FTA-TSG, FTA-LN, and FTA-LN-TSG formulation groups significantly increased to 40.71% (early phase: 37.86%, late phase: 2.85%), 43.02% (early phase: 36.60%, late phase: 6.42%), and 48.84% (early phase: 45.62%, late Phase 3.22%), all showing significant differences compared to the API (P < 0.05). The pro-apoptotic effect of the FTA-LN-TSG group was significantly superior to that of the FTA-TSG and FTA-LN groups (P < 0.05). In the cell cycle assay, all treatment groups exhibited increased S-phase cell proportions and corresponding decreases in G1-phase cell proportions compared to the blank control group (Figure 5I–K), indicating MCF-7 cell cycle arrest at the S phase. The FTA-LN-TSG formulation group showed a significant increase in the proportion of S-phase cells from 26.83% (API group) to 38.41% (P < 0.05), indicating its enhanced ability to induce S-phase arrest.

Pharmacokinetic Study of FTA-LN-TSG

Based on the plasma concentration monitoring results shown in Tables 11 and 12; Figure 6, the pharmacokinetic profiles of FTA and its formulations (FTA-LN and FTA-LN-TSG) were systematically compared. Orally administered FTA was well absorbed, reaching peak plasma concentration (14.5 μg/mL) within 30 minutes, whereas subcutaneous injection of FTA exhibited a shorter time to peak and a lower peak concentration. When FTA was formulated as a liposome (FTA-LN) or a thermosensitive gel (FTA-LN-TSG) and administered subcutaneously, its pharmacokinetic characteristics changed significantly. The half-lives (t1/2) of both formulations were 3.81-fold and 3.5-fold longer than that of subcutaneously injected FTA, respectively. The mean residence time (MRT(0-t)) was 1.83-fold and 2.57-fold longer than that of FTA, respectively, while the time to peak concentration (Tmax) was 4-fold and 6-fold longer. These results indicated that the preparation significantly prolonged the in vivo duration of drug action. However, the maximum plasma concentration detected for FTA-LN was 5.5 μg/mL, while that for FTA-LN-TSG was 3.1 μg/mL. Both values were significantly lower than those observed in the oral and subcutaneous FTA API groups. And you can see that FTA-LN-TSG shows a lower plasma AUC compared to the oral FTA group (40.1 vs 60.83). The reason for this is that, unlike oral administration, where the drug is systemically distributed and undergoes first-pass metabolism in the liver, FTA-LN-TSG is administered around the tumor, forming an in situ drug reservoir that slowly releases FTA locally at the tumor site. So, a lower plasma AUC and lower peak concentration does not mean the drug delivery is less effective—on the contrary, it shows effective local retention and reduced systemic exposure, which actually aligns with the initial design intent of our locally injectable thermosensitive gel system.

In vivo Anti-Breast Cancer Study of FTA-LN-TSG

The antitumor efficacy of the different formulations was evaluated by measuring the volume and weight of subcutaneously transplanted tumors in nude mice. Among the remaining groups, tumor volume and weight decreased in the following order: FTA group, S-1 group, FTA-LN group, FTA-TSG group, and FTA-LN-TSG group. Compared to the blank control group, all treatment groups exhibited tumor growth inhibition. The FTA-LN-TSG group demonstrated the smallest tumor volume and weight, achieving a tumor inhibition rate of 64.42%, significantly superior to other groups (FTA-TSG: 53.81%, FTA-LN: 50.15%, S-1: 44.37%, FTA: 35.30%), Compared to traditional oral administration, our new FTA-LN-TSG formulation’s local delivery strategy has the advantage of boosting tumor suppression while reducing systemic side effects. And under the same local delivery approach, results show that among the FTA-LN, FTA-TSG, and FTA-LN-TSG groups, the FTA-LN-TSG group performs the best in terms of anti-tumor effects. HE staining analysis of tumor tissues (Figure 7E) revealed minimal local necrosis in the blank control group. Meanwhile, both the FTA and S-1 groups showed only occasional focal tumor cell necrosis with nuclear fragmentation. Overall, while the raw drug FTA possesses anti-tumor proliferation effects, this activity was significantly enhanced in the FTA-LN-TSG formulation. Furthermore, HE staining analysis of tumor-bearing nude mouse tissues (heart, liver, lung, kidney) revealed superior safety profiles in the gel formulation groups compared to the raw drug group. Discussion

This study successfully developed and systematically evaluated a novel TFA-lipid thermosensitive gel (FTA-LN-TSG). The results consistently demonstrated that the successive encapsulation of FTA first into lipid nanoparticles (LN) and subsequently into a thermosensitive gel (TSG) yielded an FTA-LN-TSG formulation with significantly superior overall performance compared to the pristine drug and other intermediate formulations. These advantages are evident in physicochemical properties, in vitro release, cytotoxicity, in vivo pharmacokinetics, and ultimately, antitumor efficacy.

First, in formulation optimization and characterization, as the ultrasonication time increased from 2 min to 8 min, the particle size and PDI of FTA-LN initially decreased and then increased, reaching a maximum at 4 min. This was likely attributed to the initial ultrasonication primarily inducing “fragmentation” and “dispersion” effects, resulting in finer particles and a narrower distribution. However, the thermal effects from excessive ultrasonication induced particle re-agglomeration, leading to increased particle size and a broader distribution.27 Regular liposome formulations can improve a drug’s pharmacokinetics and anti-tumor efficiency while reducing side effects in breast cancer patients, but when given systemically, they are usually cleared quickly and accumulate in tumors only to a limited extent.8,28 The incorporation of liposomes within a thermosensitive gel matrix represents a strategy to overcome these limitations by establishing a local drug depot that sustains release directly at the tumor site. During lyophilization stabilizer screening, mannitol and lactose caused increased FTA-LN particle size and decreased encapsulation efficiency, whereas sucrose effectively maintained stability in particle size, PDI, and encapsulation efficiency before and after lyophilization. This might be related to the tendency of sucrose to form an amorphous glassy solid structure, thereby preventing crystallization-induced disruption of the liposome structure.29 In vitro release experiments demonstrated that FTA liposome release behavior conformed to first-order kinetic models. FTA-LN-TSG exhibited superior sustained-release properties compared to FTA liposomes, likely due to the thermosensitive F127/F68 material forming a semi-solid colloidal structure under experimental conditions, thereby delaying gel erosion and drug release.30

The integration of single-factor testing and Box-Behnken design-response surface methodology enabled the successful development of FTA-LN featuring well-defined particle size distribution, high drug loading, and superior stability. Validation results of the optimal formulation are highly correlated with model predictions, demonstrating the reliability and reproducibility of the optimized process. Building upon this foundation, through precise screening of the F127:F68 ratio,31–33 we obtained FTA-LN-TSG with a gelation temperature of approximately 35°C. This temperature was slightly below body temperature, ensuring the formulation remains liquid during storage for easy injection while rapidly transforming into a gel state in vivo. This facilitated drug retention at the injection site and enabled sustained release.34,35 Second, the exceptional in vitro performance of FTA-LN-TSG formed the foundation of its synergistic effects. In vitro release studies clearly elucidated the drug delivery behavior.FTA solution exhibited rapid release, while FTA-LN demonstrated pronounced sustained-release characteristics. More importantly, FTA-LN-TSG exhibited the slowest and most prolonged release, with an 8-hour cumulative release rate (52.56%) still lower than that of FTA-LN at 4 hours (68.48%). This strongly demonstrated the dual sustained-release effect of the thermosensitive gel as a “drug reservoir”.36 On one hand, the liposomes themselves provided primary encapsulation and sustained release of the drug. On the other hand, the gel network structure further restricted liposome diffusion, The two form a “gel-trapped liposome → liposome slow-release drug” secondary slow release, achieving an “ultra-long release” that a single system cannot reach.37,38 This characteristic was crucial for maintaining stable local drug concentrations and reducing dosing frequency.

In vitro cellular assays comprehensively validated the potent antitumor activity of FTA-LN-TSG. Across a series of experiments, including cytotoxicity, migration inhibition, apoptosis induction, and cell cycle arrest, FTA-LN-TSG consistently demonstrated superior performance. Notably, at a concentration of 125 μM, its proliferation inhibition rate against MCF-7 cells was 1.80 times that of the parent compound FTA. In the scratch assay, its inhibition of cell migration reduced the migration rate of the FTA group by approximately 63%. Flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated that it induced the highest level of total apoptosis (48.84%) and the most significant S-phase arrest (38.41%).Collectively, these data demonstrated that FTA-LN-TSG not only exerted a more potent direct cytotoxic effect on tumor cells but also more potently suppressed their metastatic spread, But we cannot deny that cell proliferation might affect the results of the migration experiment. Its mechanism of action likely involved enhancing apoptotic signaling and disrupting cell cycle progression. This study’s mechanistic exploration is limited to phenotypic assessment of apoptosis-related protein expression. The specific molecular pathways through which FTA-LN-TSG enhances anti-tumor effects still need to be clarified. In the future, using transcriptomics or proteomics approaches for research could help reveal the potential mechanisms of its action.

Additionally, pharmacokinetic studies provided key insights into the in vivo behavior of FTA-LN-TSG. Compared to the parent drug (administered orally or subcutaneously), both FTA-LN and FTA-LN-TSG significantly altered the in vivo fate of TFA. They exhibited longer half-lives (t1/2), mean residence times (MRT), and time to peak concentration (Tmax), confirming that the formulation indeed achieved sustained-release objectives. Although FTA-LN-TSG exhibited the lowest peak concentration (Cmax), this precisely reflected its most stable and sustained release characteristics. The pharmacokinetic behavior featuring a lower peak concentration and prolonged duration mitigated toxicity risks associated with sharp plasma concentration swings39 and simultaneously maintained effective drug concentrations, thereby providing the foundation for an improved therapeutic window.

Finally, the most compelling evidence came from in vivo antitumor studies. In the MCF-7 tumor-bearing nude mouse model, the FTA-LN-TSG group achieved the highest tumor inhibition rate (64.42%), which was significantly superior to other groups. The oral FTA group was included as a reference, representing the traditional way of giving the drug in its free form, to compare and see if our local delivery strategy has any advantage in boosting tumor suppression and reducing systemic side effects. Histological analysis (HE staining) of the tumors revealed the largest area of tumor cell necrosis in this group, alongside the lowest positive expression rates for Ki67 and VEGFR2. This pathologically confirmed that FTA-LN-TSG not only most effectively induced tumor cell death but also most potently inhibited tumor cell proliferation and angiogenesis. Although FTA-LN-TSG given around the tumor shows stronger local tumor suppression, this way of giving it might not work for every clinical situation. For deep or metastatic breast lesions, local injection could be technically challenging. We still need to do more research, like further optimizing the formulations, precisely determining delivery parameters, and figuring out how to more effectively overcome the immunosuppressive environment in breast cancer. Furthermore, HE staining results of major organs demonstrated superior safety in the formulation groups, particularly the gel formulation. Overall, FTA-LN-TSG shows acceptable preliminary biosafety. These results confirmed that the strategy of locally retaining the drug at the tumor site effectively reduced exposure and potential damage to other systemic organs.40–42 This directly addresses the issue of local recurrence in breast cancer treatment caused by the limited selectivity of drugs and systemic side effects. In recent years, the clinical translational potential of this strategy has been supported by a number of studies.18,19,43 Although our study assessed basic biosafety parameters, including changes in body weight, organ indices, and histopathological examinations of major organs, the toxicity evaluation is still at an early stage. Future studies need to include longer observation periods and comprehensive pharmacokinetic analyses and Serum stability And assessments of hematology, serum biochemistry, and local injection site toxicity to fully establish the safety profile of FTA-LN-TSG. Lastly, we should admit that all the efficacy and safety data were obtained from immunodeficient mice xenograft models, which cannot fully replicate the complexity of the human tumor microenvironment. Also, before moving to clinical use, we still need pharmacokinetic studies in large animals and production processes that meet Good Manufacturing Practice standards.

Conclusion

Through a synergistic strategy of “liposome encapsulation” and “thermosensitive gel immobilization”, this study successfully constructed the FTA-LN-TSG drug delivery system for subcutaneous administration. This system achieved sustained-release drug delivery, comprehensive enhanced cellular effects (proliferation, migration, apoptosis, and cell cycle), and optimized pharmacokinetic behavior. Ultimately, both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated its significant superiority over the parent drug and single-formulation systems. In summary, FTA-LN-TSG demonstrated promising application prospects as an innovative breast cancer therapeutic delivery system, offering novel insights for future anticancer drug development.
