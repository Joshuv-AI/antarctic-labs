Stearoyl CoA desaturase 1 deficiency increases ferroptosis susceptibility in chicken embryonic liver cells
Authors: Shuo Wei, Ruihao Yu, Liuting Wu, Yanyan Miao, Ziqi Yang, Bojia Wei, Yingjie Wang, Gang Shu, Xiaoling Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of programmed cell death driven by lipid peroxidation. It is increasingly recognized as a contributor to liver cell injury. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is a rate-limiting enzyme in monounsaturated fatty acid synthesis. It plays a key role in maintaining lipid homeostasis and may affect cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis. However, it remains unclear whether SCD1 restrains ferroptosis-associated injury in chicken embryonic liver (CEL) cells. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the role of SCD1 in regulating ferroptosis susceptibility and cellular injury related to ferroptosis in CEL cells, with a focus on its effects on lipid metabolism, oxidative stress, and iron homeostasis. The results showed that SCD1 knockdown reduced intracellular lipid droplet area, triglyceride, and total cholesterol levels in CEL cells (P < 0.05). In addition, SCD1 knockdown induced mitochondrial ultrastructural changes associated with ferroptosis, including mitochondrial shrinkage, increased membrane density, and cristae disruption. It also aggravated oxidative stress, as shown by increased reactive oxygen species, oxidized glutathione, and malondialdehyde levels and decreased glutathione content (P < 0.05). SCD1 knockdown reduced cell viability, whereas oleic acid or ferrostatin-1 treatment partially reversed this decrease (P < 0.05). Moreover, SCD1 knockdown increased ACSL4 expression and decreased SLC7A11, GPX4, and Nrf2 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (P < 0.05). SCD1 knockdown further reduced mitochondrial membrane potential and increased lipid peroxidation, intracellular Fe²⁺ levels, and total iron content (P < 0.05). In contrast, SCD1 overexpression increased lipid accumulation, reduced reactive oxygen species levels, increased glutathione content, increased SLC7A11, GPX4, and Nrf2 expression, and decreased ACSL4 protein expression (P < 0.05) without significantly affecting ACSL4 mRNA expression. These findings suggest that SCD1 regulates the susceptibility of CEL cells to ferroptosis and exerts a protective effect by improving lipid metabolism, antioxidant defenses, and mitochondrial function, indicating that SCD1 is a key regulatory factor in maintaining the homeostasis and health of chicken liver.

Introduction

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and characterized by the excessive accumulation of phospholipid hydroperoxides (Stockwell, 2022; Xie et al., 2016). The liver plays a central role in maintaining systemic metabolic homeostasis, as it coordinates iron uptake, storage, and utilization, while simultaneously supporting active fatty acid synthesis, remodeling, and export (Ipsen et al., 2018; Wang and Babitt, 2018). This unique metabolic profile makes the liver cells vulnerable to oxidative membrane damage when intracellular iron levels rise or when antioxidant defense systems are compromised (Sui et al., 2024). Under such conditions, lipid peroxidation can propagate uncontrollably, ultimately disrupting membrane integrity and impairing cellular function (Chen et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2021). Therefore, factors that govern hepatic lipid remodeling and antioxidant capacity may play pivotal roles in determining hepatocyte susceptibility to ferroptosis.

Stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) is a key enzyme in fatty acid desaturation that catalyzes the conversion of saturated fatty acids to monounsaturated fatty acids (Flowers and Ntambi, 2008). It plays an important role in the synthesis and remodeling of triglycerides, cholesteryl esters, and membrane phospholipids (Ntambi and Miyazaki, 2004). SCD1 contributes to intracellular lipid storage and influences membrane lipid composition and susceptibility to oxidation (Shi et al., 2013). Recent studies have shown that SCD1 regulates cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis by promoting fatty acid desaturation, limiting the accumulation of lipids that are prone to peroxidation, and maintaining glutathione dependent antioxidant defense (Xuan et al., 2022). In contrast, SCD1 knockdown is associated with increased lipid peroxidation, redox imbalance, and altered expression of ferroptosis related markers, suggesting that SCD1 may act as an important molecular link between lipid metabolism and the regulation of ferroptosis (Sen et al., 2025).

Current evidence for the involvement of SCD1 in ferroptosis has been derived largely from mammalian studies (Luis et al., 2021; Tesfay et al., 2019). Avian lipid metabolism shows distinct physiological features, and the liver is the principal site of de novo lipogenesis in birds (Emami et al., 2020; Zaefarian et al., 2019). Avian hepatocytes may therefore be particularly sensitive to changes in lipid synthesis, oxidative stress (Emami et al., 2020), and iron homeostasis (Capelletti et al., 2020). However, whether SCD1 exerts a similar regulatory effect on ferroptosis in avian liver cells remains unclear. Chicken embryonic liver (CEL) cells exhibit characteristics of hepatocytes (Lee et al., 2013) and provide a suitable in vitro model for evaluating changes in lipid metabolism, oxidative injury, and cell fate after manipulation of gene expression. Therefore, CEL cells are suitable for examining whether SCD1 regulates ferroptosis susceptibility in avian liver cells.

In the present study, CEL cells were used to examine the role of SCD1 in ferroptosis susceptibility. SCD1 overexpression and knockdown models were established to evaluate changes in lipid accumulation, redox status, iron homeostasis, mitochondrial function, the expression of ferroptosis related markers, and cell viability. In addition, oleic acid and ferrostatin-1 intervention experiments were included to further define the changes associated with altered SCD1 expression. This study aimed to determine whether SCD1 participates in the regulation of ferroptosis susceptibility in CEL cells and to clarify its potential role in lipid metabolism, antioxidant defense, and iron homeostasis.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, experimental design, transfection, and treatment

CEL cells were procured from Shanghai Yaji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (YS2163C; Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were assigned to the following 5 groups: a non-transfected control group (NC), overexpression negative control (OE-NC), SCD1 overexpression (OE-SCD1), siRNA negative control (si-NC), and SCD1 knockdown (si-SCD1).

In rescue experiments, cells transfected with si-NC or si-SCD1 were subsequently treated with 100 μM oleic acid (OA; MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) or 10 μM ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1; MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA). OA and Fer-1 were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare stock solutions and were diluted in culture medium before treatment. The OA concentration was selected based on previous studies showing that exogenous OA at approximately 100 μM can protect cells against ferroptosis-related injury and that MUFAs, including OA, can promote a ferroptosis-resistant cell state (Mann et al., 2024). Fer-1 was used at 10 μM because this concentration is commonly used as an effective ferroptosis inhibitor in cell-based studies (Dixon et al., 2012; Yoshida et al., 2019).

For SCD1 overexpression, cells were transfected with an SCD1 overexpression plasmid (designated pcDNA3.1-SCD1; constructed by GenePharma, Shanghai, China) or the corresponding empty vector pcDNA3.1 using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For gene knockdown, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting SCD1 (si-SCD1) or negative control siRNA (si-NC), both synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China), using the same transfection reagent and general procedure. The si-SCD1 sequence was as follows: sense, 5′-CGAUACGUCUGGAGGAAUATT-3′; antisense, 5′-UAUUCCUCCAGACGUAUCGTT-3′.

After 24 h of transfection, cells were either harvested for subsequent assays or further treated with 100 μM OA, 10 μM Fer-1, or the corresponding DMSO vehicle for an additional 24 h before the CCK-8 assay.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells grown on coverslips were washed 2 times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation, the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min at room temperature and washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each.

Subsequently, the cells were blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 30 min at room temperature. After removal of the blocking solution, the cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-cytokeratin 18 (CK18) primary antibody diluted in antibody diluent. After incubation, the cells were washed 3 times with PBS for 5 min each. The appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody was then added and incubated for 50 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by three additional washes with PBS for 5 min each.

The nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. After washing 3 times with PBS, the coverslips were mounted with anti-fading mounting medium and observed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse C1; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Detailed information on the antibodies used for immunofluorescence staining is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from CEL cells using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed, and equal amounts of RNA were then reverse-transcribed into cDNA. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was then performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq (Takara, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Protein extraction and Western blot analysis

Cells were collected, washed 2 times with PBS, and lysed on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at a ratio of 1:100. The lysates were then centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were used for subsequent analysis. Protein concentrations were determined, and equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes.

After transfer, the membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibodies. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (ECL; APExBIO, Houston, TX, USA) and imaged with a gel documentation system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) and normalized to β-tubulin as the loading control. Detailed information on the antibodies used in this study is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Oil Red O staining

Following treatment, CEL cells were gently washed with PBS. A small volume of 60% isopropanol was added to cover the cells for 15–20 s and then removed. After brief air-drying, the cells were incubated with Oil Red O working solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The staining solution was then discarded, and the cells were briefly differentiated with 60% isopropanol for 3–5 s, followed by washing 3 times with distilled water for 5 min each. The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, rinsed with water, and blued, followed by a final wash. The stained cells were maintained in PBS, and images were captured using an Olympus light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Lipid droplet accumulation was quantified as the relative Oil Red O-positive area normalized to nuclear number. For each independent biological replicate, three different fields of view were selected and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Biochemical analyses in cell experiments

At the end of treatment, CEL cells were harvested for biochemical analyses. Cellular triglyceride (TG) and total cholesterol (TC) contents were measured using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) and normalized to total protein content. Total glutathione (T-GSH) and oxidized glutathione (GSSG) levels were determined using a T-GSH/GSSG Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China), and reduced glutathione (GSH) levels were calculated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured using a commercial assay kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Ferrous iron (Fe²⁺) and total iron contents were measured using an Iron and Ferrous Ion Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). All assays were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions, and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following treatment, CEL cells were harvested by trypsinization. The cell suspension was transferred to centrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 3 min. After removal of the supernatant, the cell pellet was gently resuspended in electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and fixed for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. The fixed samples were then processed using standard transmission electron microscopy procedures, including postfixation, dehydration, resin embedding, ultrathin sectioning, and staining. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and examined under a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI HT7800; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Flow cytometry analysis

Treated CEL cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and incubated with the corresponding probes according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using a Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using a JC-1 Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Lipid peroxidation was detected using a lipid peroxidation (LPO) assay kit (BODIPY 581/591 C11, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). After staining, the cells were analyzed using a flow cytometer (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and the data were processed using CytExpert software. ROS and lipid peroxidation levels were quantified as relative fluorescence intensity, whereas mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated as the JC-1 aggregate/monomer ratio.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed after the indicated treatments using a CCK-8 kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Detection of intracellular labile Fe²⁺ by fluorescent probe

For detection of intracellular labile Fe²⁺, CEL cells were washed with PBS and incubated with FerroOrange working solution (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After incubation, the cells were washed gently with PBS to remove excess probe. Fluorescence images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ti laser scanning confocal microscope equipped with a C2 confocal system (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons between 2 groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The experimental unit was an independent cell culture well or independent cell culture sample, depending on the assay. Each experiment was performed with at least 3 independent biological replicates.

Discussion

Previous studies have suggested that SCD1 protects mammalian cells against injury related to ferroptosis (Luis et al., 2021; Tesfay et al., 2019; Xuan et al., 2022). However, whether this protective role is conserved in avian liver cells remains unclear. In the present study, knockdown of SCD1 increased the susceptibility of CEL cells to ferroptosis, as reflected by impaired cell viability and coordinated changes in lipid metabolism, redox balance, iron homeostasis, and markers related to ferroptosis. In contrast, SCD1 overexpression generally shifted lipid accumulation, antioxidant status, and selected markers related to ferroptosis in the opposite direction, although some downstream injury indicators were more responsive to SCD1 loss than to SCD1 overexpression. These findings suggest that endogenous SCD1 is important for maintaining lipid metabolic balance, redox balance, and iron homeostasis in CEL cells. Its knockdown may therefore create a cellular state that is more permissive to ferroptosis (Berndt et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2021). Therefore, the following discussion focuses on how SCD1 related changes in lipid metabolism may contribute to oxidative stress, iron dysregulation, and altered ferroptosis defense in CEL cells.

The present study showed that changes in SCD1 expression markedly affected lipid storage in CEL cells. This observation provides a metabolic starting point for understanding why SCD1 knockdown increased ferroptosis susceptibility. As a key enzyme in monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA) synthesis, SCD1 promotes the formation of MUFA enriched neutral lipids and membrane lipids (Dobrzyn and Ntambi, 2004; Ntambi and Miyazaki, 2004). SCD1 overexpression increased lipid droplet accumulation and TG and TC contents, whereas SCD1 knockdown reduced these parameters, indicating that SCD1 is involved in the regulation of lipid storage capacity in CEL cells. This finding is relevant to ferroptosis, as lipid peroxidation is influenced not only by total lipid abundance but also by lipid composition and remodeling (Kim et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022). Previous studies have shown that SCD1 activity contributes to ferroptosis resistance, and MUFAs, including oleic acid, can inhibit ferroptosis by altering membrane lipid composition and reducing phospholipids containing oxidizable polyunsaturated fatty acids (Luis et al., 2021; Magtanong et al., 2019; Mann et al., 2024; Tesfay et al., 2019). In addition, recent studies have highlighted that ferroptosis sensitivity is strongly influenced by cellular lipid synthesis, storage, and remodeling (Wenzel et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2022). Collectively, these findings suggest that SCD1 may influence ferroptosis susceptibility by linking fatty acid desaturation and lipid storage to membrane resistance against lipid peroxidation. This effect may not simply depend on changs in total lipid abundance. This lipid centered mechanism provides a basis for the subsequent redox imbalance and cell injury observed after SCD1 knockdown.

Consistent with the link between lipid metabolism and oxidative membrane damage, SCD1 knockdown resulted in higher ROS and MDA levels, decreased GSH content, increased GSSG content, and lower cell viability compared with the si-NC group. TEM further revealed mitochondria that were smaller in size, exhibited higher membrane electron density, and diminished or absent cristae, which are features typically associated with ferroptotic damage rather than classical apoptosis (Liu et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2016). These observations suggest that SCD1 knockdown weakens cellular redox buffering capacity and promotes oxidative injury related to ferroptosis (Conrad et al., 2018; Tang et al., 2020). In contrast, SCD1 overexpression reduced ROS levels and increased GSH content, although not all downstream injury indicators were significantly changed in the overexpression model. Oleic acid partially restored cell viability in the si-SCD1 group, consistent with previous studies showing that exogenous MUFAs can suppress ferroptosis by decreasing the oxidizability of cellular lipid pools (Mortensen et al., 2023). Likewise, ferrostatin-1 also partially reversed the loss of viability caused by SCD1 knockdown. Ferrostatin-1 was originally identified as a potent ferroptosis inhibitor and remains widely used as a radical trapping agent (Dixon et al., 2012). These rescue effects support the involvement of both altered lipid metabolism and oxidative injury related to ferroptosis in the cellular damage caused by SCD1 knockdown (Miotto et al., 2019; Scarpellini et al., 2023). However, in the present rescue experiments, OA and Fer-1 were evaluated mainly using the CCK-8 assay. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as evidence of partial functional recovery rather than direct evidence that all changes related to ferroptosis were reversed. Future studies should examine additional indicators, such as ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and GPX4 expression, after OA or Fer-1 treatment. Notably, neither OA nor Fer-1 completely normalized viability, suggesting that SCD1 knockdown may induce additional metabolic stress beyond pathways directly related to ferroptosis.

To further explain the increased vulnerability of CEL cells after SCD1 knockdown, we examined key regulators of ferroptosis sensitivity and antioxidant defense. Our molecular data provide further evidence supporting this interpretation. SCD1 knockdown increased ACSL4 expression and decreased GPX4, Nrf2, and SLC7A11 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. ACSL4 is widely recognized as a key determinant of ferroptosis sensitivity, as it facilitates the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids, thereby increasing the pool of membrane lipids that are prone to peroxidation (Doll et al., 2016). Furthermore, oxidized phosphatidylethanolamine species have been identified as key signals for ferroptotic death, thereby further linking phospholipid composition with ferroptosis susceptibility (Doll et al., 2019; Kagan et al., 2016). In contrast, GPX4 and the SLC7A11/GSH axis form a central defense system against ferroptosis by detoxifying phospholipid hydroperoxides (Sen et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2014). In addition, Nrf2 has been shown to exert protective effects against ferroptosis, at least in part through regulation of SLC7A11 and related antioxidant pathways (Dong et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022). Thus, SCD1 knockdown appears to shift CEL cells in two connected ways: by increasing ACSL4 related generation of oxidation prone lipid substrates and by weakening SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4 and Nrf2 mediated antioxidant defense. This molecular pattern provides a mechanistic bridge from the lipid and redox changes described above to the mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and iron imbalance observed after SCD1 knockdown.

Consistent with the molecular shift toward increased ferroptosis sensitivity, changes in mitochondrial function, lipid peroxidation, and iron homeostasis further supported the vulnerable phenotype observed after SCD1 knockdown. In the si-SCD1 group, the decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential indicated mitochondrial dysfunction, while increased C11-BODIPY oxidation provided further evidence of enhanced lipid peroxidation. These changes were accompanied by increased intracellular labile Fe2+ signals together with elevated Fe2+ and total iron contents in the si-SCD1 group. Because ferrous iron is a key catalyst of lipid peroxidation, expansion of the labile iron pool may amplify membrane lipid peroxidation and thereby reinforce cellular injury related to ferroptosis (Dixon, Lemberg, Lamprecht, Skouta, Zaitsev, Gleason, Patel, Bauer, Cantley, Yang, Morrison and Stockwell, 2012; Ru et al., 2024). The mechanism underlying this iron accumulation remains unclear. It may involve altered iron uptake and storage through transferrin receptor 1 (TFRC) and ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), impaired iron export through ferroportin (FPN), or increased nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) mediated ferritinophagy (Mancias et al., 2014), all of which could expand the labile iron pool (Chen et al., 2020). However, TFRC, FTH1, FPN, and NCOA4 were not examined in the present study, and this mechanism requires further investigation. Dysregulated iron homeostasis has also been implicated in hepatocellular ferroptosis, in which iron accumulation, ROS production, and membrane damage act together to promote liver cell injury (Mao et al., 2020). Together, these findings indicate that SCD1 knockdown links mitochondrial dysfunction, lipid peroxidation, and iron accumulation into a cellular state that is more permissive to ferroptosis. The effects of SCD1 manipulation were more pronounced in the knockdown model than in the overexpression model. The absence of significant changes in mitochondrial membrane potential, lipid peroxidation, Fe²⁺, and total iron in the OE-SCD1 group may indicate that basal SCD1 expression is sufficient to preserve mitochondrial function, limit excessive lipid peroxidation, and maintain iron homeostasis under normal culture conditions. Therefore, further SCD1 overexpression may have limited effects on these downstream injury indicators in the absence of a strong ferroptotic stimulus, whereas SCD1 knockdown may disrupt this basal protective state and more readily lead to measurable cellular stress.

Taken together, these findings suggest that SCD1 regulates ferroptosis susceptibility in CEL cells by coordinating lipid metabolism, redox balance, mitochondrial function, and iron homeostasis. From a broader perspective, our findings extend previous evidence linking SCD1 to ferroptosis susceptibility from mammalian systems to an avian liver cell model (Luis et al., 2021; Tesfay et al., 2019). Given that the liver is the major site of de novo lipogenesis in poultry, SCD1 activity may be particularly important for coordinating fatty acid desaturation, lipid remodeling, and stress adaptation in avian liver cells (Liu et al., 2020; Zaefarian et al., 2019). In this context, the present study suggests that SCD1 is not only a lipid metabolic enzyme but also a protective factor that supports redox balance and iron homeostasis when CEL cells are exposed to stress related to ferroptosis. These findings may help explain how disturbances in fatty acid desaturation influence the vulnerability of avian liver cells to oxidative membrane damage. This interpretation is consistent with growing evidence that ferroptosis contributes to liver cell injury through coordinated disturbances in lipid metabolism, antioxidant defense, and iron handling (Hemmati et al., 2025; Jaeschke and Ramachandran, 2024; Wu et al., 2024).

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, because this study was performed in a CEL cell model, further validation in primary chicken hepatocytes and in vivo models is needed to confirm the physiological relevance of these findings. Second, although our data support increased ferroptosis susceptibility after SCD1 knockdown, lipidomic profiling was not performed; therefore, we could not directly define how SCD1 affects phospholipid fatty acid composition or the relative abundance of oxidation prone lipid species. Third, although possible mechanisms underlying iron accumulation were considered, key iron metabolism regulators, including TFRC, FTH1, FPN, and NCOA4, were not examined in the present work. In addition, this study focused mainly on the ACSL4 and SLC7A11/GSH/GPX4/Nrf2 axis, and other ferroptosis defense systems, such as FSP1, were not examined. Finally, although OA and Fer-1 partially rescued the loss of cell viability, these rescue experiments were evaluated mainly by cell viability assays. Additional indicators, such as ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and GPX4 expression, should be examined after rescue treatment in future studies. Despite these limitations, our results support the conclusion that SCD1 knockdown promotes a cellular state characterized by disturbed lipid metabolism, iron accumulation, impaired antioxidant defense, and increased susceptibility to oxidative membrane damage, thereby making CEL cells more susceptible to ferroptosis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that SCD1 is an important modulator of ferroptosis susceptibility in CEL cells. Reduced SCD1 expression was associated with reduced lipid storage, impaired antioxidant defense, increased lipid peroxidation, disrupted iron homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and decreased cell viability. In contrast, SCD1 overexpression increased lipid accumulation and altered several redox indicators and markers related to ferroptosis in the opposite direction. The partial rescue of cell viability by oleic acid and ferrostatin-1 further supports the involvement of altered lipid metabolism and processes related to ferroptosis in the effects of SCD1 knockdown. These findings suggest that fatty acid desaturation mediated by SCD1 may contribute to maintaining lipid metabolic balance, redox balance, and iron homeostasis in avian liver cells, thereby providing a basis for understanding cellular injury related to ferroptosis in avian liver cells.
