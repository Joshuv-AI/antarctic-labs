Introduction

Health care is shifting from a traditional disease-centred model to a person-centred approach, in which the involvement of family caregivers is crucial for optimizing care-recipient outcomes (Jeppesen et al., 2024). Globally, population ageing and the increasing prevalence of chronic conditions have substantially increased the demand for long-term care, much of which depends on informal and often untrained caregivers. In Europe, for example, informal caregivers provide nearly 80% of long-term care (Eurocarers, 2023), with similar patterns across North America and Asia due to strong family traditions and limited formal care services. These caregivers manage care recipients’ medical, emotional, and daily living needs while simultaneously coordinating household, financial, and family responsibilities—tasks that require substantial adaptability and resilience.

Insufficient adaptability to the caregiving role may adversely affect both care-recipient outcomes and caregiver well-being, leading to increased burden and reduced quality of life. Mausbach et al. (2012) demonstrated that self-efficacy and related mastery constructs significantly influence caregivers’ psychological distress, underscoring the importance of incorporating modifiable psychosocial factors in interventions aimed at reducing caregiver stress. Building on this evidence, recent studies have introduced the theoretical construct of caregiver activation (Bratches et al., 2021). Unlike self-efficacy, which is primarily confidence-related (Fürtjes et al., 2023), and self-management, which is structured around individuals’ active responsibility for managing their own medical, role, and emotional consequences of chronic conditions (Van de Velde et al., 2019), caregiver activation is a broader, multidimensional and dynamic process. It describes how family members and friends who provide care become prepared and confident in managing care recipients’ health needs (Sadak et al., 2016; Skeens et al., 2023).

Compared with related concepts such as health literacy, caregiver activation encompasses broader psychological and behavioral domains, including confidence, skills, beliefs, and willingness to act, making it a potentially stronger target for intervention. The concept has been applied across oncology (Sadak et al., 2016), dementia care (Bakker et al., 2022), paediatric transplantation (Fauer et al., 2019), and autism spectrum disorder (McGrew et al., 2024). Greater caregiver activation has been associated with reduced caregiver stress (McGrew et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2022) and fewer acute care events among care recipients (Sadak et al., 2016), underscoring its importance for dyadic well-being.

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into care delivery, caregivers are expected to interpret health-related data and respond appropriately to technology-supported recommendations. These emerging responsibilities further heighten the need for caregiver activation, making conceptual clarity especially important in artificial intelligence-enhanced care environments.

Despite growing scholarly interest, consensus regarding the core components of caregiver activation remains limited. Existing studies have emphasized different elements, including knowledge, skills, and confidence (Crossman et al., 2020), beliefs (Ruble et al., 2018), or willingness to act (Hibbard and Greene, 2013). Some researchers have also conceptualized caregiver activation as comprising dual dimensions, such as active action and knowledge/collaboration (McGrew et al., 2024). These inconsistencies hinder measurement development, limit intervention design, and obstruct translation into nursing practice. Concept clarification is therefore necessary to advance both theoretical understanding and practical application.

From a nursing perspective, conceptual clarity is particularly important because nursing practice relies on robust conceptual frameworks to guide assessment and evidence-based support for family caregivers. Accordingly, this study uses Walker and Avant’s (2005) eight-step concept analysis method to examine caregiver activation and identify its defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences, thereby providing a foundation for future research and nursing practice.

Data sources

Concept analysis method

This study adopted the concept analysis method developed by Walker and Avant (2005), which aims to reduce conceptual ambiguity and clarify definitions, thereby minimizing the risk of conceptual misapplication in nursing theory and research. According to Walker and Avant (2005), concept analysis proceeds through eight systematic stages: (1) selecting the concept; (2) determining the aim and purpose of the analysis; (3) identifying the uses (definitions) of the concept; (4) determining the defining attributes; (5) constructing a model case that demonstrates all identified attributes; (6) identifying related, borderline, and contrary cases that either partially represent or contradict the concept; (7) identifying the antecedents (precursors) and consequences (outcomes) of the concept; and (8) defining empirical referents that illustrate how the concept can be observed and examined in real-world contexts.

Data sources and search strategy

To identify relevant literature, eleven electronic databases were searched from inception to October 2025: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Ovid, the Cochrane Library, the Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, the Chinese Science and Technology Journal Database, the Chinese Medical Database, and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database. Several dictionaries were also consulted, including the Cambridge Dictionary, Merriam-Webster, the Collins English Dictionary, and the Modern Chinese Dictionary (China Academy of Social Sciences, Institute of Linguistics).

The search terms included “caregiver activation,” “parent activation,” “caregiver participation,” “caregiver engagement,” and “caregiver empowerment.” These terms were combined using Boolean operators (“AND,” “OR”) and wildcards (“*”) to increase both sensitivity and specificity. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) studies that provided definitions, characteristics, conditions, or attributes related to caregiver activation; (2) publications written in English or Chinese; (3) peer-reviewed articles; and (4) studies with accessible full texts. In addition, the reference lists of the included studies were manually screened to identify potentially relevant sources.

To ensure methodological rigour, two researchers independently conducted the searches and assessed study eligibility on the basis of title, abstract, and full-text review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third investigator.

Study selection

A total of 19,572 records were retrieved. After removing duplicates, 11,671 records remained for preliminary screening. Three additional studies were identified through manual searches of reference lists. Following title and abstract screening and full-text evaluation according to the inclusion criteria, 51 studies were included in the final concept analysis. Results: overview of the concept

Selecting a concept (Step 1)

As healthcare continues to evolve from a disease-centred model to a person-centred model, the integration of family caregivers has become indispensable. The emerging concept of caregiver activation provides a valuable framework for understanding and supporting this shift by emphasizing caregivers’ proactive engagement across the continuum of care. Through its focus on preparedness and adaptability, caregiver activation represents a critical leverage point for reducing caregiver burden and enhancing quality of life.

Despite its increasing application across diverse contexts—such as oncology, dementia, and chronic illness management—there remains no consensus on the definition or core attributes of caregiver activation. Furthermore, parental activation is viewed in this study as a specific manifestation of caregiver activation within pediatric care and is therefore included within the analytical scope. Accordingly, this concept analysis seeks to clarify the essential characteristics and defining properties of caregiver activation to strengthen its theoretical and practical utility.

Determining the aim and purpose of the analysis (Step 2)

This study aims to elucidate the defining attributes, antecedents, and consequences of caregiver activation, thereby establishing a theoretical basis for the development of interventions that enhance caregivers’ proactive engagement in care. In doing so, the study seeks to improve the conceptual clarity of caregiver activation and provide a foundation for its application in both research and clinical practice.

Identifying the uses of the concept (Step 3)

Caregiver

The Collins English Dictionary (2025b) defines a caregiver as "someone who is responsible for looking after another person", such as an individual who is ill, disabled, or very young. In the academic literature, the term generally refers to informal caregivers—typically relatives, friends, or neighbors of care recipients (Geerlings et al., 2023). This study included caregivers of both adult and paediatric care recipients, including biological parents, adoptive parents, and stepparents, as well as other primary caregivers such as grandparents (Mirza et al., 2018). These individuals usually provide care without remuneration and often lack formal health or nursing training (Sadak et al., 2015).

Activation

According to the Collins English Dictionary (2025a), activation means “to make active or capable of action.” Within healthcare, the concept originates from patient activation, which Hibbard and Greene (2013) described as a foundational precursor to patient engagement and self-management. Conceptually, activation represents a multidimensional construct involving active participation in health management through the acquisition and application of relevant knowledge, skills, confidence, and behaviors.

Caregiver activation in the literature

Caregiver activation is defined as a multidimensional construct encompassing caregivers’ willingness and ability to prepare for, meet, and manage care recipients’ care needs (Robertson et al., 2024). Its key components include knowledge, skills, confidence, and attitudes (DeCamp et al., 2019). Bakker et al. (2022) further emphasized that caregiver activation is a modifiable construct and may serve as a pivotal mediator in interventions aimed at improving caregiver well-being and care outcomes.

Furthermore, caregiver activation has been conceptualized as a dynamic process whereby caregivers progressively prepare to assume health management responsibilities throughout the care recipient's disease trajectory (DeCamp et al., 2019). This process typically involves two dimensions: (1) active action (such as problem-solving, implementation of home-based treatment) and (2) knowledge & collaboration (such as medication understanding, effective communication with healthcare professionals) (McGrew et al., 2024). Existing evidence underscores that caregiver activation is not a static state but rather a complex, evolving construct shaped by both individual and contextual factors.

Determining the defining attributes (Step 4)

Care capabilities

Care capabilities refer to caregivers’ comprehensive capacity to deliver holistic care while simultaneously maintaining their own health and well-being. This attribute encompasses two interrelated dimensions: care-management competence and self-care competence. The former reflects caregivers’ mastery and application of essential knowledge and skills in disease management, condition monitoring, emergency response, lifestyle modification, communication with healthcare providers, and coordination of multiple caregiving roles, as well as their ability to integrate and translate these competencies into practice across diverse clinical contexts (Mundal et al., 2023; Sadak et al., 2015). The latter highlights caregivers’ proactive engagement in managing their own health through emotional regulation, stress management, health maintenance, social support seeking, and participation in leisure activities (Merluzzi et al., 2011). Accordingly, effective caregiver activation requires the integration of professional and self-care competences, emphasizing that sustaining high-quality care provision depends on caregivers’ balanced development of professional proficiency and personal well-being.

Care autonomy

Care autonomy refers to caregivers’ ability to initiate, organize, and independently execute care-related tasks. This attribute includes two dimensions: (1) the ability to perform caregiving operations without external intervention and (2) the ability to discern when professional assistance or collaboration is needed. Autonomy also encompasses caregivers’ voluntary willingness and motivation to engage in care at varying levels of participation. Collectively, these elements represent a key aspect of caregiver activation, reflecting both independence in action and judgment in care decision-making.

Care resilience

Care resilience reflects caregivers’ psychological adaptability and sustained capacity to provide care under chronic or recurring stress. For individuals with long-term or lifelong illnesses, caregiving often extends across years or decades (Yu et al., 2023). For instance, caregivers of children with developmental disabilities typically begin their role at symptom onset and continue throughout the care recipient’s lifespan (Smith et al., 2020). Prolonged exposure to physical exhaustion, financial strain, and social burdens may eventually compromise even highly motivated caregivers, leading to care disruptions (Wong Chung et al., 2020). Thus, resilience—the ability to maintain care performance and psychological stability despite persistent challenges—is a vital attribute of caregiver activation.

Self-efficacy in care provision

Self-efficacy refers to caregivers’ confidence in their ability to deliver competent and effective care. Highly activated caregivers perceive themselves as capable of meeting care needs across multiple domains (Ruble et al., 2018). Existing studies have primarily emphasized caregivers’ confidence in managing health behaviors, such as disease monitoring and lifestyle interventions (Stein et al., 2017; Sadak et al., 2015). However, Dingley et al. (2017) highlighted that confidence in interprofessional communication, recognition of caregivers’ collaborative role value, and ability to navigate interdisciplinary interactions are equally important dimensions of activation. Among parental caregivers, confidence in making educational and developmental decisions has also been identified as a crucial component (Pérez Jolles et al., 2017). Overall, self-efficacy in care provision enhances caregivers’ perceived competence and promotes proactive engagement in the caregiving process.

Identifying a model case (Step 5)

In this step, a model case is presented to fully illustrate all the defining attributes of caregiver activation in nursing. This case exemplifies how the core attributes—care capabilities, care autonomy, care resilience, and self-efficacy in care provision—manifest in real-world caregiving practice.

Case example

Z.L., a housewife, suddenly faced a crisis when her husband was diagnosed with an acute cerebral infarction. Although she initially felt anxious and overwhelmed, she quickly composed herself and believed that by acquiring proper knowledge and skills, she could contribute to his recovery (self-efficacy in care provision). On the very day of diagnosis, she sought guidance from the physician about the disease and rehabilitation process, carefully recorded key points and asked specific questions such as, “What diet should be followed?” and “When can activities safely begin?” She subsequently verified details with nurses and, when necessary, requested demonstrations. By the time of admission, she was already able to monitor fever, limb movement, and swallowing as instructed, documenting her observations diligently and seeking professional help whenever uncertainties arose (care autonomy).

Drawing on prior experience caring for her mother, together with information obtained from lectures and reference materials, she gradually developed a foundation of practical caregiving knowledge, including complication prevention and nutritional management. However, she soon realized that her husband’s condition was far more complex and required sustained effort. Consequently, she actively engaged with healthcare professionals, raised questions, confirmed her understanding, and promptly applied newly acquired knowledge in practice, thereby strengthening her care capabilities.

Recognizing the long-term nature of her husband’s illness, she anticipated that caregiving would become a prolonged and exhausting journey. At times, she feared that she would not be able to endure the sustained physical and emotional strain. Nevertheless, she reminded herself of the importance of preserving her own health and emotional stability. She deliberately allocated time for short rest breaks, sought occasional support from relatives, and practiced relaxation strategies to maintain her well-being (care capabilities). Although she frequently experienced anxiety and fatigue, she consciously adjusted her mindset, gradually adapted to the ongoing demands of caregiving, and cultivated care resilience.

After three years of consistent effort, her husband’s condition improved from complete immobility to partial independence in activities of daily living. Although Z.L. encountered moments of doubt throughout the process, her husband’s progress reinforced her initial beliefs and further strengthened her confidence in her caregiving ability (self-efficacy in care provision).

Identifying related, borderline, and contrary cases (Step 6)

Related case

A related case demonstrates similarities to caregiver activation but differs in its essential attributes. One closely related concept is caregiver empowerment (Alghamdi et al., 2022).

L. M., a daughter caring for her father after a stroke, collaborates closely with a community nurse and a physiotherapist who visit regularly. At the beginning of each visit, L.M. is encouraged to share her observations about her father’s progress and voice her concerns, which are acknowledged respectfully (mutually beneficial relationships). During the sessions, she learns rehabilitation exercises and medication routines directly from professionals and applies these skills at home with increasing confidence (knowledge and skills).

When care plans are discussed, L.M. occasionally declines recommendations she considers impractical—such as intensive exercise sessions during her father’s most fatigued hours. Instead, she proposes alternative routines that better fit his daily rhythms. Her suggestions are taken seriously and incorporated into the plan, ensuring that decisions balance clinical expertise with the family’s lived experience (self-determination and shared decision-making). In these moments, L.M. perceives her input as shaping the direction of care, demonstrating her influence over the process (sharing of social power).

Through this collaboration, L.M. views herself not only as her father’s helper but also as a trusted partner in his recovery, with her decisions directly steering his care. While this case illustrates empowerment and overlapping elements such as knowledge, confidence, and decision-making, it does not encompass the full multidimensional scope of caregiver activation.

Borderline case

A borderline case demonstrates certain attributes of caregiver activation but lacks others, thereby reflecting partial activation with evident limitations.

L.J., a retired man with hypertension, faced a major challenge when his wife was severely injured in a car accident and left permanently disabled. Although initially overwhelmed, he soon composed himself and recognized his caregiving responsibility. He actively searched for information online, sought guidance from professionals, and gradually developed confidence in his ability to provide care, maintaining optimism about the future (self-efficacy in care provision).

Following his wife’s discharge from hospital, L.J. provided round-the-clock care, mastered essential caregiving skills, and independently managed tasks such as feeding, repositioning, massage, and toileting (care capabilities; care autonomy). He even refused external assistance, firmly believing that “no one else can be as attentive as I am.”

Over time, however, the intensity and persistence of caregiving demands led him to neglect his own health. He frequently skipped meals, slept fewer than five hours per night, forgot to take his antihypertensive medication, and refused regular blood pressure monitoring (limited care capabilities). Under sustained pressure, he failed to develop effective coping strategies. Consequently, his physical condition deteriorated, eventually resulting in hospitalization for uncontrolled hypertension (insufficient care resilience).

This case illustrates partial caregiver activation. Although L.J. demonstrated confidence, caregiving skills, and autonomy, his inadequate self-care and limited resilience ultimately undermined his overall level of activation.

Contrary case

A contrary case represents a situation in which the essential attributes of caregiver activation are absent.

J.L.’s three-year-old daughter was diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder accompanied by behavioural difficulties. J.L. left her job to provide full-time care but regarded caregiving as an unwilling sacrifice rather than an active responsibility. Despite repeated explanations from healthcare professionals, she found the information too complex, quickly forgot instructions, and rarely asked questions (lack of care capabilities). At home, she became overwhelmed during her daughter’s behavioural outbursts, relied heavily on her husband for support, and postponed training activities unless repeatedly reminded to complete them (absence of care autonomy).

As her daughter grew older, J.L. became increasingly fearful of school rejection and doubted her own ability to manage interventions effectively, leading her to avoid communication with teachers and professionals (limited care capabilities). Over time, she became physically and emotionally exhausted, neglected her own health needs, and lived with persistent depressive symptoms (lack of care capabilities). Even after several years, she continued to lack confidence, frequently rejected new intervention strategies by saying, “I cannot do it,” and remained passive and hopeless regarding her caregiving role (low self-efficacy and poor care resilience).

This case exemplifies the absence of caregiver activation, illustrating how the lack of its defining attributes may result in ineffective caregiving and diminished caregiver well-being.

Identifying the antecedents (Step 7)

The antecedents of caregiver activation can be conceptualized as a series of interrelated conditions that collectively necessitate caregivers’ proactive engagement in care provision. At the macro level, demographic transitions—such as population aging, the increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, and the shift toward family- and community-based healthcare—have intensified reliance on informal caregiving. Within this context, four primary antecedents emerge: disease-driven care-recipient dependency, caregivers’ central role in care recipient–clinician collaboration, insufficient caregiving proficiency, and the persistent physical and psychological burden of care. These conditions not only establish the contextual foundation for caregiving but also act as catalysts for caregivers’ transition from passive role acceptance to active participation, thereby triggering caregiver activation. Care-recipient dependency

During the course of diagnosis and treatment, care recipients frequently experience physical or psychological impairments due to disease pathology or treatment side effects, leading to increased dependence on primary caregivers. Although nursing aims to preserve care-recipient autonomy, responsibilities related to daily living assistance and disease management are often transferred to caregivers (Dingley et al., 2017). Such dependency encompasses both emotional needs (such as psychological reassurance) and physiological needs (such as assistance with activities of daily living and treatment adherence) (Pennarola et al., 2015).

This dependency is particularly prominent among pediatric care recipients with developmental immaturity (McGrew et al., 2024), individuals with cognitive impairment (Geerlings et al., 2023), and those with mental or behavioral disorders (Green et al., 2019). Given these populations’ limited self-care capacity, caregivers are often required to provide continuous and comprehensive support, compelling them to adopt a more active caregiving role. Therefore, care-recipient dependency constitutes a fundamental antecedent of caregiver activation.

Core role of caregiver collaboration

Caregivers serve as essential intermediaries between care recipients and healthcare professionals, playing a pivotal role in disease management and communication (Mundal et al., 2023). In team-based care models, caregivers’ involvement is crucial for establishing treatment goals, ensuring adherence, and promoting shared decision-making (Dingley et al., 2017). However, communication and coordination gaps between caregivers and clinicians remain common, partly because of the under-recognition of caregivers as integral members of the healthcare team.

Addressing this issue requires systematic efforts to value, empower, and integrate caregivers into the medical decision-making process (Sadak et al., 2015). Consequently, the need to establish effective caregiver–clinician partnerships directly motivates caregivers to engage more actively in healthcare collaboration, representing an important antecedent of caregiver activation.

Insufficient caregiving proficiency

Despite their central role in care provision, many caregivers lack adequate preparation and confidence in managing complex caregiving tasks. Common challenges include fragmented access to health information (Bratches et al., 2021), limited communication with healthcare professionals (Sadak et al., 2015), and insufficient hands-on skills for home-based care (Dingley et al., 2017). Moreover, the increasing use of digital technologies and artificial intelligence-assisted tools in home monitoring and care coordination requires caregivers to interpret digital outputs and manage technology-supported tasks, often exceeding their existing skill levels (Borna et al., 2024).

These deficiencies necessitate not only external support but also an internal shift among caregivers, prompting them to actively seek knowledge, develop practical and digital competencies, and strengthen confidence in their caregiving roles. Therefore, insufficient caregiving proficiency functions as a critical antecedent driving caregiver activation.

Caregiver burden

Extensive studies (Geerlings et al., 2023; Robertson et al., 2024; Dingley et al., 2017) have documented the dual pressures faced by caregivers, including physical exhaustion, emotional strain, and psychosocial challenges. On the one hand, caregiving demands often lead individuals to prioritize care-recipient well-being at the expense of their own health (Dingley et al., 2017). On the other hand, the mismatch between care intensity and personal capacity results in cumulative stress, leading to sleep disturbances, depressive symptoms, and deterioration of physical health (Bakker et al., 2022). Economic hardship, reduced social connections, and disrupted career trajectories further exacerbate these burdens (Bakker et al., 2022). Confronting such dilemmas compels caregivers to adopt coping strategies, maintain resilience, and mobilize internal and external resources—hallmarks of caregiver activation (Dingley et al., 2017).

Identifying the consequences (Step 7)

The consequences of caregiver activation can be observed across multiple domains, including care-recipient outcomes, caregiver well-being, and caregiving practices. Existing evidence suggests that higher caregiver activation is associated with improved health service utilization, enhanced caregiver well-being, and more effective care management practices.

Improved access to health services

Caregiver activation may improve health service utilization, as reflected most clearly in mental health service use in paediatric caregiving contexts (Pérez Jolles et al., 2017). Specifically, activated caregivers are more likely to facilitate children’s access to a broader range of outpatient mental health services and specialized professional support (Thomas et al., 2020). Moreover, caregivers with higher activation levels act as important facilitators of effective mental health interventions (Bode et al., 2016), engaging more proactively with healthcare providers and thereby promoting continuity and appropriateness of care (Stein et al., 2017).

Improved quality of life of caregivers

Higher caregiver activation is associated with improvements in caregivers’ physical and psychological well-being. Geerlings et al. (2023) reported that highly activated caregivers experience fewer negative self-evaluations and lower levels of self-blame. Greater activation has also been associated with reduced caregiving burden, fewer depressive symptoms, enhanced social well-being (Bakker et al., 2022), and lower stress levels (Ruble et al., 2018). Although evidence linking activation to physical health outcomes remains limited, emerging studies suggest that higher activation may contribute to improved vitality, resilience, and overall health status among caregivers.

Enhanced care management practices

Higher caregiver activation is associated with enhanced care management practices across multiple domains (Wittenberg et al., 2024). These practices include greater disease awareness and sensitivity, earlier initiation of help-seeking behaviours (Richmond et al., 2022), and more timely diagnosis and treatment (Hamp et al., 2023). Activated caregivers also demonstrate more proactive disease management, stronger advocacy for care recipients’ rights, and more effective responses to evolving care needs.

Thomas et al. (2017) further reported that parents with lower baseline activation derived the greatest benefit from targeted interventions, underscoring the potential value of tailored strategies for improving caregiver activation and optimizing caregiving outcomes (Mirza et al., 2018).

Defining empirical referents (Step 8)

Empirical referents translate the concept of caregiver activation into measurable indicators. The Parent–Patient Activation Measure is a pediatric caregiver–specific adaptation of the Patient Activation Measure developed by Pennarola et al. (2012). Nonetheless, its applicability is constrained by limited cross-cultural transferability and inconsistent support for the original four-factor structure in some samples (Mundal et al., 2023). To address context-specific needs, derivative versions have been proposed, including the Parent-Patient Activation Measure for Mental Health for mental/behavioral health contexts (Green et al., 2019) and the Parent Activation Measure for Developmental Disabilities for developmental disability settings (Ruble et al., 2018). Beyond pediatric caregiving, the Caregiver-Patient Activation Measure captures caregivers’ knowledge, skills, and confidence in chronic disease management. In addition, Managing Your Loved One’s Health, developed by Borson et al. (2018), provides a complementary approach by assessing activation among family caregivers of individuals living with severe chronic illness.

Discussion

This study utilized Walker and Avant’s concept analysis framework to systematically clarify the defining attributes of caregiver activation, namely care capabilities, care autonomy, care resilience, and self-efficacy in care provision. These attributes suggest that caregiver activation should be understood not merely as a collection of caregiving behaviours but also as an underlying state of readiness that enables such behaviours. From this perspective, activation extends beyond the initiation of caregiving tasks; rather, a high level of activation may emerge even before caregiving formally begins. This anticipatory readiness reflects both a psychological transition—from an ordinary individual to a caregiver—and a gradual enhancement of confidence and caregiving competence. The possibility that activation may exist prior to caregiving practice further suggests that it functions not only as an outcome of role performance but also as an intrinsic motivator that drives continuous learning, adaptation, and engagement.

Previous studies (Mundal et al., 2023; Borson et al., 2018) have generally treated knowledge and skills as separate components of caregiver activation. While acknowledging their importance, the present analysis integrates these elements within the broader construct of care capabilities, thereby reducing dimensional fragmentation. Within this framework, capability is conceptualized as a synergistic integration of cognitive resources (knowledge), procedural competence (skills), and their contextualized application in real-world caregiving situations (Chan et al., 2020). Accordingly, knowledge and skills are reframed as interconnected components within a holistic and practice-oriented construct.

Importantly, care capabilities encompass both care-management competence and self-care competence. Sustained activation in long-term caregiving depends not only on technical proficiency but also on caregivers’ personal well-being (Sadak et al., 2016; Mazanec et al., 2016). Without adequate self-care, caregiver activation may gradually diminish over time. Given the conceptual overlap between care-management competence and self-care competence, these dimensions were integrated into a single attribute—care capabilities—whereas care resilience was retained as an independent dimension because of its distinct role in long-term adaptation to caregiving-related stress.

Although several studies have emphasized communication as a key component of caregiver activation (Thomas et al., 2017; Dingley et al., 2017), the present study conceptualizes communication as an integrated functional element rather than a standalone dimension. Specifically, communication-related knowledge and techniques are embedded within care capabilities; proactive and independent communication behaviours reflect care autonomy; and confidence in healthcare communication aligns with self-efficacy in care provision (Wittenberg et al., 2024). This integrative perspective positions communication as a connective mechanism that reinforces and connects the core attributes of caregiver activation.

A further conceptual clarification concerns the distinction between caregiver activation and caregiver empowerment, terms that are frequently conflated in the literature. Drawing on the patient activation literature, this study differentiates these concepts in both focus and scope (Jiao and Lane, 2019). Caregiver activation emphasizes individual readiness and capability-building, focusing on the preparedness and proactivity required to assume caregiving responsibilities—that is, being both willing and able to act (Wong Chung et al., 2020). In contrast, caregiver empowerment emphasizes agency and influence within relational and systemic contexts, highlighting the authority and capacity to influence care-related decisions and processes (Alghamdi et al., 2022). Empowerment involves advocacy, shared decision-making, and the redistribution of power within healthcare systems. Theoretically, activation may precede empowerment as a foundational condition, providing the psychological readiness and practical competence necessary for empowerment to develop. Clarifying this proposed relationship helps reduce conceptual ambiguity and strengthens both theoretical and practical understanding.

In addition, this study recognizes parental activation as a context-specific manifestation of caregiver activation within paediatric settings. Parents caring for children are required not only to possess the general competencies expected of caregivers (Mundal et al., 2023; Pennarola et al., 2012) but also to undertake responsibilities such as educational decision-making (Pérez Jolles et al., 2017) and developmental monitoring (Ruble et al., 2018). Accordingly, parental activation may be regarded as a subtype of caregiver activation that retains the core dimensions of the broader construct while incorporating child-specific contextual characteristics. The inclusion of this subtype broadens the theoretical applicability of caregiver activation and enriches the understanding of family-centred paediatric care.

In the present analysis, the consequences of caregiver activation were primarily reflected in improved health service utilization, enhanced caregiver well-being, and strengthened care management practices. However, existing evidence regarding health service utilization remains concentrated largely on mental health services, and direct evidence concerning broader care-recipient health outcomes remains limited. Future studies should therefore examine the physical, psychological, social, and care-management-related consequences of caregiver activation across diverse caregiving contexts.

Finally, caregiver activation aligns closely with several established theoretical frameworks. Orem’s self-care theory (Orem et al., 2001) corresponds with the emphasis placed on caregivers’ self-maintenance, whereas Bandura’s self-efficacy theory (Bandura, 1997) helps explain how confidence underpins proactive caregiving behaviours. Furthermore, as digital health technologies continue to evolve, caregiver activation offers a valuable theoretical foundation for the development of personalized interventions delivered through mobile applications, telehealth platforms, and online educational programmes. Integrating caregiver activation within these theoretical frameworks may strengthen its explanatory value and facilitate practice-oriented innovation.

Limitations

Although this study covered 11 databases and employed dual independent screening, the restriction to English- and Chinese-language publications and variability in study quality may have introduced selection bias, potentially influencing the interpretation of the identified attributes. Moreover, most reviewed studies were conducted within specific healthcare and sociocultural contexts. Therefore, the transferability of the identified attributes, antecedents, and consequences to other caregiving contexts should be interpreted with caution. Future studies could further examine the applicability of this conceptual framework across diverse care settings.

Implications

This study highlights the multidimensional nature of caregiver activation. Clarifying this concept not only advances future research but also provides a clearer framework for systematically identifying and comparing activation levels across different caregiving roles. Clinically, incorporating caregiver activation into routine assessments may enable healthcare professionals to identify caregivers with lower levels of activation and provide timely support, thereby enhancing caregiver well-being and improving care-recipient outcomes.

In practice, integrating caregiver activation into intervention design is equally important. Tailored educational programmes, structured caregiver training modules, and digital health tools—such as mobile applications for skill development and stress management—may serve as effective approaches for strengthening caregiver activation. Furthermore, community-based support systems and peer mentoring initiatives may help enhance caregivers’ resilience and self-care capacity. At the policy level, incorporating caregiver activation into health promotion frameworks may provide a stronger evidence base for resource allocation, caregiver-inclusive care planning, and the development of supportive policies. By linking assessment, intervention, and policy development, the concept of caregiver activation has the potential to improve not only caregiver experiences but also the overall quality of care provision.

Conclusions

This study conceptualizes caregiver activation as comprising four defining attributes: care capabilities, care autonomy, care resilience, and self-efficacy in care provision. Given caregivers’ central role in collaborating with healthcare professionals while simultaneously facing substantial physical and psychological burdens, caregiver activation is essential. Higher caregiver activation may improve health service utilization, promote caregiver well-being, and strengthen care management practices.

Beyond its immediate relevance to caregivers, the conceptual clarification of caregiver activation provides a theoretical bridge linking individual preparedness with broader goals of person- and family-centred care. By conceptualizing activation as both a transitional state and a sustained motivational process, this study underscores its importance for caregiver education, healthcare policy, and interdisciplinary collaboration. Future research should further integrate caregiver activation into health promotion and care management initiatives, with particular emphasis on the development and evaluation of targeted interventions aimed at optimizing outcomes for both caregivers and care recipients.
