 1 INTRODUCTION

1.1 OVERVIEW

 FGR­15 (Bellamy et al. 2025) lists external dose rate coefficients for radionuclides in
soil, water and air. It has five tables of coefficients for contaminated soil; one for radionuclides
on the surface of the soil and one each for radionuclides in soil layers of 1 cm, 5 cm, 15 cm and
infinite thickness. Each table contains 7512 coefficients, quantifying the external dose rate from
each of the 1252 ICRP­107 radionuclides, to each of the six reference persons of different ages.

 When viewed from the perspective of dose from a single radionuclide to a single
reference person, FGR 15 has a set of five external dose rate coefficients for contaminated soils;
one for surface contamination and four for volumetric contaminations of four different
thicknesses. RESRAD has an expression for the variation of the external dose rate coefficient
with the thickness of the contaminated soil layer, with the surface being treated as a layer of zero
thickness. The expression has four fitting coefficients. While the expression is the same, the
fitting coefficients are unique for each combination of radionuclide and reference person. The
primary purpose of this report is to describe how the fitting coefficients for each radionuclide­
reference person combination were generated from the FGR 15 data. The fitting coefficients for
each combinations of radionuclide and reference person ensure that the RESRAD expression
matches the corresponding volumetric external dose rate coefficients in FGR 15 to within ±5%.
In most cases, they match to three significant digits. Where possible, they also ensure that the
expression matches the corresponding superficial coefficient within the same limits. The reason
why it is not possible for the fitted expression to match the superficial external dose rate
coefficient for some radionuclides is discussed.

 The modifications that need to be made to the existing RESRAD model for external dose
rate coefficients to fit the FGR 15 data for radionuclides on the ground surface is arrived at by
comparing and contrasting this effort with the prior fittings of the model to three previous data
sets: the ICRP­26­based external dose rate coefficients in FGR 12 (Eckerman and Ryman 1993),
the ICRP­60­based external dose rate coefficients in the extended FGR 12 (Eckerman and
Leggett 1996), and the DCFPAK3.02 external dose rate coefficients. The distributions of the
deviations of the fittings to the dose rate coefficients in the four compilations are compared, and
the reason for the larger deviations of the fitted values from the FGR 15 ground surface dose rate
coefficients for some radionuclides is discussed. A modification to the RESRAD external
radiation model to account for these larger deviations is suggested.

 Section 1.6 outlines the organization of the remaining chapters of this report.

1.2 FGR 15 EXTERNAL DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS FOR RADIONUCLIDES
 IN SOIL

 Tables 4­1 through 4­5 of FGR 15 (Bellamy et al. 2025) list external dose rate
coefficients to six reference persons standing on contaminated soil. Table 4­1 is for superficial
contamination, i.e., the radionuclide is on the surface of the soil. The other tables are for
volumetric contamination, i.e., the radionuclide is distributed over a specified thickness of soil.

 1

All contaminations are of infinite area. Coefficients are listed for the following situations and
conditions:

 Reference persons: Each table has six columns of coefficients, one for each reference
 person of the following ages: newborn, 1­yr­old, 5­yr­old, 10­yr­old, 15­yr­old, adult.

 ICRP­107 radionuclides: Each table has a row of data for each of the 1,252 radionuclides.
 The coefficients are explicitly specified as zero for 24 of those radionuclides.

 Volumetric external dose rate coefficients: Tables 4­2, 4­3, 4­4, and 4­5 of FGR 15 list
 the coefficients for contaminated soil layers of the following four thicknesses,

 contaminated soil of density 1.6 103 kg m­3 and are in units of Sv s­1 per Bq m­3.

 Superficial external dose rate coefficients: Table 4­1 of FGR 15 lists the coefficients for a
 contaminated ground surface, in Sv s­1 per Bq m­2. The dose coefficients for the surface
 contamination in FGR 15 include the following two components.

 a. The dose to the skin from electrons, which is modeled as being from
 radionuclides at the surface of the soil.

 b. The dose from photons, which is modeled as being from radionuclides on a plane
 situated at a depth of 3 mm below the surface of soil of density 1.6 103 kg m­3, to
 account for “a variety of conditions that provide shielding from ground surface
 sources, including the presence of vegetation, surface irregularities and, of course,
 migration of the deposited activity downward into the soil” (Bellamy et al. 2025).
 These conditions are collectively referred to as surface roughness.

1.4.1 Motivation for the RESRAD Model for the External Dose Rate Coefficient in Soil

 The RESRAD software, RESRAD­ONSITE, RESRAD­OFFSITE, and RESRAD­
BUILD, will be used to model situations that do not exactly match the four volumetric and the
single superficial soil scenarios for which external dose rate coefficients are available in the
FGR 12, DCFPAK3.02, or FGR 15 compilations. The RESRAD model for the external dose rate
coefficient as a function of the thickness of the contamination is necessary to estimate the dose
rate coefficients in these cases:

 • The thickness of the contaminated soil to be modeled is not 1 cm, 5 cm, 15 cm, nor
 infinite in extent during part or all of the time horizon of the analysis.

 • The volumetric contamination to be modeled has a cover.

 • The plane source to be modeled has a cover when using DCFPAK3.02 or FGR 12
 dose rate coefficients or a cover of thickness other than 3 mm and/or a density other
 than 1.6 103 kg m­3 when using FGR 15 dose rate coefficients.

1.5 FITTING THE RESRAD MODEL FOR THE EXTERNAL DOSE RATE
 COEFFICIENT OF A RADIONUCLIDE IN SOIL TO THE COMPILED DOSE
 RATE COEFFICIENTS

 The RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients in soil, Section 1.4, contains four
fitting parameters for each radionuclide. One of them is simply the volumetric external dose
coefficient for soil of infinite thickness. The other three are the dimensionless coefficient, 𝐴(𝑖),
and the two exponential coefficients, 𝐾𝐴 (𝑖) and 𝐾𝐵 (𝑖), with units that are reciprocal to the units
of density thickness. Thus, four points are needed, at a minimum, to determine the four fitting
parameters. Depending on the need, different fittings can be made using different sets of dose
rate coefficients for a radionuclide. The following are considered in this report.

 4

 • A fitting using the four volumetric dose rate coefficients would be suitable when
 modeling only volumetric contamination.

 • A fitting using the four volumetric dose rate coefficients and the superficial dose rate
 coefficient might be preferable or even necessary when there is a need to model both
 volumetric and superficial contamination using the same fitting.

 The fitting can be performed in different ways. Some of the methods used for the fittings
discussed in this report are summarized below and are discussed in greater detail in the next few
chapters.

 • The Generalized Reduced Gradient (GRG) nonlinear solver in MS Excel was used to
 fit the RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients in soil to the DCFPAK3.02
 and FGR 12 compilations.

 • A set of analytical expressions were solved iteratively using different sets of up to
 four dose rate coefficients (either volumetric only or a combination of superficial and
 volumetric) to fit the RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients in soil to the
 FGR 15 data.

3.1.4 The Effect of the Units of the Dose Rate Coefficient in the Expression Used
 for Fitting

 The fittings were performed to minimize the sum of the squares of the deviations of the
external dose rate coefficients for contaminated soil layers of 1, 5, and 15 cm and for the smooth
plane. The units of the dose rate coefficients for the smooth plane are different from the units of
the dose rate coefficients for the volumetric contamination. Hence, there is the potential to favor
or bias the fittings toward the dose rate coefficients for the smooth plane or the volumetric
contamination depending on which has the lower numerical value in the set of units being used.
The effect of the different units can be overcome by using the fractional deviations instead of the
deviation in the optimization. This is illustrated in the next section.

 4 FITTING THE RESRAD MODEL FOR THE EXTERNAL DOSE RATE
 COEFFICIENTS IN SOIL TO THE FGR 15 DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS

 The RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients of a radionuclide in soil was
fitted to the data in the 2019 version of FGR 15 using the solver in MS Excel. Concerns arose
primarily regarding the depth profile of the external dose rate coefficients in that version of
FGR 15 for beta emitters of maximum energy of 1 MeV or less (Gnanapragasam et al. 2023).
The fittings of the RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients of a radionuclide in soil to
the 2019 version of FGR 15 is not described here because a newer version of FGR 15 was
released in 2025. The 2025 version of FGR 15 resolved the depth profiles concerns.

 Attempts to fit the RESRAD model for the external dose rate coefficients of a
radionuclide to the data in FGR 15 (2025) using the solver in MS Excel to minimize the sum of
the squares of neither the deviations nor the fractional deviations were successful.

 • A consistent method for generating initial values that ensure convergence to the
 global the minimum of the sum of the squares of the fractional deviation was not
 found.

 • For some of the radionuclides, the fitting that minimized the sum of the squares of the
 deviations produced a better fit than the fitting that minimized the sum of the squares
 of the fractional deviations.

 • For some others, the fitting that minimized the sum of the squares of the fractional
 deviations produced a better fit than the fitting that minimized the sum of the squares
 of the deviations.

 • And in a few cases, using the fitting that minimized the sum of the squares of the
 deviations as the initial values for the fitting using the sum of the squares of the
 fractional deviations produced the best fit.

 • There were also cases where the fractional deviations under both of these fittings
 were well outside the ±5% range.

 • The computations by the solver to perform the fitting for each radionuclide to the
 FGR 15 data took longer than the fitting to the DCFPAK3.02 data. There are 1,228
 radionuclides for which FGR 15 lists non­zero dose rate coefficients. There are six
 sets of dose rate coefficients for each radionuclide, one for each reference person of
 different ages. There was a risk of errors due to fatigue while the fitting for each of
 the 7,368 sets of dose rate coefficients was processed manually.

 This led to an effort to perform the fitting analytically.

 At least 1,121 radionuclides for each reference person are in this category. The

4.6.4 Algorithm to Select from Among the Five Fittings Generated for a Radionuclide

 Up to five different fittings were generated for the external dose rate coefficients to each
reference person from each radionuclide in soil using the three algorithms and the two variations
described in Sections 4.6.1, 4.6.2, and 4.6.3. The fitting to be used in the RESRAD codes
(RESRAD­ONSITE, RESRAD­OFFSITE, and RESRAD­BUILD) for each radionuclide­
reference person combination was selected using the algorithm described in this section. The
initial choice is made by comparing the fractional deviations of the volumetric external dose
coefficients because of the issues with fitting the external dose rate coefficient discussed in
Section 5.1.

 1. Compute the external dose rate coefficients for the ground surface and for the 1 cm, 5 cm,
 and 15 cm thick volumetric contamination using the fitting expressions, Section 4.2.

 2. Express the difference between each of the computed external dose rate coefficients and
 the value tabulated in FGR 15 for that coefficient, as a fraction of the latter value.

 3. Determine the largest absolute difference from among those for the four volumetric dose
 rate coefficients. Use this number to select the initial choice of the fitting, as described in
 the next few steps.

 4. Repeat steps 1 through 3 for each of the five fittings that were performed for the external
 dose rate coefficients to this receptor from this radionuclide.

 5. Compare the largest absolute differences for the five fittings. Pick the fitting with the
 smallest value. This is the initial choice.

 68

 6. Compare the deviations of the external dose rate coefficients across the five fittings. If
 there is a fitting where the largest deviation among the volumetric coefficients is within
 acceptable limits and if the deviation of the external dose rate coefficient for the ground
 surface is sufficiently better than that of the initial choice, select that as the final choice.

 Objective criteria for first pass of final choice:

 a. The acceptable deviation is less than 2.5% for volumetric external dose rate
 coefficients.

 b. The improvement in the deviation of the ground surface coefficient is at least
 twice the largest increase of deviation of the volumetric coefficient.

 7. Compare the deviations of the external dose rate coefficients across the five fittings. If
 there is a fitting where the largest deviation among the volumetric coefficients is within
 tolerable limits and if the deviation of the external dose rate coefficient for the ground
 surface is significantly better than that of the initial choice, select that as the final choice.

 Objective criteria for the second pass of the final choice:

 a. The tolerable deviation of 5% for volumetric external dose rate coefficients.

 b. The improvement in the deviation of the external dose rate coefficient for the
 ground surface is at least twice the largest increase of deviation of the volumetric
 external dose rate coefficient.

 c. The deviation of the external dose rate coefficient for the ground surface improves
 from greater than 10% to less than 10%.

5.1 INDICATIONS FOR LARGE DEVIATIONS BETWEEN THE PREDICTIONS OF
 THE FITTED RESRAD MODEL FOR EXTERNAL DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS
 AND THE FGR 15 DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS FOR GROUND SURFACES

 The last five tables in Section 4.6.5 evaluating the fitting of the RESRAD external dose
rate model to the FGR 15 dose rate coefficients show that the model could not be fit to the
external dose rate coefficient for the ground surface for approximately one fifth of the
radionuclides with non­zero dose rate coefficients. The dose rate coefficients for these
radionuclides computed using the fitting ranged from 0.05 to 0.9 times the FGR 15 values. That
section also identified two common radionuclides, 90Sr and 90Y, for which the fitted values were
just 0.1 and 0.15 times the FGR 15 coefficients. In contrast, the fitted value for 60Co was 1.01
times the FGR 15 coefficient, an excellent fit. Section 4.5.2 also identified five radionuclides
with fitted ground surface rate coefficients that were approximately 0.23, 0.5, 0.7, 0.82, and 0.92
times the FGR 15 coefficient. These were used to test the hypothesis described in this section.

 The RESRAD model for external dose rate coefficients pertains to the dose from photons,
both those generated directly from the transformations and indirectly from bremsstrahlung from
the beta emissions. Thus, it fits readily to the volumetric dose rate coefficients in FGR 15. The
expression derived in Appendix A for the external dose rate coefficient for the 3 mm ground
plane models the part of the coefficient applicable to the dose from photons.

 The external dose rate coefficients in FGR 15 for the ground surface include the
contribution of the direct electron dose to the skin in addition to the dose from photons to all the
organs, Section 1.2. Hence, the fitted coefficient will underestimate the external dose rate
coefficient listed in FGR 15 in proportion to the electron dose to the skin. The underestimate is
expected to be related to the contribution of the electron dose to skin to the FGR 15 dose rate
coefficient. While the contribution of the electron dose to skin to the FGR 15 dose rate
coefficient for the ground surface cannot be extracted from the data in FGR 15 (2025), nor the
supplementary data available in the FGR 15 webpage, https://www.epa.gov/system/files/other­
files/2025­07/fgr15_data_2025_05_28.zip, the dose from both electrons and photons to the skin
can be computed from the supplementary data. The files in the sub­folder
fgr15_data_2025_05_28.zip\Nuclide_Coefficients\GRD_Surface list the dose rate coefficients to
the tissues from each radionuclide on the ground surface. Coefficients are listed for 29 tissues or
groupings of tissues. Table 3­1 of FGR 15 has the tissue weighting factors used to compute the
dose rate coefficient to the reference person using the dose rate coefficients to the individual
tissues. These were used to compute the contribution of the skin dose to the dose rate coefficient
to the reference person as illustrated in Table 26 and Table 27.

5.2 ACCOUNTING FOR THE ELECTRON SKIN DOSE COMPONENT OF THE
 FGR 15 DOSE RATE COEFFICIENT FOR GROUND SURFACES

 The electron dose to skin will be less dependent on or independent of the area of
contamination compared to the photon dose to skin. Hence, the electron dose to skin needs to be
computed separately from the photon dose to the reference person. The RESRAD codes
RESRAD­ONSITE, RESRAD­OFFSITE, and RESRAD­BUILD should use the fittings
generated in Chapter 4 to compute the photon dose both for volumetric contamination and for
surface contamination. If necessary, the contribution of the electron dose to skin should be
computed and reported separately as follows.

 1. Attribute the residual of the 3 mm ground plane dose rate coefficient that remains after
 the fitting to electrons and report it separately.

 2. If the radionuclides are on the surface, include the contribution of the electron dose to
 skin only when there is no cover.

 3. Make no adjustment for area; the infinite area for dose from electrons will be much
 smaller than that for the dose from photons.

 6 COMPARISON OF THE FOUR COMPILATIONS OF EXTERNAL DOSE RATE
 COEFFICIENTS USED IN RESRAD CODES

 This chapter contains very brief descriptions of the four data sets of external dose rate
coefficients used in the RESRAD codes in chronological order of their compilation. The dose
rate coefficients in each compilation are compared with those in the preceding compilation. The
fittings to the external dose rate coefficients in these four compilations are compared for two
gamma emitters, 60Co, and 137mBa, three beta emitters, 90Sr, 90Y, and 137Cs, and a radionuclide
that has comparable beta and gamma emissions, 106Rh.

6.1 ICRP­26­BASED EFFECTIVE DOSE EQUIVALENT RATE COEFFICIENTS
 IN FGR 12

 Tables III.3 through III.7 of FGR 12 (1993) contain the external effective dose equivalent
rate coefficients for 804 ICRP­38 radionuclides, computed using the methodology in ICRP­26
and the transformation energy spectra in ICRP­38. The rate coefficients are also in electronic
files FGR12T33.SUR, FGR12T34.1CM, FGR12T35.5CM, FGR12T36.15C, and
FGR12T37.INF distributed by the Radiation Shielding Information Center at Oak Ridge
National Laboratory.

 Table II.1 and Section II of FGR 12 describe the calculation of the external effective dose
equivalent rate coefficients as the weighted average of the external dose rate coefficients to
11 organs. The organs included Gonads, Breast, Lung, Red Marrow, Bone Surface, Thyroid, and
the five organs other than the “skin, lens of the eye and the extremities” (FGR 12, 1993), which
had the five highest dose rates among those not listed above. The dose rate coefficient to skin
included the contribution of both electrons and photons. The dose rate coefficients to all the other
organs were due solely to photons (FGR 12 1993 and FGR 15 2025). Thus, the effective dose
equivalent rate coefficients in the FGR 12 of 1983 were due only to photons.

6.2 ICRP­60­BASED EFFECTIVE DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS IN FGR 12

 The external effective dose rate coefficients for 805 ICRP­38 radionuclides, computed
using the methodology in ICRP­60 and the transformation energy spectra in ICRP­38, are
available in electronic files FGR12f33.dat, FGR12f34.dat, FGR12f35.dat, FGR12f36.dat,
FGR12f37.dat in DCFPAK (Eckerman and Leggett 1996).

 Table II.1 and Section II of FGR 12 describe the calculation of the external effective dose
rate coefficients as the weighted average of the external dose rate coefficients to 22 organs
including the skin. The dose rate coefficient to skin includes the contribution of both electrons
and photons. The dose rate coefficients to all the other organs were due solely to photons
(FGR 12 1993 and FGR 15 2025). Thus, the effective dose rate coefficients in the expanded
FGR 12 are due to both electrons and photons.

 88

6.3 EFFECTIVE DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS IN DCFPAK3.02

 The external effective dose rate coefficients for 1,223 ICRP­107 radionuclides, computed
using the methodology in ICRP­60 and the transformation energy spectra in ICRP­107, are
available in electronic files FGR12III3.dat, FGR12III4.dat, FGR12III5.dat, FGR12III6.dat,
FGR12III7.dat in DCFPAK3.02 (Eckerman and Leggett 2013).

6.4 DOSE RATE COEFFICIENTS IN FGR 15

 Tables 4­1 through 4­5 of FGR 15 (Bellamy et al. 2025) contain the external dose rate
coefficients for 1,228 ICRP­107 radionuclides, computed using the methodology in ICRP­103
and the transformation energy spectra in ICRP­107. These coefficients are also available in
electronic files Table_4_1.DAT, Table_4_2.DAT, Table_4_3.DAT, Table_4_4.DAT, and
Table_4_5.DAT, available in the link to “FGR15 Data Files (zip)” on the FGR 15 webpage.

 Table 3.1 of FGR 15 lists the weighting factors used to compute the effective dose rate
coefficients to the reference person from the external dose rate coefficients to 29 organs. The
dose rate coefficient to skin includes the contribution of both electrons and photons. The dose
rate coefficients to all the other organs were due solely to photons (FGR 12 1993 and FGR 15
2025). Thus, the effective dose rate coefficients in FGR 15 are due to electrons and photons.

 The external effective dose rate coefficients for the ground surface include consideration
of ground roughness for the dose from the photons (Bellamy et al. 2025), but not for that from
electrons (Samuels and Leggett 2025).

6.8.1 Comparison of External Dose Rate Coefficients

 The external dose rate coefficients for these radionuclides are in Table 29. The external
dose rate coefficients for surface contamination, both with and without the reduction for surface
roughness, from ICRP­144 are also tabulated in addition to the external dose rate coefficients
from the four data sets used in the RESRAD codes. ICRP­144 does not list volumetric external
dose rate coefficients and is not useable in RESRAD­ONSITE nor in RESRAD­OFFSITE. The
differences between the dose rate coefficients in ICRP­144 and in FGR 15 are discussed in
Samuels and Leggett 2025.

 It is easier to see the trends in the external dose rate coefficients across the different
compilations if they are “normalized” by dividing them by the ICRP­26­based coefficient in
FGR 12 for the corresponding radionuclide; the normalized coefficients are shown in Table 30.
The following trends are observed in the data.

 1. The superficial and volumetric dose rate coefficients for 60Co and 137mBa, the two gamma
 emitters, decrease gradually over the four data sets in chronological order. The reductions
 are small, all less than 10%, with the largest reduction occurring for the external dose rate
 coefficient for the infinitely thick layer. The soil cover having a density thickness of
 0.5 g (cm)­2, used to account for surface roughness, causes a 30% reduction in the
 superficial dose rate coefficients in ICRP­144 and likely in FGR 15 as well.

 2. The superficial dose rate coefficients for 90Y, 137Cs, and 90Sr increased by a factor of 21,
 11, and 6, respectively, from the ICRP­26­based coefficients to both sets of ICRP­60­
 based coefficients, those in FGR 12 and those in DCFPAK3.02. They then dropped likely
 by 2%, 4%, and 9%, respectively, in FGR 15 due to the surface roughness reduction,
 which is applied only to the dose from photons and not to the dose from electrons.

 The volumetric dose rate coefficients for 90Y and 137Cs also increased; 90Y by factors 4 to
 1.6 and 137Cs by factors of 1.6 to 1.1, depending on the thickness of the contaminated soil
 layer. The incremental factor decreased with increasing thickness of the soil layer.

 The volumetric dose rate coefficient for 90Sr decreased by 11% to 15% from the
 ICRP­60­based coefficients in FGR 12 to those in FGR 15.

 3. The superficial dose rate coefficient and the volumetric dose rate coefficient for the 1 cm
 thick layer for 106Rh increased from the ICRP­26­based coefficients to both sets of
 ICRP­60­based coefficients, those in FGR 12 and those in DCFPAK3.02. The superficial
 factor likely dropped by 20% in FGR 15.

 The coefficient for the 5 cm thick layer held steady while those for the two thickest layers
 decreased by 2% and 3% from the ICRP­26­based coefficients to both sets of ICRP­60­
 based coefficients, those in FGR 12 and those in DCFPAK3.02. The volumetric dose rate
 coefficients fell by 2% to 4% from DCFPAK3.02 to FGR 15.

  Rh, Y, 137Cs, and 90Sr. The figures are ordered from radionuclides with the best fit to the

superficial dose rate coefficient in FGR 15 to the one with the worst among these seven

 Each figure has two graphs. The fitted curves for the variation of the external dose rate
coefficient with the thickness of the soil layer with no cover are shown as lines. The points
represent the coefficients to which the curves were fitted.

 The graph on the left is used to illustrate the fitting to the volumetric coefficients. The
graphs on the left of Figure 75 through Figure 80 show the curves are a good fit to the volumetric
dose rate coefficients because they either pass through or are very close to the points
representing the volumetric dose rate coefficients. The fitting coefficients used to generate these
curves were determined as described in Section 4 and are given in Table 31. The superficial dose
rate coefficient for a smooth plane, one without any reductions for surface roughness, is
represented by the slope of the fitted curve at the origin, Section 3.2.3. The superficial dose rate
coefficient in FGR 15 cannot be represented in this graph because it has a reduction for surface
roughness.

 The graphs on right side of the figures illustrate the fitting to the superficial dose rate
coefficients both for the smooth ground surfaces and for the ground plane with surface
roughness. They were created as follows:

 1. Each of the four volumetric dose rate coefficients, in (Sv s­1)/(Bq m­3), were divided by
 the thickness of the contaminated soil layers to which they apply. This expresses the four
 volumetric dose rate coefficients in terms of the activity below a square meter of soil.
 These quantities have the same units as the superficial dose rate coefficients,
         (Sv s­1)/(Bq m­2).

 2. The four quantities obtained in the previous step and the superficial dose rate coefficient
 are plotted against the thickness of the soil layer they correspond to.

 3. The fitted curve is expected to approach the superficial dose rate coefficient for a smooth
 surface as the thickness tends to zero. This can be viewed using a logarithmic scale for
 the thickness, the x­axis.

 a. But a zero thickness cannot be displayed on a logarithmic scale. Thus, the superficial
 dose rate coefficient is displayed at a very thin layer, 1 µm in these plots.

 b. If the fitting is good, (1) the fitted curve will be almost horizontal at the lower
 thickness; (2) it will pass through or be close to the superficial dose rate coefficient
 for a smooth ground surface.

 The paragraph immediately following this list evaluates the fittings to the superficial dose
 rate coefficients for the six selected radionuclide on a smooth ground surface using the
 above criterion.

 103

 4. Ground roughness reduces the dose from a contaminated ground surface. Thus, the point
 representing the superficial dose rate coefficient of a ground plane with surface roughness
 is expected to be below the fitted curve. This is because the fitted curves plotted in these
 graph are for a situation with no cover. The fitting coefficients are used to estimate the
 reduction due to the cover of density thickness of 0.5 g (cm)­2.

 a. This reduction is shown by the vertical portion, at the y­axis, of the curve fitted to the
 FGR 15 dose rate coefficients.

 b. If the fitting is good, the lower end of this vertical portion of the curve will be at or
 very close to the FGR 15 superficial dose rate coefficient.

 This criterion is used to evaluate the fittings to the superficial dose rate coefficients for
 the six selected radionuclides on a ground surface with a 0.5 g (cm)­2 in the paragraph
 after the next one.

 The curves fitted to the DCFPAK3.02, the ICRP­60­based FGR 12, and the ICRP­26­
based FGR 12 dose rate coefficients approach the point representing the superficial coefficients
in those data sets horizontally, as the thickness of the layer tends to zero, and they intersect the
y­axis at those points in the graphs on the right in Figure 75 and in Figure 77 through Figure 80.
The three curves also approach the point representing the superficial coefficients in the data sets
horizontally in Figure 76. The curve fitted to the ICRP­26­based external dose rate coefficients
passes through the point representing the superficial dose rate coefficient in FGR 12, while the
curves fitted to the two sets of ICRP­60­based external dose rate coefficients pass just below the
points representing the superficial dose rate coefficients of those data sets. These figures show
that the fitting to the superficial dose rate coefficients of the six radionuclides in these three data
sets was good.

 The lower end of the vertical portion of the curve fitted to the FGR 15 dose rate
coefficients, at the y­axis, is at or close to the point representing FGR 15 superficial dose rate
coefficients in Figure 75 and Figure 76. This indicates that a good fit was achieved for 60Co and
137m
 Ba to the FGR 15 superficial external dose rate coefficients. The dose to the skin contributed
0% and 2%, respectively, to the external effective dose rate coefficients of these two
radionuclides. Thus, the ground roughness correction was applied to almost the entirety of the
superficial dose rate coefficient in FGR 15, and a good fitting was achievable.

 The dose to the skin contributed significantly to the effective dose rate coefficients of the
other four radionuclides, 51%, 97%, 96%, and 93%, respectively, for 106Rh, 90Y, 137Cs, and 90Sr.
Part of the dose to the skin would have been from electrons and the rest from photons. Only part
of the FGR 15 superficial dose rate coefficients of these radionuclides, those due to photons,

vertical portion of the curve fitted to the FGR 15 dose rate coefficients is well below the point
representing the FGR 15 superficial dose rate coefficients for these radionuclides. The fitting to
the FGR 15 superficial dose rate coefficients of these radionuclides deteriorated in Figure 77

 7 CONCLUSIONS

1. The fitting coefficients generated as described in Chapter 4 to fit the RESRAD model for
 the variation of the external dose rate coefficient with the thickness of the contaminated
 layer to the FGR 15 data set can be used with the existing models in RESRAD­ONSITE
 and in RESRAD­OFFSITE to compute the external dose for volumetric contamination.

 These fitting coefficients can also be used to compute the dose from volumetric
 contamination in RESRAD­BUILD.

2. These fittings will also generate superficial dose rate coefficients that are within ±10% of
 those in FGR 15 for at least 960 radionuclides. Thus, if a very thin contamination with a
 3 mm soil cover of density 1.6 × 103 kg m­3 is modeled in either RESRAD­ONSITE or
 RESRAD­OFFSITE, the external dose will be computed using a superficial external dose
 rate coefficient that is sufficiently close to that in FGR 15 for 960+ radionuclides.

3. RESRAD­BUILD models surface contamination. These contaminated surfaces can be
 smooth or they can have covers of different thicknesses.

 These fitting coefficients can be used to compute the dose from superficial contamination
 in RESRAD­BUILD. But the dose to skin from electrons will need to be computed
 separately, as suggested in Chapter 5, if the density thickness of the cover or shielding is
 less than 0.5 gram per square centimeter, if the intent is to compute the dose using the
 superficial external dose rate coefficients in FGR 15. This additional step will be
 necessary for close to 300 radionuclides.
