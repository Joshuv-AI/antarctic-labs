Improved Approximation Algorithms for Non-Preemptive Throughput Maximization
Alexander Armbruster, Fabrizio Grandoni, Antoine Tinguely, Andreas Wiese

Abstract
The (Non-Preemptive) Throughput Maximization problem is a natural and fundamental scheduling problem. We are given n n jobs, where each job j j is characterized by a processing time and a time window, contained in a global interval [ 0 , T ) [0,T) , during which j j can be scheduled. Our goal is to schedule the maximum possible number of jobs non-preemptively on a single machine, so that no two scheduled jobs are processed at the same time. This problem is known to be strongly NP-hard. The best-known approximation algorithm for it has an approximation ratio of 1 / 0.6448 + ε ≈ 1.551 + ε 1/0.6448+\varepsilon\approx 1.551+\varepsilon [Im, Li, Moseley IPCO’17], improving on an earlier result in [Chuzhoy, Ostrovsky, Rabani FOCS’01]. In this paper we substantially improve the approximation factor for the problem to 4 / 3 + ε 4/3+\varepsilon for any constant ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 . Using pseudo-polynomial time ( n ​ T ) O ⁡ ( 1 ) (nT)^{O(1)} , we improve the factor even further to 5 / 4 + ε 5/4+\varepsilon . Our results extend to the setting in which we are given an arbitrary number of (identical) machines.



1
Introduction

In this paper we study the
(Non-Preemptive) Throughput Maximization
problem
(also known as
Job Interval Scheduling
) which is one of the
most basic scheduling problems. We are given a set of
n
jobs
J
,
where each job
j\in J
is characterized by its
processing
time

p_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, its
release time

r_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
, and
its
deadline

d_{j}\in\mathbb{N}
. For each job
j\in J
we define its

time window
by
\mathrm{tw}(j)\coloneqq[r_{j},d_{j})
. The goal is
to select a subset
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
of the jobs and to compute a non-preemptive
schedule for
J^{\prime}
on one machine.
More formally, we seek to compute
a start time
s(j)\in\mathbb{N}
for each job
j\in J^{\prime}
such that
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
, meaning that
we execute
j
during
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})
.
We require that for any two distinct jobs
j,j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}
their intervals
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})

and
[s(j^{\prime}),s(j^{\prime})+p_{j^{\prime}})
are disjoint. The objective is to maximize
the number of scheduled jobs, i.e., to maximize
|J^{\prime}|
. Not surprisingly,
the problem and its variants and generalization have several applications,
see, e.g.,
[
35
,
14
,
24
,
43
]

and references therein.

Throughput Maximization is (strongly) NP-hard
[
47
,
25
]
which motivates studying approximation
algorithms for it. However, from this point of view, it is not very
well understood. The currently best known approximation factor in
polynomial time (and even in pseudo-polynomial or quasi-polynomial
time) is
1/0.6448+\varepsilon\approx 1.551+\varepsilon
for any constant
\varepsilon>0
, due to Im, Li and Moseley

[
39
,
40
]
. This
improves a previous result by Chuzhoy,
Ostrovisky and Rabani
[
21
,
22
]
which
achieves an approximation ratio of
\frac{e}{e-1}+\varepsilon\approx 1.582+\varepsilon

(in fact, even for a more general version
where for each job we are given an explicit set of possible execution intervals, instead of our implicitly
defined intervals of the form
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
) and other previous results
[
52
,
10
,
11
,
13
]
.

However, Throughput Maximization is
not
known to be APX-hard
and, hence, it might still admit a PTAS! We find intriguing that the
approximability status of such a basic problem is still rather unclear.

1.1
Our Results and Techniques

In this paper we substantially improve the best known approximation
ratio for Throughput Maximization. More specifically, we obtain the
following two main results.

Theorem 1
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a polynomial-time
randomized
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for Throughput Maximization.

Using pseudo-polynomial time, we can do even better. We define
T:=\max_{j\in J}d_{j}

and observe that, hence, each job
j\in J
must be scheduled within
the interval
[0,T)
.

Theorem 2
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a randomized
(5/4+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm for Throughput Maximization with a running time of
(nT)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
.

In the following, we illustrate the main ideas behind our results. Our
starting point is the approach by Chuzhoy et al.
[
22
]
.
The authors present a polynomial-time procedure that partitions the
time horizon
[0,T)
into a collection of at most
\varepsilon|\mathrm{OPT}|
intervals
which we will call
blocks
. The blocks are defined such that
there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution in which

•

each job is scheduled entirely within one block and

•

inside each block at most
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
jobs are scheduled.

Based on the blocks, they define a configuration-LP that has a configuration
for each combination of a block
B
and a set of at most
O_{\varepsilon}(1)

jobs that can be scheduled within
B
; in particular, the LP has a polynomial
number of variables and constraints.

Given an optimal fractional solution to the configuration-LP, they
sample independently one configuration for each block. It might happen
that some job
j\in J
appears in more than one sampled configuration;
in this case, it can still be scheduled only once in the computed solution.
However, one can show that if a job
j\in J
appeared fractionally
in
y_{j}^{*}
configurations of the optimal LP-solution, then it
appears in at least one sampled configurations with probability at
least
\frac{e-1}{e}y_{j}^{*}
which yields the mentioned approximation
ratio of
\frac{e}{e-1}+\varepsilon\approx 1.582+\varepsilon
.

In both of our algorithms, we use a similar configuration-LP, but
we define the blocks in a different way and also invoke a different
rounding procedure. We start with our
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm. Like in the algorithm by Chuzhoy et al.
[
22
]
,
we sample a configuration for each block according to the optimal
LP-solution. However, we do not directly assign the jobs according
to the sampled configurations. Instead, if a sampled configuration
schedules a job
j
during some time interval
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})
,
then we interpret this interval as a
slot

during which we might schedule
some
job
j^{\prime}
whose time window
and processing time allow to process it completely during
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})
(possibly
p_{j^{\prime}}<p_{j}
and then the machine would remain idle during some parts of the interval).
Then, we use a bipartite matching routine to compute the largest set
of jobs that can be assigned in this way to the slots, i.e., each
slot gets at most one compatible job assigned to it and each job is assigned to at most one slot. In particular,
if a job
j
appears in two sampled configurations, then it creates
two slots such that we can assign
j
to one of them and potentially
another job
j^{\prime}
to the other slot. In contrast, in
[
22
]

the second slot was kept empty in this case and, hence, it was lost. On a high level, our algorithm might
not seem too different from
[
22
]
; however,
we show that in expectation our resulting matching yields an approximation
ratio of only
4/3+\varepsilon<1.334+\varepsilon
.

One key idea for proving this is a more sophisticated definition of
our blocks. We ensure that they still have the properties described
above. In addition, we define a second partition of
[0,T)
into

superblocks
, where
each superblock is the union of a large (constant) number of consecutive blocks. We show
that we can compute such a partition for which there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate
solution with a set of jobs
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
in which each job
j\in\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}

is scheduled either (see Figure
1
):

•

in the leftmost or the rightmost block that
\mathrm{tw}(j)
intersects;
we call these blocks the
boundary blocks
for
j
, or

•

in one of the superblocks
S

spanned
by
j
, i.e., such that
S\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
.

Figure 1
:
The blue and red lines delimitate the blocks and superblocks, respectively. Job
j
is global and can be scheduled within its boundary blocks
B_{j,L}
and
B_{j,R}
(marked blue) or within one of the two superblocks that it spans (marked red). It cannot be scheduled between a spanned superblock and
B_{j,L}
or
B_{j,R}
, and cannot intersect two (or more) blocks. Job
j^{\prime}
is local. Its (unique) boundary block is marked in green.

In order to prove that there exists a sufficiently large matching, a particularly
simple case is when in the optimal solution to the configuration-LP,
each job
j\in J
appears only in configurations corresponding to
its boundary blocks. Then, we can easily show that it appears in
at least one sampled block-configuration with probability at least

\frac{3}{4}y_{j}^{*}
. Hence, we can we simply match each job
j

to one of “its own” slots like
[
22
]

and obtain an approximation ratio of
4/3+\varepsilon
.

However, we need to show that we can achieve the same approximation factor also when the jobs might be fractionally scheduled in non-boundary blocks. This is
substantially more complex, and one of the main contributions of this paper.
At a high level, we show that in expectation there exists a large-enough fractional (bipartite) matching between jobs and slots. Standard matching theory then implies that the maximum matching is also large enough.

In more detail, if a job
j
belongs to the sampled configuration for at least one of its boundary blocks, say
B
, we simply integrally match
j
with the slot created by
j
in
B
. The remaining jobs are fractionally matched to slots in the superblocks spanned by them.
We need to show that the latter
jobs yield sufficiently many fractional edges. To that aim, we use a fractional version of the classical harmonic grouping technique and concentration arguments that critically exploit the fact that each of our superblocks contains many blocks. For each superblock
S
, we apply harmonic grouping
based on the fractional solution to the configuration-LP for its contained
blocks. This yields
O(1/\varepsilon)
groups of jobs where each group contains
essentially the same (fractional) number of jobs, and the processing times of the jobs in one group are not larger than the processing times of the next group. Notice that in harmonic
grouping one typically forms groups of items of similar cardinality,
while we form the groups so that they have a similar
fractional
cardinality
in terms of the computed LP-solution. Since the configuration
for each of the (many) blocks contained in
S
were sampled independently,
we can show via concentration arguments that, with sufficiently large probability, for each of the
O(1/\varepsilon)

job groups, the number of sampled slots is essentially the same as
the fractional number of jobs in that group (and, hence, also in the next group). When this event happens, we fractionally match the jobs from each job
group to the slots of the next job group (with larger processing time). For this it is crucial
that for each matched job
j
its time window
\mathrm{tw}(j)
contains
the entire superblock
S
; due to this property, we can schedule
j
arbitrarily
within
S
.

There is a subtle technical issue. In order to achieve the claimed approximation factor, we use that the fractional amount by which we assign a job
j
to slots in a superblock
S
spanned by
j
depends on the sampled configurations for the boundary blocks for
j
. More specifically, if
j
belongs to one such configuration, it cannot be also fractionally matched to a slot in a spanned superblock. Hence, these fractional amounts for the different jobs are not independent random variables, since two jobs might have the same boundary block! Therefore, we cannot use the standard Chernoff’s bound to prove that the relevant random variables are sufficiently concentrated around their respective means. However, we are able to show that the impact of such dependencies is sufficiently small. More specifically, we can still use the concentration bounds for
read
-
k
families of random variables in
[
27
]
to obtain the desired properties.

A closer look into our analysis reveals that our approximation ratio
is even
1+\varepsilon
(compared to the optimal LP solution) with respect to the
profit of jobs
j
whose time
window
\mathrm{tw}(j)
is contained in some block; we call such jobs
local
.
For the other (
global
) jobs, our approximation ratio is
4/3+\varepsilon
, again
compared to the optimal fractional solution. Therefore, we design
a second rounding routine that achieves an approximation ratio of

1+\varepsilon
w.r.t. the global jobs (but does not schedule any local
job). The best of the two solutions then has an approximation ratio
of only
5/4+\varepsilon
.

For this second algorithm, we need a different
block decomposition in which each block contains intuitively
\Theta_{\varepsilon}(\log T)

jobs on average from some near-optimal solution. However, then our
configuration-LP has
n^{\Theta_{\varepsilon}(\log T)}
variables and
we can no longer solve it trivially in polynomial time. Therefore,
we use the ellipsoid method with a new separation oracle for the dual
LP. The separation problem is a
weighted
generalization of our (initial) Throughput Maximization problem, however with the additional constraint that any solution can contain
at most

\Theta_{\varepsilon}(\log T)
jobs.
We show how to solve the latter problem in pseudopolynomial time with a suitable dynamic program and the
color coding technique
[
2
]
.

Another major difference in our second algorithm is the LP-rounding procedure. Instead of independently sampling a configuration for each block, we independently assign a job to a block
B
according
to the marginal probabilities induced by the fractional solution.
It might
be that there is a block
B
whose assigned jobs cannot all be scheduled
within
B
. However, we show that if we remove
O_{\varepsilon}(\log T)
intuitively
relatively “long” jobs from each block
B
, then due to concentration
arguments the remaining jobs can (most likely) be scheduled within

B
. More precisely, we remove a job
j
if its processing time

p_{j}
is relatively large compared to the length of its time window
within
B
, i.e.,
p_{j}
is large compared to the length of
B\cap\mathrm{tw}(j)
.
Since our blocks contain
\Theta_{\varepsilon}(\log T)
jobs on average from
some near-optimal solution, we can argue that the removed jobs determine
only an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the optimal profit.

We remark that a similar random assignment was used by Im, Li and
Moseley
[
40
]
. However, they had to disallow
to schedule a job in its boundary blocks which lost the corresponding
profit of the LP. In contrast, since we allow a block to contain up to
{O_{\varepsilon}}(\log T)
jobs and we remove the relatively long jobs,
we
can
assign a job to its boundary intervals too
and, hence, we obtain a much better approximation ratio of
5/4+\varepsilon
.

Our results extend to the generalization of Throughput Maximization on
m
(identical) machines (see Section
4
for details).

Theorem 3
.

For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there is a polynomial-time
randomized
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm and a pseudopolynomial-time randomized
(5/4+\varepsilon)
approximation algorithm for Throughput Maximization on
m
machines.

Other related Work

The first approximation algorithm for Throughput Maximization was a
2

Spieksma
[

.
While the problem is NP-hard in general

[
48

There are natural generalizations of Throughput Maximization to multiple machines and/or to the weighted case in which each job has a weight and we wish to maximize the total weight of scheduled jobs.
There is an algorithm by Bar-Noy, Guha, Naor and Schieber
[
11

that achieves a
\frac{(1+1/m)^{m}}{(1+1/m)^{m}-1}
-approximation for any number of machines
m
(also in the weighted case in pseudo-polynomial time); note that this ratio converges to
\frac{e}{e-1}
for
m\rightarrow\infty
.
Essentially the same ratio was obtained in
[
13
]
in polynomial time. There is also a
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm for the weighted case on multiple machines using the local ratio technique
[
10
]
.

The algorithm by Chuzhoy et al.
[
22
]
yields the ratio of
\frac{e}{e-1}+\varepsilon
for any number of machines but only in the unweighted case. The algorithm by Im et al.
[
40
]
yields the mentioned approximation ratio of
1/0.6448+\varepsilon<1.551+\varepsilon
for any number of machines in the unweighted case, and the authors provide also an
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation for the weighted case if the number of machines
m
is sufficiently large (for any constant
\varepsilon>0
). If we allow resource augmentation, i.e., shortening the processing time of each input job by a factor of
1+\varepsilon
, there is even a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in quasi-polynomial time known in the weighted case and for any number of machines
[
38
]
.

The Unsplittable Flow on a Path with Time Windows problem (UFPTW) is another generalization of Throughput Maximization where each job is in addition specified by a certain
demand
for a shared resource whose capacity might vary over time. In this problem, multiple jobs can be processed at the same time, provided that their total demand is within the available capacity at that time. Throughput Maximization with
m
machines is the special case where all the demands are
1
, and the available capacity is uniformly

. For UFPTW there is a polynomial time
O(\log n/\log\log n)
-approximation algorithm for the weighted case and an
O(1)
-approximation in the unweighted case
[
33
]
, improving on
[
17
]
. These results hold for a more general setting of explicitly given possible execution intervals and corresponding demands, similar to
[
22
]
. Also, there is a quasi-polynomial time
(2+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm under resource augmentation for the weighted case
[
5
]
.
In
[
5
]
it is also shown that the
problem is APX-hard (even in the unweighted setting); the hardness reduction critically exploits that the tasks can have different demands, and, hence, it does not extend to Throughput Maximization. The special case of the above problem where the length of each time window equals the respective processing time is the classical and very well-studied Unsplittable Flow on a Path problem
[

,
30
,

]
which admits a PTAS
[

]
.

Other scheduling problems on one or multiple machines include for example makespan minimization

,
42
,

]
,
minimizing the average (weighted) completion times of the given jobs
[

, minimizing the average (weighted) job’s flowtimes

We refer to

2
Polynomial time approximation algorithm

In this section, we present our polynomial time
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation
algorithm. We assume w.l.o.g. that
\min_{j\in J}r_{j}=0
and recall that

T=\max_{j\in J}d_{j}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
be a sufficiently small constant and assume that
1/\varepsilon\in\mathbb{N}
. For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
[k]:=\{1,\dots,k\}
.

In Section
2.1
, we present a method to partition
[0,T)
into subintervals
such that there exist a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution that is
“aligned” with this partition and satisfies certain additional properties
that we will exploit later in our approximation algorithm. Based on this, in Section
2.2
we define a modified configuration LP. In Section
2.3
we present our rounding algorithm, which is then analyzed in Section
2.4
.

2.1
Construction of blocks and superblocks

As a first step, we invoke a method from
[
22
]

to partition
[0,T)
into
blocks
where we define a block to
be an interval of the form
[a,b)
for some
a,b\in[0,T]
with
a<b
.
Given a set of jobs
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
and a corresponding
schedule with a starting time
s(j)\in\{0,\ldots,T\}
for each job
j\in J^{\prime}
, we say that a job
j\in J^{\prime}
is
scheduled in a block
B
if
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})\subseteq B
. We remark that the running time bound in
[
22
]
holds for a discrete version of the problem and that
property
(A4)
below is not explicitly stated in
[
22
]
. For the sake of completeness, we sketch a proof of the following lemma in the appendix.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq(\frac{6}{\varepsilon})^{3}
. In polynomial time we can compute a partition of
[0,T)
into a set of blocks
\mathcal{B}_{0}
such that there exists a set of jobs
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq J
and a feasible schedule of them with the following properties:

(A1)

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
,

(A2)

each job
j\in\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
is scheduled in some block
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
,

(A3)

for each block
B\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
there are at most
K_{0}=(1/\varepsilon)^{O(1/\varepsilon\log(1/\varepsilon))}

jobs of
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
that are scheduled in
B
,

(A4)

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|\geq|\mathcal{B}_{0}|/\varepsilon
.

To avoid ambiguity later, we refer to the blocks from Lemma
4
as
elementary blocks.

We remark that property
(A4)
of Lemma
4

implies that the
average
number of jobs in an elementary block
is at least
1/\varepsilon
, while by property
(A3)
the
maximum

number of jobs in each elementary block is bounded by
K_{0}=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
.

As a next step, we want to compute a partition of
[0,T)
with stronger
properties. Instead of just blocks, we want to compute superblocks
such that each superblock is the union of a set of consecutive blocks (which will be constantly
many blocks below).

Definition 5
.

A pair
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})
is a
block-superblock partition
if
both
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{S}
are sets of blocks that form a partition
of
[0,T)
and each block
S\in\mathcal{S}
is the union of a set of consecutive blocks

\mathcal{B}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. We call the blocks in
\mathcal{S}
also
superblocks.

Intuitively, we use the blocks in
\mathcal{B}_{0}
as a basis and, with
a suitable shifting argument, glue them together to form the blocks

\mathcal{B}
. We use a parameter
\Delta
that controls how many elementary blocks
we glue together here. For our polynomial-time algorithm in this section we will define
\Delta:=1
, while for the pseudopolynomial-time algorithm in the next section we will use
\Delta=O_{\varepsilon}(\log T)
.
Then, we define each superblock
as the union of (constantly) many consecutive blocks in
\mathcal{B}
. Thanks to the mentioned
shifting argument, we can ensure that there is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate
solution in which each job
j
may be scheduled only within a specific set of blocks and superblocks. In more detail, given a block-superblock partition

(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})
, for each job
j\in J
we define its
release
block
B_{j,L}\in\mathcal{B}
such that
r_{j}\in B_{j,L}
and its
deadline
block

B_{j,R}=[s,t)\in\mathcal{B}
such that
d_{j}\in(s,t]
. The release block and the deadline block of
j
are also called its
boundary
blocks. We remark that possibly
B_{j,L}=B_{j,R}=B
, in which case
\mathrm{tw}(j)\subseteq B
. Furthermore,
we say that
j

spans
a block
B\in\mathcal{B}
or a superblock

S\in\mathcal{S}
(and
B
or
S
are
spanned
by
j
) if
B\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
or
S\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
, resp. In the following lemma we use the value
K_{0}
as defined in Lemma
4
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{N}
be a given parameter, and assume that
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq 5K_{0}\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}
.

In time
\Delta^{O(1)}n^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
we can compute at most
1/\varepsilon
block-superblock partitions and for each one of them a value
K
with
K\leq\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}=O_{\varepsilon}(\Delta)

such that for at least one computed partition
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})
there exists a set of jobs

\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}\subseteq J
and a feasible schedule of them with the following properties:

(B1)

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq(1-2\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
,

(B2)

each job
j\in\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
is scheduled in
B_{j,L}
, or in
B_{j,R}
,
or in a block
B\in\mathcal{B}
for which
j
spans the superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}

containing
B
,

(B3)

for each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
there are at most
K
jobs from
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
that are scheduled in
B
,

(B4)

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{B}|
and
|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{K}{\varepsilon^{6}}|\mathcal{S}|
.

Proof.

Consider the partition into elementary blocks
\mathcal{B}_{0}
provided by Lemma
4
.
From the assumption
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq 5K_{0}\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}
and using
(A1)
and
\varepsilon\leq 1/5
, we have

|\mathcal{B}_{0}|\geq\frac{1}{K_{0}}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon)}{K_{0}}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq 5\Delta(1-\varepsilon)(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}\geq 4\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}.

We add up to
2\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}=O_{\varepsilon}(\Delta)
dummy blocks of type
[T,T)
so as to obtain a set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}
of elementary blocks whose cardinality is a multiple of
2\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}
. Notice that
|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}|\leq\frac{3}{2}|\mathcal{B}_{0}|
.
We define the partition into blocks
\mathcal{B}_{1}
by merging
2\Delta
consecutive blocks in
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}
, and define recursively
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\{2,\ldots,1/\varepsilon\}
by merging
2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5}
consecutive blocks of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell-1}
. For
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
, we define the partition
\mathcal{S}_{\ell}
into superblocks by merging
2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5}
consecutive blocks of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
. The pairs
(\mathcal{B}_{\ell},\mathcal{S}_{\ell})
for
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
are the block-superblock partitions in the claim, and we set the corresponding value of
K
to
K_{\ell}\coloneqq 2\Delta K_{0}(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{\ell-1}\leq\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}
, as required. We also observe that

•

|\mathcal{B}_{\ell}|=\frac{1}{2\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{\ell-1}}|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}|
;

•

|\mathcal{S}_{\ell}|=\frac{1}{2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5}}|\mathcal{B}_{\ell}|
;

•

\mathcal{S}_{\ell}=\mathcal{B}_{\ell+1}
for
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon-1]
.

Consider the solution
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
as in Lemma
4
with the associated schedule. We first observe that, for each
j\in\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
, there exists at most one partition
(\mathcal{B}_{\ell},\mathcal{S}_{\ell})
such that property
(B2)
is not satisfied. Indeed, consider one such level
\ell
. Let
B^{\ell}_{j,L}
and
B^{\ell}_{j,R}
(resp,
S^{\ell}_{j,L}
and
S^{\ell}_{j,R}
) be the corresponding boundary blocks (resp, superblocks). The violation of the property implies that
j
is either scheduled inside
S^{\ell}_{j,L}
but not in
B^{\ell}_{j,L}
, or inside
S^{\ell}_{j,R}
but not in
B^{\ell}_{j,R}
. Assume w.l.o.g. that the first case applies. Then
j
is scheduled within
B^{\ell^{\prime}}_{j,L}
for every
\ell^{\prime}>\ell
. At the same time,
j
is scheduled inside a spanning superblock of
(\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{\prime}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell^{\prime}})
for every
\ell^{\prime}<\ell
. In both cases property
(B2)
is satisfied.
Let
D_{\ell}
be the jobs in
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
that do not satisfy property
(B2)
w.r.t.
(\mathcal{B}_{\ell},\mathcal{S}_{\ell})
. By the above discussion such sets are disjoint, and therefore one such set
D_{\ell^{*}}
contains at most
\varepsilon|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|
jobs. We define
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}:=\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}\setminus D_{\ell^{*}}
, and for these jobs preserve the same schedule as for
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
.

We claim that the partition
(\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}},\mathcal{S}_{\ell^{*}})
together with
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
and the associated schedule satisfy all the desired properties. Trivially,
|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)^{2}|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq(1-2\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
, hence property
(B1)
holds. Each
j\in\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
is scheduled inside an elementary block, hence inside a block in
\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}
. By construction
j
satisfies property
(B2)
, otherwise it would belong to
D_{\ell^{*}}
. Each block of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}
consists of
2\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{\ell^{*}-1}
elementary blocks, and at most
K_{0}
jobs of
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}
(hence of
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
) are scheduled in each elementary block. Thus at most
K_{\ell^{*}}=2\Delta K_{0}(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{\ell^{*}-1}
jobs of
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
are scheduled inside each block of
\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}
as required by property
(B3)
. It remains to prove property
(B4)
. With
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
,

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime\prime}|\geq\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3\varepsilon}|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}|\geq\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{\ell^{*}-1}|\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}|\geq\frac{\Delta}{\varepsilon}|\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}|.

Furthermore,

\frac{2K_{0}K_{\ell^{*}}}{\varepsilon^{5}}|\mathcal{S}_{\ell^{*}}|=K_{\ell^{*}}\cdot|\mathcal{B}_{\ell^{*}}|=K_{0}\cdot|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}|,

hence

|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|\geq\frac{2(1-\varepsilon)}{3\varepsilon}|\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{0}|\geq\frac{4(1-\varepsilon)}{3\varepsilon}\cdot\frac{K_{\ell^{*}}}{\varepsilon^{5}}|\mathcal{S}_{\ell^{*}}|\geq\frac{K_{\ell^{*}}}{\varepsilon^{6}}|\mathcal{S}_{\ell^{*}}|.

∎

In the rest of this section, we will assume
\Delta=1
and w.l.o.g.
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq 5K_{0}\Delta(2K_{0}/\varepsilon^{5})^{1/\varepsilon}
, since otherwise

the problem can be solved exactly in polynomial time by enumeration. Therefore, we
can apply Lemma
6
. Intuitively, among the
1/\varepsilon
computed block-superblock partitions, we guess one for which properties
(B1)
-
(B4)
hold. Formally, we execute the following steps for each of them and finally output the best obtained solution.
Hence, in the analysis we may assume that we know the
partition
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})
, together with its corresponding value
K
, for which the described solution
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}

exists.

2.2
The linear program

We define a configuration-LP which intuitively computes a (fractional)
solution that satisfies
(B2)
and
(B3)
.
A configuration
C
is specified by a pair
(B_{C},J_{C})
, where
B_{C}=[s,t)
is a block and
J_{C}
is a subset of jobs that can be feasibly scheduled inside
B_{C}
according to Lemma
6
; formally, we require that
|J_{C}|\leq K
and that, for each
j\in J_{C}
, the block
B_{C}
is either a boundary block for
j
or
B_{C}
is contained in a superblock
S
spanned by
j
(i.e.,
S\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
). We denote by
s_{C}:J_{C}\rightarrow\{s,\ldots,t-1\}
an arbitrary but fixed feasible schedule of
J_{C}
inside
B_{C}
.
For each block
B
we define the set
\mathcal{C}(B)

to be the set of all configurations for
B
and we set
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mathcal{C}(B)
.
In our LP, for each configuration
C\in\mathcal{C}
we introduce a variable

x_{C}
representing whether we select
C
for its corresponding
block. Then, we introduce constraints to model that we schedule each
job at most once and we select one configuration for each block.

\begin{split}\max\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}|J_{C}|\cdot x_{C}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}:j\in J_{C}}x_{C}&\leq 1\quad\text{$\forall$}j\in J\\
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}x_{C}&=1\quad\text{$\forall$}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
x_{C}&\geq 0\quad\text{$\forall$}C\in\mathcal{C}.\end{split}

(
LP
)

Due to Lemma
6
, the optimal objective function value of (
LP
) is at least close to
|\mathrm{OPT}|
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{S})
be a block-superblock partition satisfying properties
(B1)
-
(B4)
of Lemma
6
. Then the optimal objective function value of the associated configuration LP is at least
(1-2\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
.

Proof.

Let
(x^{\prime}_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
be the integral solution of (
LP
) which encodes
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
, i.e., for each configuration
C\in\mathcal{C}
, set
x^{\prime}_{C}=1
if
J_{C}
is the set of all the jobs in
\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}
that are scheduled inside
B_{C}
, and set
x^{\prime}_{C}=0
otherwise.
Clearly,
(x^{\prime}_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
is a feasible solution to (
LP
) and, by
(B1)
,
(1-2\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|\leq|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}x^{\prime}_{C}|J_{C}|
is a lower bound of the optimum of (
LP
).
∎

Obviously, we can solve the above LP in polynomial time when
K
, i.e., the number of jobs per configuration, is upper bounded by a constant.

Lemma 8
.

Let
K
denote the maximum number of jobs in a configuration. If
K=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
,
then in polynomial time one can compute an optimal solution
(x^{*}_{C})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
to the configuration LP together with a feasible schedule
s_{C}
for each configuration
C\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

There are at most
O(n)
blocks and for each block
B
there are at most
n^{O(K)}
subsets
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
of jobs of cardinality at most
K
that can be feasibly scheduled inside
B
. For each such set
J^{\prime}
, we can check whether there exists an associated feasible schedule in time
K!\cdot n^{O(1)}
by considering any ordering of the jobs in
J^{\prime}
, and computing a greedy schedule (if any) of
J^{\prime}
respecting that order. Hence we can fully specify the configuration LP in time
n^{O(K)}
. This LP has
n^{O(K)}
variables,
O(n)
constraints besides the non-negativity ones, and integral non-negative coefficients upper bounded by
K=O(n)
. Thus the LP can be solved in time
n^{O(K)}
.
∎

2.3
Rounding algorithm

Let
(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
denote an optimal solution to (
LP
).
We describe now how to round it to an integral solution using randomized
rounding, losing at most a factor of
4/3+O(\varepsilon)
in the profit.
For each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
independently, we sample one configuration

C^{*}(B)\in\mathcal{C}(B)
with respect to the distribution given by the vector

(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}
. More precisely, each configuration

C\in\mathcal{C}(B)
is sampled with probability
x_{C}^{*}
and, deterministically,
exactly one configuration in
\mathcal{C}(B)
is sampled.

For each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
and each job
j\in J_{C^{*}(B)}
, there
is an interval
[s_{C^{*}(B)}(j),s_{C^{*}(B)}(j)+p_{j})
during which
j
is scheduled
by
s_{C^{*}(B)}
. We call this interval a
slot
and we define

Q
to be the set of all slots for all blocks
B\in\mathcal{B}
and all
jobs
j\in J_{C^{*}(B)}
. Note that the slots in
Q
are pairwise
disjoint. We want to assign a subset of the jobs in
J
to the slots
in
Q
via a bipartite matching. Possibly, this will assign a job

j\in J
to a slot
[s,t)
corresponding to a different
job
j^{\prime}\neq j
.

Formally, we define a bipartite graph
G=(V,E)
where
V=J\dot{\cup}Q
and there is an edge
\{j,[s,t)]\}\in E
connecting (the vertices corresponding to) a job
j\in J
and a slot
[s,t)\in Q

if and only if
j
can be scheduled during
[s,t)
; the latter conditions holds if and only if
\min\{t,d_{j}\}-\max\{s,r_{j}\}\geq p_{j}
, in which case
j
can be scheduled during
[\max\{s,r_{j}\},\max\{s,r_{j}\}+p_{j})
.
We compute a maximum matching
M^{*}\subseteq E
in
G
.
For each edge
e=(j,[s,t))\in M^{*}

we select job
j
and schedule it during
[\max\{s,r_{j}\},\max\{s,r_{j}\}+p_{j})
.
Finally, we output the resulting solution.

2.4
Analysis

By construction, we schedule
|M^{*}|
jobs in total. Hence, it remains
to show that
M^{*}
is sufficiently large. To formalize this, we
call a job
j\in J

local
if
\mathrm{tw}(j)\subseteq B
for some
block
B\in\mathcal{B}
(note that then
B_{j,L}=B_{j,R}
) and
global

otherwise. We denote by
J_{\mathrm{local}}
and
J_{\mathrm{global}}
the local and global jobs
in
J
, respectively. We prove that, compared to
(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
,
we lose a factor of
4/3+O(\varepsilon)
in the profit of the global jobs
and a factor of
1+O(\varepsilon)
for the local ones. For convenience,
for each job
j\in J
we define
y_{j}^{*}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}:j\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}

which is the total fractional extent to which
j
is selected in

(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
.

Lemma 9
.

For any
\Delta\geq 1
we have

{\mathbb{E}}[|M^{*}|]\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}+(3/4-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}\geq(3/4-O(\varepsilon))|\mathrm{OPT}|.

In the remainder of this section we prove Lemma
9
.
For this, we construct a feasible fractional bipartite matching, i.e., a function
f:E\rightarrow[0,1]
such that
\sum_{e:v\in e}f(e)\leq 1
for each
v\in V
.
We will prove that its expected size
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{e\in E}f(e)\right]
is at least the lower bound we want
to prove for
{\mathbb{E}}[|M^{*}|]
. Since the standard LP-relaxation of the bipartite matching problem is
integral (see
[
51
]
), there exists also an integral matching with at least
\sum_{e\in E}f(e)
edges.

Consider a job
j\in J
. If the sampled configurations
C^{*}(B_{j,L})

for its release block
B_{j,L}
contains
j
, then we match
j

integrally to the corresponding slot. Formally, in this case let
[s,t):=[s_{C^{*}(B_{j,L})}(j),s_{C^{*}(B_{j,L})}(j)+p_{j})

denote the slot corresponding to
j
; we
define
f(\{j,[s,t)\}):=1
. If this is not the case but the sampled
configurations
C^{*}(B_{j,R})
for its deadline block
B_{j,R}

contains
j
, then, similarly, we match
j
to the corresponding
slot. We do this operation for each job
j\in J
. Let
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}\subseteq J

denote the set of all jobs that were (integrally) matched in this way.

We want to define a fractional matching for the remaining jobs
J\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
.
We do this separately for each superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
. Let
J_{S}\subseteq J
denote
all jobs in
J
that span
S
. Note that this might include jobs
in
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
. We want to define a fractional matching for the jobs in

J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
. For each job
j\in J_{S}
we define the total
fractional amount by which
j
is assigned to
S
in
(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}

by
y_{j,S}^{*}:=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B):j\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}
.
Similar to harmonic grouping
[
45
,
54
]
, we partition the jobs in
J_{S}

into
1/\varepsilon
groups
J_{1},...,J_{1/\varepsilon}
ordered non-increasingly by their processing
times such that the jobs from each group contribute almost the same
to the profit of
(x_{C}^{*})_{C\in\mathcal{C}}
within
S
.

Lemma 10
.

There exists a partition
J_{1},...,J_{1/\varepsilon}

of
J_{S}
such that

(C1)

for each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon-1]
, each job
j\in J_{\ell}
, and each
job
j^{\prime}\in J_{\ell+1}
we have that
p_{j}\geq p_{j^{\prime}}
,

(C2)

for each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we have that

\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{j\in J_{S}}y_{j,S}^{*}-1\leq\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}\leq 1+\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{j\in J_{S}}y_{j,S}^{*}.

Proof.

Assume that
J_{S}=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}\}
such that
p_{j_{i}}\geq p_{j_{i^{\prime}}}
for all
i,i^{\prime}\in[k]
with
i<i^{\prime}
. Let
Y^{*}=\sum_{j\in J_{S}}y_{j,S}^{*}
denote the total fractional amount of scheduled jobs from
J_{S}
in
S
.
For each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
let
t(\ell)\in[k]
be the maximal index
t
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{j_{i},S}^{*}\leq\ell\cdot\varepsilon Y^{*}
.
Also, let
t(0):=0
.
We define our partition by setting
J_{\ell}:=\{j_{i}\in J_{S}:t(\ell-1)<i\leq t(\ell)\}
.
Clearly, this is indeed a partition of
J_{S}
.
Also for
j_{i}\in J_{\ell}
,
j_{i^{\prime}}\in J_{\ell+1}
we have
i<i^{\prime}
and thus
p_{j_{i}}\geq p_{j_{i^{\prime}}}
.

Let
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]\cup\{0\}
. By definition of
t(\ell)
and the fact that
y_{j,S}^{*}\in[0,1]
for each
j\in J_{S}
we have
\ell\cdot\varepsilon Y^{*}-1\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t(\ell)}y_{j_{i},S}^{*}\leq\ell\cdot\varepsilon Y^{*}
.
Thus we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon Y^{*}-1

\displaystyle=(\ell\cdot\varepsilon Y^{*}-1)-(\ell-1)\varepsilon Y^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t(\ell)}y_{j,S}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{t(\ell-1)}y_{j,S}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\ell\cdot\varepsilon Y^{*}-((\ell-1)\varepsilon Y^{*}-1)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon Y^{*}+1

For each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we have
\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{t(\ell)}y_{j,S}^{*}-\sum_{i=1}^{t(\ell-1)}y_{j,S}^{*}
.
Altogether, this implies
\varepsilon Y^{*}-1\leq\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}\leq\varepsilon Y^{*}+1
, which completes the proof.
∎

For each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we define
n_{\ell}:=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}
.
Note that Lemma
10
implies that
n_{\ell^{\prime}}-2\leq n_{\ell}\leq n_{\ell^{\prime}}+2

for any
\ell,\ell^{\prime}\in[1/\varepsilon]
. Intuitively, when we define the
fractional matching for the jobs in
J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
we will ignore
the jobs in
J_{1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
and for each
\ell\geq 2
we will
match the jobs in
J_{\ell}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
fractionally to the slots
corresponding to jobs in
J_{\ell-1}
. In particular, here we may
use slots that correspond to jobs in
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
(and to which we have not yet assigned any jobs). We would like that after
sampling the configurations for the blocks, for each group
J_{\ell}

we obtain (essentially)
n_{\ell}
slots in
S
corresponding to
jobs in
J_{\ell}
. If
n_{\ell}
is sufficiently large, we can
show that this is indeed the case with concentration arguments, using
that each superblock contains many blocks and the block’s configurations
are sampled independently. On the other hand, if
n_{\ell}
is small
then we can simply ignore the profit of jobs in
J_{S}
within
S
: indeed, by Lemma
6
, each superblock contains on average
\frac{K}{\varepsilon^{6}}
jobs.
Formally,
for each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we define
\bar{N}_{\ell}
to be the
(random) number of slots corresponding to jobs in
J_{\ell}
in the sampled
configurations
\{C^{*}(B)\}_{B\in\mathcal{B}:B\subseteq S}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
. With probability
at least
1-\varepsilon^{2}
we have that

\bar{N}_{\ell}\geq(1-\varepsilon)n_{\ell}-2K/\varepsilon^{3}
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{B}(S):=\{B\in\mathcal{B}:B\subseteq S\}
denotes all blocks in the superblock
S
.
For each
B\in\mathcal{B}(S)
let
Z_{B}
denote the number of jobs from
J_{\ell}
in the sampled configuration
C^{*}(B)
and let
Z_{B}^{\prime}=Z_{B}/K
.
As there are at most
K
jobs in a configuration, we have
Z_{B}^{\prime}\in[0,1]
.
Note that
\bar{N}_{\ell}=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}Z_{B}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\bar{N}_{\ell}]=n_{\ell}
.
If
n_{\ell}<\frac{2K}{\varepsilon^{3}}
, there is nothing to show. Hence in the following we assume
n_{\ell}\geq\frac{2K}{\varepsilon^{3}}
and thus
{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}_{S}}Z_{B}^{\prime}]\geq\frac{2}{\varepsilon^{3}}
. It is sufficient to show that
\bar{N}_{\ell}<(1-\varepsilon)n_{\ell}
happens with probability at most
\varepsilon^{2}
. As the random variable
Z_{B}^{\prime}
depends only on the sampled configuration for the block
B
and these configurations are sampled independently, the random variables
Z_{B}^{\prime}
for
B\in\mathcal{B}_{S}
are independent.
Thus, we can apply Chernoff’s bound
[
23
]
and obtain the following;

\displaystyle\Pr\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}Z_{B}^{\prime}<(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}Z_{B}^{\prime}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}Z_{B}^{\prime}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\leq\varepsilon^{2}.

As
\bar{N}_{\ell}<(1-\varepsilon)n_{\ell}
is equivalent to
\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}Z_{B}^{\prime}<(1-\varepsilon){\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}(S)}Z_{B}^{\prime}\right]
, this completes the proof.
∎

If the

event due to Lemma
11
does not
happen for some
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
, then we simply do not match the jobs in
J_{S}
to the slots contained
in
S
in our fractional matching. Since this happens only with probability

\varepsilon
, this influences our expected profit only marginally. Otherwise,
for each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we have essentially
n_{\ell}
slots
available corresponding to the jobs in
J_{\ell}
. Therefore, since

n_{\ell+1}\approx n_{\ell}
we can (fractionally) match essentially

n_{\ell+1}
jobs from
J_{\ell+1}
to these slots. Recall that

\sum_{j\in J_{\ell+1}}y_{j,S}^{*}=n_{\ell+1}
. Hence, we
could match each job
j\in J_{\ell+1}
to a fractional extent of

y_{j,S}^{*}
. Since the jobs in
J_{\ell+1}\cap J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
are already
matched, we can even match each remaining job
j\in J_{\ell+1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}

to a larger fractional extent than only
y_{j,S}^{*}
.

Let us define this increased extent. For each job
j\in J
,
let
y_{j,L}^{*}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B_{j,L}):j\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}

and
y_{j,R}^{*}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B_{j,R}):j\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}
,
i.e., the probabilities that
j
is contained in the sampled configuration
for
B_{j,L}
and
B_{j,R}
, resp. Hence, each job
j\in J_{\ell+1}

is
not
contained in
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
with probability
(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})
.
Thus, in expectation, the sum of the values
y_{j,S}^{*}
for the
(remaining) jobs in
J_{\ell+1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
equals
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\ell+1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}}y_{j,S}^{*}\right]=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell+1}}y_{j,S}^{*}(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})
.
Therefore, we try to match each job
j\in J_{\ell+1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}

even to an increased extent of
\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}
.
In expectation, the sum of those values is then

\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum_{j\in J_{\ell+1}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}}\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}\bigg]=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell+1}}y_{j,S}^{*}=n_{\ell+1}.

Via concentration arguments, we can argue that the sum of those values is also sufficiently concentrated around
n_{\ell+1}
, implying that we can find a matching of size close to
n_{\ell}
in expectation. As mentioned in the introduction, the dependencies among the variables do not allow us to apply the standard Chernoff’s bound. However, we are able to show that the impact of such dependencies is sufficiently small, hence we have sufficient concentration.
To define our matching formally,
let
Q_{S}\subseteq Q
denote all slots in the blocks
\mathcal{B}(S)

corresponding to jobs in
J_{S}
and let
E_{S}\subseteq E
denote
all edges in
E
that connect a vertex (corresponding to a job) in

J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
with a slot in
Q_{S}
.

Lemma 12
.

For each superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
, there exists a fractional matching
f_{S}:E_{S}\rightarrow[0,1]

such that

•

{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{e\in E_{S}}f_{S}(e)]\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{S}}y_{j,S}^{*}-2K/\varepsilon^{5}
and

•

for each
j\in J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
we have

\sum_{q:\{j,q\}\in E_{S}}f_{S}(\{j,q\})\leq\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}\leq 1.

We will prove Lemma
12
in Section
2.5
.
We combine all these matchings for the superblocks
S\in\mathcal{S}
,
together with the values for
f
that we defined already for the jobs in
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
. Formally,
for each each superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
and each edge
e\in E_{S}

we define
f(e):=f_{S}(e)
. For each edge
e^{\prime}\in E
for which we
have not defined the value
f(e^{\prime})
yet, we set
f(e^{\prime}):=0
.

It remains to argue that
\sum_{e\in E}f(e)
is sufficiently large
in expectation. For each job
j\in J
we define the extent to which

j
is matched in
f
by
g(j):=\sum_{q\in Q:\{j,q\}\in E}f(\{j,q\})
.

We can easily show for each (local) job
j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}
that
g(j)=1

with probability
y_{j}^{*}
.

Lemma 13
.

For each job
j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}
we have that

\Pr[g(j)=1]=y_{j}^{*}
.

Proof.

There is a unique block
B\in\mathcal{B}
containing
\mathrm{tw}(j)
. Hence,

g(j)=1
iff for
B
we sampled a configuration
C

containing
j
. This happens with probability
\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B):j\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}=y_{j}^{*}
.
∎

Consider a (global) job
j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
. At the beginning, we matched
j

to one slot in
B_{j,L}
or
B_{j,R}
with probability
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}-y_{j,L}^{*}\cdot y_{j,R}^{*}
.
On the other hand, the LP-solution obtains a profit of
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}

from assigning
j
fractionally to
B_{j,L}
and
B_{j,R}
. However,
since
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}\leq 1
the latter profit is by at most
a factor of
4/3
larger than the probability that we matched
j

to some slot in
B_{j,L}
or
B_{j,R}
.

Proposition 14
.

For each job
j\in J
we have that
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}\leq\frac{4}{3}(y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}-y_{j,L}^{*}\cdot y_{j,R}^{*})
.

Proof.

Note that
y_{j,L}^{*}\geq 0,y_{j,R}^{*}\geq 0
.
Due to the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality we have
y^{*}_{j,L}\cdot y^{*}_{j,R}\leq\left(\frac{y^{*}_{j,L}+y^{*}_{j,R}}{2}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{y^{*}_{j,L}+y^{*}_{j,R}}{4}
, where in the last inequality we used
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}\leq 1
. Therefore

y^{*}_{j,L}+y^{*}_{j,R}-y^{*}_{j,L}\cdot y^{*}_{j,R}\geq\frac{3}{4}(y^{*}_{j,L}+y^{*}_{j,R})
.
∎

Assume now that
j
is not matched to a slot in
B_{j,L}
nor in
B_{j,R}
, which
happens with probability
(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})
. Roughly
speaking, for each superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
spanned by
j
, we match

j
fractionally to a total extent of
(1-O(\varepsilon))\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}

to the blocks in
\mathcal{B}(S)
. More precisely, this holds on average
over all the global jobs
j
spanning each superblock
S
. Using this,
we can prove the following bound for the fractionally matched global

Lemma 15
.

For any
\Delta\geq 1
we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}g(j)\right]\geq\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-O(\varepsilon)|OPT^{\prime}|.

Proof.

Recall that we fully matched the jobs in
J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
and combined this with the matchings
f_{S}
for each superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
.
This implies

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}g(j)\right]=E\left[|J_{\mathrm{bnd}}\cap J_{\mathrm{global}}|\right]+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{e\in E_{S}}f_{S}(e)\right].

By construction we have for each
j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
that
\Pr[j\in J_{\mathrm{bnd}}]=y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}-y_{j,L}^{*}\cdot y_{j,R}^{*}
.
Using Proposition
14
we obtain
\Pr[j\in J_{\mathrm{bnd}}]\geq\frac{3}{4}(y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*})
and consequently
{\mathbb{E}}[|J_{\mathrm{bnd}}\cap J_{\mathrm{global}}|]=3/4\cdot\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}(y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*})
.
Now we combine this with Lemma
12
and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}g(j)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}(y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*})+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}}\left((1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{S}}y_{j,S}^{*}-2K/\varepsilon^{5}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}\left(y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}+\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}:S\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)}y_{j,S}^{*}\right)-2K/\varepsilon^{5}\cdot|\mathcal{S}|

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-2K/\varepsilon^{5}\cdot|\mathcal{S}|

By Lemma
6
,
(B4)
, we have

2K/\varepsilon^{5}\cdot|\mathcal{S}|\leq 2\varepsilon|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|
.
Thus together with the above, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}g(j)\right]\geq\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-2\varepsilon|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, Lemma
9
essentially follows Lemmas
7
,
13
,

15
, and the integrality of the bipartite
matching polytope.

Proof of Lemma
9
.

By Lemmas
13
and

15
, there exists a fractional matching of expected size

\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}+\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-\varepsilon|OPT^{\prime}|
.
By Lemma

we have
\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}^{*}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)\mathrm{OPT}
, which implies that the fractional matching has an expected size of at least

(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}+(3/4-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}\geq(3/4-O(\varepsilon))|\mathrm{OPT}|.

As the standard matching LP is integral on bipartite graphs, there is always an integral matching of size equal to the optimal LP value, which completes the proof.
∎

Theorem

holds by choosing
\Delta=1
and combining Lemmas

,

, and
9
.

Proof of Theorem

.

By Lemma

, the computed matching has a size of at least
\frac{3}{4}(1-O(\varepsilon))\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|
. By rescaling
\varepsilon
appropriately, we obtain an
\big(\frac{4}{3}+\varepsilon\big)

The maximum matching can be computed in time

.

.

∎

2.5
Proof of Lemma
12

Let us focus on a specific superblock
S\in\mathcal{S}
. We would like to match a job
j
to an extend of

Y_{j}:=\begin{cases}\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}&\text{ if }j\in J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}\\
0&\text{ if }j\in J_{\mathrm{bnd}}\end{cases}

Consider the sets
J_{\ell}
as defined in Lemma
10
, and
define
N_{\ell}:=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}Y_{j}
for
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]\setminus\{1\}
.
First, we show some technical properties.

Proposition 16
.

For each
j\in J_{S}
we have (deterministically)
Y_{j}\leq 1
and for each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we have
{\mathbb{E}}[N_{\ell}]=n_{\ell}
.

Proof.

Let
j\in J_{S}
.
As
y_{j,L}^{*}+y_{j,R}^{*}+\sum_{S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}:S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)}y^{*}_{j,S^{\prime}}\leq 1
due to the constraints of (
LP
), we have
y_{j,S}^{*}\leq 1-y_{j,L}^{*}-y_{j,R}^{*}\leq(1-y_{j,L})(1-y_{j,R})
. This implies
Y_{j}\leq 1
.

As
\Pr[j\in J_{S}\setminus J_{\mathrm{bnd}}]=(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})
, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j}]=y_{j,S}^{*}
Summing over all
j\in J_{\ell}
, we obtain
{\mathbb{E}}[N_{\ell}]=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}=n_{\ell}
.
∎

As already mentioned, we want to match a job
j\in J_{\ell}
to an extend of
Y_{j}
to slots corresponding to jobs in
J_{\ell-1}
.
As the time window of
j
spans
S
and each slot corresponding to a job in
J_{\ell-1}
has a length of at least
p_{j}
due to Lemma
10
, we can match
j
to any slot corresponding to any job in
J_{\ell-1}
.
The total extend to which we want to match jobs is
N_{\ell}
and the total number of slots is
\bar{N}_{\ell-1}
.
As we have a complete bipartite graph between these sets of nodes, the maximum matching has a size of
\min\{N_{\ell},\bar{N}_{\ell-1}\}
.
Therefore, the major part of the proof is devoted to show that
\min\{N_{\ell},\bar{N}_{\ell-1}\}
is in expectation close to
n_{\ell}
.

In the following paragraph, we give some intuition.
By Lemma
11
we already know that
\bar{N}_{\ell-1}
is close to
n_{\ell}
with probability
1-\varepsilon^{2}
.
So the goal is to obtain a similar bound for
N_{\ell}
.
We know that
{\mathbb{E}}[N_{\ell}]=n_{\ell}
by Proposition
16
, so we only need some kind of concentration for
N_{\ell}
.
Recall that
Y_{j}
depends on the sampled configurations for the boundary blocks for
j
.
This dependence also implies that the random variables
Y_{j}
are not independent.
Hence we cannot directly apply standard Chernoff’s bound to obtain the desired concentration for
N_{\ell}
.
To our advantage, the correlation between the random variables is still quite low:
If two random variables
Y_{j}
and
Y_{j^{\prime}}
are not independent, then there must be a block which is a boundary block for both
j
and
j^{\prime}
.
We make this dependence even weaker. We define a new random variable
\tilde{Y}_{j}
, which equals
Y_{j}
except if there is a boundary block
B
of
j
with
\Pr[j\in J_{C^{*}(B)}]<\varepsilon
and nevertheless we sample a configuration
C^{*}(B)
with
j\in J_{C^{*}(B)}
.
In this case
\tilde{Y}_{j}
behaves the same as
Y_{j}
behaves when we sample a configuration
C^{*}(B)
with
j\not\in J_{C^{*}(B)}
.
As this event only occurs with probability
\varepsilon
this sacrifices only an
\varepsilon
fraction of the profit in expectation.
The advantage of this is that for a job
j
, there are now only
2K/\varepsilon
other jobs
j^{\prime}
for which
Y_{j}
and
Y_{j^{\prime}}
are not independent. Indeed, each such job
j^{\prime}
needs to share a boundary block with
j
, and
j^{\prime}
must be scheduled in this boundary block with probability at least
\varepsilon
. Since at most
K
fractional jobs can be scheduled in a block, there can be at most
K/\varepsilon
jobs fractionally assigned to a block by an amount of at least
\varepsilon
. This is enough independence to obtain the desired concentration for
N_{\ell}
, using the results by
[
27
]
.

Now we make this formal.
First we introduce the concept of read-
k
families (see
[
27
]
), which are defined as follows.

Definition 17

(Read-
k
families
[
27
]
)
.

Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{b}
be independent random variables and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
J^{\prime}
be a finite set. For each
j\in J^{\prime}
, let
A_{j}\subseteq[b]
and let
f_{j}:(C_{p})_{p\in A_{j}}\to[0,1]
. Assume that
|\{j:\;b^{\prime}\in A_{j}\}|\leq k
for every
b^{\prime}\in[b]
.
Then the random variables
Z_{j}=f_{j}\big((C_{p})_{p\in A_{j}}\big)
for
j\in J^{\prime}
are called a
read-
k
family
.

Intuitively, in our setting the random variables
C_{1},\dots,C_{b}
are the sampled configurations
C^{*}(B)
for the blocks
B\in\mathcal{B}
. For every job
j\in J_{S}
, the set
A_{j}
is a subset of the boundary blocks for
j
, and
Z_{j}
is a random variable
close to

Y_{j}
. For each job
j\in J_{S}
, we would like to choose
A_{j}
as the two boundary blocks for
j
and
Z_{j}
as the random variable
Y_{j}
, as the value of
Y_{j}
is determined by the sampled configurations for two boundary blocks for
j
, i.e.
B_{j,L}
and
B_{j,R}
.
But this doesn’t result in a read-
k
-family (for a reasonable value of
k
) as it might be that all jobs are released within the same block and thus all have the same boundary block and all depend on the same sampled configuration.
Therefore, we will define new random variables
\tilde{Y}_{j}
with stronger independence properties (which will form the read-
k
-family) as follows:

\tilde{Y}_{j}:=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }y_{j,L}^{*}\geq\varepsilon\text{ and }j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,L})}\\
0&\text{ if }y_{j,R}^{*}\geq\varepsilon\text{ and }j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,R})}\\
\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

and let
\tilde{N}_{\ell}:=\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}\tilde{Y}_{j}
.
Using these random variables, we obtain the following properties.

Lemma 18
.

The following holds.

•

For each
\ell\in[1/\varepsilon]
we have
\tilde{N}_{\ell}\geq N_{\ell}
and
{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{N}_{\ell}-N_{\ell}]\leq O(\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}
.

•

The random variables
\tilde{Y}_{j}
for
j\in J_{S}
are a read-
(K/\varepsilon)
family.

Proof.

We start with the first property. Let
j\in J_{S}
.
By construction, the random variables
Y_{j}
and
\tilde{Y}_{j}
can only take the two values
0
and
\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}
.
Note that
\tilde{Y}_{j}=0
implies
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,L})}
or
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,R})}
. Both imply
j\in J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
and thus
Y_{j}=0
.
Thus we obtain
\tilde{Y}_{j}\geq Y_{j}
and by summing this over all
j\in J_{\ell}
we obtain
\tilde{N}_{\ell}\geq N_{\ell}
.

Let
j\in J_{S}
.
Suppose that
\tilde{Y}_{j}>Y_{j}
, i.e.,
Y_{j}=0
and
\tilde{Y}_{j}>0
.
This implies
j\in J_{\mathrm{bnd}}
and thus
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,L})}
or
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,R})}
.
The fact that
\tilde{Y}_{j}>0
implies that there exists
X\in\{L,R\}
such that
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,X})}
and
y_{j,X}^{*}<\varepsilon
.
So
\tilde{Y}_{j}>Y_{j}
implies that there is
X\in\{L,R\}
with
y_{j,X}^{*}<\varepsilon
and
j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,X})}
.

We condition on the event that
\tilde{Y}_{j}>0
.
A crucial observation in that this conditioning is independent from the sampled configuration
C^{*}(B_{j,L})
(resp.,
C^{*}(B_{j,R})
) if
y_{j,L}^{*}<\varepsilon
(resp.,
y_{j,R}^{*}<\varepsilon
).
Thus for
X\in\{L,R\}
with
y_{j,X}<\varepsilon
we have
\Pr[j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,X})}|\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]\leq y_{j,X}^{*}\leq\varepsilon
.
This implies

\displaystyle\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{j}>Y_{j}|\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{X\in\{L,R\}:y_{j,X}^{*}<\varepsilon}\Pr[j\in J_{C^{*}(B_{j,R})}|\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]\leq 2\varepsilon.

Note that
\tilde{Y}_{j}=\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}
when
\tilde{Y}_{j}>0
.
Thus

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{Y}_{j}-Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}\cdot\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{j}>Y_{j}]

\displaystyle=\frac{y_{j,S}^{*}}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}\cdot\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]\cdot\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{j}>Y_{j}|\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon\Pr[\tilde{Y}_{j}>0]}{(1-y_{j,L}^{*})(1-y_{j,R}^{*})}y_{j,S}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\varepsilon}{(1-\varepsilon)^{2}}y_{j,S}^{*}

\displaystyle=O(\varepsilon)\cdot y_{j,S}^{*}.

Again, summing over all
j\in J_{\ell}
yields
{\mathbb{E}}[\tilde{N}_{\ell}-N_{\ell}]\leq O(\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{j\in J_{\ell}}y_{j,S}^{*}
.

Now we show the second property.
By construction
\tilde{Y}_{j}
can only depend on the sampled configurations for the blocks
B_{j,L}
and
B_{j,R}
and not on any other block in
\mathcal{B}
.
Let
X\in\{L,R\}
. Then
\tilde{Y}_{j}
depends on the configuration of the block
B_{j,X}
only if
y_{j,X}^{*}\geq\varepsilon
.
Consider a block
B\in\mathcal{B}
. For
\tilde{Y}_{j}
to be dependent on
C^{*}(B)
, the above implies that
j
is scheduled to an extend of
\varepsilon
within
B
by the optimal LP solution, i.e.,
B=B_{j,L}
and
y_{j,L}\geq\varepsilon
or
B=B_{j,R}
and
y_{j,R}\geq\varepsilon
.
Notice that at most
K
jobs can be fractionally scheduled within any block
B
. Indeed,

\sum_{j\in J}y^{*}_{j,B}=\sum_{j\in J}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B):j\in J_{C}}x^{*}_{C}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}|J_{C}|x^{*}_{C}\leq K.

Therefore there can be at most
K/\varepsilon
jobs dependent on any block
B
.
This implies that the random variables
\tilde{Y}_{j}
for
j\in J_{S}
are a read-
K/\varepsilon
family, by choosing
C_{1},\dots,C_{|\mathcal{B}|}
as the sampled configurations
C^{*}(B)
for the blocks
B\in\mathcal{B}
and for each
j\in J_{S}
the set
A_{j}
contains the block
B\in\mathcal{B}
if and only if
B=B_{j,L}
and
y_{j,L}\geq\varepsilon
or
B=B_{j,R}
and
y_{j,R}\geq\varepsilon
.
∎

As we now have a read-
K/\varepsilon
family of random variables, we can apply
[
27
, Theorem 1.1]
. This states that the adjustment of Chernoff’s bound also holds for read-
k
families, when the exponent is divided by
k
.

Lemma 19

(
[
27
]
)
.

Let
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{r}
be a family of read-
k

variables taking values in
[0,1]
. Then for any
\delta>0
we have

\Pr\Big[\sum_{s=1}^{r}Y_{s}<(1-\delta){\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{s=1}^{r}Y_{s}\big]\Big]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2\cdot k}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{s=1}^{r}Y_{s}\big]\right).

Proof.

The statement in
[
27
]
is slightly more general. Let
p={\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{s}Y_{s}\big]/r
.
In
[
27
]
it is shown that, for each
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
,

\Pr\Big[\sum_{s=1}^{r}Y_{s}<(p-\varepsilon^{\prime})r\Big]\leq\exp\left(-D_{KL}(p-\varepsilon^{\prime}||p)\cdot\frac{r}{k}\right)

where
D_{KL}(q||p):=q\ln(\frac{q}{p})+(1-q)\ln(\frac{1-q}{1-p})
is the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
We use the standard lower bound

D_{KL}(q||p)\geq\frac{(p-q)^{2}}{2\cdot p}\quad\text{for }0<q\leq p<1

(1)

for obtaining a multiplicative Chernoff’s bound. We prove (
1
) later for completeness. Let us first complete the proof using this lower bound.
We choose
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\delta\cdot p
and obtain

D_{KL}(p-\varepsilon^{\prime}||p)\cdot r\geq\frac{(\delta p)^{2}}{2p}\cdot r=\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\sum_{s=1}^{r}Y_{s}\big]

which yields the desired result.

Thus it remains to prove (
1
).
Consider the function
f:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
,
f(x)=x\cdot\ln x
.
Using the fundamental theorem of calculus, we obtain some bounds in
f(x)
.
We have
f^{\prime}(x)=1+\ln x
and
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=1/x
.
For
x\geq 1
, we have
f^{\prime}(x)\geq 1
, which implies for all
x\geq 1

f(x)=f(1)+\int_{t=1}^{x}f^{\prime}(t)\,dt\geq f(1)+\int_{t=1}^{x}1\,dt=x-1

Similarly, for

\displaystyle f(x)=f(1)-\int_{t=x}^{1}f^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=f(1)-\int_{t=x}^{1}\Big(f^{\prime}(1)-\int_{s=t}^{1}f^{\prime\prime}(s)\,ds\Big)dt

\displaystyle\geq f(1)-f^{\prime}(1)(1-x)+\frac{f^{\prime\prime}(1)}{2}(x-1)^{2}=(x-1)+\frac{1}{2}(x-1)^{2}.

We can rewrite the Kullback-Leibler divergence using the function
f

D_{KL}(q||p)=p\cdot f\Big(\frac{q}{p}\Big)+(1-p)f\Big(\frac{1-q}{1-p}\Big)

As we assumed

, we have
\frac{q}{p}\leq 1
and
\frac{1-q}{1-p}\geq 1
.
Now we plug in the above bounds and obtain

\displaystyle D_{KL}(q||p)

\displaystyle=p\cdot f\Big(\frac{q}{p}\Big)+(1-p)f\Big(\frac{1-q}{1-p}\Big)

\displaystyle\geq p\cdot\left(\frac{q}{p}-1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{q}{p}-1\right)^{2}\right)+(1-p)\cdot\left(\frac{1-q}{1-p}-1\right)

\displaystyle=q-p+\frac{(q-p)^{2}}{2p}+(1-q)-(1-p)

\displaystyle=\frac{(q-p)^{2}}{2p}.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.1
Solving the linear program and instances with small optimal solutions

We resolve the above mentioned issues related to our choice of
\Delta=\Theta_{\varepsilon}(\log T)
with an algorithmic technique that, intuitively, searches for solutions with only few jobs and computes the best solution of this kind, even in a weighted generalization of our problem.
First, we show that we can solve (

)

2^{O(K)}\cdot(nT)^{O(1)}

\displaystyle\min\sum_{j\in J}\alpha_{j}+\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\beta_{B}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\sum_{j\in J_{C}}\alpha_{j}+\beta_{B}

\displaystyle\alpha_{j},\beta_{B}

The non-trivial task of the separation oracle is to determine, for a given tentative solution

Due to Lemma

In the following, we present now an alternative LP-rounding

In the latter one, we sampled a configuration for each block and then
we assigned the jobs to the blocks. Instead, now we randomly assign each

possibly by discarding some of its assigned jobs.

We ignore all the (local) jobs

For each job

in expectation.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[|J_{\mathrm{long}}(B)\cap J_{B}^{\ell}|]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}\cap J_{B}^{\ell}}Y_{j,B}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}\cap J_{B}^{\ell}}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j,B}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}\cap J_{B}^{\ell}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B):j\in C}x_{C}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}|J_{\mathrm{global}}\cap J_{B}^{\ell}\cap C|\cdot x_{C}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}4/\varepsilon^{4}\cdot x_{C}^{*}\leq 4/\varepsilon^{4}.

A union over the
\log_{2}T+1
values of
\ell
shows
{\mathbb{E}}[|J_{\mathrm{long}}(B)|]\leq 4/\varepsilon^{4}\cdot(\log_{2}T+1)

∎

Consider next the remaining
short
jobs
J_{\mathrm{short}}(B):=J(B)\setminus J_{\mathrm{long}}(B)
. For these jobs, intuitively, we have useful concentration properties. Let us initially set
Z_{j,B}=1
for all
j\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
. For each such
j
we define one or two (unlikely) bad events: when any one of those events happens, we set
Z_{j,B}=0
. The first bad event
\mathcal{E}_{total}
happens if the total processing time of the short jobs assigned to

B
is larger than
|B|
, i.e.,

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)}p_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j^{\prime})\cap B|}p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot Y_{j^{\prime},B}>|B|.

When
\mathcal{E}_{total}
happens, we set
Z_{j,B}=0
for all
j\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
. The event
\mathcal{E}_{total}
is the unique bad event for the considered jobs that span
B
, i.e., such that
B\subseteq tw(j)
.

Assume now that some
j\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
does not span
B
but that
\mathrm{tw}(j)
intersects

B
from the right, i.e.,
s<r_{j}<t
. Then we define the additional
bad event
\mathcal{E}_{right}(j)
that the total processing time of the jobs
j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
with
r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}
is larger than
|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|
. The intuition is
that
j
and all these jobs need to be processed during
\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B
.
Formally,
\mathcal{E}_{right}(j)
happens if

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B):r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j^{\prime})\cap B|,r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot Y_{j,B}>|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|.

If
\mathcal{E}_{right}(j)
happens, we set
Z_{j,B}:=0
.
We define an analogous second bad event
\mathcal{E}_{left}(j)
if
j
does not span
B

but
\mathrm{tw}(j)
intersects
B
from the left, i.e.,
s<d_{j}<t
, and
we set
Z_{j,B}
accordingly.

We can show that for each job
j\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
the defined
bad events are very unlikely and, hence, if
Y_{j,B}=1
then most
likely also
Z_{j,B}=1
.

Lemma 23
.

For each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
and each job
j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
with
p_{j}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|
and
\Pr[Y_{j,B}=1]>0
, it
holds that
\Pr[Z_{j,B}=1|Y_{j,B}=1]\geq 1-2\varepsilon.

Proof.

Let
B=[s,t)\in\mathcal{B}
and
j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
such that
p_{j}\leq\varepsilon^{4}(t-s)
.
We condition on the event that
Y_{j,B}=1
.

First we bound the probability of the event
\mathcal{E}_{total}
, i.e.

X:=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)}p_{j^{\prime}}>|B|
,
using Chernoff’s bound.
For any configuration
C\in\mathcal{C}(B)
we have
\sum_{j^{\prime}\in C}p_{j^{\prime}}\leq t-s
. This implies the following:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[X|Y_{j,B}=1]

\displaystyle=p_{j}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}\setminus\{j\}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|}p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j^{\prime},B}]

\displaystyle\leq p_{j}+\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}\setminus\{j\}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|}p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B):j^{\prime}\in J_{C}}x_{C}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{4}(t-s)+(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{C}\cap J_{\mathrm{global}}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|}p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot x_{C}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon(t-s)+(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(B)}(t-s)\cdot x_{C}^{*}

\displaystyle=(1-\varepsilon)(t-s).

For
j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
such that
p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}(t-s)
, let
Y_{j^{\prime},B}^{\prime}=p_{j^{\prime}}\cdot Y_{j^{\prime},B}
.
Then the random variables
Y_{j^{\prime},B^{\prime}}
for the
j^{\prime}
of the above type are independent, their sum
X
is bounded by
(1-\varepsilon)(t-s)
and each random variable is bounded by
\varepsilon^{4}(t-s)
.
Therefore, we can apply Chernoff’s bound and obtain:

\displaystyle\Pr[X>(t-s)]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)(t-s)}{3\cdot\varepsilon^{4}(t-s)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{3\cdot\varepsilon^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-1/\varepsilon\right)\leq\varepsilon.

We used
\varepsilon\leq 1/4
in the third inequality.
This already implies the lemma for all jobs spanning
B
, as the event
\mathcal{E}_{total}
is the only bad event for such jobs.

Now, suppose that
s<r_{j}<t
: a symmetric argument holds when
s<d_{j}<t
.
We need to upper bound the probability of the event
\mathcal{E}_{right}
, i.e.,

X^{\prime}:=\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B):r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}>|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|=t-r_{j}.

For any configuration
C\in\cal C(B)
, we have

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{C}\cap J_{\mathrm{global}}:p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|,r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}\leq(t-r_{j})

as all these jobs have to be scheduled during
[r_{j},t)
.
A calculation analogous to the one for
X
shows that
{\mathbb{E}}[X^{\prime}]\leq(1-\varepsilon)\cdot(t-r_{j})
. Note that each job
j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)
with
r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}
fulfills
p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}\cdot|\mathrm{tw}(j^{\prime})\cap B|\leq\varepsilon^{4}(t-r_{j})
as the jobs are short and
\mathrm{tw}(j^{\prime})\cap B\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B
.
As for
X
we use Chernoff’s bound, but this time applied to the random variables
Y_{j^{\prime},B}^{\prime}
only for
j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{global}}
with
p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|
and
r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}
.
We obtain
\Pr[X^{\prime}>t-r_{j}]\leq\varepsilon
.
Together, we obtain

\Pr[Z_{j,B}=1|Y_{j,B}=1]\geq 1-\Pr[X>t-s]-\Pr[X^{\prime}>t-r_{j}]\geq 1-2\varepsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

Let
APX(B):=\{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}:Z_{j,B}=1\}\subseteq J(B)
be all the jobs that we finally
assign to
B
. We show that we can compute a feasible schedule
for them in
B
(with a simple greedy algorithm).

Lemma 24
.

For each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
we can compute a schedule for
APX(B)

in
B
in polynomial time.

Proof.

Consider a block
B=[s,t)
.
Let us partition the jobs
APX(B)
into the ones
APX^{\text{left}}(B):=\{j\in APX(B):s<d_{j}<t\}
whose time window intersects
B
from the left, the ones
APX^{\text{right}}(B):=\{j\in APX(B):s<r_{j}<t\}
whose time window intersects
B
from the right, and the remaining ones
APX^{\text{mid}}(B):=APX(B)\setminus(APX^{\text{left}}(B)\cup APX^{\text{right}}(B))
, i.e., the ones whose time window spans
B
.

First, we order the jobs
APX^{\text{left}}(B)
non-decreasingly by their deadline. We schedule them in this order without idle time between them, such that the first job is starting at
s
.
Next, we order the jobs
APX^{\text{right}}(B)
non-decreasingly by their release time.
We also schedule them in this order without idle time between them, but such that the last job ends at time
t
.
Finally, we schedule the jobs
APX^{\text{mid}}(B)
in arbitrary order between the jobs
APX^{\text{left}}(B)
and the jobs
APX^{\text{right}}(B)
.

Clearly, this computation can be done in polynomial time.
It remains to show that this yields a valid schedule.
If
APX(B)=\emptyset
, then there is nothing to show.
So suppose that this is not the case. It is sufficient to prove the following two claims. First, we claim that there is enough space between
APX^{\text{left}}(B)
and
APX^{\text{right}}(B)
to schedule
APX^{\text{mid}}(B)
.
Second, we claim that the jobs in
APX^{\text{left}}(B)\cup APX^{\text{right}}(B)
are scheduled within their time window.

We start with the first claim.
As
APX(B)\neq\emptyset
, there exists
j\in APX(B)
, which therefore fulfills
Z_{j,B}=1
. Thus the event
\mathcal{E}_{total}
does not occur.
This implies

\sum_{j\in APX(B)}p_{j}\leq\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B)}p_{j}\leq|B|,

so there is enough space to schedule the jobs
APX^{\text{mid}}
between
APX^{\text{left}}
and
APX^{\text{right}}
, given that the latter two sets are scheduled without any idle time in the leftmost and rightmost portion of
B
, respectively.

For the second claim, consider any
j\in APX^{\text{right}}
(a symmetric argument works for any
j\in APX^{\text{left}}
).
We have
Z_{j,B}=1
and thus the event
\mathcal{E}_{right}(j)
does not occur. Therefore we obtain

\sum_{j^{\prime}\in APX(B):r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}\leq\sum_{j^{\prime}\in J_{\mathrm{short}}(B):r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}}p_{j^{\prime}}\leq t-r_{j}.

Note that all jobs
j^{\prime}
scheduled after (and including)
j
fulfill
r_{j}\leq r_{j^{\prime}}
. Thus their total processing time is at most
t-r_{j}
.
As the last job is completed at time
t
and there is no idle time between the jobs in
APX^{\text{right}}
, this implies that
j
is started not earlier than
t-(t-r_{j})=r_{j}
.
This shows that
j
is scheduled within its time window.
∎

We apply the procedure above to each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
. Our solution
is the set of jobs
APX:=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}APX(B)
together with the associated schedule as described above. Combining the above results yields the following lemma.

Lemma 25
.

In polynomial time we can compute a feasible solution to
the given instance whose expected number of jobs is at least

\Big((1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}\Big)-4(\log_{2}T+1)/\varepsilon^{4}\cdot|\mathcal{B}|\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-O(\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}^{*}.

Proof.

By Lemma
24
we compute a feasible schedule in time
n^{O(1)}
for each block
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
As each job is assigned only to one block, every job is scheduled at most once and thus this yields a feasible schedule altogether.
It remains to show that the computed schedule has a high enough number of jobs.

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|APX(B)|\right]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}Z_{j,B}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}\Pr[Z_{j,B}=1|Y_{j,B}=1]\cdot\Pr[Y_{j,B}=1]

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:pseudoConcentration}}{\geq}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}:p_{j}\leq\varepsilon^{4}|\mathrm{tw}(j)\cap B|}(1-2\varepsilon)\cdot\Pr[Y_{j,B}=1]

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j,B}]-{\mathbb{E}}[|J_{\mathrm{long}}(B)|]\right)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:pseudoRemovalLongJobs}}{\geq}(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\left(\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j,B}]-4(\log_{2}T+1)/\varepsilon^{4}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}\sum_{B\in\mathcal{B}}{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{j,B}]-\Delta\cdot|\mathcal{B}|

\displaystyle=(1-2\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}(1-2\varepsilon)y_{j}^{*}-\Delta\cdot|\mathcal{B}|

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:good-block-superblock-partition}}{\geq}(1-4\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-\varepsilon\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Lem. }\ref{lem:solveLP}}{\geq}(1-4\varepsilon)\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}-2\varepsilon\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}^{*}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we output the best of the two solutions computed with the algorithm of this section and with the algorithm from Section
2.3
(using the same optimal solution to the configuration LP for
\Delta=4(\log_{2}T+1)/\varepsilon^{4}
in both cases). Then, Lemmas
9
,

21
, and
25
yield
Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

By Lemma
21
, we solve the LP for
\Delta=4(\log_{2}T+1)/\varepsilon^{4}
in time
(nT)^{O_{\varepsilon}(1)}
.
By Lemma
21
, the computed solution has profit at least
(1-2\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
.
If
\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}\geq\frac{|\mathrm{OPT}|}{5}
,
we know from Lemma
9
, that the rounding procedure from Section
2.3
yields a schedule for at least

\displaystyle(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{3}{4}\sum_{j\in J}y_{j}^{*}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\left(\frac{3}{4}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|+\frac{1}{20}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\cdot\frac{4}{5}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|

jobs.
On the other hand, if
\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{local}}}y_{j}^{*}<\frac{|\mathrm{OPT}|}{5}

\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{global}}}y_{j}^{*}\geq(1-O(\varepsilon))\cdot\frac{4}{5}\cdot|\mathrm{OPT}|.

4
Extension to multiple machines

In this section, we describe how we extend our polynomial time
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation
and our pseudo-polynomial time
(5/4+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithms
to the setting of multiple (identical) machines.

First, we define the problem formally on multiple machines. In the
input, we are given a set of jobs
J
with given processing times,
release times, and deadlines as in the case of one machine. Additionally, we are given a value
m\in\mathbb{N}
that denotes the given number of (identical)
machines. Our goal is again to compute a subset of jobs
J^{\prime}\subseteq J
;
however, now we also need to compute a partition
J^{\prime}=J^{\prime}_{1}\dot{\cup}...\dot{\cup}J^{\prime}_{m}

where for each
i\in[m]
the set
J^{\prime}_{i}
corresponds to the jobs
we assign to machine
i
. Like before, for each job
j\in J^{\prime}
we
need to compute a start time
s(j)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})\subseteq\mathrm{tw}(j)
.
We require that for each machine
i\in[m]
and for any two jobs
j,j^{\prime}\in J^{\prime}_{i}

that
[s(j),s(j)+p_{j})\cap[s(j^{\prime}),s(j^{\prime})+p_{j^{\prime}})=\emptyset
. Note
that we do not require this for two jobs assigned to different machines

i,i^{\prime}
. As before, the objective is to maximize
|J^{\prime}|
. We call
the resulting problem Throughput Maximization on
m
machines.

It was shown in
[
40
]
that for each
\varepsilon>0
there
is a
(1-O(\sqrt{(\log m)/m})-\varepsilon)^{-1}
-approximation algorithm.

Theorem 26

(
[
40
]
)
.

For any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a polynomial
time
(1-O(\sqrt{(\log m)/m})-\varepsilon)^{-1}
approximation for Throughput
Maximization on
m
machines.

Hence, for each
\varepsilon>0
there is a constant
m_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
,
m=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
, such
that the theorem above yields a
(1+O(\varepsilon))
-approximation algorithm
if
m>m_{\varepsilon}
. Therefore, in the following we assume that we are
given a constant
\varepsilon>0
and an instance of Throughput Maximization
on multiple machines in which
m\leq m_{\varepsilon}
. For this setting,
we give a polynomial time
(4/3+\varepsilon)
-approximation and a pseudo-polynomial
time
(5/4+\varepsilon)
-approximation algorithm.

4.1
Construction of blocks and superblocks

First, we generalize our construction of the blocks and superblocks
to the setting of multiple machines. We start with a corresponding
statement of Lemma
4
that constructs
the elementary blocks on multiple machines. We ensure that there is
a near-optimal solution which schedules at most
O_{\varepsilon}(m)
jobs
during each block. Note that since
m\leq m_{\varepsilon}=O_{\varepsilon}(1)
this
is bounded by a constant.

Lemma 27
.

Assume that
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq(\frac{6m}{\varepsilon})^{3}
.
In polynomial time we can compute a partition of
[0,T)
into a set
of blocks
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
such that there exists a set of jobs
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}\subseteq J
and a feasible schedule of them with the following properties:

(A1)

|\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}|\geq(1-\varepsilon)|\mathrm{OPT}|
,

(A2)

each job
j\in\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}

is scheduled in some block
B\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
,

(A3)

for each block
B\in\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}

there are at most
\tilde{K}_{0}=(m/\varepsilon)^{O(m/\varepsilon\log(m/\varepsilon))}

jobs of
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}
that are scheduled in
B
,

(A4)

|\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}|\geq m\cdot|\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}|/\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Again, we use the result by
[
22
]
as described in Appendix
A
.
Their result holds for a more general problem than Maximum Throughput, called the Job Interval Selection Problem (JISP).
Instead of a release time, a deadline and a processing time, each job
j\in J
in JISP has a set
\mathcal{I}_{j}
of intervals and it can be scheduled for at most one interval in
\mathcal{I}_{j}
instead of all intervals of length
p_{j}
contained in
[r_{j},d_{j})
.
So Maximum Throughput on one machine is the special case of JISP where
\mathcal{I}_{j}=\{[t,t+p_{j}):[t,t+p_{j})\subseteq[r_{j},d_{j})\}
.
Intuitively, in the multiple machines case we have one separate interval of length
T
for each machine, i.e., a job scheduled on the
i
-th machine is scheduled during the interval
[(i-1)T,iT)
. Formally, for each
j\in J
and each
i\in[m]
let

\mathcal{I}_{j,i}:=\{[t,t+p_{j}):t\in\mathbb{N}\wedge[t,t+p_{j})\subseteq[r_{j}+(i-1)T,d_{j}+(i-1)T)\}

Also let
\mathcal{I}_{j}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{I}_{j,i}
denote the intervals in which
j
can be scheduled.
A schedule for this JISP instance corresponds to an instance of Maximum Throughput on
m
machines: A job scheduled during the interval
[(i-1)T+t,(i-1)T+t+p_{j})\in\mathcal{I}_{j,i}
in the JISP instance is scheduled on machine
i
during the interval
[t,t+p_{j})
in the Maximum Throughput instance.

Now we apply the algorithm from
[
22
]
, for
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon/(2m)
instead of
\varepsilon
. Note that even though
\mathcal{I}_{j}
is only pseudopolynomial in the input size, the described greedy algorithm can still be implemented in polynomial time (in the size of the given input instance), as each job only has
m
different time windows.
We do the same steps as in the proof of Lemma
4
, which yields, assuming that
|\mathrm{OPT}|\geq(\frac{6m}{\varepsilon})^{3}
, a partition of
[0,mT)
into a set of blocks
\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
such that there exists a set of jobs
\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}\subseteq J
and a feasible schedule of them with the following properties:

(C1)

|\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}|\geq(1-\varepsilon/(2m))|\mathrm{OPT}|
,

(C2)

each job
j\in\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
is scheduled in some block
B\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
,

(C3)

for each block
B\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
there are at most
\tilde{K}_{0}^{\prime}=(m/\varepsilon)^{O(m/\varepsilon\log(m/\varepsilon))}
jobs of
\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
that are scheduled in
B
,

(C4)

|\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}|\geq 2m\cdot|\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}|/\varepsilon
.

Now we can define the blocks
\tilde{B}_{0}
for the given instance of Maximum Throughput on
m
machines. Intuitively, we cut the blocks
\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}
at multiples of
T
, and then we shift the blocks corresponding to the interval
[(i-1)T,iT)
into the interval
[0,T)
so as to obtain a partition into blocks for machine
i
. However, we need a consistent block partitioning over the different machines.
Therefore, whenever a blocks starts or ends for some machine
i
, we use this a start or end point of a new block for each machine.
Formally, let
P=\{s,t:B=[s,t)\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}\}
denote all start and endpoints of blocks and let
P_{mod}=\{t^{\prime}\in[0,T]:\exists t\in P\text{ such that }t\equiv t^{\prime}\text{ modulo }T\}
denote the corresponding points modulo
T
.
Assume that
P_{mod}=\{t_{0}=0,\dots,t_{r}=T\}
such that for all
k\in[r]
we have
t_{k-1}<t_{k}
.
We define by
\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}=\{[t_{k-1},t_{k}):k\in[r]\}
the partition of
[0,T)
into blocks.
By construction we have
|P_{mod}|\leq|P|
and thus
|\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{0}|\leq|\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{0}|
.
We define a schedule
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime}
for the Maximum Throughput instance obtained from
\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
as follows. Consider a job
j\in\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
and assume that it is scheduled during some interval

[(i-1)T+t,(i-1)T+t+p_{j})\in\mathcal{I}_{j,i}
for some value
i
. We include
j
in our set
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime}
and we schedule
j
on machine
i

during the interval
[t,t+p_{j})
. We apply this reasoning to each job
j\in\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}
.
Clearly,
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime}
is feasible and we have
|\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime}|=|\widehat{\mathrm{OPT}}|
.
Furthermore, let
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime\prime}
denote the schedule obtained from
\widetilde{\mathrm{OPT}}^{\prime}
