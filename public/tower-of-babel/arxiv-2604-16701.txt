Enabling Lie-Algebraic Classical Simulation beyond Free Fermions
Adelina Bärligea, Matthew L. Sims-Goh, Jakob S. Kottmann

Abstract
Efficient classical simulation has matured to a critical component of the quantum computing stack, driving hardware validation, algorithm design, and the study of structured quantum dynamics. Lie-algebraic simulation ( 𝔤 ​ - ​ sim \mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim} ) is a compelling approach: it replaces exponentially large Hilbert-space evolution by dynamics in a reduced adjoint space whose dimension is set by the dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) of the circuit, enabling efficient simulation whenever the DLA grows only polynomially with system size. In practice, however, existing applications of 𝔤 ​ - ​ sim \mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim} have been confined to free-fermionic (matchgate) regimes, and it has been unclear how to extend the paradigm to other structured circuits whose generators may have large Pauli expansions. Here we enable Lie-algebraic classical simulation beyond free fermions by identifying additional non-trivial families of polynomial-dimensional DLAs and introducing symmetry-adapted basis representations that make the adjoint space mapping tractable. In particular, we develop an explicit Pauli orbit basis for permutation-equivariant dynamics, supporting cubic-dimensional algebras despite exponential Pauli support, and a subspace-adapted (modified) generalized Gell–Mann basis for bounded Hamming-weight ( U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) -equivariant) dynamics, yielding polynomial costs on fixed excitation sectors. Together with streamlined routines for free-fermionic Pauli algebras and translation-invariant variants thereof, these constructions significantly broaden the practical scope of 𝔤 ​ - ​ sim \mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim} as a unifying simulation tool for structured quantum dynamics. Numerical benchmarks confirm favorable preprocessing scaling and validate large-scale proof-of-concept simulations.

The purpose of this work is to show that Lie-algebraic simulability is meaningfully broader than the free-fermion/matchgate regime, and that the correct perspective is representation-theoretic: polynomial DLA dimension is the structural starting point, but efficient simulation hinges on choosing a basis adapted to the algebraic symmetries of the system. Concretely, we develop symmetry-adapted operator bases and efficient primitives that make
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing practical for several families of polynomial-dimensional DLAs that go beyond free fermions.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

Beyond free fermions: two further polynomial regimes with explicit bases.

Building on the sharp dichotomy for Pauli Lie algebras generated by single Pauli strings
3
;
131
;
76

(i) permutation-equivariant (collective) dynamics, whose ambient commutant algebra has cubic dimension scaling, and
(ii) Hamming-weight preserving (
U(1)

Symmetry-adapted representations that remove exponential Pauli support as an obstacle.

For translation-invariant free-fermion equivalents, we introduce Pauli cycles as a symmetry-adapted representation that reduces commutator costs by exploiting cyclic twirling.
For permutation-invariant systems, we develop the Pauli orbit basis and derive efficient inner-product and commutator primitives that close within the orbit representation with polynomial resources, despite exponential Pauli expansions of individual orbit elements. This directly refutes the necessity of slow Pauli expansion assumptions in this setting.
For bounded Hamming-weight sectors, we propose a modified generalized Gell–Mann basis for
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
and provide the analytic commutation relations enabling efficient structure-constant generation.

Efficient preprocessing with explicit complexity and benchmark evidence.

Across these regimes, we focus on the preprocessing bottleneck of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
: Lie closure (basis construction) and adjoint representation (structure-constant computation). We give explicit primitives and worst-case scaling bounds in the relevant representations and complement them with runtime benchmarks demonstrating that constant factors and average-case behavior render preprocessing practical in regimes far beyond what naive Pauli expansions would suggest.

Large-scale numerical demonstrations as proof of concept.

We provide numerical experiments that reproduce and scale up representative tasks previously limited to state-vector simulation or small hardware demonstrations, including free-fermionic TFIM-based optimization and permutation-equivariant quantum neural networks with symmetry-adapted preprocessing. These experiments are intended as feasibility demonstrations of the theory and the representations introduced here.

Open-source implementation.

To facilitate reuse and independent verification, we provide a highly optimized, open-source implementation of all proposed bases and primitives introduced in this work, together with code to reproduce the numerical experiments. These can be found in the GitHub repository of Ref.

II
Preliminaries

Lie-algebraic methods have long provided a structural route to classical simulation and exact solvability of restricted quantum dynamics. Early work by Somma et al. showed that quantum computational models whose gates and observables are confined to a compact, polynomial-dimensional Lie algebra can be exactly simulated classically with resources polynomial in the Lie-algebra dimension, even when the underlying Hilbert space is exponentially large
118
;
117
. More recently, the same paradigm has been reintroduced in the context of variational quantum algorithms (VQAs), where the central computational primitive is the repeated evaluation of expectation values of circuit-evolved states
57
.

Variational quantum algorithms
92
;
29
;
20
aim to approximately solve ground-state and related optimization problems using parameterized quantum circuits on near-term quantum devices. Given an initial state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
and a parameterized ansatz circuit
U(\bm{\theta})
, VQAs estimate the objective value

\ell_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}},O}(\bm{\theta})=\langle O(\bm{\theta})\rangle:=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[\,O\,U(\bm{\theta})\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\,U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\,\big],

(1)

where the problem is encoded in the Hamiltonian
O
(i.e., a Hermitian observable) whose ground-state energy is sought. By the variational principle, minimizing
\ell_{\rho_{\mathrm{in}},O}
over
\bm{\theta}
yields an upper bound on the true ground-state energy.

Despite their algorithmic promise, VQAs face well-known scalability barriers. Beyond the detrimental effects of hardware noise
119
;
60
;
40
;
115
and finite-shot estimation errors
116
;
11
, a major obstacle is
trainability
: many ansätze and cost functions exhibit barren plateaus
91
;
81
, i.e. exponentially suppressed gradient variance and concentrated loss at scale, and their optimization landscapes are covered by suboptimal local minima
23
;
135
;
8
.
A key development of recent Lie-algebraic treatments is that both expressivity and trainability of deep circuit families can be characterized in terms of the dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) generated by the circuit gates
78
;
105
;
49
. Importantly, the same Lie-algebraic structure that explains when VQAs become untrainable also identifies broad regimes in which the dynamics are confined to a polynomial-dimensional effective space and are therefore classically simulable, providing a concrete example of the broader conjectural link between trainability and simulability
30
.

II.1
Lie-Algebraic characterization of variational quantum circuits

For a system of
n
qubits with Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}{=}(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}}
, consider a layered (periodic) parameterized circuit of the form

U(\bm{\theta})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}U_{l}(\bm{\theta}_{l})\quad\mathrm{with}\quad U_{l}(\bm{\theta}_{l})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}e^{-i\>\theta_{l,k}\>H_{k}},

(2)

where
G=\{H_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a fixed set of Hermitian, traceless generators, and
\bm{\theta}=\{\theta_{l,k}\}
collects all real parameters. The dynamical Lie algebra (DLA) associated with
G
is defined as the smallest real Lie subalgebra of
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
containing
iH_{k}
, i.e.,

\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\langle iH_{1},\dots,iH_{K}\rangle_{\text{Lie}}\subseteq\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})

(3)

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{Lie}}
denotes closure under real linear combinations and commutators. Equivalently,
\mathfrak{g}
is the real span of all nested commutators of the elements in
iG
.

The connected Lie subgroup associated with
\mathfrak{g}
is

e^{\mathfrak{g}}\equiv\{e^{iA}\ :\ iA\in\mathfrak{g}\}\subseteq\mathrm{SU}(2^{n}).

(4)

In the language of quantum control,
\mathfrak{g}
captures the infinitesimal directions generated by the available Hamiltonians, and
e^{\mathfrak{g}}
describes the corresponding connected Lie group of unitaries reachable in principle when one concatenates evolutions under the generators with freely chosen durations and orderings; see, e.g. Ref.
42
. For a fixed ansatz architecture such as (
2
), the set of unitaries reachable at a given finite depth
L
is generally a strict subset of
e^{\mathfrak{g}}
, but
\mathfrak{g}
remains the natural algebraic object controlling expressivity and related phenomena.

A gate set is called universal if the Lie algebra generated by its infinitesimal generators is
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
, or equivalently if
e^{\mathfrak{g}}=\mathrm{SU}(2^{n})

85
. Moreover, universality is generic in the sense that “almost any” sufficiently noncommuting choice of generators yields
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})

84
. A natural basis of
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
is given by the traceless Pauli strings. Let

\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\bigl\{P^{(n)}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}\;:\;P_{i}\in\{I,X,Y,Z\},\ P^{(n)}{\neq}I^{\otimes n}\bigr\}.

(5)

Then
\{iP:\,P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
is a real basis of
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
and
|\mathcal{P}_{n}|=4^{n}-1
. In this work we are primarily interested in the restricted regime of proper subalgebras
\mathfrak{g}\subsetneq\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
whose dimension grows only polynomially with
n
.

A central insight of the recent theory on the trainability of VQAs is that the DLA
\mathfrak{g}
of a system provides a structural descriptor of both expressivity and loss variance statistics of parameterized quantum circuits. Building on a conjecture
78
, Refs.
105
;
49
proved that, under suitable large-depth assumptions, the variance of a wide class of cost functions admits the universal form

\operatorname{Var}_{\bm{\theta}}\!\left[\ell_{\rho,O}(\bm{\theta})\right]=\frac{\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)\,\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(O)}{\dim(\mathfrak{g})},

(6)

Here
\ell_{\rho,O}(\bm{\theta})
denotes the parameterized cost associated with an input state
\rho
and an observable
O
(cf. expectation value (
1
)). The quantity
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\cdot)
is the
\mathfrak{g}
-purity
of a Hermitian operator
14
;
13
;
105
. Concretely, let
\{B_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}
be a Hilbert–Schmidt orthonormal basis of
i\mathfrak{g}
with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle_{\mathrm{HS}}=\mathrm{Tr}(AB)
. Then,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(H):=\sum_{\gamma=1}^{\dim(\mathfrak{g})}\big|\mathrm{Tr}(B_{\gamma}^{\dagger}H)\big|^{2}\ \leq\ \mathrm{Tr}(H^{2}).

(7)

Intuitively,
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(H)
measures how much of
H
lies in the dynamical directions generated by the circuit dynamics.

Equation (
6
) provides a unifying explanation of several known mechanisms behind barren plateaus: increasing expressivity
91
corresponds to increasing
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
and hence suppresses the variance; strong entanglement or symmetry mismatch can reduce
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(\rho)
; and observables with little support on the dynamical directions have small
\mathcal{P}_{\mathfrak{g}}(O)
, consistent with cost-function locality effects
31
.

Since expressivity of circuits of the form (
2
) depends strongly on the depth
L
, the validity of
Eq.

6
likewise requires a sufficiently mixed, large-depth regime. In Refs.
78
;
105
;
49
, this is formalized by assuming that the circuit ensemble induced by random parameters forms an (approximate) unitary
2
-design on the relevant dynamical group (or, more generally, that it mixes sufficiently in the second Haar moment; see Ref.
93
).
In the fully controllable case,
\mathfrak{g}\cong\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
, one obtains an explicit sufficient depth condition of the form

L\ \gtrsim\ \frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\log\!\left(1/\big\|\mathcal{A}^{(2)}\big\|_{\infty}\right)},

(8)

where
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
captures the contraction of the second moment (twirling) channel associated with one circuit layer and
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
denotes the induced operator norm on the traceless subspace
78
;
105
. In practice, evaluating or tightly bounding such moment operators for a given ansatz is highly nontrivial
62
;
24
, so
\mathfrak{g}
-based characterizations are often used either in analytically controlled regimes or as qualitative predictors complemented by numerics.

Finally, beyond barren plateaus, the DLA dimension also governs other aspects of the loss landscape geometry: underparameterization can induce spurious local minima, whereas avoiding such artifacts typically requires a number of parameters scaling with
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
(overparameterization)
79
.

II.2
Lie-algebraic simulation of expectation values

The Lie-algebraic structure captured by the DLA not only underpins recent analyses of expressivity and trainability of variational circuits, but also enables efficient classical simulation in regimes where the induced algebra remains “small” (i.e., polynomially growing in system size); e.g., the discussion around the trainability–simulability conjecture in
30
. A particularly general and practically oriented framework is
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}

57
which builds on earlier ideas in quantum control and Hamiltonian complexity
118
;
117
;
136
. We briefly review the basic mechanism here.

Given an initial state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
and an observable
O
, the simulation task is to evaluate the expectation value after evolution under the circuit
U(\bm{\theta})
, which is exactly the VQA loss defined in
Eq.

1
. The key assumption in Lie-algebraic simulation is that the operators of interest are supported on a low-dimensional operator subspace that is invariant under conjugation by the dynamical Lie group
e^{\mathfrak{g}}
. For clarity of exposition we present
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
in the simplest setting here, where the relevant invariant subspace is taken as
i\mathfrak{g}
, i.e. the Hermitian operators generated by the DLA itself. More generally, however,
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
applies to arbitrary (irreducible) invariant subspaces of the adjoint action of
e^{\mathfrak{g}}
; see Ref.
57
.

Fix a Hilbert–Schmidt–orthonormal basis
\{B_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{d}
of
i\mathfrak{g}
, where
d{:=}\dim(\mathfrak{g})
. We represent the observable and the input state through their coordinates in this basis:

O=\sum_{\alpha}w_{\alpha}\,B_{\alpha},\qquad\big(\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})}\big)_{\alpha}:=\mathrm{Tr}[B_{\alpha}\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}],

(9)

so that by linearity of the trace,

\begin{gathered}\langle O(\bm{\theta})\rangle=\sum_{\alpha}w_{\alpha}\,\big(\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{out})}\big)_{\alpha},\\
\ \text{with}\quad\big(\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{out})}\big)_{\alpha}:=\mathrm{Tr}\!\big[U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})\,B_{\alpha}\,U(\bm{\theta})\,\rho_{\mathrm{in}}\big].\end{gathered}

(10)

For any
U\in e^{\mathfrak{g}}
, its adjoint action on
i\mathfrak{g}
is defined by conjugation
B\mapsto U^{\dagger}BU
. In the basis
\{B_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{d}
this defines a real matrix
\Phi^{\operatorname{Ad}}(U)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
via

U^{\dagger}B_{\alpha}U=\sum_{\beta}\big(\Phi^{\operatorname{Ad}}(U)\big)_{\alpha\beta}\,B_{\beta}.

(11)

Taking the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product with
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
yields a linear evolution rule for the output vector:

\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{out})}=\Phi^{\operatorname{Ad}}\!\big(U(\bm{\theta})\big)\,\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})}.

(12)

The corresponding infinitesimal action is determined by the
structure constants
of the basis. We define them by

i[B_{\alpha},B_{\beta}]=\sum_{\gamma}f_{\alpha\beta}^{\ \ \gamma}\,B_{\gamma},\qquad f_{\alpha\beta}^{\ \ \gamma}=\frac{\langle B_{\gamma},\,i[B_{\alpha},B_{\beta}]\rangle}{\langle B_{\gamma},B_{\gamma}\rangle},

(13)

where
\langle A,B\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)
denotes the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. The coefficients
f_{\alpha\beta}^{\ \ \gamma}
, sometimes also termed
structure factors
, are defined entirely by the chosen operator basis
\{B_{\alpha}\}
and represent the commutation relations between its elements.

For a Hermitian generator
H\in i\mathfrak{g}
, expanded as

H=\sum_{\mu}h_{\mu}\,B_{\mu},

(14)

its infinitesimal adjoint action is the linear map
\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
defined by

i[H,B_{\alpha}]=\sum_{\beta}\big(\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H)\big)_{\alpha\beta}\,B_{\beta}.

(15)

Using (
13
), its matrix elements are therefore

\big(\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H)\big)_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\mu}h_{\mu}\,f_{\mu\alpha}^{\ \ \beta}.

(16)

For a single parameterized gate
U(\theta)=e^{-i\theta H}
, the finite adjoint action is obtained by exponentiating the infinitesimal generator:

\Phi^{\operatorname{Ad}}(e^{-i\theta H})=e^{\,\theta\,\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H)}.

(17)

Thus, once the matrices
\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H_{k})
for the circuit generators are known, expectation values can be evaluated by adjoint-space propagation.

For a circuit of the form (
2
), combining (
12
) and (
17
) yields

\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{out})}=\left(\prod_{l=1}^{L}\prod_{k=1}^{K}e^{-i\theta_{l,k}\,\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(H_{k})}\right)\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})},

(18)

and the desired expectation value follows from the dot product (
10
). This replaces manipulations of
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
matrices by linear algebra operations on dimension-
d
objects. Whenever
d{=}\dim(\mathfrak{g})
grows only polynomially in
n
, this yields the basic source of exponential savings exploited by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
.

Moreover, since
\langle O(\bm{\theta})\rangle
is obtained from explicit products of matrix exponentials, exact gradients can be computed by differentiating (
18
). Writing the parameters as a single index
m\in\{1,\dots,M\}
with
M=LK
, one may express

\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{m}}\langle O(\bm{\theta})\rangle=\bm{w}^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\Phi^{\operatorname{Ad}}(U(\bm{\theta}))}{\partial\theta_{m}}\right)\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})},

(19)

and evaluate the derivative efficiently using product-rule variants (see
57
).
Finally, the same adjoint-space propagation can be extended to correlators (expectation values of operator products) by choosing an appropriate invariant operator subspace that contains the relevant product observables; see
57

II.3
Notions of classical simulability

Throughout this paper, we discuss classical simulability of quantum circuits, with a particular emphasis on Lie-algebraic simulation as outlined above. Since several notions of “simulability” coexist in the literature, we briefly fix terminology here and explain where our setting fits.

Consider a uniform family of
n
-qubit quantum circuits
\mathcal{U}_{n}
. For a given
U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
acting on an input state
\ket{\psi_{0}}
, followed by a computational-basis measurement, the outcome distribution is

p(x)=|\langle x|U|\psi_{0}\rangle|^{2},\quad x\in\{0,1\}^{n}.

(20)

A standard distinction is between
strong
and
weak
classical simulation
41
. We say that
\mathcal{U}_{n}
is
strongly simulable
if one can compute probabilities of specified measurement events (e.g.,
p(x)
for a given
x
, or more generally marginal probabilities) in time polynomial in
n
(and in the requested output precision). It is
weakly simulable
if one can sample
x
from the distribution
p(x)
in time polynomial in
n
. Strong simulation is at least as demanding as weak simulation: standard sequential sampling reduces weak simulation to computing a polynomial number of marginal probabilities, and more recent reductions show alternative routes from probability/amplitude evaluation to sampling that can improve constants depending on the available primitive
25
. In contrast, physical quantum devices are weak simulators, natively providing samples from
p(x)
and thus estimating expectation values only up to statistical error via repeated measurements.

The notion of Lie-algebraic simulability
117
studied in this work is most naturally aligned with the
strong
simulation task of computing expectation values of the form (
1
) exactly (up to classical arithmetic precision) for specified observables
O
and gate-evolved input states
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
. This setting is especially relevant for variational quantum algorithms, where objective functions are expectation values by design, motivating renewed interest in classical methods that can (approximately) evaluate such quantities for certain classes of circuits
26
;
57
;
110
.

Finally, we use the terms “efficient” and “polynomial time” interchangeably throughout. This should not be conflated with
practical
performance, which depends on constant factors and typical-instance behavior; assessing these aspects is an additional focus of this work.

III
Systems with polynomial-dimensional dynamical Lie algebras

The framework of Lie-algebraic simulation
117
;
57
(reviewed in
Section

II.2
) exploits a simple but far-reaching observation: in many structured circuit families, the physically reachable dynamics are confined to a proper invariant operator subspace whose dimension grows only polynomially with the system size
n
. When this happens, expectation values of the form (
1
) can be computed by propagating in a low-dimensional adjoint representation of the underlying DLA, rather than in the
2^{n}
-dimensional Hilbert space.

This perspective shifts the central question from “how large is the Hilbert space?” to two algebraic questions:
(i) when does a circuit family generate a DLA
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
with
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
,
and (ii) in which basis (or representation) can the basic preprocessing primitives of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
(i.e., linear-independence checks and commutator evaluations for computing the adjoint data) be implemented with polynomial resources?

A common baseline for discussing (i) is the Pauli string regime. If the generators are individual Pauli strings (i.e., one considers
Pauli Lie algebras
in the sense of
Eq.

5
), recent classification results imply a strong dichotomy: aside from essentially free-fermionic families (yielding
\Theta(n^{2})
-dimensional algebras) and a few degenerate low-dimensional cases (commuting or effectively one-body structures), connected Pauli Lie algebras are typically exponentially large in
n

3
;
131
;
76
. In this setting, efficient classical simulation is already well understood: free-fermionic circuits admit specialized techniques such as fermionic linear optics and Wick/Pfaffian methods
74
;
124
;
122
, and
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
reproduces these as a Lie-algebraic simulation paradigm (cf.
Section

IV
).

However, Pauli string generators are not the generic case in variational quantum algorithms. Many practically relevant ansätze are specified by
sums
of Pauli strings, including QAOA and Hamiltonian variational ansätze, as well as symmetry-constrained models and collective-spin constructions.
Abstractly, these generators take the general operator form

H=\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_{k}\,P_{k}^{(n)},\qquad c_{k}\in\mathbb{R},

(21)

with
P_{k}^{(n)}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
. In this sum-generator regime, polynomially growing DLAs can arise from additional structure such as symmetry, constrained connectivity, or restricted invariant subspaces. Importantly, this enlarges the landscape of polynomial DLAs beyond the free-fermionic setting and motivates
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
as an
overarching
simulability paradigm: rather than mapping each model to a bespoke representation (fermions, Schur blocks, etc.), one aims to simulate whenever the relevant invariant Lie algebra stays polynomial and the required primitives can be carried out efficiently in an appropriate basis.

This brings us to the main algorithmic bottleneck behind (ii). Even when
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
, it is not automatic that
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing is efficient in the
standard
Pauli string basis: basis elements of
\mathfrak{g}
may have exponentially large Pauli expansions, and naive commutator evaluation in that basis may become infeasible. This concern has led to the suggestion that
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
should require, in addition to
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
, that the algebra admit a basis whose elements have “slow” (polynomial-size) Pauli support
64
. In this work we show that such a restriction is not necessary. The key is to adopt symmetry-adapted or subspace-adapted bases in which the algebraic operations required by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
become efficient even when individual operators have exponentially large representations in the naive Pauli basis.

Concretely, the remainder of this paper develops explicit bases and efficient preprocessing primitives for three representative sources of polynomial DLAs, illustrating progressively richer regimes:

•

Quadratic DLAs
: free-fermion (matchgate) algebras and free-fermion equivalents arising from structured sum generators (including translation invariance), where symmetry-adapted “Pauli cycle” representations can reduce preprocessing costs (
Section

IV
).

•

Cubic DLAs
: permutation-invariant (equivariant) algebras, where a symmetry-adapted “Pauli orbit” bases provides an exponentially compressed operator representation and enables polynomial-time commutator evaluation despite exponential Pauli support (
Section

V
).

•

Higher polynomial DLAs from restricted subspaces
:
U(1)
-invariant (Hamming-weight preserving) circuits restricted to fixed-weight sectors
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where the effective dynamics live in
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
with
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
and admit explicit matrix-unit bases with closed-form commutation rules (
Section

VI
).

Together, these families substantiate the main message: Lie-algebraic simulability is best viewed as a representation-sensitive notion. Polynomial DLA dimension identifies a broad class of potentially classically tractable quantum dynamics, and the practical scope of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
is determined by whether one can find a basis (often symmetry-adapted) in which a faithful representation such as the adjoint can be computed efficiently.

IV
Free-fermion algebras

Free-fermion (or Gaussian) dynamics are generated by Hamiltonians that are at most quadratic in fermionic creation and annihilation operators. Concretely, for
n
fermionic modes with operators
\{a_{j},a_{j}^{\dagger}\}_{j=1}^{n}
satisfying the canonical anticommutation relations, consider the Majorana operators

\gamma_{2j-1}:=a_{j}+a_{j}^{\dagger},\qquad\gamma_{2j}:=-i(a_{j}-a_{j}^{\dagger}),

(22)

which satisfy
\{\gamma_{\mu},\gamma_{\nu}\}=2\delta_{\mu\nu}I
. Any free-fermion Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{\mathrm{FF}}=\frac{i}{4}\sum_{\mu,\nu=1}^{2n}\alpha_{\mu\nu}\,\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu},\qquad\alpha=-\alpha^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times 2n},

(23)

so the dynamical generators are precisely the quadratic bilinears
\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}
. The Lie algebra generated by these bilinears is isomorphic to the special orthogonal Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}\cong\mathfrak{so}(2n),\qquad\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}})=n(2n-1),

(24)

and in certain “extended” variants (allowing additional linear/odd terms, or equivalently adding an auxiliary mode) one obtains
\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)
with
\dim=n(2n+1)=2n^{2}+n
; see, e.g., Refs.
74
;
3
.

Matchgates arise as the qubit realization of such free-fermion evolutions under the Jordan–Wigner transformation. In particular, on nearest-neighbor qubits they are exactly the parity-preserving two-qubit unitaries, i.e. gates that act block-diagonally on the even- and odd-parity subspaces (
\mathrm{span}\{|00\rangle,|11\rangle\}
and
\mathrm{span}\{|01\rangle,|10\rangle\}
, respectively). Equivalently (up to a global phase), they admit the standard block form
124
;
122

U_{\mathrm{MG}}=\begin{pmatrix}U^{(1)}_{11}&0&0&U^{(1)}_{12}\\
0&U^{(2)}_{11}&U^{(2)}_{12}&0\\
0&U^{(2)}_{21}&U^{(2)}_{22}&0\\
U^{(1)}_{21}&0&0&U^{(1)}_{22}\end{pmatrix},\quad U^{(1)},U^{(2)}\in\mathrm{SU}(2).

(25)

when written in the ordered basis
\{|00\rangle,|11\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle\}
. These form precisely the class of non-interacting fermion circuits whose classical simulability was established in the early matchgate literature
74
;
124
;
122
.

Notice that under the Jordan–Wigner transformation, Majoranas map to Pauli strings

\gamma_{2j-1}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{k}\ X_{j},\qquad\gamma_{2j}=\bigotimes_{k=1}^{j-1}Z_{k}\ Y_{j},

(26)

so the quadratic bilinears
\gamma_{\mu}\gamma_{\nu}
become Pauli strings with at most two non-identity letters and (possibly) a
Z
-string in between
74
. For nearest neighbors, this reduces to a local Pauli generator set on qubits
i,i+1
,

G_{\mathrm{FF}}=\{Z_{i},\,Z_{i{+}1},\,X_{i}X_{i+1},\,Y_{i}Y_{i+1},\,X_{i}Y_{i+1},\,Y_{i}X_{i+1}\},

(27)

which generates exactly the matchgate Lie algebra on that edge. Consequently, in the Pauli-basis DLA language used throughout this paper, free-fermion/matchgate circuits correspond to quadratic-dimensional Pauli Lie algebras whose Lie closure is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(2n)
(or
\mathfrak{so}(2n+1)
), hence
{\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}})=\Theta(n^{2})}
, consistent with the quadratic families classified in Refs.
131
;
76
;
3
.

IV.1
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
for free-fermionic Pauli Lie algebras

Free-fermionic (matchgate) circuits already admit well-established, optimized classical simulation methods in the Pauli basis such as fermionic linear optics (FLO)
74
;
124
;
122
, which is why they are not the primary target of this paper.
Nevertheless, their Lie-algebraic description forms an instructive baseline for the notion of classical simulability via
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
: in this regime,
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
can be implemented particularly efficiently because the DLA admits a Pauli-string basis with fast commutator and inner-product primitives.

A natural basis of a free-fermion Pauli Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}
is a subset of the traceless Pauli strings

(
5
). Efficient preprocessing and simulation then hinge on representing Pauli strings in a way that supports fast linear independence checks and commutator evaluation. A standard choice is the binary symplectic representation.

Definition 1

Pauli strings are orthogonal under the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product, hence inner products reduce to equality checks.

Let

2^{n}$ \text{else}$\end{cases}.

Corollary 2

(Linear-time commutators of Pauli strings)

Let

In practice, wide-word bit operations can reduce the constant factors substantially; throughout the paper, we conservatively use the bound

Combining

Beyond preprocessing, Pauli-basis algebras admit further speedups during evaluation. In particular, for a Pauli basis the adjoint action of a generator on the basis is sparse: for fixed
\alpha
and
\beta
, the commutator
i[B_{\alpha},B_{\beta}]
is either
0
or a single basis element up to sign, so each adjoint generator
\Phi^{\operatorname{ad}}(B_{\alpha})
has at most one nonzero entry per row/column (after a suitable ordering). This induces a block structure consisting of
2\times 2

1\times 1
zeros, allowing
Eq.

17
to be applied efficiently via precomputed block decompositions

.

The preceding discussion treats the case where the DLA basis itself consists of single Pauli strings. In this
Pauli Lie algebra
setting, classification results imply a strong dichotomy: apart from essentially free-fermionic families, connected Pauli Lie algebras are typically exponentially large in
n

3
;
131
;
76
. In many practically relevant ansätze, however, the dynamical generators are not single Pauli strings but general operators, i.e., structured linear combintations of Pauli strings. Such “sum generators” can still induce quadratic-dimensional DLAs which correspond to free-fermion equivalent. The difference is that this free-fermion structure is no longer manifest in the Pauli basis and is often revealed only after exploiting additional algebraic or symmetry structure. This motivates the next subsection, where we demonstrate the power of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
to tackle free-fermion equivalents generated by general (non-Pauli) operators and show how symmetry-adapted representations can be essential for efficient preprocessing in this more general setting.

IV.2
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
for free-fermion algebras generated by arbitrary operators

The sum-generator regime contains many models that are free-fermion equivalents: although their generators are not given in an explicit quadratic-fermion normal form (suc as
Eq.

27
), they admit a (possibly non-local) mapping to non-interacting fermions based on structural criteria such as frustration-graph invariants and associated conserved quantities
34
;
33
;
52
. In particular, Ref.
33
gives a unified graph-theoretic solvability criterion (formulated in terms of the Hamiltonian frustration graph) guaranteeing an exact free-fermion description for broad families beyond the textbook Jordan–Wigner setting. Operationally, the frustration-graph route typically requires constructing and analyzing a graph on Pauli terms (and, depending on the case, identifying symmetry sectors), whereas
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
only needs Lie-closure primitives (commutators and linear-independence checks) in whatever operator representation one chooses.

From the perspective of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, this motivates a complementary workflow: rather than explicitly constructing a fermionization map and switching to fermionic linear optics, one can (i) probe whether a given generator set exhibits free-fermion-type Lie closure (e.g.
\mathfrak{so}(2n)
-like scaling and commutation structure) by running the same Lie-closure and structure-constant pipeline used by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, and (ii) simulate expectation values directly in the naive operator representation (Pauli strings or an appropriate symmetry-adapted basis) once a polynomial DLA is confirmed. This keeps the simulation pipeline uniform across “manifestly free-fermion”, “disguised free-fermion”, and the genuinely non-free-fermion polynomial families discussed in
Sections

V
and
VI
.

A conceptual caveat is that quadratic scaling alone does not characterize free fermions. While any subalgebra of a free-fermion algebra has
\dim(\mathfrak{g}){=}\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, the converse implication fails in general: there exist physically meaningful circuits with
\dim(\mathfrak{g}){=}\Theta(n^{2})
whose induced algebras are not isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(2n)
or
\mathfrak{so}(2n{+}1)
; see
Remark

3
for an explicit
\mathfrak{su}(n)
counterexample arising from universal subspace dynamics on the single-excitation sector.

IV.2.1
Translation-invariant systems

A general nearest-neighbor Pauli Hamiltonian on a chain that admits a Jordan–Wigner free-fermion description has the support pattern
34

H=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\ \sum_{P,P^{\prime}\in\{X,Y\}}\alpha^{PP^{\prime}}_{i}\,P_{i}P_{i+1}\;+\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}Z_{i},

(30)

i.e. all two-body couplings are restricted to the
\{X,Y\}
-plane, supplemented by on-site
Z
-fields. A convenient generating set that spans this support pattern is

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{TI}}=\Bigl\{

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}X_{i}X_{i+1},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}X_{i}Y_{i+1},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Y_{i}X_{i+1},\

(31)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Y_{i}Y_{i+1},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}\Bigr\}.

(32)

Each generator in (
32
) is quadratic under the Jordan–Wigner transformation and therefore induces dynamics contained in the free-fermion algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}
; in particular,

\mathfrak{g}=\langle iG_{\mathrm{TI}}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}\cong\mathfrak{so}(2n),

(33)

so
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
. (and for generic choices of couplings one obtains the full
\mathfrak{so}(2n)
closure). The same construction applies to periodic boundary conditions by adding the wrap-around terms
P_{n}P^{\prime}_{1}
to the two-body sums (
30
).

Beyond free-fermion structure, the periodic (translation-invariant) setting also introduces an independent symmetry constraint: commutation with the cyclic shift. Let
C_{n}=\langle\tau\rangle
be the cyclic translation group generated by the one-step shift
\tau
, and let
U_{\tau}
denote its unitary representation on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
. Define the translation-invariant operator algebra

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}:=\{A\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})\ :\ [A,U_{\tau}]=0\},

(34)

with
\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}})=\Theta(4^{n}/n)

88
. Equivalently,
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}
is the fixed-point subspace of the conjugation action of
C_{n}
on operators. The corresponding Reynolds (twirling) projector is

\mathcal{T}(A)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{\tau}^{i}\,A\,(U_{\tau}^{\dagger})^{i},

(35)

which satisfies
\mathcal{T}(A)\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}
for all
A
and
\mathcal{T}(A)=A
for all
A\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}
.
This naturally motivates a symmetry-adapted basis for subsystems of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}
(or
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}\cap\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
, excluding the identity): the twirling map
\mathcal{T}
is the orthogonal projector onto the
C_{n}
-invariant subspace, and applying
\mathcal{T}
to Pauli strings produces the corresponding cyclic sums. We refer to these symmetrized Pauli string sums as
Pauli cycles
.

Definition 2

(Pauli cycle)
.

Let
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
(
5
) be a (non-identity) Pauli string on an
n
-qubit system. Its associated Pauli cycle is the
C_{n}
-invariant operator

O_{P}:=i\mathcal{T}(P)=\frac{i}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}U_{\tau}^{i}\,P\,(U_{\tau}^{\dagger})^{i}.

(36)

We write
P{\sim}P^{\prime}
if
P^{\prime}{=}U_{\tau}^{i}P(U_{\tau}^{\dagger})^{i}
for some
{i\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}}
, i.e. if
P
and
P^{\prime}
lie in the same cycle. Choosing one representative for each equivalence class yields a set of Pauli cycles
\mathcal{C}_{n}
that spans the subspace
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}\cap\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
(or the full
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{TI}}
as soon as

is included).

In particular, after fixing a canonical representative for each cyclic orbit, testing whether two Pauli cycles coincide reduces to comparing their representatives, rather than comparing all translated summands individually. This gives the analogue of fast linear-independence checks in the cycle basis (cf.
Corollary

1
).

A naive commutator computation between two Pauli cycles in the standard Pauli basis would expand each cycle into
n
translated copies, leading to
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
pairwise Pauli-string commutators. Since each such commutator can be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time in the standard representation (cf.
Corollary

2
), this yields an overall
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
procedure. The cyclic symmetry reduces this cost by one factor of

:

Proposition 1

(Commutator of Pauli cycles)
.

For two Pauli cycles

and

defined as in
Definition

[O_{P},O_{P^{\prime}}]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ O_{i[P,P^{\prime(k)}]},

(37)

with
P^{\prime(k)}=U_{\tau}^{k}P^{\prime}(U_{\tau}^{\dagger})^{k}
.
In particular, the commutator can be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})

The identity (
37

O_{P}=i\mathcal{T}(P)
and
O_{P^{\prime}}=i\mathcal{T}(P^{\prime})
, using linearity of the commutator, and exploiting the translation invariance of
O_{P^{\prime}}
. A short derivation is given in Appendix
B.1
.

Corollary 3

(Linear-time commutators for bounded-weight Pauli cycles)
.

Let
w_{P}
and
w_{P^{\prime}}
denote the Pauli weights of
P
and
P^{\prime}
, i.e. the numbers of non-identity letters in the generating strings. If
w_{P},w_{P^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, then the commutator of the corresponding Pauli cycles can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n)
time.

Thus, for translation-invariant generators of bounded Pauli weight, passing to the symmetry-adapted basis of Pauli cycles reduces the cost of Lie-algebraic manipulations to the same asymptotic scaling as for single Pauli strings, making
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
efficient also in this symmetry-restricted free-fermion setting. A proof for
Corollary

3
is given in Appendix
B.2
.

Remark 1

(Translation-invariant sums with open boundaries)
.

The efficiency of the Pauli cycle formalism extends to translation-invariant sums on open chains via a bulk-boundary decomposition. By formally representing operators as a periodic bulk cycle supplemented by strictly localized boundary defects (e.g.,
A=O_{\mathrm{bulk}}+B_{\mathrm{left}}+B_{\mathrm{right}}
), commutators separate into bulk-bulk interactions evaluated via
Eq.

37
and purely local boundary corrections. For bounded-weight generators, these defect interactions overlap on at most
\mathcal{O}(1)
lattice sites, thus strictly preserving the
\mathcal{O}(n)
computational complexity established in
Corollary

3
.

Example 1

(Transverse-field Ising model (TFIM))
.

The transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}=J\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}Z_{i}Z_{i+1}-g\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}

(38)

for open boundary conditions, and analogously with
Z_{n}Z_{1}
added for periodic boundaries. A Hamiltonian variational ansatz (HVA) built from this model uses the generator set

G_{\mathrm{TFIM}}=\left\{\sum_{i\in V}X_{i},\ \sum_{(i,j)\in E}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right\}

(39)

for
G=(V,E)
either the path or the cycle graph on
n
vertices. This is also exactly the generator set of QAOA for MaxCut on a path or cycle graph. Up to single-qubit Clifford conjugations, it is a special case of the free-fermion support pattern (
30
); in particular, its DLA is contained in a free-fermion algebra and therefore remains polynomially bounded. More precisely, the exact DLA dimension is
n^{2}
for open boundaries
78
and
3n-1
for periodic boundaries
5
.
It is instructive to contrast this with the corresponding
freely parameterized
(edge-local) variant, in which each single-qubit and two-qubit term carries an independent parameter, i.e.

G_{\mathrm{TFIM}}^{\mathrm{free}}=\{X_{i}\}_{i\in V}\ \cup\ \{Z_{i}Z_{j}\}_{(i,j)\in E}.

(40)

The resulting DLAs have been characterized in Ref.
69
; for a path one obtains
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=n(2n-1)
, while for a cycle
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=2n(2n-1)
.

Numerical benchmarks illustrating the asymptotic advantage of working in the Pauli cycle basis are given in Appendix
B.3
for the TFIM instance in
Example

1
.

V
Permutation-invariant algebras

Permutation symmetry is a natural inductive bias for problems which are invariant under relabeling of qubits, as in exchange-symmetric (collective) many-body settings or graph-based quantum machine learning models. More generally, symmetry-respecting architectures have been observed to outperform problem-agnostic ones, and in the specific
S_{n}
-equivariant case they can exhibit improved trainability and generalization properties
87
;
112
.
This section proposes a symmetry-adapted operator basis for permutation-invariant dynamics on
n
qubits. The resulting
Pauli orbit
representation allows us to carry out the preprocessing steps of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
(basis construction and structure constants) efficiently even when the generators have exponentially large Pauli expansions.

Let
U_{\pi}
denote the unitary representation of
\pi\in S_{n}
acting on
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
by permuting tensor factors. We define the permutation-invariant operator algebra

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}:=\{A\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})\,:\,[A,U_{\pi}]=0\ \forall\pi\in S_{n}\},

(41)

where
S_{n}
is the symmetric group of all permutations of
n
elements. This algebra is the commutant of the
S_{n}
action and forms a proper subalgebra of
\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
.

Proposition 2

(Decomposition and dimension of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
)
.

The algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
is isomorphic to

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}\cong\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\mathfrak{u}(n-2k+1),

(42)

and has dimension

\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}})=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(n-2k+1)^{2}=\binom{n+3}{3}.

(43)

Proposition

2
is a standard consequence of the Schur–Weyl duality. Related statements on this decomposition and the resulting dimension of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
appear, e.g., in Refs.
4
;
7
;
5
;
100
;
112
;
69
. For completeness, we provide a short derivation in Appendix
C.1
.

Corollary 4

(Cubic upper bound for permutation-invariant DLAs)
.

Let
\mathfrak{g}=\langle\mathcal{G}\rangle_{\mathrm{Lie}}
be a DLA generated by a set of permutation-invariant generators
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
. Then
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
and hence

\dim(\mathfrak{g})\leq\binom{n+3}{3}=\mathcal{O}(n^{3}).

(44)

V.1
Explicit basis via Pauli orbits

A convenient way to construct explicit elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
is to view
S_{n}
as acting on the operator space by conjugation. Concretely, for each
\pi\in S_{n}
we have a linear map

\operatorname{Ad}_{U_{\pi}}:\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})\to\mathfrak{u}(2^{n}),\qquad\operatorname{Ad}_{U_{\pi}}(A):=U_{\pi}AU_{\pi}^{\dagger}.

(45)

The permutation-invariant algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
is precisely the fixed-point subspace of this representation. A standard construction from representation theory, the Reynolds operator, gives the orthogonal projection onto this trivial subrepresentation. Define the symmetrization map

\mathcal{S}(\cdot):=\frac{1}{|S_{n}|}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}\operatorname{Ad}_{U_{\pi}}(\cdot)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\pi\in S_{n}}U_{\pi}(\cdot)U_{\pi}^{\dagger}.

(46)

Then
\mathcal{S}(A)\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
for all
A\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
, and
\mathcal{S}(A)=A
for all
A\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
. Moreover, with respect to the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product,
\mathcal{S}
is an orthogonal projector onto
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
.

This naturally allows for defining a concrete operator basis for
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
(cf. Ref.
7
;
5
) that avoids writing generators as explicit sums over exponentially many Pauli strings.

Definition 3

(Pauli orbit)
.

For an
n
-qubit system, define the Pauli orbit labeled by
(p,q,r)
as the skew-Hermitian operator

B_{p,q,r}:=i\>\mathcal{S}(P_{p,q,r})\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}

(47)

with
p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
p+q+r\leq n
, where

P_{p,q,r}=X^{\otimes p}\otimes Y^{\otimes q}\otimes Z^{\otimes r}\otimes I^{\otimes(n-p-q-r)}.

(48)

Equivalently,
B_{p,q,r}
is (up to the global factor
i
) the permutation-invariant sum of all Pauli strings containing
X
,
Y
, and
Z
on exactly
p
,
q
, and
r
sites, and identities on the remaining sites.
The expansion of
B_{p,q,r}
in the standard Pauli basis contains

N_{\mathrm{terms}}(p,q,r;n)=\frac{n!}{p!\,q!\,r!\,(n-p-q-r)!}

(49)

distinct Pauli strings, which is exponential in
n
when
p,q,r=\Theta(n)
. The Pauli orbit representation is exponentially more efficient by storing each basis element
B_{p,q,r}
instead via the tuple
(p,q,r)
and the global system size
n
.

Proposition 3

(Pauli orbits form a basis of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
)
.

Let

\mathcal{B}_{n}:=\{\,B_{p,q,r}\ :\ p,q,r\in\mathbb{N}_{0},\ p+q+r\leq n\,\},

(50)

where
B_{p,q,r}
is the Pauli orbit defined in
Definition

3
. Then
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is a basis of the permutation-invariant operator algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
. In particular,

\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{B}_{n})=\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\mathcal{B}_{n}|=\binom{n+3}{3}.

(51)

Proof sketch.

Every
A\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
has a (unique) expansion in the Pauli-string basis. Applying
\mathcal{S}
termwise groups Pauli strings into
S_{n}
-orbits under conjugation by permutations, and produces an element of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
. Conversely, if
A\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
then
\mathcal{S}(A)=A
, so
A
equals a linear combination of such orbit-sums. Choosing for each orbit a canonical representative of the form
P_{p,q,r}
yields spanning by
\mathcal{B}_{n}
. To see linear independence, note that each
B_{p,q,r}
has disjoint Pauli support for distinct triples
(p,q,r)
. Since Pauli strings form an orthogonal basis under the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, the
B_{p,q,r}
are mutually orthogonal and hence linearly independent. The full proof can be found in Appendix
C.2
.
∎

Proposition

3
is consistent with
Proposition

2
: the set
\mathcal{B}_{n}
has cardinality
\binom{n+3}{3}
, matching
\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}})
.

Finally, note that
B_{0,0,0}
is proportional to the central element
iI_{n}
. If desired one may pass to
\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})
by omitting this single basis element.

V.2
Efficient primitives: inner products and commutators

The preprocessing stage of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
requires efficient evaluation of inner products and commutators in order to construct a DLA basis and compute the associated structure constants (cf. Fig.
2
).

Lemma 1

(Hilbert–Schmidt inner product of Pauli orbits)
.

For Pauli orbits
B_{p,q,r}
and
B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
,

\langle B_{p,q,r},B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{(p,q,r),(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime})}\,\|B_{p,q,r}\|^{2}.

(52)

In particular, the normalized inner product required for structure constants reduces to an equality check of the orbit labels and can be evaluated in constant time. Moreover, under the distinct string convention,

\|B_{p,q,r}\|^{2}=\frac{2^{n}}{N_{\mathrm{terms}}(p,q,r;n)}.

(53)

Theorem 1

(Commutator of Pauli orbits)
.

For two Pauli orbits
B_{p,q,r}
and
B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
defined as in
Definition

3
, the commutator closes in the Pauli orbit basis:

[B_{p,q,r},B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}]=\sum_{(\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r})}\>c_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}}\>B_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}},

(54)

with
B_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
In particular, at most
|\mathcal{B}_{n}|=\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
distinct output orbits can appear. Moreover, the coefficients
c_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}}
admit an explicit finite-sum formula over contingency tables and can be evaluated in
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
time; in the worst case, evaluating the full commutator by direct enumeration costs
O(n^{9})
arithmetic operations after factorial precomputation.

The explicit coefficient formula, algorithms, and runtime benchmarks are given in Appendix
C.3
. Here, we merely emphasize that, for
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, commutators close within the orbit basis and remain efficiently evaluable despite the exponential Pauli support of individual orbit elements.

Corollary 5

(Constant-time commutator coefficients for bounded-weight generators)
.

Fix any finite target set of output orbits
T=\{B_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}\ |\ \tilde{p}+\tilde{q}+\tilde{r}=\mathcal{O}(1)\}\subset\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Then for any two Pauli orbits
B_{p,q,r}
and
B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}
, all targeted coefficients
\{c_{\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r}}:(\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r})\in T\}
in (
54
) can be computed in constant time. Equivalently, the enumeration of valid contingency tables is bounded by

\mathcal{O}\left(\sum_{(\tilde{p},\tilde{q},\tilde{r})\in T}\binom{\tilde{p}+3}{3}\binom{\tilde{q}+3}{3}\binom{\tilde{r}+3}{3}\right),

(55)

which is
O(1)
for
|T|=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

In particular, for variational ansätze generated by a constant number of symmetric
k
-local Hamiltonians with
k
independent of the system size
n
, the commutator information required to build the relevant adjoint representation involves only bounded-weight orbit labels. Hence, the runtime cost of computing the commutators for the respective structure constants (
13
) are
T_{\mathrm{com}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
).

Example 2

(Permutation-equivariant quantum neural networks (QNNs))
.

A canonical bounded-weight generator set in
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
is

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i},\ \sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right\}

(56)

\displaystyle\equiv\{B_{1,0,0},\,B_{0,1,0},\,B_{0,0,2}\},

where the equivalence is in the Pauli orbit representation introduced above. This set appears, for instance, in permutation-equivariant quantum neural networks
80
;
112
;
100
and is known to generate the full commutant algebra up to the central identity, i.e. it spans
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}\cap\mathfrak{su}(2^{n})

4
;
112
. Since all generators have bounded Pauli weight, the targeted commutator coefficients required for
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing fall into the constant-time regime of Corollary
5
.

Example 3

(QAOA for MaxCut on a complete graph)
.

Consider the QAOA-type generator set

G=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i},\ \sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}Z_{i}Z_{j}\right\}\equiv\{B_{1,0,0},\,B_{0,0,2}\},

(57)

which corresponds to a MaxCut problem defined on a complete graph (and related fully-connected tranverse field Ising problems). The associated DLA is permutation-invariant and has been analyzed in Ref.
5
. Again, both generators have bounded weight, so the commutator information needed to build their adjoint representations can be obtained in constant time per targeted coefficients (Corollary
5
).

This highlights that most practical cases of
S_{n}
-invariant quantum systems can be efficiently simulated with
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
. While Theorem
1
provides a uniform worst-case polynomial guarantee, the practically relevant regime of low-weight (few-body) generators leads to much smaller effective search spaces: the required commutator information reduces to a constant-size combinatorial enumeration independent of
n
(Corollary
5
). Moreover, even when computing large collections of commutators within
\mathcal{B}_{n}
(as in structure-constant preprocessing), our benchmarks indicate that the
observed
average-case runtime per commutator grows only mildly with
n
reflecting that most orbit pairs lie near the boundary of the feasible contingency polytope and admit comparatively few overlap patterns (cf. Appendix
C.3.4
).

V.3
Algorithmic consequence for
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}

Lemma

1
and
Theorem

1
provide the primitives to execute the
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing pipeline directly in the Pauli orbit basis: constant-time inner products and efficiently computable commutators (via the explicit contingency-table formula in Appendix
C.3
). As a result, Lie closure and structure-constant computation can be carried out in polynomial time for permutation-invariant generator sets even when their expansions in the standard Pauli basis contain exponentially many terms.

Corollary 6

(Efficient
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
for permutation-invariant circuits)
.

Let
U(\bm{\theta})
be a circuit of the form (
2
) whose dynamical generators lie in
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
and are provided in Pauli-orbit representation. Then the
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing stage (basis construction and structure constants) can be implemented using arithmetic resources polynomial in
n
and in the resulting Lie-algebra dimension
\dim(\mathfrak{g})\leq\binom{n+3}{3}
.
In particular, commutators between basis elements can be evaluated in worst-case time
\mathcal{O}(n^{9})
(and, in the bounded-weight targeted regime relevant to few-body generators, with
\mathcal{O}(1)
enumeration cost per required coefficient; see Appendix
C.3.3
).

Corollary
6
shows that exponential Pauli support of individual generators does not preclude efficient
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing (as was stated in Ref.
64
). Instead, permutation symmetry admits a compact orbit description in which the Lie-algebraic operations needed by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
are polynomial-time (and often substantially faster in typical few-body settings).

Remark 2

(Relation to Schur-basis simulation methods)
.

Ref.
7
proposes an alternative classical simulation strategy for permutation-invariant (and, more generally, symmetric) quantum systems based on Schur–Weyl duality. Their approach likewise exploits a symmetry-adapted operator basis for
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
(equivalent to our Pauli-orbit basis), but proceeds by representing such operators in the Schur basis where they become block diagonal across irreducible sectors. The main bottleneck of this approach is the computation of the corresponding reduced matrix elements (the “
F
-tensor”). For qubits, the preprocessing cost for this Schur-basis data becomes
\mathcal{O}(n^{7})
via tensor network methods. By contrast, combining the complexity bounds from Section
II.2
with
Corollary

5
shows that, in typical practical
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
-regimes,
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
’s preprocessing – in particular the computation of orbit-level structure constants – scales as
\mathcal{O}(n^{6})
. A more detailed comparison with
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, which operates natively in the Pauli orbit basis via commutators, is provided in Appendix
C.4
.

VI
Bounded Hamming-weight and few-body
U(1)
-invariant algebras

Another non-trivial source of polynomially growing DLAs, beyond free-fermion algebras (with
{\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}){=}\mathcal{O}(n^{2})}
) and permutation-invariant algebras (with
{\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}){=}\mathcal{O}(n^{3})}
), is given by Hamming-weight (HW) preserving circuits, also referred to as
U(1)
-equivariant circuits. Such circuits conserve the number of excitations and therefore act invariantly on fixed-weight sectors of the computational basis. They have received increased attention as expressive yet symmetry-respecting ansätze in QML
70
;
37
;
36
;
95
and, in particular, in quantum chemistry
53
;
123
. Under standard fermionic encodings (such as the Jordan–Wigner or parity mapping), HW-preservation corresponds strictly to particle-number conservation, making these circuits the natural algebraic setting for studying bounded excitation manifolds.

Let

S_{Z}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}

(58)

denote the total magnetization operator. We define the HW-preserving (equivalently,
U(1)
-invariant) operator algebra as the commutant

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}:=\{A\in\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})\ :\ [A,S_{Z}]=0\}.

(59)

Equivalently, one may use the excitation-number operator

N:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{I-Z_{i}}{2}=\frac{n}{2}I-\frac{1}{2}S_{Z},

(60)

so that
[A,S_{Z}]=0
if and only if
[A,N]=0
. In either form,
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
consists precisely of those operators that are invariant under the global
U(1)
action generated by
S_{Z}
, i.e. conjugation by
e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}S_{Z}}
.

The eigenspaces of
N
(or
S_{Z}
) define the fixed Hamming-weight sectors

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{H}_{k}:=\mathrm{span}\{\,|x\rangle:x\in\{0,1\}^{n},\ |x|=k\,\},\\
\ \text{with}\quad\dim(\mathcal{H}_{k})=d_{k}:=\binom{n}{k}.\end{gathered}

(61)

Since every
A\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
commutes with
N
, it cannot mix basis states of different Hamming weight. Consequently, every element of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
is block diagonal with respect to the decomposition
{\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}}
.

Proposition 4

(Decomposition and dimension of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
)
.

The
U(1)
-invariant Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
decomposes as a direct sum of orthogonal subspace algebras,

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}\cong\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\ \mathfrak{u}(d_{k})

(62)

and therefore has dimension

\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}d_{k}^{2}=\binom{2n}{n}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{4^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(63)

In particular, circuits generated by elements of
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
act independently on each
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.

A full derivation of
Proposition

4
is given in Appendix
D.1

A convenient “universal” parametrization of two-qubit HW-preserving Hamiltonians follows from the observation that HW-preservation on qubits
i,j
means that the Hamiltonian acts nontrivially only on
\operatorname{span}\{\ket{01},\ket{10}\}
.

Definition 4

(Universal HW-preserving generators)
.

Any two-qubit HW-preserving Hermitian generator can be written as
133

H^{\mathrm{HW}}_{ij}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&0&0\\
0&a&b&0\\
0&\bar{b}&c&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{array}\right)_{ij}=e\,\mathbf{E}_{ij}+s\,\mathbf{S}_{ij}+r\,\mathbf{R}_{ij}+j\,\mathbf{J}_{ij},

(64)

where
a,c\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}
, and we use the real parameters

e=\frac{a+c}{2},\quad s=\frac{a-c}{2},\quad r=\operatorname{Re}(b),\quad j=\operatorname{Im}(b).

(65)

The four basis terms admit the Pauli representation

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbf{E}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(I-Z_{i}Z_{j}),\quad\mathbf{S}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(Z_{i}-Z_{j}),\\
&\mathbf{R}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}),\quad\mathbf{J}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(X_{i}Y_{j}-Y_{i}X_{j}).\end{split}

(66)

The decomposition (
66
) makes the boundary between free-fermionic (Gaussian) and genuinely interacting HW-preserving dynamics explicit. The terms
\mathbf{S}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{R}_{ij}
, and
\mathbf{J}_{ij}
are quadratic under the Jordan–Wigner transformation and thus lie within the free fermion algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}
(
24
) (cf.
Section

IV
). In particular, circuits restricted to these generators (including matchgates and nearest-neighbor Reconfigurable Beam Splitter (RBS) gates
53
;
123
;
37
;
96
) generate DLAs of dimension
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
, independently of which subspace
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is considered. By contrast,
\mathbf{E}_{ij}
contains an interaction component (i.e.,
Z_{i}Z_{j}
) which breaks free-fermionic integrability and can lead to full controllability on fixed-HW subspaces.

Ref.
133
makes this precise: under full connectivity, subspace universality on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
holds if and only if
e\neq 0
and either
j\neq 0
or (
r\neq 0\,\wedge\,s\neq 0
). In these interacting cases, the induced DLA on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
becomes the full special unitary algebra
\mathfrak{su}(d_{k})
with dimension
d_{k}^{2}-1
(or
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
with dimension
d_{k}^{2}
, if the central identity is included). In contrast, if the circuit is restricted to a real orthogonal gate set on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(e.g., non-local
\mathbf{J}_{ij}
generators), the induced algebra is contained in
\mathfrak{so}(d_{k})
with dimension
\frac{1}{2}d_{k}(d_{k}-1)

96
. These regimes therefore yield polynomially sized, yet highly expressive, DLAs beyond both free-fermion and permutation-invariant families.

Proposition 5

(Polynomial DLAs for bounded-HW circuits)
.

Although the global
U(1)
-invariant Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
has exponential dimension (cf.
Proposition

4
), the effective dynamics of any HW-preserving circuit initialized in a fixed sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is governed by the restriction of the DLA to that subspace, which is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
.
If
k
is bounded (i.e.,
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
), then
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
and hence any maximally expressive interacting DLA on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
has polynomial dimension

\dim(\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}))=d_{k}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2k}),

(67)

and is efficiently simulable via
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
be the DLA generated by the HW-preserving circuit. By definition (
59
), any generator
A\in\mathfrak{g}
satisfies
[A,N]=0
. Hence,
A
preserves the eigenspaces of
N
(
60
), implying that each fixed-weight subspace
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is invariant under the action of
\mathfrak{g}
. Therefore the effective algebra governing the evolution of any initial state supported on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a subalgebra of the space of all skew-Hermitian operators on a
d_{k}
-dimensional Hilbert space, which is exactly
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
with
\dim(\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}))=d_{k}^{2}
.
If
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, then
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}\leq n^{k}/k!=\mathcal{O}(n^{k})
, and consequently
\dim(\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}))=\mathcal{O}(n^{2k})
.
∎

Consequently, for small constant
k
(e.g.
k=1,2,\dots
), HW-preserving circuits that break matchgate structure remain efficiently simulable by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
when the simulation is carried out in the relevant invariant sector. While the
k=1
case represents a single-excitation manifold (physically corresponding to non-interacting particle hopping), for
k\geq 2
, the system supports genuine particle interactions. In this truly interacting regime of
k\geq 2
, the resulting
\mathcal{O}(n^{2k})
DLA dimension is at least quartic, exceeding both the free-fermion
\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
and permutation-invariant
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
regimes. This defines a distinct class of tractable yet expressive quantum dynamics.

Remark 3

(Counterexample for
\dim(\mathfrak{g}){=}\mathcal{O}(n^{2})
implying
\mathfrak{g}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{FF}}
)
.

It is often suggested in recent literature on Pauli Lie algebras that all quadratically scaling dynamical Lie algebras correspond to free-fermions, i.e., they are subalgebras of
\mathfrak{so}(2m)
or
\mathfrak{so}(2m+1)
(cf.
Section

IV
).
A simple physically relevant counterexample is provided by HW-preserving dynamics restricted to the single-excitation sector
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, which has dimension
d_{1}=n
. With a universal generator set as in
Definition

4
, the induced Lie algebra is
\mathfrak{su}(n)

133
and hence has quadratic dimension
\dim(\mathfrak{su}(n))=n^{2}-1
. In general,
\mathfrak{su}(n)
is not isomorphic to
\mathfrak{so}(2m)
or
\mathfrak{so}(2m{+}1)
(up to the exceptional low-dimensional coincidences
\mathfrak{su}(2)\cong\mathfrak{so}(3)
and
\mathfrak{su}(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(6)
). Thus, even though the underlying physics corresponds to a single-particle (non-interacting) sector, the resulting quadratic-dimensional DLA is algebraically distinct from the matchgate/free-fermion
\mathfrak{so}
family.

In the following, we discuss how to apply
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
to bounded-HW algebras in practice.

VI.1
Explicit basis and primitives for
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}

As in the settings discussed previously, efficient Lie-algebraic simulation hinges on an explicit operator basis for the relevant DLA together with efficient primitives for linear-independence tests, commutators, and hence structure-constant evaluation. In the bounded-HW regime, the dynamics of interest is confined to a fixed sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of dimension
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
, so the natural Lie algebra for simulation is the full subspace algebra
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
(and its subalgebras such as
\mathfrak{so}(d_{k})\subset\mathfrak{su}(d_{k})\subset\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
). For
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, we have
\dim(\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}))=d_{k}^{2}=\mathrm{poly}(n)
by
Proposition

5
.

A key technical difference from earlier sections is that Pauli strings, while linearly independent on the full Hilbert space
(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, become highly redundant after restriction to a fixed-weight sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
: many distinct Pauli strings induce the same operator on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and many others restrict to zero. Consequently, the Pauli-string basis is not well suited as a basis of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
. We therefore propose an explicit matrix-unit basis closely related to the generalized Gell–Mann (GGM) basis for
\mathfrak{su}(d)

71
;
19
, which we extend to
\mathfrak{u}(d)
(skew-Hermitian generators) by including general diagonal projectors.

Definition 5

(Modified Generalized Gell-Mann (MGGM) basis for
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
)
.

Fix an ordering
\{\ket{a}\}_{a=1}^{d_{k}}
of all computational basis states of Hamming weight
k
, so that
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\operatorname{span}(\ket{1},\dots,\ket{d_{k}})
. Define, for
1\leq a<b\leq d_{k}
,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{A}^{ab}

\displaystyle:=|a\rangle\langle b|-|b\rangle\langle a|,

(68)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{S}^{ab}

\displaystyle:=i(\ket{a}\bra{b}+\ket{b}\bra{a}),

(69)

and for
1\leq a\leq d_{k}
,

\Lambda_{P}^{a}:=i\,\ket{a}\bra{a}.

(70)

Then the joint set

\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(k)}:=\{\Lambda_{A}^{ab}\}_{a<b}\ \cup\ \{\Lambda_{S}^{ab}\}_{a<b}\ \cup\ \{\Lambda_{P}^{a}\}_{a}

(71)

forms a (real) basis of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
. Moreover,

|\{\Lambda_{A}^{ab}\}_{a<b}|=|\{\Lambda_{S}^{ab}\}_{a<b}|=\tfrac{1}{2}d_{k}(d_{k}-1)
and
|\{\Lambda_{P}^{a}\}_{a}|=d_{k}
, hence
|\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(k)}|=d_{k}^{2}
. Finally,
\mathrm{span}\{\Lambda_{A}^{ab}\}_{a<b}\cong\mathfrak{so}(d_{k})
.

Note that the standard GGM basis for
\mathfrak{su}(d)
uses
d_{k}-1
traceless diagonal generators, which we replace by the
d_{k}
diagonal projectors
\{\Lambda_{P}^{a}\}_{a}
. This yields a particularly transparent representation of HW-preserving generators, and directly spans
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
.

To apply
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, we need to express the dynamical generators (e.g. the two-qubit HW-preserving terms of
Definition

4
) in the basis
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(k)}
. Intuitively, a two-qubit HW-preserving gate on qubits
i,j
only couples weight-
k
basis states that differ by swapping the occupation of
i
and
j
. This gives rise to a sparse representation in the MGGM basis supported only on those basis pairs.

Proposition 6

(MGGM basis representation of HW-preserving generators)
.

Fix
k
and an ordering
\{|a\rangle\}_{a=1}^{d_{k}}
of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
as in
Definition

5
. For a fixed pair of qubits
i,j
, define the set of
\emph{activebasispairs}

C_{ij}^{(k)}:=\{(a,b):\,a<b,\ \langle a|n_{i}n_{j}|a\rangle\neq\langle b|n_{i}n_{j}|b\rangle\},

(72)

i.e. all ordered pairs of HW-
k
computational basis states that differ only by exchanging the occupations of
i
and
j
(equivalently, those with local patterns
01
and
10
at
(i,j)
).
Then, on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the four HW-preserving basis terms from
Eq.

66
admit the MGGM expansions

\displaystyle i\,\mathbf{J}_{ij}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}}\Lambda_{A}^{ab},

(73)

\displaystyle i\,\mathbf{R}_{ij}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}}\Lambda_{S}^{ab},

\displaystyle i\,\mathbf{S}_{ij}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}}\bigl(\Lambda_{P}^{a}-\Lambda_{P}^{b}\bigr),

\displaystyle i\,\mathbf{E}_{ij}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}}\bigl(\Lambda_{P}^{a}+\Lambda_{P}^{b}\bigr),

where the identification of indices
a,b
depends on the chosen ordering of HW-
k
basis states.
Consequently, any two-qubit HW-preserving generator
H_{ij}
of the form in
Definition

4
restricts to

\begin{gathered}iH_{ij}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}=\\
\sum_{(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}}\left[j\,\Lambda_{A}^{ab}+r\,\Lambda_{S}^{ab}+(s+e)\,\Lambda_{P}^{a}+(e-s)\,\Lambda_{P}^{b}\right],\end{gathered}

(74)

with
e,s,r,j
as in
Eq.

65
.

For fixed
k
, the number of active pairs scales as
{|C_{ij}^{(k)}|=\binom{n-2}{k-1}=\Theta(n^{k-1})}
, since a basis state contributes if and only if exactly one of the two sites
(i,j)
is occupied. A detailed derivation of
Proposition

6
is given in Appendix
D.2
.

For
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, commutators between basis element are the critical primitive, since they generate Lie closure and the structure constants required for efficient observable evolution in the adjoint space. In the MGGM basis, all commutators can be written in closed form using Kronecker-delta identities, reducing them to simple index matching problems.

Lemma 2

(Commutation relations in the MGGM basis)
.

For
1\leq a<b\leq d_{k}
and
1\leq c<d\leq d_{k}
, the MGGM basis elements satisfy

\begin{gathered}{\left[\Lambda_{P}^{a},\Lambda_{P}^{b}\right]=0},\\
{\left[\Lambda_{A}^{ab},\Lambda_{P}^{c}\right]=\delta_{bc}\,\Lambda_{S}^{ac}-\delta_{ac}\,\Lambda_{S}^{bc}},\\
{\left[\Lambda_{S}^{ab},\Lambda_{P}^{c}\right]=-\delta_{bc}\,\Lambda_{A}^{ac}-\delta_{ac}\,\Lambda_{A}^{bc}},\\[5.69054pt]
{\left[\Lambda_{A}^{ab},\Lambda_{A}^{cd}\right]=\delta_{bc}\,\Lambda_{A}^{ad}-\delta_{bd}\,\Lambda_{A}^{ac}-\delta_{ac}\,\Lambda_{A}^{bd}+\delta_{ad}\,\Lambda_{A}^{bc}},\\
{\left[\Lambda_{S}^{ab},\Lambda_{S}^{cd}\right]=-\delta_{bc}\,\Lambda_{A}^{ad}-\delta_{bd}\,\Lambda_{A}^{ac}-\delta_{ac}\,\Lambda_{A}^{bd}-\delta_{ad}\,\Lambda_{A}^{bc}},\\
{\left[\Lambda_{A}^{ab},\Lambda_{S}^{cd}\right]=\delta_{bc}\,\Lambda_{S}^{ad}+\delta_{bd}\,\Lambda_{S}^{ac}-\delta_{ac}\,\Lambda_{S}^{bd}-\delta_{ad}\,\Lambda_{S}^{bc}}.\end{gathered}

(75)

In particular, each commutator expands into at most
\mathcal{O}(1)
basis elements, and can thus be evaluated in constant time given the indices.

These relations follow by direct multiplication from the definitions in
Definition

5
. Since commutators are constant-time and sparse in this basis, the structure constants of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
can be generated efficiently without ever forming explicit
d_{k}\times d_{k}
matrices.

Theorem 2

(Structure constants of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
in the MGGM basis)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(k)}=\{\Lambda_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{d_{k}^{2}}
be an ordered MGGM basis as in
Definition

5
. Then the full set of
nonzero
structure constants

[\Lambda_{\alpha},\Lambda_{\beta}]=\sum_{\gamma}f_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\,\Lambda_{\gamma}

(76)

can be enumerated and computed in total time
\mathcal{O}(d_{k}^{3})
.

Proof.

Each MGGM basis element depends on at most two state indices:
\Lambda_{P}^{a}
carries one index
a
, while
\Lambda_{A}^{ab}
and
\Lambda_{S}^{ab}
carry a pair
(a,b)
. By
Lemma

2
, a commutator can be nonzero only if the two operands share at least one state index (otherwise all Kronecker deltas vanish). Hence nonzero commutators are supported on index patterns of size at most three: they are determined by choosing either a triple
(a,b,c)
of distinct indices (e.g. commutators among
\Lambda_{\bullet}^{ab}
and
\Lambda_{\bullet}^{bc}
), or a pair
(a,b)
together with one projector index (e.g. commutators between
\Lambda_{\bullet}^{ab}
and
\Lambda_{P}^{a}
or
\Lambda_{P}^{b}
). The number of such index patterns is
\mathcal{O}(d_{k}^{3})
. For each fixed pattern,
Lemma

2
gives an explicit
\mathcal{O}(1)
-term expansion, so computing all corresponding nonzero structure constants costs
\mathcal{O}(d_{k}^{3})
total time.
∎

Two practical consequences are worth emphasizing. First, since
\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(k)}
is defined analytically, one never needs to store the basis elements as dense matrices, but it suffices to store their labels (type and indices) and their sparse structure constants. Second, for
k=1
one has
d_{1}=n
, so
Theorem

2
implies an
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
primitive for generating the full adjoint data of
\mathfrak{u}(n)
in this basis, which is asymptotically cheaper than Pauli string preprocessing in quadratically-scaling Pauli Lie algebras (cf.
Section

IV.1
).

Corollary 7

(Efficient
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing for bounded-HW circuits)
.

Let
U(\bm{\theta})
be a HW-preserving circuit acting on a fixed sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and suppose its dynamical generators (from
Eq.

64
) are expressed in the MGGM basis on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(e.g. via
Proposition

6
). Then the
gsim
preprocessing stage on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(basis handling and structure-constant generation) can be performed in
\mathcal{O}(\mathrm{poly}(n))
time. In particular, the structure constants can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(d_{k}^{3})=\mathcal{O}(n^{3k})
time.

Example 4

(Quantum chemistry in a two electron active space)
.

Electronic-structure Hamiltonians conserve particle number. Under standard fermion-to-qubit encodings (e.g. Jordan–Wigner), the resulting qubit dynamics commute with the excitation-number operator (
60
) and hence preserve Hamming weight, i.e. they act block-diagonally on the fixed-weight subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.

A widely used chemistry-motivated VQE ansatz is unitary coupled cluster (UCC)
102
;
6
, with
U=\exp(T-T^\dagger)
where each excitation term has equal numbers of creation and annihilation operators and therefore preserves particle number. In the common UCCSD truncation one retains only single and double excitations
108
, so that a
k
-electron reference determinant (e.g. Hartree–Fock) remains supported entirely on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
For constant active electron number
k=O(1)
, the effective dynamics are confined to
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
with
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
(see Proposition
5
). In particular, for
k=2
one has
d_{2}=\binom{n}{2}=O(n^{2})
and hence
\dim(\mathfrak{g})\leq d_{2}^{2}=O(n^{4})
. Thus, even though the circuit may be highly expressive, the Lie-algebraic simulation primitives remain polynomial in
n
within
\mathcal{H}_{2}
. Typical examples include
\mathrm{H}_{2}
(in an extended orbital basis),
\mathrm{HeH}^{+}
, or effective two-electron active-space models such as
\mathrm{LiH}
with a frozen core.

Example 5

(Quantum data encoders in fixed-HW subspaces)
.

Fixed-HW subspaces also arise in practical state-preparation and QML protocols
37
;
45
, where one deliberately restricts the dynamics to a sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
of fixed Hamming weight (
61
). In particular, Ref.
45
proposes a gate-optimal amplitude encoder acting on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, which loads a real vector of length
d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
using exactly
d_{k}-1
HW-preserving gates and thereby achieves polynomial space compression of degree
k
.
The elementary building blocks of this encoder are uncontrolled and controlled reconfigurable beam-splitter (RBS) gates
53
. On two qubits, an RBS gate is a planar rotation on the subspace
\mathrm{span}\{\ket{01},\ket{10}\}
,
\operatorname{RBS}(\theta)=e^{\,i\theta H_{\mathrm{RBS}}}
with

H_{\mathrm{RBS},ij}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&-i&0\\
0&i&0&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}_{ij}=-\,\mathbf{J}_{ij},

(77)

so, up to our sign convention in
Eq.

66
, it is generated precisely by the HW-preserving term
\mathbf{J}_{ij}
. By
Proposition

6
, the restriction of such gates to a fixed sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is represented entirely by antisymmetric MGGM basis elements
\Lambda_{A}^{ab}
(
69
). Consequently, the real-valued encoder evolves inside the subalgebra
\mathfrak{so}(d_{k})\subseteq\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
. The resulting dynamics therefore fit naturally into the MGGM framework developed above and are exactly simulable with
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
; see also
Section

VII.3
.

Appendix
D.3
presents numerical benchmarks for computing adjoint data in the MGGM basis for
k=1,2,3
, illustrating the practical performance of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
in low-HW regimes.

VII
Numerical experiments

We now illustrate the practical use of the Lie-algebraic simulation framework for
\mathrm{poly}
-DLA systems developed above through a set of representative numerical examples. In each subsection, we revisit a concrete application from the literature whose numerical study was previously limited to relatively small system sizes by conventional state-vector simulation, and show how the same setup can be reproduced and extended to substantially larger sizes using
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
together with the algebra-specific primitives introduced in this work.

The goal of these experiments is mainly methodological: they are intended to demonstrate the computational feasibility of our theory across the three classes of systems considered in this paper. To this end, we include one representative example for each setting, namely a free-fermionic benchmark for the
1D
transverse-field Ising model in Section
VII.1
, a permutation-equivariant quantum neural network for graph classification in Section
VII.2
, and a fixed-Hamming-weight amplitude encoder for
q
-Gaussian state preparation in Section
VII.3
. Rather than providing exhaustive application studies, these examples serve as proof-of-principle demonstrations that the Lie-algebraic formalism can be turned into concrete large-scale simulation routines. Broader perspectives on potentially impactful applications of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
are discussed in
Section

VIII.1
. The code for all experiments is included in the accompanying software package
12
; see the Code Availability statement for details.

VII.1
A provably well-behaved VQA for TFIM ground states in a free-fermion DLA

A useful aspect of the Lie-algebraic viewpoint is that it provides a constructive recipe for variational quantum problems whose optimization landscape is well-behaved in the noiseless setting. Concretely, both barren plateau behavior and spurious suboptimal minima can be avoided by choosing a setup in which (i) the input state
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
, observable
O
, and ansatz generators are all supported on a polynomially growing DLA
\mathfrak{g}
, and (ii) the circuit is sufficiently overparameterized, in the sense that the number of independent parameters exceeds
\dim(\mathfrak{g})
(cf.
Section

II.1
). While this direction can be viewed as complementary to the (still open) conjectured implication “provable absence of barren plateaus
\Rightarrow
efficient classical simulation”
30
, here we use it in the opposite direction: we deliberately design a trainable instance and verify that
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
reproduces the expected favorable optimization behavior at system sizes far beyond state-vector simulation.

As a minimal nontrivial test case we consider the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) on a path graph and a Hamiltonian variational ansatz (HVA)
128
;
132
. We use the freely-parameterized TFIM generator set
G_{\mathrm{TFIM}}^{\mathrm{free}}
(
40
) which generates a quadratic-dimensional DLA with
\dim(\mathfrak{g})=n(2n-1)
for open boundaries
69
(cf.
Example

1
).
Our ansatz consists of
L
layers, each containing one independently parameterized gate for every generator in
G_{\mathrm{TFIM}}^{\mathrm{free}}
, i.e.

U_{\ell}(\bm{\theta}_{\ell})=\prod_{i=1}^{2n-1}e^{-i\theta_{\ell,i}H_{i}},\qquad H_{i}\in G_{\mathrm{TFIM}}^{\mathrm{free}},

(78)

so that the total parameter count is
N_{\mathrm{params}}=(2n-1)L
.
To enforce the overparameterized regime we choose
L=n
, yielding
N_{\mathrm{params}}=\dim(\mathfrak{g})
.

We minimize the energy expectation of the TFIM Hamiltonian (equivalently, we set
O=H_{\mathrm{TFIM}}
in
Eq.

1
). As initial state we take
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}=(\ket{+}\!\bra{+})^{\otimes n}
. This choice is compatible with the Lie-algebraic simulation pipeline in the Pauli-string basis:
\rho_{\mathrm{in}}
has a sparse Pauli expansion supported only on tensor products of
I
and
X
, so the corresponding input vector
\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})}
can be assembled efficiently by evaluating
Eq.

9
over the chosen Pauli basis elements
B_{\alpha}\in i\mathfrak{g}
(cf.
Sections

II.2
and
IV.1
).

We simulate this optimization problem with
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
up to
n=200
and perform
100
independent optimization runs per system size, using L-BFGS-B
28
from uniformly random initial parameters. The exact TFIM ground-state energy
E_{0}
is computed via standard free-fermion diagonalization (Jordan–Wigner / Bogoliubov transformation), which is efficient in this regime and serves only as a reference. The results in
Fig.

3
show that the optimized energies concentrate tightly around
E_{0}
across all tested system sizes, consistent with the expectation that the overparameterized, DLA-confined setup eliminates spurious minima in the noiseless setting and yields a well-behaved optimization task.

A practically relevant caveat is that this benign behavior is fragile under stochastic perturbations of the objective, as encountered with finite-shot estimation
116
;
11
or device noise
127
. Following the methodology of
11
, one can add i.i.d. Gaussian noise to each queried loss value and gradient and study the resulting degradation in the achieved relative energy error as a function of
n

VII.2
Permutation-equivariant QNN for binary graph classification

As a representative application of permutation-equivariant dynamics, we consider the graphs-state classification task studied in Ref.
112
, where an
S_{n}
-equivariant QNN (cf.
Example

2
) is used to distinguish connected from disconnected graph states. Closely related permutation-equivariant architectures have also been explored for the graph isomorphism problem
80
and quantum phase classification
100
.

Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
on
n=|V|
vertices, its graph state is defined as

\ket{G}=\Bigl(\prod_{(i,j)\in E}\mathrm{C}Z_{ij}\Bigr)\ket{+}^{\otimes n},

(79)

where
\mathrm{C}Z_{ij}
is the controlled-
Z
gate. In the full supervised learning problem one considers a dataset

\{(\rho_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{M}
with
\rho_{i}=\ket{G_{i}}\!\bra{G_{i}}
and labels
y_{i}\in\{+1,-1\}

indicating whether
G_{i}
is connected (
+1
) or disconnected (
-1
).
Since relabeling graph vertices corresponds exactly to permuting qubits, the encoding (
79
) is permutation-covariant and therefore naturally matched to an
S_{n}
-equivariant model as discussed in
Section

V
.

Following Ref.
112
, we use a permutation-equivariant QNN whose layers are generated by three permutation-invariant Hamiltonians. In our Pauli-orbit notation these correspond (up to fixed normalizations) to the orbit elements
B_{1,0,0}
,
B_{0,1,0}
, and
B_{0,0,2}
(cf.
Eq.

56
), i.e. collective
X
, collective
Y
, and collective
ZZ
interactions.
Concretely, we take each layer to be

U_{\ell}(\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell},\gamma_{\ell})=e^{-\alpha_{\ell}B_{1,0,0}}\,e^{-\beta_{\ell}B_{0,1,0}}\,e^{-\gamma_{\ell}B_{0,0,2}},

(80)

and the full circuit is
U(\bm{\theta}){=}\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}U_{\ell}(\alpha_{\ell},\beta_{\ell},\gamma_{\ell})
, with
\bm{\theta}=(\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma})
.
As measurement we take the
S_{n}
-invariant two-body
XX
observable

O=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}X_{i}X_{j},

(81)

which is proportional to the Pauli orbit
B_{2,0,0}
. The corresponding linear classification loss is

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}y_{i}\,\mathrm{Tr}[O\,U(\bm{\theta})\,\rho_{i}\,U^{\dagger}(\bm{\theta})].

(82)

In
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
, the relevant input of the adjoint-space propagation is the vector
\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})}
(
9
), expressed in the chosen Lie algebra basis. For graph states, these overlaps can be obtained from their stabilizer description (cf. Ref.
112
). While one can in principle handle general graph states efficiently by approximating their expansion in the Pauli orbit basis (
50
), we keep the present numerical demonstration deliberately simple: we restrict to disconnected graphs whose connected components have size at most
10
. In this regime, the orbit-level decomposition is straightforward to compute exactly by enumerating stabilizer elements component-wise and combining components, yielding an efficient construction of
\mathbf{e}^{(\mathrm{in})}
in the Pauli orbit basis
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.
To place the ansatz in the overparameterized regime discussed in
Section

VIII
Conclusions

As classical simulation continues to establish itself as a critical tool across the quantum computing stack, it is increasingly important to develop broadly applicable, efficient, and practical simulation frameworks. In this work, we advanced this goal by extending Lie-algebraic classical simulation beyond its previously emphasized free-fermion use cases to a much broader class of structured quantum dynamics. We did so both conceptually, by resolving previously prohibitive preprocessing bottlenecks through symmetry-adapted basis constructions and efficient primitives, and practically, through runtime benchmarks, large-scale proof-of-concept simulations, and an open-source software implementation.

Concretely, we showed that
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
can be made practical beyond matchgate/free-fermion settings by constructing symmetry-adapted representations of the underlying dynamical Lie algebra (or of a suitable invariant operator subspace) in which the key preprocessing primitives become efficient. Across the three regimes treated in this paper—free-fermionic Pauli algebras and symmetry-restricted free-fermion equivalents, permutation-invariant algebras, and bounded Hamming-weight (
U(1)
-invariant) subspace algebras—we provided explicit bases, derived efficient commutator and inner-product primitives, and gave complexity bounds for Lie-closure and structure-constant generation. Leveraging an accompanying software implementation
12
, we further verified through runtime benchmarks and large-scale proof-of-concept experiments that in these regimes, preprocessing is not merely polynomial in principle but also practical in wall-clock time. This enables adjoint-space evaluation of mean values, and gradients, at system sizes far beyond the reach of direct state-vector simulation.

These advances substantially broaden the practical scope of Lie-algebraic simulation. On the one hand, they provide the foundation to strengthen and extend existing applications of

\mathrm{supp}(P)\cap\mathrm{supp}(P^{\prime(k)})
is non-empty if and only if there exists
x\in\mathrm{supp}(P)
and
y\in\mathrm{supp}(P^{\prime})
such that

x\equiv y+k\pmod{n}\iff k\equiv x-y\pmod{n}.

(94)

Since there are exactly
w_{P}
choices for
x
and
w_{P^{\prime}}
choices for
y
, there are at most
w_{P}w_{P^{\prime}}
distinct relative shifts
k\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}
for which the supports overlap. For all other shifts,
[P,P^{\prime(k)}]=0
.

Consequently, the summation over
n
shifts reduces to at most
w_{P}w_{P^{\prime}}
non-zero terms. Computing the active shifts
k
requires
\mathcal{O}(w_{P}w_{P^{\prime}})
integer operations. For each active shift, evaluating the standard Pauli string commutator
[P,P^{\prime(k)}]
and extracting the new Pauli cycle representative takes
\mathcal{O}(n)
time in the standard dense representation.

The total time to compute the cycle commutator is thus bounded by
\mathcal{O}(w_{P}w_{P^{\prime}}\cdot n)
. Under the assumption that the local weights are bounded independent of system size,
w_{P},w_{P^{\prime}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the overall asymptotic complexity reduces strictly to
\mathcal{O}(n)
.
∎

Proof of

Proof.

Consider the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
.
Let

act on

by permuting tensor factors,
\pi\mapsto U_{\pi}
, and let
U(2)
act diagonally by

. These two actions commute.
By Schur–Weyl duality,

admits a decomposition into irreducible

\mathcal{V}_{\lambda}
is an irrep of

\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}
is the corresponding Specht module (irrep of

The Schur–Weyl duality may be phrased as a double-commutant statement:
The associative commutant of the

with the condition

to
\lfloor n/2\rfloor

\dim(\mathcal{V}_{(n-k,k)})=2J+1=n-2k+1.

(98)

C.2
Proof of
Proposition

3

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-qubit Pauli strings. The set
\{iP\mid P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
forms a basis for
\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
over
\mathbb{R}
. Because the Reynolds operator
\mathcal{S}:\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})\to\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
is a surjective linear projection, the image of this basis spans
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
.

Conjugation by a permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
acts on a Pauli string
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
purely by permuting the lattice indices of its single-qubit factors. This action preserves the total count of
X
,
Y
, and
Z
matrices within the string. Thus, the equivalence classes (orbits) of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
under
S_{n}
conjugation are uniquely and fully classified by the tuple
(p,q,r)
specifying the number of
X
,
Y
, and
Z
operators, respectively, where
p,q,r\geq 0
and
p+q+r\leq n
.

By selecting the canonical representative
P_{p,q,r}
for each orbit, we capture exactly one element from every equivalence class. Therefore, the set of projected representatives
\mathcal{B}_{n}=\{i\mathcal{S}(P_{p,q,r})\}
spans
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
.

To establish linear independence, we note that the orbit
B_{p,q,r}
is a uniform linear combination of Pauli strings with exact counts
(p,q,r)
. For any distinct tuples
(p,q,r)\neq(p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime})
, the constituent Pauli strings belong to disjoint equivalence classes. Because distinct Pauli strings are strictly orthogonal with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle A,B\rangle=\mathrm{Tr}(A^{\dagger}B)
, operators supported on disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
are also orthogonal. Consequently,
\langle B_{p,q,r},B_{p^{\prime},q^{\prime},r^{\prime}}\rangle=0
. Since the elements of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
are mutually orthogonal and non-zero, they are linearly independent and thus form a basis for
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
.

Finally, the cardinality of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is given by the number of valid tuples
(p,q,r)
. Letting
s=n-p-q-r\geq 0
denote the number of identity operators, the size of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
is the number of weak integer compositions of
n
into exactly
4
parts (
p+q+r+s=n
). By a standard stars-and-bars combinatorial argument, this is exactly
\binom{n+4-1}{4-1}=\binom{n+3}{3}
.
∎

C.4
Comparison with Schur-basis method proposed in Ref.
7

Permutation symmetry can be exploited in several (ultimately equivalent) ways to obtain polynomial-time classical simulation algorithms. Here we compare the Schur-basis method of Ref.
7
with the Pauli-orbit formalism developed in this work, and we clarify how each viewpoint connects to the Lie-algebraic simulation pipeline of
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
.

Ref.
7
also considers the
S_{n}
-invariant operator algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
as in
Eq.

41
. As in our construction, they obtain an explicit basis by applying the Reynolds (twirling) map to Pauli strings. Concretely, their symmetrized Pauli monomials
A_{i}
are obtained by symmetrizing a tensor product with fixed counts of
I,X,Y,Z
across sites. Up to normalization conventions and the global factor of
i
(we work in
\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
), this is the same object as our Pauli orbits
B_{p,q,r}
from
Definition

3
.

The core idea of Ref.
7
is to represent
S_{n}
-invariant operators in the Schur basis associated with Schur-Weyl duality, in which
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
becomes block diagonal across irreducible sectors
\lambda
(cf.
Eq.

95
). In that basis, the action of each basis element
A_{i}
is specified by its reduced matrix elements

F^{\,i,\lambda}_{q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}\;:=\;\langle\lambda,q_{\lambda},p_{\lambda 0}\,|\,A_{i}\,|\,\lambda,q^{\prime}_{\lambda},p_{\lambda 0}\rangle,

(114)

which form what they call the
F
-tensor. Once
F
is available, any permutation-invariant Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}h_{i}\>A_{i}
is represented by blocks

\hat{h}^{\lambda}_{q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}}=\sum_{i}h_{i}\,F^{\,i,\lambda}_{q_{\lambda},q^{\prime}_{\lambda}},

(115)

so ground-state energies and eigenstates reduce to diagonalizing polynomial-size block matrices.

For dynamics, Ref.
7
shows that time-evolved expectation values of invariant observables can be computed from the same
F
-tensor (
114
), and a reduced density matrix in the Schur basis with the multiplicity index traced out. Writing
{\tilde{\rho}^{\lambda}_{q,q^{\prime}}=\sum_{p_{\lambda}}\rho_{(\lambda,q,p_{\lambda}),(\lambda,q^{\prime},p_{\lambda})}}
, and evaluates schematically

\ell(\rho)=\mathrm{tr}(OU\rho U^{\dagger})=\sum_{\lambda}\mathrm{tr}\!\left(\tilde{o}^{\lambda}\,\tilde{u}^{\lambda}\,\tilde{\rho}^{\lambda}\,(\tilde{u}^{\lambda})^{\dagger}\right),

(116)

where
\tilde{o}^{\lambda}
and
\tilde{u}^{\lambda}
are obtained by contracting the coefficient vectors
(o_{i})
and
(u_{i})
against
F^{\,i,\lambda}
. Thus, after preprocessing, simulation tasks reduce to polynomial-size linear algebra within each
\lambda
sector.

This means, both approaches exploit the same commutant algebra and ultimately reduce the simulation task to polynomial-size operations. The main difference is where the symmetry is “spent” computationally, i.e., the choice of preprocessing primitive:

•

Schur-basis method
7
:
one first computes the
F
-tensor that maps symmetrized Pauli coefficients to Schur blocks; subsequent tasks (forming
\hat{h}^{\lambda}
, diagonalization, time evolution, expectation values) are then blockwise linear algebra. The reported qubit scaling is
O(n^{7})
for the preprocessing that builds
F
(via an tensor-network contraction exploiting band structure), and Ref.
7
also provides a purely combinatorial alternative with scaling
O(n^{10})
.

•

Orbit-basis
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
method (this work)
:
we remain in the Pauli orbit basis and compute the Lie-algebraic primitives required by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
directly, namely the adjoint representation of Pauli orbits. Using the fact that orbit inner products are constant-time (
Lemma

1
), and orbit commutators admit an explicit integer-combinatorial evaluation via contingency tables (
Proposition

7
) with polynomial upper bound (Appendix
C.3.2
). In the practically dominant regime where one evolves the system by a fixed, finite set of bounded-Pauli-weight operators (e.g., adjoint representations of a constant generator set), the targeted commutator coefficients have
n
-independent enumeration cost (Appendix
C.3.3
and
Corollary

5
). In this case,
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
needs
\mathcal{O}(|\mathcal{B}_{n}|^{2})=O(n^{6})
for computing the relevant structure constants.

Beyond asymptotic exponents, the two routes differ in their implementation profile. The Schur-basis approach relies on constructing and contracting structured tensor networks involving Schur transform data (Clebsch–Gordan structure), whereas our orbit-based commutator routines are expressed as pruned integer loops. The latter admits low-overhead implementations (e.g., JIT compilation) and yields favorable wall-clock performance in our benchmarks (Appendix
C.3.4
), both in the full-commutator mode and in the targeted bounded-weight mode relevant to
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing.

In summary, Ref.
7
provides an efficient, symmetry-adapted simulation strategy by explicitly block-diagonalizing
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{PI}}
in a Schur basis and then applying polynomial-size blockwise linear algebra, with preprocessing dominated by the computation of the
F
-tensor. Our contribution is to show that one can instead stay in a symmetry-adapted basis and compute the Lie-algebraic primitives required by
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
directly. In the targeted regime of finite few-body generator sets, this yields an asymptotically better preprocessing time for the relevant structure constants, with favorable constant factors in practice, thereby making permutation-invariant systems directly compatible with the
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
pipeline of Ref.
57
.

Appendix D
Details and proofs for bounded Hamming weight algebras

The Hamming-weight (HW) preserving operator algebra
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}\subseteq\mathfrak{u}(2^{n})
(
59
) is the commutant of the global
U(1)
action generated by the total magnetization
S_{Z}
(
58
), i.e., it consists of all (skew-Hermitian) operators
A
satisfying
[A,S_{Z}]=0
. Equivalently,
\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
captures exactly those dynamics that conserve excitation number and therefore act block-diagonally with respect to the Hamming-weight decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}
, where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{k})=d_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
.

Section
VI
shows that, when the dynamics are initialized in a fixed sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
with
k=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the relevant effective DLA is a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
. To enable efficient
\mathfrak{g}{\text{-}}{\rm sim}
preprocessing in this regime,
Section

VI.1
introduces the modified generalized Gell–Mann (MGGM) basis and closed-form commutation rules for computing structure constants.
This appendix collects the supporting material for Section
VI
: (i) a derivation of the decomposition and dimension statement in Proposition
4
, (ii) a proof of the MGGM-basis representation of HW-preserving two-qubit generators in Proposition
6
, and (iii) numerical runtime benchmarks for MGGM-based structure-constant computation.

D.1
Proof of
Proposition

4

Proof.

Let
N
be the excitation-number operator (
60
). Its spectral decomposition is

N=\sum_{k=0}^{n}k\,\Pi_{k},

(117)

where
\Pi_{k}
is the orthogonal projector onto the fixed Hamming-weight sector
\mathcal{H}_{k}
(
61
), and
\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pi_{k}=I
.

Let
A\in\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}
, so
[A,N]=0
. Multiplying the commutation relation on the left by
\Pi_{j}
and on the right by
\Pi_{k}
yields

0=\Pi_{j}[A,N]\Pi_{k}=\Pi_{j}AN\Pi_{k}-\Pi_{j}NA\Pi_{k}.

(118)

Using
N\Pi_{k}=k\Pi_{k}
and
\Pi_{j}N=j\Pi_{j}
, we obtain

0=k\,\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{k}-j\,\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{k}=(k-j)\,\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{k}.

(119)

Hence
\Pi_{j}A\Pi_{k}=0
for all
j\neq k
, i.e.
A
is block diagonal with respect to
\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{k}
:

A=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pi_{k}A\Pi_{k}\;\equiv\;\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}A^{(k)},\qquad A^{(k)}:=\Pi_{k}A\Pi_{k}\big|_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}.

(120)

Conversely, any block-diagonal operator
A=\bigoplus_{k}A^{(k)}
with
A^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{u}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\cong\mathfrak{u}(d_{k})
commutes with
N
, since
N
acts as the scalar
k
on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Therefore the commutant algebra is exactly the direct sum (
62
)

\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}}\cong\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}).

(121)

For the dimension, we use
\dim(\mathfrak{u}(d_{k}))=d_{k}^{2}
to obtain

\dim(\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{HW}})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}d_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}^{2}=\binom{2n}{n},

(122)

where the last equality is Vandermonde’s identity (set
m=r=n
). Finally, Stirling’s approximation
\binom{2n}{n}\sim\frac{4^{n}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}
which implies the asymptotic estimate
\binom{2n}{n}=\mathcal{O}\!\left(\frac{4^{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)
(
63
).
∎

Proof.

i

|11\rangle_{ij}

|01\rangle_{ij}

|10\rangle_{ij}

By definition, the operators
\mathbf{E}_{ij}

\mathbf{S}_{ij}
,
\mathbf{R}_{ij}

\mathbf{J}_{ij}
all act trivially (evaluating to zero) on any state where the occupations are identical. Specifically,
Z_{i}Z_{j}=I
on
|00\rangle
and
|11\rangle

\mathbf{E}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(I-Z_{i}Z_{j})
annihilates them. Similarly,
\mathbf{S}_{ij}\propto(Z_{i}-Z_{j})

\mathbf{R}_{ij}\propto(X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j})
, and
\mathbf{J}_{ij}\propto(X_{i}Y_{j}-Y_{i}X_{j})
strictly annihilate the
|00\rangle
and
|11\rangle
sectors.

Consequently, the non-trivial dynamics are entirely restricted to a direct sum of isolated 2D subspaces spanned by the active pairs
C_{ij}^{(k)}
(
72
). For each pair
(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}
, the states
|a\rangle
and
|b\rangle
are identical on the
n-2
spectator qubits but differ exactly by the local patterns
0_{i}1_{j}
and
1_{i}0_{j}
. Without loss of generality, let
|a\rangle
correspond to the
0_{i}1_{j}
configuration and
|b\rangle
to the
1_{i}0_{j}
configuration (consistent with the sign conventions of the chosen index ordering).

Evaluating the action of the anti-Hermitian generators on this 2D span yields exactly the localized MGGM basis matrices:

\displaystyle i\mathbf{E}_{ij}\big|_{\operatorname{span}(a,b)}

\displaystyle=i(|a\rangle\langle a|+|b\rangle\langle b|)=\Lambda_{P}^{a}+\Lambda_{P}^{b},

(123)

\displaystyle i\mathbf{S}_{ij}\big|_{\operatorname{span}(a,b)}

\displaystyle=i(|a\rangle\langle a|-|b\rangle\langle b|)=\Lambda_{P}^{a}-\Lambda_{P}^{b},

(124)

\displaystyle i\mathbf{R}_{ij}\big|_{\operatorname{span}(a,b)}

\displaystyle=i(|a\rangle\langle b|+|b\rangle\langle a|)=\Lambda_{S}^{ab},

(125)

\displaystyle i\mathbf{J}_{ij}\big|_{\operatorname{span}(a,b)}

\displaystyle=|a\rangle\langle b|-|b\rangle\langle a|=\Lambda_{A}^{ab}.

(126)

Summing these distinct block contributions over all active pairs
(a,b)\in C_{ij}^{(k)}
directly proves the expansions in
Eq.

73
.

Finally, multiplying the physical Hamiltonian

(127)

Grouping the diagonal terms by
\Lambda_{P}^{a}
and
\Lambda_{P}^{b}

(s+e)\Lambda_{P}^{a}+(e-s)\Lambda_{P}^{b}
, concluding the proof.
∎
