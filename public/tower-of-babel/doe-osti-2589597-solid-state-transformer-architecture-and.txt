ABSTRACT
A high-altitude electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) or similar geomagnetic disturbance (GMD) has
the potential to impact the operation of large-scale electric power grids. By introducing low-
frequency common-mode (CM) currents, these events can degrade the performance of critical
system components, such as large power transformers by introducing CM currents which can lead
to magnetic saturation of the transformer core. In this work, a solid-state transformer (SST) is
developed to replace susceptible equipment and improve grid resiliency by safely absorbing these
CM disturbances. This device will be referred to as a common-mode solid-state transformer
(CM-SST).
An SST architecture based on a four-legged AC/DC converter is developed. This architecture
enables active control of CM signals without disturbing the AC voltages or the real and reactive
power delivery capabilities. A system-level model of this architecture is created, and time-domain
simulations are performed to evaluate the SST’s performance in response to simulated CM distur-
bances.
A control strategy for mitigating CM current is also investigated. Hamiltonian surface shaping and
power flow control (HSSPFC) is used to design a nonlinear controller for the SST’s output inverter.
The objectives of the controller are to suppress CM-induced AC current offsets and regulate AC
currents to desired setpoints. Nonlinear system analysis is applied to design and validate the
controller. Two cases are tested: (a) the proposed four-leg inverter and (b) a standard three-leg
inverter. The results show that the proposed controller rapidly mitigates CM disturbances while
maintaining high-quality AC current waveforms in the four-leg configuration.
Finally, the hardware performance of an SST prototype is evaluated. In particular, the ability of
the SST to safely redirect and absorb CM currents is demonstrated, showing how it can protect
neighboring conventional transformers in the system. The study confirms that appropriate control
laws allow the SST to protect both itself and adjacent transformers during a HEMP or GMD
event.

1. INTRODUCTION

Today, a variety of man-made and natural events threaten the reliable operation of the electric power
grid, including high-altitude electromagnetic pulses (HEMP) and solar geomagnetic disturbances
(GMD) [1, 2, 3]. During such events, changes in the Earth’s magnetic field — caused by a
nuclear explosion or charged particles from a coronal mass ejection — create time- and spatially-
varying electromagnetic fields at the Earth’s surface. Coupling with long transmission lines, these
fields can induce low-frequency common-mode (CM) currents in grounded power transformers.
These geomagnetically induced currents (GICs) can drive half-cycle saturation of transformer
cores, resulting in distorted AC waveforms, increased losses, thermal stress, and, in severe cases,
extended power system disruption [2, 3].
An illustration of half-cycle saturation is shown in Fig. 1-1. The light blue lines represent the
relationship between the transformer’s core flux and primary current. As a CM signal is introduced,
a DC flux component (red line) shifts the net flux toward saturation. Once in this region, small flux
changes produce large current spikes on the primary side, increasing harmonic distortion, causing
heating and damage to the transformer, and potentially harming other connected components.
To date, several approaches for minimizing the impact of GICs on power system operation have
been investigated, including: passive blocking devices [4], corrective line switching [5], and more
recently active devices to disrupt GICs in the transformer neutral [6, 7]. At the same time, solid-state
transformers (SSTs) have emerged as a technology that aims to supplant conventional magnetic
transformers with designs based on modern solid-state switching devices and power conversion
circuits [8]. Because of the flexibility afforded by power electronics, SSTs are envisioned to enable
a new avenue for increased reliability, resiliency, efficiency, and security in the electric power
grid [9]. The goal of this research is to develop an SST which actively mitigates low-frequency CM
insults caused by a HEMP/GMD event by using a unique power electronics and controls architecture.
Specifically, this research was focused on the long time frame component of a HEMP event, referred
to as the E3 component, and, to a lesser degree, the medium time frame component, referred to as
the E2 component. The fastest time frame E1 component is too fast to consider mitigating with
a controls based approach. The E1 and E2 components are already being considered by another
LDRD project under Tyler Bowman in the Resilient Energy Systems Mission Campaign [10].
A schematic of the proposed SST is shown in Fig. 1-2. It consists of a primary-side ac-dc converter,
an intermediate dc-dc converter, and a secondary-side dc-ac inverter designed to feed downstream
loads. Because of the ac-dc-ac topology, this SST is classified as Type IV converter [8]. In addition
to the standard Type IV components, the proposed SST also includes an energy storage system
(ESS). By controlling CM current on the grid side of the SST, and by utilizing the ESS to absorb
the incident CM energy, it is expected that the SST can mitigate the HEMP/GMD insult while
maintaining the nominal input/output behavior expected of a transformer.

2. APPROACH

This project consisted of three general thrusts: architecture development, control design, and system
level simulation studies.
For the development of hardware architectures, several potential topologies were considered. They
were evaluated for range of common mode voltage outputs, disturbances on the AC voltages, and
number of components. In the end, an architecture based on a four-legged converter developed
in [11], shown in Fig. 2-1, was selected. This topology allows for control of the neutral voltage of
the system by adding a fourth leg to the standard rectifier topology connected to the neutral line
of the grid or neutral point of the output filter. The response of a four-legged converter to a CM
event is shown in Fig. 2-2. As the figure shows, the converter is able to bring the CM current
back to zero quickly with very little change in the amplitude of the AC currents. By contrast, a
three-legged converter response to the same event is shown in Fig. 2-3. As can be seen, in this case
while the average CM current is driven to zero, there are still significant oscillations in CM current.
Additionally, the magnitude of the AC currents has dropped significantly and there are increased
harmonics in the waveforms. The choice to use a four-legged converter architecture was driven by
the improved performance noticed in these graphs. The full CM-SST uses two of these converters,
one for the input side and one for the output side. The CM-SST also uses a dual-active bridge
(DAB), see Fig. 2-4, to provide step-up/step-down functionality and isolation. The full CM-SST is
shown in Fig. 2-5.

For control development, strategies to direct and control the common mode behavior of the circuit
were developed. The general strategy is shown in Fig. 2-6. In this strategy, the standard current
control loop is shown in black. This controls real and reactive power flow in the system by first
transforming the measured system currents into a rotating reference frame known as the qd0-
axis [12]. The q- and d-axis currents are then controlled to match set values determined by outer

layers of controls. The zero-sequence (or 0-axis) is typically assumed to be zero and is ignored.
However, the zero sequence is proportional to the CM behavior of the system. Therefore, by adding

separately. Derivation of the required control parameters was published in [13].

As the project progressed, a thrust to examine system level simulation behavior was added. This
task examined the capabilities of the CM-SST to protect other conventional transformers in a grid.
Fig. 2-7 shows the initial model used in these studies. In this model, transformer saturation during
the simulated insult was monitored and a metric based on magnitude and duration of saturation was
calculated. Different combinations of SST and conventional transformers in the model were then
compared to see what placements reduced saturation. Larger systems were modeled as conclusions
were drawn on SST placement and numbers.

For prototyping and hardware testing, the team leveraged the capabilities of The Secure Scalable
Microgrid Testbed (SSMTB) at Sandia National Laboratories. The SSMTB was developed to
validate controls for networked microgrids [14] and has also been configured to represent an all-
electric ship power system with multiple buses (or zones) [15, 16]. Additionally, the SSMTB has
been used to size energy storage for all-electric ship power systems [17], analyze novel components

3. KEY RESULTS

The research and development of the CM-SST produced several key results, starting with the design
and validation of the hardware architecture. This was followed by the design and testing of control
strategies for the medium-time (E2) and late-time (E3) HEMP components, and finally, system-level
studies assessing the ability of the CM-SST to protect other transformers in the grid.

3.1. Architecture Design

As a first step, the architecture was finalized, modeled, and simulated to demonstrate its ability to
control CM currents without sustaining damage from those currents. Initial design and simulation
results were presented at IEEE COMPEL 2023 and published in [22], included as Appendix A.1.
The paper details the SST design, control architecture, and simulation results showing its response
to a CM disturbance. In the paper it is shown that, in simulation, when a CM insult is applied to a
simple system with a single conventional transformer, shown in Fig. 3-1, the resulting AC currents
will be deformed by half-cycle saturation in the core. As shown in Fig. 3-2, during the insult the
currents will have large current spikes up to 60 A. The 4-legged converter used is shown in Fig. 3-3
and the full test system with the SST replacing the conventional transformer is shown in Fig. 3-4.
As shown in Fig. 3-5, the CM-SST is able to quickly bring CM currents back to zero without the
current spikes or harmonics seen in Fig. 3-2. Additionally, as shown in Fig. 3-6, the energy storage
system (ESS) is only required during the transient and therefore has a low energy cost.

4. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This project focused on developing a common-mode solid-state transformer (CM-SST) to mitigate
the effects of the E3 component of high-altitude electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) and geomagnetic
disturbance (GMD) events. The effort successfully delivered results in three key areas:

 • Architecture Development: A hardware architecture was designed to generate controlled
 common-mode (CM) voltages without disturbing AC power or voltage. A hardware pro-
 totype, based on this architecture, was built and validated against simulation models. This
 prototype also served as a reference platform for other RES Mission Campaign LDRDs, in-
 cluding a project led by Tyler Bowman that examines the susceptibility of SST architectures
 to the faster E1 and E2 components of a HEMP event.
 • Control Design: Nonlinear control strategies based on Hamiltonian Surface Shaping and
 Power Flow (HSSPF) methods were developed and implemented to maintain stability and
 accurately track current setpoints during CM disturbances. Hardware testing confirmed
 the controller’s ability to suppress CM-induced current offsets and protect inverter switches
 during simulated disturbances. The control models and findings have been shared with a
 system-level controls project under David Wilson for integration and joint analysis.
 • System-Level Simulation Studies: Reduced-order modeling and large-scale simulation
 studies demonstrated that full grid protection can be achieved without replacing every trans-
 former. In many scenarios, replacing or modifying only about half of the transformers to
 hybrid SSTs (H-SSTs) provided sufficient mitigation of CM disturbances. These studies also
 established strategies for optimal placement of CM-SST and H-SST units, which will inform
 future designs and deployment strategies.

Looking forward, the primary need is to scale the hardware to grid-relevant voltages and power
levels. This will be addressed in a related LDRD project, ‘Grid-Ready Hybrid Solid State Trans-
formers for Enhanced Grid Resilience’, beginning in FY26. This effort will focus on developing an
H-SST architecture that integrates the CM-SST with a conventional transformer. In this configura-
tion, the conventional transformer provides voltage step-down, while the power electronics operate
entirely on the low-voltage side, reducing the need for high-voltage blocking devices or complex
module stacking. This new project will also extend system-level simulation and optimization ef-
forts to models with more than 100 buses, benchmarking the hybrid SST architecture against other
proposed HEMP mitigation strategies. These efforts will advance the CM-SST technology toward
a higher technology readiness level (TRL), supporting future grid-scale testing and deployment.

 I. I NTRODUCTION
 Today’s electric power grid is susceptible to the effects of
 both man-made and naturally occurring geomagnetic distur-
 bances (GMDs), such as from a high altitude electromagnetic
 pulse (HEMP), or from space weather [1], [2]. One way (a) SST architecture

 blackouts, and other system instabilities [2].

 This work presents the design and experimental testing
 of a SST that can respond to common-mode (CM) insults,
 such as those generated by a high altitude electromagnetic
 pulse (HEMP) or solar-geomagnetic disturbance (GMD). The
 power electronics and controls architecture of the proposed
 SST are described, and hardware results of a prototype SST are
 provided. The SST is shown to function as an effective ac/ac
 converter during normal operations, and respond successfully
 to mitigate CM current during a simulated CM insult. Future

 Abstract
 In this paper, a control strategy for the mitigation of common mode (CM)
 current is examined. A 4-leg solid-state transformer (SST) architecture is
 utilized. Hamiltonian surface shaping and power flow control (HSSPFC) is
 used to design a nonlinear controller for the output inverter of the SST. There
 are two main goals for the nonlinear controller design: (1) mitigate common
 mode-induced AC current offsets; (2) drive AC currents to desired setpoints.
 Mathematical analysis of the nonlinear system is used design a controller
 which meets these goals. Four simulated cases and one hardware implemen-
 tation are presented. For all cases examined, the proposed controller was
 able to mitigate common mode currents and rapidly track current setpoints
 after insult. Additionally, the controller was able to maintain the quality of
 the AC current waveforms and protect the inverter switches from excessive
 overvoltages.
 Keywords: common mode, Hamiltonian surface shaping and power flow
 control (HSSPFC), solid-state transformer (SST), nonlinear controls

 1. Introduction
 High-altitude electromagnetic pulses (HEMP) and geomagnetic distur-
 bances (GMDs) are rare events. However, when they do occur, they can be
 highly disruptive events [1, 2, 3]. These disturbances are caused by varia-
 tions in Earth’s magnetic field. Such variations are induced by high altitude
 nuclear explosions or coronal mass ejections emanating from the Sun. These
 disturbances can cause a substantial DC offset (common mode) in network

 Particularly, this common mode voltage can result in saturation of stan-
dard iron core transformers [2, 3]. Such voltage saturation can result in
excessive current spikes which can damage sensitive equipment and shorten
transformer life. Some common methods of mitigating or blocking the ef-
fects of CM are as follows: (a) adding passive blocking devices [5], (b) im-
plementing corrective line switching [6], (c) using active devices to disrupt
geomagnetically induced currents (GICs) in the transformer neutral [7], and
(d) utilizing power electronics converters that can respond to and counteract
CM insults [8, 9].
 This paper focuses on the aforementioned option (d). The work presented
in this paper focuses on designing a power electronics-based controller which
can rapidly mitigate CM insults on the grid. Particularly, this paper focuses
on annihilation of offset in phase currents which has been induced by a CM
insult. The proposed control scheme presented in this paper is intended to be
utilized to control the output inverter of an SST. SSTs have been proposed
as a solution to allow for more efficient, autonomous control of power flow
[10].
 Additionally, distributed energy resources (DERs) are being integrated
into the grid more widely due to increased environmental, climate, and na-
tional security concerns. These DERs include solar (photovoltaic) energy,
wind energy, wave energy, and energy storage. Because these types of energy
sources are either DC in nature or operate at a frequency differing from that
of the main grid, a power electronics interface (PEI) must be used to couple
them to the main grid. For these sources to be optimally utilized, smart
control is a necessity [11].
 In this paper, the control design for a 4-leg inverter stage that can be
used in SST technologies or as an independent converter is developed. The
proposed control scheme uses the topology of the SST proposed in [9]. In

[9], simulations were used to demonstrate the SST’s capability of effectively
responding to CM insults. In [12], a Hamiltonian control method was intro-
duced to improve controller response. This paper further improves control
response and gives more in-depth mathematical theory behind the develop-
ment of the nonlinear Hamiltonian control design.

2. 4-LEG CONVERTER ARCHITECTURE
 The 4-leg inverter stage from [9] is shown in Fig. 1. In [9], this topology
was shown to be capable of responding to CM insults using a simple control
algorithm. However, the use of a sub-optimal control strategy results in a
larger DC voltage transient and the need for an energy storage system to
compensate. A better optimized control strategy will be developed here.
 The green encircled section of Fig. 1 shows the output stage of the full
SST design in [9] which performs the switching necessary to address CM in-
sults. The full converter is not considered in the scope of this paper because
CM effects do not cross the DC links or the dual active bridge (DAB) cou-
pling the input rectifier to the output inverter, as shown in [9]. The input
rectifier and the output inverter may be treated independently as it relates
to mitigation of CM current offset.
 In the proposed architecture, the fourth leg (far right leg) is shorted to
neutral. Additionally, the DC bus is floating with respect to network ground.
The output of the SST is coupled to the main grid. The CM insult voltage
is modeled as a DC offset in the neutral path.

3. Linear Controls Overview
 The standard and most widely used method for control Linear controls.
However, in practice, the system to be controlled is rarely linear. Therefore, a
linear approximation must be used to develop and analyze a control scheme.
That is, a reasonable operating point for the system is chosen and the system
is linearized about this operating point.
 The linearized system is a valid representation of the system within some
neighborhood of this operating point. However, if there is a sufficiently large
swing in any system state, the system may swing into a nonlinear region of
operation so that the linear approximation is no longer valid. That is, the
stability criteria may no longer apply and the controller may begin to drive
the system to an unstable operating point.
 Direct application of nonlinear controls to nonlinear systems helps to
circumvent some of these issues. The nonlinear controls approach utilized in
this paper is detailed in the next section.

4. Hamiltonian Surface Shaping and Power Flow Control
 In this paper, Hamiltonian surface shaping and power flow control (HSSPFC)
is used to design a nonlinear controller for the proposed inverter. As discussed
in [15], the Hamiltonian energy surface of a physical system is the sum of all
conservative energy. That is, the Hamiltonian surface is the energy across all
components which store, but do not dissipate energy. Such components do
not in reality exist. However, real components can be separated into compo-
nents for which this definition applies. For example, an inductor’s resistance
dissipates energy, but its inductance stores energy. Therefore, the inductance
is conservative and the resistance is nonconservative.

7. Conclusion
 In this paper, mitigation of E2 and E3 disturbances was addressed. A
4-leg inverter was utilized for the output portion of an SST. Additionally, a
nonlinear Hamiltonian control was proposed to mitigate the DC voltage offset
induced by the CM voltage. The Hamiltonian control was designed to serve 2
purposes: enforce Lyapunov stability; track current setpoints. Mathematical
analysis was used to establish that the proposed Hamiltonian controller would
satisfy both of these criteria. For all cases examined, the proposed controller
was successful in both stabilizing the system and driving the currents to
the desired setpoints. Additionally, the proposed control scheme was able
to protect the inverter switches from excessive and potentially damaging
overvoltage stress.
 Also, hardware results were presented. The hardware results showed that
when a potentially damaging common mode voltage step occurs, the con-
troller is able to rapidly recover the current waveform. This was established
for the prolonged E3-type insult. However, the hardware needs further de-
veloped to mitigate faster E2-type insults. This progress in ongoing and will
be presented in future work.
 Additionally, in future work, the full SST will be examined. The ability
of the controller to mitigate CM voltage impact is limited by the magnitude
of the input voltage to the output inverter. Additionally, a DAB control will
be investigated to compensate for larger CM voltage injections.

 ABSTRACT A high-altitude electromagnetic pulse (HEMP) or other geomagnetic disturbance (GMD)
 can disrupt the reliable operation of electric power grids by inducing low-frequency common-mode
 (CM) currents. Flowing through grounded transformers, these currents can saturate the magnetic core
 and significantly degrade performance. This work develops a solid-state transformer (SST) to replace
 susceptible equipment and enhance grid resilience to such threats. An overview of the required power
 electronics and control architecture is provided, alongside a description of a prototype SST hardware
 design. The SST’s performance is evaluated in steady-state and in response to a CM event, then compared
 to a conventional transformer under similar conditions. Finally, simple control laws are presented that enable
 the SST prototype to protect both itself and neighboring conventional transformers during a HEMP/GMD
 event.
