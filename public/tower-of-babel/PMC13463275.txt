Microglial clonal dynamics and the impact of clonal hematopoiesis in autologously transplanted rhesus macaques
Authors: Yifan Zhou, Diana M. Abraham, Rohan V. Hosuru, Taha Bartu Hayal, Ashley L. Gin, Xing Fan, Byung-Chul Lee, Taehoon Shin, E. Lake Potter, So Gun Hong, Mario Roederer, George S. Vassiliou, Chuanfeng Wu, Cynthia E. Dunbar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMicroglia are central nervous system (CNS)-resident macrophages, with key roles in immune surveillance, phagocytosis, and synaptic pruning. Yolk sac-derived microglia show minimal turnover from hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells (HSPCs) under steady-state conditions in mice. However, clinical benefits observed in patients receiving HSPC gene therapies for CNS disorders suggest functional integration of HSPC-derived cells. To investigate microglia replacement and the impact of clonal hematopoiesis (CH) on microglia, we analyzed microglia in rhesus macaques receiving barcoded or CRISPR-edited (TET2-mutant) HSPC transplants. We found that <2% microglia were derived from HSPCs many years following transplant, with no evidence of enhanced replacement in CH. The rare HSPC-derived tissue-resident cells exhibited a macrophage-like gene expression profile. Our results demonstrate limited long-term microglia replacement from adult HSPCs, even with CH, contrasting prior human studies. This work provides insights into microglia ontogeny and informs strategies for CNS-targeted HSPC gene therapies and interpretation of CH-related neuroprotection.

INTRODUCTION

Microglia are essential resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS). They maintain neural homeostasis by pruning synapses and providing neuronal growth factors, as well as conducting immune surveillance by responding to injury, infection, and neurodegenerative processes.1 Microglial dysfunction is implicated in a myriad of neurological diseases, including neuroinflammatory disorders such as multiple sclerosis,2–4 and in Alzheimer disease (AD),5–7 as well as inherited lysosomal storage or metabolic diseases such as metachromatic leukodystrophy.8

Given the critical role of microglia, their ontogeny and function have been the focus of intense research, particularly as targets for therapeutic development. Fate mapping studies in mice and some limited human data have supported a model for microglial ontogeny independent of the circulating hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell (HSPC)-derived precursors such as monocytes or macrophages. Yolk sac macrophages migrate to the developing brain early in primitive hematopoiesis, preceding the formation of thymus and bone marrow, and populate the microglial compartment.9–15 These cells persist throughout life via self-renewal and proliferation, without significant contribution from adult HSPC-derived hematopoiesis.16,17 However, recent murine experimental studies and very limited clinical observations involving CNS injuries, such as irradiation, or pharmacological microglial depletion followed by hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) have provided some evidence that under specific conditions, adult HSPC-derived myeloid cells can infiltrate the brain and adopt a microglia-like phenotype to varying degrees.12,18–26 These findings have led to the development of HSCT and gene therapies for inherited CNS diseases, such as adrenoleukodystrophy and metachromatic leukodystrophy, caused by impaired microglial enzymatic functions.27 Ongoing HSCT gene therapy trials have demonstrated significant clinical benefits in patients with these disorders, presumably via replacement of microglia from cells produced by lentiviral (LV) genetically modified HSPCs overexpressing functional enzymes.28–31

Another recent line of investigation on postmortem human brain tissue suggested ongoing replacement of microglia from adult bone marrow HSPCs in the absence of transplantation or other overt injury. Bouzid and coworkers detected clonal hematopoiesis (CH) somatic mutations present in adult HSPCs in microglial nuclei.32 Surprisingly, this paper also found that the presence of CH was associated with a reduced risk of AD in several large population cohorts. Investigators have hypothesized that circulating CH-mutant myeloid cells have enhanced homing to the brain, increased survival and proliferation upon differentiating into resident microglia, and/or enhanced function in removing deleterious substances implicated in AD, such as amyloid plaques.32 This study utilized clinical postmortem brain tissues, precluding comprehensive cellular characterization or sorting distinct cell populations, including microglia, from CNS macrophages or circulating myeloid cells.

To investigate the extent and dynamics of the resident microglial replacement by HSPC-derived myeloid cells and to ask whether and how CH mutations impact the microglial compartment in a large and clinically relevant animal model, we isolated and analyzed microglia and other cell populations of interest from the CNS and blood of rhesus macaques (RMs, Macaca mulatta). These RMs had undergone autologous HSPC transplantation following ablative total body irradiation (TBI) conditioning. The HSPCs had either been transduced with a lentivirus containing a DNA barcode library, allowing clonal tracking and expressing a green fluorescent protein (GFP) marker gene, or edited via CRISPR-Cas9 to introduce loss-of-function TET2 mutations, a prevalent CH-mutated gene recently found to protect from AD when introduced into HSPCs in a murine model.33 As previously reported, these TET2-engineered RMs demonstrated clonal expansion over time and a hyperinflammatory myeloid phenotype similar to human TET2-mutant CH. We incorporated intravascular staining (IVS) with anti-CD45 antibodies, which specifically stain all circulating hematopoietic and immune cells and spare tissue resident cells, allowing rigorous investigation of the ontogeny and gene expression patterns of putative tissue-resident microglial cells versus circulating myeloid cells and perivascular macrophages (PVMs), and investigated the migratory and phagocytic activities of circulating myeloid cells in CH and non-CH animals.34 We observed only very minimal replacement of resident microglia from HSPCs, even many years following ablative TBI conditioning and autologous transplantation, and found no evidence for enhanced repopulation in the setting of engineered or natural CH.

RESULTS

RM characteristics and experimental design

Twelve adult RMs were included in this study (Table 1; Figure 1A). Six RMs were previously transplanted with autologous HSPCs transduced with an LV library expressing GFP and containing high-diversity DNA barcodes following ablative TBI conditioning.36–38,40 At the time of transplantation, all but one animal (RQ3600, 18.3-year-old) were older juveniles (3.2- to 7.1-year old) (Table 1). These animals had demonstrated long-term stable multilineage and polyclonal engraftment for up to 10 years. GFP transgene expression in peripheral blood (PB) monocytes stabilized at 17%–65% across the six animals. Three animals (ZL26, H95W, and H96F) were engineered to model CH by introducing somatic TET2 mutations in HSPCs, using CRISPR-Cas9-mediated insertion-deletion (indel) non-homologous end-joining mutagenesis, followed by TBI-conditioned autologous transplantation, as previously described.39 RM ZL26 received HSPCs engineered with a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting TET2 exon 9,39 while H95W and H96F received cells engineered with a gRNA targeting TET2 exon 3 (Table S2). Following transplantation, TET2 mutation variant allele frequencies (VAFs) expanded over time and were detected at 32%–80% in PB monocytes amongst the three animals at the time of necropsies (Table 1). Three control animals who did not undergo TBI or transplantation were also studied. The initial editing efficiency, estimated number of transplanted cells, and starting clonal diversity are summarized in Table S1.

Anti-NHP CD45 IVS enables isolation of brain tissue-resident microglia and PVMs

Past work investigating whether bone marrow HSPC-derived monocytes can give rise to resident microglia have yielded conflicting results. One important technical limitation has been the differentiation of contaminating intravascular myeloid cells present within brain vasculature, as well as functionally distinct PVMs or meningeal macrophages, from true tissue-resident microglia (TRM), as they share many surface markers.41 To distinguish and purify true TRM from these other populations, we injected a fluorescence-conjugated anti-NHP CD45 antibody intravenously, five minutes before euthanasia and tissue harvesting, in addition to upper body perfusion, in order to label and distinguish any circulating and perivascular CD45+ cells in six of the study animals (Figure 1B; Table 1).34,42 This CD45 IVS methodology has been well validated in RMs, labeling virtually all circulating and immediately perivascular CD45+ cells, including PVMs, as assayed 5 min following infusion.34 In this current study, the serial intravascular staining (SIVS) technology (fluorescent anti-CD45 antibody) stained and labeled all of the circulating hematopoietic cells (all expressing CD45) and liver CD45+ hematopoietic cells with positive flourescence upon intravascular delivery (Figures S1A and S1B). Additionally, we performed upper body perfusion in all animals prior to necropsy to minimize circulating blood cell contamination of the brain tissue samples (Figure 1B). We processed multiple brain regions, including frontal cortex, occipital cortex, cerebellum, and/or hippocampus, in order to study possible regional differences in microglial replacement and clonality (Table 1). We identified and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted total microglia-like cells (TM) by the surface marker profile CD45int/CD11b+/CX3CR1+ (Figure 1C), and, in animals receiving IVS, we further distinguished and sorted IVS− TRM and IVS+ PVMs (Figure 1C).

TM comprised the majority of CD45+ mononuclear cells in all brain regions analyzed from every animal, with some variability in the proportions between animals, and overall lower proportions were observed in the transplanted GFP/barcoded (mean: 65.1% ± 16.6%) and TET2 CH (mean: 58.2% ± 26.2%) animals compared with those in non-transplanted control (mean: 84.3% ± 4.9%) animals (Figures 2A, S1C, and S1D). Variations likely resulted from inefficient perfusion in the animals ZH33 (GFP/barcoded) and H96F (TET2 CH), as these two animals had very low blood pressure at the time of perfusion and necropsy, resulting in higher residual circulating intravascular cells, including T and B cells (Figure S1D). Because six out of twelve RMs did not receive IVS due to reagent availability at the time of necropsy, we assessed the percentage of IVS− TRM among the TM. We observed that at least 98% of TM were IVS− TRM in the 6 animals that were administered IVS, indicating that this total TM population, even in the non-IVS animals, is highly representative of the TRM population (Figure 2B). Morphological immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses of brain sections showed that microglial cells expressing CD11b and IBA-1 in control animals appeared ramified with extended branches, consistent with a resting microglial phenotype (Figure 2C).

To validate the identity of the various brain cell populations, we FACS-sorted IVS− TRM, TM, IVS+ PVMs, and macrophages from concurrent peripheral blood (PBMs) (CD14+/CD163+) from two control animals (11042292 and A14V012) for bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses. Hierarchal clustering of gene expression grouped the same cell populations from the two animals together. Importantly, IVS− TRM and TM clustered tightly, with minimal transcriptomic differences, while IVS+ PVM and PBM were transcriptionally distinct and similar to each other (Figure 2D). With prior studies demonstrating that RM and human microglia gene signatures are closely overlapping,43 we asked if RM TRM gene expression matches previously reported human resident microglia gene expression patterns. Differential gene expression analysis demonstrated an overall 1,366 upregulated and 1,209 downregulated genes in PVMs compared with TRM (Figure S2A). Specifically, TRM showed elevated microglia-enriched genes (TMEM119, P2RY12, and CSF1R) and reduced macrophage-enriched genes (CD14, CD44, and CD274) compared with PBMs (Figure 2E).44 IVS+ PVMs showed intermediate expression of IVS− TRM and some circulating PBM genes (Figure 2E).45 Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify significantly enriched biological pathways in IVS+ PVMs compared with IVS− TRM. Compared with IVS− TRM, IVS+ PVMs demonstrated an upregulation of the hematopoietic cell lineage, antigen processing and presentation, cell adhesion and cytokine interaction, and oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Figure 2F). Of interest, IVS+ PVMs showed suppressed axon growth guidance genes, a likely microglia-specific function (Figure 2F). Surface protein staining also confirmed an increased expression of CD44 and CD274 on PVM and PB myeloid cells, while P2RY12 expressed most abundantly in TRM and expressed at a lower level in PVMs (Figures S2B–S2D).

HSPC-derived cells contribute minimally to TM/TRM and more substantively to PVMs in GFP/barcoded RMs

To assess the contribution of HSPC-derived microglia, we analyzed the percentages of GFP+ and thus HSPC-derived cells in TM and/or IVS− TRM and IVS+ PVMs as compared to PB monocytes in the six GFP/barcoded RMs. We first confirmed the presence of Iba1+/GFP+ microglia via immunofluorescence staining and whole-slide scanning (Figure 3A). While abundant IBA1+ microglia were observed, GFP+ microglia were extremely rare in tissue sections. Next, we quantified GFP+ microglia in TM and TMRs across animals by flow cytometry. The average GFP+ percentages in TM across different brain regions were only 0.2%–0.7%, confirming the tissue immunofluorescence microscopy results, in contrast to 15%–80% GFP+ PB monocytes concurrently in the same animals (Figure 3B). In the two GFP/barcoded RMs (RQ3600 and ZL40) receiving IVS prior to necropsy, the %GFP+ in IVS− TRM was even lower than in bulk TM (Figure 3B). The %GFP+ in IVS+ PVMs was substantially higher than in TM/TRM and closely matched the %GFP+ observed in concurrent PB monocytes (Figure 3B). These results suggest that in RMs, very few IVS− TRM—true tissue-resident microglial population of cells—are HSPC derived, suggesting that the rare GFP+ cells in TM actually represent IVS+ PVMs.

We next performed barcode clonality analyses on sorted IVS− TRM and IVS+ PVM populations from different brain regions of RQ3600 and ZL40 and compared clonality to longitudinal PB monocytes from the same animals. Consistent with the low GFP+ cell frequency, IVS− TRM exhibited markedly reduced unique clone numbers compared with PB monocytes (Figure 3C) and significantly lower Shannon diversity indices than PVM and PB monocytes (Figure 3D). Similar results were observed when comparing TM clonality to PB monocytes in the non-IVS GFP/barcoded RMs (Figure S3). Despite lower numbers of unique clones (Figure 3C), likely due to sampling limitations imposed by the number of PVMs analyzed, the PVMs from the two IVS animals maintained relatively high clonal diversity, indicating retained polyclonality similar to PB monocytes (Figures 3C and 3D). Analysis of the top-contributing clones revealed that both TRM and PVMs were populated by the clones derived from HSPCs and matched long-term persisting clones found in PB monocytes, rather than the early, transient clones present shortly after transplantation (Figures S4 and S5), suggesting ongoing turnover of PVMs, and not simply one-time reconstitution at the time of initial recovery from TBI at the time of transplantation.

TET2-mutant CH did not promote increased microglial replacement by HSPC-derived cells

In CH RMs with CRISPR-engineered loss-of-function TET2 alleles in HSPCs 0.5 to 6.5 years following autologous transplantation, we evaluated TET2 VAFs in TM (H96F) or in IVS− TRM (H95W, ZL26) compared to PB monocytes. TET2-mutant VAFs in TM or TRM were magnitudes lower than those in concurrent PB monocytes (Figure 3E). The TET2 VAFs in PVMs (ZL26, 42%; H95W, 57%) were much higher than those in TRM/TM (ZL26, 4%; H95W, 1%; H96F, 2%) and similar to those in PB monocytes (ZL26, 60%; H95W, 83%; H96F, 32%) (Figure 3E).

Given population analyses suggesting that human CH is partially protective against AD and that the detection of CH mutations in putative microglial nuclei sorted from human brain at levels within an order of magnitude of the levels found in the blood,32 we compared the relative ratio of HSPC-derived TM or IVS− TRM compared to PB monocytes between GFP/barcoded animals and the TET2 CH animals, all at least 2.5 years post-transplantation. The ratios were calculated by dividing % marking (GFP or TET2 VAF) in either TM or IVS− TRM by % of marking in PB monocytes. These ratios were not significantly different between GFP/barcoded and TET2 CH animals (Figure 3F), suggesting that the presence of even high-frequency TET2 loss-of-function mutations in HPSCs did not result in enhanced replacement of TRM, compared with non-CH GFP/barcoded HSPCs in our transplanted RM model without any known neuroinflammatory or degenerative disease. On the other hand, relative HSPC contributions to PVMs were higher in TET2 than in GFP/barcoded animals but did not reach statistical significance due to small sample numbers (Figure 3G).

GFP+ TM resemble PVMs and are transcriptionally distinct from TRM

A long-standing question in the field is whether HSPC-derived cells in the brain can adopt the identity and function of embryonically derived microglia bona fide TRM. To address this, we isolated and analyzed the transcriptomes of multiple myeloid populations from RMs via bulk RNA-seq. We successfully sorted sufficient GFP− and GFP+ TM from three barcoded RMs that had received IVS (ZJ09, ZG66, and 11021072) for analysis. In parallel, IVS− TRM and IVS+ PVMs were sorted from six IVS-labeled animals (ZL40, RQ3600, H95W, ZL26, 11042292, and A14V012), and PBMs were collected from seven RMs (ZJ09, ZG66, ZL40, H95W, ZL26, 11042292, and A14V012) for transcriptomic comparisons. Principal-component analysis (PCA) of global gene expression revealed that GFP+ TM clustered closely with IVS+ PVMs, while remaining distinct from IVS− TRM and circulating PBMs (Figure 4A). In contrast, GFP− TM clustered with IVS− TRM, suggesting a transcriptional identity consistent with embryonic-derived microglia. Marker gene analyses confirmed these findings. Global differential gene expression analysis between GFP+ TM and TRM showed 2,406 genes significantly upregulated and 2,860 genes significantly downregulated in GFP+ TM (Figure 4B). Many upregulated genes indicate an activated state associated with macrophages such as TBC1D10C and FRG—known to induce cytoskeletal remodeling for phagocytosis—and IDO1 and MAP4K2—important for JNK pathway activation and macrophage-mediated inflammatory responses. Importantly, GFP+ TM showed minimal expression of canonical TRM/microglial markers such as CSF1R, TMEM119, and P2RY12 but expressed PVM-associated markers including ADGRE1 and MHC-II at levels comparable to those in IVS+ PVMs (Figure 4C). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (gseKEGG) analysis showed that relative to IVS− TRM, GFP+ TM (similar to PVMs) were enriched for gene sets associated with adult bone marrow hematopoietic lineage and immune activation, including antigen processing and presentation and IL-17 signaling, as well as pathways related to cell motility/adhesion and cytoskeletal remodeling. GFP+ TM also showed increased enrichment of metabolic programs, including oxidative phosphorylation and pentose phosphate metabolism (Figure 4D). Notably, GFP+ TM and PVMs exhibited decreased enrichment of the neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction gene set, driven by reduced expression of several neuromodulatory receptor genes in GFP+ TM (e.g., TACR2, P2RY6, ADORA3, PTAFR, and ADRB1; padj < 0.05), consistent with incomplete acquisition of TRM-associated neuromodulatory sensing programs (Figure 4D). These results suggest that HSPC-derived cells in the brain do not transcriptionally reprogram into true embryonic-like TRM but instead adopt a phenotype more similar to PVMs. However, the GFP+ TM cells include both TRM and PVM populations without IVS-based separation, and the bulk RNA transcriptional profile does not rule out the possibility that these HSPC-derived GFP+ TM cells represent a transitional state that may eventually acquire a TRM identity. Higher-resolution single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and localization studies will be needed for further confirmation.

TRM from TET2 CH animals exhibit a modest inflammation-primed transcriptional state

Despite only minimal TRM reconstitution by HSPC-derived cells in the CH macaques, we hypothesized that paracrine effects emanating from circulating or perivascular myeloid cells could influence microglial transcriptional states and potentially modulate AD risk or have other functional consequences. We previously demonstrated that TET2 CH-engineered RMs have elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the PB.39 Transcriptional PCA of TRM from TET2 CH animals showed separation of clustering from GFP/barcoded and control animals, which closely matched, indicating a divergent transcriptional profile of TRM in the TET2 CH group (Figure 5A). Differential expression analyses revealed 271 upregulated and 182 downregulated genes in TRM from TET2 CH animals compared with GFP/barcoded animals (Figure 5B). Notably upregulated genes included TPM1, which serves as a positive regulator of microglial activation via the PKA/CREB pathway. In addition, the inflammatory response genes CCL5 and the suppressor of cytokine signaling 3 (SOCS3) were modestly upregulated in TET2 TRM (Figure 5B). We further examined canonical microglial activation markers such CD86, CD40, CXCL1, IL-6, IL-18, IL-23a, CCL2, and TNF, all of which were modestly elevated in TET2 CH TRM, though statistical significance was not reached, likely due to cohort size (Figure 5C). When comparing between the two TET2 animals (ZL26 of 60% VAFs vs. H95W of 83% VAFs), we did not observe a consistent trend of higher activation gene expressions with higher TET2 VAFs (Figure S6). Pathway enrichment analyses revealed several significantly enriched microglia activation gene sets in TRM from the CH animals, including PI3K-AKT and calcium signaling pathways. The AGE (advanced glycation end-products)-RAGE (receptor for advanced glycation end-products) signaling pathway was also significantly enriched. One of the downstream targets in this pathway is the activation of MAP kinases, which can lead to NF-κB activation and the production of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and TNF (Figure 5D). These data suggest a potentially more proinflammatory state in TRM from TET2 animals, potentially resulting from previously demonstrated chronic elevation of humoral factors such as IL-6 or other non-cell autonomous effects (animal ZL26).39

To determine whether PVMs from TET2 CH animals also display enhanced inflammatory or phagocytic function, we compared the transcriptomes of PVMs from TET2 CH (H95W and ZL26), GFP/barcoded (ZL40 and RQ3600), and two control animals. Global PCA did not reveal a clear separation among the groups (Figure S7A). Targeted analysis of genes related to migration, activation, phagocytosis, and inflammation showed only modest upregulation of IL-6 and IL-1β, with no changes in key chemokine receptors (CX3CR1 and CCR2) or classical PVM markers, such as activation markers of CD68, a transmembrane glycoprotein that promotes lysosomal function during active phagocytosis, or MRC1/CD206, a phagocytic receptor activated during neuroinflammation (Figure S7B).46,47 These findings suggest that while TET2 CH PVMs show specific elevated cytokine genes under homeostatic conditions, they do not exhibit a fully activated or alternative (“M2”) macrophage phenotype.

Analysis of phagocytosis and migration of TET2 CH monocytes and macrophages

To further explore potential mechanisms by which CH may influence AD pathophysiology, we evaluated whether PB monocytes and PBMs from TET2 CH macaques show enhanced phagocytic or migratory functions. These attributes could potentially affect AD outcomes by facilitating more robust recruitment of myeloid cells to the sites of neuroinflammation or by promoting the clearance of pathological substrates such as β-amyloid. PB myeloid cells were isolated from three TET2 CH animals (ZL26, H96F, and H95W) and three controls and incubated with fluorescently labeled E. coli for 2 h; phagocytosis was assessed by flow cytometry for engulfment. No significant increase in phagocytic capacity was observed in either monocytes or macrophages from TET2 CH animals compared to controls (Figure 6A). Furthermore, sorted phagocytic versus non-phagocytic cells from TET2 CH animals revealed no difference in TET2 VAFs between the two populations (Figure 6B), suggesting that the TET2-mutant cells do not possess enhanced phagocytic activity.

Next, we examined the migratory response to MCP-1, a neuroinflammatory chemokine that regulates monocyte and macrophage infiltration into the CNS in response to inflammation. PB monocytes and macrophages from TET2 CH and control animals (N = 3 for each) were analyzed. Migration was specifically enhanced in response to MCP-1 for all cells, with a particularly significant increase observed in macrophages (Figure 6C). However, the proportions of migrated monocytes and macrophages, with or without MCP-1, did not differ significantly between TET2 CH and control animals (Figure 6C). We then compared TET2 VAFs between the migrated and non-migrated cells and found no enrichment of TET2 VAFs among the cells that migrated in response to MCP-1 (Figure 6D), suggesting no enhancement of migration for TET2-mutant cells in these chimeric animals. Notably, CCR2 (the receptor for MCP-1) expression on PB macrophages from two TET2 CH animals (ZL26 and H95W) and two control animals (11042292 and A14V012) was not significantly different (Figure S7C).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we investigated whether TRM in RMs can be replaced by HSPC-derived cells following autologous transplantation, and whether TET2 mutations in CH enhance TRM replacement. RMs are a highly relevant preclinical model, and prior transcriptional profiling has shown that RM microglia are highly similar to human microglia.43 By combining HSPC genetic barcoding and GFP labeling or CRISPR-mediated gene editing together with and IVS technology, we achieved a comprehensive and high-resolution analysis of brain myeloid chimerism. Our findings demonstrated that true parenchymal resident microglia in RMs are largely resistant to replacement by cells derived from HSPCs transplanted following TBI conditioning, even over a decade later. Moreover, TET2-mutated HSPCs did not confer any discernible enhancement to microglia replacement, although transcriptomic analysis indicated a modestly elevated inflammatory signature in microglia from TET2 animals.

These results contribute to a long-standing debate regarding microglial ontogeny and turnover, with novel insights gained from the use of a macaque model that shares many physiological characteristics with humans. Studies in murine models have suggested that HSPC-derived cells can infiltrate the brain and adopt microglia-like phenotypes under specific conditions. Initial reports indicated that donor bone marrow cells could be found in the brain of transplant-recipient adult mice.18,20 More recent mouse studies employing busulfan conditioning and microglia depletion via CSF1R inhibition have achieved near-complete microglial replacement, without CSF1R inhibiton, it was less than 10% microglia replacement, with CSF1R inhibition, it was near 100% microglia replacement in murine studies, suggesting that aggressive niche depletion is a prerequisite for robust microglial replacement.22,48–50 The clinical benefit of allogeneic or genetically modified autologous HSCT in patients with lysosome storage disorders has offered support to the concept that microglia can be replaced by adult bone marrow HSPC-derived macrophages, at least in a disease setting.29,51,52 Of note, these human gene therapy trials utilized busulfan or other alkylating agent conditioning, instead of TBI. Studies based on the detection of opposite donor gender in allogenic transplant have reported rare male donor-specific Y chromosomes in putative microglia.25,53 However, other human studies have reported only perivascular, instead of parenchymal, donor cells in the CNS, although in the setting of non-myeloablative conditioning.54 Further, all human studies were limited to postmortem tissues collected hours or more after death, where distinguishing circulating macrophages within the CNS vasculature from PVMs and meningeal macrophages from true TRM can be challenging, particularly given the lack of unambiguous surface markers.41

Our primate data revealed a marked resistance to the replacement of microglia in RMs (average age at necropsy: 12 years) and are consistent with the relatively low replacement reported in a small study of pigtail macaques not employing IVS.55 The discrepancy between the mouse and RM models may reflect key species differences in microglial niche stability and turnover kinetics. Additionally, the RMs in our study did not receive HSCT microglial depletion or have underlying CNS pathology. The minimal detection of GFP+/barcoded cells among resident microglia, despite robust peripheral engraftment, underscores the persistence of host-derived microglia and the restricted parenchymal access without CNS injury or inflammation. Even in RQ3600, who received transplantation at 18 years of age and was necropsied at the age of 26.2 years (equivalent to the age of 70 years in humans), along with a blood-brain barrier potentially less intact compared with younger animals, we observed only minimal GFP+ TRM. Furthermore, barcode retrieval from resident microglia was often below the detection sensitivity; however, the limited barcodes detected resembled concurrent PB monocytes, suggesting ongoing replacement of PVMs.

A critical technological advance in our study was the use of IVS to distinguish true TRM (IVS−) from circulating or perivascular myeloid cells (IVS+).34 This technology, combined with transcriptomic profiling, helped us to confidently purify TRM (IVS−) from PVMs (IVS+) where surface markers could be shared. Indeed, IVS+ PVMs showed a significant higher level of GFP+ and CH VAFs than TRM, tracking closely with PB monocytes. These PVMs also expressed elevated levels of genes and/or gene set enrichment pathways associated with antigen presentation, phagocytosis, cell adhesion, hematopoietic lineage, and cytokine interactions, compared with TRM, all aligning with their bone marrow origin and potential role in immune surveillance. The GFP+ myeloid lineage cells present in the CNS were IVS+. RNA-seq of sorted GFP+ TM revealed that HSPC-derived GFP+ cells had PVM-like signatures, lacking the expression of microglia-restricted genes such as TMEM119 and P2RY12. gseKEGG pathway analysis further suggested more metabolically and immunologically active states of GFP+ TM and reduced neuroactive ligand-receptor gene sets linked to embryonically derived microglia. These findings support the concept that HSPC-derived cells in the CNS following autologous transplant are vasculature-associated PVMs, rather than TRM. However, these cells may still be able to provide critical microglia-like functions, given the benefits observed following human HPSC gene therapies for CNS metabolic disorders.

A recent report suggesting that CH protects from AD led us to utilize our robust TET2 CH-engineered RM model to ask whether there was an impact on microglial or PVM replacement.32 While we found no enhancement of microglia or PVM replacement in CH RMs, we did discover that microglia from TET2 animals had upregulation of inflammatory genes, including CD86 and CD40, and enrichment in PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. Interestingly, a recent human study suggesting that CH may promote AD progression has also found that microglia-like cells bearing CH mutations demonstrate elevated inflammation and activation signatures.56 These changes may result from microglia receptor activation, perhaps via the IL-6R, given the impact of CH on the systemic, cytokine milieus and elevated plasma IL-6. We also compared the transcriptomic profiles of PVMs among CH, GFP/barcoded, and control animals. We did not find evidence to support a significantly elevated gene expression changes in chemokine receptor, phagocytosis activity, and cell activation status. We postulated that functional differences may manifest only in the setting of inflammatory or phagocytotic stimuli. However, circulating macrophages and monocytes from TET2-mutated versus control animals studied in vitro neither exhibited enhanced phagocytosis nor showed increased chemotactic migration toward MCP-1, a potent CNS inflammatory cytokine, and the TET2 VAFs did not differ significantly between phagocytosing and non-phagocytosing or migrating and non-migrating myeloid cells, suggesting that TET2 did not endow circulating monocytes and macrophages with heightened effector functions in this context.

In conclusion, our study highlights the resilience of the microglial niche in primates and provides a nuanced framework for understanding the limitations and opportunities of HSPC-based interventions targeting the CNS. By distinguishing true microglial engraftment from PVM replacement and elucidating the subtle impact of CH, we offer valuable insights for the design of next-generation cell and gene therapies for neurological diseases.

Limitations of the study

One major limitation of our study, besides the limited number of animals included, is the absence of CNS disease or injury, which may limit relevance to established neurodegenerative or inflammatory conditions where microglial turnover may be enhanced and the recruitment of monocyte-derived macrophages is increased to influence disease progression. Our studies were carried out with animals transplanted years earlier for unrelated experiments and were not compared directly with CRISPR control-edited animals (i.e., AAVS1); while we believe that animals receiving lentivirally transduced HSPCs serve as reasonable comparators for the TET2 animals years following transplantation, given our prior studies showing similar HSPC dynamics between transduced and NHEJ CRISPR-edited cells, these were not prospectively designed comparators. TET2 animals’ TRM were studied at 2.5 and 6 years post-transplant and CRISPR engineering, which are shorter than human time scales but exceed those of any other available in vivo models. Notably, we observed a modest increase in activation-associated transcripts in PVMs from TET2 CH animals, including CD86 and several cytokine/chemokine genes. Notably, gseKEGG analysis showed significant positive enrichment of Rap1 signaling and PI3K-Akt signaling, pathways implicated in myeloid cell activation through integrin/adhesion signaling, cytoskeletal remodeling, and immune receptor-dependent responses.57,58 Together, these data are consistent with a baseline activation-primed state, although the underlying molecular mechanism cannot be resolved from transcriptomics data alone. Future biochemical and functional validation is required to define the pathways that drive these phenotypic differences. Lastly, current study specifically examined the microglial repopulation after TBI and autologous transplant; different conditioning methods should be tested (busulfan vs. TBI) to elucidate the impact on microglial replacement as murine study has shown different degrees of microglial replacement with different conditioning strategies.26,48

Despite these limitations, we believe our findings offer notable insights for HSPC-based gene therapies and CNS cell ontogenies. Current strategies for treating leukodystrophies and lysosomal storage disorders presume that donor-derived cells can replace or support dysfunctional microglia. Our data suggest that such therapeutic benefits could partly stem from PVM-mediated delivery of enzymes or other factors, rather than, or in addition to, direct microglial replacement. Though more functional validation is needed to confirm the clinical significance, strategies focusing on enhancing HSC-derived PVM function or targeting their localization to the CNS could be complementary to microglial replacement and engraftment. Future work should explore alternative conditioning regimens incorporating strategies, such as CSF1R inhibition, and utilize CNS injury or inflammation models. Finally, strategies that directly edit microglia in situ or leverage PVMs for localized delivery of therapeutic molecules may offer promising alternatives to microglial replacement.

METHOD DETAILS

Autologous barcoded HSPC transplantation

Barcoded lentivirus libraries were generated and used for RM CD34+ HSPC transduction as described,40,62 with barcode diversity confirmed to ensure a >95% probability that each barcode would be present in no more than one engrafting HSPC. Briefly, for GFP/barcoded animals, HSPCs were mobilized from the bone marrow using granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF; Amgen, 10–15 μg/kg/day for 5 days) and AMD3100 (plerixafor; Sigma, 1 mg/kg) administered on the morning of the fifth or/and sixth days. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected via apheresis on both the fifth and sixth days and CD34+ cells were isolated by immunoselection. CD34+ cells were cultured overnight on RetroNectin-coated plates (Takara) in X-VIVO 10 medium (Lonza) supplemented with 100 ng/mL each of recombinant human FLT3 ligand, stem cell factor (SCF), and thrombopoietin (TPO) (all from Miltenyi Biotec). The following day, concentrated barcoded lentiviral vector was added at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 25 in the presence of 4 μg/mL protamine sulfate (Sigma). After 24 h of transduction, the cells were collected and infused intravenously into the autologous macaques. Each animal received pre-transplantation conditioning with TBI at 5 Gy/day for 2 consecutive days.

CRISPR/Cas9-engineered TET2 HSPC transplantation

CRISPR/Cas9-edited HSPCs were generated by electroporation of Cas9 ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) complexed with single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs) targeting exons of TET2. Animal ZL26 was previously described, with editing targeting TET2 exon 9 (see Tables S2 for gRNA sequences).39 For animals H95W and H96F, a sgRNA targeting TET2 exon 3 was used (Table S2). Similar to the GFP/barcoded animals, HSPCs were mobilized with G-CSF (10–15 μg/kg/day) and AMD3100 (1 mg/kg), and the PBMCs were collected via apheresis, and CD34+ cells were isolated by immunoselection and cultured overnight in supportive cytokines. Cells were then electroporated with Cas9 RNPs targeting TET2. After overnight incubation at 32°C, the edited cells were reinfused into the autologous animal following TBI conditioning.

Anti-NHP CD45 infusions for intravascular staining (IVS)

Conjugated anti-NHP CD45 antibodies (mouse IgG1κ) were generously provided by Dr. Mario Roederer (Table S3). Prior to administration, the antibodies were diluted in sterile Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS). Each infusion consisted of 5 mL of antibody solution, delivered intravenously over approximately 30 s via a catheter inserted into the saphenous vein.34 The antibody was administered at a dose of 30 μg/kg. All infusion reagents were tested and confirmed to be endotoxin-free prior to use.

Necropsy, brain tissue processing and cell purification

Necropsies were conducted using either whole-body (ZH33, H96F) or upper-body perfusion (the remaining 10 animals), when feasible, to minimize the presence of circulating blood cells in brain tissue. Animal H96F expired just prior to the scheduled necropsy; therefore, an emergency necropsy was performed with suboptimal whole-body perfusion. For animals RQ3600, ZL40, ZL26, H95W, 11042292, and A14V102, anti-NHP CD45 antibodies were administered intravenously 5 min prior to necropsy for IVS.

Brain tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (P) (Miltenyi Biotec), following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor modifications. Briefly, up to 1600 mg of tissue per sample was sectioned, weighed, and enzymatically dissociated in C-tubes using enzyme mixtures prepared from Enzyme P, Buffer X, Enzyme A, and Buffer Y. Tissue dissociation was carried out on the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator with Heaters using the 37C_NTDK_1 program. Following dissociation, cell suspensions were filtered through 70 μm MACS SmartStrainers (pre-wetted with HBSS containing Ca2+ and Mg2+), washed with 10 mL HBSS, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Myelin debris was removed by density gradient centrifugation. Cell pellets were resuspended in 20 mL of 22% Percoll solution (prepared by mixing 110 mL Percoll, 12 mL 1.5 M NaCl, and 378 mL myelin gradient buffer), as previously described.63 The suspension was overlaid with 3 mL of PBS and centrifuged at 950 × g for 20 min (acceleration: 4; brake: 0). The myelin-containing upper layer was carefully removed. If erythrocyte contamination was observed, cell suspensions were treated with ACK lysis buffer and washed. Final cell counts and viability were assessed prior to downstream applications. Cells were either used fresh or cryopreserved in CryoStor CS10 (StemCell Technologies) for future use.

Immunohistochemical and immunofluorescence staining of brain tissues

Brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4°C, then processed and embedded in paraffin blocks. Sections of 3 μm thickness were cut using a microtome. Following heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0), paraffin-embedded sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with an anti-CD11b primary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 49420; diluted 1:200 in blocking buffer), anti-IBA1 antibody (Sigma, Cat.no: MABN92, diluted 1:200 in blocking buffer), or anti-turboGFP antibody (Origen, Cat.no:TA150071, diluted 1:200 in blocking buffer). After rinsing with PBS, for Immunohistochemical staining, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (R&D Systems, Cat. No. VC003) at room temperature for 30 min. Signal was developed using AEC substrate (Vector Laboratories, Cat. No. SK-4205), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin. For Immunofluorescence staining, after rinsing with PBS, sections were incubated with DyLight 488 labeled anti-mouse secondary antibody and DyLight 549 labeled anti-rabbit secondary antibody. Slides were incubated with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Stained sections were scanned using the APERIO VERSA slide scanner (Leica Biosystems) for imaging and analysis.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed on fresh or cryopreserved PBMCs and brain-derived single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with antibodies listed in Table S3 and analyzed using a BD Fortessa Cell Analyzer. Cell sorting was conducted on a BD FACSymphony S6 System, and data were analyzed using FlowJo v10 software (BD Biosciences). PBMCs were sorted into monocytes (Aqua−/CD14+/CD163−), macrophages (Aqua−/CD14+/CD163+), T cells (Aqua−/CD3+), and B cells (Aqua−/CD20+). Brain-resident immune cell populations were defined as follows: total microglia (TM; Aqua−/CD45int/CD11b+/CX3CR1+), tissue-resident microglia (TRM; Aqua−/CD45int/CD11b+/CX3CR1+/IVS−), and perivascular macrophages (PVM; Aqua−/CD45int/CD11b+/CX3CR1+/IVS+). Brain-derived T and B lymphocytes were identified as Aqua−/CD45high/CD11b−/CD3+ and Aqua−/CD45high/CD11b−/CD20+, respectively.

Barcode retrieval analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from purified cells using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen). Up to 500 ng of DNA per sample was used as input for low-cycle nested PCR, during which a unique sample-specific index was incorporated to allow for multiplexed sequencing.40 Primer sequences are listed in Table S4. Equal amounts of gel-purified PCR products from each sample were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Custom Python scripts were used to extract barcodes by library ID from raw sequencing reads(available at https://github.com/barthayal/barcode_extrater). Barcode sequences were corrected for small indels and sequencing errors of up to 2 base pairs, and reads falling below an empirically defined threshold for false barcodes were removed, as previously described and validated.40,60 The resulting lists of unique barcodes and their relative fractional contributions were analyzed and visualized using custom R scripts and the barcodetrackR package (available at https://dunbarlabnih.github.io/barcodetrackR).60

TET2 targeted mutation analyses

Genomic DNA was extracted from purified cells using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen). On-target regions corresponding to the TET2-targeting gRNAs were amplified from up to 25 ng of genomic DNA using site-specific primers (listed in Table S5) and KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix in a low-cycle PCR (14 cycles). The resulting PCR products were then subjected to a second round of low-cycle nested PCR (14 cycles) using NEBNext Multiplex Oligos for Illumina (Primer Set 1). Equal quantities of gel-purified PCR products were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq using paired-end 300 bp or 600 bp reads. Sequencing depth ranged from 100,000 to 500,000 reads per target site.

To account for individual-specific single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), a consensus sequence for each target site in each animal was generated from pre-transplantation sequencing data using a custom Python pipeline. Sequencing reads from all post-transplantation time points were then aligned to the appropriate consensus sequence using CRISPResso2 (https://crispresso2.pinellolab.org/submission) to quantify indel frequencies. Reads that did not appreciably change in abundance across time points (defined as < |0.1%| variation relative to the pre-transplantation sample) or that appeared only once across all time points were excluded from downstream analysis using custom Python scripts. A 20 bp window centered on the predicted Cas9 cut site was applied to focus analysis on editing outcomes. Heatmaps were generated using a custom R application.

Bulk RNA sequencing analysis

Total RNA of purified cell was extracted using Qiagen’s RNeasy MicroKit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quantity and integrity was assessed using Agilent’s High Sensitivity RNA ScreenTape Tapestation kit. Samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) > 5 were used for downstream cDNA synthesis. cDNA was synthesized using SMART-seqv4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit for Sequencing according to manufacturer’s protocol. Then, libraries were generated using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit and the Nextera XT Index Kit (Illumina). Library quantity and fragment length were assessed using Agilent’s High Sensitivity D5000 ScreenTape Tapestation kit. Libraries were pooled and shallow-sequenced on MiSeq (Pair-End 150-8-8-150) for determining the quality and balance of multiplexed libraries. Then, re-balanced libraries were deep sequenced using NovaSeq platform, SP Flow cell (Pair-End 150-8-8-150), reaching on average 30 million mapped reads per sample minimally.

RNAseq reads were aligned and quantified using SALMON.64 A customized reference genome was built by combining Mmul_10/RheMac10 reference genome (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_003339765.1/) and mitochondria genome. Data normalization and comparative analyses were performed with the DESeq2 package in R.65 Genes with an adjusted p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed with the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) functions in the clusterProfiler package66 using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes for Macaca mulatta (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg-bin/show_organism?org=mcc).

Phagocytosis assay

Phagocytic activity of PB macrophages and monocytes from TET2 CH versus healthy control monkeys was assessed using fluorescently labeled particles. FACS sorted monocytes or macrophages were incubated with pHrodo Red E. coli Bioparticles (ThermoFisher Scientific, Cat# P35361) at a ratio of 0.1mg particles per 1 × 105 cells for 30 min at 37°C in complete culture medium. After incubation, cells were washed thoroughly with cold PBS to remove non-internalized particles. Phagocytosis was quantified by flow cytometry using a BD LSRFortessa, based on fluorescence intensity of internalized pHrodo particles. Cells were then sorted into phagocytic (pHrodo-positive) and non-phagocytic (pHrodo-negative) populations using fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS). Genomic DNA was extracted from each fraction, and the VAF of the TET2 mutation was determined by targeted sequencing to assess enrichment of CH clones in phagocytic versus non-phagocytic cells. Data were obtained from duplicate wells and averaged for each condition.

Transwell migration assay

To assess the migratory capacity of PB macrophages and monocytes from TET2 CH versus healthy control monkeys, we performed a Transwell migration assay using 24-well plates with 6.5 mm inserts and 8 μm pore polycarbonate membranes (Corning, Cat# 3422). Cells (2×105 per insert) were suspended in serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber, while the lower chamber contained medium with or without the chemokine MCP-1 (final concentration of 100 ng/mL). Cells were incubated for 90 min at 37°C and 5% CO2. After incubation, non-migrated cells remaining on the upper side of the membrane, and the migrated cells on the underside of the membrane were collected and counted using a Vi-CELL BLU cell counter (Beckman Coulter). Genomic DNA was extracted from each fraction, and the VAF of the TET2 mutation was determined by targeted sequencing to assess enrichment of CH clones in migrated versus non-migrated cells. Data were obtained from duplicate wells and averaged for each condition.
