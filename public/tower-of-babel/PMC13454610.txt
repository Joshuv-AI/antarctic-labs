dITP-induced remodeling activates the filamentous effector complex in Kongming anti-phage defense
Authors: Xia Li, Min Liu, Wenxue Jiang, Guangbo Yan, Dujuan Shi, Qiong Xing, Lixin Ma, Fengtao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral antiviral immune systems based on nucleotide signaling have been identified in bacteria. One of them is the Kongming system, which includes an effector complex (KomBC), composed of a non-canonical purine NTP pyrophosphatase (KomB) and a SIR2 domain-containing protein (KomC). The Kongming system is activated by an atypical signaling nucleotide, dITP, generated upon phage infection. Here, we use structural and functional analyses to show that KomBC assembles into a helical filament, built from vertically stacked 4:4 KomB–KomC repeating units. Binding of dITP to KomB initiates progressive conformational rearrangements within the filament, ultimately remodeling the filament into a distinct architecture in which KomC adopts an active conformation with NADase activity. We further identify a key residue potentially involved in the acquisition of NADase activity in SIR2 family proteins. Thus, our results elucidate the activation mechanism of KomBC, revealing a distinct mode of effector activation by a signaling molecule.Subject terms: Bacteriophages, Cryoelectron microscopy, Enzyme mechanisms, Bacterial structural biology

Introduction

To resist phage infection, bacteria encode a wide variety of defense systems. In contrast to the previously identified CRISPR-Cas systems and restriction-modification systems, which directly target phage genomes, many newly discovered defense systems provide immunity through abortive infection by inducing cell death or dormancy in infected cells1,2. These systems detect phage infection signals and subsequently activate effector proteins that execute cell death by degrading nucleic acids, hydrolyzing the key metabolite NAD+, or disrupting the cell membrane, or through other mechanisms1,2. Among them, anti-phage defense systems mediated by nucleotide signaling molecules are widespread and have attracted considerable attention3,4.

In recent years, several classes of nucleotide-based immune signaling systems (such as CBASS5, Pycsar6, Thoeris7, and type III CRISPR8,9) have been identified and extensively studied3. These systems generally consist of two core components: signal molecule synthases, which produce signal molecules upon sensing phage infection, and effectors, which are subsequently activated by these molecules. Because different synthases generate distinct nucleotide signals and various effectors specifically recognize these signals, the combinations of diverse synthases and effectors result in highly diverse system architectures.

As second messengers, nucleotide signaling molecules can rapidly amplify the immune response by activating effectors at low concentrations, thereby enabling the system to respond quickly and effectively to phage infection. Furthermore, prokaryotic nucleotide immune signaling systems share evolutionary relationships with eukaryotic immune pathways. For example, CBASS systems are considered the evolutionary ancestors of the human cGAS-STING pathway5,10. Similarly, Thoeris systems utilize Toll/interleukin-1 receptor (TIR) domain proteins to synthesize cyclic ADP-ribose (cADPR) variants, and a similar signaling molecule has also been identified in plants11. Altogether, these features make the nucleotide-based immune signaling systems a particularly intriguing and important area of study.

The mechanisms by which signaling molecules activate effector proteins have been widely investigated3,4. A common feature among systems such as CBASS, Pycsar, Thoeris, and type III CRISPR is that many effectors assemble into filaments upon signaling molecule binding6,12–18. To date, no nucleotide signaling-based anti-phage system has been found in which the effector adopts a filamentous architecture in its apo form.

Recently, a novel nucleotide immune signaling system, named Kongming, was identified19. The system utilizes a base-modified nucleotide, 2’-deoxyinosine 5’-triphosphate (dITP), as the signaling molecule19. The system comprises three protein components: KomA, KomB and KomC, which are an adenosine deaminase, a HAM1-like non-canonical purine NTP pyrophosphatase and a silent information regulator 2 (SIR2) domain-containing protein, respectively (Fig. 1A). Among these proteins, KomA is involved in signaling molecule synthesis, whereas KomB and KomC assemble into the effector protein complex (KomBC). Upon phage infection, the system hijacks a phage-encoded deoxynucleotide monophosphate kinase (DNK) and, together with KomA and the host nucleoside diphosphate kinase (NDK), catalyzes the synthesis of dITP. The generated dITP subsequently activates the KomBC complex, leading to abortive infection. However, the molecular mechanism by which dITP activates the KomBC complex remains unclear.

In this study, we determined the structures of KomBC in its apo state as well as in its intermediate and active states upon dITP binding. We found that even in the absence of the signaling molecule dITP, KomBC is preassembled into a helical filament composed of vertically stacked repeating units with a 4:4 stoichiometry of KomB and KomC. This filament is indispensable for the activity of the Kongming system. dITP binding triggers conformational changes in KomB, which first remodel the KomB–KomC interface and ultimately drive the KomC tetramer within each repeating unit to transition from an open to a closed, cloverleaf-like architecture, thereby adopting an active NADase conformation. Comparative analysis further identifies a key residue that is conserved in SIR2 NADases but absent from NAD+-dependent deacetylase homologs, highlighting a potential molecular determinant underlying their divergent enzymatic activities.

Results

Apo-form KomBC is a filament complex

Kongming system produces an unusual signaling molecule, dITP, upon phage infection19, which is distinct from previously characterized nucleotide-based signaling systems3. This intrigued us to explore how dITP activated the KomBC complex. We cloned the Kongming system from Escherichia coli NCTC13216 and tested its anti-phage activity. Consistent with the previous study19, the Kongming system exhibited anti-phage activity against T4 phage, whereas a key residue mutation (H146A) in KomC abolished this activity (Fig. 1B). We also showed that the Kongming system conferred immunity against T5 phage (Fig. 1B), while the previous study reported no defense against T5 phage19. The discrepancy may be due to differences in experimental conditions.

To study the activity of the effector protein complex (KomBC), we co-expressed KomB and KomC with an N-terminal His-tag on KomB. Following Ni2+ affinity chromatography, we further purified the KomBC complex using a SuperoseTM 6 Increase 10/300 GL size-exclusion column (Cytiva). The majority of the protein eluted at the void volume (Fig. S1), suggesting that KomBC forms a very large higher-order assembly or exists in a non-specific aggregated state. We collected this early-eluting peak and confirmed it to be the KomBC complex by SDS-PAGE (Fig. S1). To test whether this complex could be activated by dITP, we examined its activity in vitro. The results showed that KomBC exhibits strong activity, while the mutant KomBCH146A almost completely lost activity (Fig. 1C). This indicates that the large KomBC complex is catalytically active.

We next analyzed KomBC structure using negative-stain electron microscopy and found that it forms filamentous structures both in the absence and in the presence of dITP (Fig. 1D). To elucidate the architecture of the KomBC filament, we determined the cryo-EM structures in the apo state. The apo KomBC filament, resolved at 3.01 Å (Fig. S2), adopts a helical architecture formed by vertically stacked repeating units, with a helical twist of ~69.1° and a helical rise of ~47.9 Å (Fig. 1E, F). Each repeating unit consists of two central KomC dimers in an open architecture, symmetrically flanked by KomB dimers (Fig. 1G, H).

The overall architecture of dITP-bound KomBC filaments

To elucidate the structural features of the KomBC filament activated by dITP, we determined the cryo-EM structures of the dITP-bound states. The reconstructions revealed an intermediate state at 3.20 Å resolution and an active state at 3.21 Å resolution (Fig. S3). The overall architecture of the intermediate state closely resembles that of the apo state, exhibiting a helical twist of ~69.7° and a helical rise of ~48.2 Å (Fig. 2A, B). The active state, with a helical twist of ~78.6° and a helical rise of ~47.4 Å (Fig. 2C, D), shows marked architectural differences from the intermediate state, indicating substantial conformational rearrangements (Fig. 2A–D). Within the 4:4 KomBC repeating unit, the overall structural architecture in the intermediate state is similar to that in the apo state, except that dITP molecules are clearly observed bound to the KomB dimer (Fig. 2E–G). In contrast, the repeating unit in the active state undergoes extensive conformational rearrangements, where the two central KomC dimers assemble into a symmetric, cloverleaf-like closed configuration, with the dITP-bound KomB dimers positioned on both sides (Fig. 2H–J).

We therefore interpret the intermediate state as a transitional, pre-activated conformation of the KomBC filament. In this state, dITP is already bound, whereas the overall architecture of KomBC remains largely similar to that of the apo state, with only local conformational changes (Figs. 1E, 2A). By contrast, the active state displays much more pronounced conformational rearrangements associated with activation (Fig. 2A–D).

Filament formation is indispensable for KomBC activation

KomBC filament is assembled through the vertical stacking of repeating 4:4 KomBC units, mediated by KomC–KomC interactions between adjacent units. In all three states, two KomC–KomC interfaces are consistently present between adjacent units (Fig. 3A–F): site 1, formed between KomC2 and KomC1’, and site 2, formed between KomC4 and KomC3’.

In the apo state, both interfaces contain three pairs of salt bridges. A loop (residues 148–152), here named loop1, forms salt bridges with residues R72 and K67, while R77 forms another with D76’. In addition, loop1 also establishes hydrogen bonds with R72 at both site 1 and site 2, and R77 interacts with H80’ through hydrogen bonding at site 2 (Fig. 3A, B). The number of salt bridges decreases from three in the apo state to one at both site 1 and site 2 in the intermediate state, and further to only one at site 2 in the active state (Fig. 3A–F). Although loop1 remains involved in polar interactions throughout all three states, the specific residues participating in salt bridge and hydrogen bond formation at both site 1 and site 2 vary across the apo, intermediate, and active states (Fig. 3A–F), suggesting a gradual remodeling of inter-unit interfaces during the filament transition.

Consistent with the structural observations, replacing loop1 with a flexible SGGSG linker (loop1 mutation) or mutating residue R77 completely abolished the anti-phage activity (Fig. 3G). In line with this, both the loop1 mutant and R77A mutant lost NADase activity in vitro (Figs. 3H, S4). We further assessed the molecular weights of the wild-type (WT) KomBC complex and the two mutants, and found that, unlike the WT KomBC complex, both mutants failed to form filaments but still retained the KomB–KomC and KomC–KomC interactions within each repeating unit (Fig. 3I–K). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that filament formation is indispensable for the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system.

Beyond loop1- and R77-mediated interactions, additional residues also form hydrogen bonds at both interfaces in the apo, intermediate, and active states (Fig. S5A–C). For example, residue D76 not only forms a salt bridge with R77’ in site 1 in the apo state and site 2 in the intermediate state, but also forms hydrogen bonds with S73 at both interfaces in all three states. The D76A mutation markedly reduced the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system, whereas the S73A mutation had no detectable effect (Fig. S5D), indicating that the D76–R77’ salt bridge plays a more critical role in filament formation. Although residues S82 and S82’ form hydrogen bonds at site 1 in the active state, the S82A mutation did not affect the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system (Fig. S5D). Furthermore, combined mutations at residues R72, S73, and D76 only partially reduced the anti-phage activity (Fig. S5D). Together, these results suggest that salt bridge interactions may play a key role in the assembly of the KomBC complex into a filament.

Structural analysis of dITP recognition by KomB

Previous work showed that the signaling molecule dITP binds to and activates the KomBC complex, but its precise binding mode was unknown19. Our cryo-EM structures revealed that in both intermediate and active states, dITP occupies a similar position within the KomB dimer, while the geometry of the binding pocket and the conformation of the ligand differ markedly (Figs. 4A, S6). Superimposition of the two states showed that the bound dITP undergoes a distinct reorientation of its base, ribose, and phosphate groups in the active state compared to the intermediate state (Fig. 4B). These observations suggest that dITP binding initiates conformational remodeling of the KomBC filament, while the conformation of the bound dITP changes as the KomBC filament undergoes conformational remodeling during activation. KomB exhibits high overall structural similarity to inosine triphosphatases20 (Fig. S7A, B). However, unlike canonical inosine triphosphatases, KomB has lost hydrolytic activity19. Sequence alignment shows that a conserved glutamate critical for catalysis in inosine triphosphatase is replaced by histidine (H65) in KomB19 (Fig. S7C).

Further analysis of the binding pockets revealed that the residues contributing to dITP recognition differ between the intermediate and active states (Fig. 4C, D). Residues interacting with the base, ribose, and triphosphate moieties of dITP vary between KomB1 and KomB2 in the two states, reflecting dynamic changes in dITP–KomB interactions. These conformational dynamics are likely critical for modulating KomB conformation and transmitting the activation signal to KomC.

Previous studies demonstrated that KomBC can be activated exclusively by dITP and not by NTPs, dNTPs, or ITP19. In agreement with this specificity, both structures reveal multiple residues forming hydrogen bonds with the triphosphate group (Fig. 4C, D), supporting the specific recognition of a triphosphate nucleotide. Moreover, Q141 and W142 in KomB1 of the intermediate state, as well as Q141 in KomB2 of the active state, are positioned in close proximity to the 2’ carbon of the deoxyribose, which likely causes steric hindrance around the 2’ position and thereby explains the specific recognition of dITP rather than ITP (Fig. S8A, B). For base recognition, F140 and H11 in KomB2 of the intermediate state, as well as Q141 in KomB1 of the active state, form hydrogen bonds with the carbonyl group at position 6’ of hypoxanthine base (Fig. 4C, D), ensuring discrimination against adenine base. Additional interactions, such as R168 in KomB1 of the active state, and F140 in KomB2 of the active state, further stabilize the hypoxanthine base (Fig. S8B, C).

To test the functional importance of these contacts, we mutated residues involved in base recognition (F140, Q141), phosphate interactions (K49, R8), and ribose recognition (Q141, W142, T66). Mutations of K49, F140, Q141, W142 and R8 completely abolished the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system, while mutation of T66 significantly reduced activity (Fig. 4E). We further selected several key residues involved in recognizing the base, ribose, and phosphate groups of dITP (F140, Q141, W142, and K49) and generated the corresponding mutants for in vitro activity assays. Consistent with the in vivo anti-phage activity results, mutations at these sites completely abolished KomBC activity (Figs. 4F,S9). These results demonstrate that these residues are essential for dITP recognition and functional activation of the KomBC complex. In addition, these results showed that Q141 is critical for dITP recognition. Interestingly, sequence alignment revealed that Q141 is unique to KomB compared with inosine triphosphatases (Fig. S7C), highlighting an evolutionary divergence between the two classes of proteins.

Progressive conformational changes at the KomB–KomC interaction interfaces during KomBC activation

KomB dimers are anchored on both sides of the KomC dimer of dimers, establishing KomB–KomB and KomB–KomC interfaces. Binding of dITP induces progressive conformational transitions of the KomB dimer from the apo to the intermediate and ultimately to the active state, involving minor rearrangements during the apo-to-intermediate transition but more extensive changes during the transition to the active state (Fig. 5A). Comparison of the KomB dimer structures between the apo and intermediate states shows that, upon dITP binding, the overall conformation of the KomB dimer remains largely unchanged, but a local region (residues 1–53) near the dITP-binding pocket undergoes pronounced conformational changes (Fig. 5A). In contrast, comparison between the intermediate and active states reveals a substantial conformational rotation in KomB, bringing the two KomB subunits within the dimer closer together (Fig. 5A). The conformational changes in KomB also lead to remodeling of both the KomB–KomB and KomB–KomC interfaces.

At the KomB–KomB interface, several residues form hydrogen bonds across all three states. Although the specific hydrogen-bonding patterns vary among states, an interaction between equivalent loops (residues 32–41) of the two KomB subunits is consistently maintained (Fig. S10A–10C). Mutational analysis of several interacting residues revealed that the hydrogen bonds between E36 and Q38 from the loop (residues 32–41) are critical, as disruption of this interaction abolished anti-phage activity, whereas other hydrogen-bond mutations had only modest or negligible effects (Fig. S10D). Thus, the loop–loop interaction between the two KomB subunits is indispensable for activation of the Kongming system.

The KomB–KomC interface comprises two distinct sites: site 1 (KomB1–KomC1/C2 interface) and site 2 (KomB2–KomC1/C2 interface). Interactions at these sites change progressively from the apo to the intermediate and then to the active state (Fig. 5B–G). During the apo-to-intermediate transition, interactions at site 2 remain unchanged, whereas those at site 1 are rearranged (Fig. 5B–E). Specifically, several residues (including R4, S30, K47 and R59 in KomB and D46, S245, E246 and D248 in KomC) involved in interactions in the apo state are repositioned in the intermediate state, resulting in the disruption of the original interaction network and formation of a hydrogen bond between D44 in KomC and M117 in KomB (Figs. 5B–E,S11). During the intermediate-to-active transition, interactions at site 1 remain unchanged, while those at site 2 are remodeled (Fig. 5D–G). Functional analysis revealed that interactions between residues E246 and D248 in KomC1 and residues R4 and S30 in KomB1 are essential for anti-phage activity (Fig. 5C, H).

Site 2 in the apo state, as well as both sites 1 and 2 in the intermediate and active state, share a conserved hydrogen bond formed between the amide nitrogen of D44 in KomC and the backbone carbonyl of M117 in KomB (Fig. 5C, E, G). In addition, D44 is positioned near K118, supporting a potential electrostatic interaction between these residues. M117 and K118 are both located within a loop (residues 115 to 118) connecting two α-helices, referred to here as loop2 (Fig. 5C, E, G). Replacement of this loop with a GS linker (referred to as the loop2 mutant) completely abolished anti-phage activity (Fig. 5I). Analysis of the purified proteins demonstrated that replacement of loop2 disrupted the KomB–KomC interaction (Fig. 5J, K), highlighting the critical role of this loop. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the KomB–KomC interaction is essential for activation of the Kongming system.

Formation of the active conformation of KomC

Within the KomBC filament, each repeating unit contains a KomC tetramer formed by two symmetric KomC dimers. Across the apo, intermediate, and active states, the KomC tetramer undergoes a distinct conformational transition from an open to a closed, cloverleaf-like architecture (Fig. 6A–C). Structural superposition of KomC tetramer reveals only minor conformational changes between the apo and intermediate states, whereas KomC undergoes a substantial rotational shift when transitioning from the intermediate to the active state (Fig. S12).

From the apo to the intermediate and then to the active state, the KomC–KomC interaction interfaces are progressively remodeled. Taking the KomC3–KomC4 dimer as an example, two interfaces are present in the apo and intermediate states: KomC3–KomC4 interface (site 1) and KomC3–KomC1 interface (site 2) (Fig. 6A, B). In the active state, a new interface emerges between KomC4 and KomC2 (site 3) (Fig. 6C).

Both site 1 and site 2 are stabilized by a combination of polar and hydrophobic interactions across the apo, intermediate, and active states (Figs. 6D–F, S13). Hydrophobic interactions at both sites are largely conserved across all three states (Fig. S13). Hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions at both sites change only subtly from the apo to the intermediate state, but change markedly in the active state (Fig. 6D–F). At site 1, the interaction between E56 and Q231 is maintained across all three states. Consistently, the E56K mutation completely abolished the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system (Fig. 6G), highlighting the functional importance of this residue. In addition, in the active state, salt bridges are formed between E233 and K64, and between R198 and E159 (Fig. 6F). Mutation of E233 partially reduced the anti-phage activity, indicating that this residue contributes to system function but are not strictly essential (Fig. 6G). At site 2, residues K10, N12 and S178 participate in hydrogen bond interactions in the apo and intermediate states. Mutational analysis showed that single substitutions of these residues, as well as their combinations, did not affect the anti-phage activity (Fig. S14). These results suggest that hydrophobic interactions at the KomC3–KomC1 interface play a major role in stabilizing this interface in the apo and intermediate states.

Upon activation, KomC undergoes a pronounced rotation that extensively remodels site 2 and drives the formation of site 3. In the active state, salt bridges between E169 and R177 are established at both site 2 and site 3. Substituting E169 with lysine or R177 with alanine significantly impaired anti-phage activity, highlighting its critical functional role (Fig. 6H). These results indicate that the newly formed interactions in the active state are crucial for maintaining the functional conformation of KomC and ensuring effective activation of the Kongming system.

KomC is responsible for NAD+ hydrolysis. To gain insight into substrate recognition, we employed AlphaFold3 to predict an NAD+-bound KomC structure with high confidence (Fig. S15A). Based on this model, we further mapped the putative NAD+-binding sites onto the apo, intermediate, and active states (Fig. S15B–D). By comparing the apo and intermediate state complexes, as well as the intermediate and active state complexes (Fig. 6I, J), we found that a region of KomC (residues 188–211), which contains a loop (residues 188–194) followed by a short α-helix (residues 195–211), undergoes a pronounced conformational shift in the active state, thereby positioning this segment closer to the modeled NAD+-binding site. We speculate that this movement completes the formation of the NADase active site in KomC. To test this hypothesis, we mutated two residues within this region, S193 and D194, which are located near the NAD+ molecule. The results showed that the D194A, but not the S193A, mutation completely abolished the anti-phage activity of the Kongming system (Fig. 6K). Consistent with the in vivo anti-phage activity results, the D194A mutation abolished the NADase activity of the KomBC complex (Fig. 6L). Furthermore, multiple sequence alignment showed that D194 is conserved in the immune-associated NADase effector clade of the SIR2 family, while it is not conserved in the canonical NAD+-dependent deacetylases (Figs. 6MS16). Together, the results highlight the importance of D194 and support the functional relevance of the conformational movement in this region.

Discussion

Based on our experimental results together with the previous study19, we propose a mechanistic model illustrating how the Kongming system is activated to defend against phage infection (Fig. 7A). The activation process begins when intracellular dAMP is converted to the signaling molecule dITP through a cascade of reactions involving a phage-encoded DNK, the Kongming-encoded KomA, and the host-encoded NDK. The generated dITP binds to the KomB subunits within the KomBC filament, inducing conformational changes in KomB. This, in turn, triggers a significant conformational rearrangement of KomC, leading KomC to adopt an active conformation capable of hydrolyzing NAD+ and triggering cell death, thereby providing population-level immunity through a suicide-based mechanism.

Compared with previous studies on nucleotide signaling-based defense systems, such as CBASS, type III CRISPR-Cas and Thoeris, our study reveals a distinct mode of effector activation by a signaling molecule (Fig. 7B). In previously characterized systems, the effectors typically exist as monomers or low-order oligomers (such as dimers or tetramers) in the inactive state and assemble into higher-order filaments only after binding their signaling molecules, with filament formation accompanied by the transition of the effector proteins from the inactive to the active state12,14,16–18. In contrast, our study reveals that the KomBC effector complex is already assembled into a filament in the apo state. Binding of dITP triggers progressive conformational rearrangements, ultimately generating a distinct, active filament architecture. As many effectors remain uncharacterized, and new signaling molecules and effectors continue to emerge4,21, similar activation modes may also exist in other nucleotide signaling-based defense systems.

KomC is a SIR2 domain-containing protein. Members of the SIR2 family were initially identified as NAD+-dependent deacetylases22,23, but recently they have been found to be widely distributed in diverse prokaryotic defense systems, where they function as NADase effectors. Recently, SIR2 homologs with immune-related functions have also been found in mammals, suggesting an evolutionary connection between bacterial and mammalian immunity24. Comparison of the KomC structures in the apo, intermediate, and active states revealed a pronounced conformational change in a region (residues 188–211) near the modeled NAD+-binding site. In the active state, this region shifts markedly toward the modeled NAD+ molecule. Functional analyses further identified residue D194 within this region as essential for the activity of the Kongming system. Notably, D194 is conserved among prokaryotic SIR2 effectors involved in immunity but is absent from non-immune NAD+-dependent deacetylases, suggesting that this residue may represent a key evolutionary step that enabled SIR2 proteins to acquire NADase function.

KomB is homologous to inosine triphosphatases and adopts a similar overall structural architecture. However, in the Kongming system, KomB has evolved to retain the ability to bind dITP while losing its hydrolytic activity19. As a result, KomB functions as a novel nucleotide signal sensor rather than a hydrolase. The previous study has revealed that KomB and KomC are consistently found together across Kongming systems, whereas KomA may vary or be replaced by other nucleotide-metabolizing enzymes19. Since KomA is responsible for synthesizing the signaling molecule, such variation suggests that different Kongming systems may produce distinct nucleotide signals, and that KomB has correspondingly adapted to recognize them. In addition, our structural insights provide a basis for engineering KomB to recognize alternative nucleotides, potentially enabling the development of novel tools for detecting specific nucleotide molecules.

Shortly after submission of this manuscript, two independent studies on the KomBC effector complex were published25,26. These studies reported structural and mechanistic insights into KomBC activation. Our work complements these findings by independently validating the mechanism from a distinct perspective. Importantly, we resolved an intermediate state of the dITP-bound KomBC filament, providing additional mechanistic insight into its activation, and identified a conserved residue (D194) in the SIR2 effector that is critical for NADase activity.

Methods

Plasmid construction

The Kongming system from E. coli NCTC13216 (Genbank: CAADJL010000005.1) was synthesized by two approaches: the komB and komC genes were synthesized by overlap extension PCR, and the komA gene was synthesized by Tsingke Company (Beijing, China). For expression and purification of the KomBC complex, the coding sequences of komB and komC were cloned into the pET28a plasmid, with an N-terminal His-tag (MGSSHHHHHHSSG) fused to KomB. For phage plaque assays, the entire Kongming operon was cloned into the pQE80L plasmid under the control of the T5 promoter. To detect the expression of the wild-type and mutant forms of KomB and KomC by Western blot, all coding sequences were individually cloned into the pQE80L vector with a C-terminal HA-tag (GSSYPYDVPDYA). All the site-directed mutants were constructed by whole-plasmid PCR. The primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Protein expression, purification and characterization

The plasmid encoding the KomBC complex was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3). Transformants were grown at 37 °C in LB medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin until the OD600 reached ~1.0, at which point protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) for 16 h at 18 °C. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 20 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT), and lysed by sonication. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatant was applied to Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen) by gravity flow. The resin was washed with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted with elution buffer (20 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 250 mM imidazole, 1 mM DTT). The eluted protein was concentrated and further purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a SuperoseTM 6 Increase 10/300 GL column (Cytiva) equilibrated in gel filtration buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT). Peak fractions were pooled, concentrated, analyzed by SDS-PAGE, and stored at 4 °C for cryo-EM sample preparation or at −80 °C for enzymatic activity assay.

The molecular weights of KomBC complex and its mutants (KomBCloop1 mutant and KomBCR77A) were analyzed by SEC coupled to multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS). A total of 500 μL of 1 mg/mL protein, purified by Ni-NTA resin as described above, was loaded onto the SuperoseTM 6 Increase 10/300 GL column at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, using the same gel filtration buffer. Light scattering signals were recorded with a Wyatt Dawn Heleos II detector (Wyatt Technology). SEC-MALS, and the data were analyzed using ASTRA 6 software (Wyatt Technology). All buffer pH values used were adjusted at 25 °C.

Negative stain electron microscopy

Protein sample homogeneity and overall morphology were assessed by negative staining. Samples of the KomBC complex, alone or in complex with dITP (at a molar ratio of 1:2), were diluted to 0.5 mg/mL and applied to glow-discharged 400-mesh copper grids coated with a continuous carbon film. After 60 s of incubation at room temperature, excess solution was blotted with filter paper. Grids were stained with 5 μL of saturated uryl acetate using a three-step procedure: 10 s, 10 s, and 30 s, with blotting performed between each application. After air-drying for 10 min, images were acquired on an FEI Talos 120 transmission electron microscope operated at 120 kV, with a pixel size of 1.535 Å at the specimen level.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Purified apo-KomBC complex ( ~ 0.8 mg/mL) in gel filtration buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM DTT) was used directly for cryo-EM grid preparation. For the dITP-bound KomBC sample, the same protein solution was supplemented with 0.1 mM ZnCl2 and ~32 μM dITP, incubated at room temperature for 30 min before vitrification.

Aliquots of 4 μL protein solution were applied to glow-discharged Quantifoil R1.2/1.3 Au 300-mesh grids, blotted for 5 s at 4 °C and 100% humidity, and plunge-frozen into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (FEI).

Cryo-EM data were collected on a Titan Krios transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV, equipped with a Gatan K3 direct electron detector in super-resolution mode and a GIF Quantum energy filter (20 eV slit width). Images were recorded using EPU software at a nominal magnification of 105,000×, yielding a calibrated pixel size of 0.85 Å, and a defocus range of −1.0 to −2.2 μm. A total exposure time of 2.5 s, corresponding to a total dose of 30 e−/Å2, was distributed over 40 frames.

Cryo-EM data processing

Cryo-EM datasets for KomBC, both apo and in complex with dITP, were processed using a standard workflow in CryoSPARC v4.2.1 (Figs. S2, S3). Movie frames were motion-corrected using Patch Motion Correction and binned by a factor of 3/4, followed by contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation with Patch CTF. Micrographs exhibiting a CTF resolution worse than 5 Å or significant ice contamination were discarded. Particles were picked from curated micrographs, extracted with a box size of 800 pixels, and subsequently cropped to 200 pixels. The particle stacks were then subjected to multiple rounds of 2D classification, ab initio reconstruction, and heterogeneous refinement. We also attempted helical reconstruction to potentially improve the map quality; however, this approach did not yield a higher resolution map compared to standard non-uniform refinement. Consequently, the final maps used for model building were generated through non-uniform refinement without imposing any symmetry (see Table S2 for data collection and refinement statistics).

Molecular modeling and validation

Initial atomic models for KomBC, predicted by AlphaFold227, were rigid-body fitted into the cryo-EM density maps using ChimeraX v1.6.1. The dITP substrate model was built de novo in Coot v0.9.8, refined with Phenix eLBOW, and fitted into the corresponding density. The protein models were manually adjusted in Coot and iteratively refined in real space using Phenix v1.20.1, with cycles of global minimization, rotamer optimization, and energy minimization. Model quality was validated by MolProbity (Ramachandran outliers: 0%; rotamer outliers: <1%), EMRinger (Z-score > 3), and the map-model Fourier shell correlation (FSC) at the 0.143 cutoff. All structural figures were prepared with PyMOL v2.5.2 and ChimeraX. Refinement statistics are detailed in Table S2.

Modeling of the KomC–NAD+ complex

To infer the potential NAD+-binding site of KomC, we generated a KomC–NAD+ complex model using AlphaFold328. The NAD+ molecule from the AlphaFold3-predicted model was aligned onto the cryo-EM structures of KomBC, providing modeled NAD+-binding sites for the apo, intermediate and active states.

Phage plaque assays

E. coli BL21(DE3) strains carrying recombinant vectors expressing either wild-type or mutated Kongming systems, or the empty vector control (pQE80L), were grown in LB medium supplemented with 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C for ~8 h. Then, 1 mL of each culture was mixed with 14 mL of molten 0.5% LB top agar containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 0.1 mM IPTG, and immediately overlaid onto LB agar plates (1.5%) containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin. Phages were serially diluted tenfold in LB medium, and 2 μL of each dilution was spotted onto the bacterial lawns. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for ~8 h before plaque formation was assessed.

In vitro NADase activity assay

The NADase activities of KomBC complex and its mutants were measured using ε-NAD+ (Biolog Life Science Institute), a fluorescent analogue of NAD+, as the substrate. Reaction mixtures (50 μL) contained 25 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5, 25 °C), 150 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM ZnCl2, 1 mM DTT, 50 μM ε-NAD+, the indicated concentrations of KomBC complex or its mutants, and dITP at twice the molar concentration of the KomBC complex. Assays were performed in a 96-well microplate at 37 °C. Fluorescence signals were recorded on a SpectraMax i3x Multi-Mode Microplate Reader with excitation at 300 nm and emission at 410 nm, at 30 s intervals for a total 30 min. For molar concentration calculations, the KomBC filament complex was considered as composed of KomB and KomC in a 1:1 molar ratio, and the combined molecular mass of one KomB and one KomC subunit was used for conversion.

Western blot analysis

Proteins purified using Ni-NTA agarose beads were collected from the elution fractions. Purified protein (40 µL) was mixed with 5× SDS loading buffer and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of each sample was resolved by SDS–PAGE on 15% polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system at 100 V for 120 min at 4 °C. The membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing twice with TBST (10 min each), the membranes were incubated with an anti-His monoclonal antibody (1:5000 dilution; mouse monoclonal, ABclonal, China) diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with TBST (10 min each), the membranes were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:10,000 dilution, ABclonal, China) for 1 h at room temperature. After three additional washes with TBST, protein signals were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents and visualized using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

To examine the expression of the wild-type and mutant forms of KomB and KomC in E. coli BL21(DE3), KomB and KomC (and their respective mutants) were individually expressed with a C-terminal HA tag. Whole-cell samples were analyzed by Western blot (Fig. S17) with an anti-HA monoclonal antibody (1:2000 dilution; mouse monoclonal, ABclonal, China). The experimental procedures were performed as described above.
