Biodegradable Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membranes enabling integrated osteogenic and antibacterial functions for guided bone regeneration
Authors: Jialin Liu, Tang Cai, Chengao Du, Jia She, Chunjie Jin, Dan Zhang, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBiodegradable magnesium (Mg) alloys are promising candidates for guided bone regeneration (GBR) owing to their bone-matched elastic modulus and intrinsic bioactivity. However, simultaneously achieving mechanical robustness, degradation control, and multifunctional bioactivity in a single Mg-based membrane remains a major challenge. Herein, a magnesium-strontium/magnesium-silver (Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag) bilayer GBR membrane is constructed through rational design. Enabled by on-line heating rolling, the metallurgically bonded structure exhibits enhanced mechanical performance (flexural yield strength: 463 ± 9 MPa) while maintaining a stable corrosion rate. More importantly, the spatially resolved configuration enables distinct functional domains: the Sr-containing layer promotes osteogenesis at the bone interface, whereas the Ag-containing layer provides antibacterial protection at the soft-tissue side. The resulting membrane shows approximately 90% broad-spectrum antibacterial efficacy in vitro and significantly accelerates bone regeneration in vivo, with a bone volume fraction of 57.53 ± 4.82% at 8 weeks. Transcriptomic analysis of single alloy extracts suggests that Sr-containing cues are associated with Wnt-related osteogenic programs, whereas Ag-containing cues are associated with integrin-related cell adhesion and matrix-remodeling responses. These findings establish a metallurgically bonded bilayer Mg-alloy membrane that couples osteogenic ion delivery with antibacterial protection, providing a structurally stable and biologically programmed strategy for bone-soft tissue interface regeneration in GBR.Keywords: Magnesium alloys, Guided bone regeneration, Bilayer membranes, Corrosion behavior, Osteogenesis, Antibacterial performance

Introduction

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is widely employed for the reconstruction of alveolar bone defects arising from trauma, periodontal disease or tooth loss [1,2]. By physically separating the defect from surrounding soft tissues, barrier membranes create a protected microenvironment that supports osteogenic cell migration, proliferation, and new bone formation [3,4]. Clinically available GBR membranes, however, remain suboptimal. Nonresorbable membranes, such as titanium mesh, offer robust mechanical support and effective space maintenance, yet necessitate secondary surgical removal, with exposure rates of 11-23%, increasing the risk of soft tissue injury and infection [5,6]. Resorbable collagen membranes exhibit excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability, but their limited mechanical strength renders them susceptible to deformation or collapse in large defects [7,8]. In addition, most traditional membranes lack intrinsic bioactivity, including osteoinductive and antibacterial functions, thereby compromising infection control and soft-hard tissue integration [9,10]. These limitations highlight the need for next-generation biodegradable membranes that combine mechanical integrity with biological functionality.

Magnesium (Mg) and its alloys have emerged as promising candidates for GBR membranes owing to their elastic modulus comparable to cortical bone (∼45 GPa), favorable biocompatibility, and tunable bioactivity [11,12]. Preliminary clinical evidence has suggested that Mg membranes can enhance bone regeneration in scenarios where collagen membranes fail to maintain defect space [13]. Despite these advantages, several critical challenges remain. Rapid corrosion of pure Mg under physiological environments may lead to premature loss of mechanical integrity [14,15]. In addition, intraoperative shaping can introduce stress concentrations that increase susceptibility to stress corrosion cracking (SCC) [16]. In terms of biological functionality, since GBR membranes are positioned at the interface between bone and soft tissue, they must simultaneously provide stable space maintenance and favorable biological responses on both sides. However, degradation-induced alkalization and hydrogen evolution of pure Mg may adversely affect cell adhesion, wound healing, and interfacial tissue integration [[17], [18], [19]]. Moreover, its antibacterial effect largely relies on Mg2+-induced osmotic changes and alkaline microenvironment. Once biofilms form, bacteria can tolerate pH fluctuations, limiting the ability of pure Mg membranes to support tissue healing [20].

Microalloying with bioactive elements offers an effective strategy to simultaneously enhance the mechanical and biological performance of Mg-based materials [21]. The WE43 magnesium alloy device MAGNEZIX® CS, developed by Syntellix AG (Germany), has received Conformité Européenne marking and has been commercially introduced in multiple countries. Among various alloying elements, strontium (Sr) has been reported to refine grains and reduce the corrosion rate of Mg alloys. Gu et al. reported that when the Sr content is below 2 wt%, the mechanical properties and corrosion resistance of Mg-Sr alloys are significantly improved [22]. Upon release, Sr2+ promotes osteoblast proliferation and differentiation by activating signaling pathways such as the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) and Wnt/β-catenin [23,24]. Silver (Ag), in contrast, provides potent antibacterial activity. Ag+ exhibits broad-spectrum antimicrobial effects even at low concentrations. Chen et al. demonstrated that the Zn-Mg-Ag alloy with an Ag content of <0.1 wt% achieved a bactericidal rate exceeding 90% against common oral pathogens, while maintaining favorable biocompatibility [25]. Notably, beyond its antibacterial activity, Ag+ has also been implicated in modulating fibroblast migration, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses, thereby supporting soft-tissue healing and epithelial sealing [26]. Overall, the development of a functional magnesium-based barrier membrane capable of co-releasing Sr2+ and Ag+ may simultaneously enhance mechanical performance, promote osteogenic activity, and provide effective antibacterial protection, thereby addressing the dual challenges of insufficient bone formation and inadequate infection control during bone regeneration.

Despite this potential, integrating multiple bioactive ions remains challenging due to differences in release kinetics and biological targets, which may lead to functional interference [27]. Janus architectures provide a promising solution by enabling functional separation [28]. For example, a dicalcium phosphate dihydrate (DCPD)/MgF2 Janus membrane has been developed to regulate the bone and soft-tissue interfaces, exhibiting surface selective cellular responses and promoting bone formation while maintaining regenerative space in vivo [29]. However, coating-based Janus membranes often suffer from weak coating substrate adhesion, potential delamination during degradation or deformation, and limited control over degradation behavior and ion-release kinetics, which may compromise long term structural stability. To overcome these limitations, we propose a metallurgically bonded bilayer Mg-alloy membrane with integrated functionality. Our previous research has shown that the on-line heating rolling technique can achieve excellent metallurgical bonding for different types of alloys [28]. The metallurgically bonded bilayer structure enables strong interfacial stability and mechanical durability while minimizing cross-interference between ion-release profiles. Furthermore, it allows independent regulation of degradation behavior and biological function in distinct microenvironments. Specifically, the Mg-Sr inner layer is oriented toward the bony defect to enable directional Sr2+ release and, together with Mg2+, to promote osteogenesis, whereas the outer Mg-Ag layer faces the soft-tissue side to release Ag+ and provide antibacterial protection while supporting early soft tissue integration.

In this study, a Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane with integrated functionality was fabricated via on-line heating rolling to achieve a metallurgically bonded architecture. The structural characteristics, interfacial stability, degradation behavior, and mechanical properties of the membrane were systematically investigated. Subsequently, its biological performance, including cytocompatibility, osteogenic activity, antibacterial efficacy, and cell migration behavior, was evaluated through in vitro assays. Transcriptomic analysis was performed to explore the potential molecular mechanisms underlying the regulated cellular responses induced by the bilayer structure. Furthermore, in vivo bone regeneration was assessed using a rat mandibular defect model to validate its therapeutic efficacy. This work provides a rational strategy for constructing multifunctional GBR membranes by integrating osteogenic and antibacterial properties within a biodegradable bilayer architecture for complex alveolar bone regeneration.

Results

Microstructure and mechanical properties of the bilayer membrane

Fig. 1a illustrates the fabrication process of the bilayer membrane. The heterogeneous structure of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane was characterized using electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) (Fig. 1b). The interfacial bonding region (red line) displayed fine grain sizes due to the combination of Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag layers under stress and temperature. This grain refinement at the interface was attributed to continuous dynamic recrystallization induced by the severe plastic deformation during processing. The average grain size of the bilayer membrane was measured to be 2.78 μm. In the EBSD map, the distribution of numerous low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs, 2-15°) within coarse grains was indicative of continuous dynamic recrystallization, whereas high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs, >15°) were primarily located within recrystallized grains. Fig. 1c showed the (0001) pole figure of the bilayer membrane, revealing a typical basal texture. The majority of grains, colored in blue and green, were oriented parallel to the rolling direction (RD), with a maximum texture intensity of 8.16. The kernel average misorientation (KAM) map demonstrated a consistent distribution between LAGBs and dislocations, with no significant dislocation accumulation observed (Fig. 1d). The recrystallization fraction analysis of the bilayer membrane is presented in Fig. 1g, with the proportions of recrystallized, substructured, and deformed grains quantified as 31.8%, 13.9%, and 54.3%, respectively.

Fabrication and microstructural characterization of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane. (a) Fabrication process; (b) EBSD map; (c) Pole figure; (d) KAM map; (e) Grain size distribution map; (f) Misorientation angle distribution; (g) Recrystallization fraction map.

To investigate the shear strength of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane, the interface was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 2a, no gaps were observed at the interface, indicating effective bonding and welding between the Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag layers. Compared to the Mg-Ag layer, the Mg-Sr layer contained a higher dispersion of second-phase particles. For further microstructural analysis of the interfacial bonding, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a site-specific sample prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) milling at the interface (Fig. 2b). The bright-field (BF) TEM image revealed a relatively planar interfacial morphology with a distinct transition zone (Fig. 2c). The EDS mapping results (Fig. 2d–h) revealed a continuous and uniform distribution of oxygen along the interface, indicating that the transition zone was composed of oxide compounds such as MgO. The formation of this oxide layer was attributed to the ingress of air between the sheets during the rolling process. Furthermore, enrichment of Sr and Ag elements was observed at the interface. This was attributed to the accelerated diffusion of solute atoms and their tendency to segregate at grain boundaries and interfaces, driven by dislocation multiplication and grain boundary migration induced by high-temperature plastic deformation during rolling. The TEM image showed that the Mg-side matrix exhibited a disordered arrangement comprising nanocrystalline and amorphous regions (Fig. 2i). These regions were well-bonded, with no observed pores. Furthermore, selected area diffraction pattern was performed at the interface. On the Mg-Sr side, the d-spacing for the (313) and (314) planes of a Mg-Sr intermetallic phase were measured as 0.2046 nm and 0.2024 nm, respectively. On the Mg-Ag side, due to the low Ag content, no Mg-Ag intermetallic phase was detected in this region. The d-spacing for the Mg (104) and (002) planes were 0.1178 nm and 0.2672 nm, respectively.

The tensile stress-strain curves of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane, Mg-Sr, and Mg-Ag were presented in Fig. 2j. The bilayer membrane exhibited a tensile strength of 258 ± 5 MPa, which is higher than that of Mg-Sr (243 ± 9 MPa) and Mg-Ag (230 ± 4 MPa). The elongation of the bilayer membrane was also greater than that of the individual Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag layers, and the yield strength between the values of the two constituent materials. The mechanical property data were summarized in Table S1. The flexural properties were evaluated using a three-point bending test (Fig. 2k). Compared to Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag, the bilayer membrane demonstrated the highest flexural yield strength, reaching 463 ± 9 MPa, significantly exceeding the 391 ± 7 MPa of Mg-Sr and 290 ± 6 MPa of Mg-Ag. The flexural modulus of the bilayer membrane was 68.8 ± 3.4 GPa, meeting the requirements for implant applications.

In vitro degradation behavior and interfacial stability of the bilayer membrane

To analyze and compare the corrosion resistance of the Mg-Sr, Mg-Ag, and the bilayer membrane, potentiodynamic polarization curves of the three materials were measured in modified simulated body fluid (m-SBF). Generally, a lower corrosion current density indicates better corrosion resistance [30]. As shown in Fig. 3a, the bilayer membrane exhibited a corrosion potential (Ecorr) of −1.58 V and a corrosion current density (icorr) of 1.27 × 10−4 A cm−2. In comparison, the Mg-Sr membrane had an Ecorr of −1.73 V and an icorr of 2.21 × 10−4 A cm−2, while the Mg-Ag membrane showed an Ecorr of −1.55 V and an icorr of 8.66 × 10−5 A cm−2. Notably, the Mg-Ag membrane displayed a more positive corrosion potential. This was attributed to the high standard electrode potential of Ag (+0.80 V vs. SHE). The addition of Ag elevated the electrode potential of the Mg matrix, leading to a positive shift in the corrosion potential of the Mg-Ag membrane and consequently, the best corrosion resistance among the tested materials. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and Bode plots (Fig. 3b and c) corroborated this trend, confirming the superior short-term corrosion resistance of the Mg-Ag membrane. The obtained data were fitted using an equivalent circuit model (Fig. 3b), where Rs represents the solution resistance, CPEQf and Rf denote the constant phase element and resistance of the corrosion product film (CPEQf defined by parameters Y and n), CPEQdl and Rct correspond to the constant phase element and charge transfer resistance of the double layer, and L and RL represent the inductance and inductive resistance associated with adsorbed intermediate species during corrosion. The fitted parameters are listed in Table S2.

To investigate the localized corrosion behavior of the bilayer membrane in m-SBF, scanning vibrating electrode technique (SVET) and local electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (LEIS) were employed. The SVET map revealed highly heterogeneous electrochemical activity across the sample cross-section after 24 h of immersion (Fig. 3d). LEIS results indicated a non-uniform impedance distribution (Fig. 3e). As immersion proceeded, the developing corrosion product layer partially inhibited the electrochemical reactions, suggesting a moderation of the corrosion rate after the initial stage.

As an implant material, the Mg membrane must function effectively in vivo over the long term [31]. Therefore, hydrogen evolution tests were conducted to evaluate long-term corrosion performance (Fig. 3f). The hydrogen evolution volume for the bilayer membrane after 7 days was 2.47 ± 0.25 mL cm−2, a value close to that of Mg-Sr (2.41 ± 0.24 mL cm−2) and significantly lower than that of Mg-Ag (6.88 ± 0.69 mL cm−2). This lower hydrogen evolution rate helped prevent tissue inflammation caused by excessive gas accumulation. The pH values of the solution after 7 days of immersion were shown in Fig. 3g. The Mg-Ag sample yielded the highest pH of 8.97 ± 0.02, compared to 8.66 ± 0.03 for the bilayer membrane and 8.63 ± 0.06 for Mg-Sr. Material degradation inevitably led to mass loss. The samples were immersed in a water bath at 37 ± 0.5 °C for 14 days. The corrosion rate of the bilayer membrane was 0.726 ± 0.035 mm y−1. From day 1 to 14, the corrosion rate exhibited an initial increase, followed by a decrease, and then a subsequent rise (Fig. 3h). The initial increase was attributed to the rapid dissolution of the bare Mg matrix before a stable passivation film formed, exacerbated by the galvanic potential difference between the Mg-0.5Sr and Mg-0.1 Ag layers. The subsequent decrease and stabilization resulted from the formation of a passivation film and a reduction in the galvanic effect. The final increase was likely caused by the breakdown of the passivation film, exposing fresh Mg matrix to the solution and accelerating corrosion. As shown in Fig. 3i, the mass loss of all three materials increased over time. Overall, Mg-Ag degraded the fastest, with a weight loss rate of 31.4 ± 7.3%. The bilayer membrane showed a weight loss rate of 22.7 ± 2.6% after 14 days. Notably, the weight loss data show that the Mg-Ag alloy has the poorest corrosion resistance, which at first glance seems to contradict the electrochemical results (Fig. 3a), where the same alloy exhibits a more positive corrosion potential. The reason for this discrepancy is that electrochemical tests capture mainly the instantaneous interfacial electrochemical behavior at the moment of measurement, whereas hydrogen evolution and weight loss experiments reflect the cumulative degradation built up over prolonged immersion. The more positive corrosion potential of the Mg-Ag alloy in the electrochemical tests can be attributed to the formation of high-potential Mg-Ag intermetallic compounds from the trace Ag present. These compounds serve as effective cathodic sites and shift the corrosion potential in the positive direction. Over longer immersion times, however, Ag-containing phases or Ag-enriched regions can act as local cathodes and drive galvanic corrosion; at the same time, the repeated breakdown and re-formation of the corrosion product film further accelerate long-term degradation. Thus, the corrosion resistance of Mg-Ag as evaluated by electrochemical tests is not directly equivalent to the long-term hydrogen evolution and weight loss results.

However, in the oral environment, bilayer membrane is exposed to continuous mechanical disturbance and fluid flow, which can compromise the integrity of the corrosion product layer. To better mimic these in vivo dynamic conditions, the long-term degradation behavior was further examined under agitated immersion. Briefly, the bilayer membrane was immersed in simulated body fluid (SBF) at 37 °C and subjected to constant horizontal shaking at 100 rpm using a thermostatic water bath vibrator (Guowang Instrument, SHA-CA). Fig. 4a–c summarizes the dynamic corrosion of materials. At 14 days, the corrosion rates of Mg-Sr, Mg-Ag, and the bilayer membrane were 0.856 ± 0.072, 3.67 ± 0.29, and 0.926 ± 0.104 mm/y, respectively, with corresponding mass losses of 28.1 ± 2.2%, 93.3 ± 2.2%, and 33.8 ± 4.4% (Fig. 4a and b). Notably, under dynamic shaking conditions, the bilayer membrane degraded faster than under static immersion for the same duration, likely because the continuous fluid flow facilitates the removal of the corrosion product layer and accelerates mass loss. After 7 days of immersion, the pH of the bilayer membrane was 8.53 ± 0.02, falling between those of Mg-Sr (8.42 ± 0.03) and Mg-Ag (8.78 ± 0.03) (Fig. 4c). In addition, the cumulative releases of Mg2+, Sr2+, and Ag+ ions from the bilayer membrane in SBF were measured by inductively coupled plasma (ICP) at various time points, and the results are shown in Fig. 4d–f. The release rate of Mg2+ decreased during days 8-21 compared to the first 7 days, whereas the release of Sr2+ gradually slowed over the first 20 days, and Ag+ release continuously increased throughout the first 21 days. These release trends can be reasonably described by the corrosion kinetic equation presented in Fig. 4. It should be pointed out that this equation is only applicable to fitting the corrosion behavior in the short term, the longer-term kinetic behavior remains to be investigated.

Typically, Mg alloys possess a native MgO film in air. Upon contact with m-SBF, this MgO layer reacts with H2O to form a product layer containing Mg(OH)2 and MgO. Cross-sectional EDS characterization of the bilayer membrane was performed after 7 and 14 days of immersion (Fig. S1a and b). The corrosion products deposited on the bilayer membrane consisted primarily of Mg, Sr, P, O, and Ca. The thickness of the corrosion layer increased with immersion time, and the accumulation of P and Ca elements at the interface was particularly significant, suggesting substantial deposition of oxides and hydroxyapatite. Furthermore, no significant gaps were observed at the bilayer membrane interface with prolonged immersion. The thickness of the corrosion product film on the Mg-Sr side remained almost unchanged, with no obvious cracks detected. In contrast, the corrosion product film on the Mg-Ag side increased in thickness from 13.0 μm to 21.4 μm and developed more pronounced cracks.

The stress generated during the practical application of the bilayer membrane is a key factor influencing its corrosion behavior. As GBR membranes often need to be bent clinically to conform to various bone defect morphologies, stress concentration occurs in the deformed regions. The morphology of pure Mg and the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane (Fig. S1c) after U-bend treatment was examined. Cracks formed on the pure Mg sample perpendicular to the bending stress direction. These surface cracks gradually expanded with immersion time, forming large corrosion pits. In contrast, the surface of the bilayer GBR membrane remained nearly crack-free, demonstrating its superior resistance to deformation and structural stability for practical applications.

Osteogenic performance of the bilayer membrane

Given that Mg-based GBR membranes directly contact bone tissues, their cytocompatibility and osteogenic potential were evaluated using MC3T3-E1 preosteoblasts as the in vitro model (Fig. S2a). As shown in Fig. S2b, cell proliferation in the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag group remained comparable to the control over 5 days, indicating favorable cytocompatibility. Live/dead staining at day 3 further confirmed high cell viability, with predominantly green fluorescence observed across all groups (Fig. S2c). Consistently, cytoskeletal (phalloidin) and nuclear (DAPI) staining revealed well-spread morphology and pronounced filopodial extensions, indicating favorable cell adhesion (Fig. S2d).

Osteogenic differentiation was further investigated using early and late markers. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining and quantitative assays at days 7 and 14 demonstrated significantly enhanced early osteogenic differentiation in the Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag groups compared with Mg and Mg-Ag (Fig. 5a and b). At the mineralization stage, Alizarin Red S (ARS) staining revealed substantially increased calcium deposition in Sr-containing groups after 21 days (Fig. 5c). Immunofluorescence staining of runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) at day 7 and osteocalcin (OCN) at day 14, representing early and late osteogenic markers, revealed higher expression in all experimental groups relative to the control, with the strongest enhancement in Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag (Fig. 5d–g). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) at day 14 was used to quantify the expression of ALP, Runx2, collagen type I (COL-1), and OCN (Fig. 5h–k). All Mg-containing groups significantly upregulated these osteogenesis-related genes, with expression in Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag markedly exceeding that of pure Mg. Taken together, these results demonstrated that Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag membranes more effectively promote osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, which may be attributed to the introduction of Sr2+ and its facilitation of osteogenesis-related signaling pathways. These findings underscore the potential of Sr-containing Mg membranes for bone tissue engineering applications.

Cell migration capacity of the bilayer membrane

GBR membranes serve to prevent gingival soft-tissue overgrowth and protect the underlying regenerating bone. Inadequate primary closure or wound dehiscence may lead to membrane exposure and clinical complications. The barrier function and soft-tissue compatibility of the membranes were evaluated using L929 fibroblasts. Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) results showed slightly higher cell proliferation in all Mg-based groups compared with the control (Fig. S3b). Live/dead staining and phalloidin staining further confirmed high cell viability and extensive cytoskeletal spreading across all experimental groups (Fig. S3c and d).

Cell migration behavior was investigated using scratch wound and Transwell assays. L929 cells cultured in Ag-containing extracts demonstrated faster wound closure compared to the control, suggesting enhanced migratory capacity (Fig. 5l and m). To simulate early degradation conditions, pre-corroded membranes were introduced into the Transwell system [32]. Minimal cell migration to the lower chamber was observed across all groups, indicating that the membranes effectively restricted soft-tissue translocation without impairing overall cell viability or motility (Fig. S4a–c). These results demonstrated that the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane supported fibroblast migration, which may contribute to accelerated soft-tissue wound healing. Simultaneously, the membrane exhibited effective barrier function, reducing the risk of gingival tissue penetration and membrane exposure during early healing.

Mechanistic analysis of selective cell proliferation

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulated cellular responses, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed using extracts from the corresponding monolithic alloys. Specifically, MC3T3-E1 cells were cultured in Mg-Sr or pure Mg extracts, and L929 fibroblasts were cultured in Mg-Ag or pure Mg extracts, with pure Mg serving as the reference control. In MC3T3-E1 cells, 329 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between the Mg-Sr and Mg groups, including 176 up-regulated and 153 down-regulated genes (Fig. 6a). The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were mainly involved in Wnt signaling, cell adhesion molecules and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Fig. 6b). Consistently, gene ontology (GO) enrichment highlighted biological processes closely associated with osteogenesis and mineralization, including mesenchymal cell differentiation and calcium-ion binding (Fig. S5a). Several genes involved in Wnt signaling-associated osteogenic regulation, including Tnfrsf19, were significantly up-regulated, whereas the ERK/TGF-β pathway inhibitors Dusp6 and Skil were significantly down-regulated in the Mg-Sr group (Fig. 6c). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further suggested a significant positive enrichment of the Wnt signaling pathway in Mg-Sr versus Mg (NES =1.5, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6d). These data indicated that the Mg-Sr inner surface activated an osteogenic transcriptional programme centred on Wnt signaling and, by enhancing the expression of genes involved in cell adhesion, matrix remodeling and angiogenesis, creating a favorable microenvironment for osteoblast adhesion, migration and differentiation.

To elucidate the biological role of the Mg-Ag outer layer during the early stage of wound healing, RNA-seq analysis was performed on L929 fibroblasts collected on day 3. As shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 6e), a total of 634 DEGs were identified in the Mg-Ag group compared to the Mg group, including 406 upregulated and 228 downregulated genes. GO enrichment analysis suggested that the DEGs were primarily enriched in terms such as extracellular region, cell adhesion, cell migration, and cytokine-mediated signaling pathway (Fig. 6f), indicating that the Mg-Ag outer layer significantly modulated key biological processes associated with ECM remodeling, cellular adhesion, and motility. Consistent with this, the heatmap of DEGs showed notable upregulation of genes closely related to ECM remodeling and cytoskeletal regulation in the Mg-Ag group, including Ccn1 and Plat (involved in matrix remodeling), Cdc42ep3 (implicated in cytoskeletal organization), as well as Il33 and Cryab, which are known to play critical roles in tissue repair (Fig. 6g). The upregulation of these genes collectively points to enhanced matrix remodeling and cell migration dynamics. In addition, GSEA also associated with this trend, showing significant positive enrichment of the pathway regulation of cell adhesion mediated by integrin (GO:0033628) in the Mg-Ag group (NES = 1.52, p < 0.05) (Fig. 6h). Meanwhile, KEGG pathway analysis further showed the activation of ECM-related and migration-associated signaling pathways (Fig. S5b). The enriched pathways included ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, regulation of actin cytoskeleton, and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, all of which were critically involved in fibroblast remodeling, cytoskeletal reorganization, and lamellipodia extension-processes essential for early tissue repair. In summary, RNA-seq results demonstrate that the Mg-Ag outer layer significantly modulates ECM-related gene expression, activates integrin-mediated adhesion signaling, and enhances cytoskeletal remodeling during the early phase of wound healing. These findings suggest that the Mg-Ag outer layer promotes fibroblast migration-related responses and may facilitate early tissue repair.

Antibacterial performance of the bilayer membrane

To systematically assess the antibacterial performance of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane, oral pathogen-Porphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis), Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli) were used as model strains (Fig. 7a). Qualitative and quantitative assays were performed using 24 h material extracts prepared in BHI or LB medium. Colony-forming unit (CFU) counts on agar plates revealed a substantial reduction in bacterial viability across all experimental groups compared to the control (Fig. 7b and S6a). The Ag-containing groups exhibited the strongest inhibition, with approximately 90% decreases in colony numbers. Specifically, the antibacterial rates against P. gingivalis reached 89.85 ± 3.96% for Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag, while that against S. aureus was 91.75 ± 0.82%, respectively (p < 0.001), indicating excellent antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 7e and S6b). The Mg-Sr group exhibited moderate antibacterial activity, outperforming pure Mg but remaining less effective than the Ag-containing groups. Complementary live/dead staining showed a similar trend (Fig. 7d and S6c). Biofilm formation on material surfaces was assessed by crystal violet staining. Dense biofilms formed on control and pure Mg surfaces, whereas biofilm accumulation was visibly reduced in the Mg-Sr group and nearly absent in the Ag-containing groups (Fig. 7f and S6d). Quantification at 590 nm (Fig. 7g and S6e) corroborated these findings: absorbance values in the Ag-containing groups were significantly lower than in the other groups, consistent with Ag+-mediated membrane disruption. To further evaluate the surface-contact antibacterial performance of the membrane, bacteria were directly cultured on the material surfaces. SEM observation and bacterial viability analysis revealed that the Mg-Ag surface significantly reduced bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation compared with Mg-Sr surfaces, with evident disruption of bacterial membranes (Fig. 7c and S6h). Intracellular ROS levels were evaluated by fluorescence staining to assess oxidative stress (Fig. 7h and S6f). Semi-quantitative analysis demonstrated significantly higher ROS-associated fluorescence in the Ag-containing groups compared with the control. For P. gingivalis, ROS fluorescence intensities reached 38.74 ± 5.12 a.u. and 31.51 ± 2.21 a.u. in the Mg-Ag and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag groups, respectively, while only negligible fluorescence was detected in the control group (0.80 ± 0.12 a.u.; p < 0.001). A similar trend was observed for S. aureus and E. coli (Fig. 7i and S6g). These results indicate that Ag incorporation markedly promotes intracellular ROS generation in bacteria. Taken together, the Mg-Ag and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag membranes exhibited broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria through dual mechanisms involving membrane disruption and oxidative stress induction.

In vivo osteogenesis and degradation behavior of the bilayer membrane

A rat mandibular defect model was used to evaluate the in vivo bone regeneration capacity of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer GBR membrane (Fig. 8a). Three-dimensional micro-CT reconstructions and quantitative morphometry were obtained at weeks 4 and 8 to assess new bone formation (Fig. 8b). At week 4, compared with the immediate (0 week) defect, limited bone ingrowth from the defect margins was observed in the control and Mg groups. In contrast, both the Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag groups exhibited more pronounced bone formation extending from the margins toward the defect center. By week 8, newly formed bone further coalesced from the defect margins to the center, and the residual defect area was markedly smaller in the Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag groups. Quantitative morphometric analysis further confirmed these observations: at week 8, bone volume fraction (BV/TV) reached 60.95 ± 4.17% in the Mg-Sr group and 57.53 ± 4.82% in the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag group (Fig. 8c). Trabecular thickness (Tb.Th) and bone mineral density (BMD) showed a similar trend (Fig. 8d and e). Histological evaluation using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson's trichrome staining showed soft-tissue ingrowth into the defect area in the control group. In contrast, the Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag groups exhibited larger areas of newly formed bone and higher tissue density than the control and Mg groups at both time points (Fig. 8f and g). To further probe osteogenic differentiation during healing, ALP immunofluorescence staining was performed. The Sr-containing groups exhibited significantly more positive cells than the other three groups after 8 weeks, indicating that Sr2+ effectively enhanced osteogenic differentiation and promoted bone regeneration (Fig. 8h). Regarding biosafety, no obvious histopathological changes were detected in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, or kidneys of the experimental groups (Fig. S7). Taken together, the Mg-Sr and Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag membranes demonstrated superior osteogenesis-promoting effects in vivo.

Discussion

Mechanical strengthening mechanisms of the bilayer membrane

The microstructure, including grain size, texture intensity, and residual dislocations, serves as a critical factor determining the properties of Mg alloys [[33], [34], [35]]. In this study, grain refinement and the heterogeneous structure are key contributors to the significant enhancement in the mechanical performance of the bilayer membrane. For Mg alloys, classical slip and twinning dominate the plastic deformation in coarse-grained microstructures. In contrast, the hot-rolled bilayer membrane possesses a refined grain size (<5 μm), shifting the plastic deformation mechanism from dislocation slip and twinning to grain boundary sliding, which substantially improves ductility. On the other hand, the reduction in grain size increases the number of grain boundaries, enhancing resistance to dislocation slip and further contributing to improved plasticity [36].

To elucidate the tensile strengthening mechanisms of the bilayer membrane, the contributions of various strengthening mechanisms to the overall strength were compared based on Formulas S(1) - S(7) in the Supplementary Information. The interfacial shear stress (σobstacels) and back stress (σHDI) of the bilayer membrane, as measured and shown in Fig. S9, were determined to be 76.8 MPa and 69 MPa, respectively. Table S3 lists the required parameters for the calculations. Based on the aforementioned formulas, the contributions of various strengthening mechanisms to the tensile strength were computed. According to these calculations, the ultimate tensile strength of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane is derived as 245.81 MPa. The calculated strength values exhibit a slight discrepancy compared to the actual mechanical property test results. This deviation is primarily attributed to inherent measurement errors in determining the misorientation angle, as well as the fact that the volumetric ratio of the two constituent alloys forming the bilayer membrane is not perfectly equal.

The Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane developed in this study exhibited the optimal three-point bending performance. The underlying mechanism can be interpreted based on the fundamental principles of texture and deformation mechanisms. In Mg alloys, two primary deformation mechanisms contribute to strain accommodation at room temperature. The first is {10-12} extension twinning, which reorients parts of the grain structure and facilitates further deformation. The second is basal <a> slip on the (0001) plane, which is the most easily activated slip system in Mg [37].

The bilayer membrane developed in this study exhibited the optimal three-point bending performance among all tested groups. This superior bendability can be attributed to two primary factors, which are closely related to its unique bilayer architecture and the compositional effect of Ag.

The bilayer configuration inherently introduces a mechanical heterogeneity that is particularly advantageous for resisting bending deformation. As illustrated in the schematic of the observation planes (Fig. 10a), the inner Mg-Sr layer and the outer Mg-Ag layer experience different stress states during bending: the inner layer is subjected to compressive stress, while the outer layer bears tensile stress. This deformation mismatch leads to a shift of the neutral axis toward the tensile side [38] (i.e., the Mg-Ag layer), as schematically shown in Fig. 10b. Consequently, the actual tensile strain experienced by the outer layer is effectively reduced, which delays the initiation and propagation of cracks on the tensile surface—the most vulnerable region during bending. This protective effect is particularly beneficial for the Mg-Ag layer, which, despite its crucial antibacterial function, is intrinsically more susceptible to localized corrosion and mechanical failure. Furthermore, on the compressive side (Mg-Sr layer), the stress state favors the activation of {10-12} extension twinning, which helps accommodate strain and further contributes to the overall bendability of the membrane.

Microstructure of the bilayer membrane after bending. (a) Schematic diagram showing the OM and EBSD observation planes of the bilayer membrane; (b) KAM map; (c) and (g) EBSD maps; (d) and (h) Slip system activation; (e) and (i) Misorientation angle distributions; (f) and (j) Pole figures.

In the present study, bending deformation promoted the activation of prismatic ⟨a⟩ slip [39], as evidenced by the slip system analysis shown in Fig. 10d and h. The activation of this non-basal slip mode reduces the stacking fault energy of the Mg matrix, facilitates the formation of extended dislocations, and promotes dislocation cross-slip. These combined effects enhance the plasticity and strain hardening capability of the material, allowing the bilayer membrane to accommodate larger bending deformation without premature fracture. In contrast, in the outer Mg-Ag layer, where tensile stress is dominant, the formation of extension twins is limited, but the improved ductility from Ag addition still contributes to resisting crack growth.

The excellent bending performance of the bilayer membrane arises from the synergistic effect of its bilayer-induced mechanical heterogeneity and the Ag-enhanced ductility. The microstructural evidence from EBSD and pole figure analyses (Fig. 10c–j) further supports these interpretations, showing texture evolution and twinning characteristics consistent with the proposed mechanisms.

Corrosion mechanism of the bilayer membrane

The key advantage of the present bilayer membrane lies in the coupling of metallurgical interfacial stability with degradation regulation under mechanically demanding GBR conditions [40]. Owing to its low standard potential of −2.37 V, the Mg matrix is highly susceptible to corrosion in aqueous environments, as described by the following reactions [41]:

The dissolution of the Mg matrix releases Mg2+, which combines with OH− to form a Mg(OH)2 film [42,43]. The solubility product (Ksp) of Mg(OH)2 is 5.61 × 10−12, rendering it virtually insoluble in water [44]. HA has a very low solubility product constant (7.36 × 10−60 [45]) in m-SBF solution. It deposits on the surface of the Mg substrate, providing corrosion protection. Consequently, the corrosion resistance of the alloy increases during the initial immersion stage due to the buildup of the product layer.

As described in Section 2.2, the Mg-Ag layer exhibits an Ecorr of −1.55 V, while the Mg-Sr layer has an Ecorr of −1.73 V. During the early stage of immersion, a certain potential difference exists between the two sides of the bilayer membrane. The Mg-Ag side acts as the cathode, shifting the corrosion potential to a more positive value and causing anodic polarization of the Mg-Sr side. The Mg-Ag side generates a significant amount of OH−, leading to the preferential deposition of corrosion products on this side, thereby protecting the Mg-Ag layer. This is consistent with Fig. S1, which shows that the corrosion product film is thicker on the Mg-Ag side during the early stage of corrosion.

During long-term immersion, the breakdown of the corrosion product layer exposes more Mg matrix to the m-SBF solution, thereby reducing the alloy's corrosion resistance. Mg(OH)2 can form hydrates by bonding with H2O molecules. During dehydration of these hydrates, cracks can form and MgO is generated. As shown in Fig. 9b, an accumulation of oxygen elements was observed at the bonding interface. Research by Shen et al. [46,47] indicated that a larger grain size in the α-Mg matrix corresponds to a lower adsorption energy of the Mg(OH)2 product layer, suggesting that the corrosion product layer becomes prone to spalling and exhibits lower compactness. Furthermore, a smaller grain size enhances resistance to corrosive ions such as Cl−. According to Fig. 1b, the Mg-Ag side has the largest grain size, while the bonding region has the smallest grain size, resulting in the slowest corrosion rate at the interface during the initial corrosion stage. The long-term corrosion mechanism is illustrated Fig. S10 schematically. After extended immersion, the most severe corrosion occurs on the Mg-Ag side compared to the Mg-Sr side, and cracks initially propagate from the Mg-Ag side toward the bonding interface. Consequently, following long-term corrosion, cracks extend from the susceptible Mg-Ag side to the bonding region, eventually causing gaps at the internal material interface. This observation aligns with the findings from the material examined after 4 weeks of in vivo implantation.

Notably, it is worth noting that the coexistence of Sr and Ag in the Mg-based membrane raises a legitimate concern regarding possible galvanic corrosion, as both elements can form cathodic intermetallic phases relative to the Mg matrix. In the present study [26], Sr and Ag were introduced as minor alloying additions, resulting in fine and uniformly dispersed second-phase particles rather than macroscopic structural heterogeneities. Such microstructural refinement is generally associated with more uniform corrosion behavior: the presence of numerous fine cathodic sites promotes a homogeneous distribution of micro-galvanic effects, thereby reducing the risk of severe localized attack. Moreover, previous studies have reported that both Ag and Sr can facilitate the formation of a more protective surface film during degradation, which may partially offset the additional cathodic driving force. Consistent with this, the bilayer membrane in our study exhibited an intermediate corrosion rate between those of Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag during immersion testing.

Integrated biofunctional design and translational relevance of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane

Beyond structural stability, the central feature of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane developed in this study lies in its integrated biofunctional design for the two distinct tissue interfaces involved in GBR. Conventional homogeneous magnesium alloy membranes generally release all alloying elements into the same local microenvironment, which may limit tissue interface-specific regulation and increase the potential for functional interference between different bioactive components. In contrast, the bilayer membrane developed in this study spatially allocates different functional components to opposite sides of the membrane: the Mg-Sr side is designed to face the bone defect to support osteogenesis, whereas the Mg-Ag side is designed to face the soft tissue to provide antibacterial activity and promote soft tissue healing. Therefore, the Janus-like characteristic described in this study should primarily be understood as a bilayer structural design with side-associated biological functions, rather than as a completely unidirectional or fully isolated ion delivery system.

On the bone-facing side, Mg2+ and Sr2+ jointly regulate the osteogenic microenvironment. Mg2+ provides a baseline osteogenic stimulus and helps shift the inflammatory microenvironment toward a pro-regenerative state [48]. Sr2+ further enhances osteoblastic differentiation and bone-matrix deposition [49]. Extensive evidence indicates that Sr2+ promotes osteoblast differentiation by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling and suppressed osteoclastogenesis by downregulating receptor activator of NF-κB ligand and upregulating osteoprotegerin, thereby helping maintain bone metabolic homeostasis [23]. The transcriptomic data are consistent with this mechanism, showing significant enrichment of Wnt-related pathways and upregulation of genes involved in extracellular matrix remodeling and angiogenic bone coupling (Fig. 6b–d). These molecular events collectively drive the transition of osteoblast precursors from a proliferative state toward matrix maturation and mineralization, which is consistent with the enhanced ALP, Runx2, OCN, and in vivo BV/TV results. On the soft tissue side, an early antibacterial barrier is critical for maintaining a permissive healing milieu. Although Mg degradation products exhibit some antibacterial effects, they can be readily buffered in the complex oral microenvironment and show limited activity against mature biofilms [50]. Accordingly, Ag+ is incorporated into the outer layer to confer broad-spectrum and durable antibacterial activity. Prior studies have shown that silver ions effectively eradicate a range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria [51]. To mitigate the potential cytotoxicity of silver ions, an alloy with a 0.1 wt% silver content was adopted to strike a balance between antibacterial performance and biocompatibility. Beyond infection control, the outer layer actively contributes to early soft-tissue healing. Magnesium ions may also contribute to soft tissue healing by modulating human gingival fibroblast behavior through activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, which promotes fibroblast adhesion and migration in a dose-dependent manner [52]. Moreover, the anti-inflammatory and pro-healing functions of silver-based biomaterials have been documented. For instance, Ag loaded collagen scaffolds promote fibroblast migration and wound closure in vitro, further supporting the soft-tissue reparative effects observed here [25]. Transcriptomic analysis, using pure Mg as a control, indicated that ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling were significantly activated in L929 fibroblasts under Ag+ exposure, consistent with a pro-repair phenotype characterized by lower inflammation, enhanced migration, and active remodeling. These findings suggest that Ag+, beyond its antibacterial role, may act integratively with Mg2+ to support early soft-tissue integration.

Despite these encouraging findings, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the transcriptomic analysis and part of the in vitro experiments were performed using extracts from the individual Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag alloys. These experiments were primarily designed to elucidate the biological contributions of Sr and Ag-containing alloy signals, respectively, and therefore cannot fully represent the complex biological behavior of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane under in vivo degradation conditions. Following implantation, the biological performance of the bilayer membrane is likely influenced by multiple factors, including degradation kinetics, ion diffusion, interfacial stability, tissue fluid exchange, and local cellular responses. Second, direct in vivo comparisons with clinically established GBR membranes, such as collagen membranes, titanium meshes, and commercially available magnesium membranes, were not performed. As summarized in Table S4, each of these materials possesses distinct advantages and limitations. In comparison, the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane developed in this study demonstrated stable early mechanical support, a relatively controlled degradation rate, and integrated osteogenic as well as antibacterial/soft-tissue reparative functions. Nevertheless, its long-term translational advantages require further validation. Finally, future studies should further validate the proposed molecular mechanisms. In addition, bilateral ion-release characterization, multispecies oral biofilm models, and large-animal alveolar bone defect models will be necessary to more comprehensively evaluate the long-term biological performance and translational potential of the Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study presents a metallurgically bonded Mg-Sr/Mg-Ag bilayer membrane as a Janus platform for guided bone regeneration. The bilayer architecture simultaneously addresses the key clinical requirements of mechanical support, controlled biodegradation, infection prevention, and tissue regeneration. The robust alloy-based interface ensures excellent structural integrity and deformation tolerance. Despite the incorporation of Ag, the bilayer membrane maintains controlled degradation behavior and excellent volume stability, ensuring sustained barrier function during the healing process. The partitioned design is intended to enable differentiated biological regulation at the bone-soft tissue interface: the Mg2+/Sr2+-releasing inner layer promotes osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization, whereas the Ag+-containing outer layer provides effective antibacterial protection and supports early soft-tissue integration. More importantly, this work establishes a rational structure-function integration strategy for multifunctional biodegradable metallic membranes, offering a broadly translatable design paradigm for next-generation GBR systems and other interface-regenerative biomaterials for complex bone defect repair.

Materials and methods

Material preparation: Pure magnesium (99.9 wt%), pure silver (99.99 wt%), and an Mg-20Sr (wt.%) master alloy was used in this study. The materials were melted in a stainless steel crucible at 740 °C under a protective atmosphere of CO2 and SF6 (99:1 ratio). The resulting magnesium alloy ingots were processed into billets with a diameter of 80 mm and subsequently hot-extruded at 430 °C using an XJ-500 horizontal extrusion machine to obtain Mg-0.5Sr (wt.%) and Mg-0.1 Ag (wt.%) sheets with a thickness of 1 mm. Prior to rolling, the sheets were polished to remove surface oxide layers. The Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag sheets were stacked and hot-rolled at 150 °C with a speed of 0.05 m/s. A reduction of 50% per pass was applied, and a bilayer membrane with a thickness of 0.5 mm was obtained after 3 passes, which was further rolled to a final thickness of 0.3 mm. Monolithic Mg-Sr and Mg-Ag with a thickness of 0.3 mm were prepared using the same process as controls. The final rolled materials were annealed at 200 °C for 30 min. The detailed rolling schedule is described in Table S5.

Microstructural characterization: Cross-sections of the bilayer membrane were polished using an argon ion polisher (Gatan 697), followed by microstructural characterization of grain size, orientation, and texture via field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, JEOL JSM-7800F) equipped with EDS and EBSD systems. Imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a beam current of 10 μA. Phase identification was carried out using an X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku/Max 3c) and analyzed with MDI Jade 6 software. To investigate interfacial bonding, morphology, and elemental distribution, TEM observations were conducted using an FEI Talos F200× microscope. Due to the compositional difference between the two sides of the composite Mg membrane, TEM specimens were prepared by FIB. A region of 4 × 3 μm2 containing the interface at its center was extracted using an ion beam at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV.

Weight loss test: Magnesium-based membrane samples (10× 10 × 0.3 mm) with an initial mass (m0) were immersed in m-SBF at an immersion ratio of 20 mL: 1 cm2 and a temperature of 37 ± 0.5 °C. The pH of the solution was monitored at various time points over 7 days. After immersion, the samples were removed and ultrasonically cleaned in a chromic acid solution and alcohol to remove corrosion products. The final mass (m1) of the cleaned samples was measured to calculate the corrosion rate. The bilayer membrane was immersed in SBF at 37 ± 0.5 °C and subjected to continuous horizontal shaking at 100 rpm using a thermostatic water bath vibrator (Guowang Instrument, SHA-CA) to simulate dynamic corrosion conditions.

Electrochemical experiment: Electrochemical experiments were conducted to measure the open-circuit potential (OCP), EIS, and polarization curves of the alloys. A conventional three-electrode system was adopted, where the working electrode was the magnesium alloy specimen, the reference electrode was a saturated calomel electrode (SCE), the auxiliary electrode was a platinum sheet electrode, and the test electrolyte was the m-SBF solution. For the EIS test, the frequency range was set from 105 to 10−2 Hz, with an alternating current (AC) amplitude of 5 mV. For the potentiodynamic polarization curve test, the potential range was from −0.3 V to 0.5 V relative to the open-circuit potential, and the scanning rate was 0.5 mV/s.

Micro-area electrochemical experiment: Micro-area electrochemical experiments were used to characterize the second phase in the composite sheet, the corrosion behavior between the two alloys, and the effect of the interface on corrosion. An Ametek-Princeton VersaSCAN electrochemical scanning system was employed to measure the SVET on the sample surface after 24 h of immersion. The platinum-iridium microprobe had a tip diameter of 5 μm, and the distance between the probe tip and the sample surface was approximately 100 μm. The amplitude and vibration frequency of the microprobe were 30 μm and 80 Hz, respectively. The LEIS was conducted using a three-electrode system, and the impedance values of different regions were measured at fixed frequencies of 100 Hz and 1 kHz above the sample. During the scanning process, the step size was controlled at 67 × 67 lines, and the scanning area was 200 × 200 μm2.

Mechanical properties: The experimental equipment used for the tensile specimens was an MTS EDT503A universal testing machine. The gauge length of the tensile specimens was 25 mm, and the cross-section was 6 mm × 0.3 mm. The tensile rate was set to 1 mm/min, and three parallel specimens were tested for each alloy. The three-point bending test was selected to quantitatively evaluate the flexural stiffness and resistance to deformation of the membranes under controlled loading conditions. These parameters are directly related to the ability of GBR membranes to resist soft tissue compression and maintain the regenerative space after implantation. The test was performed using an MTS EDT503A universal testing machine (China). In accordance with the ASTM G30-97 standard, and considering the stress state of the barrier membrane in vivo, the bending test was conducted on the bending specimens with a size of 60 × 36 mm. The pressing rate was set to 0.5 mm/min, the span was 22 mm, and the diameter of the bending indenter was 10 mm. Three parallel specimens were measured for each alloy composition to avoid errors.

Cell culture and biocompatibility: The mouse pre-osteoblast cell line MC3T3-E1 (National Certified Cell Resource Center, China) was cultured in α-modified minimum essential medium (α-MEM, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin-mphotericin B solution (Gibco, USA). Animal experiments: To evaluate osteogenic repair in a rat mandibular defect, all procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: XM2025102201).
