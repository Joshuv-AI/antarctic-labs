Deep learning models for the detection of dental-findings and tooth-types using video data
Authors: Julia Michelin, Ayesha Nooruddin, Aamna Khalid, Syed Muhammad Faizan Ahmed, Brett King, Muhammad Muthar Shaikh, Antonio Joaquim Terras Neto, Fahad Umer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral diseases have an estimated incidence of 35.5% worldwide, yet access to dental care is often hindered by a shortage of dental professionals and limited diagnostic resources. While Artificial Intelligence (AI) has shown promise in dental diagnostics, most existing models rely on high-quality static images captured in controlled clinical settings. This study aimed to develop and validate Deep Learning (DL) models capable of detecting dental findings and identifying tooth types using video data from a handheld, toothbrush-mounted intraoral device.MethodsIn this validation study, 708 videos were captured using an electronic toothbrush integrated with a miniature intraoral camera. A custom python script extracted 16,552 image frames, which were filtered to 7,963 eligible frames for the dental-findings model and 3,799 frames for the tooth-type model. Two dental experts annotated findings, including decay, fillings, plaque, and staining as well as tooth types using bounding boxes. The YOLOv8s architecture was employed for both the models.ResultsThe dental-findings model achieved an overall Mean Average Precision (mAP) of 83.0% and an F1-score of 77.9%. It performed best in detecting amalgam fillings (94.5% mAP) and decay (84.2% mAP). The tooth-type model demonstrated an overall mAP of 88.6% and F-1 score of 86.2%. The highest mAP was observed for molars (97.7%) and premolars (96.7%). Despite the lack of a standardized video capture sequence, the models maintained strong performance across diverse visual variations.ConclusionThis study establishes a proof of concept for embedding AI diagnostics into everyday oral care devices. By accurately detecting clinical findings from user-captured video, this technology has the potential to facilitate remote monitoring, support early intervention, and optimize healthcare resource allocation, particularly in underserved regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08861-y.

Introduction

Oral diseases are major contributors to the global burden of disease with an estimated incidence of 35.5% worldwide [1, 2]. For the population aged 60 or older, the statistics are even more harrowing with 23% of the global population suffering from edentulism showing the inevitable end game of poor prevention regimen [3]. These conditions not just compromise oral function and aesthetics but also have systemic implications thereby affecting overall well-being, quality of life, and economic productivity [4]. Their high prevalence arises from factors such as limited awareness, resource constraints, unhealthy dietary habits, longer waiting times for dental consultations and the list is far from exhaustive [5]. The situation is further aggravated by a global shortage of dental professionals, with only about 3.3 dentists per 10,000 people, insufficient to meet population needs [6].

To help address these challenges, the integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) into dental diagnostics has emerged as a transformative approach, enabling the development of tele-dentistry systems that can enhance access to dental care [7]. Studies based on AI-driven frameworks, particularly those based on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), have shown remarkable ability to identify and classify diverse dental findings from intraoral data [8, 9]. These systems can detect clinically relevant features such as plaque, gingival inflammation, fractured restorations, discoloration, and calculus deposits, offering a more comprehensive view of oral health [10].

However, most existing studies rely on high-quality images captured under controlled clinical settings using professional-grade DSLR cameras or premium smartphones [11, 12]. Such datasets fail to represent the variability inherent in real-world imaging environments and therefore do not accurately assess model performance on data acquired under routine, non-clinical conditions [13]. Furthermore, the majority of existing literature has focused on static clinical images or radiographs, with limited exploration of video-based intraoral data [14]. Despite its significant potential to capture dynamic views, improved field coverage, and reduced misdiagnosis arising from angle or lighting variations this area remains largely unexplored.

To overcome these limitations, the use of handheld intraoral devices presents a practical and scalable solution [15]. Devices commonly used in daily oral care, such as powered toothbrushes integrated with intraoral cameras, allow individuals to conveniently capture videos of their teeth without clinical assistance. When paired with AI-based diagnostic tools, these devices have the potential to enable real-time, continuous monitoring of oral health, complement routine dental visits, and facilitate timely interventions [16]. Unlike prior studies that primarily evaluated clinician-operated handheld devices predominantly on images, the present study aims to develop and validate Deep Learning (DL) models that can detect multiple dental findings and identify different tooth-types trained on videos captured using a toothbrush-mounted intraoral device designed for independent use by both patients and clinicians [17]. These models are referred to as the dental-findings and tooth-type model in the manuscript hereafter.

Methods

Study setting

This multicenter study was conducted in collaboration with several international partner institutions. These include LumaBrush Medical Equipment Trading, based in Abu Dhabi (United Arab Emirates) which served as the primary technology and AI development partner; MeDenTec (medentec.com) based in Pakistan, contributed to data annotation, AI model development, and validation processes [18]; Centro Universitário Sudoeste Paulista (UniFSP) in São Paulo, Brazil, which contributed to clinical expertise and access to intraoral video data; and Instituição Chaddad de Ensino Ltda; also located in Brazil, which functioned as an affiliated academic and clinical collaborator; The Aga Khan University Hospital (AKUH) from Pakistan also contributed to project oversight.

The intraoral videos used in the study were provided by the respective instituition, UniFSP, and were collected through university and community-based oral health screening programs. All videos were fully de-identified prior to analysis, and no personally identifiable information was included in the dataset. Ethical approval was obtained for the study by the Ethical Review Committee (ERC) of AKUH under ERC # 2026-12346-39436 and an exemption was granted. The ERC also waived the requirement for informed consent due to the retrospective use of fully anonymized data with no direct patient identifiers and no additional participant interaction. Additionally, all study procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [19].

Study design & sample size

As this was a validation study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The sample size was increased pragmatically until model convergence was achieved. This study is reported in adherence to the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies Involving Artificial Intelligence (STARD-AI) guidelines [20].

Device design

Prior to data collection, a prototype toothbrush was developed [17]. It had an interchangeable head that allows the user to attach either a provided electronic automated toothbrush or a miniature intraoral camera with a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels. It incorporates a Ultraviolet (UV) light-emitting lamp as the excitation source and a receiver system for image capture. The camera head measures approximately 1 cm in width, allowing easy intraoral maneuvering and enabling image and video capture from multiple angles, including posterior regions that are typically difficult to visualize. A dedicated mobile application was also developed to support video recording and data transfer. The device connects to the smartphone through its inbuilt Wi-Fi system, which enables it to record videos while utilizing the mobile application’s interface.

Data collection, conversion and filtration

The intraoral video recordings were acquired by third and final year dental students under the supervision of licensed dentists. A total of 851 videos captured using the handheld device were stored in MOV or MP4 format. The anonymized data were then shared with the research team via a secure Google Drive link, accessible only to the Principal Investigator and authorized annotators. After the removal of duplicate recordings with similar file names, 708 unique videos remained. This step was performed to prevent data redundancy and avoid potential data leakage between training and testing subsets. These unique videos underwent preprocessing before being used. Each video was converted into image frames using a custom Python script (https://github.com/MeDenTec/Video-Frames-Extraction, medentec.com), resulting in 16,552 images as shown in (Fig. 1).

Image frames that were blurred, poorly illuminated, inadequate visualization of dental structures, or obscured by saliva, blood, or debris were excluded by two general dentists (A.N and A.K). Following this filtration process, a total of 7,963 eligible image frames displaying frontal, occlusal, buccal, lingual, or labial views were retained, while 8,589 frames were excluded. These 7,963 frames were then utilized for training the dental-findings model. This criterion was applied to ensure that the retained images contained clinically interpretable information suitable for reliable annotation.

For the tooth-type model, the same set of 7,963 image frames was reviewed and only those showing at least three teeth per image were included. After this filtration, a total of 3799 eligible image frames were retained, while 4164 frames were excluded. This criterion was necessary because isolated or partially visible teeth might limit the model’s ability to learn contextual morphological relationships required for accurate tooth-type classification.

Data annotation

All included image frames were annotated by two calibrated dental experts with a minimum of two years of clinical experience (A.N and A.K) in two separate passes. In the first pass, each expert independently annotated dental findings including decay, composite fillings, amalgam fillings, plaque/calculus, staining using bounding boxes through the LabelMe (version 5.4.1) annotation tool [21]. In the second pass, different tooth-types including ‘central incisors’, ‘lateral incisors’, ‘canines’, ‘premolars’, ‘molars’ and ‘wisdom’ were annotated, with a separate ‘missing’ class assigned to absent teeth. In cases of disagreement between the two annotators, a third experienced dentist (F.U) reviewed and finalized the annotations to ensure accuracy and consistency. All finalized annotations were saved in JSON format and subsequently used for model training.

Model training

The YOLOv8s DL architecture was employed to train and validate two separate models on the annotated dataset referred to as “dental-findings model” and the “tooth-type model” within the manuscript. The models were retrained for 300 epochs per iteration using standard hyperparameters, early stopping (patience = 50), and the Adam optimizer on an Nvidia RTX 4090 (15 GB GPU). The dataset was divided into training, validation, and testing subsets in a ratio of 80:10:10. For the dental-findings model, 7,963 image frames were included, of which 6,371 were allocated for training, 796 for validation, and 796 for testing. For the tooth-type model, 3,799 image frames were included, with 3,041 used for training, 379 for validation, and 379 for testing.

Statistical analysis

The models’ diagnostic performance was evaluated on the test set using established metrics, including precision, recall, F1-score, and Mean Average Precision (mAP) at Intersection over Union (IoU) 0.5.

Discussion

In recent years, there has been a noticeable rise in self-awareness about oral health [22]. While over half of the global population brush their teeth atleast twice a day, one-third regularly clean their tongue, reflecting growing recognition of the importance of oral hygiene and preventive care [23]. As individuals have become more proactive about maintaining their oral health, clinicians are also turning to intelligent systems that simplify workflows and support early detection of oral diseases [24]. Several AI-driven diagnostic tools integrated with CNN models have also been developed, such as Diagnocat, Overjet, and Pearl AI [25]. However, for technology to be truly impactful, it must also be accessible [26]. High-performing algorithms lose relevance when their use depends on costly or complex environments [27]. Consequently, while these platforms demonstrate remarkable accuracy, they remain proprietary and are not readily available to clinicians or patients [25].

Following the principle of accessibility, it became essential to integrate the diagnostic capability of AI into a familiar and readily available device. To address this, technologies that are user-friendly, widely accessible, and require minimal technical expertise have been developed [28]. One such innovation is this AI-powered automated toothbrush. Most of the toothbrushes currently available in the market are designed to monitor and improve brushing techniques, provide real-time plaque visualization, and detect early signs of gingival disease by tracking inflammatory markers in saliva [29]. Although research in this area remains limited, a previous study demonstrated the use of a toothbrush-based system for capturing dental selfies for caries detection [16]. Another study explored the use of intraoral video data, however, its application was restricted to tooth-type identification [30]. To the authors’ knowledge, the present study is therefore the first to utilize video data captured via a handheld electronic toothbrush to detect both dental-findings and tooth-types, hence providing a more comprehensive assessment of oral health.

The models developed in this study achieved strong detection performance for both dental-findings and tooth-type identification. The dental-findings model performed particularly well for amalgam restorations and decay, suggesting that the YOLOv8s architecture effectively recognizes well-defined, high-contrast structures [31]. Detection performance for composite fillings was also favorable, which may be attributed in part to the fluorescence-related optical properties of composite materials that differ from natural enamel [32]. The intraoral camera used in this study was equipped with a UV light head, which may have enhanced this effect by improving the visual contrast of restorative materials under UV illumination. Under UV, these materials may appear either brighter, darker, or differently colored compared to the surrounding tooth tissues. This variation creates additional visual contrast, which can assist AI systems in identifying restorative margins. However, the accuracy for composite fillings was slightly lower compared to decay and amalgam restorations, likely due to their close resemblance to the natural tooth color and translucency, which reduces visual contrast against enamel and makes delineation more challenging for the model.

Similarly, the detection of staining remained challenging due to its highly variable appearance, influenced by factors such as diet, oral hygiene, and smoking habits. This variability was compounded by the wide multispectral presentation of stains, for instance, smokers often exhibit dark, tar-like deposits that can be misinterpreted as carious lesions an ambiguity also reported in prior studies [33].

The tooth-type model achieved high accuracy across almost all categories, exceeding 85%. Its strong performance can be attributed to the model’s ability to effectively distinguish teeth from the surrounding oral cavity. Its impressive performance also highlights its capability to robustly differentiate between various tooth types, as distinct morphological features like cusp patterns, crown contours, and occlusal surface anatomy provide visual cues that facilitate accurate classification [30].

Interestingly, the model demonstrated slightly lower accuracy for wisdom teeth (mAP = 87.3%) compared to other molars (mAP = 97.7%). This may be because wisdom teeth are often partially erupted, obscured by soft tissue, or positioned at extreme angles. Such anatomical constraints can reduce the quality of feature extraction and increase localization errors. Moreover, the relatively smaller number of annotated wisdom tooth instances in the dataset (n = 58) may have further contributed to reduced detection accuracy. In this study, wisdom teeth were analyzed separately from the molar class because they hold distinct clinical importance compared to the other molars. Identifying them individually is crucial, as third molars are often associated with conditions such as impaction or pericoronitis, making their detection clinically relevant.

However, a modest drop was seen in the performance for the ‘missing’ class, likely due to the limited number of examples representing edentulous regions in the training dataset [34]. Another contributing factor could be the lack of uniform presentation among these cases. Edentulous spaces exhibit considerable variability with some displaying narrow interdental gaps, while others show wide alveolar ridge exposure. Additionally, adjacent teeth may drift or tilt into the vacant space, altering its geometry and confusing the model’s bounding box localization. This heterogeneity makes it challenging for the model to learn a consistent visual representation for the ‘missing’ class, leading to reduced detection accuracy.

Additionally, in a prior study, the data collection process was standardized, with video recording consistently beginning from the right maxillary third molar, covering the maxillary arch, and then proceeding to the mandibular arch [30]. In that study, model performance declined significantly and produced inaccurate predictions when the starting point or orientation of the video differed from the pattern used during training [30]. In contrast, the dataset utilized in this study intentionally lacked a standardized capture sequence. Videos were captured according to clinical convenience, resulting in variability in imaging sequence, angulation, and field of view. This approach was adopted to better mimic real-world conditions and improve the robustness of the AI model to diverse acquisition patterns. Despite this variability, the model maintained strong performance, suggesting adaptability to non-standardized intraoral video acquisition.

If AI models with such strong performance are integrated into everyday devices like toothbrushes, they could bring substantial benefits to the healthcare sector [15]. These systems could enable remote patient assessment and assist clinicians in triaging urgent cases while also supporting chairside education [15]. Moreover, Community Health Workers (CHWs) in remote or underserved regions could be easily trained to operate such devices, allowing the AI system to act as a force multiplier. Instead of requiring a dentist to be present for every screening, the AI could perform preliminary assessments, enabling dental professionals to focus their expertise on high-risk cases identified by the system [35]. This approach could optimize healthcare resource allocation and provide a temporary but practical solution to the global shortage of dentists [36].

For patients, such systems can transform oral hygiene into an interactive, technology-driven experience [36]. They can facilitate self-monitoring of oral health and encourage users to seek timely professional advice when abnormalities are detected [36]. Viewing one’s own oral condition reinforces behavioral awareness, motivating preventive action at earlier stages [37]. Additionally, by reducing the need for in-person consultations, these systems may help minimize waiting times and alleviate patient anxiety [38].

Despite these potential advantages, translation of AI-enabled systems into real-world clinical deployment would require careful consideration of regulatory and legal frameworks [25, 39]. Since the proposed system performs AI-assisted oral health assessment, it may qualify as a ‘Software as a Medical Device’ (SaMD) and therefore require compliance with frameworks such as the ‘EU Artificial Intelligence Act’ [40]. Furthermore, remote processing and storage of intraoral video recordings introduce important privacy and data governance considerations, as oral health data may be classified as sensitive health-related information under regulations such as the ‘General Data Protection Regulation’ (GDPR) [41].

In addition to the utility of the system, the study itself had several strengths. The use of video data enabled the collection of a substantially larger number of frames compared to still images, while also being less time and effort-intensive. Additionally, the data captured was more diverse, allowing the model to be trained on a wider range of visual variations, which contributed to improved generalization. Another key strength was the use of a low-cost, handheld device, which demonstrated that high-quality diagnostic data can be acquired without specialized or expensive equipment. Furthermore, the study established a proof of concept for embedding AI diagnostics into an everyday device.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations were observed. The model faced challenges in detecting proximal caries and remained susceptible to false positives, particularly when extrinsic stains were misclassified as decay. There was also a potential for misclassification in teeth with suboptimal appearances due to wear, caries, or poorly contoured restorations. Similarly, model performance in cases of partial or complete edentulism was not specifically evaluated in this study. Additionally, the dataset exhibited class imbalance, for certain categories such as the ‘missing’ class having fewer instances, which contributed to uneven performance metrics. The present study did not incorporate formal uncertainty quantification techniques, as it was a validation study focused on evaluating object-detection performance using standard metrics. Furthermore, uncertainty-aware frameworks are computationally complex and are more commonly integrated into later-stage clinically deployable AI systems rather than initial proof-of-concept investigations [42].

Additionally, although the multicenter nature of the dataset improved diversity and enhanced real-world variability, differences in acquisition conditions, operator handling, patient demographics, and oral disease prevalence across centers may have introduced dataset heterogeneity and potential bias. Broader external validation across geographically and demographically diverse populations will therefore be necessary prior to clinical implementation.

Future research should focus on expanding datasets across diverse populations, device types and additional clinical findings such as dental prostheses and tooth crowding. It should also aim to combine X-ray data with a larger intraoral dataset to build a more comprehensive and accurate diagnostic system. Integration of multimodal data sources, such as fluorescence or infrared imaging, could further enhance sensitivity for detecting early lesions and soft-tissue abnormalities. Incorporating explainability tools such as Grad-CAM heatmaps will be essential to ensure that the model’s attention corresponds to clinically relevant regions, thereby strengthening clinician trust and facilitating clinical adoption. Furthermore, the authors plan to develop a unified framework that integrates both the dental-finding and tooth-type models, enabling holistic, simultaneous analysis of oral conditions. In parallel, studies comparing dentists and AI architectures should also be conducted to further evaluate the clinical reliability of such systems. Finally, optimizing the model for on-device inference and exploring federated learning frameworks could support privacy-preserving model updates and enable real-time deployment.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that a DL model can accurately detect several dental findings and tooth-types from video data captured using a toothbrush-mounted intraoral device. The model showed strong performance and adaptability despite variations in video capture conditions, highlighting its potential for real-world use. By integrating AI into low-cost, everyday devices, this approach can make oral healthcare more equitable, accessible, and affordable. Furthermore, such technology can inform NGOs and other organizations who can be better equipped to serve remote, underserved communities where untreated oral disease occurs at much higher incidence due to lack of dentist access.
