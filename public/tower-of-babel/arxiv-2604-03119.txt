Range of random ℤ \mathbb{Z} -homomorphisms on weak expanders
Dingding Dong, Jinyoung Park

Abstract
. We prove that random ℤ \mathbb{Z} -homomorphisms on weakly expanding bipartite graphs exhibit a strong “flatness” phenomenon. Extending prior work of Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff for expanders, we first show that on any bipartite ( n , d , λ ) (n,d,\lambda) -graph with λ ≤ ( 1 − δ ) ​ d \lambda\leq(1-\delta)d , a uniformly chosen ℤ \mathbb{Z} -homomorphism has a range at most O ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(\log\log n) with high probability, which is tight up to a constant factor. This provides an affirmative answer to their question in the spectral setting. As a concrete application, we prove that a random ℤ \mathbb{Z} -homomorphism on the middle layers of the Hamming cube takes at most 5 5 values with high probability. This shows that the O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) -flatness for the full Hamming cube, proved by Kahn and Galvin, persists even when the rigid structural properties are relaxed.



1.
Introduction

Discrete height functions, particularly integer-valued Lipschitz functions and
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on graphs, are widely studied at the intersection of combinatorics, probability, and statistical mechanics. The primary focus of this paper is the “flatness” phenomenon of random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on weak expanders. Given a graph
\Gamma
, a
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism
is a function
h:V(\Gamma)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
such that adjacent vertices receive adjacent integer values (i.e.,
|h(u)-h(v)|=1
for all edges
\{u,v\}
). Clearly, such a function can only exist if the underlying graph
\Gamma
is bipartite. This framework provides a natural generalization of the simple random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
; indeed, an
n
-step simple random walk corresponds exactly to a uniformly random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism from a path of length
n
to
\mathbb{Z}
. Extending this probabilistic perspective, a uniformly random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism from a general bipartite graph
\Gamma
, rooted by mapping a fixed vertex
v_{0}
to
0
, is also called a
\Gamma
-indexed random walk
(see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
]
).

Formally, let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a connected bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
. For a distinguished vertex
v_{0}\in V
, we define

\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma):=\{h:V\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}\mid h(v_{0})=0\text{ and }\{u,v\}\in E\implies|h(u)-h(v)|=1\}.

We will call an element of
\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
a
rooted
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism
from
\Gamma
(with respect to
v_{0}
). Define the
range
of
h\in\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
as

R(h)=|\{h(x):x\in V\}|.

1.1.
Main results

Our first main result,
1.1
, concerns the typical range of
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on bipartite spectral expanders, and is motivated by a question raised by Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff
[
28
]
. As a slight variant of standard terminology, we define a
bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph
to be a
d
-regular bipartite graph with
n
vertices in each part, such that its second largest eigenvalue is at most
\lambda
.

In
[
28
]
, Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff investigated the typical range of integer-valued Lipschitz functions and
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on expanders. Focusing on their results for
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms, we note that they proved that if the underlying graph is a sufficiently strong expander—meaning the parameter
\lambda
is much smaller than the degree
d
, specifically
\lambda\leq\frac{d}{300\log d}
—then a typical
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism takes only
O\left(\frac{\log\log n}{\log(d/\lambda)}\right)
values with high probability.
1
1

1

To be precise, the notion of expanders used in
[
28
]
was defined via the Expander Mixing Lemma (EML)
[
1
]
: they defined a
d
-regular bipartite graph
\Gamma
with vertex bipartition
\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
such that
|\mathcal{E}|=|\mathcal{O}|=n
to be a
\lambda
-expander
if
\left|e(S,T)-\frac{d}{n}|S||T|\right|\leq\lambda\sqrt{|S||T|}\quad\text{for all $S\subseteq\mathcal{E},T\subseteq\mathcal{O}$}
. While the EML condition is sufficient in their strong expansion regime (
\lambda\ll d
), our focus on the much weaker expansion regime (where
\lambda
is close to
d
) necessitates the standard algebraic definition of (bipartite)
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs. See
Remark

1.3
for more explanation.

To understand this bound, it is helpful to compare it with the trivial upper bound. It is a standard fact that the diameter of a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph is
O\big(\frac{\log n}{\log(d/\lambda)}\big)
. Since the values of a
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism change by exactly
1
along any edge, the range of
any

\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism is deterministically bounded by the diameter of the graph (plus one). Thus, the result of
[
28
]
shows a strong “flatness” phenomenon: the typical range is double-logarithmic in
n
, which is much smaller than the logarithmic diameter. Furthermore, this upper bound is tight: an earlier result by Benjamini, Yadin, and Yehudayoff
[
6
]
established a matching lower bound, proving that the typical range is indeed
\Omega\big(\frac{\log\log n}{\log(d/\lambda)}\big)
.

Following their result, Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff naturally asked whether a similar flatness phenomenon continues to hold for graphs with a much smaller spectral gap (expansion), such as when
\lambda=(1-\delta)d
for small
\delta>0
. Our first main theorem answers this question affirmatively:

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
\delta>0
, there exist constants
d_{0}=d_{0}(\delta)>0
and
C=C(\delta)>0
such that the following holds. Let
\Gamma
be a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with vertex bipartition
V(\Gamma)=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
such that
d\geq d_{0}
and
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)d
. Fix any
v_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
. Let
\mathbf{h}
be a random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism chosen uniformly from
\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
. Then, with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
,

R(\mathbf{h})\leq C\log\log n.

In
1.1
, we state the result for a fixed constant
\delta>0
for clarity of presentation. However, our argument still holds even if
\delta
decays as a function of
d
(i.e.,
\delta=o_{d}(1)
). As a concrete application of our framework in the
\delta=o_{d}(1)
regime, our second main result,
1.2
, shifts the focus to a specific combinatorial structure: the middle layers of the Hamming cube.

Our investigation in this setting is naturally motivated by two related lines of research. The first motivation is given by the study of the “flatness” of typical
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on the Hamming cube,
Q_{d}
. While the maximum possible range of a
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism on
Q_{d}
is trivially
d+1
(its diameter plus one), it was initially conjectured in
[
4
]
that the typical range is sublinear,
o(d)
. Kahn
[
15
]
resolved this in a strong form, proving that the typical range is actually
O(1)
. Galvin
[
8
]
later improved this constant, demonstrating that a random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism on
Q_{d}
takes at most
5
values with high probability. These results—later vastly generalized to high-dimensional tori by Peled
[
27
]
—show an extreme rigidity in the typical behavior of
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms. Kahn’s proof makes crucial use of the sophisticated structure of the Hamming cube, and Galvin’s subsequent refinement builds on Kahn’s earlier work.

Given the phenomenon explored by Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff
[
28
]
, a natural question arises: is this extreme strong flatness (taking only at most 5 values) a consequence of the rigid structure of the Hamming cube, or does it still hold in more general settings? To answer this, we turn to a natural testbed: the middle layers of the Hamming cube. While this specific subgraph has attracted independent interest (e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
7
,
11
,
23
]
), for our present purposes, it provides a concrete setting to explore how much we can weaken the structural requirements.

Before formally stating our result, we introduce some relevant definitions. Let
Q_{2d-1}
denote the
(2d-1)
-dimensional Hamming cube. For a vertex
v\in V(Q_{2d-1})=\{0,1\}^{2d-1}
, let
\|v\|
denote its Hamming weight (the number of 1’s in the coordinates of
v
), and let

L_{k}:=\{v\in V(Q_{2d-1}):\|v\|=k\}

denote the
k
-th layer
of
Q_{2d-1}
. Let
Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}:=Q_{2d-1}[L_{d-1}\cup L_{d}]
denote the induced subgraph of
Q_{2d-1}
on the two middle layers. Observe that
Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}
is a balanced bipartite
d
-regular graph with parts
L_{d-1}
and
L_{d}
. It is a known fact (see, e.g.,
[
32
, Chapter 2]
) that the second largest eigenvalue of
Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}
is
\lambda=d-\Theta(1)
. This places
Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}
in the
\delta=\Theta(1/d)~(=o_{d}(1))
regime of
1.1
.

Our second main theorem shows that the strong
O(1)
-flatness for the full Hamming cube still holds in this less rigid structure.

Theorem 1.2
.

Fix an arbitrary vertex
v_{0}\in L_{d-1}
. Let
\mathbf{h}
be a random
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism chosen uniformly from
\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}})
. Then, with probability
1-o_{d}(1)
,

R(\mathbf{h})\leq 5.

We remark that our proof of
1.2
primarily relies on the isoperimetric properties of the middle layers—following the approach in the proof of
1.1
—rather than their exact combinatorial structure. Because the expansion of the middle layers is strictly weaker than that of the full Hamming cube, our method naturally recovers the results of Kahn
[
15
]
and Galvin
[
8
]
for the full cube, again without relying on its specific structure.
2
2

2

Galvin
[
8
]
actually provided a much more detailed description of the distribution of the range. Furthermore, one could potentially obtain even more precise probabilistic information by employing the cluster expansion method of Jenssen and Perkins
[
14
]
. While our current approach can be extended to yield similar distributional results, we do not pursue this direction here for the sake of simplicity and to avoid excessive repetition.

The second motivation for studying
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on the middle layers stems from the study of graph homomorphisms on these layers. In this direction, Balogh, Garcia and Li
[
3
]
determined the asymptotic number and typical structure of independent sets on the middle layers. Following their work, Li, McKinley, and the second author
[
23

determined the typical structure of

3

This gap in the literature makes the study of
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on the middle layers particularly interesting. On the full Hamming cube, there is a natural mapping—reducing values modulo
3
—between
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms and proper

[
8

, allowed Galvin to immediately deduce the typical structure of
3

\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms. However, on the middle layers, this connection simply fails. Consequently, without this bijection and with
3

\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms on the middle layers remains an interesting problem on its own.

1.2.
Proof overview and key ideas

Historically, a well-established framework for proving typicality results for graph homomorphisms and related discrete models (such as ordinary/maximal independent sets, proper colorings and Lipschitz functions) on bipartite (weak) expanders relies heavily on a two-step “ground state” paradigm (e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
21
,
23
,
28
,
30
,
31
]
). This framework first establishes that a typical configuration globally aligns with a rigid ground state (e.g., for
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms, even vertices take
0
and odd vertices take
\pm 1
). The second step then employs a Peierls-type contour argument (see, e.g.,
[
26
,
20
]
) to bound the connected components of “defects” deviating from this ground state, showing that large deviations result in a large entropic penalty.

Peled, Samotij, and Yehudayoff
[
28
]
also used this framework. Their starting point was a key structural lemma that establishes a global ground state. They proved that for any bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph on
(V_{0},V_{1})
, every
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism
f
mostly takes a single value on one side. That is, there exist
i\in\{0,1\}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(1)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:psy-ground-state}|\{v\in V_{i}:f(v)\neq k\}|\leq\frac{2\lambda n}{d}.

Recently, Krueger, Li, and the second author
[
21
]
pushed the expansion requirement for the flatness of typical
M
-Lipschitz functions
to
\lambda<d/5
, improving upon the bound
O(d/(M\log(Md^{2})))
in
[
28
]
. While they noted that their approach could be adapted to yield similar results for
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms, (
1
) remains a fundamental bottleneck. For the inequality in (
1
) to provide a meaningful restriction—specifically, to ensure that a majority of vertices in
V_{i}
actually take the value
k
—we must have
\frac{2\lambda n}{d}<n
, which requires
\lambda<d/2
. Consequently, this key lemma does not provide a useful bound for weak expanders, where
\lambda
can be as large as
(1-\delta)d
. To bypass this limitation, our proof does not rely on establishing a global ground state.

Instead, we introduce a local smoothing procedure called the
merge
operation. Rather than comparing a configuration to a single rigid ground state, we analyze how the number of valid configurations changes as we “flatten” local extrema. Specifically, we demonstrate that applying the merge operation to a set of
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms with large peaks maps them into a space of flatter configurations that is significantly larger. To carefully bound the size of the preimage of this operation, we employ the graph container method, a powerful tool in this area. While our container construction closely follows
[
8
]
, the key to our proof is the combination of these containers with the newly introduced merge operation. As a consequence, configurations with tightly bounded fluctuations constitute almost all of the set of valid configurations, allowing us to deduce the typical structure directly without establishing a global ground state. Crucially, by avoiding this step, our argument no longer requires any special structural properties or strong expansion assumptions on the underlying graph.

Remark 1.3
.

As noted in an earlier footnote, our definition of an expander differs from the one used in
[
28
]
. They defined a bipartite expander based on the conclusion of the Expander Mixing Lemma
[
1
]
. This specific formulation yields the following vertex expansion (see Proposition 2.8 in
[
28
]
): for every subset
A\subseteq V(G)
,

|N(A)|\geq\min\left\{\frac{n}{2},\frac{d^{2}}{4\lambda^{2}}|A|\right\}.

Crucially, this bound does not provide an expansion guarantee when
\lambda>d/2
. To avoid this technical limitation, we adopt the standard spectral definition of a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph. Since every
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph satisfies the conclusion of the Expander Mixing Lemma, the assumption in
[
28
]
is strictly weaker than ours. However, we emphasize that adopting this stronger definition is not merely to bypass the bottleneck encountered in
[
21
,
28
]
. The fundamental obstacle regarding (
1
) applies equally under both definitions; our resolution relies entirely on the introduction of the merge operation.

2.2.
Isoperimetry in bipartite spectral expanders

We begin by stating a vertex expansion bound for bipartite graphs based on their second largest eigenvalue. The following lemma is a bipartite variant of the classical Tanner bound
[
34
]
(for a broader overview of such spectral techniques, we refer the reader to
[
10
]
).

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
, such that
\lambda=\alpha d
.
If
S\subseteq\mathcal{E}
(or
S\subseteq\mathcal{O}
) and
|S|=\rho n
, then we have

\frac{|N(S)|}{|S|}\geq\frac{1}{\rho(1-\alpha^{2})+\alpha^{2}}.

Proof.

Let
A
be the
2n\times 2n
adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
. Since
\Gamma
is a bipartite
d
-regular graph, its eigenvalues occur in pairs
(\mu,-\mu)
, with top eigenvalues
d
and
-d
, and all remaining eigenvalues of absolute value at most
\lambda=\alpha d
.

Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis
v_{1},\dots,v_{2n}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}}
so that

v_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}},\qquad v_{2n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n}}(\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{E}}-\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{O}}),

with eigenvalues
d
and
-d
, respectively. Write
\mathbf{1}_{S}:=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}a_{i}v_{i}
.
Since
S\subseteq\mathcal{E}
and
|S|=\rho n
, we have

a_{1}=\mathbf{1}_{S}\cdot v_{1}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}},\qquad a_{2n}=\mathbf{1}_{S}\cdot v_{2n}=\rho\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}.

Therefore,

\|A\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\lambda_{i}^{2}a_{i}^{2}=d^{2}\rho^{2}n+\sum_{i=2}^{2n-1}\lambda_{i}^{2}a_{i}^{2}\leq d^{2}\rho^{2}n+\alpha^{2}d^{2}\sum_{i=2}^{2n-1}a_{i}^{2}.

Now,

\sum_{i=2}^{2n-1}a_{i}^{2}=\|\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}^{2}-a_{1}^{2}-a_{2n}^{2}=|S|-\rho^{2}n=\rho n-\rho^{2}n,

so

(2)

\displaystyle\|A\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}^{2}\leq d^{2}\rho^{2}n+\alpha^{2}d^{2}(\rho n-\rho^{2}n)=d^{2}|S|(\rho+\alpha^{2}(1-\rho)).

On the other hand, since
\Gamma
is bipartite and
S\subseteq\mathcal{E}
, the vector
A\mathbf{1}_{S}
has entries equal to zero on
\mathcal{E}
. For every
y\in\mathcal{O}
, we have
(A\mathbf{1}_{S})_{y}=d_{S}(y)
,
so
A\mathbf{1}_{S}
is exactly supported on
N(S)
, with

\sum_{y\in N(S)}(A\mathbf{1}_{S})_{y}=\sum_{y\in N(S)}d_{S}(y)=d|S|.

By Cauchy–Schwarz, we have

(3)

\displaystyle d^{2}|S|^{2}=\Bigl(\sum_{y\in N(S)}(A\mathbf{1}_{S})_{y}\Bigr)^{2}\leq|N(S)|\cdot\|A\mathbf{1}_{S}\|_{2}^{2}.

Combining (
2
) and (
3
) gives

d^{2}|S|^{2}\leq|N(S)|\cdot d^{2}|S|(\rho+\alpha^{2}(1-\rho)),

and hence

\frac{|N(S)|}{|S|}\geq\frac{1}{\rho+\alpha^{2}(1-\rho)}=\frac{1}{\rho(1-\alpha^{2})+\alpha^{2}}.\qed

Corollary 2.6
.

Fix
\delta>0
. Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
, such that
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)d
. Then every set
S\subseteq\mathcal{E}
(or
S\subseteq\mathcal{O}
) with
|S|\leq\frac{3n}{4}

satisfies

|N(S)|\geq\Bigl(1+\frac{\delta}{4}\Bigr)|S|.

Proof.

Write
\alpha=\lambda/d
, so
\alpha\leq 1-\delta
. If
|S|=\rho n\leq 3n/4
, then
2.5
gives

\frac{|N(S)|}{|S|}\geq\frac{1}{\rho(1-\alpha^{2})+\alpha^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\rho)}.

Using the inequality
\frac{1}{1-x}\geq 1+x
for
x\in(0,1)
, and noting that
1-\rho\geq 1/4
and
1-\alpha^{2}=(1-\alpha)(1+\alpha)\geq 1-\alpha\geq\delta
, we have

\frac{|N(S)|}{|S|}\geq 1+(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\rho)\geq 1+\frac{\delta}{4}.\qed

2.3.
Isoperimetry in
Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}

We will utilize the symmetry between

For

Theorem 2.8

(Lovász

Lemma 2.9

3.
Graph containers

In this section, we introduce graph container lemmas, which we will use in subsequent sections to bound the number of specific vertex subsets in our bipartite graphs. While this technique was initially pioneered by Sapozhenko
[
29
]
to enumerate independent sets in a certain class of bipartite graphs, the specific formulation we employ here is due to Galvin
[
8
]
– particularly, the introduction of the associated sets
B
and
H
, as well as the use of approximating quadruples.

3.1.
The
\psi
-approximating quadruples

Given a subset
A
, we define the following sets associated with
A
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
.
For every

, define the following four sets of vertices associated with

:

\displaystyle G=G(A)

\displaystyle:=N(A),

\displaystyle[A]

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathcal{E}:N(v)\subseteq N(A)\},

\displaystyle B=B(A)

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathcal{O}:N(v)\subseteq A\},

\displaystyle H=H(A)

\displaystyle:=N(B).

Let

,

,
a^{\prime}=|[A]|
,

, and

. Let

and

Note that from the above definitions, we have

and

. Also, when

is a

-regular graph, we always have

(since

Definition 3.2

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition

. For integers

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(a^{\prime},g,b,h,v)

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{A\subseteq\mathcal{E}:\ A\text{ is $2$-linked},\ |[A]|=a^{\prime},\ |G|=g,\ |B|=b,\ |H|=h,\ v\in G\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(a^{\prime},g,b,h)

\displaystyle:=\bigl\{A\subseteq\mathcal{E}:\ A\text{ is $2$-linked},\ |[A]|=a^{\prime},\ |G|=g,\ |B|=b,\ |H|=h\bigr\}.

The following definition introduces a tuple of sets that closely approximates

Definition 3.3

(

Let

be a

-approximating quadruple

(F,S,P,Q)\in 2^{\mathcal{O}}\times 2^{\mathcal{E}}\times 2^{\mathcal{E}}\times 2^{\mathcal{O}}
such that:

(1)

and

(2)

(3)

(4)

and

(6)

for every

.

4.
Reduction: from homomorphisms to partitions

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
, and fix an arbitrary vertex
v_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
. For any
h\in\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
and any two vertices
u,v\in\mathcal{E}
at distance 2, their values under
h
differ by exactly 0 or 2. By rescaling these values, we obtain a structure we call a
legal labeling
of
\mathcal{E}
.

Definition 4.1

(Legal labeling)
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
, and
v_{0}\in\mathcal{E}
.
A
legal labeling
of
\mathcal{E}
is a map
f:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{Z}
such that for every pair
u,v\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mbox{\rm{dist}}(u,v)=2
, we have
|f(u)-f(v)|\leq 1
. We say that a legal labeling
f
is
centered
(with respect to
v_{0}
) if
f(v_{0})=0
.

For every centered legal labeling
f
of
\mathcal{E}
, we define its
image
as

\mathrm{im}(f):=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:\exists v\in\mathcal{E}\text{ such that }f(v)=x\},

noting that
|\mathrm{im}(f)|\leq\mathrm{diam}(\Gamma)/2+1
. For each
i\in\mathrm{im}(f)
, the
preimage
of
i
under
f
is
f^{-1}(i):=\{v\in\mathcal{E}:f(v)=i\}
. Note that the level sets
\{f^{-1}(i)\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
naturally partition the vertex set
\mathcal{E}
.

Recall the set
B(A)
from
3.1
:

B(A):=\{v\in\mathcal{O}:N(v)\subseteq A\}.

A key observation, inspired by the approaches in
[
8
,
28
]
, is that there is a one-to-many correspondence between centered legal labelings and
\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For every centered
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphism
h:\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{Z}
, the rescaled restriction
f:=\frac{1}{2}h|_{\mathcal{E}}
is a centered legal labeling of
\mathcal{E}
. Conversely, for any centered legal labeling
f:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{Z}
, the number of centered
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms
h:\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy
h|_{\mathcal{E}}=2f
is exactly

(5)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline ef:P(f)}P(f):=\prod_{i\in\mathrm{im}(f)}2^{|B(f^{-1}(i))|}.

We call
P(f)
the
weight
of
f
.

Proof.

If
h(v_{0})=0
, then
f(v_{0})=0
, so
f
is centered. For any
u,w\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mbox{\rm{dist}}(u,w)=2
, there exists a common neighbor
v\in\mathcal{O}
. Since
|h(u)-h(v)|=1
and
|h(w)-h(v)|=1
, we must have
|h(u)-h(w)|\in\{0,2\}
. Dividing by 2 shows
|f(u)-f(w)|\leq 1
, so
f
is a centered legal labeling.

For the converse, fix a centered legal labeling
f
. This determines
h|_{\mathcal{E}}
, and consider the possible values for
h(v)
for each
v\in\mathcal{O}
. For
h
to be a homomorphism with
h|_{\mathcal{E}}=2f
,
h(v)
must be adjacent to every value in
\{2f(u):u\in N(v)\}
.

•

If
f
is constant
i
on
N(v)
(i.e.,
N(v)\subseteq f^{-1}(i)
; equivalently,
v\in B(f^{-1}(i))
), then
h(N(v))=\{2i\}
. In this case,
h(v)
can be either
2i-1
or
2i+1
, giving
2
choices.

•

If
f
is not constant on
N(v)
, then since
|f(u)-f(w)|\leq 1
for all
u,w\in N(v)
, the set
f(N(v))
must be
\{i,i+1\}
for some
i
. Then
h(N(v))=\{2i,2i+2\}
. The only integer adjacent to both values is
2i+1
, giving exactly
1
choice.

Since the choices for each
v\in\mathcal{O}
are independent, the total number of homomorphisms is
2^{\sum_{i}|B(f^{-1}(i))|}
, which equals
P(f)
.
∎

Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the set of all centered legal labelings of
\mathcal{E}
. It follows from
4.2
that
|\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)|
can be expressed as the total weight of these labelings:

(6)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:task}|\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)|=\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}P(f).

5.
Merge

The goal of this section is to introduce the
merge
operation as a crucial tool to bound the sum in (
6
).

5.1.
The merge operation

The purpose of the merge operation is to smooth out some of the “peaks” in a given legal labeling, which we call
maximum components
.

Definition 5.1

(Maximum component)
.

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
.
Let
f:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a legal labeling of
\mathcal{E}
. We say that
K\subseteq\mathcal{E}
is a
maximum component
(of
f
) if:

(i)

K
is 2-linked; and

(ii)

for every
x\in K
and
y\in N^{2}(K)\setminus K
, we have
f(y)<f(x)
.

Observe that if
K
is a maximum component of
f
, then for every
y\in N^{2}(K)\setminus K
,
f(y)=\min\{f(x):x\in K\}-1
.

Definition 5.2

(Merge)
.

Let
f:\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{Z}
be a centered legal labeling. Suppose there is a maximum component
K\subseteq\mathcal{E}
of
f
. Define the
merge
(with respect to
f
and
K
), denoted by
m_{K}(f):\mathcal{E}\to\mathbb{Z}
, as follows:

•

If
v_{0}\notin K
, then let

m_{K}(f)(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)-1&\text{if }x\in K;\\
f(x)&\text{if }x\notin K.\end{cases}

•

If
v_{0}\in K
, then let

m_{K}(f)(x)=\begin{cases}f(x)&\text{if }x\in K;\\
f(x)+1&\text{if }x\notin K.\end{cases}

Lemma 5.3
.

If
f
is a centered legal labeling and
K
is a maximum component of
f
, then
m_{K}(f)
is also a centered legal labeling. Furthermore, we have
P(m_{K}(f))=2^{\,|N(K)|-|B(K)|}\,P(f)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
m_{K}(f)(v_{0})=f(v_{0})=0
. To verify that
m_{K}(f)
is a legal labeling, we must show that for every pair of vertices
u,w\in\mathcal{E}
with
\mbox{\rm{dist}}(u,w)=2
,
|m_{K}(f)(u)-m_{K}(f)(w)|\leq 1
.

For simplicity, write
\widetilde{f}
for
m_{K}(f)
. We consider three different cases up to symmetry. If
u,w\in K
or
u,w\notin K
, it is clear from the definition that
|\widetilde{f}(u)-\widetilde{f}(w)|\leq 1
. If
u\in K
and
w\notin K
, then since
w\in N^{2}(K)\setminus K
, we must have
f(w)=f(u)-1
(by the definition of a legal labeling and the observation following
5.1
). Therefore, if
v_{0}\notin K
, we have
\widetilde{f}(w)=f(w)=f(u)-1=\widetilde{f}(u)
; if
v_{0}\in K
, we have
\widetilde{f}(w)=f(w)+1=f(u)=\widetilde{f}(u)
.

To prove that
P(\widetilde{f})=2^{\,|N(K)|-|B(K)|}\,P(f)
, we first express the weight
P(f)
in a more combinatorial form. By
4.2
,

P(f)=\prod_{i\in\mathrm{im}(f)}2^{|B(f^{-1}(i))|}=2^{\sum_{i\in\mathrm{im}(f)}|B(f^{-1}(i))|},

where

\sum_{i\in\mathrm{im}(f)}|B(f^{-1}(i))|=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{im}(f)}|\{v\in\mathcal{O}:\text{$f|_{N(v)}$ is constant }i\}|=|\{v\in\mathcal{O}:\text{$f|_{N(v)}$ is constant}\}|.

We now count how this quantity changes between
f
and
\widetilde{f}
.

Assume first that
v_{0}\notin K
, so
\widetilde{f}=f-1
on
K
and
\widetilde{f}=f
outside
K
. We partition
\mathcal{O}
into three sets:

•

\mathcal{O}\setminus N(K)
: For all
v\in\mathcal{O}\setminus N(K)
, we have
N(v)\subseteq\mathcal{E}\setminus K
, so
\widetilde{f}|_{N(v)}=f|_{N(v)}
. Thus,
\widetilde{f}
is constant on
N(v)
if and only if
f
is.

•

B(K)
: For all
v\in B(K)
, we have
N(v)\subseteq K
, so
\widetilde{f}|_{N(v)}=f|_{N(v)}-1
. Thus,
\widetilde{f}
is constant on
N(v)
if and only if
f
is.

•

N(K)\setminus B(K)
: For all
v\in N(K)\setminus B(K)
,
N(v)
intersects both
K
and
\mathcal{E}\setminus K
. Letting
k:=\min f|_{K}
, we find that
f|_{N(v)\setminus K}
is constant
k-1
, and
f|_{N(v)\cap K}
is constant
k
. Thus,
\widetilde{f}|_{N(v)\setminus K}
and
\widetilde{f}|_{N(v)\cap K}
are both equal to
k-1
. This implies that
\widetilde{f}
is constant on
N(v)
, whereas
f
is not.

Altogether, it follows that
|\{v\in\mathcal{O}:\text{$\widetilde{f}|_{N(v)}$ is constant}\}|=|\{v\in\mathcal{O}:\text{$f|_{N(v)}$ is constant}\}|+|N(K)\setminus B(K)|
, which yields
P(\widetilde{f})=2^{\,|N(K)|-|B(K)|}\,P(f)
. The other case (
v_{0}\in K
) follows similarly.
∎

such that

f_{0}=f,\qquad f_{i+1}=m_{K_{i}}(f_{i})\ \ \text{for }0\leq i\leq\ell-1,

x\in K_{0}\subseteq K_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K_{\ell-1},\qquad\text{and}\qquad|[K_{\ell-1}]|\leq\frac{3n}{4}.

In this case, we say that
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)
is
witnessed
by the sequence
K_{0},\dots,K_{\ell-1}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\delta>0
, and let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a bipartite
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
and
\lambda\leq(1-\delta)d
. For any
x\in\mathcal{E}
, if
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)
is witnessed by
K_{0},\dots,K_{\ell-1}
, then

K_{i}\supseteq N^{2i}(x)

for every
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
. Consequently,
|K_{i}|\geq\bigl(1+\frac{\delta}{4}\bigr)^{2i}
for every
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
.

Proof.

We show that
K_{i+1}\supseteq N^{2}(K_{i})
for every
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
. Since
K_{i}
is a maximum component of
f_{i}
, the values of
f_{i}
on
N^{2}(K_{i})\setminus K_{i}
are identically equal to
\min f_{i}|_{K_{i}}-1
. Thus for
f_{i+1}=m_{K_{i}}(f_{i})
we have
f_{i+1}|_{N^{2}(K_{i})\setminus K_{i}}=\min f_{i+1}|_{K_{i}}
. This implies that any maximum component of
f_{i+1}
containing
K_{i}
will necessarily contain
N^{2}(K_{i})
as well. Since
x\in K_{0}
, it immediately follows by induction that
K_{i}\supseteq N^{2i}(x)
for every
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
.

Finally, for all
0\leq i\leq\ell-1
,
|[K_{i}]|\leq|[K_{\ell-1}]|\leq\frac{3n}{4}
, so
2.6
guarantees that
|N^{2i}(x)|\geq(1+\frac{\delta}{4})^{2i}
, which yields
|K_{i}|\geq\Bigl(1+\frac{\delta}{4}\Bigr)^{2i}
for all
1\leq i\leq\ell-1
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For every
\ell\geq 1
and
x\in\mathcal{E}
, we have

\frac{\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)}P(f)}{\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}P(f)}\leq 2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2\ell}/\log d)}.

Proof.

Suppose
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)
is witnessed by
K_{0},\dots,K_{\ell-1}
.

Recall from
5.3
that
f_{0},\dots,f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
, and the weights satisfy
P(f_{i})=2^{-|N(K_{i})|+|B(K_{i})|}P(f_{i+1})
.
Iterating this relation gives

P(f)=P(f_{\ell})\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}2^{-|N(K_{i})|+|B(K_{i})|}.

Notice that each
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)
is determined by a final labeling
f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
and witnesses
K_{0},\dots,K_{\ell-1}
. Therefore, we can upper bound the sum over
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)
by summing over all possible choices of
f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}
and nesting sequences
K_{i}
(relaxing the strict condition that
K_{i}
must be a maximum component). This yields

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)}P(f)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}}P(f_{\ell})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in K_{0}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K_{\ell-1}\\
|K_{i}|\geq(1+\delta/4)^{2i}\\
|[K_{i}]|\leq 3n/4\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}2^{-|N(K_{i})|+|B(K_{i})|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}}P(f_{\ell})\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}\left(\sum_{m\geq(1+\delta/4)^{2i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{E},\,|K_{i}|=m\\
|[K_{i}]|\leq 3n/4\\
x\in K_{i}\end{subarray}}2^{-|N(K_{i})|+|B(K_{i})|}\right).

To bound the inner sum for each
i\geq 0
, we apply
3.7
. By picking an arbitrary
v\in N(x)
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{m\geq(1+\delta/4)^{2i}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}K_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{E},\,|K_{i}|=m\\
|[K_{i}]|\leq 3n/4\\
x\in K_{i}\end{subarray}}2^{-|N(K_{i})|+|B(K_{i})|}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{(1+\delta/4)^{2(i+1)}\leq g}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a^{\prime},b,h\leq g\\
a^{\prime}\leq 3n/4\end{subarray}}\mathcal{H}(a^{\prime},g,b,h,v)2^{-g+b}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{(1+\delta/4)^{2(i+1)}\leq g}g^{3}\cdot 2^{-\Omega(t_{1}/\log d)}.

Noting that
t_{1}=g-a^{\prime}=\Omega(g)
(since
g\geq(1+\delta/4)a^{\prime}
so
g-a^{\prime}\geq g(1-1/(1+\delta/4))=\Omega(g)
for any fixed
\delta
), the last expression is at most

2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2(i+1)}/\log d)}.

Substituting this bound back into our product, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}(x)}P(f)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}}P(f_{\ell})\prod_{i=0}^{\ell-1}2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2(i+1)}/\log d)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}}P(f_{\ell})2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2\ell}/\log d)}.

Dividing both sides by
\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}P(f)
completes the proof.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main theorem.

Proof of
1.1
.

Recall the definition of the range
R(h)=|\{h(v):v\in V\}|
. Since the values of
h\in\mbox{\rm{Hom}}_{v_{0}}(\Gamma)
on adjacent vertices differ by exactly 1, the image
h(V)
is a set of consecutive integers. Thus,
\max_{v\in V}h(v)-\min_{v\in V}h(v)=R(h)-1
. Since every vertex in
V
is adjacent to (or equal to) a vertex in
\mathcal{E}
, we have
\max_{v\in\mathcal{E}}h(v)\geq\max_{v\in V}h(v)-1
and
\min_{v\in\mathcal{E}}h(v)\leq\min_{v\in V}h(v)+1
. Consequently,

\max_{v\in\mathcal{E}}h(v)-\min_{v\in\mathcal{E}}h(v)\geq R(h)-3.

Recall that the legal labeling
f
is obtained by rescaling
h
on
\mathcal{E}
by a factor of
1/2
. Let
m:=\max_{v\in\mathcal{E}}f(v)
and
m^{\prime}:=\min_{v\in\mathcal{E}}f(v)
. Since
h(v)=2f(v)
for all
v\in\mathcal{E}
, the above inequality yields
2(m-m^{\prime})\geq R(h)-3
.

Suppose
R(h)>2t+3
, which implies
m-m^{\prime}>t
. By replacing
t
with
3\lfloor t/3\rfloor
if necessary, we may assume that
t
is a multiple of 3. By the pigeonhole principle, we either have
|f^{-1}(\{m,m-1,\dots,m-t/3\})|\leq n/2
or
|f^{-1}(\{m^{\prime},m^{\prime}+1,\dots,m^{\prime}+t/3\})|\leq n/2
(since
(m-t/3)-(m^{\prime}+t/3)=m-m^{\prime}-2t/3>t/3\geq 1
, the top and bottom intervals are disjoint). Without loss of generality, suppose the former holds.

Take any
x\in\mathcal{E}
with
f(x)=m
. For every
0\leq i\leq t/3
, let
K_{i}
denote the 2-linked component of the set
\{v\in\mathcal{E}:f(v)\geq m-i\}
containing
x
. (Note that by maximality,
f<m-i
on
N^{2}(K_{i})\setminus K_{i}
.) By construction,
K_{i}
is a maximum component of the appropriately merged labeling
f_{i}
, and we have the nested sequence
x\in K_{0}\subseteq K_{1}\subseteq\dots\subseteq K_{t/3-1}
. Furthermore, for all
1\leq i\leq t/3-1
, the assumption implies
|[K_{i}]|\leq|K_{i+1}|\leq n/2\leq 3n/4
. Thus,
f\in\mathcal{F}_{t/3}(x)
is witnessed by
K_{0},K_{1},\dots,K_{t/3-1}
.

Recall from
4.2
that picking
h
uniformly at random corresponds to picking
f\in\mathcal{F}
with probability exactly proportional to its weight
P(f)
. Applying
6.3
(with
\ell=t/3
), we can bound the probability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bf h}(R({\bf h})>2t+3)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\bf f}(m-m^{\prime}\geq t)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{x\in\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{P}_{\bf f}({\bf f}\in\mathcal{F}_{t/3}(x))

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot 2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2(t/3)}/\log d)}

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot 2^{-\Omega((1+\delta/4)^{2t/3}/\log n)}.

Since
d\leq n
, we have
\log d\leq\log n
. Setting
t=C_{0}\frac{\log\log n}{\delta}
for a sufficiently large constant
C_{0}=C_{0}(\lambda/d)>0
, the exponent dominates
\log n
, and the above probability becomes
o_{n}(1)
. Rescaling the constant to
C=2C_{0}
, this concludes the proof that

\mathbb{P}(R({\bf h})\leq C\log\log n)=1-o_{n}(1).\qed

Proof of
1.2

Let
\Gamma=Q_{2d-1}^{\mathrm{mid}}
be the middle layers of the
(2d-1)
-dimensional Hamming cube, with vertex bipartition
V=\mathcal{E}\cup\mathcal{O}
where
\mathcal{E}=L_{d-1}
,
\mathcal{O}=L_{d}
.
Fix any
v_{0}\in L_{d-1}
. As in the previous section, let
\mathcal{F}
denote the family of centered legal labelings of

.

7.1.
Setup

We will classify legal labelings based on the existence of maximum components with “intermediate size,” and define a legal labeling to be
max-good
(and similarly
min-good
) if it lacks such components. We will show, using results from
Section

3
, that legal labelings that are not max/min-good are amenable to a merge operation that strictly increases
P(f)
.

Definition 7.1

(Max-good)
.

Let
f
be a legal labeling of
L_{d-1}
. Let
c=1/10
. We say that
f
is
max-good
if there is no maximum component
K
such that

(7)

|K|\geq d/2
and
|[K]|\leq(1-c)\binom{2d-1}{d}
.

We denote by
\mathcal{F}_{0}~(\subseteq\mathcal{F})
the collection of centered max-good legal labelings.

The following lemma is central to our argument, as it establishes that almost all
\mathbb{Z}
-homomorphisms correspond to max-good legal labelings.

Lemma 7.2
.

\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}}P(f)=(1+o(1))\sum_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{0}}P(f)
.

The proof of
7.2
requires some preparation. As in the previous section, we classify
\mathcal{F}
using the notion of “level,” (
cf.

6.1
) but in a slightly different way.

Definition 7.3

(Level)
.

For every
\ell\geq 1
, we say that
f\in\mathcal{F}
is
at level
\ell
(denoted
f\in\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
) if there is a sequence of functions
f_{0},\dots,f_{\ell}
such that

(i)

f_{0}=f
,
f_{\ell}\in\mathcal{F}_{0}
;

(ii)

for every
i=0,\dots,\ell-1
,
f_{i+1}=m_{K_{i}}(f_{i})
where
K_{i}
is a maximum component of
f_{i}
with
|K_{i}|\geq d/2
and
|[K_{i}]|\leq(1-c)\binom{2d-1}{d}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Every
f\in\mathcal{F}
belongs to some
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
where
\ell
is at most the crude bound
\ell_{0}:=d^{2}{2d-1\choose d}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{0}
. For each
f\in\mathcal{F}
, define the potential function

S_{f}:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in L_{d-1}\\
\operatorname{dist}(x,y)=2\end{subarray}}|f(x)-f(y)|.

Observe that
S_{f}\geq 0
. Since
\operatorname{dist}(x,y)=2
implies
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq 1
, we have an upper bound

S_{f}\leq|\{(x,y)\in L_{d-1}\times L_{d-1}:\operatorname{dist}(x,y)=2\}|\leq d^{2}|L_{d-1}|.

We claim that the merge operation strictly reduces the potential
S_{f}
:

Claim 7.5
.

For
f\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\mathcal{F}_{0}
, let
K
be a maximum component of
f
such that
|K|\geq d/2
and
|[K]|\leq(1-c)\binom{2d-1}{d}
. Then
S_{m_{K}(f)}<S_{f}
.

Proof.

We analyze the contribution of each pair
(x,y)
with
\mbox{\rm{dist}}(x,y)=2
depending on their membership in
K
:

•

If
x,y\in K
, then
m_{K}(f)(x)-m_{K}(f)(y)=f(x)-f(y)
.

•

If
x,y\in L_{d-1}\setminus K
, then
m_{K}(f)(x)-m_{K}(f)(y)=f(x)-f(y)
.

•

If
x\in K
and
y\in L_{d-1}\setminus K
, then
y\in N^{2}(K)\setminus K
(since
\mbox{\rm{dist}}(x,y)=2
), which implies
f(y)=f(x)-1
. On the other hand, by the merge operation,
m_{K}(f)(x)=m_{K}(f)(y)
. Thus,
|m_{K}(f)(x)-m_{K}(f)(y)|<|f(x)-f(y)|
.

It follows that

S_{m_{K}(f)}=S_{f}-|\{(x,y):x\in K,y\in L_{d-1}\setminus K,\operatorname{dist}(x,y)=2\}|.

Since
|K|<\binom{2d-1}{d}
, the set on the right-hand side is non-empty, so
S_{m_{K}(f)}<S_{f}
.

∎

Suppose for the sake of contradiction that a function
f\in\mathcal{F}
is not at level
\ell
for any
\ell\leq\ell_{0}
. This implies there exists a sequence of functions
f_{0},\dots,f_{\ell_{0}+1}
such that:

(i)

f_{0}=f
, and none of
f_{0},\dots,f_{\ell_{0}}
lie in
\mathcal{F}_{0}
;

(ii)

For every
i=0,\dots,\ell_{0}
,
f_{i+1}=m_{K_{i}}(f_{i})
, where
K_{i}
is a maximum component of
f_{i}
with
|K_{i}|\geq d/2
and
|[K_{i}]|\leq(1-c)\binom{2d-1}{d}
.

By
5.3
, we know that
f_{i}\in\mathcal{F}
for all
i
. Furthermore, since
S_{f_{0}}\leq\ell_{0}
and the potential strictly decreases at each step, we have

S_{f_{\ell_{0}+1}}\leq S_{f_{0}}-(\ell_{0}+1)\leq\ell_{0}-\ell_{0}-1=-1,

which contradicts the fact that
S_{f_{\ell_{0}+1}}\geq 0
. Thus, every
f\in\mathcal{F}
must be at level
\ell
for some
0\leq\ell\leq\ell_{0}
.
∎

Note that a legal labeling
f
may initially belong to multiple levels
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
, as there might be different sequences of mergings that turn
f
into a max-good function. To resolve this, we uniquely assign each
f
to
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
where
\ell
is the minimum such index. This ensures that every
f\in\mathcal{F}
has a unique membership for some
\mathcal{F}_{\ell}
.

Proof of the key lemma

With the setup established, we are now ready to prove

.

Proof of
7.2
.

Consider

for some

. Let

Recall from

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:P/P}P(\widetilde{f})/P(f)=2^{|N(K)|-|B(K)|}.

Now, since for any

We first establish the following lemma.

Lemma 7.11
.

Suppose

To prove

, the

Proof of
7.11
.

Let
k=\max(h|_{L_{d-1}})
and let

be the maximum components in
L_{d-1}
such that
h=k
on each

. Since

Case 1:

We check the following:

d_{F}(u)\geq d-\psi

Lemma C.1
.

If

is a
\psi

\displaystyle|S|\leq|F|+O(\psi t_{1}/(d-\psi)),

Observe that we have

Since each vertex of
(G\setminus F)\cup(S\setminus[A])
contributes at least
d-\psi
edges to
e(G,\mathcal{E}\setminus[A])
, a set of size
gd-a^{\prime}d=t_{1}d
, we get the first bound. The second inequality follows analogously.

Proof of
3.6
.

Recall from the assumption that
t_{1}=g-a^{\prime}=\Omega(g/d)
and
t_{2}=h-b=\Omega(h/d)
. Given
(F,S,P,Q)
and
b\leq h\leq a^{\prime}\leq g
, we say that
Q
is tight if
|Q|<b+\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}
and slack otherwise; we say that
S
is tight if
|S|<g-\frac{t_{1}}{10\log d}
and slack otherwise.

We now define the following procedure to specify
A
.

•

We first pick some
D\subseteq A
as follows. If
Q
is tight, we pick
B\subseteq Q
with
|B|=b
and take
D=N(B)\subseteq A
; if
Q
is slack we take
D=P
.

•

If
S
is tight, we choose
A\setminus D\subseteq S\setminus D
.

•

If
S
is slack, we first pick
G
by picking
G\setminus F\subseteq N(S)\setminus F
. In this case
|S|\geq g-\frac{t_{1}}{10\log d}
. Then we know from
C.1
that
|F|\geq|S|-O(\psi t_{1}/d)\geq g-\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}
. Thus
|G\setminus F|\leq\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}
.

Observe that
G
determines
[A]
. We then choose
A\setminus D\subseteq[A]\setminus D
.

We have the following observations.

•

If
Q
is tight, then the number of choices of
D
is at most

\binom{O(h)}{\leq\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}}\leq 2^{\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}\cdot\log(10h\log d/t_{2})}.

In this case
|D|=h
.

•

If
Q
is slack there is 1 choice for
D
. In this case
|D|=|P|\geq|Q|-O(\psi t_{2}/(d-\psi))\geq b+\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}-O(\psi t_{2}/(d-\psi))\geq b+\frac{t_{2}}{20\log d}
.

•

If
S
is slack, then since
|N(S)|\leq d|S|=O(gd)
, the number of possible choices for
G\setminus F
is at most

\binom{O(gd)}{\leq\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}}\leq 2^{\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}\cdot\log(5gd\log d/t_{1})}.

Finally we bound the number of choices in each of the four cases.

(1)

Suppose both
S
and
Q
are tight. In this case
|S\setminus D|\leq g-\frac{t_{1}}{10\log d}-h
. Thus the number of choices of
A

(2)

Suppose
S

Q

|S\setminus D|\leq g-\frac{t_{1}}{10\log d}-b-\frac{t_{2}}{20\log d}
. So the number of choices of
A

2^{g-\frac{t_{1}}{10\log d}-b-\frac{t_{2}}{20\log d}}
.

(3)

Suppose
Q
is tight and
S
is slack. In this case
|[A]\setminus D|=a^{\prime}-h
. Then the number of choices of
A
is

\displaystyle 2^{\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}\cdot\log(10h\log d/t_{2})}\cdot 2^{\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}\cdot\log(5gd\log d/t_{1})}\cdot 2^{a^{\prime}-h}

\displaystyle<2^{\frac{t_{2}}{10\log d}\cdot\log(10h\log d/t_{2})}\cdot 2^{\frac{t_{1}}{5\log d}\cdot\log(5gd\log d/t_{1})}\cdot 2^{g-t_{1}-b-t_{2}}

(4)

Suppose both
