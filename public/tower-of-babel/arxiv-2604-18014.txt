Exponential quantum space advantage for Shannon entropy estimation in data streams
Weijun Feng, Yongzhen Xu, Lvzhou Li, Gongde Guo, Song Lin

Abstract
Near–term quantum devices with limited qubits motivate the study of space-bounded quantum computation in the data stream model. We show that Shannon entropy estimation exhibits an exponential separation between quantum and classical space complexity in this setting. Technically, we develop a two–stage quantum streaming algorithm based on a quantum procedure with an explicitly constructed oracle derived from the streaming input. This algorithm achieves logarithmic space complexity in the accuracy parameter, whereas any classical streaming algorithm requires polynomial space. In sharp contrast, existing results for Shannon entropy estimation in the quantum query model achieve only a quadratic speedup. Our work establishes a natural problem with practical applications in computer networking that admits an exponential quantum space advantage, revealing a fundamental gap between quantum query complexity and streaming space complexity.

1
Introduction

The pursuit of exponential quantum speedup is a central goal of quantum information science, aiming to identify computational tasks where quantum algorithms can be rigorously shown to outperform classical methods. Despite significant progress, provable exponential quantum–classical separations remain relatively rare and are known only for a limited number of computational problems.

The most prominent example is Shor’s algorithm
Shor 1994
for solving the factoring problem, which achieves exponential speedup over the best known classical algorithms in terms of time complexity. More recently, Decoded Quantum Interferometry
Jordan et al. 2025
has shown that combining quantum Fourier transforms with powerful decoding primitives can yield superpolynomial improvements over the best known classical polynomial-time methods on certain structured discrete optimization problems. Beyond time complexity, existing research has also revealed exponential separations between quantum and classical approaches in query complexity
Simon 1994
;
Childs et al. 2003
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Ben-David et al. 2020
;
Yamakawa and Zhandry 2022
;
Babbush et al. 2023
;
Li and Li 2025
;
Xu et al. 2026
and communication complexity
Raz 1999
;
Bar-Yossef et al. 2004
;
Gavinsky et al. 2006
;
Gavinsky et al. 2007
;
Montanaro 2011
;
Gilboa et al. 2024
.

In contrast, much less is known about quantum space complexity. Investigating quantum space is motivated by two complementary objectives. The first is to establish provable separations between quantum and classical memory requirements. The second is to develop quantum algorithms for early fault-tolerant quantum computers with a relatively limited number of physical qubits. Recent work has begun to explore quantum space advantages in streaming models, showing that some problems
Gall 2006
;
Gavinsky et al. 2007
;
Montanaro 2016
;
Kallaugher et al. 2024
admit exponential space savings while others exhibit no quantum advantage
Kallaugher and Parekh 2022
. This raises the natural question of whether there exist natural streaming problems that admit exponential quantum space advantages.

In this work, we answer this question affirmatively by showing that estimating the Shannon entropy of a data stream exhibits an exponential quantum–classical space separation. We consider the standard streaming model defined as follows.
Let
A=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\rangle
be a data stream over an alphabet
[n]
, where elements arrive sequentially and are observed one at a time. Algorithms in this model may perform one or more passes over the stream
A
. Our goal is to design algorithms that use as little space as possible to accomplish the given computational task. In the classical streaming model space is represented in bits whereas in the quantum streaming model it is represented in qubits.

Two reasons motivate the study of entropy estimation in the streaming setting. First, Shannon entropy
Shannon 1948
plays a fundamental role in information theory, underlying both classical
Cover 1999
and quantum
Wilde 2013
;
Watrous 2018
. Second, it has important applications in computer networking, including network anomaly detection
Lakhina et al. 2005
and traffic analysis
Xu et al. 2005
. At a broader level, problems that simultaneously combine real-world applications and provable exponential quantum advantages across different computational models are rare, which further motivates the study of Shannon entropy estimation in this setting.

Shannon entropy estimation has been extensively investigated in the classical streaming model
Chakrabarti et al. 2006
;
Guha et al. 2006
;
Chakrabarti et al. 2007
;
Harvey et al. 2008
;
Li and Zhang 2011
;
Clifford and Cosma 2013
, while to the best of our knowledge it has not been studied in the quantum streaming model. In this work, we provide a systematic investigation of this problem. Our main findings are summarized as follows. For a fixed number of passes over the data, our quantum algorithm approximates the entropy using logarithmic space, whereas any classical streaming algorithm requires space that grows polynomially with the desired accuracy. This establishes an exponential quantum-classical space separation for a natural problem with practical applications. In contrast, existing results for Shannon entropy estimation in the quantum query model achieve only a quadratic speedup
Li and Wu 2019
;
Bun et al. 2018
;
Gilyén and Li 2020
;
Shin and Jeong 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
.

To achieve this separation, we design a quantum query algorithm for Shannon entropy estimation with an efficiently implementable oracle in the streaming model.
This construction is analogous to Shor’s algorithm
Shor 1994
, which constructs quantum query algorithms using explicitly oracle in the time complexity setting, whereas our focus is space complexity in the streaming model.
This approach differs from previous quantum query algorithms
Li and Wu 2019
;
Bun et al. 2018
;
Gilyén and Li 2020
;
Shin and Jeong 2025
;
Chen et al. 2025
for Shannon entropy estimation, where oracle access is typically assumed in a black-box form. In our setting, the oracle is explicitly constructed from the streaming input and can be efficiently implemented under space constraints. Building on this query algorithm, we develop a two-stage quantum streaming algorithm for Shannon entropy estimation. This query–to–streaming transformation is of independent interest and offers a promising approach to designing space-efficient quantum streaming algorithms. We further prove a matching classical streaming space lower bound, thereby completing an exponential separation between quantum and classical space complexity.

Results

In this section, we first introduce the problem of Shannon entropy estimation in data streams, together with the quantum streaming model. We then present our main results, including quantum algorithms for this problem and corresponding classical hardness results, establishing an exponential quantum advantage.

2.1
Problem definition and computational model

For any integer
n>0
, we define
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}.

\log
denotes the base-2 logarithm, and
\ln
denotes the natural logarithm with base
e
. For given
\varepsilon,\delta\in(0,1)
, an algorithm is said to output an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-
approximation
of the value
V
if it returns a value
\widehat{V}
with probability at least
1-\delta
satisfying
(1-\varepsilon)V\ \leq\ \widehat{V}\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)V
.

We now introduce the Shannon entropy estimation problem in the data stream setting. Let
A=\langle x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}\rangle
be an input stream of length
m
, where each element
x_{j}
belongs to the set
[n]=\{1,\cdots,n\}
.
For every symbol
i\in[n]
define its frequency in the stream by

m_{i}:=\big|\{\,j:a_{j}=i\,\}\big|,

and set the empirical probability

p_{i}:=\frac{m_{i}}{m}.

We denote the induced empirical distribution by
p=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})
.
The Shannon entropy of the empirical distribution
is defined by

H(p)\;:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}-p_{i}\log p_{i}.

Our goal is to design algorithms that compute an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of the entropy
H(p)
while using as few bits of memory as possible.

To further explore potential quantum advantages, we consider the quantum streaming model, which generalizes the classical framework by allowing quantum state manipulation. A quantum streaming algorithm operates in the following manner, with the objective of minimizing the number of qubits required under a fixed number of passes over the stream:

1.

Initialization.

The algorithm prepares an initial quantum state using a limited number of qubits, which constitute its workspace.

2.

Streaming update.

As each element
x_{j}
arrives, the algorithm applies a unitary transformation

U(x_{j})
to its current quantum state.
The algorithm may make one or more passes over the stream, but within each pass the elements must be processed in order as they arrive.

3.

Measurement.

After the final pass, the algorithm performs a measurement on the quantum state to produce the output.

First, we design a quantum algorithm that efficiently approximates the Shannon entropy of a data stream using minimal space. 3
Discussion

A central question in quantum information science today is what small quantum computers can efficiently advantages ove classical counterparts. In this work, we provide a partial answer by showing that quantum streaming algorithms can achieve an exponential saving in space over classical methods for Shannon entropy estimation. This result suggests that near-term quantum devices with a limited numbers of qubits may already offer advantages for memory-constrained data processing, and motivates further investigation of quantum space advantages in streaming models. Moreover, our techniques suggest that transforming quantum query algorithms into streaming algorithms may provide a general approach to studying quantum space advantages.

Our work also raises several open questions. First, our results are obtained in the multi-pass streaming setting. It is natural to ask whether similar exponential separations can be achieved in the single-pass model. Second, we focus on Shannon entropy. It remains to be understood whether Rényi entropy, Tsallis entropy, and other entropy generalizations also admit quantum space advantages. Third, this work focuses on vector data in the streaming model. A natural extension is to consider matrix data and related tasks. Very recent work
Zhao et al. 2026
has demonstrated exponential space advantages for certain matrix problems under sampling access models, which are fundamentally different from the streaming setting considered here. Whether similar advantages can be achieved in the streaming model remains open.

4
Methods

4.1
Quantum algorithm for Shannon entropy estimation

The idea behind our quantum streaming algorithm is to estimate the Shannon entropy of a data stream by designing a quantum query algorithm that assumes access to a carefully constructed oracle. A natural question is whether this oracle can be implemented, as in Shor’s algorithm
Shor 1994
. Fortunately, we show that it can indeed be realized within the data stream model. This provides a concrete way to translate the quantum query algorithm into a quantum streaming algorithm.

In addition, the query complexity depends on the structure of the underlying distribution. In particular, it is influenced by the presence of a majority element in the stream. To address this issue, we partition the algorithm into two stages. In the first stage, we detect whether a majority element exists. In the second stage, we implement the quantum query algorithm conditioned on this structure. This yields an efficient realization in both cases.

4.1.1
Quantum query algorithm with an implementable oracle

In this section, we present a quantum query algorithm for estimating the Shannon entropy in the streaming setting.

To this end, we begin by reducing the entropy estimation problem to the expectation of a suitably defined random variable derived from the data stream.
For a data stream
A=\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\rangle
, we define
X
based on a random suffix of the stream so that its expectation equals the entropy of the underlying empirical distribution. Specifically, we first draw an position
q
uniformly at random from the set
\{1,\dots,m\}
. Let
r_{q}
denote the number of occurrences of the element
x_{q}
from position
q
to the end of the stream, that is,

r_{q}=\bigl|\{\,j\in\{q,\ldots,m\}:x_{j}=x_{q}\,\}\bigr|.

In other words,
r_{q}
counts how many times the element at the randomly chosen position
q
appears in the remainder of the stream. Define the random variable

X(r_{q})=\lambda_{m}(r_{q})-\lambda_{m}(r_{q}-1),

where
\lambda_{m}(r_{q})=r_{q}\log\frac{m}{r_{q}}
and
\lambda_{m}(0)=0
. For simplicity, we denote by
X_{q}
the random variable
X(r_{q})
associated with position
q
. The construction of this function in terms of
r_{q}
follows the approach outlined in
Chakrabarti et al. 2007
, tracing back to
Alon et al. 1996
. With this definition, it can be shown that

\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{q:x_{q}=i}X_{q}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\log\frac{m}{m_{i}}=H(p).

Therefore, estimating the Shannon entropy reduces to estimating the expectation of
X_{q}
.

We now turn to the quantum setting. Suppose that we are given access to a quantum oracle that outputs
X_{q}
. This is formalized as follows.

Definition 1

(Quantum Oracle
O
)
.

Given a position
q\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, there exists a quantum oracle
O
such that

|q\rangle|0\rangle\xrightarrow{O}|q\rangle|X_{q}\rangle,

acting on
O(\log m+\log n)
qubits of space, corresponding to the encoding of the stream position and the associated estimator value.

With access to this oracle, we can now design a quantum algorithm to estimate
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
efficiently, as stated in Lemma
1
. The proof of Lemma
1
is provided in Supplementary Note 1.

Lemma 1

(Quantum query algorithm.)
.

Let
X_{q}
be a random variable satisfying
-a\leq X_{q}\leq b
for some
a,b\geq 0
, and assume oracle access to
X_{q}
as given in Definition
1
. If
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]\geq 0
, then there exists a quantum query algorithm that estimates an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation
\tilde{\mu}
of the value
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]

using

O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)\sqrt{(a+b)(\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a)}}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\right)

queries, and requiring

O\!\left(\log m+\log n+\log\frac{a+b}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\right)

qubits of space.

Having established the query algorithm, the next step is to convert it into a quantum streaming algorithm. This requires two key considerations: first, constructing the oracle in the streaming setting, and second, ensuring that both the space complexity and the number of passes over the stream meet the desired quantum bounds, thereby exceeding classical methods.
The construction of the oracle is addressed in Lemma
2
, with the proof provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Lemma 2

(Oracle
O
)
.

For any
q\in[m]
with each
x_{q}\in[n]
in the stream
A
, there exists a quantum circuit to implementing
|q\rangle|0\rangle\xrightarrow{O}|q\rangle|X_{q}\rangle
, and using
O(\log m+\log n)
qubits of space in two passes over the stream.

4.1.2
Two-stage quantum streaming algorithm

The complexity of the quantum entropy estimation algorithm depends critically on the expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
in Lemma
1
.

This dependence can be understood directly from the structure of the entropy.
Let
p_{\max}:=\max_{i}p_{i}
denote the largest probability. When a majority element
x
exists with
p_{\max}
close to
1
, the entropy admits the decomposition

H(p)=p_{\max}\log\frac{1}{p_{\max}}+\sum_{i\neq x}p_{i}\log\frac{1}{p_{i}}.

As
p_{\max}\to 1
, the first term vanishes since
\log(1/p_{\max})\to 0
, while the second term is suppressed by the remaining
1-p_{\max}
. Consequently, when a majority element dominates the stream,
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]=H(p)
becomes small, and the factor
1/\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
becomes large. Since the quantum query complexity in Lemma
1
scales with
1/\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
, this leads to large space usage and number of passes in the streaming model.

To overcome this issue, we design a two-stage quantum streaming algorithm that treats streams with and without a majority element separately.

Stage 1: Majority element detection.

We perform two passes over the stream to determine whether there exists an element
x
whose frequency
m_{x}
exceeds
m/2
.
The first pass identifies a candidate majority element
x
using the method of
Boyer and Moore 1991
, and the second pass counts its frequency
m_{x}
. If
m_{x}>m/2
, it corresponds to the frequency of the majority element.

Stage 2: Entropy estimation.

Depending on the outcome of Stage 1, we proceed as follows.

Case 1: No majority element (
m_{x}\leq m/2
).

In this regime, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
is bounded below by a constant. We directly apply the quantum expectation estimation procedure (Lemma
1
) in the streaming setting to obtain an estimate
\tilde{\mu}
of
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
that satisfies

|H(p)-\tilde{\mu}|\leq\varepsilon H(p).

By Lemma
1
, this requires

O\!\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log m}}{\varepsilon}\right)

passes over the stream and

O\!\left(\log m+\log n+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)

quantum and classical bits of space.

Case 2: Majority element present (
m_{x}>m/2
).

When a majority element exists, the expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
may be too small for direct estimation. To address this, we remove all occurrences of the majority element
x
and consider the induced substream of length
m-m_{x}
. Let
X^{\prime}_{q}
denote the corresponding random variable on the reduced stream, and let
\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}
be an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of
\mathbb{E}[X^{\prime}_{q}]
obtained via similar quantum procedure in case 1. We have

\left|H(p)-\frac{m-m_{x}}{m}\,\tilde{\mu}^{\prime}+\frac{m_{x}}{m}\log\frac{m}{m_{x}}\right|\leq\varepsilon H(p).

In this case, the pass complexity and space complexity are the same as in Case 1.

In summary, the two-stage algorithm produces an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of the Shannon entropy in both scenarios. Full implementation details and the corresponding complexity analysis are provided in the Supplementary Note 3. We summarize the algorithm’s result in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

For parameters
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, there exists an
O(\log(1/\delta)\sqrt{\log m}/\varepsilon)
-pass quantum streaming algorithm that outputs an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation to the entropy of a data stream. The algorithm uses

O\!\left(\log m+\log n+\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)

quantum and classical bits of space.

4.2
Classical space lower bounds for streaming entropy estimation

We establish a classical space lower bound for streaming entropy estimation via a reduction from the Gap Hamming Distance (GHD) problem. In the GHD problem, two parties, Alice and Bob, are given binary strings
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, and must distinguish between the following two cases:

\text{GHD}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}\text{near},&\text{if }d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\frac{m}{2}-\sqrt{m},\\
\text{far},&\text{if }d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq\frac{m}{2}+\sqrt{m}.\end{cases}

where
d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
denotes the Hamming distance. It is known that any randomized communication protocol for GHD requires
\Omega(m)
bits of communication
Chakrabarti et al. 2007
.

To reduce GHD to entropy estimation, we construct a data stream
A
of length
2m
over a polynomial-size universe determined by
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. The stream is formed by concatenating two substreams
A_{\mathbf{x}}
and
A_{\mathbf{y}}
, each of length
m
, generated respectively by Alice and Bob. The resulting empirical distribution has Shannon entropy

H(A)=\log m+\frac{d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{m},

so that the entropy directly encodes the Hamming distance between the inputs. Thus, distinguishing the “near” and “far” cases reduces to approximating the entropy within sufficiently small multiplicative error.

This encoding yields an entropy gap of
\Theta(1/\sqrt{m})
between the two cases. Since
H=\Theta(\log m)
, a multiplicative
\varepsilon
-approximation incurs additive error
O(\varepsilon\log m)
, so distinguishing the cases requires
\varepsilon\log m<1/\sqrt{m}
. This implies

m=\Theta\!\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}\right).

A
T
-pass streaming algorithm using
S
bits of memory induces a
(2T-1)
-round communication protocol with total communication at most
(2T-1)S
. Combining this with the
\Omega(m)
lower bound for GHD gives
(2T-1)S=\Omega(m)
, and hence
S=\Omega(m/T)
. Substituting the above choice of
m
yields

S=\Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{T\varepsilon^{2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}\right).

We summarize the result in the following theorem. The full proof is deferred to Supplementary Note 4.

Theorem 2

(Lower bound on streaming entropy estimation)
.

Any randomized
T
-pass streaming algorithm that outputs an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of the Shannon entropy must use

\Omega\!\left(\frac{1}{T\varepsilon^{2}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)}\right)

bits of space.

Appendix A
Supplemental Note 1. Proof of Lemma
1
–quantum query algorithm

In this section, we describe a quantum query algorithm which, given oracle access to a random variable
X_{q}
, outputs an estimate of its expectation
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
with high probability. Our quantum query algorithm assumes the existence of a realizable quantum oracle
O
that implements the mapping
|q\rangle|0\rangle\xrightarrow{O}|q\rangle|X_{q}\rangle
using
O(\log m+\log n)
qubits of space. A formal description of the oracle
O
is provided in Section
B
. We now prove the correctness of Lemma
1
by describing the quantum query algorithm.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

We first check that the expected value of
X_{q}
is indeed the desired quantity

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{q:x_{q}=i}X_{q}\\
&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{q:x_{q}=i}\lambda_{m}(r_{q})-\lambda_{m}(r_{q}-1)\\
&=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\log\frac{m}{m_{i}}=H(p)\end{split},

(1)

where
\lambda_{m}(r_{q})=r_{q}\log\frac{m}{r_{q}}
and
\lambda_{m}(0)=0
.

We then describe how to obtain an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of
H(p)
, i.e., an estimate
\tilde{H}(p)
that satisfies
|\tilde{H}(p)-H(p)|\leq\varepsilon H(p)
with probability at least
1-\delta
. This requires
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]\geq 0
, otherwise the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation cannot be achieved. Next, we reduce the relative estimation of
H(p)
to that of
\nu:=\frac{H(p)+a}{a+b}\in[0,1]
. We then employ a quantum subroutine to approximate
\nu
, which in turn provides an approximation of
H(p)
. Finally, we analyze the overall resource required by the algorithm, including space complexity and the number of oracle calls.

Define a new random variable
Y_{q}=(X_{q}+a)/(a+b)\in[0,1]
, and let
\nu=\mathbb{E}[Y_{q}]=(H(p)+a)/(a+b)
. Assume access to a coherent sampler
O_{X_{q}}
, which prepare the state

O_{X_{q}}|0\rangle=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{p_{q}}|\psi_{q}\rangle|X_{q}\rangle,

(2)

where
\mathcal{Q}
represents the set of possible positions for
q
,
p_{q}
is the probability of the random variable
X_{q}
, and
|\psi_{q}\rangle
being the corresponding normalized quantum states. Prior to estimating an approximation of
\nu
, we prepare the quantum state

\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{p_{q}}|\psi_{q}\rangle|X_{q}\rangle|Y_{q}\rangle,

(3)

where
Y_{q}=\frac{a+X_{q}}{a+b}
, which can be realized via a quantum arithmetic operation. Then, we apply a controlled rotation
R_{y}(2\arcsin{\sqrt{Y_{q}}}):|Y_{q}\rangle|0\rangle\to|Y_{q}\rangle(\sqrt{Y_{q}}|1\rangle+\sqrt{1-Y_{q}}|0\rangle)
to the above state, yielding

\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}\sqrt{p_{q}}|\psi_{q}\rangle|X_{q}\rangle|Y_{q}\rangle\left(\sqrt{Y_{q}}|1\rangle+\sqrt{1-Y_{q}}|0\rangle\right).

(4)

This defines the unitary operator
U
as

U|0\rangle=\sqrt{\nu}|\psi_{1}\rangle|Y_{q}\rangle|1\rangle+\sqrt{1-\nu}|\psi_{0}\rangle|Y_{q}\rangle|0\rangle,

(5)

where
\nu=\mathbb{E}[Y_{q}]
denotes the expected value of the random variable
Y_{q}
, and
|\psi_{1}\rangle
and
|\psi_{0}\rangle
are two mutually orthogonal states. The probability of measuring “1” in the last qubit is given by

\Pr\left(1\right)=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}\cdot Y_{q}=\mathbb{E}[Y_{q}]=\nu.

(6)

Note that
\nu=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}\cdot Y_{q}
is sufficient to recover
\mu=\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}\cdot X_{q}
, since

(a+b)\nu-a=(a+b)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}\cdot Y_{q}-a=(a+b)\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}\cdot\frac{a+X_{q}}{a+b}-a=\mu,

(7)

where
Y_{q}=\frac{a+X_{q}}{a+b}
and
\sum_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}p_{q}=1
. Now we can reduce the relative estimation of

to the relative estimation of
\nu:=\frac{H(p)+a}{a+b}
. Given a parameter
\tilde{\varepsilon}
and an oracle

, the standard amplitude estimation algorithm

can be used to estimate an
(\tilde{\varepsilon},\delta)
-approximation of
\nu
. Specifically, to obtain this estimate
\tilde{\nu}
, one must consider the error between the estimate
\tilde{\nu}
and the true value
\nu
, which determines the accuracy parameter for the amplitude estimation algorithm:

\left|\tilde{\nu}-\nu\right|\leq C\left(\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{t}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\right),

(8)

where
t
denotes the algorithm’s accuracy parameter and
C
is a universal constant. To achieve a relative error bound
\left|\tilde{\nu}-\nu\right|\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}\nu
, it suffices to take
t=O(1/(\tilde{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\nu}))
. This estimation succeeds with probability at least
8/\pi^{2}
, which can be amplified to
1-\delta
by repeating the procedure
O\big(\log(1/\delta)\big)
times. However, providing only an approximate estimate of
\nu
is insufficient to achieve an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of
H(p)
, as the relationship between the parameters
\tilde{\varepsilon}
and
\varepsilon
has not been established, and therefore no guarantee can be provided for attaining an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of
H(p)
. We define the entropy estimator as
\tilde{H}(p):=(a+b)\tilde{\nu}-a
, which is determined by the approximation
\tilde{\nu}
of
\nu
. Then,

|\tilde{H}(p)-H(p)|=(a+b)|\tilde{\nu}-\nu|\leq(a+b)\tilde{\varepsilon}\nu.

(9)

To ensure a relative error bounded by
|\tilde{H}(p)-H(p)|\leq\varepsilon H(p)
, it suffices to set

\tilde{\varepsilon}=\frac{\varepsilon H(p)}{(a+b)\nu}.

(10)

Then, with
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]=H(p)
, and by substituting
\tilde{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon H(p)/(a+b)\nu
and
\nu=(H(p)+a)/(a+b)
, we set the accuracy parameter as

t=O\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(a+b)(\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a)}}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\Bigg).

(11)

Given the parameters
a,b,t,\varepsilon,\delta
, as well as the knowledge of the oracle
U
and
\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]
, quantum amplitude estimation can be employed to obtain a
\left(\frac{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}{\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a},\delta\right)
-approximation of
\nu
. Consequently, the estimated value
\tilde{\nu}
can be used to compute an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-approximation of
H(p)
,

|\tilde{H}(p)-H(p)|=(a+b)|\tilde{\nu}-\nu|\leq(a+b)\tilde{\varepsilon}\nu=(a+b)\frac{\varepsilon H(p)}{(a+b)\nu}\cdot\nu=\varepsilon H(p).

(12)

Moreover, we analyze the overall resource requirements of the algorithm. In terms of space complexity, a coherent sampler requires
O(\log m+\log n)
qubits, as it generates the distribution of all random variables. Quantum arithmetic operations contribute at most
\log(a+b)
qubits to store the value of
Y_{q}
, while a controlled rotation
R_{y}(2\arcsin{\sqrt{Y_{q}}})
adds one additional qubit. Furthermore, following the analysis of quantum amplitude estimation, the algorithm requires an extra quantum register to store the estimate of
\nu
, which scales logarithmically with the number of coherent sampler calls, i.e.,
O\left(\log\frac{\sqrt{(a+b)(\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a)}}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\right)

O\left(\log m+\log n+\log\frac{a+b}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\right).

(13)

The total number of oracle
O

O\Bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(a+b)(\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a)}}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\cdot\log(1/\delta)\Bigg)=O\left(\frac{\log(1/\delta)\sqrt{(a+b)(\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]+a)}}{\varepsilon\mathbb{E}[X_{q}]}\right).

(14)

The correctness (in terms of relative error and failure probability) follows from the correctness guarantee of the underlying quantum amplitude estimation subroutine.

Appendix D
Supplemental Note 4. Proof of Theorem
2

In this section, we prove Theorem
2
by reducing the Gap Hamming Distance (GHD) problem to streaming entropy estimation, thereby establishing a space complexity lower bound for any multi-pass streaming entropy algorithm. We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

We begin by describing a reduction from the GHD problem to the entropy estimation problem. This reduction takes the input of the GHD problem and produces a decision on whether the input pair belongs to the “near” or “far” category based on the result of the streaming entropy algorithm. A key prerequisite for establishing a space complexity lower bound for multi-pass streaming entropy algorithms is to transform any such algorithm into a multi-round randomized communication protocol for GHD.

Before presenting the reduction, we formally define the GHD problem.

Definition 2

(Definition of GHD)
.

Two parties, Alice and Bob, each hold binary strings
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{j}\}^{m},\mathbf{y}=\{y_{j}\}^{m}\in\{0,1\}^{m}
, respectively, and must decide whether the Hamming distance
d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
satisfies one of the following conditions:

d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq m/2-\sqrt{m}\quad\text{or}\quad d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq m/2+\sqrt{m}.

The goal is to determine the value of

\text{GHD}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\begin{cases}\text{near},&\text{if $d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq m/2-\sqrt{m}$},\\
\text{far},&\text{if $d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq m/2+\sqrt{m}$}.\end{cases}

(47)

It is known that any randomized protocol for the GHD problem requires
\Omega(m)
bits of communication
Chakrabarti and Regev 2011
.

The reduction proceeds in three steps. First, the input of the GHD problem is mapped to an input stream for entropy estimation. Second, we show that the estimated entropy from the protocol can be used to solve GHD by selecting an appropriate error parameter
\varepsilon
. Third, a multi-pass streaming algorithm is employed to construct a multi-round communication protocol. We now detail the reduction process.

First, Alice and Bob jointly construct the stream
A
for the entropy estimation problem based on their respective inputs from the GHD problem. Specifically, each party constructs the substreams
A_{\mathbf{x}}
and
A_{\mathbf{y}}
based on their inputs
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}

as follows:

•

, where the mapping function

maps each
x_{j}
to
j+mx_{j}
for
j\in[m]
;

•

Bob constructs the sequence
A_{\mathbf{y}}=\{j+my_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
, where the mapping function
f(y_{j},j,m)
maps each
y_{j}
to
j+my_{j}
for

.

The full stream is then given by

Second, we will analyze the estimated entropy

\displaystyle H(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{2d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{2m}\log 2m+\left(m-d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)\frac{2}{2m}\log\frac{2m}{2}=\log m+\frac{d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{m},

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)
