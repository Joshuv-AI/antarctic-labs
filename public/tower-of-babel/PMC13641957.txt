Insulin Resistance and Hepatocellular Carcinoma Development in Chronic Hepatitis B Patients Under Antiviral Therapy
Authors: Dong Hyun Sinn, Kyung-Ah Kim, Jung Il Lee, Geum-Youn Gwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a significant concern in patients with liver cirrhosis due to chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, even among those who achieve a virological response with antiviral therapy. This study aimed to evaluate whether insulin resistance (IR), assessed using the Homeostatic Model Assessment 2 for Insulin Resistance (HOMA2-IR) index, can serve as a surrogate marker for stratifying HCC risk in this population.MethodsIn this multicenter prospective cohort study, 334 patients with HBV-related cirrhosis who achieved virological response to nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy were enrolled. The primary outcome was the incidence of HCC during follow-up. Baseline HOMA2-IR was calculated using serum C-peptide and fasting glucose levels.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 7.45 years (range, 0.5–8.9 years), 62 patients (18.6%) developed HCC. In a fully adjusted Cox proportional hazards model, each unit increase in HOMA2-IR was associated with a higher risk of HCC (hazard ratio [HR], 1.53; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.16–2.04). Patients with HOMA2-IR ≥ 2.3 had a significantly higher 5-year cumulative incidence of HCC compared to those with HOMA2-IR < 2.3 (30.6% vs. 12.3%; adjusted HR, 2.62; 95% CI, 1.40–4.91). The association between HOMA2-IR and HCC risk was consistent across subgroups, including those with and without metabolic comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia.ConclusionIR, as measured by HOMA2-IR, independently predicted HCC development in cirrhotic patients with chronic HBV infection. HOMA2-IR may serve as a useful surrogate marker for identifying high-risk individuals. Intervention trials are warranted to determine whether reducing HOMA2-IR can lower HCC risk in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a leading cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a major contributor to cancer-related mortality worldwide.12 Although antiviral therapy for HBV using nucleos(t)ide analogue (NA) effectively reduces HBV replication and improves liver-related outcomes, HCC continues to develop in patients receiving long-term NA therapy.345 This suggests that additional risk factors contributing to HCC risk should be identified and, if possible, managed in patients with chronic HBV infection undergoing antiviral therapy.

Epidemiological studies have shown that patients with diabetes have an increased risk of developing cancer, including HCC.67 Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between diabetes and cancer,8 and insulin resistance (IR) has been suggested as a major underlying factor in cancer development.9 Several pharmacologic agents that can directly or indirectly reduce IR are available.10 From this perspective, identifying and reducing IR may represent a promising strategy to lower HCC risk in patients with chronic HBV infection undergoing antiviral therapy.

There are several ways to quantify IR.1112 Among them, the Homeostasis Model Assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and its updated version, Homeostatic Model Assessment 2 (HOMA2), are established surrogate markers for IR.1314 In a previous retrospective cohort study involving 1,696 patients with chronic HBV infection from our group, we found that HOMA2-IR was associated with the risk of HCC.15 However, the study population was recruited from individuals undergoing a health check-up program at a health promotion center, the incidence of HCC was low (24 patients [1.4%] over a median follow-up of 5.0 years), and, due to the retrospective design, some key variables—such as antiviral therapy status and serum HBV DNA levels—were missing. These limitations made it difficult to determine whether HOMA2-IR could serve as a reliable surrogate marker for HCC risk in patients with chronic HBV infection. Therefore, in the present study, we focused on cirrhotic patients receiving NA therapy who demonstrated a virological response, a population in which HCC risk remains substantial despite effective antiviral treatment.4 This study aimed to evaluate whether HOMA2-IR could serve as a surrogate marker for stratifying HCC risk using a prospective cohort study design.

METHODS

Study design, setting and participants

This prospective cohort study was conducted at three tertiary institutions in South Korea. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) adults with chronic HBV infection aged 30 years or older; 2) liver cirrhosis, defined by meeting any of the following criteria—histological evidence of cirrhosis within one year of enrollment; radiological or endoscopic evidence of cirrhosis characterized by a nodular liver surface, caudate lobe hypertrophy, splenomegaly, or varices (abnormally enlarged veins detected by upper endoscopy or cross-sectional imaging)16 within six months of enrollment; liver stiffness measurement > 9 kPa by transient elastography within one year of enrollment17; or serum aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index > 1 within one month of enrollment, which corresponds to the intermediate cut-off balancing a sensitivity (66.1%) and a specificity (73.7%) for cirrhosis18; and 3) receiving NA therapy and showing virological response, defined as serum HBV DNA levels < 2,000 IU/mL at the time of enrollment. Exclusion criteria were: 1) co-infection with hepatitis C virus or human immunodeficiency virus; 2) heavy alcohol intake (≥ 40 g/day in women and ≥ 60 g/day in men)19 or other chronic liver diseases such as autoimmune hepatitis; 3) current or past history of malignancy; and 4) current or past history of organ transplantation. Eligibility was screened at outpatient clinics over a two-year period (March 2016 to February 2018). After obtaining informed consent, blood samples were collected to calculate the HOMA2-IR index for each participant. A total of 356 patients were enrolled during the study period and underwent HOMA2-IR assessment. Of these, 22 patients were excluded from the analysis for the following reasons: 19 patients violated inclusion criteria (age < 30 years [n = 1]; serum HBV DNA levels > 2,000 IU/mL at enrollment [n = 4]; no evidence of cirrhosis [n = 14]); one patient had C-peptide levels outside the reference range used to calculate HOMA2-IR (0.2–3.5 nmol/L); and two were lost to follow-up within six months. Thus, 334 patients were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Variables

This was a prospective observational cohort study in which HOMA2-IR values were collected for research purposes. No specific intervention or management targeting IR was applied. At enrollment, structured interviews were conducted to obtain information related to potential risk factors for HCC. Fasting glucose and C-peptide levels were measured at the time of enrollment for research purposes, while other variables were collected from data generated during routine clinical practice. The collected variables included age, sex, body mass index calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared, history of hepatic decompensation, first-degree family history of HCC, alcohol use (current or none), smoking status (current or none), waist circumference, presence of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) status at initiation of antiviral therapy, current medications, and levels of alanine aminotransferase, albumin, bilirubin, platelets, and alpha-fetoprotein (AFP). Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting serum glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, a history of diabetes, or current use of insulin or antidiabetic medications. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg, a history of hypertension, or current use of antihypertensive medications. Dyslipidemia was defined as plasma triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein-cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men and < 50 mg/dL in women, or use of lipid-lowering medications. Abdominal obesity was defined when waist circumference was ≥ 90 cm in men, and ≥ 85 cm in women.20 The modified PAGE-B score was also calculated for each participant.21

IR was estimated by calculating HOMA2-IR using the computer-based model provided by the Diabetes Trials Unit, Oxford Centre for Diabetes, Endocrinology and Metabolism (http://www.dtu.ox.ac.uk/homa).22 HOMA2-IR can be calculated using either serum insulin or C-peptide levels. As HOMA is a steady-state model, clinically accepted input values should be used in fasting patients. The accepted input ranges were as follows: plasma C-peptide level, 0.2–3.5 nmol/L; fasting glucose level, 3.5–25.0 mmol/L. Serum C-peptide levels were measured using a C-peptide immunoradiometric assay kit (Immunotech, Prague, Czech Republic), with a detection range of 0.01–566.10 nmol/L. We used the C-peptide–based model because serum C-peptide is not significantly cleared by the liver, whereas insulin clearance is impaired in advanced hepatic fibrosis.2324

Follow-up

Patients were managed by their attending physicians according to clinical discretion. Management included HCC surveillance using liver ultrasonography and serum AFP measurement at six-month intervals. Patients were followed from the date of cohort entry until the diagnosis of HCC or the last outpatient clinic follow-up—whichever occurred first (reference date: April 30, 2025). The primary outcome of interest was incident HCC during follow-up. HCC was diagnosed according to regional HCC guidelines.25

Statistical methods

Variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range), or number (percentage), as appropriate. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to assess the association between HOMA2-IR and HCC risk. HOMA2-IR was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per unit increase) and as a categorical variable (quartiles and dichotomized using a receiver operating characteristic [ROC]–derived cut-off value). Models were adjusted for potential confounders, including age (per year), sex, current smoking (yes vs. no), current alcohol use (yes vs. no), first-degree family history of HCC (yes vs. no), diabetes (yes vs. no), hypertension (yes vs. no), dyslipidemia (yes vs. no), body mass index (kg/m2), HBeAg status at initiation of antiviral therapy (positive vs. negative), modified PAGE-B score (high/intermediate vs. low), and previous hepatic decompensation (yes vs. no). Cumulative incidence of HCC was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and incidence rates between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age, sex, presence of metabolic comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia), and modified PAGE-B score. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Samsung Medical Center (IRB number: 2016-01-103). Informed consent was submitted by all subjects when they were enrolled.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics of participants are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 57.7 years, and 60.8% were male. Metabolic comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia) were present in 127 participants (38.0%). There were 41 patients with decompensated cirrhosis, defined by ascites (n = 35), variceal bleeding (n = 2), hepatic encephalopathy (n = 1), or combination of ascites, varix bleeding or hepatic encephalopathy (n = 3). All the patients had radiological evidence of cirrhosis, and no one was included solely based on histologic, endoscopic or non-invasive criteria. Among 132 patients out of 334 (39.5%) had available liver stiffness measurement value (> 11 kPa in 42 patients [32%], 9–11 kPa in 20 patients (15%) and < 9 kPa in 70 patients [53%]) (Table 1). At cohort entry, HBV DNA levels were undetectable (< 20 IU/mL) in 97.9% of participants. The median HOMA2-IR value was 1.49 (interquartile range: 1.14–1.98). Values were missing in 7 patients (2.1%) for abdominal obesity, 8 patients (2.4%) for HBeAg status, and 2 patients (0.6%) for serum creatinine level.

During a median follow-up of 7.5 years (range: 0.5–8.9 years), 62 participants (18.6%) developed HCC. During the study period, there were two cases of mortality (both diagnosed with HCC prior to death) and two cases of new-onset hepatic decompensation (both without HCC). The cumulative incidence of HCC was 10.2%, 14.7%, and 17.5% at 3, 5, and 7 years, respectively. In the multivariable-adjusted model, the variance inflation factor was tested for multicollinearity, which showed no evidence of multicollinearity among the analyzed variables. HOMA2-IR was associated with increased HCC risk, with a fully adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 1.53 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.16–2.04) per unit increase (Table 2). When participants were categorized into quartiles, the risk of HCC increased in those within the third and fourth quartiles compared to the first quartile, in a dose-dependent manner (Table 2). The ROC curve analysis showed that the cut-off value of 2.3 provided the highest sensitivity and specificity with an area under the curve of 0.592 (Supplementary Fig. 1). When categorized into two groups based on a ROC–derived cut-off value of 2.3, participants with HOMA2-IR ≥ 2.3 had a higher 5-year cumulative incidence of HCC than those with HOMA2-IR < 2.3 (30.6% vs. 12.3%, P < 0.05; Fig. 2) and demonstrated an increased risk of HCC (Table 2). Findings were virtually the same when analysis was limited to patients without previous hepatic decompensation (n = 293) (Supplementary Table 1). In subgroup analyses, the association between HOMA2-IR and HCC risk remained consistent across all subgroups examined, without significant interaction (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this prospective cohort study of 334 patients with chronic HBV–related cirrhosis receiving NA therapy and achieving virological response, higher IR, as measured by HOMA2-IR, was independently associated with an increased risk of HCC. When participants were categorized based on a ROC–derived cut-off, those with HOMA2-IR ≥ 2.3 exhibited a high cumulative incidence of HCC (30.6% at 5 years). This association remained robust across clinically relevant subgroups, including individuals with and without metabolic comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, hypertension, or dyslipidemia. Since patients with a history of hepatic decompensation may have a different natural course compared to those with compensated cirrhosis, we performed a subgroup analysis limited to patients without prior decompensation. The results were consistent, indicating that HOMA2-IR was associated with HCC risk irrespective of hepatic decompensation. A key strength of this study is its prospective design involving cirrhotic patients with confirmed virological suppression, a substantial follow-up duration (median, 7.5 years), and a relatively high number of HCC cases (n = 62), which provided sufficient statistical power. These findings suggest a potential causal relationship between IR and HCC development and indicate that HOMA2-IR may serve as a surrogate marker for identifying high-risk individuals among virologically suppressed cirrhotic patients with chronic HBV infection undergoing antiviral therapy.

Consistent with our findings, epidemiological data support an association between IR and increased risk of liver cancer.26 In a nested case–control study, HOMA-IR was identified as a risk factor for incident primary liver cancer.27 HOMA2-IR has also been linked to HCC risk in patients with chronic HBV infection.15 Among patients receiving NA therapy, the presence of diabetes has been shown to increase the risk of HCC.28 Although the exact mechanisms linking IR to HCC are not fully understood, IR induces compensatory hyperinsulinemia and increases levels of bioavailable insulin-like growth factor, which may promote tumor initiation and progression.29 IR is also strongly associated with obesity and hepatic inflammation—both of which may contribute to tumorigenesis through cytokine release and/or the generation of oxidative stress.30 Moreover, IR is associated with severity of liver disease,31 and liver fibrosis progression.32 Therefore, IR may increase HCC risk by directly participating in hepatocarcinogenesis, or indirectly promoting hepatocarcinogenesis through fibrosis progression, or by both mechanisms.

In the present study, IR was estimated using HOMA2-IR derived from C-peptide levels. While HOMA-IR is calculated using fasting insulin level, HOMA2-IR can be derived from either insulin or C-peptide level. Insulin is primarily cleared by the liver,23 whereas C-peptide undergoes negligible hepatic clearance.24 Therefore, it is likely that C-peptide–based HOMA2-IR reflects IR accurately by avoiding the confounding effects of impaired hepatic insulin clearance in patients with hepatic dysfunction such as cirrhosis. HOMA2-IR is a relatively simple biomarker that can be calculated using routine blood tests. The measurement of HOMA2-IR is straightforward and readily applicable in clinical practice. In subgroup analyses, we observed an association between HOMA2-IR and HCC risk across all subgroups examined, including those with and without metabolic comorbidities (Table 3). This finding suggests that even patients without overt metabolic comorbidities may benefit from HOMA2-IR assessment. Identifying patients with elevated IR also has potential therapeutic implications. Several interventions are known to influence IR,33 and variations in cancer risk have been reported among patients with diabetes depending on the type of antidiabetic medication used.3435 Therefore, assessing HOMA2-IR may help not only to identify high-risk patients, but also to guide therapeutic strategies aimed at improving insulin sensitivity. To date, there has been no standardized cut-off for HOMA2-IR to define IR.36 In our previous study with chronic hepatitis B patients recruited from a health check-up cohort, the optimal HOMA2-IR cut-off for stratifying HCC risk which was derived from ROC curve analysis was 1.2. In the present study comprised of patients with cirrhosis, ROC curve analysis identified a cut-off of 2.3 with the highest sensitivity and specificity for stratifying HCC risk. It is assumed that liver disease severity is responsible for determining the optimal cut-offs in predicting HCC risk, which requires further evaluation.

This study has several limitations. All participants were Korean, among whom HBV genotype C is predominant1; therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups and other HBV genotypes remains uncertain. The cohort consisted exclusively of cirrhotic patients, so the applicability to non-cirrhotic patients with chronic HBV infection is yet to be determined. Residual confounding may also be present. We defined diabetes as fasting serum glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL (n = 9), a prior history of diabetes (n = 3), or current use of insulin or other antidiabetic agents (n = 50). Since we did not evaluate HbA1c or 2-hour plasma glucose level during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, there might be undiagnosed diabetes patients, leading to potential misclassification. Also, patients were treated with various antidiabetic medications, including DPP-4 inhibitors (n = 15), SGLT-2 inhibitors (n = 1), insulin (n = 8), and thiazolidinediones (n = 2). Type of antidiabetic medication can be associated with HCC risk,37 however, due to the small number of patients using each drug class, adjustment for these medications as covariates was not feasible. There was no consideration for patients with impaired renal function. C-peptide is cleared by the kidneys, and its circulating levels can be elevated in patients with impaired renal function.38 Also, renal dysfunction may affect HCC incidence.39 Although serum creatinine level was not associated with HCC risk (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.14–4.06; P = 0.749) in the fully adjusted model, the generalizability of our findings to patients with renal impairment remains to be evaluated in the future studies. The discriminative ability of HOMA2-IR was modest, as reflected by an AUC of 0.592 in ROC analysis. This limited performance may be attributable to the multifactorial nature of hepatocarcinogenesis, such as the severity of underlying liver disease, alcohol use, smoking status, antiviral treatment response, etc., which cannot be fully captured by HOMA2-IR alone. HOMA2-IR was measured only at baseline; no follow-up assessments were performed. Hence, although we proposed that interventions aimed at reducing HOMA2-IR might lower HCC risk, it remains unknown whether reducing HOMA2-IR is associated with a corresponding decrease in HCC incidence.

The burden of metabolic comorbidities is increasing in the modern era.40 Managing metabolic health is becoming an important consideration in the antiviral treatment era of chronic hepatitis B.41 The high incidence of HCC observed in this study underscores that viral suppression alone is insufficient to mitigate HCC risk.41 This study demonstrates that IR, as assessed by a simple blood-based model (HOMA2-IR), may serve as a valuable surrogate marker for identifying patients at high risk of HCC. Chemoprevention strategies aimed at reducing IR may represent a promising approach for cirrhotic patients with chronic HBV infection who have achieved a virological response to NA therapy, and warrant further investigation.
