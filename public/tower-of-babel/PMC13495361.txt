MicNet: integrating spatially resolved transcriptomes and pathology images by contrastive deep neural network
Authors: Shidan Wang, Qin Zhou, Yuansheng Zhou, Peiran Quan, Xue Xiao, Zifan Gu, Lei Guo, Yueshuang Xu, Danni Luo, Ruichen Rong, Xiaowei Zhan, Tao Wang, Lin Xu, Guanghua Xiao, Yang Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent breakthroughs in spatially resolved transcriptomic technologies have enabled molecular characterization of cells while preserving spatial and morphological contexts. However, integrating transcriptomic profiles and pathology images remains a challenge. Here, we developed a novel unsupervised representation learning method, MicNet, to project pathology image and transcriptomic data onto a shared representative domain for biological interpretation. MicNet maximizes the correlation between image and molecular features from the same sample while minimizing it for different samples. MicNet outperformed existing approaches in multiple analysis tasks, including spatial domain detection, spatially variable gene identification, and spatial organization visualization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04090-2.Keywords: Representation learning, Multi-omics, Spatial transcriptomics, Pathology images, Deep learning

Background

Exploring the spatial organization and morphology of cells in tandem with their mRNA expression is crucial for understanding how diverse cellular origins contribute to the formation of distinctively structured and functional tissues. The recent advancements in spatially resolved transcriptomics (SRT) technologies, notably image-based methods such as STARMap [1], seqFISH [2, 3], and MERFISH [4, 5], as well as sequencing-based techniques such as Slide-seq [6, 7], HDST [8], and Visium by 10X, have revolutionized our capacity to profile individual cells at the molecular level while preserving their spatial orientation and histological context.

SRT datasets typically include hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or immunofluorescence (IF) stained tissue images paired with gene expression measurements at specific spatial locations. While gene expression profiling offers insights into transcriptional events, tissue images reveal cell morphology, spatial organization, and cell interactions within microenvironments. Coordinated spatial variations in gene expression frequently coincide with morphological shifts in tissues and cells. Thus, identifying spatial patterns shared between transcriptomic and image data reveals significant biological patterns. However, integrating transcriptomics and image data is challenging due to their origins in separate information domains characterized by unique dimensions and analytical methods. To address these challenges, Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) was introduced to link data from various modalities using learned feature embeddings rather than predefined features. However, CCA is limited by its sensitivity to noise, and its reliance on linear transformations, which cannot capture complex non-linear relationships among different data modalities. Additionally, CCA and its derivatives, such as deep CCA [9], struggle with scaling in large datasets due to the computational burden of matrix decomposition and full-batch optimization. Although stLearn has been proposed to use deep learning to extract image features, its model lacks optimization flexibility across different tissues or tasks [10]. Hence, a scalable, adaptable, and fully trainable method is essential for robustly extracting the spatially variable information underlying molecular and image data from SRT experiments.

Identifying spatial domains is a critical step in SRT data analysis, serving as the foundation for subsequent tasks like cell type annotation and the identification of spatially variable gene(s) (SVGs). However, current existing clustering methods [11–16] that originally designed for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) are not well-suited for direct application to SRT data, as they do not integrate spatial locations and morphological context. Recently, two new state-of-the-art algorithms, BayesSpace [17] and SpaGCN [18], were developed for spatial domain identification with SRT data. BayesSpace employs a Bayesian approach, prioritizing physically close spots or cells, while SpaGCN applies a graph convolutional network (GCN)-based approach to integrate gene expression with spatial locations by constructing an undirected weighted graph. Although both methods can cluster spot-level or cell-level data into distinct spatial domains, they only use limited spatial information (e.g., a single weighted mean of pixel intensities in red, green, and blue (RGB) channels), rather than comprehensive cell-to-cell distances and interaction information from the pathology image. Thus, new data integration methods that combine pathology images and transcriptomics data are needed to enhance the accuracy and sensitivity of downstream analysis.

Here, we developed an unsupervised representation learning method, Molecular-Image Coding Network (MicNet), which projects both spatial transcriptomics and image data onto a common representation domain without requiring manual annotations for SRT data. MicNet links multi-modality data through corresponding end-to-end trainable deep neural network encoders and extracts the biologically meaningful information with a high signal-to-noise ratio utilizing contrastive learning [19]. Contrastive learning, which enhances representation by contrasting positive and negative instance pairs, demonstrates excellent performance across a wide range of tasks [20–22]. This strategy enables MicNet to handle large-scale, high-dimensional data integration tasks with tissue image and spatial transcriptomics data. In this study, we demonstrated that MicNet is ready to be applied to molecular- and image-based SRT platforms. Our results further showed that MicNet outperformed a broad set of state-of-the-art methods, including BayesSpace [17], SpaGCN [18], GraphST [23], SiGra [24], STCluster [25], xSiGra [26], MISO [27], SpatialGlue [28], STAGATE [29], and SpatialPCA [30], in detecting spatial domains and visualizing spatial organization.

Results

MicNet algorithm design

MicNet is designed to identify the biological signals that influence both molecular profiles and histological phenotypes by translating paired molecular and image data into consistent N-length features through a molecular encoder and an image encoder (Fig. 1). MicNet encourages a dual-contrastive alignment strategy in which paired image and gene features from the same spot are explicitly pulled together, while features from different spots are pushed apart. This approach ensures that the learned embeddings capture biologically meaningful cross-modal relationships. By optimizing the alignment between these paired images and transcriptomic features, MicNet captures shared signals that effectively highlight the underlying biological signals influencing the observed spatial variabilities. The fused N-length features are then used to identify spatial domains in the tissue, representing regions with coherent spatial expression and image patterns. These domains enable further analyses, such as identifying spatial domain-associated spatially variable gene(s) (sdaSVGs) and discerning spatial trajectory patterns. To demonstrate MicNet's utility, we applied it to six public spatial profiling datasets covering various physiological and pathological conditions, including methods like 10X Genomics' Visium and the image-based MERFISH technology.

Schematic diagram of MicNet algorithm. Image and molecular information are separately encoded into image and molecular features. The encoders are trained to extract the shared underlying signals. The features from various domains could be concatenated together for downstream analysis, including spatial domain detection, domain-specific spatially variable gene(s) detection, and spatial trajectory pattern visualization

MicNet stands out by pioneering the integration of molecular and image data, featuring a customizable, trainable image feature extractor specifically optimized for capturing biologically pertinent signals. In contrast, SpaGCN relies on average pixel intensities, while stLearn utilizes a static image feature extractor, both of which overlook tissue architectures and inherent biological processes. MicNet also does not require explicit spatial information, granting flexibility to accommodate diverse tissue sections and datasets. We compared MicNet with ten state-of-the-art methods—GraphST, SiGra, STCluster, xSiGra, MISO, SpatialGlue, BayesSpace, SpaGCN, STAGATE, and SpatialPCA—across six benchmark datasets (MOB, MPB, HBC, MERFISH, ovarian, and prostate; see Methods for details). Across these datasets, MicNet achieved the highest clustering accuracy (ARI) in five cases (MOB, MPB, HBC, MERFISH, and ovarian), consistently outperforming other state-of-the-art methods (Additional file 2: Table S1). In the prostate dataset, MicNet’s performance was comparable to the top-performing methods (GraphST, STAGATE, and xSiGra), further demonstrating its robustness across diverse tissue contexts. Moreover, MicNet offers a modular design that facilitates integration with existing spatial analysis pipelines. For example, spatial information, such as adjacency graphs used in SpaGCN, can be incorporated as additional constraints when clustering MicNet embeddings. Similarly, frameworks like stLearn can directly utilize MicNet embeddings in place of their default image features. In this way, MicNet serves not only as an end-to-end framework but also as a flexible feature extractor that can be combined with other methods.

Application to the mouse olfactory bulb (MOB) dataset

To demonstrate the strength of features corroborated by MicNet, we analyzed the MOB dataset [31], which includes 19,025 genes with five manually annotated regions: granular cell layer (GCL), outer plexiform layer (OPL), glomerular layer (GL), mitral cell layer (MCL), and olfactory nerve layer (ONL) (Fig. 2A). We compared MicNet’s accuracy in identifying spatial domains against ten state-of-the-art methods and quantified the concordance between detected domains and manual annotations. Among all methods, MicNet achieved the highest clustering accuracy, as measured by both the Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI) (Fig. 2B-C, Additional file 1: Fig. S1A, B). Methods such as SpaGCN, GraphST, MISO, and xSigra failed to detect the outer plexiform layer (Fig. 2A, circled in red, Additional file 1: Fig. S1C), whereas MicNet and BayesSpace successfully detected the outer plexiform layer (Fig. 2A). The outer plexiform layer is histologically distinct from its neighboring layers, with significantly fewer nuclei than the neighboring mitral cell layer or glomerular layer. The failure of SpaGCN to capture this information indicates that using simple histological representation, such as a single weighted mean of pixel intensities in RGB channels is insufficient to capture detailed histology information. It is noteworthy that all the five clusters detected by MicNet and BayesSpace agree well with the five cell layers annotated by the pathologist (Fig. 2A), enabling direct assignment of each cluster to a known cell layer. To directly evaluate the contribution of joint feature learning, we compared MicNet’s integrated representation against unimodal counterparts using the mouse olfactory bulb dataset (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). The integrated image and transcriptome representation yielded markedly higher accuracy (ARI = 0.677; AMI consistent with this trend) than unimodal, demonstrating that joint feature learning substantially improves spatial domain detection over unimodal features.

In order to explore domain-specific differentially expressed genes, we then applied two widely applied algorithms on differential expression analysis from edgeR and Seurat packages to detect sdaSVGs based on five known histology domains (clusters) (Fig. 2A, color labels) defined by mouse olfactory bulb dataset [31] (see Method). We compared sdaSVGs defined by MicNet, BayesSpace, and SpaGCN, respectively, with known olfactory bulb-specific gene set defined in Harmonizome database (PMID: 27,374,120) (Fig. 2D, Additional file 1: Fig. S2B). In four out of the five histology domains, the sdaSVGs detected by MicNet showed higher overlap with known olfactory bulb-specific gene set than the ones defined by BayesSpace or SpaGCN, indicating that MicNet-defined sdaSVGs align better with known biological knowledge. The insignificant enrichment of the resting MCL-associated SV genes detected by all three methods is probably due to a few spots (orange spots in Fig. 2A) belonging to the mitral cell layer.

Additionally, as shown in Additional file 1: Fig. S3C, MicNet identified Spon1 and Tcf4 as a GCL&GL-associated SV gene, which is consistent with their molecular functions in promoting neural cell adhesion and neurite extension [32] and influencing the length and number of dendrites in the olfactory bulb [33], respectively; MicNet identified Fstl5 as MCL&OPL-associated SV genes, which is consistent with their molecular functions on maintaining odor perception [34], separately; MicNet identified Pcdh10 as a MCL&ONL-associated SV gene, which is consistent with its known role in olfactory sensory neuron axon guidance [35]; MicNet identified Sall1 as a ONL&OPL-associated SV gene, which is consistent with its molecular function on olfactory nerve extension in the developing olfactory bulb [36]. These results aligned with their functional relevance to the development of olfactory bulb, which further corroborates the biological significance of MicNet-defined sdaSVGs.

To further illustrate MicNet’s ability to uncover molecular mechanisms underlying intercellular communication, we applied a spatial interaction score, defined as the Spearman correlation of gene expression between nearest-neighbor spots across adjacent histological domains. Using this metric, we identified several significant cross-domain correlations (Fig. 2E). We observed a significant correlation between Spon1 expression in GCL cells and Fstl5 expression in MCL cells (Spearman rho = 0.43, P = 9.4 × 10–3), between Fstl5 expression in OPL cells and Ppm1h expression in MCL cells (Spearman rho = 0.42, P = 0.015), and between Tcf4 expression in GL cells and Pcdh10 expression in ONL cells (Spearman rho = 0.61, P = 8.2 × 10–4). All these results suggest previously unknown mechanisms of intercellular communications among distinct spatial domains within the olfactory bulb.

Furthermore, we have also explored the utility of MicNet representations in visualizing spatial organization patterns. Two classic dimension reduction functions, UMAP and t-SNE, were applied to further project the MicNet representations into a two-dimensional space (Fig. 2F). Compared with raw transcriptomics data (Additional file 1: Fig. S4), MicNet features clustered spots from the same histologic regions together and showed higher concordance as estimated by Silhouette score with ground truth annotation (Fig. 2G).

Application to the mouse posterior brain (MPB) dataset

Next, we analyzed a mouse posterior brain (MPB) dataset generated from the 10X Visium technology. We investigated the ability of MicNet in detecting more refined tissue structures. We focused on the spots in manually annotated different isocortex layers together with subregions in hippocampal formation, i.e., pyramidal layer in field CA1 and the subiculum (Fig. 3A-C) following the previous work [29]. Compared with BayesSpace, SpaGCN and eight other state-of-the-art methods, MicNet achieved higher overall clustering accuracy (Fig. 3G, H, Additional file 1: Fig. S5A, B). Notably, MicNet is the only method that effectively distinguished the subiculum and hippocampal CA1 region, while other methods erroneously mixed them together (Fig. 3D-F). Such separation demonstrates MicNet’s effective utilization of morphological information, as the pyramidal layer in hippocampal CA1 region is characterized by compact neuronal somata with a distinct function [30]. Although MicNet achieved superior overall accuracy against ground-truth labels using both ARI and AMI, it misclustered the retrosplenial area as part of the isocortex. In contrast, BayesSpace, SpaGCN, STCluster, Sigra, STAGATE, and SpatialPCA successfully separated the retrosplenial area from the main isocortex layers (Fig. 3D–F, Additional file 1: Fig. S5C). Nevertheless, despite this limitation, MicNet successfully distinguished the posterior parietal association area (PTLp), another isocortex region, from the main isocortex layers (cluster 3 in Fig. 3D, Additional file 1: Fig. S5C). Such separation is consistent with the observation that anatomical gene expressions are different among different isocortex regions [37], emphasizing the necessity for more comprehensive clustering via spatial transcriptomics study with larger scale and higher spatial resolution. Compare with the MicNet’s unimodal counterparts (Additional file 1: Fig. S6), MicNet’s integrated representation achieved the highest accuracy (ARI = 0.284 vs. 0.238 and 0.283).

To further validate the biological significance of spatial domains defined by MicNet, we extracted sdaSVGs by differential expression analysis among different domains. We have identified differentially expressed genes in isocortex, subiculum, and hippocampus, respectively, by edgeR (Fig. 3I) and Seurat approaches (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B). Notably, when comparing with known isocortex- and subiculum-specific gene sets from the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas [38], MicNet outperformed BayesSpace and SpaGCN with higher enrichment based on both edgeR (Fig. 3I) and Seurat approaches (Additional file 1: Fig. S7B), confirming that MicNet-identified spatial domains and domain-specific expressed genes (i.e. sdaSVGs) are consistent with known biological knowledge. This result could not only support the biological significance of spatial domains defined by MicNet but also validate MicNet’s capability in detecting genes that are histology-associated. We also observed MicNet defined sdaSVGs in the posterior brain that have experimental evidence to support their functional relevance. For example, Camk2n1 (P < 0.001 in layer2/3 and layer6) is highly expressed in mouse brain and critical in synaptic long-term potentiation [39]; Lxn [40] (P = 0.01 in subiculum) is specifically expressed in subiculum; Hpca [41] (P < 0.001 in hippocampus) is abundantly expressed in hippocampus and involved in activity-dependent neuron plasticity (Fig. 3J). Taken together, our experiments demonstrated the consistent superiority of MicNet in detecting refined tissue structure and sdaSVGs.

In addition, we have also explored the spatial organization pattern by visualizing MicNet features on a two-dimensional space (Fig. 3K, Additional file 1: Fig. S8). Interestingly, although the image and transcriptomic features extracted by MicNet did not encode the spatial information, both UMAP and tSNE of MicNet features illustrated the layer-wise distribution of isocortex structures. Moreover, MicNet features clearly separated subiculum and hippocampal regions. On the contrary, direct usage of raw transcriptomics data failed to visually distinguish the posterior brain structures and illustrate the spatial organization patterns, with lower concordance with manual annotations (Silhouette score in Fig. 3L) and spatial approximations (rs in Additional file 1: Fig. S8). The spatial organization pattern as visualized by MicNet features provide a powerful tool to understand tissue transcriptomic and morphologic similarities when combined with classic dimension reduction methods.

Application to the human breast cancer (HBC) dataset

In the above examples, we demonstrated MicNet’s ability to accurately identify spatial domains, functionally related genes, and spatial organization patterns for normal developmental tissues (e.g., posterior brain and olfactory bulb). To further validate MicNet’s capability with diseased tissue, we evaluated its performance against 10 state-of-the-art methods using a more complex HBC spatial transcriptomics dataset (Additional file 1: Fig. S9A, B). This dataset is generated by Visium platform, includes 17,649 genes from 2,518 spots. Spots were manually annotated by a pathologist into five histology types: necrosis, immune, invasive tumor, fibrous, and fat (Fig. 4A). MicNet, SpaGCN, STAGATE, and SpatialPCA detected similar patterns across necrosis, immune, invasive tumor, and fibrous domains, whereas BayesSpace, stCluster, and SpatialGlue failed to detect small and scattered immune areas (Fig. 4A, Additional file 1: Fig. S9C). As shown in Fig. 4B and Additional file 1: Fig. S9A-B, MicNet achieved the highest concordance with the ground-truth annotations (ARI = 0.538), outperforming other state-of-the-art methods. A similar performance advantage of MicNet was also observed when compared with unimodal feature representations (Additional file 1: Fig. S10). To further evaluate the robustness of the MicNet model, we introduced Gaussian noise separately into the gene-expression profiles, into the image data, and into both modalities simultaneously. MicNet was then trained on these noisy datasets independently. The performance of spatial domain identification was quantified using ARI and AMI metrics (Additional file 1: Fig. S11). Compared to unimodal models trained with noisy data, which showed a dramatic decrease in accuracy (Image with noise: ARI = 0.148 vs. 0.464 without noise; Gene with noise: ARI = 0.116 vs. 0.270 without noise), MicNet demonstrated substantially higher accuracy when integrating both modalities (Image with noise: ARI = 0.422; Gene with noise: ARI = 0.530). Notably, even when noise was introduced into both image and transcriptomic data simultaneously, MicNet still outperformed unimodal models trained on noisy inputs. These results highlight the importance of integrating dual modalities in spatial transcriptomics analysis and demonstrate the robustness and broad applicability of MicNet under noisy conditions.

We further detected sdaSVGs for each of the five histology types and explored their biological significance by querying with known breast cancer genes. In four of the five histology types excepting the fat tissue region, MicNet-defined sdaSVGs showed higher overlap with known breast cancer genes than the sdaSVGs defined by SpaGCN and BayesSpace through edgeR (Fig. 4C) and Seurat algorithms (Additional file 1: Fig. S12A), implicating that MicNet might be able to identify disease-relevant sdaSVGs that could play important roles in disease development, progression, and immune response. MicNet-defined sdaSVGs can identify many experimentally validated breast cancer genes, including but not limited to MUC1 [42, 43], TCF7L2 [44], IRX3 [45], MYO10 [46], UBE2C [47], and SQLE [48] (Fig. 4D, Additional file 1: Fig. S12B), demonstrating that MicNet can correctly identify disease-relevant sdaSVGs from integrating pathology image and spatial transcriptomics data.

Furthermore, we explored the spatial organization pattern. MicNet consistently outperformed other methods in distinguishing regions with distinct tissue compositions, showing higher concordance with ground truth annotations and spatial approximations compared to raw transcriptomics data (Fig. 4E-F, Additional file 1: Fig. S13). Interestingly, MicNet features preserved the spatial information and evolutionary trajectory between tumor and necrosis, whereas direct projection of raw transcriptomics data onto the reduced dimension space failed to capture this relationship, highlighting MicNet as a potentially powerful tool for spatial organization pattern analysis in combination with UMAP and tSNE.

Application to the mouse hypothalamus dataset

Next, we applied MicNet to a mouse Hypothalamus dataset generated from a different SRT technology, MERFISH, which provides single-cell resolution and records the expression of 161 genes of 5555 cells [49]. This dataset includes ten annotated hypothalamus nuclei and one unspecified region. For comparison with previous studies, we used the SpaGCN result from its original publication [18], which contains 12 clusters, with some cells labeled as 'undefined' due to indistinguishability. To maintain consistency, we set the number of clusters to 12. MicNet successfully detected hypothalamus nuclei which aligns well with the annotations (Fig. 5A-D). Quantitative evaluation demonstrated that MicNet achieved the highest Adjusted Rand Index (ARI = 0.447) among all evaluated methods, indicating superior concordance with ground-truth annotations. In terms of Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI), MicNet (0.561) performed on par with other leading approaches, including stCluster (0.595), SpaGCN (0.527), and SpatialPCA (0.511) (Fig. 5E–F, Additional file 1: Fig. S14). This demonstrates MicNet’s ability to efficiently incorporate nuclear microenvironment information. Consistent with this trend, MicNet demonstrated superior performance relative to its unimodal representations (Additional file 1: Fig. S15). Although the current implementation utilizes a reconstructed pseudoimage, using original fluorescence data with more detailed cell morphological information could further improve the performance of MicNet.

We further detected sdaSVGs for each annotated hypothalamus nuclei using MicNet, and then queried sdaSVGs with a known gene set specifically expressed in hypothalamus defined by the Allen Mouse Brain Atlas [38]. In most of these spatial domains, we observed that MicNet-defined sdaSVGs showed higher overlap with known hypothalamus gene set than sdaSVGs defined by BayesSpace and SpaGCN, by both edgeR (Fig. 5G) and Seurat algorithms (Additional file 1: Fig. S16). Moreover, we further explored these MicNet-defined sdaSVGs, and found that multiple of them (e.g. FEZF1 [50–52], ESR1 [53–55], and OXT [56, 57]) have experimental evidence to support their functional relevance to hypothalamus (Fig. 5H).

Consistent with earlier examples with better performance, MicNet features outperformed raw MERFISH reads in visualization and preserving the concordance with ground truth annotations as well as spatial adjacencies (Fig. 5I, Additional file 1: Fig. S17). For instance, medial preoptic area (MPA) and anterior commissure (ACA), which play distinct roles in regulating motivated behaviors [58] and interconnection between brain hemispheres [59] respectively, were clearly distinguished using MicNet features compare raw MERFISH reads.

Application of MicNet to human ovarian cancer and prostate cancer datasets

To strengthen robustness and expand tissue diversity, we applied MicNet to two additional human datasets: ovarian cancer and prostate cancer. The evaluation of MicNet now covers six tissue types in total, evenly distributed between mouse (olfactory bulb, posterior brain, hypothalamus) and human (breast, ovarian, prostate) samples, providing a balanced cross-species assessment. The human ovarian dataset contains 4,617 spots with 18,061 genes measured using the 10 × Genomics Visium platform. Pathologist manually annotated three main regions: tumor, tumor-surrounding, and non-tumor regions (Additional file 1: Fig. S19A). The tumor regions are fragmented into multiple pieces separated by surrounding tissue, which posed additional challenges for spatial domain identification. On this dataset, MicNet achieved the highest concordance with pathologist annotations, with an ARI of 0.4944 and an AMI of 0.517 (Additional file 1: Fig. S19B, C), outperforming methods such as GraphST (ARI = 0.421, AMI = 0.462), STAGATE (ARI = 0.450, AMI = 0.478), and xSiGra (ARI = 0.453, AMI = 0.495). Notably, MicNet not only efficiently separated tumor from non-tumor regions but also successfully captured small microenvironments surrounding the tumor, which are critical for understanding cancer progression, tumor–stroma interactions, and the spatial distribution of immune infiltration. The human prostate dataset includes 4,371 spots, of which 3,760 were annotated by pathologist into domains including invasive carcinoma, fibro-muscular tissue, normal gland, fibrous tissue, nerve, blood vessel, and immune cells. MicNet attained an ARI of 0.473 (Additional file 1: Fig. S20), performing comparably to GraphST (0.479), STAGATE (0.474), and SpatialGlue (0.475), and outperforming lower-performing methods such as STCluster (0.290) and MISO (0.297). MicNet more accurately distinguished tumor from normal gland regions and captured the spatial architecture of nerve regions, features that are important for characterizing tumor invasion patterns and the tumor–nerve microenvironment. Across both human ovarian and prostate datasets, MicNet consistently demonstrated strong and reliable performance, reinforcing its robustness and generalizability across diverse tissue contexts.

Discussion

Pathology images and molecular data are two distinct data modalities that offer complementary insights into tissue and disease activity, but extracting robust information from them is challenging due to the complexity and variability of tissue images and high noise in SRT data [60]. Extracting shared information between these modalities is desirable as it is more reliable, has a higher signal-to-noise ratio, and better reveals the biological mechanisms linking transcriptomic profiles to cell morphology and spatial organization.

Here, we present MicNet, a deep learning approach for comprehensively analyzing SRT data by integrating pathology images with molecular data. MicNet employs molecular and image encoders to extract relevant features and employs contrastive loss to enhance feature similarity while minimizing noise. MicNet has demonstrated superior clustering performance across various spatial profiling platforms, outperforming methods like SpaGCN and BayesSpace in identifying spatial domains, domain-specific genes, and spatial organization patterns. It effectively analyzes SRT data from both normal and diseased tissues, pinpointing physiological and pathological domains, functional genes, and pathways. As SRT datasets expand, MicNet's ability to minimize unpaired image and molecular features positions it for broader applications in this emerging field. Additionally, MicNet can be further extended to integrate multi-modal data such as image, transcriptome, and proteome, by applying contrastive loss across all input domains. This allows MicNet to adapt seamlessly to the integration of image-molecular data in emerging spatial multi-modal datasets.

With the growing attention of SRT data in clinical research, MicNet provides a powerful framework that integrates histology and gene expression. By jointly leveraging morphological and molecular information, MicNet not only enables more precise delineation of spatial domains but also generates dual-modality features that can be flexibly applied to multiple tasks, including tissue classification, improving diagnostic accuracy, informing therapeutic strategies by identifying molecularly and morphologically distinct niches associated with treatment response, and advancing studies of disease mechanisms by uncovering spatial signatures that link tissue morphology with gene regulation. Although current applications are limited by the availability of large clinical SRT cohorts, MicNet establishes a strong methodological foundation for bridging histopathology and transcriptomics in ways that can support future diagnostic and therapeutic advances.

It is noteworthy that MicNet is functioning effectively regardless of whether image data is available or not. For example, when analyzing the mouse hypothalamus MERFISH dataset, whose original image data was unavailable, constructing a pseudoimage with cell location information still shows advantages in distinguishing different hypothalamus nuclei. One possible reason is that pseudoimage still partially captured local microenvironment information like cell density and cell–cell spatial interactions, providing additional biologically meaningful signals to guide hypothalamus sub-structure detection. It is anticipated that the performance could be boosted if the original MERFISH images were accessible with more detailed characteristics such as cell morphology and sub-cellular level RNA locations.

Conclusion

In this study, we presented MicNet, a contrastive deep learning framework for integrating spatially resolved transcriptomics and pathology images. By projecting molecular and image data onto a shared representation domain, MicNet enables accurate spatial domain detection, identification of spatially variable gene(s), and visualization of spatial organization patterns. MicNet consistently outperformed state-of-the-art methods across six benchmark datasets spanning mouse and human tissues, including both normal and diseased conditions. The modular design of MicNet allows flexible integration with existing spatial analysis pipelines. As spatially resolved transcriptomic datasets continue to grow in scale and clinical relevance, MicNet provides a robust foundation for bridging histopathology with molecular profiling to advance biological discovery and translational research.

Methods

Rationale of MicNet

The essential goal of MicNet is to maximize the similarity between image and transcriptomic information on the latent representation domain (Fig. 1A). A naïve approach, such as directly maximization of similarity (canonical correlation) between encoded features of the same sample, often leads to degenerate solutions in which all samples collapse to identical representations. To overcome this, MicNet adapted a contrastive learning strategy that simultaneously (i) pulls together paired image and gene embeddings from the same spot (positives) to maximize intra-sample correlation, and (ii) pushes apart embeddings from different spots (negatives), thereby minimizing inter-sample correlation. To accelerate computation by avoiding repetitive calculation of encoded features of negative samples, the strategy of the memory bank [62] is used to temporarily store features of every training sample on the representation domain. This step is important to make this method scalable to high-dimensional datasets. In each training step with batch size B, only B positive samples undergo forward and backward propagation, while the encoded features on the representation domain of the B*K negative samples are directly read out from the memory bank; the encoded features of the B positive samples are also used to update the memory bank. To smooth the training process, the memory bank is updated with momentum.

MicNet structure and implementation details

Figure 1B illustrates a general form of the MicNet structure. Paired image and transcriptomic information are encoded separately with the image encoder and the transcriptomic encoder, yielding two vectors representing image and transcriptomic features. Contrastive loss is used to measure the ability to pair encoded image features with corresponding transcriptomic features among noisy samples. Through minimizing the contrastive loss, the shared information between image and transcriptomic domain is represented by the encoded feature vectors.

In constructing and training MicNet for all six of the datasets, MicNet employs a ResNet101 [63] backbone pretrained on ImageNet for image information, which was further fine-tuned on histopathology images to adapt the histopathology-specific morphological patterns, and dense feed-forward network was used to encode transcriptomic information. Images were standardized (centered and scaled with global mean and variance) for each RGB channel and intensively augmented (random projective transformation with random color augmentation [64]) to mitigate the possibility of overfitting and learning noises during training phase. RNA expression data were Counts Per Million (CPM) normalized followed by dividing with maximum CPM value of each individual gene (the division step could be skipped in case of the global maximum value < 1). In the training phase, the batch size was set to 20; a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithm was chosen as the optimizer; the learning rate was set to 0.01; the momentum was set to 0.9; L2 penalty for regularization was used with weight decay set as 0.0001. For the Visium MOB, MPB, HBC, Human Ovarian Cancer and Human Prostate Cancer datasets, we used a feature-vector length of 100, which provided stable and accurate results. For the MERFISH dataset, we reduced the feature dimension to 50, since only 161 genes were detected in this experiment. In general, the choice of feature dimension should reflect the size of the gene set: larger datasets with many thousands of genes can support higher-dimensional representations, whereas smaller datasets with fewer genes benefit from lower dimensions to avoid overfitting. We further validated these choices through ablation studies on the Visium breast cancer and MERFISH datasets, which confirmed that 100 and 50 features, respectively, provide the most effective balance between accuracy and efficiency (Additional file 2: Tables S2 and S3). The best model was selected according to the average Spearman correlation between image and molecular features of paired samples in the validation set. For a dataset with 2,518 spots and 17,649 non-zero genes, MicNet required ~ 23.4 s per epoch (21.7 min for 50 epochs) with peak GPU memory usage of 5,058 MiB, and inference completed in 8.9 s using 2,012 MiB (Additional file 2: Table S4).

Clustering with MicNet features

In the inference step, MicNet can be directly applied to both image and molecular profiles and compute image features and molecular features representing the underlying biological signals, respectively. To fully utilize both image and molecular features, for each sample with paired image and molecular profile, the image feature (length = N) and molecular feature (length = N) were directly concatenated and yielded a length = 2 N feature vector. Such scaled and informative feature vectors are generally acceptable for classic clustering algorithms, including KMeans and Spectral clustering. In this study, KMeans was selected for the MOB, HBC, Human Ovarian Cancer, and Human Prostate Cancer datasets when the clusters were linearly separable in the reduced dimension space, and Spectral was selected for the MPB and hypothalamus dataset when the clusters were non-linearly separable in the reduced dimension space. For each dataset, the number of clusters was fixed to match the number of annotated domains defined by pathologists, enabling a direct comparison between MicNet predictions and expert labels.

Evaluation of clustering performance

To evaluate the concordance between ST samples clustering results and ground truth annotations, two criteria were used: Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Adjusted Mutual Information (AMI). ARI is determined by computing the proportion of all sample pairs that consistently fall into the same or distinct clusters when comparing the predicted clusters and the annotated ones, normalized to ensure zero value for random labeling and independence of the number of clusters. AMI is determined by the shared information between two distributions and normalized for the number of clusters. A higher score of both ARI and AMI indicates a better concordance between clustering and annotations.

Spatial domain associated spatially variable gene(s) (sdaSVGs) detection

To ensure a fair and systematic comparison of the ability to identify sdaSVGs between MicNet features and other state-of-the-art methods, we performed differential expression (DE) analysis using spots from a target domain versus all other domains. The goal is to detect genes that are differentially expressed in the target domain, which then were defined as spatial domain-associated spatially variable gene(s) (sdaSVGs) for this target domain. To achieve a robust list of sdaSVGs, we applied edgeR-based [65] and Seurat-based [66] pipelines, both of which are state-of-the-art DE analysis methods. The two-tailed statistics tests were applied in edgeR and Seurat packages. sdaSVG genes were defined as those with adjusted p-values (FDR) < 0.05 after multiple testing correction in edgeR and Seurat. In order to validate the biological significance of detected sdaSVGs, we performed gene-set enrichment analysis between sdaSVGs detected by various approaches and known biologically functional genes. Two-tailed Fisher exact test was applied. P value smaller than 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Genomic co-regulation between neighboring histology types

We developed the spatial interaction score to quantify the genomic interaction and co-regulation between adjacent histology subgroups. To investigate the interaction between each pair of subgroups (such as GCL and MCL), every spot from the first subgroup (such as GCL) was paired with its nearest neighbor from the 2nd subgroup (such as MCL). The spot was discarded if its nearest distance exceeded a certain threshold, which was defined as the spot center-to-center distance for the MOB dataset (center-to-center distance = 200um). After obtaining the spot pairs, for each pair of genes of interest, the spatial interaction score for the selected pair of histology subgroups was calculated as the Spearman correlation between the gene expression levels of first gene of the spots belonging to the first subgroup and the second gene of the spots belonging to the second subgroup.

Spatial organization visualization and evaluation

To assess the ability of MicNet in capturing spatial organization information and visualizing similarity and development trajectory among samples, we utilized UMAP and tSNE to project length = 2 N MicNet features onto a two-dimensional space. To quantify the performance in representing spatial organization, two measurements were computed: Silhouette coefficient for concordance between pairwise Euclidean distances on the representation space and ground truth annotations, and spearman rank correlation (rs) for concordance between Euclidean distances on the two-dimension representation space and pairwise physical spatial distances.

Pseudo-image generation for MERFISH dataset

Since raw histological images were not available for the MERFISH mouse hypothalamus dataset, we generated pseudo-images to approximate spatial morphology. Cell centroids were first re-based so that spatial coordinates began near the image origin, with a 25-pixel margin added for padding. Spatial resolution was set to ~ 1 µm per pixel by applying a 4 × zoom, consistent with the average intercellular distance (~ 20 µm). Each cell was represented as a circle with a radius of ~ 26 µm and mapped onto the coordinate grid. Unique integer IDs were assigned to each cell, and regions were expanded outward from the centroid using a watershed algorithm, ensuring compact and non-overlapping boundaries between neighboring cells. This procedure produced pseudo-images that preserve cellular organization without requiring raw histology. To approximate the Visium platform resolution, classification labels were further smoothed using a K-nearest neighbors approach (K = 190). These pseudo-images were then used for joint feature learning with MicNet (Additional file 1: Fig. S18).

Datasets

Mouse olfactory bulb (MOB) dataset

Spatial transcriptomics data and corresponding H&E-stained pathology images were collected [31], yielding 1080 image patches (256 × 256 pixels) paired with RNA sequencing data for 19,025 genes, used for MicNet training. An independent STOB image was used for inference. Ground truth annotations followed the original publication [31], and clustering results were obtained from previous studies [18].

Mouse posterior brain (MPB) dataset

MPB data from 3,355 spots with 21,353 non-zero genes were sourced from 10X Visium. Of these, 3,187 (95%) spots were used for MicNet training, with the remainder as validation. Ground truth annotations for the isocortex and hippocampus CA1 region were based on the Mouse Brain Atlas [38]. Clustering results were taken from prior studies [18].

Human breast cancer (HBC) dataset

Spatial transcriptomics data with 17,649 non-zero genes from 2,518 spots were collected from 10X Visium. 2,014 (80%) spots were used for training, with the rest as validation. Ground truth annotations were acquired from the 10X Visium website. Clustering results were computed locally following original publications.

Mouse hypothalamus (MERFISH) dataset

MERFISH technology provided single-cell resolution spatial transcriptomics data for 5,555 cells with 161 genes at 0.26 Bregma. 5,388 (97%) cells were used for training, and the rest for validation. The ground truth annotations for ten hypothalamus nuclei were derived from the original study [49], with clustering results obtained from previous studies [18].

Human ovarian cancer dataset

We obtained a publicly available spatial transcriptomics dataset containing 18,061 genes across 4,617 spots from the 10 × Genomics Visium website. For analysis, 3,739 spots (80%) were designated for training, while the remaining spots were reserved for validation. Ground truth annotations were provided by pathologist, who categorized regions into tumor, non-tumor, and surrounding tumor. Clustering was performed on MicNet-derived embeddings using k-means.

Human prostate cancer dataset

A publicly accessible spatial transcriptomics dataset with 16,905 genes across 4,371 spots was downloaded from the 10 × Genomics Visium website. In this dataset, 3,496 spots (80%) were used for training and the remainder served as validation. Ground truth annotations were obtained directly from the 10 × Genomics repository. Clustering was conducted on MicNet-derived embeddings using k-means.
