On the instability of some upward propagating, exact, nonlinear mountain waves
Christian Puntini

Abstract
. Using the short-wavelength instability method, we investigate the linear instability of an exact solution describing upward-propagating mountain waves, derived in A. Constantin, J. Phys. A: Math. Theor. (2023), under the assumption of a dry adiabatic flow. Within this approach, the stability problem reduces to analysing a system of ordinary differential equations along fluid trajectories. Our results show that the flow becomes unstable when the wave steepness exceeds the critical threshold of 1 3 \frac{1}{3} . Given the representation of the solution in Lagrangian coordinates, the instability analysis will show the existence of an unstable layer of few hundred meters beneath the tropopause where instability may occur, finally leading to a chaotic 3-dimensional fluid motion.

1.
Introduction

Mountain waves are a specific type of atmospheric gravity wave that form downwind of a long topographic barrier (e.g., a mountain) when a stable, stratified flow with a strong cross-ridge component is forced to ascend over it. As the air rises, buoyancy perturbations develop and generate disturbances that propagate away from the mountain as gravity (buoyancy) waves. Because the atmosphere is compressible and stratified, these waves can propagate both horizontally and vertically (see
[
Durran(1990)
,
Durran(2003)
]
). As air density decreases with altitude, their amplitude increases, and wave steepening and overturning may occur as the waves penetrate the free atmosphere.

While the orographic lift over the ridge is relatively uniform, the flow downstream of the crest is much more complex. It typically features a laminar layer where smooth mountain waves travel, and layers of clear-air turbulence—irregular, high-frequency motions that are not visible to the naked eye. In the lower part of the downstream flow, rotors are often present: rapidly rotating vortices generated by frictional and viscous effects in the descending air near the surface.

The meteorological literature distinguishes two main types of mountain waves: vertically propagating waves and trapped lee waves. See Fig.
1
. Vertically propagating waves arise when, above the peak, temperature and density decrease with height and wind speed does not increase markedly; their amplitude grows upward and they can produce strong vertical motions (exceeding
30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
). If they break before reaching the tropopause, they can generate significant clear-air turbulence, creating important hazards to aviation (see e.g.
[
Lilly(1978)
,
Guarino et al.(2016)
,
Bramberger et al.(2020)
]
).

Trapped lee waves, by contrast, occur when vertical shear or a low-level inversion creates a waveguide that confines energy near ridge height, yielding a stationary downstream wave train with limited vertical propagation, often marked by stacked lenticular clouds and sometimes accompanied by rotors beneath the first crest (see
[
Teixeira(2014)
]
and
[
Constantin(2023)
,
Constantin and Weber(2025)
]
).

Sketch of the two main types of mountain waves: upward propagating and trapped lee waves. Image generated (partially) with the use of
u:ai
of the University of Vienna.

In
[
Constantin(2023)
]
, the author proposed an explicit and exact solution for mountain waves propagating upwards—in the Lagrangian framework—consisting of oscillation superimposed on a mean current propagating upward.
Although explicit solutions—however complicated they may be—are idealised and therefore approximate representations of reality, they can be a fruitful tool for a better understanding of the phenomenon under analysis, eventually as a starting point for a perturbation analysis (see e.g.
[
Constantin(2014)
,
Abrashkin and Pelinovsky(2022)
,
Puntini(2026a)
]
for exact solutions in oceanography). Once an explicit solution has been obtained, a natural question is whether it is stable or unstable and, in the latter case, under what conditions. This is the scope we pursue in this work.

We adopt the short-wavelength instability approach—in the most general description given by
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
]
(see also
[
Bayly(1987)
,
Friedlander and Vishik(1991)
]
)—, which has proved to be particularly suited for the instability analysis of explicit solutions in Lagrangian coordinates for oceanic flows (see e.g.
[
Leblanc(2004)
,
Constantin and Germain(2013)
,
Ionescu-Kruse(2016)
,
Ionescu-Kruse(2017)
,
Puntini(2026b)
]
. It has to be noted however, that, even if the general approach of
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
,
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
or
[
Friedlander and Vishik(1991)
,
Friedlander and Yudovich(1999)
]
has been used only in the last decade or so, the analysis of short-wavelength instabilities has been an important, classical subject in the theory off hydrodynamic stability (see e.g.
[
Eckhoff and Storesletten(1978)
,
Eckhoff and Storesletten(1980)
,
Lebovich and Stewartson(1983)
]
.

This paper is structured as follow: in Section
2
we review the solution proposed in
[
Constantin(2023)
]
, and then, in Section
3
, which is the main subject of this work, we use the short-wavelength instability approach to prove that such a solution is unstable. We conclude in Section
4
with some qualitative and quantitative results.

2.
Review of the exact solution in
[
Constantin(2023)
]

The equations of motion for the description of mountain waves are: the Euler equations

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{Du}{Dt}=-\frac{1}{\rho}P_{x},\qquad\frac{Dv}{Dt}=-\frac{1}{\rho}P_{y},\qquad\frac{Dw}{Dt}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\rho}P_{z}-g,

for a velocity field
\mathbf{u}=(u,v,w)
, the pressure
P
and density
\rho
, and where
\frac{D}{Dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla
is the material derivative. These are coupled with the equation of mass conservation

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+u\,\rho_{x}+v\,\rho_{y}+w\,\rho_{z}+\rho\left(u_{x}+v_{y}+w_{z}\right)=0,

the equation of state for an ideal gas,

(3)

\displaystyle P=\rho{T},

for the temperature
T
, and the first law of thermodynamics

(4)

T_{t}+u\,T_{x}+v\,T_{y}+w\,T_{z}-\frac{\mu}{\rho}\left(P_{t}+u\,P_{x}+v\,P_{y}+w\,P_{z}\right)=0.

Here, the equations have already been put into non-dimensional form, to which dimensional quantities (with primes) are related by

\displaystyle t^{\prime}=\frac{L^{\prime}}{U^{\prime}}t,\quad(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})=L^{\prime}(x,y,z),\quad(u^{\prime},v^{\prime},w^{\prime})=U^{\prime}(u,v,w),

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}=\bar{\rho}^{\prime}\rho,\quad p^{\prime}=\bar{\rho}^{\prime}U^{\prime 2}p,\quad T^{\prime}=\frac{U^{\prime 2}}{\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}}T.

In this context, the typical scales are
L^{\prime}=2\;\mathrm{km}
,
U^{\prime}=20\;\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}
and
\bar{\rho}^{\prime}=1\;\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}
, while
\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}\approx 287\;\mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}\,K^{-1}}
is the gas constant for dry air, and, the non-dimensional constants in (
1
) and (
4
) are

g=g^{\prime}\frac{L^{\prime}}{U^{\prime 2}}\approx 49,\qquad\mu=\frac{\mathfrak{R}^{\prime}}{c_{p}^{\prime}}\approx 0.287,

where
g^{\prime}
is the gravitational acceleration and
c_{p}^{\prime}\approx 1000\;\mathrm{m^{2}\,s^{-2}\,K^{-1}}
is the specific heat of dry air at an atmospheric pressure of
1000\;\mathrm{mb}
. It is worth noting that, as the orographic lifting of air particles can be considered a dry adiabatic process outside regions of active precipitation (see
[
Holton and Hakim(2011)
]
), setting the right-hand side of (
4

. Moreover, we denoted by

3.
Short-wavelength instability analysis

In order to prove that the above solution is (linearly) unstable, we adopt the short-wavelength instability method of
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
]
(and further developed in
[
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
) for compressible flow. We denote by
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
,
\mathfrak{p}
and
\varrho
small perturbations of the velocity
\mathbf{u}
, pressure
P
and density
\rho
, and then we adopt the Eckart transform (see
[
Eckart(1960)
,
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
,
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
) to introduce new variables
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
such that

(11)

\varrho=\frac{\rho}{\texttt{C}}(\mathfrak{m}+\mathfrak{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{p}=\frac{\gamma P}{\texttt{C}}\mathfrak{n},

where we have introduced the local speed of sound as
\texttt{C}=\sqrt{\gamma\frac{P}{\rho}}
, and
\gamma=\frac{1}{1-\mu}
. The linearized equation of motion for the perturbations are given by (see
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
,
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
)

(12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{D\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}}{Dt}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}-\texttt{C}\nabla g_{1}\mathfrak{m}+\texttt{C}\nabla\mathfrak{n}+\texttt{C}\nabla g_{2}\mathfrak{n}=0,\\
&\frac{D\mathfrak{m}}{Dt}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\cdot\texttt{C}\nabla g_{3}=0,\\
&\frac{D\mathfrak{n}}{Dt}+\texttt{C}\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}+\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}\cdot\texttt{C}\nabla g_{1}=0.\end{aligned}\right.

The short-wavelength instability approach consists in the study of the evolution in time of the solutions of the linearized system (
12
) and (
12
) in the following WKB form

(13)

\displaystyle\left[\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}(\mathbf{X},t),\,\mathfrak{m}(\mathbf{X},t),\,\mathfrak{n}(\mathbf{X},t)\right]=\left[\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X},t),\,{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{X},t),\,0\right]e^{\frac{i}{{\varepsilon}}\Phi(\mathbf{X},t)}+\mathcal{O}({\varepsilon})

where
{\varepsilon}
is a small parameter,
\mathbf{a}
is a vector functions, while
{\mathrm{b}}
and
\Phi
are scalar functions. Substituting the expression for
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}
,
\mathfrak{m}
and
\mathfrak{n}
given by (
13
) into (
12
), we obtain that the evolution in time of
\mathbf{X}
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}=:\nabla\Phi
,
\mathbf{a}
and
{\mathrm{b}}
, at leading order in
{\varepsilon}
, is given by the following coupled system of ODEs

(14)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dot{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{X},t),\\
&\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=-(\nabla\mathbf{u})^{T}\boldsymbol{\xi},\\
&\dot{\mathbf{a}}=-(\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}+2\frac{\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot[(\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}]}{||\boldsymbol{\xi}||^{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\texttt{C}\left(\nabla g_{1}-\frac{\nabla g_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}}{||\boldsymbol{\xi}||^{2}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right){\mathrm{b}},\\
&\dot{{\mathrm{b}}}=-\mathbf{a}\cdot\texttt{C}\nabla g_{3},\end{aligned}\right.

where we denoted by
\dot{}=\frac{D}{Dt}
, and with initial condition

(15)

\mathbf{X}(0)=\mathbf{X}_{0},\qquad\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\qquad\mathbf{a}(0)=\mathbf{a}_{0}=\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{u}}_{0}(\mathbf{X}_{0}),\qquad{\mathrm{b}}(0)={\mathrm{b}}_{0}={{\mathfrak{m}}_{0}(\mathbf{X}_{0})},

satisfying the constraint
\mathbf{a}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=0.
The first equation in (
14
) describes the particle trajectory of the basic flow (see
[
Bennett(2006)
]
), while the other three describe—at leading order in
{\varepsilon}
—the evolution of the local wave vector and the amplitude of the perturbation along the particle trajectory, respectively. This system therefore describe the evolution in time of a sharply-peaked initial disturbance, located at time
t=0
around
\mathbf{X}_{0}
, that is advected by the basic flow
\mathbf{u}
, and in doing so, its amplitude may or may not increase. If it increases, that is

(16)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\\
\text{s.t.}\ \mathbf{a}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=0\end{subarray}}\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\left[\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{\ }{\xi}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},{\mathrm{b}}_{0};\,\tau),\,{\mathrm{b}}(\mathbf{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0},\mathbf{a}_{0},{\mathrm{b}}_{0};\,\tau)\right]=\infty

the flow is unstable (see
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
,
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
for more details, or
[
Ionescu-Kruse(2017)
]
for a review of this method in the case of incompressible geophysical flows).

With the intention of applying this method to the proposed solution of
[
Constantin(2023)
]
, we compute the gradient tensor of the basic flow
\mathbf{u}
to get

(17)

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}u_{x}&u_{y}&u_{z}\\
v_{x}&v_{y}&v_{z}\\
w_{x}&w_{y}&w_{z}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}u_{a}&u_{s}&u_{b}\\
v_{a}&v_{s}&v_{b}\\
w_{a}&w_{s}&w_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{x}&s_{x}&b_{x}\\
a_{y}&s_{y}&b_{y}\\
a_{z}&s_{z}&b_{z}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}-\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}&0&\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta-e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}\\
0&0&0\\
\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta+e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}&0&\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, if
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=(0,1,0)^{T}
, from the second of (
14
) it follows that
\boldsymbol{\xi}(t)=(0,1,0)^{T}
for all
t
. Moreover, note that, due to (
17
), we have
\boldsymbol{\xi}\cdot[(\mathbf{a}\cdot\nabla)\mathbf{u}]=0
, as well as
\nabla g_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}=0
since (
1
) coupled with (
6
) ensures that
P_{y}=0
. Furthermore, due to (
8
), it follows that
\rho_{y}=\rho_{b}b_{y}=0
.

Before proceeding further, we derive some other thermodynamic relations from the model in Section
2
, which were not present in
[
Constantin(2023)
]
, but will prove very useful in the subsequent computations.

Combining (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
), we obtain

(18)

\frac{DP}{Dt}+\gamma P\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0.

Moreover, coupling (
3
) and (
4
), it follows that

(19)

\frac{D\ln T}{Dt}=\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}\frac{D\ln\rho}{Dt},

so that dividing by
T
and integrating along particle paths, yields
T\propto\rho^{\mu/(1-\mu)}
, leading to

P=\rho T\propto\rho^{\,1+\mu/(1-\mu)}=\rho^{\,1/(1-\mu)}
, resulting in the relation

(20)

P=K\,\rho^{\gamma},

where
K
is constant along particle paths. As a consequence of (
20
), it follows that
\nabla g_{3}=0
along particle paths.

Therefore, the last two equations of (
14
) read as

(21)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \begin{pmatrix}\dot{a_{1}}\\
\dot{a_{2}}\\
\dot{a_{3}}\end{pmatrix}&=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}&0&\frac{kce^{kb}(e^{kb}-\cos\theta)}{1-e^{2kb}}\\
0&0&0\\
-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta+e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}&0&-\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{a_{1}}\\
{a_{2}}\\
{a_{3}}\end{pmatrix}+{\mathrm{b}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{P_{x}}{\texttt{C}\rho}\\
0\\
\frac{P_{z}}{\texttt{C}\rho}\end{pmatrix},\\
\vskip 14.22636pt\dot{{\mathrm{b}}}&=0.\end{aligned}\right.

In particular
\dot{a_{2}}=0
, and due to the constraint
\mathbf{a}_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi}_{0}=0
we set
a_{2}(0)=0
, giving
a_{2}(t)=0
for all
t
. The system (
21
) therefore reduces to

(22)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \dot{\boldsymbol{A}}&=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}&-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta-e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}\\
-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta+e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}&-\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A}+{\mathrm{b}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{P_{x}}{\texttt{C}\rho}\\
\frac{P_{z}}{\texttt{C}\rho}\end{pmatrix},\\
\vskip 14.22636pt\dot{{\mathrm{b}}}&=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\boldsymbol{A}=(a_{1},\,a_{3})^{T}
. Setting the initial condition
{\mathrm{b}}_{0}=0
allow us to reduce the system to the non-autonomous planar linear system

(23)

\dot{\boldsymbol{A}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}&-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta-e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}\\
-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos\theta+e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}&-\frac{kce^{kb}\sin\theta}{1-e^{2kb}}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{A},

typical of Gerstner waves (see
[
Leblanc(2004)
]
). Denoting
\boldsymbol{Q}=P^{T}\boldsymbol{A}
, where
P
is the rotation matrix

P(t)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\!\big(\tfrac{kct}{2}\big)&\sin\!\big(\tfrac{kct}{2}\big)\\
-\sin\!\big(\tfrac{kct}{2}\big)&\cos\!\big(\tfrac{kct}{2}\big)\end{pmatrix},

the non-autonomous system (
23
) is transformed into the autonomous one

(24)

\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\left(\dot{P^{T}}P+P^{T}MP\right)\boldsymbol{A}

where
M
is the
2\times 2
time-dependent matrix in (
23
). It is straightforward to verify that this expression can be rewritten as

(25)

\dot{\boldsymbol{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{kce^{kb}\sin(ka)}{1-e^{2kb}}&-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos(ka)-e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}-\frac{kc}{2}\\
-\frac{kce^{kb}(\cos(ka)+e^{kb})}{1-e^{2kb}}+\frac{kc}{2}&-\frac{kce^{kb}\sin(ka)}{1-e^{2kb}}\end{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}

implying that the asymptotic behaviour of
\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
, as

, is determined by the eigenvalues of the

matrix in (
25
), which are given by
\lambda^{2}=\frac{k^{2}c^{2}}{4(1-e^{2kb})}(9e^{2kb}-1)
. As a consequence, if

(26)

e^{kb}>\frac{1}{3}\qquad\left(\Longleftrightarrow b>-\frac{\ln 3}{k}\right)

where
e^{kb}
is the wave steepness of the wave motion in(
5
), an exponential growth of
\boldsymbol{Q}(t)
occurs as
t\rightarrow\infty
, leading to the onset of instability for the proposed solution in (
5
).

4.
Discussion and conclusions

We have presented an instability analysis of the nonlinear solution for mountain waves discovered in
[
Constantin(2023)
]
. Adopting the short-wavelength instability approach of
[
Lifschitz and Hameiri(1991)
]
and
[
Lebovitz and Lifschitz(1992)
]
for compressible flows, we have been able to transform the study of the instability of a complicated flow, given in Lagrangian coordinates, into the analysis of a coupled system of ODEs. In general, dealing with a compressible flow makes the problem more complicated respect the incompressible case (see e.g.
[
Constantin and Germain(2013)
,
Ionescu-Kruse(2016)
,
Ionescu-Kruse(2017)
,
Puntini(2026b)
]
for similar studies in the incompressible case), as also the coupling between
\mathbf{a}
and
{\mathrm{b}}
has to be considered. However, under the assumption of a dry adiabatic flow for an ideal gas, the problem simplifies significantly. We aim in future works to extend such analysis to more complicated flows (e.g. those in
[
Henry(2023)
]
) or to those that account for precipitations, such as
[
Lyons(2025)
,
McCarney and Lyons(2026)
]
.

Let us also remark that, the presented analysis, provide a sufficient condition—namely (
26
)—for the onset of instability, based on the assumption that
{\mathrm{b}}_{0}=0
. However, this may not be optimal, in the sense that, due to the coupling of
\mathbf{a}
and
{\mathrm{b}}
in (
22
), there might exist a different initial condition
{\mathrm{b}}_{0}\not=0
giving a threshold in (
26
) smaller than
\frac{1}{3}
.

In order to provide some quantitative insights into the instability result obtained, let us recall that the most natural choice for the wavenumber is
k=2\pi
, with multiples describing shorter waves, and that the length scale chosen for this problem was
L^{\prime}=2\,\mathrm{km}
.

The tropopause—the upper boundary of the troposphere—is situated between
9
and
17\,\mathrm{km}
above the Earth’s surface (see
[
Holton and Hakim(2011)
]
), and to obtain numerical thresholds, we fix
b=0

Short‑wavelength instabilities in inviscid incompressible flows are believed to be responsible for the breakdown of two‑dimensional coherent structures into three‑dimensional chaotic motion (see
[
Lifschitz(1991)
]
and references therein). In the present, compressible setting, the same mechanism is expected to persist. Our analysis therefore suggests that the coherent two‑dimensional solution (
5
) may be destabilized by short-wavelength perturbations. The ensuing growth would tilt and stretch material structures, promote three‑dimensionality through vortex‑line bending, and facilitate energy transfer to smaller scales (similarly to the mechanism observed for Kelvin-Helmholtz waves in
[
Klaassen and Peltier(1985)
]
), ultimately seeding turbulence in the upper troposphere and near the tropopause. This scenario is consistent with the broader view that (short‑wavelength) instabilities provide a generic route from quasi‑two‑dimensional organized flow to fully three‑dimensional fluid motion (see e.g.
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

]
