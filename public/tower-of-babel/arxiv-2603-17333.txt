Grid Spatial Understanding: A Dataset for Textual Spatial Reasoning over Grids, Embodied Settings, and Coordinate Structures
Risham Sidhu, Julia Hockenmaier

Abstract
We introduce GSU, a text-only grid dataset to evaluate the spatial reasoning capabilities of LLMs over 3 core tasks: navigation, object localization, and structure composition. By forgoing visual inputs, isolating spatial reasoning from perception, we show that while most models grasp basic grid concepts, they struggle with frames of reference relative to an embodied agent and identifying 3D shapes from coordinate lists. We also find that exposure to a visual modality does not provide a generalizable understanding of 3D space that VLMs are able to utilize for these tasks. Finally, we show that while the very latest frontier models can solve the provided tasks (though harder variants may still stump them), fully fine-tuning a small LM or LORA fine-tuning a small LLM show potential to match frontier model performance, suggesting an avenue for specialized embodied agents.



1
Introduction

With the advent of more powerful LLMs, their applications have broaden from text generation to complex problems ranging from advanced mathematical reasoning to multimodal agents. Many of these tasks require the ability to reason about space, whether to solve geometry problems
(
Zhao et al. 2025
;
Gao et al. 2025
)
or to understand verbal commands about their surroundings as an embodied agent
(
Zhang et al. 2024
;
Feng et al. 2025
)
, to complete tasks while interacting with objects in complex environments
(
Padmakumar et al. 2021
;
Shridhar et al. 2020
)
, to describe visual scenes
(
Fu et al. 2024a
)
, or to reason over 2- and 3-D images
(
Cheng et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2024
)
.

Approaches to such tasks incorporate additional modalities through vision encoders, projections of point cloud data, or more specialized representations, but using these effectively necessitates updates to base LLM architecture or additional training. Furthermore, even with such additions, it is unclear if fundamentally non-physical models that have been trained primarily on unpaired text can truly understand 3D space. Despite recent advances on various spatial reasoning tasks, the internal reasoning processes of LLMs, VLMs, and variations thereof are often not interpretable, meaning that merely measuring performance is distinct from evaluating understanding.
And even when performance is strong, further investigations into how additional modalities, like vision, are used in spatial tasks reveal that multi-modal LLMs receive little additional information from images when provided with rich text representations
(
Wang et al. 2024a
)
.

In that case, textual descriptions of physical space may serve as a convenient proxy for complex visual inputs when evaluating spatial reasoning. By removing visual perceptions, they offer a controlled method of probing whether LLMs posses robust internal representations of space, and if such representations can be manipulated with textual descriptions alone. Given that grids are an established representation for 3D space, they are a natural choice to express 3D layouts and movements.

In this work, we present GSU, a dataset composed of 3 simple text-only grid spatial reasoning tasks aimed at applications in embodied domains, to serve as a diagnostic for the inherent spatial reasoning abilities of existing models and identify where further work is needed to induce the spacial capabilities embodied agents require.

2
The GSU Dataset

We now expand on each of these relatively simple spatial tasks and the specific facets of textual coordinate reasoning they test. For more details on generation and dataset statistics, please refer to Appendix
A
.

2.1
The Navigation Task

The first task measures models’ understanding of textual coordinates as a representation of physical space. In this task, models must correlate natural language directions (i.e. "left", "down", "backward") with grid traversals across coordinates differing in 1 dimension at a time, i.e. equating moving from (0, 0) to (2, 0), to (2, 1) with moving "right 2" and "forward 1".

2.1.1
Instructor vs. Follower

VLN tasks are generally a conversation between two counterparts: an instructor and a follower. While the instructor is often a person and the follower an agent, this is not necessarily always the case (e.g. a collaborative system with multiple agents,
Narayan-Chen et al. 2019
’s MDC Architect task, voice assistants, etc). Models should thus be proficient in both directions of this mapping between verbal spatial directions and coordinate traversals.

In the
Follower
setting, models receive a set of natural language instructions explaining how to traverse the grid (e.g.
"move right 2 steps and then forward 1"
) and must output a coordinate reflecting their final location (e.g.
(2,1)
). Whereas in the
Instructor
setting, models receive a grid traversal expressed as a series of intermediate coordinates (e.g.
(0,0),(2,0),(2,1)
) and must output the natural language instructions that correlate to that path (e.g.
"move right 2 and forward 1"
).

2.1.2
Spatial Frames of Reference

We consider two frames of reference for the spatial directions of the grid. In the
Cardinal
setting, the spatial directions are fixed to the axes of the grid and never change, i.e. to move forward always means to increase the y value, while to move left always means to decrease the x value. While many grid based tasks use this fixed perspective of spatial relations (e.g.
Aghzal et al. 2025
), it does not transfer well to practical collaborative settings where an embodied agent or a human it is instructing have their own frame of reference that may not align with a fixed grid.

The
Egocentric
formulation aligns spatial directions with the perspective of an embodied subject navigating the grid, orienting the spatial directions with their body, and so rotating where they are relative to the fixed axes of the grid. For example, in the
Egocentric
setting to go right 2 steps and then forward 1 step would first entail turning to the right (90
∘
clockwise to face
+X
) and moving two steps in that direction. Then, moving 1 step forward would no longer mean increasing the
y
value, but rather continuing forward in our current heading/direction of travel, i.e. increasing our
x
value. This would be equivalent to moving 2 steps right and then 1 more step right in the
Cardinal
setting. (See Figure
1
for a visualization.)

Additionally, we include a simpler form of the
Egocentric Instructor
setting,
Card2Ego
. Rather than going from coordinates to egocentric spatial directions,
Card2Ego
asks models to translate cardinal spatial directions (North/South/East/West) into egocentric spatial directions (left/right/forward/backward).

2.2
The Object Localization Task

The object localization task focuses on understanding how grid coordinates are situated spatially relative to one another. Specifically, given a viewer at a location and with a particular heading, we ask models to determine where a target at a randomly generated coordinate is relative to that viewer (
Egocentric
) or where it is relative to a reference at another randomly generated coordinate (
Allocentric
). In the former, models are expected to reply with something to the effect of the object is "to
my
right" and in the latter with the target is "to
the
right of" the reference.

In this task, we vary the heading of the viewer (i.e. which axis they are facing parallel to), the distance between the target and the reference (are they adjacent to each other or further away), and add distractors (see Appendix
A
for more).

2.3
The Structure Composition Task

The final task explores a model’s ability to understand how blocks/voxels can be composed into larger overall structures. Not only does it require an understanding of how coordinates relate to real space, but also a knowledge of basic shapes/forms (e.g. a series of blocks that differ only in their z-dimension are stacked vertically and thus a column) and the ability to compose large lists of coordinates into meaningful information.

2.3.1
Composition Types

In the most basic setting,
Simple
, the selected coordinates form one structure of a single color and the model must interpret what 3D shape their arrangement forms (e.g.
"an orange row 5 blocks long"
or
"a 3 x 3 x 3 green cube"
). Slightly more complicated is the
Cohesive
setting where multiple colors of blocks form a single structure and the challenge is to understand how those colors interact with the structure (e.g. compare
"a row with alternating orange and blue blocks"
and
"a row with an orange left half and a blue right half"
). Finally, the
Composite
setting places 3 single-colored shapes adjacent to each other, asking the model to identify the 3 shapes, their colors, and where they are relative to each other.

3.1
Metrics

3.1.1
Navigation Metrics

For the
Follower
task, we evaluate the accuracy of the predicted final coordinates as an exact match (i.e. there is no partial credit for getting the x-coordinate coordinate correct if the y-coordinate in incorrect) and for the
Instructor
we measure the exact match accuracy of the generated instruction chain (i.e. every step must be correct for the model to get credit). For a less exacting metric, we computed the distance between the true final coordinates and: the generated coordinates in
Follower
and the final coordinates obtained by executing the generated instruction chain in
Instructor
. As they tracked closely with the accuracies they are reported in see Appendix Table
8
.

3.1.2
Object Localization Metrics

To measure model performance on the Object Localization tasks, we compute,
O_{s}
, the term overlap of the spatial relations in the predicted text,
p
, and the ground truth text,
g
.

O_{s}=\frac{E_{r}(p)\cap E_{r}(g)}{E_{r}(p)\cup E_{r}(g)}*100

where the function
E_{r}
creates a set from each individual spatial relation extracted from the text. Essentially, it takes how many of the spatial terms in the generated sequence
p
are correct and divides this by the size of the set union of all correct spatial terms in the ground truth,
g
, and all generated spatial terms in
p
. For example, if the correct spatial relations,
E_{r}(g)
are {"front", "left"}, the model may score a 0 (
E_{r}(p)
= {"right"}), a 33.33 (
E_{r}(p)
= {"front", "above"}), a 50 (
E_{r}(p)
= {"front"}), a 66.67 (
E_{r}(p)
= {"above", "front", "left"}), or a 100 (
E_{r}(p)
= {"front", "left"}). This penalizes models for predicting incorrect terms, while reducing this penalty for harder examples with more spatial relations to account for.

3.1.3
Structure Composition Metrics

We continue to use the spatial overlap metric,
O_{s}
, described in
3.1.2
and add three additional overlap metrics:
O_{c}
, which measures the overlap of the color terms,
O_{n}
, which measures numerical overlap, and
O_{f}
, which measures the overlap of the structure formation terms.
For
O_{n}
, we use the same simple overlap as
O_{s}
, dividing how many correct numbers were generated by the size of the union of all the ground truth numbers and all generated numbers. However, unlike the Object Localization task, we have the opportunity for multiple structures to have the same color in the
Composite
setting, so the formulation for
O_{c}
is slightly different:

O_{c}=\frac{\sum_{\mathit{colors}}\min(C_{c}(p,\mathit{color}),C_{c}(g,\mathit{color}))}{\sum_{\mathit{colors}}\max(C_{c}(p,\mathit{color}),C_{c}(g,\mathit{color}))}*100

where
C_{c}(\mathit{text},\mathit{term})
counts the occurrence of
\mathit{term}
or its synonyms in the text
\mathit{text}
. Thus, a generation that only identifies one yellow shape in a structure with 2 yellow sub-structures is penalized, as is a model that predicts 2 yellow shapes in a structure with just 1.

O_{f}
follows the same base logic as
O_{c}
, but with more varied synonyms checked in
C_{f}
and partial credit afforded for predicting similar but not correct shapes. E.g. rather than getting no credit for predicting
"a blue row on the ground"
for a blue column, we award partial credit based on how similar the shapes are. (see Appendix
A.1
)

4
Results and Discussion

In this section we will briefly cover the 3 GSU task results shown in Table
1
before covering broader trends: manipulating spatial reference frames (
4.1
), effects of a visual modality (
4.2
), frontier model capability (
4.3
), and improving small LLMs (
4.4
).

Navigation:
We see that the
Instructor
setting provides a greater challenge for prompted models, with the
2D Egocentric Instructor
in Table
1
generally showing significant drops compared to the
2d Egocentric Follower
. The
Egocentric
variant is also more difficult as we see in the accuracy difference between the
Cardinal
and
Egocentric Instructor
. (These patterns hold over all settings, see Appendix
D
for more details.)

Object Localization:

In the
Egocentric
case we see that smaller models receive scores below 50, consistent with further analysis that they often generate spatial relations for axes in which the target and reference do not differ. Larger models adeptly identify the reasoning structure from the example and isolate which axes coordinates differ on, but then fail to assign the correct spatial relation.

Figure 2:
Allocentric
front/back flipping

Most models perform worse on the
Allocentric
variant, suggesting that models struggle to reason about how two grid coordinates are situated relative to each other from the agent perspective (a third coordinate). Those that performed better were likely benefiting from the consistent
+Y
heading in the
Allocentric
setting rather than displaying a particular strength at the task itself. Notably, many models flip the forward and backward directions as their main source of error in this variant. If the target is between the viewer and the reference, many LLMs describe it as "behind" the reference, rather than "in front" of the reference, as if the reference is the viewer. (see Figure
2
).

Structure Composition:
In this task, we see similar spatial (
O_{s}
) performance to the
OL
task, with models often opting to not describe where shapes were relative to each other unless they are certain, and strong color (
O_{c}
) performance, with mistakes due to omissions rather than hallucinations. The numerical overlap (
O_{n}
) errors are caused by models miscounting or due to unconventional ways of breaking up structure descriptions: e.g. rather than describing a 3 x 3 x 8 rectangular prism with those dimensions, one generation describes it as a group of 10 columns each 7 blocks high. While this could describe a similar shape (though it could also be a 7 x 10 plane) and certainly accounts for a similar number of blocks, it receives no credit for either metric.

Overall, the generated descriptions are comprehensible and seem reasonable when viewed independently of the ground truth. In 0-shot settings (Appendix Table
14
), models generated vague answers, e.g.:
"a continuous layer of blocks"
rather than concrete shapes, e.g.
"a 4 x 7 rectangle"
. This behavior extends into the few-shot setting, with numerous productions of "structure", "formation", and "platform" modified by rich adjectives, such as
"a large, flat, expansive structure"
. Models also flatten 3D shapes, outputting "square" for cube and "rectangle" for rectangular prisms. Other times they name a shape incorrectly, but still display insight into the formed structure, such as calling a 2 x 2 x 8 rectangular prism a
"cube with height 8 and width 2"
(fundamentally not a cube, but still displaying spatial understanding that is rewarded by the
O_{n}
metric instead).
A notable threshold that stronger models cross is distinguishing rows and columns: stronger models identify which axes the blocks differ on and rarely conflate the two, instead mixing up cubes with rectangular prisms or vertical rectangular prisms with columns. Generally, weaker models also omit reasoning for the shapes they produce, while stronger models like Gemini explain how the coordinate ranges influence its productions, which is reflected in its scores.

4.1
Manipulating Spatial Reference Frames

The reasoning traces of the models corroborate the score disparity between
Cardinal
and
Egocentric Navigation
tasks in Tables
1
and
10
. In the former setting, most models output neat, contained logic with only occasional errors,
while in the letter, models fail to keep the
X
and
Y
axes oriented consistently, leading to generations such as
"we are facing
-Y
with
+Y
to our right"
and
"we turned towards
+X
, so we’re facing
-X
"
. Larger models, though less likely to make these mistakes, calculate updated headings after a turn and then "forget" to use them in the next step, outputting logic and final coordinates consistent with the fixed grids of the
Cardinal
setting.

In the
Egocentric Object Localization
tasks, this aversion to non-standard spatial reference frames manifests as non sequitur logic where the model establishes the correct heading in the premise but completes the generation with an incorrect deduction. These deductions are generally consistent with the standard
+Y
heading, e.g. while facing
-Y
:
"I know that
-X
is to my right
[correct premise]
"so x values smaller than me are to my left"
[incorrect deduction consistent with a
+Y
heading instead]. The spatial term overlap,
O_{s}
is also higher for the
+Y
heading than any other (Appendix Table
12
). This phenomenon might also explain poor performance on the
Allocentric
variant in Table
1
, particularly the forward/backing flipping described in Figure
2
, as models struggle to reconcile how two frames of reference interact with each other.

We suspect that the inability to correctly reorient the spatial reference frame is due in part to exposure to the standard Cartesian orientation in pre-training and in part due to the complexity of updating it. This is supported by the Table
1

Navigation Card2Ego
results, where any mathematical reasoning about textual grid coordinates is completely absent, replaced by converting Cardinal directions into relative ones. LLMs have an easier time with
Card2Ego
than the
Egocentric Instructor
but still fall short, showing that, in addition to struggling with grid coordinates, LLMs consistently struggle with the concept of rotating spatial frames of reference outside of explicit grids too.

4.2
Effects of the Visual Modality

As shown by the 4 VLMs shown in light blue under their LLM counterparts in Table
1
, there is no clear correlation between exposure to a visual modality and improved reasoning over textual coordinate grids, especially in the smaller models. For example, on the
Navigation
tasks, Pixtral outperforms Mistral, while Qwen outperforms Qwen-VL, and both Llama models perform similarly, though perhaps the consistent pattern within each model family can be attributed to how vision is integrated into their respective VLMs. In some of the tasks (e.g.
Object Localization
), we see that Qwen-32B-VL outperformed Qwen-32B and in others (i.e.
Structure Composition
), all VLMs outperform their LLM counterparts: these patterns may suggest that VLMs need at least a certain LLM size before being able to utilize information gained from interacting with a textual modality or that they confer more benefits when reasoning over coordinate structures rather than spacial relations.

4.3
Frontier Capabilities

As expected, the frontier models outperform smaller LLMs, though notably Qwen-72b is able to match their performance on some tasks and even outperform GPT-4o. While proficient in the
Cardinal
setting, GPT-4o struggles to keep track of spatial references in the
Egocentric
one, incorrectly computing the current heading or forgetting to act according to an updated heading, especially in longer sequences (see Appendix Table
11
). For example, GPT-4o generates that it was
"currently facing
-X
, but need[ed] to move right [and moving] right means turning 90 degrees clockwise so that’s
+X
"
. This final heading should have been
+Y
, a rotation that GPT handles successfully in shorter sequences. However, a right turn leading to facing
+X
is consistent with the fixed
Cardinal
frame of reference, which the model seems to default to, even in the midst of egocentric-compliant reasoning.

The latest Gemini model solves multiple tasks completely, even harder variants of the tasks such as arbitrarily long navigation sequences with non-integer step lengths or non-axis aligned turns. It also performs surprisingly well on distinguishing independent block structures from scrambled lists of individual blocks. Given this, we must acknowledge that Gemini-3-Pro has spatial understanding when it comes to spatial frames of reference relative to an embodied agent and composing blocks into larger structures. At the very least, it must have been exposed to similar problems, perhaps in a mathematical context.

However, that is not to say there is no room for improvement within the domain of textual grid based tasks. We further prompt Gemini on a task combining each of the 3 task categories: an egocentric agent must follow navigation instructions and then answer where two block-based structures are relative to each other (see
E.6
). This requires the model to: (a) successfully complete egocentric navigation and identify its final heading, (b) distinguish the target and reference structures from distractor objects, all presented as a single list of blocks, and (c) identify where the target is relative to the reference. Here, we find that even Gemini fell short, with a final spatial overlap
O_{s}
of 0.62: it sometimes mixes up similar shapes, struggles with the forward/backward issue, and makes slight errors in relative spatial relations given the 3D nature of block structures.

4.4
Improving Small LLMs

We anticipate that more frontier models will develop enough spatial reasoning to solve the base tasks, and eventually the harder variants as well. However, the simplicity of GSU and the subsequent gap between it and real-world scenarios, motivates us to want stronger small LLMs. To that end, we train our worst performing small LLM (Llama-8b) with LORA to elicit an underlying spatial understanding.
Initial experiments reduced performance on the
Navigation
task and so to better match natural LLM outputs, we generate synthetic reasoning traces to accompany the ground truth answers for the
Navigation
and
Object Localization
tasks. (This also matched our prompting findings in Appendix Tables
9
and
15
that showed a single example with strong reasoning improved performance more than multiple examples without reasoning.) As we show in Table
1
, with these reasoning traces, Llama-8b shows improved performance on the
Object Localization
tasks (the reasoning traces offered slight LORA performance boosts). It also more than doubles its performance on the
Navigation Follower
tasks after LORA, outperforming GPT-4o and almost matching Gemini. For the
Navigation Instructor
tasks, improvement is even more drastic for the
Egocentric
and
Card2Ego
versions it initially struggled with, increasing performance tenfold. With this, we show that textual spatial reasoning is not out of reach for smaller models and their potential to match frontier models.

We also note that fully finetuning a small model like FlanT5 (<1B params) is sufficient to outperform many LLMs and match performance with some frontier models, especially on sequence generation tasks like
Navigation Instructor
(Table
1
). This highlights how simple the tasks should be to solve: a lightweight model a fraction of the size is better at identifying shapes from coordinate lists of blocks, where those shapes are relative to each other, and generating instructions for grid navigation.

5
Related Works

Spatial Reasoning

Instead of text, physical information is generally conveyed to LLMs via images, either from external POVs like observing scenes from above
(
Li et al. 2024a
)
or from the perspective of embodied agents for applications like VLN, and pointcloud embeddings
(
Hong et al. 2023
)
. However, even after integrating additional modalities and achieving strong performance on many visual tasks, VLMs could not understand or reason about physical space as capably as humans, struggling with spatial VQA, particularly object orientations in images
(
Liao et al. 2024
;
Liu et al. 2023
)
and mental transformations like rotations
(
Stogiannidis et al. 2025
)
. They fall well short of human performance localizing objects within scenes and reasoning about object dimensions
(
Majumdar et al. 2024
)
. Some have proposed addressing these spatial reasoning shortcomings with training datasets that align 2D images with textual tasks about the orientation of, distance between, and location of nodes
Tang et al. 2025
, while others have questioned what VLMs are actually perceiving in images and found that they perform just as well with rich text descriptions instead of input images
(
Wang et al. 2024a
;
Fu et al. 2024b
)
.

Combined with the difficulty of isolating spatial performance from visual perception, this motivates text-only spatial reasoning to evaluate the "mental" models LLMs have of space. Some use graphs, exploring graph traversals in which LLMs hallucinate nodes, take suboptimal paths, and deteriorate on denser graphs
(
Momennejad et al. 2023
)
or describing spatial relations between graph nodes using multihop reasoning over intermediary nodes
(
Shi et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2024b
)
. Others are nominally set in grids, such as
Yamada et al. 2024
which explores 2D grid navigation but only on closed loop paths using relative descriptions of positions and
Mayer et al. 2025
, which proposes a visual puzzle game on grid, but compares visual representation to a chess-style notation for the textual representation. Few consider coordinates for text-only spatial reasoning tasks (a notable exception being
Aghzal et al. 2025
’s evaluation of LLM path planning through obstacles on fixed 2D grids) despite coordinate grids being a standard for representing space and likely plentiful in LLM pretraining data.

One context in which LLMs may have encountered coordinates or reasoning about them is math problems. While most coordinates in such problems are paired with text and the associated reasoning is unrelated to spatial relations, the success in mathematical reasoning
(
Shao et al. 2024
;
Ahn et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
and a variety of math datasets
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
;
Chen et al. 2022
)
suggests that coordinate spatial reasoning is not outside the realm of LLM abilities.

Applications

GSU distills the spatial reasoning required in embodied settings to its simplest form. As such, the main applications of this work are in embodied AI, from VLN
(
Thomason et al. 2019
)
to cooperative task completion
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
. While there are several intermediate steps before we can extend our findings to multimodal models, virtual tasks may benefit more directly. Take the MDC
(
Narayan-Chen et al. 2019
)
, in which two agents collaborate to build a block-based structure in a 3D grid. Existing approaches struggle with shifting spatial reference frames caused by agents moving around the environment during the course of building (and thus might benefit from methods that improve performance on our
Egocentric Navigation
task). The
Object Localization
task addresses the issue of generating spatial relations for blocks relative to existing structures while the
Structure Composition
aligns with the MDC Architect describing a target structure, a part of the task that remains largely unexplored as recent work has focused on the instruction-following Builder
(
Jayannavar et al. 2025
)
.

Our focus on smaller or unimodal models is further motivated by the practical constraints of real-world embodied AI, where the latencies of frontier models can significantly hinder a physical agent
(
Park et al. 2026
;
Pollini et al. 2025
)
, hardware constraints may require lightweight models that can be fully housed inside a small robot, privacy concerns may necessitate local models or eschewing multimodal inputs at times
(
Marchang and Di Nuovo 2022
;
Moncrieff et al. 2008
)
, and specialized tasks may need further training anyway.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we propose GSU, a novel evaluation framework providing a controlled environment to test spatial reasoning and spatial communication without confounding factors like visual perception. Evaluating a variety of models, we found that most, even some frontier models, struggle to track rotating frames of spatial reference and understand how blocks at grid coordinates may be composed into structures, though recent models are crossing this threshold of spatial understanding. We also provide empirical evidence that exposure to a visual modality does not inherently guarantee improved spatial reasoning, or at least, communication about it, especially in smaller models, which goes against the current assumptions about VLM capabilities and raises questions about how the parametric knowledge gained from the modality is stored and accessed by models. Finally, we show that training lightweight models and small LLMs can match or outperform massive foundation models on these textual grid spatial reasoning tasks, supporting their use as specialized embodied agents.

Limitations

This work is, in part, limited by the nature of the proposed tasks. While the simplicity allows for extremely controlled observations on understanding, it is noted that such tasks may not directly generalize to real world settings outside of virtual environments. In particular, embodied scenarios that involve continuous space, visual noise, and partial observability, are not easily represented in the current formulation of GSU. That being said, we think generalizing to continuous space on a grid should be quite feasible, as math is not generally the feature that models struggle the most with. However, having more angles of rotation available to models in
Navigation
tasks will certainly increase the difficulty movement would no longer be nicely axes aligned (though arguable with the egocentric setting the most challenging component is keeping track of headings). In the future, it would be interesting to expand textual problems to incorporate partial observability, exploring if models can tell what would be in their line of sight and reasoning about what may be obscured. It might also present a means of checking information stored during exploration, creating more interesting problems such as reconciling information gathered from different viewpoints (that may not be expressed relative to the same overarching fixed grid). The most direction extension of grid coordinates into more physical problems would likely be in pointclouds and we would be interested to see if our findings generalize to those richer, longer contexts.

Within the confines of the task, we think there is room to further improve smaller models on GSU, explore possibilities such as using performance on a
Follower
task with more direct signal to improve
Instructor
performance, and see if forcing models to communicate about and reason over textual grids can force stronger internal representations or help connect parametric knowledge to visual inforamtion in multimodal models.

A.1
Shape Metric

For the shape metric, we check for synonyms in the generation and map them to their corresponding shape. The synonyms are built around the base vocabulary and then appended to with common outputs in actual generations. For columns, we accept "column", as well any production with "vertical" or "upright" and "line" or "row". For rows, we accept any generation with "row" or "line" that does not mention verticality. For towers (multiple columns grouped together), we accept "tower", "rectangular prism", and "pillar". For planes (rectangular prisms only one block thick), we accept "plane", "platform", "rectangle", "wall", "square", "ring", and "O". For cubes, we accept "cube".

Furthermore, since shapes can be similar to others, if a generation does not match at least one of the ground truth shapes, we offer it partial credit based on how similar the shape it predicted was. Confusing rows and columns leads 0.6 of the full 1.0 received for a total match, as does mixing up a column and a tower. Since towers and cubes are both rectangular prisms, we offer half credit for such mistakes, and 0.1 for towers and planes or planes and cubes, and 0.2 for rows and towers.

Appendix B
Experimental Information

B.1
Training Details

For the LORA fine-tuning runs, we experimented with various ranks, learning rates, and training set sizes. Overall, we found that the model performance plateaued at its highest level with rank 64, a learning rate between 5e-4 and 1e-5 depending on the task, and a training set of size 3000. We used alpha=8 as recommended in the original paper and an AdamW optimizer. We allowed the models to train for 4 epochs, but chose the model with the highest validation accuracy as the one to evaluate. For the FlanT5-large and BART-base runs, we allow the model to train for 15 epochs or until the loss converges using an AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5e-5 for Flan and 1e-4 for BART.

This project was run on up to 4 gpuA100x4’s using approximately 1500 GPU hours of compute.

At the start of the generated reasoning traces, models seem to understand the standard Cartesian layout (when facing positive y, positive x is to your right), but as they progress through the instruction sequences they fail to keep this consistent with the actions they take. Sometimes, these incorrect justifications are followed by correct steps though, as in:
"we are facing negative x with positive x to the right
[incorrect,
+Y
would be to the right]
so that means that turning right means going towards positive x …
[rest of the step]
… now, we are facing positive y"
[
+Y
being the correct axes to the right of
-X
, and thus the correct heading upon finishing the step].

For the
Instructor
task, we see that performance is weaker than its
Follower
counterpart, but some models are still able to produce strong instructions sequences that get quite close to the desired endpoint, with Qwen-72b even solving the task completely. But the
Egocentric
setting again breaks what "understanding" these models possess, leading to performances well below a simple model of 875M parameters fully fine-tuned on the problem. Even Qwen-72b and GPT-4o, which show some promise on shorter sequences, get turned around in longer sequences.

To better understand the source of the errors in the
Egocentric
setting, we proposed the
Card2Ego
setting, which just required understanding how cardinal directions would change relative to an embodied traveller, removing the necessity of reasoning about specific coordinates and where they fall relative to each other. We found that models performed better in this setting, with medium sized models seeing notable improvements, but were still far from completely solving the task.

Looking at the reasoning traces generated in more detail, we saw that model accuracy dropped on paths with more steps, though this decrease was much more subtle in the cardinal task, occurring more often in weaker or fine-tuned models and due calculation errors / minor mistakes. For egocentric navigation, the dropoff in performance and corresponding increase in the "distance off" metric were much sharper. Many of the weaker models went from accuracies in the 70s and 80s for shorter sequences to 0 or almost 0 in the longer sequences, but even strong models saw performance drops from almost perfect accuracy to numbers in the 60s and 70s.

Observing the reasoning traces produced by the models for the navigation tasks show that in addition to preferring shorter sequences, most models perform better on backward direction changes as opposed to right/left direction changes. Models were also more likely to produce incorrect conclusion in these longer sequences, particularly in the egocentric navigation tasks. Other times, models across families (GPT, Qwen, Llama) would correctly compute the new headings but not make use of them, falling back to the cardinal version of the problem. Or, they would twist the logic to make the egocentric direction changes align with the direction in a cardinal setting. For example, when facing negative x:
"Moving 8 steps back means moving opposite the direction we are facing. So we will move along positive x, i.e. increase the x value by 8.
[consistent with facing the
-X
direction]
However, since we are facing negative x, we need to move along negative x, i.e. decrease the x value by 8.
[the complete opposite]
So, our new position is (-3 - 8, 0) = (-11, 0). Since we turned to move, we are now facing negative x and positive y is to our right"
(Llama-8b). This often led models to perform very well on shorter egocentric sequences where the error had not yet compounded, but rapidly diverge on longer sequences. We also found that models produced similar errors within their model family (e.g. Qwen-7b predicting the same incorrect coordinates as the 72B version or the vision version). Smaller models were also less internally consistent and likely to make mistakes about how axes were oriented relative to each other ("since we are facing positive x, negative y is to our left") or other errors (e.g. when moving 9 steps in one direction, incrementing all the x-, y-, z- values by 9). These errors were much less prevalent in the cardinal variants of these tasks, leading us to believe that basic math and reasoning are not the issue in these tasks, but rather keep the rotations / current heading state consistent with previous move and keeping the axes consistent with each other.

D.3
Structure Composition

We saw spatial results similar to the
Allocentric
ones, with just slight improvements in some models, likely due to the perspective difference in the task definition: in the
Allocentric
setup we treat the reference as having its own spatial reference (i.e. it has a left and right of its own, mirroring those of the viewer), whereas in the Structure Composition setup we defer to a viewer facing along the
+Y
axis for spatial terms to preserve the simplicity of the task. This simpler spatial referencing is offset by the higher number of blocks and the need to group and then orient them correctly.

Naturally, the
O_{f}
subtask was the most difficult since it requires models to match groupings of coordinates to real-life shapes that exist outside of grid and coordinate settings, making the fully fine-tuned model’s performance all the more impressive. Reflective of the wide vocabluary that models might apply to these shapes, we used generous synonyms in the shape metric (see Appendix
A.1
). As such, an
O_{f}
score around 50, reflects a mastery of simple shapes like columns and rows in their individual and
Composite
settings and some limited success with more complex shapes such as towers, rectangular prisms, and cubes, whereas one near 90 suggests a mastery of individual shapes and composite structures, with occasional errors, usually on larger shapes. Finally, we did see shape preference in the model outputs, with certain models generally not predicting certain types of shapes (depending on the model, this was generally a cube or a tower).
