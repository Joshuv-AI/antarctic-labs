Cytomegalovirus and BK polyomavirus in belatacept-treated kidney transplant recipients: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ibrahim Tawhari, Fatma Yamani, Manal Alotaibi, Hany El Hennawy, Mohammed Tawhari, Muath Alqahtani, Khalid Asiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the risk of cytomegalovirus (CMV) and BK virus infections in kidney transplant recipients receiving belatacept-based immunosuppression compared to calcineurin inhibitor (CNI)-based regimens. Seven studies (six randomized controlled trials and one cohort study) involving 1,625 patients were analyzed. Pooled results showed little to no evidence of a difference in CMV infection risk between belatacept and CNI-treated patients (odds ratio [OR] 1.24, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.80–1.94; P = 0.34). However, there was little to no evidence of a difference in BK virus infection risk, though the point estimate suggested a possible increase (OR 2.11, 95% CI 0.71–6.33, P = 0.18). Heterogeneity was low across studies (I² = 0%). A sensitivity analysis excluding the single non-randomized cohort study (i.e., restricted to the randomized controlled trials) showed little to no evidence of a difference in CMV infection risk (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.57–2.37; P = 0.69). While belatacept offers renal and metabolic advantages over CNIs, the potential for CMV and BK viremia necessitates careful monitoring, particularly in high-risk patients. Standardized viral surveillance and further research in larger cohorts are needed to clarify long-term infection risks.

Background

Kidney transplantation is the standard of care for patients with end-stage renal disease, significantly improving both survival and quality of life [1]. While advancements in post-transplant immunosuppressive management have successfully reduced acute rejection rates and improved short-term outcomes, comparable improvements in long-term kidney-allograft survival have not been observed [1]. While calcineurin inhibitors (CNIs) are the standard maintenance immunosuppressive agents utilized, those agents can adversely affect long-term allograft and patient survival due to their possible nephrotoxicity and effects on blood pressure control, lipid levels, and glycemic control [1, 2]. This translates to the demand for more selective immunosuppressive agents capable of improving the long-term graft- and patient survival while decreasing the side effects of chronic toxicities associated with nonselective immunosuppressive agents [1].

Belatacept, a T-cell costimulation blocker, has emerged as a potential solution, as it binds to CD80 and CD86 on antigen-presenting cells, which inhibits the activation of T-cells [1, 2]. Belatacept was developed to circumvent renal and non-renal toxicity of CNIs and has since been shown to be effective in the prevention of acute rejection and maintenance of renal allograft function [1, 3]. Clinical trials showed that belatacept-based regimen was associated with better long-term renal function, improved cardiovascular and metabolic profile, and a lower probability of de novo donor-specific antibodies in comparison with CNI-based regimen [1–5]. Belatacept, approved in 2011 based on phase III clinical trials, including BENEFIT and BENEFIT-EXT, has been considered a major alternative maintenance immunosuppressive therapy [4, 5].

Transplant patients are more susceptible to opportunistic infections, particularly viral infections, due to impaired host immune defense by immunosuppressive medications [1]. Cytomegalovirus (CMV), a known major viral pathogen that affects transplant recipients, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality and may indirectly contribute to graft rejection and lymphoproliferative disorders [1, 6]. The prevalence of CMV infection in transplant recipients can be high, with the majority of infections occurring within the first 1 to 6 months after transplantation, depending on the intensity of immunosuppression and donor–recipient CMV serostatus [1, 6]. Another concerning infection is the BK virus (BKV), which has been linked to polyomavirus-associated graft nephropathy among transplant recipients, causing progressive decline in graft function and graft loss [1]. It is essential to monitor for such viral infections regularly to enable early diagnosis and initiation of appropriate treatment, including adjustment of immunosuppressive therapy [1, 7].

Although prior studies mainly focused on the efficacy of belatacept and improvement of graft survival and renal function, there remains a lack of definitive evidence and persistent discrepancies regarding the infection risks, particularly CMV and BKV, compared with CNIs [1, 2, 6, 7]. Early studies showed similar rates of CMV and BKV infections among belatacept-treated recipients compared to cyclosporine-treated recipients [1]. However, other studies, particularly in conversion regimens, showed a higher rate and atypical manifestation of CMV disease as well as the development of resistance to first-line medications and persistent viremia in higher-risk patients who receive belatacept [6, 7]. These inconsistencies highlight the limitations of previous studies in terms of their small sample sizes, variability of results, incomplete reporting of infection outcomes, and methodological heterogeneity [1, 6–8]. Further research is needed to gain better insight into the effect of belatacept on the spectrum of viral infections in kidney transplant recipients.

Rationale

The introduction of Belatacept, CD28 costimulation blocker, in kidney transplant maintenance immunosuppression may negatively affect cellular immunity predisposing recipients to infections, particularly viral infections. In propensity-matched groups of recipients undergoing conversion to belatacept, the incidences of CMV disease (17.7% vs. 2.8%) and late-onset invasive disease in older or donor seropositive/recipient seronegative (D +/R -) recipients were much higher [6]. Similarly, a cohort study found that belatacept-based therapy was associated with increased incidences of severe CMV viremia (> 25,000 IU/mL) and severe BK viremia (> 10,000 IU/mL) [9]. In patients at high risk, belatacept-treated patients experienced more protracted CMV viremia than tacrolimus-treated patients [7]. This review will attempt to systematically synthesize the available evidence in order to quantify the emerging risks of CMV, BK viremia and BK nephropathy.

Objectives

This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to characterize the infectious risks of belatacept-based maintenance immunosuppression in adult kidney transplant recipients with particular emphasis on two viral complications: cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection and BK virus-related complications such as BK viremia or BK virus-associated nephropathy. Specifically, this review aims to:

Compare and quantify the incidence of CMV infection in kidney transplant recipients receiving belatacept-based maintenance therapy compared with those maintained on calcineurin inhibitor (CNI)-based regimens.Evaluate the frequency of BK viremia or BK nephropathy in kidney transplant recipients receiving belatacept therapy compared with a CNI-based regimen.Determine whether belatacept-based immunosuppression increases the risk of these viral infections compared with standard care.

Information sources

We conducted an extensive literature search using the following databases: PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. The search covered articles published from January 1, 2010, to June 30, 2025. A predefined search strategy was applied on PubMed and Cochrane Library, whereas Google Scholar was used to retrieve some additional relevant studies and grey literature. The databases were last searched on August 1, 2025. Language and publication-date limitations were applied from the initial search.

Selection process

All titles and abstracts retrieved from the database searches were screened for eligibility by two independent reviewers using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria (see Table 1). Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were then retrieved and assessed independently by the same two reviewers to determine final inclusion. Any disagreements during either the title/abstract or full-text screening phases were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, by consultation with a third reviewer. The reviewers worked independently throughout the selection process. The selection process was documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Fig. 1), detailing the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included at each stage.

Data collection

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form that was pilot-tested on a small sample of included studies. The reviewers worked independently to minimize bias and ensure accuracy. Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year, country, design), participant characteristics (sample size), intervention details (belatacept dosing, concomitant immunosuppression), comparator regimens, outcome definitions, and incidence of CMV infection, BK viremia, and BK nephropathy. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Where key data were missing or unclear, attempts were made to contact study authors via email to obtain additional information or clarification.

Study risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using established tools appropriate to each study design. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2) tool was employed, evaluating five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result (Table 3; Fig. 2). For non-randomized studies, we used the Risk of Bias In Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool, which assesses bias across seven domains including confounding, selection, classification of interventions, deviations, missing data, measurement, and reporting (Table 4). Two reviewers independently assessed each study’s risk of bias. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached. The results were tabulated and used to inform both sensitivity analyses and the overall strength of evidence synthesis.

Effect measures

For all dichotomous outcomes, including cytomegalovirus (CMV) infection, BK viremia, and BK virus-associated nephropathy (BKVAN), the principal effect measures used in the synthesis were odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All outcomes were synthesized using a random-effects model due to anticipated clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Findings were interpreted using a levels-of-evidence approach rather than a dichotomous significance threshold [10].

Synthesis methods

To determine study eligibility for synthesis, we tabulated key characteristics such as immunosuppressive regimen, CMV and BK virus outcomes and compared them against predefined PICOS criteria (see Table 1). Data preparation involved converting incidence or event percentages into raw numbers. Outcomes were tabulated in summary tables (Table 2) and visually displayed using forest plots, with funnel plots establishing whether the included studies had publication bias. Meta-analyses were conducted using a random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird), with odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) as the primary effect measure. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic, and analyses were performed in RevMan Web and R (RStudio). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings: for the BK virus analysis, by excluding Bassil et al. [1], a small, partially-blinded study; and for the CMV analysis, by excluding the single non-randomized cohort study (a leave-one-out analysis restricted to the randomized controlled trials).

Reporting bias assessment

To assess the risk of bias due to missing results (reporting bias), we generated a funnel plot for the primary CMV analysis and assessed asymmetry by visual inspection (Fig. 3.2). Because no outcome included ten or more studies, formal statistical tests for small-study effects or publication bias (e.g., Egger’s test) were not performed, as these are unreliable when based on few studies.

Results

Study selection

The systematic review began with a comprehensive search across three major databases, PubMed, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, yielding a total of 777 records. After removing 87 duplicates, 690 records underwent title and abstract screening, during which 378 were excluded for irrelevance to the study’s focus on belatacept and viral infections in kidney transplant recipients. The remaining 312 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, with 305 excluded for reasons such as non-human studies, non-belatacept interventions, lack of a comparator group, missing quantitative CMV or BK virus data, non-original research, overlapping cohorts with other studies, or publication outside the specified date range (2010 to 2025). Ultimately, seven studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (see Fig. 1). These comprised six randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and one cohort study, collectively representing data from 1,625 patients. The selected studies spanned diverse geographic regions, including the United States and Europe. Most of the included trials were open-label and carried some risk-of-bias concerns, and data on viral complications beyond five years post-transplant were limited, which should be considered when interpreting the pooled estimates. This methodological approach adhered to PRISMA 2020 guidelines, ensuring transparency and minimizing bias in study selection.

Risk of bias in studies

The risk of bias assessment for the seven included studies revealed varying levels of methodological rigor (see Tables 3 and 4). Among the six randomized controlled trials (RCTs), Budde et al. [2] and Grinyó et al. [11] demonstrated low overall risk of bias, with robust randomization processes, minimal missing data, and objective outcome measurements. The remaining four RCTs (Vincenti et al. [5], Charpentier et al. [3], Bassil et al. [1], and Tawhari et al. [12]) raised some concerns, primarily due to open-label designs introducing potential detection bias, uneven viral monitoring protocols, or protocol deviations, with Tawhari et al. [12] additionally affected by early termination (Table 3). The lone cohort study [7] carried moderate risk, largely from residual confounding in its non-randomized design (Table 4). While most studies reported outcomes transparently, the variability in bias, particularly in measurement and confounding, underscores the need for cautious interpretation of pooled results, especially for CMV/BK infection rates. Sensitivity analyses were employed to mitigate these limitations.

Subgroup analysis

In the CMV high-risk (D+/R-) subgroup, three studies (n = 813 participants; belatacept = 466, CNI = 347) contributed data. The pooled analysis showed that there was little to no evidence of a difference between belatacept and CNI-based regimens in terms of CMV infection risk (random-effects OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.73–1.96; common-effect OR 1.20, 95% CI 0.73–1.96). The point estimate indicated little to no evidence of a difference, with only a slight trend toward increased CMV risk with belatacept. The heterogeneity was low (I2 = 0%, p = 0.4880), suggesting consistent results among studies (Fig. 5). Nevertheless, the small number of included studies and the dominant contribution of a single study (Karadkhele et al., 2021, which contributed 76.1% of the total weight) limited the analysis (Fig. 5), which may impact the strength of the pooled estimate.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the risk of CMV and BK virus infections in kidney transplant recipients receiving belatacept-based immunosuppression compared to CNI-based regimens. The pooled analysis of seven studies (six RCTs and one cohort study) demonstrated little to no evidence of a difference in CMV infection risk between belatacept and CNI-treated patients (OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.80–1.94; P = 0.34), with low heterogeneity (I² = 0%). A sensitivity analysis excluding the single non-randomized cohort study (a leave-one-out analysis restricted to the randomized controlled trials) showed little to no evidence of a difference in CMV infection risk (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.57–2.37; P = 0.69), indicating that the higher point estimate in the primary analysis was driven by this single cohort study. For BK virus infections, there was also little to no evidence of a difference, although the point estimate suggested an increased risk associated with belatacept therapy (OR 2.11, 95% CI 0.71–6.33; P = 0.18), which persisted in sensitivity analyses (OR 2.35, 95% CI 0.68–8.15; P = 0.18). The wide confidence intervals and borderline P-values indicate insufficient evidence to confirm this association, highlighting the need for larger, high-quality studies.

The findings show little to no evidence of a difference in CMV risk with belatacept; this was reinforced by a sensitivity analysis restricted to the randomized trials (excluding the single cohort study), which showed no signal (OR 1.16, P = 0.69) and indicated that the cohort study accounted for the higher point estimate in the primary analysis. These findings nonetheless raise some concern about its potential association with BK viremia, possibly due to impaired T-cell-mediated viral clearance. Notably, the included studies exhibited variability in viral monitoring protocols and definitions of infection, which may have influenced outcome reporting. For instance, Karadkhele et al. [7] reported prolonged CMV replication in high-risk belatacept recipients, underscoring the importance of tailored prophylaxis in this subgroup. Limitations of this review include the predominance of open-label randomized controlled trials and variability across studies in viral surveillance protocols, prophylaxis strategies, diagnostic thresholds, and outcome definitions, all of which may have introduced detection bias. Additionally, a detailed subgroup analysis based on specific CMV prophylaxis strategies (e.g., valganciclovir antiviral prophylaxis versus preemptive CMV DNAemia monitoring) was not feasible due to variability in prophylactic approaches across studies and incomplete reporting of these data in the primary studies.

Most notably, a significant methodological concern is the bias introduced from the lone cohort study, Karadkhele et al. [7]. With its large sample size, this study accounted for 61.6% of the total weight in the primary CMV analysis and 76.1% in the CMV high-risk subgroup analysis. In a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis excluding this cohort, the pooled CMV estimate attenuated to OR 1.16 (95% CI 0.57–2.37; P = 0.69), confirming that the higher primary point estimate was largely attributable to this single non-randomized study. Because Karadkhele et al. [7] is a large cohort study, unlike other studies, it is hard to comment on residual clinical confounding issues or issues concerning the study sample. Therefore, extreme caution should be exercised when interpreting such CMV outcomes, as they are based on a non-randomized dataset, and may not reflect the actual clinical risk profiles. Furthermore, the meta-analysis was underpowered to detect rare outcomes such as BK virus-associated nephropathy, and long-term data beyond five years post-transplant were limited.

Clinical implications suggest that there was little to no evidence overall that belatacept increased CMV risk, and this finding was reinforced when the single non-randomized cohort study was excluded; nonetheless, given the higher CMV rates reported among high-risk (D+/R-) recipients in that cohort, cautious monitoring for CMV remains prudent in high-risk patients, and vigilance for BK viremia. Future research should prioritize standardized viral monitoring, longer follow-up periods, and subgroup analyses (e.g., D⁺/R⁻ CMV serostatus) to refine risk stratification. Overall, belatacept remains a viable alternative to CNIs with distinct infection risks, necessitating balanced consideration of its benefits (e.g., improved renal function) against potential viral complications.

The results from subgroup analyses presented in Fig. 5, which evaluated CMV reactivation in the high-risk D+/R− subgroup, and Fig. 6, which evaluated CMV infection risk according to de novo versus conversion belatacept use, should be interpreted with caution, because the subgroup analysis relied on a small number of studies and events and thus is underpowered to identify clinically significant differences. Therefore, the findings are to be viewed as exploratory, but not confirmatory.

While age may be an important modifier of the outcomes reported in this analysis, an age-stratified meta-analysis was not feasible as not all primary studies reported the mean age and standard deviation (e.g., Grinyó et al. [11] did not report age). Moreover, the lack of variation in mean ages across studies (most fell in the mid-40s to mid-50s) restricts the ability to find an age-specific effect. Given these limitations, the results might not be applicable to the elderly recipients (age over 65 years) who were the minority of the participants in the trials included (e.g., 17% in Budde et al. [2]).

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the risk of CMV and BK virus infections in kidney transplant recipients receiving belatacept-based immunosuppression compared to CNI-based regimens. The findings indicate little to no evidence of a difference in CMV infection risk (OR 1.24, 95% CI 0.80–1.94), with little to no evidence of a difference, though the point estimate suggested increased BK viremia (OR 2.11, 95% CI 0.71–6.33). While belatacept offers advantages such as improved renal function and reduced CNI toxicity, the potential for BK virus complications warrants vigilance, particularly in high-risk patients. Limitations include variability in study designs and potential publication bias. Future research should prioritize standardized viral monitoring, long-term follow-up, and subgroup analyses to refine risk stratification. Clinicians should weigh belatacept’s benefits against its infection profile when selecting immunosuppressive regimens.
