Risk factors for nontuberculous mycobacteria infection in lung transplant recipients
Authors: Qi Wang, Qing Zhang, Jiankang Zhao, Mengyin Chen, Chun Wang, Li Zhao, Bin Xing, Lijuan Guo, Hong Chen, Bin Cao, Wenhui Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence and number of deaths of nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) infection in lung transplant recipients is on the rise. Risk factors for NTM infection after lung transplant are not well characterized.MethodsThis was a retrospective, single-center case-control study including patients who underwent lung transplantation (LTx) at the China-Japan Friendship Hospital between March 2018 and April 2024. A total of 23 NTM infection patients and 474 controls were enrolled in the study. Cox regression analysis and competing risks models were used to identify risk factors for NTM infection after LTx.ResultsThe incidence of antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) (26.1% versus 8.2%; p value = 0.012) was higher in the NTM infection group than in controls. A 1:2 propensity score matching resulted in 18 NTM infection patients and 36 controls. The incidence of AMR in NTM infection group remained higher than in controls. Univariable Cox regression analysis revealed that the occurrence of AMR (hazard ratio [HR]5.77; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.45-23.00; p value = 0.013) and CLAD (HR 3.40; 95% CI 1.03–11.19; p value = 0.044) were risk factors for NTM infection. Adjusted multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that the occurrence of AMR was independently associated with increased risk of NTM infection. Competitive risk models showed that LTRs with AMR had a substantially higher cumulative incidence of NTM infection.ConclusionsOur findings suggest a possible association between AMR and NTM infection. In addition, this study provides clinicians with relevant information regarding NTM infection in this setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04337-5.Keywords: Lung transplant, NTM, Risk factors, AMR

Introduction

Lung transplantation (LTx) is an important treatment option for end-stage lung disease, aimed at improving long-term survival and quality of life [1]. Lung transplant recipients (LTRs) must take immunosuppressants to prevent allograft rejection [2]. However, as a consequence of immunosuppressive therapy and anatomical alterations of the respiratory tract, LTRs are at substantially increased risk of infection, which constitutes a principal cause of post-transplant mortality [3].

Nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) can cause opportunistic infection that are often easily overlooked and challenging to treat. NTM infection predominantly occur in immunocompromised individuals or in immunocompetent patients with underlying chronic airway diseases [4–6]. Established risk factors for NTM infection include chronic respiratory disease, prior pulmonary infection, and immunosuppression, among others [7–9]. Additionally, patients undergoing solid organ transplantation are particularly susceptible to NTM infection, with LTRs exhibiting the highest infection rates [3]. LTRs may be particularly susceptible due to transplant‑specific factors, including altered airway anatomy, impaired mucociliary clearance, chronic graft injury, and prolonged immunosuppression. Together, these factors increase the likelihood of NTM infection. Previous studies have demonstrated that NTM infection is associated with an increased risk of mortality and the development or progression of chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD) [10, 11]. Therefore, researching the risk factors for NTM infection is essential for improving clinical management, reducing adverse outcomes, and enhancing patient’s prognosis.

The risk factors for NTM infection are frequently inconsistent across various studies, likely owing to variations in populations and the geographical diversity. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed a cohort of LTRs at China-Japan Friendship Hospital (CJFH) to describe the clinical characteristics and potential risk factors for NTM infection.

Materials and methods

Study design

We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study of all patients who underwent LTx at CJFH between March 2018 and April 2024. The follow-up period concluded in March 1, 2026. Inclusion criteria included: (1) patients who underwent LTx during the study period; (2) age ≥ 18 years at the time of transplantation; (3) postoperative survival of at least 3 days; and (4) availability of complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria included: (1) NTM infection prior to LTx, (2) a single positive NTM sputum culture, or (3) failure to meet the inclusion criteria. This exclusion criterion was implemented to ensure the analysis focused solely on incident NTM infection post-transplantation, thereby minimizing potential confounding from previous infection or chronic colonization. If a patient underwent twice LTx during the study period, only the most recent transplantation was included. The NTM group was defined as patients with the first positive NTM culture detected in respiratory samples following LTx. To minimize the imbalance of variables between different groups, a 1:2 propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted. Patients were matched to controls based on transplant age (± 1 year) and transplant date (± 6 months). This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Beijing China-Japan Friendship Hospital (2025-KY-189). As this study was retrospective, informed consent from the participants was not required.

Definitions

NTM may cause both asymptomatic infection and symptomatic disease in humans. NTM infection was diagnosed by either a single positive culture from bronchoalveolar lavage (BALF), or by at least two positive sputum cultures obtained on different days. Patients with only a single positive sputum culture were excluded. In the study, all patient’s positive results were obtained from BALF culture. The infection site was confirmed to be the lung in all NTM-infected patients. Patients with NTM infection were further classified according to the ATS/IDSA 2007 guidelines into those with NTM pulmonary disease (NTM-PD), defined by the presence of respiratory symptoms together with radiographic abnormalities on chest computed tomography consistent with NTM infection, and those without NTM-PD, who were classified as having NTM colonization [12]. Colonization with specific pathogens might play a significant role in allograft rejection [13]. Therefore, both colonization and active infection warrant careful attention. In the study, NTM-PD and NTM colonization were combined into the NTM infection group for analysis. The latest chest computed tomography (CT) scans obtained after NTM detection were independently reviewed by two board-certified pulmonologists in a blinded fashion regarding disease classification. Radiological assessments were completed by consensus. The presence of nodules, cavitary lesions, and bronchiectasis was evaluated. The severity of bronchiectasis was quantified using a modified Reiff scoring system, with each lobe (with the lingula considered to be a separate lobe) scored from 0 to 3 (0: none, 1: cylindrical, 2: vein-like, 3: cystic) [14]. Based on radiological findings, the disease phenotype was classified into two categories: nodular/bronchiectatic type and fibro-cavitary type. The nodular/bronchiectatic type was diagnosed in patients presenting with pulmonary nodules and bronchiectasis, as well as those with pulmonary nodules in the absence of bronchiectasis. Fibro-cavitary disease was diagnosed in patients exhibiting pulmonary parenchymal opacities, and/or cavitary lesions, and/or extensive fibrotic lesions predominantly located in the upper lobes or apical segments of the lower lobes [15].

Airway complications following LTx include dehiscence, stenosis, malacia, and ischemia and necrosis [16]. Bronchoscopy was used to evaluate for the occurrence of airway complications. Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR) is defined by the presence of antibodies directed against donor human leukocyte antigens and characteristic lung histology [17].Cell-mediated rejection (CMR) was diagnosed based on histological evidence of perivascular and/or peribronchiolar lymphocytic infiltration, as identified via transbronchial lung biopsy [18]. CLAD is defined as a substantial and persistent decline of at least 20% in forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) relative to the baseline value. The baseline is established as the mean of the two best postoperative FEV1 measurements, taken more than three weeks apart [19]. Selected post-transplant comorbidities, including airway complications, AMR, and CLAD, were collected because of their potential relevance to subsequent NTM infection.

Data collection

For all eligible patients who underwent LTx, the data were manually extracted from the electronic medical records. The collected data encompassed demographic characteristics (sex, age at transplantation, and age at infection), body mass index (BMI), clinical symptoms, date and type of transplantation, underlying diseases, comorbidities, laboratory parameters, duration of intensive care unit (ICU) stay, immunosuppressive regimen, and post-LTx complications (airway complications, AMR, CMR, CLAD). We also recorded survival status and overall survival time. Laboratory parameters specifically included white blood cell (WBC), neutrophil (NEUT), and lymphocyte (LYMPH) counts; liver function tests (alanine aminotransferase [ALT], aspartate aminotransferase [AST]); renal function tests (urea, serum creatinine [Scr]); albumin (ALB); lymphocyte subsets; immunoglobulins; arterial pressure of oxygen/inspiratory fraction of oxygen ratio (PaO2/FiO2); and CMV serological status. Additionally, data on the timing of NTM infection and NTM species were collected. Baseline data were recorded at the time of lung transplantation. Laboratory parameters were collected at the time of NTM infection to assess the clinical status at diagnosis.

Statistical analysis

The chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were performed to compare differences between groups for categorical variables. For continuous variables, Student t test or Mann-Whitney U test was utilized to analyze differences between different groups, depending on data distribution. Continuous variables were summarized by mean ± SD, where data were normally distributed; otherwise, they were summarized by median and interquartile range (IQR). To identify risk factors for NTM infection, variables were first assessed using univariable Cox regression analyses. Variables with a p value < 0.05 in univariable COX regression analyses, together with clinically relevant covariates identified from prior literature, were included in the multivariable Cox regression analyses. Death could act as a competing risk event for NTM infection, potentially lowering its incidence. Therefore, we used cumulative incidence function (CIF) and competitive risk model to compare NTM infection rates between groups. Fine–Gray model reported subdistribution hazard ratios (sHR) with 95% confidence interval (CI). Statistical analysis and visualization were performed using SPSS (v.25.0), R software (v.4.4.1). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study design

A total of 546 patients who underwent LTx at the CJFH between March 2018 and April 2024 were enrolled in this study. A total of 49 patients were excluded from the study. The reasons for exclusion were as follows: missing clinical data (n = 16), age under 18 years (n = 3), pre-transplantation NTM infection (n = 4), mortality within 3 days post-transplantation (n = 25), and a single positive NTM sputum culture that did not meet the diagnostic criteria for NTM infection (n = 1).This resulted in a final cohort of 497 patients, including 23 in the NTM group and 474 in the control group. Baseline characteristics

NTM patients included 4 (17.4%) females, with an average age of 60.0 years (IQR 49.0–68.0). Control patients included 93 (19.6%) females, with an average age of 61.0 years (IQR 54.0–66.0). The NTM group included 12 patients with NTM-PD and 11 patients with NTM colonization. The NTM-PD group was significantly older than the NTM colonization group (64.0 vs. 52.0 years; p value = 0.042). While baseline characteristics, perioperative management, post-transplant complications, and NTM infection characteristics (including NTM species, time from infection to surgery, laboratory data, and treatment status) were comparable between the two groups, chest CT features differed significantly (Supplementary Table S1, Table 3). Specifically, the Modified Reiff score, which assesses the extent and radiographic severity of bronchiectasis, was higher in NTM-PD patients compared to those with NTM colonization (3.0 vs. 0.0; p value = 0.033). NTM-PD patients also had a greater number of affected lung lobes (1.0 vs. 0.0; p value = 0.033) and a higher frequency of the nodular/bronchiectatic pattern (66.7% vs. 9.1%; p value = 0.009).

Risk Factors for NTM Infection

To investigate risk factors for NTM infection after LTx, univariable and multivariable Cox regression were analyzed. Prior to the onset of NTM infection, five patients developed airway complications, four experienced AMR, and three developed CLAD. The result of univariable time-dependent Cox regression analysis revealed that AMR (hazard ratio [HR] 5.77; CI 1.45-23.0; p value = 0.013) and CLAD (HR 3.40; 95% CI 1.03–11.19; p value = 0.044) were independently associated with an increased hazard of NTM infection (Table 5, supplementary Table S2). After adjusting for covariates including age, sex, and BLT, multivariable time-dependent Cox regression analysis further showed that AMR (HR16.84; 95% CI 6.61–42.91; p value < 0.001) was independent predictors of NTM infection (Table 5).

Analysis of the competitive risk models

When death was considered as a competing event, analyses using cumulative incidence functions and competitive risk models indicated a significant association between the occurrence of AMR after LTx. LTRs with AMR had a substantially higher cumulative incidence of NTM infection compared to those without AMR (Fig. 2). After adjusting for sex and age at transplantation, AMR showed a statistically significant association with NTM infection in the Fine–Gray competing risk regression analysis (sHR 2.69; 95% CI 0.01-522.92; p value = 0.041) (Table 6).

Discussion

The present study retrospectively characterized the risk factors for the development of NTM infection in LTRs. A novel finding from our study is that LTRs who have previously experienced AMR may be at increased risk for NTM infection.

In this study, NTM‑PD and NTM colonization were combined into a single NTM infection group for analysis. This approach was taken because colonization with specific pathogens may play a significant role in allograft rejection, both through direct interaction with the host immune system and via the production of immunomodulatory metabolites [13]. For example, transplant recipients colonized with multidrug‑resistant organisms are known to face an increased risk of subsequent infection and higher all‑cause mortality [20, 21]. Therefore, both colonization and active infection warrant careful attention.

AMR is an antibody‑mediated form of rejection. Certain specific pathogens may contribute to an increased risk of AMR. Prior research indicates that Pseudomonas aeruginosa enhances acute intragraft lymphocytotoxicity, leading to AMR after LTx [22]. Our research has revealed that patients with a prior history of AMR are at an increased risk of NTM infection. Consistent with our findings, a case‑control study reported that LTRs and those with acute rejection were at a high risk of NTM infection [23].It has been observed in previous research that the lung tissue of AMR patients undergoes structural changes, such as endothelial swelling, endothelial vacuolization, and neutrophil margination [24, 25]. Disruption of lung structure creates a more favorable environment for NTM infection. Concurrently, patients with AMR require immunosuppressive treatment, such as high-dose corticosteroids, which significantly increases the risk of NTM infection [25].

According to previous studies, NTM infection is associated with an increased risk of CLAD and mortality [11]. Interestingly, the NTM group had a higher incidence of CLAD in this study. Univariate time-dependent Cox regression analysis identified CLAD as a potential predictor of NTM infection. However, after adjusting for covariates including age, sex, and BLT, CLAD was not independently associated with NTM infection. This may be due to the limited sample size of the present study, which resulted in insufficient statistical power in the multivariable model and may have masked the true effect of CLAD. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to further clarify the role of CLAD in NTM infection.

In the present study, LTRs in NTM group had a higher prevalence of underlying BOS, as well as comorbid chronic kidney disease and chronic rhinosinusitis. BOS impairs airway mucociliary clearance and creates a niche for NTM colonization [26]. Chronic kidney disease may lead to immune dysfunction that increases the risk of NTM infection [27]. According to an epidemiological report, 13/195 extrapulmonary infections arose from the paranasal sinuses [28]. Chronic rhinosinusitis may serve as a reservoir for NTM, facilitating pulmonary inoculation. These findings suggest that clinicians should maintain a low threshold for NTM screening in patients with these characteristics.

In this study, patients with NTM infection had a shorter postoperative ICU stay. This may be explained by the fact that most of these patients had a chronic disease course and were relatively stable before transplantation, thus requiring less prolonged ICU support after surgery. However, given the limited sample size of this study, these findings need to be validated in larger cohorts.

This study has several limitations. First, the low incidence of NTM infection resulted in a small number of enrolled cases. Following matching, the sample size was reduced, which might introduce bias. Consequently, further validation with a larger number of cases was required. Second, the NTM infection group lacked LTRs for underlying conditions such as bronchiectasis and cystic fibrosis, which were present in the control group. This potential selection bias may limit the generalizability of our findings to transplant populations with a higher prevalence of these specific diseases. Third, due to its retrospective, single-center nature, this study’s ability to establish causal inference for the observed associations is limited. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as exploratory, and further multicenter studies are required to validate these results.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest a possible association between AMR and NTM infection. Nevertheless, the limited sample size of this study precludes definitive conclusions, and larger, multicenter studies are warranted to further elucidate the role of pre-existing AMR in LTRs with NTM infection.
