Convergence Theory for Iterative LLM-Based Neural Architecture Search: A Parametric Cross-Entropy Framework with Closed-Form Proxy Reliability
Santosh Premi Adhikari, Radu Timofte, Dmitry Ignatov

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as generators in iterative neural architecture search (NAS), yet no formal convergence theory exists for this class of algorithms. We model iterative LLM-NAS as a parametric Cross-Entropy (CE) method over executable programs and prove six results: (1) iterative LLM fine-tuning on elite architectures is equivalent to the CE update restricted to the LLM parametric family; (2) expected architecture quality is monotonically non-decreasing across cycles; (3) elite-set probability converges to a fixed point at a geometric rate C t ≥ 1 − ( 1 − ρ 0 ) t C_{t}\geq 1-(1-\rho_{0})^{t} ; (4) delta-based generation achieves a strictly higher valid-generation rate than full-code generation under a first-order Markov token-error model, replacing the independent-token assumption of prior work; (5) the MinHash-Jaccard novelty filter prevents mode collapse; (6) proxy reliability admits the closed-form ρ S = 6 π ​ arcsin ⁡ ( ρ P ​ ( SNR ) / 2 ) \rho_{\mathrm{S}}=\tfrac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\bigl(\rho_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathrm{SNR})/2\bigr) , yielding the practical diagnostic σ arch 2 ≫ σ noise 2 \sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}\gg\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}} as a necessary condition for trustworthy proxy-based rankings. Testing against a 22-cycle, three-LLM, six-dataset experiment with 3,300 generated architectures: two predictions are consistent quantitatively (monotone quality trend, persistent novel admissions), two at direction-of-effect level (elite-concentration plateau converging to a LoRA ceiling; DeepSeek non-significance under low SNR), and two depart from calibrated values (delta-rate ratio 1.41 vs. predicted 2.23; Qwen Spearman 0.635 vs. predicted 0.10 due to ceiling truncation). Proxy-reliability validation confirms the predicted LLM ordering (Mistral, Qwen, DeepSeek) with a coherent ordinal pattern matching the SNR ranking, Mistral the sole result surviving Bonferroni correction ( p = 7.7 × 10 − 7 p{=}7.7\times 10^{-7} ), and explains the proxy-reliability ceiling effect previously reported empirically but left unexplained.

Introduction

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has evolved from early reinforcement-learning
controllers operating within predefined cell topologies
(
31
;
20
)
to
gradient-based differentiable search
(
15
)
and one-shot super-network
methods
(
19
;
2
;
7
)
. A recent paradigm shift
replaces hand-crafted search spaces entirely, using large language models as open-ended
generators of executable
torch.nn.Module
programs. This approach was pioneered
by
11
and
6
, who showed empirically that iteratively
fine-tuning an LLM on its own successful generations progressively improves both
generation reliability and architecture quality.
1
further
demonstrated that generating unified
diffs
(deltas) rather than complete
implementations reduces output length by 75–85% while improving the valid-generation
rate from 50.6% to 66–75%.

Despite these empirical successes, fundamental theoretical questions remain unanswered.

Does iterative LLM fine-tuning provably improve architecture quality? Under what
conditions does the process converge? Why does delta generation achieve higher valid
generation rates? What role does novelty filtering play in preventing degenerate
solutions?
We address all four questions by establishing a formal mathematical
framework. Our main contribution is to show that iterative LLM-NAS is a parametric
instance of the Cross-Entropy (CE) method
(
22
)
—a well-studied
optimisation algorithm with known convergence properties—and to derive consequences
specific to the LLM-NAS setting.

Section
3

Section
4
states and proves the five main convergence theorems.
Section
5
develops proxy-reliability theory.
Section
6
presents an illustrative case study.
Section
7
discusses limitations and implications.

Related Work

Neural Architecture Search.

NAS methods span three broad families.
Reinforcement-learning
methods

(
31
;
20
;
26
)
use a controller to propose architectures
evaluated on a proxy task, but are computationally prohibitive.
One-shot

methods
(
19
;
2
;
7
;
29
)
train a
weight-sharing super-network and inherit weights for sub-architectures.

Differentiable
methods such as DARTS
(
15
)
relax the
discrete search space to a continuous one and apply gradient descent. All three operate
within predefined cell or operation search spaces; LLM-based NAS is distinguished by
its use of open-ended program synthesis.

LLMs as Optimisers.

30
showed that LLMs can serve as optimisers by iteratively refining
candidate solutions expressed in natural language.
16

introduced Self-Refine, a feedback-driven iterative prompting strategy.
21
used LLMs inside an evolutionary loop to discover novel
mathematical functions. The present work provides the first
convergence
guarantees
for such iterative LLM-based search procedures.

Cross-Entropy Method and Estimation of Distribution Algorithms.

The CE method was introduced by
22
for rare-event simulation and
extended to combinatorial optimisation by
4
. Its application to
reinforcement learning was demonstrated by
25
. Estimation of Distribution
Algorithms (EDAs)
(
14
;
18
;
17
)
share the same
update structure: fit a parametric distribution to elite samples and resample.
Convergence of EDAs to global optima is established under conditions including
finite search spaces and sufficient model expressiveness
(
23
)
.
Our work establishes an analogous convergence result for the LLM parametric family,
subject to explicit assumptions that we discuss in detail.

LoRA and Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning.

8
introduced LoRA, which restricts fine-tuning updates to a low-rank
subspace of the weight matrices. While LoRA dramatically reduces computational cost,
it introduces a model-expressiveness constraint that is central to our analysis. We
explicitly characterise the projection error introduced by this restriction and treat it
as an open problem for future work, rather than absorbing it silently into the theory.

3
Mathematical Framework

3.1
Architecture Space and Quality Function

Definition 1

(Architecture Space)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
denote the set of all syntactically valid, executable Python programs
that define a
torch.nn.Module
subclass. For delta generation with baseline

b\in\mathcal{A}
, define
\mathcal{A}_{\delta}(b)\subseteq\mathcal{A}
as the set
of all architectures obtainable by applying a valid unified diff to
b
.

Definition 2

(Quality Function)
.

A quality function
q:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
maps each architecture to its
first-epoch validation accuracy on a fixed dataset. The elite set at
threshold
\tau\in(0,1)
is

\mathcal{E}_{\tau}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}:q(a)\geq\tau\}
.
The choice of
1
epoch as the proxy length follows the convention of

11
;
1
; varying this parameter would rescale
both
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}
and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}}
in
Section
5
, leaving the
\mathrm{SNR}
-driven predictions of
Theorem
8
qualitatively unchanged but changing absolute
correlation magnitudes. Section
5.1
reports the
actual SNR values that result from this convention.

3.2
The Iterative LLM-NAS Process

Let
\Theta
be the parameter space of a pre-trained LLM with LoRA
adaptation
(
8
)
, and let
p_{\theta}:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
denote the
generation distribution induced by parameters
\theta
. The parametric family is

\mathcal{F}=\{p_{\theta}:\theta\in\Theta\}
.

Definition 3

(Iterative LLM-NAS)
.

Given initial parameter
\theta_{0}
and static corpus
\mathcal{L}
(the LEMUR
dataset
(
5
;
27
)
of
\sim
626 hand-curated, peer-reviewed

torch.nn.Module
architectures across image-classification, segmentation
and regression tasks; in the experiments of
1
this
corpus is restricted to its image-classification subset which serves as the
warm-start prior), the process proceeds for cycles
t=0,1,\ldots,T
:

1.

Generate:
Sample
N
candidates
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}\sim p_{\theta_{t}}
.

2.

Evaluate:
Compute
q(a_{i})
for each
i
.

3.

Filter:
Form
\mathcal{A}^{+}_{t}=\{a_{i}:q(a_{i})\geq\tau\}\cap\mathrm{Novel}_{t}
, where
\mathrm{Novel}_{t}
denotes architectures passing the
MinHash–Jaccard novelty filter against the current corpus.

4.

Update corpus:

S_{t}=S_{t-1}\cup\mathcal{A}^{+}_{t}
.

5.

Fine-tune:

\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}-\sum_{a\in S_{t}\cup\mathcal{L}}\log p_{\theta}(a)
.

3.3
CE Method Background

The CE method for combinatorial optimisation
(
4
)
maintains a
distribution
p_{t}
over solutions and iterates:

p_{t+1}=\arg\min_{p\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\hat{p}^{\tau\text{-elite}}_{t}\,\|\,p\right),

where
\mathrm{KL}(\cdot\,\|\,\cdot)
denotes the Kullback–Leibler
divergence
(
13
)
and
\hat{p}^{\tau\text{-elite}}_{t}
is the
empirical distribution of the top-
\tau
solutions sampled from
p_{t}
. When

\mathcal{F}
is the set of all distributions, this reduces to
maximum-likelihood estimation on the elite set.

4
Main Theoretical Results

4.1
Equivalence to the Parametric CE Method

Theorem 1

(CE Equivalence)
.

The fine-tuning step of Definition
3
is equivalent to the parametric CE
update restricted to
\mathcal{F}
:

\theta_{t+1}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\hat{p}_{S_{t}}\,\|\,p_{\theta}\right),

where
\hat{p}_{S_{t}}
is the uniform empirical distribution over
S_{t}\cup\mathcal{L}
.

Proof sketch.

The cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|S_{t}\cup\mathcal{L}|}\sum_{a}\log p_{\theta}(a)
differs from
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}_{S_{t}}\|p_{\theta})
only by the constant
H(\hat{p}_{S_{t}})
; minimising either over
\Theta
yields the same optimum. Full proof in Appendix
A
.
∎

4.2
Monotone Improvement of Expected Quality

We state two explicit assumptions that govern the two regimes of the proof.

Assumption 1

(Regularity)
.

(a) Bounded quality:
q:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
.
(b) Positive initial mass:
\mathbb{P}_{a\sim p_{\theta_{0}}}(a\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau})=\rho_{0}>0
.
(c) Consistent estimation: as
N\to\infty
,
\hat{p}_{S_{t}}\to p^{\mathrm{elite}}_{\theta_{t}}

in distribution, where
p^{\mathrm{elite}}_{\theta_{t}}(a)\propto p_{\theta_{t}}(a)\cdot\mathbf{1}[a\in\mathcal{E}_{\tau}]
.
(d) Parametric expressiveness:
\mathcal{F}
contains a distribution that
assigns strictly positive probability to every element of the (finite)
elite set
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
. Part (d) is required only for
Theorem
3
Part 3 and Corollary
4
; it is generically
violated for LoRA-adapted LLMs and we discuss its consequences in
Remark
1
.

Assumption 2

(Quality-Monotone Projection)
.

Let
\mu_{p}=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim p}[q(a)]
denote the mean quality under distribution
p
.
The CE projection
\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}:p\mapsto\arg\min_{p^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{KL}(p\|p^{\prime})
is
quality-monotone
: for any
p
with
\mu_{p}\geq\mu_{p_{\theta_{t}}}
,
we have
\mu_{\Pi_{\mathcal{F}}(p)}\geq\mu_{p_{\theta_{t}}}
.

Theorem 2

(Monotone Quality Improvement)
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the mean quality

Q_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{a\sim p_{\theta_{t}}}[q(a)]
satisfies
Q_{t+1}\geq Q_{t}-\varepsilon_{t}
,
where
\varepsilon_{t}=O(N^{-1/2})
is the finite-sample estimation error that vanishes
as
N\to\infty
.

Proof sketch.

Let
p^{*}_{\theta_{t+1}}
be the ideal CE update using the infinite-sample elite distribution. By concentration inequalities,
Q_{t+1}\geq\mu_{p^{*}_{\theta_{t+1}}}-\varepsilon_{t}
with
\varepsilon_{t}=O(N^{-1/2})
. When
Q_{t}\leq\tau
, the elite distribution has mean quality
\geq\tau\geq Q_{t}
; when
Q_{t}>\tau
, the elite distribution stochastically dominates
p_{\theta_{t}}
. In both cases, Assumption
2
gives
\mu_{p^{*}_{\theta_{t+1}}}\geq Q_{t}
. Full proof in Appendix
A
.
∎

4.3
Convergence of the Generation Distribution

Definition 4

(Elite Concentration)
.

The elite concentration at cycle
t
is

C_{t}=\mathbb{P}_{a\sim p_{\theta_{t}}}(q(a)\geq\tau)
.

Theorem 3

(Convergence to Elite Distribution)
.

Under Assumption
1
:

1.

\{C_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is non-decreasing:
C_{t+1}\geq C_{t}-\varepsilon_{t}
.

2.

C_{t}\to C^{*}
as
t\to\infty
, where
C^{*}
is a fixed point of the CE
operator on
\mathcal{F}
.

3.

If
|\mathcal{E}_{\tau}|<\infty
and
\mathcal{F}
contains a distribution
assigning positive probability to every element of
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}

(Assumption
1
(d)), then
C^{*}=1
.

Proof sketch.

Part 1 applies Theorem
2
to
\tilde{q}(a)=\mathbf{1}[q(a)\geq\tau]
. Part 2 follows from the Monotone Convergence Theorem (
\{C_{t}\}
bounded, non-decreasing). Part 3 uses a contradiction: if
C^{*}<1
, a further CE step toward the uniform distribution on
\mathcal{E}_{\tau}
would increase
C
, violating the fixed-point property. Full proof in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 1

(LoRA expressiveness ceiling)
.

Part 3 requires Assumption
1
(d), which is generically violated for LoRA-adapted LLMs. The realistic prediction is Part 2: convergence to some
C^{*}\leq 1
. The observed 73–76% plateau is consistent with a LoRA-expressiveness ceiling but is not a quantitative prediction of the theory (see Appendix
B
).

Corollary 4

(Convergence Rate, Unrestricted Family)
.

Under Assumption
1
(d):

C_{t}\geq 1-(1-\rho_{0})^{t}
, requiring at most

t^{*}=\lceil\log(\delta)/\log(1-\rho_{0})\rceil
cycles for
C_{t}\geq 1-\delta
.
For LoRA-adapted LLMs,
C_{t}\to C^{*}\leq 1
by Theorem
3
Part 2 but neither
C^{*}
nor the rate is characterised. See Appendix
B
for discussion.

4.4
Valid-Rate Advantage of Delta Generation

We replace the original independent token-error model (which is inconsistent with LLM
autoregressive generation) with a first-order Markov chain model that captures
error correlation.

Definition 5

(Markov Token-Error Model)
.

The LLM generates tokens via a two-state Markov chain over

\{\mathrm{correct},\mathrm{error}\}
with transition probabilities:

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{error}\mid\mathrm{correct})=\varepsilon_{t}\in(0,1)
(base error
rate) and
\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{error}\mid\mathrm{error})=\gamma_{t}\in[\varepsilon_{t},1)

(error persistence, capturing autocorrelation). Setting
\gamma_{t}=\varepsilon_{t}

recovers the independent model. A generated sequence is
valid
if it contains no
error tokens
and
satisfies format-level structural constraints. Let
\pi_{\mathrm{full}}

and
\pi_{\delta}
denote the format-validity probabilities for full programs and diffs,
respectively, conditional on token-level correctness.

Under Definition
5
, the stationary probability of the correct state is
\pi_{c}=(1-\gamma_{t})/(1-\gamma_{t}+\varepsilon_{t})
. Starting from stationarity, a length-
L
error-free sequence requires
L-1
correct
\to
correct transitions:

P(\text{no error}\mid L,\varepsilon_{t})=\pi_{c}\cdot(1-\varepsilon_{t})^{L-1}=C(\gamma_{t},\varepsilon_{t})\,(1-\varepsilon_{t})^{L},

(1)

where
C(\gamma_{t},\varepsilon_{t})=\pi_{c}/(1-\varepsilon_{t})
is independent of
L
and
\lambda_{t}=1-\varepsilon_{t}
is the per-token validity probability. The
\gamma_{t}
-dependence enters only through
C
, not the decay rate (see Appendix
A
for the absorbing-error analysis).

Theorem 5

(Valid Rate Advantage of Delta Generation)
.

Let
r^{\mathrm{full}}_{t}
and
r^{\delta}_{t}
denote the valid generation rates under
full-code and delta generation, with mean output lengths
L_{\mathrm{full}}
and

L_{\delta}=\alpha L_{\mathrm{full}}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Under
Definition
5
, with per-token validity probability

\lambda_{t}=1-\varepsilon_{t}\in(0,1)
,

\frac{r^{\delta}_{t}}{r^{\mathrm{full}}_{t}}\;=\;\lambda_{t}^{(\alpha-1)L_{\mathrm{full}}}\cdot\frac{\pi_{\delta}}{\pi_{\mathrm{full}}}.

Since
\alpha<1
, the exponent
(\alpha-1)L_{\mathrm{full}}<0
, so

\lambda_{t}^{(\alpha-1)L_{\mathrm{full}}}>1
for all
\varepsilon_{t}>0
.
If additionally
\pi_{\delta}\geq\pi_{\mathrm{full}}
, then

r^{\delta}_{t}>r^{\mathrm{full}}_{t}
.

Proof sketch.

From (
1
), the constants
C(\gamma_{t},\varepsilon_{t})
cancel in the ratio, yielding the stated expression. Since
\alpha<1
and
\lambda_{t}\in(0,1)
, the exponential term exceeds 1. Full derivation in Appendix
A
.
∎

Corollary 6

(Direction-of-effect ratio prediction)
.

For
\lambda_{t}\geq\underline{\lambda}
and
\pi_{\delta}/\pi_{\mathrm{full}}\geq 1
:

r^{\delta}_{t}/r^{\mathrm{full}}_{t}\geq\exp[(1-\alpha)L_{\mathrm{full}}\log(1/\underline{\lambda})]>1.

With
\alpha\approx 0.20
,
L_{\mathrm{full}}\approx 200
,
\underline{\lambda}\approx 0.995
(post-hoc calibrated, not first-principle), this gives
\approx 2.23
. The empirical ratio is
1.41
, confirming the direction (
r^{\delta}>r^{\mathrm{full}}
) but undershooting the calibrated value due to format-invalid diffs and longer-range token correlations not captured by the first-order model (see Appendix
B
).

4.5
Novelty Filter Prevents Mode Collapse

Definition 6

(Mode Collapse)
.

The process mode-collapses at cycle
t
if
p_{\theta_{t}}(a^{*})\to 1
for some single
architecture
a^{*}
.

Definition 7

(Novelty-Separated Corpus)
.

A corpus
S
is
(1-\tau_{\mathrm{nov}})
-separated if for all
a\neq a^{\prime}\in S
:

d_{J}(a,a^{\prime})\geq 1-\tau_{\mathrm{nov}}
, where
d_{J}
is the Jaccard distance.

Theorem 7

(Novelty Filter Prevents Mode Collapse)
.

Suppose the MinHash–Jaccard novelty filter at threshold
\tau_{\mathrm{nov}}\in(0,1)

is applied at each cycle. Then:

1.

S_{t}
remains
(1-\tau_{\mathrm{nov}})
-separated for all
t
.

2.

If
p_{\theta_{t+1}}
minimises
\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}_{S_{t}}\|p_{\theta})
and

|S_{t}|\geq 2
, then
p_{\theta_{t+1}}(a)\leq 1-\delta
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
,
where
\delta>0
depends on
\tau_{\mathrm{nov}}
and
|S_{t}|
.

3.

H(p_{\theta_{t+1}})\geq H_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta)>0
, where

H_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta)=-\delta\log\delta-(1-\delta)\log(1-\delta)

is the binary entropy function.

Proof sketch.

Part 1 is by induction on the admission criterion. For Part 2, writing
K^{*}=\mathrm{KL}(\hat{p}_{S_{t}}\|p_{\theta^{*}})<\infty
, each corpus element satisfies
p_{\theta^{*}}(a)\geq\exp(-K^{*}|S_{t}|)=:\delta_{t}>0
, so
\max_{a}p_{\theta^{*}}(a)\leq 1-\delta_{t}
. Part 3 follows by the data-processing inequality:
H(p_{\theta^{*}})\geq H_{\mathrm{bin}}(\delta_{t})>0
. Note that
\delta_{t}
decays with
|S_{t}|
, so the bound prevents degenerate mode collapse at any finite cycle but is not uniform across cycles. Full proof and discussion of the
\delta_{t}
-dependence in Appendix
A
.
∎

5
Proxy Reliability Theory

Definition 8

(Signal-to-Noise Ratio of Proxy)
.

Given architectures
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
with first-epoch accuracies

\hat{q}_{1},\ldots,\hat{q}_{k}
and fully-trained accuracies
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}

\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}}{\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}}},

where
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}=\mathrm{Var}_{i}[q_{i}]
is the cross-architecture
variance and
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}}=\mathrm{Var}[q_{i}-\hat{q}_{i}]
.

Theorem 8

(Proxy Reliability)
.

Suppose
(q_{i},\hat{q}_{i})
are i.i.d. samples from a
bivariate Normal

distribution

1

1

\hat{q}_{i}=q_{i}+\xi_{i}
,
\xi_{i}
independent of
q_{i}
and
of variance
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}}
, and
q_{i}
of variance

\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}
. Let

\rho_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathrm{SNR})=(1+\mathrm{SNR}^{-1})^{-1/2}
denote the population Pearson

satisfies the closed form

\rho_{\mathrm{S}}=\tfrac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\!\bigl(\rho_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathrm{SNR})/2\bigr),

\mathrm{SNR}
, satisfies

\rho_{\mathrm{S}}\to 0
as
\mathrm{SNR}\to 0
and
\rho_{\mathrm{S}}\to 1

\mathrm{SNR}\to\infty

Apply the Kruskal–Kendall identity

7
Discussion and Limitations

Relation to classical CE theory.

Theorem
1
is a domain-specific instantiation of a standard algebraic identity; the novelty lies in identifying LLM fine-tuning with the parametric CE update. Theorems
5
,
7
, and
8
have no direct precedent we are aware of.

Limitations.

(i) The first-order Markov model (Theorem
5
) cannot capture attention-induced long-range token correlations; its calibrated
2.23
overshoots the observed
1.41
. (ii) LoRA restricts
\Theta
to a low-rank subspace, making Assumption
2
currently unverifiable; Theorem
2
should therefore be read as conditional. (iii) All empirical validation is drawn from a single existing LLM-NAS experiment
(
1
)
with small per-LLM samples (
N\in\{12,15,16\}
); replication on third-party LLMs is necessary. (iv) Theorem
8
assumes bivariate Normal
(q,\hat{q})
; accuracy-ceiling truncation explains the Qwen discrepancy. Extended discussion in Appendix
C
.

Broader impact.

This is a theoretical paper introducing no new datasets or models. The novelty filter (Theorem
7
) does not protect against convergence to a narrow
class
of architectures; practitioners should monitor the dataset distribution of admitted architectures.

8
Conclusion

We have developed a formal framework identifying iterative LLM-based NAS as a parametric Cross-Entropy method, establishing six main results: CE equivalence (Theorem
1
), monotone quality improvement (Theorem
2
), convergence to a fixed point at geometric rate
C_{t}\geq 1-(1-\rho_{0})^{t}
(Theorem
3
), delta valid-rate advantage under a first-order Markov token-error model that replaces the independent-token assumption of prior work (Theorem
5
), mode-collapse prevention (Theorem
7
), and proxy-reliability theory yielding the closed-form
\rho_{\mathrm{S}}=\tfrac{6}{\pi}\arcsin\bigl(\rho_{\mathrm{P}}(\mathrm{SNR})/2\bigr)
with practical diagnostic
\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{arch}}\gg\sigma^{2}_{\mathrm{noise}}
(Theorem
8
). After
C_{t}
plateaus, the framework characterises continued cycles as a fixed-point maintenance phase, explaining the empirically observed post-concentration regime.

Our case study against
1
produces a mixed picture (Table
2
): two predictions consistent quantitatively, two at direction-of-effect level, and two departing from calibrated values; proxy-reliability predictions are further supported by a coherent three-way ordinal pattern (Mistral
\hat{\rho}=0.926
, Qwen
\hat{\rho}=0.635
, DeepSeek
\hat{\rho}=0.495
) consistent with the SNR ranking, with Mistral the sole result surviving Bonferroni correction (
p{=}7.7\times 10^{-7}
). External validation on third-party LLMs is a necessary next step. Future work includes convergence rates under the LoRA low-rank constraint, rank-correlation theory under truncated accuracy supports, and extension of the SNR diagnostic to adaptive proxy-length schedules.

Appendix B
Extended Remarks

Static corpus as regulariser.

The static corpus
\mathcal{L}
acts as a regulariser analogous to a prior in Bayesian CE updates
[
4
]
, preventing the generation distribution from drifting arbitrarily far from the pre-trained LLM’s capabilities.

Quality-monotone projection.

Assumption
2
holds exactly when
\mathcal{F}
is the set of all distributions. For LoRA, it requires that the low-rank projection does not decrease mean quality, which holds when the LoRA rank is sufficient to represent the quality-increasing component. Characterising the minimum rank required is an open problem.

LoRA expressiveness ceiling (extended).

The empirically observed 73–76% plateau across DeepSeek, Qwen and Mistral is consistent with a LoRA-expressiveness ceiling. Our framework predicts the existence and qualitative shape of such a plateau, and that its level depends on the LoRA rank and the geometry of the elite set, but it does not predict the specific numerical value
C^{*}\approx 0.74
from first principles.

Post-concentration regime.

For
\rho_{0}=0.50
, Corollary
4
predicts
C_{t}\geq 0.95
within
t^{*}\leq 5
cycles. Even after
C_{t}
plateaus, continued cycles contribute diverse novel architectures (enforced by the novelty filter). The 22-cycle experiment explores this post-concentration regime, which the theory characterises as a fixed-point maintenance phase.

Direction-of-effect ratio (extended).

Corollary
6
was reformulated from an absolute gap bound to a ratio bound, correcting the eigenvalue from
(1-\varepsilon)(1-\gamma)/(2-\varepsilon-\gamma)
to
1-\varepsilon
. Under the corrected model,
\gamma_{t}
-dependence enters only through
C(\gamma_{t},\varepsilon_{t})
which cancels.

Novelty bound
\delta_{t}
dependence.

The constant
\delta_{t}=\exp(-K^{*}|S_{t}|)
is strictly positive for every finite cycle but decays as
|S_{t}|
grows. The novelty filter prevents degenerate mode collapse at any finite cycle but does not guarantee a uniform entropy bound across cycles; the empirical persistence of admissions across all 22 cycles is consistent with this weaker guarantee.

MinHash approximation.

In practice,
d_{J}
is estimated via MinHash signatures of length
k=128

[
3
]
, yielding an unbiased estimator with standard deviation
O(1/\sqrt{k})
, making the slack negligible at
\tau_{\mathrm{nov}}=0.90
.

Proxy scope and falsifiable predictions.

Theorem
8
delivers two falsifiable predictions: (i) the LLM with higher SNR should have higher
\rho_{\mathrm{S}}
; (ii) at very small SNR,
\rho_{\mathrm{S}}
should be non-significant. The auxiliary inequality (
3
) is non-trivial only when
\mathrm{SNR}>0.55
, which holds for none of our LLMs. Under accuracy-ceiling truncation, Pearson and Spearman can decouple; the Qwen row (
\mathrm{SNR}=0.011
,
\hat{\rho}=0.635
) is such a case.

DeepSeek ceiling (extended).

The observed
\hat{\rho}_{\mathrm{S}}=0.495
exceeds the population prediction of
\sim 0.10
, consistent with finite-sample fluctuation at
N=12
and mild deviation from bivariate-Normal assumptions.
