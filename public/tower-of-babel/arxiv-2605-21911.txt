Noise Schedule Design for Diffusion Models: An Optimal Control Perspective
Seo Taek Kong, Weina Wang, R. Srikant

Abstract
We develop a principled framework for analyzing and designing noise schedules in diffusion models. We show that one can recast this design problem as an optimal control problem, whose state is the Fisher information of the diffusion process which evolves according to an ODE and the control input is the noise schedule. The objective of the optimal control problem is a functional involving the Fisher information, which is shown to be an upper bound on the Kullback-Leibler sampling error. By solving this optimal control problem, we obtain sufficient conditions on noise schedules under which state-of-the-art 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d / n ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n) sampling error is achievable, where d d is the data dimension and n n is the number of discretization steps. While existing theoretical work also prove that 𝒪 ~ ​ ( d / n ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n) sampling error bounds are achievable, these results hold for specific noise schedules, which do not include the schedules used in practice. Under a further parametric assumption on the data distribution, we show that one can obtain closed-form expressions for the noise schedules. These noise schedules generalize standard empirical schedules such as exponential and sigmoid schedules by allowing additional parameters that can be tuned. Systematically tuning the parameters of these schedules yields new schedules that achieve superior FID scores on image generation benchmarks.

Introduction

Score-based generative models

,

,

,

have emerged as a leading framework for approximate sampling.
These methods formulate the generative process as the simulation of a time-reversed stochastic differential equation (SDE) driven by a learned score function.
While the training objective is well-understood

,
11
,
10
,
41
]
, the design of noise schedules governing the forward and reverse processes remains largely heuristic.
Consequently, standard noise schedules
[

,
30
,
25
,
40
,

]
are often selected through empirical sweeps rather than theoretical guidance.

Noise schedule design plays a critical role in generating high-quality data using diffusion models
[

,
30
,
13
]
.
This importance is partially reflected in existing theory.
When utilizing constant noise schedules, the best-known bounds on the sampling error measured in the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence remain sub-optimal in the problem constants
[
6
,
11
,
9
,
15
]
.
Specifically, these upper bounds typically scale as
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{2}/n)
or
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\max\{\mathcal{J}_{\star},d\}/n)
, where
\mathcal{J}_{\star}
is the Fisher information of the data distribution,
d
is the data dimension, and
n
is the number of sampling steps.
Because
\mathcal{J}_{\star}
can be much larger than
d
, these guarantees suffer from severe problem-dependent penalties.

The above upper bounds can be improved significantly using time-varying noise schedules.
State-of-the-art upper bounds
[
4
,
15
]
on the KL divergence sampling error scale as
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
, yielding a linear dependence on the data dimension
d
.
While a matching lower bound is not known, the linear scaling in
d
is optimal
[
4
]
.
Currently, achieving this rate requires utilizing specific noise schedules that are rarely deployed in practice
[
39
]
.
Conversely, the noise schedules favored by practitioners lack comparable theoretical guarantees, leaving a gap between diffusion model theory and practice.
We refer the reader to
Appendix

A
for a more detailed discussion of related work.

In this work, we establish a new theoretical framework for designing noise schedules, which enlarges the class of schedules that are known to achieve
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
KL error bounds.
Directly minimizing the KL divergence is analytically intractable because the densities are governed by the Fokker-Planck partial differential equation (PDE).
We circumvent this by using Bochner’s formula to reduce the PDE constraint into a tractable, one-dimensional ODE governing the trajectory of the Fisher information.

By analyzing the optimized Fisher information trajectory under a special class of data distributions (strongly log-concave and Gaussian mixture models), we extract a closed-form expression for a family of noise schedules which we term Affine-Coupled Schedules (ACS).
This analysis shows that standard empirical heuristics, such as VE-Exponential
[
36
]
and VP-Sigmoid
[
25
,
40
]
, are special cases, where VE refers to variance exploding and VP refers to variance preserving models.
More importantly, this framework provides a principled method to introduce new degrees of freedom into schedule design, enabling a systematic optimization of schedules across different computational budgets.

Our contributions are summarized below:

1.

Optimal Control:

We formulate noise schedule design as an optimal control problem.
The cost functional involves a variational bound on the discretization error with a non-trivial Lagrangian that admits path-dependent variations, and it replaces the intractable PDE representation of probability density evolution with a one-dimensional Fisher information ODE. In addition to Girsanov’s theorem which is now widely used to theoretically study diffusion-based generative AI models
[
35
,
11
,
28
]
, we also use a number of other tools from probability and information theory such as Bochner’s formula, concavity of entropy power, and Blachman-Stam inquality to derive the optimal control formulation.

2.

Error Bounds for a Class of Noise Schedules:

We show that the solution to the optimal control problem achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
error bounds.
This result extends state-of-the-art sampling error guarantees to a large class of noise schedules.
For special classes of data distributions, we show that a parameterized class of noise schedules achieves the error bounds, and further the parameteric class includes certain widely-used practical noise schedules.

3.

Principled Degrees of Freedom:

We translate our schedules into a practical algorithm by treating the introduced parameters as hyperparameters, which can be optimally configured for any given Neural Function Evaluation (NFE) budget.
We empirically demonstrate that such a systematic tuning of these hyperparameters yields schedules that achieve superior FID scores on image datasets compared to standard heuristic baselines.

2
Preliminaries and Problem Setup

Diffusion models are based on two processes: the
forward process
, which transforms data into noise, and its time reversal, often referred to as the
reverse process
, which transforms noise back into data.
The
noise schedule
controls the rates at which these transformations occur.

Forward process.

Let
p_{\star}
denote the density function of the target data distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The forward process is a stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the following form:

dX_{t}=-f(t)X_{t}dt+\sqrt{g(t)}dB_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq T,\quad X_{0}\sim p_{\star},

(1)

where
(B_{t}\colon t\in[0,T])
is a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
independent of
X_{0}
, and
f(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are real-valued functions of time.
Let
p_{t}
denote the density function of
X_{t}
.
We call the function pair
(f,g)
the
noise schedule
.
Many theoretical studies of diffusion models focus on the simplified setting
f(t)=1
and
g(t)=2
for all
t
, in which case the forward process is the standard Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process.

To see how the noise schedule controls the distribution of the forward process, note that the marginal distribution of
X_{t}
admits the representation

X_{t}\overset{d}{=}\alpha_{t}X_{0}+\sigma_{t}Z,\quad Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(2)

where

\alpha_{t}=\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}f(\tilde{t})d\tilde{t}\right),\quad\sigma_{t}^{2}=\alpha_{t}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{g(\tilde{t})}{\alpha_{\tilde{t}}^{2}}d\tilde{t}.

In the OU setting where
f(t)=1,g(t)=2
, these quantities reduce to
\alpha_{t}=e^{-t},\sigma_{t}^{2}=1-e^{-2t}
.
More generally, a larger value of
f(t)
leads to a faster decay of the contribution of
X_{0}
in the distribution of
X_{t}
, while a larger value of
g(t)
corresponds to a faster injection of Gaussian noise.
In this paper, we consider the class of noise schedules
\Theta=\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{G}
defined as

\mathcal{F}=\left\{f:f(t)\geq 0\text{ for all }t\in[0,T]\right\}\;\text{and}\;\mathcal{G}=\left\{g:g(t)>0,\lvert g^{\prime}(t)\rvert<\infty\text{ for all }t\in[0,T]\right\}.

(3)

The noise schedules used in most diffusion model implementations
[
39
]
fall under the class
\Theta
.

Reverse process.

The reverse process is the time reversal of the forward process
(X_{t}\colon t\in[0,T])
in (
1
).
That is, the reverse process
(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}\colon\tau\in[0,T])
is defined by
X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}\overset{d}{=}X_{T-\tau},\tau\in[0,T]
.
Then the reverse process admits the following SDE representation
[
2
,
21
]
:

\begin{gathered}dX_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\left[f(T-\tau)X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+g(T-\tau)s_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\right]d\tau+\sqrt{g(T-\tau)}dB_{\tau},\\
0\leq\tau\leq T,\quad X_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T},\end{gathered}

(4)

where
(B_{\tau}\colon 0\leq\tau\leq T)
is a standard Brownian motion on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
independent of
X_{0}^{\leftarrow}
, and the function
s_{t}(\cdot)
defined as
s_{t}(x)\triangleq\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
is called the score function.

2.1
Noise Schedule Optimization

To turn the reverse process (
4
) into an implementable algorithm for generating data, several approximations need to be made:
(1) The score function
s_{t}
is unknown since it depends on the unknown data distribution, and is therefore replaced by an approximation
\hat{s}_{t}
, typically learned through a training process called score matching;
(2) The reverse SDE cannot be solved exactly, but rather must be solved numerically with time discretization;
(3) The reverse SDE cannot be initialized exactly from
p_{T}
, and is instead initialized from a Gaussian distribution so the initial state can be easily sampled.

A central question in the analysis of diffusion models is how these approximations affect the error between the distribution of the generated samples and the target distribution
[
6
,
11
,
4
,
15
]
.
Existing analysis shows that the overall error can be separated into three additive terms: (1) score-matching error, (2) discretization error, and (3) initialization error, corresponding to the three approximations above.
The score-matching error arises from the training stage, while the discretization error and the initialization error arise from the sampling stage when one solves the reverse SDE to generate data samples.

In our theoretical analysis, we consider the setting where the score functions are perfectly learned, and focus on the error arising from sampling, i.e., the sum of the discretization error and initialization error.
Our goal is to understand how this error depends on the noise schedule
(f,g)
, and how it can be minimized through noise schedule design.
The setting where the score matching is non-zero can be addressed in one of two possible ways: (i) if a pre-trained score function with appropriate SNR coverage is available, then we can reuse the score function for the noise schedules that result from our analysis or (ii) learn the score function for our noise schedules. In both cases, one can incorporate the score matching error into our analysis; see
Section

B.2
.

Specifically, we consider the following solving method for the reverse SDE.
We select discretization time points
0=\tau_{0}<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\dots<\tau_{n}=T
, where
\tau_{k}=kh,k=0,1,2,\dots,n
with a step size
h>0
, and solve the following SDE:

\begin{gathered}d\hat{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}=\left[f(T-\tau)\hat{X}_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+g(T-\tau)s_{T-\tau_{k}}(\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}}^{\leftarrow})\right]d\tau+\sqrt{g(T-\tau)}dB_{\tau},\\
\tau\in[\tau_{k},\tau_{k+1}),\quad k=0,1,2,\dots,n-1,\quad\hat{X}_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{T}^{2}I).\end{gathered}

(5)

Note that here we take the approach where we use a constant step
h
and let the noise schedule
(f,g)
vary over time.
This is without loss of generality, as discussed in
Section

B.3
.

Let
\hat{p}_{\star}
denote the density of
\hat{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}
, i.e., the output of the approximated reverse SDE in (
5
).
Recall that
p_{\star}
is the density of the data distribution.
Then our goal is to minimize the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence between
p_{\star}
and
\hat{p}_{\star}
, i.e., to solve the following problem

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle(f, g) \in\Theta}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle(f,g)\in\Theta}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(6)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{(f, g) \in\Theta}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle p_{\star}

\displaystyle\text{ is the density of }X_{T}^{\leftarrow}\text{ in \eqref{eq:reverse_sde}},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\hat{p}_{\star}

\displaystyle\text{ is the density of }\hat{X}_{T}^{\leftarrow}\text{ in \eqref{eq:exponential_integrator},}

where the constraints are specified by partial differential equations (PDEs) for the density functions of
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
and
\hat{X}_{t}^{\leftarrow}
given by the Fokker–Planck equations.
Solving optimization problems subject to PDE constraints is notoriously difficult
[
22
,
31
]
, especially in high dimensions.
In this work, we present a principled framework to find the optimal noise schedule while circumventing the difficulty involved with solving this PDE-constrained optimization problem.

Regularity assumption on data distribution.

For convenience, we use
X_{\star}\sim p_{\star}
to denote a sample from the data distribution with density
p_{\star}
.
We also generally use the subscript
\star
for quantities associated with the data distribution.
Let
s_{\star}\triangleq\nabla\log p_{\star}
be the score function of
p_{\star}
.
In our analysis, we frequently use the
Fisher information
of a distribution.
For a density
p
with score function
s
, its Fisher information is defined as

\mathcal{J}(p)\triangleq\lVert\nabla\log p\rVert_{L_{2}(p)}^{2}=\lVert s\rVert_{L_{2}(p)}^{2}.

(7)

The Fisher information of the data distribution is denoted by
\mathcal{J}_{\star}\coloneqq\lVert s_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
.

Assumption 1
.

We assume that
\lVert X_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
,
\lVert s_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
,
\lVert\nabla s_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}
, and
\lVert\nabla^{2}s_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}
are all finite.

The
L_{2}
norms in
1
are all with respect to
p_{\star}
.
When
1
is not satisfied, early stopping can be applied where the target distribution
p_{\star}
is replaced by
p_{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
as in
[
11
,
4
,
7
,
27
]
.
See
Section

C.4
for details.

3.1
An Optimal Control Formulation for Noise Schedule Design

Our objective is to solve the optimization problem (
6
), which minimizes
\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})
, the KL-divergence between the data distribution
p_{\star}
and the output distribution
\hat{p}_{\star}
.
Here, the densities
p_{\star}
and
\hat{p}_{\star}
are functions on the high-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and they depend on the noise schedule
(f,g)
through PDEs given by the Fokker–Planck equations.
This PDE dependence makes directly solving the KL-minimization problem analytically intractable.

To circumvent this difficulty, a key idea in our approach is to work with the Fisher information
\mathcal{J}_{t}=\mathcal{J}(p_{t})
.
Unlike the density
p_{t}
, the Fisher information
\mathcal{J}_{t}
is a scalar quantity.
Thus, rather than having its dynamics described by a PDE as
p_{t}
does,
\mathcal{J}_{t}
evolves according to an ODE, derived using the heat flow and the Bochner formula (
Lemma

4
):

\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}=2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}.

(8)

Theorem

1
below shows that the KL objective can be upper bounded in terms of
\mathcal{J}_{t}
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
1
holds.
For any noise schedule
(f,g)\in\Theta
and any sufficiently small step size
h
, it holds that

\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\lesssim\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(p_{\star})}+hd\int_{0}^{T}\left(2f(t)-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}}\right)^{2}dt.

(9)

Theorem

1
allows us to formulate the noise schedule design problem as the following optimal control problem:

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle(f, g) \in\Theta}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][l]{\underset{\displaystyle(f,g)\in\Theta}{\mathrm{minimize}}}\quad\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(p_{\star})}+hd\int_{0}^{T}\left(2f(t)-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}}\right)^{2}dt\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

(10)

\displaystyle\mathmakebox[\widthof{$\underset{\displaystyle\phantom{(f, g) \in\Theta}}{\mathrm{subject~to}}$}][c]{{\mathrm{subject~to}}}\quad

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}

\displaystyle=2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}},\hfil\hfil\displaystyle\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{0}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}_{\star},\quad\mathcal{J}_{T}=J_{T}.

This optimal control problem (
10
) is significantly more tractable thanks to the ODE constraint.
Moreover, we show in
Theorem

2
that its solution, although obtained by minimizing an upper bound on the KL objective, indeed achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
sampling error in the KL divergence.

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
1
holds.
For any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists
g^{*}\in\mathcal{G}
such that

\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\lesssim\frac{d}{n}\log^{2}\left(\frac{\lVert X_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}}{d}\cdot\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\star}}{d}\cdot n\right).

(11)

Theorem

2
informs the following noise schedule design approach.
One can choose
f
freely, and then choose the corresponding optimal
g^{*}
in
Theorem

2
.
The proof of
Theorem

2
reveals that the optimal
g^{*}
takes the form

g^{*}(t)=\lambda^{*}\frac{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{*}}{\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}},

(12)

where
\lambda^{*}
is a constant independent of
t
and
(\mathcal{J}_{t}^{*}:t\in[0,T])
is the optimal Fisher information trajectory.
We will demonstrate how this design approach leads to noise schedules with closed-forms in Section
3.2
.

3.2
Noise Schedule Design

In this section, we consider target data distributions that satisfy the following additional assumption.

Assumption 2
.

The target data distribution is either strongly log-concave (SLC), or belongs to a class of isotropic Gaussian mixture models (GMMs).
Specifically, the density
p_{\star}
of the data distribution satisfies one of the following conditions:

(i)

(SLC)

For some
m_{\star},M_{\star}>0
,

m_{\star}I\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{\star}\preceq M_{\star}I.

(13)

(ii)

(GMM)
For some mixture weights
\{\pi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
, means
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{L}
with
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\nu>0
, the density
p_{\star}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\varphi(x;\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
,
where
\varphi(x;\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
is the density of
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
.

SLC distributions and GMMs are two representative classes of distributions.
An SLC distribution represents a distribution with good concentration properties, while a GMM is a prototypical multi-modal distribution.
We show in
Proposition

1
that these two classes lead to the same form of characterization of
g^{*}
.
Based on this characterization, we derive a class of noise schedules with closed-form expressions in
Corollary

1
.
Although real-world data distributions are highly complex and may not belong to either of these two classes, the noise schedules we obtain using insights from these two classes show strong empirical performance on complex real-world distributions, as demonstrated in
Section

5
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose
1
holds.
When the data distribution additionally satisfies
2
, there exists a problem constant
\kappa\geq 1
such that the
g^{*}
found in
Theorem

2
satisfies

a_{1}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}(2f(\tilde{t})-\lambda^{*})d\tilde{t}\right)\leq g^{*}(t)\leq a_{2}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}(2f(\tilde{t})-\lambda^{*})d\tilde{t}\right),

(14)

where
a_{1}=\lambda^{*}d/(\mathcal{J}_{\star}\kappa^{2})
and
a_{2}=\lambda^{*}d/\mathcal{J}_{\star}
.

In particular, it holds that for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, there exists a time-independent constant
g_{0}
such that

g^{\circ}(t)=g_{0}\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}(2f(\tilde{t})-\lambda^{*})d\tilde{t}\right)

(15)

achieves
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
sampling error in the KL-divergence.

Proposition

1
establishes that the functional form of the optimal schedule can be bounded by static properties of the target distribution, specifically its initial Fisher information
\mathcal{J}_{\star}
and a problem constant
\kappa
.
By replacing the dependence on the trajectory with these constants, the schedule
g^{\circ}
circumvents the need to estimate the trajectory or solve the Fisher information ODE.
From a practical standpoint, this decouples the schedule design from the dynamics of the SDE; these parameters simply act as intrinsic problem constants that can either be directly estimated from the data distribution prior to sampling or treated as tunable hyperparameters.

Proposition

1
gives the functional form of the schedule
g^{\circ}
in terms of
f
, and leaves
f
as a free choice.
We now further restrict the class of schedules to obtain more concrete closed-form expressions.
In particular, we consider noise schedules in which
f
and
g
have an affine relationship.
We call this class the affine-coupled schedules (ACSs):

\Theta_{\textrm{ACS}}=\left\{(f,g)\in\Theta:f(t)=\theta g(t)+\omega\text{ for some }\theta,\omega\geq 0\right\}.

(16)

This ACS class encompasses Variance Exploding (VE) schedules where
f(t)=0

[
34
]
, with parameter choice
(\theta,\omega)=(0,0)
, and Variance Preserving (VP) schedules where
f(t)=g(t)/2

[
23
,
36
,
30
,
13
]
, with parameter choice
(\theta,\omega)=(1/2,0)
.
VE and VP schedules are widely used in standard diffusion models.
After restricting to ACSs, we obtain closed-form expressions for the noise schedules
(f,g^{\circ})
.

Corollary 1
.

Noise schedules of the form
(f,g^{\circ})
in (
15
) within the ACS class (
16
) can be written as

\begin{split}\text{when }2\omega\neq\lambda^{*},\quad&f(t)=\frac{\theta g_{0}(2\omega-\lambda^{*})}{(2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-\lambda^{*})e^{(2\omega-\lambda^{*})t}-2\theta g_{0}}+\omega,\\
&g^{\circ}(t)=\frac{g_{0}(2\omega-\lambda^{*})}{(2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-\lambda^{*})e^{(2\omega-\lambda^{*})t}-2\theta g_{0}},\\
\text{when }2\omega=\lambda^{*},\quad&f(t)=\frac{\theta g_{0}}{1+2\theta g_{0}t}+\omega,\quad g^{\circ}(t)=\frac{g_{0}}{1+2\theta g_{0}t},\end{split}

(17)

with parameters
(\theta,\omega,g_{0})
.

The noise schedule design in
Corollary

1
generalizes several empirically successful schedules.

•

VE-Exponential:
Setting
(\theta,\omega)=(0,0)
, (
17
) simplifies to
g^{\circ}(t)=g_{0}e^{\lambda^{*}t}
, which is the exponential schedule proposed by
Song and Ermon 2019
.

•

VP-Sigmoid:

Setting
(\theta,\omega)=(1/2,0)
, (
17
) simplifies to a sigmoidal function

2f(t)=g^{\circ}(t)=\frac{g_{0}\lambda^{*}}{g_{0}+(c-g_{0})e^{-\lambda^{*}t}},

which has been used in
[
40
,
25
,
13
]
.

Our results provide a theoretical explanation for the empirical success of these schedules.
Moreover, the noise schedule design in
Corollary

1
introduces additional tunable parameters,
g_{0}
and

, allowing practitioners to go beyond the commonly used schedules above and further improve performance.

11
,
28

), we can decompose the total sampling error
\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})
into the initialization error and the accumulated discretization error over the reverse SDE steps:

\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\leq\underbrace{\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{2\sigma_{T}^{2}}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(p_{\star})}}_{\text{initialization error}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}g(t)\left\lVert s_{t}(X_{t})-s_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})\right\rVert_{L_{2}(p_{t})}^{2}dt}_{\text{discretization error}}.

(18)

Here the discretization error is written in terms of forward process quantities using the distributional equivalence
X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}\overset{d}{=}X_{T-\tau},\tau\in[0,T]
, and
t_{k}=T-\tau_{n-k}
.
The remainder of the proof focuses on bounding the discretization error.

Step 2: Local Discretization Error.

We now bound
\left\lVert s_{t}(X_{t})-s_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})\right\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
for
t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}]
.
For this, it is more convenient to work with
s_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})
and show that it satisfies an SDE (

ds_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})=-f(T-\tau)s_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})+\sqrt{g(T-\tau)}\nabla s_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})dB_{\tau}.

(19)

Analyzing the movement of this SDE gives an upper bound on
\lVert s_{T-\tau}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})-s_{T-\tau_{n-k}}(X_{\tau_{n-k}}^{\leftarrow})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
, which can be converted into the following upper bound:

\lVert s_{t}(X_{t})-s_{t_{k}}(X_{t_{k}})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\lesssim hg(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}},\quad\forall t\in[t_{k},t_{k+1}].

(20)

Here when analyzing the movement of an SDE within a small time interval of length
h
, the key fact to leverage is the Itô isometry, which says for a stochastic integral with respect to Brownian motion,
\mathbb{E}[(\int Y_{t}dB_{t})^{2}]=\int\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}^{2}]dt.

This makes the diffusion term in (
50
) the dominant term, which gives the
O(h)
bound in (

O(h^{2})
.

Step 3: Fisher Information ODE.

Now a key term to tackle is
\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}
in the bound (
20
).
Using Bochner’s formula, we show that it is related to the Fisher information via the following ODE (

4

\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}=2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}=2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}.

(21)

Step 4: Final Error Bound.

Substituting (
21
) into the discretization error gives

\text{discretization error}\lesssim h\int_{0}^{T}g(t)(2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t})dt.

(22)

However, this is still not easy to optimize since it involves
f(t),g(t),J_{t}
, which are related.
Therefore, we eliminate
g(t)
by writing the bound as

h\int_{0}^{T}g(t)(2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t})dt=h\int_{0}^{T}\frac{(2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t})^{2}}{\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}}dt,

(23)

and using the concavity of entropy power
\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}\leq d\lVert\nabla s_{t}(X_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
(
Lemma

5

\text{discretization error}\lesssim hd\int_{0}^{T}\left(2f(t)-\frac{\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}}\right)^{2}dt.

(24)

Proof Sketch for
Theorem

2
:

For any
f

This problem can be solved via the Euler-Lagrange equation, which states that to minimize a functional

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial u_{t}}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{u}_{t}}=0.

(25)

Let

Balancing the initialization error and the discretization error by choosing

Conclusion

We introduced an optimal control framework for designing noise schedules, utilizing a Fisher information ODE to bypass intractable PDE constraints.
Our approach guarantees state-of-the-art
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
KL divergence bounds and yields a novel class of Affine-Coupled Schedules (ACS) that dynamically adapt to compute budgets, achieving superior FID scores on standard benchmarks.
Future work will explore extending our theoretical characterizations of noise schedules that achieve the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
bound to broader classes of complex data distributions.

Appendix A
Additional Related Works

Early theoretical analyses of diffusion models
[
6
,
7
,
11
]
focused predominantly on SDE samplers utilizing first-order discretization schemes under constant noise schedules.
A primary objective in this line of work has been to obtain a bound on the sampling error, typically measured in the total variation (TV) distance.
Because their analysis is restricted to constant noise schedules, their bounds scale sub-optimally with respect to the data dimension
d
and problem constants.

Chen et al. 2023a
,
Conforti et al. 2025
strengthened these results by establishing bounds on the KL divergence, which implies a similar scaling in the TV distance through Pinsker’s inequality.

The effect of time-varying noise schedules have been explored more recently.

Benton et al. 2024
,
Conforti et al. 2025
showed that by utilizing time-varying noise schedules, the KL error bound’s dependence on the dimension
d
and problem constants can be improved significantly such that it grows linearly with
d
.
Their results imply that the iteration complexity
n\asymp d/\epsilon^{2}
is sufficient to achieve
\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\lesssim\epsilon^{2}
error bounds.
These improved error bounds are achieved by considering constant
(f,g)
and time-varying discretization step size
\{h_{k}\}
.
As mentioned in
[
9
, Remark 3]
, such schedules can be viewed as fixing a constant-in-time
f
and using
g
that increases exponentially with time.
While these works establish
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
rates, the specific schedule constructions required by their analyses depart from the standard affine-coupled schedules commonly favored in empirical implementations.

Beyond tuning the standard time-reversal schedule, a conceptually related problem explored by
Chen et al. 2025
seeks to design a reverse process that differs from the exact time-reversal of the forward process.
To design this mismatched schedule,
Chen et al. 2025
propose to minimize the Lipschitz constant of the resulting reverse drift.
However, such generation processes also depart from standard empirical pipelines.
In contrast, our framework aligns with practice by maintaining the exact time-reversal of the true forward SDE and directly minimizes a rigorous upper bound on the KL sampling error, rather than relying on a regularity proxy.

Lastly, noise schedule design was considered in the context of Probability Flow ODEs.

Gao and Zhu 2024
analyze the Wasserstein-2 error, requiring the data distribution be strongly log-concave.
Under this regime, they conclude that VE schedules exhibit worse iteration complexity than VP schedules.
In contrast, we analyze stochastic SDE samplers using KL divergence and establish an optimal control framework based on Fisher information dynamics.
We require only mild regularity conditions to prove
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
bounds, where a strongly log-concave or a Gaussian mixture model is assumed only to derive closed-form parametrizations.
Under this setting, we prove that both VE-exponential and VP-sigmoid schedules for SDE samplers can achieve the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(d/n)
error rate.

B.1
Statistical Equivalence of Score Functions

We describe how to map a score function trained for one noise schedule to the score function corresponding to another noise schedule using their statistical equivalence as discussed in
[
14
]
.
In this section, we use
\tau_{t}
to be a map from one time-scale
t
to another time-scale
\tau_{t}
.

Let
X_{t}^{(1)}=\alpha_{t}^{(1)}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}^{(1)}Z
and
X_{t}^{(2)}=\alpha_{t}^{(2)}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}^{(2)}Z
be two random variables parameterized by
(\alpha_{t}^{(i)},\sigma_{t}^{(i)})
.
We first identify the equivalent time
\tau_{t}
in the first schedule that matches the SNR of the second schedule at time
t
:

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{(2)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}

Because the SNRs are identical,
X_{t}^{(2)}
and
X_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}
differ strictly by a scaling factor.
Specifically,

\displaystyle X_{t}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{(2)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}X_{\star}+Z\right)=\sigma_{t}^{(2)}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}X_{\star}+Z\right)=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}X_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}.

Applying the change of variables formula for probability densities,
p_{t}^{(2)}(x)=\left(\frac{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}\right)^{d}p_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}x\right)
. Taking the gradient of the logarithm of this density with respect to
x
yields the translation:

s_{t}^{(2)}(x)=\frac{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}s_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}\left(\frac{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}}x\right)

(33)

Score Function from Noise Prediction Model:

It is common to train a noise prediction model

\displaystyle S_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}[Z\mid X_{t}=x]

in place of the score function
s_{t}
.
The score of the conditional distribution of
(X_{t}\mid X_{\star})\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}X_{\star},\sigma_{t}^{2}I)
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{x_{t}}\log p(x_{t}\mid x_{\star})=-\frac{x_{t}-\alpha_{t}x_{\star}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}=-\frac{Z}{\sigma_{t}}.

The marginal score is the expectation of the conditional score, which gives the general relationship

\displaystyle s_{t}(x)=-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}S_{t}(x)

B.2
Non-Zero Score Matching Error

For any noise schedule
(f,g)
, define the score matching error
\epsilon_{sc}
as

\displaystyle\epsilon_{sc}^{2}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}g(t)\lVert s_{t}-\hat{s}_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}dt.

Applying the Girsanov theorem and the data processing inequality as in
[
11
,
4
,
15
]
and following the analysis in
Section

4.1
, the bound (
18
) is replaced by

\mathrm{KL}(p_{\star}\|\hat{p}_{\star})\leq\epsilon_{sc}^{2}+\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{2\sigma_{T}^{2}}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\int_{\tau_{k}}^{\tau_{k+1}}g(t)\left\lVert s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})-s_{t_{k}}(X_{\tau_{k}}^{\leftarrow})\right\rVert_{L_{2}(p_{t})}^{2}d\tau.

(34)

To address the impact of the score matching error
\epsilon_{sc}^{2}
, we evaluate two distinct paradigms: utilizing an existing pre-trained score network, and training a new model from scratch.

Pre-Trained Statistical Equivalence:

Let
(f_{1},g_{1})
and
(f_{2},g_{2})
denote two distinct noise schedules with corresponding marginal distributions characterized by parameters
(\alpha_{t}^{(1)},\sigma_{t}^{(1)})
and
(\alpha_{t}^{(2)},\sigma_{t}^{(2)})
. Suppose there exists a monotonically increasing time-map
\tau_{t}
such that the Signal-to-Noise Ratios (SNRs) of the two schedules are strictly equivalent, i.e.,

\frac{\alpha_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}{\sigma_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}}=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{(2)}}{\sigma_{t}^{(2)}},\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{2}].

(35)

As established in
Section

B.1
, under this SNR equivalence, the random variables
X_{t}^{(2)}
and
X_{\tau_{t}}^{(1)}
differ only by a deterministic spatial scaling.
Consequently, the score network
\hat{s}^{(2)}
corresponding to schedule
(f_{1},g_{1})
can be derived from a pre-trained score function
\hat{s}^{(1)}
as in (
33
).
By applying this change of variables, the score matching error
\epsilon_{sc}^{2}
evaluated under
(f_{2},g_{2})
is fundamentally governed by the pre-trained approximation error under
(f_{1},g_{1})
.
Thus, implementing our optimal optimal noise schedule design natively preserves the learned capabilities of an existing pre-trained model without incurring unaccounted optimization penalties.

Universal Approximation:

Alternatively, when training a score network
\hat{s}_{t}(x)
from scratch under noise schedule
(f,g)\in\Theta
, the score matching error can be driven arbitrarily close to zero.
To formalize this, we rewrite the score matching objective as an
L_{2}
distance with respect to a joint space-time measure.
Let us define the measure
d\mu(x,t)=g(t)p_{t}(x)dxdt
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
.
The score matching error is then equivalently given by:

\epsilon_{sc}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\hat{s}_{t}(x)-s_{t}(x)\|^{2}d\mu(x,t).

(36)

Because
p_{t}(x)
integrates to
1
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the total measure is
\mu(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T])=\int_{0}^{T}g(t)dt
.
For any schedule
(f,g)\in\Theta
, this integral is finite, making
\mu
a finite measure.
Furthermore, the target score function
s_{t}(x)
resides within the space
L_{2}(\mu)
, as its norm is strictly bounded by the integrated Fisher information:

\|s_{t}\|_{L_{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\int_{0}^{T}g(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}dt<\infty.

(37)

The finiteness of this integral follows directly from the integrability of
g(t)
and our previous derivation that the Fisher information
\mathcal{J}_{t}
remains bounded over the finite interval
t\in[0,T]
(under
1
).
Under these conditions, the universal approximation theorem (e.g.,
[
24
]
) guarantees that the class of neural networks is dense in
L_{2}(\mu)
.
Therefore, given sufficient model capacity and adequate samples from the forward process, there exists a parameterization such that the score matching error
\epsilon_{sc}^{2}
can be rendered arbitrarily small, regardless of the noise schedule chosen.

B.3
Constant Step Size Suffices for Time-Varying Noise Schedules

We show that choosing
(f,g)
with constant step size is without loss of generality.
Consider a choice of noise schedule
(f_{1},g_{1})
and time-varying step size with time grid
\{\tau_{k}^{(1)}\}
.
Denote by
\{\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}^{(1)}}^{\leftarrow,(1)}\}
the EI discretized process according to this noise schedule and time grid.
We show that there exists a noise schedule
(f_{2},g_{2})
and time grid
\{\tau_{k}^{(2)}\}
generated by constant step size
h

\tau_{k}^{(2)}=kh
, such that the EI discretized process
\{\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}\}
admits the same distribution as
\{\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}^{(1)}}^{\leftarrow,(1)}\}

Fix
(f_{1},g_{1})

\{\tau_{k}^{(1)}\}
, and
\{\tau_{k}^{(2)}\}
where
\tau_{k}^{(2)}=kh

Let
\phi:\tau_{k}^{(2)}\mapsto\tau_{k}^{(1)}

We consider the construction

f_{2}(T-\tau^{(2)})=f_{1}(T-\phi(\tau^{(2)}))\cdot\phi^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)}),\quad g_{2}(T-\tau^{(2)})=g_{1}(T-\phi(\tau^{(2)}))\cdot\phi^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)}),

(38)

for all
\tau^{(1)}\in\{\tau_{k}^{(1)}\}
and
\tau^{(2)}\in\{\tau_{k}^{(2)}\}

\phi^{\prime}
is the temporal derivative of
\phi

The SDE governing
\{\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}^{(1)}}^{\leftarrow,(1)}\}

d\hat{X}_{\tau^{(1)}}^{\leftarrow,(1)}=\left[f_{1}(T-\tau^{(1)})X_{\tau^{(1)}}^{(1)}+g_{1}(T-\tau^{(1)})s_{T-\tau_{k}^{(1)}}(X_{\tau_{k}^{(1)}}^{(1)})\right]d\tau^{(1)}+\sqrt{g_{1}(T-\tau^{(1)})}dB_{\tau^{(1)}}

(39)

Substituting the differentials

\displaystyle d\tau^{(1)}=\phi^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)})d\tau^{(2)},\quad dB_{\phi(\tau^{(2)})}=\sqrt{\phi^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)})}dB_{\tau^{(2)}}

for (
39

\displaystyle d\hat{X}_{\tau^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}

\displaystyle=\Big[f_{1}(T-\phi(\tau^{(2)}))\hat{X}_{\tau^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}

\displaystyle+g_{1}(T-\phi(\tau^{(2)}))s_{T-\tau_{k}^{(1)}}(\hat{X}_{\tau_{k}^{(2)}}^{(2)})\Big]v^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)})d\tau^{(2)}+\sqrt{g_{1}(T-\phi(\tau^{(2)}))\phi^{\prime}(\tau^{(2)})}dB_{\tau^{(2)}}.

Substituting (
38
) for the above equation, we obtain

\displaystyle d\hat{X}_{\tau^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}

\displaystyle=\left[f_{2}(T-\tau^{(2)})\hat{X}_{\tau^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}+g_{2}(T-\tau^{(2)})s_{T-\phi(\tau_{k}^{(2)})}(X_{\tau_{k}^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)})\right]d\tau^{(2)}

\displaystyle+\sqrt{g_{2}(T-\tau^{(2)})}dB_{\tau^{(2)}}.

Since
\hat{X}_{\tau^{(2)}}^{\leftarrow,(2)}\overset{d}{=}\hat{X}_{\tau^{(1)}}^{\leftarrow,(1)}
, the sequence of variables at the respective grid points
\{\tau_{k}^{(1)}\}
and
\{\tau_{k}^{(2)}\}
have the same distribution.

Preliminaries

Throughout the paper, we use the notation
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
to denote the coefficients of
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z
, which is the solution to the forward SDE (
1
).
The coefficients can be evaluated as
\alpha_{t}=\exp(-\int_{0}^{t}f(\tilde{t})d\tilde{t})
and
\sigma_{t}^{2}
as a solution to the ODE

\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{t}^{2}=-2f(t)\sigma_{t}^{2}+g(t),\quad\sigma_{0}=0.

(40)

The name “variance preserving” is used to refer to schedules satisfying
f(t)>0
and
g(t)=2f(t)
because they preserve the variance when
\mathrm{Var}(X_{\star})\coloneqq\lVert X_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}=\lVert Z\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
.

Notations:

For the proofs in the appendix, we use
t
and
\tau=T-t
to denote the corresponding forward and reverse time indices.
The notation
\otimes
is used to denote the tensor product, and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is used to denote the Hilbert-Schmidt norm for order
r
tensors in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\otimes r}
.
The divergence of a vector-valued function
\phi
is denoted by
\nabla\cdot\phi
.

C.1
Noise Schedules

In this section, we describe the coefficients
(f,g)
that correspond to various noise schedules used in practice.

Exponential:

Variance exploding models were used in
[
34
]
, where the corresponding SDE form
[
36
]
is given by

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\sqrt{g(t)}dB_{t},

where
g(t)
is chosen to grow exponentially with
t
.

Sigmoid:

The sigmoid schedule
[
13
]
corresponds to the forward process (
43
) with

\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma(h(1))-\sigma(h(t))}{\sigma(h(1))-\sigma(h(0))},\quad h(t)=\frac{t(\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min})+\theta_{\min}}{\tau_{sig}},

where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.
The map
t\mapsto h(t)
is interpreted as the corresponding timescale.
Using the relation
f(t)=-(2\bar{\alpha}_{t})^{-1}\frac{d}{dt}\bar{\alpha}_{t}
and the evaluation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\bar{\alpha}_{t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min}}{\tau_{sig}}\cdot\frac{\sigma^{\prime}(h(t))}{\sigma(h(1))-\sigma(h(0))},

we have

f(t)=\frac{\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min}}{2\tau_{sig}}\left[\frac{\sigma(h(t))(1-\sigma(h(t)))}{\sigma(h(1))-\sigma(h(t))}\right].

(41)

Using the approximation
\sigma(h(1))\approx 1
, the above becomes

f(t)\approx\frac{\theta_{\max}-\theta_{\min}}{2\tau_{sig}}\sigma(h(t)).

(42)

In
[
13
]
,
\theta_{\max}
is set to
3
, which yields
\sigma(h(1))\approx 0.95
.

The sigmoid schedule used in
[
40
]
is the sigmoid parametrization (
42
), and not the form in (
41
).

Linear:

The linear schedule, proposed in
[
23
]
, parametrizes the sample path solution

X_{t}=\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}X_{\star}+\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{t}}Z,\quad\bar{\alpha}_{t_{k}}=\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}(1-\beta_{t_{j}}).

(43)

The sequence
\{\beta_{\tilde{t}}\}
is a linear function
\beta_{t}=\beta_{\min}+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\cdot(t/T)
.
As shown in
[
36
]
, the expression for
f
in the limit
n\to\infty
is

\displaystyle f_{lin}(t)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\beta_{\min}+(\beta_{\max}-\beta_{\min})\frac{t}{T}\right).

The parameters are set with
\beta_{\min}=0.1/T,\beta_{\max}=20/T
.
While
(f_{lin},g_{lin})
are obtained in the limit
n\to\infty
, we refer to the pair as linear schedules.

Cosine:

The cosine schedule
[
30
]
refers to the choice

\displaystyle\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}=\frac{\cos\left(\frac{t/T+s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(\frac{s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right)},s=0.008.

Let
\theta(t)=(t/T+s)\pi/(2(1+s))
so that
\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}=\cos\theta(t)/\cos\theta(0)
.
In the SDE (
1
), this is equivalently written as the VP constraint
f=g/2
with
\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{t}f(\tilde{t})d\tilde{t}\right)
, which yields

\displaystyle f(t)=-\frac{d}{dt}\log\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{d}{dt}\left(\log\cos\theta(t)-\log\cos\theta(0)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\theta(t)}{\cos\theta(t)}\theta^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2T(1+s)}\tan\left(\frac{t/T+s}{1+s}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}\right).

As a result, the cosine schedule corresponds to
g(t)=(\pi/T(1+s))\tan((t/T+s)/(1+s)\cdot(\pi/2))
.

C.2
Generalized Blachman-Stam Inequality

Recall the Blachman-Stam inequality
[
37
,
5
]
which states that for
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z)}\geq\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{t}X_{\star})}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{J}(\sigma_{t}Z)}=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\mathcal{J}_{\star}}+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{d}.

Denote by
\mathrm{PS}(a,b)
for two positive scalars
a,b
to be the parallel sum
(x^{-1}+y^{-1})^{-1}
.
Rearranging and using the notation
\mathcal{J}_{t}=\mathcal{J}(\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z)
, we obtain the Blachman-Stam inequality

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{t}\leq\mathrm{PS}\left(\frac{\mathcal{J}_{\star}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}},\frac{d}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right).

We prove a generalization of Stam’s inequality that holds for higher order derivatives.

Lemma 1
.

Let
p_{t}
and
p_{t|\star}
be the density and (Gaussian) transition kernels of
X_{t}=\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z
with
X_{\star}\perp Z
.
Suppose
\nabla^{m}p_{\star}\in L_{1}(\mathrm{Leb}^{d})
.
Then,

\left\lVert\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}}{p_{t}}\right\rVert_{L_{2}}\leq\mathrm{PS}\left(\alpha_{t}^{-m}\left\lVert\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}}{p_{\star}}\right\rVert_{L_{2}},\frac{\lVert H_{m}\rVert_{L_{2}(\gamma^{d})}}{\sigma_{t}^{m}}\right).

(44)

Importantly, this bound interpolates the
L_{2}
norm of
\nabla^{m}p_{\star}/p_{\star}
and that of the limit
\alpha_{t}/\sigma_{t}\to 0
.

Proof.

First, we show that
1
implies that the boundary condition vanishes sufficiently fast for the integration by parts formula

\int\nabla_{x_{\star}}^{m}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}=-\int\nabla^{m-j}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})\otimes\nabla^{j}p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star},\quad\forall j\in\{1,\cdots,m\}

(45)

to hold.
Denote by
\phi
the Gaussian transition kernel.
The case for
j=1
simplifies to

\displaystyle\int(\nabla^{2}\phi)p_{\star}dx=-\int\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla p_{\star}dx\Leftrightarrow\int\nabla(p_{\star}\nabla\phi)dx_{\star}=0.

The second identity above holds if
p_{\star}\nabla\phi
and
\nabla(p_{\star}\nabla\phi)
are in
L_{1}
.
Since
\nabla\phi
is bounded,

\displaystyle\int\lVert p_{\star}\nabla\phi\rVert dx_{\star}\leq\lVert\nabla\phi\rVert_{\infty}\int p_{\star}dx_{\star}<\infty.

Therefore,
p_{\star}\nabla\phi\in L_{1}(\mathrm{Leb}^{d})
.
Observe
\nabla\cdot(p_{\star}\nabla\phi)=p_{\star}\nabla^{2}\phi+\langle\nabla\phi,\nabla p_{\star}\rangle
.
Since
\nabla^{2}\phi
is bounded, the first term is in
L_{1}
.
The second term is bounded in
L_{1}
since

\displaystyle\int\lVert\nabla p_{\star}\rVert dx_{\star}=\int\frac{\lVert\nabla p_{\star}\rVert}{p_{\star}}p_{\star}dx_{\star}\leq\left(\lVert\nabla\log p_{\star}\rVert^{2}p_{\star}dx_{\star}\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

The case for
j=2
follows by observing that

\displaystyle-\int\nabla\phi\otimes\nabla p_{\star}dx_{\star}=\int\phi\nabla^{2}p_{\star}dx_{\star}\Leftrightarrow\int\nabla(\phi\nabla p_{\star})=0,

and repeating the same steps above, observing that
\nabla\cdot(\phi\nabla p_{\star})
involves
\nabla\log p_{\star}
and
\nabla^{2}\log p_{\star}
.
The same argument applies to any
m
and
j\in\{1,\cdots,m\}
.

Let
H_{m}(x)=(-1)^{m}e^{x^{2}/2}\nabla^{m}e^{-x^{2}/2}
be the
m
-th order Hermite polynomial.
It holds by the marginalization
p_{t}(x)=\int p_{t|x}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}
and the identity

\nabla^{m}p_{t|0}(x|x_{\star})=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\sigma_{t}^{m}}H_{m}\left(\frac{x-\alpha_{t}x_{\star}}{\sigma_{t}}\right)p_{t|0}(x|x_{\star})

(46)

that

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\sigma_{t}^{m}}H_{m}(z)\frac{p_{t|0}(x|x_{\star})}{p_{t}(x)}p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\sigma_{t}^{m}}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{m}(Z)|X_{t}=x\right],

(47)

where
m=1
recovers Tweedie’s formula
[
32
,
17
]

s_{t}(x)=\frac{\alpha_{t}\mathbb{E}[X_{\star}|X_{t}=x]-x}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(48)

Observe the identity

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}}\nabla_{x_{\star}}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star}),

which holds by
p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})
being a Gaussian transition probability
\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}x_{\star};\sigma_{t}I)
.
Applying the same derivatives
m
times, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{m}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\nabla_{x_{\star}}^{m}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star}).

Marginalizing, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{m}p_{t}(x)=\nabla_{x}^{m}\int p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\nabla_{x_{\star}}^{m}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}

Using the integration by parts property (
45
) repeatedly, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{m}}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\int\nabla_{x_{\star}}^{m}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{m-1}}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\int\nabla^{m-1}p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})\otimes\nabla p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\int p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})\nabla^{m}p_{\star}(x_{\star})dx_{\star}.

Dividing by
p_{t}(x)
, we obtain the identity

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha_{t}^{m}}\int\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}(x_{\star})}{p_{\star}(x_{\star})}\frac{p_{t|\star}(x|x_{\star})p_{\star}(x_{\star})}{p_{t}(x)}dx_{\star}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}(X_{\star})}{p_{\star}(X_{\star})}\mid X_{t}=x\right].

To summarize, we obtained above and in (
47
) the two identities

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}^{-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}(X_{\star})}{p_{\star}(X_{\star})}\mid X_{t}=x\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}(x)}{p_{t}(x)}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{-m}(-1)^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{m}(Z)\mid X_{t}=x\right].

For any
\theta
, it holds that their linear combination is equal to
\nabla^{m}p_{t}(x)/p_{t}(x)
.
By Jensen’s inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{t}}{p_{t}}\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left\|(1-\theta)\alpha_{t}^{-m}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}(X_{\star})}{p_{\star}(X_{\star})}\mid X_{t}=x\right]+\theta\sigma_{t}^{-m}(-1)^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[H_{m}(Z)\mid X_{t}=x\right]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\theta)^{2}\alpha_{t}^{-2m}\left\|\frac{\nabla^{m}p_{\star}}{p_{\star}}\right\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\theta^{2}\sigma_{t}^{-2m}\left\|H_{m}\right\|_{L_{2}(\gamma^{d})}^{2},

where the last step used
Z\perp X_{\star}
and
\mathbb{E}H_{m}(Z)=0
.
Minimizing over
\theta
, we obtain (
44
).
∎

A consequence of Stam’s inequality is that
\alpha_{T}^{2}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\ll\sigma_{T}^{2}d
implies
\mathcal{J}_{T}\approx\sigma_{T}^{-2}d
.
Recall Tweedie’s formula (
48
), which implies

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{T}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbb{E}[Z|X_{T}]\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\left(d-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(Z|X_{T})\right).

Using
Z=\sigma_{T}^{-1}(X_{T}-\alpha_{T}X_{\star})
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(Z|X_{T})=\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{\star}|X_{T})\leq\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{\star}).

Substituting for Tweedie’s formula above, we have

\frac{1}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\left(d-\frac{\alpha_{T}^{2}}{\sigma_{T}^{2}}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\right)\leq\mathcal{J}_{T}\leq\frac{d}{\sigma_{T}^{2}},

(49)

which implies
\mathcal{J}_{T}\approx\sigma_{T}^{-2}d
when
\alpha_{T}^{2}\lVert X_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\ll\sigma_{T}^{2}d
.

C.3
Score Function Dynamics Along the Reverse SDE

The SDE describing the score function
s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})=\nabla\log p_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})
along the reverse process
(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})
with constant schedules was established in
[
15
, Proposition 3.2]
.
Assuming the regularity conditions in
1
, we derive the corresponding SDE for time-varying schedules.

Lemma 2
.

Under
1
, it holds that

ds_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})=-f(t)s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})+\sqrt{g(t)}\nabla s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})dB_{\tau}

(50)

is well-defined, and
\lVert s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}},\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}<\infty
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

The Fokker-Planck equation governing the evolution of the density
p_{t}
along the SDE (
1
) is

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}=\nabla\cdot(f(t)xp_{t})+\frac{1}{2}g(t)\nabla\cdot\nabla p_{t}.

(51)

By the chain rule, we obtain the point-wise evaluation

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log p_{t}=f(t)d+f(t)\langle x,s_{t}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}g(t)(\nabla\cdot s_{t}+\lVert s_{t}\rVert^{2}).

Using the identities
\nabla(x\cdot s_{t})=s_{t}+(\nabla s_{t})x
,
\nabla\lVert s_{t}\rVert^{2}=2(\nabla s_{t})s_{t}
and taking the gradient on both sides of the above equation, we obtain

\partial_{t}s_{t}=f(t)s_{t}+f(t)(\nabla s_{t})x+\frac{1}{2}g(t)\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})+g(t)(\nabla s_{t})s_{t},

(52)

where
(\nabla s_{t})x
is understood as the matrix-vector product of the Jacobian
\nabla s_{t}
and the vector
x
.
Using the Itô formula, we obtain that the drift driving
ds_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})
along the reverse process is

\displaystyle\quad\left(-\partial_{t}s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\right)(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})+\nabla s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\left[f(t)X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}+g(t)s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})\right]+\frac{g(t)}{2}\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})

\displaystyle=-f(t)s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow}).

Substituting for the Itô formula, we obtain

\displaystyle ds_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})

\displaystyle=-f(t)s_{t}(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})+\sqrt{g(t)}(\nabla s_{t})(X_{\tau}^{\leftarrow})dB_{\tau}.

Since
\nabla s_{t}=\frac{\nabla^{2}p_{t}}{p_{t}}-\frac{\nabla p_{t}\nabla p_{t}^{\dagger}}{p_{t}}
, the finiteness of
\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
follows from
Lemma

1
.
∎

C.4
Regularity Properties of the Score Function

Recall the definition
\Psi(t)=g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}
.

Lemma 3
.

Under
1
, it holds for any
(f,g)\in\Theta
such that

\lvert\Psi^{\prime}(t)\rvert<\infty,\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

(53)

Proof.

We first show that
\Psi^{\prime}(t)
is a polynomial of
\lVert s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}},\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
, and
\lVert\nabla\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
which in turn depends on
\nabla^{2}s_{t}
.

Using
\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{t}=\nabla\cdot s_{t}
, (
52
), and the Fokker-Planck equation, we evaluate
\Psi^{\prime}
as

\Psi^{\prime}(t)=g^{\prime}(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}+g(t)\left[2\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{t}\partial_{t}\nabla s_{t}+\int\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\partial_{t}p_{t}dx\right].

(54)

The expression for
\partial_{t}s_{t}
was evaluated in (
52
), and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\nabla s_{t})\nabla(\partial_{t}s_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\nabla s_{t})\nabla\left[f(t)s_{t}+f(t)(\nabla s_{t})x+\frac{1}{2}g(t)\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})+g(t)(\nabla s_{t})s_{t}\right].

Only the third term on the RHS depends on a third order derivative, which is resolved using integration by parts

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla s_{t})(\nabla^{2}(\nabla\cdot s_{t}))=\int p_{t}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{t}\nabla^{2}(\nabla\cdot s_{t})dx

\displaystyle=-\int\left\langle\nabla\cdot(p_{t}\nabla s_{t}),\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})\right\rangle dx.

By evaluating the divergence in the first argument, we obtain that the above equates to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla s_{t})(\nabla^{2}(\nabla\cdot s_{t}))

\displaystyle=-\int p_{t}\left\langle(\nabla s_{t})s_{t}+\nabla\cdot(\nabla s_{t}),\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})\right\rangle dx

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\lVert(\nabla s_{t})s_{t}\right\rVert_{L_{2}}+\lVert\nabla\cdot(\nabla s_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}\right)\lVert\nabla(\nabla\cdot s_{t})\rVert_{L_{2}}.

Therefore, we conclude that
\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{E}(\nabla s_{t})\nabla(\partial_{t}s_{t})
is a polynomial function of
\lVert s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}},\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
, and
\lVert\nabla^{2}s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
.
By
1
and
Lemma

1
, all terms above are bounded for
(f,g)\in\Theta
.

The last term of (
54
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle\int\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\partial_{t}p_{t}dx

\displaystyle=\int\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\nabla\cdot\left(fxp_{t}+\frac{g}{2}\nabla p_{t}\right)dx

\displaystyle=-\int\left\langle fxp+\frac{g}{2}p_{t}s_{t},\nabla\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\right\rangle dx

\displaystyle=-f(t)\mathbb{E}\langle x,\nabla\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\rangle-\frac{g(t)}{2}\mathbb{E}\langle s_{t},\nabla\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}\rangle,

where the second equality used integration by parts.
Again, this quantity is a polynomial of
\lVert s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}},\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
, and
\lVert\nabla^{2}s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}
, which were all shown to be finite by
1
and
1
.
Because
\Psi^{\prime}(t)
is a polynomial on a bounded domain with finite coefficients,
\lvert\Psi^{\prime}(t)\rvert<\infty
for all
t
.

∎

Gaussian mixture models form an important class of distributions that is often used to model real-world data.
We show that
1
is satisfied by a Gaussian mixture model.
Consider
p_{0}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}w_{i}\mathcal{N}(x;0,\Sigma_{i})
, where we assume centered components without loss of generality.
The score function
s_{0}(x)
can be evaluated as in
[
12
]
, where

\displaystyle s_{0}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\pi_{i}(x)\Sigma_{i}^{-1}x,\quad\pi_{i}(x)=\frac{w_{i}\mathcal{N}(x;0,\Sigma_{i})}{p_{0}(x)}.

Taking the gradient on both sides, we obtain

\displaystyle\nabla s_{0}(x)=-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\pi_{i}(x)\Sigma_{i}^{-1}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\pi_{i}(x)(-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}x-s_{0}(x))(-\Sigma_{i}^{-1}x-s_{0}(x))^{\dagger}.

There exists a single Gaussian component that dominates as
\lVert x\rVert\to\infty
, with
\pi_{i}(x)
decaying to zero exponentially fast for all other
i
.
Therefore,
\lVert\nabla s_{0}(x)\rVert
is bounded in
L_{\infty}
.
The same can be deduced for higher order derivatives.
By
Lemma

1
, the same holds for
t>0
.

C.5
Fisher Information Dynamics

The following states the Fisher information dynamics in (
8
).

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
1
holds.
For any
(f,g)\in\Theta
,

\dot{\mathcal{J}}_{t}=2f(t)\mathcal{J}_{t}-g(t)\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}.

Proof.

In this proof, we use the notation
p_{t}^{X}=p_{t}
to distinguish between the density
p_{t}^{X}
of the process
(X_{t})
from the process
(Y_{t})
defined as

X_{t}=\alpha_{t}Y_{t}.

(55)

By the Itô formula, the two processes are related through the SDE
dX_{t}=\dot{\alpha}_{t}Y_{t}dt+\alpha_{t}dY_{t}
.
Substituting
\dot{\alpha}_{t}=-f(t)\alpha_{t}
and using (
55
), we obtain

\displaystyle dY_{t}=-\frac{\dot{\alpha}_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}Y_{t}dt+dX_{t}

\displaystyle=f(t)Y_{t}dt-f(t)\frac{X_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}dt+\frac{\sqrt{g(t)}}{\alpha_{t}}dB_{t}=\frac{\sqrt{g(t)}}{\alpha_{t}}dB_{t}.

(56)

The density
p_{t}^{Y}
of
Y_{t}
is a convolution between
p_{0}^{Y}
and a Gaussian kernel, and differentiating under the integral yields the well-known identity (e.g.,
[
37
, Section 5]
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p_{t}^{Y}=\frac{g(t)}{2\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Delta p_{t}^{Y}.

Differentiating

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{J}(p^{X}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left[-2\alpha^{-3}\dot{\alpha}_{t}\right]\mathcal{J}(p_{t}^{Y})+\alpha_{t}^{-2}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{J}(p_{t}^{Y})

\displaystyle=2f(t)\alpha_{t}^{-2}\mathcal{J}(p_{t}^{Y})+\alpha_{t}^{-2}\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{J}(p_{t}^{Y}).

Proof.

Recall that the solution

to (

).
Because the density

with a Gaussian density,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\nabla\log p_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{E}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t},\quad\forall t\in(0,T]

The relation

is shown next:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\lVert\nabla\log p_{t}\rVert^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{E}\nabla\log p_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}^{\dagger}

.

Under
2(i)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla s_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{M_{\star}}{m_{\star}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{J}_{\star}^{2}.

Recall the integration by parts formula
\mathbb{E}\lVert s_{\star}\rVert^{2}=-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{\star}

By the log-concavity assumption, it holds that
-\mathrm{Tr}\mathbb{E}\nabla s_{\star}\geq m_{\star}d
.
Moreover, the upper bound implies
\lVert\nabla s_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}(\nabla s_{\star})^{2}\leq M_{\star}^{2}d

Combining, we obtain

E.2
Prékopa–Leindler inequality

The constants
(m_{t},M_{t})
of the SLC condition in

are evaluated along the SDE (

For the following, denote by

the standard Gaussian density (of the random variable

).

Lemma 7
.

Suppose the density
p_{\star}
satisfies (
13
).
For any positive constants
(\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t})
, it holds that the density
p_{t}
of

satisfies

\mathrm{PS}\left(\frac{m(p_{\star})}{\alpha_{t}^{2}},\frac{m(p_{Z})}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)I\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}\preceq\mathrm{PS}\left(\frac{M(p_{\star})}{\alpha_{t}^{2}},\frac{M(p_{Z})}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right)I.

Proof.

We recall some properties of the cumulant generating function and duality theory from
[
8
]
.
Let
\Lambda_{X}(\theta)
denote the cumulant generating function
\Lambda_{X}(\theta)=\log\mathbb{E}e^{\langle\theta,X\rangle}
so that

\Lambda_{X_{t}}(\theta)=\Lambda_{\alpha_{t}X_{\star}}(\theta)+\Lambda_{\sigma_{t}Z}(\theta)=\Lambda_{X_{\star}}(\alpha_{t}\theta)+\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^{2}.

(76)

For a density

\nabla^{2}\Lambda_{X}(\theta)
is the covariance matrix associated with the density proportional to

\nabla^{2}V(x)

Therefore,
\Lambda_{X}
and

are related through the Fenchel-Legendre transform.
By duality, the potential
V_{\star}
defined as
p_{\star}(x)\propto e^{-V_{\star}(x)}
is
(m_{\star},M_{\star})
-SLC if and only if

\displaystyle\frac{1}{M_{\star}}I\preceq\nabla^{2}\Lambda_{X_{\star}}(\theta)\preceq\frac{1}{m_{\star}}I.

Taking the second derivatives of (

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{M_{\star}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)I\preceq\nabla^{2}\Lambda_{X_{t}}(\theta)\preceq\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{m_{\star}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)I

Applying duality once more, we obtain

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{m_{\star}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)^{-1}I\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x)\preceq\left(\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{M_{\star}}+\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)^{-1}I.

Lemma 8
.

Suppose

and

\frac{d}{\kappa^{2}}\leq\frac{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}{\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}\leq d,\quad\forall t\in[0,T],

where the constant

•

•

where
\bar{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\mu_{i}
and

In particular, if
\max_{1\leq i,\ell\leq L}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert\leq R_{\mu}
for some

, then

\displaystyle\kappa\leq\left(1+\frac{R_{\mu}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{d}\frac{R_{\mu}^{2}}{\nu^{2}}\right).

(79)

Proof.

The upper bound
\frac{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}{\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}\leq d
directly follows from
Lemma

5
, and it does not require
2
.

In the remainder of the proof, we focus on proving the lower bound
\frac{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}{\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}\geq\frac{d}{\kappa^{2}}
, or equivalently,
\frac{\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}\leq\frac{\kappa^{2}}{d}
.

Log-Concave Distributions.

Because
p_{t}
remains log-concave for all
t
, its Hessian can be bounded in the positive-definite (Löwner) order
\prec
:
m_{t}I\preceq-\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}\preceq M_{t}I
.
We define
\kappa_{t}=M_{t}/m_{t}
as the condition number of this log-concave density.
Adding Gaussian noise decreases the condition number and hence, the time-varying condition number is bounded by that associated with the target density:
\kappa=\sup_{t}M_{t}/m_{t}=M_{\star}/m_{\star}
.

The above remark is described formally.
By
Lemma

6
, there exists some
(m_{\star},M_{\star})
such that

\lVert\nabla s_{\star}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{d}\left(\frac{M_{\star}}{m_{\star}}\right)^{2}\mathcal{J}_{\star}^{2}.

By
Lemma

7
, for any
t\in[0,T]
, the condition number of the smoothed density
p_{t}
can be bounded pointwise.
Using
m(\gamma^{d})=M(\gamma^{d})=1
and
m_{t},M_{t}
defined the lower and upper bounds of (
75
), the condition number
\kappa_{t}=M_{t}/m_{t}
evaluates to

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}\coloneqq\frac{M_{t}}{m_{t}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{M_{\star}}{m_{\star}}\right)\frac{1+m_{\star}\sigma_{t}^{2}/\alpha_{t}^{2}}{1+M_{\star}\sigma_{t}^{2}/\alpha_{t}^{2}}.

The condition number
\kappa_{t}
is bounded uniformly over time (over all values of SNR
\alpha_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t}^{2}
), and consequently there exists a global constant
\kappa=M_{\star}/m_{\star}
such that:

\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}\leq\kappa^{2}\frac{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}{d},\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

Gaussian Mixture Models.

Recall that we assume that the data distribution is a mixture of
L
Gaussian components.
Specifically,
p_{\star}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\varphi(x;\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
,
where
\varphi(x;\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
is the density of
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},\nu^{2}I)
.

From the solution of the forward process
X_{t}\overset{d}{=}\alpha_{t}X_{\star}+\sigma_{t}Z
, it holds that the law of
X_{t}
is a GMM with density

p_{t}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\varphi(x;\alpha_{t}\mu_{i},v_{t}^{2}I),\quad v_{t}^{2}=\alpha_{t}^{2}\nu^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}.

(80)

Define the random variable
M
which takes the value
\mu_{i}
with probability
\pi_{i}
.
Let
\tilde{Z}
be a standard Gaussian vector independent of
Z
and
M
, so that

X_{t}\overset{d}{=}\alpha_{t}M+v_{t}\tilde{Z}.

(81)

Observe that
(\alpha_{t}M\mid X_{t}=x)
takes values in
\{\alpha_{t}\mu_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{t}\mu_{L}\}
, where
(\alpha_{t}M\mid X_{t}=x)
is
\alpha_{t}\mu_{i}
with probability

\frac{\pi_{i}\varphi(x;\alpha_{t}\mu_{i},v_{t}^{2}I)}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}\pi_{j}\varphi(x;\alpha_{t}\mu_{j},v_{t}^{2}I)}.

(82)

The score function for GMMs can be evaluated using Tweedie’s formula as in
[
19
, Section 3.2]
, where

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{X_{t}}(x)=\frac{1}{v_{t}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}M\mid X_{t}=x]-x\right).

The Jacobian of the conditional expectation with respect to
x
is then

\displaystyle\nabla\mathbb{E}[\alpha_{t}M\mid X_{t}=x]=\frac{1}{v_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}(\alpha_{t}M\mid X_{t}=x)=\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x).

Therefore, the second order Tweedie’s formula evaluates to

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{v_{t}^{2}}I-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)

and we use integration by parts as in the proof of
Lemma

5
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{t}=-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(X_{t})=\frac{d}{v_{t}^{2}}-\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{4}}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}).

From
\mathcal{J}_{t}\geq 0
, we have

\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t})\leq d.

(83)

Using the integration by parts
\mathbb{E}\lVert s_{t}\rVert^{2}=-\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\nabla s_{t}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}(p_{t})}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{v_{t}^{4}}-\frac{2\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{6}}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t})+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{4}}{v_{t}^{8}}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{v_{t}^{4}}+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{4}}{v_{t}^{8}}\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t})\right)^{2}.

(84)

By the semi-positive definiteness of
\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)
, there exists some
\lambda_{\max}
such that
\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)\preceq\lambda_{\max}I
.
Multiplying both sides by
\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)
and taking the trace, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)\right)^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x).

which implies

\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)\right)^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}d\frac{v_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}.

(85)

We next obtain an upper bound on
\lambda_{\max}
.
Define
\bar{\mu}_{x}=\mathbb{E}[M\mid X_{t}=x]
.
For any unit vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle u^{\dagger}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)u

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(M=\mu_{i}\mid X_{t}=x\right)u^{\dagger}(\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}_{x})(\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}_{x})^{\dagger}u

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}(u^{\dagger}M\mid X_{t}=x).

The random variable
u^{\dagger}M
takes values in the set
\{u^{\dagger}\mu_{1},u^{\dagger}\mu_{2},\cdots,u^{\dagger}\mu_{L}\}
, and

\displaystyle\lvert u^{\dagger}(\mu_{i}-\mu_{\ell})\rvert\leq\lVert u\rVert\lVert\mu_{i}-\mu_{\ell}\rVert\leq\Delta_{\mu}.

Using the definition
\lambda_{\max}=\sup_{\lVert u\rVert_{2}=1}u^{\dagger}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)u
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}=\max_{u:\lVert u\rVert_{2}=1}u^{\dagger}\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)u\leq\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}.

Substituting for (
85
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathrm{Cov}(M\mid X_{t}=x)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{d}{4}\frac{v_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}.

Substituting for (
84
), we obtain

\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq\frac{d}{v_{t}^{4}}+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{4}}{v_{t}^{8}}\frac{d}{4}\frac{v_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}=\frac{d}{v_{t}^{4}}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}\right).

(86)

A lower bound on
\mathcal{J}_{t}
is obtained using the CRLB (
74
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{t}\geq\frac{d^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\mathrm{Cov}(X_{t})}

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\alpha_{t}^{2}\mathrm{Cov}(M)+v_{t}^{2}I\right\}}.

Substituting

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(M)=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}(\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu})(\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu})^{\dagger},

we obtain

\mathcal{J}_{t}\geq\frac{d^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert^{2}+v_{t}^{2}d}.

(87)

Combining (
86
) and (
87
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d^{2}}{v_{t}^{4}}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}\right)\cdot\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert^{2}+v_{t}^{2}d}{d^{2}}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}\right)\left(1+\frac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{v_{t}^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert^{2}\right).

The bound is further simplified by using
v_{t}^{2}\geq\alpha_{t}^{2}\nu^{2}
, which yields

\displaystyle\frac{d\lVert\nabla s_{t}\rVert^{2}_{L_{2}}}{\mathcal{J}_{t}^{2}}\leq\left(1+\frac{1}{4\nu^{2}}\Delta_{\mu}^{2}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{\nu^{2}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{L}\pi_{i}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert^{2}\right).

The upper bound in (
79
) is obtained by observing that
\max_{1\leq i,\ell\leq L}\lVert\mu_{i}-\bar{\mu}\rVert\leq R_{\mu}
implies
\Delta_{\mu}^{2}\leq 4R_{\mu}^{2}
.

∎

G.1
Choosing ACS Parameters

Recall the choice of
g^{\circ}
in (
32
).
While
Proposition

1
provides closed-form expressions for the ACS framework, deploying these schedules in practice requires properly configuring the hyperparameters
(\theta,\omega,\gamma,g_{0})
.
A constraint common in existing widely-used schedules is that the forward process must inject noise monotonically (
g^{\prime}(t)>0
).
The bound in (
11
) is obtained by setting the terminal energy
\mathcal{E}=\int_{0}^{T}f(t)dt
to balance the initialization and discretization errors.
The proof of
Proposition

1
shows how the parameters
(g_{0},\gamma)
should scale with problem constants.
In this section, we set
T=1
.

Proposition 5
.

Consider the schedule
g^{\circ}
in (
32
).
Given any
\mathcal{E}>0
, the constraints in
\Theta_{ACS}
and
g^{\prime}(t)>0
are satisfied for all
(\theta,\omega,\gamma)
when

\displaystyle\theta\in(0,\infty),\quad\omega\in[0,\mathcal{E}),\quad\gamma\in\left[2\frac{\mathcal{E}}{\lambda^{*}},\infty\right),\quad g_{0}=\frac{2\omega-\gamma\lambda^{*}}{2\theta}\cdot\frac{e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)-1}-1}{1-e^{-(2\omega-\gamma\lambda^{*})}}.

Proof.

The constraints on
(g_{0},\gamma,\theta,\omega)
are derived for the case
T=1
and
2\omega\neq\gamma\lambda^{*}
.
Recall the expression for
g^{\circ}
in (
32
), where we use
c=\gamma\lambda^{*}
:

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{0}(2\omega-c)}{(2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-c)e^{(2\omega-c)t}-2\theta g_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g_{0}(2\omega-c)e^{-(2\omega-c)t}}{(2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-c)-2\theta g_{0}e^{-(2\omega-c)}}..

Using the change of variables
u(t)=(2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-c)-2\theta g_{0}e^{-(2\omega-c)t}
, we have

\int_{0}^{1}g(t)dt=\frac{1}{2\theta}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{du}{u}=\frac{1}{2\theta}\log\frac{2\theta g_{0}+(2\omega-c)-2\theta g_{0}e^{-(2\omega-c)}}{2\omega-c}.

(101)

Using the ACS relation
f(t)=\theta g(t)+\omega
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}f(t)dt=\theta\int_{0}^{1}g(t)dt+\omega=\mathcal{E},

which implies

\int_{0}^{1}g(t)dt=\frac{\mathcal{E}-\omega}{\theta}.

(102)

Equating (
101
) and (
102
) and solving for
g_{0}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{0}=\frac{2\omega-c}{2\theta}\cdot\frac{e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)}-1}{1-e^{-(2\omega-c)}}.

The condition
\omega<\mathcal{E}
is ensures
g_{0}>0
.
To see this, observe that the
(2\omega-c)/(1-e^{-(2\omega-c)})
term in
g_{0}
is of the form
x/(1-e^{-x})
.
This function is strictly positive for all
x\neq 0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\omega<\mathcal{E}\Rightarrow 2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)>0\Rightarrow g_{0}\propto e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)}-1>0.

The condition
c\geq 2\mathcal{E}
is described next.
Evaluating
g^{\prime}(t)
and substituting for the inequality
g^{\prime}(t)\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle 2\theta g_{0}+2\omega-c\leq 0.

Substituting the expression for
g_{0}
, this inequality becomes

\displaystyle\frac{2\omega-c}{1-e^{-(2\omega-c)}}\left[e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)}-e^{-(2\omega-c)}\right]\leq 0.

As established earlier, the first term in the parentheses is strictly positive for
2\omega\neq c
and we have

\displaystyle e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)}-e^{-(2\omega-c)}\leq 0\Leftrightarrow e^{2(\mathcal{E}-\omega)}\leq e^{c-2\omega}.

This is equivalent to the requirement
c\geq 2\mathcal{E}
, which in turn is
\gamma\geq 2\mathcal{E}/\lambda^{*}
.
∎

As established in
Theorem

2
and discussed in
Section

D.3

defined in (
26

.
Specifically, we parametrize the base decay rate using the Lambert-
\mathcal{W}

\lambda_{n}=\mathcal{W}\left(Kn\right),\quad\mathcal{E}_{n}=\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2},\quad c_{n}=\gamma\lambda_{n},

p_{\star}

By selecting structural variables
\theta

\omega

\mathcal{E}_{n}

All generation experiments in this section were initialized with

To strictly align the empirical evaluation with our theoretical analysis, generation was performed using a custom implementation of the EI sampler that exactly integrates the linear drift of the analyzed SDEs.
Generation quality was evaluated by computing the FID score using 50k generated samples.
For CIFAR-10, the 50k real images were acquired from the CIFAR-10 training set as is standard in the

For ImageNet experiments, we evaluated performance against the pre-computed statistics reported by

G.3
Constant Noise Schedules Achieve Poor Trade-Offs

A VP-constant schedule can be obtained from
Proposition

1
by setting

\displaystyle\theta=0,\quad 2\omega=c,\quad g_{0}=c,

which implies
f=\omega,g=2\omega
.
Because
c=g_{0}\approx\mathcal{E}/T
is necessarily a constant for the VP-constant schedule, it is forced to maintain a high diffusion rate across the reverse process.
We proved in
Proposition

4
and (
97
) that constant noise schedules are highly sensitive to the specific choice of constant.
The bound shows that the discretization error bound scales linearly with the data conditioning parameter as
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{E}\mathcal{J}_{\star}/d)
.
When the target distribution is highly anisotropic, this linear penalty results in a large discretization error unless extremely small step sizes are used.

Here we demonstrate through numerical experiments that the bound in (
97
) accurately describes the severity.

4
illustrates the sampling error suffered by the VP-constant schedule.
For these experiments, we set
T=1
and sweeped through the constant schedules
f=\mathcal{E}
and
g=2\mathcal{E}
over various values of
\mathcal{E}
.
The plots were generated in
d=2
dimensions with
n=100
discretization steps.

2\sigma

f=\mathcal{E},g=2\mathcal{E}
) to parameters of the target Gaussian distribution.
The leftmost panel plots the true initialization error and the true empirical sampling error (as opposed to upper bounds), confirming that success is bottlenecked by the sensitivity with respect to
\mathcal{E}
.

illustrates the sensitivity of constant noise schedules when generating an anisotropic 2D Gaussian target distribution (variances

).
The leftmost panel plots the KL divergence alongside the initialization error as a function of the scaling constant
\mathcal{E}
.

The total sampling error exhibits a U-shaped profile, precisely validating the quadratic dependence on

in (

).
For small values of
\mathcal{E}

When
\mathcal{E}
is poorly scaled, the reverse process suffers from large initialization error when

is large.
These empirical results highlight the danger of utilizing data-agnostic constant schedules in practice.
A practitioner must guess the parameter
\mathcal{E}
within a remarkably narrow window dictated by the intrinsic Fisher information and condition number of the data distribution.
A slight mis-estimation forces the sampler into catastrophic failure.
