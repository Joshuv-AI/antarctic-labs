Executive Summary
Information about home energy costs is essential for appraisers, lenders, and buyers, but reliable data are
often unavailable in real estate transactions. To fill this gap, the National Laboratory of the Rockies
(NLR) developed the Energy Cost Estimate (ECE) tool. The tool provides flexible, data-driven estimates
of annual household energy use and costs across the contiguous United States, using only a small
number of inputs typically found in mortgage appraisals. This report presents the methodology behind
version 2.0 of the ECE tool, demonstrates how users can generate estimates through its interface, and
discusses its applications and limitations.

The ECE tool relies on over 2.2 million simulated homes from NLR’s ResStock™ dataset, which
models the diversity of the U.S. housing stock using physics-based building simulations. Seven key
home attributes—location, building type, vintage, size, foundation type, heating fuel, and cooling type—
are translated into features for quantile regression models. The models return 25th, 50th, and 75th
percentile predictions for annual electricity, natural gas, propane, and fuel oil use, as well as total annual
household energy costs. The model performance is assessed using cross-validation, with median
predictions compared directly to test data and estimated interquartile ranges used to validate the 25th and
75th percentiles. This validation indicated that the models provide reliable median estimates for most
homes, though they underpredict very high energy users due to limited input data. To account for this
uncertainty, the ECE reports ranges rather than single point estimates. Energy costs are calculated by
applying state-level average fuel prices, while leaving flexibility for users to substitute custom rates.

ECE results are delivered via a public application programming interface (API) with three endpoints:
direct building characteristics, Fannie Mae’s Uniform Residential Appraisal Report (Form 1004), and
the U.S. Department of Energy’s Home Energy Score. This design makes the tool adaptable to multiple
industry workflows, supporting applications such as appraisal-based valuation and serving as a
comparable baseline to home energy labels.

The tool is intended as an initial estimator and not as a substitute for detailed energy audits. Its strength
lies in its accessibility, providing consistent, credible estimates where detailed building information is
lacking. These estimates can help lenders factor energy costs into underwriting, assist appraisers in
valuing efficiency improvements, and enable buyers to more accurately assess the total cost of
homeownership. While limited to only fixed attributes available during appraisals, the tool establishes a
practical foundation for integrating energy performance into the real estate and finance markets.

1 Introduction
The overall energy performance of a home is the result of many complex (and often hidden) features and
characteristics. In the absence of energy labels and disclosure guidelines, it can be difficult to understand
the efficiency of homes and estimated energy costs. As a result, researchers at the National Laboratory
of the Rockies have developed the Energy Cost Estimate (ECE) tool to provide energy cost estimates for
residential units in the contiguous United States, based on nationally standardized data, for: (1)
appraisers to more easily determine home value adjustments for energy efficiency leveraging a holistic
metric, (2) lenders to underwrite energy costs and expand the market for home energy improvement
financing products, and (3) homebuyers to more accurately budget for whole-home operating costs and
make more informed investment decisions.

These energy cost estimates are intended to mitigate risk for both borrowers and lenders by providing a
more comprehensive consideration of the costs of homeownership and serving as a basis for comparing
the energy costs of homes. Through these mechanisms, energy cost estimates can encourage market
participants to pursue in-field assessments to consider efficiency upgrades and access efficiency
financing products. In addition, the estimated energy costs can provide a synthetic “comparable” home
to a home that has a more detailed energy assessment and documentation of energy efficiency features.
This method provides a way to better value those improvements in an appraisal.

In this report, we present a methodology for estimating the typical range of annual residential energy use
and costs, using high-level building information commonly available from Fannie Mae’s Uniform
Residential Appraisal Report (Form 1004). 1 Our approach relies on seven key building inputs from the
appraisal form as features of a regression model trained on synthetic building data from ResStockTM
(Reyna et al. 2025). The regression model produces quartile estimates of energy use by energy source
and annual energy costs. The ECE tool provides access to the predictions of this regression model
through several application programming interface (API) endpoints depending on the use case.

This report describes the development of the underlying regression model and its application using
inputs from the uniform appraisal form or Home Energy Score 2 assessments. Given the limited number
of home inputs, the ECE tool provides estimates for a typical home with these characteristics, but these
can be subject to considerable uncertainty. To illustrate this uncertainty, we provide predictions for the
25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, which indicate the bounds where the most likely values for a given home
configuration occur. Additional information may be required to obtain a more accurate representation of
a specific home. Examples of attributes not captured in the features that may cause a home to fall
outside the estimated range include significant energy efficiency retrofits, atypical occupant behavior,
electric vehicle charging or other uncommon loads, higher- or lower-than-typical equipment
efficiencies, or the presence of rooftop photovoltaic panels. Although the ResStock public datasets
attempt to capture much of this diversity, the limited set of inputs available for this tool means that the
estimates represent typical conditions rather than more accurate, home-specific predictions.

2 Model Methodology
We use quantile regression models to predict ranges of annual energy costs for a set of seven building
inputs trained on synthetic energy data from the building stock. These inputs are selected based on their
availability in the Universal Residential Appraisal Report (Form 1004), and the model is adapted to
work with Home Energy Score data by mapping the fields to the appropriate inputs. The regression
models directly predict energy usage for four fuel types, and postprocessing steps provide smooth
predictions across floor areas, restrict unlikely input combinations, and calculate estimated costs from
energy usage. This section describes the underlying training data (Section 2.1), the selection of features
and variables (Section 2.2), the training and validation (Section 2.3), and the final adjustments before
making the predictions (Section 2.4). Figure 1 shows the high-level process for the ECE tool.

2.1 Training Data
ResStock uses granular building characteristic data, advanced physics-based energy modeling, and high-
performance computing to generate millions of models of residential units and simulate the energy use
of a diverse nationwide residential building stock. We applied the baseline simulation data from the
v2024.1 ResStock dataset (Present et al. 2024) to train the ECE regression model. This dataset contains
the building model inputs and energy usage for residential units in the contiguous United States. Table 1
summarizes the training dataset, which includes approximately 2.2 million residential building models.
Each model includes annual energy usage by fuel type and end use, simulated using weather data from a
typical meteorological year (TMY3). Since the ECE tool is designed to estimate annual fuel costs under
typical weather conditions, this dataset met the requirements for training, because it provides annual fuel
use outputs using TMY3 weather data and contains a large volume of data points.

ResStock assigns baseline physical characteristics and operational details to individual homes in the
building stock, most of which depend on location, vintage, house size, and/or heating fuel. Its sampling
approach draws on many different data sources and ensures a distribution of characteristics that
represents the diversity of the existing housing stock (Reyna et al. 2025). We limited the housing
attributes used in the regression model to align with those included in Form 1004. However, due to the
dependency structure of the probability distributions, the model results reflect not only the limited set of
input features but also broader characteristics that influence energy usage. For example, the model
incorporates correlations between heating fuel and water-heating fuels, as well as differences in
appliance ownership that arise from factors like location and vintage. The following subsection
describes the requirements to translate ResStock inputs into the fields of the appraisal form. Due to
limited training data, the model does not currently support predictions for Alaska or Hawaii. While
predictions can be made for new construction, the training data does not include 2020s vintages, so
homes built in 2010 or later are represented using 2010s vintage data.

Degree days allow us to account for the local effects of outdoor temperature, which influence energy
usage primarily through heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) loads. We also mapped each
county to the IECC climate zone (integer) and IECC moisture zone (string), incorporating them as
numeric and categorical variables, respectively. These values provide insight into weather dependencies
and help identify attributes driven by energy code requirements when combined with other features such
as HDD, CDD, build year, and building type. In the final implementation, the user is expected to input
the ZIP Code of the dwelling unit, which is translated to the county before mapping to weather features.

The remaining features more directly correspond to ResStock housing characteristics. Vintage (build
year) and finished floor area are sampled as bins in ResStock and then converted to numeric values. We
introduced cooling type, heating fuel, foundation type, and building type as categorical variables. These
categorical features were encoded using one-hot encoding, so that specific options (e.g., “Central
Cooling”) were transformed into their own columns, with a value of “1” if the option was present and
“0” if not, resulting in a total of 26 features. In practice, we developed separate models for each building
type and fuel output to minimize the model sizes and address algorithmic limitations discussed in
Section 2.3. We trained the regression models to predict four annual energy target variables: electricity,
natural gas, propane, and fuel oil. All energy results in this section are reported in kilowatt-hours, the
default units used in the ResStock dataset publication. Figure 2 shows distributions for the training
features and output variables in the ResStock dataset. To improve the readability of the output energy
distributions, we excluded values above the 99.9th percentile.

2.3 Regression Model Development
To capture the prediction variability of energy cost estimates, we trained models to predict the 25th, 50th,
and 75th percentiles of fuel usage, and report all values when the ECE tool is invoked. We selected
XGBoost (Chen et al. 2025) as the training algorithm because it supports quantile regression and
balances prediction accuracy with training and prediction efficiency, making it well-suited for
integration into the ECE API. Quantile regression in XGBoost is limited to regression models that
predict a single target variable. As a result, we developed a separate regression model for every
combination of fuel output and quantile. For example, one model predicts the 25th percentile of annual
electricity usage while a separate model predicts the 50th percentile. Additionally, we separated
regression models by building type, as each floor area bin in ResStock maps to values unique to the
building type, which introduced undesirable dependencies in the model where discrete floor area values
were tightly coupled to the building type. In total, there are 36 models accessible by the API depending
on user inputs. Predictions for single-family attached and multifamily building types represent
individual unit energy usage and costs, as opposed to whole-building results.

The metrics used to assess performance and tune hyperparameters included R2, mean absolute error, and
mean bias deviation. We present these outputs in Section 2.3.1 only for the cross-validation results of
the middle quantile, whereas the interquartile range (IQR) is validated against IQRs in the cross-
validation test data. Our regression model performance is evaluated using unseen synthetic training data
from ResStock, which has been validated against AMI data at the regional level. The EnergyPlus
simulation engine used to generate the dwelling unit models has also been broadly validated against

real-world data (Wilson et al. 2022). Further validation comparing our model outputs with actual utility
bills and energy values may improve confidence in the results.

2.3.1 Cross-Validation and Model Training
Despite using only seven building inputs, the training data maintain significant diversity across both
inputs and outputs. The complexity of modeling energy usage in buildings requires a validation
approach with sufficient data. To maximize the amount of test data, we employed k-fold cross-
validation, which allows training and testing on independent subsets of the full ResStock dataset. We
implemented cross-validation with five folds to assess the performance of the 50th percentile models,
tune hyperparameters, and analyze predicted quartile ranges. As a result, each ResStock record is used
once as test data and four times as training data. In each fold, approximately 80% of the data are used for
training and 20% are held out for testing, ensuring independence between the two sets.

The initial application of cross-validation focused on assessing the performance of the 50th percentile
regression models for each fuel type prediction and building type. Median predictions are expected to be
the most accurate, and are directly comparable to the test dataset, unlike the other quartile regression
models, which cannot be validated against test data as easily. Figure 3 shows scatterplots comparing the
ResStock modeled test data and the median predicted energy usage by fuel type across all building
types. The data presented include all predicted values from the cross-validation, so the number of data
points shown equals the number of training records. Importantly, because we used cross-validation, the
test data points were not seen by the models during training, ensuring an unbiased evaluation of
performance.

The regression models generally provide accurate predictions for most homes but fail to predict high-
energy users accurately. This inconsistency is likely due to the increased variability among high-energy
users in ResStock. Several of the most important features that impact energy usage are incorporated into
the model; however, unseen information can have outsized impacts for homes already expected to have
high energy usage. For example, larger homes tend to exhibit a wider distribution of energy usage, since
their demand depends more on space heating and cooling, which are strongly influenced by envelope
insulation, air sealing, thermostat setpoints, and HVAC performance. Without access to these more
detailed inputs, the model often underpredicts usage in large, inefficient homes. This may result from
variable operational inputs such as thermostat settings or differences in the thermal envelope.

To represent uncertainty and maintain flexibility for ECE applications, we report the quartiles of annual
energy use and costs for the four fuel types. Direct comparisons of the 25th and 75th percentile models to
test data are not possible because the ResStock dataset provides a single modeled energy outcome per
unit, rather than a distribution of possible outcomes. Therefore, we empirically computed the 25th and
75th percentiles for segments of the training data to generate comparison points. Using the full set of
ResStock training data, we segmented dwelling unit models by generating unique combinations of
building type, vintage, floor area, heating fuel, foundation type, and county, and selected annual energy
usage for segments with at least 20 data points, provided the energy usage is greater than zero for a
given fuel output. A probability density function fit to each segment of energy usage enables estimation
of the 25th and 75th percentiles and IQRs. For example, while iterating through all combinations of the
selected options, if the ResStock segment of homes in ZIP Code 80214 with natural gas heating and
finished basements has at least 20 data points, we calculated the IQR of energy for each fuel to use as a
comparison point.

Using the valid segments of homes, we extracted the 25th and 75th percentiles from the cross-validation
prediction data and calculated the average IQRs that can be directly compared to the training data.
Because the ECE prediction interquartile ranges are based on cross-validation test folds, the underlying
test data were not visible to the model during training, ensuring that the comparison reflects out-of-
sample performance rather than training fit. Figure 5 shows the comparisons between the training data
and ECE interquartile ranges. As with the analysis of median predictions, differences in IQR values
stem from the limited set of input variables used to train the ECE regression models. There is less bias in
the IQR results, however, as the underprediction is consistent for both the 25th and 75th percentiles.

Distributions of the IQR percent differences are shown in Figure 6. Homes with electricity loads account
for the largest number of segments, followed by those with nonzero natural gas loads. Although all
homes consume electricity, total electricity usage varies widely. In homes with significant fuel use,
major end uses such as space heating, water heating, and cooking are often met by fuels other than
electricity, resulting in relatively low electricity consumption. This pattern contributes to greater
variability in electricity IQR percent differences across segments and to poorer performance in
predicting electricity usage. A key source of this variability is uncertainty in end-use fuel assignments
beyond space heating. While the heating fuel input identifies the primary fuel for space heating, it does
not fully capture water heating and cooking fuels. Although these fuel types are correlated in the training
data, it is common for homes to use different fuels for each end use. Without visibility into these inputs,
the regression models may misattribute fuel types, leading to greater uncertainty and variability in the
predicted electricity use for segments with mixed fuel configurations.

2.3.2 Hyperparameter Tuning
We performed a sensitivity analysis to identify appropriate hyperparameters for the XGBoost model.
The learning rate, maximum tree depth, number of estimators, L2 regularization term, and L1
regularization term were iteratively adjusted to determine an appropriate combination of parameter
values using cross-validation. We calculated the mean absolute error and mean absolute percent error for
the 50th percentile predictions and IQR using the cross-validation datasets. Additionally, the R2 and
mean bias deviation were calculated for the 50th percentile predictions. Finally, we counted the
occurrences of cross-validation predictions with overlapping quartiles. We defined overlapping quartiles
as instances in which a higher quartile prediction was lower than a smaller quartile prediction, for
example, when the 25th percentile predicted higher energy usage than the 50th percentile. Although this
is uncommon, it is an important scenario to avoid, and the likelihood of occurrence was sensitive to the
hyperparameters.

2.4 Postprocessing
Before returning predictions in the ECE tool, we applied several steps to ensure the results aligned with
the limitations of the training data and to prevent invalid or unrealistic values.

2.4.1 Smoothing Across Floor Areas
Floor area inputs in ResStock are sampled as bins and converted to nine discrete values specific to each
building type. Because of these discrete inputs, the tree-based structure of the ECE regression model
cannot accurately infer values between them. To enable continuous predictions across floor areas, we
used a piecewise cubic Hermite interpolator fit to model predictions at the discrete floor areas.
Specifically, we generated nine predictions for each fuel output and quartile of a building configuration
by varying only the floor area and fitting the monotone cubic function to those outputs. This method

 11

preserves the general trend of predictions and the positive correlation between energy usage and floor
area while avoiding overshooting or unrealistic fluctuations. Figure 7 shows how predictions are
smoothly interpolated across floor areas for two sample buildings. This process is repeated for each
nonzero fuel output and quartile.

2.4.2 Constraining Inputs and Outputs
We applied constraints on allowable inputs to ensure they are inclusive of those available in the training
data and reflect realistic building configurations. The initial constraints occur at the API level discussed
in Section 3, in which numeric inputs cannot be higher or lower than those in ResStock, and categorical
values fall within the available enumerations. For example, only cooling types of central AC, room AC,
and none are allowed, and ZIP Codes are limited to those in the contiguous United States.

Although the most common segments of building inputs have a representative set of models available
for training in ResStock, less common building input combinations can lead to low confidence or poor
performance from the ECE tool. To assess the prevalence of problematic predictions, we first performed
a grid search across possible input combinations and made energy predictions. To capture the bounds of
our predictions across the discrete number of input combinations, the grid search included varied
locations, vintages, floor areas, cooling types, foundations, and building types. We identified a set of
parameters for each option, and made predictions for each combination, totaling approximately 113,000
unique building configurations. For each IECC climate zone, we selected a ZIP Code representing a
rural area and one representing an urban area, as well as four vintages, three floor areas, and each valid
enumeration of the categorical variables (building type, foundation, heating fuel, and cooling type).
Warnings from the predictions were collected to identify combinations likely to have negative quartile
ranges or negative energy predictions. These types of predictions occur when buildings are not well
represented in the training data, which also reflects that they are not likely to occur in reality, as
ResStock generates more samples for more common housing features. Information from the grid search
analysis informed the need to constrain inputs prior to a prediction to prevent input combinations that do
not or are unlikely to exist. A common example is region-specific foundation type, where certain parts
of the country are unlikely to have certain foundations (e.g., basements in Florida).

 12

To implement these constraints, we relied on the stock characteristics present in ResStock to identify
how frequently input combinations occur. We scanned combinations of county and heating fuel, county
and foundation type, and building type and foundation type in ResStock, and identified combinations
that did not exist. Based on this analysis, the tool flags any input combination that lacks sufficient
training data without returning results.

Although this step greatly reduced the occurrence of invalid prediction values, we also constrained the
outputs to avoid invalid predictions. The logic for this included:

 • If the 25th percentile is greater than the 75th percentile, set all quartile predictions to the mean of
 the 25th and 75th percentiles.

 • If the 25th percentile is greater than the 50th percentile, set the 50th percentile equal to the 25th
 percentile.

 • If the 50th percentile is greater than the 75th percentile, set the 50th percentile equal to the 75th
 percentile.

 • If the prediction is less than 0, return 0.

This occurred most often for combinations of inputs with low energy usage, such as small units with
minimal HVAC demand, in which overlapping quartiles or negative predictions may be possible.

2.4.3 Estimating Costs
Once constraints were resolved and predictions made, we estimated energy costs by applying average
state fuel rates to the predicted energy totals by fuel type. We used fuel and electricity price estimates
from EIA (2024a), consistent with the approach in Home Energy Score. This process was applied for
each percentile, and the final output includes cost predictions for each quartile. As with the energy
predictions, we ensured that quartile ranges did not overlap, which could result from differences
between electricity and fuel prices. However, we did not explicitly smooth final cost predictions across
floor areas, as the underlying energy predictions already account for this. Users may alternatively apply
custom rates to the energy predictions to generate region-specific cost estimates; however, only average
rates can be applied, as time-varying rates are not possible within the current framework.

4 Discussion
This report presents an approach and API for estimating the annual energy use and costs of residential
dwelling units in the United States using a limited set of inputs commonly reported during a home
appraisal. Although buildings are complex systems that require detailed inputs for highly accurate
modeling, this work shows that models using a small number of impactful inputs can yield reasonable
estimates when complete building data are unavailable or difficult to obtain. This tool addresses a
critical industry need by offering energy use and cost estimates that support decision-making when
stakeholders lack the knowledge or resources to conduct detailed building simulations. Common
applications include real estate transactions, in which inspections and appraisals may lack detailed
energy-relevant information and users may not have the expertise or resources to create detailed energy
models. Potential users include lenders, appraisers, and building occupants seeking accessible energy
insights without the need for detailed audits.

We leveraged a large dataset produced with the ResStock analysis tool to train a regression model that
estimates the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of annual energy use and cost. This ResStock dataset
includes two million dwelling unit simulations that capture the diversity of residential building
configurations and operations, with its assumptions and outputs calibrated to reflect real-world energy
use (Wilson et al. 2022). We validated the regression model using five-fold cross-validation across the
full dataset. For the median value, we directly compared training and test predictions, whereas we
evaluated the 25th and 75th percentiles by comparing the predicted IQR to IQRs estimated from
sufficiently large training data segments. Postprocessing is applied to the predictions to avoid invalid
outputs and maintain consistency across similar building configurations. Specifically, the postprocessing
steps prevent errant predictions for housing segments not well-represented in the training data and
ensure estimates increase with percentiles and floor areas.

We recommend using this approach as a first-pass estimation tool, suitable for early decision-making
stages or as a point of comparison. Limiting the inputs makes the tool broadly accessible, but it also
limits the precision of its estimates. The predictions are designed to reflect the most typical or
representative buildings given the inputs, based on patterns observed in the broader building stock. To
help users interpret the predictions, we provide a range from the 25th to 75th percentile, rather than a
single-point estimate. This context is important, as some building configurations may yield a wide range
of outputs, reflecting the underlying diversity in the building stock that cannot be captured with limited
input data. While the training data account for a wide range of energy use by modeling diverse occupant
behavior, appliance usage, and construction details, the regression model generalizes outputs to typical
energy usage patterns. Further, the ECE tool does not capture features that can significantly alter energy
usage but are not widely represented in the training data, such as rooftop solar, electric vehicle charging,
and energy-efficiency retrofits. This balance between accessibility and specificity is a trade-off in which
the model avoids overfitting to niche scenarios but may underrepresent buildings that fall outside typical
consumption patterns.

This approach is well-suited to early home-buying stages, in which data availability is typically limited
to preliminary sources such as public data or appraisals. Although the current implementation is
designed around the input fields commonly available in the appraisal form (Form 1004), the model
framework is extensible to other use cases. As additional inputs become available, the regression model
can be expanded or retrained to improve accuracy and better capture nuances in building characteristics.

 20

For example, the model can be trained to accept null input values so that the prediction automatically
adjusts and increases in precision as more inputs become known.

The annual energy cost is estimated using state-average retail fuel costs to provide a generalizable
baseline. Although custom rates can be applied to produce more geographically specific estimates, the
tool does not support time-varying or dynamic pricing structures. This should be considered when
applying the model in contexts where energy pricing variability significantly affects total energy costs
and consumption behavior.
