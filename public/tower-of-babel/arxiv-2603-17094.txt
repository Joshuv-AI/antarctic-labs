Evaluating LLM-Simulated Conversations in Modeling Inconsistent and Uncollaborative Behaviors in Human Social Interaction
Ryo Kamoi 2 †, Ameya Godbole 3 †, Longqi Yang 1, Rui Zhang 2, Mengting Wan 1, Pei Zhou 1

Abstract
Simulating human conversations using large language models (LLMs) has emerged as a scalable methodology for modeling human social interaction. However, simulating human conversations is challenging because they inherently involve inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors, such as misunderstandings and interruptions. Analysis comparing inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in human- and LLM-generated conversations remains limited, although reproducing these behaviors is integral to simulating human-like and complex social interaction. In this work, we introduce CoCoEval , an evaluation framework that analyzes LLM-simulated conversations by detecting 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors at the turn level using an LLM-as-a-Judge. Using CoCoEval , we evaluate GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and Claude Opus 4 by comparing the frequencies of detected behaviors in conversations simulated by each model and in human conversations across academic, business, and governmental meetings, as well as debates. Our analysis shows that (1) under vanilla prompting, LLM-simulated conversations exhibit far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than human conversations; (2) prompt engineering does not provide reliable control over these behaviors, as our results show that different prompts lead to their under- or overproduction; and (3) supervised fine-tuning on human conversations can lead LLMs to overproduce a narrow set of behaviors, such as repetition. Our findings highlight the difficulty of simulating human conversations, raising concerns about the use of LLMs as a proxy for human social interaction.

Introduction

Simulating human conversations by generating utterances for all participants using large language models (LLMs) has recently emerged as a scalable methodology for modeling human social interaction, with potential applications in prototyping social platforms such as social media
(
23
)
, testing hypotheses in social science
(
17
)
, and helping decision-makers forecast how people may respond to new policies
(
28
)
. Simulated conversations are typically evaluated based on consistency with conversation history and collaborativeness in pursuing shared goals or resolving conflicts
(
23
;
36
;
4
)
. However, greater consistency or collaborativeness does not necessarily indicate a better simulation, because human conversations inherently involve inconsistent behaviors such as misunderstandings and repetition, as well as uncollaborative behaviors such as interruptions and disagreements
(
10
;
27
;
25
)
. Simulating these behaviors is essential for generating human-like conversations and capturing the complexity of human social interaction. Nevertheless, it has not been sufficiently analyzed whether LLM-based simulations can reproduce inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors with frequencies and distributions comparable to those observed in human conversations.

To address this gap, we introduce
CoCoEval
, an evaluation framework for analyzing inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations. The framework consists of an evaluation scheme and a benchmark. First, we propose a fine-grained evaluation that detects inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors at the turn level. We define 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors based on taxonomies for dialogue generation and conversation simulation
(
11
;
30
)
and use an LLM-as-a-Judge to identify turns exhibiting each type of behavior. In this evaluation, a simulation is considered successful if these turn-level behaviors occur with frequencies and distributions comparable to those in human conversations. This evaluation complements a commonly used coarse-grained LLM-as-a-Judge approach that assigns conversation-level scores
(
36
;
29
)
, such as consistency and collaborativeness rated on a 1-10 scale. Second, we introduce the
CoCoEvalBench
benchmark to evaluate LLMs on the task of generating 30-turn conversation continuations given conversational metadata and history. It is created from real-world human conversations in QMSum
(
34
)
, NCPC
(
18
)
, SIM
(
8
)
, and IQ2
(
32
)
, spanning business, academic, and governmental meetings as well as debates.

Using
CoCoEval
, we evaluate GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, and Claude Opus 4 by comparing conversations simulated by each model with human conversations. The results highlight the difficulty of reproducing inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors with frequencies comparable to those observed in human conversations, as shown in Figure
1
. (a) First, under vanilla prompting, LLMs generate conversations with far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than human conversations. (b) Second, prompt engineering does not provide reliable control over these behaviors. LLMs overproduce inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors when explicitly instructed to include them at frequencies observed in human conversations. (c) Third, supervised fine-tuning on human conversations fails to capture the diversity of their inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors. Instead, fine-tuned GPT-4.1 overproduces a narrow range of behaviors such as repetition and redundancy. (d) Finally, generating an entire conversation in a single LLM call results in substantially fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than generating each turn in a separate call.

In summary, our analysis indicates that inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations exhibit different frequencies and distributions from those in human conversations. Their frequencies are sensitive to the simulation setup, making them difficult to control. These findings highlight the difficulty of simulating human conversations and raise concerns about using LLMs as proxies for human social interaction. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce
CoCoEval
, a framework for evaluating how well LLM-simulated conversations can reproduce inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors inherent in human conversations.

•

We introduce a fine-grained evaluation scheme that detects 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors at the turn level.

•

We show that, in a vanilla prompting setting, LLM-simulated conversations include far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors compared to human conversations.

•

Our analysis shows that neither prompt engineering nor supervised fine-tuning provides reliable control of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations.

Inconsistent Behaviors

Logical Contradictions

Utterances that contradict earlier statements by the same speaker or the speaker’s information in the metadata.

One participant says “The remote control runs on a single AA battery,” and later says “It requires two AA batteries.”

Factual Inaccuracies

Utterances that contain factually incorrect statements.

A participant claims that infrared signals can pass through walls.

Misunder-standings

Utterances that reflect a misinterpretation of information provided by other participants earlier in the conversation history.

A participant says the remote control only uses a touch screen, but another later mistakenly assumes that it has physical buttons.

Redundant Information

Utterances that contain unnecessarily lengthy content and add little new informational value.

A participant gives a long explanation about previous projects without adding new information.

Repetition

Utterances that unnecessarily repeat information stated earlier in the conversation.

One participant says “I will bring the documents,” and another asks “So you will bring the documents, right?” without adding new information.

Uncollaborative Behaviors

Persistent Disagreement

Utterances that repeatedly reject or contradict others’ positions despite established consensus or clear supporting evidence.

A participant continues to reject a proposed plan even after others agree and provide supporting evidence for it.

Interruptions

Utterances that disrupt the conversation by cutting off another participant.

A participant interrupts another speaker mid-explanation and leaves the speaker’s utterance unfinished, like “… the key point here is”.

Off-topic Responses

Utterances that deviate substantially from the current conversational topic.

During the middle of a project meeting discussion, a participant shares unrelated personal stories.

Under Answering

Utterances that give evasive responses and fail to address preceding questions.

When asked “Will this policy increase taxes?”, a participant replies “We are focused on supporting working families.”

Unclear Intent

Utterances that are ambiguous or fail to clearly convey the speaker’s intentions.

When asked whether to agree or disagree, a participant responds only with “Maybe.”

3
CoCoEval

We introduce
CoCoEval
, an evaluation framework for analyzing LLM-simulated conversations with a focus on inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors.
CoCoEval
consists of an evaluation scheme (§
3.1
) and a benchmark (§
3.2
). Human conversations inherently contain inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors
(
27
;
10
;
25
)
, so higher consistency or collaborativeness does not necessarily indicate a better simulation. Instead, a successful simulation should generate inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors with frequencies and distributions comparable to those in human conversations. The evaluation scheme and benchmark of
CoCoEval
are designed to facilitate comparison between human- and LLM-generated conversations with respect to these behaviors.

3.1
Evaluation Scheme

CoCoEval
evaluates the consistency and collaborativeness of LLM-simulated conversations using two complementary LLM-as-a-Judge approaches: overall and fine-grained evaluation.

Overall evaluation.

The overall evaluation assigns two conversation-level scores, one for consistency and one for collaborativeness, each on a 1-10 scale. This form of evaluation is commonly used in conversation simulation
(
36
;
29
)
. Our prompt for LLM-as-a-Judge includes definitions of consistency and collaborativeness, together with scoring rubrics describing the criteria for each score range. The definitions of consistency and collaborativeness in
CoCoEval
follow prior discussions in conversation evaluation.
Consistency
in this work includes coherence with previous utterances by all speakers, adherence to the specified persona, and correctness with respect to world knowledge and social norms
(
23
;
9
;
1
)
, as well as the avoidance of unnecessary redundancy and repetition
(
11
;
14
)
.
Collaborativeness
is defined as the extent to which a speaker’s contributions support shared group objectives and facilitate the goals of other participants
(
36
;
24
;
26
;
19
)
, without engaging in actions that delay or impede progress and agreement.

Fine-grained evaluation.

To provide a more detailed and interpretable evaluation, we introduce a fine-grained evaluation that detects 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors at the turn level. We derive 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors shown in Table
1
by adapting nine types of behaviors from evaluation taxonomies in dialogue generation
(
11
;
15
)
, and we added “persistent disagreement” that reflects collaborativeness in negotiation and debates
(
30
;
14
)
. For each category, the LLM-as-a-Judge is instructed to detect turns that exhibit the corresponding behaviors and count the number of occurrences in each conversation.

Agreement between human evaluation and LLM-as-a-Judge.

We collect human evaluations with two annotators to assess agreement with the LLM-as-a-Judge. The human annotators receive the same instructions used for the LLM-as-a-Judge and perform manual annotation. We evaluate OpenAI o4-mini
(
22
)
and Claude Opus 4
(
3
)
, and select o4-mini because it shows higher agreement with human evaluation. For
overall evaluation
, the two annotators evaluate the overall consistency and collaborativeness of 54 conversations generated by humans and GPT-4.1 on a 1-10 scale. The average Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between human scores and LLM-as-a-Judge scores using o4-mini are 0.301 for consistency and 0.380 for collaborativeness. These values are comparable to the human-human agreement of 0.281 and 0.268, respectively. For
fine-grained evaluation
, the two annotators detect the 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors at the turn level across 1,080 turns from 36 conversations generated by humans and GPT-4.1. In the turn-level binary detection setting, the average agreement between human evaluation and o4-mini is 0.253 in Cohen’s kappa and 0.275 in Matthews correlation coefficient. These values are comparable to the human-human agreement of 0.174 and 0.186.

3.2
CoCoEvalBench

We introduce the
CoCoEvalBench
benchmark for evaluating LLMs on the conversation continuation task shown in Figure
2
. The benchmark covers real-world scenarios spanning business, academic, and governmental meetings, as well as debates.

Evaluation task.

The conversation continuation task in
CoCoEvalBench
requires LLMs to generate continuations that follow the provided conversational metadata and history. The input consists of metadata
M
containing the conversation goals and participant information, the 30 preceding turns
x=[x_{-30},\ldots,x_{-1}]
, and a paragraph-length summary
S
of the conversation prior to
x
. Each turn in
x
is represented as a speaker-utterance pair
x_{i}=(p_{i},u_{i})
. Given this input, LLMs are instructed to generate 30 continuation turns
y=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{30}]\sim\mathrm{LLM}(M,S,x)
, where each turn is a pair of a speaker and an utterance. To assess the simulation of long-term dynamics in human conversations, our setup generates 30-turn continuations, a larger number of turns than in most prior work
(
36
;
4
)
.
Our benchmark includes a human-generated reference continuation
r=[r_{1},\ldots,r_{30}]
to support direct comparison between LLMs and humans. In this setup, both the simulated continuation
y
and the human reference continuation
r
follow the same context
(M,S,x)
and are expected to reflect the frequencies and distributions of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors observed in the conversation history
x
. This design facilitates comparison between human- and LLM-generated conversations, as substantial deviations in these behaviors indicate simulation failures.

4
Evaluation Setup

We evaluate LLMs in the conversation continuation task under multiple settings to examine factors that influence the inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in simulated conversations.

4.1
Simulation Settings

Prompt.

Our prompts include an instruction in addition to the conversational context
(M,S,x)
. We evaluate two types of instructions for conversation simulation to assess the influence of prompt engineering. First, in
vanilla prompting
, we instruct LLMs to generate
“human-like conversations by referring to the conversation history”
and
“mimic the communication style and content in the metadata and the conversation history.”
Second, in
taxonomy-guided prompting
, we explicitly instruct LLMs to incorporate inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors with frequencies and distributions comparable to those observed in the conversation history, and provide the list of the 10 behavior types in Table
1
as follows:

Human conversations may involve inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors […] The following list outlines representative inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors: […]

Generating multiple turns per LLM call.

When simulating conversations with LLMs, generating multiple consecutive turns in a single call is computationally efficient. However, generating one turn at a time better reflects the turn-by-turn nature of human conversations
(
35
)
. To examine the impact of these generation strategies, we evaluate settings that generate 1, 5, and 30 turns per LLM call, as illustrated in Figure
3
.

Supervised fine-tuning is a standard approach for aligning LLMs for conversation simulation

;

. We fine-tune GPT-4.1 on human conversations from the training set of
CoCoEvalBench
. We fine-tune three models that generate 1, 5, and 30 turns per call, respectively.

5.2
Prompt Engineering

The overall evaluation in Table
3
shows that taxonomy-guided prompting, which explicitly instructs LLMs to reproduce inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in human conversations, reduces overall consistency and collaborativeness relative to vanilla prompting. The effect is stronger when fewer turns are generated within a single LLM call, leading to conversations that exhibit much lower consistency and collaborativeness than human conversations. The fine-grained evaluation in Figure
4(e)
further shows that this prompt substantially increases the frequency of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors when generating a single turn per call, and that the resulting distributions differ substantially from those observed in human conversations. Although taxonomy-guided prompting does not instruct LLMs to increase these behaviors but rather to simulate their frequencies in human conversations, it nevertheless leads to overproduction of these behaviors.

Answer to RQ2 about Prompt Engineering

Prompt engineering does not provide reliable control over the frequencies or distributions of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations.

5.3
SFT on Human Conversations

The overall evaluation in Table
3
shows that GPT-4.1 fine-tuned on human conversations with SFT exhibits levels of consistency and collaborativeness comparable to those of human conversations, especially when generating one turn per call. However, the fine-grained evaluation in Figure
4(g)
shows that the frequencies and distributions of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors do not match those of human conversations. Specifically, fine-tuned GPT-4.1 overproduces repetition and redundancy, whereas human conversations display more diverse behavioral patterns. These results indicate that even when fine-tuned directly on human conversations via SFT, LLM-simulated conversations still deviate from human conversations.

Answer to RQ3 about SFT

SFT on human conversations can lead LLMs to overproduce a narrow subset of inconsistent behaviors, remaining misaligned with human social interaction, which exhibits a diverse range of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors.

5.4
Generating Multiple Turns Per LLM Call

The results in Table
3
and Figure
4
consistently show that generating multiple turns per LLM call, particularly 30 turns, introduces a strong bias toward reducing inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors. In particular, Figure
4(d)
shows that generating 30 turns in a single LLM call almost eliminates the effect of taxonomy-guided prompting, whereas it substantially increases inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors when one turn is generated in a single call. However, generating fewer turns per call also fails to reproduce the distribution of these behaviors observed in human conversations and instead tends to overrepresent repetitive and redundant utterances, even with the vanilla prompting, as shown in Figure
4(c)
.

Answer to RQ4 about Generation Strategy

The number of turns generated in a single LLM call strongly influences inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in simulated conversations, but adjusting this parameter alone does not reproduce the frequencies and distributions observed in human conversations.

6
Analysis and Discussion

Quantitative analysis.

To further analyze the results in Section
5
, we conduct a manual analysis of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations.
With
supervised fine-tuning
for GPT-4.1, the fine-grained evaluation in Figure
4(g)
shows a high frequency of repetitive and redundant utterances in generated conversations. Manual analysis suggests that this result reflects unnatural repetition in LLM-simulated conversations rather than human-like inconsistent behaviors. Table
4
presents an example generated by the fine-tuned GPT-4.1 (generating one turn per call) on QMSum Product, in which the same speaker repeatedly expresses agreement with prior utterances. This example suggests that SFT on human conversations can introduce unnatural repetitive behavior.
With
prompt engineering
, the fine-grained evaluation in Figure
4(e)
shows that taxonomy-guided prompting produces overly large numbers of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors when generating one turn per call. Manual analysis suggests that this result reflects behaviors that deviate from those observed in human conversations. Table
5
presents an example generated by GPT-4.1 with the taxonomy-guided prompt (generating one turn per call) on QMSum Product. The example exhibits persistent disagreement, with two speakers dominating the conversation despite the presence of multiple participants. This example illustrates the sensitivity of uncollaborative behaviors to prompt engineering, even though our prompt explicitly instructs
“to mimic the communication style and content in the metadata and the conversation history.”

Advantages of fine-grained evaluation.

The fine-grained evaluation in
CoCoEval
uses turn-level detection of 10 types of behaviors to provide a more detailed assessment than a common LLM-as-a-Judge approach that generates coarse conversation-level scores
(
36
;
29
)
. Our results highlight the advantages of our approach for analyzing differences between conversations generated by humans and LLMs. For example, the overall evaluation in Table
3
does not reveal differences between human- and LLM-generated conversations for fine-tuned GPT-4.1 when generating one turn per call, as it achieves scores comparable to those of human conversations. In contrast, the fine-grained evaluation in Figure
4(g)
reveals that fine-tuned GPT-4.1 overproduces repetition and redundancy in simulated conversations. These findings demonstrate the value of
CoCoEval
’s fine-grained evaluation and suggest that turn-level analysis with detailed behavior classification provides an effective method for comparing conversations generated by humans and LLMs.

7
Conclusion

We introduce
CoCoEval
, an evaluation framework for analyzing inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in LLM-simulated conversations. Our analysis shows that LLMs with vanilla prompting exhibit far fewer inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors than human conversations, and that neither prompt engineering nor supervised fine-tuning reliably controls these behaviors. These results highlight the challenge of simulating human conversations, particularly in reproducing their inherent inconsistency and uncollaborativeness.

Limitations

Dataset diversity.

Our analysis is constrained by the limited availability of publicly accessible datasets containing real-world multi-party conversations. Existing datasets are heavily skewed toward governmental meetings, while datasets from other domains such as business meetings remain scarce. Collecting datasets from a broader range of real-world settings would support the study of social interaction across more diverse scenarios.

Language scope.

Our study focuses on conversations in English.

Appendix B
LLM-as-a-Judge

This section provides details on the agreement between human evaluation and LLM-as-a-Judge in
CoCoEval
discussed in Section
3.1
.

B.1
Setup of Human Evaluation

For the evaluation of the agreement between human evaluation and the LLM-as-a-Judge in Section
3.1
, two PhD students working on NLP research conducted the human evaluation. They were provided with the same instructions as those used in the prompt for the LLM-as-a-Judge, and the annotators were not informed whether the conversations were generated by humans or by LLMs. The inputs
(M,S,x)
are randomly selected from the evaluation set of
CoCoEval
and evenly sampled from instances created from the six source datasets. They are informed about this study and provide consent for their annotations to be used.

Overall evaluation.

Two human annotators manually evaluate the overall consistency and collaborativeness of 54 conversation continuations generated by humans, GPT-4.1, and fine-tuned GPT-4.1, where LLMs generate 30 turns per call. They assign scores on a 1-10 scale by referring to the instructions and rubrics provided to LLM-as-a-Judge.

Fine-grained evaluation.

Two human annotators manually detect the 10 types of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in 1,080 turns from 36 human conversations and conversation continuations simulated by GPT-4.1 (generating 30 turns per call). The annotators provide the indices of turns that exhibit each type of behavior.

B.2
Model Selection for LLM-as-a-Judge

This paper uses o4-mini as the primary LLM-as-a-Judge model, as it shows reasonable alignment with human evaluation. We also evaluate Claude Opus 4 as an LLM-as-a-Judge, but select o4-mini due to the misalignment between Claude Opus 4 and human evaluation. For overall evaluation, the average Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between Claude Opus 4 and human scores are -0.004 for consistency and 0.171 for collaborativeness. For fine-grained evaluation, the average agreement between Claude Opus 4 and human evaluation is 0.121 in Cohen’s kappa and 0.093 in Matthews correlation coefficient. In both evaluations, agreement between Claude Opus 4 and human evaluation is substantially lower than both human-human agreement and the agreement between human evaluation and o4-mini. Specifically, the overall evaluation by Claude Opus 4 follows a different trend from that of human evaluation and o4-mini, tending to be more sensitive to repetition and redundancy and assigning lower scores to conversations generated with one turn per call and fine-tuned GPT-4.1. In the fine-grained evaluation, Claude Opus 4 often detects inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors more aggressively, showing recall against human detection comparable to o4-mini (30.5 by Claude Opus 4 and 42.7 by o4-mini) but with lower precision (7.1 by Claude Opus 4 and 20.2 by o4-mini).

B.3
Details of Human-LLM Agreement

Table
7
presents detailed results on the agreement between human evaluation and the LLM-as-a-Judge using o4-mini for the fine-grained evaluation. The overall values in the last row are computed by concatenating the binary detection results across all categories. The results show that the number of inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors identified by the o4-mini largely follows the trends observed in the human evaluation, although o4-mini appears more sensitive in detecting these behaviors and often identifies more occurrences than human annotators.

B.4
Coverage of Fine-grained Evaluation

To verify that the 10 types of behaviors used in the fine-grained evaluation of
CoCoEval
cover most inconsistent and uncollaborative behaviors in human conversations, we instructed the human annotators to identify any behaviors not captured by these categories when conducting human evaluation for the fine-grained evaluation. In the reviewed conversations, the annotators did not identify any behaviors outside the 10 types of behaviors.
