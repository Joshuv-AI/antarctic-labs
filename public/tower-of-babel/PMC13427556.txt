Genomics in containment: BSL-4-compatible workflows enable high-resolution genomic and transcriptomic analyses of Risk Group 4 viruses
Authors: Lirong Peng, Shawn Hirsch, Andrew P Platt, David Drawbaugh, Gregory Kocher, Michael R Holbrook, Monika Mehta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryGenomic research on high-consequence Risk Group 4 (RG4) viruses is significantly hindered by the stringent biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) containment requirements, because virus inactivation prior to removal from containment often compromises nucleic acid integrity. As a focused solution to retain nucleic acid integrity as well as safety compliance, we developed and validated a comprehensive suite of optimized workflows compatible with BSL-4 containment and next-generation sequencing applications. We systematically assessed inactivation, homogenization, and RNA extraction methods to establish robust bulk RNA sequencing workflows for diverse sample types. We also adapted state-of-the-art single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) by integrating inactivation steps without compromising data quality. To demonstrate feasibility, we conducted scRNA-seq analysis on cells exposed to Lassa virus. Additionally, we optimized viral genome enrichment strategies for high-depth sequencing of multiple RG4 viruses. These validated workflows enable safe, reproducible, and high-resolution genomic analyses of RG4 pathogens, significantly expanding the experimental toolkit for BSL-4 research.Keywords: BSL-4, risk group 4 viruses, virus inactivation, next-generation sequencing, transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, fixed RNA profiling, single-cell immune profiling, viral genome sequencing, high-containment

Introduction

Although infectious diseases have existed since the dawn of civilization, their impact on human mortality has decreased significantly due to the availability of effective medical countermeasures and diagnostic methods developed through the systematic study of pathogens and their interaction with host physiology. Key scientific and technological advancements in the last two decades, such as high-throughput next-generation sequencing (NGS) and accessible data analysis tools, have revolutionized infectious disease research, offering powerful methods to investigate viral evolution, transmission dynamics, and host transcriptomic and immune responses at high resolution.1,2 While the development of numerous medical countermeasures has helped to curtail the mortality and morbidity caused by a variety of pathogens, the emergence and re-emergence of high-consequence pathogens, particularly Risk Group 4 (RG4) viruses (such as Lassa virus [LASV], Ebola virus [EBOV], and Nipah virus [NiV]), pose a significant and ongoing threat to global health.3

A major operational and technical barrier to applying many genomic tools to RG4 viruses is the absolute requirement for complete virus inactivation before samples can be removed from biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) laboratories. The chemical methods used to render samples non-infectious,4,5,6 such as treatment with guanidine-based chaotropic salts7 or cross-linking with paraformaldehyde (PFA),8 are often harsh. These treatments can degrade or chemically modify nucleic acids, compromising the integrity of genetic material, especially RNA, which is essential for sensitive downstream applications such as the sequence analysis of enveloped RNA viruses, which comprise the majority of RG4 viruses.

Additionally, different sample types (e.g., cell culture, blood cells, complex tissues) and diverse sequencing applications require distinct and optimized preparation protocols. For instance, host transcriptomic analysis requires high-quality, intact RNA, whereas commonly used high-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) workflows necessitate intact outer cell membranes until the genetic material can be barcoded.9,10 While single-cell studies from high-containment samples have been reported, they have either involved conducting the entire workflow in a BSL-4 laboratory and inactivating the final library11 or using less commonly used methods for single-cell sequencing12 or inactivation methods that may not be broadly applicable across virus families.13,14

Enrichment of viral genomes from a complex background of host nucleic acids—a prerequisite for high-confidence viral genomic analysis—relies on either targeted amplification15,16,17 or probe-based capture,18,19,20 which in turn depend on targeting reagents (primers and probes) efficiently binding to unique viral sequences in the inactivated samples. Although sequence-independent universal methods have been developed,21 targeted enrichment remains the most widely used method for generating high-depth virus sequence data.

Though various sequencing and data analysis strategies have been reviewed earlier,22,23 information regarding broadly applicable sample preparation methods for high-containment samples is not readily available. Several protocols for sample inactivation and NGS exist; however, there is a lack of systematically evaluated workflows specifically tailored to the unique constraints of BSL-4 environments and the diverse range of samples and genomic applications relevant to studying RG4 pathogens and their associated disease pathogeneses.

This study addresses this critical gap by methodically optimizing and validating a suite of sample preparation protocols to bridge the gap between BSL-4 safety requirements and the technical demands of multiple NGS applications. We compared multiple well-established virus inactivation reagents (TRIzol LS, AVL buffer plus ethanol [AVL+EtOH], RLT buffer plus EtOH [RLT+EtOH], PFA), tissue homogenization techniques (mechanical bead-beating and enzymatic dissociation), and RNA extraction methods (various manual and automated kits) to develop robust workflows for bulk RNA sequencing. The suitability of these optimized protocols was validated across a variety of clinically relevant sample types, including cell lines, mammalian blood and tissues, and virus stocks. We further adapted and validated state-of-the-art single-cell technologies, including 10× Genomics Flex gene expression (fixed RNA profiling) for single-cell analysis of tissues and 5′ single-cell immune profiling (with T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR) sequencing and cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing [CITE-seq]), incorporating inactivation steps to ensure BSL-4 compatibility. Finally, we optimized methods for viral genome enrichment and both long- and short-read sequencing for multiple RG4 viruses. The resulting validated protocols provide a critical resource to the scientific community, enabling the safe and effective application of powerful genomic tools to investigate the pathogenesis of high-consequence viral diseases at high resolution.

Results

Optimization for host transcriptomic analysis: Sample inactivation and RNA extraction from cells

To establish robust protocols for transcriptomic analysis of samples from high-containment settings, we started with the optimization of methods for virus inactivation and RNA extraction from both cell lines and primary blood cells.

Inactivation with AVL+EtOH or RLT+EtOH yields high-quality and high-concentration RNA from cell lines

We compared four common methods for inactivating high-containment enveloped RNA viruses, using one million Vero E6 cells for each treatment (Figure S1A). After inactivation and RNA extraction, we quantified the RNA and assessed its integrity. Our initial comparison showed that the guanidine-thiocyanate-based inactivation agents AVL+EtOH and RLT+EtOH yielded the highest RNA concentrations among the conditions tested in this screen (Figure 1A), with greater than 87% of RNA fragments above 200 bp (DV200) and RNA integrity number (RIN) equivalent values above 7 (Figures 1A and S2A). TRIzol LS treatment also produced high-quality RNA, with approximately 2-fold lower yield than AVL+EtOH and RLT+EtOH in this experiment. In contrast, samples inactivated with 4% PFA using our standard RNA extraction protocol yielded very little RNA (>80-fold less than the other three methods), prompting a separate optimization for this common fixative.

Prolonged PFA treatment is compatible with optimized RNA extraction

We were concerned that the 16-h PFA treatment time required in our standard operating procedures for validated virus inactivation might degrade RNA. To investigate this, we treated Vero E6 cells with 4% PFA for 1, 2, or 16 h and tested two different de-cross-linking conditions during RNA extraction (Figure S1A). In this experiment, the 16-h PFA treatment did not have any obvious detrimental effect on the quality or quantity of the extracted RNA (Figure 1B). For the 16-h treatment, de-cross-linking condition “A” (56°C for 1 h24) resulted in a higher RNA yield and quality compared to condition “B” (56°C for 15 min, followed by 80°C for another 15 min).

We further compared the performance of the Promega SV kit against the Qiagen miRNeasy kit for extracting RNA from PFA-treated cells. Although RNA extracted with both kits showed high intactness in this screen, the Qiagen miRNeasy kit yielded more RNA than the Promega SV kit at all three PFA treatment time points (Figure 1C). For the 16-h PFA treatment, the Qiagen miRNeasy extraction yielded the most intact RNA, with a DV200 score approaching 80%, confirming the robustness of the protocol for fixed samples.

PBMC isolation yields the most intact RNA from inactivated blood samples

To identify the best method for analyzing blood samples, we processed nonhuman primate (NHP) blood in three different ways: frozen whole blood, blood cells after red blood cell (RBC) depletion, and isolated PBMCs. All of these processed samples were treated with TRIzol LS to mimic virus inactivation. We then extracted RNA using six different methods (Figure S1B). Included were five manual RNA extraction preparations (SV Total RNA Isolation System [Promega], QIAamp RNA Blood Mini Kit [Qiagen], Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit [New England Biolabs], PureLink RNA Mini Kit [Invitrogen], Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kit [Zymo Research]) and one automated extraction platform (KingFisher Flex purification system with MagMAX mirVana Total RNA Isolation kit with or without an additional cleanup using the Clean and Concentrator-25 kit [Zymo Research]), though not every extraction method was used for each sample type. The quantitative and qualitative analyses (Figure 1D; Figure S2B) showed that, although RNA yield and integrity varied by extraction method, all three sample preparation types (whole blood, blood after RBC depletion, and PBMCs) yielded usable RNA in this screen. RNA yields in Figure 1D are normalized to a 1 mL whole-blood equivalent for each preparation, allowing direct comparison across whole blood, RBC-depleted blood, and PBMC samples. RNA extracted from PBMCs tended to be more intact, with DV200 scores over 50% and RIN values greater than 5 for most extraction methods, while whole blood and RBC-depleted samples often produced higher yields with slightly lower integrity. These observations suggest that PBMC isolation may be advantageous in this context when RNA integrity is the primary consideration for transcriptomic analysis.

Invitrogen PureLink, Promega Total RNA, and QIAamp RNA Blood kits outperformed the rest for all three sample types for RNA intactness and performed roughly the same as the rest for RNA yield (Figure 1D; Figure S2B). While the KingFisher-based automated extraction resulted in RNA yield as high as the highest-performing manual extractions, some loss in RNA intactness was observed for the whole blood samples (the only sample type for which a parallel comparison was made).

Optimization for host transcriptomic analysis: Sample inactivation, homogenization, and RNA extraction from tissues

We next optimized protocols for complex solid tissues, focusing on homogenization, inactivation, and RNA extraction from NHP lung and brain samples. First, using TRIzol-LS-treated lung tissue samples, we evaluated different homogenization and extraction methods. We compared mechanical bead-beating (two versions) with an enzymatic-and-mechanical approach using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator, with and without pre-treatment with RNAlater-ICE (Figure S3). In this method-development screen, we observed an apparent trade-off between yield and integrity: bead-beating workflow version 1 (BBv1) yielded a higher quantity of RNA, while the second version (BBv2) and the gentleMACS platform produced more intact RNA, as assessed by DV200 (Figures 2A and S2C). Addition of RNAlater-ICE led to a slight increase in the DV200 score for all samples, while it either did not affect or resulted in a small decrease in the RNA yield.

We also compared three manual RNA extraction kits (PureLink RNA Mini [Invitrogen], RNeasy Plus Mini [Qiagen] and Direct-zol RNA Miniprep), and in this screen, the Invitrogen PureLink RNA Mini kit resulted in the most intact RNA across all homogenization methods tested (Figure 2A). We then compared this top-performing manual kit to two automated extraction methods on the KingFisher Flex system (Thermo Fisher): MagMAX mirVana Total RNA isolation kit (Thermo Fisher) and Direct-zol-96 MagBead RNA kit (Zymo Research), each with and without pre-treatment with RNAlater-ICE. As seen in Figures 2B and S2D, automated extraction using the KingFisher Flex system with the MagMAX mirVana kit, after using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator or bead-beating workflow version 3 (BBv3), generated RNA with DV200 > 80% and substantial yields. Addition of RNAlater-ICE to the workflow improved the yield for the BBv3 and gentleMACS homogenates and increased the RIN value for the gentleMACS sample (Figures 2B and S2D).

Next, we compared the inactivation methods using NHP brain tissue samples. The tissue was homogenized using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator and treated with either AVL+EtOH or TRIzol LS, followed by automated RNA extraction. As shown in Figure 2C, while both methods yielded high-quality RNA (DV200 > 80%), the RNA yield was about 10-fold higher after AVL+EtOH treatment compared to TRIzol LS treatment. Addition of RNAlater-ICE to the tissue sample before homogenization led to improved quality of the RNA but decreased the yield of the TRIzol-LS-treated sample.

Guided by these exploratory results, we further optimized protocols for AVL+EtOH-inactivated lung tissues. We compared homogenization by BBv3 versus the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator, followed by automated RNA extraction. RNA extracted using both homogenization methods showed very high integrity (DV200 > 95%; Figure 2D). The gentleMACS Octo Dissociator yielded more RNA than BBv3 in this experiment; thus, we used the gentleMACS-AVL+EtOH workflow for subsequent tissue analyses.

Optimization of RNA extraction from virus stocks

We compared an automated RNA extraction method using the MagMAX Viral/Pathogen II (MVPII) nucleic acid isolation kit (Thermo Fisher) and the KingFisher Flex system to a manual RNA extraction method using the PureLink RNA Mini kit for TRIzol-LS-inactivated lysates of LASV-infected cells for four biological replicates. As seen in Figure 3A, the automated KingFisher Flex system produced RNA of higher quality, as measured by DV200 (p = 0.011 for a two-tailed paired t test). The total yield of RNA was similar (no statistically significant difference) for both the automated and manual extraction using the PureLink kit.

When comparing two different automated extraction kits (MVPII and the MirVana RNA extraction kits on the KingFisher Flex system) for TRIzol-LS-inactivated lysates of cells (n = 17) infected with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), we found that, while both performed well, the MVPII kit produced RNA with slightly, yet statistically significantly better DV200 and RIN values (Figures 3B and S2E), with p values of 0.001 for DV200 and 0.0001 for RIN using two-tailed paired t-tests.

To further enhance RNA recovery, we optimized the KingFisher Flex system for TRIzol-LS-inactivated lysates from lung tissue samples from SARS-CoV-2-exposed hamsters. We compared the use of plates with 96 or 24 deep wells and performed multiple bead-binding steps (sample loadings). Because the lysates from different animals showed a wide range in quality, we used multiple sets of samples that had all undergone TRIzol LS treatment, with the number of biological replicates per set ranging from 13 to 27. We observed a small improvement with the larger well format in a subset of samples (Figure 3C). One of the sets (BA.2; two sample loads in 96-well plates vs. two loads in 24-well plates) had significantly improved normalized yield (yield per 100 μL of input) when using the 24-well plate compared to the 96-well plate (p value = 0.0206; two-tailed paired t test); however, there was no significant difference in yield for the other sets. Similarly, one of the sets (Iota; three sample loads in 96-well plates vs. two loads in 24-well plates) had significantly improved DV200 scores when using a 24-well plate compared to a 96-well plate (p value = 0.0003; two-tailed paired t test) with no significant difference for the other sets. While evaluating the effect of multiple loadings with the 24-well plates, we observed an increase in the normalized yield with two loadings, though there was no significant difference in quality between the two workflows (Figure 3D).

It should be noted here that while SARS-CoV-2 is an RG3 pathogen, it was used as a practical surrogate for testing and optimizing workflows that are relevant to multiple high-containment viruses.

Optimization for host transcriptomic analysis using single-cell RNA sequencing

We successfully modified the GEM-X Flex gene expression analysis method from 10× Genomics for generating single-cell gene expression profiling data to make it compatible with BSL-4 biosafety procedures. We incorporated an additional 72 h of treatment with 4% PFA to mimic the conditions required in our facility for the inactivation of enveloped RNA viruses. The modified workflow highlighting the steps to be performed in a BSL-4 laboratory is shown in Figure 4A.

For the lung samples, the extended PFA treatment led to an improvement in most quality-control (QC) metrics (higher number of cells captured, genes and transcripts detected per cell, and higher proportion of confidently mapped reads in cells; Table 1). A similar effect was not observed for the liver samples. This difference could be due to a tissue-specific effect of the PFA treatment, or to variation in the number of reads per sample, with the more deeply sequenced samples (liver at 24 h and lung at 72 h) showing better metrics than their counterparts.

Despite the differences in QC metrics, both protocols generated biologically consistent data. Cell cluster annotation based on gene signatures indicated that all cell types present in the hamster lung reference sample were identified in the query samples (Figure 4C). Additionally, a high degree of overlap was seen in the transcriptional profiles of cells, with similar proportions of cell types identified in the 24-h samples and 72-h samples for both liver and lung tissues (Figures 4C–4E). This work validated a workflow with an RG4 virus inactivation step for single-cell gene expression profiling of tissue samples.

Optimization for host transcriptomic analysis and immune profiling using 5′ single-cell RNA sequencing

We modified the 10× Genomics 5′ Gene Expression workflow by incorporating a virus inactivation step to enable immune analysis of samples originating in high-containment laboratories (Figure S7A). The modified workflow treated amplified complementary DNA (cDNA) with guanidine-based chaotropic salts (TRIzol LS or AVL+EtOH) to mimic the virus inactivation step. We conducted several pilot experiments to optimize the workflow and to test various single-cell applications.

A virus inactivation step using AVL+EtOH does not negatively impact scRNA-seq data quality

We used cells from an established cell line (Vero E6, a line of green monkey kidney cells) and primary cells (PBMCs isolated from human blood) for the workflow optimization. A standard 10× Genomics workflow (for 5′ single-cell sequencing library generation) was followed to generate barcoded cDNA. The amplified cDNA was divided into three equal parts, with one part left untreated and the other two treated with TRIzol LS or AVL+EtOH, respectively. The cDNA recovered after the TRIzol LS treatment was insufficient for library preparation. The untreated control and AVL+EtOH-treated cDNA aliquots were used for sequencing library preparation for gene expression analysis in accordance with the 10× Genomics workflow.

The highly similar QC metrics (Table 2, sample numbers 1–4), and nearly identical curves on the barcode rank plots for untreated and AVL+EtOH-treated fractions of both samples (Vero E6 cells and PBMCs; Figure S8A, top row) indicated no obvious degradation of the samples during AVL+EtOH treatment. Additionally, level 1 and 2 cell type annotation using Seurat tools identified all expected cell populations in similar proportions in both treated and untreated PBMC samples (Figure 5A; Table 3). An overlay of the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for untreated and AVL+EtOH-treated PBMCs showed the two samples to be practically indistinguishable (Figures 5A(iii)). Heatmaps showing expression levels of top differentially expressed genes for all four samples are shown in Figures S7B–S7E. Thus, we demonstrated that AVL+EtOH treatment of amplified barcoded cDNA is an effective method for virus inactivation for single-cell sequencing of high-containment samples.

The inactivation workflow is suitable for both freshly frozen and long-term cryopreserved PBMCs

We tested the effect of AVL+EtOH inactivation on single-cell transcriptome data from stored PBMCs by comparing human PBMC samples cryopreserved and stored for approximately 1 month (“new”) to those that had been in liquid nitrogen storage for approximately 3 years (“old”). The workflow described earlier (Figure S7A) was followed for scRNA-seq library preparation for gene expression analysis, along with sequencing of the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) that spans the junction between the variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) gene segments (V(D)J junction) for TCR and BCR repertoires for both new and old PBMC samples.

Highly similar QC metrics (Table 2, sample numbers 5 and 6) and curves on the barcode rank plots for new and old frozen PBMCs (Figures S8A, second row) indicated the suitability of PBMC samples cryopreserved for up to 3 years for single-cell sequencing after incorporating AVL+EtOH treatment for the virus inactivation step. Furthermore, all expected cell populations were identified in both the new and the old AVL+EtOH-treated PBMC samples (Figures 5A(vi)), with a complete overlap of the UMAP plots for the two samples (Figures 5A(V)). Highly similar proportions of the different populations (Figure 5B) further demonstrated that the gene expression profiles of the stored PBMCs did not show any significant change from the recently frozen samples.

Both new and old samples were also used to sequence the TCRs and BCRs. While the barcode rank plots for the new and the old PBMC samples were very similar (Figure S8A), there were some minor differences in the overall QC metrics (Table 4; Table S2). Use of the older samples resulted in the capture of more cells (and thus a higher number of cells expressing TCR and BCR transcripts, and more cells with productive rearrangements of the TCRs and BCRs), while the newer samples had a higher proportion of cells with productive V(D)J spanning pairs. A list of the top 10 clonotype CDR3 sequences for TCRs and BCRs showed expected distribution from “normal” human blood, with many common clonotypes detected in both the new and old PBMCs (Tables 3 and S4). Thus, we concluded that the AVL+EtOH treatment does not interfere with single-cell immune repertoire analysis. Additionally, older PBMCs that had been cryopreserved for approximately 3 years did not show any significant deterioration in data quality, as seen by single-cell gene expression analysis and TCR and BCR sequencing, though a slight decrease in the proportion of cells with productive V(D)J rearrangements was observed.

CITE-seq and cell hashing are compatible with the inactivation workflow

To determine if cell surface protein analysis (CITE-seq) could be integrated into the workflow with inactivation, we generated a panel of oligo-tagged anti-human antibody clones with reported cross-reactivity to NHP PBMCs (Table S5) and tested it with human and NHP PBMC samples. Two pilot experiments were performed with individual antibodies at different concentrations, with two antibody cocktails tested in pilot 2 (Table S6). Additionally, the human PBMCs were multiplexed using TotalSeq-C hashtag antibodies (BioLegend) to test compatibility with cell hashing. Because no tagged version of anti-CD3 antibody with cross-reactivity to NHP PBMCs was commercially available, we prepared an oligo-tagged version of mouse anti-human CD3 Clone SP34-2 (Figure S9C) and added it to the pilot 2 antibody cocktail 2b-high (Table S6).

All samples were processed using the AVL+EtOH inactivation workflow (Figure S7A). QC metrics (Table 2, sample numbers 7–10; Table 5) and barcode rank plots (Figure S8A) showed that the addition of antibody staining and hashing steps did not compromise the quality of the resulting gene expression libraries for human PBMCs. Additionally, the NHP PBMC sample showed a similar profile to that of the human PBMCs (Figure S8A).

Proof of principle: Single-cell analysis of LASV-infected cells

Finally, we applied the fully optimized workflow (Figure S7A) in a proof-of-principle experiment to analyze LASV-infected Vero E6 cells inside a BSL-4 laboratory. As the LASV transcripts are not polyadenylated, LASV-specific primers (Table S8) were added to the reverse transcription mix for efficient capture of LASV transcripts and identification of LASV-infected cells. To assess the impact of cDNA amplification, one aliquot of cDNA was amplified for the standard 12 cycles for 2,000–6,000 targeted cells (“Vero-1” sample), while a second aliquot underwent five additional cycles (“Vero-2” sample). The amplified cDNA for both samples was inactivated using AVL+EtOH before removal from the high-containment laboratory for the preparation of single-cell sequencing libraries for gene expression analysis.

Barcode rank plots showed good quality for both samples (Figure S8A, last row). The Vero-2 sample, which had undergone additional cDNA amplification, showed improved QC metrics, including a higher proportion of reads mapped to host genome and transcriptome (80.5 vs. 46.2% and 47.4 vs. 23.1%), a greater median number of genes and transcripts detected per cell (276 vs. 166 and 328 vs. 189) and a higher total number of genes detected (14,348 vs. 13,400) (Table S9). Of the total reads, 2–5.5% mapped to the LASV genome, with a median of 60 and 24 virus transcripts detected per cell in the Vero-2 and Vero-1 samples, respectively. All four viral genes were detected in both samples.

Gene expression analysis identified clusters of cells with differentially expressed genes for both samples (Figure 6A). Based on the proportion of viral transcripts (glycoprotein precursor and nucleocapsid protein), cluster 1 in Vero-1 and cluster 4 in Vero-2 were identified as being high in virus gene expression and, thus, likely to contain the virus-infected cells, while cluster 3 in Vero-1 and cluster 2 in Vero-2 were identified as having low virus gene expression, likely to consist mainly of uninfected cells (Figure 6). Expression levels of the LASV large-segment transcripts, RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, and the Z protein were found to be much lower than the small segment. Cell clusters with a high viral load showed increased expression of multiple host genes, including FTL, RPS12, RPLP1, and CRYAB. In contrast, clusters with low viral load showed higher expression of host genes, including CST3, ACTB, SPP1, and LOC119619962 (Figure 6B).

Optimization of virus enrichment for viral genome sequencing

We tested and optimized two widely used methods for enriching viral genomic material from inactivated samples: amplification using custom primer panels and capture using a commercial probe panel.

Amplification-based enrichment yields long amplicons suitable for genome sequencing

To optimize the generation of long amplicons from inactivated viral RNA, we compared different reverse transcriptase (RT) and DNA polymerase enzymes by amplifying the 4.3-kb SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) gene (Figure 7A). The SARS-CoV-2 variants used included Washington 01 (WA-01), Alpha (B.1.1.7), Delta (B.1.617), and Omicron BA1 (B.1.1.529). We inactivated infected cell lysates using TRIzol LS and extracted RNA using an automated system as described earlier (Figure 3B). We tested two RT enzymes—SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis system (Thermo Fisher) and qScript Ultra Flex (Quantabio), along with three amplification mixes with different DNA polymerase enzymes—repliQa HiFi ToughMix (Quantabio), Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (New England Biolabs), and Platinum SuperFi II PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher).

When comparing the three DNA polymerase systems, the repliQa polymerization system most consistently produced the target amplicon with the fewest off-target products and worked well with both RT enzymes tested (Figure 7A). Both the RT enzymes tested (SuperScript IV and qScript Ultra Flex) performed similarly for the Q5 and repliQa systems; however, the Platinum SuperFi system only worked with the SuperScript IV system.

To further ensure the suitability of these 4.3-kb amplicons for long-read sequencing, nanopore libraries were generated using the amplicons from the repliQa system and sequenced on a MinION device. The resulting long-read data showed high alignment accuracy to the reference sequence, a mode read length of 4.3 kb, and average quality scores with a range of 11.5–13.5 (Figures 7B and S10).

Building on this optimization, we designed primer panels to amplify the entire genomes of multiple high-containment viruses (including LASV, Rift Valley fever virus [RVFV], and others) as a series of long, overlapping amplicons (1.5–3.5 kb) (Tables 6 and S10). These primer panels successfully generated the expected amplicons for RVFV (Figure 7C), LASV (Figure 7D), and other viruses listed in Table 6. Subsequent short-read sequencing of libraries prepared using these amplicons resulted in reasonably even sequence coverage across the viral genomes for all viruses tested (Table 6; Figure S11).

Probe-capture-based enrichment yields complete genomes for most tested viruses

As an alternative to amplification, we tested a commercial probe panel (Illumina Viral Surveillance Panel v1) for its ability to enrich genomes from multiple strains of eight high-containment viruses. The probe capture method successfully yielded full genome sequences with reasonably even coverage for all tested strains of Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV), EBOV, LASV, NiV, RVFV, and SARS-CoV-2 (Table 6; Figure S11). It is noteworthy that the panel was effective in generating good-quality genome assemblies across a wide range of input RNA amounts (Table 6).

However, this panel was less effective for other viruses. Sequencing libraries generated from probe-captured material showed significant gaps in the genomes of three yellow fever virus (YFV) strains and two mpox virus (MPXV) strains. The panel failed to capture sufficient genomic material for a successful genome assembly of YFV strain BA 55. This reduced performance for specific samples is likely related to the probe set design. Because the exact probe sequences and target regions are proprietary, we could not directly assess probe-genome mismatch. The YFV and MPXV strains/isolates used in this study were likely different from the ones used for the optimization of the panel by the manufacturer. Even modest sequence divergence or gaps in probe coverage can substantially reduce hybrid-capture efficiency, leading to the incomplete and uneven genome coverage observed here. The manufacturer has since released a newer version (v2) of the panel that includes many more viruses and strains and may result in better coverage of these strains. Future work will include testing the newer version of the probe panel with more viruses and strains.

The sequence coverage plots in Figure S11 illustrate the performance of both amplification and probe-based enrichment for EBOV, LASV, and NiV genome sequencing. Our results demonstrate that both amplification-based and probe-capture-based virus enrichment methods are effective for most viruses tested, offering validated options for viral genomic surveillance.

Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of sample preparation workflows for genomic and transcriptomic analysis of RG4 enveloped RNA viruses and their associated disease pathogeneses, addressing the central challenge of balancing stringent biosafety requirements with the need for high-quality nucleic acids for NGS. This work presents a validated toolkit of optimized protocols that enable a wide range of NGS applications, from bulk tissue transcriptomics to single-cell immune profiling and viral genomics, using samples originating from high-containment laboratories. A key finding of our work is that there is no single universally optimal protocol; instead, the ideal workflow is highly dependent on both the sample type and the intended downstream application.

A critical early step when working with samples generated in high-containment laboratories is virus inactivation, which enables safe handling at lower biosafety levels and facilitates sample manipulations that would otherwise be difficult in the restrictive environment of BSL-4 laboratories. We tested the chemical cross-linking agent PFA and guanidine-thiocyanate-based compounds that have been shown by various groups to inactivate multiple families of enveloped RNA viruses4,6,8,25,26,27 using previously published inactivation workflows6,7,8,28 that were extensively tested and validated in our facility. The chaotropic properties of guanidine-thiocyanate-based compounds (e.g., TRIzol LS, AVL, and RLT) lead to the inactivation of nucleases, disruption of cell and viral membranes, and denaturation of proteins, thereby contributing to their widespread use in nucleic acid extraction and virus inactivation workflows. Since RLT and AVL have been shown to result in incomplete inactivation under certain conditions when used without an additive such as ethanol or Triton X-100,5,26,27,29 we used RLT and AVL only in combination with ethanol, as per established protocols.26,28 Our motivation to include PFA, known to preserve cell structures, was its recent use in scRNA-seq workflows.7

In concordance with previously published studies,30,31,32 our tests indicated that guanidine-based inactivation reagents, particularly AVL+EtOH, tended to provide a favorable balance of RNA yield and integrity across the sample types examined, which is aligned with their function as potent RNase denaturants that preserve nucleic acid structure (Figure 1A). While TRIzol LS treatment also resulted in high-quality RNA, the yield was lower than the other reagents tested (Figure 1A). Conversely, while 4% PFA initially appeared suboptimal due to RNA degradation, we demonstrated that pairing a 16-h PFA treatment (required in our facility for the inactivation of some virus-containing samples) with an optimized de-cross-linking and extraction method preserved RNA quality and considerably improved RNA yield in our experiments (Figure 1). This finding is critical for studies requiring cellular or tissue architecture preservation and enables workflows such as single-cell sequencing using fixed cells and spatial transcriptomics for high-containment samples.

Our investigation extended to complex primary samples, such as blood and tissues, which are vital for understanding systemic host responses. For blood, we confirmed that while most extraction methods produced sufficient RNA from all sample types, isolating PBMCs prior to lysis yielded RNA with the highest integrity (as measured by DV200 and RIN values) in our tests, likely due to more efficient removal of inhibitors and RNases present in whole blood (Figure 1D). This is a key consideration for studies focused on immune cell transcriptomics, where preserving RNA quality is paramount.

For solid tissues, our optimization screens suggested a trade-off for the choice of homogenization method: bead-beating tended to maximize RNA yield, while gentler mechanical-enzymatic disruption using the gentleMACS platform better preserved RNA integrity (Figure 2). RNAlater-ICE provided modest but consistent improvements in RNA integrity in these tests, suggesting its utility in preserving samples collected in the field or during complex necropsies. AVL+EtOH again appeared highly effective as the inactivation reagent for clarified tissue homogenates, yielding RNA of exceptional quality (DV200 > 95%) and better yields than TRIzol LS in this comparison (Figure 2C). Integration of these optimized homogenization and extraction workflows with automated platforms, such as the KingFisher Flex system (Figures 2B, 3A, and 3B), facilitated high-throughput processing, which is necessary for large-scale animal studies and clinical sample cohorts. Our protocols for multiple loadings and larger-well formats (Figures 3C and 3D) offer practical guidance for maximizing RNA yield from limited or precious samples without sacrificing quality.

The adaptation of scRNA-seq for the BSL-4 environment by the incorporation of virus inactivation steps is a major advancement presented in this work. We modified the 10× Genomics Fixed RNA Profiling workflow, which utilizes samples fixed using 4% PFA for 16–24 h. Multiple studies have previously evaluated this assay and found the single-cell data from 4% PFA-fixed cells to be comparable to those from unfixed cells in various systems.33,34,35 Using the same assay, we established that an extended 72-h PFA treatment, sufficient to inactivate a broad range of viruses, was compatible with generating high-quality single-cell transcriptomic data from both lung and liver tissue samples, confidently identifying all expected cell populations (Figure 4; Tables 1 and S1). The successful application of a mouse transcriptome probe panel to hamster tissues also broadens the utility of this platform for studies involving non-standard animal models without having to use expensive custom probes. This success is particularly noteworthy as it opens the door to detailed cellular-level investigation of host responses to RG4 viruses from ex vivo tissues.

For 5′ scRNA-seq, which is essential for immune repertoire analysis, post-amplification inactivation of the barcoded cDNA using AVL+EtOH was a critical innovation. It did not compromise gene expression or cell type identification from human PBMCs, including samples cryopreserved and stored for more than 3 years (Figures 5 and S8A; Tables 2, 3, 4, S2, S3, and, S4). Treatment with TRIzol LS, on the other hand, led to a low yield of cDNA, likely due to a larger fraction of cDNA partitioned into the organic/interphase layers. For this study, we did not implement any additional steps to recover material from the interphase/organic phases, focusing instead on the aqueous phase to maintain a simpler, higher-throughput workflow compatible with BSL-4. Further experiments are required to optimize a workflow using TRIzol LS, if required under specific circumstances. Our workflow with AVL+EtOH treatment was compatible with CITE-seq and cell hashing, enabling simultaneous analysis of cell surface proteins and transcripts from human and NHP samples after a mock inactivation step (Figures 5 and S8; Table 5). We also designed and validated a panel of antibodies for immune phenotyping of NHP PBMCs (Table S5) that would be especially helpful for correlation between flow cytometry and scRNA-seq data.

In this experiment, the fraction of usable ADT reads was relatively low, resulting in modest ADT UMI counts per cell (Table S7). This likely reflects a combination of suboptimal antibody titration and incomplete removal of unbound antibody oligos prior to GEM generation due to removal of a washing step, as per the protocol for low-viability cells. Future optimization of the CITE-seq workflow would include systematic titration of the antibody panel, increased post-staining wash stringency, and removal of any cell clumps using a 40 μm cell strainer to increase the fraction of informative antibody reads. Although these limitations reduced the dynamic range of the protein measurements, they do not affect the main transcriptomic conclusions of the study and highlight practical considerations for implementing 10× Genomics 5′ Feature Barcoding in high-containment settings.

As a proof of principle, we applied our workflow to LASV-infected Vero E6 cells within a BSL-4 laboratory, generating host and viral single-cell transcriptomes and identifying distinct populations of infected and bystander cells (Figure 6; Table S9). Use of virus-specific probes (Table S8) enabled capture of non-polyadenylated LASV transcripts, supporting the method’s applicability to other non-poly-A targets. Among the host genes positively correlated with viral load, we identified host ribosomal proteins (RPS12, RPLP1), indicating increased protein synthesis; CRYAB, an alpha crystallin and a member of the small heat shock protein family that act as molecular chaperones; and L-ferritin, one of the subunits of the ferritin protein that mediates iron uptake and storage. Interestingly, ferritin has been shown to play a critical role in inflammation, contributing to cytokine production, immune activation, and disease progression.36 Recent studies indicate a strong association between elevated ferritin levels and various infectious diseases, particularly bacterial and viral infections.37,38,39 Although we could not find any studies investigating ferritin during LASV infection, the frequent occurrence of hyperferritinemia in infection with similar RG4 viruses40 suggests that ferritin may be a valuable biomarker worth investigating in patients with Lassa fever.

The host genes negatively correlated to viral levels included CST3 (codes for an inhibitor of cysteine proteases), ACTB (encodes one of the actin proteins, a cytoskeletal protein involved in intercellular signaling), SPP1 (osteopontin, also acts as a cytokine that upregulates the expression of interferon-gamma and interleukin-12), and LOC119619962, an uncharacterized gene. These findings demonstrate the real-world utility of our optimized workflows for high-resolution cellular analysis of RG4 virus infections.

Prior single-cell studies under high containment either processed the entire workflow in BSL-4 with terminal-stage inactivation or used heat-based or alternative chemistries.11,12,13,14 In contrast, we showed that a 72-h PFA regimen is compatible with 10× Flex tissue profiling, and that post-amplification AVL+EtOH inactivation of 5′ barcoded cDNA preserves gene expression, V(D)J, and CITE-seq signal in human and NHP PBMCs, including after long-term cryostorage. The proof-of-principle LASV experiment further demonstrates simultaneous host-virus transcriptomics with virus-specific capture for non-poly (A) transcripts. Additionally, while we demonstrated the robust performance of the AVL+EtOH inactivation workflow using 5′ scRNA-seq, the same method with an AVL+EtOH inactivation strategy can be utilized for a 3′ scRNA-seq workflow for whole-transcriptome analysis, avoiding the use of probe panels and PFA-fixed cells. Indeed, while this manuscript was in production, a method describing a similar workflow for 3′ scRNA-seq of Marburg-virus-infected cells was published,41 further validating the success of this approach.

Finally, we addressed the need for reliable viral genome sequencing using two orthogonal enrichment strategies: tiled-amplicon and hybrid-capture. We optimized long-range polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for generating amplicons suitable for both long-read and short-read sequencing and developed overlapping amplicon schemes for whole-genome sequencing of EBOV, LASV, NiV, RVFV, and other high-consequence pathogens (Figure 7; Table S10). Tiled amplicon approaches have proven outbreak utility for EBOV and other RNA viruses, offering rapid, sensitive, and low-cost sequencing,15,16,17 though it requires periodic primer-scheme updates to avoid dropout in divergent regions. In contrast, probe (hybrid-capture) enrichment19,20,42 increases recovery from low-titer, host-rich samples and is more robust to sequence variation when probes are designed to capture known diversity. Our comparison of this amplicon-based approach with probe-capture enrichment using a commercially available probe panel showed that both methods are effective, though their performance can be virus- and strain-dependent (Table 6). This provides researchers with validated tools to choose from based on their specific goals, whether it be deep sequencing of a known virus or broader surveillance.

Some of our earlier experiments (Figures 1 and 2) were exploratory, method-development screens carried out under BSL-4/limited-material constraints, and each condition was tested in a single biological sample (n = 1, with occasional technical replicates). We therefore present these panels descriptively and do not draw inferential conclusions. Consistent directional trends were observed across distinct matrices and conditions in these exploratory screens: (1) AVL+EtOH inactivation yielded greater RNA integrity and yield than TRIzol LS both in Vero E6 cells (Figure 1A) and NHP brain samples (Figures 2C and S2A); (2) BBv3 homogenization produced more intact RNA (with higher DV200) in TRIzol-LS-treated (Figures 2A, 2B, S2C, and S2D) and AVL+EtOH-treated lung tissue (Figure 2D); and (3) the PureLink kit generally produced higher integrity and yield across PBMCs (Figures 1D and S2B) and TRIzol-LS-treated NHP lung samples (Figures 2A, 2B, S2C, and S2D). The lack of biological replicates for the early screens is a limitation of this study and precludes formal hypothesis testing and generalizable performance ranking. The observed trends should be interpreted as internally consistent signals that motivated the replicated assessments presented later (Figure 3 and scRNA-seq experiments where we used PFA and AVL+EtOH-based inactivation successfully with multiple sets of samples).

This study presents a rigorously validated suite of protocols that overcome key barriers to genomic research on high-consequence pathogens. By systematically optimizing sample inactivation, RNA extraction, tissue processing, and both bulk and single-cell workflows, we provide the field with scalable, reproducible tools for safely investigating RG4 viruses at high resolution. These protocols unlock critical opportunities for pathogenesis studies, immune profiling, and surveillance efforts in BSL-4 settings, thus expanding the boundaries of what is technically and safely achievable in the study of the world’s most dangerous viruses.

Limitations of the study

This study was designed to develop practical workflows for genomic analyses of samples generated in high-containment laboratories. Because of the constraints associated with containment, some of the early optimization experiments, especially those shown in Figures 1 and 2, were exploratory screens performed with one biological sample per condition, with occasional technical replicates. These results were interpreted descriptively and used to guide later validation experiments.

The workflows described here were optimized for specific sample types, inactivation methods, extraction approaches, and sequencing applications. Therefore, no single protocol should be considered universally optimal for every high-containment sample. These workflows may need to be adjusted for different tissue types, viruses, sample quality, or input amounts to generate high-quality genomic data. In particular, CITE-seq and cell hashing may benefit from further antibody titration and washing optimization, and viral genome enrichment methods may require updated primer or probe designs as viral sequences diverge.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Healthy volunteers (two males, ages 35 and 38 years) were recruited between June 1, 2020 and July 1, 2023 under NIH protocol 17-CC-0148 (“Samples from Human Subjects to Facilitate Basic, Translational, and Clinical Research”). The NIH Institutional Review Board approved the protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent. Samples were provided by Dr. Daniel Chertow (NIH Clinical Center). PBMC samples from these two donors were used for methods-development and workflow-validation experiments. Samples were allocated to experimental workflows based on sample availability, cryopreservation duration, and assay requirements; no randomization was performed because the study was designed to optimize workflows rather than test donor-level biological effects. The influence of sex or gender on the results was not assessed because only two male donors were included and the study was not powered to evaluate sex- or gender-associated differences. Race, ethnicity, and ancestry information were not analyzed for this study.

Animals

All animal studies were conducted in facilities accredited by AAALAC International. Experimental procedures were approved by the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases Animal Care and Use Committee and complied with the Animal Welfare Act, Public Health Service Policy, and the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition).

Lung lysates from SARS-CoV-2-exposed hamsters were obtained from animals (6–8 weeks old male and female Mesocricetus auratus, acquired from Envigo, IN, USA) described in a previous study.44 Nonhuman primate (NHP) lung and brain tissue samples were obtained from uninfected animals (one 7-year-old male and one 6-year-old female Indian-origin rhesus macaques) described in a previous study.43 NHP blood samples were collected during routine animal health assessments and physical exams under an approved animal study protocol. Rhesus monkey whole blood for RNA extraction optimizations was procured from BioIVT (Westbury, NY, USA). Age and sex information for commercially procured rhesus blood and PBMC samples was not available. These samples were used for workflow optimization and validation, and the study was not designed or powered to assess the influence of sex or gender on assay performance.

Cell lines

Vero E6 cells were obtained from BEI Resources. Vero E6 cells expressing human TMPRSS2 were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank. SW13 and BSC-1 cells were obtained from ATCC. All cell lines were maintained according to supplier recommendations. Cell lines were not independently authenticated after receipt. Mycoplasma testing was performed and the cell lines were confirmed to be mycoplasma negative before use.

Viruses

All virus stocks were propagated in biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) containment at the Integrated Research Facility at Fort Detrick (National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases, National Institutes of Health, Frederick, MD, USA).

Viruses used in this study included Ebola virus (EBOV) Mayinga-Yambuku and Kikwit variants; Lassa virus (LASV) Josiah, Liberia-812879, and guinea-pig-adapted variants; Marburg virus (MARV) Angola variant; Nipah virus (NiV) Malaysia and Bangladesh variants; Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV) ZH501; yellow fever virus (YFV) strains; Crimean-Congo hemorrhagic fever virus (CCHFV) strain IbAr10200; mpox virus (MPXV) strains; and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) variants including WA1, B.1.1.7, B.1.617.2, BA.2.12.1, BA.4, EG.5.1, and JN.1.

EBOV, LASV, MARV, NiV, RVFV, and YFV stocks were propagated in Vero E6 cells, and SARS-CoV-2 stocks were grown in Vero or TMPRSS2 Vero E6 cells. CCHFV and MPXV stocks were propagated in SW-13 and BSC-1 cells, respectively. All live cultures were maintained under containment conditions appropriate for each virus. Sources of virus isolates and cell lines are provided in the Key Resources Table.

Method details

Virus inactivation

TRIzol LS inactivation

As described by Kumar et al.,28 a minimum of three parts of TRIzol LS reagent (Invitrogen) was added to one part of sample (10% clarified homogenate prepared from tissue sample or cell suspension) and incubated for 10 min following thorough mixing and transfer of the entire volume to a new tube.

AVL+EtOH and RLT+EtOH inactivation

As described by Kumar et al.,28 a minimum of four parts of AVL buffer (Qiagen) was added to one part of sample (10% clarified homogenate prepared from tissue sample or cell suspension) and incubated for 10 min following thorough mixing. After a quick spin, 100% molecular grade ethanol (EtOH; the same volume as the AVL buffer) was added and mixed thoroughly before transfer of the entire volume to a new tube. A similar method was used for RLT+EtOH treatment, in which a minimum of four parts of RLT buffer (Qiagen) was added to one part of 10% clarified homogenate, mixed thoroughly, and incubated for 10 min. After a quick spin, the same volume (as RLT buffer) of 100% molecular-grade EtOH was added and mixed thoroughly before the entire volume was transferred to a new tube.

Paraformaldehyde inactivation

Cells or minced tissue samples were treated with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; diluted from 37% paraformaldehyde [Thermo Fisher]) at a ratio of one part PFA to one part sample for cell suspensions or 10 parts fixative to one part sample for minced tissue samples. For Vero E6 cells, one million cells were washed and treated with PFA for 1, 2, or 16 h at 4°C. Following PFA treatment, RNA was extracted from cell lysates using the Qiagen miRNeasy kit.

Sample processing

Blood processing

Whole blood: Peripheral blood was collected from NHPs (rhesus and green monkeys) in tubes treated with ethylenediamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA; Invitrogen), gently inverted eight to 10 times, and mixed with room temperature dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich) to a final concentration of 10% DMSO. One-mL aliquots of whole blood were transferred to sterile cryovials and stored in a freezing container (Mr. Frosty, Thermo Fisher) at −80°C. Vials of cryopreserved whole blood were thawed rapidly in a water bath at 37°C with gentle agitation and then immediately placed on ice.

Red blood cell depletion: One mL of whole blood (rhesus monkey) was added to 10 mL of ACK Lysing Buffer (Thermo Fisher) at room temperature, incubated for 3–5 min, and centrifuged at 300 xg for 5 min. Cell pellets were washed once with 5 mL of cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Invitrogen) and resuspended in PBS at 2°C–8°C for further processing.

Human PBMC isolation: Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient centrifugation in Ficoll-Paque Plus (Sigma-Aldrich) in SepMate tubes (StemCell Technologies) and then cryopreserved in fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher) + 10% DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) and stored in liquid nitrogen until use. Frozen cells were thawed using CryoThaw Tube Adapters (Medax International Inc.) by placing on top of 15-mL conical tubes with 9 mL of 37°C Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) medium without DNase and RNase (Thermo Fisher) to avoid degradation of nucleic acids.

NHP PBMC isolation: NHP (rhesus monkey) PBMCs were isolated by density gradient centrifugation in Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich) in SepMate tubes (StemCell Technologies) and washed with 1X Hanks’ balanced salt solution +2% FBS. PBMCs were cryopreserved in FBS +10% DMSO and stored in liquid nitrogen until use. Rhesus monkey PBMCs and whole blood for RNA extraction optimizations were procured from BioIVT.

Tissue homogenization

Homogenization by bead-beating: Frozen NHP lung tissue samples were weighed and either used directly for preparation of 10% homogenate using ice-cold PBS (version 1) or TRIzol LS (Invitrogen; version 2 and 3) or incubated with >10 volumes of chilled RNAlater-ICE (Life Technologies) for >16 h at −20°C before preparing the 10% homogenate. The tissue samples were then homogenized using Omni Bead Ruptor 24 (Revvity) with a single 90-s pulse (version 1), three 30-s pulses with 1 min of incubation on ice after every pulse (version 2), or a single 60-s pulse (version 3) at 500 revolutions per min. After 1 min of incubation on ice, the lysate was transferred to pre-chilled tubes for a 10-min spin at 2,500 xg. The clarified supernatant was then inactivated using TRIzol LS or AVL+EtOH as described above.

Homogenization using gentleMACS Octo Dissociator: Frozen NHP lung tissue samples were weighed and transferred (directly or after incubation with >10 volumes of chilled RNAlater-ICE for >16 h at −20°C) to gentleMACS M tubes (Miltenyi). Homogenization was performed using the Miltenyi gentleMACS Octo Dissociator program RNA_01 (for fresh tissues, used for samples treated with RNAlater-ICE) or RNA_02 (for frozen tissues, used for samples not treated with RNAlater-ICE) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. This was followed by a 10-min spin at 2,500 xg to generate the clarified supernatant, which was then inactivated using TRIzol LS or AVL+EtOH.

RNA extraction

Manual RNA extraction

RNA was extracted from Vero E6 cells using the Promega SV kit (Promega) as per the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by purification and concentration using the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research). For the 4% PFA-treated samples, RNA was also extracted using the Qiagen miRNeasy FFPE kit (Qiagen) from an additional set of PFA-inactivated cells per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were thawed in 240 μL of buffer PKD with Proteinase K at room temperature and mixed gently. For the de-cross-linking step, the cell lysates were incubated at either 56°C for 1 h (condition “A”24), or at 56°C for 15 min, followed by 80°C for another 15 min (condition “B”). Following a 20 min centrifugation at 20,000 xg, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and 25 μL of DNase Booster buffer and 10 μL of DNase I stock solution were added and mixed. After a 15 min incubation at room temperature, 500 μL buffer RBC and 1,750 μL of 100% EtOH were added, mixed and the samples were applied to the MinElute spin columns. After two washes with 500 μL RPE buffer, RNA was eluted in 20 μL RNase-free water. The eluted RNA was further purified and concentrated by passing it through the RNA Clean and Concentrator-25 column. RNA quantitation was done using Qubit HT (Thermo Fisher) and TapeStation (Agilent) was used to assess the quality of RNA.

For RNA extraction from blood samples (PBMCs, blood after red blood cell depletion, and whole blood), SV Total RNA Isolation System (Promega), QIAamp RNA Blood Mini (Qiagen), Monarch Total RNA Miniprep (New England Biolabs), PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen), Direct-zol RNA Miniprep (Zymo Research) kits were used as per manufacturers’ instructions.

RNA extraction from inactivated NHP lung homogenates was performed manually using PureLink RNA Mini (Invitrogen), RNeasy Plus Mini (Qiagen) and Direct-zol RNA Miniprep kits as per manufacturers’ instructions.

Automated RNA extraction

Total RNA was isolated from tissue samples using MagMAX mirVana Total RNA Isolation kit (Thermo Fisher) and KingFisher Flex purification system (Thermo Fisher) using 96 deep-well or 24 deep-well plates and a modified version of the manufacturer-recommended protocol. Briefly, chloroform, isopropanol, and binding beads mix were added to the inactivated tissue lysates with shaking after each addition for binding RNA to the beads. Once the sample loading was complete, the RNA-bound beads were washed, treated with DNase and washed again before RNA elution.

For multiple sample loading steps, the KingFisher Flex program was modified to (i) repeat the bead-binding step; (ii) prompt the user to manually replace the sample input plate with another input plate on position 1 after the first bead-binding step; and (iii) to transfer the RNA-bound beads from the first input plate to the second one. As an example, for two sample loads with 24 deep-well input plates, 0.5 mL of TRIzol-LS-inactivated tissue lysate was added to two wells, one in each of two separate 24-well input plates. In each well, the lysate was mixed with 0.625 mL Lysis Binding Mix and 113 μL chloroform and shaken at 500 rpm for 2 min, followed by the addition of 1.125 mL isopropanol and mixing for 2 min, and finally 20 μL of fresh Binding Beads Mix with shaking at 950 rpm for 2 min. Following this initial binding, the KingFisher Flex run was initiated. The instrument ran the RNA-bead binding step with input plate 1 (without any additional reagents, beads or sample input), then prompted the user to manually replace plate 1 with input plate 2 on the same position. When the plate was replaced, the RNA-bound beads from plate 1 were transferred into input plate 2, which already contained a bead–lysate mixture. The RNA-bead binding step was then repeated with plate 2 (without any additional reagents, beads or sample input). The run then proceeded through two standard washes (600 μL in Positions 2 and 3), DNase treatment (200 μL in Position 4), reagent addition to the DNase plate (100 μL Rebinding Buffer and 200 μL isopropanol), additional washes (600 μL in Positions 5 and 6), and final elution in 50 μL at Position 7. For three sample loads, three plates were prepared in parallel, and three rounds of the RNA-bead binding step were performed on the instrument with the beads from the first two plates transferred to the third one.

Similar procedure was followed for multiple loadings using the 96 deep-well plates, with the only difference being the reagent volumes. 100 μL lysate was used per well, with 10 μL chloroform, 100 μL isopropanol, and 20 μL Binding Beads Mix. All washes were 150 μL, 50 μL each of DNase and Rebinding Buffer were used and the RNA was eluted in 50 μL Elution buffer.

The eluted RNA was further purified using the Clean and Concentrator-25 kit by following the manufacturer-recommended protocol. Briefly, the RNA sample was added to the magnetic beads, and after a brief incubation, the beads were washed and the RNA was eluted.

Viral RNA was extracted using the KingFisher Flex purification system and MagMAX Viral/Pathogen II (MVPII) Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 400 μL of inactivated sample was added to 550 μL of binding solution and magnetic beads. After binding and washing with 80% EtOH (VWR), samples were eluted into 50 μL of elution buffer (Thermo Fisher).

Single-cell RNA sequencing using GEM-X flex gene expression

PFA inactivation and tissue dissociation

Frozen hamster lung and liver tissue (Rockland Immunochemicals, Inc.) samples were weighed and minced on glass petri plates kept on a cold block. Ten volumes of 4% PFA were then added to the minced tissue pieces in a centrifuge tube and incubated at 4°C. After a 72-h incubation, the supernatant was removed, and the samples were processed using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Preparation Kit (10x Genomics) as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Briefly, the samples were fixed further using fixation and permeabilization buffer (10x Genomics) with 4% formaldehyde (Thermo Fisher) for 16–18 h at 4°C. For tissue dissociation into single cells, the tissue pellets were washed with PBS and resuspension buffer (50 mM Tris pH 8.0 [VWR], 0.02% Bovine Serum Albumin [Millipore Sigma], and 0.24 U/μL Protector RNase inhibitor [Millipore Sigma] in 1X PBS [Corning]) and then resuspended in pre-warmed dissociation solution (0.2 mg/mL Liberase TL [Millipore Sigma] in RPMI [Thermo Fisher] for tissue dissociation using the gentleMACS C tubes [Miltenyi] and gentleMACS Octo Dissociator [Miltenyi]). Cells were then washed with PBS, resuspended in quenching buffer (10x Genomics) and used immediately for probe hybridization.

Probe hybridization, GEM generation, library preparation, and sequencing

After the PFA treatment, tissue samples were dissociated into single cell suspensions and 106 cells for each of the four samples were used for probe hybridization using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell Fixed RNA Sample Preparation Kit (10x Genomics) as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Cells were resuspended in the hybridization mix with the mouse whole-transcriptome probe set with a unique probe barcode for each sample and hybridization at 42°C for 16–24 h. Unhybridized probes were then washed off and equal numbers of cells from each set were mixed to generate one pool. Pooled cells were then taken through the 10x Genomics workflow to generate gel beads in emulsion (GEMs) using a Chromium iX instrument (10x Genomics). The left- and right-hand sides of the hybridized bipartite probes were ligated inside individual GEMs. Cell- and transcript-specific barcodes were added to the probes during extension of the ligated probes bound to the probe capture sequence on the gel bead-bound oligos (Figure 4A). Sequencing libraries were prepared from the barcoded probes and sequenced using short-read sequencing on NextSeq2000 (Illumina).

Data analyses

To enable the detection of both host and viral transcripts, a custom reference genome combining mouse (mm10) and SARS-CoV-2 sequences was built. The reference genome sequence from refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A was concatenated with the SARS-CoV-2 genome SARS_CoV_2.ASM985889v3.dna.toplevel.fa, and the corresponding gene annotations were merged by combining the genes.gtf file of mm10 with SARS_CoV_2.ASM985889v3.101.gtf. The probe set Chromium_Mouse_Transcriptome_Probe_Set_v1.0.1_mm10-ASM985889v3-2020-A.csv was further appended with custom probe sequences specific to SARS-CoV-2 genes. This modified reference was then built using the mkref command (Cell Ranger v7.1.0).58

Raw Illumina reads from pooled libraries were demultiplexed and processed using Cell Ranger multi, which performed alignment, barcode processing, and unique molecular identifier (UMI) counting based on the custom reference. The resulting feature-barcode matrices were then imported into Seurat v445 for downstream quality control, normalization, clustering, annotation, and integration.

For each sample, raw gene expression matrices (HDF5 format) were imported into R using Seurat (v4+). Seurat objects were created with minimal filtering (minimal cells = 3, minimal features = 200), followed by SCTransform normalization, principal component analysis (PCA), Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) for visualization, and clustering using the Leiden algorithm (resolution = 0.4). Cell type annotation was performed via the Azimuth pipeline using the built-in lung reference dataset. Individual Seurat objects were integrated using anchor finding and SCTransform normalization based on reciprocal PCA (RPCA).

Single-cell immune profiling using 5′ scRNA-seq

Cell processing and complementary DNA (cDNA) generation

Vero E6 cells and PBMCs for inactivation testing: Frozen cells for each of the two samples were thawed, washed and ≈6,000 cells for each sample (≈90% viability for Vero E6 cells and ≈75% viability for PBMCs) were used for cell capture. Standard 10x Genomics workflow for 5′ single-cell sequencing was followed until the amplification of barcoded and pooled cDNA. The amplified cDNA was divided into three portions. One-third of the cDNA was treated with TRIzol LS, one-third was treated with AVL+EtOH, and one-third was left untreated.

“Old” and “new” PBMCs for testing suitability of stored samples: Frozen cells at 1 month (“new”) and 3 years (“old”) post-cryopreservation were used to determine the effects of extended sample preservation on sequencing quality. The samples were thawed, and ≈6,000 cells for each sample (≈89% viability for the new PBMC sample and ≈87% viability for the old PBMC sample) were used for cell capture.

Antibody cocktail preparation and cell staining for human and NHP samples: For the first pilot experiment (pilot 1), ≈10,000 cells for each sample (≈85% viability) were used for cell capture, after staining with antibody cocktail listed in Table S6 (concentrations in column 2). For pilot 2, two antibody cocktails were prepared (pilot 2a-low and pilot 2b-high, Table S6). Following the antibody staining using the revised cocktail, ≈10,000 cells for the NHP PBMC sample (≈85% viability) were used for cell capture, and sequencing libraries were prepared for single-cell gene expression and feature barcode analysis. Two human PBMC samples (sample 1 and sample 2) were taken, and each was divided into four equal parts. Four aliquots (two for each human sample) were stained with antibody cocktail with a lower concentration of antibodies (pilot 2a-low, Table S6) with TotalSeq-C anti-human hashtag antibodies 1 and 2 added for sample 1, and 5 and 6 added for sample 2. The remaining four aliquots were stained with antibody cocktail with a higher concentration of antibodies (pilot 2a-high, Table S6) with TotalSeq-C anti-human hashtag antibodies 3 and 4 added for sample 1, and 7 and 8 added for sample 2. The vendor-recommended protocol for low viability cells (removed one washing step) was used for antibody staining and washing. The separately stained pools were combined, and ≈42,000 of those cells were used for capture and library preparation.

Generation of oligo-tagged CD3 antibody: For the pilot 2 NHP PBMC sample, an oligo-tagged version of Mouse Anti-Human CD3 Clone SP34-2 (BD Biosciences) was prepared. The barcoding oligo (/5AmMC12/CGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAG-NNNNNNNNNN-GCATTGCGTCAGGCT-NNNNNNNNN-CCCATATAAGAAA) was designed as per the 10x Genomics guidelines, and 5′ Feature Barcode Antibody Conjugation Kit (Abcam) was used for the oligo conjugation. Conjugation was verified by gel electrophoresis of conjugated and unconjugated antibody on a NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen) followed by staining with the Pierce Silver Staining Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Unconjugated oligo was removed as per the manufacturer’s protocol. This anti-CD3 antibody was added to the panel to prepare pilot 2 antibody cocktail (pilot 2b-high, Table S6).

Amplified cDNA inactivation

Inactivation with TRIzol LS: After the first polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of cDNA as described above, one-third of the cDNA pool was inactivated with TRIzol LS as described above. The aqueous phase was removed, and the DNA-containing interphase was precipitated with 100% EtOH, washed, and resuspended as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Inactivation with AVL+EtOH: After the first PCR amplification of cDNA as described above, one-third of the cDNA pool was inactivated with AVL+EtOH as described above. cDNA was then recovered using a QIAamp Viral RNA Mini kit (Qiagen) by applying the AVL+EtOH mixture to the column, washing as per the manufacturer’s protocol, and eluting two times with 50 μL of Buffer AVE to meet the expected volume for library cleanup. Carrier RNA was found to interfere with downstream library quantification and so was not included.

cDNA cleanup

SPRIselect (Beckman Coulter) cleanup and cDNA quantification of the AVL+EtOH inactivated and untreated samples was performed as per the manufacturer’s protocol, treating each condition as a separate sample.

Library preparation and sequencing

Gene expression libraries: Construction of gene expression libraries for all experiments was carried out using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v2 (10x Genomics) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. Unless noted separately, the manufacturer’s recommended number of PCR cycles were used.

T cell receptor (TCR) and B cell receptor (BCR) libraries: To test new vs. old PBMCs, TCR and BCR libraries were generated using Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v2 (10x Genomics) with Chromium Single Cell Human TCR Amplification Kit (10x Genomics) and Chromium Single Cell Human BCR Amplification Kit (10x Genomics) for each sample as per the manufacturer’s protocol.

Feature barcode libraries: For the validation and optimization of feature barcode antibody staining, libraries were generated as per the manufacturer’s protocol using the first supernatant from the SPRIselect cleanup step.

BSL-4 pilot study with LASV-infected Vero E6 cells

Approximately 6,000 Vero E6 cells infected with the Josiah strain of LASV were used for cell capture using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 5′ Reagent Kit v2 (10x Genomics) in the BSL-4 laboratory as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Because LASV transcripts lack a poly-A tail, LASV-specific primers were designed (Table S8) and added to the assay mix for efficient capture of LASV transcripts and identification of virus-infected cells. Owing to low cDNA yield after 12 rounds of amplification, a fraction of cDNA was amplified for an additional five cycles. The amplified cDNA from the first set (with 12 rounds of amplification) was called Vero-1, while that from the second set (with 17 rounds of amplification) was called Vero-2. The amplified cDNA for both the samples was inactivated using AVL+EtOH, as described earlier. Inactivated cDNA samples were brought to the BSL-2 laboratory, where single-cell sequencing libraries were prepared for gene expression analysis and shallow short-read sequencing was performed to generate low-depth sequence data.

Data analyses

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and quality control: Raw sequencing data were processed using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger software (v7.0.0) to perform demultiplexing, alignment to the appropriate reference genome, and UMI counting to generate a cell-by-gene count matrix. For immune repertoire (V(D)J) analysis, Cell Ranger vdj (v7.0.0) was used with GRCh38 (for human samples) and Macaca mulatta (for NHP PBMC samples) genome reference files. Intron mode was used for all analyses. Data visualization included barcode rank plots, violin plots, heatmaps, and dimensionality reduction plots. Loupe Browser (8.1.2) was also used for data visualization.

Cell type annotation and differential gene expression analysis: Downstream single-cell analyses were performed using the Seurat package in R using the standard Seurat workflow and the SCTransform method. Doublets were detected and removed using DoubletFinder. Cells were annotated automatically using Seurat’s Data Transfer method. The human PBMC reference dataset generated by Hao et al.57 was used as the reference to annotate the cells and visualized using a UMAP projection. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify genes that were significantly enriched in each cluster. The top differentially expressed genes were visualized using heatmaps.

Immune repertoire (V(D)J) analysis: TCR and BCR sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger vdj. This pipeline assembled V(D)J gene segments to identify full-length TCR and BCR sequences, determine clonotype frequencies, and assess the productivity of rearrangements for each cell. Top 10 TCR and BCR complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) sequences were tabulated, and productive rearrangements were defined based on the presence of in-frame, non-stop codons spanning V–J segments.

CITE-seq and cell hashing analysis: For CITE-seq experiments, antibody-derived tag (ADT) counts were obtained and normalized using a centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation. These normalized protein expression levels were used alongside the RNA expression data for multimodal cell clustering and annotation. For multiplexed samples, cell hashing data were demultiplexed to assign each cell to its sample of origin using Cell Ranger for demultiplexing.

Viral transcript analysis: For LASV–infected Vero E6 cell experiments, custom LASV-specific capture primers were included during cDNA synthesis. Viral reads were quantified by alignment to the green monkey and LASV reference genomes and host and viral gene expression per cell was quantified by UMI counts.

Viral genome sequencing

Amplicon-based enrichment

SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) gene amplicons: Extracted SARS-CoV-2 RNA from four variants (Washington 01 [399], Alpha [433], Delta [458], and Omicron BA1 [469]) was reverse-transcribed using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis system (Thermo Fisher) or qScript Ultra Flex (Quantabio) as per manufacturers’ protocols, using random hexamers for priming the reverse transcription reaction. S-gene-specific primers (Table S10) were used to amplify the SARS-CoV-2 S gene using repliQa HiFi ToughMix (Quantabio), Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (New England Biolabs), and Platinum SuperFi II PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher) for 35 cycles.

Long-amplicon virus panels, library preparation, and sequencing: Purified RNA was converted into cDNA using SuperScript IV and random hexamers. Long (1.5–2.0 kb) overlapping amplicons were generated using custom-designed primer panels (Table S10) and repliQa HiFi ToughMix (Quantabio). Primer panels were designed using the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Primer-BLAST56 or primalscheme v3.0.2 by the ARTIC network.15 The scheme is licensed under CC BY-SA 4.0. For all primer sets designed using primalscheme, two pools of PCR reactions were prepared, and equimolar amounts were pooled before library preparation. Libraries were prepared using the DNA Prep kit (Illumina) and sequenced using iSeq100, MiSeq or NextSeq2000 sequencers (Illumina) using 300 cycle reagent kits.

Data analysis: Sequence analysis and assembly were performed using a pipeline developed in house. Briefly, FASTQ files were quality-trimmed and then adaptor- and primer-trimmed using BBDuk (Joint Genome Institute). Trimmed FASTQ files were then aligned separately to the reference genome using the MEM algorithm in Burrows–Wheeler Aligner v0.7.17.48 Consensus sequences were generated using SAMtools v1.16.47 Variant analysis was performed using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) v4.4.0.0 haplotype caller.49

Nanopore sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing

Nanopore libraries were prepared from SARS-CoV-2 S gene amplicons generated using the repliQa polymerization system using the Native Barcoding Kit 24 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) as per the manufacturer’s recommendations. Briefly, DNA ends of the 500-ng and 4-kb amplicon samples were repaired and prepared for adapter attachment using the NEBNext Ultra II End repair with dA-tailing Module reagents (NEB). Next, barcode ligation to the DNA ends and cleanup were performed using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter). Sequencing adapters were then ligated, followed by wash using the Long Fragment Buffer. The eluted library was sequenced using an R9.4.1 flow cell on a MinION Mk1C device as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Data analysis

Sequence reads were basecalled using Guppy v5.1.13 through MinKNOW v21.11.7 with a high-accuracy model. The sequence data were analyzed using the Fastq Custom Alignment workflow by EPI2ME v2021.11.26 (Metrichor). Briefly, FASTQ files were concatenated and quality-assessed with fastcat v0.22.1 (EPI2ME Labs), producing per-read and per-file quality-control metrics. Reads were aligned to a custom reference using Minimap2 v2.22,46 and alignments were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed using Samtools.47 Alignment statistics and depth of coverage across reference sequences were generated with Mosdepth55 and BAMStats (EPI2ME Labs).

Probe-capture-based virus enrichment

Library preparation and sequencing

Probe-capture-based virus enrichment was done using the Viral Surveillance Panel v1 (Illumina) and RNA Prep with Enrichment (Illumina) kits as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 0.9–39 ng of RNA extracted from inactivated virus-exposed cells was converted into cDNA and tagmented, and indexing adapters were added to the samples during amplification. For MPXV, 100 ng of genomic DNA was directly used for tagmentation. The libraries were normalized and pooled. Multiplexed viral genome sequences were then captured using the Viral Surveillance Panel v1 (Illumina) and enriched using streptavidin magnetic beads. The enriched libraries were amplified and cleaned, and short-read sequence data were generated using NextSeq2000 (Illumina) using P1 300 cycle reagent kit.

Data analysis

The viral sequences were analyzed with the Viral Assembly Variant Calling pipeline developed by the Bioinformatics and Computational Biosciences Branch at the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases (https://github.com/niaid/viral-assembly-variant-calling). In brief, raw Illumina reads were trimmed and quality-filtered with BBDuk, followed by error correction with spades_ec.50 For reference-based workflow, ConsIter was used to perform alignment with Bowtie2,51 followed by variant-calling with GATK HaplotypeCaller49 and an iterative updating of the consensus sequencing until the alignment rate plateaued. To identify low-frequency variants among the virus populations, the consensus file generated from ConsIter was used as reference for LoFreq52 and/or iVar53 to call variants. In parallel, a de novo assembly-based workflow was used to validate and supplement reference-based results. Error-corrected reads were assembled into contigs using the SPAdes assembler. Resulting contigs were scaffolded against a known reference genome using RagTag,54 producing a pseudo-reference, which was further polished using Clean_RagTag. The cleaned pseudo-reference was then used as the reference sequence for variant calling using ConsIter as described in the reference-based workflow.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.4.1. For data presented in Figures 1 and 2, no statistical tests were performed because each condition was evaluated in a single biological sample and results are presented descriptively. For Figure 3, differences between methods were assessed using two-tailed paired t tests. Two-sided p values, 95% confidence intervals, and Cohen’s d effect sizes are reported.
