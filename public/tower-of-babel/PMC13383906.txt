UHPLC–MS based metabolomics study of the ErZhi formula on skeletal muscle against osteosarcopenia focusing on energy metabolism
Authors: Yuqing Pang, Ningning Li, Yujie Ding, Shuzhen Hou, Jing Liu, Xiuxue Liu, Erwei Liu, Xiaopeng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ErZhi formula (EZF) exhibits sound therapeutic effects on osteosarcopenia (OS). However, EZF's therapeutic effects on skeletal muscles are rarely reported. This study explored the mechanism of EZF in skeletal muscle during OS by integrating energy metabolism and metabolomics.MethodsAfter an ovariectomized rat model was established for 4 weeks, the rats were subjected to a 12‐week intervention of EZF and alendronate. The rats' body weight, gastrocnemius muscle mass, degree of myofiber fibrosis, and myofiber cross‐sectional area (CSA) were measured to evaluate the pathological state of the gastrocnemius muscle. The mitochondrial membrane potential and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected to assess mitochondrial function. Then, energy metabolite analysis and metabolomics were performed on the gastrocnemius muscle.ResultsCompared to the model group, EZH increased CSA by 5.58% and decreased myofiber fibrosis by 15.11%. Notably, compared to the model group, EZH exhibited a 52.66% increase in mitochondrial membrane potential and a 44.32% reduction in ROS levels. Starch and sucrose metabolism, insulin secretion, insulin resistance, and galactose metabolism were the most significantly affected pathways in energy metabolism. Thirty‐five differential metabolites were found in the metabolomics of the gastrocnemius muscle, and EZF could effectively inhibit the sphingolipid metabolism pathway. Correlation analysis identified 13 differential metabolites that were significantly associated with skeletal muscle mass, ROS production, muscle fibrosis, and mitochondrial function, suggesting that these metabolites may play important roles in the progression of skeletal muscle lesions.ConclusionsEZF alleviates OS by regulating skeletal muscle physiological indicators, mitochondrial function, and energy metabolism.

INTRODUCTION

With the rapid aging of the world's population, musculoskeletal system disease has become a popular and common aspect of geriatric research throughout the entire life cycle. 1 Osteosarcopenia (OS) is a degenerative musculoskeletal syndrome in the presence of low muscle mass and function and low bone mineral density (BMD). When individuals suffer from OS, negative outcomes, such as the risk of frailty, falls, fractures, disability, hospitalization, and mortality, increase. Not only does it burden the lives of older adults, but it also increases the socioeconomic costs of health systems around the world. 2 OS is a relatively new term, so its epidemiology is limited, and treatment is lacking. Due to different diagnostic thresholds for sarcopenia (SP) and diagnostic classifications for osteoporosis (OP) (based on fracture history or bone density), as well as differences in observational populations and ethnic groups, estimates of OS prevalence range from 5% to 37%. 3 There are no specific drugs available for OS treatment, so exploring dual‐target therapies for OS is urgent.

The study of OS from the perspective of energy metabolomics is of great significance for developing an innovative treatment method for OS. Skeletal muscle is an important, high‐energy‐consuming metabolic organ of the human body. Energy metabolism is essential for the contraction and normal metabolism of skeletal muscles. The normal physiological function of skeletal muscles depends on the participation of multiple energy supply pathways, including glucose metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and fatty acid oxidation.

Studying the metabolic changes in OS with high metabolic characteristics from the perspective of muscle is of irreplaceable importance for understanding musculoskeletal comorbidity. Musculoskeletal degeneration, including OS, often causes drastic metabolic changes in the body, accompanied by metabolic disorders. The muscle is the main organ affected by SP and participates in systemic metabolic function through myokines. Research has found that an increase in lipid content within muscle cells is a common characteristic of muscle metabolic disorders. 4 Skeletal muscle is not only an important organ for glucose metabolism, but it also secretes various cytokines to promote tissue cell communication, 5 such as interleukin, fibroblast growth factor, insulin‐like growth factor, affecting the metabolism and homeostasis of the body.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS), an important component of metabolomics, is a widely used technique for the qualitative or quantitative analysis of endogenous small‐molecule metabolites in the body. The metabolic changes in energy substance metabolites in muscle can be accurately identified by highly sensitive high‐performance LC–MS (HPLC–MS). Thus, this study uses LC–MS to investigate OS from the perspectives of energy substance metabolism and untargeted metabolomics.

ErZhi formula (EZF) is a classic formula in traditional Chinese medicine theory that improves “the deficiency of liver–kidney yin,” which originates from “Fushou Jingfang” written in 1530 AD. This prescription comprises two traditional Chinese medicines: wine‐steamed Ligustri Lucidi Fructus (LLF) and Ecliptae Herba (EH), each in equal amounts. It can be used to treat liver and kidney yin deficiency, dizziness and tinnitus, dryness of the throat and nose, soreness of the waist and knees, and menorrhagia. Pharmacological studies show that EZF shows beneficial effects on bone metabolism, OP, arthritis, menopausal symptoms, anti‐aging, and diabetes, among other conditions. 6 , 7 In recent years, EZF has been widely and comprehensively studied in the treatment of OP. 8 Studies have indicated the anti‐osteoporosis effect of EZF, which promotes the formation of osteoblasts and inhibits the differentiation of osteoclasts, thereby affecting bone differentiation and improving bone reconstruction. 9 Potential active ingredients in EZF, such as oleanolic acid, kaempferol, quercetin, and ursolic acid, demonstrate clinical application prospects for treating OS. 10 There are fewer studies on the effect of EZF on skeletal muscle, so this research conducts a study on muscle and investigates the mechanism.

Consequently, this study first evaluated the intervention effect of EZF on the OS model rats' muscles and further explored the regulatory effect of EZF on muscle mitochondria. After this, research on energy metabolites was conducted using the Sciex QTRAP 6500+, and EZF action pathways were predicted. Finally, UHPLC‐Orbitrap MS was used to investigate metabolic changes and pathways in EZF‐treated samples using untargeted metabolomics.

METHODS

Chemicals and reagents

LLF and EH were provided by Hebei Chunkai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (batch numbers: 210801040 and 211201481) and identified by Professor Xiankuan Li at Tianjin University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. LLF is derived from the dried ripe fruits of Ligustrum lucidum W.T. Aiton (Oleaceae). EH is the aerial part of Eclipta prostrata (L.) L. (Astraceae). The plant names of LLF and EH have been verified with http://www.theplantlist.org (June 25, 2024). Alendronate (ALE) was purchased from Organon Pharmaceutical Technology Co., Ltd. Leucine enkephalin was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio‐Technology Co., Ltd.

HPLC‐grade formic acid (purity ≥99%) was purchased from ACS (USA). Mass‐spectrum‐grade methanol and acetonitrile were obtained from Fisher Scientific. Ultrapure water was produced using a Milli‐Q water purification system. Ammonia, ammonium acetate, and all standards were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Mitochondria isolation kit, JC‐1 detection kit, and ROS detection kit were purchased from Beijing Pulilai Gene Technology Co., Ltd., Biyuntian Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and Nanjing Jiancheng Technology Co., Ltd., respectively.

Preparation of EZF and ALE

EZF was prepared in accordance with the Pharmacopeia of the People's Republic of China (2020). Appropriate amounts of EH were taken, 10 times water was added, and soaked for 30 min, then refluxed twice for 1 h each time. The extracts were filtered and mixed, and then subjected to rotary evaporation and dried under vacuum. The extraction yield of EH was 21.436%. EZF comprised EH extracts and LLF powder in a ratio of 1:1. The quantitative and qualitative characteristics of EZF extract have been published. 11

ALE was suspended with 0.2% sodium carboxymethylcellulose (CMC‐Na) solution and ultrasonicated for 15 min. The samples were stored in a refrigerator below 4°C for later use. Experimental design is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Currently, there are no approved or recognized therapeutic drugs for OS. 3 As the first non‐hormonal agent approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of OP, ALE can effectively inhibit bone resorption, increase bone mineral density, and reduce the risk of fractures. 12 Studies on ALE for OP have shown that, while exerting anti‐osteoporotic effects, ALE also improves muscle mass and strength in patients with OP. 13 Another animal experiment demonstrated that low‐dose oral ALE ameliorated muscular dysfunction in mouse models of denervation‐induced muscle atrophy and glycerol‐induced muscle injury, accompanied by the negative regulation of SIRT3 expression. 14 As a foundational drug for the prevention and treatment of OP that also exerts positive effects on skeletal muscle, ALE was selected as the positive control in this study.

Animal experiment

Six‐week‐old female Sprague–Dawley rats (180 ± 20 g) were purchased from the Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., license number: SCXK (Beijing) 2019‐0008. All rats were housed in the standard specific pathogen‐free (SPF) animal center. Five to six rats were placed in a cage. The environmental temperature was controlled at 22 ± 2°C, the humidity was at 40% ± 10%, and a 12‐h day/night cycle was used. The animals were acclimated to the laboratory for seven consecutive days. During the housing period, rats were allowed to eat and drink water freely.

Rats were randomly allocated into two initial groups for surgical intervention: the sham group (n = 15) and the ovariectomy group (n = 75). For rats in the sham group, an equivalent amount of adipose tissue adjacent to the ovaries was excised. Rats in the ovariectomy group received bilateral ovariectomy. After 4 weeks of surgery, three rats were randomly selected from the ovariectomy group to assess bone mineral density (BMD). If the BMD of ovariectomized rats was below 1.0 g/cm3, it indicated successful model replication. 15 The BMD of the three selected rats was below this threshold, confirming successful model replication. No rat deaths occurred during surgery or the postoperative recovery period; however, three rats in the ovariectomy group and one rat in the sham group died during the subsequent drug administration period.

Subsequently, the successfully modeled rats were divided into five intervention groups, with 15 rats in each group: model group, ALE group (1.05 mg/kg), EZF low‐dose group (EZL, 0.945 g/kg), EZF medium‐dose group (EZM, 1.89 g/kg), and EZF high‐dose group (EZH, 3.78 g/kg). The clinical dose for humans is 18 g/day, which is equivalent to a rat dose of 1.89 g/kg (EZM). The high‐dose group received twice this dose, whereas the low‐dose group received half this dose. All rats were administered the corresponding agents by intragastric gavage, whereas the sham and model groups received an equal volume of normal saline, administered continuously for 12 weeks. Rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water before the experiment. The relevant animal grouping and experimental procedures are illustrated in Figure 1. Biological context is shown in Supplementary Material 2.

Sample collection

After 12 weeks of continuous dosing, all six rat groups were weighed separately and then anesthetized with 2.5% pentobarbital sodium. The gastrocnemius muscles were rapidly removed and weighed. A portion of the gastrocnemius muscle was cut for fixation; others were stored immediately in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80°C for later use.

HE and Sirius red staining

Gastrocnemius muscles were fixed at 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. After dehydration with ethanol solutions of different concentrations, samples were embedded in paraffin and cut into thin slices of approximately 4 μm. Later, they were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for general histological examination and then with Sirius red for assessment of muscle fibrosis, respectively.

Transmission electron microscopy

Fresh gastrocnemius muscles were cut into two to three small tissue pieces (1 mm3) in the same area between different groups. The muscle tissues were then placed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde fixative at 4°C for 12–24 h. Then the muscle samples were fixed with 0.1% osmium acid solution for 1–2 h and rinsed thrice with 0.1 mol/L PBS (pH 7.4). Dehydration in ethanol was followed by embedding in resin and then baking at 37°C for 8 h. The wax block was then cut into 70–90 nm ultrathin sections, followed by staining with water‐mix uranyl acetate for 30 min and lead citrate for 5 min. The sections were observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) at a magnification of 13 000×.

Mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS

A mitochondria isolation kit was used to separate the rat gastrocnemius muscle mitochondria. A mitochondrial membrane potential detection kit was used to assess mitochondrial membrane potential. The operation steps were followed according to the JC‐1 detection kit instructions: 200 μL of the prepared mitochondrial suspension was added to 1.8 mL of JC‐1 working solution, mixed well, and incubated in the dark at 37°C for 5 min. When detecting the JC‐1 monomer form, the excitation wavelength was set at 488 nm and the emission wavelength at 530 nm. When the JC‐1 was in the form of aggregates, the excitation wavelength was set at 530 nm and the emission wavelength at 590 nm.

The ROS detection system adopted in this study is based on the 2′,7′‐dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH‐DA) probe, combined with a fluorescence microplate reader for fluorescence intensity detection. ROS levels were measured according to the instructions for the ROS detection kit. The underlying mechanism is as follows: intracellular ROS levels were detected using the DCFH‐DA probe. Nonfluorescent DCFH‐DA crosses the cell membrane and is hydrolyzed into membrane‐impermeable DCFH. Under ROS action, DCFH is oxidized to the highly green‐fluorescent DCF. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader at an excitation wavelength of 502 nm and an emission wavelength of 530 nm, and the fluorescence intensity was proportional to the intracellular ROS level. The operation steps were as follows: 200 μL of mitochondrial suspension was mixed with 1.8 mL of ROS working solution and incubated in the dark at 37°C for 10 min, and then 100 μL of each sample was added to a 96‐well plate. ROS levels were measured using a fluorescence microplate reader, with the excitation wavelength set at 488 nm and the emission wavelength at 530 nm.

Energy substances metabolism

Sample preparation

Standard stock solution and other solutions were prepared with methanol at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. All stock solutions were stored at −20°C, and the stock solution was diluted with methanol before analysis.

An amount of 50 mg (±2.5 mg) of the muscle sample was weighed into a centrifuge tube, and 500 μL of precooled 70% methanol aqueous solution was added. The samples were vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 16 900g for 10 min at 4°C. A 300 μL of supernatant was transferred into a new centrifuge tube, kept in a −20°C refrigerator for 30 min, and centrifuged again at 16 904g for 10 min at 4°C. Subsequently, 200 μL of supernatant was transferred through a protein precipitation plate for further LC–MS analysis.

During the instrument analysis process, 20 μL of each prepared sample was extracted and mixed to form the quality control (QC) samples. A QC sample was usually inserted every 10 analysis samples to assess instrument stability and data reliability.

UHPLC–MS conditions

Data collections were detected using an LC‐ESI‐MS/MS system (Waters ACQUITY H‐Class; MS, SCIEX QTRAP 6500+). The LC–MS/MS conditions were as follows:

The chromatography separation of compounds in samples was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (internal diameter 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm), and the column temperature was maintained at 40°C. The solvent systems consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate and 0.3% ammonium hydroxide in water (solution A) and 90% acetonitrile/water (V/V) (solution B). The elution gradient conditions were as follows: 0–1.2 min: 95%–95% (B); 1.2–8 min: 95%–70% (B); 8–9 min: 70%–50% (B); 9–11 min: 50%–50% (B); 11–11.1 min: 50%–95% (B); 11.1–15 min: 95%–95% (B). The mobile phase flow was 0.4 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL.

The temperature of electrospray ionization (ESI) was maintained at 550°C. Voltage was set to 5500 V in both positive and negative ion modes. Curtain gas (CUR) was set to 35 psi. Scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) was used for the analysis of energy and its metabolites. Data acquisition was performed using Analyst 1.6.3 software (SCIEX). Multiquant 3.0.3 software (SCIEX) was used to quantify all metabolites.

Untargeted metabolomics

Preparation of the sample solution

Appropriate amounts of leucine enkephalin were accurately weighed, and then the standard stock solution was diluted with methanol to prepare an internal standard solution of 1 μg/mL.

An amount of 50 mg of a gastrocnemius muscle sample was placed in a centrifuge tube, and 10 μL of internal standard solution, 200 μL of ice water, and 790 μL cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, V/V) were added. Then, each centrifuge tube was placed in a tissue grinder (Tissuelyser‐32, Jingxin, Shanghai, China) with three magnetic beads at 65 Hz for 120 s. After 10 min of ultrasound in the ice‐water bath, the samples were centrifuged for 15 min (19 700g, 4°C). The supernatant was blown to near dryness with nitrogen flow, then redissolved in 200 μL of acetonitrile, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged again. The obtained supernatant (100 μL) was used for further analysis.

The processing method for QC samples was the same as described in Section 2.8.1.

UHPLC‐Orbitrap MS conditions

Metabolomics data were collected using a UHPLC‐Orbitrap Exploris 240 MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ESI source. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.9 μm) column; the system was protected by a Shim‐pack GIST‐HP(G) C18 (2.1 × 1.0 mm, 1.9 μm) column, and the column temperature was maintained at 35°C. The mobile phase flow was 0.2 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The separation was performed by gradient elution with mobile phase A (0.1% formic acid in water) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile). The elution gradient conditions were as follows: 0–7 min: 5%–45% (B); 7–14 min: 45%–95% (B); 14–15.5 min: 95%–95% (B); 15.5–16 min: 95%–5% (B); 16–20 min: 5%–5% (B), and the total run time was 20 min.

The MS conditions are shown below: full scan of positive and negative ions ranging from 100 to 1000 m/z with collision energy at 20/40/60 eV; full MS resolution, 60 000; ms/ms resolution, 15 000; positive ions of source spray voltage, 3500 V; negative ions of source spray voltage, 3000 V; auxiliary gas flow, 10 Arb; sheath gas flow, 35 Arb; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 300°C; ion transfer tube temperature, 350°C. Data acquisition and analysis were performed using the Thermo Fisher Xcalibur and Compound Discoverer 3.3 software. Analytical methods are described in Supplementary Material 3.

Data analysis and statistics

For the pharmacodynamic data, statistical analyses of individual sample results were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 with one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for group comparisons. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In energy substance metabolism data analysis, all targeted metabolites were detected using MetWare (http://www.metware.cn/) for qualitative and quantitative analysis, and principal component analysis (PCA) and orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS‐DA) were performed using R‐related functions to show differences between groups. To avoid overfitting of OPLS‐DA, a permutation test (200 permutations) was performed. Significantly regulated metabolites between groups were determined using variable importance in projection (VIP) and fold change (FC). Metabolites with VIP >1 and FC ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5 were considered as potential biomarkers and were further analyzed for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment.

In untargeted metabolomics analysis, raw MS data were processed using Compound Discoverer 3.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) to extract peaks, align retention times, and integrate peaks based on molecular weights and MS/MS fragmentation patterns. Peaks with more than 50% missing values across all samples were removed, and the remaining missing values were imputed with the minimum observed peak area. Data normalization was performed using area normalization, logarithmic transformation, and Pareto scaling. The normalized data were imported into SIMCA 14.1 (Umetrics, Sweden) for multivariate statistical analysis, including unsupervised PCA and supervised OPLS‐DA to evaluate group differences. Metabolite annotation was carried out by matching against multiple databases, including HMDB (http://www.hmdb.ca/), MassBank (https://www.massbank.jp/), METLIN, mzCloud, and ChemSpider. Differential metabolites were identified with thresholds of p < 0.05 and FC ≥2 or ≤0.5. These metabolites were further uploaded to MetaboAnalyst for metabolic pathway enrichment analysis. Pathways with an impact value >0.1 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly correlated with OS. Data processing is presented in Supplementary Material 4. Pearson's correlation analysis was performed between the identified metabolites with significant differential expression and biological indicators of the gastrocnemius muscle, including skeletal muscle mass, degree of myofiber fibrosis, skeletal muscle atrophy, ROS production, and JC‐1 fluorescence intensity, using the SRplot online plotting software (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/srplot) to explore their potential relationships.

RESULTS

Pharmacodynamics

EZF modulates skeletal muscle mass, skeletal muscle atrophy, and fibrosis

In this study, the gastrocnemius muscle mass index was used to assess skeletal muscle mass in each rat group after treatment. The gastrocnemius mass index was defined as the value of gastrocnemius mass (mg)/subject weight (g). 16 The calculated statistical results are shown in Figure 2A. Compared to the sham group, the gastrocnemius muscle mass index in the model group showed a downward trend. The ALE and EZH groups showed a trend toward the sham group, indicating that EZF can increase muscle mass in rats.

To further quantify the effects of EZF intervention on the morphology of rat skeletal muscle cells, HE staining on the CSA of the gastrocnemius muscle was performed (Figure 2B). The average cell CSA was statistically analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, USA). As shown in Figure 2C, CSA in the model group was significantly reduced compared to the sham group (p < 0.001). The ALE group and other groups showed increased CSA, whereas the EZH group showed a significant improvement (p < 0.01). The results indicate that EZF could prevent skeletal muscle atrophy in ovariectomized rats by maintaining the gastrocnemius muscle CSA.

Fibrosis is a characteristic pathological change in ovariectomized rats. Sirius red staining is a specific method for detecting collagen fibers, in which collagen fibers are stained pink, and skeletal muscle cells are stained yellow. The staining results are shown in Figure 2D. The results (Figure 2E) show that collagen fiber deposition was significantly higher in the model group than in the sham group (p < 0.001) and decreased significantly in the ALE group (p < 0.05). The EZH group demonstrated a significant advantage in reducing collagen fiber deposition (p < 0.01). The results suggest that EZF mitigates skeletal muscle fibrosis in rat gastrocnemius muscle.

EZF ameliorates mitochondrial swelling and myofiber disorganization

Figure 3A reveals that gastrocnemius muscle cells in the sham group had a relatively complete cellular structure, with neatly arranged myofibers and uniform mitochondrial sizes. The model group showed blurred sarcomeres, disordered muscle fibers, and mitochondria arranged in a disordered manner, with swollen and vacuolar mitochondria that were dissolved or even disappeared. Although some mitochondria in the ALE group showed dissolution and vacuolar swelling, the overall arrangement of myofibers improved compared to the model group. In the EZL group, the arrangement of myofibers was relatively neat, but a small number of mitochondria were dissolved and disappeared with the blurred arrangement of myofibers. In the EZM group, some mitochondria showed swelling and cristae rupture, but overall, myofibers were neatly arranged. The arrangement of mitochondria in the EZH group was more orderly than that in the model group, with some mitochondria showing dissolution and disappearance.

EZF restores mitochondrial membrane potential and reduces ROS accumulation

As shown in Figure 3B, the mitochondrial membrane potential in the model group was significantly lower than that in the sham group (p < 0.001), whereas the ALE group showed an increase (p < 0.01), and all other EZF groups significantly increased mitochondrial membrane potential (p < 0.001).

The level of ROS reflects the health and metabolic state of the mitochondria. When mitochondria are damaged, ROS levels increase. As shown in Figure 3C, the level of ROS in the model group was significantly higher than that in the sham group (p < 0.001). After ALE intervention, ROS levels significantly decreased (p < 0.001). It was found that ROS levels in the EZM and EZH groups were significantly lower (p < 0.001).

Pearson's correlation analysis was performed between the individual biological indicators of the gastrocnemius and the 35 metabolites identified as significantly different in the metabolomics profiling. Using the clustering heatmap (Figure 5), the Pearson correlation coefficients of the compounds were utilized to visualize the correlations between each biological parameter, including skeletal muscle mass, myofiber fibrosis degree, ROS production, and JC‐1 fluorescence intensity, and the corresponding differential metabolites. 1_8‐Diazacyclotetradecane‐2_9‐dione, (5E)‐2,10‐diamino‐5‐{[(5‐amino‐5‐carboxypentyl)amino]methyl}‐5‐undecenedioic acid, N, N‐dimethyldecylamine N‐oxide, sphinganine, 2‐aminio‐1,3,4‐octadecanetriol, and UR‐144 N‐heptyl analog showed extremely significant positive correlations with skeletal muscle mass and ROS production (p < 0.01), whereas 4‐hydroxy‐5‐methylfuran‐3(2H)‐one and streptidine exhibited significant negative correlations. DISCUSSION

Effects of EZF on myofibers

OS, including OP and SP, are comorbidities that share the same mechanism of action. 4 Animal models of OP are appropriate tools, with the ovariectomized rat model being the most commonly used. 17 Research has found that, in addition to decreased bone density or strength, postmenopausal OP patients in clinical experience show changes such as muscle atrophy and decreased muscle strength, ultimately leading to the development of OS. 18 In ovariectomized rats, OP symptoms appeared, accompanied by corresponding changes in skeletal muscles. 19 Therefore, the classic animal model of ovariectomy was used to assess its effects on muscles.

The results showed that skeletal muscle in ovariectomized rats was affected, and EZF increased gastrocnemius muscle mass, enhanced myofiber CSA, and reduced the severity of skeletal muscle fibrosis in OS rats. Therefore, EZF plays a role in alleviating OS by regulating various aspects of skeletal muscle quality.

Effects of EZF on mitochondrial function

Mitochondria are the centers of cellular metabolism and energy production in the body. They play important functions in muscle cells, including energy production, calcium balance regulation, cell proliferation regulation, and apoptotic signal integration. 20 Skeletal muscle adaptability and plasticity are closely related to mitochondrial function, and mitochondrial dysfunction is the main factor promoting the occurrence and development of OS. In the present study, the mitochondrial membrane potential in the model group significantly decreased, indicating mitochondrial dysfunction. 21 EZF has a potential role in maintaining mitochondrial function and repairing skeletal muscle lesions by increasing mitochondrial membrane potential. The stability of mitochondrial function is key to cell survival, and mitochondrial membrane potential is an effective indicator of mitochondrial health and function. The main function of mitochondrial membrane potential is to drive adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase to produce ATP, which is the main source of cellular energy. Studies have found that the mitochondrion is the center of oxidative respiration and is particularly susceptible to oxidative stress and subsequent damage, usually manifested as disorders in ATP synthesis and changes in mitochondrial membrane permeability. 22 , 23 In this study, EZF was found to positively regulate mitochondrial function, suggesting a positive effect on muscle and the potential to prevent or delay OS.

In the ROS results, EZF was found to significantly downregulate ROS levels, and the ROS downregulation was positively correlated with dosage. The mitochondria are the main source of intracellular oxidants and the primary site of oxidative stress. It has been reported that the disruption of mitochondrial hypercomplexes and cell membrane structural integrity, as well as damage to mitochondrial function, can increase ROS levels. 24 At the same time, a large accumulation of ROS also accelerates autophagy and the degeneration of skeletal muscles, 25 , 26 thereby interfering with physiological excitation–contraction coupling and ultimately leading to SP. Other studies have shown that excessive ROS disrupts redox homeostasis, which in turn causes protein oxidation and cytochrome C release from mitochondria into the cytoplasm, inducing mitochondrial apoptosis and ultimately damaging the structure and function of skeletal muscles. 27 , 28

Effects of EZF on energy metabolism

Skeletal muscles are high‐energy‐consuming organs, and their energy metabolism is the key to their normal physiological functions. EZF affected mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS in the skeletal muscles of ovariectomized rats, thereby improving mitochondrial function. Given the importance of mitochondria in regulating energy metabolism, this study continued to investigate energy metabolomics to further explore the mechanism by which EZF regulates energy metabolism. Based on the experimental results, starch and sucrose metabolism was a pathway shared by the three EZF dose groups; insulin resistance also played an important role in treatment.

Starch and sucrose are hydrolyzed by multiple enzymes in the body into glucose and fructose. These molecules are released into the bloodstream and transported to various tissues throughout the body, providing a large amount of nutrients and energy for cell growth. Glucose is a common metabolite of the starch and sucrose metabolic pathways; 80% of it is taken up by skeletal muscle and converted to glycogen for storage, 29 with muscle glycogen storing the most. The glycolysis of muscle glycogen is an important mechanism by which carbohydrates maintain energy homeostasis. 30 Meanwhile, muscle glycogen is a metabolic primer for fat oxidation, and glucose deficiency reduces the capacity of fat oxidation to supply energy. Other studies have reported that, in addition to being an important metabolic fuel during prolonged strenuous exercise and implicated in the etiology of muscle fatigue, glycogen may play a key role in the regulation of metabolic and cellular processes within skeletal muscle. 31

Insulin has positive effects on skeletal muscle by inhibiting glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis, promoting glucose transport into skeletal muscle, stimulating glycolysis, and promoting glycogen synthesis. 32 The loss of skeletal muscle tissue reduces glycogen storage, leading to an increase in blood glucose concentration in the body and insulin resistance. Excessive advanced glycation end products disrupt cellular function and cause cell death, leading to declines in skeletal muscle function and muscle strength and, ultimately, sarcopenia. 33 , 34 , 35 Insulin resistance has been found to inhibit phosphoinositol 3‐kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt) signaling, leading to muscle atrophy. 36 , 37

Effects of EZF on muscle metabolomics

The distinguished metabolites identified in metabolomics also display energy relationships. As shown in Table S4, significant changes in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP) were detected in muscle metabolism, which is closely related to energy metabolism and belongs to nucleotide class substances. In energy metabolism, ATP appeared multiple times as a potential biomarker, with significant differences between the EZL and EZH groups. ATP and ADP are substances that store and convert energy. Nucleotide substances detected in muscle metabolism, such as ATP, ADP, and guanosine triphosphate (GTP), participate in the body's energy metabolism, and are closely related to the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA). 38

In this study, the most significantly related metabolic pathways enriched were sphingolipid metabolism, spinatoside, and 2‐Aminio‐1,3,4‐octadecanetriol, which increased significantly after administration. Sphingolipids are essential components of the cell membrane and important signal transduction molecules involved in cellular processes. It is a class of lipid molecules containing long‐chain bases and fatty amide skeletons, such as ceramides, glycolipids, and sphingomyelins. Studies suggest that sphingolipid signaling affects skeletal muscle contractile function through oxidation‐dependent and oxidation‐independent mechanisms. Long‐term effects can also alter the type of myofibers and muscle quality, resulting in affecting skeletal muscle metabolism. 39 Sphingolipid metabolites not only participate in regulating the development, adaptation, and repair processes of skeletal muscle after damage but also play an important role in the plasticity of skeletal muscle and the regulation of insulin metabolism. 40

Sphingolipids also play an important role in bone remodeling and bone disease. 41 Recent studies indicate the multifaceted influence of the sphingolipid on osteoblasts and osteoclasts, as well as the pivotal interaction underlying bone homeostasis–osteoblast and osteoclast crosstalk, and highlight the multifaceted roles of sphingolipid metabolism in bone remodeling. 42 , 43 Sphingosine‐1‐phosphate (S1P) is a bioactive sphingolipid that plays a central role in bone diseases, including OP. S1P acts as an osteoclast–osteoblast coupling factor to promote osteoblast proliferation and bone formation. 44

Sphingolipid metabolism may offer new possibilities for OP treatment. The OS proposal is based on the common pathological and physiological basis of SP and OP, which share a pathogenesis and adverse health consequences. 45 They include the influence of mechanical forces, genetic factors, endocrine regulation, nutritional imbalance, fat invasion, etc. 46 The main regulatory mechanisms of EZF on OP included PI3K‐Akt, Hypoxia‐Inducible Factor‐1 (HIF‐1), Mechanistic Target of Rapamycin (mTOR), the rheumatoid arthritis pathway, and the insulin resistance pathway. The mechanism of the anti‐osteoporotic effect of EZF promotes osteoblast formation, inhibits osteoclast activity by affecting the OPG/RANK/RANKL signaling axis, 47 and improves bone remodeling by activating Sirt1/PGC‐1α/Glut4. 48

Correlation analysis demonstrated that the identified differential metabolites were significantly correlated with skeletal muscle mass, ROS production, muscle fibrosis, and mitochondrial function, suggesting that these metabolites may play important roles in the progression of skeletal muscle lesions.

CONCLUSION

In this study, the mechanism by which EZF treats OS was explored. The influence of EZF on gastrocnemius muscle quality and mitochondrial function is important. The energy metabolites were significantly associated with EZF treatment for OS. Sphingolipid metabolism is a key pathway in muscle and may present a new therapeutic target in OP treatment. Thirteen metabolites may play important roles in the progression of skeletal muscle lesions. In summary, this study provides insights and methods for the treatment of OS with EZF. Further molecular biological validation of key proteins and pathways in muscles can be carried out in the future.
