Life-Threatening Hemoptysis Secondary to a Pulmonary Artery Retained Calcified Fibrin Sheath Embolus Related to a Central Venous Catheter: A Case Report
Authors: Hwangseon Ju, Min Ji Son, Seung Min Yoo, Kwan Wook Kim, Ah-Young Kwon, Seung-Yul Lee, Charles S White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A 45-year-old man presented to the emergency department with massive hemoptysis. His medical history included chronic renal failure, ischemic heart failure, and permanent catheter exchange 2 months prior to presentation. CT revealed elongated filling defects in the left lower lobe pulmonary artery, characterized by central low attenuation and peripheral rim-like calcifications, with wedge-shaped consolidation suggestive of pulmonary infarction. The differential diagnosis based on CT findings was a calcified pulmonary embolus or a fractured and embolized perm catheter tip, either of which could have caused the pulmonary infarction. The patient underwent an emergency left lower lobectomy and pulmonary thromboendarterectomy. The pathological diagnosis was a retained fibrin sheath embolism. Although rare, an embolized retained fibrin sheath should be suspected in patients with a central venous catheter when a calcified pulmonary embolus is observed on CT.

INTRODUCTION

Fibrin sheaths may form around long-term indwelling central venous catheters (CVCs). Recurrent irritation of the CVC over the venous wall can cause endothelial injury. The recovery response to endothelial injury, thrombus formation, collagen deposition, and/or calcification may lead to fibrin sheath formation (1). The fibrin sheath attached to the CVC often demonstrates a mesh-like connection to the venous wall. The fibrin sheath often remains during CVC removal because of its firm adherence to the venous wall, which results in the formation of a retained fibrin sheath (RFS) (12). RFS may be associated with infection, venous stenosis or obstruction, and, rarely, embolization. Only a few case reports in the English literature have described pulmonary embolism secondary to RFS (34567), and none have documented cases presenting with massive hemoptysis. Herein, we report a case of RFS pulmonary embolism presenting with massive hemoptysis in a patient with a history of long-term CVC use and exchange.

CASE REPORT

A 45-year-old man presented to the emergency department with massive hemoptysis. The hemoptysis started 1 week prior and progressively worsened, reaching approximately 200 mL/day on the day of presentation. The patient’s medical history included chronic renal failure, ischemic heart failure, and permanent catheter exchange via the right internal jugular vein 2 months earlier due to catheter malfunction. Subsequently, the exchanged perm catheter functioned normally. Enhanced chest CT was performed at the emergency department to evaluate massive hemoptysis. The CT revealed elongated filling defects (approximately 40 mm in length) in the left lower lobe pulmonary artery, characterized by central low attenuation with peripheral rim-like calcifications. Subpleural consolidation and ground-glass attenuation with internal air pockets were also observed in the left lower lobe, suggesting pulmonary infarction (Fig. 1). Precise evaluation of the presence of a fibrin sheath around the CVC was limited due to motion artifacts on CT. No calcified lesion was observed within the left pulmonary artery on the previous non-enhanced chest CT performed 1.5 months earlier (Fig. 2). However, tubular calcification was observed around the perm catheter in the right brachiocephalic vein on that same prior scan, suggesting a calcified fibrin sheath or RFS (Fig. 2); these findings were overlooked at the time. The differential diagnosis based on CT findings was a calcified pulmonary embolus or a fractured and embolized catheter tip, leading to pulmonary infarction. Given the patient’s multiple serious comorbidities and the severity of the hemoptysis, a multidisciplinary team, including cardiac and chest surgeons and cardiologists, determined that emergency left lower lobectomy and pulmonary embolectomy, with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support, was preferred over conservative management. Emergent left lower lobectomy and pulmonary thromboendarterectomy were performed on the same day. The pathological report revealed a calcified thrombus with a fibrin sheath and collagen deposition in the left lower lobe of the pulmonary artery, leading to pulmonary infarction (Fig. 3). The patient was discharged without further hemoptysis or complications.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CHA University Bundang Medical Center (IRB No. 2026-01-022). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

DISCUSSION

This case underscores that embolized RFS can present not only as an incidental calcified pulmonary embolus but also as a life-threatening cause of massive hemoptysis requiring surgical intervention. A previous study indicated that the prevalence of RFS was relatively high (13.6%, n = 20/147) in enrolled patients. In this study, only long-term indwelling catheters (>7 days), such as tunneled catheters, peripherally inserted central catheters, and implantable ports, were used. In that study, calcification was high (45%, n = 9/20) in patients with RFS (1). The most common CT appearance of RFS is an irregular tubular or linear filling defect with or without calcification (1). The most common location of RFS was the right internal jugular vein followed by the brachiocephalic vein in the study (1). In contrast to the relatively high prevalence of RFS, embolized RFS of the pulmonary artery is extremely rare, with only a few case reports (34567). A possible explanation for the discrepancy in the prevalence between RFS and embolized RFS is the nature of the fibrin sheath. The fibrin sheath often has a mesh-like appearance with firm adherence to the venous wall (12). The texture and configuration of the RFS may prevent embolization of the pulmonary artery (2). Tubular calcification was observed around the perm catheter at the right brachiocephalic vein, suggesting a calcified fibrin sheath or RFS on the previous non-enhanced chest CT; however, we overlooked the CT findings at the time, potentially due to misinterpretation as a motion or streak artifact related to the thick perm catheter. Regarding the differential diagnosis of a calcified pulmonary embolus, we could not completely exclude the possibility of a fractured catheter tip. Previous studies indicated that a calcified fibrin sheath can be misdiagnosed as a fractured catheter tip (78). In a retrospective review of CT images, we found that a diameter difference between the proximal and distal ends of the calcified embolus was noted, such as non-uniform diameter, suggesting a low probability of a fractured catheter tip (1), and that the fractured catheter tip may demonstrate equal diameter at the proximal and distal ends of the calcified embolus. In previous cases of fractured catheter tip embolism, a disrupted catheter tip was demonstrated on chest radiography (910). In contrast, no evidence of a fractured catheter tip was noted on chest radiographs in our case.

In conclusion, although rare, embolized RFS should be suspected in patients with CVC when a calcified pulmonary embolus is observed on CT.
