From Schreier graphs to secure s-boxes using a group-theoretic design framework
Authors: Abdul Razaq, Muhammad Ramzan, Qin Xin, Hanan Alolaiyan, Dilshad Alghazzawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Secure substitution boxes (S-boxes) are a fundamental design element of modern symmetric cryptography because they provide the necessary nonlinearity to achieve confusion. This paper presents a new \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8\times\:8$$\end{document} S-box construction framework, which combines group theory, graph theory and cryptography. The construction starts from the action of the modular transformation group on the projective line over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{257}$$\end{document}, which produces Schreier graphs whose cycle structure is used to form an initial S-box. The design is then refined through a non-abelian permutation group acting on S-box positions. The proposed S-box is rigorously verified against conventional cryptographic criteria, including nonlinearity, differential uniformity, strict avalanche criterion, bit independence criterion, and linear approximation probability, which demonstrate that it is highly resistant to classical attacks. Besides, texture analysis is performed using the S-box in the image encryption setting that completely relies on substitution. The findings indicate enhanced randomness and reduced pixel correlation, as measured by gray-level co-occurrence matrix characteristics, such as contrast, correlation, energy, homogeneity, and entropy. The proposed methodology provides a strong theoretical foundation and demonstrates effectiveness for secure cryptographic and image processing applications.

Introduction

Background

With the rapid growth of digital communication and data exchange, there is a need to safeguard sensitive and confidential data1–3. Cryptographic algorithms can be classified into two main categories: symmetric and asymmetric encryption algorithms. The most widely recognized symmetric encryption algorithms are the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) and the Data Encryption Standard (DES)1. DES was created by IBM and became a U.S. federal standard in 1977. DES is now considered deprecated for modern cryptographic use because of its limited key length, although DES-based implementations may still be encountered in some legacy systems. Triple DES was later introduced as a transitional DES-based variant, but it is also no longer recommended for new encryption applications.

The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) was chosen by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) in 2001. Since then, AES has become widely used not only in the United States, but also globally. The key size of an encryption algorithm plays an important role in determining its security strength. AES is available in three different key lengths: 128 bits, 192 bits, and 256 bits. Claude Shannon2 proposed that a secure cryptosystem should primarily be based on two basic principles: confusion and diffusion. The S-box is a very important component of cryptographic algorithms, as it offers the required confusion property due to its nonlinear nature. In most symmetric ciphers such as AES and DES, it is the main element that causes this confusion. Being in many cases the only nonlinear component, it creates a complex relationship between plaintext and ciphertext. In one round of AES, four different operations are performed: SubBytes, ShiftRows, MixColumns, and AddRoundKey3–5. The S-box is applied in the SubBytes step, which is the only nonlinear transformation among these four. Cryptanalysis has shown that weaknesses in S-box design are a major vulnerability of DES.

Therefore, the creation of secure cryptographic algorithms needs not only sound approaches to the design of S-boxes, but also sound criteria to assess them in terms of nonlinearity, algebraic degree, differential uniformity, and the strict avalanche criterion. This research paper develops a cryptographically secure 8 × 8 S-box and discusses its security properties.

Literature review

Khan and Shah6 suggested a new S-box design based on a fractional Rossler chaotic system that exhibited a high level of nonlinearity and was deemed appropriate for secure communication based on a thorough cryptographic study. Ahmad et al.7 proposed an effective 8 × 8 S-box architecture based on chaos theory and the Travelling Salesman Problem, which was shown to have better cryptographic measures than recent methods. Ullah et al.8 proposed a novel process of creating S-boxes using chaotic dynamics with a higher chaotic range. Their design had high cryptographic properties in various performance tests. Ahmed et al.9 proposed a cryptographic design technique based on an S-box design that integrates a discrete chaotic map and the firefly algorithm to optimize cryptographic performance, minimize a fitness function, and satisfy fundamental evaluation criteria such as nonlinearity and differential uniformity. The S-box generation technique presented in10 takes advantage of the activity of the modular group on the graph represented by adjacency matrices on finite fields, which leads to high-quality cryptographic performance and feasible efficiency in image encryption. Siddiqui et al.11 proposed an S-box design that used a modified Pascal triangle and elliptic curves and showed better nonlinearity and resistance to statistical attacks than more traditional S-boxes such as AES and Gray. Hayat et al.12 introduced a new dynamic S-box construction based on elliptic curves and total orderings over finite rings, which offered strong security properties with better performance than most current S-box generators. Fadhil et al.13 created secure S-boxes with good statistical characteristics, a fast inverse S-box, and the ability to be used in lightweight cryptographic systems using a 1D chaotic system. Another group-theoretic approach to building highly nonlinear S-boxes suggested by Razaq et al.14 demonstrated better cryptographic characteristics than the AES S-box and was effective in secure image encryption operations. Mahboob et al.15 combined finite field theory and permutation algorithms to design a secure S-box, the strength of which was validated by various statistical and cryptographic tests. Arshad16 proposed a new S-box design using elliptic curve points and group theory that showed great nonlinearity and was applicable in high-security image steganography. Abd-El-Atty17 suggested a quantum-inspired S-box based on quantum walks and PSO, which attained high entropy and security in image cryptosystems. Ullah et al.18 proposed a new S-box construction based on the fractional-order Rabinovich-Fabrikant chaotic system, improved with key-based permutation, with better statistical performance and effectiveness in secure image encryption applications. Tita et al.19 emphasised the use of S-boxes with low correlation values based on irreducible polynomials, which are appropriate for enhancing encryption against contemporary cyber threats. Liu et al.20 built secure keyed S-boxes in a systematic way through 2D chaotic maps and Boolean functions that were found to be more resistant to known cryptographic attacks and statistical weaknesses. The algorithm proposed by Shadab et al.21 provided a scalable and secure algorithm for S-box design, which was tested by comparing it to several cryptographic benchmarks. A secure S-box design with a 1D hybrid chaotic map and finite field theory was introduced by Jamal et al.22, and this design was found to have high nonlinearity and to remove the weaknesses of fixed points and short cycles. Bilal et al.23 designed a dynamic S-box based on Möbius transformations and elliptic curves, which is resistant to cryptanalytic attacks and can be used to perform encryption in real time. In24, finite algebraic structures are employed to construct Galois-field-based S-boxes and P-boxes for multiple color image encryption, enhancing confusion, diffusion, and resistance against common cryptographic attacks. Razaq et al.25 proposed a chaotic model that combines the Lorenz and Hénon systems to create powerful S-boxes with effective cryptographic properties. The hybrid chaotic map approach was utilized by Tolpa et al.26 to generate secure lightweight S-boxes to be integrated into low-resource cryptographic devices. Zhao et al.27 proposed an alternative encryption algorithm based on a complete chaotic map and dynamic S-boxes in order to achieve better image protection and anti-attack capabilities. Qayyum et al.28 proposed a finite-algebraic-structure-based multiple color image encryption scheme using Galois-field-driven S-boxes and P-boxes to improve confusion, diffusion, and resistance against common cryptographic attacks. The literature review shows that recent studies are dominated by chaos-based, optimization-based, and elliptic-curve-driven constructions, whereas comparatively fewer works develop S-boxes through an explicitly reproducible group- and graph-theoretic framework.

Motivation, research gap, and contributions

Most of the current methods are based on randomness or heuristic optimization, which are not necessarily well-grounded in mathematics or easily reproducible. Moreover, group-theoretic and graph-based constructions, which are potentially fruitful, have not been exhaustively studied in a coherent framework that systematically utilizes their structural richness. Specifically, the potential of modular transformation groups and Schreier graphs to produce highly nonlinear and well-structured S-boxes is not yet well developed in the literature. Also, little focus has been placed on the assessment of S-box performance in terms of texture and image-processing characteristics, which is a crucial aspect in real-world encryption.

In order to fill these gaps, this paper presents a new S-box construction model based on Schreier graph theory and modular transformation group actions. The main contributions of the work are as follows: (i) a mathematically rigorous approach for constructing permutation structures through the action of triangle groups on finite projective lines; (ii) the creation of an initial S-box based on cycle decomposition and its systematic improvement using a non-abelian permutation group; (iii) extensive testing using standard cryptographic metrics to demonstrate high resistance to known attacks; (iv) the use of texture-based analysis to confirm the usefulness of the proposed S-box in image encryption applications.

Proposed S-box method

The S-box construction is based on the action of the triangle group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\: < \sigma \:,\tau \:\:|\:\sigma ^{2} = \tau \:^{3} = \left( {\sigma \:\tau \:} \right)^{{16}} = 1 > \:$$\end{document}on the projective line over \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{F}_{257}$$\end{document}. Under this action, the permutation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}\tau\:$$\end{document} decomposes into sixteen disjoint cycles of length 16 and two fixed points, collectively covering all 258 elements.

Step A:

The initial \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8\times\:8$$\end{document} S-box is constructed from the cycle decomposition of the permutation\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{\upsigma\:}\tau\:$$\end{document}. Although the group action is defined on\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:{F}_{257}\cup\:\left\{\infty\:\right\}$$\end{document}, the elements \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:256\:$$\end{document}and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\infty\:$$\end{document} are excluded, as their presence is only to make the group action possible.

The permutation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\upsigma\:}\tau\:$$\end{document} decomposes into 18 disjoint cycles\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\{{\gamma\:}_{1},{\gamma\:}_{2},{\gamma\:}_{3},\dots\:,{\gamma\:}_{18}\}$$\end{document}, of which 16 have length 16 and two are fixed points. Their combined length is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:258$$\end{document}, exceeding the required 256 entries of an \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8\times\:8$$\end{document} S-box by two, which corresponds to the removal of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:256\:$$\end{document}and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\infty\:$$\end{document}.

Since the cycles are disjoint, they can be concatenated in arbitrary order, yielding 18! distinct sequences, which reduce to 256-element sequences after removing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:256\:$$\end{document}and\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\infty\:$$\end{document}. Here, 18! represents the theoretical number of possible cycle orderings. In practice, only a selected subset of these orderings was evaluated, and the final arrangement was chosen from the tested candidates on the basis of cryptographic performance. Each sequence is written row-wise into a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:16\times\:16$$\end{document} matrix to form an S-box. Among all tested arrangements, we adopt the ordering\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left[{\gamma\:}_{3},{\gamma\:}_{14},{\gamma\:}_{18},{\gamma\:}_{13},{\gamma\:}_{5},{\gamma\:}_{17},{\gamma\:}_{16},{\gamma\:}_{4},{\gamma\:}_{9},{\gamma\:}_{15},{\gamma\:}_{11},{\gamma\:}_{2},{\gamma\:}_{1},{\gamma\:}_{7},{\gamma\:}_{8},{\gamma\:}_{10},{\gamma\:}_{12},{\gamma\:}_{6}\right],$$\end{document}

which produces the strongest cryptographic performance. The resulting S-box, shown in Table 3, achieves a nonlinearity of 108.

Step B:

In the second stage, the initial S-box is further enhanced by applying a structured family of permutations generated by the group\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:G={C}_{3}\times\:{C}_{3}\times\:{C}_{15}\times\:{S}_{3}$$\end{document}

This group is generated by three permutations a,b, and c acting on the index set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\{0,\dots\:,255\}.$$\end{document} Their cycle decompositions are:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a=\left(\mathrm{5,251,106}\right)\left(\mathrm{31,246,114}\right)\left(\mathrm{36,143,60}\right),$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:b=\left(\mathrm{5,251}\right)\left(\mathrm{53,83,162}\right)\left(\mathrm{8,242,78}\right)\left(\mathrm{12,26,109}\right)\left(\mathrm{18,112,146}\right)\left(\mathrm{42,147,227}\right),$$\end{document}\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:c=\left(\mathrm{5,251}\right)\left(\mathrm{64,76,238,111,67}\right)\left(\mathrm{71,199,237}\right)\left(\mathrm{98,115,256}\right).$$\end{document}

GAP is used here as a computational algebra system to verify the defining relations of the generated permutation group and to confirm its algebraic structure. According to GAP, the group G admits the presentation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\: < a,b,c:a^{3} = b^{6} = c^{{30}} = bcb^{{ - 1}} c^{{ - 1}} = ab^{2} a^{{ - 1}} b^{{ - 2}} = acba^{{ - 1}} c^{{ - 1}} b^{{ - 1}} = ab^{{ - 1}} aca^{{ - 1}} c^{{ - 1}} a^{{ - 1}} b = 1 > ,$$\end{document}

and is a non-abelian group of order 810

Every element of G only acts on the positions of S-box entries, but not the values; thus, bijectivity is always maintained. Suppose that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document} is the 256-element first S-box generated by the triangle-group cycles. We flatten the S-box into a vector:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}=\left\{{s}_{0},{s}_{1},{s}_{2},\dots\:,{s}_{255}\right\}.$$\end{document}

The action of any permutation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:g\in\:G$$\end{document} is:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{g}\left[i\right]={S}_{0}\left[{g}^{-1}\left(i\right)\right],$$\end{document}

i.e., the value that was initially in position \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{g}^{-1}\left(i\right)$$\end{document} of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document} is moved to position i of the new S-box. This generates a systematic positional rearrangement that preserves all 256 distinct output values.

G is non-abelian, so its 810 elements generate a large set of rearrangements, allowing an extensive search space of possible S-box candidates. All group elements were applied to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{S}_{0}$$\end{document}, and each of the resulting S-boxes was tested with nonlinearity, SAC, BIC, differential uniformity, and linear approximation probability.

Of all candidates, the permutation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}^{2}{b}^{5}{c}^{17}$$\end{document} gives the best overall cryptographic performance, with an average nonlinearity of 111.25. Bit independence criterion (BIC)

Webster and Tavares31 proposed the Bit Independence Criterion (BIC), which is a necessary condition in the evaluation of the security of cryptographic S-boxes. It provides that the avalanche variables of the various output bits must be statistically independent. This property is assessed by calculating the correlation coefficients of all pairs of avalanche variables, which quantify their level of dependence.

Formally, a vectorial Boolean function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:F=\left({f}_{1},{f}_{2},\dots\:,{f}_{n}\right):{\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}}^{n}\to\:{\left\{\mathrm{0,1}\right\}}^{n}$$\end{document} satisfies the BIC: given two different output bits \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{j}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{k}\:\left(j\ne\:k\right)$$\end{document}, the Boolean function \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{f}_{j}⨁{f}_{k}$$\end{document} satisfies both the strict avalanche criterion and high nonlinearity. This guarantees that variations in one input bit cause independent and unpredictable variations in other output bits, which increases diffusion.

Linear approximation probability (LAP)

Another aim of S-box design is to create a highly nonlinear connection between the input and output of a cipher, which produces ciphertext that appears random to an attacker. Linear cryptanalysis can be used to exploit any linearity in this mapping. The Linear Approximation Probability (LAP)32 is defined as follows:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:LAP=\underset{\alpha\:,\beta\:\ne\:0}{\mathrm{max}}\left|\frac{\#\left\{x\in\:X|x.\alpha\:=S\left(x\right).\beta\:\right\}}{{2}^{n}}-\frac{1}{2}\right|,$$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\alpha\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\beta\:\:$$\end{document}are the input and output masks, respectively. A smaller LAP means that the S-box is more resistant to linear attacks. In the case of the proposed S-box, the theoretical maximum LAP is 0.5, but the actual maximum is 0.1328, which is significantly lower than 0.5, indicating that there is very little bias and a high resistance to linear cryptanalysis.

Recent algebraic image-encryption schemes based on non-chain rings49, DNA encoding50, and Henon map51 typically combine S-box construction with additional permutation, diffusion, or XOR layers. In such frameworks, the reported security behaviour reflects the combined effect of the full encryption pipeline rather than the isolated strength of a single 8 × 8 S-box. By contrast, the present work focuses on the nonlinear component itself and shows that the proposed S-box alone already attains an average nonlinearity of 111.25. At the same time, the dynamic and key-dependent features used in some algebraic image ciphers remain an interesting direction for future extensions of the present framework.

Table 11 shows that the suggested S-box significantly alters the texture of all test images. The contrast values in the original images are relatively low (e.g., 49.632 in the case of Peppers), and correlation is extremely high (maximum 0.98857), which means that there is a high similarity between adjacent pixels. Following the implementation of the suggested S-box, contrast significantly increases (e.g., 7849.7 in the case of Peppers), and correlation reduces significantly (to 0.19131), which indicates a high level of reduction in pixel dependency.

It should be noted that the entropy does not change in all S-boxes. The reason is that the substitution operation is bijective and leaves the histogram of pixel intensities unchanged; thus, entropy, which is only sensitive to the gray-level distribution, is also invariant under substitution-only transformation.

The same can be said about the Rice and Fabric images, in which the contrast increases to 8393.2 and 9310.1, respectively, and the correlation reduces to 0.12631 and 0.055213, respectively. Concurrently, the homogeneity values also decrease significantly in all images (e.g., from 0.4617 to 0.21484 in Peppers), which means that smooth texture structures are lost. Such transformations confirm that the given S-box is indeed very effective in breaking local intensity associations and introducing a higher degree of randomness into the image.

By comparison, the AES and Hayat S-boxes have similar behavior in all three images. In certain instances, their contrast values are slightly higher, while correlation and homogeneity show similar decreasing trends.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present a new method for constructing a cryptographically sound \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:8\times\:8$$\end{document} S-box by utilizing both group theory and graph theory. The technique described works by applying the modular transformation group to the projective line over a finite field, and the resulting Schreier graphs, together with their strong structural properties, are then used to derive permutation cycles, which are used to construct S-boxes. To further enhance the cryptographic strength, a refinement process is applied based on a non-abelian permutation group, ensuring improved diffusion and randomness without loss of bijectivity.

The proposed S-box has been tested with respect to standard cryptographic indicators, including nonlinearity, differential uniformity, strict avalanche criterion, bit-independence criterion, and linear approximation probability. The results show that the designed S-box is nonlinear and resistant to both linear and differential cryptanalysis, and its performance is balanced and competitive with existing methods. Furthermore, the use of texture-based analysis via substitution-only image experiments demonstrates that the proposed S-box enhances randomness and reduces statistical dependencies in image data.

These findings indicate that group-theoretic constructions are a promising direction for S-box design with strong mathematical security guarantees. One limitation is that the current design is neither dynamic nor key-dependent; however, variants of the design can be explored in future work. Another limitation is that the image analysis is based on substitution-only experiments, which are useful for isolating the effect of the S-box but do not represent a complete image-encryption system. Therefore, future work may extend the proposed framework toward key-dependent S-box generation, dynamic variants, and full cipher-level security evaluation.
