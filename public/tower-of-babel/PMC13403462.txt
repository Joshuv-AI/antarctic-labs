Causal mediation analysis of contact with COVID-19 patients and burnout syndrome in physiotherapists in Japan: secondary analysis of a multicentre observational study
Authors: Shuko Nojiri, Tetsuya Takahashi, Yupeng Wang, Narimasa Katsuta, Hiroyuki Daida, Yuji Nishizaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Physiotherapists played central roles in high-risk ‘red-zone’ care during COVID-19 and reported substantial distress, yet mechanisms linking exposure to burnout remain unclear.Objective: To examine whether the association between direct patient contact and burnout operates through a stress-related behavioural mediator (feeling like quitting/taking a break) and whether organisational resources moderate this pathway.Methods: We conducted a secondary analysis of a nationwide, multicentre, web-based survey of Japanese physiotherapists engaged in COVID-19 care (March 2021). Burnout was defined using the Japanese MBI-GS case algorithm. Counterfactual mediation analysis with bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstraps (5,000 resamples) was used to estimate the average causal mediation effect (ACME), average direct effect (ADE), and total effect on the risk-difference scale. Models were adjusted for age, sex, years in practice, and cohabitation.Results: Burnout prevalence was 19.4%. A small indirect effect was observed (ACME = 0.00932; 95% CI 0.00188–0.0200), whereas the direct and total effects were not statistically significant. Appreciation/respect was associated with attenuation of the indirect effect (ACME = 0.00007), whereas endorsement of enhanced reimbursement for two-therapist care of critically ill patients was associated with a larger indirect effect (ACME = 0.00637; 95% CI 0.00122–0.0100). Because the primary mediation analysis used a binary mediator and binary outcome with logistic link functions, a formal ρ-based sensitivity analysis for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding was not available under this specification.Conclusions: Among Japanese physiotherapists, the association between direct COVID-19 patient contact and burnout was consistent with an indirect pathway operating through a stress-related behavioural mediator. Recognition appeared to buffer this pathway, whereas reimbursement-linked resource strain appeared to amplify it. These findings suggest potentially relevant organisational targets for future intervention, but should be interpreted cautiously given the cross-sectional design and the causal assumptions required for mediation analysis.KEYWORDS: Causal mediation analysis, COVID-19, physiotherapists, burnout, occupational stress

Introduction

Physiotherapy-specific demands during the pandemic warrant further emphasis. Bedside mobilisation, airway clearance, and early rehabilitation for patients with acute respiratory failure required prolonged proximity and frequent manual handling under layers of personal protective equipment (PPE), often for multiple sessions per day. Such work combined sustained physical exertion with high cognitive demands, including dynamic risk assessment, infection-control choreography, and communication through masks and shields, plausibly elevating perceived threat and effort–recovery imbalance (McEwen, 1998; Thomas et al., 2020; Yang & Chang, 2020). In Japan, where rehabilitation coverage and workforce density are comparatively high (Yamaguchi et al., 2022), redeployment to COVID-19 units and the need to maintain non-COVID rehabilitation likely added to workload complexity. Japanese case reports have described early mobilisation and respiratory rehabilitation for patients with COVID-19 in acute care settings, often delivered by physiotherapists in high-acuity wards (Sakai et al., 2021). The Japanese Physical Therapy Association has explicitly acknowledged these frontline roles and called attention to the pandemic-related burden on physiotherapists (Japanese Physical Therapy Association, 2021). Early PPE shortages and training variability further disrupted routines (Inaba et al., 2020), and overtime norms have been associated with greater psychological distress (Ishida et al., 2020). Within Job Demands–Resources and Conservation of Resources frameworks, these conditions represent high, chronic demands with inconsistent access to replenishing resources (Bakker & Demerouti, 2023; Hobfoll, 2011; Kalimo et al., 2003), thereby increasing vulnerability to burnout and withdrawal. From a psychotraumatology perspective, repeated close-contact exposure to severely ill patients and to family distress under visitation restrictions may also have introduced elements of secondary traumatic stress and moral injury, particularly when perceived recognition or fairness was limited (Coimbra et al., 2024; Greenberg, 2020; Williamson et al., 2020).

Healthcare professionals experienced marked psychological burden during the COVID-19 pandemic, including anxiety, depressive symptoms, post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS), moral injury, and burnout (Maslach & Leiter, 2016). Meta-analyses have documented high prevalence of depression, anxiety, and PTSD among healthcare workers globally (Andhavarapu et al., 2022; Pappa et al., 2020). Repeated and cross-sectional surveys in Japan have likewise reported substantial psychological distress, insomnia, and turnover intention among hospital and oncology staff (Awano et al., 2022; Ishikawa et al., 2021; Kuriyama et al., 2023; Okamura et al., 2023), and qualitative work has highlighted themes of moral distress, isolation, and workload strain (Kotera et al., 2022). Physiotherapists may have been especially vulnerable because their work often involved prolonged close contact, repeated manual handling, and physically demanding rehabilitation procedures performed under PPE. These occupation-specific stressors may help explain why organisational resources could plausibly shape the pathway from patient contact to burnout. Clarifying these occupation-specific mechanisms may help inform more targeted organisational responses during future public health crises.

Existing Japanese studies have reported meaningful burnout among physiotherapists and have linked burnout to work-environment features such as staffing inadequacies, reduced recovery opportunity, and infection-control burden (Morisawa et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2020; Yang & Chang, 2020). However, these analyses were based primarily on correlational data, leaving unclear whether and how occupational exposures may translate into burnout and which modifiable processes might carry such effects. We adopted a counterfactual-based framework for causal mediation analysis (Imai et al., 2010; Pearl, 2001; Richiardi et al., 2013; Valeri & VanderWeele, 2013; VanderWeele, 2015, 2016; VanderWeele & Vansteelandt, 2014). This framework decomposes the total effect of an exposure into natural direct and indirect effects under explicit causal assumptions. For a broader introduction to statistical mediation in the social and health sciences, including traditional regression-based approaches, see MacKinnon (2008). Analyses were implemented in R using the mediation package (Tingley et al., 2014), and regression models followed standard recommendations for linear and generalised linear models in epidemiological research (Vittinghoff et al., 2012). In the present study, grounded in Job Demands–Resources and Conservation of Resources theories, we hypothesised that direct contact with patients with COVID-19 may increase perceived threat and effort–recovery imbalance, thereby giving rise to stress-related withdrawal cognitions such as feeling like quitting or taking a break. These withdrawal cognitions, in turn, may increase the risk of burnout. We make these assumptions explicit in a directed acyclic graph and interpret our mediation estimates as mechanism-oriented and hypothesis-generating rather than as definitive evidence of causality.

Methods

Design and setting

Morisawa et al. (2022) conducted a multicentre web-based survey of physiotherapists directly providing physiotherapy to patients with COVID-19 in ‘red zones’ across 487 hospitals in Japan. Using the Japanese version of the Maslach Burnout Inventory–General Survey, they found that 17.5% of 566 physiotherapists met criteria for burnout, which was associated with shorter physiotherapy experience, perceived burden of infection control procedures, lack of staffing standards according to bed numbers, and reduced time for self-improvement and relaxation. We used anonymized data from the Japanese Physical Therapy Association’s nationwide, multicentre, web-based survey of physiotherapists engaged in COVID-19 care (March 5–29, 2021). Responses were received from 4,169 physiotherapists representing 487 designated medical facilities across all prefectures (response rate 46%). Of these, 3,982 records with complete exposure, mediator, and outcome data were analysed.

Measures

Burnout was defined using the Japanese Maslach Burnout Inventory–General Survey (MBI-GS) case algorithm, namely high Exhaustion (≥ 3.5) plus either high Cynicism (≥ 3.5) or low Professional Efficacy (≤ 2.5), consistent with prior Japanese studies using the MBI-GS (Matsuo et al., 2021; Morisawa et al., 2022; Nagasaki et al., 2022). The MBI-GS comprises 16 items across three dimensions: Exhaustion (5 items), Cynicism (5 items), and Professional Efficacy (6 items). Each item is rated on a 7-point frequency scale from 0 (‘never’) to 6 (‘every day’), and subscale scores were computed as mean scores ranging from 0 to 6, following the recommendations of the MBI manual (Maslach et al., 2016). Higher scores on Exhaustion and Cynicism indicate more severe burnout, whereas lower scores on Professional Efficacy indicate greater burnout-related impairment. The Japanese version of the MBI-GS has been validated previously (Kitaoka, 2004). The binary burnout case variable used in the primary analysis followed the original survey scoring protocol.

For sensitivity analyses, we additionally constructed a continuous burnout severity indicator as the mean of the Exhaustion and Cynicism subscale scores. This continuous indicator was used as a related severity measure for robustness checks and should not be interpreted as the exact continuous counterpart of the binary burnout case definition, because the binary definition is based on a multi-component case algorithm rather than a single cutpoint on one continuous scale.

Exposure was direct contact with patients with COVID-19 (yes/no). The mediator was feeling like quitting or taking a day off because of stress (yes/no). We measured both relatively time-invariant sociodemographic characteristics and current work-environment characteristics. In the primary mediation models, however, adjustment variables were limited a priori to age, sex, years of experience as a physiotherapist, and the presence of cohabiting family members, because these variables were considered more likely to be antecedent to the exposure and less likely to be affected by it.

Analysis

We adopted a counterfactual-based causal mediation framework rather than the traditional regression-based ‘steps’ approach (Baron & Kenny, 1986), which has been criticised and largely superseded in recent methodological work (Hayes, 2009; Imai et al., 2010; VanderWeele, 2016; Xu et al., 2023). In the primary mediation models, we adjusted for age, sex, years of experience as a physiotherapist, and the presence of cohabiting family members, which were defined a priori as pre-exposure covariates unlikely to be affected by direct contact with patients with COVID-19. We measured (i) relatively time-invariant sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, certification status, years of experience, cohabiting family members), which were candidates for adjustment as pre-exposure covariates, and (ii) current work-environment characteristics (sleep, patient load, perceived stress, etc.), which were used to describe the sample and in exploratory analyses. These assumptions are summarised in the directed acyclic graph in Figure 1a. Because they cannot be fully verified in cross-sectional data, our estimates of the average causal mediation effect and average direct effect should be interpreted as reflecting whether the data are compatible with the hypothesised pathway, rather than as definitive causal effects. We estimated the Average Causal Mediation Effect (ACME), Average Direct Effect (ADE), and total effect using parametric models and the R mediation package with bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) 95% confidence intervals based on 5,000 bootstrap resamples, on the risk-difference scale. Moderated mediation analyses evaluated (i) appreciation/respect from others as desirable support and (ii) endorsement of double reimbursement for two-therapist treatment of critically ill patients. Both the mediator (frequent thoughts of quitting/taking leave) and the outcome (burnout case status) were modelled as binary variables using logistic regression. Because the current implementation of the mediation package does not provide the ρ-based sensitivity analysis (medsens function) for the combination of a binary mediator and a binary outcome with logistic link functions, no formal sensitivity analysis for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding was conducted. Our mediation estimates should therefore be interpreted with this limitation. Ethics approval and informed consent were obtained in the parent study.

For the MBI-GS items, missing responses were rare. Subscale scores were calculated only for participants who answered all items within that subscale; we did not apply a half-rule or other item-level imputation procedures. For the present mediation analysis, we required complete data on the exposure, mediator, outcome, and covariates. No imputation of missing values was performed. All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.4.1). A fully commented script implementing the mediation and regression models used in this study is provided in the Supplementary Material.

Supplementary analyses

We conducted several supplementary analyses to examine the robustness and interpretability of the primary findings. First, we evaluated sleep duration as an alternative candidate mediator using the same general causal mediation framework. Second, we examined moderated mediation according to endorsement of increased staffing support. Third, we repeated the analyses using an alternative continuous burnout severity indicator instead of the binary burnout case outcome. Because the binary and continuous outcomes are defined on different scales and contain different amounts of information, these analyses were not interpreted by directly comparing p-values across models. Rather, the continuous-outcome models were used as a qualitative robustness check to examine whether the overall direction and substantive interpretation of the mediation and moderation findings were broadly similar across alternative outcome parameterisations.

Results

Main analysis

Burnout prevalence was 19.4%. The average ACME was 0.00932 (95% CI 0.00188–0.0200), with near-identical estimates for the effect on the controls and the effect on the treated (Table 1). The ADE was small (0.00605; 95% CI −0.05603–0.0700), and the total effect was likewise small (0.01538; 95% CI −0.04756–0.0800). The proportion mediated (∼0.19) was imprecisely estimated, as expected when the total effect was close to zero.

Supplementary analyses

A directed acyclic graph (DAG)-informed mediation model evaluating sleep duration as an alternative candidate mediator did not provide clear evidence of an indirect effect. The ACME estimate was close to zero, and the corresponding confidence interval included values near zero. The ADE and total effect estimates were small, with confidence intervals also including values near zero.

Moderated mediation analyses suggested that endorsing increased staffing as desirable support was associated with a larger estimated indirect effect than not endorsing such support (Supplementary Figure S2; Supplementary Table S2).

In sensitivity analyses, we repeated the models using an alternative continuous burnout severity indicator instead of the binary burnout case outcome. Because the binary and continuous outcomes are defined on different scales and contain different amounts of information, these analyses were not interpreted by directly comparing p-values across models. Rather, the continuous-outcome models were used as a qualitative robustness check to examine whether the overall direction and substantive interpretation of the mediation and moderation findings were broadly similar across alternative outcome parameterisations.

Because the primary mediation analysis used a binary mediator and binary outcome with logistic link functions, a formal ρ-based sensitivity analysis for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding was not available under this specification.

Discussion

In this secondary analysis of Japanese physiotherapists engaged in COVID-19 care, the observed association between direct contact with patients with COVID-19 and burnout was consistent with an indirect pathway operating through a stress-related behavioural mediator, namely feeling like quitting or taking a break. Although the estimated indirect effect was small on the absolute risk scale, it was estimated with greater precision than the direct or total effects. The overall pattern is consistent with Job Demands–Resources and Conservation of Resources frameworks, in which sustained high demands and insufficient recovery or recognition may increase withdrawal-related cognitions and, in turn, burnout risk.

The moderation findings should be interpreted cautiously but are theoretically coherent. Appreciation and respect were associated with attenuation of the estimated indirect effect, suggesting that social recognition may weaken the extent to which withdrawal-related cognitions translate into burnout. By contrast, endorsement of enhanced reimbursement for two-therapist care of critically ill patients was associated with a larger estimated indirect effect, which may reflect perceived resource strain, fairness concerns, or moral burden in high-intensity care settings. These findings do not by themselves establish causal organisational mechanisms, but they are consistent with the possibility that social and structural resources shape how stress-related withdrawal is translated into burnout.

Similarly, in supplementary analyses, the estimated indirect effect was more evident in some organisational and work-content strata than in others, but subgroup differences should not be overinterpreted. In strata where the estimated ACME was small and imprecise, the data did not provide clear evidence for an indirect effect; this should not be interpreted as evidence that no such effect exists. More generally, the supplementary analyses are best viewed as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

The analyses using sleep duration as alternative candidate mediators did not support a clearly estimated indirect effect. One possible interpretation is that these variables, at least as measured here, may function more as downstream correlates or broad markers of work strain than as proximal mediators linking direct COVID-19 patient contact to burnout. By contrast, the behavioural mediator of feeling like quitting or taking a break may have been more proximally aligned with the hypothesised mechanism under stress.

The practical implications of these findings should also be stated with caution. Our results do not justify strong prescriptive conclusions from a single cross-sectional observational study. Rather, they suggest that organisational recognition, staffing support, and remuneration structures may be relevant targets for future intervention and evaluation (Maben et al., 2018). In this sense, the findings are consistent with potentially modifiable organisational processes, but prospective and interventional studies are needed before firm recommendations can be made.

Several limitations merit emphasis. First, the cross-sectional design precludes direct verification of temporal ordering among exposure, mediator, and outcome. Our mediation analyses therefore rely on theoretically motivated causal assumptions and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Second, although we adjusted for a limited set of plausible pre-exposure covariates, residual confounding remains possible. Third, the mediator was measured using a single dichotomous item, which may have introduced measurement error and attenuated associations. Fourth, because the primary mediation analysis used a binary mediator and binary outcome with logistic link functions, a formal ρ-based sensitivity analysis for unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding was not available under this specification. Finally, proportion-mediated quantities are unstable when the total effect is close to the null and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Overall, our findings support a cautious interpretation in which the association between direct COVID-19 patient contact and burnout among Japanese physiotherapists is compatible with an indirect stress-related behavioural pathway. Organisational recognition may buffer this pathway, whereas perceived resource strain may strengthen it. These observations provide a basis for future longitudinal and interventional studies to test whether modifying workplace resources can reduce burnout risk in crisis-care settings.

Conclusions

In Japanese physiotherapists providing COVID-19 care, the association between direct contact and burnout was transmitted mainly through a modifiable behavioural mediator. Recognition buffered – and reimbursement-linked resource strain amplified – this pathway. Trauma-informed organisational strategies that target withdrawal/avoidance processes and strengthen recognition, staffing and remuneration warrant prioritisation and prospective evaluation (Hobfoll et al., 2007; World Health Organization, War Trauma Foundation, & World Vision International, 2011).
