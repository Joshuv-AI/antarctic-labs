Dynamic Lagrange Multipliers in a Non-concave Utility Framework
Yang Liu, Alexander Schied, Zhenyu Shen

Abstract
In continuous-time portfolio selection for non-concave utility functions, the martingale duality approach is widely adopted in complete markets, while the dynamic programming approach may sometimes lead to singular solutions of the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. We propose “dynamic Lagrange multipliers” in a non-concave utility framework, bridging two approaches and demonstrating that the Lagrangian multiplier function (in the martingale duality approach) equals the conjugate dual point related to the value function (in dynamic programming), which is exactly its partial derivative with respect to wealth. Moreover, the dynamic multiplier process exhibits homogeneity via the optimal wealth and pricing kernel processes, offering intuitive economic interpretations as a dynamic shadow price of the envelope theorem. Finally, classical optimal results are recovered and numerically validated by non-concave utility examples. MSC(2010) : 91B16, 91G10. JEL Classifications : G11, C61. Keywords: Non-concave utility optimization; Dynamic Lagrange multipliers; Stochastic control; Martingale duality approach; Legendre-Fenchel transform; Dynamic programming.



1
Introduction

Utility maximization is a fundamental problem in continuous-time portfolio selection. We begin with formulating the
Merton 1969
;
Merton 1971
problem:

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[0,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[U(X_{T})|X_{0}=x_{0}\right],

(1)

where
T>0
is the terminal time horizon and
\{X_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is the wealth process governed by
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. For a fixed time
t\in[0,T)
,
\mathcal{V}[t,T]
denotes the set of admissible controls
\boldsymbol{\pi}
from time
t
to
T
. Here,
x_{0}
represents the initial wealth and
U
is the utility function.

Under the standard framework with a strictly concave utility, the seminal works of
Merton 1969
;
Merton 1971
pioneered the stochastic control approach to utility maximization. This approach formulates the problem within a dynamic programming framework, where solutions are derived through the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation. It yields both the value function (
u(t,x)
, representing the optimal expected utility) and the optimal portfolio strategy (
\boldsymbol{\Pi}(t,X_{t}^{*})
, expressed as a feedback control of the optimal wealth process
\{X_{t}^{*}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
). The martingale duality approach, developed in
Pliska 1986
,
Karatzas et al. 1987
, and
Cox and Huang 1989
, offers an alternative approach and remains valid beyond Markovian market settings. This method utilizes the pricing kernel process
\{\xi_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
to establish a risk-neutral measure, employing duality analysis and martingale representation to characterize the optimal terminal wealth. The optimal wealth process and the portfolio process
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{*}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
are then constructed through replication arguments.

Financial practice often requires extensions beyond concave utilities. The best-known example are the
S
-shaped utility functions proposed by
Markowitz 1952
and in the prospect theory of
Kahneman and Tversky 1979
. Another example arises in hedge fund compensation structures. Convex schemes (e.g., call options in
Carpenter 2000
and first-loss contracts in
He and Kou 2018
) are commonly employed to align hedge fund managers’ incentives with fund performance, but create non-concavities in objective functions with respect to terminal wealth. To address this, the prevailing methodology applies concavification techniques to transform the utility
U
into its concave envelope (the smallest concave function larger than or equal to
U
), after which the martingale duality approach is implemented; see
Carpenter 2000
,
Reichlin 2013
,
Bichuch and Sturm 2014
,
Dong and Zheng 2020
, and
Liang and Liu 2020
. In contrast, the dynamic programming approach faces analytical challenges due to the singularity in the associated HJB equations in such cases. Overall, these motivations and challenges have spurred extensive research in non-concave utility optimization; see
Chen et al. 2019
,
Nguyen and Stadje 2020
,
Dai et al. 2022
,
Liang et al. 2025
,
Qian and Yang 2026
, and
Qin et al. 2026
.

Although both martingale duality and dynamic programming approaches yield identical solutions (
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}(t,X_{t}^{*})
) in the classical framework, their analytical structures and economic interpretations differ fundamentally. The key distinction lies in their temporal focus:
While the martingale method solves the portfolio problem in one step by optimizing a static terminal payoff, dynamic programming requires that optimality be verified locally and infinitesimally along the whole time horizon.
In this paper, our aim is to bridge these approaches and establish their dynamic relationships.

Our contribution is to demonstrate the relationship between the dynamic Lagrange multiplier function
\mathcal{Y}(t,x)
in the martingale duality method and the conjugate dual point
\lambda(t,x)
related to the value function in dynamic programming, under a framework of non-concave utilities. We establish two main results: (i) the dynamic Lagrange multiplier function equals the conjugate dual point, which is exactly the partial derivative of the value function with respect to wealth:
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}u(t,x)=\lambda(t,x)=\mathcal{Y}(t,x)
; and (ii) the dynamic Lagrange multiplier process
\lambda(t,X^{*}_{t})
exhibits homogeneity via the optimal wealth and the pricing kernel:
\lambda(t,X^{*}_{t})=\lambda(0,x_{0})\xi_{t}
. These results can be seen as the dynamic version of the envelope theorem (well-known in economics) related to the Lagrange multiplier (known as the shadow price). Furthermore, we derive the optimal portfolio in a feedback form using these dynamic conjugate relationships (
\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}^{*}=\boldsymbol{\Pi}(t,X_{t}^{*})
). Specifically, a key challenge in our duality analysis in the non-concave utility framework is that the function obtained via the Legendre-Fenchel transform (denoted by
I
in Equation (
5
)) fails to be continuous, and consequently, it is not differentiable. To overcome this obstacle, we employ tools from distribution theory (
Stein and Shakarchi 2010
), which allow us to handle such irregularities in a rigorous manner.

The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
formulates the model setup and Section
3
introduces the tools of convex analysis. Section
4
presents the main theoretical results and Section
5
gives the detailed proof. Section
6
provides a numerical example that highlights the economic intuition. Additional proofs are deferred to the appendix.

2
Model Setting and Problem Formulation

Consider the filtered probability space
(\Omega,\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T},\mathbb{P})
satisfying the usual condition, in which
\{\mathbf{W}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.
We assume that the market contains
(d+1)
assets, denoted by
\{S_{i,t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
,
i=0,1,\ldots,d
. Among these assets,
\{S_{0,t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
represents the risk-free asset (i.e., bond) and the others are risky assets (i.e., stocks) denoted by
\{\mathbf{S}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}:=\{(S_{1,t},\ldots,S_{d,t})^{\top}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
. Let
\boldsymbol{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a constant vector and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a constant matrix. We further assume that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\top}\right)\boldsymbol{\eta}}\geqslant\varepsilon\|\boldsymbol{\eta}\|_{2}^{2}\hskip 11.74988pt\text{for any }\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

which implies that
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
is invertible.
The assets satisfy the Black-Scholes model

\mathrm{d}S_{0,t}=rS_{0,t}\mathrm{d}t,\hskip 11.74988pt\mathrm{d}S_{i,t}=\mu_{i}S_{i,t}\mathrm{d}t+\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{\top}S_{i,t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t},\hskip 11.74988pti=1,\ldots,d,

where
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}
denotes the
i
-th row of
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
. Let
\mathbf{1}_{d}=(1,\ldots,1)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Define the vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}-r\mathbf{1}_{d})
and the pricing kernel
\{\xi_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
by

\xi_{t}=\exp\left\{-\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)t-\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{t}\right\},

where
\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\theta_{i}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
is the Euclidean norm of the vector. Finally,
Harrison and Pliska 1981
and Chapter 18.5 of
Shreve 2004
show that such a market is complete, i.e., every contingent claim can be hedged. In fact, all the results in this paper can be generalized to the case where the market coefficients are deterministic processes. Still, we let them be constant numbers/vectors for notational simplicity.

Let
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{(\pi_{1,t},\ldots,\pi_{d,t})^{\top}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
denote the portfolio process, where the
i
-th component represents the money that the investor allocates to the
i
-th risky asset. A portfolio
\boldsymbol{\pi}=\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is called admissible if
\boldsymbol{\pi}
is an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
-progressively measurable
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued process and
\int_{0}^{T}\|\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\,\mathrm{d}t<\infty
almost surely. Let
\{X_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
denote the investor’s wealth process, whose dynamic is characterized by the following stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}X_{t}&=\left(r\left(X_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\mu_{i}\right)\mathrm{d}t+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}^{\top}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{W}_{t},\hskip 11.74988pt0\leqslant t\leqslant T,\\
X_{0}&=x_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

The investor is assumed to have a utility function
U:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
. Define the effective domain
\text{\rm dom\,}U:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}|U(x)>-\infty\}
. We further propose the following assumption on the utility function:

Assumption 1
.

The utility function
U:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
has the following properties:

(i)

There exists an interval
\text{\rm dom\,}U\subset\mathbb{R}
with its left endpoint
L
being finite and right endpoint being
\infty
, such that
U(x)=-\infty
for all
x\notin\text{\rm dom\,}U
and
U(x)
is finite otherwise. Hence,
\text{\rm dom\,}U=[L,\infty)
or
(L,\infty)
.

(ii)

U
is differentiable except at countably many points and

\lim_{x\uparrow\infty}U^{\prime}(x)=0
.

(iii)

U
is increasing (i.e., non-decreasing) and
\lim_{x\uparrow\infty}U(x)=\infty
.

(iv)

U
is upper semicontinuous.

(v)

There exist
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
such that
U(x)<Ax+B
for all
x\in\text{\rm dom\,}U
.

It is worth noting that the utility function can be discontinuous and non-concave over the entire domain. Moreover, these conditions guarantee the existence of the concave envelope of
U
,
which is the smallest concave function greater than or equal to
U
; see Equation (
4
) later.
Under the settings above, we investigate Problem (
1
).
A wealth process
\{X_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
is called admissible if it is driven by an admissible control and
X_{t}\in\text{\rm dom\,}U
for all
t\in[0,T]
. We define the set of all admissible terminal wealth variables as

\mathcal{X}_{T}:=\{X_{T}\mid\{X_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\text{ is an admissible wealth process}\}.

3
Preliminary Analysis and Methodological Foundations

To reveal the connection between the martingale duality and dynamic programming methods, we apply each separately to solve Problem (
1

Under our market setting, Problem (

\sup\Big\{\mathbb{E}[U(X)]\mid\text{$X$ is $\mathcal{F}_{T}$-measurable, $X\in\text{\rm dom\,}U$ $\mathbb{P}$-a.s., and $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{Q}}[e^{-rT}X]\leqslant x_{0}$}\Big\},

We apply the Legendre-Fenchel transform:

\displaystyle:=\sup_{x\in\text{dom }U}\{U(x)-xy\},

\displaystyle y>0,

\displaystyle U^{**}(x)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{y\in(0,\infty)}\{V(y)+xy\},

Here,

Lemma 1
.

Suppose that Assumption
1

(a)

(b)

The effective domain
\text{\rm dom }U^{**}

\text{\rm dom }U
.

(c)

The set

is open. In particular,

can be written as the disjoint union of countably many open intervals
(a_{k},b_{k})
,
k=1,2,\dots

(d)

is affine on each of the intervals
(a_{k},b_{k})
from part (c).

Despite the properties shown in Lemma
1

Assumption 2
.

The concave envelope is assumed to satisfy the following condition:

\lim_{x\uparrow\infty}(U^{**})^{\prime}(x)=0.

Moreover, if

U(x)=\begin{cases}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(n+(x-n)/n)\mathbbmss{1}_{[n,n+1)}(x),&\text{$x\geqslant 1$};\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

If
\text{\rm dom\,}U

4
Main Results

We first introduce the dynamic conjugate functions and reveal the connections between the martingale duality approach and the dynamic programming approach through these functions. Remarkably, we will show later in Lemma

For any

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v(t,y)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{U}}\left\{u(t,x)-xy\right\},\\
&i(t,y)=\arg\max_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{U}}\left\{u(t,x)-xy\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(13)

The conjugate dual point of the function

is

\lambda(t,x)=\arg\min_{y>0}\{v(t,y)+xy\},\hskip 11.74988pt0\leqslant t<T,\hskip 11.74988ptx\in\mathcal{D}_{U}.

(14)

Moreover, we define

,

, and

The conjugate function and conjugate point in (

and

. Moreover, as the functions are defined through the Legendre-Fenchel transform of the value function

Theorem 4

Suppose that Assumptions

-

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[V(yZ_{t,T})\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(I\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\right)-yZ_{t,T}I\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\right],

\displaystyle i(t,y)

Theorem 5

Suppose Assumptions

-
3

(i)

\mathcal{Y}(t,x)=\lambda(t,x)=\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}.

(15)

(ii)

Theorem
5
is the main result of our dynamic Lagrange multiplier theory for a non-concave utility. It shows that for a given wealth value

. Moreover,

In economics, the Lagrange multiplier corresponding to the utility maximization problem is called the

In our case,

On the other hand, for fixed

Finally, we provide the optimal results in the non-concave utility framework with our proposed dynamic Lagrange multiplier, which are different from those in the literature (e.g.,

5.1
Technical Discussion

In this subsection, we introduce the core concepts supporting our proofs: distribution theory and distributional derivatives (
Stein and Shakarchi 2010
).
In theory, the Schwartz space on
\mathbb{R}
, denoted by
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, is a space of test functions. For
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
,
\varphi
has derivatives of all orders and satisfies

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R},\alpha,\beta\leqslant N}|z^{\alpha}\varphi^{(\beta)}(z)|<\infty,\hskip 11.74988pt\forall N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}.

We can define a subset of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
by

\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty):=\left\{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}):\varphi(z)=0,\;\forall z\leqslant 0\text{ and }\lim_{z\downarrow 0}\left|z^{-\alpha}\varphi^{(\beta)}(z)\right|=0,\;\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}_{+}\right\}.

It is easy to check that
\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
, and we endow it with the relative topology. The space
\mathcal{S}_{0}^{\prime}(0,\infty)
of all continuous linear functionals on
\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
is called the space of
Schwartz distributions
or
tempered distributions
on
(0,\infty)
. For instance, consider a locally integrable function
H
on
(0,\infty)
. If there exists
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
such that

\lim_{z\downarrow 0}|z^{-\alpha_{0}}H(z)|=\lim_{z\uparrow\infty}|z^{-\alpha_{0}}H(z)|=0,

(16)

then
H
defines a continuous linear functional
\mathcal{H}
on
\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
that is given by

\mathcal{H}\left(\varphi\right)=\langle H,\varphi\rangle:=\int_{0}^{\infty}H(z)\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z<\infty,\hskip 11.74988pt\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty).

Remarkably, the distribution on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is called the
tempered distribution
, which is of polynomial growth at both negative and positive infinities. When restricting the domain of test functions to
(0,\infty)
, it is natural that the distribution should be of polynomial growth at the endpoints of the domain, i.e.,
0
and
\infty
. This is the reason we define the subspace
\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
.

Since
I
and
U^{**}\circ I
are decreasing, they are locally integrable on
(0,\infty)
. Under Assumption
3
, it is clear that both
I
and
U^{**}\circ I
satisfy the condition given by (
16
). On the other hand, we can compute the cumulative density function (CDF) of
Z_{t,T}
as follows:

\displaystyle F_{Z_{t,T}}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{\exp{\left\{-\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left(T-t\right)-\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}N\right\}\leqslant z}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left\{N\geqslant-\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\left(\log\left(z\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left(T-t\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=1-\Phi\left(\hat{d}\left(t,z\right)\right),

where
N
is a standard normal random variable,
\Phi
is the standard normal CDF, and
\hat{d}(t,z):=-\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\left(\log\left(z\right)+\left(r+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}/2\right)\left(T-t\right)\right)
.
Then by differentiating
F_{Z_{t,T}}
, we can get the probability density function (PDF)

f_{Z_{t,T}}(z)=\frac{1}{z\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\Phi^{\prime}\left(\hat{d}\left(t,z\right)\right)\text{ for }z>0\text{ and $0$ otherwise}.

Define
\psi(z):=zf_{Z_{t,T}}(z)
.
It is easy to check that
f_{Z_{t,T}}\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
and
\psi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle g(t,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y}\left\langle I,\frac{\psi}{y}\right\rangle<\infty,\hskip 11.74988ptu(t,x)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Y}\left(t,x\right)}\left\langle U^{**}\circ I,\frac{f_{Z_{t,T}}}{\mathcal{Y}\left(t,x\right)}\right\rangle<\infty,

which solve the finiteness problem pointed out in Remark
1
.
This also shows that
g(t,y)
is continuous in
y
and
u(t,x)
is continuous in
x
.
Notably, Assumption
3
also plays an important role later in the proof of Lemma
7
. Treating the expectations from the perspective of distribution theory, we can apply functional analysis techniques to exchange the order of differentiation and expectation. We will see this point later.

Remark 2
.

We have shown that we can treat
I
and
U^{**}\circ I
as Schwartz distributions, which is a crucial conclusion for later proofs. Here, the finiteness results,
g(t,y)<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}[U^{**}(I(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}))]<\infty
, are direct corollaries. A simpler way to show the finiteness is by noting that Assumption
3
gives

LZ_{t,T}\leqslant Z_{t,T}I(yZ_{t,T})\leqslant C_{0}y^{-M}Z_{t,T}^{1-M},

and that
I
is of polynomial decay at infinity. Then
Z_{t,T}I(yZ_{t,T})
is bounded by two log-normally distributed random variables and is thus integrable. This is similar for u(t,x).

Lemma 7
.

Suppose that Assumptions
1
-
3

We have the following identity:

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\mathbb{E}\left[U^{**}\left(I\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\right)\right]=y\frac{\partial g(t,y)}{\partial y},\hskip 11.74988pt(t,y)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{I}.

Proof.

Recall the distribution theory discussed in Section
5.1

\mathcal{I}
be the Schwartz distribution on
\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
defined by
I

\mathcal{I}\left(\varphi\right):=\langle I,\varphi\rangle=\int_{0}^{\infty}I(z)\varphi(z)\,dz,\hskip 11.74988pt\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty).

Then we can define the distributional (weak) derivative of

I^{\prime}
. We want to show that the following equation holds:

\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int_{0}^{\infty}I(yz)\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z=\int_{0}^{\infty}I^{\prime}(yz)z\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z,\hskip 11.74988pt\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty).

(17)

To show this, we begin by computing the right-hand side of the equation:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}I^{\prime}\left(yz\right)z\varphi(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\infty}I^{\prime}\left(u\right)\frac{u}{y}\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{y^{2}}\left\langle I^{\prime},u\mapsto u\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{y^{2}}\left\langle I,u\mapsto\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)+\frac{u}{y}\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\right\rangle,

where the last equality comes from the definition of the distributional derivative. We show some important properties before computing the left-hand side of Equation (
17

\varphi_{y}(z):=\varphi(z/y)

\mathcal{S}_{0}(0,\infty)
is closed under differentiation and polynomial multiplication.
Note that the linearity of the functional
\mathcal{I}

\frac{\mathcal{I}\left(\varphi_{y+h}\right)-\mathcal{I}\left(\varphi_{y}\right)}{h}=\mathcal{I}\left(\frac{\varphi_{y+h}-\varphi_{y}}{h}\right).

Taking

\frac{\partial\mathcal{I}(\varphi_{y})}{\partial y}=\mathcal{I}\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{y}}{\partial y}\right).

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\int_{0}^{\infty}I(yz)\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{1}{y}\int_{0}^{\infty}I(u)\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{y^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}I(u)\left(\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)+\frac{u}{y}\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{y^{2}}\left\langle I,u\mapsto\varphi\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)+\frac{u}{y}\varphi^{\prime}\left(\frac{u}{y}\right)\right\rangle.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(t,x,\varphi;\boldsymbol{\pi}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988pt+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\right)\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}.

Assume that
u(t,x)
is finite and continuous for all
(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{U}
. Then it is a viscosity solution of the following HJB equation:

-\frac{\partial\varphi(t,x)}{\partial t}-\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathcal{L}\left(t,x,\varphi;\boldsymbol{\pi}\right)=0,\hskip 11.74988pt(t,x)\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{U}.

(22)

Suppose that there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
t\in[0,T)
such that
u(t,\cdot)
is linear on
(a,b)
. It follows that
\frac{\partial^{2}u(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}=0
for
x\in(a,b)
.

We first show that
u(t,x)
is strictly increasing in
x
for fixed
t\in[0,T)
. The definition of
u(t,x)
and the attainability of the solution to Problem (
3
) show that there exists
\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]
such that
u(t,x)=J(t,x;\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
. From the definition of
U
and Equation (
2
), for two initial values
x_{1}<x_{2}
, we have
J(t,x_{1};\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})<J(t,x_{2};\bar{\boldsymbol{\pi}})
. This shows that for any
t\in[0,T)
,
u(t,\cdot)
is strictly increasing in
x
.

Since
u(t,x)
is strictly increasing for any
t\in[0,T)
,
\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}
is a positive constant and
\frac{\partial^{2}u(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}=0
for
x\in(a,b)
. Utilizing the distributional derivative, the differentiability of
u(t,x)
in
t
can be proved by a similar argument in the proof of Lemma
7
.
From Equations (
12
) and (
20
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathbb{E}\left[U^{**}\left(I\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{**}\left(I\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)z\right)\right)f_{Z_{t,T}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}z^{2}\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\frac{\partial\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}{\partial t}\left(I|_{\text{ctn}}\right)^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)z\right)f_{Z_{t,T}}(z)\,\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\hskip 11.74988pt+\frac{\partial\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}{\partial t}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}\left(I(a_{j}^{+})-I(a_{j}^{-})\right)\frac{a_{j}^{2}}{\mathcal{Y}(t,x)^{2}}f_{Z_{t,T}}\left(\frac{a_{j}}{\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 11.74988pt+\int_{0}^{\infty}U^{**}\left(I\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)z\right)\right)\frac{\partial f_{Z_{t,T}}(z)}{\partial t}\,\mathrm{d}z,

which is continuous in both
t
and
x
since each component is continuous in
t
and
x
.
Further, Proposition 4.5.6 in
Yong and Zhou 1999
gives an equivalent definition of viscosity solution, which utilizes the
second-order parabolic (super-)subdifferential
. In the case where
u(t,x)
is differentiable in
t
and twice differentiable in
x
at some point
(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})
, the second-order parabolic (super-)subdifferential simply consists of the classic derivatives of
u(t,x)
. Hence, the proposition shows that
u(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})
satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial t}\right|_{(t,x)=(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})}+\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\left(r\left(x-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}\pi_{i,t}\right)\left.\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}\right|_{(t,x)=(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})}\right.

(23)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}\right)\left.\frac{\partial^{2}u(t,x)}{\partial x^{2}}\right|_{(t,x)=(\tilde{t},\tilde{x})}\right\}=0.

We have shown that
u(t,x)
is continuously differentiable in
t
. For any
(\bar{t},\bar{x})
such that
u(\bar{t},\cdot)
is linear in a neighborhood of
\bar{x}
, the second-order partial derivative term in the Hamiltonian in Equation (
23
) vanishes. Thus, the following equation holds:

\left.\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial t}\right|_{(t,x)=(\bar{t},\bar{x})}+\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\left(r\left(x-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{i,t}\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}\pi_{i,t}\right)\cdot\left.\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}\right|_{(t,x)=(\bar{t},\bar{x})}\right\}=0.

Recall that
\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}|_{(t,x)=(\bar{t},\bar{x})}>0
. Since
\{\boldsymbol{\pi}_{t}\}_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}
can be unbounded, the Hamiltonian is unbounded, i.e., the supremum term equals
\infty
. For a function to be the viscosity solution of the HJB equation, it is supposed to be continuous and hence finite. This leads to a contradiction, which concludes the proof.

Numerical Example and Economic Insight

In this section, we present a numerical example to illustrate the economic insight of our main Theorem
5
.

We consider the non-concave and discontinuous utility
U:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

U(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&&x\in[0,1];\\
&\log(x)+1,&&x\in(1,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

We can directly compute the Legendre-Fenchel transform (
4
) of this utility function:

\displaystyle V(y)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\log(y),&&y\in(0,1);\\
&0,&&y\in[1,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988pt

\displaystyle I(y)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &y^{-1},&&y\in(0,1);\\
&0,&&y\in[1,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

(33)

\displaystyle U^{**}(x)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &x,&&x\in[0,1];\\
&\log(x)+1,&&x\in(1,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.\hskip 11.74988pt\hskip 11.74988pt

\displaystyle\Lambda(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &1,&&x\in[0,1];\\
&x^{-1},&&x\in(1,\infty).\end{aligned}\right.

Define the function

d(t,y):=\hat{d}\left(t,\frac{1}{y}\right)=-\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)(T-t)\right).

For fixed
t\in[0,T)
and
x\in[0,\infty)
, we can compute the value function:

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(I\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}\right)\right)\right]

(34)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[U^{**}\left(I\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\log\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}\right)\right)\mathds{1}_{\{\mathcal{Y}(t,x)Z_{t,T}<1\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\Phi\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)\right)\left(1-\log\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left(T-t\right)\right)

\displaystyle+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}\Phi^{\prime}\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right).

Next, we can compute the explicit form of
g(t,y)
:

g(t,y)=\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{t,T}I\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{y}\mathbb{P}\left\{yZ_{t,T}\in(0,1)\right\}=\frac{1}{y}\left(1-\Phi\left(d(t,y)\right)\right).

(35)

Then the partial derivative of
g(t,y)
with respect to
y
is

\frac{\partial g(t,y)}{\partial y}=-\frac{1}{y^{2}}\left(1-\Phi\left(d(t,y)\right)+\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\Phi^{\prime}\left(d(t,y)\right)\right).

(36)

Similarly, we can directly compute the partial derivative of
u(t,x)
with respect to
x
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}{\partial x}\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)^{-1}\left\{1-\Phi\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\right.

(37)

\displaystyle\left.\times\left(1-\log\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left(T-t\right)\right)+d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\Phi^{\prime}\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}{\partial x}\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)^{-1}\left(1-\Phi\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)+\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\left(d\left(t,\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)\right)}{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\right).

Recall the fact that

\frac{\partial\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}{\partial x}=\frac{1}{\left.\frac{\partial g(t,y)}{\partial y}\right|_{y=\mathcal{Y}(t,x)}}.

(38)

Substituting (
38
) into (
37
) and combining with (
36
), we get

\frac{\partial u(t,x)}{\partial x}=\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)^{2}\cdot\left(\mathcal{Y}(t,x)\right)^{-1}=\mathcal{Y}(t,x),

which validates the second equality in Equation (
15
) of Theorem
5
. To show the first equality in Equation (
15
), we try to find
\lambda(t,x)
by the definition in (
14
). From Theorem
4
, we can compute

as follows:

\displaystyle v(t,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[V\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}\left[\log\left(yZ_{t,T}\right)\mathds{1}_{\left\{yZ_{t,T}\in\left(0,1\right)\right\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(1-\Phi\left(d(t,y)\right)\right)\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\right)\left(T-t\right)\right)+\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}\Phi^{\prime}\left(d(t,y)\right).

Then the partial derivative of
v(t,y)
with respect to
y

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v(t,y)}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{y\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}\sqrt{T-t}}\Phi^{\prime}\left(d(t,y)\right)\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)+\left(r+\frac{\|\boldsymbol{\theta}\|_{2}^{2}}{2}\left(T-t\right)\right)\right)

(39)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{y}\left(1-\Phi\left(d(t,y)\right)\right)-\frac{1}{y}d(t,y)\Phi^{\prime}\left(d(t,y)\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{y}\left(1-\Phi\left(d(t,y)\right)\right).

From (

. Comparing (

) and recalling the definition of

\mathcal{Y}(t,x)=\lambda(t,x)
. This validates Equation (

) in Theorem

.

Proof of Lemma
1
.

(a) If there are
x<y
such that
U^{**}(x)\geqslant U^{**}(y)
, then the concavity of
U^{**}
and the monotonicity of
U
imply that
U^{**}(z)\to-\infty
or
U^{**}(z)\to C
for some constant
C
as
z\uparrow\infty
, in contradiction to the fact that
U(z)\to\infty
as
z\to\infty
.

(b) The inclusion
\text{\rm dom }U\subseteq\text{\rm dom }U^{**}
follows from the fact that
U^{**}\geqslant U
. The converse inclusion will follow if we can show that
U^{**}(L)=-\infty
if
U(L)=-\infty
. To this end, let us assume that
U(L)=-\infty
but
U^{**}(L)=C>-\infty
. The upper semicontinuity of
U
implies that there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
U(x)<C-1
for all
x\in(L,L+\varepsilon]
. Thus, replacing
U^{**}
on
[L,L+\varepsilon]
with the straight line from
C-1
to
U^{**}(L+\varepsilon)\geqslant U^{**}(L)=C
produces a concave function that dominates
U
and is dominated by
U^{**}
, in contradiction to the fact that
U^{**}
is the concave envelope of
U
.

(c) The upper semicontinuous concave function
U^{**}
is continuous on its effective domain
\text{\rm dom }U^{**}
. Hence,
U-U^{**}
is upper semicontinuous on
\text{\rm dom }U^{**}=\text{\rm dom }U
, and so
\{x\in\text{\rm dom }U\mid U^{**}(x)>U(x)\}
is relatively open in
\text{\rm dom }U
. Since we always have
U^{**}(L)=U(L)
, the assertion follows.

(d) Suppose by way of contradiction that
U^{**}
is not affine on
(a_{k},b_{k})
with
a_{k}<b_{k}
. Then there are
x,y\in(a_{k},b_{k})
such that
x<y
and
U^{**}((x+y)/2)>(U^{**}(x)+U^{**}(y))/2
. Let
H
be the affine function on
[x,y]
that coincides with
U^{**}
at the endpoints
x,y
. Since
U^{**}
is continuous and
U
is upper semicontinuous on
[x,y]
, the infimum of
U^{**}-U
is attained on
[x,y]
and strictly positive. Hence, there is
\varepsilon>0
such that
K:=\varepsilon H+(1-\varepsilon)U^{**}
is still strictly larger than
U
on
[x,y]
. Let

\widetilde{U}:=K\mathbbmss{1}_{[x,y]}+U^{**}\mathbbmss{1}_{[x,y]^{c}}
. Then
\widetilde{U}
is strictly smaller than
U^{**}
and still concave since
\widetilde{U}^{\prime}_{+}(x)\leqslant(U^{**})^{\prime}_{+}(x)
and
\widetilde{U}^{\prime}_{-}(y)\geqslant(U^{**})^{\prime}_{-}(y)
. But this contradicts the fact that
U^{**}
is the smallest concave function dominating
U
.

Proof of Lemma
2
.

To prove that
I
is finite,
consider
y>0
. Since
\lim_{x\uparrow\infty}(U^{**})^{\prime}(x)=0
, there exists
x_{y}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(U^{**})^{\prime}(x)<y
for all
x\geqslant x_{y}
. By definition, we have
I(y)\leqslant x_{y}<\infty
. Hence,
I(y)<\infty
. On the other hand, we always have
I(y)\geqslant L>-\infty
.

Now we prove (
6
).
Since the function
U^{**}
is concave,
I(\cdot)
satisfies

U^{**}\left(I(y)\right)-yI(y)=\sup_{x\in\text{\rm dom\,}U^{**}}\{U^{**}(x)-xy\}=\sup_{x\in\text{\rm dom\,}U}\{U^{**}(x)-xy\},

where the second equality comes from Lemma
1
(b). Part (c) of that lemma states furthermore that the set
S=\{x\mid U^{**}(x)>U(x)\}
can be represented as the union of a sequence
\{(a_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
of disjoint open intervals.
Consider
y>0
. If
y=(U^{**})^{\prime}(x_{k_{0}})
for some
x_{k_{0}}\in(a_{k_{0}},b_{k_{0}})
, then we have
I(y)=a_{k_{0}}\notin S
, since
U^{**}
is affine on
(a_{k_{0}},b_{k_{0}})
by Lemma
1
(d). On the other hand, it is clear that
I(y)\notin S
if
y\notin(U^{**})^{\prime}(S)
.
This shows that
I(y)\notin S
, and so
U^{**}(I(y))=U(I(y))
for all
y>0
. Hence, we have

U\left(I(y)\right)-yI(y)=U^{**}\left(I(y)\right)-yI(y)=\sup_{x\in\text{\rm dom\,}U}\{U^{**}(x)-xy\}\geqslant\sup_{x\in\text{\rm dom\,}U}\{U(x)-xy\}.

It remains to show that
I(y)\in\text{\rm dom\,}U
. This is trivial if
\text{\rm dom\,}U
is closed. If
\text{\rm dom\,}U
is open, then the upper semicontinuity of

and the fact that

is finite imply that

Proof of Lemma
9
.

The proof is an extension of the proof of Theorem 4.5.2 in
Yong and Zhou 1999
.
We begin by proving the dynamic programming principle (DPP), i.e., we are going to show that for
t\leqslant s\leqslant T
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{U}
,

u(t,x)=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}}\right)\right],

where
\mathbb{E}_{t,x}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}\left[\cdot|X_{t}=x\right]
and
\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}
denotes the control process from
t
to
s
. Since the wealth process is Markovian, we have

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{s}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}},\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[s,T]}}\right)\right|X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}\in\mathcal{V}[t,s]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[s,T]}\in\mathcal{V}[s,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.U\left(X_{T}^{s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}},\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[s,T]}}\right)\right|X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}\in\mathcal{V}[t,s]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)\right].

On the other hand, for an arbitrary control
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]
, we have

u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[t,s]}}\right)\geqslant\mathbb{E}\left[\left.U\left(X_{T}^{s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[s,T]}}\right)\right|X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[t,s]}}\right].

Taking expectation on both sides:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[t,s]}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[\left.\mathbb{E}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[s,T]}}\right)\right|X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}|_{[t,s]}}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)\right].

Taking supremum on both sides, we get

\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[u\left(s,X_{s}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}|_{[t,s]}}\right)\right]\geqslant\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathcal{V}[t,T]}\mathbb{E}_{t,x}\left[U\left(X_{T}^{t,x,\boldsymbol{\pi}}\right)\right]=u(t,x).

Now, we show that
u(t,x)
is a viscosity supersolution. Let
\varphi\in C^{1,2}\left([0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{U}\right)
be such that
u-\varphi
attains a local minimum at
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{U}
and
u(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})=\varphi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})
.
By the definition of local minimum, there exist
h>0
and
\varepsilon>0
such that
t^{\prime}+h<T
and

u(t,x)\geqslant\varphi(t,x)
for all
(t,x)\in[t^{\prime},t^{\prime}+h]\times B_{\varepsilon}(x^{\prime})
, where
B_{\varepsilon}\left(x^{\prime}\right):=\{x\in\mathcal{D}_{U}:|x-x^{\prime}|<\varepsilon\}
. Fixing a constant control
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\{X_{t}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}\leqslant t\leqslant T}
be the state trajectory under the constant control.
Then from DPP and the linearity of the SDE of
\{X_{t}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\}_{t^{\prime}\leqslant t\leqslant T}
, there exists
h>0
small enough such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}_{t^{\prime},x^{\prime}}\left[\left(u\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\right)-\left(u\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}_{t^{\prime},x^{\prime}}\left[\varphi\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\right].

By Itô’s formula, taking
h\downarrow 0
, we have

-\frac{\partial\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\varphi;\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\right)\geqslant 0.

(40)

Taking
\sup_{\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{d}}
on
\mathcal{L}\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\varphi;\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\right)
, we get that
u
is a viscosity supersolution.

On the other hand, suppose that
u-\varphi
attains a local maximum at
(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\in[0,T)\times\mathcal{D}_{U}
. From DPP, for any
\varepsilon>0
and
h>0
small enough, there exists
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\varepsilon,h}\in\mathcal{V}[t^{\prime},T]
such that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{E}_{t^{\prime},x^{\prime}}\left[\left(u\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\right)-\left(u\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\varepsilon,h}}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\varepsilon,h}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon h+\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\varepsilon,h}}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\right].

Dividing both sides by
h
, we get

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{1}{h}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi\left(t^{\prime}+h,X_{t^{\prime}+h}^{t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\varepsilon,h}}\right)-\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)\right].

Taking
h\downarrow 0
and since
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we get

-\frac{\partial\varphi\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime}\right)}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}\left(t^{\prime},x^{\prime},\varphi;\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\prime}\right)\leqslant 0.

(41)

Combining Equations (
40
)-(
41
), we prove
u(t,x)
is a viscosity solution of Equation (
23
).
