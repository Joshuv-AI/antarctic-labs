Lung Cancer Screening Uptake Among Veterans
Authors: Soumya J Niranjan, Joshua S Richman, K Ria Hearld, Krystle Pew, Devika Das, Mark T Dransfield, Nichole Tanner
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Lung cancer is the second most common cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths.1,2 Survival rates depend on stage of diagnosis, with 63% 5-year survival rate in the localized or early stage.3 Early detection using low-dose CT (LDCT) scan reduces mortality.4

Veterans have higher smoking prevalence, greater lung cancer incidence, and lower survival than the general population.5 The Veterans Affairs medical centers (VAMCs) follows the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) guidelines, recommending lung cancer screening (LCS) for individuals 50 to 80 years of age who currently or previously smoked cigarettes who quit within the past 15 years and who have at least a 20 pack-year history of smoking.4,6 Despite these recommendations, uptake remains highly variable.

Although these data precede more recent screening guideline updates, understanding preimplementation variation is essential for guiding future expansion. This study examines LCS rates, predictors, and racial disparities across VAMCs during 2014 to 2019, providing a national preimplementation baseline before the Veteran's Affairs (VA’s) Lung Precision Oncology Program (LPOP) launch in 2021. These findings help identify structural and geographic inequities that can inform equitable LCS expansion under the updated 2021 USPSTF criteria.

Methods

Patient-level data were obtained from the Veteran Health Administration’s (VHA) Corporate Data Warehouse via VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure, a centralized, national repository of integrated electronic health record data for the VHA.7 We conducted a national retrospective cohort study identifying veterans eligible for LDCT screening from 2014 to 2019 across 140 VA health care centers, based on the original USPSTF criteria. Although these data predate the 2021 USPSTF guideline update, they provide a comprehensive baseline for examining facility-level variation, predictors, and disparities within the VA system. Inclusion criteria included 55 to 80 years of age, no prior lung cancer diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 162.9, ICD-10 C34.90 within 10 years), and a history of smoking (F17.210, F17.211, F17.213, F17.218, F17.219, Z87.891). We acknowledge these codes serve as a surrogate for specific pack-year criteria, which are not consistently captured as discrete variables in national electronic health record data.

Data from 2014 to 2019 were analyzed as a pooled aggregate to provide a robust national baseline of the VHA system. The primary outcome was LCS using LDCT scan (71271: CT scan, thorax, low dose for LCS, without contrast material[s]) completion (screened vs not screened). Demographic variables included age (mean-centered), gender, race/ethnicity, rurality (distance to tertiary care), and socioeconomic status (Area Deprivation Index). Comorbidity was captured using a domain count aggregating 15,000 International Classificiation of Diseases -9th Revision-Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes into 202 conditions grouped into 17 domains (eg, cardiovascular, respiratory).

Continuous variables were summarized as means, and categorical variables were summarized as frequencies, stratified by screening status. The χ2 and Student t tests compared groups. Mixed-effects logistic regression models identified factors associated with LCS uptake, accounting for facility-level clustering. Facilities were classified as high or low screening (< 24% vs ≥ 24%, respectively).8

We also conducted exploratory classification tree analyses using recursive partitioning to identify variables most strongly associated with screening. Models included eligibility, demographic, comorbidity, and geographic variables, with and without facility-level screening rates, to assess predictor importance. This study obtained ethics approval from the VA institutional review board (No. 1571135).

Results

Across 130 VAMCs, 1,088,012 veterans were eligible for LCS, with 856,758 (78.8%) not screened (Table 1). Nearly one-third of VAMCs (serving 230,270 veterans) had no evidence of screening, and another one-third (serving 320,207 veterans) reported rates < 10%, revealing wide facility-level variation. Screened veterans were predominantly male (> 93%), and less likely to be White (72.2% vs 80.1%; P < .001) but more likely Black (22.2% vs 14.7%; P < .001). They also had higher comorbidities, shorter drive distances (83.6 vs 722.2 miles; P < .001), and lower area deprivation scores (58.0 vs 62.6; P < .001). To explore drivers of this variation, multivariable analyses (Table 2) revealed that older age and male gender were positively associated with screening, whereas Black race predicted lower odds (OR, 0.80; P < .001). Chronic pulmonary disease was the strongest individual clinical predictor of screening (OR, 2.65 and 2.24 across strata; P < .001). Overall comorbidity burden also appeared to influence screening utilization patterns. Exploratory classification tree analyses reinforced these findings: chronic pulmonary disease consistently emerged as the most influential single predictor, and travel distance was a major determinant regardless of whether facility-level rates were included.

Discussion

This pre-LPOP analysis of the nation’s largest integrated health care system reveals substantial opportunities for improvement that can guide current implementation efforts. Although the data precede LPOP and the 2021 USPSTF expansion, they provide the only national baseline for evaluating progress toward equitable screening implementation.

First, there was significant heterogeneity in LCS uptake across facilities. Although our analysis presents an aggregate baseline rather than a longitudinal trend, the identified variation (where 60% of facilities had screening rates < 10% or no screening at all) underscores the significant gaps existing immediately before formal LPOP implementation. Understanding this baseline heterogeneity is critical for measuring the LPOP’s impact and identifying facilities requiring additional support. Nearly one-third of facilities demonstrated no evidence of screening, and another one-third had rates < 10%. In contrast, the remaining one-third of facilities achieved screening rates of ≥ 5%, with top performers demonstrating that systematic LCS implementation is feasible within the VA system. This marked variation—from zero to substantial screening rates—suggests that successful facilities may have implemented structural supports, provider education, or patient outreach strategies that could be scaled across the LPOP to elevate low-performing sites.

Second, structural inequities were apparent. Screened veterans lived in less disadvantaged areas and traveled shorter distances, consistent with prior studies showing that individuals in socioeconomically deprived or rural areas are less likely to undergo cancer screening.9 The VA’s hub-and-spoke model, concentrating advanced services in urban hubs, may exacerbate these disparities. Expanding LCS access through regional coordination, such as the VA New England’s hub-and-spoke program, could mitigate these gaps.10

Third, racial disparities persisted. Black veterans were less likely to be screened even in high-performing facilities (OR, 0.81-0.70; P < .001), underscoring the need for equity-focused interventions under the LPOP. These findings align with prior studies and underscore the need for equity-focused interventions, particularly given the potential benefit of screening in Black populations.11

Fourth, overall comorbidity burden appeared to influence screening utilization patterns. Chronic pulmonary disease was consistently associated with higher screening utilization, which is clinically plausible given its close relationship to smoking history and pulmonary risk. Lower screening among patients with advanced illness may reflect appropriate clinical judgment regarding competing health risks and screening appropriateness. These findings underscore the importance of structured shared decision-making when considering LCS among medically complex veterans.

Our study has limitations. Findings may not generalize to non-VA populations; however, the large national VA sample offers important insights into a diverse open-access system. Some veterans may have undergone LCS outside the VA, which could underestimate true uptake. Finally, smoking history was determined by diagnostic codes rather than direct documentation, which may misclassify eligibility.

This national pre-LPOP analysis demonstrates limited and uneven LCS uptake across VAMCs, shaped by facility variation, racial and socioeconomic inequities, and comorbidity burden. These findings establish a baseline for evaluating the LPOP’s impact and guiding targeted efforts to expand equitable, high-quality screening within the VA system.
