Seasonal Trends, Environmental Drivers, and Lessons Learned From the 2023 Cholera Outbreak in Chattogram, Bangladesh: A Descriptive Study
Authors: Immamul Muntasir, Md Jahidur Rahman, Sayik Bin Alam, Abir Shaqran Mahmood, Tanvir Ahmed, Mithun Bahadur, Farzana Islam Khan, Kyaw Thowai Prue Prince, Mohammad Rashedul Hassan, Zakir Hossain Habib, Tahmina Shirin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAcute watery diarrhea (AWD) remains a major global public health problem, and cholera continues to cause substantial morbidity and mortality in settings with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH). Chattogram, a coastal district of Bangladesh, is particularly vulnerable due to seasonal hydroclimatic changes, salinity intrusion, and high population density. On May 6, 2023, an increase in AWD cases was reported, prompting an outbreak investigation. We aimed to describe the epidemiology, identify the causative pathogens and source, assess environmental contributors, examine seasonal trends, and recommend preventive measures.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive investigation from May 7 to May 15 2023, in two tertiary hospitals, two Upazila Health Complexes, and affected communities. Five‐year historical data were reviewed to assess seasonal patterns, and facility‐based surveillance data from April 2023 were analyzed. Active community case finding, standardized interviews, environmental assessments, stool culture and sensitivity testing, and water‐quality testing for coliforms, Vibrio cholerae, and salinity were performed.ResultsBetween April 1 and May 15 2023, 7956 AWD hospital admissions were reported, with sustained transmission during the pre‐monsoon period. Among 166 interviewed patients, adults aged 18–40 years were most affected (65/166, 39%); all had AWD, and 85% (141/166) reported vomiting. Six (6) of 23 stool cultures yielded V. cholerae. Coliform was detected in two (2) of three (3) tube wells and all pond samples, while non‐O1/non‐O139 V. cholerae was identified in multiple water sources. Salinity ranged from 210 to 2320 mg/L, with several sources exceeding recommended limits. Urban cases mainly relied on intermittent piped water, whereas rural households depended on tube wells and contaminated surface water with low treatment practices.ConclusionsThe outbreak was associated with unsafe and saline water, intermittent supply, and inadequate household water treatment. Strengthening climate‐resilient water systems, routine water‐quality monitoring, sanitation, and pre‐monsoon preparedness is essential to prevent recurrent cholera‐associated AWD outbreaks in coastal Bangladesh.Keywords: Bangladesh, cholera, disease outbreaks, salinity, seasons, trends

Introduction

Diarrheal diseases have been considered a major public health problem and are estimated to be the eighth leading cause of mortality globally [1]. The etiological agents of diarrheal disease include bacterial pathogens (e.g., Vibrio cholerae, Escherichia coli, Shigella spp., and Salmonella spp.), viral agents (e.g., rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus, and astrovirus), protozoan parasites (e.g., Giardia lamblia, Entamoeba histolytica, and Cryptosporidium spp.), as well as other parasitic organisms such as Cyclospora cayetanensis and Isospora belli) [2, 3, 4, 5]. Most burden estimations have focused on children due to their high prevalence in under five (1.7 billion episodes annually among children under five), even though a substantial burden is seen in adults [1, 6]. In 2017, while worldwide rotavirus was responsible for most deaths among children under 5 years old due to diarrhea [7], V. cholerae was the leading cause of diarrhea mortality among adults [8]. In Bangladesh, diarrheal diseases are the most common reason for seeking hospital‐level care [9, 10].

Cholera, as one of the causes of the AWD outbreak, is still a major global public health concern, particularly in nations with poor water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) and a weak healthcare system that makes it difficult to identify and treat patients quickly and prevent transmissions. With an estimated 1.3 billion people at risk, an annual incidence of 2.9 million (Confidence Interval: 1.3–4.0 million), and 95,000 deaths (Confidence Interval: 21,000–143,000), cholera continues to pose a significant global burden. Sub‐Saharan Africa and Southern Asia bear 60% and 29% of the burden, respectively [11, 12]. The true global burden of cholera is unknown due to possible under‐reporting [13].

Bangladesh, located in the heart of the Ganges Delta and considered the ancestral home of cholera, continues to face the disease as a major public health problem [14, 15]. Bangladesh experiences seasonal cholera outbreaks—typically peaking in the pre‐monsoon and post‐monsoon months ‐ due to a combination of its unique riverine geography, climate‐driven environmental changes, and systemic challenges with water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure [16, 17]. A large proportion of the population lives in high‐risk, densely populated environments with poor access to safe water and sanitation [18]. It is suggested that 66 million people are at risk of cholera in Bangladesh, with an incidence rate of 1.64/1000 population, 100,000 cases, and 4500 deaths annually [11].

Chattogram district, located along Bangladesh's southeastern coast, is particularly vulnerable to diarrheal outbreaks due to its low‐lying, coastal geography, exposure to tidal water, and salinity intrusion [19]. Water scarcity, climate‐driven changes in rainfall and flooding, and high population density further exacerbate the risk of acute watery diarrhea (AWD) in both urban and rural communities. Monitoring outbreaks in this coastal region can help identify sources, affected populations, and environmental contributors, providing evidence to guide control and prevention measures [20].

On May 6, 2023, the Civil Surgeon (District Health Manager) of Chattogram notified the Director of the Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR), Dhaka, regarding an increasing number of acute diarrhea cases in different areas of Chattogram district and requested appropriate public health action. In response, IEDCR deployed an outbreak investigation team on May 7, 2023. The objectives of this investigation were to describe the epidemiology of this AWD outbreak; identify the causative pathogens; determine the source of the outbreak; assess contributing environmental factors; recommend measures to prevent future outbreaks; and examine the seasonal trends of AWD in Chattogram district.

Methodology

Study Design and Period

We conducted a descriptive study comprising epidemiological, environmental, and laboratory investigations from May 7 to May 15, 2023.

Place of Investigation

We conducted this outbreak investigation in Chattogram district, Bangladesh, including the following health facilities and community: Chattogram Medical College Hospital (CMCH), Bangladesh Institute of Tropical and Infectious Diseases (BITID), Upazila (sub‐district) Health Complex (UHC) Boalkhali, UHC Patiya, and the affected community of West Gomdandi Union in Boalkhali Upazila.

Epidemiological Investigation

AWD Trend Analysis

We reviewed admissions for AWD in 15UHCs in Chattogram district using data from the District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2), the national health management information system of the Directorate General of Health Services. Five‐year historical data (2018–2022) were analyzed to describe baseline seasonal patterns.

To assess the 2023 outbreak, we analyzed daily AWD admissions at Chattogram Medical College Hospital (CMCH), the Bangladesh Institute of Tropical and Infectious Diseases (BITID), and selected UHCs from April 1, 2023, onward.

Case Identification

Cases were identified in healthcare facilities and the affected community.

Facility‐based cases were detected through a systematic review of admission registers and medical records at CMCH, BITID, UHC Boalkhali, and UHC Patiya using the facility case definition.

Community‐based cases were identified through active household visits in West Gomdandi Union, Boalkhali, using the community case definition, irrespective of healthcare‐seeking behavior.

All suspected and confirmed cases were compiled into a standardized electronic line list to describe the outbreak by time, place, and person.

Case Definitions

Suspected Case (Facility‐Based)

Any person of any age admitted to CMCH, BITID, UHC Boalkhali, or UHC Patiya from April 1, 2023 onward with ≥ 3 loose or watery stools within 24 h.

Suspected Case (Community‐Based)

Any resident of the affected area who developed ≥ 3 loose or watery stools within 24 h from April 1 2023 onward, regardless of healthcare‐seeking behavior.

Confirmed Case

A suspected case with laboratory confirmation by culture, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, or molecular testing.

Data Collection Methods

Data were collected through retrospective record review, prospective case investigation, active community case finding, environmental assessment, and key informant interviews.

Facility data were obtained through medical record abstraction and interviews with admitted patients. Community data were collected through face‐to‐face interviews during household visits. Environmental observations were conducted concurrently. Key informant interviews were undertaken to contextualize outbreak dynamics and response activities.

Data Collection Tools and Instruments

Standardized and pretested tools were used to ensure consistency across all investigation components. A semi‐structured case investigation form was administered to suspected cases or caregivers to collect demographic characteristics, symptom onset date, clinical presentation, dehydration status, treatment received, healthcare‐seeking pathway, and exposures within 7 days prior to illness onset, including drinking water source, water treatment practices, sanitation, food consumption outside the home, travel history, and contact with diarrheal patients.

A structured medical record abstraction checklist was used to extract clinical diagnoses, laboratory findings, management details, and discharge outcomes from hospital records.

All cases were entered into a standardized electronic line listing template capturing demographic, clinical, laboratory, exposure, treatment, and outcome variables.

Environmental assessments were conducted using an observational checklist documenting water sources, storage practices, sanitation infrastructure, drainage conditions, and potential contamination points. Geographic coordinates were recorded where feasible to support spatial analysis.

Semi‐structured interview guides were used for key informant interviews with physicians, nurses, and local health authorities to obtain contextual information on surveillance, case management, and outbreak response.

Laboratory Investigations

To confirm the causative organism, we collected stool samples from the cases and tested them with a rapid diagnostic tool (RDT) for cholera kit on‐site and then sent the samples to the IEDCR laboratory for culture and sensitivity and molecular testing.

We collected water samples from various suspected sources in the inspected area, including tubewells and ponds and sent them to the environmental laboratory of International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b).

Environmental Investigations

We conducted an environmental assessment in affected areas of Chattogram district, including urban City Corporation communities and Poshchim Gomdondi Union in Boalkhali Upazila, a rural coastal community. Household water sources, including tube wells and surface water, and sanitation conditions were systematically assessed.

Statistical Analysis

Data collected from case record forms and line listings were entered, cleaned, and analyzed using STATA 17. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic, clinical, epidemiological, and exposure‐related variables. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and proportions. Trends of acute watery diarrhea cases were assessed by constructing epidemic curves based on hospital admissions. Comparative analyses between city corporation and upazila cases were performed using proportions to describe differences in age, sex, water sources, sanitation practices, and key exposures. Laboratory and environmental testing results were summarized descriptively. No inferential statistical tests were applied, as the investigation was primarily descriptive in nature.

Ethical Considerations

The investigation was conducted as part of an outbreak response under the authority of the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS), Government of Bangladesh, and was implemented as a public health emergency activity rather than a conventional research study. The activity was determined to be exempt from formal ethical review due to its emergency public health response nature. Participation was entirely voluntary. All participants were informed about the purpose of the investigation in an appropriate and understandable manner prior to data collection. Verbal informed consent was obtained in accordance with the operational constraints of the field emergency setting. Confidentiality was strictly maintained, and all personal identifiers were removed prior to analysis. Participants were assured that refusal to participate or withdrawal at any stage would not result in any adverse consequences or affect the services they received.

Results

Epidemiological Overview

An outbreak of AWD was identified in four upazilas (Boalkhali, Patia, Anwara, Chandanaish) (Map 1, 1) as well as in the Chattogram City Corporation. The number of reported cases began increasing from the first week of April in Anwara and Patia, from the second week in Chattogram City Corporation (CMCH and BITID), and from the third week in Boalkhali and Chandanaish (Figure 1, 1). Between April 1 and May 15, 2023, reported admitted cases were 1087 in Boalkhali, 1596 in Patia, 1039 in Anwara, 1033 in Chandanaish, 1334 at Chattogram Medical College Hospital, and 1867 at BITID. Epidemic curves showed sustained transmission over several weeks, consistent with repeated exposure rather than a single point source. Seasonal trends from 2018 to 2023 suggest the outbreak coincided with the pre‐monsoon period (Figure 2).

Water purification practices were reported by 53% (37/70) of City Corporation households but by only 17% (16/96) of Upazila households. Exposure to street food was relatively low overall, though it was higher in Upazila areas (13/96, 14%) compared with City Corporation areas (3/70, 4%), while water scarcity was reported more frequently in City Corporation settings (37/70, 53%) than in Upazila settings (16/96, 17%) (Table 2).

Environmental Findings

During informal discussions with community members in City Corporation areas, we found that water scarcity often forced residents to rely on water delivered by carts with small tanks or drums‐ a method where a provider transport limited quantities of water into the community. The residents themselves could not ensure whether this water was safe for consumption.

In Poshchim Gomdondi Union in Boalkhali Upazila, a rural coastal union affected by the outbreak, environmental assessments showed that the primary source of drinking water was tube wells supplying groundwater via submersible pumps, while household water came from both tube wells and surface water. Most community members did not practice any water purification methods. Although residents reported that the tube wells were deep, we observed that their depth was generally no more than 250 ft. We also observed that affected community members were using surface water from ponds for washing and bathing. These water sources were visibly dirty, and in some ponds, latrines were being emptied directly into the water. Community members reported that during the hot season, water levels in the coastal area declined, leading to seawater backflow and increased salinity of surface water.

Laboratory Findings

Among 23 stool samples collected from hospitalized AWD patients during the investigation, 12 samples tested positive using the RDT kit for cholera. All 23 samples were subsequently tested by microbiological culture and sensitivity, and six (6) samples (26%) were confirmed positive for V. cholerae (non O1/non O139).

Eight (8) water samples (three (3) tube wells and five (5) ponds) from one (1) of the affected communities where environmental investigation was also conducted during the AWD outbreak in Chattogram in May 2023. These samples were tested at the environmental laboratory of icddr,b and analyzed to detect total coliforms, V. cholerae, and salinity.

Coliforms were detected in two (2) of the three (3) tube wells, with the highest total coliform count being 880,000 CFU/100 mL. V. cholerae (non O1/non O139) was detected in one (1) tube well. Salinity in the tube wells ranged from 210 to 235 mg/L.

Also, coliforms were detected in all five (5) pond samples, with the highest total coliform count being 230,000 CFU/100 mL. V. cholerae (non O1/Non O139) was detected in all five (5) pond samples. Salinity in the ponds ranged from 283 to 2320 mg/L, with two (2) ponds showing salinity levels above 1000 mg/L (Table 3).

Public Health Action

In order to increase awareness of prevention of diarrhea in the affected areas, the investigation team discussed the sanitation and hygiene regulations in the visited areas, such as safe drinking and use of water, water purification methods, importance of sanitary latrines, proper management of household waste, regular hand washing with soap. Besides, with the help of the Civil Surgeon's Office, it was suggested to distribute water purification tablets in the affected areas until the situation is brought under control. In addition, attention was drawn to providing treatment for hospitalized patients through proper verification of dehydration as per the guidelines for diarrhea.

Discussion

This investigation confirmed an AWD outbreak affecting both urban and rural areas of Chattogram district during April–May 2023, with epidemiological and laboratory evidence of V. cholerae circulation and widespread contamination of community water sources. The temporal increase in cases from early April, the sequential involvement of multiple upazilas, and increased admissions to tertiary facilities indicate a district‐wide outbreak rather than isolated focal events.

The outbreak occurred during Bangladesh's dry pre‐monsoon season, consistent with the country's well‐documented bimodal cholera pattern, with peaks typically observed during April–June and again in August–October [21]. The persistence of transmission through May suggests sustained environmental exposure rather than a single‐point source outbreak. Previous studies from Bangladesh have demonstrated that during the dry season, declining river flow and groundwater levels may facilitate concentration and persistence of V. cholerae in aquatic environments [20, 21, 22]. Seasonal water scarcity further compels communities to rely on alternative and often unsafe water sources, thereby increasing exposure risk [23]. The timing and duration of this outbreak are therefore epidemiologically consistent with known seasonal cholera dynamics in coastal Bangladesh.

Notably, a similar AWD outbreak was reported in the City Corporation area in the preceding year, during which water scarcity and laboratory‐confirmed cholera were also documented. The recurrence of comparable epidemiological and environmental conditions suggests persistent structural vulnerabilities in urban water supply and sanitation systems. Rather than representing isolated events, these repeated outbreaks indicate ongoing systemic challenges ‐ particularly during the pre‐monsoon period ‐ when declining freshwater availability, increased salinity, and intermittent water supply may collectively heighten cholera transmission risk.

Geographically, the involvement of both City Corporation wards and surrounding upazilas reflects the endemic nature of cholera in Bangladesh. National surveillance data indicate that Chattogram district is among the high‐burden areas, alongside other hotspots such as Narayanganj, Cumilla, and Cox's Bazar [21]. The distribution observed in this outbreak mirrors these national patterns, highlighting the interconnected transmission dynamics between urban and peri‐urban or rural populations.

Demographically, AWD affected all age groups, with the highest proportion among adults aged 18–40 years, followed by those aged 40–60 years. Although children under five constituted a smaller proportion of interviewed cases, previous endemic‐area studies in Chattogram have demonstrated that young children often experience the highest incidence rates [20, 23].

Clinically, the presentation was consistent with classical cholera, characterized by profuse watery diarrhea and frequent vomiting [24]. Laboratory findings supported cholera as an important etiological agent. V. cholerae O1 Ogawa was isolated from stool specimens, confirming ongoing transmission. This serotype is consistent with the predominant epidemic strain circulating in Bangladesh in recent years. However, among 12 RDT‐positive samples, six were subsequently culture‐confirmed. The discrepancy between RDT positivity and culture confirmation may be attributable to prior antibiotic use, delays in transport, or inherent limitations of RDT specificity and sensitivity in field settings [24]. Although cholera RDTs have limitations in specificity and sensitivity, they remain valuable tools for rapid outbreak detection and preliminary field‐level screening in resource‐limited settings, particularly when timely culture confirmation is not immediately feasible.

Environmental and water‐use findings strongly implicate unsafe water as a central driver of the outbreak. In City Corporation areas, reliance on WASA (supply water combined with frequent reports of water scarcity suggests intermittent supply and possible contamination within compromised distribution networks. WASA is responsible for water treatment and distribution; however, intermittent supply, aging pipelines, leakage, and pressure fluctuations may create conditions that allow ingress of contaminated water into the system. Previous studies in Bangladesh have shown that intermittent piped systems can facilitate negative pressure and ingress of contaminated water, thereby increasing diarrheal disease risk [20]. In contrast, upazila residents predominantly depended on deep or shallow tube wells and surface water, with limited household‐level water treatment. Low compliance with water purification practices further heightened vulnerability.

Environmental assessments revealed that many tube wells perceived by communities as “deep” were relatively shallow and located near pit latrines. Studies from Bangladeshi floodplains have documented fecal contamination of shallow aquifers when wells are situated within close proximity to sanitation structures [25]. The detection of coliforms and V. cholerae in multiple water samples indicates systemic water safety failures rather than isolated contamination events. Pond water used for washing and bathing was visibly polluted, and in some instances, latrine effluent drained directly into ponds‐ an established risk factor for enteric pathogen transmission in rural Bangladesh [25, 26].

Salinity appeared to be an additional contributing factor. Measured salinity levels in several water sources exceeded recommended esthetic limits [27]. Coastal Bangladesh is increasingly affected by seawater intrusion, particularly during the dry season, leading to elevated groundwater salinity [28, 29]. High drinking‐water salinity has been associated with increased diarrheal morbidity in coastal populations [30]. Moderate salinity may also enhance V. cholerae survival in brackish environments, potentially contributing to environmental persistence [31, 32]. Community reports of declining freshwater availability and increased brackish conditions during the hot season further support the role of ecological drivers in this outbreak.

The relatively low reported exposure to street food suggests that waterborne transmission likely played a more prominent role than foodborne exposure in this event. The concurrent detection of V. cholerae in both clinical and environmental samples supports a classic fecal–oral transmission cycle. Importantly, most isolates identified in environmental samples were non‐O1/non‐O139 strains. Although these strains are typically not associated with epidemic cholera [24], their presence indicates fecal contamination and unsafe water conditions, which facilitate broader enteric disease transmission. The confirmed presence of epidemic V. cholerae in stool samples confirms that cholera contributed substantially to the outbreak.

This investigation has several limitations. The study design was descriptive and did not include analytical components to quantify exposure–disease associations. Laboratory testing was performed on a subset of cases, potentially underestimating the true burden of cholera. Environmental sampling was geographically limited and may not represent all affected areas, as laboratory testing of water was primarily conducted in a rural coastal community. However, similar conditions and a documented AWD outbreak in the City Corporation area during the previous year suggest that the findings are likely relevant to urban settings as well. The reliance on facility‐based surveillance likely resulted in under‐detection of milder community cases, leading to possible underestimation of outbreak magnitude. Operational challenges, including service disruptions from Cyclone Mocha, affected data collection and field activities.

The cholera burden in Bangladesh remains substantial; however, comparison with other high‐risk regions such as sub‐Saharan Africa should be interpreted cautiously. While Bangladesh has long‐standing endemic transmission with relatively strong surveillance systems and frequent outbreak detection, many countries in sub‐Saharan Africa experience high case‐fatality ratios and large underreported outbreak‐driven epidemics, often in settings with weaker surveillance infrastructure. Therefore, observed differences in reported burden likely reflect both true epidemiological variation and differences in detection, reporting capacity, and health system access rather than direct comparability of incidence estimates. Recent comparative analyses have similarly highlighted that cholera persists as a complex, multi‐regional public health challenge influenced by contextual factors including water infrastructure, conflict, climate variability, and health system resilience [33].

Despite these limitations, the investigation provides important epidemiological, laboratory, and environmental evidence linking AWD transmission to systemic water and sanitation vulnerabilities across both urban and rural settings of Chattogram district. The convergence of seasonal ecological drivers, unsafe water infrastructure, salinity intrusion, and inadequate household‐level water treatment underscores the multifactorial nature of cholera risk in coastal Bangladesh. The recurrence of similar outbreaks in consecutive years further signals persistent structural weaknesses rather than episodic failures. These findings highlight the urgent need for sustained investment in resilient water supply systems, strengthened routine water‐quality monitoring, improved sanitation infrastructure, and targeted pre‐monsoon preparedness strategies. Strengthening early warning systems and integrating environmental surveillance with clinical reporting may enhance timely detection and response. Addressing these systemic determinants is critical not only for preventing future outbreaks in Chattogram but also for advancing broader national and global cholera control goals in climate‐vulnerable coastal regions.

In Bangladesh, cholera control is guided by the National Cholera Control Plan, which targets elimination by 2030 through a multi‐pronged approach that includes mass Oral Cholera Vaccination (OCV), Water, Sanitation and Hygiene (WASH) interventions, case management with Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS), and targeted micro‐hotspot surveillance. In addition, several adaptation measures addressing coastal salinity intrusion are being implemented, including expansion of piped water supply systems, promotion of rainwater harvesting, and localized WASH interventions; however, coverage remains limited, and salinity intrusion continues to compromise drinking water safety in high‐risk coastal settings. Sustained implementation and scale‐up of these interventions will be essential to reduce recurrent outbreaks in high‐risk settings.

Conclusion

This investigation demonstrates that the AWD outbreak in Chattogram during April–May 2023 was driven by systemic water, sanitation, and environmental vulnerabilities affecting both urban and rural populations. The interaction of seasonal pre‐monsoon ecological conditions, intermittent and unsafe water supply, salinity intrusion, fecal contamination of multiple water sources, and low household water‐treatment practices created a setting conducive to sustained cholera transmission. The recurrence of similar outbreaks in consecutive years indicates persistent structural weaknesses rather than isolated failures. These findings underscore the need for climate‐resilient and reliable water‐supply systems, routine water‐quality monitoring, improved sanitation infrastructure, strengthened pre‐monsoon preparedness, and integrated clinical and environmental surveillance to enable early detection and rapid response. Addressing these systemic determinants is essential for preventing future outbreaks in coastal Bangladesh and for achieving national and global cholera control targets.
