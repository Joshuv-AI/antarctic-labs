A stability theorem for embedding bounded degree spanning trees
Béla Csaba

Abstract
We prove that if an n n -vertex graph G G is non-extremal and T T is a bounded degree tree on n n vertices, then T ⊂ G T\subset G even when the minimum degree of G G is less than n / 2 n/2 by a linear term. We avoid the use of the Regularity lemma, instead we apply a vertex decomposition theorem by the author, which does not require a tower-type lower bound for n . n.



keywords:
tree embedding, decomposition of graphs, regularity

1

Introduction

We consider only simple graphs in this paper.
In 1978 Bollobás
[
2
]
conjectured that if
G
is a graph on
n
vertices,
n
is large enough, and

\delta(G)\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
for some
\varepsilon>0,
and
T
is a bounded
degree tree on
n
vertices, then
T\subset G.
The problem was solved in the affirmative by Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi
[
7
]

for large graphs. Few years later they strengthened their result (see
[
8
]
), and proved, that
\Delta(T)
need not be bounded: there exists a constant
c
such that

T\subset G
if
\Delta(T)\leq cn/\log n,

\delta(G)\geq(1/2+\varepsilon)n
and
n
is large. The Regularity lemma played an essential role in both proofs of the Bollobás conjecture.
In
[
4
]
the author, Levitt, Nagy-György and Szemerédi proved the theorem below, without using the Regularity lemma.

Theorem 1.1

Assume that
T
is a bounded degree tree on
n
vertices with
\Delta(T)=D.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices. Assume further that
\delta(G)\geq n/2+c_{D}\log{n},
where
c_{D}
is a constant depending
only on
D.

Then there exists a number
n_{0}
such that
T\subset G
for
n\geq n_{0}
. Furthermore, the bound on
\delta(G)
is tight: there exists a graph
G
with

\delta(G)>n/2+\log{n}/17
such that the complete ternary tree on
n
vertices is not a subgraph of
G
if
n
is sufficiently large.

That is, for embedding a bounded degree tree on
n
vertices, it is sufficient if the minimum degree of the host graph is
n/2+O(\log n),

moreover, an additive term of
\Omega({\log n})
above
n/2
is necessary.

Let us mention a closely related theorem by Kathapurkar and Montgomery
[
6
]
. They proved, without the Regularity lemma, that if the minimum semidegree in a
n
-vertex directed graph
D
is at least
(1/2+\varepsilon)n,
then
D
contains every spanning
oriented tree with maximum degree
o(n/\log n).
This theorem implies the analogous result in
[
8
]

for spanning trees in undirected graphs.

The main result of the present paper is a stability theorem for embedding bounded degree trees, strengthening
Theorem
1.1
for so called non-extremal host graphs. First we need a definition for non-extremality.
Let
0<\gamma<1/4
be a number. A graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
vertices is called
\gamma
-non-extremal
, if
for every, not necessarily disjoint subsets
A,B\subset V,

|A|=|B|=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
we have
e(A,B)>\gamma n^{2},
otherwise we
say that
G
is
\gamma
-extremal.
Note, that
\gamma\leq 1/4
for every graph, and the only
1/4
-non-extremal graph is the complete graph.

It is well-known (see eg. in
[
4
]
) that if
G
has minimum degree at least
n/2
and is extremal, then, roughly speaking, either it is close to a balanced complete bipartite graph, or to the union of two vertex disjoint complete
graphs on
n/2
vertices each. Here closeness is measured in edit distance.

Let us remark, that due to the minium degree conditions, the Bollobás conjecture and the two papers
[
7
,
8
]

by Komlós, Sárközy and Szemerédi are on embedding spanning trees into
\varepsilon
-non-extremal host graphs, similarly
to the paper by Kathapurkar and Montgomery
[
6
]
.
It turns out that if
G
is non-extremal, then the minimum degree requirement can be relaxed significantly.

Theorem 1.2

Let
D\geq 2
be an integer and
\gamma,\nu
real numbers with
0<\nu\ll\gamma\ll 1.
Then there exists a threshold number
n_{0}=n_{0}(D,\gamma,\nu)
such that the following holds. If
n\geq n_{0},

G
is a
\gamma
-non-extremal graph on
n
vertices having minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq(1/2-\nu)n,
and
T
is an
n
-vertex tree with
\Delta(T)\leq D,
then

T\subset G.

Observe that, according to the above theorem, the minimum degree of
G
can be strictly less than
n/2,

if
G
is non-extremal.
Careful examination of the proof shows that with parameters
\gamma\leq 1/100
and
\nu\leq\gamma/50,

the theorem holds. However, it is easier to follow the computations if throughout the proof we apply the more flexible hierarchy
0<\nu\ll\gamma\ll 1
in the statement of the theorem.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
has three cases: the case of a non-extremal
G,
the almost complete bipartite case and
the union of two almost
complete subgraphs case. The minimum degree requirement of Theorem
1.1
is tight for the extremal
cases, as is proved in
[
4
]
, while Theorem
1.2
shows that it can be substantially relaxed for non-extremal host graphs.
Hence, the stability version we prove in this paper together with the proofs
of the extremal cases in
[
4
]
not only gives a new proof for this tree embedding problem, it is
also a stronger result.

In the proof of Theorem
1.2
we use several tools, ideas that were developed for use with the Regularity lemma of Szemerédi
[
11
]
, but we replace the Regularity lemma with an alternative graph decomposition result by the author
[
3
]
.
Roughly speaking,
we can use the advanced, well developed techniques of the Regularity Method – using only a “super matching” containing super-regular pairs, which we find without the Regularity lemma. Due to this fact the new proof applies for graphs of “practical” size, not just for astronomically large ones.

The paper is organized as follows. In the second section we review the basic definitions and tools we need. Beginning with the third section we focus on the proof of the tree embedding theorem. The third section includes the preprocessing of the host graph
G,
the fourth section contains the preprocessing of the tree to be embedded. A few further tools
are introduced in the fifth section. Finally, in the sixth section we put everything together, and prove the tree embedding theorem.

Notation, definitions, main tools

Given a graph
G=(V,E)
we use the notation
v(G)=|V|
and
e(G)=|E|.
Given a set

X\subset V,

G[X]=(X,E_{X}),
where
E_{X}=\{uv:uv\in E,\ u,v\in X\}.
For
disjoint subsets
X,Y\subset V,
we let

G[X,Y]
denote the bipartite subgraph of

G
with parts
X
and
Y
that contains all the edges of
G
with one endpoint in
X
and the other endpoint in
Y.
For every vertex
v\in V,
the
neighborhood
of
v
is denoted by

N_{G}(v),
and the
degree
of
v
is denoted by
\deg_{G}(v)=|N_{G}(v)|.
Given a set
S\subset V,
we let
N_{G}(v,S)=N_{G}(v)\cap S
and

\deg_{G}(v,S)=|N_{G}(v,S)|,
the subscripts may be omitted. For an
H\subseteq G
and
v\in V(G),
the number of neighbors of
v
in
H

is sometimes denoted by
deg_{G}(v,H).
If we write
G=(A,B;E),
this means that
G
is bipartite with parts
A
and
B,
and edge set
E.
If it is clear from the context, that a graph in question is bipartite, we may only write out
the vertex parts, and omit the letter “
E
”. We call a bipartite graph
balanced
, if the two parts have the same cardinality.

The
density
of
G
is defined to be
d_{G}=e(G)\cdot{\binom{v(G)}{2}}^{-1}.
The
bipartite density
of bipartite subgraphs of
G
with parts
A
and
B

is
d_{G}(A,B)=\frac{e(G[A,B])}{|A|\cdot|B|}.
Sometimes the subscript may be omitted when there is no confusion. Similarly, when a graph in question is bipartite, density will mean bipartite density.

Given numbers
x,y
we say that
z=x\pm y,
if
x-y\leq z\leq x+y.
If
n\geq 1
is an integer, then we let

[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}.

For numbers
0<\alpha,\beta<1
the notation “
\alpha\ll\beta
” means that
\alpha
is sufficiently smaller than
\beta.
We remark, that whenever this notation is used in the paper, the relation of
\alpha
and
\beta
can be explicitly calculated, and will always
mean that
\alpha\leq\beta^{c}
for some positive integer constant
c.
Still, using “
\ll
” enables us to concentrate on the essential parts
of the proofs.

2.1

Regular pairs

While we avoid using the Regularity lemma, the notion of regularity plays an essential role in the paper. Below
is a brief review of the basics in the area.

Definition 2.1

Let
0<\varepsilon,\delta<1
be real numbers.
We say that a bipartite graph
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair, if for every
X\subseteq A,

Y\subseteq B

with
|X|\geq\varepsilon|A|
and
|Y|\geq\varepsilon|B|
we have

|d_{H}(A,B)-d_{H}(X,Y)|\leq\varepsilon.

We call
H
an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-super-regular pair, if in addition every
v\in A
has at least
\delta|B|
neighbors and every
u\in B
has at least

\delta|A|
neighbors.

The following well known fact below will prove to be useful, the proof is omitted.

Fact 2.2

Assume that
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density
d_{H}.
Let
A^{\prime}=\{x\in A:deg(x)<(d_{H}-\varepsilon)|B|\}
and

A^{\prime\prime}=\{x\in A:deg(x)>(d_{H}+\varepsilon)|B|\}.
Similarly, let
B^{\prime}=\{x\in B:deg(x)<(d_{H}-\varepsilon)|A|\}
and

B^{\prime\prime}=\{x\in B:deg(x)>(d_{H}+\varepsilon)|A|\}.
Then
|A^{\prime}|,|A^{\prime\prime}|\leq\varepsilon|A|
and
|B^{\prime}|,|B^{\prime\prime}|\leq\varepsilon|B|.

We will use the so called Slicing lemma
[
10
]
at various points in the paper.

Fact 2.3

Assume that
H=(A,B)
is an
\varepsilon
-regular pair with density
d_{H},
and for some
\alpha>\varepsilon
let
A^{\prime}\subset A,

|A^{\prime}|\geq\alpha|A|
and
B^{\prime}\subset B,

|B^{\prime}|\geq\alpha|B|.
Then
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
is an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-regular pair with

\varepsilon^{\prime}=\max\{\varepsilon/\alpha,2\varepsilon\}
and for its density
d^{\prime}
we have
|d^{\prime}-d|<\varepsilon.

Given an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-super-regular pair
H(A,B)
and a vertex
v\not\in A\cup B,
we may insert
v
into
A

without significantly reducing the “super-regularity” of the pair, if
deg(v,B)\geq\delta|B|.
In fact we may even insert a small linear number of vertices into one vertex class, if these have sufficiently large number of neighbors in the opposite
vertex class.

Lemma 2.4

Let
H(A,B)
be an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-super-regular pair with density
d\geq\delta.
Assume that
0\leq s\leq\varepsilon^{2}|A|
and
0\leq t\leq\varepsilon^{2}|B|
are numbers,
S_{A}=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{s}\}
and

S_{B}=\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{t}\}
are sets of vertices such that
S_{A}\cap A=S_{B}\cap B=\emptyset,
and

deg(v,B)\geq d|B|
for every
v\in S_{A}
and
deg(u,A)\geq d|A|
for every
u\in S_{B}.

Then the new pair
H^{\prime}(A\cup S_{A},B\cup S_{B})
is
(3\varepsilon,\delta-\varepsilon)
-super-regular with density

d^{\prime}=d^{\prime}(A\cup S_{A},B\cup S_{B})=d\pm\varepsilon.

Proof:
Let
A^{\prime}=A\cup S_{A}
and
B^{\prime}=B\cup S_{B}.
The lower bounds
(\delta-\varepsilon)|B^{\prime}|
and

(\delta-\varepsilon)|A^{\prime}|
for the degrees of vertices in
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime},
respectively, follows easily from the degree bounds for
S_{A}
and
S_{B}.

Since
S_{A}
and
S_{B}
are very small compared to
A,
respectively,
B,
a simple calculations shows
that
d^{\prime}
cannot deviate from
d
by more than
\varepsilon.

Next we verify the
3\varepsilon
-regularity of the new pair. Let
X^{\prime}\subset A^{\prime}
with
|X^{\prime}|=3\varepsilon|A^{\prime}|
and
Y^{\prime}\subset B^{\prime}

with
|Y^{\prime}|=3\varepsilon|B^{\prime}|,
by convexity of density (see e.g. in
[
10
]
) it is enough to consider
subsets of this size. Set
X=X^{\prime}-S_{A}
and
Y=Y^{\prime}-S_{B}.
Clearly,

|X|\geq 3\varepsilon|A^{\prime}|-\varepsilon^{2}|A|\geq\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon)|A^{\prime}|>\varepsilon|A|,

and similarly,

|Y|\geq 3\varepsilon|B^{\prime}|-\varepsilon^{2}|B|\geq\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon)|B^{\prime}|>\varepsilon|B|.

We need upper and lower bounds for the number of edges between
X^{\prime}
and
Y^{\prime}.
For the lower bound
we can use the
\varepsilon
-regularity of the original pair
H(A,B),
since
X
and
Y
are sufficiently large:

e(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq(d-\varepsilon)|X|\cdot|Y|\geq(d-\varepsilon)\varepsilon^{2}(3-\varepsilon)^{2}|A^{\prime}|\cdot|B^{\prime}|.

This implies that

d^{\prime}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\frac{e(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})}{|X^{\prime}||Y^{\prime}|}\geq(d-\varepsilon)\frac{\varepsilon^{2}(3-\varepsilon)^{2}|A^{\prime}|\cdot|B^{\prime}|}{9\varepsilon^{2}|A^{\prime}||B^{\prime}|},

here we used that
|X^{\prime}|=3\varepsilon|A^{\prime}|
and
|Y^{\prime}|=3\varepsilon|B^{\prime}|.
Simple calculation shows that the latter expression
is larger than
d-3\varepsilon,
hence,
d^{\prime}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq d-3\varepsilon.

For estimating the upper bound, we assume the worst case, when vertices of
S_{A}\cup S_{B}
have full degree into the opposite
part:

e(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\leq e(X,Y)+e(S_{A}\cap X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})+e(X^{\prime},S_{B}\cap Y^{\prime}).

Set
x=|X^{\prime}\cap S_{A}|
and
y=|Y^{\prime}\cap S_{B}|.
Then, applying
\varepsilon
-regularity for
e(X,Y),
we have

e(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\leq(d+\varepsilon)|X||Y|+x|Y^{\prime}|+y|X^{\prime}|.

Dividing by
|X^{\prime}||Y^{\prime}|
we obtain that

d^{\prime}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\leq(d+\varepsilon)\frac{|X||Y|}{|X^{\prime}||Y^{\prime}|}+\frac{x}{|X^{\prime}|}+\frac{y}{|Y^{\prime}|}\leq d+\varepsilon+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|A|}{3\varepsilon|A^{\prime}|}+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}|B|}{3\varepsilon|B^{\prime}|}\leq d+3\varepsilon.

This finishes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

We use the decomposition theorem of the author
[
3
]
below for replacing the Regularity lemma.

Theorem 2.5

Let
0<\varepsilon<1/10
be a number, and assume that
d
is a real number such that
10\varepsilon^{1/5}\leq d<1/3.

Assume further that
G=(V,E)
is a balanced bipartite graph on
2n
vertices with bipartition
V=A\cup B
and
r(1-\lambda)\leq deg(v)\leq r

for every
v\in V,
where
r\geq d^{1/3}n
and
0\leq\lambda\leq d^{4}/2.

If
n>\exp(150\log(1/d)/\varepsilon^{2}),
then there exists a natural number
K\leq 8\frac{d_{G}}{d}\cdot d^{-100/\varepsilon^{2}}
such that
G
admits the following decomposition:

(i)

A=A_{1}\cup\dots\cup A_{K}\cup A_{0}
and
B=B_{1}\cup\dots\cup B_{K}\cup B_{0},

where
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=B_{i}\cap B_{j}=\emptyset,
whenever
i\neq j,

(ii)

|A_{0}\cup B_{0}|<8\sqrt[3]{d}n,

(iii)

|A_{i}|,|B_{i}|\geq d^{101/\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{4}=\Omega\left(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{-1/2}\right)
for every

1\leq i\leq K,

(iv)

||A_{i}|-|B_{i}||\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}|A_{i}|
for every
1\leq i\leq K,

(v)

the bipartite subgraphs
G[A_{i},B_{i}]\subset G
for
i\geq 1
are all
(\varepsilon^{\prime},d_{i})
-super-regular, where

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq(64\varepsilon)^{1/5}
and
d_{i}\geq d-3\varepsilon.

We remark, that in
[
3
]
a slightly stronger result is proved: for
i\geq 1
the
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
pairs
are not only super-regular, there is an upper bound for the degrees in such a pair. We do not need this
stronger notion in the present paper.

The sets
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{K}
are called the
non-exceptional clusters
of the decomposition,
and the sets
A_{0}
and
B_{0}
are the
exceptional clusters,
analogously to the decomposition of the Regularity lemma.
Note, that the exceptional clusters, while can be made small by choosing
d
to be small, could be much larger than the
non-exceptional clusters. Analogously to the Regularity lemma, sometimes we will call the union of the

G[A_{i},B_{i}]
super-regular pairs the
reduced graph
of
G.
The vertices of this reduced graph are the clusters, the edges are the

G[A_{i},B_{i}]
super-regular pairs, these constitute a matching in the reduced graph.
Note, that the
G[A_{i},B_{i}]
pairs may have only a very small fraction of the edges of
G
itself.
Hence, the vast majority of the edges of
G
do not belong to quasirandom subgraphs.
On the other hand, we do not need that
n
is a tower function of
\varepsilon.

The partitioning in Theorem
2.5
is less powerful than that of the Regularity lemma.
Still, the quasirandomness of the

G[A_{i},B_{i}]
pairs, together with the (unstructured) set of remaining edges of
G
not belonging to these pairs,
can be used in embedding problems. This is the governing idea which enables us to use the
above decomposition theorem for tree embedding.

2.2

Probabilistic tools

We use random methods at various points in the paper, and need large deviation bounds for discrete probability distributions.
The following inequality, a generalized version of Chernoff’s bound, is Theorem 2.8 in
[
5
]
.

Theorem 2.6

Assume that
X
is the sum of
k
independent indicator random variables:
X=X_{1}+\ldots+X_{k}.
If
0\leq\lambda\leq 3/2,
then

P(|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|\geq\lambda\mathbb{E}[X])\leq 2e^{-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{3}\mathbb{E}[X]}.

We will also need another inequality, in which we do not assume independence of the variables. It was proved by Hoeffding and also by Azuma, sometimes it is called Azuma’s inequality.
A sequence of random variables
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
is a
martingale
if

{\mathbb{E}}[X_{i+1}|X_{0},\ldots,X_{i}]=X_{i}
for each
i\geq 0.

We have the following important inequality, see e.g. in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 2.7
(Azuma-Hoeffding inequality)

Assume that the sequence
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
is a martingale, and let
\sigma_{i}>0
for all
i\geq 1.

If
|X_{i}-X_{i-1}|\leq\sigma_{i}
for each
i\geq 1
and
a>0
is a real number, then for each
n\geq 1
we have

P(|X_{n}-X_{0}|\geq a)\leq 2e^{-a^{2}/2\sigma^{2}}

where
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\sigma^{2}_{i}.

3

Preprocessing of
G

In order to prove Theorem
1.2
, we need to preprocess the host graph
G
and the tree
T
as well. In this section we
focus on
G.

First we find a bipartite
r
-regular spanning subgraph of
G
in subsection
3.1
. This enables us to use Theorem
2.5
, and to construct vertex disjoint quasirandom pairs

G[A_{1},B_{1}],\ldots,G[A_{K},B_{K}],
which together cover almost every vertex of
G.

Since we want to embed a spanning tree, the exceptional vertices of
V_{0}=A_{0}\cup B_{0}
will be inserted
into the non-exceptional clusters
A_{1},\ldots,A_{K}
and
B_{1},\ldots,B_{K}.

A few vertices will also change cluster in order to make sure that every pair in the decomposition is balanced. This is not an easy
task, it is the subject of subsections
3.2
and
3.3
.
Our main goal in this section is to prove the statement below.

Proposition 3.1

Assume that
\varepsilon,d,\nu
and
\gamma
are numbers such that
0<\varepsilon\ll d\ll\nu\ll\gamma\ll 1.
Let

G=(V,E)
be an
n
-vertex,
\gamma
-non-extremal graph having minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq(1/2-\nu)n,
where
n\geq n_{0}=n_{0}(\nu).
Then one can divide
V
into the
disjoint sets
A
and
B
with
|A|=|B|=\lfloor n/2\rfloor
and possibly an extra vertex
v_{0}
such that
the following hold. The subsets
A
and
B
are decomposed into disjoint clusters:

A=\widehat{A}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\widehat{A}_{K}
and
B=\widehat{B}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\widehat{B}_{K},
such that for every
i\in[K]
we have:

1.

m_{i}=|\widehat{A}_{i}|=|\widehat{B}_{i}|\geq d^{101/\varepsilon^{2}}\cdot\frac{n}{4},

2.

\widehat{A}_{i}=A^{\prime}_{i}\cup A^{\prime\prime}_{i},

A^{\prime}_{i}\cap A^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\emptyset
and
\widehat{B}_{i}=B^{\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i},

B^{\prime}_{i}\cap B^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\emptyset,

3.

|A^{\prime\prime}_{i}|,|B^{\prime\prime}_{i}|\leq\gamma^{4}m_{i}

4.

G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}]
is a
(2\varepsilon,d/3)
-super-regular pair,

5.

if
v\in A^{\prime\prime}_{i},
then
deg(v,B^{\prime}_{i})\geq\gamma^{3}|B^{\prime}_{i}|/3,
and
deg(w,A^{\prime}_{i})\geq\gamma^{3}|A^{\prime}_{i}|/3

for every
w\in B^{\prime\prime}_{i}.

Observe, that the proposition does not claim that the
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]
pairs are super-regular.
Instead, we have a large super-regular sub-pair of
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]

(large, since the clusters
|A^{\prime}_{i}|,|B^{\prime}_{i}|\geq(1-\gamma^{4})m_{i}
), and vertices in
the
irregular
parts
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
and
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
have large degrees to the opposite side. We call
\bigcup_{i}(A^{\prime\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i})
the
set of
irregular vertices
. The irregular vertices need a special care when proving Theorem
1.2
.

Proposition
3.1
is a general result, we think its use is not restricted to tree embedding, it could be
applied to other problems.

3.1

Finding a bipartite
r
-regular spanning subgraph of
G

We begin with a structural fact about
\gamma
-non-extremal graphs. Recall, that
G
is a
\gamma
-non-extremal graph with

\delta(G)\geq(1/2-\nu)n,

where
\nu\ll\gamma.
For every vertex
v\in V
we
define a subset of
V
:

S_{v}=\{u\in V:deg(u,\overline{N(v)})<\gamma n/2\},

where
\overline{N(v)}
denotes the complement of
N(v).

Lemma 3.2

Let
v\in V
be any vertex with
deg(v)\leq n/2+\gamma n/2.
Then
|S_{v}|\leq n/2-\gamma n/2.

Proof:
Suppose on the contrary that
|S_{v}|>n/2-\gamma n/2.
We count the number of edges between
S_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}.

First observe, that

e(S_{v},\overline{N(v)})<\frac{\gamma n}{2}|S_{v}|\leq\gamma n^{2}/2

by the definition of
S_{v}.

Next, if necessary, we add extra vertices to
S_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}
so that the resulting new sets both have
at least
n/2
vertices. If
S_{v}
or
\overline{N(v)}
has at least
n/2
vertices, we leave it as is.
For complementing
S_{v}
we need less than
\gamma n/2
new vertices using our assumption on the cardinality of
S_{v}.
Similarly, we need at most

\gamma n/2

new vertices for
\overline{N(v)},
since
deg(v)\leq n/2+\gamma n/2.
Denote the new sets we have just obtained by
S^{\prime}_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}^{\prime}.

By
\gamma
-non-extremality of
G,
we have at least
\gamma n^{2}
edges going in between
S^{\prime}_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}^{\prime}.

On the other hand, with the newly added vertices we could increase the number of edges
between
S^{\prime}_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}^{\prime}
by at most
2\gamma\frac{n}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{2}=\gamma n^{2}/2.
Hence,
we can add at most
\gamma n^{2}/2
edges to the strictly less than
\gamma n^{2}/2
edges that were supposedly present between
S_{v}
and

\overline{N(v)},
so the total number of edges between
S^{\prime}_{v}
and
\overline{N(v)}^{\prime}
is less than
\gamma n^{2}
– thus we arrived at a contradiction.

\Box

Next we randomly split
V
into two sets,
A
and
B,
of sizes
|A|=|B|=n/2,
if
n
is even. If
n
is odd, we
set aside an arbitrary vertex, denoted by
v_{0},
before the random splitting. That vertex will be inserted back to
G
at the
end of the preprocessing.

The random splitting procedure goes as follows. In the beginning,
A
and
B
are empty sets. For every
v\in V
we flip a coin, independently from other choices. If the outcome is heads, we add
v
to
A,
otherwise

v
is added to
B.

If
|A|\neq|B|,
we will make them equal as follows. Say, that after the random splitting
|A|>|B|.
Then we pick
(|A|-|B|)/2
vertices of
A
arbitrarily, and relocate them into
B.
Note that with high probability the number of relocated vertices is at most
O(\sqrt{n\log n}).

The proof of the lemma below is implied by Theorem
2.6
, we leave the details for the reader.

Lemma 3.3

For every
v\in V
we have

deg(v,A),deg(v,B)\geq\frac{deg(v)}{2}-\nu n\geq n/4-2\nu n

with high probability. Furthermore,
if
deg(v)\leq n/2+\gamma n/2,
then the following properties also hold with high probability:

(i)

|S_{v}\cap A|,|S_{v}\cap B|\leq n/4-\gamma n/5,
and

(ii)

if
w\not\in S_{v},
then
deg(w,\overline{N(v)}\cap A),deg(w,\overline{N(v)}\cap B)\geq\gamma n/5.

The following lemma shows that for spanning random bipartite subgraphs a certain form of non-extremality is inherited with high probability.

Lemma 3.4

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
\gamma
-non-extremal graph on
n
vertices with minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq(1/2-\nu)n.
Divide
V
randomly into two parts,
A
and
B,
having equal cardinality
n/2,
as described above. Then the following holds with high probability:
for every
X\subset A,

Y\subset B
with
|X|=|Y|=n/4
we have
e(X,Y)\geq\gamma^{2}n^{2}/50.

Proof:
Assume, that
X\subset A
and
Y\subset B
both have
n/4
vertices, and assume on the
contrary, that
e(X,Y)<\gamma^{2}n^{2}/50.
Then by averaging there exists a vertex
v\in X
such that
deg(v,Y)\leq\gamma^{2}n/12.
This implies that
deg(v)\leq n/2+\gamma n/2.
Moreover, using Lemma
3.3
, we must have
|\overline{Y}-N(v)|\leq 2\nu n+\gamma^{2}n/12.
Hence, using the definition of the set
S_{v}
and Lemma
3.3
,
A
has at least
n/4+\gamma n/5
such vertices
which all have at least
\gamma n/5-2\nu n-\gamma^{2}n/12\geq\gamma n/10
neighbors in
Y.
Since
X
must contain at least
\gamma n/5
from them, the number of edges between
X
and
Y
is more than
\gamma^{2}n^{2}/50,
as desired.

\Box

Next we show that the induced subgraph
G[A,B]
has an
r
-regular spanning subgraph with
r=\nu n.

This is done by finding
r
edge-disjoint perfect matchings in
G[A,B].
After finding a 1-factor, we delete its
edges from
G[A,B],
and look for another one. Thus, during this procedure we decrease the degrees
of the vertices. It is easy to see that for the existence of the
r
edge-disjoint 1-factors it is sufficient to prove the following.

Lemma 3.5

Assume, that the edges of less than
r=\nu n
edge-disjoint perfect matchings were deleted from
G[A,B].
Denote
G^{\prime}[A,B]

the subgraph what is left. Then
G^{\prime}[A,B]
has a 1-factor.

Proof:
We will check the Kőnig-Hall conditions in three steps.

In the first step, using Lemma
3.3
, we have that the minimum degree in
G^{\prime}[A,B]
is at least

n/4-2\nu n-r=n/4-3\nu n.
Hence, for every
A^{\prime}\subset A
with
|A^{\prime}|\leq n/4-3\nu n
we have that

|N(A^{\prime})|\geq n/4-3\nu n\geq|A^{\prime}|.

In the second step we assume that
A^{\prime}\subset A
with
n/4-3\nu n<|A^{\prime}|\leq n/4+3\nu n,
and show that
|N(A^{\prime})|>n/4+3\nu n.
Observe first that
if there is a vertex
v\in A^{\prime}
with
deg(v)>n/2+\gamma n/2,
then by Lemma
3.3
,

deg(v,B)\geq n/4+\gamma n/4-\nu n>n/4+3\nu n,

implying
|N(A^{\prime})|>n/4+3\nu n.

Next we assume that there is a vertex
v\in A^{\prime}
with
deg(v)\leq n/2+\gamma n/2.
Lemma
3.3
implies that

|S_{v}\cap A|\leq n/4-\gamma n/5.
Since

|S_{v}\cap A|\leq n/4-\gamma n/5<n/4-3\nu n\leq|A^{\prime}|,

there exists a vertex
w\in A^{\prime}-S_{v}.
Hence, by Lemma
3.3
we have

|N(A^{\prime})|\geq deg(v,B)+deg(w,\overline{N(v)}\cap B)\geq n/4-3\nu n+\gamma n/5>n/4+3\nu n.

Finally, in the third step we may assume that
|A^{\prime}|\geq n/4-3\nu n+1.
By Lemma
3.3

every vertex of
B
will have at least one neighbor in
A^{\prime},
hence, in this case
N(A^{\prime})=B,
finishing
the proof of the lemma.

\Box

Corollary 3.6

3.2

A lemma for relocating vertices of
G

During the embedding of
T
we may need to relocate a small number of vertices, that is, some vertices have to
change their clusters.
Given a vertex
v\in V
and a cluster
A_{i}
(where
1\leq i\leq K
) we say that the
(v,u,w)
triple is a
relocating

v-A_{i}
path
, if there exists
1\leq s,t\leq K
such that the following conditions hold:

1.

|\{u,v,w\}|=3

2.

deg(v,B_{s})\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{s}|

3.

u\in A_{s}
and
deg(u,B_{t})\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{t}|

4.

w\in A_{t}
and
deg(w,B_{i})\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{i}|

If we have a relocating
v-A_{i}
path
(v,u,w),
then the relocation is done as follows: first, we put
v

into the cluster
A_{s},
then
u
is relocated from
A_{s}
into the cluster
A_{t},
and finally, we place
w
into
A_{i}
from
A_{t}.
Note, that this way the cardinalities of the clusters
A_{s}
and
A_{t}
do not change, while the size
of
A_{i}
increases by one.
The definition of a relocating
v-B_{i}
path is very similar, one only has to exchange the letters “
A
” and “
B
” in the above conditions.

We say, that
v
is the
first vertex
in the
(v,u,w)
triple,
u
is the
second vertex,
and
w
is the
third vertex.

The triples
(v,u_{1},w_{1})
and
(v,u_{2},w_{2})
are

disjoint
relocating paths, if
|\{u_{1},w_{1},u_{2},w_{2}\}|=4,
that is, if the second and third vertices are different.

Lemma 3.8

Let
v\in V
be any vertex, and
A_{s},B_{t}\subset V-V_{0}
be two clusters, where
1\leq s,t\leq K.

Then there are at least
\gamma^{6}n/30
disjoint
v-A_{s}
relocating paths and similarly, at least

\gamma^{6}n/30
disjoint
v-B_{t}
relocating paths.

Proof:
Without loss of generality, we will only consider
v-A_{1}
relocating paths.
Theorem
2.5
implies that
n/2-8d^{1/3}n\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}|A_{i}|\leq n/2
and
similarly,
n/2-8d^{1/3}n\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}|B_{i}|\leq n/2.
Using Lemma
3.3
we have, that

deg(v,\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}A_{i}),deg(v,\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}B_{i})\geq n/4-2\nu n-8d^{1/3}n.

Set
\eta=2\nu+8d^{1/3},

so the number of neighbors of any vertex is at least
n/4-\eta n
in
\cup_{i=1}^{K}A_{i}

and in
\cup_{i=1}^{K}B_{i}.

Let
I=\{i:deg(v,B_{i})>\gamma^{3}|B_{i}|\}.
Set
B_{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
and
A_{I}=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}.

Claim 3.9

We have
|B_{I}|\geq(\frac{1}{2}-2\gamma^{3})\frac{n}{2}.

Proof:
(of the claim) Let
\alpha=|B_{I}|/(n/2).
For estimating
\alpha
from below, we assume the worst case, that is,

v
has full degree into every
B_{i}
for
i\in I,
and has precisely
\gamma^{3}|B_{t}|
neighbors in
B_{t},
whenever
t\not\in I.

Then we have the following inequality:

\alpha\frac{n}{2}+\gamma^{3}(1-\alpha)\frac{n}{2}\geq\frac{n}{4}-\eta n.

Observe, that here we have
(1-\alpha)n/2
for

\sum_{i\not\in I}|B_{i}|
– it is easy to see, that using the upper bound
n/2
for the total number of vertices
in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}B_{i}
results in a smaller, therefore valid, lower bound for
\alpha.
After rearranging we have

\alpha(1-\gamma^{3})\geq\frac{1}{2}-2\eta-\gamma^{3},

implying that

\alpha\geq\frac{\frac{1}{2}-2\eta-\gamma^{3}}{1-\gamma^{3}}\geq\frac{1}{2}-2\gamma^{3},

since
\eta<\gamma^{3}.

\Box

Claim 3.10

We have
|A_{I}|\geq(\frac{1}{2}-3\gamma^{3})\frac{n}{2}.

Proof:
(of the claim) By Theorem
2.5
,
||A_{j}|-|B_{j}||\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}|A_{j}|
for every

1\leq j\leq K.
Hence,

1-\varepsilon\leq\frac{|B_{j}|}{|A_{j}|}\leq 1+\varepsilon.

From this we obtain

\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}|B_{I}|\leq|A_{I}|.

Since

(1+\varepsilon)(\frac{1}{2}-3\gamma^{3})<\frac{1}{2}-2\gamma^{3},
the claim follows.
\Box

We need a new set of indices: let

J=\{j:\exists S\subset A_{j}\ {\rm with\ }|S|=\gamma^{3}|A_{j}|\ {\rm such\ that\ }deg(w,B_{1})>{\gamma^{3}}|B_{1}|\ {\rm for\ every\ }w\in S\}.

Set
A_{J}=\bigcup_{j\in J}A_{j}
and
B_{J}=\bigcup_{j\in J}B_{j}.

Claim 3.11

We have
|A_{J}|\geq(\frac{1}{2}-3\gamma^{3})\frac{n}{2}
and
|B_{J}|\geq(\frac{1}{2}-4\gamma^{3})\frac{n}{2}.

Proof:
(of the claim) Let
\alpha=|A_{J}|/(n/2).
We use assumptions similar to the ones in Claim
3.9
: if
j\in J,
then every vertex
A_{J}
has full degree into
B_{1},
and for

t\not\in J,

A_{t}
has precisely
\gamma^{3}|A_{t}|
vertices that have full degree into
B_{1},
the rest,

(1-\gamma^{3})|A_{t}|,
have
precisely
\gamma^{3}|B_{1}|
neighbors in
B_{1}.
Then we obtain the following inequality:

\alpha\frac{n}{2}|B_{1}|+(1-\alpha)(\gamma^{3}+(1-\gamma^{3}){\gamma^{3}})\frac{n}{2}|B_{1}|\geq|B_{1}|(\frac{n}{4}-\eta n).

Dividing by

|B_{1}|\cdot\frac{n}{2}
and then rearranging we get

\alpha(1-2\gamma^{3}+\gamma^{6})\geq\frac{1}{2}-2\eta-2\gamma^{3}+\gamma^{6}.

Since
\gamma\leq 1/100,
this implies
the claimed bound for
\alpha.
The lower bound for
|B_{J}|
can be obtained similarly to the proof of Claim
3.10
, we
leave the details for the reader.

\Box

The previous claims help us to use bipartite non-extremality as follows.

Claim 3.12

We have
e(A_{I},B_{J})\geq\gamma^{2}n^{2}/100.

Proof:
(of the claim) We begin with adding
n/4-|A_{I}|\leq 2\gamma^{3}n
vertices to
A_{I},
if necessary, and similarly,
n/4-|B_{J}|\leq 2\gamma^{3}n
vertices to
B_{J}
in order to achieve that these subsets have cardinality at least
n/4.
These are the
extra
vertices.
The upper bounds for the number of extra vertices follow from Claim
3.10
and Claim
3.11
, respectively.

Using Lemma
3.4
we have at least
\gamma^{2}n^{2}/50
edges between the two subsets.
The number of edges which have at least one extra vertex endpoint is at most
2\cdot 2\gamma^{3}n\cdot n/4=\gamma^{3}n^{2}.

Since
\gamma\leq 1/100,
we proved what was desired.

\Box

Claim 3.13

There are at least
\gamma^{2}n/50
such vertices in
A_{I}
which all have at least
2\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|
neighbors in
B_{J}.

Proof:

We assume that
|A_{I}|,|B_{J}|\leq n/4.
If not, one can arbitrarily leave out vertices from them. Let
\alpha|A_{I}|
denote the number of
vertices in
A_{I}
which all have at least
2\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|
neighbors in
B_{J}.
We have the following inequality:

\alpha|A_{I}|\cdot|B_{J}|+(1-\alpha)2\gamma^{3}|A_{I}|\cdot|B_{J}|\geq\gamma^{2}\frac{n^{2}}{100}\geq\gamma^{2}\frac{4}{25}|A_{I}|\cdot|B_{J}|.

Dividing by
|A_{I}|\cdot|B_{J}|,
after rearranging we have

\alpha(1-2\gamma^{3})\geq\frac{4}{25}\gamma^{2}-2\gamma^{3}.

Hence,
\alpha\geq\gamma^{2}/10.
Using that
|A_{I}|\geq(1/2-3\gamma^{3})n/2,
the claimed bound follows.

\Box

Given the set
J
and a vertex
u
we define a new set of indices
J(u)\subseteq J
as follows:

J(u)=\{j:j\in J\ {\rm and\ }deg(u,B_{j})>\gamma^{3}|B_{j}|\}.

Set
B_{J(u)}=\bigcup_{j\in J(u)}B_{j}
and
A_{J(u)}=\bigcup_{j\in J(u)}A_{j}.

Claim 3.14

Let
u\in A_{I}
be a vertex which has at least
2\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|
neighbors in
B_{J}.
Then
|B_{J(u)}|\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|/2

and
|A_{J(u)}|\geq\gamma^{3}|A_{J}|/3.

Proof:

Let
\alpha=|B_{J(u)}|/|B_{J}|.
The following inequality is satisfied:

\alpha|B_{J}|+(1-\alpha)\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|\geq 2\gamma^{3}|B_{J}|.

Dividing by
|B_{J}|
and rearranging gives

\alpha\geq\frac{\gamma^{3}}{1-\gamma^{3}}>\frac{\gamma^{3}}{2},

as desired.
The second part of the statement follows easily, as in Claim
3.10
, from the fact that
|A_{j}|
and
|B_{j}|
may differ only in a small proportion
for every
j.

\Box

After these preparations we are ready to prove the lemma. Consider the triples of the type
(v,u,w),
where

u\in A_{i}\subseteq A_{I}
and
w\in A_{j}\subseteq A_{J(u)},
so
i\in I
and
j\in J(u)\subseteq J.

By the definition of the index sets
I,J
and
J(u),

A_{I}
has at least
\gamma^{2}n/50
vertices
u
with

deg(u,B_{j})\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{j}|
which all can be second vertices. For a given second vertex
u,
if
j\in J(u),
the cluster

A_{j}
has at least
\gamma^{3}|A_{j}|

such vertices
w
for which
deg(w,B_{1})\geq\gamma^{3}|B_{1}|.
Since

|A_{J(u)}|\geq\gamma^{3}|A_{J}|/3\geq\frac{\gamma^{3}}{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}-3\gamma^{3}\right)\frac{n}{2}>\gamma^{3}\frac{n}{15},

we have that for any given
v,
one can choose at least
\gamma^{2}n/50
second vertices, and for any such
second vertex
u
one can choose at least
\gamma^{3}|A_{J(u)}|>\gamma^{6}{n/15}
third vertices. This means
that there are at least
\gamma^{5}n^{2}/750
relocating triples for

Of course, the disjointness requirement is not satisfied.

We can find the disjoint triples from the set of all triples using a greedy algorithm. In the beginning every vertex
in the triples will be
available
. The key observation is that whenever we
choose an available second vertex
u
and an available third vertex
w,
both
u
and
w
will be deleted from the set
of available vertices. This procedure guarantees the disjointness of the triples.

Using that the number of possible second vertices is at least
\gamma^{2}n/50>2\gamma^{6}n/30,
and for each

\gamma^{6}n/15=2\gamma^{6}n/30

\gamma^{6}n/30

\Box

3.3

Balancing procedure

Our goal in this section is to distribute all vertices in
V_{0}
among the non-exceptional clusters, and if necessary, a small
proportion of vertices in
V-V_{0},
such that when the procedure have finished, all pairs in the (new) decomposition are balanced.
The main tool for this is the relocation algorithm given by Lemma
3.8
, which will be applied
O(d^{1/3}n)
times
during the balancing. We remark, that towards the end of the embedding, Lemma
3.8
will also play an important role,
in a slightly different kind of balancing procedure.

We require that no cluster is “overused”, more precisely, the proportion of vertices which participate in any relocating triple during
the balancing must not be larger than
\gamma^{4}
in any cluster.

Recall, that in an applicaton of Lemma
3.8
three vertices change their locations, and the second and the third one of
the relocating triple belong
to some non-exceptional cluster. At any point in time during the balancing, for every
i\in[K],
we denote by
A^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq A_{i}
and
B^{\prime}_{i}\subseteq B_{i}
the sets of those vertices, which
belonged
to

A_{i},
respectively,
B_{i},
in the beginning, and
A^{\prime\prime}_{i},B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
denote the sets of those vertices, which were
added
to the clusters
of the
i
th pair. The clusters in the
i
th pair are denoted by
\widehat{A}_{i}=A^{\prime}_{i}\cup A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
and
\widehat{B}_{i}=B^{\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i}

during the balancing.

These sets may change dynamically.
In the beginning,
\widehat{A}_{i}=A_{i}=A^{\prime}_{i}
and
\widehat{B}_{i}=B_{i}=B^{\prime}_{i}
and
therefore
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}=B^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\emptyset
for every
i\in[K].
At any point in time we have
A^{\prime}_{i}=\widehat{A}_{i}\cap A_{i},

A^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\widehat{A}_{i}-A_{i},

B^{\prime}_{i}=\widehat{B}_{i}\cap B_{i},
and
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\widehat{B}_{i}-B_{i}.

When the balancing algorithm finishes, we have

V_{0}=A_{0}\cup B_{0}\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in[K]}(A^{\prime\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i}).

Recall, that Lemma
3.8
provides at least
\gamma^{6}n/30
disjoint relocating triples for every
v\in V
and every
non-exceptional cluster. The number of vertices to be relocated is in the order
O(d^{1/3}n),
which is
much larger than
dn.
Hence,
we have to be careful, otherwise we may lose the minimum degree in the originally
super-regular pairs. For this reason we will only pick relocating triples that contain second and third vertices
which are “spread out”, we pick them from random subsets of the clusters.

Note that the quasirandomness is destroyed,
since relatively many vertices are incorporated into the regular pairs, even though
G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}]
remains
2\varepsilon
-regular. Still, it is possible to obtain the quasirandomness back for
large subpairs, but for achieving it we have to work hard later during the embedding, in Section
5.2
.

More precisely, we do the following:
for every vertex in the non-exceptional clusters we flip a coin, independently from other choices,
randomly dividing the non-exceptional clusters into two subsets, depending on the outcome of the coin flip:
for every
i\in[K]
we let
A_{i}=A_{i}^{(1)}\cup A_{i}^{(2)}
and
B_{i}=B_{i}^{(1)}\cup B_{i}^{(2)},
where

A_{i}^{(1)}\cap A_{i}^{(2)}=B_{i}^{(1)}\cap B_{i}^{(2)}=\emptyset.

Set
S=\bigcup_{i}(A_{i}^{(1)}\cup B_{i}^{(1)}).
By Chernoff’s inequality the random subsets of a
cluster are about the same size, and we also have other nice properties, in particular, we can maintain
large minimum degrees during the balancing into the sets
A_{i}^{(2)}
and
B_{i}^{(2)}.
This will be discussed in more detail later.

Whenever we need to choose a relocating triple, we will pick one among those triples that have their second
and third vertices in
S.
Since the probability that both a second and a third
vertex of a triple belongs to some sub-cluster in
S,
is 1/4, we expect about at least

\gamma^{6}n/120
triples for every
v\in V
and cluster, which remain for relocation. Here we used the disjointness of
the triples. By Chernoff’s inequality, for every
v\in V
and sub-cluster in
S
there will remain at least
\gamma^{6}n/150
relocating triples
with high probability.

Assume that we are after applying Theorem
2.5
for
G[A,B].

Given a pair
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]
we say that it has a
surplus,
if
|\widehat{A}_{i}|>|\widehat{B}_{i}|,

and has a

if
|\widehat{A}_{i}|<|\widehat{B}_{i}|,
otherwise we call the pair
balanced.
Note, that this definition
works throughout the balancing algorithm.
During the algorithm we call a triple
(v,u,w)

After these preparations the description of the
Balancing algorithm
is as follows.

1.

If
A_{0}
is non-empty and there is a pair
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]

with deficiency, we pick an arbitrary vertex
v\in A_{0},
and then randomly, uniformly a relocating

v-\widehat{A}_{i}
triple
among the available ones with
first vertex
v
and second and third vertices belonging to

and apply Lemma
3.8
with vertex
v

and cluster
\widehat{A}_{i}.

Delete
v
from
A_{0}.
Maintain the sets (cluster and sub-clusters) used in the relocation accordingly.

2.

If
A_{0}
is non-empty and there are no pairs with deficiency, then we pick a pair
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]
with minimum surplus (this could be a balanced pair), an arbitrary
v\in A_{0},
and a random available

v-\widehat{A}_{i}
relocation triple, and apply Lemma
3.8
with vertex
v

and cluster
\widehat{A}_{i}.
Delete
v
from
A_{0}.
Maintain the sets (clusters and sub-clusters) used in the relocation accordingly.

3.

If
A_{0}=\emptyset
and
B_{0}\neq\emptyset,
then there exists at least one pair with a surplus.
Let
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]
be such a pair. Pick an arbitrary
v\in B_{0},
choose randomly an available

v-\widehat{B}_{i}
triple, and apply Lemma
3.8
with vertex
v

and cluster
\widehat{B}_{i}.
Delete
v
from
B_{0}.
Maintain the sets (clusters and sub-clusters) used in the relocation accordingly.

4.

If

and there is a pair

with a surplus and a pair

with a deficiency, then pick an arbitrary vertex
v\in A_{i}^{(1)}
as a first vertex,
and randomly choose a relocating triple among the available ones with second and third vertices
belonging to

Apply Lemma
3.8
with vertex
v

and cluster
A_{j}.
Maintain the sets (clusters and sub-clusters) used in the relocation accordingly.

5.

If
A_{0}=\emptyset,
and there is no pair with surplus, then all pairs are balanced and
B_{0}=\emptyset,

We stop.

6.

Repeat the above steps until all pairs become balanced, and

become empty.

Claim 3.15

The number of relocations is at most
|V_{0}|+2\varepsilon^{2}n\leq 9d^{1/3}n.

Proof:
Recall, that
|V_{0}|=|A_{0}|+|B_{0}|<8d^{1/3}n
by Theorem
2.5
. The total number of relocations done in steps #1 and #2 is
|A_{0}|.

One can perform step #3 precisely
|B_{0}|
times.
It is clear, that if
A_{0}=B_{0}=\emptyset,
then the cluster sizes in the
i
th pair may differ by at most
2\varepsilon^{2}|A_{i}|,

this value follows from
(iv)
of Theorem
2.5
.
Hence, in step #4 the total number of relocations is bounded above by
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}||A_{i}|-|B_{i}||\leq 2\varepsilon^{2}n.

It is easy to see that if the condition of step #5 is satisfied, then all pairs
G[\widehat{A}_{j},\widehat{B}_{j}]
must
be balanced, hence the procedure stops.
Since
\varepsilon\ll d,
we proved what was desired.

\Box

Claim 3.16

For every
i\in[K]
we have
|A_{i}^{\prime\prime}|,|B_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\leq\gamma^{4}|\widehat{A}_{i}|
with high probability.

Proof:
Fix an
i\in[K].
It is clear that
A_{i}
and
B_{i}
may not contain more than
2\varepsilon^{2}|A_{i}|

first vertices from relocating triples, these are the ones used in step #4.

The second and third vertices of relocating triples were randomly chosen from the available ones.
Let
\ell
denote the number of relocations, and for
j=1,\ldots,\ell,
define the random variable
X_{j}
to be 1, if
A^{(1)}_{i}
contains a vertex
from the triple of the
j
th relocation. Since the number of relocating triples is at least
\gamma^{6}n/150

for every first vertex in the beginning, and one relocation decreases the number of available
triples for all available first vertices by 2 (the second and the third vertex may destroy 2 triples of an available
first vertex), the number of available triples for all first vertices is larger than

\gamma^{6}n/200.
Here we used that
d^{1/3}\ll\gamma^{6}.

In Theorem
2.5
there is no upper bound for the size of the non-exceptional clusters. This forces us to consider two cases. The first case is when
|A^{(1)}_{i}|\geq\gamma^{6}n/200.
Then even if all
relocating triples use a vertex from
A^{(1)}_{i},
the claim clearly holds. From now on we assume the
second case, when
|A^{(1)}_{i}|<\gamma^{6}n/200.

Let
X=\sum_{j}X_{j},
then
X
is the number of second and third vertices of
relocating triples we used during the balancing from
A^{(1)}_{i}.

Clearly, for every
j\in[\ell]
we have

P(X_{j}=1)\leq\frac{200|A^{(1)}_{i}|}{\gamma^{6}n},

thus

\mathbb{E}X=O\left(\frac{d^{1/3}|A^{(1)}_{i}|}{\gamma^{6}}\right)<\frac{\gamma^{4}}{2}|A^{(1)}_{i}|.

We define a set of random variables:
Y_{j}=\mathbb{E}[X|X_{1},\ldots,X_{j}]
for
1\leq j\leq\ell,
hence,
Y_{\ell}=X.
Also, let
Y_{0}=\mathbb{E}[X].

Observe, that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{j+1}|Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{j}]=Y_{j},
hence,
Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{\ell}
is a martingale process. It is easy to see that
Y_{\ell}=X
and
\sigma_{j}=|Y_{j}-Y_{j-1}|\leq 200|A^{(1)}_{i}|/(\gamma^{6}n).
Azuma’s inequality asserts that

P(Y_{\ell}\geq\mathbb{E}[X]+\lambda)\leq 2\exp(-\lambda^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})),
where
\sigma^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\sigma_{j}^{2}.

Substituting
\lambda=\gamma^{4}|A^{(1)}_{i}|/2
we have that

P(X\geq\gamma^{4}|A^{(1)}_{i}|)\leq P(X\geq\mathbb{E}[X]+\gamma^{4}|A^{(1)}_{i}|/2)\leq 2e^{-\mu n},

where
\mu=\gamma^{20}d^{-1/3}/(72\cdot 200^{2}),
a constant. It is easy to see that if
n
is sufficiently large, then the probability in question is less than

1/n.
Since the same reasoning works for
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
as well, we proved what was desired.
\Box

Finishing the proof of Proposition
3.1

We are ready to finish the proof. By Claim

, in less than

m_{i}=|\widehat{A}_{i}|=|\widehat{B}_{i}|
for every

Claim

implies that
|A^{\prime\prime}_{i}|,|B^{\prime\prime}_{i}|\leq\gamma^{4}m_{i}
for every

Since

and

using Chernoff’s inequality,
|A^{\prime}_{i}|\geq 2|A_{i}|/3
with high probability. Similar inequality holds for the cardinality of

Chernoff’s inequality also
implies that for every

if
deg(v,A^{\prime}_{i})\geq(d-\varepsilon)|A^{\prime}_{i}|,
then we have

deg(v,A^{\prime}_{i})\geq deg(v,A^{(2)})\geq deg(v,A_{i})/3

and

4

Preprocessing of
T

Recall, that
T
is a tree on
n
vertices with maximum degree
D,
where
D
does not depend on
n.

An important ingredient of the decomposition of
T
is the following folklore result.

Lemma 4.1

Let
\Gamma
be any tree on
t
vertices. Then
\Gamma
has a
split vertex

x\in V(\Gamma)
such that it is possible to group the
vertices of
\Gamma-x
into two forests,
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
such that
t/3\leq v(\Gamma_{1}),v(\Gamma_{2})\leq 2t/3
and there is
no edge connecting
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
in
\Gamma-x.

Let
0<\eta\ll 1
be a real number. Let us apply Lemma
4.1
repeatedly, until each subtree we obtain has at most

\eta n
vertices. This way one can arrive at a decomposition of the tree
T
into the split vertices
s_{1},\ldots,s_{k},

and the sub-forests
F_{1},\ldots,F_{t},
such that (a)
|F_{i}|\leq\eta n
for every
i,

(b)
k\leq D/\eta,
and (c)
T
has no edge between
F_{i}
and
F_{j},

whenever
i\neq j,
while we may have edges between split vertices. Perhaps property (b) is not immediate,
it follows from the fact that even the smallest forest we obtain must have at least
\eta n/D
vertices.

Let
T^{\prime}
denote the
smallest subtree
of
T
that contains all split vertices. This subtree has at most
k
leaves,
since only a split vertex can be a leaf in
T^{\prime}.
Next we
mark
the split vertices and also those vertices in

V(T^{\prime})
which have at least 3 neighbors in
T^{\prime},
the other vertices of
T^{\prime}
are called
unmarked
.

Fact 4.2

The number of marked vertices in
T^{\prime}
is at most
2k.

Proof:
Recall that every leaf of
T^{\prime}
is a split vertex. The desired inequality follows from the fact that
in a tree the number of vertices with degree at least 3 is a lower bound for the number of leaves.

\Box

A path
x_{1}e_{1}x_{2}\ldots x_{t}e_{t}x_{l+1}
of length
l
in
T^{\prime}
is called a
line
if except possibly the endpoints
x_{1}
and
x_{l+1},
every
vertex of the path has degree two in
T^{\prime},
and all the inner vertices
x_{2},\ldots,x_{l}
are unmarked.

Let us consider the maximal lines in
T^{\prime}.
The endpoints of the maximal lines are marked vertices. If the length of a maximal line is at least 10,
we call it
long
, otherwise we call it
short
. The inner vertices of the long lines are deleted from
T^{\prime},
while we keep every vertex of the short ones.
Denote the resulting sub-forest of
T^{\prime}
by
T_{S},
this is the
skeleton
of
T.

Fact 4.3

The skeleton
T_{S}
has at most
20k
vertices.

Proof:

By Fact
4.2
the number of maximal lines in
T^{\prime}
is at most
2k.
Hence, the short maximal lines contain a total of at most
18k
vertices. Taking the marked vertices into account we obtain the desired
upper bound for
|V(T_{S})|.

\Box

Clearly, every component of
T-T_{S}
has at most
\eta n
vertices, since
T_{S}
contains every split vertex.
Since the maximum degree of
T
is
D,
the number of components in

T-T_{S}
is at most
20kD\leq 20D^{2}/\eta.

Lemma 4.4

If
F
is a component in
T-T_{S},
then it
may have either one or two neighbors in
T_{S}.

Proof:
Observe first, that every component of
T-T^{\prime}
is connected to
T^{\prime}
by
precisely one edge. We must have at least one edge, since
T
is connected, and we cannot have 2 or more,
since
T^{\prime}
is connected and
T
is cycle-free. The endpoint of such an edge which belongs to
V(T^{\prime})
is either a marked vertex,
or an inner vertex of a maximal line.

Say, that
F
is a component in
T-T^{\prime},
such that its only neighbor in
V(T^{\prime})
is a marked vertex
x.
Then

F
remains a component in
T-T_{S},
and its only neighbor remains
x\in V(T_{S}).

If
F_{1},\ldots,F_{t}
are components in
T-T^{\prime}
such that all of them have their neighbors in the same long maximal line
\ell,

then they will belong to the same component in
T-T_{S},
which also includes all the inner vertices of
\ell.

On the other hand, if
F_{1},F_{2}
are components in
T-T^{\prime}
such that their neighbors belong to
different maximal lines, then no component of
T-T_{S}
will contain both, since that would mean
a cycle in
T.

Hence, whenever two or more components in
T-T^{\prime}
are united in
T-T_{S},
then these
must have their
T^{\prime}
-neighbors in the same long maximal line. But then this new, larger component has
precisely two neighbors in
T_{S}
: the two marked vertices, which are the two endpoints of a long maximal line.
This finishes the proof of the lemma.

\Box

We summarize the properties of the tree decomposition below.

Proposition 4.5

Let
T
be a tree on
n
vertices with maximum degree
D,
and let
0<\eta<1
be a real number. Then there exists a subforest
T_{S}\subset T
with the following properties:

1.

T_{S}
has at most
20D/\eta
vertices;

2.

every component of
T-T_{S}
has at most
\eta n
vertices;

3.

the number of components in
T-T_{S}
is at most
20D^{2}/\eta;

4.

if
F
is a component in
T-T_{S},
then
1\leq|N(F)\cap V(T_{S})|\leq 2,
moreover,
e(F,T_{S})\leq 2;

5.

if a component
F
in
T-T_{S}
has two neighbors
y,y^{\prime}\in V(T_{S}),
then
y
and
y^{\prime}
are the endpoints of a long maximal line in
T^{\prime},
they belong to different components of
T_{S},
and their neighbors in
V(F)
are
x,x^{\prime}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}.

The following definition will prove to be useful later. Given a rooted tree
\Gamma
with root
\rho
we define
level sets
of
\Gamma
: for
i\geq 0
the

i
th level set,
L_{i}(\Gamma)
includes those vertices of
\Gamma
which are at distance
i
from
\rho.

In particular,
L_{0}(\Gamma)=\{\rho\}.

Finally, we define the
imbalance
of a tree
\Gamma.
Assume that
\chi
is a good 2-coloring of
\Gamma,

V_{1}
denotes the set of vertices colored 1 by
\chi,
and
V_{2}
is the set of vertices colored 2.
Then we let

Imb(\Gamma)=\max\{|V_{1}|,|V_{2}|\}-\min\{|V_{1}|,|V_{2}|\}.

If
\Gamma
is a forest with components

\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{t},
then we let

Imb(\Gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}Imb(\Gamma_{i}).

Clearly,

0\leq Imb(\Gamma)<v(\Gamma)
for every tree or forest
\Gamma.

This notion plays a crucial role in the proof of Theorem
1.2
, as
the embedding is done in increasing order of imbalances.

5

Further tools for the proof of Theorem
1.2

After the preprocessing of
G
and
T
we need one further step before we can start the proof of Theorem
1.2
. Our goal is to find an edge preserving bijective mapping, that is, an embedding function

\Phi:V(T)\longrightarrow V(G).
We construct the mapping
\Phi
in several steps, beginning with the skeleton
T_{S},

and then extending this partial embedding by finding the images of the components of
T-T_{S},
one by one. Once we have determined
v=\Phi(x)
for some
x\in V(T)
and
v\in V(G),
we will not change
\Phi(x).

We construct the function
\Phi
by a randomized algorithm. This algorithm has three phases.
In the first phase, after preprocessing
G
and
T,
we embed the skeleton
T_{S}.

In the second phase we cover the irregular vertices (recall, that these are the vertices of
\bigcup_{i}(A^{\prime\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i})
) by components
in
T-T_{S}.
Finally, in the third phase the vast majority of
T
is embedded, using the very powerful Blow-up Lemma
[
9
]
.

Before presenting the three phases, we need two lemmas, which play essential roles in the embedding algorithm.
The first of these is used for connecting a subtree to be embedded to the already
embedded skeleton. The second lemma is used for covering almost all irregular vertices, a small discrepancy can be tolerated.
We state and prove these lemmas in Sections
5.1
and
5.2
, respectively. Finally, Section
6
includes the embedding algorithm and its proof of correctness.

We need the following hierarchy of the values of the constants used throughout the proof:

0<\eta\ll\varepsilon\ll d\ll\nu\ll\gamma<1/4,

here
\eta
is the new constant we used for decomposing
T.

5.1

Building connections for vertices of
T

The embedding of
T
will begin with the skeleton
T_{S}.
After fixing the images of the vertices of
T_{S},

we will distribute the components of
T-T_{S}
among the cluster pairs
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}],

i\in[K].

Given a component
F
in
T-T_{S}
we need to build the connection between
F
and the skeleton. The lemma
below shows how to find this using the non-extremality of
G.

Lemma 5.1

Let
u\in V
be an arbitrary vertex,
H\subset V
be a set with
|H|=3D^{3}/\gamma,
and

W\subset V-V_{0}-H
be a set with
|W|\leq\gamma n/10.
Assume, that
\Gamma
is rooted tree with
root
\rho
and maximum degree
D
such that
L_{3}(\Gamma)\neq\emptyset
and

L_{4}(\Gamma)=\emptyset.

Then
G
has a copy of
\Gamma
such that the image of
\rho
is
u,
the vertices of
L_{3}(\Gamma)

are mapped onto vertices of
H,
and the images of
L_{1}(\Gamma)\cup L_{2}(\Gamma)\cup L_{3}(\Gamma)
avoid
W.

Proof:
Let
N^{-}(u)=N(u)\cap(V-V_{0}-W-H),
then
|N^{-}(u)|\geq(1/2-\gamma/4)n.

Next we estimate the number of those vertices in
G
which have at least
D^{3}
neighbors in
H.

Denote
\alpha n
the number of those vertices which have
less
than
D^{3}
neighbors in
H,

then
G
has
(1-\alpha)n
vertices with at least
D^{3}
neighbors. Using that the number of edges
incident to vertices of
H
is at least
|H|(1/2-\gamma/4)n,
we obtain the following inequality:

\alpha nD^{3}+(1-\alpha)n|H|\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)|H|n.

Rearranging gives that

\alpha\leq\frac{|H|}{|H|-D^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)=\frac{3}{3-\gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\gamma}{4}\right)<\frac{1+\gamma}{2}.

Hence, there is a set

U\subset V-W-H

with
|U|\geq(1-\gamma)n/2-\gamma n/10-|H|\geq n/2-3\gamma n/4

such that
deg(a,H)\geq D^{3}
for every
a\in U.
If we add at most
\gamma n/4
new vertices to

N^{-}(u)
to make it a set with cardinality precisely
n/2,

and similarly, if we add at most
3\gamma n/4
vertices to
U
in order to obtain a set with precisely

n/2
vertices, then we may add at most
(\gamma n/4+3\gamma n/4)\cdot n/2=\gamma n^{2}/2

new edges to the bipartite subgraph
G[N^{-}(u),U].

Hence, by
\gamma
-non-extremality of
G,
we have

e(N^{-}(u),U)\geq\gamma n^{2}-\gamma n^{2}/2=\gamma n^{2}/2.

This implies that
N^{-}(u)
has at least
\gamma n/2>|L_{1}(\Gamma)|
such vertices which each has at least
\gamma n/2>|L_{2}(\Gamma)|
neighbors in
U.
Since
|L_{3}(\Gamma)|<D^{3},

using the definition of
U
we can find the desired copy of
\Gamma,
which satisfies all requirements.

\Box

5.2

Covering the vast majority of the irregular vertices

For embedding the vast majority of

T
we will use the Blow-up lemma
[
9
]
, but for applying it we need super-regularity.
Recall, that
\widehat{A}_{i}=A^{\prime}_{i}\cup A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
and similarly,
\widehat{B}_{i}=B^{\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i},
for
1\leq i\leq K,

where
A^{\prime}_{i}=A_{i}\cap\widehat{A}_{i}
and
B^{\prime}_{i}=B_{i}\cap\widehat{B}_{i},

while
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
and
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
denote those vertices,
that either belonged to
V_{0},
or changed cluster during the balancing procedure. Recall also, that
V^{\prime\prime}=(\cup_{i}A^{\prime\prime}_{i})\cup(\cup_{i}B^{\prime\prime}_{i})
is the set of the
irregular
vertices. Let us fix
i\in[K]
for the rest of this subsection, and without loss
of generality, we describe, how to cover
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}\cup B^{\prime\prime}_{i}.

If
|A^{\prime\prime}_{i}|,|B^{\prime\prime}_{i}|\leq\varepsilon^{2}m_{i}
(note, that
m_{i}=|\widehat{A}_{i}|=|\widehat{B}_{i}|
),
then
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}]
is a
(6\varepsilon,d/3-\varepsilon)
-super-regular pair by Lemma
2.4
and Fact
2.3
, and we do not need any further preparations,
we can apply the Blow-up lemma.

If not, either
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}
or
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
is large, then we cannot guarantee super-regularity.
In this case we will cover almost all irregular vertices with a few components from
T-T_{S},
the total
number of vertices in these components will be at most
4(|A^{\prime\prime}_{i}|+|B^{\prime\prime}_{i}|).
Hence, the vast majority of

\widehat{A}_{i}\cup\widehat{B}_{i}
will remain vacant.

We will use a simple greedy method for covering the irregular vertices, which is outlined below in Fact
5.2
.
Proving the correctness of it is straightforward, we leave it for the reader.

Fact 5.2

Let
\Gamma
be a forest with levels sets
L_{0},\ldots,L_{k},
and
J
be a bipartite graph with parts
U_{0}
and
U_{1}.

Assume, that the partial embedding function
\phi
have already been found for the first
t\leq k-1
level sets of
\Gamma

such that
\phi(L_{i})\subseteq U_{\langle i\rangle}
for
0\leq i\leq t
(here
\langle{i}\rangle=i\mod 2
).
Let
U^{\prime}_{\langle t+1\rangle}
denote the vacant subset of
U_{\langle t+1\rangle}.
If
deg(u,U^{\prime}_{\langle t+1\rangle})\geq|L_{t+1}|
for every
u\in\phi(L_{t}),
then
\phi
can be extended
to a partial embedding of the first
t+1
levels of
\Gamma.

As it was mentioned earlier, the embedding of the forests of
T-T_{S}
is done in increasing order of their imbalances.
Hence, after finishing the second phase, the imbalances of unembedded forests will be at least as large as the largest
imbalance of those forests, which have already been embedded.

We will follow a general scheme. Let us consider
A^{\prime\prime}_{i}

(analogous method is used for
B^{\prime\prime}_{i}
): using Lemma
5.3
below, we embed components of

T-T_{S}
one by one into
G[\widehat{A}_{i},\widehat{B}_{i}],
such that at least about 25% of the vertices in the components are mapped onto
vertices of
A^{\prime\prime}_{i},
and at most about 75% are mapped into
G[A^{\prime}_{i},B^{\prime}_{i}].
Only the first three levels of a component could be scattered around in
G,
since for these we use Lemma
5.1
.
Therefore, after finishing the third phase, at most about
3|A^{\prime\prime}_{i}|

V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{r}
of sizes
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{r}
(blowing up). We construct two
graphs on the same vertex set
V=\cup V_{i}.
The first graph
{\mathbf{R}}
is obtained by replacing each
edge
v_{i}v_{j}
of
R
with the complete bipartite graph between the corresponding vertex sets

V_{i}
and
V_{j}.
A sparser graph
G
is constructed by replacing each edge
v_{i}v_{j}
with an
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-super-regular
pair between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}.
If a graph
H
with
\Delta(H)\leq D
is embeddable into
{\mathbf{R}}

then it is already embeddable into
G.

Moreover, the following strengthening also holds. Given
c>0,
there are positive numbers
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta,D,r,c)

and
\alpha=\alpha(\delta,D,r,c)
such that
H
is embeddable into
G,
if, for
every
i,
there are certain vertices
x
to be embedded into
V_{i}
whose images are a priori
restricted to certain sets
C_{x}\subset V_{i}
provided that

(i)

each
C_{x}
within a
V_{i}
is of size at least
c|V_{i}|,
and

(ii)

the number of such restrictions within a
V_{i}
is not more than
\alpha|V_{i}|.

In our case the graph
R
is a matching on
K
edges, and
\mathbf{R}
is the set of super-regular pairs
G[\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i}]
for
i\in[K].
The graph
H
to be embedded is the union of the available chunks.

Below we sketch the final steps of the embedding as an itemized list.

Step 1.

First we will assign the chunks to the clusters in the pairs such that
for every cluster the difference of the number of assigned tree vertices and the size of the cluster is at most
3\varepsilon^{4}m.

Step 2.

Use Lemma
5.1
for finding the connections between the clusters of assigned chunks and the skeleton.

Step 3.

Use Lemma
3.8
, the relocation lemma, in order to achieve that the number of tree vertices assigned to a cluster is equal to the size of that cluster.

Step 4.

Apply the Blow-up lemma.

We analyze the above steps as follows.

6.3.1

Step 1.

For any subset
\mathcal{C}
of a cluster let
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})
denote the set of tree vertices which are assigned to
\mathcal{C}.

Right after the second phase

\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})=\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i})=\emptyset
for every
i\in[K],
since
\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i}

denote the vacant subclusters of
\widehat{A}_{i}
and
\widehat{B}_{i},
respectively.
By Observation
6.3
, the total imbalance of chunks embedded into the
i
th pair

G[\widetilde{A}_{i}|,\widetilde{B}_{i}|]
is

||\widetilde{A}_{i}|-|\widetilde{B}_{i}||\leq 10\gamma^{4}m_{i}.

Recall, that at this point every
available
chunk has imbalance
at least as large as the largest imbalance of chunks in the second phase. We use a simple method for assigning
nearly the same number of tree vertices to clusters as the cluster size. For every
i\in[K]
we repeat the following:
take an available chunk with smallest imbalance, and assign its parts to clusters of the
i
th pair so that if

|\widetilde{A}_{i}|-|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})|\leq|\widetilde{B}_{i}|-|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i})|,
then the larger
part is assigned to
\widetilde{A}_{i}

and the smaller is assigned to
\widetilde{B}_{i}.
Otherwise assign the larger
part to
\widetilde{B}_{i}
and the smaller part to
\widetilde{A}_{i}.
After every assignment update the sets
\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})

and
\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i}).

We stop assigning chunks to the
i
th pair, if both of its
\mathcal{T}
-sets become at least as large as its cluster,
that is, when
|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})|\geq|\widetilde{A}_{i}|
and
|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i})|\geq|\widetilde{B}_{i}|
. If
i<K,

we continue the algorithm with the
(i+1)
st pair.

The statement below follows from the fact that the imbalances of the chunks in the third phase are not smaller than the
imbalances of chunks in the second phase. We leave the proof for the reader.

Lemma 6.5

(1) Assume, that an
r
element subset of chunks, denoted by
CH,
was used for covering the irregular vertices of the
i
th pair for

i\in[K]
in the second phase. Then we can assign a subset of chunks
CH^{\prime}
with at most
3r
chunks such that
|Imb(CH)-Imb(CH^{\prime})|\leq 3\varepsilon^{4}m.
This follows from the fact that every chunk has size between
\varepsilon^{4}m
and
3\varepsilon^{4}m.

(2) After assigning
CH^{\prime},
at every iteration step of the above assigning algorithm we have that

\left||\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})|-|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i})|\right|\leq 3\varepsilon^{4}m,

which is
an upper bound for the size of chunks.

(3) Every chunk will be assigned by the algorithm to some pair, no chunk will
remain unassigned. This follows
from the fact that the total number of vertices in available chunks is equal to the number of vacant vertices in
V(G).

(4) By the stopping rule, we have that

\left||\widetilde{A}_{i}|-|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{A}_{i})|\right|\leq 3\varepsilon^{4}m\ and\ \left||\widetilde{B}_{i}|-|\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{B}_{i})|\right|\leq 3\varepsilon^{4}m

for every
i\in[K].

6.3.2

Step 2.

Given an arbitrary pair
G[\widetilde{A}_{i},\widetilde{B}_{i}]
for
i\in[K],
let
F
be a component, which was assigned to this pair. We will use Lemma
5.1
in order to connect
F
to its neighbor or neighbors in

T_{S},
the same way as it was described in Lemma
5.3
for connecting those tree components to
T_{S}
which
covered the irregular vertices. When we find one such connection to the skeleton, we fix the image of

|L_{0}(F)\cup L_{1}(F)\cup L_{2}(F)\cup L_{3}(F)|\leq 2(1+D+D^{2}+D^{3})
vertices. Recall, that
\Phi(L_{3}(F))\subset\widetilde{A}_{i}\cup\widetilde{B}_{i},
while we do not have full control over the images of
L_{0}(F)\cup L_{1}(F)\cup L_{2}(F),
although we can
rule out a set
W\subset V
with
|W|\leq\gamma n/10.

Assume that
\Phi(x)\in\widetilde{A}_{i}
for
x\in L_{3}(F).
We prepare for the application of the Blow-up lemma. For every
y\in N_{T}(x)\cap L_{4}(F)
we let

C_{y}=N_{G}(\Phi(x)\cap\widetilde{B}_{i}).
If
\Phi(x)\in\widetilde{B}_{i},
then let
C_{y}=N_{G}(\Phi(x)\cap\widetilde{A}_{i}).

In the first case we have
|C_{y}|\geq d|\widetilde{B}_{i}|/4,
in the second case
|C_{y}|\geq d|\widetilde{A}_{i}|/4,
here we used Observation
6.3
. The
C_{y}
set is the
restriction set

for the image of the
restricted vertex

y.
Restriction sets of restricted vertices will be used in Step 4, when applying the Blow-up lemma.

Since altogether the number of tree components is at most
\mathcal{K}_{T},
in this step we fix the image of a total of at most

2\mathcal{K}_{T}(1+D+D^{2}+D^{3})<3D^{3}\mathcal{K}_{T}
vertices. This implies, that the number of restricted vertices is less than

D\cdot 3D^{3}\mathcal{K}_{T}=3D^{4}\mathcal{K}_{T},
a constant.

In the beginning of Step 2 the set
W
will contain those vertices which were covered during the second phase,
and whenever we fix the image of a vertex in this step, we add it to
W.

During phase 2 we covered at most
10\gamma^{4}n
vertices, and in this step less than
3D^{3}\mathcal{K}_{T}
vertices get covered.
Hence,
|W|\leq\gamma n/10
at every point in time, so we never get stuck when finding the connection of tree components
with the skeleton.

6.3.3

Step 3.

Let
\mathcal{C}\in\bigcup_{i\in[K]}(\widetilde{A}_{i}\cup\widetilde{B}_{i})
be an arbitrary cluster. We say, that it has a
deficiency
, if

|\mathcal{C}|<|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|,
and has a
surplus
, if
|\mathcal{C}|>|\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{C})|.
We will use Lemma
3.8
for eliminating
deficiencies, and therefore, surpluses of clusters – clearly, if there is a cluster with deficiency, then there must exist a
cluster with surplus and vice versa.

Using Lemma
6.5
, the total deficiency (and therefore the total surplus) of clusters
is at most
K\cdot 6\varepsilon^{4}m\leq 6\varepsilon^{4}n.
This means that applying Lemma
3.8
at most
6\varepsilon^{4}n
times we may
achieve that no cluster in the decomposition has either deficiency or surplus.
It is crucial to do the relocations so that super-regularity is not destroyed in any cluster, and the constant number
of restriction sets must remain large enough after the relocations.
A very similar task was solved in Section
3.3
when distributing the irregular vertices, so we will only
sketch the procedure.

Lemma
3.8
guarantees the existence of at least
\gamma^{6}n/30
disjoint relocation paths for any vertex
v
and
any cluster
\mathcal{C},
where
\mathcal{C}
is a cluster obtained by Theorem
2.5
. Fix
v
and
\mathcal{C}.

Some of the
v-\mathcal{C}
relocation paths were possibly destroyed:
(I)
in the first phase, in the preprocessing of
G,
we
distributed the irregular vertices,
(II)
still in the first phase the skeleton was embedded,
(III)
in the second phase
we covered the irregular vertices, and finally,
(IV)
in Step 2 of the third phase we fixed the image of the first three levels of
components. Note, that in
(II)
and
(IV)
only a constant number of vertices are effected, so we focus our attention
to the other two cases.

Recall, that for distributing the irregular vertices we restricted ourselves for using only a random subcluster for every
cluster. That is, almost half of every cluster remained intact. Similarly, when covering the irregular vertices, random
subclusters were used, moreover, these random choices were independent. Assume, that
(v,u,w)
is a relocating
triple. The probability that neither
u,
nor
w
were included in subclusters when relocating the irregular vertices is
1/4,

and similarly, the probability that neither
u,
nor
w
were included in subclusters used for covering the
irregular vertices is
1/4.
Hence, with probability
1/16
both
u
and
w
belong to intact subclusters.
By Chernoff’s bound, with high probability the number of
v-\mathcal{C}
relocation paths is at least
\gamma^{6}n/30\cdot\frac{1}{20}=\gamma^{6}n/600.

Assume, that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
are clusters such that
\mathcal{C}_{1}
has a surplus and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
has a deficiency. Then we pick an arbitrary
v\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
among the available ones, randomly choose a
v-\mathcal{C}_{2}
relocating path. This is done at most
6\varepsilon^{4}n
times, hence,
there are always more than

\gamma^{6}n/1000
relocation paths to choose from, even for eliminating the last surplus.
Using martingales and Azuma’s inequality as in Section
3.3
, we get that with high probability
every cluster
\mathcal{C}
will participate in less than
\varepsilon^{3}|\mathcal{C}|
relocations with high probability.

Hence, for every cluster
\mathcal{C}
and every
v\in\mathcal{C}
the degrees may change a little, by less than
\varepsilon^{3}|\mathcal{C}|.

Using Lemma
2.4
we also have that the pairs remain
(9\varepsilon,d/4-\varepsilon)
-
super-regular, as they were
(3\varepsilon,d/4)
-super-regular before. Finally, since the restriction sets in
cluster
\mathcal{C}
had at least
d|\mathcal{C}|/4
vertices, after this step every restriction set has more than
d|\mathcal{C}|/5
vertices.

6.3.4

Step 4.

Since every requirement of the Blow-up lemma is satisfied, we can apply it, and embed the majority of
T.

This finishes the embedding, and thereby the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
\Box
