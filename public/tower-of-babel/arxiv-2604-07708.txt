Fredholm alternative for a general class of nonlocal operators Supported by the Australian Research Council Laureate Fellowship FL190100081 and by the Australian Future Fellowship FT230100333.
Francesco De Pas, Serena Dipierro, Enrico Valdinoci

Abstract
We develop a Fredholm alternative for a fractional elliptic operator ℒ \mathcal{L} of mixed order built on the notion of fractional gradient. This operator constitutes the nonlocal extension of the classical second order elliptic operators with measurable coefficients treated by Neil Trudinger in [ Tru73 ] . We build ℒ \mathcal{L} by weighing the order s s of the fractional gradient over a measure (which can be either continuous, or discrete, or of mixed type). The coefficients of ℒ \mathcal{L} may also depend on s s , giving this operator a possibly non-homogeneous structure with variable exponent. These coefficients can also be either unbounded, or discontinuous, or both. A suitable functional analytic framework is introduced and investigated and our main results strongly rely on some custom analysis of appropriate functional spaces.

Introduction

A classical problem in many areas of mathematics is determining whether a given equation possesses a solution and, if so, whether the solution is unique. In some cases, physical constraints introduce restrictions, known as “resonances”, in which
specific parameter configurations either prevent solutions from existing or lead to their high multiplicity.

The case of finite-dimensional linear equations is perhaps the simplest to analyze, yet it already illustrates the potential effects of resonances caused by parameter choices. For example, consider a matrix
A
, a scalar
\lambda
, and a vector
b
. Elementary linear algebra examines the number of solutions
x
for the vector equation

Ax=\lambda x+b.

Two complementary scenarios arise:

•

\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
A
(resonance): The equation is solvable only if
b
is orthogonal to the null space of
(A-\lambda I)^{*}
, where
I
is the identity matrix and the superscript
*
denotes the conjugate transpose (or transpose in the real case). When this compatibility condition holds, the equation has infinitely many solutions.

•

\lambda
is not an eigenvalue of
A
(non resonance): The matrix
A-\lambda I
is invertible, and the equation has a unique solution, given by
x=(A-\lambda I)^{-1}b
.

This simple example illustrates the interplay between resonances, compatibility conditions and solution structure, serving as a finite dimensional model to understand more complex scenarios.

The Fredholm Alternative extends these ideas to infinite-dimensional spaces, addressing linear equations involving compact operators and providing a dichotomy analogous to the finite-dimensional case. As such, the Fredholm Alternative has become a cornerstone of both abstract functional analysis and applied fields such as quantum mechanics, fluid dynamics and linear partial differential equations.

In the context of elliptic partial differential equations, a thorough description of this dichotomy was introduced by Neil Trudinger in
[
Tru73
]
. This framework allowed for very general linear equations, including lower-order terms and with only minimal regularity requirements on the coefficients involved.

In this article, we extend the Fredholm Alternative to nonlocal operators of fractional type. Relying on the notion of fractional gradient, we develop a framework for inhomogeneous equations and address cases involving superposition of operators of different fractional orders, including infinite sums. This extension is particularly relevant for applications, as these operators model phenomena such as biological species in which individuals exhibit diffusive patterns characterized by different Lévy exponents.
In this regard, the distribution of the measure weighing operators of different orders describes the proportion of a biological population adopting a certain dispersal strategy (e.g., for breeding or foraging purposes).
Moreover, this approach offers a “unified” treatment of both classical and nonlocal cases, with the former emerging as a specific instance of the broader theory.

The following paragraphs outline the classical setting, introduce the formalism required for nonlocal operators, and present our main results.

1.1
The operator under consideration

In
[
Tru73
]
,
Neil Trudinger developed a Fredholm alternative for an elliptic operator
\mathcal{T}
of the form

\mathcal{T}u:=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{i}}\left(a^{ij}(x)u_{x_{j}}+a^{i}(x)u\right)+b^{i}(x)u_{x_{i}}+a(x)u,

(1.1)

whose coefficients are measurable functions on a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

(according to custom, here above and in the rest of the paper, the repeated index summation
convention is employed). The goal of this work is to extend this theory to a more general operator
\mathcal{L}
of nonlocal nature.

To this end, we recall that the
fractional gradient
operator of order
s\in(0,1)
can be defined as

D^{s}u(x):=c_{s,n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(u(y)-u(x))}{|x-y|^{n+s+1}}(y-x)\,dx,

(1.2)

where
c_{s,n}
is a normalizing constant vanishing as
s
approaches
1
(see Section
2.1
below). We will denote the
i^{th}
component of the vector
D^{s}u

by
D_{i}^{s}u
.

According to
[
Sil20
, Section 1]
,
the first appearance of the fractional gradient dates back to the papers
[
Hor59
,
Hor77
]
.
The operator in (
1.2
) has been treated in the recent literature (see e.g.
[
SSS15
,
SS15
,
SSVS17
,
SSS18
,
SS18
,
CS19
,
Sil20
,
BCM20
,
BCM21
]
), though a complete understanding of its rather complex behavior is still under development, and plays an important role in the definition of nonlocal
counterparts of classical elliptic operators.

To be consistent with the classical case,
it is customary (see e.g.
[
Sil20
]
) to extend the setting
in (
1.2
) to the case
s=1
, by taking
D^{1}u

as the classical gradient of
u
, here denoted by
Du
. This choice will be formally justified in Proposition
2.6

below. In general, we refer the reader to Section
2.1
for a self-contained introduction to the operator in (
1.2
).

Now, let
\mu(s)
be a nonnegative measure on
[0,1]
, whose support is bounded away from
0
. We formally define the operator
\mathcal{L}
as follows:

\mathcal{L}u:=\int_{(0,1]}\Big(-D^{s}_{i}\left(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}(s,x)u\right)+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\,d\mu(s)+a(x)u.

(1.3)

We stress that if
\mu(s)
is a Dirac delta at
s=1
, than the operators
\mathcal{T}
and
\mathcal{L}
coincide. Furthermore,
\mu(s)
can be either continuous, or discrete, or of mixed type, giving
\mathcal{L}
the nature of a fractional differential operator of mixed order (see Section
1.5
for some practical examples).

We remark that the study of operators of mixed order is important both from the theoretical perspective and in view of concrete applications: indeed, on the one side, these operators often pose challenging theoretical questions due to their lack of scale invariance, and, on the other side, they appear naturally
in biological models, since animals of different species, and also different individuals of the same species,
in many instances exhibit different diffusive patterns of fractional type with different Lévy exponents, see e.g.
[
DV21
]
.
The operator considered in (
1.3
) is however structurally different than several instances already studied in the literature that dealt with general Lévy measures, since
here we aim at capturing the salient features encoded specifically
by a “divergence-type” design arising from spatial inhomogeneity (but, due to the complicated
structure of the equation under consideration, the development of the theory cannot simply rely on variations of
the classical second order theory of elliptic operators in divergence form).

From the technical standpoint, one of the main issues is
the construction of a variational framework for our Fredholm alternative.
Since the fractional gradient appears in
\mathcal{L}
, we are led to consider suitable Bessel-type spaces
H_{0}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{H_{0}^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\left(\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

These spaces have been extensively treated in the literature (see e.g.
[
SS15
,
CS19
,
BCM20
]
),
nevertheless some particular features emerge regarding
\mathcal{L}
which will require some bespoke arguments.

Indeed, first of all,
\mathcal{L}
weighs the fractional parameter
s
over a possibly continuous measure, making it difficult, for a given
\bar{s}\in(0,1]
, to make
D^{\bar{s}}
appear explicitly in our operator
(notice that, in the generality that we consider, it can well be that
\mu\{\bar{s}\}=0
).

In addition,
\mathcal{L}
depends on coefficients that are functions of
s
and
x
. In particular, none of these coefficients are required to be bounded and it is always possible for them to be either discontinuous, or unbounded, or both, and the operator
\mathcal{L}
may not have a specific order of differentiation.

Also, Fredholm alternatives in their simplest formulations are often set in Hilbert spaces: however, in our case,
asking for
\mathcal{L}
to be well-defined in
H_{0}^{s,2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
would force us to impose stronger regularity assumptions on the matrix
[a^{ij}]
, as we will show in Section
4
. To avoid this additional restriction, a suitable functional analytic framework will be
presented and investigated: in particular, a thorough analysis of the operator
\mathcal{L}
will hinge on some bespoke analysis of appropriate functional spaces.

Hypotheses on
\mathcal{L}

This paragraph collects the assumptions we make on the operator
\mathcal{L}

). These hypotheses are meant to hold throughout the whole article.

We ask

1.4
Main results

To cope with the functional analytic difficulties mentioned in Section

Let
\Omega

We denote by

Definition 1.1

Let

We define

To ease the notation, when

A fundamental role will be played by the notion of compact boundedness:

Definition 1.2

We say that a nonnegative measurable function

\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(1.10)

The notion of compact boundedness plays a relevant role in our framework, since it allows us to retrieve a compact embedding for the space

With Definition

This section presents some paradigmatic examples for the operator

Example 1.7
.

If we take

Example 1.8
.

Furthermore, we can take

for any

, and

.

We can also consider more general operators, taking into account a measure

\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}c_{k}<+\infty

and we define

\mu(s):=\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}c_{k}\,\delta\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right).

Then, we obtain the non-homogeneous operator

\mathcal{L}u=-\sum_{k=2}^{+\infty}c_{k}\,a^{ij}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)D_{i}^{1-\frac{1}{k}}D_{j}^{1-\frac{1}{k}}u\,.

Example 1.10
.

While the previous examples deal with a discrete measure, it is also possible to consider a continuous contribution. For this, let
\phi\in L^{1}(0,1)
be positive and vanishing in a right neighbourhood of
0
and suppose that

d\mu(s)=\phi(s)\,ds.

Then,

\mathcal{L}u=\int_{0}^{1}\left[-D^{s}_{i}\left(a^{ij}D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}u\right)+b^{i}D^{s}_{i}u\right]\phi(s)\,ds+au.

We point out that, when
a^{i}=b^{i}=a=0
, the function
f
defined in (
1.11
) is null and thus it is compactly bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
. When it is not the case, instead, in order to apply Theorems
1.4
and
1.5
, we must check the compact boundedness of
f
in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
(sufficient conditions are provided in Theorems
A.1
and
A.2
).

2
A glance at the fractional gradient

This section provides a self-contained introduction to the fractional gradient and its
connection with the Riesz potential.

The results shown in this section are already present, at least in some form, in the literature,
but we provide here a simple and self-contained introduction to the basics of the fractional gradient and we extend the known results to the level of generality needed for our goals.

2.1
The fractional gradient

Let
n
denote the space dimension. For any
s\in[-1,1)
, we set

c_{s,n}:=\frac{2^{s}\pi^{-\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{n+s+1}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})},

(2.1)

where
\Gamma
is the Euler Gamma function. Since in this work the dimension
n
is given once and for all, from now on, we will refer to this constant simply as
c_{s}
.

We point out that the range
s\in[-1,1)
for the definition of
c_{s}
is needed when discussing
the fractional Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in (
2.14
) and Corollary
2.9
.

We mention here that the following results hold true for
c_{s}
(see
[
BCM21
, Lemma 2.4]
):

\sup_{s\in[-1,1)}\frac{c_{s}}{(1-s)}<+\infty

(2.2)

and

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}\frac{c_{s}}{(1-s)}=\frac{1}{\omega_{n}}.

(2.3)

In line with
[
CS19
,
Sil20
]
, we define the fractional gradient as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We define the fractional gradient of
u
as

D^{s}u(x):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle c_{s}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{zu(x+z)}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz&\mbox{ if }s\in(0,1),\\
Du(x)&\mbox{ if }s=1.\end{cases}

(2.4)

In Lemma
2.4
here below we show that, for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with compact support,
D^{s}u
is defined pointwise for any
s\in(0,1)
. Moreover, to justify this definition for
s=1
, we will show in Proposition
2.6
below
that the fractional gradient
D^{s}u
converges to the classical gradient
Du
, as
s\nearrow 1
.

In line with
[
BCM21
, Definition 2.2]
, we now extend the definition in (
2.4
) to a broader class of functions.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
p\in[1,+\infty)
.
Let
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be such that there exists a sequence
(u_{k})\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
converging to
u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
and for which
(D^{s}u_{k})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Then, we define
D^{s}u
as the limit in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
D^{s}u_{k}
as
k\to+\infty
.

Remark 2.3
.

Definition
2.2
depends neither on the sequence
(u_{k})
nor on the exponent
p
(see
[
BCM21
, Lemma 2.3]
). Also, for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
coincide.

We note that, by odd symmetry, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=0.

(2.5)

Hence, applying the change of variable
y:=z+x
, we obtain an equivalent definition of the fractional gradient when
s\in(0,1)
, that is

\displaystyle D^{s}u(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{s}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{s}\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{(y-x)(u(y)-u(x))}{|x-y|^{n+s+1}}\,dy.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Let
\overline{R}>0
be such that
B_{\overline{R}}
contains the support of
u
.

Then, for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

x\in B_{1}(x_{0})
and
R\geqslant|x_{0}|+\overline{R}+1
, we have that

\begin{split}D^{s}u(x)&=c_{s}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\\
&=c_{s}\int_{B_{R}(x)}\frac{(y-x)(u(y)-u(x))}{|x-y|^{n+s+1}}\,dy.\end{split}

(2.6)

Proof.

We use the short notation
R_{0}:=|x_{0}|+\overline{R}+1
. Let now
R\geqslant R_{0}
.
We notice that, for every
x\in B_{1}(x_{0})
and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}
,

|x+z|\geqslant|z|-|x-x_{0}|-|x_{0}|>R-1-|x_{0}|\geqslant R_{0}-1-|x_{0}|=\overline{R}

and accordingly
u(x+z)=0
.

Therefore, recalling also (
2.5
), we find that, for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{zu(x+z)}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{zu(x+z)}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.

Consequently,

\begin{split}D^{s}u(x)&=c_{s}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\\
&=c_{s}\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.\end{split}

(2.7)

Now we denote by
L_{u}
the Lipschitz constant of
u
and by
\mathds{1}_{A}
the characteristic function of a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, we set

\displaystyle g(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{L_{u}\mathds{1}_{\{|z|\leqslant 1\}}}{|z|^{n-(1-s)}}+\dfrac{2\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\mathds{1}_{\{|z|>1\}}}{|z|^{n+s}}

\displaystyle{\mbox{and }}\quad f_{\varepsilon}(z)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\dfrac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\mathds{1}_{\{|z|>\varepsilon\}}.

Since
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
|f_{\varepsilon}|\leqslant g
for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to obtain that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{B_{R_{0}}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}}\dfrac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{B_{R_{0}}}f_{\varepsilon}(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{0}}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz,

which, together with (
2.7
), establishes
the first line in (
2.6
).
Moreover, by the change of variables
z=y-x
, we obtain the second line in (
2.6
).
∎

With the aid of Lemma
2.4
we now prove the following continuity result for the fractional gradient:

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\lim_{x\to x_{0}}D^{s}u(x)=D^{s}u(x_{0}).

Proof.

If
s=1
the result is trivial. Thus, from now on, we suppose that
s\in(0,1)
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
\overline{R}>0
be such that
B_{\overline{R}}
contains the support of
u
.
Thus, by Lemma
2.4
, for all
R\geqslant|x_{0}|+\overline{R}+1
and for all
x\in B_{1}(x_{0})
we have that

D^{s}u(x)=c_{s}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\quad\mbox{for any}\quad x\in(x_{0}-1,x_{0}+1).

(2.8)

Now we define

f(z):=\frac{\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|z|^{n-(1-s)}}

and we note that
f\in L^{1}(B_{R})
.

Moreover, for any
z\in B_{R}
,

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{|z|^{n+s}}\left|\int_{0}^{1}Du(x+tz)\cdot z\,dt\right|\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\frac{\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|z|^{n+s-1}}=f(z).\end{split}

As a consequence, we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem and exploit the continuity of
u
to obtain that

\lim_{x\to x_{0}}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=\int_{B_{R}}\frac{z(u(x_{0}+z)-u(x_{0}))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz,

which gives the desired result.
∎

We are now ready to provide a justification to the definition given in (
2.4
) for
s=1
. The following proposition, indeed, shows that the fractional gradient converges pointwise to the classical gradient as
s\nearrow 1
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}D^{s}u(x)=Du(x).

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
In order to establish the claim of Proposition
2.6
, we prove that, for any
i=1,\ldots,n
,

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}D^{s}_{i}u(x)=D_{i}u(x).

(2.9)

For this, we suppose that the support of
u
is contained in some ball
B_{R}
and set
R_{x}:=|x|+R+1
. In this way, by Lemma
2.4
, we have that

D^{s}_{i}u(x)=c_{s}\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z_{i}(u(x+z)-u(x))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.

Thus,

D^{s}_{i}u(x)=c_{s}\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z_{i}(D_{k}u(x)z_{k}+O(|z|^{2}))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=A_{s}(x)+B_{s}(x),

(2.10)

where

\displaystyle A_{s}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle c_{s}D_{k}u(x)\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z_{i}z_{k}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz

\displaystyle{\mbox{and }}\qquad B_{s}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle c_{s}\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z_{i}O(|z|^{2})}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.

We observe that

\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z_{i}z_{k}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz=0,

whenever
k\neq i
, and therefore

\displaystyle A_{s}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{s}D_{i}u(x)\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{z^{2}_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{s}}{n}D_{i}u(x)\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n-(1-s)}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{c_{s}}{(1-s)}\frac{R_{x}^{1-s}S_{n-1}}{n}D_{i}u(x).

Using (
2.3
) and the relation
\omega_{n}=S_{n-1}/n
, we thereby obtain that

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}A_{s}(x)=D_{i}u(x).

Accordingly, to complete the proof of (
2.9
),
it remains to check that

\lim_{s\nearrow 1}B_{s}(x)=0.

(2.11)

For this, we notice that

|B_{s}(x)|\leqslant c_{s}\int_{B_{R_{x}}}\frac{O(|z|^{2})}{|z|^{n+s}}\,dz=\frac{c_{s}O(R_{x}^{2-s})}{2-s},

which vanishes as
s\nearrow 1
(thanks to (
2.3
)), thus establishing (
2.11
)
and completing the proof of (
2.9
), as desired.
∎

We mention that, as
s\nearrow 1
, the convergence of the fractional gradient to the classical gradient does not hold only pointwise. For example, for any
p\in(1,+\infty)
and
u\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that (see
[
BCM21
, Theorem 3.2]
)

D^{s}u\to Du
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
s\nearrow 1
.

Now, we study the decay rate at infinity of the fractional gradient operator, that, under suitable integrability assumptions on
u
, can be shown to be polynomial:

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
R>0
be such that
B_{R}
contains the support of
u
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}
,

|D^{s}u(x)|\leqslant\frac{2^{n+s}c_{s}\|u\|_{L^{1}\left(B_{R}\right)}}{|x|^{n+s}}.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}
. For any
y\in B_{R}
, we have that

|x-y|\geqslant|x|-|y|\geqslant|x|-\frac{|x|}{2}=\frac{|x|}{2}.

As a consequence, making use of Lemma
2.4
, we find that

\displaystyle|D^{s}u(x)|\leqslant c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\,dy=c_{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad=c_{s}\int_{B_{R}}\frac{|u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\,dy\leqslant\frac{2^{n+s}c_{s}}{|x|^{n+s}}\|u\|_{L^{1}\left(B_{R}\right)},

as desired.∎

Regarding the regularity properties of the fractional gradient, we recall that, for any
s\in(0,1)

and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, one has that (see
[
BCM20
, Lemma 3.1]
)

D^{s}u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
p\in[1,+\infty]
.

(2.12)

In particular, the next lemma ensures that the
L^{\infty}
norm of the fractional gradient can be bounded in terms of the
L^{\infty}
norm of the classical gradient, uniformly with respect to
s\in(0,1]
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and let
R>1/3
be such that
B_{R}
contains the support of
u
.

Then, there exists a positive constant
C
, depending only on
n
and
R
, such that

\sup_{s\in(0,1]}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

We notice that, if
s=1
, we can take
C=1
to obtain the desired estimate. Hence, from now on, we suppose that
s\in(0,1)
.

We observe that, since
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can choose
x_{0}\in B_{R}
satisfying
u(x_{0})=0
. Furthermore, applying the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, we obtain that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\begin{split}&|u(x)|=|u(x)-u(x_{0})|=\left|\int_{0}^{1}Du(tx+(1-t)x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\,dt\right|\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant 2R\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}.\end{split}

As a consequence,

\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\leqslant 2R\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}.

From this and Proposition
2.7
, we have that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}
,

\begin{split}&|D^{s}u(x)|\leqslant\frac{2^{n+s}c_{s}\|u\|_{L^{1}(B_{R})}}{|x|^{n+s}}\leqslant\frac{2^{n+s}c_{s}|B_{R}|\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}}{|x|^{n+s}}\\
&\qquad\leqslant\frac{c_{s}|B_{R}|\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}}{{R}^{n+s}}\leqslant\frac{2c_{s}|B_{R}|}{{R}^{n+s-1}}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}.\end{split}

(2.13)

Furthermore, by Lemma
2.4
, we have that,
for any
x\in B_{2R}
,

\begin{split}|D^{s}u(x)|&\leqslant c_{s}\int_{B_{3R}(x)}\frac{|u(y)-u(x)|}{|x-y|^{n+s}}\,dy\\
&\leqslant c_{s}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}\int_{B_{3R}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n-(1-s)}}\\
&=\frac{c_{s}S_{n}(3R)^{1-s}}{(1-s)}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}.\end{split}

This and (
2.13
) entail that

\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})}\leqslant\left(\frac{2c_{s}|B_{R}|}{{R}^{n+s-1}}+\frac{c_{s}S_{n}(3R)^{1-s}}{(1-s)}\right)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}.

In light of (
2.2
) and (
2.3
), we obtain that the constant appearing
in the above estimate depends only on
n
and
R
, but is independent of
s\in(0,1)
, as desired.
∎

There exists a fractional Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, valid for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, stating that, for any
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

u(y)-u(x)=c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{(z-x)}{|z-x|^{n+1-s}}-\frac{(z-y)}{|z-y|^{n+1-s}}\right)\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz.

(2.14)

While we refer to
[
CS19
, Theorem 3.12]
for a detailed proof of (
2.14
), we show here a useful consequence.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
. Then, for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

u(x)=c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(x-z)}{|x-z|^{n-s+1}}\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz.

Proof.

We take
R_{1}>0
such that
B_{R_{1}}
contains the support of
u
. Then, for any
R>2R_{1}
we define

\displaystyle A_{R}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(z)|}{|z-x|^{n-s}}\,dz

\displaystyle{\mbox{and }}\qquad E_{R}(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(z)|}{|z-x|^{n-s}}\,dz.

We claim that

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}A_{R}(x)=0

(2.15)

and

\lim_{R\to+\infty}\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}E_{R}(x)=0.

(2.16)

To prove (
2.15
), we notice that for any
R>0
,
z\in B_{R}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R+1}
,

\frac{|D^{s}u(z)|}{|z-x|^{n-s}}\leqslant|D^{s}u(z)|.

As a consequence, since
D^{s}u\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by (
2.12
), we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem and conclude that

\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}A_{R}(x)\leqslant\int_{B_{R}}\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\left(\frac{1}{|z-x|^{n-s}}\right)|D^{s}u(z)|\,dz=0.

Taking the limit as
R\to+\infty
, we establish (
2.15
).

Now we prove (
2.16
). For the sake of clarity, we use the
notation
B^{c}_{r}(p):=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{r}(p)
for every
r>0
and
p\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Then, for any
R>2R_{1}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, using Proposition
2.7
,
we conclude that

\displaystyle E_{R}(x)\leqslant 2^{n+s}c_{s}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{B_{R}^{c}}\frac{dz}{|z-x|^{n-s}|z|^{n+s}}

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 2^{n+s}c_{s}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\frac{1}{R^{n+s}}\int_{B^{c}_{R}\cap B_{1}(x)}\frac{dz}{|z-x|^{n-s}}+\int_{B^{c}_{R}\cap B_{1}^{c}(x)}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n+s}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant 2^{n+s}c_{s}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(\frac{1}{R^{n+s}}\int_{B_{1}(x)}\frac{dz}{|z-x|^{n-s}}+\int_{B^{c}_{R}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n+s}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{2^{n+s}c_{s}S_{n}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{s}\left(\frac{1}{R^{n+s}}+\frac{1}{R^{s}}\right).

Since this estimate does not depend on
x
, letting
R\to+\infty
we obtain (
2.16
), as desired.

Now, we point out that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(z-x)}{|z-x|^{n+1-s}}\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz\right|\leqslant A_{R}(x)+E_{R}(x)

and therefore,
from (
2.15
) and (
2.16
), we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(z-x)}{|z-x|^{n+1-s}}\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to+\infty}\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(z-x)}{|z-x|^{n+1-s}}\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to+\infty}\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\Big(A_{R}(x)+E_{R}(x)\Big)=0.

As a result, taking the limit as
|x|\to+\infty
in (
2.14
), we obtain that

\displaystyle u(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}\Big(u(y)-u(x)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\to+\infty}c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\frac{(z-x)}{|z-x|^{n+1-s}}-\frac{(z-y)}{|z-y|^{n+1-s}}\right)\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{(y-z)}{|z-y|^{n+1-s}}\cdot D^{s}u(z)\,dz,

as desired.
∎

The next proposition deals with the differentiability properties of the fractional gradient. For any multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we set
|\alpha|:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
and

\partial^{\alpha}u:=\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}u}{\partial x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}}\,.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, we have that
D^{s}u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
,

\partial^{\alpha}\left(D^{s}u\right)=D^{s}\left(\partial^{\alpha}u\right).

(2.17)

Proof.

If
s=1
, then Proposition
2.10
is obviously true, thus we suppose from now on that
s\in(0,1)
.
We point out that the continuity of
D^{s}u
follows from Corollary
2.5
.
Throughout the proof, for the sake of clarity, we denote the partial derivative with respect to
x_{i}
by either
\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{i}}
or
u_{x_{i}}
.

Also, since
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that

D^{s}u(x)=\frac{c_{s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{z(u(x+z)-u(x-z))}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.

We claim that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\frac{\partial D^{s}u}{\partial x_{1}}(x)=D^{s}u_{x_{1}}(x).

(2.18)

With the aim of proving (
2.18
), we set

f(z):=\frac{2\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|z|^{n+s}}

and we observe that
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1})
.

Moreover, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
and
|h|\leqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{z\big[(u(x+he_{1}+z)-u(x+he_{1}-z))-(u(x+z)-u(x-z))\big]}{h|z|^{n+s+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{|h|\,|z|^{n+s}}\left|\int_{0}^{h}u_{x_{1}}(x+z+te_{1})\,dt-\int_{0}^{h}u_{x_{1}}(x-z+te_{1})\,dt\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{2\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|h|\,|z|^{n+s}}=f(z).

As a consequence, we can apply the Dominated Convergence Theorem to find that

\begin{split}&\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{z\big[(u(x+he_{1}+z)-u(x+he_{1}-z))-(u(x+z)-u(x-z))\big]}{h|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\\
&\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}\frac{z\left(u_{x_{1}}(x+z)-u_{x_{1}}(x-z)\right)}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.\end{split}

(2.19)

Now, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
z\in B_{1}
and
|h|\leqslant 1
,

\footnotesize{\begin{split}&\Bigg|\frac{\big(u(x+he_{1}+z)-u(x+he_{1}-z)\big)-\big(u(x+z)-u(x-z)\big)}{h}-\big(u_{x_{1}}(x+z)-u_{x_{1}}(x-z)\big)\Bigg|\\
&=\left|\left(\frac{1}{h}\int_{0}^{h}u_{x_{1}}(x+z+\tau e_{1})-u_{x_{1}}(x-z+\tau e_{1})\,d\tau\right)-\big(u_{x_{1}}(x+z)-u_{x_{1}}(x-z)\big)\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{|h|}\left|\int_{0}^{h}\Big(\left[u_{x_{1}}(x+z+\tau e_{1})-u_{x_{1}}(x-z+\tau e_{1})\right]-\big(u_{x_{1}}(x+z)-u_{x_{1}}(x-z)\big)\Big)\,d\tau\right|\\
&=\frac{1}{|h|}\left|\int_{0}^{h}\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x+z+te_{1})-\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{1}^{2}}(x-z+te_{1})\,dt\right)\,d\tau\right|\\
&\leqslant|z|\,|h|\sup_{{|\alpha|=3}\atop{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}u(x)\right|.\end{split}}

As a consequence,

\displaystyle\lim_{h\to 0}\left|\int_{B_{1}}\frac{z\big[(u(x+he_{1}+z)-u(x+he_{1}-z))-(u(x+z)-u(x-z))\big]}{h|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.-\int_{B_{1}}\frac{z\left(u_{x_{1}}(x+z)-u_{x_{1}}(x-z)\right)}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\lim_{h\to 0}\int_{B_{1}}|z|^{1-n-s}\,|h|\sup_{{|\alpha|=3}\atop{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left|\partial^{\alpha}u(x)\right|\,dz=0.

Combining this information with (
2.19
) we establish (
2.18
), as desired.

Now, since
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
we have that
\partial^{\alpha}u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
too. Consequently, we can apply the same argument as above to each derivatives of
u

in order to obtain (
2.17
).
∎

For further reference, we also recall the following “integration by parts” formula for the fractional gradient
(see
[
BCM21
, Lemma 2.2]
):

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Let
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
v_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for any
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Then, for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}D^{s}_{i}v_{i}\,\phi\,dx=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}v\cdot D^{s}\phi\,dx.

(2.20)

2.2
The Riesz Potential

This section aims at showing some relations occurring between the fractional gradient and the Riesz potential operator. After a brief introduction on this operator, Proposition
2.15
below will provide an explicit form of the Fourier transform of the fractional gradient. Then, Theorem
2.16
will constitute the main theorem of this section and it will be used to study embedding properties of the functional spaces under consideration in this paper (see the forthcoming
Proposition
3.11
). For a complete discussion on this topic, we refer the reader to
[
Ste70
,
Hed72
,
Gra14
]
.

The Riesz potential of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
is formally defined as

I_{\alpha}u(x):=K_{\alpha}\ast u(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(y)}{|x-y|^{n-\alpha}}\,dy,

(2.21)

where
K_{\alpha}
is defined by

K_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha,n}}|x|^{-n+\alpha}\qquad{\mbox{ with }}\qquad\gamma_{\alpha,n}=\frac{2^{\alpha}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n-\alpha}{2})}.

(2.22)

Since the space dimension
n
constitues a fixed parameter in this work, we will refer to
\gamma_{\alpha,n}
simply as
\gamma_{\alpha}
. We stress that, for any
s\in(0,1)
, the relation between
\gamma_{s}
and
c_{s}
is the following

c_{s}=\frac{n+s-1}{\gamma_{1-s}}.

(2.23)

It is known (see e.g.
[
Ste70
, I, Theorem 1]
) that if

p\in\left(1,\frac{n}{\alpha}\right)\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad q:=\frac{np}{n-\alpha p},

then, for some
C>0
, it holds that

\|I_{\alpha}u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(2.24)

Now we proceed by computing the Fourier transform of the Riesz potential.
To this end, we establish the following preliminary result:

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}\,\widehat{u}(\xi),

where the Fourier transform is intended in the sense of distribution.

Proof.

We claim that

K_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.25)

To this end, for every
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define

\langle K_{\alpha},\phi\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\phi(x)}{\gamma_{\alpha}|x|^{n-\alpha}}\,dx.

In this way,
K_{\alpha}
is identified with a linear
functional from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathbb{R}
.
Moreover,

\begin{split}&\langle\gamma_{\alpha}\,K_{\alpha},\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}\,dx=\int_{\{|x|<1\}}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}\,dx+\int_{\{|x|>1\}}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}\,dx\\
&\qquad\leqslant\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\{|x|<1\}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{n-\alpha}}+\|x\,\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\int_{\{|x|>1\}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{n+1-\alpha}},\end{split}

for some
C>0
, which proves (
2.25
).

As a consequence of (
2.25
), since
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can write that

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)(\xi)=\mathcal{F}(K_{\alpha}\ast u)(\xi)=\widehat{K_{\alpha}}(\xi)\widehat{u}(\xi).

(2.26)

Now, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
, with
\mbox{Re}z>-n
, we define the function

u_{z}(x):=\frac{\pi^{\frac{z+n}{2}}|x|^{z}}{\Gamma(\frac{z+n}{2})}.

We observe that, since
u_{z}\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we can compute its Fourier transform, which is given by (see
[
Gra14
, Theorem 2.4.6.]
)

\widehat{u}_{z}(\xi)=u_{-n-z}(\xi).

(2.27)

Thus, taking
z:=-n+\alpha
,

u_{-n+\alpha}(x)=\frac{\pi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|x|^{-n+\alpha}}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}

and, by (
2.27
),

\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{\pi^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}|x|^{-n+\alpha}}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\right)=\widehat{u}_{-n+\alpha}(\xi)=u_{-\alpha}(\xi)=\frac{\pi^{\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}|\xi|^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\frac{n-\alpha}{2})}.

As a result, recalling the definitions of
K_{\alpha}
and
\gamma_{\alpha}
in (
2.22
), we obtain that

\widehat{K_{\alpha}}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{\alpha}}|x|^{-n+\alpha}\right)(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}.

(2.28)

Together with (
2.26
), this entails the desired result. ∎

Remark 2.13
.

We stress that, if
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for all
p\in[1,n/\alpha)
. Indeed, in light of Lemma
2.12
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\xi|^{-\alpha p}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{p}\,d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}|\xi|^{-\alpha p}|\widehat{u}(\xi)|^{p}\,d\xi\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant C\left(\int_{B_{1}}|\xi|^{-\alpha p}\,d\xi+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}|\xi|^{-(n+\alpha p)}\,d\xi\right)\leqslant C,

up to renaming
C>0
line after line.

With the aid of Lemma
2.12
and a density argument, we now show the following:

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
u\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for some
p\in(1,n/\alpha)
. Then,

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}\widehat{u}(\xi),

where the Fourier transform is intended in the sense of distribution.

Proof.

To start with, we claim that

I_{\alpha}u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(2.29)

To this end, for any
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we define

\langle I_{\alpha}u,\phi\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{\alpha}u(x)\,\phi(x)\,dx.

As a result,
I_{\alpha}u
is identified with a linear functional from
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore,
we set

q:=\frac{np}{n-\alpha p},

and we use the Hölder inequality and (
2.24
) to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{\alpha}u(x)\,\phi(x)\,dx\leqslant\|I_{\alpha}u\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\phi\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant C\|u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\phi\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<+\infty,

which completes the proof of (
2.29
).

Now, by density, we take a sequence
(u_{k})_{k}\subset\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that converges to
u
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
. Thanks to Lemma
2.12
, we know that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, in the sense of distribution,

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u_{k})(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}\widehat{u_{k}}(\xi).

(2.30)

We point out that

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u_{k}).

(2.31)

Indeed, by the Hölder inequality and (
2.24
), for all
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{\alpha}(u_{k}-u)(x)\,\phi(x)\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|I_{\alpha}(u_{k}-u)\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\|\phi\|_{L^{\frac{q}{q-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

which gives that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}I_{\alpha}u_{k}=I_{\alpha}u.

This implies (
2.31
), as desired.

Moreover, we have that, in the sense of distribution,

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\widehat{u_{k}}=\widehat{u}.

(2.32)

To check this, we notice that, for all
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(\widehat{u_{k}}(\xi)-\widehat{u}(\xi)\Big)\phi(\xi)\,d\xi\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big({u_{k}}(\xi)-{u}(\xi)\Big)\widehat{\phi}(\xi)\,d\xi\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|{u_{k}}-{u}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|\widehat{\phi}\|_{L^{\frac{p}{p-1}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

from which (
2.32
) follows.

Gathering together (
2.30
), (
2.31
) and (
2.32
), we conclude that, in the sense of distribution,

\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u)=\lim_{k\to+\infty}\mathcal{F}(I_{\alpha}u_{k})=\lim_{k\to+\infty}|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}\widehat{u_{k}}(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{-\alpha}\widehat{u}(\xi),

which is the desired result.
∎

We now compute the Fourier transform of the fractional gradient.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,

\mathcal{F}(D^{s}u)(\xi)=i(2\pi)^{s}\xi|\xi|^{s-1}\widehat{u}(\xi).

(2.33)

Proof.

We let
R>0
such that the support of
u
is contained in
B_{R}
.

Also, we point out that

D^{s}u(x)=\frac{c_{s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz.

Thus, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have that

{\mathcal{F}}(D^{s}_{i}u)(\xi)=\frac{c_{s}}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\right)e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\,dx.

(2.34)

We claim that

the function
\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}

belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
.

(2.35)

To check this, we observe that, when
z\in B_{1}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\right|\leqslant\frac{|u(x+z)-u(x-z)|}{|z|^{n+s}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{|z|^{n+s}}\left|\int_{-1}^{1}\nabla u(x+tz)\cdot z\,dt\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{|z|^{n+s-1}}\sup_{t\in(-1,1)}|\nabla u(x+tz)|

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{\mathds{1}_{B_{R+1}}(x)\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|z|^{n+s-1}}=:f_{1}(x,z).

Moreover, when
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\right|\leqslant\frac{|u(x+z)|+|u(x-z)|}{|z|^{n+s}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant\frac{\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}(x+z)+\mathds{1}_{B_{R}}(x-z)}{|z|^{n+s}}\,\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=:f_{2}(x,z).

From these observations, we deduce that

\left|\frac{[u(x+z)-u(x-z)]z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}e^{2\pi i\xi\cdot x}\right|\leqslant f_{1}(x,z)\mathds{1}_{B_{1}}(z)+f_{2}(x,z)\mathds{1}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{1}}(z),

which belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
, thus proving (
2.35
).

In light of (
2.35
), we can exploit Fubini-Tonelli Theorem and obtain from (
2.12
) and (
2.34
) that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\left(D^{s}_{i}u\right)(\xi)

\displaystyle=c_{s}i\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\left(e^{i2\pi z\cdot\xi}-e^{-i2\pi z\cdot\xi}\right)}{2i}\frac{z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\right)\widehat{u}(\xi)

\displaystyle=c_{s}i\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sin(2\pi z\cdot\xi)\frac{z_{i}}{|z|^{n+s+1}}\,dz\right)\widehat{u}(\xi)

\displaystyle=c_{s}i(2\pi)^{s}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sin(\zeta\cdot\xi)\frac{\zeta_{i}}{|\zeta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\zeta\right)\widehat{u}(\xi).

Then, by (
2.1
) and by Proposition
C.3
, we see that

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}\left(D^{s}_{i}u\right)(\xi)&=c_{s}i(2\pi)^{s}2^{-s}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n+s+1}{2})}\xi_{i}|\xi|^{s-1}\widehat{u}(\xi)\\
&=i(2\pi)^{s}\xi_{i}|\xi|^{s-1}\widehat{u}(\xi).\qed\end{split}

With the work done so far, we can now relate the fractional gradient with the Riesz potential via the following result.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
\bar{s}\in(0,1)
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, for any
s\in(\bar{s},1]
,

D^{\bar{s}}u=I_{s-\bar{s}}D^{s}u.

(2.36)

Proof.

We point out that
D^{\sigma}u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, thanks to (
2.12
),
for all
\sigma\in(0,1]
. Moreover, by (
2.25
) we have that
K_{s-\bar{s}}\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and therefore
I_{s-\bar{s}}D^{s}u=K_{s-\bar{s}}\ast D^{s}u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Also, by Propositions
2.14
and
2.15
,

\mathcal{F}(I_{s-\bar{s}}D^{s}u)(\xi)=|2\pi\xi|^{\bar{s}-s}\widehat{D^{s}u}(\xi)=i(2\pi)^{\bar{s}}\xi|\xi|^{\bar{s}-1}\widehat{u}(\xi)=\mathcal{F}(D^{\bar{s}}u)(\xi),

from which the desired result follows.
∎

3
The space
H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
: embeddings and inequalities

In this section we present the function spaces that naturally arise when dealing with fractional gradients and partial differential equations. A detailed account on these function spaces can be found in
[
SS15
,
CS19
,
BCM20
,
BCM21
]
.

In line with
[
SS15
,
CS19
,
BCM21
]
, we define the following spaces.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
p\in[1,+\infty)
. We define the norm

\|\phi\|_{H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\left(\|\phi\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\|D^{s}\phi\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

(3.1)

and the space

H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\overline{\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{\|\cdot\|_{H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}.

(3.2)

It is worth observing that the definition above
is well posed, since if
u\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
u
,
D^{s}\phi\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Remark 3.2
.

We stress that, for any function
u\in H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
s\in(0,1]
and
p\in[1,+\infty)
, we have that
D^{s}u
is well-defined according to Definition
2.2
.

Furthermore, in a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have the following setting
(see
[
BCM21
, Section 2.3]
):

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain. Let
s\in(0,1)
and
p\in[1,+\infty)
. We define

H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega):=\overline{\mathcal{D}(\Omega)}^{\|\cdot\|_{H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}.

(3.3)

We remark that
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is a subspace of
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
and
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Also, let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then, the spaces
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
are reflexive.

Proof.

We point out that
H^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is reflexive, thanks to
[
Bre11
, Proposition 8.1]
.

Hence, we now focus on the case
s\in(0,1)
.
To this end,
we notice that the product space
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
is reflexive. Also, we define the operator
T:H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
Tu:=(u,D^{s}u)
and observe that
T

is an isometry from
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
As a consequence,
T(H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is a closed subspace of
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
therefore
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is reflexive
(see e.g.
[
Lax02
, Theorem 15 on page 82]
).

Since
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is a closed subspace of
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it is reflexive as well.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

In
[
SS15
, Theorem 1.7]
, it was established, for any
s\in(0,1)
and
p\in(1,+\infty)
, the identification of the spaces
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the classical Bessel spaces (see
[
SS15
, Definition 2.1]
for a formal definition of Bessel spaces). We refer the interested reader to
[
SS15
, Theorem 2.2]
for a detailed characterization of these spaces.

Now, we present an embedding result for the spaces
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
,
p\in(1,+\infty)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let also

\begin{cases}q\in[1,p^{*}_{s}]&\mbox{ if }sp<n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty)&\mbox{ if }sp=n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty]&\mbox{ if }sp>n,\end{cases}

(3.4)

where
p^{*}_{s}:=np/(n-sp)
is the so-called critical exponent.

Then,

H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
continuously embeds into
L^{q}(\Omega)
.

(3.5)

Furthermore, if
q
satisfies

\begin{cases}q\in[1,p^{*}_{s})&\mbox{ if }sp<n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty)&\mbox{ if }sp=n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty]&\mbox{ if }sp>n,\end{cases}

(3.6)

then, for any sequence
(u_{k})\subset H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
, for some
u\in H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that
u\in H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
and

u_{k}\to u
in
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
.

(3.7)

Proof.

When
sp<n
, the claim in (
3.5
) is immediate from
[
SS15
, Theorem 1.8]
.
When
sp=n
, the claim in (
3.5
) comes from
[
SS15
, Theorem 1.10]
. When
sp>n
,
(
3.5
) plainly follows from
[
SS15
, Theorem 2.2,
(e)
]
.

The second part of Theorem
3.6
is a straightforward consequence of
[
BCM21
, Theorem 2.8]
.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
p\in(1,+\infty)
. Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

and
q
satisfy (
3.6
).
Then, the embedding of
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
into
L^{q}(\Omega)
is compact.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
, we know that
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
is reflexive. Also, by
Theorem
3.6
, we have that the embeddings of
H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
into
L^{q}(\Omega)
are continuous if
q
satisfies (
3.6
). Then, by the theory of compact operators, we obtain that the embeddings are compact if they map sequences converging in the weak topology to sequences that converge in the strong sense (i.e., in the norm topology); namely if for any
(u_{k})\subset H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
and
u\in H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
such that
u_{k}\rightharpoonup u
in
H^{s,p}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
, we have that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}=0.

(3.8)

Aiming at proving (
3.8
), we observe that, since
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)\subset H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the reverse inclusion for the dual spaces is valid, i.e.
\left(H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\right)^{*}\subset\left(H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)\right)^{*}
. Accordingly, any sequence
(u_{k})
weakly converging to
u
in
H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
, weakly converges to
u
in
H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then,
we are in the position of using the second statement in Theorem
3.6
and therefore (
3.8
) is a consequence of (
3.7
).
∎

We stress that, if
sp<n
, Corollary
3.7
constitutes a fractional counterpart of the classical Sobolev embedding.

Now, let
s\in(0,1)
,
p\in(1,+\infty)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. According to
[
BCM21
, Theorem 2.9]
, we know that there exists a positive

, depending only on

, such that, for any

\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C}{s}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(3.9)

Our aim is now to extend this result to the case

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\rho>0
be such that
{\Omega\subset[-\rho,\rho]^{n}}
.

Then, there exists
C>0
, depending only on
n
, such that, for any
u\in H_{0}^{s,1}(\Omega)
,

\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C\,\rho^{s}}{s}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Proof.

Let us set

\tilde{c}:=\sup_{s\in(-1,1)}c_{s},

which is finite, thanks to (
2.2
), and

R:=\left(2^{\frac{n+1}{n}}\left(\frac{{\tilde{c}}^{2}S_{n-1}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{n}}+1\right){\rho},

(3.10)

so that
\Omega\subset B_{R}
.

We establish the desired result for
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, then we will apply a density argument to complete the proof of the claim in its full generality.

We observe that, since
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, we infer from Corollary
2.9
that, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

|u(x)|\leqslant c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-s}}\,dy.

As a result,

\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant A+B,

(3.11)

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle c_{-s}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{dx}{|x-y|^{n-s}}\right)|D^{s}u(y)|\,dy

\displaystyle{\mbox{and }}\qquad B

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle c_{-s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{dx}{|x-y|^{n-s}}\right)|D^{s}u(y)|\,dy.

We first estimate
A
. To this end, we change variable
z:=x-y
and obtain that

\begin{split}&A\leqslant\tilde{c}\left(\int_{B_{2R}}|D^{s}u(y)|\,dy\right)\left(\int_{B_{3R}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n-s}}\right)\\
&\qquad\leqslant\frac{3\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}\,{R}^{s}}{s}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\frac{C\,\rho^{s}}{s}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\end{split}

(3.12)

for some
C>0
depending only on
n
.

Now we estimate
B
. For this, we observe that if
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}
and
x\in\Omega
, then

\frac{1}{|x-y|^{n-s}}\leqslant\frac{2^{n-s}}{|y|^{n-s}}.

Thus, by Proposition
2.7
and the definition of
R
in (
3.10
), we obtain that

\displaystyle B\leqslant c_{-s}2^{n-s}|\Omega|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|y|^{n-s}}\,dy\leqslant{\tilde{c}}^{2}2^{2n}\rho^{n}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{2n}}\right)\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{{\tilde{c}}^{2}S_{n-1}2^{n}\rho^{n}}{n\,R^{n}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

From this, (
3.11
) and (
3.12
), we conclude that

\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C\,\rho^{s}}{s}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\frac{1}{2}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)},

(3.13)

which proves the thesis for
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
.

Now we take
u\in H^{s,1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then, by definition there exists a sequence
(\phi_{k})\subset\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
converging to
u
in
H^{s,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
k\to+\infty
. Hence, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C\,\rho^{s}}{s}\|D^{s}\phi_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(3.14)

Passing to the limit in
k
concludes the proof.
∎

We observe that Proposition
3.8
in the case
s=1
reduces to the classical Poincaré inequality.
Also, relying on (
3.9
) and Proposition
3.8
, we obtain the following properties for the space
H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
,
p\in[1,+\infty)

and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then, there exists a positive constant
{\overline{C}}
, depending only
on
n
,
s
,
p
and
\Omega
, and independent of
u
,
such that, for any
u\in H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
,

\|u\|_{H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\overline{C}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(3.15)

Moreover, if
s\in(0,1)
, the constant
\overline{C}
can be made independent of
p
and takes the form

{\overline{C}}=\left(\frac{C}{s}+1\right),

with
C>0
depending only on
n
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

When
s=1
, (
3.15
) is an straightforward consequence of the Poincaré inequality.

When
s\in(0,1)
, we exploit
either Proposition
3.8
if
p=1
or (
3.9
) if
p\in(1,+\infty)
, obtaining that

\begin{split}&\|u\|_{H^{s,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\left(\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}+\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\\
&\qquad\leqslant\left(\left(\frac{C}{s}\right)^{p}+1\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\left(\frac{C}{s}+1\right)\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\end{split}

for some
C>0
depending only on
n
and
\Omega
.
∎

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then, there exists
{C}>0
, depending only on
n
,
p
and
\Omega
, such that, for all
s\in(0,1]

and
R>0
with

s^{2}R^{s}>C,

(3.16)

we have that, for any
u\in H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant 2\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(B_{R})}.

(3.17)

Proof.

By density, it is enough to prove the inequality for
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
.

When
s=1
the thesis plainly follows taking
C:={\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x|}
.

When
s\in(0,1)
, we take

C:=\max\left\{1+2\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x|,\frac{2^{p}\,\widetilde{C}}{2^{p}-1}\right\},

(3.18)

for a suitable
\widetilde{C}
that will be specified later on (depending only on
n
,
p
and
\Omega
).

With this choice, if
R>0
satisfies (
3.16
), we have that
\Omega\subset B_{R/2}
, and therefore
we can exploit Proposition
2.7
to deduce that

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2^{(n+s)p}c_{s}^{p}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{(n+s)p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2^{(n+s)p}\,c_{s}^{p}\,S_{n-1}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}}{(n(p-1)+sp)R^{n(p-1)+sp}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{2^{(n+s)p}\,c_{s}^{p}\,S_{n-1}|\Omega|^{p-1}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}}{(n(p-1)+sp)R^{n(p-1)+sp}}.

Now we use either (
3.9
) if
p\in(1,+\infty)
or
Proposition
3.8
if
p=1
and we see that

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}\leqslant\frac{\widetilde{C}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{s\,(n(p-1)+sp)R^{n(p-1)+sp}},

for some
\widetilde{C}>0
depending only on
n
,
p
and
\Omega
.

We notice that, in light of the definition of
C
in (
3.18
) and the condition in (
3.16
),
we have that
R>1
, and consequently the map
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[0,+\infty)}\ni\tau\mapsto\tau R^{\tau}
is increasing.
Thus, since
n(p-1)+sp\geqslant s
, we have that
(n(p-1)+sp)R^{n(p-1)+sp}\geqslant sR^{s}
. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}\leqslant\frac{\widetilde{C}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{s^{2}R^{s}}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(B_{R})}+\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{R})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(B_{R})}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2^{p}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

which gives the desired result.
∎

Now, let
0<\bar{s}<s_{0}<1
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of

. Then, in light
[
BCM21
, Proposition 4.1]
, there exists a constant

, depending only on

,

(3.19)

In the following proposition, we provide a new proof of (

),
and in fact we extend the result to the case

, which seemed not

Moreover, we show that the dependence of

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\bar{s}\in(0,1)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Then, there exists
C>0
,
depending only on
n
,
\bar{s}
and
\Omega
,
such that, for any
s\in[\bar{s},1)
,
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
u\in H_{0}^{s,p}(\Omega)
,

\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(3.20)

Proof.

If
s=\bar{s}
, the desired result holds true by taking
C=1
, thus from now on
we suppose that
s\in(\bar{s},1)
.
In this case, we first establish Proposition
3.11
for
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
and then we apply a density argument to complete the proof.

We take
R>0
such that
\Omega\subset B_{R}
. Also, recalling (
2.23
), we see that

\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}=\frac{n-(s-\bar{s})}{c_{1-(s-\bar{s})}},

and therefore, in light of (
2.2
),

\widetilde{\gamma}:=\sup_{s\in(\bar{s},1)}\frac{n-(s-\bar{s})}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}(s-\bar{s})}=\sup_{s\in(\bar{s},1)}\frac{c_{1-(s-\bar{s})}}{s-\bar{s}}<+\infty.

(3.21)

Now we prove the desired result for
p=1
.
To this end, we define

\displaystyle A_{I}:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)\,dx,

\displaystyle A_{II}:=\frac{1}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)\,dx

and

\displaystyle B:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}|D^{\bar{s}}u(x)|\,dx.

We first estimate
A_{I}
: making use of (
3.21
), we find that

\begin{split}A_{I}&\leqslant\frac{1}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\left(\int_{B_{6R}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&=\frac{S_{n-1}(6R)^{s-\bar{s}}}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}(s-\bar{s})}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leqslant\frac{6S_{n-1}\widetilde{\gamma}\max\{1,R\}}{n-(s-\bar{s})}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leqslant\frac{C_{1}}{\bar{s}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\end{split}

(3.22)

for some
C_{1}>0
, depending only on
n
and
\Omega
.

Now we estimate
A_{II}
.
To this end, we set
\tilde{c}:=\sup_{s\in(-1,1)}c_{s}
, which is finite, thanks to (
2.2
).
As a result,
exploiting Propositions
2.7
and
3.8
,

\begin{split}A_{II}&\leqslant\frac{2^{n+s}\,\tilde{c}}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{1}{|y|^{n+s}}\left(\int_{B_{2R}}\frac{dx}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\right)\,dy\right]\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{3n+\bar{s}}\,\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}R^{n}}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{2n+\bar{s}}}\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{n}\,\tilde{c}\,S^{2}_{n-1}}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}\,R^{\bar{s}}}\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{n}\,\tilde{c}\,\widetilde{\gamma}\,S^{2}_{n-1}(s-\bar{s})}{(n-(s-\bar{s}))R^{\bar{s}}}\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{n}\,\tilde{c}\,\widetilde{\gamma}\,S^{2}_{n-1}}{\bar{s}\,\min\{1,R\}}\,\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{C_{2}}{\bar{s}^{2}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\end{split}

(3.23)

for some
C_{2}>0
, depending
only on
n
and
\Omega
.

In order to estimate
B
, we use Proposition
2.7
and
3.8

to obtain that

\begin{split}&B\leqslant 2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{n+\bar{s}}}\right)\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad=\frac{S_{n-1}2^{n}\tilde{c}}{\bar{s}\,R^{\bar{s}}}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{C_{3}}{\bar{s}^{2}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\end{split}

(3.24)

for some
C_{3}>0
, depending only on
n
and
\Omega
.

Now we recall that, in light of Theorem
2.16
,
D^{\bar{s}}u=I_{s-\bar{s}}D^{s}u
, and thus, recalling the definition of
I_{s-\bar{s}}

in (

)

\displaystyle\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\bar{s}}u(x)|\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|K_{s-\bar{s}}\ast D^{s}u(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{D^{s}u(y)}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right|\,dx.

As a consequence of this and the estimates provided in (
3.22
),
(
3.23
) and (
3.24
), we conclude that

\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant A_{I}+A_{II}+B\leqslant\frac{C_{4}}{\bar{s}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\bar{s}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for some
C_{4}>0
, depending only on
n
and
\Omega
.
Hence, the proof of the desired claim
is complete for
p=1
and
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
.

We now establish the desired result for
p\in(1,+\infty)
.
For this, we define

\displaystyle A_{I}(p):=\frac{2^{p}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)^{p}\,dx,

\displaystyle A_{II}(p):=\frac{2^{p}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}}\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)^{p}\,dx

and

\displaystyle B(p):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}|D^{\bar{s}}u(x)|^{p}\,dx.

We first estimate
A_{I}(p)
. For this purpose, we observe that, if
x\in B_{2R}
, setting
p^{\prime}:=\frac{p}{p-1}
and employing the Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{\frac{n-(s-\bar{s})}{p}}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\frac{n-(s-\bar{s})}{p^{\prime}}}}\,dy

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\int_{B_{6R}}\frac{dz}{|z|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left(\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{S_{n-1}(6R)^{s-\bar{s}}}{s-\bar{s}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}\left(\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\frac{6S_{n-1}\max\{1,R\}}{(s-\bar{s})^{\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}}}\left(\int_{B_{4R}}\frac{|D^{s}u(y)|^{p}}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\,dy\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

Consequently,

\begin{split}A_{I}(p)&\leqslant\frac{(12S_{n-1}\max\{1,R\})^{p}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}(s-\bar{s})^{\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}}}\int_{B_{4R}}\left(\int_{B_{R_{2}}}\frac{dx}{|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\right)|D^{s}u(y)|^{p}\,dy\\
&=\frac{12^{p+s-\bar{s}}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p}\,R^{s-\bar{s}}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}(s-\bar{s})^{\frac{p}{p^{\prime}}+1}}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&=\frac{12^{p+s-\bar{s}}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p}\,R^{s-\bar{s}}}{\big(\gamma_{s-\bar{s}}(s-\bar{s})\big)^{p}}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leqslant\frac{12^{p+1}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p+1}\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{\big(n-(s-\bar{s})\big)^{p}}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\\
&\leqslant\frac{12^{p+1}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p+1}\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{\bar{s}^{p}}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\end{split}

Now we estimate
A_{II}(p)
. By Proposition
2.7
and equation (
3.9
), we see that

\begin{split}A_{II}(p)&\leqslant\frac{2^{p+n+s}\tilde{c}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}}\left[\int_{B_{2R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{n+s}|x-y|^{n-(s-\bar{s})}}\right)^{p}\,dx\right]\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{p}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}}{\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{4R}}\frac{dy}{|y|^{2n+\bar{s}}}\right)^{p}\|u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{p}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{p}|\Omega|^{p-1}}{(4R)^{p(n+\bar{s})}\gamma^{p}_{s-\bar{s}}}\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\\
&\leqslant\frac{2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{p}|\Omega|^{p-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{(4R)^{p(n+\bar{s})}\bar{s}^{p}}\|u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p}\\
&\leqslant\frac{C\,2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{p}|\Omega|^{p-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{(4R)^{p(n+\bar{s})}\bar{s}^{2p}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}^{p},\end{split}

where
C
depends only on
n
and
\Omega
.

Now we estimate
B(p)
. We will proceed in a similar way as for
p=1
,
using Proposition
2.7
and equation (
3.9
). The details go as follows:

\begin{split}B(p)&\leqslant(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{2R}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{p(n+\bar{s})}}\right)\|u\|^{p}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&=\frac{(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}S_{n-1}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)R^{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}S_{n-1}\,|\Omega|^{p-1}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)R^{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}}\|u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\\
&\leqslant\frac{C(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}S_{n-1}\,|\Omega|^{p-1}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)R^{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}\bar{s}^{p}}\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}.\end{split}

Gathering these estimates and recalling
Theorem
2.16
, we conclude that

\begin{split}&\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant A_{I}(p)+A_{II}(p)+B(p)\\
&\qquad\leqslant\left(\frac{12^{p+1}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p+1}\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{\bar{s}^{p}}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{C\,2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{p}|\Omega|^{p-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{(4R)^{p(n+\bar{s})}\bar{s}^{2p}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{C(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}S_{n-1}\,|\Omega|^{p-1}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)R^{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}\bar{s}^{p}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}.\end{split}

(3.25)

Now we observe that, for all
a
,
b\geqslant 0
, we have that
a^{p}+b^{p}\leqslant(a+b)^{p}
, therefore,
for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{j}\geqslant 0
, we have that

\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}^{p}\leqslant\left(\sum_{i=1}^{j}a_{i}\right)^{p}.

Using this into (
3.25
), we thereby obtain that

\begin{split}\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}&\leqslant\left(\frac{12^{p+1}S_{n-1}^{p+2}\max\{1,R\}^{p+1}\,\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{\bar{s}^{p}}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad+\frac{C\,2^{p+n+s+p(n-s+\bar{s})}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{p}|\Omega|^{p-1}\widetilde{\gamma}^{p}}{(4R)^{p(n+\bar{s})}\bar{s}^{2p}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{C(2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c})^{p}S_{n-1}\,|\Omega|^{p-1}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)R^{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}\bar{s}^{p}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}\\
&\leqslant\left(\frac{12^{1+\frac{1}{p}}S_{n-1}^{1+\frac{2}{p}}\max\{1,R\}^{1+\frac{1}{p}}\,\widetilde{\gamma}}{\bar{s}}\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad+\frac{C^{\frac{1}{p}}\,2^{1+n-s+\bar{s}+\frac{n+s}{p}}\tilde{c}^{\frac{1}{p}}\,S_{n-1}|\Omega|^{\frac{p-1}{p}}\widetilde{\gamma}}{(4R)^{n+\bar{s}}\bar{s}^{2}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\left.+\frac{C^{\frac{1}{p}}2^{n+\bar{s}}\tilde{c}\,S_{n-1}^{\frac{1}{p}}\,|\Omega|^{\frac{p-1}{p}}}{(n(p-1)+\bar{s}p)^{\frac{1}{p}}R^{\frac{n(p-1)+\bar{s}p}{p}}\bar{s}}\right)\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})}.\end{split}

(3.26)

Moreover, we point out that, for all
M\geqslant 0
, we have that

\min\{1,M\}\leqslant M^{\frac{1}{p}}\leqslant\max\{1,M\}.

From this and (
3.26
), we infer that

\|D^{\bar{s}}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\widetilde{C}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R})},

for some
\widetilde{C}>0
, depending only on
n
,
\bar{s}
and
\Omega
, as desired.

Now we complete the proof Proposition
3.11
by
employing a density argument. More precisely, let
u\in H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
and
(\phi_{k})\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
converging to
u
in
u\in H^{s,p}_{0}(\Omega)
as
k\to+\infty
. Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\|D^{\bar{s}}\phi_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C\|D^{s}\phi_{k}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

(3.27)

Furthermore, since
(D^{s}\phi_{k})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we see that
(D^{\bar{s}}\phi_{k})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
too,
thanks to (
3.27
).
As a result, the thesis plainly follows passing to the limit as
k\to+\infty
in (
3.27
).
∎

In addition to Proposition
3.11
, we have the following statement, that takes
into account the
L^{p}
-norm of the classical gradient.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
s\in(0,1]
,
p\in[1,+\infty)
and
\Omega
be a bounded domain of

.

Then, there exists

, depending only on
n
,

and

, such that, for all

,

\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant\frac{C}{s}\|Du\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Proof.

If

, then the claim is obviously true.
If instead

, we recall
[
BCM21
, Proposition 2.7]
to conclude that

∎

4
The function spaces
L^{p}(h,\Omega)
and
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

In this section we introduce the function spaces related to the operator
\mathcal{L}
in (
1.3
).

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t\in[1,+\infty]

and
h:\Omega\to[0,+\infty]
be a measurable function such that
h^{-1}\in L^{t}(\Omega)
. For any
p\in[1,+\infty]
,
we define the norm

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{p}(h,\Omega)}=\begin{cases}\left(\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}h(x)|u(x)|^{p}\ dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\mbox{ if }p\in[1,+\infty),\\
\displaystyle\esssup_{x\in\Omega}|u(x)|&\mbox{ if }p=+\infty,\end{cases}

where the
\esssup
is intended with respect to the measure
\nu(A):=\int_{A}h(x)\,dx
, for any set
A\subset\Omega
.

Then, we write
L^{p}(h,\Omega)
for the Banach space of functions satisfying
\|u\|_{L^{p}(h,\Omega)}<+\infty
.

Remark 4.1
.

We point out that the space
L^{p}(h,\Omega)
is not empty. Indeed, for any
p\in[1,+\infty)
, let

A_{h}:=\left\{x\in\Omega:h^{-1}(x)\geqslant 1\right\}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad B_{h}:=\left\{x\in\Omega:h^{-1}(x)<1\right\}.

(4.1)

Then, if
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function satisfying

|u(x)|\leqslant C\begin{cases}h^{-\frac{1+t}{p}}(x)&\mbox{if }x\in A_{h},\\
h^{-\frac{1}{p}}(x)&\mbox{if }x\in B_{h},\end{cases}

(4.2)

for some constant
C>0
, we have that

\int_{\Omega}h(x)|u(x)|^{p}\leqslant C\left(\int_{A_{h}}h^{-t}(x)\,dx+\int_{B_{h}}\,dx\right)<+\infty.

Thus,
u
belongs to
L^{p}(h,\Omega)
.

Moreover, we notice that any measurable function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
such that
|u(x)|\leqslant Ch^{-1}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega
, for some constant
C>0
, lies in
L^{\infty}(h,\Omega)
.

Furthermore, if
h\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
, then
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)\subset L^{p}(h,\Omega)
for any
p\in[1,+\infty)
.

In this setting, we have the following result:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
t\in[1,+\infty]
and
h^{-1}\in L^{t}(\Omega)
. Let also

p\in\left[\frac{t+1}{t},+\infty\right)

(4.3)

Then, for all measurable functions
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
,

\|u\|_{L^{\frac{pt}{t+1}}(\Omega)}\leqslant\|h^{-1}\|^{\frac{1}{p}}_{L^{t}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{p}(h,\Omega)}.

Proof.

We set

q:=\frac{pt}{t+1}

(4.4)

and notice that
q\geqslant 1
, thanks to (
4.3
).

If
t\in[1,+\infty)
, we apply the Hölder inequality with exponents
\alpha:=\frac{t+1}{t}
and
\beta:=t+1

and find that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{q}\,dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{q}h^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}(x)h^{-\frac{1}{\alpha}}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{q\alpha}h(x)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(\int_{\Omega}h^{-\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}(x)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{\frac{q(t+1)}{t}}h(x)\,dx\right)^{\frac{t}{t+1}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left|h(x)\right|^{-t}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{t+1}}.

The desired result follows from (
4.4
).

When
t=+\infty
, we have that
q=p
, and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{q}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{p}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}h^{-1}(x)h(x)|u(x)|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\|h^{-1}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{L^{p}(h,\Omega)}^{p},

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we deal with the definition of the space
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.
For this, we let
\delta>0
be given by (
1.5
) and set

p(\delta):=\frac{1+\delta}{1+\frac{\delta}{2}}.

(4.5)

We stress that
p(\delta)\in(1,2)
.

We recall the assumptions made on
\mathcal{A}
and on
\mu(s)
in Section
1.3
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
g\in L^{1}(\Omega,[0,+\infty))
.

Then, the bilinear form

\begin{split}&\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\\
&:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}a^{ij}_{S}(s,x)D_{i}^{s}u(x)D_{j}^{s}v(x)\,d\mu(s)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}g(x)u(x)v(x)\,dx\end{split}

(4.6)

defines a real scalar product on
\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
s_{0}\in(0,1]
denote the minimum of the support of
\mu

(we stress that
s_{0}>0
since the support of
\mu
is assumed to be bounded away from
0
).
Let
R
be the constant given by condition (
1.5
) and
R_{1}>0
be such that
\Omega\subset B_{{R_{1}}/2}
.
Let
C
be the constant appearing in Proposition
3.10
and

R_{2}:=2\sup_{s\in[s_{0},1]}\left(\frac{C}{s^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}.

(4.7)

Let also
\overline{R}:=\max\{R,R_{1},R_{2}\}
.

We first claim that, for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,

\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}<+\infty.

(4.8)

For this purpose, recalling (
1.5
) and Proposition
2.7
, we estimate

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}}\int_{(0,1]}\Lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(s)\,dx\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\|{\phi}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}}\int_{(0,1]}\frac{4^{n+s}c_{s}^{2}\Lambda(x)}{|x|^{2n+2s}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant 4^{n+1}\sup_{s\in(0,1]}c_{s}^{2}\,\mu([s_{0},1])\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}}\frac{\Lambda(x)}{|x|^{2n}}\,dx\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant C4^{n+1}\sup_{s\in(0,1]}c_{s}^{2}\,\mu([s_{0},1])\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus B_{\overline{R}}}\frac{dx}{|x|^{2n-p}}\\
&\qquad\qquad<+\infty.\end{split}

(4.9)

Moreover,
we observe that, in light of (
1.5
),

\displaystyle\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(\overline{B}_{R})}=\int_{B_{\overline{R}}}|\Lambda(x)|\,dx=\int_{B_{R}}|\Lambda(x)|\,dx+\int_{B_{\overline{R}}\setminus B_{R}}|\Lambda(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(B_{R})}+C\int_{B_{\overline{R}}\setminus B_{R}}|x|^{p}\,dx,

which is a finite quantity.

Accordingly,
exploiting Lemma
2.8
,

\begin{split}&\int_{B_{\overline{R}}}\int_{(0,1]}\Lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,d\mu\,dx\leqslant\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(B_{\overline{R}})}\int_{[s_{0},1]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\overline{B}_{R})}\,d\mu\\
&\qquad\leqslant\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(B_{\overline{R}})}\mu([s_{0},1])\sup_{s\in[s_{0},1]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{\overline{R}})}\\
&\qquad\leqslant C\|\Lambda\|_{L^{1}(B_{\overline{R}})}\mu([s_{0},1])\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<+\infty.\end{split}

(4.10)

As a consequence, from (
1.4
), (
4.9
) and (
4.10
) we have that

\displaystyle\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{[0,1]}\Lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(s)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}g(x)|u(x)|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle+\infty,

which establishes (
4.8
).

Now, we show that the bilinear form defined in (
4.6
) is a scalar product. It is clearly linear and symmetric, thus we only need to show that it is positive definite. To this end, we use condition (
1.4
) to see that

\displaystyle\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{[s_{0},1]}\lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{[s_{0},1]}\int_{B_{\overline{R}}}\lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,dx\,d\mu(s).

Moreover, taking
t:=1+\delta
,
p:=2
,
\Omega:=B_{R}
and
h:=\lambda

in Lemma
4.2
(used here with
D^{s}u
in place of
u
), we see that

\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(B_{R})}\leqslant\|\lambda^{-1}\|^{-1}_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{R})}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{2}(\lambda,B_{R})}^{2}.

(4.11)

Accordingly, we obtain that

\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\geqslant\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{\overline{R}})}^{-1}\int_{[s_{0},1]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(B_{\overline{R}})}\,d\mu(s).

Therefore, using Proposition
3.10

(notice that the assumption in (
3.16
)
is satisfied thanks to (
4.7
)), we obtain that

\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\geqslant\frac{\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{\overline{R}})}^{-1}}{2}\int_{[s_{0},1]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,d\mu(s).

This and Propositions
3.11
(used here with
\bar{s}:=s_{0}
) and
3.12
(used with
s:=s_{0}
) give that

\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\geqslant\frac{C\mu([s_{0},1])\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{\overline{R}})}^{-1}}{2}\|D^{s_{0}}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Therefore, using Proposition
3.10

(notice that the assumption in (
3.16
)
is satisfied thanks to (
4.7
)), we obtain that

\displaystyle\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\geqslant C\mu([s_{0},1])\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{\overline{R}})}^{-1}\|D^{s_{0}}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

up to renaming
C>0
.

This and Corollary
3.9
give that

\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\geqslant C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s_{0},p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Accordingly, if we have that
\langle u,u\rangle_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}=0
,
recalling (
3.1
)
we find that
\|u\|_{L^{p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=0
and consequently

This shows
that the bilinear form in (
4.6
)
is positive definite.
∎

.

We stress that, if the measure
\mu

,

Let

Then, the space

.

Moreover, if

Let
s_{0}
denote the minimum of the support of
\mu
.
Let
C
be the constant appearing in Proposition
3.10
and take

R\geqslant\sup_{s\in[s_{0},S_{0}]}\left(\frac{C}{s^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{s}}.

In this way,
R
satisfies the assumption
in (
3.16
) for any
s
in the support of
\mu
, and therefore Proposition
3.10
can be used in this setting.

Moreover, taking
t:=1+\delta
,
p:=2
,
\Omega:=B_{R}
and
h:=\lambda

in Lemma
4.2
(used here with

in place of

\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(B_{R})}\leqslant\|\lambda^{-1}\|^{-1}_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{R})}\|D^{s}u\|_{L^{2}(\lambda,B_{R})}^{2}.

(4.12)

), (

)
and Proposition

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}}\int_{(0,S_{0}]}\lambda(x)|D^{s}u(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{R})}^{-1}\int_{(0,S_{0}]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(B_{R})}\,d\mu(s)

\displaystyle\frac{\|\lambda^{-1}\|_{L^{1+\delta}(B_{R})}^{-1}}{2}\int_{(0,S_{0}]}\|D^{s}u\|^{2}_{L^{p(\delta)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,d\mu(s)

(4.13)

) holds true with

, and

∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

Then, for any

(4.14)

(ii)

compactly embeds into

for any

in the range

\begin{cases}q\in[1,p(\delta)^{*})&\mbox{ if }sp(\delta)<n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty)&\mbox{ if }sp(\delta)=n,\\
q\in[1,+\infty]&\mbox{ if }sp(\delta)>n.\end{cases}

(4.15)

Moreover, if
\mu(\{S_{0}\})>0
, then the above embeddings hold for any

.

Proof.

Proposition
4.5
establishes a continuous embedding of

into

for any

(and also for

if

).

Thus, the thesis follows from
Theorem
3.6
if

(notice indeed that the assumptions in (

) and (

) are guaranteed to hold in cases
(i)
and
(ii)
, respectively) and from the

∎

5
Boundedness in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)

In this section, we introduce the concepts of boundedness and compactness in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
. The compactness result stated in Theorem
5.3
here below provides crucial information needed to develop the Fredholm alternative for
\mathcal{L}
.

Let
f
be a nonnegative measurable function on
\Omega
. Then,
f
is said to be
bounded
in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
, if there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi^{2}(x)\,dx\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}.

(5.1)

Corollary 5.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(i)

f
is bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

(ii)

H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
continuously embeds into
L^{2}(f,\Omega)
.

(iii)

There exist two positive constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
such that, for any
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
,

C_{1}\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g+f,\Omega)}\leqslant\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\leqslant C_{2}\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g+f,\Omega)}.

Proof.

We first show that
(i)
implies
(ii)
(the reverse implication is trivial).
In order to do this, we show that

(
5.1
) is valid for any
u\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

(5.2)

For this, we take
u\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
and, in light of
Definition
1.1
,
we consider
(\phi_{k})\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
that converges to
u
in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
as
k\to+\infty
.
Thus, from (
5.1
) we have that, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi_{k}^{2}(x)\,dx\leqslant C\|\phi_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}.

Now, thanks to Proposition
4.6
, we have that
(\phi_{k})
converges a.e. in
\Omega
and therefore, by Fatou’s Lemma,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x)u^{2}(x)\,dx\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi_{k}^{2}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\leqslant C\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\phi_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}=C\|u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)},

which establishes (
5.2
).

We now show that
(ii)
implies
(iii)
. We notice that, being
f
and
g

nonnegative functions, for any
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
,

\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\leqslant\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g+f,\Omega)},

Also, we deduce from
(ii)
that, for any
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
,

\begin{split}\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g+f,\Omega)}&=\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+\int_{\Omega}f(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}\,dx\\
&\leqslant\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+C\|u\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}^{2}.\end{split}

Combining these observations, we obtain that
(iii)
holds true.

To complete the proof of Corollary
5.1
, it remains to establish
that
(iii)
implies
(ii)
.
For this, we observe that, for any
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi^{2}(x)\,dx\leqslant\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g+f,\Omega)}\leqslant\frac{1}{C_{1}^{2}}\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)},

which entails
(ii)
, as desired.
∎

We now recall the definition of compact boundedness as given in Definition (
1.2
) and we point out that this is a stronger property than boundedness, as the next corollary points out.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
f
be compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
. Then,
f
is bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

Proof.

Taking
\varepsilon:=1
in (
1.10
)
and exploiting Proposition
4.6
, we obtain that, for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)

\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi^{2}(x)\,dx\leqslant\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{1}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}.\quad\qed

We stress that the converse does not hold true.
We refer the reader to Appendix
B
for an example of function
f
that is bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
but not compactly bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

Now we present a compactness result.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
f
be compactly bounded in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
. Then, the embedding of
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
into
L^{2}(f,\Omega)
is compact.

Proof.

We first check that

the inequality in (
1.10
) holds true for every
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.

(5.3)

To this aim, we let
\phi\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
and
(\phi_{k})
be a sequence
of functions in
{\mathcal{D}}(\Omega)
such that
\phi_{k}
converges
to
\phi
in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
as
k\to+\infty
. Thus, for all
k
, we deduce from (
1.10
) that

\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon\|\phi_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

Moreover, by Proposition
4.6
, we know that
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
compactly embeds into
L^{1}(\Omega)
,
and therefore
\phi_{k}
converges
to
\phi
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
as
k\to+\infty
. These considerations and the Fatou’s Lemma give that

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left(\varepsilon\|\phi_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon}\|\phi_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\varepsilon\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},

thus establishing (
5.3
).

Now, let
(u_{k})
be a sequence of functions in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)

that converges weakly to some
u
in
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
.
In light of (
5.3
), we have that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|u_{k}-u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon}\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon\left(\|u_{k}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}+\|u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)}\right)+K_{\varepsilon}\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle M\varepsilon+K_{\varepsilon}\|u_{k}-u\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2},.

for some
M>0
.

This and the compact embedding of
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
into
L^{1}(\Omega)
entail that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\phi_{k}-\phi\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{k\to+\infty}\left(M\varepsilon+K_{\varepsilon}\|\phi_{k}-\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)=M\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, we conclude that

\lim_{k\to+\infty}\|\phi_{k}-\phi\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}=0.

This says that the embedding of
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
into
L^{2}(f,\Omega)
is compact.
Since
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
is a Hilbert space and thus reflexive, we obtain the desired result.
∎

6
The Fredholm alternative and proofs of Theorem

This section presents the proofs of the main results stated in Theorem

and Remark

.

Let us recall here that the function
f
defined in (

H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
. Also, we recall the notation for the quantities

We present the following observation, that provides a justification to the definition of the operator in (

in (

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Suppose that there exists a constant

such that

\begin{split}&\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=\displaystyle\sup_{{s\in(0,1]}\atop{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}}|\mathcal{A}(s,x)|\leqslant C,\qquad\tilde{a}:=\displaystyle\sup_{{x\in\Omega}\atop{i=1,\ldots,n}}|a^{i}(x)|\leqslant C,\\
{\mbox{and }}\quad&\tilde{b}:=\sup_{{x\in\Omega}\atop{i=1,\ldots,n}}|b^{i}(x)|\leqslant C.\end{split}

(6.1)

Also, suppose that
a^{ij}(s,\cdot)\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
for all
s\in(0,1]
, for all
i
,
j=1,\dots,n
.

Moreover let
a^{i}(s,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for all
s\in(0,1]
, for all
i=1,\dots,n
.

In addition, let
a\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
.

Then, for any
u
,
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,

\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}u(x)\phi(x)\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{(0,1]}\Big(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}uD^{s}_{i}\phi+a^{i}(s,x)uD^{s}_{i}\phi+b^{i}(s,x)\phi D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\,d\mu(s)\right)\,dx\\
&\quad\quad+\int_{\Omega}a(x)u(x)\phi(x)\,dx.\end{split}

(6.2)

In particular, for any
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
, the map

\mathcal{D}(\Omega)\ni\phi\mapsto\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}u\phi\,dx.

(6.3)

defines a distribution, namely is linear and continuous.

Proof.

Let us consider
u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
. Then, by Proposition
2.10
, we gather that, for any
i=1,\ldots,n

and
s\in(0,1]

a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}(s,x)u\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega).

As a consequence, exploiting Lemma

\displaystyle\int_{(0,1]}\int_{\Omega}\Big(D^{s}_{i}\left(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}(s,x)u\right)+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\phi(x)\,dx\,d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{(0,1]}\int_{\Omega}\Big(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}uD^{s}_{i}\phi+a^{i}(s,x)uD^{s}_{i}\phi+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\phi\Big)\,dx\,d\mu(s).

Therefore, by (
6.1
) and Lemma
2.8
,

\begin{split}&\int_{(0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|\Big(D^{s}_{i}\left(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}(s,x)u\right)+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\phi(x)\right|\,dx\,d\mu(s)\\
&\quad\leqslant C\Big(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|D\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\tilde{a}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|D\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\tilde{b}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\Big),\end{split}

which is a finite quantity.

Accordingly, we can employ the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem and see that

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi\mathcal{L}u\,dx\\
&=\int_{(0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(-D^{s}_{i}\left(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}u+a^{i}(s,x)u\right)+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\phi(x)\,dx\,d\mu(s)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Omega}a(x)u\phi\,dx\\
&=\int_{(0,1]}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}uD^{s}_{i}\phi+a^{i}(s,x)uD^{s}_{i}\phi+b^{i}(s,x)D^{s}_{i}u\phi\,dx\,d\mu(s)\\
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Omega}a(x)u\phi\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}\Bigg[\int_{(0,1]}\Big(a^{ij}(s,x)D^{s}_{j}uD^{s}_{i}\phi+a^{i}(s,x)uD^{s}_{i}\phi+b^{i}(s,x)\phi D^{s}_{i}u\Big)\,d\mu(s)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+a(x)u\phi\Bigg]\,dx,\end{split}

this proving (
6.2
).

Moreover, the map in (
6.3
) is clearly linear, and its continuity follows from (
6.2
).
∎

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
K_{\mathcal{A}}
be given by (
1.6
). Then, for any
u
,
v\in H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)
,

|(\mathcal{L}u,v)|\leqslant\big(3\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}+1\big)\|u\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}.

Proof.

Exploiting (
1.6
), (
1.7
) and Lemma
D.2

(used here with
\xi_{i}:=D^{s}_{i}v
and
\psi_{i}:=a^{i}
, and also with
\xi_{i}:=D^{s}_{i}u
and
\psi_{i}:=a^{i}

\displaystyle|(\mathcal{L}u,v)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}\Big(|u(x)||f(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+|v(x)||f(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big)\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}f(x)|u(x)v(x)|\,dx

\displaystyle=A+B+C,

where

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad+\int_{\Omega}f|uv|\,dx,

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{(0,1]}|u(x)||f(x)|^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle{\mbox{and }}\qquad C

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{(0,1]}|v||f|^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx.

We first estimate
A
. For this, we observe that, by the Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)^{\frac{1}{2}}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u\,d\mu(s)\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v\,d\mu\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|u\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}

and

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega}f|uv|\,dx&\leqslant\left(\int_{\Omega}f|u|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}f|v|^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\
&\leqslant\|u\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}.\end{split}

As a consequence,

A\leqslant\big(\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}+1\big)\|u\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}\|v\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},f,\Omega)}.

(6.4)

In order to estimate

\Omega

(0,1]
and we have that

\begin{split}B&\leqslant\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\Omega}\left(|u||f|^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\right)\,dx\\
&\leqslant\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}}\left(\int_{\Omega}|u|^{2}|f|\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}v^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}v)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,d\mu(s)\right)^{2}\,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\\

Proposition 6.3

There exists

\begin{split}(\mathcal{L}u,u)&\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\int_{(0,1]}\left(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u+(a^{i}+b^{i})\,u\,D^{s}_{i}u\right)\,d\mu(s)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}a|u|^{2}\,dx\\

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}u,u)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\|u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{(0,1]}(D^{s}u^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}D^{s}u)\,d\mu(s)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad-\big(2K_{\mathcal{A}}\,\mu((0,1])+1\big)\int_{\Omega}|f||u|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}-\big(2K_{\mathcal{A}}\,\mu((0,1])+1\big)\int_{\Omega}|f||u|^{2}\,dx.

Accordingly, the desired result holds true with

∎

Appendix A

Theorem A.1
.

Let
S_{0}\in(0,1]
be the maximum of the support of
\mu
. Let
\delta
be given by (
1.5

If

, assume that

\delta>\frac{n-2S_{0}}{2S_{0}}.

(A.1)

Then, for any

(A.2)

it holds that

\begin{split}&{\mbox{if~$f\in L^{q}(\Omega)$ with~$f^{-1}\in L^{1}(\Omega)$,}}\\

(A.3)

If

(A.4)

(A.5)

Proof.

We prove Theorem

Appendix B
Examples of functions that are bounded but not compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)

In this section we provide examples of functions that
are bounded but not compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
, thus establishing that
the notions of boundedness and compact boundedness on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},g,\Omega)
do not coincide.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
\mu=\delta_{\bar{s}}
for some
\bar{s}\in(0,1)
and

\delta:=\frac{n-2\bar{s}}{2\bar{s}}.

(B.1)

Then, any constant function
f
is bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
but not compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.

Proof.

We point out that, according to the specific choice of
\mu
, the spaces
H^{\bar{s},p(\delta)}_{0}(\Omega)
and
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
coincide.
Moreover, by (
4.5
) and (
B.1
), we have that

p(\delta)=\frac{2+\frac{n-2\bar{s}}{\bar{s}}}{2+\frac{n-2\bar{s}}{2\bar{s}}}=\frac{2n}{n+2\bar{s}}

(B.2)

and thus

p(\delta)_{\bar{s}}^{*}=\frac{np(\delta)}{n-p(\delta)\bar{s}}=2.

Then, by Theorem
3.6
and Proposition
4.5
, we obtain that, for any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}|\phi|^{2}f\,dx\leqslant C\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{s,p(\delta)}_{0}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)},

(B.3)

up to changing
C>0
,
namely, that
f
is bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.

We now show that
f
is not compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.
To prove this, we argue towards a contradiction and we suppose that
f
is compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
.

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
,
\lambda>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we define
\phi_{\lambda,\alpha}(x):=\lambda^{\alpha}\phi(\lambda x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Hence, changing variable
\zeta:=\lambda y
, we see that

\begin{split}D^{\bar{s}}\phi_{\lambda,\alpha}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\left(\phi_{\lambda,\alpha}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)-\phi_{\lambda,\alpha}(y)\right)}{\left|\frac{x}{\lambda}-y\right|^{n+\bar{s}+1}}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}-y\right)\,dy\\
&=\lambda^{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\left(\phi\left(x\right)-\phi(\lambda y)\right)}{\left|\frac{x}{\lambda}-y\right|^{n+\bar{s}+1}}\left(\frac{x}{\lambda}-y\right)\,dy\\
&=\lambda^{\alpha+\bar{s}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\left(\phi\left(x\right)-\phi(\zeta)\right)}{\left|x-\zeta\right|^{n+\bar{s}+1}}\left(x-\zeta\right)\,d\zeta\\
&=\lambda^{\alpha+\bar{s}}D^{\bar{s}}\phi(x).\end{split}

(B.4)

Now, we set

\bar{\alpha}:=\frac{n-\bar{s}p(\delta)}{p(\delta)}=\frac{n}{2}.

(B.5)

From (
B.2
) and (
B.4
) we infer that

\begin{split}&\|\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|^{p(\delta)}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|D^{\bar{s}}\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}(x)|^{p(\delta)}\,dx=\lambda^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\bar{s}}\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\left(\frac{y}{\lambda}\right)\right|^{p(\delta)}\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad=\lambda^{p(\delta)(\bar{\alpha}+\bar{s})-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\bar{s}}\phi(y)\right|^{p(\delta)}\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|D^{\bar{s}}\phi(y)\right|^{p(\delta)}\,dy=\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}^{p(\delta)}.\end{split}

(B.6)

Also, by (
B.5
), we have that

\|\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}=\lambda^{2\bar{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}f(x)|\phi(\lambda x)|^{2}\,dx=\lambda^{2\bar{\alpha}-n}\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}=\|\phi\|^{2}_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}

(B.7)

and that

\begin{split}&\|\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}\left|\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}(x)\right|\,dx=\lambda^{\bar{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\phi(\lambda x)\right|\,dx\\
&\qquad=\lambda^{\bar{\alpha}-n}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.\end{split}

(B.8)

Now, let
\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
(not vanishing identically)
and set

M:=\|\phi\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}\qquad{\mbox{and}}\qquad L:=\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Furthermore, for all
\lambda>0
, we set

\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}:=\frac{\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}}{\|\phi_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}}.

(B.9)

We observe that, thanks to (
B.6
) and (
B.8
),

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}=1,\qquad\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}=M

\displaystyle{\mbox{and}}\qquad\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\lambda^{-\frac{n}{2}}\|\phi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Now, if
f
is compactly bounded on
H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)
, given
\varepsilon_{0}:=1/{2M^{2}}
, there exists
K_{\varepsilon_{0}}>0
such that

\displaystyle 1=\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|_{L^{2}(f,\Omega)}^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon_{0}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{H^{0}(\mathcal{A},\Omega)}+K_{\varepsilon_{0}}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{2M^{2}}M^{2}+K_{\varepsilon_{0}}\|\widetilde{\phi}_{\lambda,\bar{\alpha}}\|^{2}_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=\frac{1}{2}+K_{\varepsilon_{0}}L\lambda^{-n}.

From this, sending
\lambda\to+\infty
, we obtain that
1\leqslant 1/2
, which
gives the desired contradiction.
∎

Appendix C
Some integral computations towards the proof of Proposition
2.15

In this section we prove some integral results that are used in Proposition
2.15
in order to compute the Fourier transform of the fractional gradient.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
s\in(0,1)
and
\Gamma
denote the Euler Gamma function. Then,

\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(t)}{t^{1+s}}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{2\Gamma(1+s)}.

(C.1)

Proof.

For any
t>0
, the change of variable
z:=t\tau
gives that

\Gamma(1+s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}z^{s}e^{-z}\,dz=t^{s+1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\tau^{s}e^{-t\tau}\,d\tau.

Hence, denoting by
B
the Beta function, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin t}{t^{1+s}}\,dt=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\tau^{s}e^{-t\tau}\sin t\,d\tau\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\tau^{s}\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-t\tau}\sin t\,dt\right)\,d\tau

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1+s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\tau^{s}}{\tau^{2}+1}\,d\tau

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2\Gamma(1+s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\zeta^{\frac{s-1}{2}}}{\zeta+1}\,d\zeta=\frac{B(\frac{1+s}{2},\frac{1-s}{2})}{2\Gamma(1+s)},

where we have used the Fubini Tonelli Theorem and applied the change of variable
\tau:=\sqrt{\zeta}
.

Now, recalling the property relating the Beta fuction to the Euler Gamma function

B(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(z_{1})\Gamma(z_{2})}{\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})}

(C.2)

and that
\Gamma(1)=1
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
. Then,

\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\omega_{1}|^{1+s}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}=2\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}.

(C.3)

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
x=(x_{1},x^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
.
For all
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with
|x^{\prime}|\leqslant 1
, we define the function
\psi(x^{\prime})=\sqrt{1-|x^{\prime}|^{2}}
.
Then,

\partial B_{1}^{+}:=\left\{\omega\in\partial B_{1}:\omega_{1}>0\right\}=\left\{(\psi(\omega^{\prime}),\omega^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:|\omega^{\prime}|\leqslant 1\right\}.

In addition, since

D\psi(\omega^{\prime})=-\omega^{\prime}\left(1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},

we can write

d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}=\sqrt{1+|D\psi(\omega^{\prime})|^{2}}d\omega^{\prime}=\sqrt{1+\frac{|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}}{1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}}}d\omega^{\prime}=\left(1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}d\omega^{\prime}.

Accordingly, using polar coordinates and the change of variable
\rho:=\sqrt{\zeta}
,

\begin{split}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\omega_{1}|^{1+s}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}&=2\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\omega_{1}^{1+s}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}\\
&=2\int_{\{|\omega^{\prime}|\leqslant 1\}}\left(1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{1+s}{2}}\left(1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\,d\omega^{\prime}\\
&=2\int_{\{|\omega^{\prime}|\leqslant 1\}}\left(1-|\omega^{\prime}|^{2}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\,d\omega^{\prime}\\
&=2(n-1)\omega_{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\rho^{2}\right)^{\frac{s}{2}}\rho^{n-2}\,d\rho\\
&=(n-1)\omega_{n-1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\zeta)^{\frac{s+2}{2}-1}\zeta^{\frac{n-1}{2}-1}\,d\zeta\\
&=(n-1)\omega_{n-1}B\left(\frac{n-1}{2},\frac{s+2}{2}\right),\end{split}

(C.4)

where
B
is the Beta function.

Now, exploiting the equality

\omega_{n-1}=\displaystyle\frac{\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}+1\right)},

the property of the Gamma function that
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)

and (
C.2
),
we infer from (
C.4
) that

\begin{split}\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\omega_{1}|^{1+s}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}&=(n-1)\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}\\
&=2\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}.\qed\end{split}

Proposition C.3
.

Let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, for all
j\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=2^{-s}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{n+s+1}{2})}.

(C.5)

Proof.

Let
\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n})
. Suppose that
\xi_{j}=0
for some
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. In this case, we claim that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=0.

(C.6)

For this, we perform the change of variable
\tilde{t}:=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{j-1},-t_{j},t_{j+1},\ldots,t_{n})
and we see that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot\tilde{t})\tilde{t}_{j}}{|\tilde{t}|^{n+s+1}}\,d\tilde{t},

(C.7)

which entails (
C.6
).

Suppose now that
\xi_{j}\neq 0
(in which case
|\xi|\neq 0
). In this case, we use the change of variable
\tau:=|\xi|t
to get that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=|\xi|^{s}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\tau)\tau_{j}}{|\tau|^{n+s+1}}\,d\tau.

(C.8)

Now we consider a rotation matrix
\mathcal{R}=[\mathcal{R}_{ik}]
such that
\mathcal{R}e_{j}=\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}
. Notice that
\mathcal{R}_{ij}=\frac{\xi_{i}}{|\xi|}
, for all
i\in\{1,\cdots,n\}
. Hence,
changing variable
\eta:=\mathcal{R}^{-1}\tau
,

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\frac{\xi}{|\xi|}\cdot\tau)\tau_{j}}{|\tau|^{n+s+1}}\,d\tau=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\mathcal{R}e_{j}\cdot\tau)\tau_{j}}{|\tau|^{n+s+1}}\,d\tau\\
&\qquad\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\mathcal{R}e_{j}\cdot\mathcal{R}\eta)\left(\mathcal{R}\eta\cdot e_{j}\right)}{|\eta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\eta\\
&\qquad\quad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\eta_{j})\left(\mathcal{R}\eta\cdot e_{j}\right)}{|\eta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\eta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{R}_{ji}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\eta_{j})\eta_{i}}{|\eta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\eta\\
&\qquad\quad=\mathcal{R}_{jj}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\eta_{j})\eta_{j}}{|\eta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\eta=\frac{\xi_{j}}{|\xi|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\eta_{j})\eta_{j}}{|\eta|^{n+s+1}}\,d\eta.\end{split}

Plugging this information into (
C.8
), we infer that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(t_{j})t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt.

(C.9)

From now on, we consider separately the cases
n=1
and
n>1
.

If
n=1
, we exploit (
C.9
) and Lemma
C.1
to find that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin(t)t}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt=2|\xi|^{s-1}\xi\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(t)}{t^{n+s}}\,dt

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})\Gamma\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(1+s)}=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi\frac{2^{-s}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1-s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2+s}{2}\right)},

where the last equality exploits the Legendre duplication formula for the Gamma function

\Gamma(z)\Gamma\left(z+\frac{1}{2}\right)=2^{1-2z}\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma(2z)

(C.10)

used here above with
z:=(s+1)/2
. This completes the proof of Proposition
C.3
for
n=1
.

Hence, we now focus on the case
n>1
. We set

\partial B_{1}^{+}=\left\{\omega\in\partial B_{1}:\omega_{j}>0\right\}

and, using polar coordinates and the change of variable
r:=\rho/\omega_{j}
, from (
C.9
) we obtain that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt&=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\int_{\partial B_{1}}\omega_{j}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(r\omega_{j})}{r^{s+1}}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}\\
&=2|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\omega_{j}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin(r\omega_{j})}{r^{s+1}}\,dr\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}\\
&=2|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}^{+}}\omega_{j}^{1+s}\,d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin\rho}{\rho^{s+1}}\,d\rho\right)\\
&=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\left(\int_{\partial B_{1}}|\omega_{1}|^{1+s}d\mathcal{H}_{\omega}^{n-1}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\sin\rho}{\rho^{s+1}}\,d\rho\right),\end{split}

where we also exploited the rotation invariance of the integral in the last equality.

From this and Lemmata
C.1
and
C.2
, we obtain that

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin(\xi\cdot t)t_{j}}{|t|^{n+s+1}}\,dt&=|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+2}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{2\Gamma(1+s)}\\
&=\frac{\pi^{\frac{n-1}{2}}}{2}|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\frac{s\Gamma(\frac{1+s}{2})\Gamma\left(\frac{s}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(1+s)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}\\
&=2^{-s}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\frac{s\Gamma(s)}{\Gamma(1+s)}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}\\
&=2^{-s}\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}|\xi|^{s-1}\xi_{j}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{1-s}{2})}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n+s+1}{2}\right)}.\end{split}

where we used that
\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
and the
Legendre duplication formula in (
C.10
) (used here with
z:=s/2
).
This completes the proof of Proposition
C.3
for
n>1
.
∎

Appendix D
Properties of
\mathcal{A}
and technical results about matrices

In this section we prove some algebraic results related to the matrix
\mathcal{A}
, that are mostly used in Section
6
in the study of the operator
\mathcal{L}
.

We introduce the following norm in the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
:

\|\mathcal{A}\|:=\sup_{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\atop{|x|=1}}|\mathcal{A}x|.

We will say that
\mathcal{A}
is bounded if
\|\mathcal{A}\|<+\infty
.

Lemma D.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a positive definite matrix.

If
\mathcal{A}
is symmetric, then (
1.6
) holds true with
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}=1
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is bounded and strictly elliptic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with constant
c>0
, then (
1.6
) holds true with

\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}=\left(\frac{\|\mathcal{A}\|}{c}\right)^{2}.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{A}
positive definite and symmetric, the form

\langle\xi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}:=\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}\psi\qquad\mbox{for any~$\xi$, $\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$}

defines a scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Thus, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that, for any
\xi
,
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\left|\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}\psi\right|^{2}=\left|\langle\xi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\right|^{2}\leqslant\langle\xi,\xi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}=\left(\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}\xi\right)\left(\psi^{T}\mathcal{A}\psi\right),

which is (
1.6
) with
\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{A}}=1
.

If
\mathcal{A}
is bounded and strictly elliptic in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we have that, for any
\xi
,
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\begin{split}&\left|\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}\psi\right|^{2}=(\mathcal{A}\psi\cdot\xi)(\mathcal{A}^{T}\xi\cdot\psi)\leqslant\|\mathcal{A}\|^{2}|\xi|^{2}|\psi|^{2}\\
&\qquad=\frac{\|\mathcal{A}\|^{2}}{c^{2}}\left(c|\xi|^{2}\right)\left(c|\psi|^{2}\right)\leqslant\left(\frac{\|\mathcal{A}\|}{c}\right)^{2}\left(\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}\xi\right)\left(\psi^{T}\mathcal{A}\psi\right),\end{split}

which gives (
1.6
) in this case.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be positive definite matrix satisfying (
1.6
) and let
\mathcal{A}_{S}
be its symmetric part.
Let
\mathcal{B}=[b^{ij}]
be the inverse matrix of
\mathcal{A}
.

Then, for any
\xi
,
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

|\xi\cdot\psi|\leqslant\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\xi^{T}\mathcal{A}_{S}\xi\big)\big(\psi^{T}\mathcal{B}\psi\big)}.

Proof.

For any
\xi
,
\psi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, exploiting (
1.6
), we have that

\displaystyle|\xi\cdot\psi|=|\mathcal{B}\mathcal{A}\xi\cdot\psi|=|\mathcal{A}\xi\cdot\mathcal{B}^{T}\psi|\leqslant\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\mathcal{A}\xi\cdot\xi\big)\big(\mathcal{A}\mathcal{B}^{T}\psi\cdot\mathcal{B}^{T}\psi\big)}

\displaystyle\qquad=\sqrt{K_{\mathcal{A}}\big(\mathcal{A}\xi\cdot\xi\big)\big(\mathcal{B}^{T}\psi\cdot\psi\big)}.

Moreover,

\big(\mathcal{A}\xi\cdot\xi\big)\big(\mathcal{B}^{T}\psi\cdot\psi\big)=\big(\mathcal{A}_{S}\xi\cdot\xi\big)\big(\mathcal{B}\psi\cdot\psi\big)=\big(\mathcal{A}_{S}\xi\cdot\xi\big)\big(\mathcal{B}\psi\cdot\psi\big).

These observations give the desired result.
∎
