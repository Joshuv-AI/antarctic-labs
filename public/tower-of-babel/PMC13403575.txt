Key factors associated with the development of childbirth-related acute and posttraumatic stress symptoms: a prospective cohort study on the role of pain-related constructs
Authors: Chantal Berna, Camille Deforges, Alain Lacroix, Antje Horsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Overwhelming pain during childbirth can contribute to the development of childbirth-related posttraumatic stress disorder (CB-PTSD). However, no single study investigating the path towards CB-PTSD has included multiple pain-related constructs and considered childbirth-related acute stress disorder (CB-ASD) as an outcome.Objective: This study aimed to evaluate the complex interactions and mediating roles of pain-related factors contributing to the development of CB-ASD and CB-PTSD, including pain experienced prior to and during childbirth, pain catastrophizing and childbirth-related pain expectations, while considering the global childbirth experience, prior trauma, anxiety and depression.Methods: This prospective population-based cohort questionnaire study (N = 172) assessed prenatal measures (pain and its functional impact, pain catastrophizing, past trauma exposure, depression and anxiety symptoms, childbirth pain expectation) and childbirth-related measures (pain intensity and catastrophizing, perceived pain-related support, and global childbirth experience). Outcomes of interest were CB-ASD 1-week and CB-PTSD 1-month post-partum. Several structural equation modelling (SEM) models, grounded in a theoretical framework, were evaluated and compared to identify the combinations of variables that best fit the observed data and understand their mutual influences.Results: In the final model, childbirth-related pain expectations and the functional impact of pain during pregnancy were related to childbirth pain intensity. Prenatal pain catastrophizing was associated with childbirth pain catastrophizing, and childbirth pain expectation was associated with perceived pain-related support during childbirth. Childbirth pain catastrophizing played a mediating role between childbirth pain intensity and global childbirth experience, and a partial mediating role between pain support during childbirth and the global childbirth experience. The global childbirth experience was related to CB-ASD symptoms following childbirth, which in turn were associated with CB-PTSD symptoms.Conclusion: Pain catastrophizing appears as a key factor associated with CB-ASD and CB-PTSD symptoms. Pain management and childbirth outcomes might be optimized through preventive interventions targeting at-risk pregnant women.KEYWORDS: Pregnancy, labour pain, catastrophizing, traumatic birth, childbirth experience, acute stress disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder

Introduction

Optimal childbirth pain management is a constant preoccupation for healthcare professionals working in the labour ward (Carvalho & Mhyre, 2016; Jones et al., 2012). The aim is for the parturient to have a positive birth experience overall, and risks for trauma due to important degrees of physical stress and pain are well-considered (Horsch & Garthus-Niegel, 2019). Yet, 9–50% of women perceive their childbirth as traumatic (Horsch et al., 2024) and the birth can fulfil the traumatic stressor criteria (i.e. the mother perceiving a threat of death, or serious injury to herself and/or her baby; American Psychiatric Association, 2013). This potentially traumatic experience can lead to childbirth-related acute stress disorder (CB-ASD) symptoms, i.e. symptoms between three days and one month, or later to childbirth-related posttraumatic stress disorder (CB-PTSD) symptoms (Horesh et al., 2021). CB-ASD and CB-PTSD are both trauma- and stressor-related disorders (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). They share the following symptom clusters: re-experiencing, hyperarousal and reactivity symptoms, avoidance, negative cognitions and mood, but CB-ASD has one additional symptom cluster, namely dissociation (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). One recent prospective study showed that parents with probable CB-ASD are 14 times more likely to develop probable CB-PTSD (Schobinger et al., 2020).

CB-PTSD is a major public health issue, with approximately 4.7% of mothers diagnosed 1–14 months postpartum (Heyne et al., 2022). This has important and potentially long-term negative consequences for the whole family, such as maternal psychological distress, lower couple relationship satisfaction, disrupted mother–child attachment, developmental delay in the child or postponed subsequent pregnancies (Cook et al., 2018; Dekel et al., 2019; Garthus-Niegel et al., 2018; Horsch et al., 2024; Horsch & Garthus-Niegel, 2019). Therefore, prevention is crucial (Dekel et al., 2024; Furuta et al., 2018; Hollander et al., 2017; Horsch et al., 2024) and key risk factors for CB-PTSD have to be identified (Ayers et al., 2016), particularly those on which it is possible to intervene. Apart from CB-ASD, different risk factors for CB-PTSD have been described (Handelzalts et al., 2025), including levels of social support (Duroux et al., 2023; Mayopoulos et al., 2021; Soet et al., 2003), history of traumatic events (Freedman et al., 2020; Lev-Wiesel et al., 2009; MacKinnon et al., 2018; Shlomi Polachek et al., 2016), coping skills and traits (Soet et al., 2003; Stramrood et al., 2011), anxiety or depression (Goutaudier et al., 2019; Shlomi Polachek et al., 2016; Soet et al., 2003; Sommerlad et al., 2021), as well as perinatal distress or unplanned events (Boudou et al., 2007; Lev-Wiesel et al., 2009; Sommerlad et al., 2021). Yet, childbirth-related pain (Boudou et al., 2007; Garthus-Niegel et al., 2014; Kountanis et al., 2022; Lev-Wiesel et al., 2009; Soet et al., 2003; Stramrood et al., 2011), its anticipation (Goutaudier et al., 2019; Shlomi Polachek et al., 2016), or post-partum pain (Froeliger et al., 2024) seems the most frequently identified and preventable associated factors. In fact, more generally, uncontrolled pain during a traumatic event is linked to PTSD (El-Gabalawy et al., 2019), and several studies have explored the complex ties between pain and PTSD (Ravyts et al., 2026; Siqveland et al., 2017) Therefore, in the context of childbirth, there is potential for prevention through appropriate analgesic support (Deforges et al., 2021).

Nevertheless, pain is a complex experience, and predisposing factors need to be considered also in the perinatal period: women can suffer from pain during their pregnancy; they might have specific expectations regarding childbirth pain, and they could have personal predispositions to deal more or less well with an acute painful experience based on their specific patterns of interaction between their mind, brain, body, and social environment (Whitburn, 2013; Whitburn et al., 2017a). Two qualitative studies on childbirth pain are illustrative of how contextual factors, such as the social environment and the woman’s state of mind, as well as their interpretation of their sensory perceptions, shape the pain experience (Whitburn et al., 2014, 2017b). Further work underlines the importance of the loss of control and intense pain in a negative birth experience (Henriksen et al., 2017). A large observational prospective study (N = 1893) suggested that the relationship between labour pain and PTSD was mediated by the overall birth experience (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2014).

Pain catastrophizing, i.e. a negative cognitive and emotional response to actual or upcoming pain, is a well-known predictor of adverse postsurgical pain outcomes (Sobol-Kwapinska et al., 2016) and, more globally, is considered one of the most relevant predictors of outcomes in chronic pain (Edwards et al., 2016). Pain catastrophizing was also shown to be a relevant factor between pain and PTSD following severe injury (Carty et al., 2011) or orthopaedic surgery (McDermott et al., 2024), and was underlined in a recent meta-analysis of these interactions (Ravyts et al., 2026). However, the association of catastrophizing with postnatal outcomes remains underexplored, as demonstrated by a recent review that identified only four studies (Carmo et al., 2024). Women with high catastrophizing scores experienced more late pregnancy pain (Wilson et al., 2023), anticipated and experienced more labour pain, had less control, and presented poorer physical recovery, compared to women with low catastrophizing scores (Flink et al., 2009; Peralta et al., 2024). However, despite higher fear of pain, women scoring high on a catastrophizing scale did not benefit from more frequently require epidural analgesia, possibly due to an associated fear of needles (Van den Bussche et al., 2007; Veringa et al., 2011). Catastrophizing is tied to fear of childbirth, and fear of childbirth and pain are linked to preferences for elective caesarean sections (Peralta et al., 2024; Rondung et al., 2019). Furthermore, catastrophizing has been shown to mediate the relationship between fear of pain and the preference for an elective caesarean section (Dehghani et al., 2014).

To our knowledge, no single study investigated factors contributing to childbirth-related ASD and PTSD symptoms by including multiple pain-related constructs, from the prenatal to the postnatal period, from the pain intensity itself to social (e.g. perceived pain-related support during childbirth) and cognitive components (e.g. pain expectation, pain catastrophizing) of the pain experience. This prospective cohort study therefore aimed to explore complex relationships and mediating roles between variables of a theoretical model (Figure 1), where pain-related factors during pregnancy would be prospectively associated with pain intensity and pain catastrophizing during childbirth, which in turn would interact with CB-ASD and CB-PTSD symptoms, while including well-known relevant variables such as prior trauma exposure, anxiety and depression symptoms as well as global childbirth experience.

Method

Participants

This study is part of a larger prospective population-based cohort (Lausanne Well-being Cohort), with data collected during the third trimester of pregnancy up to 6 months postpartum between 2013 and 2021, that has already allowed a number of publications (Duroux et al., 2023; Gilbert et al., 2024; Schobinger et al., 2020; Stuijfzand et al., 2020). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Canton of Vaud (480/2012) with an amendment in September 2016 to include this sub-study on pain (PB_2016-02219). Women in their third trimester of pregnancy were recruited during their antenatal visit at a Swiss university hospital, using a convenience sampling method. Eligibility criteria included being pregnant, ≥ 18 years, in the third trimester of pregnancy, and speaking French fluently. For the current sub-study, participants had to have filled in the pain-related questionnaires (included from 2016 onwards). Pregnant women were excluded if they had an insufficient knowledge of French to complete the questionnaires or if they presented with an acute psychosis or a suicidal risk. Mothers who had a stillbirth or whose infants had serious complications, or who died shortly after birth, were subsequently excluded from the study for ethical reasons.

Participant selection

Between 2013 and 2021, 567 participants were included in the study, with 412 recruited after September 2016. Among these, 97 were excluded as they did not complete the Brief Pain Inventory (BPI) questionnaire during the third trimester of pregnancy. Of the remaining 315 participants, 143 were further excluded due to incomplete responses across the questionnaires of interest. The missing data exhibited an all-or-nothing pattern, suggesting it was not missing at random, which raised concerns about the applicability of multiple imputation techniques. As a result, 172 participants with complete responses to all questionnaire items (i.e. complete cases) were retained. Following this, six outliers were identified and removed based on Mahalanobis distances calculated from the questionnaire scores. Consequently, the final analytical sample comprised 166 participants.

Study procedure

After providing informed written consent, women completed data during the third trimester of pregnancy, at one week postpartum, and at one month postpartum (see Figure 1). Self-report questionnaires were completed either on paper or online using SurveyMonkey, depending on participants’ preferences. Obstetric and neonatal data were extracted from the hospital electronic records shortly after birth.

Measures

Data was collected at three time points: before birth during the third trimester of pregnancy, 1 week after childbirth, and 1 month after childbirth (see Figure 1).

Measures collected during the 3rd trimester

Current pain (brief pain inventory, BPI)

The validated BPI was completed by participants to assess the presence of pain (Tan et al., 2004) prior to the birth. This 14-item self-report questionnaire assesses the presence of pain beyond ‘usual non-daily aches’ over the last 8 days (‘yes’/‘no’). If present, the intensities of pain right now, as well as the most intense, least intense and average over past 24 h, each ranging from 0 = ‘no pain’, to 10 = ‘worst pain imaginable’, are collected. Furthermore, the functional impact of pain over the last 24 h (mean of 8 items: daily activity, mood, walking, work, social interactions, sleep, life enjoyment, and sexuality; ranging from 0 = ‘no impact’, to 10 = ‘large impact’). The item ‘sexuality’ was added to the standard scale, given the population and the type of pain that could be of interest for the present study.

The first item of the BPI determined whether a participant had daily pain beyond usual aches and led to a branching logic for the other items of this scale. Therefore, we assigned a zero value to each of the BPI remaining items for participants having answered ‘no’ to this first item. This coding was necessary to have the benefit of the whole population for our regression models. The Cronbach’s alpha of the BPI intensity of pain (α = 0.82) and the BPI impact of pain (α = 0.88) were considered good.

Anxiety and depression symptoms (hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS))

The validated 14-item HADS was completed by participants during the third trimester of pregnancy. It consists of two subscales: 7 items assess anxiety and 7 items assess depression symptoms (Langevin et al., 2011; Zigmond & Snaith, 1983). The items of the two subscales were summed up to create separate anxiety and depression scores. A higher score indicated greater severity of anxiety or depression symptoms, i.e. normal: 0–7 points; mild: 8–10 points; moderate: 11–15 points and severe:16–21 points. The Cronbach’s alpha of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (α = 0.78), HADS Anxiety (α = 0.70) and HADS Depression (α = 0.69) were considered acceptable.

Past trauma exposure

A binary question (In the past, have you experienced a traumatic event? yes/no) from the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (Foa, 1995) was used to assess past traumatic exposure.

Childbirth pain expectation

A single item created for this study assessed participants’ expectations of the pain they thought they would experience during the upcoming childbirth. Participants responded using a Likert scale from 0 = ‘no pain’ to 10 = ‘worst pain imaginable’, with a higher score indicating a higher pain expectation.

Pain catastrophizing (pain catastrophizing scale (PCS))

This validated scale (Sullivan et al., 1995), frequently used to assess negative thoughts and emotions about pain including rumination, magnification, and helplessness, was completed during the third trimester of pregnancy. The 13 items are rated on a Likert scale from 0 = ‘not at all’ to 4 = ‘all the time’, with a higher score indicating higher pain catastrophizing. The PCS has demonstrated good internal consistency, predictive value, and internal consistency (Sullivan et al., 2001). The Cronbach’s alpha of the Pain Catastrophizing Scale (α = 0.94) was considered excellent.

Measures collected one week after childbirth

Global childbirth experience

A single item created for this study assessed the global childbirth experience, to which participants responded one week after childbirth on a Likert scale ranging from 0 = ‘extremely bad’, to 10 = ‘extremely good’, with a higher score indicating a more positive childbirth experience.

Pain intensity during childbirth

Participants were asked about the pain experienced during childbirth using two items from the BPI (average and most intense pain during time of childbirth), which they rated on a Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) from 0 = ‘no pain’, to 10 = ‘worst pain imaginable’, with a higher score indicating higher pain. The mean of the two items was then used in the analyses.

Pain support during childbirth

Participants were also asked to rate the amount of support they had received on two separate visual analogue scales ranging from 0 = ‘no support’, to 10 = ‘extreme support’, by professional staff and birth partners (‘To what extent did you feel supported in your experience of pain by your healthcare team/partner?’). The correlation between the two items of the Perceived childbirth pain support (c = 0.76) was considered strong. The perceived childbirth pain support was calculated as the sum of the two items, with a higher score indicating higher perceived support.

Childbirth pain catastrophizing

The 6 items of the ‘Situational Catastrophizing Scale’, which was developed to be used with acute painful stimuli (Edwards et al., 2006), were completed related to childbirth pain. This scale assesses the catastrophizing during acute pain, with thoughts participants could have had in an acute pain setting such as ‘I felt that I couldn’t stand it’ or ‘I kept wishing that it would be over’, rated on a categorical scale from 0 = ‘not at all’, to 4 = ‘all the time’. The 6 items were added to give the total score, with a higher score indicating more catastrophizing. The Cronbach’s alpha of the childbirth catastrophizing scale (α = 0.93) was considered excellent.

Childbirth-related acute stress symptoms (acute stress disorder scale)

Participants completed the 14-item validated scale one week after childbirth (Bryant & Harvey, 2000; Bryant et al., 2000). Participants rated childbirth-related acute stress disorder symptoms on a Likert scale from 1 = ‘not at all’, to 5 = ‘a lot’. Their acute stress was calculated as the sum of all items, with a higher score indicating higher severity. The Cronbach’s alpha of the Acute Stress Disorder Scale (α = 0.86) was considered good.

Measures collected one month after childbirth

Childbirth-related posttraumatic stress symptoms (posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS))

Participants completed the validated 17-item Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (PDS) (Foa, 1995; Hearn et al., 2012), with items ranging from 0 = ‘not at all or once’, to 3 = ‘5 times a week and more or always’. Participants were instructed to complete the PDS in relation to childbirth. Posttraumatic stress was calculated as the sum of all items, with a higher score indicating higher severity. The Cronbach’s alpha of the Posttraumatic Diagnostic Scale (α = 0.89) was considered good.

Statistical analysis

Theoretical model

We hypothesized that the pain intensity during childbirth would be positively associated with acute stress (CB-ASD) and subsequent PTSD symptoms (CB-PTSD). Our aim was not only to identify correlates of CB-PTSD but to examine how these variables influence one another across time. For this reason, we used a theory-driven structural equation modelling (SEM) framework, which allowed simultaneous estimation of direct and indirect pathways and provided a more integrated understanding of the processes leading to CB-ASD and CB-PTSD. In the structural model based on our reading of the literature (Figure 2), we specified pregnancy-period variables (current pain intensity and impact, past trauma exposure, pain catastrophizing (PCS), and pain expectation, depression and anxiety) as antecedent constructs that could influence childbirth-related assessments and experiences (childbirth pain, childbirth support, childbirth pain catastrophizing, and global childbirth experience). These childbirth variables were then modelled as associated with acute stress one week postpartum, which in turn was specified as associated with PTSD symptoms at one month.

Theoretical model linking the different pain-related constructs of interest to childbirth-related acute stress (ASD) and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms.

Even though the childbirth variables were measured at one week postpartum, they referred to childbirth. Consequently, we postulated relationships between these variables and the CB-ASD measured at one week postpartum. To further improve the model, we postulated that the global childbirth experience variable also reflected the pain, support, and catastrophizing experiences of childbirth. However, no causal relationship was postulated. The HADS anxiety and depression subscales measures during the third trimester of pregnancy were also added as potential confounders to the model. To simplify the models and to maximize the tests’ statistical power, all variables of interest and potential confounders included were modelled as observed (rather than latent) variables based on sums or means of the item scores of the questionnaires.

Analysis

We first conducted Pearson and/or Spearman's rank correlations to measure the strength of the associations between the variables in scope. The associations were considered as negligible when the correlation coefficients were below 0.10, weak when the correlation coefficients were < 0.40, moderate when < 0.70, and strong when the correlation coefficients were > 0.70 (Schober et al., 2018).

To estimate the magnitude and significance of hypothesized relationships between pain, acute stress, posttraumatic stress, and other variables, we conducted analyses using Structural Equation Modelling (SEM). We began by specifying an initial theoretical model that included all hypothesized paths. Subsequent models were iteratively refined by adding or removing paths, when it was theoretically justified, based on techniques and indices such as L1 regularization, modification indices, and partial coefficient of determination helping to identify potential improvements to the model fit (see Supplementary Material). Each model was evaluated and compared using goodness-of-fit indices, including the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). To ensure robustness and generalizability, we used bootstrapping to obtain robust standard errors and confidence intervals for the path coefficients and fit indices.

RMSEA values below .06, TLI and CFI values above .95, and SRMR values below .09 indicate a good fit (Hu & Bentler, 1999; Schumacker & Lomax, 2010). We also used the χ2 statistic adjusted by its degrees of freedom instead, χ2/df values below 3.0 indicating a reasonable fit (Kline, 2015). To account for the non-normally distributed variables, we used a Maximum Likelihood Estimation with Robust Standard Errors and a Satorra-Bentler Scaled Test Statistic (MLR) estimator.

We introduced confounders in regression models when they were correlated with both the independent and dependent variables. To increase precision in the regression model coefficients estimates, we did not control for covariates that were not correlated with the dependent variable and highly correlated with the independent variables.

A statistician (AL) conducted these analyses with R v4.0.2 running under RStudio v1.1.463, with the lavaan v0.6-9 and mediation v4.5.0 packages to analyze the SEM. Standardized scores, two-tailed significance, and α < .05 were used for all statistical tests.

Results

Population

The descriptive statistics of the sample are shown in Table 1. Participants were mostly married or in a relationship; the mean age was 33.3 (SD = 4.2) years. Half of the participants were primiparous (51%) and/or had not experienced a past trauma (54%). During the third trimester, 51% of participants reported experiencing current pain, with low average intensity (M = 2.39/10, SD = 1.40) and low-to-moderate average impact on their life (M = 3.28/10, SD = 1.78). Furthermore, 35% of participants reported clinical levels of anxiety and 8% of depression symptoms, although the mean anxiety and depression scores were below the clinical cut-off. During pregnancy, women expected severe pain during childbirth (M = 7.9/10; SD = 2.04), but had an average low pain catastrophizing score (M = 12.07, SD = 10.05). During childbirth, pain intensity was rated as high (M = 7.63/10, SD = 2.45), with low situational catastrophizing (M = 1.96, SD = 1.16) and a high level of perceived support (M = 8.48/10, SD = 1.62). The global childbirth experience was rather good (M = 6.5/10, SD = 2.61).

Bivariate correlations

The correlations results (Table 2) showed a strong positive association between the intensity of pain reported in the third trimester of pregnancy and its functional impact (rho = 0.93, p < .001). Childbirth pain catastrophizing was moderately and positively associated with pain intensity during childbirth (rho = 0.62, p < .001), as well as moderately and negatively associated with the global childbirth experience (rho = −0.52, p < .001). Finally, CB-ASD symptoms were moderately and negatively associated with the global childbirth experience (rho = −0.37, p < .001) but strongly and positively associated with CB-PTSD symptoms (rho = 0.66, p < .001). No significant associations were found between past trauma exposure and any of the variables in scope.

Structural equation modelling

Baseline structural model

The baseline model (Figure 3) was composed of all paths postulated in the proposed theoretical structural model (Figure 2). The baseline model indices showed a poor fit to the data (Figure 3, and details in Supplementary materials). Except for the SRMS = 0.083 [0.083, 0.084], none of the other indices (CFI = 0.698 [0.695, 0.702], TLI = 0.182 [0.172, 0.191], RMSEA = 0.186 [0.185, 0.188], and χ2/df = 6.86 [6.79, 6.93]) met the required thresholds.

Baseline structural model: The different relationships are indicated by the arrows, with their related standardized beta coefficients. Only the significant paths are shown. R2 is the total variance of the related variable explained by the model. Significance: * p < .05; ** p < .01; *** p < .001.

Retained structural model

This baseline model was successively refined using the approach mentioned in the methods. Nine competing structural equation models (SEMs) evaluated the hypothesized relationships among the variables. These models varied in their specification of direct and indirect pathways, as well as the inclusion of additional covariates. Moreover, variables not contributing to any relationships were removed (current pain intensity during pregnancy and the past trauma exposure).

Among these models, the retained model presented in Figure 4 demonstrated the best overall fit indices to the data (see Supplementary Materials for excluded models due to statistical inferiority or lack of additional theoretical insights and further details on the retained model). The significant relationships and the related standardized coefficients are presented in Table 4. Compared to the baseline model (Table 3), the AIC was slightly reduced (AICbaseline = 2593 [2589, 2597], AICmodel6 = 2492 [2487, 2497]), and the fit indices were improved (CFI = 0.915 [0.912, 0.918], TLI = 0.853 [0.847, 0.858], RMSEA = 0.092 [0.091, 0.094], SRMS = 0.074 [0.073, 0.075], χ2/df = 2.469 [2.419, 2.519]), indicating a moderate to acceptable fit to the data.

Discussion

This prospective cohort study of pregnant women aimed to evaluate the complex interactions and mediating roles of pain-related factors in the development of acute (CB-ASD) and chronic childbirth-related stress (CB-PTSD) symptoms, while considering well-known relevant variables, such as prior trauma exposure, anxiety and depression symptoms.

A structural equation model allowed for the exploration of these interactions, demonstrating that childbirth pain expectation as well as the functional impact of pain during pregnancy were associated with pain intensity during childbirth. Pain catastrophizing was associated with childbirth catastrophizing, and childbirth pain expectation was negatively associated with perceived pain support during childbirth. During childbirth, pain intensity, pain catastrophizing and pain-related support interacted significantly and had a significant relation with the global childbirth experience. Specifically, childbirth pain catastrophizing played a mediating role in the relations between childbirth pain intensity and global childbirth experience, and a partial mediating role between pain support during childbirth and global childbirth experience. The global childbirth experience was correlated with CB-ASD symptoms, which in turn was associated with CB-PTSD symptoms.

Correlates of childbirth pain

Whatever the pain intensity during pregnancy, the more important its functional impact was, the higher the self-reported childbirth pain intensity was. Chronic pain during pregnancy is not yet well investigated, with a review of the literature unable to provide a global prevalence, beyond specific disorders such as pelvic and low back pain that can occur in up to 71% of pregnant women (Ray-Griffith et al., 2018). Yet, numbers emerged from a large American retrospective observational study comparing chronic pain reports in pregnant women (20%) and age-matched non-pregnant controls (36%) (Dalton et al., 2023). A few studies do report specific prevalence of pain during pregnancy, such as a Canadian sample (55%) (Munro et al., 2017), which is similar to the finding in our sample (51%). Pain during pregnancy should be better assessed and addressed to prevent functional impact that could contribute to a negative birth experience.

Childbirth-related pain expectation during pregnancy was also related to more intense pain, confirming findings from two prior studies in the perinatal context (Goutaudier et al., 2019; Shlomi Polachek et al., 2016), and many more in the general pain literature (Fields, 2018; Markfelder & Pauli, 2020). In a prior study, expectations regarding pain during childbirth were also tied to requests for more analgesia (Veringa et al., 2011). In our study, higher childbirth-related pain expectations were associated with receiving less pain-related support during childbirth. Obviously, treating pain is a multimodal endeavour during childbirth, and pharmacological methods are often essential. However, intervening on expectations has become part of non-pharmacological pre-operative interventions, as shown with positive outcomes in cardiac surgery (Rief et al., 2017). Such interventions still need to be applied and investigated in the perinatal context to verify if they could prevent consequences of negative expectations, such as intense childbirth pain, requests for large amounts of analgesia, and a perception of receiving insufficient support.

Factors correlating with CB-ASD and CB-PTSD symptoms

The global childbirth experience is a well-known predictor of CB-ASD/PTSD (Garthus-Niegel et al., 2014; Horsch et al., 2024; Schobinger et al., 2020), and it showed key interactions with CB-ASD in our study. Interestingly, prior studies that reported childbirth pain intensity as a predictor of global childbirth experience and of CB-ASD did not consider catastrophizing, and focused on pain intensity (Boudou et al., 2007; Garthus-Niegel et al., 2014; Kountanis et al., 2022; Lev-Wiesel et al., 2009; Soet et al., 2003; Stramrood et al., 2011). Our results suggest that possibly, the intensity of pain is a less important and direct factor in the global childbirth experience than how much worry and negative thoughts focus on it. In fact, in some studies, the levels of pain during childbirth were not correlated with PTSD (Kountanis et al., 2022), and childbirth pain does not necessarily represent a negative experience leading to trauma. The complexity of the pain experience is such that for some childbearing women, even severe pain may be seen positively, without any traumatizing elements, depending on their psychological makeup, interpersonal context, and social connections (Whitburn, 2013).

Pain catastrophizing during childbirth

Pain catastrophizing is a key psychological predictor of adverse pain experiences (Rogers & Farris, 2022), especially relevant to the post-surgical context (Sobol-Kwapinska et al., 2016). Pain catastrophizing is becoming of interest in the perinatal field (Carmo et al., 2024; Flink et al., 2009; Peralta et al., 2024; Roman et al., 2021; Rondung et al., 2019). Here, we were able to show correlations between pain catastrophizing (i.e. measured by the PCS) and situational pain catastrophizing during childbirth.

Of note, catastrophizing did not correlate with childbirth pain intensity, contrary to prior findings (Flink et al., 2009; Peralta et al., 2024), but in line with a smaller study (Carvalho et al., 2014). These discrepant findings could be partially due to our study sample, in which the average catastrophizing score was low. In fact, a prior study in a Swedish sample had 46% of participants scoring high on the PCS (median split at 20 points) (Flink et al., 2009) while another one with a British sample had 11.5% scoring high on the PCS (cut-off of 17 points) (Peralta et al., 2024). Of note, both these cut-offs for high PCS scores are relatively low compared to the scale’s clinical cut-off at 30 points. There might also be a cultural element to the PCS, given higher levels of catastrophizing in a Nepalese sample of first time pregnant women, where 55.9% had a PCS > 20, and 17.1% a score > 30 (Clark et al., 2024).

However, we collected the additional measure of situational catastrophizing (Edwards et al., 2006) during childbirth, which mediated the relationship between childbirth pain intensity and global childbirth experience with ensuing CB-ASD/PTSD symptoms. Childbirth pain catastrophizing is, therefore, of interest in this setting. The situational pain catastrophizing scale has shown its interest for other acute pain settings (Campbell et al., 2010; Grosen et al., 2016), and might deserve further use as a predictor for long-term outcomes of acute, painful experiences in people without chronic pain.

Our findings highlight the central role of pain catastrophizing within the network of pain-related factors surrounding childbirth, consistent with its importance in other trauma-related contexts (Rosenbloom et al., 2016). Pain catastrophizing is interestingly a modifiable factor. Catastrophizing management interventions can rely on pain science education (Moseley et al., 2024) or exposure therapy (Terradas-Monllor et al., 2023), cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or mind–body techniques. Based on a meta-analysis, CBT, multimodal treatment (CBT and exercise) as well as acceptance and commitment therapy have shown the best level of evidence (Schütze et al., 2018). Perioperative interventions on catastrophizing exist mostly in the orthopaedic context, with small to moderate effects (e.g. Patel et al., 2022; Riddle et al., 2019). To our knowledge, no application to the obstetric context has yet been made. Nevertheless, antenatal education classes (Avignon et al., 2022) could be an opportunity to target specifically negative expectations or catastrophizing, in addition to more global pain management and coping strategy development (Escott et al., 2009) or mindfulness interventions (Veringa et al., 2016).

Limitations

Some limitations to this work need to be addressed. Past trauma exposure was measured with a binary question, without collecting details about the nature of the events. This limits the depth of these results. Nevertheless, past exposure to trauma was non-relevant in the model. When measuring pain in the third trimester, we did not clarify since when it was present, nor whether it was related to pregnancy. Therefore, we cannot distinguish between women with chronic pain and those who experienced pain linked to their pregnancy (e.g. low back pain), or acute pain of another cause. While establishing current and chronic pain prevalence during pregnancy was not the aim of this study, it deserves further research. The self-report measures regarding the birth were collected one week later, which may introduce potential retrospective report biases. Yet, new mothers are difficult to reach earlier to fill in questionnaires, and this allowed for a unique data collection point with the CB-ASD measure. At the statistical level, although our approach was theory-driven, testing multiple competing SEM models introduces complexity and a potential risk of overfitting (MacCallum et al., 1992). Furthermore, mediation effects identified in these models should be interpreted cautiously, as they depend on temporal assumptions and model specification. While our study employed a longitudinal design, some mediations involved variables measured at the same timepoint (retrospective collection of variables related to childbirth with current CB-ASD symptoms) which limits the ability to establish temporal precedence and causal pathways (Cole & Maxwell, 2003; Maxwell & Cole, 2007). Finally, mediation findings should be interpreted as consistent with, but not as proof of, a mediating process. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs should involve measures of catastrophizing to allow in-depth analyses of these interactions.

Conclusion

In summary, this article brings forward pain-related factors that prospectively were linked to acute and chronic post-partum stress disorder symptoms, namely: an experience of pain that is impacting function during pregnancy, negative expectations regarding childbirth pain, a prenatal tendency for pain catastrophising as well as situational catastrophizing during childbirth. These factors could be targeted by preventive measures during pregnancy and childbirth. Such preventive efforts would be excellent opportunities for interdisciplinary interventions. Hopefully, these could promote a positive childbirth experience, which in turn could have empowering effects, with increased self-efficacy and a potential impact for future encounters with pain. Future research into these complex interactions could add measures of pain coping and self-efficacy to increase our understanding of these pain-related factors, especially if an intervention was to be planned.
