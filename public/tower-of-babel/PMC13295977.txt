Emerging treatments for Sjögren’s disease: promising drugs on the horizon
Authors: Carlos Marques-Gomes, Simon J Bowman, Saba Nayar, Benjamin A Fisher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSjögren’s disease (SjD) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disorder characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, leading to dryness, fatigue and variable extraglandular involvement. Despite its significant clinical and socioeconomic burden, no disease-modifying therapies have yet been approved. Current management remains largely symptomatic, reflecting the complexity and heterogeneity of the disease and the limitations of past clinical trials. Recent advances in the understanding of SjD immunopathogenesis have clarified key mechanisms including epithelial activation, interferon-driven inflammation, B-cell hyperactivity and T-cell dysregulation. These insights have fuelled the development of targeted therapies designed to modulate specific immune pathways, aiming to interrupt the self-perpetuating cycle of epithelial injury and immune activation. Emerging strategies include the modulation of B-cell survival and signalling, inhibition of IgG recycling and intracellular kinase pathways and blockade of co-stimulatory interactions between immune cells. Although recent phase II and phase III studies have shown encouraging signs of efficacy, they have largely focused on patients with moderate-to-severe systemic disease. Addressing the unmet therapeutic need in individuals with predominantly glandular or low systemic activity remains a major challenge. Continued refinement of outcome measures and patient stratification will be essential to translate mechanistic advances into effective, personalised treatments capable of altering the natural course of SjD.

Introduction

Sjögren’s disease (SjD), formerly known as Sjögren’s syndrome (SjS),1 is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune condition, which affects approximately 10–100 people per 1 000 000 per year, with a prevalence reported in some population-based studies between 0.01% and 0.05%.2 There is a female-to-male predominance of 14:1 and a peak incidence at approximately 50 years of age.2 3 It is characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, leading to sicca symptoms, such as dry eyes and dry mouth, along with other hallmark features of severe fatigue and arthralgia. Beyond glandular involvement, SjD may present with a broad spectrum of extraglandular manifestations observed in up to 40% of patients, including peripheral neuropathy, pulmonary disease, interstitial nephritis, vasculitis and increased risk of B-cell lymphoma.3 4 These manifestations significantly contribute to disease burden and impair health-related quality of life, primarily due to disabling fatigue and joint pain, with associated loss of work productivity.5 6 Moreover, patients with SjD have a higher mortality risk compared with the general population.2

Despite decades of research into the pathogenesis of SjD, no disease-modifying therapies (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs)) have yet been licensed specifically for this condition. Current management remains predominantly symptomatic, with immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory drugs prescribed off-label according to clinical presentation and disease severity, on a case-by-case basis.7 This therapeutic gap reflects the disease’s complexity and heterogeneity and the shortcomings of previous clinical trials, many of which failed to achieve their primary end points. Patient stratification according to baseline characteristics remains a major challenge, as heterogeneity results in a wide range of systemic and symptomatic trajectories, potentially driven by distinct underlying pathogenic profiles.8 9 Recent advances in immunology and molecular mechanisms have led to a deeper understanding of critical pathways in SjD, particularly those involving B cells, T cells, cytokine signalling and co-stimulatory interactions. Furthermore, there has been growing recognition of the disease’s impact and socioeconomic burden.10 The development of validated outcome measures, notably the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) Sjögren’s Syndrome Disease Activity Index (ESSDAI),11 has enabled more robust and comparable clinical trial designs. These developments have collectively fuelled a new wave of commercially sponsored trials of targeted therapies, marking a turning point in the therapeutic landscape of SjD.

In this narrative review, we summarise ongoing and completed phase II/III studies of candidate DMARDs in primary SjD, situating each approach within its mechanistic rationale, reported outcomes and developmental stage, to provide an integrated perspective on this rapidly evolving field.

Despite increasing numbers of targeted therapies entering clinical development, interpretation of efficacy in SjD remains challenging due to marked disease heterogeneity, frequent reliance on small sample sizes and substantial placebo responses.

Methodology

This narrative review was informed by a structured literature search conducted using PubMed and ClinicalTrials.gov. Searches were performed without restrictions on publication date to capture both foundational and emerging evidence relevant to the evolving therapeutic landscape of SjD. The literature search was conducted primarily between July and September 2025, with additional relevant publications identified and incorporated on an ongoing basis during manuscript preparation up to the time of submission. The review focused predominantly on phase II and phase III clinical trials evaluating targeted therapeutic strategies in SjD. Search terms included combinations of keywords related to SjD and emerging therapies, such as “Sjögren’s disease”, “Sjögren’s syndrome”, “B-cell-targeted therapy”, “BAFF”, “APRIL”, “interferon”, “plasmacytoid dendritic cells”, “JAK inhibitors”, “BTK inhibitors”, “CAR-T cells”, “T-cell engagers” as well as trial-related terms including “clinical trials”, “phase 2”, “phase 3”, “randomized controlled trial” and “randomized study”. ClinicalTrials.gov was searched to identify ongoing and completed interventional studies. In addition, targeted searches using terms related to disease pathophysiology and patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were performed to inform the respective sections of the manuscript. Supplementary searches using Google were undertaken to identify relevant conference abstracts, early reports and recently published articles not yet indexed in bibliographic databases. The review incorporated peer-reviewed publications, clinical trials, few case reports and selected conference abstracts where mechanistic or translational relevance was evident. Study selection was guided by clinical relevance, biological plausibility, stage of drug development and contribution to understanding therapeutic mechanisms, rather than by formal quantitative synthesis.

Pathogenesis of Sjögren’s disease

SjD is a complex condition with multifactorial pathogenesis involving genetic, epigenetic, environmental, inflammatory and immunological components.12 The pathogenesis of SjD has been extensively reviewed elsewhere, but in brief, it is generally assumed that disease onset is initiated by aberrant activation of salivary gland epithelial cells (SGECs), which triggers both innate and adaptive immune responses. Genetic susceptibility involving human leucocyte antigen (HLA) and non-HLA loci, together with epigenetic alterations, predisposes to this abnormal epithelial and immune activation. Viral triggers such as Epstein-Barr virus or cytomegalovirus are thought to induce type I interferon (IFN-I) signalling in SGECs, leading to antiviral and pro-apoptotic programmes, exposure of autoantigens and recruitment of immune cells. These processes drive the formation of periductal lymphocytic infiltrates and establish a self-perpetuating loop of epithelial injury and chronic inflammation that sustains both glandular dysfunction and systemic disease.3 4 13 14

Innate immune dysregulation is a central feature of SjD, particularly in its early stages. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells (pDCs), the major professional producers of IFN-I, are hyperactivated and, together with other innate and epithelial cells capable of IFN-I secretion, respond to nucleic acids and DAMPs released by apoptotic epithelial cells via toll-like receptors (TLRs). This leads to increased IFN-I production and initiation of a pro-inflammatory cascade. The resulting IFN signature, defined by increased expression of IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), is present in a large subset of patients with SjD and reflects sustained activation of IFN pathways.4 12 13 Although IFNs are primarily innate cytokines, they exert major effects on adaptive immunity. In SjD, IFN-α (type I) is particularly associated with anti-SSA (Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A) antibody positivity, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II haplotypes and more severe systemic manifestations, suggesting it may act as a primary driver of disease. IFN-γ (type II) is more often linked to the risk of lymphoma.15 IFNs activate the janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signalling pathway: type I and III IFNs signal primarily through JAK1 and tyrosine kinase 2 (TYK2), while type II IFNs signal primarily through JAK1 and JAK2, leading to phosphorylation of STAT proteins and transcription of ISGs.16 These ISGs include those encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin (IL)-6, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α), chemokines (eg, C-X-C motif chemokine ligand (CXCL)10), MHC molecules and B-cell activating factor (BAFF), a TNF family cytokine essential for B-cell survival, proliferation and differentiation, thus highlighting the link between pDCs, IFN signalling and Bcell hyperactivity.17

Adaptive immunity plays a critical role in maintaining chronic inflammation in SjD, primarily through dysregulated B-cell and T-cell responses. On binding to B-cell activating factor receptor (BAFF-R), the main BAFF-R on B cells also known as TNFRSF13C or BR3, BAFF activates both the canonical and alternative nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathways, promoting B-cell survival and antibody production.4 Consequently, the NF-κB pathway may represent a key therapeutic target for modulating immune activation in SjD. The other two BAFF receptors, transmembrane activator and calcium modulator and cyclophilin ligand interactor (TACI) and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA), can also bind a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL), another TNF family cytokine that supports plasma cell survival and helps maintain chronic autoantibody production.18 The pro-inflammatory environment, together with continuous B-cell hyperactivation, promotes the formation of ectopic germinal centres (GC-like structures) within the salivary gland parenchyma.12 These structures support B-cell maturation, isotype switching, somatic hypermutation and clonal selection, mimicking secondary lymphoid organs. Activated B cells infiltrate the epithelium, forming lymphoepithelial lesions, a histological hallmark of SjD.4 12 13 This interplay sustains a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammation, lymphocytic infiltration, hypergammaglobulinaemia and potential malignant transformation.

CD4+ T cells contribute by differentiating into T helper (Th)1, Th17 and T follicular helper (Tfh) subsets under cytokine stimulation.4 Tfh cells promote B-cell maturation via IL-21 and CXCL13, fostering migration into GC-like structures.19 Meanwhile, cytotoxic CD8+ T cells also accumulate around epithelial cells, contributing to glandular injury.12

Crucially, co-stimulatory signals mediate interactions between innate and adaptive immunity: antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and B cells express CD80/86 and CD40, which interact with CD28 and CD40L on T cells, promoting full T-cell activation and humoral responses.13 20

Outcome measures in clinical trials

Over the past two decades, a EULAR taskforce has developed and validated specific tools to evaluate both systemic activity and patient-reported symptoms in SjD, aiming to standardise assessment in clinical trials.11 21 22 Two of the most widely used indices are the ESSDAI (see also above) and the EULAR Sjögren’s Syndrome Patient Reported Index (ESSPRI). The ESSDAI measures systemic disease activity across 12 organ-specific domains based on the physician’s judgement.11 The ClinESSDAI excludes the biological domain, making it a more suitable outcome measure for drugs that have a direct pharmacodynamic effect on IgG.21 By contrast, the ESSPRI captures patients’ subjective experience of dryness, fatigue and pain using 0–10 scales, offering a simplified but informative measure of symptom burden.22 These single-outcome tools face important limitations, particularly regarding sensitivity to change, ability to detect clinically meaningful responses and vulnerability to high placebo response rates. Furthermore, systemic disease activity often shows poor concordance with PROs,23 24 suggesting they measure different aspects of this heterogenous disease and also maybe also reflecting the impact of comorbidities and damage on patient symptoms. This discordance is amplified in studies enrolling patients with low baseline systemic activity, which might obscure treatment effects on systemic disease even when biological target engagement is evident. Such challenges may have contributed to the failure of several promising therapies to meet primary end points in phase II and III trials, despite encouraging biological effects. More recently, composite measures such as Sjogren’s Tool for Assessing Response (STAR), developed by the NEw Clinical Endpoints in Sjögren's Disease: an Interventional Trial based on sTratifYing patients (NECESSITY) consortium and Composite of Relevant End points for Sjögren’s Syndrome (CRESS) have been proposed to better capture therapeutic benefit in trials.25 26 These aim to integrate both objective and subjective outcomes, acknowledging the multidimensional nature of the disease. However, the heterogeneity of SjD manifestations underlines the need for a core set of domains, as emphasised by the recent Outcome Measures in Rheumatology scoping review, which found wide variability in outcomes across previous trials and called for harmonisation of measures in future studies.27 In 2025, the NECESSITY group provided specific guidance to optimise trial design, addressing aspects such as optimal study duration, end point selection, statistical power and strategies to reduce placebo responses while preserving sensitivity to clinically meaningful change.28

Therapeutic strategies

B-cell-targeted therapies

Several randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated rituximab (RTX), an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody which depletes the B-cell lineage from the pre-B to the mature B-lymphocyte stage, in SjD, with mixed results. The first open-label phase II study was published in 2005. Pijpe et al investigated the safety and efficacy of RTX in the treatment of 15 patients with early, active SjD, including patients with associated lymphoma. An improvement of subjective symptoms and an increase in salivary gland function was observed.29 In 2008, a small randomised, double-blind pilot study in 17 patients with SjD showed that RTX significantly reduced fatigue and improved quality of life compared with placebo.30 Similarly, another placebo-controlled trial published in 2010, including 30 patients with primary SjD, suggested that RTX might improve salivary function and subjective outcomes, such as fatigue and sicca symptoms.31 However, the encouraging signals from these early trials were not confirmed in two larger phase III studies,Tolerance and Efficacy of Rituximab in Primary SjS (TEARS); one course of RTX) and The TRial for Anti-B-Cell Therapy In patients with primary SjS (TRACTISS); two courses). Both failed to meet their primary end points, showing no significant benefit in dryness, fatigue or global disease activity scores.32 33 In TEARS, dryness visual analogue scale improved significantly in the RTX group but by <30 mm, raising the possibility that the chosen end point was not sensitive enough to detect clinically meaningful change. Fatigue scores also showed greater improvement with RTX than placebo at weeks 6, 16 and 24, but this effect was not sustained.32 In both trials, baseline systemic activity (ESSDAI) was generally low, which may have limited the ability to demonstrate changes in systemic disease activity, although TEARS did show a significant reduction in immunoglobulin levels.32 Importantly, at the time that these studies were conducted, standardised response criteria in SjD had not yet been established, complicating interpretation of efficacy. TRACTISS attempted to address the possibility that prolonged therapy could enhance response by administering two courses of RTX, but no significant improvement was observed. Nevertheless, RTX was associated with stabilisation of salivary gland function, salivary gland tissue inflammation and major salivary gland ultrasound findings.3336 The persistence of clonally expanded, immunoglobulin-producing plasma cells and tissue-resident CD20+ B cells within salivary glands even after B-cell depletion in peripheral blood, together with RTX-induced increases in BAFF levels that promote the repopulation and survival of autoreactive B cells, may drive disease relapse and explain the limited efficacy of RTX in SjD.37 38 Considering this, phase II studies have been conducted to assess the benefit of targeting BAFF. In 2013, a phase II proof-of-concept study, the Belimumab in Sjogren's Syndrome trial, evaluated belimumab, an anti-BAFF monoclonal antibody, in patients with SjD. Belimumab was administered intravenously at a dose of 10 mg/kg on days 0, 14 and 28, and then every 28 days for a total of 28 weeks. The study reported that 60% of patients (18/30) achieved the predefined primary end point, with improvement in symptoms of dryness and systemic disease activity, but these results need to be interpreted with caution as this was an open-label trial.39 Another phase II RCT further investigated a combination therapy with belimumab and RTX.40 The results demonstrated that simultaneous targeting of the BAFF axis and B cells is a promising therapeutic approach in SjD. Importantly, the safety and tolerability profile of belimumab plus RTX was consistent with that of the individual monotherapies, with no new safety signals detected. In the belimumab+RTX group, there was a reduction in all CD19+ B-cell subsets, including memory B cells, along with delayed B-cell repopulation and sequestration of free BAFF, which persisted after RTX infusion. Moreover, there was a more uniform reduction of tissue CD20+ B cells at week 24 in the combination group, whereas incomplete depletion prevailed in the belimumab, RTX and placebo arms. Responder analysis for ESSDAI indicated that, compared with placebo, there was a numerically higher proportion of responders with belimumab+RTX, although no significant improvements were observed in PROs such as oral dryness or mean total ESSPRI and ESSPRI domain scores. Although TEARS and TRACTISS enrolled over 100 patients each, the considerable clinical and biological heterogeneity of SjD, together with modest effect sizes and substantial placebo responses, may have limited statistical power to detect differences in stringent primary end points, thereby contributing to the failure to meet these outcomes.

Ianalumab is a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that exerts a dual mechanism of action by inducing B-cell depletion through enhanced antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and simultaneously inhibiting BAFF receptor-mediated signalling pathways involved in B-cell survival and function. A phase IIb dose-finding RCT demonstrated the acceptable tolerability and safety profile of ianalumab in patients with primary SjD.41 The study revealed a dose-dependent effect on disease activity, with the largest reduction in ESSDAI and highest number of ESSDAI responders observed in the 300 mg weekly group. Some secondary outcomes also showed statistically significant improvements, including physician’s global assessment (PhGA) and stimulated salivary flow, supporting both systemic and glandular benefits. However, no significant improvements were observed in PROs. While this may highlight a possible gap between objective measures of disease activity and patients’ subjective symptom experience, it may also reflect high placebo responses due to expectation bias making additional improvements with drug difficult to demonstrate. An extension of this trial up to 52 weeks showed that the efficacy of ianalumab, at the 300 mg dose, was sustained over 1 year, with continued reductions in ESSDAI and maintenance of salivary gland function. Nominal trends to symptom improvement were seen in placebo patients transitioning to active drug and deterioration in those switching from ianalumab to placebo. Importantly, the long-term safety profile remained consistent with the initial study, with no new safety signals emerging, thereby reinforcing ianalumab as a promising therapeutic candidate in SjD.42 Two phase III RCTs (NEPTUNUS-1 and NEPTUNUS-2) evaluating ianalumab in patients with SjD have been published in abstract form.43 Both studies met their primary end points, demonstrating statistically significant although modest placebo-adjusted reductions in systemic disease activity (ESSDAI) over 52 weeks, against the background of high placebo responses. Small improvements in patient global assessment were statistically significant in NEPTUNUS-1 but not NEPTUNUS-2 with improvements on other key PROs not reaching statistical significance in the individual trials and awaiting full publication. The safety profile remained favourable and consistent with previous studies, without new safety concerns. A separate, open-label mechanistic study, also reported in abstract form, reported that tissue B-cell density was reduced by 84% (80% CI 68 to 91) after 25 weeks of ianalumab, although there was only a trend to reduction in focus score.44 These pivotal trials represent the first phase III success in SjD, marking a potential breakthrough toward the first disease-modifying therapy specifically approved for SjD.43 Notably, the ianalumab phase III programme enrolled substantially larger patient populations than earlier B-cell-targeted studies in SjD, with NEPTUNUS-1 and NEPTUNUS-2 including approximately 275 and 504 patients, respectively. This represents a clear methodological advantage over the earlier RTX and belimumab trials and strengthens confidence in the observed efficacy signals. However, interpretation across B-cell-targeted therapies remain influenced by high placebo responses and the persistent challenge of demonstrating clinically meaningful improvements in PROs despite clear biological activity.

Despite the improved depth and durability of B-cell depletion achieved with newer agents such as ianalumab, complete eradication of pathogenic B cells and long-lived plasma cells within inflamed tissues remains challenging. This has fuelled interest in more intensive immune-depleting strategies, including chimeric antigen receptor T (CAR-T) cell therapy, which may enable deeper and more sustained immune reset.4547 Although not yet systematically studied in SjD, emerging case reports and cross-indication data suggest potential relevance. Remission of SjD manifestations has been reported in patients receiving anti-CD19 CAR-T cell therapy for B-cell malignancies, supporting the concept that profound B-cell depletion can modulate autoimmune disease activity.48 In support of this, translational studies in humans have demonstrated that CD19-CAR-T cell therapy can induce deep depletion of B cells within lymphoid and non-lymphoid tissues, exceeding the degree of tissue depletion typically achieved with anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, and reinforcing the potential for broad immune modulation in autoimmune disease settings.49 In addition, supplementary data from CAR-T studies targeting the plasma cell marker BCMA, aimed at directly depleting antibody-producing cells, in related autoimmune conditions have included patients with concomitant SjD who demonstrated clinical improvement.50 T-cell-engaging antibodies targeting CD19 or BCMA represent an alternative strategy aimed at deep depletion of B cells and/or plasma cells. Case reports using teclistamab, a BCMA-directed T-cell engager, have demonstrated improvement in autoimmune disease activity, further supporting a pathogenic role for plasma cells in disease perpetuation.51

However, these approaches are associated with important safety considerations, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Moreover, therapies targeting CD19 may have limited impact on autoantibody titres such as anti-Ro, potentially allowing disease relapse if plasma cells persist. While BCMA-targeted strategies may theoretically offer more complete plasma cell depletion, consistent seroconversion of disease-specific autoantibodies has not yet been demonstrated. Ongoing efforts to reduce toxicity, cost and treatment burden, including allogeneic CAR-T products, CAR-NK cells, stem-cell-derived cellular therapies and optimisation of T-cell-engager design,46 will ultimately determine the feasibility and clinical positioning of these approaches in SjD.

Several early-phase basket clinical trials evaluating CD19-directed or BCMA-directed CAR-T cell therapies and T-cell engagers, as well as one study focused entirely on SjD (NCT07041099), are currently registered on public clinical trial databases, underscoring the growing interest in deep immune-reset strategies for severe or refractory disease.

Following the advances achieved with BAFF-R-targeted therapies such as ianalumab, attention has also turned to dual BAFF/APRIL blockade. Telitacicept, a TACI-Fc fusion protein that inhibits both pathways, was evaluated in a phase II randomised clinical trial conducted in China.52 Among the 30 patients who completed the study, telitacicept 160 mg significantly improved ESSDAI and ClinESSDAI scores compared with placebo, with effects evident as early as week 12, although the small sample size and missing data limited the robustness of these findings.52 Building on these results, a large phase III randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled multicentre trial in China including 381 patients with SjD confirmed and extended these findings.53 Both telitacicept 160 mg and 80 mg significantly reduced ESSDAI scores at week 24 compared with placebo, with sustained and consistent improvements across secondary end points, including ESSPRI and STAR response rates. The 160 mg dose showed the most pronounced benefit, and both regimens were well tolerated, with mainly mild or moderate adverse events.53 The ability to detect change on primary and secondary outcomes was greatly enhanced by the much lower placebo responses seen in this trial compared with many other recent SjD trials. Therefore, data from multinational trials would be helpful for comparative purposes. Given a potential role for autoantibody and immune complexes in SjD, an ongoing phase II clinical trial is investigating sibeprenlimab (NCT06928142).54 Sibeprenlimab neutralises APRIL alone which, unlike BAFF, is a plasma cell survival factor. Phase III data for sibeprenlimab in IgA nephropathy has shown good efficacy and safety.55

IgG-targeted approach

Beyond direct B-cell depletion and BAFF/APRIL blockade, alternative approaches aim to modulate humoral immunity downstream of B-cell activation (table 2). One such strategy involves blocking the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which enables IgG recycling, thereby accelerating IgG catabolism and reducing pathogenic autoantibody levels. In 2025, Noaiseh et al published the results of a phase II RCTs (DAHLIAS) that assessed the change from baseline in ClinESSDAI (≥6) score at week 24 of treatment with nipocalimab in individuals with moderate-to-severe active SjD. The arm receiving 15 mg/kg of nipocalimab showed a significant reduction in ClinESSDAI compared with placebo, with a favourable safety profile, namely no cases of severe hypogammaglobulinaemia and a low rate of serious infections, despite the relatively small sample size when compared with larger randomised trials such as those evaluating ianalumab. Dose-dependent reductions in total IgG, anti-Ro60, anti-La and anti-Ro52 levels were observed from weeks 2 through 24 of nipocalimab treatment. The ClinESSDAI was selected as primary outcome due to the very substantial pharmacodynamic changes in IgG, a key biomarker criterion in scoring the ESSDAI biological domain.56 Numerical improvements favouring the highest dose were seen in saliva flow and some symptom scores. This important trial validates a role for autoantibodies in the pathogenesis of SjD and is supported by data from a much smaller phase II trial of efgartigimod, another FcRn inhibitor.57 However, given the complex immunopathogenesis of SjD, it is unclear if all disease manifestations will respond, or only some that are directly mediated by autoantibodies or immune complexes.58

Targeting plasmacytoid dendritic cells and type I interferon pathways

As outlined above, pDCs are a major source of IFN-I and play a central role in the early innate immune activation observed in SjD. Sustained IFN-I signalling contributes to epithelial activation, induction of ISGs, upregulation of BAFF and downstream B-cell hyperactivity, thereby linking innate and adaptive immune dysregulation.4 12 13 This strong mechanistic rationale has prompted interest in therapeutic strategies aimed at inhibiting IFN-I signalling or depleting pDCs as upstream disease-modifying approaches.

Direct inhibition of IFN-I signalling has been explored using anifrolumab, a monoclonal antibody targeting the IFN-I receptor subunit IFNAR1. While anifrolumab has demonstrated clear clinical efficacy in SLE,59 data in SjD remain limited. The ANIfrolumab Treatment for 24 Weeks in Patients With Primary SjS – Efficacy and Safety Assessment in a Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled Phase IIa Trial study is a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase IIa proof-of-concept trial designed to evaluate the safety and efficacy of anifrolumab in 30 patients with SjD, with the primary outcome being the CRESS response at week 24.60 IFN-1 signalling will also be reduced by inhibition of JAK1 or TYK2 intracellular signalling (see the section below - targeting intracellular signalling pathways).

In parallel, alternative strategies aimed at reducing pDC numbers or function are under investigation, including inhibition of FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand, a key growth factor for pDC development.61 Although clinical experience with pDC-targeted therapies in SjD is still at an early stage, these approaches offer the potential advantage of modulating upstream drivers of inflammation and may complement downstream cytokine or B-cell-directed therapies in selected patient subsets.

Targeting intracellular signalling pathways: Bruton’s tyrosine kinase, spleen tyrosine kinase, JAK and TYK2 inhibitors

Recent research has increasingly focused on intracellular signalling pathways as therapeutic targets in SjD, particularly the JAK-STAT and Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) pathways. IFNs activate the JAK-STAT cascade, promoting transcription of ISGs and production of cytokines such as BAFF, thereby enhancing B-cell activation. BTK, together with spleen tyrosine kinase (SYK) and phosphoinositide 3-kinase delta (PI3Kδ),62 acts as a key intracellular mediator of B-cell receptor (BCR) and Fc-receptor-dependent activation in B cells and myeloid cells, linking antigenic stimulation to autoantibody production and inflammatory damage.63 Inhibition of these pathways (JAK, BTK, SYK or PI3Kδ) therefore represents complementary strategies to attenuate the interconnected networks of innate and adaptive immune activation that characterise SjD (table 3).

Remibrutinib, a next-generation, highly selective oral BTK inhibitor, has demonstrated potent immunomodulatory properties and a favourable safety profile in previous studies, supporting its evaluation in SjD through the phase II LOUiSSE trial.64 This double-blind phase II study evaluated the efficacy, safety and molecular effects of remibrutinib in 73 patients with moderate-to-severe SjD. Patients received oral remibrutinib once or twice daily for 24 weeks. Treatment significantly improved systemic disease activity (ESSDAI) versus placebo, with a favourable safety profile. Remibrutinib rapidly reduced CXCL13 levels (~50%), a potential biomarker of tissue inflammation in SjD,65 66 decreased IgM and IgG (especially in patients with high baseline levels) and lowered titres of disease-related autoantibodies (SSA/Ro and SSB/La). A trend towards increased salivary flow was also observed, suggesting potential glandular benefit. Despite these encouraging findings, the small sample size and short treatment duration limit interpretation, and PROs did not improve, possibly reflecting the need for longer exposure to achieve symptomatic benefit. Overall, these findings support remibrutinib as a potentially effective oral disease-modifying therapy for SjD, warranting confirmation in larger and long-term studies, although no phase III trials are currently planned.64

Price et al conducted a phase II trial evaluating the safety and efficacy of filgotinib (a JAK-1 preferential inhibitor), lanraplenib (a potent and selective SYK inhibitor) and tirabrutinib (a selective BTK inhibitor) in patients with primary SjD.67 The primary end point was an unusual composite of improvement in CRP levels and PROs (pain, oral and ocular dryness and fatigue), which did not differ between groups. However, trends towards a greater improvement in ESSDAI, particularly in those with high disease activity at baseline, were seen with filgotinib and biomarker analyses revealed pharmacodynamic effects consistent with target engagement. Despite these findings and a favourable safety profile, none of the study drugs achieved statistically or clinically meaningful differences versus placebo in primary or secondary end points.67 There are limited published data on the use of baricitinib, a selective JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor and tofacitinib, which primarily targets JAK1 and JAK3, in the treatment of SjD. While no randomised clinical trials have yet been published, a recent non-randomised, uncontrolled cohort study evaluating tofacitinib reported significant improvements ESSDAI and ESSPRI, accompanied by reductions in Th17, Tfh and Tph cell subsets and decreased phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 activation, suggesting both clinical and immunological benefits with a favourable safety profile.68 Given the notable responses in ESSDAI and ESSPRI observed in several RCT placebo arms, these uncontrolled results need to be interpreted with caution.

Seletalisib, an oral selective PI3Kδ inhibitor aimed at reducing B-cell-mediated immune activation was evaluated in a phase II study that failed to fully enrol and was therefore underpowered. Although trends towards clinical and tissue improvement were observed, drug-related adverse events and discontinuations occurred more frequently in the seletalisib arm.69

TYK2 is a JAK enzyme, which mediates signalling of key cytokines such as IFN-I, IL-10, IL-12 and IL-23. Deucravacitinib is an oral, selective, allosteric TYK2 inhibitor that binds the regulatory domain and locks the enzyme in an inactive conformation. Unlike JAK inhibitors that target the conserved active site, this limits cross-reactivity with other members of the JAK family (JAK 1–3). Preclinical studies have shown that deucravacitinib effectively inhibits TYK2 activation and downstream signalling mediated by IFN-I and ILs.70 The PAISLEY phase II trial in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) demonstrated higher SRI-4 response rates and favourable safety compared with placebo. Genome-wide association studies have identified TYK2 single nucleotide polymorphisms as risk alleles in both SLE and SjD71 and there are pathophysiological similarities in anti-Ro-positive patients regardless of the diagnosis.72 Patients with anti-Ro-positive SLE in the PAISLEY trial showed improvements with deucravacitinib in pain and fatigue, which are important symptoms for patients with SjD.73 Based on these findings, deucravacitinib offers a novel approach in SjD by targeting cytokine pathways that drive inflammation and glandular dysfunction.74 Although no clinical data are yet available in SjD, the ongoing POETYK SjS-1 trial (NCT05946941), a phase III randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, aims to evaluate its efficacy and safety in adults with active disease.75

T-cell targeting

Given the central role of T-cell dysregulation in the immunopathogenesis of SjD, several therapeutic strategies (table 4) have aimed to modulate T-cell activation, differentiation and immune tolerance. Low-dose IL-2 favours T regulatory cell (Treg) numbers and function over that of T effector cells, due to their high expression of the high-affinity IL-2 receptor. A phase II single-centre RCT showed that low-dose IL-2 significantly improved disease activity and key symptoms in primary SjD, while expanding regulatory T and B cells (including the ratio of Tregs to effector T cells) and demonstrating a favourable safety profile.76 While encouraging, the relatively low placebo responses in this trial make it difficult to compare the results with other recent trials and larger multicentre trials would be required. Recombinant low-dose IL-2 is considered to have a narrow therapeutic window but recent attempts with IL-2 muteins to overcome this have had generally underwhelming results in clinical trials in other autoimmune diseases, and it is uncertain if Tregs will always retain their immunosuppressive function when entering sites of chronic inflammation.

Activation of helper T cells requires multiple signals including the presentation of cognate antigenic peptide by MHC class II molecules on APCs. Peptide binding to MHC class II molecules requires enzymatic removal of the invariant chain that otherwise blocks the peptide binding groove and cathepsin S is non-redundant in this process. Petesicatib is a cathepsin S inhibitor that was studied in a phase IIa clinical trial. Target engagement was shown by increased plasma cathepsin levels but no clinical benefits were observed over and above the high placebo responses.77 However, modest reductions in immunoglobulins and rheumatoid factor were seen raising the possibility that a trial duration longer than the 12-week primary end point may have been needed to observe a clinical effect.77

Inhibition of IL-7 receptor (IL-7R) signalling has been proposed to suppress activated effector T cells while sparing Tregs; however, a recent phase II trial with an anti-IL-7R antibody (S95011; OSE-127; lusvertikimab) failed to demonstrate meaningful biomarker or clinical improvements after 12 weeks of treatment, despite a strong mechanistic rationale.7880 While this is another short duration trial with high placebo responses, the absence of any biomarker changes suggests it is a genuinely negative result that might reflect a redundancy of T-cell survival factors in SjD tissue.

Programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) is an inhibitory receptor on activated T cells that is expressed at particularly high levels in T follicular helper (Tfh) and T peripheral helper (Tph) cells that both have a role in supporting B-cell function. Notably, PD-1 blockade is associated with immune-related adverse events that include sicca syndrome. JNJ-67484703 depletes cells with high PD-1 expression and was studied in the phase II proof-of-biology PARIS basket trial, which tested its ability to deplete Tfh/Tph in disease-relevant target tissue. JNJ-67484703 depleted Tfh/Tph in RA synovium, as well as reducing overall tissue inflammation and DAS-28 clinical scores. However, in SjD, no changes were observed in the proportion of salivary gland Tfh/Tph, histological inflammation measures or peripheral biomarkers. A reduction in ESSPRI score was seen but this did not differ between high-dose and low-dose groups and most likely reflected a placebo response. This trial illustrates the value of early phase proof-of-biology tissue studies and cautions that doses of drug effective in one autoimmune disease may not be applicable to another.81

Costimulation blockade

Helper T-cell activation requires antigen presentation and activation of costimulation pathways. Abatacept is a cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 fusion protein that blocks the CD28/CD80 pathway and is licensed in RA. Phase III trials in SjD were negative and despite some modest biological changes in the periphery, no overall tissue improvements were observed.82 83 It is unclear if this reflects a relative redundancy of this pathway to SjD pathogenesis or if, as above, an effective drug dose in RA was not transferrable to SjD. Conversely, increasing attention has been given to targeting the CD40-CD40L pathway. CD40-CD40L interactions are important for T-cell activation and are central to T-cell and B-cell interactions leading to such effects as B-cell proliferation, antibody class switching and GC formation.84 Iscalimab and dazodalibep, an anti-CD40 monoclonal blocking antibody and a non-antibody Tn3 fusion protein targeting CD40L, respectively, have been investigated as novel therapeutic approaches in SjD. In a phase II randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, proof-of-concept study, the safety and preliminary efficacy of iscalimab were evaluated in patients with primary SjD. The highest dose tested was associated with improvements in ESSDAI and reductions in CXCL13 levels.85 Building on these promising findings, the TWINSS phase IIb trial was designed to further assess the dose-response relationship, safety and efficacy of iscalimab in two distinct patient subgroups: those with moderate-to-high systemic disease activity and those with high symptom burden but low systemic involvement.86 The primary end point, a significant dose-response relationship for iscalimab based on placebo-adjusted change from baseline in ESSDAI at week 24, was met. In patients with high symptom burden, the ESSPRI total score showed a trend towards improvement with iscalimab 600 mg at week 24 with dryness (LS mean treatment difference –1.0 (95% CI –1.8 to –0.2); p=0·016) and fatigue (LS mean treatment difference –0.8 (–1.7 to 0.1); p=0·067) being the main drivers of improvement. Combining the two cohorts revealed a statistically significant placebo-adjusted LS mean difference in ESSPRI at week 24.86 Nevertheless, further clinical development of iscalimab in SjD was subsequently discontinued by the manufacturer as they considered the overall benefit-risk profile for this indication to be unfavourable. A randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled, phase II crossover trial evaluating dazodalibep also assessed the efficacy, safety and tolerability in two populations of patients with SjD: one with moderate-to-severe systemic disease activity (74 randomised patients) and another with an unacceptable symptom burden but limited systemic involvement (109 randomised patients). Interestingly, the trial included patients with associated SjD, a group largely excluded from most recent studies.87 The primary end points were the change from baseline in ESSDAI total score (for population 1) and in ESSPRI total score (for population 2) at day 169. Both primary end points were met. In population 1, a numerically greater improvement in ESSPRI total score was observed in dazodalibep-treated participants compared with placebo, a secondary end point in this subgroup. The individual ESSPRI domains also showed numerical trends towards improvement from baseline to day 169. In population 2, statistically significant improvements were observed in fatigue, dryness and pain over the treatment period, supporting dazodalibep’s potential to address both systemic and symptomatic aspects of SjD.87 Nevertheless, interpretation of these findings should consider the phase II design and moderate sample size, and lower than expected placebo responses in population 2, although the inclusion of glandular and symptom-predominant patients and the consistency of effects across ESSPRI domains represent important strengths of this trial.

Frexalimab, an anti-CD40L monoclonal antibody, was also evaluated in a phase II clinical trial for SjD.88 Despite a well-tolerated safety profile the programme was subsequently discontinued as insufficient clinical efficacy was demonstrated. Nevertheless, the iscalimab and dazodalibep (table 5) data are consistent in suggesting the potential of CD40 pathway inhibition for efficacy on symptoms as well as systemic disease and phase III dazodalibep data are anticipated.

Another co-stimulatory pathway of interest in SjD involves the inducible T-cell co-stimulator (ICOS) and its ligand (ICOS-L). A phase IIa randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of MEDI5872/AMG557, a fully human anti-ICOS-L monoclonal antibody, did not demonstrate clinically meaningful benefit on key disease end points compared with placebo.89 These findings underscore the challenges of translating co-stimulatory blockade into effective clinical outcomes in SjD and suggest that redundant or compensatory co-stimulatory pathways may limit the therapeutic impact of ICOS-L inhibition in this condition.

Drug repurposing and combination approaches

Based on the hypothesis that effective treatment of SjD requires simultaneous targeting of multiple immune pathways and cell types, combination or repurposed therapies using classical synthetic DMARDs, such as hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), leflunomide (LFN) and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF), have been investigated as potential strategies to achieve broader immunomodulation and improved clinical efficacy.90 91 The rationale for combining HCQ and LFN in SjD stems from their complementary immunomodulatory mechanisms: HCQ dampens innate immune activation through inhibition of TLR signalling, while LFN reduces lymphocyte proliferation by blocking pyrimidine synthesis. In a randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase II trial, this dual approach achieved significant reductions in systemic disease activity (ESSDAI) and improvements in PROs, without new safety concerns.90 Benefits were particularly seen in domains reflecting systemic inflammation and glandular involvement. These findings were recently confirmed in the Repurposing drugs for SjS phase IIb trial, which demonstrated a significant ESSDAI reduction and improvements in serum IgG, rheumatoid factor and complement C4 levels, consistent with the results of the earlier RepurpSS-I study. Although no significant differences were observed in ESSPRI or objective dryness measures, higher response rates in the STAR and CRESS composite indices further supported the efficacy and safety of the LFN-HCQ combination, reinforcing its role as a promising, accessible immunomodulatory option for systemic SjD.91 Both RepurpSS trials were undertaken in a single centre but the combination of LEF and HCQ is being further evaluated alongside a combination of MMF and HCQ in the NECESSITY project (NCT05113004) trial, a large, multicentre, double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled European study designed to validate sensitive outcome measures and explore potential disease-modifying treatments for primary SjD.92 The trial includes two cohorts (one comprising patients with high symptom burden and low systemic activity and another with moderate-to-high systemic activity regardless of symptom level) and three treatment arms (placebo, HCQ+LFN, HCQ+MMF and their respective placebos). The primary end point for both cohorts is the proportion of patients achieving a clinical response according to the STAR composite index at week 24, and the study will also provide information on potential biomarkers as predictors of treatment response.

Discussion

Over the past decade, advances in the understanding of SjD immunopathogenesis have led to the development of targeted agents aimed at distinct pathogenic pathways. B-cell-directed therapies remain the most extensively studied, while inhibitors of intracellular signalling are showing promise in modulating both innate and adaptive immune responses.

Despite encouraging results from several phase II and III trials, most benefits have been observed in patients with moderate-to-severe systemic disease, in accordance with trial eligibility criteria. As discussed throughout this review, smaller trials may be underpowered to detect symptom improvement or modest systemic treatment effects against a background of disease heterogeneity and high placebo responses. Nevertheless, recent phase II/III successes in targeting systemic disease raise an important unresolved question: why does symptom-predominant SjD remain largely refractory to current therapeutic strategies? A consistent observation across these multiple trials is the limited improvement in patient-reported symptoms, particularly dryness, fatigue and pain, even when systemic disease activity improves. Several factors may explain this disconnect. First, symptom burden may reflect irreversible glandular damage, particularly in patients with long disease duration, limiting the potential for functional recovery despite immune modulation. In this context, delayed diagnosis is a major challenge in SjD. Many patients are diagnosed only after significant glandular damage has occurred, reducing the likelihood of achieving symptom-based end points in clinical trials. Earlier intervention, potentially during preclinical or early clinical phases, may increase therapeutic efficacy. However, translating this concept into clinical trial design is challenging given the non-specific manifestations of early disease and frequent classification as undifferentiated connective tissue disease or overlap syndromes. Second, dominant pathogenic pathways driving symptoms may lie upstream of, or outside, the therapeutic targets of current agents. Epithelial-CD8+ T-cell interactions, as well as neural and autonomic dysfunction, are increasingly recognised contributors to symptom generation and might not be fully addressed by B-cell-directed therapies alone. Indeed, in patients with milder glandular lesions, lymphocytic infiltrates may be predominantly T-cell driven.93 94 In addition, non-immune mechanisms and comorbidities, including central sensitisation, autonomic dysfunction, mood disorders and overlapping pain syndromes, may substantially contribute to fatigue and pain, further limiting the impact of immunomodulatory therapies on symptom burden. Recent stratification studies support this concept, identifying patient subsets with high symptom burden that are weakly associated with B-cell or IFN-driven molecular signatures.95 Transcriptomic clustering analyses have also described a ‘healthy-like’ SjD subgroup with limited inflammatory activity and poor response to immunomodulatory therapy.96

Addressing this unmet need through targeted strategies and refined outcome measures remains a key frontier for future research. Moreover, as B-cell hyperactivity and chronic inflammation are linked to lymphomagenesis, early effective immune modulation may ultimately contribute to lymphoma prevention.

Conclusion

A recurring limitation across the current therapeutic landscape in SjD is the reliance on small, early-phase trials and the consistently high placebo responses observed across multiple outcome domains. While several agents demonstrate biological activity and modest systemic benefit, no global phase III study to date has convincingly shown sustained improvement across both systemic disease activity and the symptoms that matter most to patients. Future progress will require continued therapeutic innovation and improved understanding of symptom pathophysiology, refined patient stratification and trial designs that reduce placebo effects. Without these advances, there is a risk that SjD may follow a trajectory similar to that seen in SLE, where promising phase II signals frequently fail to translate into phase III success.

Encouragingly, the first phase III successes in SjD with the anti-BAFF receptor antibody ianalumab and the dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor telitacicept mark a potential breakthrough toward the first disease-modifying therapies specifically approved for this condition. Other candidates, including nipocalimab, efgartigimod and dazodalibep, have also shown promising phase II results and may further advance the field. In parallel, early-phase randomised clinical trials investigating CD19-directed or BCMA-directed CAR-T cell therapies and T-cell engagers highlight a growing interest in deep immune-reset strategies for severe or refractory disease. Continued refinement of patient stratification and validated composite end points will be essential to translating these advances into effective individualised care for all patients with SjD.
