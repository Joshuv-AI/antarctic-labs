Washington, D.C., September 25, 2025 - Newly published documents and photographs concerning the radiation and blast effects of the August 1945 bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki provide graphic details on how U.S. and British officials first assessed the deadly impact of the atomic weapons. These and other records published today are among the data and evidence that helped undermine the public claim made at the time by Manhattan Project director Gen. Leslie Groves that radiation sickness was “a pleasant way to die.”

The new collection is a declassified update to a previous National Security Archive e-book on the radiation and blast effects of the bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki and is part of a series of Archive publications marking the 80th anniversary of the atomic bombing of Japan.

Records published today for the first time include the “Final Report” of the Manhattan Project’s “Investigative Group,” whose mission during the fall of 1945 was to probe nuclear weapons effects in both cities to ensure that radiation would not harm U.S. occupation forces. Its report included a detailed medical study on the injuries caused by the explosions along with photographic reports on blast and fire damage. Also published for the first time is a report on “flash burns” compiled by Robert Serber, a Los Alamos physicist who was a member of the U.S. investigative team in Japan.

Other records added to the collection today include a January 1946 report from the British government that found that people near the detonations were killed “several times over” by the various effects of the nuclear explosions and that postulated a “standardized kill rate” of 50,000 fatalities for an atomic bomb. Worried about the eventual vulnerability of Great Britain to nuclear attack, the British found that the “most serious danger presented by the atomic bomb is in producing casualties,” with the problem of “protection against gamma ray casualties in particular [being] exceptionally grave and difficult.”

Groves understood that many thousands of people had been killed by the massive explosive blasts and fire effects of the atomic bombs, but the idea that thousands more died or became severely ill from radiation effects made him uncomfortable. The Manhattan Project leadership dismissed initial reports from Japan about radiation sickness as “propaganda” and remained in denial for months. But research in Japan conducted by the Investigative Group’s medical experts dispelled any such illusions, and by the summer of 1946, with the publication of John Hersey’s “Hiroshima,” the grim reality of radiation sickness became widely known.

Reexploring the Manhattan Project and the Problem of Radiation Effects, 1944-1945

The Manhattan Project leadership and the Los Alamos scientists who designed and produced the first atomic weapons assumed from the outset that they would have tremendous explosive blast effects but they did not yet understand that the radiation released by an atomic explosion would cause slow deaths and potentially more lasting injuries. Even weeks after Hiroshima and Nagasaki, Manhattan Project leaders dismissed reports about deadly radiation as “Japanese propaganda.” Nevertheless, the team of Manhattan Project scientists and military officers that went to Japan in September 1945 were soon recounting and analyzing what the people of Nagasaki and Hiroshima had been experiencing.

When the Roosevelt administration began the Manhattan Project in 1942, the goal was a deliverable atomic weapon that could be used in World War II to defeat Germany and possibly Japan. The project raised tremendously complex political, scientific, engineering, and logistical challenges, but by spring of 1945 the objective was in sight. Manhattan Project leaders expected the nuclear detonation to cause tremendous damage and devastation. But as preparations for the first atomic test were underway, medical experts raised the special dangers of radioactivity—that the test could create health hazards. That became evident in the wake of the Trinity Test on 16 July 1945 when the Manhattan Project’s Chief Medical Officer, Dr. Stafford Warren, stated in a top secret report that, “the dust outfall from the various portions of the [mushroom] cloud was potentially a very serious hazard over a band almost 30 miles wide extending almost 90 miles northeast of the site.” Warren reported a few days later that there was still “a tremendous amount of radioactive dust floating in the air.” [See Document 20 in The Nuclear Vault’s publication on the 80th Anniversary of the Trinity Test]

Distressed by news that cast a bad light on the new weapon, Manhattan Engineer District (MED) chief General Leslie R. Groves told an adviser that the reports were “propaganda” that had to be dispelled. Whether he had read Stafford Warren’s report or not, Groves could no longer claim that there were no deaths from radiation sickness, but he continued to mislead. Thus, he told U.S. Senators that there was “no radioactive residue” at the bombed cities and that radiation sickness was a “very pleasant way to die.”

In contrast to Groves, Los Alamos Laboratory director J. Robert Oppenheimer was silent on the problem of radiation injuries, although he was aware of their likelihood. Discussing safety measures to protect bomber crews from the effects of atomic explosions, Oppenheimer wrote to General Thomas Farrell in May 1945 that the radiation effects would be “injurious within a mile” and “lethal within a radius of about six-tenths of a mile” [See Document 1]. But making sensitive information public was another matter. After the bombing of Hiroshima, when inaccurate information concerning the long-run impact appeared in the U.S. media, the Manhattan Project issued a denial quoting Oppenheimer as saying there “would be no appreciable [radioactive] activity on the ground,” [See Documents 4 and 5]. This published collection supplements the previous National Security Archive posting “The Atomic Bomb and the End of World War II” by presenting key documents from 1944-1946 that provide a close look at the problem of radiation effects of the atomic detonations—from the first weapons test (“Trinity”) to military use in Japan. Also included are major reports on the blast damage caused by the atomic bombings, a phenomenon that led to the analyzing of hundreds of photos of devastated buildings and infrastructure taken by U.S. military officers and British experts.

The documents in this collection were declassified years ago, some as far back as the 1970s, but few are readily available online. A review of these records offers fresh insights into a number of important issues that are relevant to this day. One theme in the collection is how MED leaders, who were planning for the first atomic test at Alamogordo, New Mexico, found it necessary to authorize health and safety protocols to ensure that the event did not contaminate project workers or anyone living nearby. Top officials such as Gen. Groves initially pushed back against the warnings of radioactive fallout, but the uncertainty about the physical effects of the test and pressure from Stafford Warren prompted him to approve recommended safety measures.

The collection also sheds light on U.S. government assessments of radiation sickness in post-attack Hiroshima and Nagasaki. U.S. reactions to reports from Japan ranged from outright denial of radiation disease to acknowledgement that the reports were accurate. The scientists who worked to design the first atomic bomb sought a weapon of extraordinary power with massive explosive yield. That its effects could produce long-term illness was surprising to many, who thought that the blast effects would be the primary cause of deaths. Within a month after the bombings, U.S. teams were in Hiroshima and Nagasaki collecting evidence of radiation sickness. Nevertheless, Gen. Groves remained in denial as indicated by his later Senate testimony.

Also included in the posting is the report by a witness to the bombing of Hiroshima, Johannes Siemes, a German Jesuit priest and university professor who met MED scientists in Tokyo. His first-hand account of the impact of radiation sickness that raised questions about the ethics of using atomic weapons circulated among U.S. officials in Japan. The posting also publishes the initial MED and U.S. Navy reports on the medical effects of the atomic bombings, using information from Japanese doctors and scientists. For the Manhattan Project leadership, radiation safety was never a priority compared to the foremost goal of producing a deliverable weapon but plans to produce large quantities of fissile material made health and safety protocols essential. Plutonium, which had never existed before in nature, was especially dangerous, although the press of wartime work led some scientists to take fatal risks. Early in the history of the MED, project leaders organized a Health Division at the Chicago Metallurgical Laboratory (Metlab), the unit that had responsibility for fissile materials production at Site X (Clinton, Tennessee) and Site W (Hanford, Washington). The division established a standard of 0.1 roentgen (r) per day as an upper limit for full-body exposure, the standard that had been widely accepted in the radiation industry. It was a more or less arbitrary number and a relatively high level of exposure compared to standards adopted in 1946 and later. In any event, most workers at those sites did not know the secret purpose of their work or what the hazards were, but plant managers monitored exposure levels of workers to radiation.It was a more or less arbitrary number and a relatively high level of exposure compared to standards adopted in 1946 and later. By 1944, if not earlier, MED health officials developed safety standards for Site Y (Los Alamos) where scientists were working with plutonium to develop implosion bomb technology. [2]

Months before the Trinity test, some MED experts understood the “hazards” of radioactive fallout, which encouraged them to recommend special precautions to minimize risk to the public and to avoid legal problems. While Gen. Groves had worried about legal issues, his main concern was security and preventing leaks, and he was exasperated when MED officials argued for special precautions for the first test. It took more convincing before Groves signed off on plans for special precautions. [3]

The precautionary measures taken on 16 July 1945 were necessary but insufficient. Not only did the Trinity test produce radioactive fallout that endangered nearby people in New Mexico, but recent research has revealed that it also spread across much of the United States and Canada. The state of knowledge at the time was such that the possibility of fallout spreading beyond nearby areas was not understood, nor were the possible adverse health effects of low doses of radiation. [4]

In the weeks after the atomic bombings, reports emanated from Japan about mysterious lingering deaths of survivors who had not been injured by blast or fire effects. While not initially described as radiation sickness, the Japanese were soon discussing “a-bomb disease,” although occupation authorities would clamp down on media coverage. MED health experts were well aware of the biological consequences of radiation exposure, but there were many unknowns about the impact of ionizing radiation on living tissues. And MED physicists were far from well versed on the problem. Physicist Norman Ramsey (Scientific and Technical Deputy to Captain William “Deak” Parsons) initially dismissed the Japanese reports as “propaganda.” Like others at Los Alamos, his priority had been building a deliverable weapon with huge explosive effects which, he assumed, would kill instantly anyone who otherwise might have died from radiation exposure. As Ramsey later put it, they would have been “killed with a brick first.” Ramsey and others learned that some near ground zero were in strong structures that made them “accidental survivors of the blast effects” and vulnerable to radiation. [5]

In the weeks after the bombings, following Japan’s capitulation and U.S. moves to deploy occupation forces, Groves and his advisers sent MED medical experts to Japan to investigate weapons effects in both cities and to determine whether occupation troops could safely enter those cities. In early September, a few weeks after the surrender, an MED group led by Brigadier General Thomas Farrell arrived in Japan with the medical team led by Colonel Stafford Warren. Also included were experts on blast effects, including William Penney, a member of the British group at Los Alamos. The leader of the MED mission to Japan, General Thomas Farrell hoped that its findings would controvert Japanese reporting, but that damage control aspiration was to no avail. In a press briefing on 7 September, Farrell dismissed Australian journalist Wilfred Burchett’s accounts of radiation sickness as “propaganda,” [7] but his own reports and the more detailed studies by doctors on his staff, included uncomfortable facts. According to Farrell, “Summaries of Japanese reports previously sent are essentially correct, as to clinical effects from single gamma radiation dose.” Moreover, Stafford Warren’s interim study on the medical effects of the explosions would report that “delayed effects” involving severe symptoms “were due to radiation.”

The preliminary report that Warren presented to Groves in early December was prepared with the assistance of James F. Noland. It included medical findings, radioactivity measurements, and estimates of casualties and damage to structures. On casualties, Warren provided detailed information on the kinds of injuries suffered, including thermal burns and those caused by blast effects, and the delayed symptoms of radiation poisoning which showed up after three to thirty days. He reported that of the “approximately 4000 patients admitted to hospitals [in both cities], 1300 or 33% showed effects of radiation and of this number approximately one-half died.”

The “residual radioactivity” problem was associated with what became a controversy in Japan over the impact of fallout. While not as severe as the fallout from the Trinity test, the blasts produced what became known as “black rain”—radiation-laced raindrops that fell on residents of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. That was a theme in Masuji Ibuse’s extraordinary novel, Black Rain (1969). Elements of the investigative teams sent by the Army, Navy, and MED eventually merged into what became known as the “Joint Commission for the Investigation of the Effects of the Atomic Bomb in Japan,” which also included Japanese scientists and other staff. In 1946, the Joint Commission produced a secret six-volume report, which the Atomic Energy Commission published in 1951, with a first volume that included detailed eyewitness accounts by residents of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The work of the Joint Commission set the stage for the long-term research conducted by the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission (ABCC), which developed from an authorization signed by President Truman in November 1946. Uncertain Knowledge of Atomic Bomb Casualties

The “Final Report” of the MED investigative group included its “best estimates” of atomic bomb casualties as of spring 1946. For fatalities in Hiroshima, the number was 78,000; for those in Nagasaki, the number was 35,000, with injuries estimated at 37,000 and 30,000, respectively. The collapse of civic institutions (hospitals, police departments and other agencies), the “state of utter confusion” after the detonations, the lack of censuses and registration information, and that fact that the “great fires that raged” in both cities “totally consumed many bodies” all worked against accurate counts.

Those estimates were on the low side because assessments at the other end have gone as high as 140,000 for Hiroshima and 110,000 for Nagasaki. Varley’s report on Hiroshima suggested a fatality number far higher than the main “Final Report”: “probably between the official figure of 92,000 and 126,000 reported by the Vice-Director of the Red Cross Hospital at Hiroshima.” To this day, the total number of deaths in both cities is unknowable, with only guesswork possible for totals in the higher and the lower ranges. The lower estimates are based on reports prepared after the bombings; the higher estimates are from a 1977 re-estimate. The fatalities included Korean and Chinese workers, Japanese Americans stranded after Pearl Harbor, and U.S. and Allied prisoners of war who were held in Hiroshima and Nagasaki at the time. [11]

Just as uncertain are the numbers of people in both cities who died or suffered from radiation sickness in the weeks, months, and years that followed the bombings. In 1946, Stafford Warren estimated that six to eight percent of the deaths were from radiation sickness, but the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey projected even more – 15 to 20 percent, although the numbers could be higher.In 1946, Stafford Warren estimated that six to eight percent of the deaths were from radiation sickness, but the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey projected even more – 15 to 20 percent, although the numbers could be higher. In 1984, the journalist Peter Wyden published an estimate of 20,000 deaths from radiation sickness and 20,000 additional injuries. The fact that exposed people experienced a greater than usual incidence of leukemia mortality in the years that followed is incontestable. [12]

While official medical reports about radiation sickness would remain classified for years, ongoing events reduced the secrecy. Philip Morrison, a scientist at Los Alamos who was a member of the U.S. group that traveled to Hiroshima and Nagasaki in September 1945, gave candid testimony about the devastating effects of gamma rays before the Special Committee on Atomic Energy that was quoted in The New York Times [See Document 22]. In June 1946, the White House released the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey on the atomic bombings, and its disclosures about radiation sickness and its course provided the substance for articles in The Washington Post and The New York Times . Only weeks later, on 31 August, the writer John Hersey put faces on the victims of radiation disease, when The New Yorker published “ Hiroshima ” based on his recent research and interviews in Japan. With its striking treatment of the impact of radiation sickness on the lives of individuals, Hersey’s report became an international sensation. Behind the veils of secrecy, President Truman had already imagined human faces when confronted with the fact of massive civilian casualties in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. After learning about the many thousands of civilians and “all those kids” that had been killed in the attack, Truman decided that no more atomic weapons would be dropped over Japan without his express approval. Suspecting that atomic bombs were not even usable and deciding that they were too terrible to be left under military control, Truman secretly asserted his commander-in-chief authority so that he would have final decision-making power over any proposals for combat use. [14]

President Truman’s atomic command-and-control decisions in the last days of World War II remained secret for years, as did the records of the MED. It was not until the 1960s, 70s, and 80s that sufficient archival material became available for historians and social scientists to write knowledgeably about the first use of atomic weapons and their radiation effects. It also took years for U.S. government officials to publicly acknowledge that nuclear weapons tests and explosions produced harmful radioactive fallout.It also took years for U.S. government officials to publicly acknowledge that nuclear weapons tests and explosions produced harmful radioactive fallout. The Atomic Energy Commission insisted that fallout posed no health risks, but its position was undermined by another incident involving Japan, the exposure of the crew members of the fishing boat Lucky Dragon to the effects of the 1954 multi-megaton Castle Bravo test . That incident played a key role in bringing the fallout problem to light, though not without more denials. It would take almost another decade, however, before the U.K., the U.S., and the Soviet Union did something about the problem by agreeing to halt atmospheric testing in 1963 (although China and France continued for years). [15]

In Japan, the survivors of the attacks would struggle for recognition and social support. In the United States, the “Hiroshima maidens” would stir public interest, but looking back at the bombings had little appeal. While the Truman administration would release some information about radiation sickness—such as in reports of the U.S. Strategic Bombing Survey—it, along with former officials, had little interest in a searching public examination of the first use of nuclear weapons.

While eventual releases of primary sources would complicate the picture, the Compton and Stimson arguments held sway for years. [16]

Interested in having the press visit the Trinity test site but concerned about the Japanese reports of radiation sickness, Groves telephoned Louis Hempelmann and asked him about the present level of radioactivity. Groves then requested that Hempelmann write a short memorandum on radioactivity “stating how long it is in the general area covered and showing how it diminishes from time to time.” With respect to the crater, Hempelmann said there was a “fair amount” of radioactivity around it and that it was “fairly intense” for 100 feet (probably from ground zero). When Groves asked if it was safe for reporters to visit, Hempelmann replied that “they wouldn’t be so safe.” Thus, when Groves and Oppenheimer brought reporters to the site on September 11, 1945, everyone in the group wore protective white booties as a safety measure.

When U.S. officials faced media reports of Japanese radiation sickness, they continued the denial approach. On 7 September, U.S. occupation authorities in Tokyo held a press conference after Australian reporter Wilfred Burchett published a story in the British Daily Express , “The Atomic Plague,” based on his visit to Hiroshima a few days earlier. The purpose of the briefing, led by General Farrell, was to deny the story. Burchett arrived near the end of the briefing; according to his account, when he declared that he had witnessed the harmful impact of radiation exposure (although he did not use those words), the general declared that he was a “victim [of] Japanese propaganda.” Farrell brought the briefing to an end. [28]

The next week, Farrell held a press briefing that began with a statement downplaying the radiation danger. Farrell asserted that “no measurable radioactivity [was] found under the point of detonation or elsewhere on the ground, streets, in the ashes or on other materials.” Citing Stafford Warren, he further asserted that “patients who were effected by radiation resulted from a single exposure to a dose of gamma radiation at the time of detonation” not from “deposit of dangerous amounts of radioactivity on the ground.” Equivocating a bit, Farrell declared that it was “not the whole truth … that personnel coming into the area to assist in evacuation were seriously injured.” It was “the opinion of our experts that there would be no residual radioactivity on the ground in dangerous amounts.” Although residual radiation would dissipate, those claims were doubtful because Japanese volunteers who helped with recovery efforts during the days after the detonations experienced radiation sickness. Stafford Warren to Leslie Groves, “The Use of the Gadget as a Tactical Weapon Based on Observations Made during Test II,” July 25, 1945, Top Secret [Partial transcript prepared by Aaron Gimpel]
Jul 25, 1945 | Source Department of Energy Open-Net, Nuclear Testing Archive
The experience of the Trinity detonation influenced Stafford Warren’s report to Groves about the military use of the “gadget.” (MED officials referred to a “gadget,” instead of a bomb, partly for security reasons but perhaps also to “ease their minds” about the purposes of their work [18] Warren discussed the impact of an explosion over a city, ranging from the radiation hazards to the production of fires. The fireball would require pilots to use special goggles to protect their eyes, and the radiation produced by the bomb could endanger troops entering a city after an explosion. Warren was giving advice on how to use the “gadget” as an “assault weapon,” and he recommended specific tactics to do so. At the end of the memo, Warren included a chart showing the dangers to “normal active troops” in terms of levels of exposure and the possibility of injuries. The range of exposure was from 30 to 500 roentgen, depending on how long a person was in the danger zone. At the highest levels of exposure, Warren estimated that many troops could experience “permanent damage.”

Major General L.R. Groves to Chief of Staff [General George C. Marshall], 30 July 1945, Top Secret
Jul 30, 1945 | Source RG 77, MED Records, Top Secret Documents of Interest to General Groves, box 3, Folder 5B, Memos, Directives, etc
Groves’ report to General Marshall on Trinity emphasized blast effects but made a strikingly misleading statement about radiation effects, especially in light of what Stafford Warren had already written to him (Document 10). As Sean Malloy has noted, when Groves wrote that “No damaging effects are anticipated on the ground from radioactive materials,” he “stripped away virtually all of Warren's caveats and precautions (as well as his estimates of safe and unsafe dosages for occupying troops)." [19]

Groves Diary, 8 August 1945, unclassified
Aug 8, 1945 | Source National Archives, Donated Records of General Leslie R. Groves, Visitations and Telephone Call Diaries, box 3
A few days after the Hiroshima bombing, Harold Jacobson , a New York City-based scientist who had briefly worked for the MED, wrote a widely distributed report, first published in the San Francisco Examiner , asserting erroneously and sensationally that the radiation would render Hiroshima uninhabitable for 70 years. Groves telephoned Oppenheimer about Jacobson’s article. Oppenheimer had not seen it and said “this is of course lunacy.” Oppenheimer also said that “based on the test in New Mexico there would be no appreciable activity on the ground and what little there was would decay very rapidly.” He authorized Groves to use that statement. Under pressure from federal agents, Jacobson disowned his report, but it received wide publicity and reached Japanese audiences very quickly, on whom it had a significant impact. [20]

Dr. Robert Stone [Health Division, Metlab], to Colonel Hymer Friedell, 9 August 1945, unclassified
Aug 9, 1945 | Source Department of Energy Open-Net, Nuclear Testing Archive
Soon after the atomic bombings, Robert Stone, director of the health division at the Metlab, expressed concern about radiation effects in a letter to a former student, Hymer Friedell, Stafford Warren’s deputy based at Site X. Wondering whether the “Japanese were warned that radiation hazards would remain after the explosion hazard had passed away,” Stone worried that health workers would be dangerously exposed. He wrote that failure to warn raised the possibility of guilt for the “unnecessary destruction of life.” Especially distressing to him was Oppenheimer’s response to the Jacobson report (as quoted above). “I could hardly believe my eyes when I saw a news release said to be quoting Oppenheimer and giving the impression that there is no radioactive hazard. Apparently all things are relative.”

“Toxic Effects of the Atomic Bomb,” 12 August 1945, Top Secret
Aug 12, 1945 | Source RG 77, MED Records, Top Secret Documents of Interest to General Groves, box 3, File 5G (copy from microfilm, courtesy of Alex Wellerstein)
A few days after the bombing of Nagasaki, a report from persons unknown went to General Groves on the “toxic effects” of the bomb. With Groves’ aversion to associating the idea of toxins and poisons with the bomb, he may have disliked this report, if he read it. It described in detail the creation of fallout and the biological impact of radiation doses at various levels and periods of time. It is possible that the report was prepared as background for Groves’ decision to approve the dispatch of a survey team to Japan to assess the damage and determine safety conditions before U.S. occupation troops arrived. In that connection, the table on page 4 concerning levels of exposure is similar to the table on page 3 of Stafford Warren’s memo to Groves on 25 July (see Document 2).

Chief of Staff To Commander-in-Chief, Army Forces, Pacific Command, 12 August 1945, Top Secret
Aug 12, 1945 | Source George C. Marshall Papers, George C. Marshall Library, Lexington, VA (copy courtesy of Barton J. Bernstein)
With Japan’s surrender expected, the U.S. high command accelerated planning for the occupation. Marshall informed Gen. Douglas MacArthur that Gen. Groves had directed MED officials on Tinian (where they had supervised the atomic bombing operations), to send investigative groups to Hiroshima and Nagasaki to assess the impact of the bombings and to determine whether conditions there were safe enough to admit U.S. forces. In addition, Groves wanted Farrell to send a team to Tokyo to check the status of Japanese nuclear research. When Japan surrendered, moving these plans forward became a complex task because the teams required optimum numbers of scientific and technical experts. By 5 September the first group had arrived in Japan. [21]

General Groves to Chief of Staff [George C. Marshall], 24 August 1945,Top Secret
Aug 24, 1945 | Source RG 77, MED Records, Top Secret Documents of Interest to General Groves, box 3, folder 5B Directives, Memos, etc.
Reminding Marshall that he had sent “teams of investigators” to Japan, Groves further informed him in this memo that he wanted the teams to determine the impact of radioactivity on the Japanese. He believed that Japanese casualties from radiation were “unlikely,” but the “facts” had to be established.

P[aul] L. Henshaw and R[obert] R. Coveyou to H.J. Curtis and K. Z. Morgan, “Death from Radiation Burns,” 24 August 1945, Confidential [Control sheet attached]
Aug 24, 1945 | Source Department of Energy Open-Net, Nuclear Testing Archive
Truman’s announcement that the U.S. had dropped an atomic bomb on Hiroshima enabled Japanese scientists to make sense of reports of casualties with mysterious symptoms that soon emerged in Hiroshima and then Nagasaki. Two scientists at Oak Ridge’s Health Division, Paul Henshaw, a physicist (later a leader of the U.S. Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission [ABCC]), and Robert Coveyou, a mathematician, drew some conclusions about the health effects of the bombings as reported by United Press International: people who had looked healthy became weak “for unknown reasons,” and many died. Henshaw and Coveyou’s review of atomic bomb effects led them to find that “it seems highly plausible that a great many persons were subjected to lethal and sub-lethal dosages of radiation in areas where direct blast effects were possibly non-lethal.” It was “probable,” therefore, that radiation “would produce increments to the death rate and “even more probable” that a “great number of cases of sub-lethal exposures to radiation have been suffered.” [22]

Groves Diary, 25 August 1945, unclassified
Aug 25, 1945 | Source National Archives, Donated Records of General Leslie R. Groves, Visitations and Telephone Call Diaries, box 3

Memorandum of Telephone Conversation Between General Groves and Lt. Col. Rea, Oak Ridge Hospital, 9:00 a.m., 25 August 1945, Top Secret
Aug 25, 1945 | Source RG 77, Top Secret Correspondence/Manhattan Project, box 3, folder 5H Damage Reports
Radio broadcasts from Japan reported the effects of radiation sickness, but Gen. Groves and Dr. Charles Rea, a surgeon who was head of the base hospital at Oak Ridge, dismissed them as “propaganda.” Both agreed that the injuries were nothing more than “good thermal burns.” Rea had no specialized knowledge of radiation, but if Groves had read the secret memoranda on that subject that he had received, he may have been speaking disingenuously. Perhaps he was in denial about the radiation and was seeking assurance from Rea. If Groves was worried about the broader implications of bomb-caused radiation sickness, a strategy of denial was a way to protect the new weapon from charges that it, like poison gas, was on the other side of a “crucial moral threshold.” [23] What further upset Groves about the reports from Japan, as he disclosed at the end of the conversation, was that an “American started them off,” referring to the report by Harold Jacobson.

G[eorge]. B. Kistiakowsky [Director of Los Alamos X Division] to J. Robert Oppenheimer, “My Activities During Your Absence,” n.d. [circa 1 September 1945], Confidential
Sep 1, 1945 | Source Department of Energy Open-Net, Nuclear Testing Archive
In this memorandum to Oppenheimer, George B. Kistiakowsky , who led explosives research at Los Alamos, reviewed pending issues, including the expansion of Los Alamos’s Site-S (which produced special lenses for plutonium bombs) and the future of the “Project.” But he began by briefly recounting the internal discussion over how to respond to Japanese reports about radiation sickness. [24] Kistiakowsky described how, in response to queries from Gen. Groves about the reports, he had teletyped recommendations that the Manhattan Project director had rejected. Kistiakowsky then asked Louis Hempelmann and others to prepare a memorandum for Groves on the effects of the bombings but seems to have hesitated in sending it after Groves said, during a press conference at Oak Ridge, Tennessee, that the accounts from Japan were “propaganda” and that radiation had not caused any deaths. In several conversations on September 7 Groves continued to dismiss reports of Japanese deaths from radiation effects. Speaking with Harvard University President James B. Conant, who was also a top Manhattan Project official, Groves said there was “no basis” to reports of deaths from radiation, but said that Col. Stafford Warren, the chief medical officer, was going to Japan to look into the problem. Groves also had a conversation with Dr. Charles Rea, head of the Oak Ridge base hospital, who explained to him the difference between leukemia, an increase in white blood cells, and leukopenia, a decrease in white cells that was experienced by people exposed to radiation effects. Groves mentioned a document discussing “three different causes of casualties,” possibly a reference to the recent Los Alamos memorandum (which may have eventually reached Groves, despite Kistiakowsky’s hesitations). When Groves asked whether “diet” could be a cause of leukopenia, Rea said it wasn’t but suggested that “it could be caused by some medicines like benzidrine [sic].” As in an earlier conversation [See Document 11], Rea believed that post-explosion deaths in Japan were caused by “heat” but he added that deaths that occurred later “could be caused from the blast,” noting, for example, the cases of “people who are in accidents and who walk away apparently unharmed and die the next day.” Speaking with Oppenheimer later that morning, it was evident that Groves bought into Rea’s story and not the findings of the Los Alamos staff. When Oppenheimer mentioned “delayed effects from radiation,” Groves said that Rea had accounted for the deaths as “delayed heat and blast cases.” On that, Oppenheimer made no comment. Groves would eventually accept that delayed radiation effects caused deaths [See Document 21].

Stafford Warren to Major General L. R. Groves, “Preliminary Report – Atomic Bomb Investigation,” 27 November 1945, Secret
Nov 27, 1945 | Source Stafford Warren Papers, University of California Los Angeles Libraries Special Collections, box 68, Folder 7
After returning from Japan, Warren, with the assistance of James F. Nolan, prepared a detailed preliminary report on the results of the atomic attacks on Hiroshima and Nagasaki. More information would be gleaned by the Navy’s team and later through the preparation of the report by the U.S.-Japan “Joint Commission of the Investigation of the Effects of the Atomic Bomb in Japan.” Even more data would be gathered by the Joint Commission’s successor, the Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission. The report focused on medical findings, radioactivity measurements, and estimates of damage to structures. On casualties, Warren provided detailed information on the kinds of injuries suffered, including thermal burns and those caused by blast effects, and the delayed symptoms of radiation poisoning, which showed up after three to thirty days. He reported that of the “approximately 4000 patients admitted to hospitals [in both cities], 1300 or 33% showed effects of radiation and of this number approximately one-half died.” Warren found persisting low levels of radiation at ground zero and several kilometers away. While finding that the “intensity of radiation is quite low [it] is measurable with the very sensitive instruments used.” In some cases, the acceptable tolerance dose “is exceeded slightly.” Referring to the spread of fallout, he cited Japanese data “that patients in Nagasaki as far away as 4 kilometers [showed] effects of radiation.” The MED’s 1946 public report made similar points, denying any health injuries from induced or residual radiation. “Black rain” was not yet on Warren’s mental map, although the phenomenon is mentioned in the Navy report.

Robert Serber, “Flash Burn at Nagasaki and Hiroshima,” enclosed with letter from Serber to General Farrell, 27 November 1945, Secret, with document receipt attached
Nov 27, 1945 | Source RG 77, General Administrative Files: Reports Pertaining to the Effects of the Atomic Bomb, 1945–1946, box 90, Robert Serber “Report on Flash Burn at Nagasaki and Hiroshima”
Robert Serber , an early Manhattan Project recruit, joined Los Alamos Laboratory in 1943, where he played a significant role in weapons design projects. This report on flash burns was one of the results of his participation in the MED mission to Japan in the September 1945 [Serber appears in Figure 58 in Document 29B]. Serber reported on the effects of the radiated energy—“the total energy liberated … into radiant heat and light”—produced by an atomic explosion. The flash burn produced by the radiated energy was not a major cause of damage, but its impact was “very evident” and caused many casualties, which were described in the Final Report [See Document 26]. Serber’s report reviewed other flash burn effects, including the charring of telephone poles and the yellowing of foliage that had been green. Serber was able to deduce the height of burst from the angle of shadows cast by window frames and preserved on the walls of a room on the fourth floor of the Hiroshima post office building. “This turned out to be 1880’ plus/minus 50’, the probable error being estimated from the agreement between measurements made on different shadows.” According to Serber, 1850 feet was the expected altitude for the detonation, but later estimates indicated that it was 1898 plus or minus 50 feet. Serber found it difficult to reach “any definite conclusion on the efficacy of the flash in setting fires, either directly, or, conceivably, through the spreading of burning debris by the blast.” The problem was that Japanese houses included various “fire making agencies, such as stoves,” which the blast effects would have overturned. “At both Nagasaki and Hiroshima the radius of nearly complete demolition of Japanese housing by blast was larger than the radius of complete coverage by fire.”

In striking contrast to Groves’ testimony was the statement of Los Alamos physicist Philip Morrison, who had been with the U.S. group surveying weapons damage in Hiroshima and Nagasaki during September-October 1945. A leading member of the British mission to the Manhattan Project, physicist and mathematician William Penney , conducted research that helped establish the optimum height of burst for the detonations above Hiroshima and Nagasaki to maximize their destructiveness. The Mission arrived at the 50,000 number after reviewing the destruction caused by the atomic explosions, beginning with the blast and fire effects and then the “New Effects” of radiation and burns. Fire caused by explosive blast but also by heat effects was “the cause of much of the destruction.” Following a detailed account of structural and other damage caused by the blast (p. 53), the authors provided estimates of the explosive yields that were “somewhat academic and of doubtful value.” For Hiroshima, they estimated some 2600 tons of TNT; for Nagasaki, some 3270 tons. Captain C[lifton] G. Grimes, Chief, Naval Technical Mission to Japan, to Chief of Naval Operations, “Miscellaneous Targets [-] Atomic Bombs, Nagasaki and Hiroshima Article 2 Medical Effects, Supplementary Studies,” 29 May 1946, Index No. X-28-2, Top Secret

Prepared by Shields Warren, this is a study in the histology, or microscopic anatomy, of atomic bombing victims based on data from autopsies conducted by Japanese and U.S. medical specialists. Written for the use of specialists, the report provides detailed coverage of the impact of ionizing radiation on the body’s organs. Slightly more accessible are the conclusions, which include a chronological presentation of the “pattern of injuries” from the moment of detonation through the weeks that followed, as more and more sickened or died from radiation exposure. Part II of the report discussed residual radioactivity at Nagasaki on the basis of the research of Japanese scientists with Kyushu Imperial University (now Kyushu University) and the Institute of Physical and Chemical Research in Tokyo. Their data, evaluated by Warren as “essentially sound,” was the basis of diagrams at the close of the report, including the “detailed location of the explosion center” and the “pattern of deposition of fission products.”

Captain E. J. Hakansson to Lt. Colonel Leigh C. Fairbank, Corps of Engineers, Military Operations Division, Manhattan Project Headquarters, War Department, “Measurement of the Residual Radiation Intensity at the Hiroshima and Nagasaki Atomic Bomb Sites, report on, forwarding of,” 20 June 1946, Secret
Jun 20, 1946 | Source RG 77, Reports Pertaining to the Effects of the Atomic Bomb 1945-1946, box 90
This highly technical report on residual radioactivity by the Naval Medical Research Institute at Bethesda complemented the work of the Naval Medical Mission on the same topic. It found that the “deposition of fission products” (not yet called fallout) from the atomic bombings produced residual radiation in areas around Hiroshima and Nagasaki. The other source of residual radioactivity was “the production of radioactive isotopes in the ground materials by the extremely intense neutron and gamma ray production incident to the explosion.” Two areas of Hiroshima and Nagasaki received close scrutiny. One was the village of Takasu, downwind from Hiroshima. There, the “residual activity in the Hiroshima area as a whole was far below physiologically significant intensity.” In the Nishiyama area of Nagasaki, however, the report found that “the maximal tolerance dose of radiation was exceeded for a sufficient length of time … to produce significant physiological effects but that it fell below the maximal tolerance dose fairly quickly.” Further research by Japanese experts with Kyushu Imperial University found “significant, although slight physiological effects” that were “capable of producing a measurable physiological change.”
