Dual metabolic intervention nanoplatform co-delivering BAY-876 and L-cystine for Wilms tumor therapy via disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal collapse
Authors: Junyi Luo, Tao Mi, Jinkui Wang, Liming Jin, YongLin Mou, Xiaohai Hu, Li Lei, Jiaxin Rao, Peng Guo, Dawei He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPulmonary metastasis and limited treatment tolerability remain major bottlenecks underlying the poor prognosis of high-risk Wilms' tumor (WT). Disulfidptosis, a recently defined metabolic cell-death modality driven by disulfide stress and characterized by F-actin cytoskeletal collapse, offers a new route to exploit tumor metabolic vulnerabilities; however, the therapeutic application of this concept is constrained by SLC7A11-associated metabolic heterogeneity and the impracticality of systemic glucose deprivation. Guided by the context-dependent metabolic effect of L-cystine in WT cells, we propose a “supply restriction–load increase” framework and develop a CD276-targeting cell-membrane-camouflaged biomimetic nanoplatform, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, to achieve lesion-enriched co-delivery of BAY-876 and L-cystine. Mechanistically, by inhibiting glucose uptake while increasing intracellular cystine/disulfide burden, the nanoplatform amplifies redox imbalance and disulfide stress, induces high-molecular-weight aggregation/smearing of cytoskeleton-associated proteins under non-reducing conditions, and causes pronounced F-actin disruption, consistent with disulfidptosis-associated phenotypes. Increased intracellular ROS levels and a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio further indicate perturbed redox homeostasis. In orthotopic WT and pulmonary metastasis models, this nanoplatform markedly suppresses tumor progression, reduces pulmonary metastatic burden, and prolongs survival without obvious systemic toxicity under the current dosing regimen. Collectively, this work provides a potential design principle for exploiting metabolic vulnerability through coordinated pathway stress and tumor-associated marker-guided biomimetic delivery, supporting metabolic synergistic therapy for high-risk WT.Graphic abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04640-7.Keywords: Wilms’ tumor, Disulfidptosis, Dual metabolic intervention, CD276, Biomimetic nanomedicine

Introduction

Wilms' tumor (WT) is one of the most common malignant solid tumors of the pediatric urinary system, accounting for approximately 5%–7% of childhood malignancies [1, 2]. Multimodal therapy centered on surgery combined with chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy has enabled favorable long-term survival for most patients [3]. However, outcomes remain suboptimal in high-risk WT, particularly in cases with diffuse anaplasia or pulmonary metastasis, where treatment responses are less consistent and the risks of relapse and progression remain high [4–6]. Meanwhile, treatment intensification is frequently accompanied by substantial therapy-related toxicities, including doxorubicin-associated cardiotoxicity and chemotherapy-induced myelosuppression, which impose a persistent health burden on children during critical stages of growth and development [7–9]. Therefore, improving antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity remains a key challenge in the evolution of WT therapy.

Disulfidptosis, a recently proposed form of regulated cell death, offers a new conceptual framework for targeting tumors with specific metabolic vulnerabilities [10]. Unlike apoptosis and ferroptosis, disulfidptosis is linked to abnormal intracellular accumulation of disulfides, which can drive catastrophic remodeling and collapse of the F-actin cytoskeleton, ultimately leading to structural damage and cell death [11–13]. Mechanistically, many tumor cells upregulate SLC7A11 (the cystine/glutamate antiporter) to increase cystine uptake, thereby supporting glutathione (GSH) synthesis and redox homeostasis [14, 15]. This adaptation comes at a metabolic cost: reducing cystine to cysteine consumes NADPH [16]. When NADPH supply becomes limiting, inadequately reduced cystine/disulfides may accumulate, promoting aberrant disulfide crosslinking of cytoskeleton-associated proteins and triggering disulfidptosis [17]. Given the metabolic demand of WT cells and the cancer-relevant role of SLC7A11-mediated antioxidant defense, SLC7A11-associated cystine metabolism may represent a targetable metabolic vulnerability in WT [18, 19].

Notably, a single metabolic insult often fails to produce robust and durable therapeutic effects. First, tumor cells possess redundant NADPH-generating pathways (e.g., the pentose phosphate pathway and folate cycling), which can partially compensate for NADPH depletion and buffer redox stress [20, 21]. Second, cystine uptake can vary across tumor states; tumor cells may modulate SLC7A11 expression to adapt to stress, thereby reducing sensitivity to cystine/disulfide overload [22, 23]. In addition, certain metabolic inhibitors (including small molecules targeting GLUT1) may have suboptimal solubility and pharmacokinetic profiles, and nonspecific exposure can cause systemic adverse effects, limiting translational potential [24]. These considerations highlight the need to optimize both mechanistic design and delivery strategy to improve the efficiency and safety window of disulfidptosis induction.

Nanocarrier biointerface engineering provides an effective strategy to improve the in vivo performance of therapeutic agents by enhancing drug stability, prolonging systemic circulation, reducing nonspecific clearance, and improving biodistribution [25–27]. Among these approaches, membrane-coated biomimetic nanoparticles are particularly attractive because the membrane interface can preserve membrane-associated recognition functions while protecting therapeutic cargos and facilitating targeted delivery [28–30]. In addition, integrating membrane coating with a stimuli-responsive nanoparticle core can further enable controlled cargo release within pathological microenvironments [26, 29, 30]. These properties are highly relevant for metabolic nanomedicine, where therapeutic efficacy depends not only on the potency of metabolic intervention but also on enhanced tumor exposure and reduced systemic toxicity.

Based on this rationale, we propose a synergistic metabolic intervention that simultaneously restricts NADPH supply and increases intracellular cystine/disulfide burden to amplify disulfide stress and reduce the likelihood of metabolic escape. Specifically, BAY-876, a potent GLUT1 inhibitor, is used to restrict glucose uptake and thereby limit glucose-dependent NADPH regeneration (supply restriction) [31, 32]. In parallel, exogenous cystine is delivered through nanoparticles to further increase intracellular cystine/disulfide burden (burden escalation). This “supply restriction–burden escalation” strategy is expected to create a more pronounced and sustained NADPH deficit in tumor cells, thereby promoting F-actin cytoskeletal injury associated with disulfidptosis. For targeted delivery, we selected CD276 (B7-H3), which is highly expressed in various solid tumors and is associated with poor prognosis in WT [33, 34]. Accordingly, nanoparticles were coated with CD276-targeting engineered 293T cell membranes (EM-CD276) to improve biocompatibility and in vivo stability and to enhance accumulation in CD276-positive tumor tissues. As the carrier core, we used hollow MnO₂ (hMnO₂), which can undergo tumor-microenvironment-responsive degradation to facilitate cargo release. In addition, under acidic and H₂O₂-rich conditions, hMnO₂ may catalyze endogenous H₂O₂ decomposition to generate O₂, and its reduction/decomposition process may also consume intracellular glutathione (GSH). Therefore, besides serving as a responsive carrier, hMnO₂ may provide an auxiliary tumor-microenvironment-modulating effect [35, 36] (Scheme 1).

After intravenous administration, the nanoparticles preferentially accumulate in CD276-positive Wilms’ tumor tissue and are internalized by tumor cells. Intracellularly, released BAY-876 inhibits GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake, thereby reducing pentose phosphate pathway (PPP)-derived NADPH production, whereas delivered cystine further increases the demand for intracellular reducing equivalents during its reduction to cysteine. This dual metabolic disruption induces disulfide stress and ROS accumulation, leading to F-actin cytoskeletal collapse and ultimately inducing disulfidptosis

In this study, we engineered a biomimetic nanoplatform, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, designed to promote disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal injury in CD276-positive WT through combined “glucose-dependent NADPH regeneration restriction” and “cystine/disulfide burden escalation.” We systematically evaluated tumor targeting, F-actin cytoskeletal disruption, redox imbalance, and mechanistic evidence of disulfide stress at molecular, cellular, and animal levels, and further validated its inhibitory effects on orthotopic WT growth and pulmonary metastasis. This work provides experimental support for optimizing WT therapy through a biomimetic dual metabolic intervention strategy with improved tumor exposure and reduced systemic toxicity.

Materials and methods

Primary antibodies used for Western blotting included anti-FLNA (Cat A3738, ABclonal Technology, Wuhan, China), anti-MYH9 (Cat A0173, ABclonal Technology, Wuhan, China), anti-Drebrin (Cat 10260-1-AP, Proteintech Group, Wuhan, China), anti-SLC7A11 (Cat 12691, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti-CD276 (Cat HA721245, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), anti-GLUT1 (T55360, Abmart, Shanghai, China), anti-GLUT2 (WL03363, Wanleibio, Shenyang, China), anti-GLUT3 (WL05064, Wanleibio, Shenyang, China), and anti-GLUT4 (R1402-3, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China). The NADP⁺/NADPH colorimetric assay kit (WST-8 method) was purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Cell culture

The WiT49 cell line was obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and the WT-CLS1 cell line was provided by the Capital Institute of Pediatrics (Beijing, China). WiT49, WT-CLS1, and HEK293T cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For experiments under glucose-free or cystine-deficient conditions, cells were switched to the corresponding customized media as indicated. For the cellular uptake comparison, HUVECs and HK-2 cells were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) and cultured in the recommended RAW264.7-specific complete medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical samples

Clinical specimens were obtained from patients with Wilms' tumor who underwent surgical resection at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all patients prior to sample collection. This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. (2022) Ethics Review (Research) No. 170).

Construction of the engineered cell line and lentiviral transduction

Construction and packaging of the lentiviral vectors were outsourced to GENE (Shanghai, China). The VH and VL sequences of the anti-CD276 (B7-H3) single-chain variable fragment (scFv) were retrieved from the variable-region sequences of the anti–B7-H3 antibody NP040 disclosed in patent CN120209147A (VH: SEQ ID NO: 1; VL: SEQ ID NO: 5; corresponding nucleotide sequences: SEQ ID NO: 19/20) and assembled into a VH-linker-VL scFv format (CN120209147A) [37].

The scFv sequence was cloned into a lentiviral vector to generate a membrane-anchored fusion protein driven by the CMV promoter. From the N-terminus to the C-terminus, the construct comprised an HA tag, the scFv, a Myc tag, a (GGGGS)_2 flexible linker, and the DAF (CD55) GPI-anchoring signal sequence, enabling stable display on the cell membrane and facilitating subsequent detection; this DAF–GPI anchoring strategy was based on previous reports [38].

After viral packaging, HEK293T cells were transduced with lentiviral particles according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, lentiviral particles and transduction reagents were added to 1 × 106 HEK293T cells and cultured for 72 h in DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Cells were then subjected to antibiotic selection in complete medium containing puromycin to obtain stably anti-CD276 scFv-expressing cell populations. Finally, expression of the scFv transcript and the fusion protein was confirmed by qRT–PCR (scFv-specific primers) and Western blotting (anti-HA antibody), respectively.

Cell viability assay (CCK-8)

Cell viability was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Cat K1018, APExBIO Technology LLC, Houston, TX, USA). WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 10³ cells per well. After attachment, cells were treated according to the experimental design. At the end of treatment, CCK-8 working solution (CCK-8: medium = 1:9, v/v) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm (OD450) was measured using a Multiskan GO microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA), and relative cell viability was calculated.

Equal amounts of RNA were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a first-strand cDNA synthesis kit (Takara, Japan). mRNA expression levels were measured using a qRT-PCR detection kit (ABclonal). Reducing and non-reducing Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (RIPA: Beyotime, Shanghai, China; protease inhibitors: MCE, USA). Lysates were briefly sonicated to reduce viscosity and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein were loaded for electrophoresis. For reducing conditions, samples were mixed with 5× Laemmli sample buffer containing a reducing agent and denatured at 95 °C for 10 min. For non-reducing conditions, samples were mixed with non-reducing loading buffer (without DTT or β-mercaptoethanol), and exposure to air was minimized. Proteins were separated by 6% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with RapidBlock™ (ABclonal) for 10 min at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using an ECL kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), imaged using a GelDoc XR+ system (Bio-Rad, USA), and quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad).

Intracellular glucose content assay

Intracellular glucose content was measured using a glucose assay kit with o-toluidine (S0201S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁶ cells per well. After 24 h of incubation, cells were assigned to the Control, Bay, G4, G5, or G6 for 24 h. Bay denotes BAY-876. For nanoparticle formulations, Bay-equivalent concentrations were used. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS, collected, and lysed using the lysis buffer provided in the kit. The lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for glucose measurement. Glucose standards were prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and glucose content was calculated based on the standard curve. Absorbance was measured at 630 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax). Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit, and glucose content was normalized to total protein concentration and expressed as µg/mg protein. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Preparation of engineered cell membrane vesicles (EM-CD276)

Engineered cell membrane vesicles (EM-CD276) were prepared using membrane extraction followed by, sequential extrusion and ultracentrifugation. Briefly, anti-CD276 scFv-expressing HEK293T cells (HEK293T-anti-CD276 scFv) were collected, resuspended in pre-chilled PBS, and washed 2–3 times. Cells were then resuspended in hypotonic lysis buffer (supplemented with protease inhibitors) and incubated on ice for 30 min, followed by mechanical homogenization. Differential centrifugation was performed to remove nuclei and unbroken cells/large debris (3,000 × g and 10,000 × g at 4 °C; centrifugation times were optimized in pilot experiments). The membrane-containing supernatant was extruded using a mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL, USA) through polycarbonate membranes with decreasing pore sizes (10 μm → 5 μm → 1 μm; at least 3 passes per membrane). The extruded suspension was then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 60 min at 4 ℃ to pellet the membrane vesicles. The pellet was resuspended in PBS to obtain uniform engineered membrane vesicles (EM-CD276). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay, and aliquots were stored at −80 °C (avoiding repeated freeze–thaw cycles).

Non-targeted membrane vesicles (EM) were prepared from wild-type HEK293T cells as described above. Exosomes (Exos) were isolated from HEK293T culture supernatants by differential centrifugation and ultracentrifugation. Supernatants were sequentially centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, 2,000 × g for 10 min, and 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, transferred to ultracentrifuge tubes, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C (Beckman Optima L-80XP). The pellet was resuspended in PBS, washed by a second ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4 °C, resuspended in PBS, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C.

Synthesis of (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 biomimetic nanocomplexes: CTAB (0.4 g) was dissolved in 10 mL deionized water, followed by addition of triethylamine (100 µL) and adjustment of pH to 9.5. The mixture was stirred at 95 °C for 30 min. TEOS (1.2 mL) was then added dropwise, and the reaction was continued for 4 h. The product was collected by centrifugation and washed thoroughly with ethanol/water to obtain solid mesoporous silica nanospheres (sSiO₂). Next, 400 mg sSiO₂ was ultrasonically dispersed in 50 mL deionized water, and KMnO₄ (10 mg) was added. After ultrasonication for 1 h, the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 6 h to allow in situ growth of MnO₂ on the silica surface. The particles were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 24 mL Na₂CO₃ solution (2 mol/L), stirred at 60 °C for 3 h, and then reacted with 3% NaHCO₃ solution for an additional 10 h to etch and remove the internal silica template. The resulting hollow MnO₂ nanospheres (hMnO₂) were washed repeatedly with deionized water until neutral pH was reached.

To improve drug loading and dispersion stability, 200 mg hMnO₂ was mixed with 5 mL PEI solution (0.5 mg/mL) and stirred at room temperature for 2 h for surface modification. For simplicity and consistency in nomenclature, hMnO₂ in the abbreviated formulation names refers to the PEI-modified hollow MnO₂ core used for drug loading and membrane coating. After removing free PEI by centrifugation, the particles were resuspended in PBS and incubated with L-cystine (20 mg) and BAY-876 (0.5 mL, 2.5 mg/mL in DMSO/PBS; the DMSO fraction was controlled within an acceptable range) under stirring at 4 °C in the dark for 24 h to facilitate drug loading. Unencapsulated drugs were removed by centrifugation and dialysis, yielding the co-loaded core (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂.

For membrane coating, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂ and EM-CD276 vesicles were mixed at a mass ratio of core to membrane protein of 1:1, followed by brief sonication in an ice bath (~ 5 min). The mixture was extruded sequentially through 400 nm and 200 nm polycarbonate membranes (10 passes each) to obtain (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276. Visible aggregates were removed by low-speed centrifugation (2,000 × g), and the product was stored at 4 °C. Control formulations (Bay@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, Cys@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and the non-targeted (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM) were prepared using the same procedure, except that the corresponding cargo was omitted or EM-CD276 was replaced with membrane vesicles derived from wild-type HEK293T cells (EM).

F-actin cytoskeleton staining

Cytoskeletal F-actin was visualized using rhodamine–phalloidin (Cat 16002, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, Chengdu, China). Cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates and treated according to the experimental design. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA in PBS, pH 7.4) for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min at room temperature, and blocked with 0.5% BSA for 30 min. Cells were stained with rhodamine–phalloidin (1:100 in PBS) for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, followed by nuclear staining with DAPI (Solarbio, China) for 10 min. Samples were mounted using an antifade mounting medium (Solarbio, China) and kept protected from light until imaging. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ti2 fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

2-NBDG glucose uptake assay

Glucose uptake was assessed using the fluorescent glucose analog 2-NBDG (MC4006, Meilun Bio, Dalian, China). Briefly, WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates and allowed to attach overnight. Cells were then treated with BAY-876 (Bay) at the working concentration used in the mechanistic experiments for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with freshly prepared 2-NBDG working solution (100 µM in glucose-free serum-free medium) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular 2-NBDG. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were acquired immediately using a fluorescence microscope. 2-NBDG fluorescence was detected using the FITC channel. Mean 2-NBDG fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ and expressed relative to the control group.

Cellular uptake assay

(Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 was fluorescently labeled with the lipophilic dye DiL (Beyotime, China). WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured in complete medium for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with Dil-labeled (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 for 4 h. After incubation, cells were washed gently with PBS three times to remove non-internalized nanoparticles. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with rhodamine–phalloidin for F-actin (20 min), and counterstained with DAPI (1 µg/mL, 10 min). Intracellular fluorescence distribution was observed using a confocal microscope. For comparative uptake analysis, EM and EM-CD276 vesicles were labeled with DiO and incubated with WiT49, WT-CLS1, HK-2, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells for 4 h. Fluorescence images were acquired under identical settings, and mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The size distribution and particle concentration of membrane vesicles or extracellular vesicle samples were measured by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) using a NanoSight system. Samples were diluted in PBS to an appropriate concentration before measurement. Each sample was recorded under identical acquisition settings, and the data were analyzed using the corresponding NTA software. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Dynamic light scattering and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of the nanocomplexes (Cys@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, Bay@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM) were measured using a Brookhaven Instruments dynamic light scattering analyzer. Samples were dispersed in deionized water to an appropriate concentration and equilibrated at 25 °C before measurement. Each sample was measured at least three times, and the results were averaged.

HPLC quantification of cystine and BAY-876

Cystine content: To quantify cystine loading, nanocomplexes (Cys@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM) were mixed with 9 volumes of methanol and sonicated for 10 min to promote cargo release. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected for derivatization. Cystine was quantified using pre-column derivatization with Fmoc-OSu. Briefly, 200 µL supernatant was mixed with 200 µL borate buffer (0.2 M, pH 8.5), followed by addition of 1.0 mL Fmoc-OSu solution (4 mg/mL in acetonitrile). The mixture was incubated at 40 °C for 1 h in the dark. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 5 min), the supernatant was analyzed on an Agilent 1260 HPLC system with a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at 35 °C. The mobile phase was methanol/0.1% formic acid in water (70:30, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min. Detection was performed at 300 nm with an injection volume of 20 µL. A cystine standard curve processed identically was used for quantification.

BAY-876 content: To quantify BAY-876, purified nanocomplex dispersions (Bay@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM) were mixed with 10 volumes of methanol and sonicated at high power for 15 min to facilitate drug release. After centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter and analyzed by HPLC (Agilent 1260) using a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) at 30 °C. Isocratic elution was performed with methanol/ultrapure water containing 0.1% formic acid (85:15, v/v) at 1.0 mL/min. UV detection was set at 295 nm with an injection volume of 20 µL. BAY-876 content was calculated using a standard calibration curve generated under the same conditions.

In vitro release study

A dialysis method was used to evaluate the release of L-cystine and BAY-876 under simulated physiological and tumor-microenvironment conditions. Two milliliters of nanocomplex dispersion was loaded into a dialysis bag (MWCO 3500 Da) and placed in 20 mL release medium at 37 °C with magnetic stirring (100 rpm). Three release conditions were used: (1) PBS (pH 7.4); (2) MES buffer (pH 6.5) supplemented with 10 mM reduced glutathione (GSH); and (3) acetate buffer (pH 5.5) supplemented with 10 mM GSH. At predetermined time points (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h), 50 µL of release medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of pre-warmed fresh medium to maintain a constant volume. L-cystine and BAY-876 in the samples were quantified by HPLC using their respective methods, and cumulative release profiles were calculated with correction for dilution.

In vitro oxygen generation kinetics

Dissolved oxygen (DO) was monitored using a portable dissolved oxygen meter (JPSJ-605 F, Shanghai Yitian Precision Instruments) to evaluate oxygen generation catalyzed by the nanocomplex in the presence of H₂O₂. Experiments were conducted in two buffer systems (20 mL each): PBS (pH 7.4) and acetate buffer (pH 5.5). After the probe reading stabilized, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 nanoparticles (NPs) were added to a final concentration of 100 µg/mL. The system was maintained at 37 °C under magnetic stirring (100 rpm), and DO values were recorded. Two controls were included: an “NPs only” group (without H₂O₂) and an “H₂O₂ only” group (without NPs). DO values were recorded at predetermined time points (0, 2, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 min), and oxygen generation kinetic curves were plotted.

XPS sample preparation

For X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), samples were prepared as pressed pellets. Briefly, 5–10 mg CyshMnO₂-PEI powder was ground in an agate mortar (cleaned with anhydrous ethanol) for 15–20 min to ensure homogeneity. The powder was transferred to a pellet die, pre-pressed at 1 MPa for 30 s, and then pressed at 8–10 MPa for 2 min to form a dense pellet. The pellet was dried in a vacuum oven (25 °C, 0.1 mbar) for 2 h and mounted onto the XPS sample holder using conductive carbon tape to ensure good electrical contact. No sputter coating was applied, and samples were analyzed directly.

TEM characterization and EDS elemental mapping

For TEM imaging of morphology, Cys@hMnO₂, Cys@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, Bay@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM were dispersed in deionized water (0.1 mg/mL) and sonicated. A small aliquot was dropped onto pretreated copper grids. After 60 s adsorption, excess liquid was removed with filter paper. Samples were negatively stained with 2.5% phosphotungstic acid (pH 6.8) and imaged by TEM at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV to visualize the membrane/vesicle structures.

For elemental composition and distribution analysis, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 was prepared on ethanol-cleaned ultrathin carbon-coated copper grids. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) imaging and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping were performed using a field-emission TEM equipped with EDS (FEI Talos F200S) at 200 kV. Mapping was performed for Mn, O, C, N, S, and Si to qualitatively assess elemental distribution within the nanocomplex.

Animal models and housing

All animal protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. CHCMU-IACUC20220323001) and conducted in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines for animal care and use. Male NOD/SCID mice (6 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at 21 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 10% relative humidity with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with ad libitum access to irradiated chow and sterile water. Mice were acclimated for at least 1 week before tumor inoculation.

Orthotopic renal tumor model

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the left renal area was shaved and disinfected. A small incision was made in the left flank to expose the kidney. WiT49 cells (1 × 10⁶) suspended in an appropriate volume of DMEM were slowly injected into the upper pole of the left kidney. After injection, the site was gently pressed to prevent leakage, the kidney was repositioned, and the incision was closed in layers. Mice were allowed to recover on a warming pad and monitored postoperatively with analgesic care as required.

Tumor establishment was confirmed by bioluminescence imaging (BLI) on day 12 post-implantation. Mice were then randomized into six groups: Control (PBS), EM-CD276, Cys@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, Bay@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, and (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM. Treatments were administered via tail vein injection at the predefined dose every 48 h until the experimental endpoint. Tumor progression was monitored by in vivo imaging, and body weight and general condition were recorded.

At the endpoint, mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation, and cervical dislocation was performed if necessary to confirm death. Tumors and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung) were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for histological analyses. Paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to evaluate tumor morphology. Serum biochemical indices (ALT, AST, CK, CREA, and UREA) were measured by the clinical laboratory of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. An independent cohort was used for survival analysis and was treated according to the same grouping and dosing schedule, with survival monitored longitudinally. All procedures followed the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and local animal welfare regulations.

Lung metastasis model

Luciferase-expressing WiT49 cells (5 × 10⁵) were resuspended in 100 µL DMEM and injected via the tail vein to establish a lung metastasis model. Starting on day 7 post-injection, D-luciferin potassium salt (150 mg/kg; Meilunbio, China) was administered intraperitoneally every 2 days, and bioluminescence imaging (BLI) was performed using an IVIS Spectrum system to monitor pulmonary metastatic burden. Lung metastasis was confirmed by BLI on day 14 post-injection, after which treatment was initiated according to the predefined grouping and dosing schedule. BLI monitoring was continued to track changes in signal intensity. At the experimental endpoint, lungs and major organs were collected for further analyses. Lungs were excised, rinsed with PBS, and photographed under consistent conditions. Visible metastatic nodules on the lung surface were counted and expressed as nodule number per lung.

Intracellular Cys-equivalent quantification

Intracellular Cys-equivalent levels were estimated using a cysteine assay kit (BC0180, Solarbio, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells were treated with G1–G6 for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed three times with cold PBS and lysed using the extraction buffer provided in the kit. Cell lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for analysis. Absorbance was measured at 600 nm using a microplate reader, and Cys-equivalent concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve. Total protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit, and the results were normalized to total protein concentration and expressed as µg/mg protein. Reagent blanks and nanoparticle background controls were included to minimize potential optical interference. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Determination of GSH/GSSG in cells and tissues

A GSH/GSSG assay kit (Cat S0053, Beyotime, China) was used to quantify glutathione levels in treated Wilms tumor cells and mouse kidney tissues. Cell samples were washed with PBS and lysed on ice using the protein removal reagent M. Kidney tissues (10–30 mg) were homogenized on ice in protein removal reagent M. Lysates/homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for analysis.

Total glutathione was measured using the DTNB/glutathione reductase recycling method by reading absorbance at 412 nm and calculating concentrations based on a standard curve (Total = GSH + 2 × GSSG). For GSSG measurement, an aliquot of the supernatant was incubated with a GSH scavenger at 25 °C for 60 min to remove reduced GSH and then measured using the same method. Reduced GSH was calculated as: GSH = Total glutathione − 2 × GSSG. Cell results were normalized to protein content determined by BCA (nmol/mg protein), and tissue results were normalized to wet weight (µmol/g tissue). Reagent blanks and nanoparticle background controls were included to minimize potential optical interference.

In vitro GSH consumption assay

The GSH-consuming capability of hMnO₂-based nanoparticles was evaluated using a GSH/GSSG assay kit (Cat. S0053, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Briefly, GSH solution (10 mM) was incubated with hMnO₂ or G5, namely (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, in PBS at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, nanoparticles were removed by centrifugation, and residual GSH in the supernatant was measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The GSH-alone group was used as the control and set to 100%. Reagent blanks and nanoparticle background controls were included to minimize potential optical interference. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Measurement of ROS in cells and tissues (DCFH-DA)

Cellular ROS: Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA. After treatment, cells were washed twice with pre-warmed PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 µM) in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. Cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular probe. Nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342 (2 µg/mL, 10 min). Fluorescence images were acquired immediately using a fluorescence microscope.

Tissue ROS: Fresh tumor tissues were collected at the end of animal experiments, embedded in OCT, and sectioned (8–10 μm). Sections were briefly rinsed with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA working solution (10 µM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark in a humidified chamber. After PBS washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI and mounted. Images were acquired using identical imaging settings to evaluate ROS levels in tumor tissues.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v10.0.2. Two-group comparisons were conducted using two-tailed Student’s t-test. Nonparametric two-group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test where appropriate. Multiple-group endpoint comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Time-course data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA followed by multiple-comparison tests as appropriate. Survival curves were generated using the R survival package and visualized using survminer, and differences in overall survival were assessed by the log-rank test. A two-sided α of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance notation in figures: ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Validation of CD276 expression and characterization of engineered biomimetic membrane vesicles

Validation of CD276 expression and characterization of engineered biomimetic membrane vesicles

To identify a suitable drug-delivery target for Wilms' tumor (WT), we first evaluated CD276 (B7-H3) expression in clinical specimens. Immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed markedly stronger CD276 immunoreactivity in WT tissues than in adjacent non-tumor kidney tissues. Semi-quantitative H-score analysis further confirmed significantly elevated CD276 protein levels in tumor tissues (Fig. 1B), indicating that CD276 may serve as a promising anchor for tumor-associated delivery. Previous studies have shown that B7-H3/CD276 is broadly overexpressed across pediatric solid tumors and represents an attractive target for therapeutic and delivery applications. Majzner et al. reported B7-H3-targeting CAR-T cells and demonstrated robust antitumor activity in pediatric solid tumor models, supporting CD276 as a tumor antigen amenable to specific recognition [39]. In addition, Kendsersky et al. reported that a B7-H3-targeting antibody–drug conjugate (ADC), m276-SL-PBD, exhibited pronounced efficacy in multiple pediatric solid tumor preclinical models, further highlighting the developability of CD276 as a delivery-relevant target [33].

Next, we generated an engineered cell line stably displaying an anti-CD276 single-chain variable fragment (scFv) on the cell membrane, designated 293T-CD276, referring to anti-CD276 scFv-expressing 293T cells. Western blotting detected a specific band corresponding to the HA-tagged fusion protein at the expected molecular weight in engineered cells (Fig. 1C), and qRT-PCR confirmed stable expression of the scFv transcript (Fig. 1D), collectively verifying successful establishment of the engineered cell line.

Based on this platform, engineered membrane vesicles (EM-CD276) were prepared from 293T-CD276 cells using membrane extraction, purification, and sequential extrusion. Membrane vesicles derived from parental 293T cells (EM) and native exosomes (Exos) were prepared as controls (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed that all three preparations exhibited nanoscale vesicular morphology (Fig. 1E). NTA indicated peak diameters of 131.7 nm for Exos, 147.1 nm for EM, and 161.8 nm for EM-CD276 (Fig. 1E), and comparative size analysis showed that EM-CD276 was slightly larger than EM and Exos (Fig. 1F). All three preparations displayed negative ζ-potentials, with EM-CD276 measuring approximately − 6 mV (Fig. 1G). Protein profiling further showed that EV-associated proteins, including Alix, TSG101, and CD63, were detectable in Exos, EM, and EM-CD276 (Fig. 1H). Importantly, the HA-tagged anti-CD276 scFv was markedly enriched in EM-CD276 but was nearly undetectable in EM (Fig. 1I). These results confirm the successful generation of CD276-targeting engineered biomimetic membrane vesicles, providing a foundation for subsequent targeted delivery studies. Because the proposed therapeutic mechanism relies on cystine/disulfide metabolism, we further examined SLC7A11 expression in WT specimens. IHC staining showed stronger SLC7A11 immunoreactivity and significantly higher H-scores in WT tissues than in adjacent normal kidney tissues (Figure S1). Western blot analysis further corroborated the upregulation of SLC7A11 in WT tissues (Figure S2).

2. Construction and physicochemical characterization of the dual-drug biomimetic nanoparticles

Construction and physicochemical characterization of the dual-drug biomimetic nanoparticles

We first validated the context-dependent metabolic effects of L-cystine in Wilms’ tumor (WT) cells. Under glucose-replete conditions, exogenous L-cystine had minimal impact on the viability of WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells. In contrast, under glucose-deprived conditions, L-cystine supplementation, particularly at higher concentrations, significantly reduced cell viability and induced pronounced F-actin cytoskeletal contraction and rearrangement (Figure S3A–C). Consistent with these morphological changes, non-reducing Western blotting revealed high-molecular-weight aggregation/smearing of FLNA and MYH9, which was attenuated under reducing conditions, indicating disulfide bond-mediated aberrant crosslinking (Figure S3D). Next, we established a “limited glucose supply” metabolic context using the GLUT1 inhibitor BAY-876. BAY-876 showed dose-dependent cytotoxicity in both WT cell lines, with IC50 values of 0.679 µM in WiT49 cells and 1.049 µM in WT-CLS1 cells (Figure S4A). When combined with L-cystine, BAY-876 further decreased cell viability (Figure S4B), increased the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio (Figure S4C), and enhanced non-reducing aggregation signals of MYH9 and Drebrin, accompanied by more severe F-actin disruption (Figure S4D, E). Collectively, these results suggest that BAY-876 and L-cystine jointly amplify disulfide stress and redox imbalance through a “restricted supply + increased load” mechanism, thereby facilitating disulfide-linked cytoskeletal damage.

To enable efficient delivery of this “dual metabolic intervention” strategy, we constructed a biomimetic engineered nanoplatform, abbreviated as (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276. In this nomenclature, hMnO₂ refers to the PEI-modified hollow MnO₂ core used for Bay/Cys loading before membrane coating. TEM showed that the hMnO₂ core was spherical with a hollow structure; after coating with membrane vesicles, a continuous pale shell appeared at the particle periphery, displaying a core–shell morphology and indicating successful membrane assembly (Fig. 2A). HAADF-STEM and elemental mapping demonstrated strong Mn and O signals within the particles, with C and N signals matching the particle contour; an S signal was detectable, providing qualitative support for the presence of sulfur-containing components (Fig. 2B). XPS survey spectra showed characteristic Mn 2p, N 1s, and S 2p signals (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, high-resolution Mn 2p XPS deconvolution suggested the coexistence of Mn(III) and Mn(IV) species in the hMnO₂ nanoplatform (Fig. 2D). The peaks centered at approximately 653.29 eV and 641.65 eV were assigned to Mn(III), whereas those at approximately 654.08 eV and 642.60 eV were assigned to Mn(IV). Based on the summed fitted peak areas of the corresponding Mn 2p components, Mn(III) and Mn(IV) accounted for approximately 65.2% and 34.8%, respectively. In addition, HPLC chromatograms showed that the peaks in extracts of (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 matched the retention times of the BAY-876 and L-cystine standards, whereas no corresponding peaks were detected in blank nanoparticles, confirming successful co-loading of both cargos (Fig. 2E). Physicochemical characterization showed that the nanoparticles across formulations were approximately 200 nm in hydrodynamic diameter by DLS (Fig. 2F). Zeta potential measurements indicated that membrane coating shifted the surface charge toward the negative values characteristic of cell membranes (Fig. 2G), further supporting successful coating. For Cys-containing formulations, quantification showed appreciable L-cystine loading capacity (Fig. 2H) and high L-cystine encapsulation efficiency (Fig. 2I), confirming successful incorporation of the disulfide-burden component into the hMnO₂-based nanoplatform. Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), membrane-coated particles markedly suppressed the release of L-cystine and BAY-876 compared with uncoated cores (Figure S5), indicating that the membrane shell acts as a physical barrier to reduce premature leakage during circulation. Under acidic/reducing conditions, both L-cystine and BAY-876 exhibited accelerated release in a pH- and GSH-dependent manner (Fig. 2J, K), supporting tumor-microenvironment-responsive cargo release. Moreover, in the presence of H₂O₂, the nanoplatform continuously increased dissolved oxygen levels, with more pronounced oxygen generation at pH 5.5 than at pH 7.4 (Fig. 2L), indicating catalytic H₂O₂-to-O₂ activity. This oxygen generation can be attributed to the reaction between MnO₂ and H₂O₂ under acidic conditions: MnO₂ + H₂O₂ + 2H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + O₂ + 2H₂O. These results indicate that the hMnO₂ core retains H₂O₂-responsive oxygen-generating activity and may contribute to tumor-microenvironment modulation.

To further evaluate whether the hMnO₂ core could contribute to redox modulation beyond serving as a carrier, we performed a cell-free residual GSH assay. Compared with the GSH-alone control, hMnO₂ markedly reduced residual GSH levels, supporting the intrinsic GSH-consuming capability of the MnO₂ core. G5 also significantly decreased residual GSH levels, indicating that the final formulation retained this GSH-responsive redox-modulating activity (Figure S6). Finally, Dil-labeled nanoparticles were incubated with WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells; confocal imaging showed punctate cytoplasmic red fluorescence enriched in the perinuclear region (Figure S7), suggesting efficient cellular uptake and supporting subsequent intracellular delivery and mechanistic studies. To preliminarily evaluate the potential off-target concern of CD276-guided delivery, we compared the uptake of DiO-labeled EM and EM-CD276 in WT cells and representative non-malignant cells. EM-CD276 significantly enhanced uptake in WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells, whereas no comparable enhancement was observed in HK-2, HUVECs, or RAW264.7 cells under the same conditions (Figure S8). These results suggest a relative WT-associated delivery preference of EM-CD276, although they do not indicate absolute tumor specificity.

3. (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 enhances cytotoxicity and induces disulfidptosis-consistent cytoskeletal injury in vitro

(Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 enhances cytotoxicity and induces disulfidptosis-consistent cytoskeletal injury in vitro

Before evaluating the full nanoplatform, we first verified whether BAY-876 alone could functionally establish a glucose supply-restricted state in WT cells at the working dose used in this study. BAY-876 alone significantly increased the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio in both WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells, indicating impaired NADPH availability and disrupted redox balance (Figure S9). We next measured intracellular glucose content using an o-toluidine-based glucose assay. BAY-876 markedly reduced intracellular glucose content in both WT cell lines, and Bay-containing formulations, including G4, G5, and G6, also decreased intracellular glucose content under Bay-equivalent conditions (Figure S10). In addition, 2-NBDG fluorescence imaging further confirmed that BAY-876 treatment reduced glucose uptake in both WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells (Figure S11). To further assess whether WT cells could compensate for GLUT1 inhibition through alternative glucose transporters, we examined GLUT1, GLUT2, GLUT3, and GLUT4 expression after Bay or G5 treatment. GLUT1 expression was reduced after Bay or G5 treatment, supporting suppression of the GLUT1-mediated glucose uptake axis. GLUT2 was increased and GLUT3 showed mild or condition-dependent changes, suggesting partial adaptive transporter remodeling. However, GLUT4 did not show compensatory upregulation. Importantly, despite these adaptive changes, glucose uptake, intracellular glucose content, and NADPH availability remained reduced, indicating that alternative GLUT adaptation was insufficient to functionally bypass Bay-mediated glucose restriction (Figure S12). Together, these results demonstrate that BAY-876 reduces intracellular glucose availability, restricts glucose uptake, and impairs NADPH availability in WT cells, thereby supporting its role as the “supply restriction” component of our dual metabolic intervention strategy.

Prior to mechanistic studies, we evaluated the antiproliferative potency of the complete nanocomplex (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 in WT cells. CCK-8 assays showed clear dose-dependent cytotoxicity in both WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells, with IC50 values of 118.5 nM and 290.3 nM, respectively (Figure S13). Compared with free BAY-876, which showed IC50 values of 0.679 µM in WiT49 cells and 1.049 µM in WT-CLS1 cells (Figure S4A), the nanoformulation exhibited markedly enhanced in vitro potency. Unless otherwise indicated, subsequent mechanistic experiments were performed at 2 × IC50 of the full-featured nanocomplex. Across treatment groups (G1–G6), PBS (G1) and blank vesicles (G2) showed negligible effects on cell viability. The Cys-loaded formulation (G3) produced only limited cytotoxicity, whereas the Bay-loaded formulation (G4) significantly reduced cell viability. Notably, the dual-cargo full-featured formulation G5, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, achieved the strongest cytotoxicity and outperformed the non-targeted counterpart G6, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM (Fig. 3A). These results support the contribution of both Bay/Cys co-delivery and CD276-guided membrane coating to enhanced in vitro antitumor efficacy.

To further evaluate the “burden escalation” component, intracellular Cys-equivalent levels were estimated using a colorimetric cysteine assay and normalized to total protein concentration. G5 induced the highest intracellular Cys-equivalent level in both WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells. In particular, Cys-equivalent levels in the G5 group were significantly higher than the Bay-only G4 group and the non-targeted G6 group, indicating that Cys cargo loading and CD276-guided delivery enhanced intracellular cystine/disulfide-related burden (Figure S14). These results support the “load increase” component of the dual metabolic intervention strategy. Consistently, G5 treatment markedly increased the NADP⁺/NADPH ratio and decreased the GSH/GSSG ratio (Fig. 3B, G), indicating pronounced redox disruption. Under non-reducing conditions, Western blotting revealed more prominent high-molecular-weight smearing/aggregation of cytoskeletal proteins FLNA and Drebrin in the G5 group (Fig. 3C), accompanied by severe F-actin contraction and collapse by phalloidin staining (Fig. 3D), which is consistent with disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal injury. In parallel, DCFH-DA imaging and quantification showed the highest ROS levels in G5 (Fig. 3E, F). Together, these results indicate that (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276 amplifies redox imbalance and cytoskeletal injury, resulting in enhanced in vitro cytotoxicity. To further clarify whether G5-induced cytotoxicity was predominantly dependent on disulfide stress, we performed inhibitor rescue assays using TCEP, a disulfide-reducing agent, together with Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor, and Z-VAD-FMK, a pan-caspase inhibitor. In both WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells, TCEP significantly restored G5-induced loss of cell viability, whereas Fer-1 and Z-VAD-FMK showed no obvious protective effects (Figure S15A). Consistently, phalloidin staining showed that G5 induced pronounced F-actin contraction and cytoskeletal collapse, which were markedly alleviated by TCEP but not obviously reversed by Fer-1 or Z-VAD-FMK (Figure S15B). Moreover, non-reducing Western blotting demonstrated that G5 induced high-molecular-weight aggregation/smearing of FLNA, MYH9, and Drebrin, whereas these aggregation signals were reduced after TCEP treatment (Figure S15C). These rescue results further support that G5-induced cytotoxicity is mainly associated with disulfide bond-dependent aggregation of cytoskeleton-associated proteins and disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal collapse, rather than ferroptosis or caspase-dependent apoptosis as the dominant mechanism.

Tumor-bearing kidneys from the G5 group were visibly smaller and had the lowest weights among all groups (Fig. 4E, F). H&E staining revealed extensive tumor infiltration and architectural disruption of renal tissue in the control groups, whereas the tumor area was markedly reduced in the G5 group, with relatively better preservation of renal structure (Fig. 4H).

Notably, the antitumor effect of the targeted co-loaded group G5 exceeded that of the non-targeted co-loaded control G6, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM, suggesting that CD276-guided membrane coating contributed to improved in vivo therapeutic benefit. Survival analysis showed significantly prolonged survival in the G5 group (Fig. 4D). Meanwhile, body weight remained generally stable during treatment, with no obvious weight loss across groups (Fig. 4G), indicating acceptable overall tolerability of this dosing regimen.

To assess the in vivo mechanism, tumor tissues were further analyzed. Fluorescence staining showed enhanced ROS fluorescence signals in tumors from the G5 group (Fig. 4J). Importantly, non-reducing Western blotting of tumor lysates revealed more pronounced high-molecular-weight smearing/aggregation of MYH9 and Drebrin in the G5 group (Fig. 4I), consistent with disulfide bond-mediated aberrant crosslinking of cytoskeleton-associated proteins. Together with the increased intratumoral ROS signals and the in vitro phalloidin/rescue evidence, these findings provide supportive in vivo molecular evidence for disulfide stress-associated cytoskeletal injury. In addition, PCNA immunofluorescence staining of tumor sections showed that, compared with the PBS and EM-CD276 control groups, the CD276-targeted co-loaded group G5 exhibited markedly reduced PCNA-positive signals, indicating more effective suppression of tumor cell proliferation in vivo; this reduction was less pronounced in the non-targeted co-loaded group G6 (Figure S16).

Discussion

High-risk Wilms' tumor (WT), particularly cases with pulmonary metastasis or anaplastic histology, remains difficult to treat because further therapeutic intensification is limited by systemic toxicity and suboptimal responses in high-risk disease [40, 41]. In this study, we explored a metabolic vulnerability of WT and developed a CD276-guided biomimetic nanoplatform, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, to implement a dual metabolic intervention that couples glucose-uptake blockade with cystine/disulfide burden escalation. Rather than relying on a single metabolic perturbation, this strategy was designed to simultaneously restrict reducing-power supply and increase intracellular cystine/disulfide burden, thereby creating a redox state that is difficult for tumor cells to compensate for.

At the mechanistic level, this study focuses on disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal injury as a central entry point. Gan and colleagues proposed disulfidptosis in 2023, showing that, under heightened SLC7A11 activity, restricted glucose availability causes insufficient reducing power, thereby provoking disulfide stress and catastrophic cytoskeletal damage characterized by F-actin collapse [19, 42]. However, direct translational application of this concept faces two major barriers: pronounced tumor heterogeneity in SLC7A11-associated metabolism and the physiological infeasibility of systemic glucose deprivation [43]. To address these limitations, we first confirmed in WT cells that the cytotoxicity of exogenous L-cystine is highly dependent on the metabolic context. We then used Bay (BAY-876), a GLUT1 inhibitor, to establish a glucose-restricted metabolic background [44]. Bay-alone validation further supports its role as the “supply restriction” component by showing reduced intracellular glucose availability, suppressed 2-NBDG uptake, and impaired NADPH availability in both WT cell lines. To further determine whether WT cells could bypass this restriction through alternative glucose transporters, we characterized the GLUT profile after Bay or G5 treatment. GLUT profiling showed that Bay or G5 reduced GLUT1 expression and induced adaptive changes in alternative GLUT transporters, particularly GLUT2 and, to a lesser extent, GLUT3, whereas GLUT4 did not show compensatory upregulation. Importantly, despite this partial transporter adaptation, glucose uptake, intracellular glucose content, and NADPH availability remained reduced, indicating that Bay-mediated glucose restriction was not functionally bypassed under the tested conditions.

The “load increase” component was supported by intracellular Cys-equivalent quantification. G5 produced a higher protein-normalized intracellular cystine/disulfide burden than the Bay-only and non-targeted formulations, suggesting that nanoparticle-mediated L-cystine delivery provides an additional intracellular burden input rather than relying solely on endogenous SLC7A11-mediated cystine transport. Therefore, the antitumor effect of G5 can be interpreted as the result of a “supply restriction–load increase” mode of action: Bay restricts glucose uptake and NADPH availability, whereas nanoparticle-mediated Cys delivery increases cystine/disulfide burden and reducing-equivalent demand. This dual metabolic pressure more robustly amplifies redox imbalance and strengthens disulfide stress-associated cytoskeletal injury phenotypes.

The nomenclature of regulated cell death requires cautious interpretation, particularly because metabolic perturbation, redox imbalance, and ROS accumulation may overlap among different death-associated pathways. In this study, the rescue experiments support a predominantly disulfide stress-driven mechanism. TCEP, a disulfide-reducing agent, significantly rescued G5-induced cell viability loss, alleviated F-actin cytoskeletal disruption, and reduced the non-reducing aggregation/smearing of FLNA, MYH9, and Drebrin. In contrast, Fer-1 and Z-VAD-FMK showed no obvious rescue effects, suggesting that ferroptosis or caspase-dependent apoptosis are unlikely to be the predominant mechanism under our experimental conditions. Nevertheless, we cannot exclude the possibility that additional redox-related or stress-associated processes may contribute to the terminal cytotoxic response. Therefore, we describe the mechanism as “disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal collapse” or “predominantly disulfide stress-driven cytotoxicity” rather than claiming an exclusive cell-death modality.

Recent advances in cancer nanomedicine indicate that antitumor efficacy can be enhanced through coordinated interactions among nanomaterial properties, biological barriers, tumor-cell recognition, tumor-microenvironment responsiveness, immune or metabolic remodeling, and cooperative therapeutic cargos [45–47]. In metastatic tumor models, catalytic or antigen-capturing nanosponges have been used to coordinate lesion penetration, immune-cell modulation, antigen capture, and gene-silencing effects to enhance antitumor immunity [48, 49]. Similarly, cascade-responsive or remotely activated nanoplatforms have been developed to combine tumor-specific activation, antigen capture, cell-death induction, and immune reprogramming for improved tumor therapy [50, 51]. Consistent with these principles, our system integrates CD276-mediated tumor-associated recognition, biomimetic membrane-assisted delivery, hMnO₂-based TME responsiveness, and Bay/Cys-driven dual metabolic intervention to enhance tumor exposure and suppress WT progression.

Delivery efficiency is essential for translating this mechanism. We used CD276/B7-H3 as a targeting anchor and engineered a membrane coating to enhance lesion exposure relative to the non-targeted counterpart [52, 53]. This feature is particularly relevant for pulmonary metastasis, where small and diffusely distributed foci make adequate drug accumulation difficult. The biodistribution patterns and the corresponding therapeutic benefits suggest that CD276-guided biomimetic delivery can improve effective exposure at both primary and metastatic sites. The inclusion of blank EM-CD276 vesicles (G2) helped distinguish the role of the CD276-targeting engineered membrane from that of the metabolic cargos. Because G2 did not show obvious cytotoxicity or NADP⁺/NADPH disturbance under the tested conditions, the glucose/NADPH-related “supply restriction” effect is mainly attributed to Bay-mediated GLUT1 inhibition, whereas EM-CD276 is considered to function primarily as a tumor-targeting and delivery-enhancing module. Nevertheless, broader pathway profiling would be required to fully exclude subtle CD276-associated signaling effects.

CD276/B7-H3 has attracted increasing attention as a therapeutic target in pediatric solid tumors, including CAR-T and antibody-drug conjugate platforms [34]. Nevertheless, CD276 should be regarded as a tumor-associated rather than tumor-exclusive antigen. Its expression may extend beyond malignant cells to stromal, endothelial, epithelial, and immune compartments, raising potential concerns regarding off-target interactions [33, 53]. In this study, comparative uptake showed that EM-CD276 enhanced uptake in WiT49 and WT-CLS1 cells but did not produce comparable uptake enhancement in HK-2 renal epithelial cells, HUVECs, or RAW264.7 macrophage-like cells. These findings support a relative WT-associated delivery preference, but they do not establish absolute tumor specificity. Importantly, the present study was performed under short-term and controlled dosing conditions; off-target uptake and systemic toxicity may become more relevant under larger tumor burden, dose escalation, or prolonged administration. Future studies using primary stromal and immune cells, tumor-associated macrophage-like models, long-term repeated dosing, larger tumor-burden models, and comprehensive biodistribution and toxicity analyses will be necessary to more rigorously assess the translational safety window of this CD276-guided biomimetic delivery strategy.

The hMnO₂ core may provide auxiliary therapeutic support beyond cargo loading. Under acidic and H₂O₂-rich TME conditions, hMnO₂ can catalyze endogenous H₂O₂ decomposition to generate O₂ through the reaction MnO₂ + H₂O₂ + 2 H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + O₂ + 2 H₂O [54–56]. MnO₂-mediated oxygen generation has been widely explored as a strategy to modulate tumor hypoxia in nanomedicine, and tumor hypoxia is closely associated with metabolic adaptation, malignant progression, and therapeutic resistance in solid tumors [57]. Therefore, in our system, the oxygen-generating property of hMnO₂ may provide supportive TME modulation rather than acting as the primary therapeutic mechanism. In addition, the cell-free residual GSH assay showed that hMnO₂-based formulations could directly consume reduced GSH, potentially through the reduction of MnO₂ by GSH to generate Mn²⁺ and GSSG. This GSH-consuming capability may partially contribute to the decreased GSH/GSSG ratio observed in cells. However, because a fully matched dual-cargo-loaded non-responsive nanoparticle control was not included, the independent therapeutic contribution of the MnO₂ core cannot be quantitatively isolated in the present study. Accordingly, hMnO₂ is interpreted primarily as a TME-responsive carrier with supportive redox/microenvironment-modulating properties, whereas the dominant antitumor mechanism is attributed to BAY-876/L-cystine-mediated dual metabolic intervention. Future studies using an appropriately matched non-responsive nanoparticle control would be valuable for more directly dissecting the independent contribution of the MnO₂ core.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, although the rescue experiments support a predominantly disulfide stress-driven mechanism, additional genetic perturbation experiments, such as SLC7A11 modulation or cytoskeleton-associated protein-focused validation, would further strengthen causal attribution to disulfidptosis-associated cytoskeletal injury. Second, although the current data suggest effective Bay-mediated glucose restriction, cancer cells may rewire glucose transporter usage or broader metabolic pathways under prolonged selective pressure; therefore, long-term metabolic adaptation should be evaluated in future studies. Third, pharmacokinetics, long-term safety, repeat-dose tolerability, and batch-to-batch consistency should be examined in more clinically relevant models. Given that doxorubicin-associated cardiotoxicity remains a major limitation in intensified WT therapy, the absence of CK elevation, together with normal cardiac histology, supports the preliminary cardiac tolerability of this doxorubicin-free metabolic intervention strategy under the current dosing regimen. Despite these considerations, the present study outlines a practical design principle: enforcing amplified disulfide stress and redox imbalance through dual metabolic intervention while maximizing lesion exposure through CD276-guided biomimetic delivery. This integrated strategy achieved robust efficacy in both orthotopic and metastatic WT models with favorable preliminary tolerability.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a CD276-targeting biomimetic nanoplatform, (Bay/Cys)@hMnO₂@EM-CD276, to couple GLUT1 inhibition with L-cystine delivery for dual metabolic intervention in Wilms’ tumor. This “supply restriction–load increase” strategy restricts glucose/NADPH availability while increasing intracellular cystine/disulfide burden, thereby amplifying redox imbalance and disulfide stress-associated cytoskeletal injury. In both orthotopic and pulmonary metastatic WT models, this integrated nanotherapeutic strategy achieved pronounced tumor suppression and survival benefit with an overall favorable tolerability profile. These findings provide a potential design framework for exploiting metabolic vulnerability through coordinated pathway stress and lesion-focused biomimetic delivery, supporting further translational evaluation in clinically relevant WT models.
