Prevalence, risk factors, and conservative management of low back pain in competitive gymnasts: a systematic review
Authors: Yufeng Qin, Danli Song, Ji Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow back pain (LBP) is among the most prevalent musculoskeletal complaints in competitive gymnasts, yet synthesized evidence evaluating its epidemiological burden and conservative management in this population remains limited. This systematic review aimed to investigate the prevalence, risk factors, and efficacy of conservative interventions for LBP in competitive gymnasts.MethodsThis review was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines and registered with PROSPERO (ID: 420251238218). Five databases (PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science) were searched from inception to February 2025. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, and quasi-experimental studies involving competitive gymnasts aged 12 years or older with LBP. Methodological quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool for RCTs and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for observational studies. Due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, a narrative synthesis was performed.ResultsTen studies (7 RCTs, 3 cross-sectional) involving 736 participants were included. LBP prevalence ranged from 25% to 50% across disciplines, with higher rates observed in rhythmic gymnastics. Prevalence increased with advancing age and greater weekly training volume. Core stability training reduced pain intensity by 1.5 to 3.1 points on a 10-point VAS/NRS scale, while postural re-education methods (notably the Mézières method) yielded reductions of 1.2 to 2.5 points. Multimodal protocols integrating core training with postural correction demonstrated the largest reductions (1.8 to 3.2 points). No adverse effects on athletic performance were reported.ConclusionsLBP is highly prevalent among competitive gymnasts and is closely associated with cumulative training exposure. Core stability training and global postural re-education lasting 8 to 12 weeks can produce clinically meaningful pain reductions. However, the current evidence base is limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, and inherent challenges in participant and personnel blinding. Future large-scale, multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome measures and long-term follow-up are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-026-01800-y.

Introduction

Low Back Pain (LBP) is a multifaceted clinical entity that encompasses nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic pain and remains the leading cause of productivity loss and years lived with disability worldwide [1]. In the context of competitive sports, particularly gymnastics, the burden of LBP is exceptionally high. Epidemiological studies have reported that the prevalence of LBP among gymnasts ranges from approximately 25% to 85%, substantially exceeding that observed in the general population and in many other athletic disciplines characterized by lower spinal loading demands. According to the latest consensus statement extending the International Olympic Committee (IOC) guidelines, standardized reporting is essential to understand the true burden of injuries in this discipline [2].

Gymnastics disciplines, including artistic, rhythmic, and trampoline, necessitates complex maneuvers characterized by repetitive spinal hyperextension, hyperflexion, and rotation under high-impact conditions [3, 4]. Biomechanical studies indicate that during landings and swings, the spine absorbs axial loads and shear forces equivalent to 6.5 to 9.2 times the athlete’s body weight [5]. These specific biomechanical demands render the lumbar spine exceptionally vulnerable to pathologies such as spondylolysis, disc degeneration, and muscle strain [6, 7]. Recent imaging studies using upright MRI have further elucidated this vulnerability, revealing that 23% of elite gymnasts demonstrate dynamic posterior element abnormalities in extension that are missed on standard supine scans, confirming the sport-specific “nutcracker” mechanism on the spine [8]. This mechanism refers to the compression of posterior spinal elements (pars interarticularis, facet joints) between the superior articular process above and the inferior articular process below during repetitive hyperextension movements, analogous to a nutcracker. Due to early specialization and intensive training loads, the growing spines of young gymnasts are exceptionally vulnerable to overuse injuries [9, 10].

Consequently, LBP has emerged as one of the most prevalent musculoskeletal complaints in this population, with epidemiological data indicating alarming prevalence rates across various disciplines. A large-scale prospective study involving elite rhythmic gymnasts reported that 72% of athletes experienced musculoskeletal pain, with the lumbar spine being the most frequently affected region (25%), a rate significantly higher than that of age-matched non-athletes [11]. Similarly, baseline screenings in Teamgym athletes revealed a LBP point prevalence of 47% [12], Specific to the adolescent population, recent data revealed that 74% of high-performance gymnasts sustained injuries, with the lower back being the most common site (32%) [13]. In terms of cumulative risk, a scoping review by Dal Farra et al. substantiated the critical impact of cumulative exposure, noting that LBP prevalence increases significantly with higher weekly training volumes and years of practice [14].

The persistence of LBP has profound implications for an athlete’s career and long-term health. It can hinder training efficiency, degrade competitive performance, and potentially lead to early retirement [15]. Research indicates that athletes frequently normalize this discomfort, continuing to train and compete despite pain, a behavior that accelerates tissue damage and impedes recovery [14, 16]. This phenomenon is compounded by functional deficits. LBP in athletes is a multidimensional condition that extends beyond sensory pain to severely restrict functional performance and quality of life [17]. Without timely and effective management, these acute episodes risk transitioning into chronic pain syndromes, potentially necessitating early retirement and affecting daily living beyond the sports career [1, 18].

Despite the substantial morbidity associated with LBP in gymnasts, establishing a gold-standard conservative management protocol remains elusive. While Grooten et al. posited that no single exercise modality demonstrates superiority in the general population [19], extrapolating such generic findings to gymnasts—who navigate distinct biomechanical constraints—may be suboptimal. Contemporary literature has thus pivoted towards sport-specific modalities. Cabrejas et al., for instance, substantiated the efficacy of integrated functional core and plyometric training for enhancing stability in rhythmic gymnasts [20]. Building on this, Deodato et al. recently identified specific atrophy in the lumbar multifidus of gymnasts with LBP, demonstrating that a protocol focused on postural re-education and deep muscle activation significantly improves the flexor-to-extensor ratio and reduces pain [21]. Whereas Cugusi et al.’s meta-analysis underscored the imperative of postural re-education to rectify neuromuscular deficits [22]. Additionally, specific segmental muscle control exercises targeting the “hinge point” at L5/S1 effectively reduced the number of pain days in young female gymnasts, further validating the need for targeted rather than general intervention [12].

Yet, clinical implementation remains inconsistent. Fawcett et al. revealed a persistent discordance in management strategies between medical and coaching staff, indicating an absence of standardized care pathways [8]. This gap was highlighted in a 2025 systematic review by Huang et al. on prevention and treatment outcomes, which confirmed that while trunk-focused exercise programs generally show positive effects, the current evidence base is limited by high heterogeneity and a lack of consensus on the optimal exercise protocols for this specific population [23]. Consequently, a systematic synthesis evaluating these novel, discipline-specific interventions is notably absent from the current literature. A recent systematic review by Huang et al. [23] addressed conservative treatment outcomes in competitive artistic and team gymnasists but included only four studies and did not assess epidemiological burden or cover rhythmic gymnastics and trampoline disciplines. This underscores the need for a more comprehensive synthesis encompassing all gymnastic disciplines and integrating both prevalence data and intervention efficacy.

In the broader context of LBP management, current guidelines advocate for multimodal conservative approaches integrating physical modalities, manual therapy, and exercise therapy [24]. Transcutaneous Electrical Nerve Stimulation modulates pain via the gate control mechanism at high frequencies and promotes endogenous opioid release at low frequencies [25]. Manual therapy combined with stabilization exercises has demonstrated superior functional outcomes compared with Kinesio Taping alone [26]. Among exercise-based approaches, the McKenzie method has shown modest advantages for chronic LBP but limited benefit in acute settings [22]. More recently, Cognitive Functional Therapy has demonstrated superiority over group-based exercise by addressing maladaptive movement behaviors and fear-avoidance beliefs [27]. However, the comparative efficacy of specific exercise types remains equivocal in the general population [28], and the applicability of these findings to gymnasts who navigate distinct biomechanical constraints warrants investigation.

Given these unique biomechanical demands [4], generic evidence from the general population may not adequately address the rehabilitation needs of competitive gymnasts. Although a recent review attempted to consolidate treatment evidence [20], a comprehensive synthesis integrating epidemiological data with intervention outcomes across all gymnastic disciplines is lacking. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review is to investigate the prevalence, risk factors, and efficacy of conservative management for low back pain in competitive gymnasts. Specifically, this review addresses three questions: (1) What is the prevalence and associated risk factors of LBP in gymnasts? (2) Which conservative interventions are most effective in reducing pain and disability? (3) What is the methodological quality of the current evidence base?

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration number: 420251238218.)

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they met the following PICOS criteria: (1) Population: Competitive gymnasts (artistic, rhythmic, trampoline, or teamgym) aged \ge 12 years, with or without LBP. The age threshold of 12 years was selected to capture the adolescent period when intensive sport specialization typically commences and the growing spine becomes vulnerable to overuse injuries [9]. (2) Intervention/Exposure: For interventional studies, any conservative therapeutic intervention (e.g., postural re-education, core stability training, integrative rehabilitation). For observational studies, exposure to gymnastics training to assess prevalence or risk factors. (3) Comparator: Control groups receiving usual care, alternative therapies, or non-athlete controls. (4) Outcomes: Primary outcomes were pain intensity (VAS/NRS) and LBP prevalence. Secondary outcomes included functional disability and athletic performance. (5) Study Design: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, and quasi-experimental studies were included. Pilot studies were eligible if they reported quantitative outcomes and met the PICOS criteria.

Search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted from inception to February 2025 across five electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to two core concepts: (1) gymnastics and its sub-disciplines, and (2) low back pain and related spinal pathologies. The complete search strategies and results for each database are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Study selection process

Titles and abstracts of all retrieved records were screened independently by two reviewers to identify potential eligibility. During this initial screening, clearly irrelevant records were excluded based on title and abstract assessment. Full-text articles of potentially relevant records were then retrieved for detailed eligibility assessment. During this stage, clearly irrelevant records were excluded, and articles that appeared to meet the inclusion criteria were retained for further assessment.

The second stage involved the comprehensive review of the full text of relevant and potentially relevant articles by two independent reviewers. Both reviewers evaluated the suitability of each study against the pre-defined eligibility criteria and then met to determine final inclusion via consensus. Any discrepancies regarding study inclusion were resolved through discussion or referred to a third reviewer for arbitration.

Studies were included if they: (1) were full-text original research published in the English language; (2) involved competitive gymnasts (including artistic, rhythmic, or trampoline disciplines); (3) addressed non-specific low back pain (LBP) prevalence, risk factors, or conservative management; and (4) reported quantitative outcomes such as pain intensity (VAS/NRS), functional disability, or athletic performance. This review focused specifically on conservative rehabilitation and epidemiological factors. Therefore, studies investigating surgical interventions, invasive procedures (e.g., injections), or those lacking specific data for gymnastic populations were excluded to ensure the findings were applicable to the daily training and rehabilitation context.

The initial comprehensive search across five electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science) yielded a total of 1011 records. Following the removal of 106 duplicates, 905 distinct titles and abstracts were screened for relevance. Of these, 863 records were excluded as they did not meet the preliminary criteria, leaving 42 full-text articles for detailed eligibility assessment. After a rigorous full-text review, 32 studies were further excluded primarily due to irrelevance to the specific research questions or failure to meet the PICOS criteria. Ultimately, 10 studies satisfied all inclusion requirements and were included in this systematic review.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records and independently assessed full-text articles against the eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or referral to a third reviewer. Potentially relevant full-text articles were then retrieved and assessed against the eligibility criteria. Data from the included studies were extracted independently by the same two reviewers using a standardized data extraction form. The extracted information included: study characteristics (author, year, country, design), participant demographics (sample size, age, gymnastics discipline, level of competition), intervention or exposure details (type, frequency, duration), and main outcomes (prevalence, pain intensity, functional disability).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed independently by two reviewers. The choice of assessment tool depended on the study design:

RCTs: The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) was employed. This tool evaluates five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome, and selection of the reported result. Each domain was graded as “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk [29]”.

Observational Studies (Cross-sectional/Cohort): The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for cross-sectional studies was used. The adaptation involved modifying the follow-up adequacy domain to assess representativeness of the sample and quality of reporting, as cross-sectional studies do not have follow-up periods. This scale assesses three domains: selection of the study group, comparability of the groups, and ascertainment of the exposure/outcome. Studies were awarded stars (0–9), with scores of 7–9 considered high quality, 4–6 moderate quality, and 0–3 low quality [30].

Data synthesis

Due to substantial clinical heterogeneity across the included studies in terms of intervention type, dosage, comparator conditions, and outcome measurement tools, a quantitative meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a narrative synthesis was conducted following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines [31]. Studies were grouped by research question: (1) prevalence and risk factors, and (2) intervention efficacy. For intervention studies, results were synthesized according to intervention type (core stability training, postural re-education, and multimodal protocols). Pain intensity outcomes were summarized as ranges of change scores across studies rather than pooled estimates. The direction and consistency of effects across studies were assessed to determine the overall strength of evidence for each intervention category.

The certainty of the overall body of evidence was not formally assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) framework. This was due to the inability to generate pooled effect estimates, a prerequisite for standard GRADE appraisal, given the substantial heterogeneity in intervention types and outcome measures across included studies. Instead, the strength of evidence for each intervention category was evaluated narratively based on: (1) consistency in the direction of effects; (2) magnitude of observed changes relative to established minimal clinically important differences (MCID, approximately 2 points on the VAS/NRS); (3) methodological quality of contributing studies as assessed by the RoB 2.0 and NOS; and (4) number of studies and total sample size contributing to each intervention category. Studies were classified as providing ‘strong,’ ‘moderate,’ or ‘limited’ evidence based on these criteria.

Geographically, the included studies originated from seven countries across Europe, Asia, and North America: Spain (n = 4), Sweden (n = 1), the United States (n = 1), Japan (n = 1), Austria (n = 1), Croatia (n = 1), and China (n = 1). Publication years spanned from 2007 to 2025, with a notable concentration of studies published after 2022 (n = 7), reflecting growing research interest in this area. Individual study sample sizes ranged from 10 to 243 participants (median = 40). The majority of participants were female (approximately 95% of the total pooled sample), consistent with the demographic composition of aesthetic gymnastics disciplines. Regarding competitive level, most studies recruited elite or national-level athletes (n = 7), while three studies included collegiate-level gymnasts. Across the 10 studies, four gymnastic disciplines were represented: rhythmic gymnastics was the most frequently studied discipline (n = 7), followed by artistic gymnastics (n = 2), teamgym (n = 1), and trampoline (n = 1). In terms of study design, the seven interventional studies evaluated four broad categories of conservative treatment: the Mézières method of global postural re-education (n = 4), specific segmental muscle control exercises (n=1), preseason trunk muscle endurance training (n=1), and integrative rehabilitation combining traditional Chinese medicine with exercise therapy (n=1). The duration of follow-up assessment across included RCTs ranged from immediate post-intervention to 12 weeks post-intervention, with most studies assessing outcomes only at the immediate post-intervention time point.

Selection Bias: Four studies [15, 33, 34, 36] were rated as low risk for randomization process. However, Ma et al. (2024) [17] and Harringe et al. (2007) [12] were classified as high risk due to the lack of true randomization or the use of team-based allocation methods.

Performance Bias: Blinding of participants and personnel was the most significant source of bias, rated as high risk in 6 out of 7 studies. This is an inherent limitation in exercise therapy research where blinding the therapist or athlete to the intervention is often unfeasible.

Detection Bias: Most studies (n = 6) successfully implemented blinding of outcome assessors, minimizing the risk of detection bias.

Attrition Bias: The risk of incomplete outcome data was generally low, as most studies reported high retention rates or used appropriate intention-to-treat analyses.

Main findings

Prevalence and associated factors

Three observational studies and baseline data from several interventional studies provided evidence on the prevalence and associated factors of LBP across gymnastic disciplines. Supplementary Table S2 summarizes baseline LBP prevalence rates across all included studies, including both observational and interventional studies.

Sabeti et al. (2015) conducted a prospective comparison between 144 elite international rhythmic gymnasts and 99 age-matched non-athlete university students in Austria [11]. Among the athlete group, 72% reported musculoskeletal pain, and the lumbar spine was identified as the most frequently affected region, with 25% of gymnasts reporting LBP. The mean pain intensity among athletes (VAS 4.26 ± 3.11) was significantly higher than that of the control group (VAS 2.16 ± 2.55), confirming that elite rhythmic gymnasts experience a substantially greater pain burden than the general population.

Miletic et al. (2023) surveyed 99 competitive female gymnasts aged 8 to 18 years in Croatia using the Self-Estimated Functional Inability because of Pain (SEFIP) questionnaire, covering 14 anatomical regions [36]. The study reported that the lower back was among the most commonly affected pain sites in both artistic and rhythmic gymnasts. However, the study did not include a non-athlete control group, which limits direct prevalence comparisons.

Tsutsui et al. (2025) examined 95 Japanese collegiate female rhythmic gymnasts and identified that 46% of participants reported current or recent LBP [9]. Physical examination revealed that gymnasts with LBP exhibited significantly reduced hip internal rotation range of motion and lower hip abductor strength compared to those without LBP, suggesting that hip biomechanical deficits may serve as modifiable risk factors.

Among the interventional studies, baseline data further corroborated high prevalence rates. Harringe et al. (2007) reported a 47% point prevalence of LBP among 42 young female team gym athletes in Sweden [12]. Lena et al. (2022) enrolled 130 elite rhythmic gymnasts with confirmed LBP, with baseline VAS scores ranging from 2.54 to 3.04, indicating a persistent mild-to-moderate pain burden in this population [34].

Collectively, LBP prevalence across the included studies ranged from 25% to approximately 50%, with the highest rates observed in rhythmic gymnastics and among athletes with greater competitive exposure. Analysis of associated factors revealed two consistent patterns. First, a positive association between LBP prevalence and chronological age was observed; while adolescent gymnasts typically reported prevalence rates of 20% to 30%, rates escalated to 30% to 50% among collegiate or adult elite athletes. Second, a dose-response relationship between training load and LBP was evident across multiple studies: athletes with higher weekly training volumes (typically exceeding 20 hours per week) and longer cumulative training histories were significantly more likely to report LBP, supporting the central role of cumulative microtrauma in LBP pathogenesis among gymnasts. Age-related trends were examined narratively. Adolescent gymnasts (typically aged 12–18 years) generally reported lower LBP prevalence rates (20%−30%) compared to collegiate or adult elite athletes (30%−50%), suggesting a positive association between age and LBP prevalence. However, the limited number of studies and inconsistent age reporting precluded a formal age-stratified analysis.

A total of 10 included studies, comprising seven interventional studies (including one quasi-randomized trial) and three cross-sectional studies, reported pain intensity as a primary or secondary outcome, involving an aggregate sample of 736 aesthetic athletes. All studies utilized the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) or the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) (0–10 scale) to assess the intensity of low back pain.

Baseline pain levels varied across studies, with VAS/NRS scores ranging from 1.25 to 5.78, reflecting mild to moderate pain intensity at entry. This variability likely reflects differences in study populations, with some studies enrolling athletes with active LBP symptoms and others including gymnasts with subclinical or intermittent pain. This indicates that participants carried a clinically significant pain burden prior to intervention. The variability in baseline pain levels between studies was minimal, suggesting a degree of consistency in participant characteristics and pain severity across the included cohorts.

Subgroup analysis by intervention type revealed the following:

Core Stability Training: This modality was evaluated in four studies [17, 21, 34, 35], with intervention durations typically ranging from 8 to 12 weeks. Post-intervention reductions in pain intensity ranged from 1.5 to 3.1 points on a 10-point VAS/NRS scale. The majority of these studies reported reductions exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID, approximately 2 points) and reaching statistical significance (Lena et al., 2022 [34]: p < 0.05; Lena et al., 2025a [33]: p < 0.01; Ma et al., 2024 [17]: p < 0.001).

Postural Correction and Re-education: Interventions focusing on postural correction were reported in three studies [15, 35, 37]. Pain intensity reductions ranged from 1.2 to 2.5 points across these studies, with two studies demonstrating statistically significant improvements.

Comprehensive Interventions: Integrative protocols combining core training, postural correction, and physical therapy demonstrated the most pronounced analgesic effects in two studies. These interventions yielded the largest reductions, with pain intensity decreasing by up to 3.2 points (p < 0.001), suggesting a potential synergistic benefit of multimodal approaches in pain management. Ma et al. (2024), which reported the largest reduction (up to 3.2 points, p < 0.001), received a ‘some concerns’ rating due to quasi-randomization methods. This finding should be interpreted with caution pending replication in higher-quality studies.

In summary, despite heterogeneity in intervention modalities and follow-up durations, the vast majority of studies observed a significant reduction in pain intensity. The high consistency in the direction of outcomes supports the efficacy of rehabilitation strategies centered on active training and postural interventions for alleviating low back pain in aesthetic athletes.

Functional and athletic performance outcomes

A subset of the included RCTs reported outcomes related to functional performance and athletic capacity beyond pain intensity. Although the number of studies reporting these outcomes is limited, the available evidence provides preliminary insight into the functional impact of conservative interventions.

Regarding lower limb explosive power, Lena et al. (2025b) assessed countermovement jump (CMJ) performance in 17 elite adolescent rhythmic gymnasts [35]. The Mézières intervention group demonstrated a mean CMJ improvement from 5.75 ± 0.886 to 1.625 ± 0.744 (unit: performance score), representing a change of 4.125 points, while the isostretching control group improved from 5.778 ± 0.833 to 0.889 ± 0.928, representing a change of 4.889 points (p < 0.001 for both groups). These results indicate that both global postural re-education and active stretching interventions produced substantial improvements in jump efficiency, with the control group exhibiting a marginally greater magnitude of change.

Regarding trunk neuromuscular function, Durall et al. (2009) evaluated the effect of a 10-week preseason trunk muscle endurance training program in 30 collegiate artistic gymnasts [32]. The intervention group (n = 15) completed a structured protocol targeting trunk extensors, lateral flexors, and flexors using static endurance tests (Biering-Sorensen test, side bridge, and trunk flexor endurance test). Post-intervention, the training group demonstrated significant improvements in trunk extensor and lateral flexor endurance compared to the control group, and the incidence of LBP during the competitive season was lower in the intervention group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance due to the small sample size.

Todri et al. (2025) used the Spinal Mouse device to evaluate sagittal spinal alignment in 30 elite rhythmic gymnasts following either the Mézières method or isostretching [15]. Post-intervention, the Mézières group demonstrated significant improvements in thoracic kyphosis and lumbar lordosis angles toward normative values, suggesting that global postural re-education can effectively modify the excessive sagittal curvatures commonly observed in gymnasts.

Tsutsui et al. (2025), while cross-sectional in design, provided complementary evidence by demonstrating that gymnasts with LBP exhibited significantly weaker hip abductor strength and reduced hip internal rotation compared to asymptomatic peers [9], reinforcing the rationale for including hip-focused exercises in LBP rehabilitation protocols for gymnasts.

Across all included studies, no adverse effects on athletic performance were reported following conservative pain management interventions. The consistency of this finding, despite heterogeneity in intervention modalities and outcome measurement tools, suggests that structured rehabilitation programs can safely be integrated into the training regimen of competitive gymnasts without compromising performance capacity. Ma et al. (2024) reported improvements in lower limb power following integrative rehabilitation (p < 0.05), and Lena et al. (2025a) demonstrated improvements in trunk mobility and flexibility following the Mézières method. However, the limited number of studies reporting performance outcomes and the absence of standardized functional test batteries across trials preclude definitive conclusions regarding the comparative effects of different intervention modalities on athletic performance.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

This systematic review ultimately included 10 studies involving a total of 736 athletes from artistic gymnastics, rhythmic gymnastics, trampoline, and teamgym disciplines. The present study reveals three core findings. First, the prevalence of low back pain (LBP) among gymnasts is relatively high (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\%-50\%$$\end{document}) and demonstrates a significant upward trend with increasing age and training exposure. Second, conservative interventions—primarily core stability training and global postural re-education (e.g., the Mézières method)—can significantly reduce pain intensity (with an average VAS reduction of 1.8 to 3.2 points), achieving clinically meaningful improvements without negatively impacting athletic performance. Finally, the current evidence mainly consists of moderate-to-low quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cross-sectional studies; the primary limitations are small sample sizes and difficulties in implementing blinding. These findings provide essential evidence-based guidance for the clinical rehabilitation and long-term injury management of gymnasts.

Prevalence of LBP and associated risk factors

The analysis of this review indicates that the prevalence of LBP in gymnasts ranges between 25% and 50%, a finding largely consistent with previous epidemiological investigations of aesthetic and acrobatic sports. For example, Sabeti et al. reported that up to 72% of elite rhythmic gymnasts had experienced musculoskeletal pain, with the lumbar spine being the most frequently affected anatomical region [11]. Notably, this prevalence is not only significantly higher than that of the general adolescent population (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$15\%-20\%$$\end{document}) [38], but also generally exceeds that of athletes in non-hyperextension sports, such as swimming or soccer [39].

When compared with other hyperextension-dominant sports, the LBP prevalence observed in gymnasts (25% to 50%) is broadly comparable to that reported in figure skating and diving but higher than that in ballet dancers. A systematic review of figure skaters reported LBP prevalence rates of 30% to 45%, attributed to repetitive jump landings and spinal rotation under axial load [39]. In competitive diving, prevalence rates of 35% to 43% have been documented, primarily associated with repetitive hyperextension during takeoff and entry phases [40]. By contrast, professional ballet dancers exhibit somewhat lower rates (20% to 30%), potentially because ballet training emphasizes controlled spinal alignment and places relatively less emphasis on high-impact landings compared to gymnastics [41, 42]. These cross-sport comparisons suggest that the combination of high axial loading, repetitive hyperextension, and high-impact landings, rather than flexibility demands alone, constitutes the primary biomechanical driver of LBP in aesthetic and acrobatic sports.

This exceptionally high risk can be primarily attributed to the unique biomechanical loading characteristics of gymnastics. During jumps, landings, and complex swinging maneuvers, the athletes’ spines must absorb axial loads and shear forces equivalent to 6.5 to 9.2 times their body weight [43]. As pointed out by Tsutsui et al. [9], repetitive spinal hyperextension and asymmetrical loading (such as compensatory mechanisms triggered by restricted unilateral hip range of motion) pose a massive challenge to the posterior spinal structures of growing adolescents, rendering them highly susceptible to spondylolysis and lumbar muscle strains. Furthermore, this study confirms a positive correlation between LBP prevalence and both training load and age, which epidemiologically supports the central role of “cumulative micro-trauma” in the pathogenesis of LBP in gymnasts.

Efficacy of conservative interventions

Regarding the efficacy of conservative interventions, this review found that core stability training and postural interventions decreased LBP intensity in gymnasts by an average of 1.8 to 3.2 points. This reduction exceeds the widely accepted threshold for the minimal clinically important difference (MCID, approximately 2 points). Interestingly, the specific intervention effects observed in the gymnastics population appear superior to the conclusions of several systematic reviews focusing on chronic LBP in the general population, which typically conclude that no single exercise modality (e.g., general aerobic or strength training) demonstrates greater efficacy in pain relief, as measured by VAS/NRS reduction [44].

The significant clinical benefits demonstrated by gymnasts following core and postural training may be attributed to the precise correction of “sport-specific biomechanical deficits” provided by these interventions. Previous mechanistic studies have shown that patients with LBP often present with selective atrophy of the lumbar multifidus and delayed activation of deep core musculature [45]. The specific segmental muscle control exercises utilized by Harringe et al. [12] precisely target this neuromuscular deficit, effectively restoring the dynamic stabilization mechanism of the spine. Additionally, the series of RCTs by Lena and Todri et al. confirmed that global postural re-education, represented by the Mézières method, also yields substantial clinical benefits [15, 33, 34]. Due to sport-specific demands, gymnasts frequently adopt extreme lumbar lordosis and anterior pelvic tilt postures, which often lead to severe shortening and stiffness of the posterior myofascial chain [46]. The Mézières method effectively breaks this compensatory pathological posture through whole-body eccentric stretching and respiratory pattern reconstruction. Furthermore, the maximum analgesic effect demonstrated by comprehensive intervention protocols (e.g., Ma et al. [17], which combined core training with traditional Chinese physical therapy) suggests that multimodal rehabilitation programs targeting both myofascial and core components may offer potential synergistic therapeutic advantages for gymnasts exposed to long-term extreme loading. These findings extend and complement those of a recent systematic review by Huang et al. [23], which included four studies focusing on artistic and teamgym gymnasts and reported that 50% to 100% of gymnasts experienced improvement following conservative treatment. The present review, by incorporating a broader range of gymnastic disciplines and a larger number of studies, provides more granular evidence regarding specific intervention modalities and their comparative effectiveness. Notably, the consistent superiority of postural re-education approaches observed across multiple RCTs in the current review was not captured in the narrower scope of the prior synthesis.

Clinical implications

The findings of this review carry several practical implications for clinical practice, coaching, and organizational policy.

At the individual practitioner level, clinicians designing rehabilitation protocols for gymnasts with LBP should prioritize core stability training and global postural re-education for a minimum duration of 8 to 12 weeks. Therapeutic goals should extend beyond pain reduction to include targeted reactivation of the deep lumbar multifidus, restoration of flexor-to-extensor muscular balance, and correction of excessive lumbar lordosis. Given the evidence supporting the Mézières method, incorporation of global posterior chain re-education may offer additional benefit, particularly for rhythmic gymnasts who routinely adopt extreme sagittal postures. The consistent absence of adverse performance effects across all included interventional studies suggests that clinicians can confidently integrate these protocols into athletes’ ongoing training schedules.

At the coaching level, the strong dose-response relationship between training volume and LBP prevalence identified in this review warrants a reassessment of current training load management practices. Coaches should implement systematic monitoring of training volume, particularly during periods of intensified preparation, and consider periodized reductions in spinal loading for athletes reporting early symptoms [37, 47]. The practice of training through pain, which epidemiological evidence indicates accelerates tissue damage and delays recovery [48, 49], should be actively discouraged through education of both coaches and athletes.

Regarding return-to-sport progression, the available evidence, while limited, suggests that a graduated approach to resuming gymnastics-specific activities may be appropriate once pain intensity is reduced and functional capacity is restored. However, based on the intervention outcomes observed in this review, a graduated return-to-training approach may be recommended when the following conditions are met: (1) pain intensity reduced to below 2 points on a 10-point VAS/NRS scale; (2) restoration of symmetric hip range of motion; and (3) core endurance benchmarks reaching pre-injury levels or normative values. These criteria require prospective validation in future studies.

At the organizational level, sports governing bodies should consider mandating standardized preseason biomechanical screening programs that include assessments of hip range of motion symmetry, core endurance (e.g., Biering-Sorensen test), and sagittal spinal alignment. Such screening could facilitate early identification of at-risk athletes and enable timely preventive interventions. Furthermore, the establishment of a multidisciplinary support team integrating sports medicine physicians, physiotherapists, and coaches is essential for ensuring consistent and evidence-based management of LBP throughout the athlete’s career. The discordance between medical and coaching staff in LBP management strategies, as documented by Fawcett et al. [8], underscores the need for standardized care pathways that bridge clinical and training environments.

Limitations and future directions

This systematic review has several limitations. First, the number of included RCTs is relatively limited (n=7), and the sample sizes are generally small. Some studies lacked a rigorous randomization process, which somewhat limits the statistical power and the generalizability of the results. Second, there was substantial heterogeneity across the included studies regarding intervention dosages, control group settings, and outcome measurement tools, precluding the possibility of conducting a quantitative meta-analysis. Third, the follow-up periods in most trials were relatively short (typically not exceeding 12 weeks); thus, the long-term maintenance effects of conservative treatments and their clinical value in preventing LBP recurrence remain unclear. Finally, the existing study populations predominantly consisted of female athletes, and the applicability of these conclusions to male gymnasts requires further validation [50].

Additionally, this review included only English-language publications, which may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages, particularly given the global distribution of gymnastics research. The absence of grey literature searching (e.g., conference abstracts and dissertations) may also contribute to publication bias. Grey literature can complement database searches by capturing preliminary findings, ongoing trials, and studies that have not yet undergone or completed the peer-review process, thereby providing a more comprehensive overview of the available evidence and reducing the risk of selective inclusion. Furthermore, the substantial heterogeneity across interventions, comparators, and outcome measures precluded quantitative synthesis. Finally, it should be noted that a substantial proportion of the interventional evidence originates from a single research group (Lena, Todri et al.), which may limit the generalizability of findings related to the Mézières method until independent replication is available. It should also be noted that the original NOS was developed for cohort studies, and some criteria are not directly applicable to cross-sectional designs. To address this, we used the adapted version as recommended by Modesti et al. (2016), which modifies the follow-up adequacy domain to assess sample representativeness and reporting quality. Despite these adaptations, we acknowledge this as a limitation of the quality assessment methodology.

Future research should prioritize the design of multicenter, large-sample, high-quality RCTs utilizing standardized pain and athletic performance measurement tools, and incorporate long-term follow-up mechanisms to comprehensively evaluate the sustained effects of different rehabilitation strategies on the athletes’ functional outcomes and career longevity [51, 52].

Conclusion

This systematic review provides a systematic synthesis of the prevalence, risk factors, and conservative management of LBP in competitive gymnasts across four disciplines. The findings confirm that LBP is highly prevalent (25% to 50%) in this population and is positively associated with advancing age and cumulative training exposure. Core stability training and global postural re-education, particularly the Mézières method, administered over 8 to 12 weeks, can produce clinically meaningful reductions in pain intensity that exceed the established MCID threshold, without compromising athletic performance. Multimodal protocols integrating core training with postural correction appear to yield the greatest analgesic benefit. However, the current evidence base is constrained by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, limited blinding feasibility, and a concentration of interventional evidence from a single research group. Future multicenter RCTs with standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up, and diverse gymnastic populations are needed to establish definitive rehabilitation guidelines for this high-risk athletic population.
