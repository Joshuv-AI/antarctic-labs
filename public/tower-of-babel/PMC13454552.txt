Bacterial Volatile Organic Compounds Mediate a Cooperative Relationship Between Bacteria and Plants Under Heavy Metal Stress
Authors: Anahita Barghi, Eun‐Nam Joe, Jong‐Rok Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBacterial volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) are increasingly recognised as effective signalling molecules that stimulate plant growth and stress responses. Although BVOC production may impose metabolic costs on bacteria, the benefits gained by VOC‐emitting bacteria through BVOC‐mediated communication with plants remain unclear. In this study, we established Pb‐stressed conditions for Bacillus megaterium , Arabidopsis thaliana or both organisms to investigate their reciprocal interactions. Pb‐stressed bacteria emitted a distinct VOC profile and exhibited significant reprogramming of sugar and nitrogen‐containing carbon metabolism compared with unstressed bacteria. Plant growth inhibition caused by Pb was alleviated by VOCs emitted from unstressed bacteria; however, this alleviation was enhanced by 36% when the bacteria were exposed to Pb stress, indicating that Pb‐induced changes in bacterial VOC metabolism trigger distinct plant responses. Specifically, Pb accumulation increased by up to 95% in plant shoot, whereas oxidative damage markers, including malondialdehyde and H2O2, decreased by up to 25% in plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed bacteria compared with those exposed to VOCs from Pb‐unstressed bacteria. These effects were associated with increased expression of antioxidant enzyme‐related genes in plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed bacteria. Finally, we confirmed that the Pb‐stressed bacteria‐mediated increases in plant growth and Pb uptake were accompanied by alleviation of the Pb stress‐induced reduction in bacterial population. Overall, our results demonstrate that VOC‐emitting bacteria can benefit from modulating plant physiology and suggest that a cooperative relationship between bacteria and plants can be established through BVOC‐mediated communication.Keywords: bacterial metabolism, bacterial organic volatiles, cooperative relationship, heavy metal stress, plant stimulation

Introduction

Bacterial volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) are known to serve as signalling molecules in plant–microbe interactions by modulating plant physiological and metabolic processes (Fincheira et al. 2021; Russo et al. 2022). These phenomena have been reported in diverse bacterial genera, including Pseudomonas, Bacillus and Paenibacillus and in several plant species, such as Arabidopsis thaliana , rice and maize (Cho et al. 2008; Wang et al. 2023; Lopes et al. 2024; Narayanasamy and Uthandi 2024), suggesting that BVOCs represent one of the major routes of plant–microbe communication. Although the dedicated sensing machinery by which plants perceive BVOCs has not yet been reported, BVOCs have been shown to promote plant growth and enhance tolerance to abiotic stresses (Fincheira et al. 2021; Lopes et al. 2024; Narayanasamy and Uthandi 2024). Several plant gene products affected by BVOCs have also been characterised (Chang et al. 2024).

The production of BVOCs may entail a metabolic cost, as it involves the release of bacterial metabolites that might otherwise serve as carbon and energy sources. In addition, BVOCs are structurally diverse and many are not typical intermediates of central carbon metabolism, implying that their biosynthesis depends on dedicated enzymatic pathways (Kanchiswamy et al. 2015; Almeida et al. 2023). Despite this potential metabolic investment, most studies of BVOC‐mediated plant–bacteria interactions have focused primarily on plant responses to BVOCs rather than on the benefits that VOC production may confer on the emitting bacteria. Investigating the ecological and physiological advantages of BVOC production for bacteria is therefore of considerable interest. In other plant–bacteria interactions, reciprocal cooperative relationships are well established. Carbon and nitrogen exchange between legumes and rhizobia, for instance, has been well characterised (Cao et al. 2022), while the supply of plant‐derived labile carbon to arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi promotes nitrogen and phosphorus transfer by the fungi (Thirkell et al. 2020). Similarly, root exudates can facilitate colonisation by plant growth‐promoting bacteria by serving as accessible carbon sources, supporting the idea that bacterial stimulation of plants is frequently accompanied by reciprocal plant support for bacteria.

In this study, we investigated how bacteria and plants communicate with each other via BVOCs when both organisms are exposed to heavy metal stress. Lead (Pb), A. thaliana and Bacillus megaterium GNU‐01 (Barghi et al. 2021) were selected as the model toxic heavy metal, the BVOC‐responsive plant and the VOC‐emitting bacterium, respectively. Five experimental conditions were established to examine Pb stress‐induced changes in BVOC‐mediated interactions between A. thaliana and B. megaterium : (i) unstressed plants alone, (ii) unstressed plants exposed to BVOCs from unstressed bacteria, (iii) Pb‐stressed plants alone, (iv) Pb‐stressed plants exposed to BVOCs from unstressed bacteria and (v) Pb‐stressed plants exposed to BVOCs from Pb‐stressed bacteria (Figure 1). Specifically, mRNA expression patterns and colony‐forming unit (CFU)‐based survival rates were analysed in B. megaterium , whereas stress tolerance responses and biomass were assessed in A. thaliana .

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Materials

B. megaterium GNU‐01 and A. thaliana (Col‐0 background) seeds were obtained from the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (no. 13087BP) and Dr. Joon‐Yung Cha (Gyeongsang National University, Jinju, Republic of Korea), respectively. Lead nitrate [Pb(NO3)2], nutrient broth, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), thiobarbituric acid (TBA), 2‐butanone, sucrose and trichloroacetic acid (TCA) were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich, while Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium and agar for plant and bacterial culture were from Duchefa‐Biochemie. Magnesium sulphate (MgSO4), potassium iodide (KI), concentrated nitric acid (60%) and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) were purchased from Junsei Chemical (Tokyo, Japan), while 1,3‐propandiol was obtained from TCI (Tokyo, Japan).

Plant and Bacteria Cultivation

A. thaliana seeds were surface‐sterilised in 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by immersion in 2% Clorox for 5 min and thorough rinsing with sterile distilled water. The sterilised seeds were vernalised in the dark at 4°C for 4 days and at 23°C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod for 2 days. The seeds were then transferred to plates containing MS medium supplemented with 0.9% (w/v) agar and 2% (w/v) sucrose, adjusted to pH 5.8. The plates were incubated to obtain A. thaliana seedlings in a plant growth chamber at 23°C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and 50%–60% relative humidity. B. megaterium was grown overnight in nutrient broth at 30°C with shaking at 160 rpm. The cells were harvested by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in 10 mM MgSO4 solution to a final optical density (600 nm) of 0.5, corresponding to approximately 1 × 109 CFU mL−1.

For the coculture of B. megaterium and A. thaliana , bipartite Petri dishes with two physically separated compartments were used. One compartment containing MS agar was used to grow nine 6‐day‐old A. thaliana seedlings, whereas the other compartment containing MS agar was inoculated with B. megaterium . For coculture without a physical barrier, a sterile wooden stick was placed at the centre of the plate and removed before agar solidification, thus allowing Pb ions to move freely through the agar during plant–microbe co‐incubation. For bacterial inoculation, 15 μL of the bacterial suspension prepared as described above was spotted onto the MS agar 1 day before the transfer of A. thaliana and the same volume of autoclaved distilled water was used as a negative control. The plates were then incubated in a plant growth chamber at 23°C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod and 50%–60% relative humidity. Heavy metal stress was imposed in the bipartite plate experiments by adding Pb(NO3)2 to the MS agar before solidification. After 10 days of Pb stress, A. thaliana plants were harvested and their fresh weight was measured immediately. Dry weight was then determined after drying at 80°C for 1 day. We confirmed that no contamination by endogenous microorganisms from the seeds occurred in any of the cultures.

To determine the CFUs of B. megaterium on the agar, the agar region containing bacterial colonies was excised during A. thaliana harvest and washed with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) to detach the bacteria. The wash solutions were collected, centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min and the resulting bacterial pellets were washed one time with PBS. The final bacterial suspension was plated on nutrient agar for colony enumeration.

For the monoculture of B. megaterium , nutrient broth supplemented with Pb(NO3)2 was used to determine the optimal growth conditions for the bi‐plate experiment with A. thaliana (Figure S1A) and the culture was incubated at 30°C with shaking at 160 rpm. Optical density at 600 nm (OD600) was measured over the incubation period. For A. thaliana monoculture, the above‐described MS agar supplemented with Pb(NO3)2 was used to determine the optimal growth conditions for the bi‐plate experiment with B. megaterium (Figure S1B,C).

All experiments examining A. thaliana growth on MS agar were performed as three independent experiments, with nine A. thaliana seedlings used in each experiment.

B. megaterium VOC Profiling

B. megaterium was cultured in glass vials containing MS agar in the presence or absence of Pb(NO3)2 (0.3 mM). This experiment was conducted in the absence of A. thaliana . The vials were sealed with steel crimp caps fitted with Teflon/silicone septa to minimise VOC loss during cultivation. VOCs were collected by headspace solid‐phase microextraction (HS‐SPME) using a 50/30 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fibre (Supelco). Before extraction, the vials were equilibrated at 80°C, after which the SPME fibre was exposed to the headspace for 20 min. The extracted VOCs were thermally desorbed at 250°C in the gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) inlet and analysed using a GC–MS system (QP2020NX, Shimadzu) equipped with a DB‐5 capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm i.d., 0.25 μm film thickness; Agilent). The oven temperature was initially held at 40°C for 3 min, raised to 80°C at 7°C min−1 and then increased to 240°C at 25°C min−1 with a final hold for 5 min with a helium flow rate of 1 mL/min. Mass spectra were recorded in electron impact mode over an m/z range of 40–500. VOCs were identified by comparison with spectra in the NIST mass spectral library.

Transcriptomic Analyses of B. megaterium in the Presence or Absence of Pb

Transcriptomic analyses were performed by the MOAGEN (Daejeon, South Korea). For RNA extraction, B. megaterium colonies grown in bipartite plates with A. thaliana for 11 days were aseptically harvested using PBS, collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min and washed once with PBS. Total RNA was extracted from the pellets using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. To remove residual genomic DNA, an additional on‐column DNase digestion step was performed using the Zymo Research DNase I Set (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol and the absence of DNA contamination was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. RNA integrity and quality were assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the Agilent RNA 6000 Nano Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) of ≥ 7.5 were used for library preparation. RNA purity (A260/A280) and concentration were measured using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer and RNA concentration was further quantified using a Qubit RNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Samples that passed quality control based on RNA integrity, purity and concentration were used for sequencing.

RNA libraries were constructed using the TruSeq RNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, ribosomal RNA was removed from the total RNA and the remaining RNA was fragmented and reverse‐transcribed into first‐strand cDNA using random hexamer primers. Second‐strand cDNA synthesis was then performed to generate double‐stranded cDNA. The resulting cDNA fragments were ligated to sequencing adapters, size‐selected by agarose gel electrophoresis and PCR‐amplified to generate the final libraries. The libraries were quantified by qPCR according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and qualified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform.

Raw read quality was assessed using FastQC v0.10.0. An automated RNA‐seq analysis pipeline was used for quality control, read trimming, alignment, transcript assembly and differential expression analysis. Filtered reads were mapped to the reference genome of B. megaterium SR7 (accession nos. CP022674–CP022679) using TopHat v2.1.1, allowing up to two mismatches per read. Only uniquely mapped reads were retained and all other parameters were set to the default values. Transcript assembly was performed using Cufflinks v2.1.1 based on the genome annotation of B. megaterium SR7. Gene expression levels were estimated by Cuffdiff in the Cufflinks package using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on fold changes and statistical significance was assessed using Student's t‐tests implemented in Cuffdiff.

DEGs identified from the RNA‐seq analysis were functionally annotated using the UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB). Gene ontology (GO) terms associated with the annotated UniProt entries were retrieved and classified. The GO classification results were used to summarise the functional distribution of the DEGs.

Lead Content in Arabidopsis Shoots and Roots

Pb accumulation in A. thaliana shoots and roots was quantified using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP‐OES; iCAP 7400, Thermo Scientific). For sample preparation, 70 mg of dried biomass (80°C for 1 day) was digested with 2–5 mL of nitric acid (60%, v/v) at 105°C for 12 h using a heat block. After complete digestion, the samples were filtered through a 0.2‐μm membrane filter and diluted with deionised water prior to analysis.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and Hydrogen Peroxide Quantification in A. thaliana

MDA content was determined using a modified TBA method according to Heath and Packer (1968). Fresh leaf tissue of A. thaliana (0.2 g) was homogenised in 1.5 mL of 5% TCA using a mortar and pestle and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The reaction mixture, consisting of 0.5 mL of the supernatant and 1 mL of 0.5% TBA prepared in 20% TCA, was incubated at 95°C for 25 min, then rapidly cooled on ice and centrifuged again at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Absorbance was measured at 532 and 600 nm using a spectrophotometer and MDA concentration was calculated using an extinction coefficient of 155 mM−1 cm−1.

Hydrogen peroxide levels in fresh leaf tissues were determined spectrophotometrically using a KI‐based assay according to Alexieva et al. (2001), with minor modifications. Fresh leaf tissue (0.2 g) of A. thaliana was homogenised in 1.5 mL of 0.1% TCA using a mortar and pestle and centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The reaction mixture, consisting of 0.5 mL of the supernatant, 0.5 mL of potassium phosphate buffer (100 mM; pH 7.0) and 2 mL of 1 M KI, was incubated in the dark for 1 h and the absorbance was then measured at 390 nm. Hydrogen peroxide concentration was determined using a calibration curve generated from H2O2 standards of known concentrations.

Determination of Catalase and Ascorbate Peroxidase Activities

Fresh leaf samples (0.2 g) were harvested, immediately frozen and finely ground in liquid nitrogen using a mortar and pestle and 2 mL of ice‐cold potassium phosphate buffer (50 mM; pH 7.8) supplemented with 1 mM EDTA. Homogenates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C and the resulting supernatant was collected and used for enzymatic assays of catalase (CAT) and ascorbate peroxidase (APX).

CAT (EC 1.11.1.6) activity was determined by monitoring the decomposition of H2O2 at 240 nm according to Aebi (1984), with minor modifications. Briefly, 0.1 mL of crude enzyme extract was added to a reaction mixture containing 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) and H2O2 (final concentration, 10 mM) in a final volume of 3.0 mL. The decrease in absorbance at 240 nm was recorded immediately after enzyme addition at 15 s intervals for 1 min using a spectrophotometer. CAT activity was calculated from the initial linear decrease in A240 using the molar extinction coefficient of H2O2 at 240 nm (ε = 39.4 mM−1 cm−1) and expressed as μmol H2O2 decomposed min−1 mg−1 protein. Soluble protein concentration was determined using the Warburg–Christian method by measuring absorbance at 260 and 280 nm and calculated as follows: protein concentration (mg mL−1) = 1.55 × A280 − 0.76 × A260.

APX (EC 1.11.1.11) activity was assayed in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM ascorbate, 0.1 mM H2O2 and 0.1 mL of enzyme extract in a final volume of 1.0 mL. The decrease in absorbance at 290 nm due to ascorbate oxidation was monitored for 3 min and the initial linear rate was used to calculate enzyme activity. APX activity was calculated using the extinction coefficient of ascorbate at 290 nm (ε = 2.8 mM−1 cm−1) and expressed as μmol ascorbate oxidised min−1 mg−1 protein, according to Nakano and Asada (1981).

Statistics

Statistical significance was determined by one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments (n = 3). Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Pb Toxicity Screening for BVOC‐Based Interactions Between B. megaterium and Arabidopsis

Three different Pb concentrations (100, 300 and 500 μM) were applied to B. megaterium cultures to identify a concentration at which Pb toxicity would not severely interfere with bacterial growth. As shown in Figure S1A, Pb treatments up to 300 μM maintained approximately 95% of the bacterial population relative to the control without Pb, whereas treatment with 500 μM Pb reduced the population to approximately 70% of the control, indicating that Pb concentrations up to 300 μM imposed relatively mild toxicity without the severe population reduction and were therefore suitable for subsequent VOC‐emission experiments.

Five Pb concentrations (1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 mM) were applied to A. thaliana in the presence of VOCs emitted by non‐stressed B. megaterium to determine the Pb concentration that imposed sufficient stress on the plants. As shown in Figure S1B, reductions in plant biomass were negligible at Pb concentrations of 1–2 mM, whereas exposure to 4 mM Pb resulted in a 68% reduction in biomass compared with plants grown without Pb. In addition, VOCs from B. megaterium significantly promoted plant growth under 4 mM Pb stress. Therefore, 4 mM Pb was selected for subsequent bi‐plate experiments.

The growth‐promoting effects of VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium on A. thaliana were also evaluated to determine the Pb concentration applied to B. megaterium that would be appropriate for enhancing the growth of A. thaliana . As shown in Figure S1B,C . megaterium exposed to Pb concentrations at 300 μM significantly enhanced the growth of A. thaliana , with effects greater than those observed under the bacterial conditions without Pb and with 100 μM. Therefore, 300 μM Pb for B. megaterium and 4 mM Pb for A. thaliana were selected for further analyses.

VOCs From Pb‐Stressed B. megaterium Promote A. thaliana Growth and Pb Uptake

A. thaliana seedlings were exposed to B. megaterium –derived VOCs in the presence or absence of Pb to evaluate changes in plant growth and Pb uptake under heavy‐metal stress. Exposure to VOCs emitted by non‐stressed B. megaterium did not significantly increase the total fresh weight of Arabidopsis seedlings under nonstress conditions (Figure S2). Likewise, shoot and root fresh weights were comparable to those of the control group under non‐stress conditions (Figure 2A,B). Application of 4 mM Pb markedly reduced both shoot and root growth by approximately 70% due to Pb‐induced stress. However, exposure of Pb‐stressed plants to VOCs derived from B. megaterium grown under non‐stress conditions effectively alleviated this inhibition, increasing shoot and root fresh weights by 76% and 147%, respectively, compared with those of Pb‐stressed plants without BVOCs. VOCs produced by Pb‐stressed B. megaterium promoted shoot growth more strongly than those produced by the unstressed bacteria, resulting in a 41% increase in shoot biomass. In contrast, root fresh weight under this treatment was comparable to that observed in plants exposed to VOCs from the unstressed bacteria (Figure 2A,B). In conclusion, Pb‐stressed B. megaterium enhanced the total fresh growth of Pb‐stressed A. thaliana by 27% more than unstressed B. megaterium , as reflected by the increase in shoot biomass (Figure S2).

Dry weights of A. thaliana were also evaluated to examine whether VOCs derived from unstressed and Pb‐stressed B. megaterium promoted photosynthesis‐based carbon fixation. In the absence of Pb stress to plants, shoot and root dry weights tended to increase slightly, by up to approximately 19% and 22%, respectively, in response to VOCs from unstressed B. megaterium , confirming the well‐known effectiveness of BVOCs (Barghi et al. 2021; Fincheira et al. 2021; Lopes et al. 2024). Overall, the remaining treatments showed trends similar to those observed for fresh weights, except that root dry weight was approximately 27% higher in plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium than in those exposed to VOCs from nonstressed B. megaterium (Figure 2C,D and S2). Taken together, these results indicate that our experimental setup reproduces previous findings showing that BVOCs can alleviate the adverse effects of heavy‐metal stress in plants and further suggest that VOCs emitted by stressed bacteria exert stronger growth‐promoting effects than those emitted by nonstressed bacteria.

To clarify the mechanisms by which unstressed and stressed B. megaterium mitigate the adverse effects of heavy‐metal stress in plants, Pb accumulation in plant tissues was quantitatively analysed (Figure 3). In the absence of BVOC exposure, Pb‐stressed plants accumulated Pb in both shoots and roots at levels up to approximately sixfold higher than those in non‐stressed plants, primarily reflecting the higher Pb concentration in the growth medium. In plants exposed to VOCs from unstressed B. megaterium , Pb accumulation per unit dry weight increased by approximately 23% and 52% in shoots and roots, respectively, while total Pb content increased by fourfold in shoots and twofold in roots, respectively. These results suggest that BVOC‐mediated mitigation of heavy metal stress is associated with enhanced Pb accumulation in plant tissues. Notably, Pb accumulation per unit dry weight was approximately 31% higher in plant shoots exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium than in those exposed to VOCs from the unstressed bacteria. Although root Pb concentrations on a unit weight basis were comparable between plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium and those exposed to VOCs from unstressed bacteria, total shoot and root Pb contents were approximately 95% and 28% higher in the former group, likely owing to the observed enhancement of plant growth. Collectively, these results suggest that BVOCs emitted by stressed bacteria may accelerate plant defense responses to heavy metal stress through distinct mechanisms.

Pb Stress‐Induced Changes in VOC Emission and Gene Expression of B. megaterium

To determine whether Pb‐induced stress modifies the airborne signalling capacity of B. megaterium , VOCs emitted under Pb‐stressed conditions were compared with those produced under non‐stress conditions, as previously described by Barghi et al. (2021). Three stress‐dependent VOCs—2‐butanone, 1,3‐propanediol and methoxypyrazine—were detected exclusively under Pb‐stressed conditions and were absent under non‐stress conditions. In contrast, several VOCs, including 2‐pentanone, oxalic acid, 1‐pentanol and nonane, were detected under normal conditions but not under Pb‐stressed conditions. These results suggest that Pb stress alters VOC‐associated metabolic processes, resulting in a distinct VOC profile (Table 1).

We further examined whether the newly emitted VOCs under Pb stress, namely 2‐butanone and 1,3‐propanediol, could stimulate the growth of Pb‐stressed A. thaliana . As shown in Figure S3, exposure to 2‐butanone tended to increase fresh shoot and root biomass by 6% and 52%, respectively, compared with plants not exposed to this volatile. Dry weights showed a similar trend. These results are consistent with previous findings showing the plant‐stimulatory effects of 2‐butanone (Song et al. 2019; Poulaki and Tjamos 2023) and partially support the idea that carbon metabolism in stressed bacteria is reprogrammed to emit plant‐stimulatory VOCs.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis of B. megaterium grown on normal MS agar and Pb‐containing MS agar was conducted to confirm whether the observed change in VOC profiles is ascribed to change in bacterial metabolism and revealed substantial changes in bacterial gene expression patterns. A total of 274 genes were differentially expressed at p < 0.05, including 248 upregulated genes and 26 downregulated genes with Pb stress. To characterise the transcriptional response to Pb exposure, the DEGs were subjected to GO classification analysis. The genes upregulated under Pb stress were distributed across 18, 35 and 14 categories of biological process, molecular function and cellular component, respectively (Figure S4). This suggests that Pb stress at the tested concentration broadly boosts the metabolism and physiology of B. megaterium . As shown in Figure 4A, genes associated with the metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, lipids and organic acids exhibited marked changes in expression, suggesting that Pb exposure may promote the production of novel metabolites through the activation of specific metabolic pathways. Because 2‐butanone, 1,3‐propanediol and methoxypyrazine are structurally presumed to originate from sugar and amino acid metabolism (Tong et al. 2025), the enrichment of upregulated genes related to carbohydrate metabolism and amino acid and derivative metabolism is consistent with the altered VOC profile.

Gene ontology classification of upregulated DEGs (Log2 Fold change > 0; p < 0.05) associated with (A) metabolic processes and (B) stress responses by Pb‐stressed Bacillus megaterium .

Stress response‐related GO is depicted in Figure 4B. Oxidative defense‐related genes exhibited the largest portion, which is consistent that heavy metals cause oxidative stress. Several general stress and DNA repair genes were found to be upregulated, indicating that Pb‐stressed B. megaterium expresses systemic stress responses. As bacterial stress responses are energy‐consuming processes, the slight population decline observed in Figure S1A may be ascribed to the onset of systemic stress responses. We also detailed heavy metal stress‐specific genes upregulated in the presence of Pb, as shown in Table S1. Several genes involved in bacterial redox homeostasis, including thioredoxin reductase, thioredoxin, peroxiredoxin and glutathione peroxidase, were upregulated by up to approximately twofold on a log2 scale, indicating that Pb‐induced oxidative stress triggered the expression of these defense‐related genes in B. megaterium . In addition, genes encoding a metal transporter and a metal chaperone, which are associated with metal handling and stress‐related protein protection, respectively, were also upregulated, likely in response to heavy metal toxicity. This concurrent upregulation of carbon metabolism‐ and stress response‐related genes is consistent with previous transcriptomic studies showing that bacterial stress adaptation often involves coordinated remodelling of central carbon/energy metabolism and activation of oxidative, metal or nutrient stress response pathways (Ter Beek et al. 2008).

Reduction of MDA and Hydrogen Peroxide Levels in A. thaliana

MDA and H2O2 levels were measured to evaluate oxidative damage in plant tissues and to examine whether BVOCs enhance plant defense against stress (Figure 5). Pb‐treated A. thaliana plants in the absence of B. megaterium VOCs showed approximately 2‐fold and 2.4‐fold increases in MDA and H2O2 levels, respectively, relative to the control, indicating that Pb accumulation causes substantial oxidative damage in plant tissues. By contrast, exposure to VOCs from non‐stressed B. megaterium reduced MDA and H2O2 levels by approximately 25% and 18%, respectively. Despite the increased Pb accumulation observed in BVOC‐exposed plants, these findings suggest that BVOCs effectively activate antioxidant defense mechanisms in plants. Notably, this effect was more pronounced in plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium , in which MDA and H2O2 levels decreased by approximately 45% and 35%, respectively. This result is consistent with the Pb accumulation patterns and suggests that the altered BVOC emission profile under Pb stress enhances plant tolerance to heavy metal‐induced oxidative stress.

Increase of Anti‐Oxidant Enzyme Levels in A. thaliana

Oxidative stress responses were evaluated based on the expression levels of catalase and ascorbate peroxidase to determine whether BVOCs can enhance oxidative stress‐defense mechanisms in A. thaliana (Figure 6). As expected, Pb‐treated A. thaliana plants not exposed to BVOCs showed up to approximately 1.6‐fold higher expression levels of catalase and ascorbate peroxidase than plants grown without Pb stress. In contrast, exposure to VOCs from either Pb‐stressed or non‐stressed B. megaterium further increased catalase expression by approximately 37%, suggesting that the observed changes in Pb accumulation and oxidative damage may be attributable, at least in part, to BVOC‐induced upregulation of antioxidant enzymes. Interestingly, ascorbate peroxidase expression was more than 25% higher in plants exposed to VOCs from Pb‐stressed B. megaterium than in those exposed to VOCs from the non‐stressed bacteria, indicating that stress‐induced changes in bacterial VOC emissions may exert stronger effects on plant stress‐defense mechanisms.

VOC‐Driven Cooperation Between B. megaterium and Arabidopsis

To determine whether B. megaterium also benefits from its interaction with A. thaliana under the conditions described above, the physical barrier used to ensure that BVOCs were the only factor affecting A. thaliana was removed, as shown in Figure 7. CFUs of B. megaterium were then assessed under this condition, based on the hypothesis that enhanced Pb uptake by plants might reduce bacterial Pb stress and thereby promote bacterial proliferation. As expected, under the barrier‐separated condition, Pb‐stressed B. megaterium showed approximately 5% lower log10‐transformed CFUs than the unstressed bacteria, likely due to Pb toxicity. In contrast, this reduction in bacterial population was no longer observed when the physical barrier was removed, indicating that Pb uptake by plants may reduce the amount of Pb remaining in the agar medium and thereby alleviate Pb toxicity to the bacteria. Although the residual Pb content in the agar after plant cultivation was not measured, the enhanced Pb uptake by A. thaliana exposed to VOCs emitted by Pb‐stressed B. megaterium (Figure 3) provides support for the proposed mechanism. Because VOCs emitted by Pb‐stressed B. megaterium had more pronounced effects on enhancing growth and oxidative stress‐defense responses in Pb‐stressed A. thaliana , these findings suggest that BVOCs may mediate cooperative interactions between bacteria and plants under stress conditions (Figure 8).

Discussion

Given the well‐documented stimulatory effects of BVOCs on plant growth and tolerance to salt stress (Farag et al. 2013), it is unsurprising that VOCs emitted by B. megaterium enhance the growth of A. thaliana under Pb stress. Ethylene‐ and salicylic acid‐mediated signalling pathways have been reported to play pivotal roles in BVOC‐induced systemic resistance in plants (Ryu et al. 2004; Kong et al. 2018). Because both pathways are known to promote the upregulation of antioxidant enzymes (El‐Esawi et al. 2017; Gautam et al. 2022), the observed increases in catalase and ascorbate peroxidase activities may be attributed to the activation of these signalling pathways. Moreover, the enhanced accumulation of heavy metals suggests that BVOC application may have potential for the phytoremediation of contaminated soils and that bioaugmentation with VOC‐emitting bacteria could improve remediation efficiency.

Although the stress‐related physiology of B. megaterium has been well characterised, stress‐induced changes in its VOC emissions remain poorly explored. The distinct VOC profiles observed under Pb stress are consistent with previous reports showing that bacterial VOC patterns are strongly influenced by microbial growth stage and substrate conditions (Misztal et al. 2018). In addition, this phenomenon is in line with previous findings that metal stress can induce major reorientations of central metabolism in bacteria (Luche et al. 2016). Accordingly, Pb‐induced reprogramming of bacterial metabolism may underlie the observed changes in VOC emissions. This notion is further supported by comparative transcriptomic analysis between normal and Pb‐stressed B. megaterium , which showed that several genes involved in organic acid production and organonitrogen metabolism were upregulated in the presence of Pb stress. The enhancement of several metabolic processes under Pb stress also suggests that VOC production may be linked to a hormesis‐like response, in which low‐level toxicity stimulates specific biological processes (Fan et al. 2021). Thus, connecting toxicity‐defense pathways to VOC emission patterns in bacteria may offer a novel strategy for modulating plant responses. The upregulation of metabolic genes directly related to 2‐butanone and 1,3‐propanediol biosynthesis was not identified. Therefore, further studies involving genetic manipulation of such target genes will be needed to clarify the functional importance of specific VOC production under Pb stress.

The dependence of plant responses on BVOC composition has been demonstrated in virus‐infected pepper plants (Kong et al. 2018), suggesting that the specific sets of plant genes upregulated by BVOCs may vary depending on VOC structure and blend composition. BVOCs with different chemical structures may interact with plant components in distinct ways, thereby inducing different physiological responses in plants. Based on this concept, Pb‐induced reprogramming of bacterial carbon and nitrogen metabolism may be linked to distinct plant responses. Understanding how changes in bacterial metabolisms affect their own VOC profiles will thus be important for advancing our understanding of BVOC‐mediated bacteria–plant communication. Therefore, future studies should focus on elucidating bacterial metabolic fluxes and their relationship with VOC profiles using metabolomic approaches.

The observed association between improved survival of B. megaterium under Pb stress and enhanced Pb uptake by A. thaliana suggests that BVOCs may increase bacterial fitness by modulating plant stress‐defense responses. Because BVOCs can act over distance, this strategy may enable bacteria to influence other organisms in their surrounding environment more efficiently. The cooperative interaction observed here further supports the hypothesis that the capacity of micro‐organisms to emit diverse VOCs has been evolutionarily favoured owing to the adaptive advantages associated with modulating the physiology of other organisms. This interpretation is in line with experimental evidence demonstrating that novel metabolic capabilities can emerge through natural selection when they confer ecological and physiological fitness benefits (Blount et al. 2008). Although this study focused solely on heavy‐metal stress conditions, other biological contexts, such as nutrient acquisition and pathogen infection, may provide additional opportunities to investigate cooperative interactions between VOC‐emitting bacteria and plants. Overall, our findings partially address the question of why bacteria emit VOCs towards plants and underscore the need for further research on the fitness benefits that VOC emission confers on bacteria themselves.

Limitations of This Study

Although our proposed mechanism is supported by the experimental data, other factors may also have contributed to the observed phenomenon. First, plants can emit VOCs that influence bacterial behaviour and physiology (Schulz‐Bohm et al. 2018). Therefore, under our experimental conditions, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that plant‐derived VOCs, emitted in the presence or absence of Pb stress, affected the Pb‐stressed bacteria. Profiling VOCs emitted by A. thaliana under Pb‐stressed and non‐stressed conditions would help clarify this possibility.

Second, root exudates containing organic carbon sources for heterotrophic microorganisms may have promoted microbial proliferation on the agar in the absence of a physical barrier. Therefore, enhanced Pb uptake by the plants may not have been the sole factor alleviating Pb stress in B. megaterium . Profiling organic compounds in A. thaliana root exudates under Pb‐stressed and non‐stressed conditions would further contribute to understanding the BVOC‐initiated reciprocal interactions between plants and bacteria.
