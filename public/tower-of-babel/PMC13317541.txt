Diagnostic Value of Pupil Involvement and Yield of Vascular Imaging in Isolated Third Cranial Nerve Palsy: A 10-Year Retrospective Study
Authors: Panwad Uthiyo, Yuwares Chantem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIsolated third cranial nerve palsy may result from presumed microvascular ischemia or clinically significant structural lesions. The reliability of pupil involvement for distinguishing non-microvascular causes remains uncertain.ObjectiveTo describe the etiologic spectrum of isolated third cranial nerve palsy and evaluate the diagnostic performance of pupil involvement and the yield of neuroimaging.MethodsThis retrospective observational study reviewed adult patients with isolated third cranial nerve palsy at Mukdahan Hospital, Thailand, between January 2015 and August 2025. Patients with traumatic, postoperative, recurrent, multiple cranial nerve palsies, or incomplete records were excluded. Clinical features, neuroimaging findings, etiologies, and outcomes were analyzed.ResultsOf 61 records assessed, 51 patients were included. Mean age was 62.3 ± 8.7 years, and 26 patients (51.0%) were male. Presumed microvascular ischemia was the most common etiology (42/51, 82.4%). Non-microvascular causes were identified in 9 patients (17.6%), including aneurysm in 6, nasopharyngeal carcinoma in 1, cavernous sinus meningioma in 1, and invasive aspergillosis in 1. Pupil involvement was more frequent in the non-microvascular group than in the presumed microvascular group (66.7% vs 23.8%, p = 0.020). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were 66.7%, 76.2%, 37.5%, and 91.4%, respectively. In multivariable analysis, pupil involvement remained independently associated with non-microvascular etiology (adjusted OR 8.00, 95% CI 1.49–42.85, p = 0.015). The diagnostic yield of neuroimaging was 18.8% overall and 25.0% among patients undergoing vascular imaging.ConclusionPupil involvement was associated with non-microvascular etiology but had only moderate diagnostic performance; pupil sparing did not reliably exclude clinically significant lesions. These findings suggest that vascular imaging may be clinically informative when structural causes cannot be confidently excluded, particularly given that all non-microvascular cases in this cohort were identified through vascular imaging.

Introduction

Isolated third cranial nerve (CN3) palsy is a common neuro-ophthalmic condition with etiologies ranging from self-limited microvascular ischemia to life-threatening compressive lesions such as intracranial aneurysm, neoplasm, or invasive infection.1–4 Early identification of serious causes is critical, as delayed diagnosis may lead to significant morbidity or mortality.

Pupillary involvement has traditionally been used to distinguish compressive from ischemic etiologies, with pupil-involving palsy suggesting compression and pupil-sparing palsy suggesting microvascular ischemia.5–7 However, accumulating evidence indicates that this distinction is not absolute, as clinically significant lesions, including posterior communicating artery aneurysms, can occur in pupil-sparing cases.8,9 Pupillary status alone may therefore be insufficient for reliable etiologic differentiation.

Advances in vascular neuroimaging, particularly computed tomography angiography (CTA) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), have led to increasing advocacy for early imaging in isolated CN3 palsy,10,11 with prospective studies reporting a non-negligible proportion of non-microvascular etiologies even among patients with presumed microvascular profiles.2,4,12,13 In a contemporary cohort study of 47 patients evaluated between 2010 and 2019, causative pathology was identified on neuroimaging in 60% of cases, with aneurysm alone accounting for 40% of the cohort, supporting the case for routine vascular imaging regardless of pupil status.14 Nevertheless, clinical practice remains variable, and some patients, particularly older individuals with vascular risk factors and pupil-sparing presentations, continue to be managed conservatively without immediate imaging.

Uncertainty therefore persists regarding whether all patients with isolated CN3 palsy require neuroimaging, the diagnostic yield of such imaging, and the reliability of pupillary involvement in guiding imaging decisions. Furthermore, most published data derive from Western tertiary centers, and regional epidemiological data from Southeast Asia remain limited. The primary novelty of the present study is the provision of contemporaneous data on both the diagnostic performance of pupillary involvement and the yield of vascular imaging in a Thai tertiary care cohort, while also characterizing the regional etiologic spectrum. This study aimed to describe the clinical characteristics and etiologic spectrum of isolated CN3 palsy in a tertiary care setting, evaluate the diagnostic performance of pupillary involvement in identifying non-microvascular etiologies, and assess the diagnostic yield of neuroimaging in this population.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Mukdahan Hospital, a tertiary care hospital in Mukdahan Province, Thailand. Medical records of patients diagnosed with isolated oculomotor nerve (CN3) palsy between January 2015 and August 2025 were reviewed.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Mukdahan Hospital (approval number: MEC25/69). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were anonymized and handled in accordance with applicable data protection regulations to ensure confidentiality. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eligibility Criteria

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met all of the following criteria: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) diagnosis of isolated CN3 palsy made by an ophthalmologist or neurologist; (3) clinical features consistent with unilateral CN3 palsy, defined as ptosis and/or ophthalmoplegia involving abnormal ocular movement in adduction, infraduction, supraduction, or cycloduction, with or without pupillary involvement; and (4) no concurrent abnormalities of other cranial nerves.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) third cranial nerve palsy with involvement of other cranial nerves, including the fourth or sixth cranial nerve; (2) recurrent third cranial nerve palsy, in which only the first episode would otherwise have been eligible; (3) traumatic or postoperative third cranial nerve palsy; and (4) incomplete medical records, defined as missing more than 50% of prespecified study variables.

A total of 61 records were assessed for eligibility. Ten patients were excluded, including 2 with traumatic CN3 palsy, 2 with multiple cranial nerve palsies, and 6 who did not meet the diagnostic criteria for CN3 palsy. Data Collection

Data were extracted from electronic medical records using a standardized case record form. The following variables were collected: demographic characteristics (age and sex); vascular risk factors (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking history); clinical features (laterality, symptom duration, periocular pain, headache, foul nasal odor, visual loss, and proptosis); ophthalmic examination findings (ptosis severity, pupil status, and pattern of ophthalmoplegia); neuroimaging data (imaging modality and findings); and clinical outcomes (initial diagnosis, final diagnosis, treatment, and recovery status at the last follow-up visit).

Definitions

Isolated CN3 palsy was defined as unilateral oculomotor nerve palsy without involvement of other cranial nerves, orbital disease, or clinical signs of raised intracranial pressure.

Pupil-involving palsy was defined as anisocoria of at least 1 mm, with the affected pupil being larger and having reduced or absent light reaction. Pupil-sparing palsy was defined as anisocoria of less than 1 mm.

Presumed microvascular third cranial nerve palsy was defined as isolated palsy in a patient with vascular risk factors, no structural lesion identified on vascular imaging or clinical work-up when performed, no aberrant regeneration during follow-up, and spontaneous clinical recovery during follow-up consistent with a microvascular course. It is acknowledged that spontaneous recovery is included both as a diagnostic criterion and as a study outcome; etiologic classification was performed prospectively during clinical care before final outcome assessment, and patients who did not recover or who developed new features underwent further investigation and may have been reassigned to non-microvascular etiologies accordingly.

Complete recovery was defined as complete resolution of ptosis and ophthalmoplegia, with normalization of pupil function when previously abnormal. Time to recovery was defined as time to documented complete recovery at follow-up visits. Ptosis severity was dichotomized into partial ptosis and complete ptosis based on clinical documentation in the medical record. Partial ptosis included cases previously classified as mild or moderate ptosis, whereas complete ptosis referred to complete upper eyelid drooping. Recovery status at the last documented visit was categorized as complete recovery, incomplete recovery, or referred for further management.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 19.5 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR), depending on distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Categorical variables were summarized as frequency and percentage.

Comparisons between the microvascular and non-microvascular groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U-test for age. The diagnostic performance of pupil involvement for identifying non-microvascular etiology was assessed by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), likelihood ratios, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of non-microvascular etiology. Variables with p < 0.10 on univariable analysis, or those considered clinically relevant, were considered for inclusion in the multivariable model. Pupil involvement and pain were selected for multivariable analysis based on clinical relevance and univariable association. Diabetes mellitus, although inversely associated on univariable analysis, was not included in the final model because of the small number of non-microvascular events and the risk of overfitting. The final multivariable model was intentionally parsimonious to reduce model instability. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 51 patients with isolated third cranial nerve palsy were included in the final analysis. The mean age was 62.3 ± 8.7 years, and the median age was 63 years (IQR, 55–68; range, 49–85). Twenty-six patients (51.0%) were male.

Diabetes mellitus was present in 37 patients (72.6%), hypertension in 36 (70.6%), and dyslipidemia in 16 (31.4%). Pain was reported in 31 patients (60.8%). Ptosis was classified as partial in 15 patients (29.4%) and complete in 36 patients (70.6%). Pupil involvement was observed in 16 patients (31.4%). Clinical Outcomes

At the last documented follow-up, 34 patients (66.7%) had complete recovery and 8 patients (15.7%) had incomplete recovery. Nine patients (17.7%) were referred for further management and did not have final outcome assessment available at our center.

Kaplan–Meier analysis in patients with presumed microvascular third cranial nerve palsy demonstrated progressive documented complete recovery over time (Figure 2). Imaging Yield

The overall diagnostic yield of neuroimaging for identifying non-microvascular causes was 18.8% (9/48), corresponding to a number needed to image (NNI) of 5.3.

Among the 36 patients who underwent vascular imaging (CTA or MRA), the diagnostic yield for identifying non-microvascular etiologies was 25.0% (9/36), corresponding to an NNI of 4.0. Notably, all non-microvascular cases in this cohort were identified in patients who underwent vascular imaging.

Among clinically significant lesions, aneurysm was detected in 6 patients, yielding 16.7% (6/36) among those who underwent vascular imaging, corresponding to an NNI of 6.0. Discussion

In this retrospective study over a 10-year period, presumed microvascular ischemia was the most common etiology of isolated third cranial nerve palsy, accounting for more than four-fifths of cases. However, non-microvascular causes were identified in nearly one-fifth of patients, indicating that clinically significant structural lesions remain an important consideration in the evaluation of isolated CN3 palsy. This overall pattern is consistent with prior series showing that microvascular disease is a common cause of acquired ocular motor nerve palsy, while aneurysm, tumor, and other structural lesions remain important alternative diagnoses.1–4

Aneurysm was the most common non-microvascular cause in this cohort. This finding is clinically important because aneurysmal third nerve palsy is a potentially life-threatening condition requiring urgent recognition and treatment.5,10,11,15 In our study, aneurysm accounted for 11.8% of all isolated CN3 palsy cases and two-thirds of the non-microvascular group, emphasizing that a substantial minority of patients may harbor serious underlying pathology, in keeping with prior reports.3,10,15

Among clinical characteristics, pupil involvement was the only variable that significantly differed between groups and remained independently associated with non-microvascular etiology in multivariable analysis. It was present in 66.7% of non-microvascular cases compared with 23.8% of microvascular cases (p = 0.020). The diagnostic performance of pupil involvement was moderate, with an AUC of 0.71. Although specificity was acceptable and the negative predictive value was high, the sensitivity was only 66.7%, indicating that absence of pupil involvement could not reliably exclude non-microvascular causes. Notably, one-third of patients with non-microvascular etiologies had pupil-sparing palsy. This is a critical limitation: a sensitivity of only 66.7% means that relying on pupil status alone as a gating criterion for imaging would miss approximately one in three non-microvascular cases, including potentially life-threatening aneurysms. The high negative predictive value of 91.4% should be interpreted with caution because it is strongly influenced by the relatively low prevalence of non-microvascular disease (17.6%) in this cohort. In contrast, the positive LR of 2.80 and negative LR of 0.44 are more informative in this setting because they are independent of prevalence and indicate only modest diagnostic discrimination.

In contrast, other clinical variables, including age, sex, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, pain, and ptosis, did not significantly differ between groups. The median age was similar between microvascular and non-microvascular patients (62.5 [56–68] vs 66 [51–67]; p = 0.990), and vascular risk factors were common in both groups. These findings highlight the substantial clinical overlap between presumed microvascular and non-microvascular CN3 palsy and underscore the limitations of relying solely on clinical features to guide etiologic classification.

These findings support the view that pupil status alone should not be used to rule out serious structural lesions in isolated CN3 palsy. In settings with a high burden of vascular risk factors, such as our cohort, the clinical picture of non-microvascular palsy may overlap substantially with presumed microvascular disease.16 Broad use of neuroimaging, particularly vascular imaging, should therefore be considered, especially when red flags or atypical clinical features are present.5,10,11,17

The diagnostic yield of neuroimaging in our cohort was 18.8% (9 of 48 patients), with a number needed to image (NNI) of 5.3 to identify one non-microvascular case. This yield is consistent with prior prospective studies reporting that a clinically meaningful proportion of patients with isolated acute ocular motor nerve palsies harbored non-microvascular causes identified on early neuroimaging.12,13 The overall yield in our cohort was lower than the 60% reported by Patel and Burdon in a contemporary UK cohort,14 a difference that likely reflects the higher proportion of presumed microvascular cases in our population—driven in part by the high prevalence of diabetes mellitus (72.6%) and hypertension (70.6%)—as well as differences in referral patterns and case mix between a Southeast Asian tertiary center and a Western neuro-ophthalmology unit. Among patients who underwent vascular imaging (CTA or MRA), the diagnostic yield increased to 25.0% (9 of 36), corresponding to an NNI of 4.0. For clinically significant lesions such as aneurysm, the yield was 16.7% (6 of 36), with an NNI of 6.0. Furthermore, one-third of non-microvascular cases in our series presented with pupil-sparing palsy, reinforcing that selective imaging based on pupil status alone would have missed a substantial proportion of structural lesions. These findings suggest that vascular imaging may be particularly useful in patients with isolated third cranial nerve palsy, especially when clinical red flags or atypical features are present. However, because imaging modality was selected in routine practice rather than according to a standardized protocol, the observed diagnostic yield should be interpreted with caution.10–13

Importantly, all non-microvascular etiologies in this study were identified exclusively in patients who underwent vascular imaging (CTA or MRA), whereas no clinically significant lesions were detected using non-vascular imaging alone. Conventional CT and contrast-enhanced CT are limited in their ability to detect vascular lesions such as intracranial aneurysm, which may explain the absence of clinically significant findings in the non-vascular imaging group. These findings are consistent with current recommendations that emphasize the role of vascular imaging in the evaluation of cranial neuropathies when compressive or vascular etiologies are suspected.11 Beyond standard vascular imaging, advanced MRI techniques such as 3D gadolinium-enhanced constructive interference in steady-state (CISS) sequences have recently been reported to help confirm a microvascular etiology when conventional imaging is unrevealing,17 although such techniques were not available in our setting.

Kaplan–Meier analysis suggested that most patients with microvascular CN3 palsy achieved documented complete recovery within the first several months of follow-up. The estimated median time to documented complete recovery was 4.8 months. However, the estimated recovery time should be interpreted as time to documented recovery rather than exact biological time to recovery, because some patients may have recovered before their scheduled follow-up visit. This pattern is broadly compatible with previous reports that vasculopathic third nerve palsy often improves spontaneously over time.1,10

The case of invasive aspergillosis in our series is particularly noteworthy. The patient initially appeared to fit a vasculopathic profile, yet persistent symptoms and foul nasal odor ultimately led to the correct diagnosis. This case underscores the importance of recognizing atypical features, such as persistent symptoms and foul nasal odor, as potential warning signs of invasive fungal sinusitis with orbital apex or cavernous sinus extension. Early investigation is crucial, as delayed diagnosis may have serious consequences.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Second, the sample size was relatively small, particularly for the non-microvascular subgroup, limiting the statistical power of subgroup analyses and the stability of multivariable estimates. Third, follow-up duration was not uniform across patients. Fourth, outcome assessment was incomplete for referred patients. Fifth, the allocation of imaging modality was based on real-world clinical practice rather than a standardized protocol; therefore, the observed differences in diagnostic yield between vascular and non-vascular imaging may reflect selection bias rather than a direct effect of modality. Sixth, neuroimaging was performed in 94.1% of patients, but not all underwent vascular imaging; accordingly, the study cannot completely exclude the possibility that some patients classified as having presumed microvascular palsy harboured undetected structural lesions. Finally, recovery time may have been overestimated because complete recovery could have occurred before the scheduled follow-up visit. Accordingly, the Kaplan–Meier estimates in this study represent time to documented recovery at follow-up rather than the exact time of recovery.

Despite these limitations, this study provides clinically useful data on the etiologic spectrum of isolated CN3 palsy in a tertiary care setting in Thailand and highlights the limited reliability of pupil sparing for excluding non-microvascular causes.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study of isolated third cranial nerve palsy, presumed microvascular ischemia was the most common etiology, while non-microvascular causes accounted for 17.6% of cases. Aneurysm was the most frequent non-microvascular lesion.

Pupil involvement was independently associated with non-microvascular etiology and demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance. However, pupil-sparing palsy did not reliably exclude non-microvascular causes.

These findings highlight the substantial clinical overlap between etiologies and suggest that vascular imaging may be particularly informative in the evaluation of isolated third cranial nerve palsy, particularly in the presence of clinical red flags or atypical features. Although this retrospective study does not directly compare universal with selective imaging strategies, the results indicate that clinicians may reasonably consider vascular imaging when structural causes cannot be confidently excluded on clinical grounds alone. In this cohort, vascular imaging identified all detected non-microvascular etiologies and demonstrated a diagnostic yield of 25.0% among imaged patients. Reliance on clinical features alone, including pupil status, may be insufficient for accurate etiologic differentiation.
