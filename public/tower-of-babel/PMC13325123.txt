Diagenesis and Petroleum Significance of Veins in Marine Carbonate: A Case Study from the Subsalt Ordovician Fourth Member of the Majiagou Formation, Ordos Basin, China
Authors: Wenyi Sun, Dongdong Zhang, Xiaoyan Chen, Qianping Wang, Tong Wang, Fengjiao Li, Mingyang Ma, Xiaohan Guan, Xiaofeng Wang, Wenhui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluid activity in marine carbonate strata is commonly recorded by calcite veins, which are important archives of diagenesis and hydrocarbon charging. However, the origin and evolution of veins in subsalt carbonate reservoirs remain poorly constrained. This study investigates calcite veins from the subsalt Fourth Member of the Ordovician Majiagou Formation (O2m4) in the central-eastern Ordos Basin, China. Petrography, carbon and oxygen isotopes, in-situ trace elements, and fluid inclusion studies were integrated to constrain vein-forming fluids, vein evolution, and their relationship with hydrocarbon charging. The results show that the veins were predominantly formed during a single precipitation stage and that their parent fluids were mainly host-rock-buffered burial fluids, with limited contribution from adjacent strata. These veins were subsequently modified to a limited extent by later hydrocarbon-bearing fluids. Fluid inclusion assemblages and homogenization temperatures indicate that hydrocarbon activity recorded by the O2m4 vein system can be divided into three stages: (a) limited early hydrocarbon charging during shallow burial, (b) widespread oil charging during the Late Triassic to Early Jurassic, and (c) later cracking of previously accumulated oil to gas during the Early Cretaceous. The hydrocarbon records preserved in O2m4 therefore indicate mature-oil charging followed by later oil cracking. This study provides new constraints on the coupling among vein formation, fluid evolution, and hydrocarbon accumulation in subsalt marine carbonate reservoirs and offers implications for natural gas exploration in the Majiagou Formation.

Introduction

Fluid activity is widespread in marine carbonate strata, where frequent fluid migration commonly promotes vein formation. As key products of tectono-diagenesis in petroliferous basins, veins record episodes of paleofluid activity and preserve information about fluid properties. In marine carbonate strata, secondary calcite, a key product of subsurface fluid activities, occurs throughout all stages of diagenesis, reservoir formation, and hydrocarbon accumulation. Fluid inclusions, commonly found in calcite, are subsurface fluids trapped within lattice defects during mineral formation. These inclusions preserve original information about fluid composition, pressure, and temperature, providing key constraints on the processes of diagenetic evolution and hydrocarbon migration.

Accordingly, petrography, stable isotopes, cathodoluminescence (CL), in situ elemental analysis, and fluid inclusion studies have been widely applied to characterize the geochemical signatures of secondary calcite and the paleofluid history it records. Recent reviews have further shown that petroleum inclusions can record paleo-petroleum compositions and charging processes when optical, spectroscopic, and geochemical methods are integrated. Studies from other carbonate reservoirs have shown that calcite veins and associated inclusions can provide important constraints on fluid sources, diagenetic evolution, and hydrocarbon charging. In the Shunnan–Gucheng area of the Tarim Basin, calcite veins were used to evaluate fluid sources and their implications for hydrocarbon accumulation. Similarly, fracture-cavity veins in the Silurian Shiniulan Formation of the southeast Sichuan Basin were used to reconstruct mineral sequences, fluid sources, and paleopressure evolution. In subsalt carbonate reservoirs, vein and inclusion data from the Amu Darya Right Bank Block further helped distinguish four diagenetic periods and three hydrocarbon accumulation periods. Taken together, these studies show that carbonate vein systems are valuable archives of fluid evolution and hydrocarbon charging.

Given its considerable hydrocarbon potential, the Majiagou Formation is a key target for gas exploration in the Ordos Basin. Recently, intensified exploration in subsalt reservoir units of the Majiagou Formation has revealed high-volume industrial gas flows in wildcat wells such as MT1, which targets the fourth member of the Majiagou Formation (abbreviated as O2m4). This discovery demonstrates that the dolomites of O2m4 have favorable exploration potential. Previous studies on O2m4 have centered on its lithofacies paleogeography, , dolomite reservoir development, and natural gas genesis and sources. Research on fluid activities within the Majiagou Formation has mainly addressed hydrothermal activities in southern portions of the basin and fluid activities within the suprasalt weathering crust. Despite these advances, research specifically focused on the subsalt O2m4 vein system in the central-eastern Ordos Basin remains limited. Several key aspects of this system are still poorly constrained, including the origin of vein-forming fluids, the diagenetic setting and timing of vein precipitation, and the relationship between vein evolution and multistage hydrocarbon charging. These veins may record not only host-rock dissolution and burial fluid circulation but also later hydrocarbon emplacement and postcharge alteration. Therefore, the limited understanding of the subsalt O2m4 vein system hinders a more complete reconstruction of fluid evolution and hydrocarbon accumulation in the O2m4 reservoir.

To address this limitation, this study focuses on calcite-dominated veins in marine carbonates of the subsalt O2m4 interval from representative wells in the central-eastern Ordos Basin. Here, we evaluate the coupling among vein evolution, fluid activity, and hydrocarbon charging, thereby providing new constraints on the diagenetic evolution and petroleum significance of the O2m4 reservoir. By integrating petrographic observations, carbon and oxygen isotope analyses, in situ trace element data, and fluid inclusion studies, we aim to (1) constrain the source and nature of vein-forming fluids; (2) reconstruct the diagenetic timing and paragenetic sequence of calcite veins; and (3) establish the coupling among fluid activity and hydrocarbon charging history.

Geological Setting

The Majiagou Formation constitutes a sedimentary system characterized by carbonate-evaporite association. According to distinct lithological characteristics, the Majiagou Formation is stratigraphically divided into six members (O2m1 to O2m6), with O2m5 further subdivided into 10 substages downward (O2m5 1–10). During the Majiagou period, three large-scale transgressive-regressive cycles occurred. The regressive phases, deposits of O2m1, O2m3, and O2m5, developed restricted platforms and evaporitic platforms dominated by interbedded dolomite and evaporite. In contrast, the transgressive phases, O2m2, O2m4, and O2m6, were dominated by open marine environments, consisting mainly of limestone intercalated with dolomite of variable thickness.

O2m4 has been divided into three substages from top to bottom: O2m4 1, O2m4 2, and O2m4 3. The upper O2m4 1 consists of marine transgressive deposits dominated by limestone and dolomite. The middle-lower section of O2m4 1 and O2m4 2 consists of regressive marine deposits, primarily dolomite, calcareous dolomite, dolomitic limestone, and limestone interbeds. The middle-lower part of O2m4 1 also features dolomitic marlstone and marly dolomite, interbedded with thin layers of dolomite, calcareous dolomite, and dolomitic limestone. O2m4 3 is composed of transgressive deposits primarily consisting of limestone and dolomitic limestone, interbedded with thin layers of calcareous dolomite and dolomite (Figure b). Previous reservoir studies show that O2m4 dolomite reservoirs are primarily distributed along the Central Paleouplift and the Shenmu-Zhidan Low Paleouplift, thinning toward the central depression of the basin. Compared with the Central Paleouplift, the O2m4 reservoir developed within the Shenmu-Zhidan Low Paleouplift, occupying a tectonic-elevated position with favorable trap conditions. This environment promotes natural gas migration and entrapment, presenting excellent exploration prospects (Figure a).

Samples

Analytical Methods

Petrographic Observation and Cathodoluminescence

Petrographic observation and mineral identification were performed on polished thin sections by using a Nikon ECLIPSE LV100N polarizing microscope. Thin sections were prepared from representative vein-bearing domains to preserve the textural relationships among veins, pore-filling minerals, and the adjacent carbonate matrix. Mineral assemblages, cross-cutting relationships, inclusion occurrences, and vein-host-rock contacts were documented under plane-polarized and cross-polarized light.

Cathodoluminescence (CL) observations were conducted using a CLF-1 D28 cathodoluminescence microscope, operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, a beam current of 300 μA, and a vacuum of 0.03 mbar. CL images were used to compare luminescence characteristics between vein calcite, pore-filling calcite, and carbonate matrix and to help identify possible calcite generations and paragenetic relationships.

Carbon and Oxygen Isotopes

Carbon and oxygen isotope analyses were performed using a Thermo Scientific 253 Plus isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS) equipped with a GasBench II system, following the analytical procedure of Li et al. Based on petrographic observations, optically clean domains of vein calcite, dissolution pore-filling calcite, and carbonate matrix were microsampled using a microdrill, avoiding visible impurities, sulfate minerals, and bitumen-rich areas. The powder was ground to 200 mesh, and approximately 0.3 mg of each sample was reacted with phosphoric acid at 70 °C for 2 h. The generated CO2 was introduced into the IRMS using He as the carrier gas. Isotopic results are reported relative to the Vienna Pee Dee Belemnite (VPDB) standard and calibrated using the Chinese reference material GBW04405. One reference standard was analyzed for every seven samples to monitor instrumental drift. Analytical precision for both δ13C and δ18O was better than ±0.1‰, and repeated analyses of the reference material yielded a standard deviation of less than 0.08‰.

LA–ICP–MS

Quantification of trace elements in carbonate minerals was conducted by laser ablation–inductively coupled plasma–mass spectrometry (LA–ICP–MS) using a 193 nm ArF excimer laser ablation system (RESOLution S155-LR, ASI) coupled to an Agilent 7900 ICP–MS. Based on petrographic observations, clean, homogeneous, and contamination-free areas were selected for analysis, and the petrographic occurrence of each analytical spot was recorded. The analyses were conducted using a laser beam of 100 μm in diameter and 6 Hz frequency, with a He flow of 0.28 L/min and an Ar flow of 1.16L/min. Each spot analysis consisted of background collection for 20 s, followed by an additional 45 s of ablation for signal acquisition and 50 s of wash time to reduce the memory effect. NIST 610, NIST 612, BCR-2G, and BHVO-2G were used as reference materials to calibrate the element compositions of carbonates. The CGSP-A reference material was used as the unknown sample to verify the accuracy of the calibration method, and the obtained results agreed with recommended values within ±15% for the majority of trace elements. The concentrations of all elements used for interpretation in this study were above their respective limits of detection. The relative standard deviations (RSDs) for all elements were <10% on the reference materials (NIST 610, NIST 612, BCR-2G, BHVO-2G, and CGSP-A). A series of procedures, including off-line signal selection and integration, time-drift correction, and quantitative calibration, were carried out using the specialized software ICPMSDataCal 10.8. The analytical approach was similar to that outlined in Zhian et al.

Fluorescence Observation and Raman Spectroscopy

Fluorescence observations of fluid inclusions and bitumen were conducted on doubly polished fluid inclusion wafers using an Olympus BX51 transmitted-reflected fluorescence polarizing microscope. Blue light excitation at 460–490 nm and ultraviolet excitation at 330–385 nm were used to distinguish hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions and bitumen with different fluorescence characteristics.

Laser Raman analyses of aqueous inclusions, hydrocarbon inclusions, and bitumen were conducted using a HORIBA LabRAM Odyssey Raman spectrometer. Well-preserved inclusions and bitumen domains were selected from doubly polished fluid inclusion wafers based on petrographic and fluorescence observations. A 532 nm laser was used for excitation. Wavenumber calibration was performed using a Si wafer, and the calibrated Raman shift of the Si peak was 521.09 cm–1. Spectra were collected using a 50× objective over the range of 100–4000 cm–1, with a spot size of approximately 1–2 μm, an integration time of 3 s, and one accumulation. Laser power was adjusted during analysis to reduce fluorescence interference and avoid thermal damage.

Fluid Inclusion Microthermometry

Fluid inclusion microthermometric measurements were performed on doubly polished fluid inclusion wafers using a Linkam THMSG600 heating-freezing stage mounted on a ZEISS Axioscope 5 microscope, following the procedure of Liu et al. Well-preserved aqueous inclusions with clearly visible vapor bubbles were selected for analysis. Before measurements, the stage was calibrated using artificial pure H2O inclusions and 25% H2O–CO2 fluid inclusion international standards. During homogenization measurements, inclusions were heated at 20 °C/min, and the heating rate was reduced to 5 °C/min when the vapor bubble approached disappearance. The temperature at which the vapor bubble completely disappeared was recorded as the homogenization temperature (Th). Freezing runs were attempted by cooling the inclusions to −100 °C at 30 °C/min and then reheating them at 10 °C/min. Near final ice melting, the heating rate was reduced to 3 °C/min, and the final ice-melting temperature (Tm) was recorded when observable. The THMSG600 has an operating temperature range from −195 to 600 °C, a temperature stability of <0.01 °C, and a temperature resolution for fluid inclusion characterization better than 0.1 °C. Repeated measurements were performed on selected inclusions, where possible, to check analytical reproducibility.

Results

Petrography and Mineralogy

The subsalt carbonate samples from O2m4 in the study area mainly consist of gray to gray-black massive limestone and dolomite, with variable degrees of dolomitization observed under the microscope. Reservoir spaces are dominated by fractures and dissolution pores (Figure a,b), which are mainly filled with calcite and anhydrite, locally with some pyrite (Figure d,e,f), and minor bitumen. Calcite veins are commonly white and exhibit significant variations in their width.

Calcite is the main filling mineral in the veins and dissolution pores of O2m4, coexisting with dolomite, anhydrite, pyrite, and quartz in the mineral assemblage (Figure d,e,f). Under CL microscopy, calcite exhibits fuchsia and orange-red luminescence. Despite local variations and subtle zonation, calcite in veins and pore-filling cements shows broadly similar CL characteristics (Figure j,k,l). Numerous bitumen inclusions are present within calcite and locally fill intercrystalline spaces (Figure c). Under CL microscopy, dolomite emits magenta luminescence (Figure j,k), predominantly distributed in the matrix surrounding calcite veins. It forms well-developed idiomorphic crystals that grow within the matrix and along the margins of the secondary calcite. Granular or tabular-prismatic anhydrite is embedded in calcite crystals (Figure d,e). In well S168, anhydrite intensely dissolves calcite within veins, leaving only minor calcite remnants (Figure i,l). Pyrite occurs in a stippled pattern at vein-matrix interfaces (Figure f), predominantly as subhedral grains. Additionally, a few idiomorphic, nonluminescent minerals embedded within calcite have been identified as quartz via laser Raman spectroscopy (Figure h).

Geochemical Characteristics

Carbon and Oxygen Isotopes

Based on petrographic observation, representative samples of fracture veins, dissolution pore-filling cement, and carbonate matrix were carefully chosen for carbon and oxygen stable isotope analysis. A total of 52 measurements were obtained, including 15 from fracture veins, 17 from dissolution pore-filling cements, and 20 from carbonate matrix. The results (normalized to VPDB) show that calcite veins have δ13C values ranging from −2.3‰ to 0.7‰ (average: −0.6‰) and δ18O values ranging from −8.2‰ to −4.2‰ (average: −6.8‰). The δ13C values of dissolution pore-filling cements range from −2.6‰ to 1.3‰ (average: −0.4‰), and their δ18O values range from −8.4‰ to −4.5‰ (average: −6.3‰). Carbonate matrix has δ13C values ranging from −0.9‰ to 1.2‰ (average: −0.1‰) and δ18O values ranging from −7.7‰ to −3.4‰ (average: −5.9‰). Overall, fracture veins and dissolution pore-filling cements are slightly depleted in both δ13C and δ18O relative to the carbonate matrix, although the three groups show broadly overlapping isotopic ranges.

In-Situ Trace Elements of Calcite

Analysis of the contents of trace and rare earth elements (REEs) in secondary calcite and carbonate matrix was conducted via laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (LA–ICP–MS). A total of 39 measurements were obtained, including 20 from secondary calcite and 19 from the carbonate matrix. The results show that secondary calcite has a total content of rare earth elements (ΣREE) ranging from 0.06 × 10–6 to 83.09 × 10–6 (average: 10.54 × 10–6. The light REE (ΣLREE) content ranges from 0.02 × 10–6 to 76.36 × 10–6 (average: 9.26 × 10–6), middle REE (ΣMREE) from 0.02 × 10–6 to 5.41 × 10–6 (average: 1.00 × 10–6), and heavy REE (ΣHREE) from 0.01 × 10–6 to 1.32 × 10–6 (average: 0.28 × 10–6). Ce content varies from 0.01 × 10–6 to 37.68 × 10–6 with an average of 4.57 × 10–6, Ce anomalies (Ce/Ce*) range from 0.82 to 1.37 (mean: 1.02), Y ranges from 0.05 × 10–6 to 6.94 × 10–6 (mean: 1.55 × 10–6), and Y/Ho ratios vary between 21 and 78 (mean: 46).

Carbonate matrix has ΣREE ranging from 1.18 × 10–6 to 79.58 × 10–6, with an average of 15.09 × 10–6. ΣLREE varies from 1.00 × 10–6 to 72.59 × 10–6 (average: 13.49 × 10–6), ΣMREE from 0.14 × 10–6 to 5.92 × 10–6 (average: 1.30 × 10–6), and ΣHREE from 0.04 × 10–6 to 1.07 × 10–6 (average: 0.29 × 10–6). Ce content varies from 0.50 × 10–6 to 36.39 × 10–6 (average: 6.75 × 10–6), Ce anomalies (Ce/Ce*) range from 0.96 to 1.06 (mean: 1.00), Y ranges from 0.23 × 10–6 to 6.17 × 10–6 (mean: 1.59 × 10–6), and Y/Ho ratios vary between 27 and 56 (mean: 38). Secondary calcite and matrix samples show weak to absent Ce anomalies, whereas Y/Ho ratios are generally higher in secondary calcite than in the matrix.

REEs and Y are commonly discussed together as REYs because of their similar chemical properties. The REY content obtained from in situ elemental analysis of calcite was normalized (subscripted as SN) to PAAS (Postarchean Average Australian Shale) values. The PAAS-normalized REY patterns (REYSN; Figure ) show that most carbonate matrix samples exhibit relatively flat distribution patterns, with only one sample displaying a slight HREE depletion (Figure a). The REESN distribution patterns of some secondary calcites are also flat and comparable to those of the carbonate matrix (Figure b), while a few exhibit LREE depletion yet similar HREE content (Figure c). Furthermore, both secondary calcite and carbonate matrices show either no or only slight CeSN anomalies, slight negative PrSN and GdSN anomalies, and pronounced negative EuSN anomalies.

Inclusion Characteristics

Inclusion Petrography and Fluorescence Characteristics

Observations reveal that the secondary calcite from O2m4 within the central-eastern Ordos Basin hosts abundant fluid inclusions and a large number of bitumen inclusions (Figure a,b). Additionally, calcite contains numerous pure aqueous inclusions, some of which contain small amounts of bitumen (Figure d).

Hydrocarbon-related materials in the samples can be divided into two main types on the basis of their petrographic occurrence and fluorescence behavior. Type I hydrocarbon-related materials show orange fluorescence under blue light excitation but only weak dark-purple fluorescence under UV excitation (Figure g–i). Type II hydrocarbon-related materials are mainly bitumen-bearing inclusions or fracture-filling bitumen and commonly show blue to blue-white fluorescence under UV excitation (Figure b,c,e,f).

The fluorescence behavior of these inclusions is used here as a qualitative petrographic criterion rather than as a direct indicator of the hydrocarbon composition or maturity. Previous studies have shown that fluorescence in sedimentary organic matter and petroleum-related materials is mainly related to organic fluorophores and the hydrocarbon composition. Therefore, the variable fluorescence responses observed in this study are interpreted as evidence for organic-rich, hydrocarbon-related materials rather than as detailed compositional or maturity indicators. ,

Inclusion Spectral Characteristics

Laser Raman spectroscopy was conducted on mineral-hosted fluid inclusions, bitumen-bearing inclusions, and bitumen in intercrystalline fractures. The spectral interpretation followed the Raman classification scheme for hydrocarbon and bitumen inclusions proposed by Zhang et al. ,

Type I orange fluorescent hydrocarbon inclusions are characterized by a strong fluorescence background during Raman analysis. This background partly masks weak Raman peaks and forms a broad fluorescence hump, indicating that these inclusions contain complex hydrocarbon-related organic components (Figure a). Because of the strong fluorescence interference, these spectra are used only as qualitative evidence for hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions.

Raman spectroscopy characteristics of hydrocarbon compounds. (a) Bitumen; (b) carbonaceous-bearing asphaltene; (c) carbonaceous asphaltene; (d) N-alkanes (blue fluorescence is excited by UV light, and orange fluorescence is excited by blue light).

Type II bitumen-bearing inclusions show two Raman spectral patterns. The first pattern contains two pairs of Raman peaks, including bands near 1342 cm–1 and 1606 cm–1, and additional high-wavenumber peaks or broad humps near 2946 cm–1 and 3216 cm–1. These features suggest the presence of carbonaceous or asphaltene-rich organic matter together with additional hydrocarbon components (Figure b). The second pattern is characterized by one pair of Raman bands near 1342 cm–1 and 1606 cm–1, which are consistent with D- and G-band features of carbonaceous or asphaltene-rich organic matter (Figure c). ,

In addition, peaks near 2871 cm–1 and 2933 cm–1 were observed in some bitumen-bearing inclusions and aqueous inclusions. These peaks are assigned to C–H stretching vibrations of aliphatic hydrocarbons and are consistent with n-alkane-bearing components (Figure d). Because the spectra are locally affected by strong fluorescence backgrounds and overlapping organic bands, these Raman results are used here as qualitative evidence for distinguishing Type I orange-fluorescent hydrocarbon-related materials from Type II bitumen-bearing materials, rather than for detailed molecular characterization. The detection of n-alkane Raman peaks in some bitumen-bearing inclusions and aqueous inclusions further defines a Raman-recognized n-alkane-bearing subgroup for microthermometric comparison.

Inclusion Homogenization Temperature

Following the principles of fluid inclusion assemblage analysis, microthermometric interpretation was based on the petrographic relationship between hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions and adjacent aqueous inclusions. Because most hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions are bitumen-bearing or contain nonaqueous hydrocarbon components, their homogenization temperatures could not be directly measured. Therefore, aqueous inclusions within calcite that show a similar petrographic occurrence to hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions were treated as petrographically associated inclusions and used to approximate the temperature conditions of hydrocarbon-related fluid entrapment. A total of 238 homogenization temperature measurements were obtained from aqueous inclusions and hydrocarbon-related inclusion assemblages.

Aqueous inclusions petrographically associated with Type I orange-fluorescent hydrocarbon inclusions (n = 26) yield homogenization temperatures ranging from 86 to 187 °C, with an average of 118.08 °C. In contrast, the Raman-recognized n-alkane-bearing inclusions and their petrographically associated aqueous inclusions (n = 15) show higher homogenization temperatures, ranging from 138 to 225 °C, with an average of 164.67 °C.

For comparison, homogenization and freezing measurements were also conducted on aqueous inclusions hosted in secondary calcite that do not show a clear petrographic association with hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions. However, reliable final ice-melting temperatures could not be obtained, and only homogenization temperatures are reported here. These aqueous inclusions without clear hydrocarbon association in secondary calcite (n = 197) show a bimodal homogenization temperature distribution, mainly concentrated at 110–140 °C and 140–170 °C (Figure ). These two temperature ranges broadly overlap with the homogenization temperature distributions of Type I-associated aqueous inclusions and the Raman-recognized n-alkane-bearing inclusion assemblages, respectively.

Discussion

Petrographic observations, carbon and oxygen isotopes, trace element data, REY signatures, and fluid inclusion data reveal three key findings. First, the O2m4 calcite veins were mainly precipitated from host-rock-buffered burial fluids with limited external fluid input. Second, the veins record paragenetic evolution that is closely linked to fracture development and fluid migration. Third, hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions and bitumen indicate that the vein system preserves evidence for multiple hydrocarbon-charging events. These findings are discussed below in terms of the vein origin, paragenetic evolution, and petroleum geological implications.

Origin and Fluid Sources of Calcite Veins

The origin of calcite veins and the source of vein-forming fluids can be constrained by integrating petrographic relationships, carbon and oxygen isotopes, trace element indicators, and REY patterns. These proxies collectively indicate a dominant host-rock-buffered fluid source with limited input of migrating interformational fluids.

Petrographic observations show that calcite mainly fills fractures and dissolution pores in O2m4 carbonates (Figure a,b). Vein calcite and pore-filling calcite exhibit broadly similar CL characteristics, with local luminescence zoning observed within some crystals (Figure g–l). These features suggest that calcite precipitation was mainly controlled by preexisting fracture-pore systems and local water-rock interaction. The similar CL responses also indicate that the calcite veins and pore-filling cements were likely formed during one dominant precipitation stage rather than through several clearly separated generations of vein formation.

Carbonate minerals generally have low contents of REE and are highly susceptible to contamination by materials with high REE contents, such as shales, oxides, and sulfide minerals. To avoid interference from noncarbonate components, samples were collected from clean, unpolluted locations. In this study, both Zr and Rb concentrations of samples were low (averaging 1 ppm), and no significant correlation exists between Zr abundances and Y/Ho ratios (Figure a), indicating minimal contamination from shales or silicate minerals. The contents of Ni, Cu, Pb, and Sc can indicate whether the sample is contaminated with oxides or sulfides. Ni and Sc concentrations in uncontaminated samples were both below 20 ppm, while in contaminated samples, Pb and Cu levels surpassed 100 ppm. In the samples analyzed in this study, both Ni and Sc were below 2.5 ppm, while both Cu and Pb were below 3.5 ppm, suggesting negligible contamination from oxides or sulfides. Furthermore, Y/Ho ratios show no significant correlation with Ni or Sc (Figure b,c), further supporting negligible contamination from noncarbonate components. Therefore, the measured ΣREE and REESN distribution patterns adequately represent the inherent REE characteristics of the carbonate samples themselves.

Correlations of Y/Ho with Zr (a), Ni (b), and Sc (c) in carbonate minerals of O2m4, central-eastern part of the Ordos Basin.

Typically, Mn/Sr < 10 indicates that carbonate samples have not undergone intense alteration and can retain their original isotopic signatures, whereas 0.1 < Mn/Sr < 2.6 suggests good preservation of the original seawater isotopic composition. In this study, the analyzed samples have Mn/Sr ratios ranging from 0.01 to 0.38, with an average of 0.12. These low Mn/Sr ratios indicate that the studied carbonates were not strongly altered and that their carbon and oxygen isotope compositions can still provide useful constraints on fluid sources and diagenetic evolution.

On this basis, carbon and oxygen isotopes can be used to compare secondary calcite with the local carbonate matrix and to evaluate whether the vein-forming fluids were externally derived or mainly host-rock-buffered. Stable carbon and oxygen isotopes are key geochemical tracers for evaluating carbonate fluid sources and diagenetic evolution because they are sensitive to carbon sources, fluid composition, temperature, and postdepositional alteration. In the δ13C-δ18O correlation diagram, secondary calcite and carbonate matrix show broadly similar isotopic compositions and mostly fall within the marine carbonate field (Figure a). The δ13CVPDB values of Ordovician seawater range from −1.3 to 1.8‰, and the δ18OVPDB values range from −9.5 to −4.1‰. The carbonate matrix in this study closely matches this Ordovician seawater range, indicating that it largely preserves a seawater-related isotopic signature and can be used as a local reference for evaluating secondary calcite (Figure b). Compared with the carbonate matrix, some dissolution pore-filling and vein-filling calcites show slightly lower δ13C and δ18O values (Figure b). The slight decrease in δ13C may indicate a minor input of carbon related to organic matter or hydrocarbon-bearing fluids, whereas the slight decrease in δ18O may reflect precipitation under higher burial temperatures or limited interaction with evolved burial fluids. Combined with the broadly similar CL characteristics and the lack of clearly separated growth stages in calcite veins (Figure g–l), these isotopic features suggest that secondary calcite was mainly precipitated from host-rock-buffered burial fluids, with limited geochemical overprinting by later hydrocarbon-bearing fluids.

The Al–Fe–Mn ternary diagram provides an additional constraint on the nature of the vein-forming fluids. , In this diagram, the carbonate matrix plots mainly in the nonhydrothermal field, whereas some secondary calcite samples plot toward the hydrothermal-related field (Figure ). However, this feature should be evaluated together with the carbon and oxygen isotope and REY data. The calcite veins show only slight δ18O depletion relative to the carbonate matrix, rather than the strong δ18O depletion expected for high-temperature hydrothermal precipitation (Figure b). In addition, negative to absent Eu anomalies also argue against a dominant high-temperature hydrothermal fluid source (Figure c). Therefore, the hydrothermal-related affinity shown by some secondary calcite in the Al–Fe–Mn diagram is more likely to reflect evolved burial fluids or localized interformational fluid flow during burial diagenesis, rather than a separate regional hydrothermal event. These features indicate that the O2m4 calcite veins were not dominated by high-temperature hydrothermal fluids and were not strongly overprinted after precipitation.

REY patterns provide additional constraints on the source and evolution of vein-forming fluids because REEs are mainly incorporated into carbonate minerals by substituting for cations in the crystal lattice, and LREE-HREE fractionation is sensitive to dissolution-recrystallization processes and ionic-radius differences. Micritic limestone commonly undergoes limited metamorphic modification and may retain the geochemical signature of its precursor seawater. − In this study, most limestone samples, except T112-4, show similar PAAS-normalized REY patterns (Figure a). Their ΣREE contents are lower than 5.67 ppm, consistent with the generally low REE contents of carbonate rocks. These samples also show REY patterns comparable to previously reported micritic limestone from the Majiagou Formation, despite differences in absolute REE contents. Dolomite and limestone display broadly similar REY patterns, with only one matrix sample showing distinct HREE depletion (Figure a), indicating that dolomite generally inherited the REY signature of the limestone matrix. The HREE depletion in sample T112-4 may reflect enhanced burial-fluid migration, as HREEs are generally more mobile than LREEs in diagenetic fluids transported as dissolved carbonate complexes. Therefore, the carbonate matrix can be used as a local geochemical reference for evaluating the source and evolution of secondary calcite.

The PAAS-normalized REY patterns of secondary calcite reflect the geochemical characteristics of its parent diagenetic fluids. Secondary calcite in pores and fractures can be divided into two types. The first type shows relatively flat REY patterns similar to those of the carbonate matrix (Figure b). This pattern suggests that the parent fluids were strongly buffered by local host-rock dissolution and that water-rock interaction allowed the fluids to partially inherit the REY signature of the surrounding carbonate matrix. The second type is characterized by LREE depletion and relatively higher HREE proportions (Figure c), indicating a different fluid evolution pathway. During diagenetic burial, prolonged water-rock interaction in a relatively closed system can increase REE concentrations in fluids through continuous REE migration from the host rocks. However, the ΣREE contents of secondary calcite in this study are generally lower than those of the carbonate matrix, suggesting a relatively open fluid system or incomplete equilibration with the host rocks. Because HREEs are generally more mobile than LREEs during diagenetic fluid migration, the LREE-depleted patterns likely reflect more evolved burial fluids with a higher degree of migration, a relatively higher fluid/rock ratio, or limited equilibration with the local carbonate matrix. Combined with the Al–Fe–Mn ternary diagram and carbon and oxygen isotope data, these REY features indicate that most secondary calcite precipitated from host-rock-buffered burial fluids, whereas some calcite was influenced by migrating interformational burial fluids. The slightly lower δ13C values of some calcite veins, together with minor δ18O depletion, further suggest a minor influence of hydrocarbon-bearing fluids during burial diagenesis.

The weak to absent Ce anomalies in both secondary calcite and the carbonate matrix indicate limited redox-related fractionation during fluid-rock interaction and suggest that the host-rock-buffered geochemical signal was largely retained. ,, In contrast, the generally higher Y/Ho ratios of secondary calcite relative to the matrix suggest that some vein-forming fluids were more mobile or more evolved than the local matrix-buffered fluids. ,, This interpretation is consistent with the LREE-depleted REY patterns observed in part of the secondary calcite because both features point to migrating burial fluids that were not fully equilibrated with the local carbonate matrix. Therefore, the REY data, together with Ce anomalies and Y/Ho ratios, support a dominant host-rock-buffered fluid source but also record limited input from migrating interformational burial fluids.

Sulfate fillings dominated by anhydrite provide additional petrographic evidence of limited interformational fluid input. The O2m4 interval is dominated by limestone and contains only minor sulfate minerals, whereas evaporite or gypsum-salt successions are widely developed in the overlying O2m5 interval. , Under the microscope, sulfate minerals are rare in the O2m4 carbonate matrix but locally occur within secondary calcite veins. In well S168, some secondary calcite veins were strongly replaced by sulfate minerals, mainly anhydrite, forming veins dominated by sulfate minerals (Figure i,l). In well T112, the sample closer to the overlying O2m5 interval contains more anhydrite fillings in calcite veins and shows HREE depletion in the carbonate matrix, suggesting enhanced burial-fluid migration (Figures d and a). Notably, sample T112-1 has lower REE contents in the matrix but higher REE contents in the veins, with a REY pattern similar to that of T112-4 (Figure d). These features suggest that part of the diagenetic fluids may have been influenced by the overlying gypsum salt interval.

In summary, the O2m4 calcite veins were mainly precipitated from burial diagenetic fluids buffered by surrounding carbonate rocks. Similar carbon and oxygen isotopic compositions and flat REY patterns indicate dominant host-rock buffering, whereas local LREE depletion, relatively high Y/Ho ratios, anhydrite-dominated sulfate fillings, and sample-scale REY variations indicate limited interformational fluid input, probably from the overlying gypsum-salt interval.

Paragenetic Evolution and Hydrocarbon Charging History

On the basis of the fluid source interpretation above, the O2m4 calcite veins record a paragenetic history involving early fracture and pore development, calcite precipitation, and later hydrocarbon charging. This history can be reconstructed by integrating petrographic relationships, oxygen isotope thermometry, fluid inclusion assemblages, and basin thermal evolution.

Marine carbonates in O2m4 in the central-eastern Ordos Basin exhibit abundant dissolution pores, fractures, and veins. These features provide the main spaces and pathways for diagenetic fluid migration and mineral precipitation. Previous studies indicate that the O2m4 dolomite reservoirs experienced two main dolomitization stages. The first stage was penecontemporaneous dolomitization by Mg2+-rich brines generated from the evaporative concentration of seawater. The second stage was burial dolomitization and recrystallization, during which earlier dolomite with residual grain textures was modified into fine- to medium-crystalline dolomite and silt- to fine-crystalline dolomite. U–Pb dating constrains this later burial recrystallization to the early Carboniferous, at approximately 342 Ma.

In this study, fine- to medium-crystalline dolomite is commonly observed around veins and dissolution pores, indicating a close spatial relationship between burial dolomitization and fracture and pore systems. These fractures and dissolution pores may have acted as conduits for burial dolomitic fluids and later calcite-precipitating fluids. After the deposition of the Majiagou Formation, multiple tectonic events promoted fracture development. Late Caledonian uplift and subsidence generated cryptic strike-slip faults in the Ordovician cover, and basement faults near the study area remained active into the late Paleozoic. These episodic tectonic activities promoted the formation of intraformational and interformational fractures in the Majiagou Formation, providing pathways and precipitation spaces for later burial fluids.

Oxygen isotope fractionation during calcite precipitation is mainly controlled by temperature when the parent fluid composition is constrained, allowing carbonate precipitation temperatures to be estimated from the δ18O values of calcite and paleofluids. , As discussed above, the carbon and oxygen isotope and REY data indicate that secondary calcite was mainly precipitated from burial fluids buffered by the carbonate host rocks. These fluids may have retained a seawater-related carbonate signature, allowing oxygen isotope thermometry to provide an approximate estimate of calcite precipitation temperature. In a relatively closed and host-rock-buffered system, burial recrystallization may partly preserve the seawater-related isotopic signature of the carbonate host rocks. However, this process may also reset or slightly modify the original δ18O values, especially during burial heating or limited interaction with evolved burial fluids. , Therefore, the calculated temperatures should be regarded as approximate constraints on early calcite precipitation. Previous studies indicate that Middle Ordovician seawater temperatures were approximately 30 °C. In this study, the δ18O values of the carbonate matrix ranged from −7.7‰ to −3.4‰, while those of secondary calcite ranged from −8.4‰ to −4.2‰. Combining the calcite oxygen isotope fractionation equation with the oxygen isotope fractionation diagram (Figure ), the average δ18O value of Ordovician seawater is calculated as −2.9‰ (SMOW). The average precipitation temperatures are estimated to be 31.7 °C for connected dissolution-pore-filling calcite and 34.5 °C for calcite veins. These low temperatures suggest that the main calcite precipitation occurred during an early shallow burial stage.

Numerous aqueous inclusions occur within secondary calcite in each well (Figure d), but they do not necessarily represent the initial stage of calcite precipitation. Some inclusions may have been trapped during calcite growth, whereas others may have formed during later burial fluid migration or hydrocarbon charging. This interpretation is supported by the fact that their homogenization temperatures are clearly higher than the oxygen-isotope temperatures calculated for early calcite precipitation. Therefore, aqueous inclusions in secondary calcite more likely record later fluid events superimposed on the early calcite cementation stage. Because most fluorescent hydrocarbon inclusions are bitumen-bearing or solid–liquid inclusions and cannot be directly homogenized, aqueous inclusions occurring in the same calcite domains and closely associated with hydrocarbon-related inclusions were used as indirect temperature constraints. In this study, aqueous inclusions associated with type I orange-fluorescent hydrocarbon inclusions and those associated with Raman-recognized n-alkane-bearing inclusions show different homogenization temperature ranges, suggesting that the O2m4 vein system recorded more than one episode of hydrocarbon-related fluid activity. However, homogenization temperatures constrain fluid trapping or reequilibration conditions rather than absolute geological ages. Therefore, these temperature populations were correlated with the burial thermal history of the Ordos Basin to constrain the timing of hydrocarbon-related fluid activities. ,,

The first stage (Stage I) represents early calcite precipitation and limited hydrocarbon-bearing fluid activity. This stage is recorded by calcite veins, aqueous inclusions, rare hydrocarbon-bearing liquid inclusions, and bitumen-bearing solid–liquid inclusions. The hydrocarbon-bearing liquid inclusions are mostly elliptical and relatively well preserved, suggesting that small amounts of hydrocarbon-bearing fluids were present during or shortly after early calcite precipitation. On the basis of petrographic relationships, oxygen isotope thermometry, and the timing of Early Carboniferous burial dolomitization, this stage is inferred to have occurred after burial dolomitization and before the main Mesozoic oil-charging event. The occurrence of solid bitumen in some inclusions indicates that these early hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions were later affected by thermal maturation.

The second stage (Stage II) is represented by abundant fluorescent bitumen inclusions within calcite veins and intercrystalline pores. This stage postdates the main calcite precipitation and records substantial migration of liquid hydrocarbons through the fracture and pore system. Associated aqueous inclusions yield homogenization temperatures of 86–187 °C, with an average of 118.08 °C and a main interval of 95–135 °C. When correlated with the basin’s thermal history, this temperature range corresponds mainly to the Late Triassic to Early Jurassic. Therefore, Stage II is interpreted as the main mature oil-charging event recorded by the O2m4 vein system.

The third stage (Stage III) corresponds to later hydrocarbon alteration and oil cracking. Raman spectra show characteristic n-alkane peaks in some bitumen and aqueous inclusions (Figure d), suggesting a later hydrocarbon-related fluid event. The n-alkane-bearing inclusions and their associated aqueous inclusions have homogenization temperatures of 138–225 °C, with an average of 164.67 °C and a main interval of 155–170 °C. When combined with the basin thermal history, these temperatures correspond mainly to the Early Cretaceous. This stage is, therefore, interpreted to record oil cracking, gas generation, and bitumen alteration during later burial heating.

When combined with the basin’s thermal evolution history (Figure ), the inclusion evidence suggests that hydrocarbon-related fluid activity in O2m4 was mainly associated with two later events after early calcite precipitation. Regional studies indicate that hydrocarbon generation in the Majiagou Formation began at a low rate during the Middle to Late Permian, which is consistent with the limited early hydrocarbon activity recorded by rare hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions. The abundant fluorescent bitumen inclusions are more consistent with mature oil charging during the Late Triassic to Early Jurassic. Later, continued burial and heating during the Early Cretaceous promoted oil cracking and gas generation in the O2m4 reservoir. The Ro values of source rocks in the study area, ranging from 1.7% to 1.9%, also correspond to the stage of liquid hydrocarbon cracking to wet gas. Therefore, the O2m4 vein system records early limited hydrocarbon activity, mature oil charging, and later oil cracking, and bitumen alteration.

The diagenetic-hydrocarbon evolution discussed above is summarized in the paragenetic model shown in Figure . In this model, the first event represents early Carboniferous burial dolomitization and related fracture-pore development, which provided pathways and spaces for later calcite precipitation and limited early hydrocarbon-bearing fluid activity. This early diagenetic stage was followed by mature oil charging during the late Triassic to early Jurassic and later oil cracking, gas generation, and bitumen alteration during the early Cretaceous. Therefore, the O2m4 calcite veins record not only early burial cementation but also later hydrocarbon migration and thermal overprinting.

Petroleum Significance and Remaining Uncertainties

The reconstructed history of vein formation and hydrocarbon charging has important implications for evaluating fluid migration, reservoir modification, and hydrocarbon accumulation in the O2m4 subsalt carbonate interval.

In this study, calcite veins in O2m4 occur mainly within fractures and dissolution pores (Figure a,b), indicating that these fracture-pore systems once acted as pathways for burial fluids and hydrocarbons. Although calcite cementation partly reduced local pore space, the vein-bearing intervals preserve evidence of both burial fluid migration and hydrocarbon charging, including anhydrite-dominated sulfate, bitumen, and hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions (Figures c–e, ). These mineral and inclusion assemblages suggest that the O2m4 reservoir experienced coupled calcite precipitation, sulfate mineral input, hydrocarbon charging, and later thermal overprinting. Therefore, the calcite vein system provides a useful record of fluid migration pathways and reservoir modification in the O2m4 subsalt carbonate interval.

The occurrence of bitumen- and hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions in O2m4 calcite veins and dissolution pores also provides a basis for discussing the possible source of these hydrocarbon-related materials. The Majiagou Formation has experienced a high degree of thermal evolution, whereas the carbonate matrix of O2m4 has low TOC values. Previous studies indicate that marine carbonate source rocks with TOC values ranging from 0.25% to 0.5% may still have hydrocarbon generation potential because of their relatively high carboxylate salt contents. However, TOC values of O2m4 are commonly lower than 0.2%, suggesting a limited hydrocarbon generation potential of the O2m4 matrix itself. Therefore, the hydrocarbon-bearing materials preserved in dissolution pores and calcite veins are unlikely to have been sourced solely from the O2m4 matrix carbonates. Adjacent gypsum-salt-bearing intervals, such as O2m3 and O2m5, may have contributed hydrocarbons or hydrocarbon-related fluids because gypsum-salt successions may favor organic matter preservation, hydrocarbon generation, and sealing conditions in adjacent strata.

These observations provide implications for exploration of O2m4 subsalt reservoirs. Vein-bearing intervals close to gypsum-salt layers may be favorable positions for identifying hydrocarbon migration pathways through fracture-pore systems, as well as later reservoir modification. For O2m4 reservoir evaluation, key factors include fracture and pore connectivity, vein-related cementation and dissolution, gypsum-salt sealing capacity, and later hydrocarbon charging or oil cracking.

Several limitations were also noted. First, the hydrocarbon source interpretation is mainly based on the regional geological context and the occurrence of hydrocarbon-bearing inclusions, rather than direct biomarker or gas isotope correlation. Second, hydrocarbon charging timing is constrained by fluid inclusion homogenization temperatures and burial thermal history, rather than by direct age constraints on hydrocarbon-bearing materials. Third, the present samples are from a limited number of wells, and lateral variations in vein development, fracture connectivity, and hydrocarbon charging remain insufficiently constrained. These limitations do not undermine the identification of multistage hydrocarbon activity in the O2m4 vein system, but they indicate that future work should combine vein petrography with gas geochemistry, biomarker correlation, and additional basin modeling.

Conclusions

1.Secondary calcite in the O2m4 subsalt marine carbonates of the central-eastern Ordos Basin mainly occurs in fractures and dissolution pores. These calcites show broadly uniform petrographic and cathodoluminescence characteristics, indicating that they formed during one major calcite cementation stage. The occurrence of bitumen and hydrocarbon-related inclusions records later hydrocarbon activity within the calcite-filled pore-fracture system.2.Carbon–oxygen isotopes and in situ elemental compositions indicate that the calcite-forming fluids were mainly host-rock-buffered burial fluids, with limited interformational input. These fluids acquired their geochemical signatures mainly through the dissolution and reprecipitation of the surrounding carbonate rocks. Slight carbon isotope depletion and hydrocarbon-related inclusions in some calcites suggest limited modification by hydrocarbon-bearing fluids after calcite precipitation.3.By integration of petrographic, geochemical, fluid inclusion, and basin thermal-history evidence, the diagenetic-hydrocarbon evolution recorded by the O2m4 vein system can be divided into three stages. The first stage involved early shallow-burial calcite precipitation after early Carboniferous burial dolomitization, with limited hydrocarbon activity probably beginning during the middle-late Permian. The second stage was widespread oil migration and accumulation during the late Triassic-early Jurassic. The third stage was thermal cracking of retained oil to gas during the middle to late early Cretaceous.4.The O2m4 vein system records the coupling among burial fluid migration, calcite cementation, hydrocarbon charging, and later thermal overprinting. This study shows that secondary calcite veins and associated fluid inclusions can effectively record fluid evolution and hydrocarbon accumulation in subsalt marine carbonate reservoirs. These findings provide useful constraints for natural gas exploration in the Majiagou Formation and similar deeply buried carbonate systems in the Ordos Basin. Future work should combine vein petrography with gas geochemistry, biomarker correlation, and additional basin modeling to better constrain hydrocarbon sources and charging processes.
