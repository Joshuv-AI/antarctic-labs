Pulpal Pressure Aggravates Pulpitis by Mechano-Inflammatory Signal Synergy
Authors: Weiqi Hu, Hao Cui, Yajing Fu, Xinghong Luo, Lan Li, Zhengyan Wang, Lili Bao, Shengkai Gong, Haotian Luo, Siying Liu, Hui Wang, Xiaoshan Yang, Shiyu Liu, Dandan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and AimsPulpitis is a common oral disease characterized by severe pain and markedly impairs quality of life. A hallmark of its pathogenesis is a sharp increase in pulpal pressure due to vasodilation and oedema. However, the specific role of the elevated pulpal pressure in pulpitis progression remains unknown. This study aimed to investigate how pulpal pressure influences pulpitis progression and its underlying mechanism.MethodsRat models of pulpitis were established, and changes in pulpal pressure were quantified by immunofluorescence for mechanosensitive markers. Double immunofluorescence and colocalization analysis were used to assess the ability of macrophages to respond to pulpal pressure during pulpitis at 0, 24, 72 hours (n = 6/group/timepoint). Furthermore, an in vitro pressure-culture model of inflamed teeth was established at 0 and 180 kPa (n = 6/group), and pulp inflammation was assessed. An in vitro cellular model was established in which lipopolysaccharide-prestimulated macrophages were exposed to hydrostatic pressure. Finally, pulp inflammation after infiltration of a Piezo1 inhibitor was assessed by H&E and immunofluorescence.ResultsElevated pulpal pressure was positively correlated with pulpitis severity, and the capacity of macrophages to sense the pressure significantly increased with the progression of pulpitis (r = 0.672 ± 0.105 at 24 hours and 0.805 ± 0.077 at 72 hours, P < .001). Experimentally applied hydrostatic pressure significantly exacerbated both pulp inflammation and macrophage inflammatory responses (P < .05). Mechanistically, the synergistic interaction occur between signals induced by the inflammatory stimulus lipopolysaccharide and hydrostatic pressure in macrophages.ConclusionsElevated pulpal pressure synergized with inflammatory signals to exacerbate pulpitis. This mechano-inflammatory transduction pathway potentiated the TRAF6-NF-κB pathway and synergistically activated TRAF6-YAP pathway in macrophages, thereby leading to hyper-inflammatory activation and increased pulpitis.Clinical SignificanceOur work highlights the synergistic interplay between pulpal pressure and inflammation in disease progression, supporting a therapeutic strategy that integrates the mechanical microenvironment for effective inflammatory control.Key words: Inflammation, Pulpitis, Endodontics, Biomechanics, Mechanotransduction

Introduction

Pulpitis is a common oral disease primarily of microbial origin that progresses from severe inflammation to pulp necrosis, severely impacting the life quality of patient.1,2 Pulpitis leads to increased pulpal pressure due to vasodilation, congestion, and oedema. Confined by the rigid dentinal walls, the pulp tissue is trapped in a unique low-compliance microenvironment of persistently elevated pressure, and can progress to irreversible ischemia and necrosis.3 Although the increase in pulpal pressure during inflammation is clinically recognized, its mechanistic role in disease progression has not been fully studied. Therefore, exploring the role of pulpal pressure in pulpitis is critical for developing innovative therapeutic targets and enhancing pulp preservation strategies.

Cellular mechanotransduction is an important biological process that converts mechanical signals into biochemical signals regarding cell activities and metabolism. Typical mechanical forces mainly include hydrostatic pressure, fluid shear stress, tensile force, and extracellular matrix stiffness.4 Hydrostatic pressure from fluids within human body compartments includes intracranial, intraocular, and bladder pressure. Studies have shown that hydrostatic pressure within the cellular microenvironment plays an essential role in regulating cell differentiation, migration and proliferation.5 Pulpal pressure originates mainly from the hydrostatic pressure exerted by the interstitial fluid in the pulp interstitium.3 Studies have demonstrated that pulpal pressure is significantly elevated during pulpitis in humans, macaques, and cats compared to healthy states.6, 7, 8 In vitro studies have established that mechanical forces such as cyclic strain and compressive force can activate human dental pulp cells, triggering inflammatory factors secretion and oxidative stress responses.9,10 However, it has yet to be determined whether hydrostatic pressure exacerbates the inflammatory response of already-inflamed cells within pulpitis microenvironment, where mechanical forces and inflammation coexist.

Macrophages are pivotal innate immune cells that maintain tissue homeostasis and modulate inflammatory responses.11 Through continuous sensing and responding of diverse microenvironmental cues, including soluble stimuli, oxygen concentration, and mechanical forces, macrophages enable dynamic transition among functional phenotypes.12, 13, 14 Notably, recent studies indicate that macrophages are able to sense hydrostatic pressure and convert it into proinflammatory signalling,15 which in turn exacerbates the inflammatory response in already primed macrophages.16 Macrophages are the predominant resident immune cells in the dental pulp.17 Upon inflammation, the macrophage population is replenished through monocyte recruitment and differentiation to drive a substantial release of inflammatory mediators.18,19 However, the role of macrophages in the mechano-inflammatory microenvironment of pulpitis remains to be elucidated.

Here, using a rat pulpitis model and in vitro pressure-culture model, we demonstrate that the synergy between pulpal pressure and inflammation as a pivotal driver of pulpitis progression. We show that a mechano-inflammatory crosstalk mechanism exists between the inflammatory stimulus lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and hydrostatic pressure. Specifically, LPS initiates macrophage inflammation by activating the canonical tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 6 (TRAF6)-NF-κB inflammatory pathway. Meanwhile, superimposed hydrostatic pressure activates Piezo1 channels, eliciting Ca2+ influx that amplifies TRAF6 signalling and drives YAP translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus. This mechano-inflammatory transduction pathway ultimately potentiates the TRAF6-NF-κB pathway, synergistically leading to hyper-inflammatory activation and increased pulpitis severity. Taken together, our findings reveal a critical mechano-inflammatory synergy driven by pulpal pressure, establishing its targeting as a novel therapeutic strategy for pulpitis and other inflammatory diseases.

Materials and methods

Animals

All animal experiments were performed on 8-week-old male Sprague-Dawley rats (250-300 g). All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the national guidelines for the Animal Ethics Procedures (Approval No. 20250104). Adequate measures were taken to minimize pain or discomfort.

Experimental pulpitis model

Experimental pulpitis was induced by topical application of bacterial LPS (L2880, Sigma) to the exposed pulp. The rats were anaesthetized with intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (40 mg kg−¹). As previously described,20 the occlusal surface of the maxillary first molar was drilled to create a cavity. The pulp was subsequently exposed using a #20 endodontic hand file by penetrating the remaining dentin floor. After haemostasis, a sterile cotton pellet saturated with LPS (10 mg/mL) was placed on the exposure site, and the cavity was immediately sealed with glass-ionomer cement (3M). Contralateral intact molars served as healthy controls (n = 6).

In vitro pressure-culture model of inflamed teeth

Pulpitis was first induced in rat maxillary first molars as described above. At 24 hours postinduction, the animals were euthanized and the molars extracted using dental forceps. After removal of surrounding soft tissue, the teeth were immediately transferred to 24-well plates containing Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium with 10% foetal bovine serum. The glass-ionomer cement was gently drilled out to re-expose the pulp chamber. Following three washes with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the teeth were transferred to fresh Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium containing 5 µg/mL LPS (Sigma, L2880). Teeth were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6). The experimental group was subjected to 180 kPa hydrostatic pressure for 3 hours in a pressure-loading device (comprising a cell culture system, a loading control system, and a data processing system; School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University).21 The negative control group was maintained under ambient pressure (0 kPa).

In vivo treatment with Piezo1 inhibitor in pulpitis model

Rats were randomly allocated to two groups. For the experimental group, after drilling and pulp exposure of maxillary first molars, a sterile cotton pellet saturated with 80 μM GsMTx4 (MCE, HY-P1410) was applied to the exposure site for 30 minutes and then removed. For the negative control group, PBS was applied using the same procedure. Subsequently, a fresh sterile cotton pellet containing 10 mg/mL LPS was placed on the pulp, and the cavity was sealed with glass-ionomer cement (3M). Rats were euthanized at 24 and 72 hours after LPS application (n = 6).

Tissue sample harvesting

For the experimental pulpitis model and Piezo1 inhibitor treatment, rats were euthanized at 0, 24, and 72 hours after LPS application, and maxillae were harvested from each group. For the in vitro pressure-culture model of inflamed teeth, maxillary first molars were collected following 3 hours of culture under 0 and 180 kPa hydrostatic pressure. Sagittal sections were prepared from the collected molars for H&E and immunofluorescence staining.

Cell culture and hydrostatic pressure loading

Human monocytic leukaemia cell line 1 (THP-1) cells (iCell, iCell-h213) were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640) medium (Procell, PM150110P) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere. To generate M0 macrophages, THP-1 cells (5 × 10⁵ cells/well in 6-well plates) were treated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA; MCE, HY-18739) for 24 hours. After three washes with PBS, the macrophages were stimulated with 100 ng/mL LPS (Sigma, L2880) for 4 hours. LPS-primed macrophages were then subjected to hydrostatic pressure (60 kPa, 0.1 Hz, 1 hour) using a pressure-loading device (School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University). Control cells were maintained under identical culture conditions without pressure exposure.

Cell experiment agonists and inhibitors

The following reagents were obtained from MedChemExpress (MCE): Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 (HY-18723), Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 (HY-P1410), Ca²⁺ chelator BAPTA-AM (HY-100545), TRAF6 inhibitor C25-140 (HY-120934), and YAP inhibitor verteporfin (VP; HY-B0146). Stock solutions were prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma, D2650) and stored at −80°C. On the day of use, aliquots were diluted in complete RPMI-1640 to the indicated final concentrations: Yoda1 (10 μM), GsMTx4 (5 μM), BAPTA-AM (5 μM), C25-140 (80 μM), and VP (5 μM). An equivalent amount of DMSO served as the negative control. All compounds were applied simultaneously with 100 ng/mL LPS and maintained throughout the 4-hour stimulation period.

Quantification of intracellular Ca²⁺ changes and calcium imaging

For calcium imaging, cells were seeded into 15 mm glass-bottom confocal dishes (Sorfa, 201200) at a density of 3 × 10⁵ cells per dish. Intracellular Ca²⁺ levels were monitored using the cell-permeant Ca²⁺ indicator Fluo-4 AM (YeaSen, 40704ES). After rinsing with Ca²⁺-free Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS; YeaSen, 60148ES76), cells were loaded with 3 μM Fluo-4 AM in Ca²⁺-free HBSS for 30 minutes at 37°C. Following incubation, cells were washed three times with Ca²⁺-free HBSS and incubated for 20 minutes to allow complete de-esterification. During imaging, cells were stimulated with 10 μM Yoda1 at 1 minute after recording began. Fluorescence images were acquired every 5 seconds for 5 minutes (60 frames total) using NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon). Data were analysed with ImageJ (version 1.8.0).

Histology analysis

Maxillary first molars were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours at 4°C and decalcified in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid for 1 month, with solution changes twice per week. Samples were embedded in paraffin, and serial sagittal sections (5 μm) were cut and stained with HE staining on an automated stainer (Leica Autostainer XL). Histological images were obtained with an Olympus BX41 Microscope (Olympus). The histological score of pulp tissue was graded according to the number of inflammatory cells and the extent of inflammatory infiltration22: 0 – no inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating no inflammation; 1 – focal inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating mild inflammation; 2 – patchy inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating moderate inflammation; 3 – diffuse inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating severe inflammation.

Immunofluorescence staining

For cellular immunofluorescence, THP-1 cells were seeded on 12 mm glass adhesion slides, treated as indicated, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature (RT). Cells were permeabilized with 0.01% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes and blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin for 1 hour at RT. Subsequently, cells were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (1:200; Abcam, ab15323), anti-Piezo1 (1:200; Proteintech, 15939-1-AP), anti-TRAF6 (1:200; Cell Signalling Technology, 8028), anti-NF-κB p65 (1:200; Proteintech, 10745-1-AP), and anti-YAP (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology, 14074). After rinsing with PBS, cells were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen) conjugated to either Cy3 (goat anti-rabbit IgG, A10520) or FITC (goat anti-rabbit IgG, F-2765) for 1 hour at RT in the dark. Following three additional PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Coolaber, SL1841) for 5 minutes. Fluorescence images were captured using an immunofluorescence microscope (Nikon). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.8.0). For tissue immunofluorescence, maxillary first molars were fixed, decalcified, cryoprotected in 30% sucrose, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound, and sectioned at 10 μm. Sections were blocked with 5% normal goat serum (Boster; AR0009) for 1 hour at RT and then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-iNOS (1:200; Abcam, ab15323), anti-Piezo1 (1:200; Proteintech, 15939-1-AP), anti-TRPV4 (1:200; Abcam, ab314454), anti-vinculin (1:200; ABclonal, A14193), anti-lamin A/C (1:200; ABclonal, A19524), anti-CD68 (1:400; Abcam, ab125212), and anti-CD86 (1:200; Abmart, T55238). After rinsing with PBS, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:200, Invitrogen) conjugated to either Cy3 (goat anti-rabbit IgG, A10520) or FITC (goat anti-rabbit IgG, F-2765) for 1 hour at RT in the dark. Following three additional PBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Coolaber, SL1841) for 5 minutes. Fluorescence images were captured using an immunofluorescence microscope (Nikon). For colocalization quantification, maxillary first molar sections from 6 rats were subjected to immunofluorescence staining. Random fields of view were captured, and 30 cells were selected for Pearson correlation analysis based on previous studies.23,24

Real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol LS (Life Technologies) and its concentration was quantified on a Nanodrop 2000 UV-visible spectrophotometer. Only samples with A260/280 1.8 to 2.0 were used. Reverse transcription was performed with 1000 ng of total RNA in a 20 μL reaction using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (TaKaRa, RR037A) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Real-time PCR was carried out in 96-well plates with SYBR Premix Ex Taq II (TaKaRa, RR820B) on a CFX96 Touch system (Bio-Rad). The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with β-actin serving as the housekeeping gene. The primer sequences were listed below: IL-6 (Forward: 5′-ACTCACCTCTTCAGAACGAATTG-3′; Reverse: 5′-CCATCTTTGGAAGGTTCAGGTTG-3′), IL-1β (Forward: 5′-CTTATTACAGTGGCAATGAGGATG-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTTCAACACGCAGGACAGGTACA-3′), TNF-α (Forward: 5′-CCTCTCTCTAATCAGCCCTCTG-3′; Reverse: 5′-CCATCTTTGGAAGGTTCAGGTTG-3′), and β-actin (Forward: 5′-GTGGGGCGCCCCAGGCACCA-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTCCTTAATGTCACGCACGATTTC-3′).

Protein isolation and Western blot analysis

Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice for 30 minutes in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich, S8830). After centrifugation at 12,000 × g, 4°C for 15 minutes, supernatants were collected. Nuclear and cytosolic proteins were isolated using a Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, P0027) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein concentration was assessed with the BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein (30 μg per lane) were loaded and separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (AGE) gels (80 V 30 minutes, then 120 V 60 minutes), and transferred to 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, 03010040001) at 200 mA for 145 minutes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk in PBST (0.1% Tween-20) for 1.5 hours at RT and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After three 10-min washes with PBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at RT. Protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescent detection system (Tanon-Bio) and quantified with ImageJ software (version 1.8.0). The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Piezo1 (Proteintech, 15939-1-AP), anti-iNOS (Abcam, ab15323), anti-TLR4 (Proteintech, 19811-1-AP), anti-MyD88 (Abcam, ab2064), anti-TRAF6 (Cell Signaling Technology, 8028), anti-NF-κB p65 (Proteintech, 10745-1-AP), anti-YAP (Cell Signaling Technology, 14074), Anti-HDAC1 (Cell Signaling Technology, 34589S) and Anti-β-actin (Cwbio, CW0096A).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple groups were analysed by one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Expressions of mechanosensitive markers are elevated in pulpitis

To investigate the role of pulpal pressure in pulpitis progression, we established a rat pulpitis model in a relatively enclosed environment (Figure 1A). The results showed that at 24 hours, inflamed pulp displayed progressive inflammatory cell infiltration, vasodilation, haemorrhage, and structural disorganization compared to healthy controls (Figure 1B; Figure S1A). At 72 hours, H&E staining and subsequent histopathological evaluation revealed extensive necrosis in most of the coronal pulp, accompanied by evident oedema in the radicular pulp (Figure 1B; Figure S1A). This histopathological progression was paralleled by a progressive upregulation of the inflammatory marker iNOS at 24 and 72 hours (Figure 1C; Figure S1B), collectively confirming the validity of the pulpitis model.

Direct evidence from the 1970s, obtained via in-situ micropuncture in humans, macaques, and cats, revealed significantly higher pulpal pressure in inflamed teeth.6, 7, 8 To further assess pulpal pressure elevation at the molecular level, we quantified the changes in pulpal pressure by measuring the expression of four well-established mechanosensitive markers located at distinct subcellular compartments: Piezo1 and transient receptor potential vanilloid 4 (TRPV4) (cell membrane), vinculin (cytoplasm), and lamin A/C (nucleus). The expression of Piezo1, TRPV4, vinculin, and lamin A/C was significantly upregulated at 24 and 72 hours compared to healthy controls (Figure 1D, E). Notably, Piezo1 expression consistently surpassed that of the other three markers at all time points (Figure 1D, E). Given its predominant expression, we focused subsequent investigations on the role of Piezo1 in pulpitis progression.

Recent studies have established that mechanical forces can modulate macrophage function and drive inflammatory responses.14,25 To investigate the roles of macrophages in sensing pulpal pressure and modulating inflammation during pulpitis, we performed dual immunofluorescence and colocalization analysis for CD68 (macrophages) and Piezo1. We observed a time-dependent increase in the proportion of Piezo1⁺CD68⁺ macrophages, which was corroborated by the Pearson correlation between Piezo1 and CD68 fluorescence intensities (r = 0.672 ± 0.105 at 24 hours and 0.805 ± 0.077 at 72 hours; Figure 1F). These data indicated that Piezo1 activation in macrophages likely contributes to the progression of pulpitis. Collectively, these findings demonstrate a marked increase in pulpal pressure alongside disease severity, and that the capacity of macrophages to sense the pressure increases with the progression of pulpitis.

Hydrostatic pressure exacerbates pulp inflammation

To investigate whether the elevated pulpal pressure exacerbates pulpitis, we established an in vitro pressure-culture model using inflamed teeth, which simulates the elevated pulpal pressure and overcomes the technical challenges of applying pressure to the pulp in vivo (Figure 2A). Specifically, maxillary first molars were extracted from rats 24 hours after pulpitis induction and cultured under hydrostatic pressure in LPS-supplemented medium using a custom-designed device. Compared to the 0 kPa control, exposure to 180 kPa pressure markedly aggravated inflammatory cell infiltration and resulted in extensive pulp necrosis (Figure 2B; Figure S2A). Furthermore, both iNOS expression and the proportion of CD86+ proinflammatory macrophages were significantly elevated under 180 kPa pressure (Figure 2C, E). Consistently, the expression of mechanosensitive markers, including Piezo1, vinculin, and lamin A/C, was also substantially upregulated in the 180 kPa group (Figure 2D, E). These data validate the efficacy of the pressure-culture model using inflamed teeth in recapitulating the mechanical microenvironment within the inflamed pulp. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hydrostatic pressure exacerbates the inflammatory response in pulpitis, highlighting the role of mechanical pressure in disease progression.

Hydrostatic pressure exacerbates macrophage inflammation via Piezo1

Since tissue-level observations indicated that hydrostatic pressure exacerbates pulpitis, we next determined whether a similar proinflammatory effect occurs at the cellular level. Given our prior data that macrophages in inflamed pulp sense elevated tissue pressure, and that hydrostatic pressure increased the proportion of proinflammatory macrophages (Figures 1F and 2C), we investigated whether elevated local tissue pressure directly potentiates inflammatory response in macrophages. Macrophages were prestimulated with LPS to establish an inflammatory state and then exposed to 60 kPa hydrostatic pressure for 1 hour (Figure 3A). Under pressure, these cells showed elevated mRNA levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Figure 3B), along with increased iNOS protein expression (Figure 3C, D; Figure S3A, B). These findings indicate that hydrostatic pressure synergizes with inflammatory stimuli to drive macrophages towards a hyper-inflammatory state.

To explore the underlying mechanism, we focused on the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1, which was most highly upregulated in the rat pulpitis model (Figure 1D, E). We hypothesized that Piezo1 activation mediates the pressure-induced inflammatory enhancement. Consistent with the in vivo results, Piezo1 expression was significantly elevated in macrophages under 60 kPa pressure compared with the 0 kPa control (Figure 3C, E; Figure S3A, C). As functional activation of Piezo1 requires further validation, we assessed its activity using the Piezo1-selective agonist Yoda1 and measured intracellular Ca2+ with or without concurrent application of 60 kPa hydrostatic pressure. The results showed that hydrostatic pressure augmented Yoda1-induced Ca2+ influx in macrophages (Figure 3F; Figure S3D), indicating that Piezo1 activity is enhanced in a pressure-dependent manner. Furthermore, the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 significantly suppressed the expression of iNOS, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Figure 3G-I; Figure S3E, F). To validate these findings in vivo, GsMTx4 was applied topically to exposed pulp in a rat pulpitis model (Figure 3J). Histological analysis revealed that GsMTx4 markedly attenuated the progression of pulp inflammation. Specifically, at 24 hours, necrosis was confined to the exposure site in GsMTx4-treated rats, whereas it spread throughout the coronal pulp in PBS controls. At 72 hours, necrosis remained restricted to the coronal pulp in the GsMTx4 group but extended into the root canal in the PBS group (Figure 3K; Figure S3G). Correspondingly, immunofluorescence staining revealed that iNOS expression was significantly lower in GsMTx4-treated pulp at both time points than that in the PBS group (Figure 3L; Figure S3H). Together, these results demonstrate that hydrostatic pressure exacerbates macrophage-mediated inflammation via Piezo1 activation, and that Piezo1 inhibition effectively attenuates the progression of pulpitis.

Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx drives TRAF6 upregulation to exacerbate inflammation

Having demonstrated that hydrostatic pressure promotes the macrophage inflammatory response through Piezo1 activation, we next investigated the underlying molecular mechanism. In the canonical LPS-activated inflammation pathway, Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) recruits myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88), leading to TRAF6 activation and subsequent NF-κB nuclear translocation, which initiates inflammatory transcription.26 While hydrostatic pressure did not alter the expression of TLR4 or MyD88, it specifically upregulated TRAF6 at the protein level (Figure 4A; Figure S4A). This increase was further confirmed by immunofluorescence of TRAF6 (Figure 4C; Figure S4C). Similarly, treatment with the Piezo1 agonist Yoda1 also elevated TRAF6 expression without altering TLR4 or MyD88 (Figure 4B; Figure S4B). To assess the functional contribution of TRAF6, we inhibited TRAF6 signalling using the small-molecule inhibitor C25-140 and observed a corresponding reduction in iNOS, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β expression (Figure 4D, F; Figure S4D-F). Moreover, treatment with the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 decreased TRAF6 expression (Figure 4E, G; Figure S4G, H). These results demonstrated that Piezo1 activation is required for the pressure-induced upregulation of TRAF6, establishing a Piezo1-TRAF6 signalling axis that drives proinflammatory responses in macrophages.

To investigate whether Piezo1 modulates TRAF6 through a calcium-dependent mechanism, we next focused on calcium signalling. Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that hydrostatic pressure elevates intracellular Ca²⁺ levels in macrophages, and this effect was attenuated by the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4 (Figure 4H, I). Moreover, chelation of intracellular Ca²⁺ with BAPTA-AM suppressed TRAF6 upregulation (Figure 4J, L; Figure S4I, J) and downregulated the expression of iNOS, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Figure 4K, M; Figure S4K-M). These findings indicate that Piezo1-mediated Ca²⁺ influx is required for TRAF6 upregulation and subsequent inflammatory amplification.

TRAF6 promotes the nuclear translocation of YAP to exacerbate the inflammatory response

Given that nuclear translocation of NF-κB is the canonical downstream effector of TRAF6-dependent signalling,26 we next investigated whether hydrostatic pressure augments the nuclear accumulation of NF-κB in macrophages. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that hydrostatic pressure increased nuclear localization of NF-κB (Figure 5A). Moreover, Western blotting showed a corresponding rise in nuclear NF-κB protein levels without altering its cytoplasmic levels (Figure 5B; Figure S5A, B). These data indicated that hydrostatic pressure enhances NF-κB transcriptional activity. Beyond the canonical NF-κB pathway, recent evidence suggests that TRAF6 also facilitates YAP nuclear translocation to promote proinflammatory gene expression.27 We therefore investigated whether YAP signalling is engaged by the Piezo1-TRAF6 axis under hydrostatic pressure. Our results showed that hydrostatic pressure significantly promoted YAP nuclear translocation, accompanied by a decrease in the cytoplasmic fraction (Figure 5C, D; Figure S5C, D). This process was Piezo1-dependent, as GsMTx4 treatment reduced both nuclear localization and total protein levels of YAP (Figure 5E, G; Figure S5E, F). Similarly, chelation of intracellular Ca²⁺ with BAPTA-AM or inhibition of TRAF6 with C25-140 suppressed YAP nuclear accumulation and downregulated total YAP expression (Figure 5F, H, I, K; Figure S5G-J). Finally, to evaluate the functional contribution of YAP, we inhibited YAP using VP and observed reduced expression of iNOS, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Figure 5J, L; Figure S5K-M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hydrostatic pressure not only enhances the canonical NF-κB pathway but also activates a noncanonical Piezo1-Ca²⁺-TRAF6-YAP signalling axis, which synergistically amplifies the inflammatory response in macrophages.

Discussion

Mechanical forces are known to modulate inflammatory processes by influencing the activation, migration, and phagocytic activity of both innate and adaptive immune cells.28 Accumulating evidence suggests that mechanical forces not only arise from but also actively exacerbate the progression of inflammatory diseases, including pancreatitis and arthritis.29,30 In this study, we reveal that the pathogenesis of pulpitis is mediated by a synergistic crosstalk between pulpal pressure and inflammation. While our investigation focuses on pulpitis, the mechano-inflammatory crosstalk identified here may extend to other pressure-related inflammatory diseases.

Accurate pulpal pressure determination remains technically challenging. Recent advances in cellular mechanics have identified mechanosensitive biomarkers, including ion channels (eg, Piezo1/TRPV4), focal adhesions, adhesion G protein-coupled receptors (aGPCRs) (eg, GPR114), and LINC complex proteins.25,31 The expression thresholds of mechanosensitive markers probably correlate quantitatively with mechanical force magnitude. For instance, Piezo1 upregulates at 45 to 60 mmHg in bone marrow-derived macrophages16; TRPV4 activates at 0.2 to 0.4 MPa in nucleus pulposus cells32; vinculin increases at 5/50 MPa in human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells,33 and lamin A rises at 4 dyn/cm2 shear stress in bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells.34 These studies provide a novel perspective for estimating pulpal pressure level. Utilizing the identified mechanosensitive markers Piezo1, TRPV4, vinculin, and lamin A/C,35, 36, 37, 38 we tracked pressure changes during pulpitis and visualized their spatial distribution across the inflamed pulp. We found that under inflammatory conditions, pulp cells enhance mechano-electrical transduction capacity, with upregulated mechanosensitive markers particularly enriched at the junction of necrotic and non-necrotic pulp tissue. Our findings bridge classical micropuncture manometry and contemporary cellular mechanobiology, offering a new perspective for pressure assessment within sealed soft-tissue compartments.

The pulp chamber is a rigid, dentin-enclosed cavity where cells experience multidirectional forces,39 whereas the in vitro cellular model applies uniaxial force only, which required 60 kPa uniaxial stress to equalize volumetric compression. The in vitro pressure-culture model of inflamed teeth applies 180 kPa to account for both biological scaling from cells to intact pulp tissue and stress attenuation by enamel and dentin constraints. Moreover, previous studies using the same pressure-loading device have shown that similar pressure values do not induce cell death.40, 41, 42 The pressure values employed in our study were nondestructive and remained within the safe tolerance range.

Regarding the mechanism by which pressure exacerbates inflammation, we further delineate the cellular machinery underlying the synergy between mechanosensation and inflammatory responses. It has been reported that transient receptor potential cation channel, subfamily M, member 7 (TRPM7), a mechanosensitive channel, regulates TRAF6 expression via Ca2+ influx.43 Although hydrostatic pressure-induced Ca2+ influx via Piezo1 has been previously reported,15,44,45 our study reveals for the first time that this process further promotes TRAF6 expression and induces inflammatory responses. Furthermore, TRAF6 was identified as a signalling integration hub that not only mediates the Piezo1-Ca²⁺-TRAF6-YAP signalling axis but also interacts with the classical TRAF6-NF-κB pathway, thereby synergistically amplifying the inflammatory cascade.

Uncovering how mechanical pressure amplifies inflammation facilitates the translation of the concept of ‘the tissue pressure off-loading’ into early clinical management of pulpitis. In this study, we show that GsMTx4-mediated Piezo1 inhibition effectively attenuates pulpitis, providing key evidence for targeting mechanosensitive signalling as a therapeutic strategy. Notably, a recent clinical case report demonstrated that the use of a unidirectional decompression membrane to decrease pulpal pressure effectively alleviated pulp inflammation.46 These findings offer a new therapeutic perspective for preserving the vital pulp in irreversible pulpitis and support a paradigm shift from antimicrobial therapy alone to multitarget strategies that also modulate the mechanical microenvironment.

We acknowledge that this study has certain methodological limitations arising from technical constraints. For instance, the pulpal pressure was obtained through indirect estimation rather than direct measurement. Future technological advances in commercial instruments such as implantable microdevices will enable real-time pulpal pressure monitoring for basic research and clinical diagnosis of pulpitis.47 Additionally, the established in vitro pressure-culture model represents a certain degree of simplification compared to the complex mechanical environment in vivo. Future studies will require more comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments coupled with robust statistical analysis. Furthermore, due to the lack of stable rat dental pulp macrophage cell lines and the low yield and phenotypic loss of primary rat macrophages, we utilized the genetically uniform human THP-1 cell line. However, this cross-species approach is methodologically limited. Future studies should establish species-matched cell models for further validation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that mechanical forces can induce odontoblast differentiation48 and fibroblast proliferation,49 suggesting that pulpal pressure may exert multifaceted biological effects beyond its role in inflammation regulation. Moreover, mechanical forces can alter interstitial fluid flow,50 induce local hypoxia,51 deform the cytoskeletal architecture,52 and modify the mechanical properties of the extracellular matrix.53 While interstitial fluid flow initially mitigates pulpal pressure, persistently elevated pulpal pressure during pulpitis impairs circulation and induces hypoxia.3,54 Beyond direct proinflammatory effects, investigating whether pulpal pressure exacerbates inflammation via altered interstitial flow and hypoxia is essential for understanding the mechanical microenvironment of pulpitis.

Conclusions

The synergistic interplay between pulpal pressure and inflammatory signals serves as a critical factor accelerating the progression of pulpitis. The underlying molecular mechanisms involve TRAF6-mediated dual pathway activation, wherein the classical proinflammatory NF-κB pathway is enhanced while the TRAF6-YAP pathway in macrophages is simultaneously activated. This study expands the understanding of the mechanical and inflammatory microenvironment of pulpitis and reveals how immune cells simultaneously perceive inflammatory and mechanical cues.
