Structural brain injury on magnetic resonance imaging in heart failure and identifying patients at high-risk
Authors: Wenyi Tang, Qing Xie, Zihong Huang, Mengjiao Shao, Taoyu Jia, Feng Hu, Ani Wang, Fang Zhou, Zhiling Zhu, Yutao Pan, Lizi Jin, Cunxue Pan, Jian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Structural brain abnormalities are common in HF.•Smoking, diabetes, and elevated NT-proBNP predict large brain infarcts; age, hypertension, elevated fibrinogen, and higher high-density lipoprotein cholesterol are associated with WMH severity; and age and systemic immune-inflammation index are linked to cerebral atrophy.•A clinical subtype with heightened inflammation and poorer nutrition may face increased risk of brain injury.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by impaired pumping or filling function of the heart to meet the metabolic demands of the body. HF poses a significant global health challenge, affecting approximately 64.3 million individuals worldwide. It is associated with markedly reduced quality of life, recurrent hospitalizations, rising healthcare expenditures, and elevated rates of premature mortality[1]. Patients with HF can experience a range of neuropsychiatric symptoms, including dizziness, fatigue, anxiety, and depression, and face an increased risk of stroke. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that progressive HF may lead to cognitive dysfunction and dementia in certain patients[2,3]. Such neurological impairment can further compromise self-management abilities and reduce treatment adherence, thereby worsening overall prognosis[4].

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is a valuable, widely used tool for detecting cerebral abnormalities and offers insights into the mechanisms underlying poor neurological outcomes in HF. Structural brain pathologies, such as infarcts, white matter hyperintensities (WMH), lacunar lesions, or focal atrophy, observed on MRI have been increasingly reported in patients with HF[2]. These brain abnormalities are linked to a higher risk of depression, anxiety, neurodegenerative disorders, and impaired physical function [[5], [6], [7]]. Nevertheless, brain MRI is not currently a routine part of the work-up for HF patients in clinical practice. Existing literature has mainly focused on differences between HF patients and age- and sex-matched healthy controls free of clinical cardiovascular disease, seeking neuroimaging markers of cognitive deficits. Research on this topic remains relatively scarce, and most existing studies have sample sizes of fewer than 80 participants, though a few recent large-sample cohorts have been reported[5,[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. The neuroimaging signatures in HF are an important yet understudied issue. Also, the factors that may contribute to brain injury in HF remain poorly defined. Hence, we conducted the current retrospective cross-sectional study to characterize structural neuroimaging features and their associated clinical correlates in patients with HF. Additionally, we aim to identify clinical phenotypes associated with varying risks of brain injury.

Patients and methods

Study design and participants

This is a cross-sectional observational study. We reviewed the medical records of consecutive patients aged 18 to 75 years with HF who were admitted to the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine at the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University from January 2019 to October 2024. HF was defined in accordance with the current guidelines[16]. Patients were eligible if they underwent a brain MRI at our institution after a HF diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included previous clinical stroke or overt psychiatric illness before HF diagnosis, neurosurgery, head trauma, internal carotid artery or vertebral artery stenosis exceeding 50%, chronic kidney disease stage 4-5, liver failure, severe structural lung disease, malignancy, cardiac arrest, infective endocarditis, autoimmune disease, and poor MRI scan quality or missing MRI sequences. Demographic data, comorbidities, laboratory data, and echocardiographic parameters, following the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines[17], were collected within 1 week of brain MRI. The systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), a metric of systemic inflammatory response, was calculated from peripheral blood cell counts using the formula: SII = (neutrophils × platelets) / lymphocytes[18]. This study adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. Because it was a retrospective observational study, patient consent was waived, but patient privacy was well protected.

Assessment of brain injury on MRI

Brain MRI scans were acquired on Siemens (Magnetom Aera 1.5T and Magnetom Verio 3.0T), General Electric (Signa Pioneer 3.0T and Signa Architect 3.0T), and Philips (Ingenia Amnition 1.5T) scanners, with a slice thickness of 5 mm and no interslice gaps. The assessment of structural brain injury included cerebral infarcts, white matter hyperintensities, and cerebral atrophy. Sagittal T1-weighted, axial T2-weighted, fast fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and coronal FLAIR images from a standardized imaging protocol were evaluated independently by two experienced neuroradiologists (X.Q. and P.C.), who were blinded to the clinical information. When scores differed, the grade was determined by consensus following open discussion between two independent radiologists. A senior radiologist adjudicated any persistent disagreements. When multiple brain MRIs were available for each participant, we used the most recent examination.

Large brain infarcts were defined as hyperintense lesions on FLAIR >20 mm in diameter on axial sections, consistent with ischemic infarction in the territory of a perforating arteriole. In contrast, lacunar infarcts were lesions ≤20 mm in diameter[19]. The severity of white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) was assessed visually using the semiquantitative Scheltens and Fazekas scores simultaneously. The Scheltens scale consisted of scoring the WMHs in four regions: the periventricular, deep white matter, basal ganglia, and infratentorial areas (range = 0–84). For each location, the score increased with the number and size of lesions. Periventricular hyperintensities were scored as follows: absence of lesions = score 0, ≤5 mm lesion = score 1, and a score of 2 is given if the lesion exceeds 5 mm but not 10mm. WMHs located in the deep white matter, basal ganglia, and infratentorial foci were graded according to the following criteria: score 0 = no abnormalities, score 1 = lesions less than 3 mm, ≤5 lesions, score 2 = lesions <3 mm, more than 6 lesions, score 3 =lesions 4 to 10 mm, ≤5 lesions, score 4 = same as 3 but >6 lesions, score 5 = lesions >11 mm, >1 lesion, score 6 = confluent lesions[20]. The Fazekas scale evaluated WMHs in deep white matter and periventricular white matter (range = 0–6). Periventricular WMHs were rated as 0 (absent), 1 (symmetrical caps), 2 (smooth halo), or 3 (irregular hypoattenuations extending into deep white matter). Deep WMHs were rated as 0 (absent), 1 (punctuate foci), 2 (beginning confluence of foci), or 3 (large confluent areas). A Fazekas score of ≥2 in either the periventricular or the deep white matter was defined as at least moderate white matter lesions[21]. Cerebral atrophy was graded using the global cortical atrophy (GCA) scale, which measured cortical atrophy across 13 brain regions, including dilation of the frontal, parieto-occipital, and temporal sulci, as well as ventricular dilation. The GCA scale assigned each region an atrophy score of 0 (absent), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), or 3 (severe). The total GCA score was the sum of all scores in the 13 regions (range = 0–39)[22]. Because large infarcts could obscure the underlying brain parenchyma, WMHs and brain atrophy were evaluated only in patients without large brain infarcts.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.3.3 (www.r-project.org). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. During variable selection for cluster analysis, variables with missing data rates exceeding 20% were excluded; missing values for the remaining variables were handled using multiple imputation.

Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), and categorical variables as number (percentage). Unpaired t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, and Chi-square or Fisher's exact test were used to compare the severity of brain infarcts, WMHs, and cerebral atrophy across patient comorbidities, as appropriate. According to the corresponding medians of our study, a Scheltens score of 12, a Fazekas score of 2, and a GCA score of 6.5 were used as thresholds for univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate predictors of structural brain abnormalities. Variables that were statistically significant at the 10% level in the univariate logistic analysis were then included in a stepwise forward multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify the independent association of each risk factor with the brain lesions.

To explore potential subgroups associated with differential risk of brain injury based on clinical laboratory biomarkers, we performed a cluster analysis using principal component analysis (PCA) followed by K‑means clustering. Based on the available sample size and the objective of identifying interpretable clinical subtypes, relevant laboratory biochemical variables were first categorized into three domains: inflammation‑nutrition (including SII, fibrinogen, hemoglobin, and albumin), metabolism [high‑density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‑C), non‑HDL‑C, body mass index (BMI), fasting plasma glucose, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and triglycerides], and organ function [alanine aminotransferase, total bilirubin, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and log10‑transformed N‑terminal pro‑brain natriuretic peptide (NT‑proBNP)]. PCA was applied separately to each domain for dimensionality reduction. All continuous variables were standardized to z-scores prior to PCA to ensure comparability across scales. According to Kaiser's criterion, components with eigenvalues >1 were retained for subsequent K‑means clustering. Finally, key demographics, comorbidities, and structural brain lesions were compared across the clusters.

Result

Brain MRI findings in participants with HF

Among all participants with HF, the prevalence of large brain infarcts was 20.3%, and that of lacunes was 51.0%. Of these, the prevalence of large telencephalic infarcts was 17.5%. The right parietal lobe was the most commonly involved site for large infarcts (4.2%). Among the 114 patients without large brain infarcts, the median total Scheltens, Fazekas, and GCA scores were 12 (6, 20.2), 2 (2, 3), and 6.5 (0, 12), respectively. Periventricular Fazekas score did not differ significantly from deep Fazekas score (1 vs. 1, P = 0.532). WMHs and cerebral atrophy were observed in the majority (93.9% and 72.8%, respectively). Moderate-to-severe WMHs and moderate-to-severe cerebral atrophy were present in 29 (25.4%) and 35 (30.7%) of HF patients, respectively. Factors associated with structural brain lesions in logistic regression analysis

As shown in Table 3, multivariable logistic regression indicated that smoker (odds ratio [OR]: 5.069, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.691–15.189, P=0.004), history of diabetes (OR: 3.140, 95% CI: 1.130– 8.772, P=0.028), serum NT-proBNP ≥900 pg/mL (OR: 3.472, 95% CI: 1.189-10.131, P=0.023), and use of antithrombotic agents before admission (OR:3.155, 95%CI: 1.111– 8.961), P=0.031) were associated with a higher risk of large brain infarcts. Regarding WMHs rated by the semiquantitative Scheltens scale, multivariable regression revealed that age (OR: 1.075, 95% CI: 1.027–1.125, P=0.002), history of HTN (OR: 3.075, 95% CI: 1.200–7.881, P=0.019), higher plasma fibrinogen level (OR: 2.039, 95% CI: 1.172–3.546, P=0.012), and increased serum HDL-C (OR: 7.100, 95% CI: 1.086–46.416, P=0.041) were predictors of a higher Scheltens score. Discussion

The current study aimed to investigate detailed structural brain abnormalities identified on brain MRI and to characterize the features of the population at higher risk for brain injury in HF. Our main and novel findings were as follows: (1) Among all participants with HF, approximately one-fifth had large brain infarcts, while over half had lacunes. Both WMHs and cerebral atrophy were prevalent in HF patients, affecting 25.4% and 30.7% with moderate-to-severe manifestations, respectively.​ (2) Among patients with HF, smoking, diabetes, and elevated serum NT-proBNP (≥900 pg/mL) were significantly associated with the presence of large brain infarcts. Age, history of hypertension, plasma fibrinogen level, and serum HDL-C level were positively associated with WMH burden rating by the Scheltens score. Higher age and increased SII were predictors of pronounced cerebral atrophy. (3) Cluster analysis identified two distinct subgroups with different risks for structural brain injuries. The group with higher systemic inflammatory response, poorer nutritional status, and relatively worse cardiac, hepatic, and renal function has a greater risk of lacunar infarct and cerebral atrophy.

Although brain injury in patients with chronic HF has garnered increasing scientific attention, comprehensive neuroimaging studies characterizing these pathologies remain limited. In the Cognition. Matters-HF cohort, the largest study to date (N = 148) investigating cognitive domains and brain lesions in HF, Frey et al. reported prevalences of 12.9% for brain infarctions, 19.0% for lacunar infarction, 86.8% for WMH (Fazekas score ≥ 1), and 73.9% for regional advanced medial temporal lobe atrophy[5]. Concurrently, a 2024 meta-analysis reported the following prevalence rates in HF and cardiomyopathy populations: 32% for silent brain infarction, 26% for lacunes, 72% for WMH, and 82% for cerebral atrophy[23]. Our cohort exhibited a similar burden of WMH and cerebral atrophy but markedly higher prevalence of brain infarctions (20.3%) and lacunes (51.0%) than the Cognition. Matters-HF cohort. This discrepancy might be attributed to our cohort’s significantly higher proportion of smokers and patients with coexisting atrial fibrillation or diabetes. Our study further revealed that patients with HF were at substantial risk for structural cerebrovascular injuries. These findings underscore the need for cardiovascular specialists to integrate neuroprotective strategies into HF management protocols. Crucially, intervention remains warranted even after an HF diagnosis. Our data show that smoking, diabetes, and NT-proBNP ≥900 pg/mL are associated with large brain infarcts, whereas hypertension, fibrinogen, and elevated HDL-C levels (potentially reflecting lipid dysregulation) are linked to WMH burden, and SII is associated with cerebral atrophy. Therefore, implementing aggressive smoking cessation, stringent glycemic and blood pressure control, antithrombotic and lipid-modifying therapies, and anti-inflammatory modulation is warranted. For patients without baseline injury, these measures serve as primary prevention; for those with established damage, they are essential to slow further structural deterioration. Our study also revealed that large territorial infarcts most frequently involved the left parietal lobe. Although the precise pathophysiological link between left parietal lobe infarction and HF remains unclear, there is evidence to support that infarction involving the left parietal lobe was associated with a significantly elevated risk of cardiac events and a hazard ratio of 4.45 for cardiac death[24].

Stroke is a devastating comorbidity among HF patients, occurring irrespective of AF status. Prior studies have revealed that insulin-treated diabetes mellitus, prior stroke history, and elevated NT-proBNP are independent predictors of stroke in non-AF HF cohorts[25,26]. Our findings corroborated and extended this evidence: diabetes history (OR 3.140) and NT-proBNP ≥900 pg/mL (OR 3.472) emerged as independent risk factors for MRI-confirmed large brain infarcts in patients with HF. Crucially, smoking demonstrated a substantially elevated risk (OR 5.069) exceeding conventional stroke risk estimates (typical OR/HR 1.5-3.0) documented in the general population[27]. This suggests that the HF milieu may potentiate tobacco-induced neurovascular toxicity. However, LVEF was not predictive of large brain infarcts in our cohort, despite some, but not all, previous studies suggesting otherwise[28,29]. Surprisingly, atrial fibrillation, a well-known contributor to stroke, was also found to be unrelated to large brain infarcts or lacunes. This probably reflects that, in our HF group, most patients with atrial fibrillation had been prescribed anticoagulants. In our cohort, 45 of 51 patients with AF (88.2%) met the criteria for oral anticoagulant therapy based on a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of ≥2. Among this subgroup, 28 patients (62.2%) were receiving cardiologist-directed anticoagulation at admission.

Cerebral WMH burden is an established neuroimaging marker associated with cognitive impairment, elevated risks of stroke, dementia, and all-cause mortality[30]. Previous studies have also shown that greater WMH burden correlated with depression, anxiety symptoms, and poor sleep in HF patients[[31], [32], [33]]. WMHs are typically interpreted as surrogates for cerebral small vessel disease, though their underlying pathophysiology exhibits considerable heterogeneity. While age and hypertension are well-known determinants of WMH progression[34,35], our study also identified elevated plasma fibrinogen and serum HDL-C levels as novel pathophysiological factors independently associated with increased WMH burden in patients with HF. While the precise mechanisms linking elevated fibrinogen to WMHs in HF remain incompletely characterized, fibrinogen, as a dual biomarker of thrombosis and inflammation, induces endothelial injury via ICAM-1/NF-κB activation, promotes hypercoagulability through enhanced platelet aggregation, and drives cerebral small vessel atherogenesis. These pathophysiological cascades collectively cause hemorheological dysfunction and WMH progression[36]. On the other hand, our analysis unexpectedly revealed a significant inverse association between serum HDL-C levels and WMH burden in the HF cohort. This finding challenges the notion of HDL-C as solely “good” cholesterol. It remains unclear how high HDL-C promotes WMHs in patients with HF. But there has been evidence showing that very high levels of HDL-C can be associated with increased risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or cognitive decline[37,38]. Previous evidence suggested that both the composition and function of HDL-C would be significantly altered in the context of an inflammatory milieu, transforming the particle from a vasoprotective anti-atherogenic particle to a noxious pro-atherogenic equivalent[39]. Increased inflammatory properties of HDL-C or impaired HDL-C antioxidant have been observed in patients with HF[40,41].

Cerebral atrophy is a loss of brain cells and the connections between them. Patients with cerebral atrophy suffer from cognitive impairment, movement disturbance, and neuropsychiatric disorders[42]. Atrophy of multiple brain regions has been observed in patients with HF[9,43,44]. Our results showed that, aside from age, increased SII was independently linked to significant cerebral atrophy in HF patients. SII is a comprehensive inflammatory index that integrates counts of neutrophils, lymphocytes, and platelets, reflecting both local immune response and systemic inflammatory status. Inflammatory response is now considered one of the main contributors to the development of chronic HF[45].

HF is a complex, heterogeneous disease with variable clinical outcomes[46]. Cluster analysis is a statistical method that assesses relationships among given characteristics and groups patients into homogeneous subgroups. In our study, using laboratory biomarkers of inflammation, nutrition, metabolism, and organ function, cluster analysis identified two distinct phenotypic clusters among patients with HF. Although most inflammatory, nutritional, and liver- and kidney-function-related biochemical indicators were within the normal range, there were differences in lacunar infarction and cerebral atrophy between the 2 clinical subtypes. Cluster 1 is characterized by a multifaceted risk profile that includes cardio-hepato-renal dysfunction, a pro-inflammatory and pro-thrombotic state, and indicators of poorer nutritional status. The concurrent elevation of the SII and fibrinogen suggests persistent immune activation and hypercoagulability. Pathophysiologically, these factors may converge to promote endothelial dysfunction, impaired cerebral blood flow autoregulation, and increased blood-brain barrier permeability, which are central mechanisms in cerebral small-vessel disease, including lacunes and brain atrophy[36]. The lower levels of albumin, BMI, and non-HDL-C in Cluster 1 may reflect chronic illness, malnutrition, or chronic wasting status, which could exacerbate neurological vulnerability and tissue atrophy. In contrast, the absence of significant differences in the prevalence of large-area cerebral infarction or in the burden of WMHs across clusters is instructive. This suggests that the risk factors defining Cluster 1 are not preferentially associated with large-artery atherothromboembolism, which typically causes major territorial infarcts. The non-differential burden of WMHs may indicate that, as a highly heterogeneous marker, WMHs are influenced by a broader set of age- and vascular-related risk factors common to both groups. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to apply cluster analysis to patients with HF to preliminarily explore subtypes with varying risk of brain injury.

In the present study, sex was not associated with any of the neuroimaging markers assessed, including large territorial infarcts, lacunar infarcts, WMH burden, or cerebral atrophy. Although this appears inconsistent with previous literature reporting sex-based differences in HF-related brain injury[47,48], the findings by Kuipers et al. offer a nuanced comparison. In their cohort of 57 women and 91 men with cognitive complaints, Kuipers et al. identified sex differences specifically in non-lacunar (sub)cortical and lacunar infarcts, yet found no disparities in brain atrophy or WMH burden, paralleling the null findings in our study for these latter markers. Theoretical considerations suggest that physiological distinctions between sexes, such as the female preponderance of HFpEF, differing vascular risk profiles, estrogen-mediated neuroprotection, and variations in neurovascular coupling, collectively influence cerebral vulnerability to cardiac dysfunction[48]. However, our ability to detect such differences was likely constrained by the relatively small number of female participants (n=40) compared with males (n=103), potentially limiting statistical power to detect subtle imaging phenotypes.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study need to be considered. Firstly, it was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Because conventional brain MRI was not routinely performed in HF patients, a substantial proportion were excluded for not having undergone a brain MRI. Patients with metal implants, such as pacemakers, or those who could not tolerate MRI scans, were also excluded from our study. Meanwhile, the majority of patients in this study underwent brain MRI due to some sort of psychiatric or neurological symptoms. Our study likely overestimates the prevalence of subtle cerebrovascular disease among patients with less symptomatic heart failure. Although this limits the generalizability of our findings to stable outpatients, it provides crucial insights into the substantial neuroimaging burden present in this high-risk, clinically vulnerable population. Second, given the retrospective nature of the study, we are unable to determine the temporal sequence between the occurrence of these symptoms and HF. This may also lead to selection bias. These preliminary findings warrant validation through larger longitudinal prospective studies and randomized controlled trials. Such studies should assess whether multidisciplinary interventions targeting hemodynamic stability, systemic inflammation, or metabolic optimization can mitigate structural brain changes. Furthermore, the severity of HF-related brain injury may vary by sex. In future studies with larger sample sizes, we aim to investigate this further and conduct sex-stratified analyses. Third, we used visual rating scales rather than volumetric MRI analysis to evaluate brain injury in the present study. However, visual rating scales, which require no specialized software or technical expertise, are also widely recognized as a simple and reliable framework​ for leveraging structural imaging data.

Conclusions

In conclusion, structural brain abnormalities are highly prevalent among HF patients. Smoking, diabetes, and elevated NT-proBNP levels predict large brain infarcts. Age, hypertension, elevated fibrinogen, and higher HDL-C are associated with WMH severity; and age and SII are linked to cerebral atrophy. A clinical subtype with a stronger systemic inflammatory response and poorer nutritional status may carry a higher risk of structural brain injuries in HF.
