Block Structure and Spectrum of Zero-Divisor Graphs of Lipschitz Quaternion Rings Modulo n \displaystyle n
Bilal Ahmad Rather

Abstract
We investigate the adjacency matrices of zero-divisor graphs derived from Lipschitz quaternion rings modulo n \displaystyle n . For odd primes p \displaystyle p , utilizing the isomorphism 𝕃 p ≅ M 2 ​ ( 𝔽 p ) \displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{p}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}) , we categorize vertices by kernel-image type and demonstrate that the adjacency matrix possesses a block structure as a blow-up of a projective incidence matrix. This produces a reduced matrix on the class-constant subspace, with precise formula for th e lower bound for the nullity and the multiplicity of the eigenvalue − 1 \displaystyle-1 , as well as a closed expression for the spectral radius through an equitable partition. For the two-adic family, we precisely ascertain the graph at n = 2 \displaystyle n=2 and demonstrate that for t ≥ 2 \displaystyle t\geq 2 , the graph G 2 t \displaystyle G_{2^{t}} encompasses substantial cliques derived from the ideal filtering, which yield definitive lower bounds for the spectral radius. We also examine the implications for graph energy and provide a systematic construction of the adjacency matrix.

The interplay between algebra and graph theory has produced a significant amount of research during the past four decades. A fundamental foundation was Beck’s graph-theoretic methodology concerning ring elements

Since that time, the geometry of zero-divisor graphs has been examined from numerous perspectives: connectivity, diameter, girth, domination, Eulerian properties, and behavior under direct products have all been investigated across various classes of rings

Adjacency matrices are fundamental entities in spectral graph theory. They regulate walk enumeration, spectral limits, partition-based reductions, rank and nullity phenomena, graph energy, and algorithmic compression; refer to

An explicit matrix model for a family of graphs derived from a ring frequently uncovers concealed symmetries that are not apparent through coarse invariants like dimension or girth. Specifically, the identification of repeating rows, block structures, or equitable partitions allows for the analysis of characteristic polynomials and spectral radii without the necessity of enumerating all ring members. This is particularly appealing for graphs associated with noncommutative finite rings, as the size of the ambient ring increases rapidly, rendering brute-force multiplication costly. Among the most natural noncommutative examples are the Lipschitz quaternion rings modulo
\displaystyle n

\mathbb{L}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}_{n}[i,j,k]=\{a+bi+cj+dk:\ a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\},

with the usual quaternion relations inherited modulo
\displaystyle n

At present, the adjacency matrix serves as an intermediary connecting the finite-ring aspect to spectral graph theory, prompting questions into rank, nullity, multiplicities of specific eigenvalues, reduced quotient matrices, and efficient building techniques. None of these characteristics is addressed in
[
13
]

The technical importance of the issue is dual-faceted. Initially, under the scenario of an odd prime
\displaystyle n=p
, the isomorphism
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{p}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
enables the classification of every nonzero zero divisor by a pair of projective lines via its kernel and its image. This clearly indicates that the adjacency relation should allow for an incidence interpretation on
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

Consequently, we provide precise degree formulas, an exact edge count, a decomposition of the characteristic polynomial, an extensive forced nullspace, and a definitive spectral-radius formula.

Secondly, in the two-adic scenario, the graph is not governed by a matrix-ring model. Nonetheless, the filtration by powers of
\displaystyle 2
within
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{2^{t}}
generates substantial complete subgraphs. This provides definitive principal submatrices of the form
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}-I
, and hence yielding explicit lower bounds for the spectral radius and the edge count. For the minimal modulus
\displaystyle n=2
, the graph can be fully characterized, and its adjacency matrix can be precisely formulated. We demonstrate that this graph is the friendship graph
\displaystyle F_{3}
, so allowing its spectrum to be calculated in closed form. The problem we study can therefore be stated precisely as follows. For the zero-divisor graph
\displaystyle G_{n}=\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{n}),
we determine structural and spectral properties of its adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A_{n}=A(G_{n}).
We compute or bound spectral invariants such as
\displaystyle\rho(A_{n})
,
\displaystyle\operatorname{nullity}(A_{n})
, and special eigenvalue multiplicities, and produce matrix-theoretic reductions that are substantially smaller than the orginal graph.
For odd primes
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle A_{p}
is permutation similar to
\displaystyle H_{p}\otimes\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-D_{p}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p-1},
where
\displaystyle H_{p}
is a projective incidence matrix on ordered line-pairs,
\displaystyle D_{p}
records the diagonal types, and
\displaystyle\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product. This block model produces precise formulas for degrees and edges, along with a decomposition of the characteristic polynomial. We subsequently identify an equitable two-cell quotient and obtain a closed formula for the spectral radius. For
\displaystyle 2

\displaystyle n=2

Preliminaries and notation

\displaystyle n\geq 2

\mathbb{L}_{n}=\mathbb{Z}_{n}[i,j,k]=\{a+bi+cj+dk:\ a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}\}

\displaystyle n

Its undirected zero-divisor graph
\displaystyle G_{n}=\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{n})

\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{n}

\displaystyle x,y

\displaystyle xy=0

\displaystyle yx=0

\displaystyle G_{n}

\displaystyle A_{n}=A(G_{n})

\displaystyle(0,1)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\rho(A_{n})
for the spectral radius,
\displaystyle\operatorname{nullity}(A_{n})
for the nullity, and
\displaystyle\chi_{A_{n}}(\lambda)=\det(\lambda I-A_{n})
for the characteristic polynomial.

For a graph
\displaystyle G
with adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A(G)
, the
nullity
of
\displaystyle G
is
\displaystyle\operatorname{nullity}(A(G))
, and the
rank
is
\displaystyle\operatorname{rank}(A(G))
. A partition
\displaystyle V(G)=V_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{r}
is called
equitable
if every vertex in
\displaystyle V_{i}
has the same number of neighbors in
\displaystyle V_{j}
for each pair
\displaystyle i,j
. The associated
\displaystyle r\times r
quotient matrix will be denoted by
\displaystyle Q
, see
[
11
,
9
]
.

The following facts are standard and will be used repeatedly.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle R
be a finite ring with identity. Then the following holds.

(i)

Every nonunit of
\displaystyle R
is a zero divisor.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle R=R_{1}\oplus R_{2}
, then
\displaystyle(a_{1},a_{2})
is a zero divisor if and only if at least one of
\displaystyle a_{1},a_{2}
is a zero divisor.

(iii)

If
\displaystyle p
is an odd prime, then
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{p}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
.

Proof.

Part (i) is standard for finite rings, see
[
16
, Chapter 1]
. Part (ii) is immediate from coordinatewise multiplication. Part (iii) is proved in

For later use, we also record a matrix-ring characterization of adjacency at prime modulus.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\displaystyle p
be an odd prime and identify
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{p}
with
\displaystyle M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. If
\displaystyle A,B\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
are nonzero singular matrices, then

\displaystyle AB=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(B)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(A),
and
\displaystyle BA=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(B).
As
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
have rank
\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle AB=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(B)=\operatorname{Ker}(A),
and
\displaystyle BA=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(A)=\operatorname{Ker}(B).

Proof.

The implications
\displaystyle AB=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(B)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(A)
and
\displaystyle BA=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(A)\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(B)
hold for arbitrary linear maps. For nonzero singular
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrices over the field
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}
, rank-nullity gives
\displaystyle\dim\operatorname{Ker}(A)=\dim\operatorname{Im}(A)=1
, and similarly for
\displaystyle B
. Hence, the relevant inclusions are equivalent to equality of lines.
∎

3
Odd prime modulus: a block decomposition of the adjacency matrix

Throughout this section,
\displaystyle p
denotes an odd prime, and from Proposition
2.1
, we use the identification
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{p}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. Let
\displaystyle V=\mathbb{F}_{p}^{2}
, and let
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
be the set of
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional subspaces of
\displaystyle V
. As the cardinality of projective line is
\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{F}_{p})|=p+1
, so we have
\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}|=p+1
. For a nonzero singular matrix
\displaystyle A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
, both
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(A)
lie in
\displaystyle\mathcal{L}
. This motivates the following classification.

Definition 3.1
.

For
\displaystyle L,M\in\mathcal{L}
, let

\mathcal{C}_{L,M}:=\{A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p}):A\neq 0,\ \det(A)=0,\ \operatorname{Ker}(A)=L,\ \operatorname{Im}(A)=M\}.

We call
\displaystyle(L,M)
the
type
of
\displaystyle A
, and the family
\displaystyle\{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}\}_{L,M\in\mathcal{L}}
the
type partition
.

The following lemma gives the order of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}.

Lemma 3.2
.

For every
\displaystyle L,M\in\mathcal{L}
, we have
\displaystyle|\mathcal{C}_{L,M}|=p-1.
Consequently, the type partition has
\displaystyle(p+1)^{2}
parts, each of cardinality
\displaystyle p-1
, and
\displaystyle|V(G_{p})|=(p+1)^{2}(p-1)=p^{3}+p^{2}-p-1.

Proof.

For
\displaystyle L,M\in\mathcal{L}
, a nonzero linear transformation
\displaystyle A:V\to V
with
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)=L
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(A)=M
factors uniquely as

V\longrightarrow V/L\xrightarrow{\ \varphi\ }M\hookrightarrow V,

where
\displaystyle\varphi:V/L\to M
is a nonzero linear map between
\displaystyle 1
-dimensional
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}
-vector spaces. Such maps are in bijection with
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times}
, and hence there are exactly
\displaystyle p-1
possibilities. Now, the vertex count follows immediately.
∎

We now give the first main theorem of the paper.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
, be indexed by ordered pairs of lines, and let a
\displaystyle(p+1)^{2}\times(p+1)^{2}
matrix
\displaystyle H_{p}=(h_{\alpha,\beta})_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{T}}
be defined as

h_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }M=L^{\prime}\text{ or }M^{\prime}=L,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Also, let the diagonal matrix
\displaystyle D_{p}=(d_{\alpha,\beta})_{\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{T}}
be

d_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }(L,M)=(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\text{ and }L=M,\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A_{p}=A(G_{p})
is permutation similar to

A_{p}\sim H_{p}\otimes\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-D_{p}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p-1}.

Equivalently, after ordering the vertices by type classes
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, the block indexed by
\displaystyle(L,M)
and
\displaystyle(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})
is

B_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1},&\text{if }(L,M)=(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\text{ and }L=M,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathbf{J}_{p-1},&\text{if }(L,M)\neq(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\text{ and }(M=L^{\prime}\text{ or }M^{\prime}=L),\\[2.84526pt]
\mathbf{O}_{p-1},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Choose an ordering of the vertices of
\displaystyle G_{p}
obtained by listing first all elements of one type class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, then all elements of the next type class, and so on over all ordered pairs
\displaystyle(L,M)\in\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
. With this ordering, the adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A_{p}
is decomposed into
\displaystyle(p+1)^{2}\times(p+1)^{2}
blocks, each block having size
\displaystyle(p-1)\times(p-1)
, as by Lemma
3.2
every type class has cardinality
\displaystyle p-1
. For two type classes
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
, and let
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M},B\in\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}.
Then by definition of type, we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)=L,\operatorname{Im}(A)=M,\operatorname{Ker}(B)=L^{\prime},
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(B)=M^{\prime}.
As
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are nonzero singular
\displaystyle 2\times 2
matrices over

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{p}
, they both have rank
\displaystyle 1
, so by Lemma
2.3
, we have
\displaystyle AB=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(B)=\operatorname{Ker}(A)\iff M^{\prime}=L,
and
\displaystyle BA=0\iff\operatorname{Im}(A)=\operatorname{Ker}(B)\iff M=L^{\prime}.
Since, two distinct vertices in
\displaystyle G_{p}
are adjacent exactly when one of the two products is zero. So, we have

A\sim B\iff(AB=0\text{ or }BA=0)\iff(M^{\prime}=L\text{ or }M=L^{\prime}).

The point to note that this condition depends only on the types
\displaystyle(L,M)
and
\displaystyle(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, and not on the particular choices of

\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
and
\displaystyle B\in\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
. Now, assume first that
\displaystyle(L,M)\neq(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})
. Then
\displaystyle A
and
\displaystyle B
are
automatically distinct vertices, so the above criterion shows: (i) if
\displaystyle M=L^{\prime}
or
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L
, then every vertex of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
is adjacent to every vertex of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
, (ii) the whole block between these two classes is the all-ones matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}
, and (iii) if neither
\displaystyle M=L^{\prime}
nor
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L
holds, then there are no edges at all between the two classes, so the block is the zero matrix
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
.
This determines all off-diagonal blocks. It remains to find the diagonal block related to a fixed class

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
. If
\displaystyle A,B\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
with
\displaystyle A\neq B
, then
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)=\operatorname{Ker}(B)=L,
and
\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}(A)=\operatorname{Im}(B)=M.
With the same adjacency idea as above, we have
\displaystyle A\sim B\iff(M=L
or
\displaystyle M=L),
which is equivalent to
\displaystyle A\sim B\iff L=M.
Now, we two cases to consider. (a) If
\displaystyle L=M
, then every two distinct vertices in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,L}
are adjacent, so the induced subgraph on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,L}
is the complete graph on
\displaystyle p-1
vertices, and its adjacency matrix is
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1}
. (b) If
\displaystyle L\neq M
, then no two distinct vertices in
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
are adjacent, so the induced subgraph on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
is empty and its block is
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}.
Combining the description of the off-diagonal and diagonal blocks, the blocks indexed by
\displaystyle(L,M)
and
\displaystyle(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})
are

B_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1},&\text{if }(L,M)=(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\text{ and }L=M,\\[2.84526pt]
\mathbf{J}_{p-1},&\text{if }(L,M)\neq(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\text{ and }(M=L^{\prime}\text{ or }M^{\prime}=L),\\[2.84526pt]
\mathbf{O}_{p-1},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Finally, if
\displaystyle H_{p}
is the
\displaystyle(0,1)
-matrix on the type set

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
defined by

h_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }M=L^{\prime}\text{ or }M^{\prime}=L,\\
0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

and if
\displaystyle D_{p}
is the diagonal matrix with

d_{(L,M),(L,M)}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }L=M,\\
0,&\text{if }L\neq M,\end{cases}

then the above block description is precisely the Kronecker expression

A_{p}\sim H_{p}\otimes\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-D_{p}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{p-1},

where
\displaystyle\sim
denotes permutation similarity.
∎

The above theorem has immediate row-sum consequences.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be a vertex of
\displaystyle G_{p}
. Then the following hold.

(i)

If
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)=\operatorname{Im}(A)
, then
\displaystyle\deg(A)=2p(p-1)+(p-2)=2p^{2}-p-2.

(ii)

If
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)\neq\operatorname{Im}(A)
, then
\displaystyle\deg(A)=(2p+1)(p-1)=2p^{2}-p-1.

In particular,
\displaystyle G_{p}
is biregular with the two degree values differing by exactly
\displaystyle 1
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
. Then by Theorem
3.3
, the neighboring type classes are exactly those
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
satisfying
\displaystyle M=L^{\prime}
or
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L
. If
\displaystyle L\neq M
, the union of these classes consists of

\{(M,X):X\in\mathcal{L}\}\ \cup\ \{(X,L):X\in\mathcal{L}\},

which has
\displaystyle 2(p+1)-1=2p+1
distinct types, none of which is
\displaystyle(L,M)
. Each class contributes
\displaystyle p-1
vertices, thereby giving

\displaystyle\deg(A)=(2p+1)(p-1).
If
\displaystyle L=M
, then the same union has
\displaystyle 2p+1
types, and it includes its own class
\displaystyle(L,L)
. The other
\displaystyle 2p
classes contribute
\displaystyle 2p(p-1)
neighbors, while the own class contributes
\displaystyle p-2
neighbors. Thus,
\displaystyle\deg(A)=2p(p-1)+(p-2).

∎

Corollary 3.5
.

The number of edges of
\displaystyle G_{p}
is
\displaystyle|E(G_{p})|=\frac{(p^{2}-1)(2p^{3}+p^{2}-2p-2)}{2}.

Proof.

There are
\displaystyle(p+1)(p-1)=p^{2}-1
vertices with
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)=\operatorname{Im}(A)
, and
\displaystyle p(p+1)(p-1)
vertices with
\displaystyle\operatorname{Ker}(A)\neq\operatorname{Im}(A)
. From Corollary
3.4
, summing degrees and dividing by
\displaystyle 2
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle 2|E(G_{p})|

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(p^{2}-1)(2p^{2}-p-2)+p(p+1)(p-1)(2p^{2}-p-1)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=(p^{2}-1)(2p^{3}+p^{2}-2p-2).

∎

4
Spectral consequences for odd prime modulus

We now exploit the block decomposition from Theorem
3.3
to obtain new spectral information. In this sense, the following theorem shows that all forced eigenvalues arising from row repetition can be isolated explicitly, and that the remaining spectrum lives on a much smaller reduced matrix of order
\displaystyle(p+1)^{2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle p
be an odd prime, and let
\displaystyle A_{p}=A(G_{p})
. For each type class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, let

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{L,M}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}
be its indicator vector. Let
\displaystyle U=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}_{L,M}:\ (L,M)\in\mathcal{T}\},
be the class-constant subspace, and for each class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
let

W_{L,M}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}:\ \operatorname{supp}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{L,M},\ \sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}x_{v}=0\right\}.

Then the following hold.

(i)

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}=U\oplus\bigoplus_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}W_{L,M}
is an
\displaystyle A_{p}
-invariant direct sum decomposition.

(ii)

On
\displaystyle U
, the matrix of
\displaystyle A_{p}
relative to the basis
\displaystyle\{\mathbf{1}_{L,M}\}_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}
is
\displaystyle B_{p}:=(p-1)H_{p}-D_{p}.

(iii)

If
\displaystyle L\neq M
, then
\displaystyle A_{p}|_{W_{L,M}}=0
.

(iv)

If
\displaystyle L=M
, then
\displaystyle A_{p}|_{W_{L,L}}=-I
.

Consequently, the characteristic polynomial of
\displaystyle A_{p}
is

\chi_{A_{p}}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\,p(p+1)(p-2)}(\lambda+1)^{(p+1)(p-2)}\chi_{B_{p}}(\lambda).

Proof.

For each type
\displaystyle(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{L}\times\mathcal{L}
, then by Lemma
3.2
,
\displaystyle n=|\mathcal{C}_{L,M}|=p-1
. We denote
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}
as the coordinate subspace of
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}
associated with the class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
. Thus,
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}=\bigoplus_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}},
since the classes
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
form a partition of
\displaystyle V(G_{p})
. We initially examine one class individually. So, for
\displaystyle(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}
, by definition,
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{L,M}
is the all-ones vector on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, augmented by zeros outside of
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
. Inside the coordinate space
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}\cong\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
, the subspace
\displaystyle W_{L,M}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}:\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}x_{v}=0\right\}

is the kernel of the nonzero linear functional
\displaystyle x\longmapsto\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}x_{v}.
Hence,
\displaystyle W_{L,M}
has codimension
\displaystyle 1
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}
, so we obtain
\displaystyle\dim W_{L,M}=|\mathcal{C}_{L,M}|-1=(p-1)-1=p-2.
Moreover,
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}=\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{L,M}\oplus W_{L,M},
since
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{L,M}\notin W_{L,M}
, as its coordinate sum is
\displaystyle p-1\neq 0
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
, and the dimensions add up
\displaystyle 1+(p-2)=p-1=\dim\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}.

Summing such decomposition over all
\displaystyle(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}
, we obtain

\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}=\bigoplus_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{R}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{L,M}\oplus W_{L,M}\bigr)=U\oplus\bigoplus_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}W_{L,M},

where
\displaystyle U=\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf{1}_{L,M}:(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}\}.
This forms a direct sum, as the supports of vectors from distinct classes are disjoint. In particular,
\displaystyle\dim U=|\mathcal{T}|=(p+1)^{2}
, since the vectors
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{L,M}
have pairwise disjoint supports and are linearly independent. Also,

\sum_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}\dim W_{L,M}=(p+1)^{2}(p-2),

so we have

\dim U+\sum_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}\dim W_{L,M}=(p+1)^{2}+(p+1)^{2}(p-2)=(p+1)^{2}(p-1)=|V(G_{p})|.

Next, we prove
\displaystyle A_{p}
-invariance. From Theorem
3.3
with vertices order by type classes, the matrix
\displaystyle A_{p}
is a block matrix whose block from
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
is consists of
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1},
or, on a diagonal class
\displaystyle(L=L^{\prime}=M=M^{\prime}),\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1}.
For
\displaystyle x\in W_{L,M}
, then
\displaystyle x
is supported entirely on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, and
\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}x_{v}=0.
For any other class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}\neq\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, the contribution of the coordinates of
\displaystyle x
to the
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
-coordinates of
\displaystyle A_{p}x
is obtained by multiplying
\displaystyle x
by the corresponding off-diagonal block, which is either
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}
. If the block is
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
, the contribution is zero, else if the block is
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}x=\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal{C}_{L,M}}x_{v}\right)\mathbf{1}=0,
where
\displaystyle\mathbf{1}
is the all-ones vector of length
\displaystyle p-1
. Thus, every
off-class contribution vanishes. So,
\displaystyle A_{p}x
is supported on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
, so
\displaystyle W_{L,M}
is
\displaystyle A_{p}
-invariant for every
\displaystyle(L,M)
. Now, the action on each
\displaystyle W_{L,M}
is determined by the diagonal block. (1), If
\displaystyle L\neq M
, then by Theorem
3.3
, the diagonal block on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
is
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
. Since, all off-class contributions vanish on
\displaystyle W_{L,M}
, so we get
\displaystyle A_{p}|_{W_{L,M}}=0.
(2), If
\displaystyle L=M
, then by Theorem
3.3
, the diagonal block on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,L}
is
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1}
. For
\displaystyle x\in W_{L,L}
, we have
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}x=0
, since the coordinate sum of
\displaystyle x
is zero. Therefore, we obtain
\displaystyle A_{p}x=(\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1})x=-x,
so
\displaystyle A_{p}|_{W_{L,L}}=-I.
Thus, parts (iii) and (iv) are done. Now, we identify the action on
\displaystyle U
. So, let
\displaystyle e_{L,M}:=\mathbf{1}_{L,M}.
So, the block structure of
\displaystyle A_{p}
depends only on the types,
\displaystyle A_{p}
maps class-constant vectors to class-constant vectors, and hence
\displaystyle U
is
\displaystyle A_{p}
-invariant. We compute
\displaystyle A_{p}e_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
in the basis
\displaystyle\{e_{L,M}\}_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}
. For fixed
\displaystyle(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
, and for a class
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}\neq\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
, the block from
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
is either
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}
or
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
. If it is
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
, then the contribution to the
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L,M}
-coordinates is zero. If it is
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}
, then
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}\mathbf{1}=(p-1)\mathbf{1},
so the contribution is
\displaystyle(p-1)e_{L,M}
. Thus, by Theorem
3.3
, it happens exactly when
\displaystyle M=L^{\prime}
or
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L.
Hence, for
\displaystyle(L,M)\neq(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})
, the coefficient of
\displaystyle e_{L,M}
in
\displaystyle A_{p}e_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
is
\displaystyle(p-1)h_{(L,M),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}.
It remains to compute the coefficient of
\displaystyle e_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
. (a), If
\displaystyle L^{\prime}\neq M^{\prime}
, then the diagonal block on
\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{L^{\prime},M^{\prime}}
is
\displaystyle\mathbf{O}_{p-1}
, so the self-contribution is
\displaystyle 0
. Since in this case
\displaystyle d_{(L^{\prime},M^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=0
, so the coefficient is

0=(p-1)h_{(L^{\prime},M^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}-d_{(L^{\prime},M^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}.

Thus, if
\displaystyle L^{\prime}\neq M^{\prime}
, then neither condition
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L^{\prime}
nor
\displaystyle M^{\prime}=L^{\prime}
holds, so
\displaystyle h_{(L^{\prime},M^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},M^{\prime})}=0
. (b), if
\displaystyle L^{\prime}=M^{\prime}
, then the diagonal block is
\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1}
, and therefore

(\mathbf{J}_{p-1}-\mathbf{I}_{p-1})\mathbf{1}=\bigl((p-1)-1\bigr)\mathbf{1}=(p-2)\mathbf{1}.

So, the self-contribution is
\displaystyle(p-2)e_{L^{\prime},L^{\prime}}=((p-1)-1)e_{L^{\prime},L^{\prime}}.
On the other hand, in this case
\displaystyle h_{(L^{\prime},L^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},L^{\prime})}=1,

\displaystyle d_{(L^{\prime},L^{\prime}),(L^{\prime},L^{\prime})}=1,
and hence
\displaystyle(p-2)=(p-1)\cdot 1-1.
Now, combining the off-diagonal and diagonal coefficients, if follows that relative to the basis
\displaystyle\{e_{L,M}\}_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}
, the matrix of
\displaystyle A_{p}|_{U}
is precisely
\displaystyle B_{p}=(p-1)H_{p}-D_{p}.
This proves part (ii). Finally, by part (i), we have the
\displaystyle A_{p}
-invariant direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{V(G_{p})}=U\oplus\bigoplus_{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}}W_{L,M},
and by parts (ii)–(iv) the operator
\displaystyle A_{p}
acts block diagonally with: one block
\displaystyle B_{p}
on
\displaystyle U
, the zero operator on each
\displaystyle W_{L,M}
with
\displaystyle L\neq M
, and the operator
\displaystyle-I
on each
\displaystyle W_{L,L}
. Therefore, the characteristic polynomial factors as the product of the characteristic polynomials of these blocks is

\chi_{A_{p}}(\lambda)=\chi_{B_{p}}(\lambda)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}\\
L\neq M\end{subarray}}\lambda^{\dim W_{L,M}}\prod_{L\in\mathcal{L}}(\lambda+1)^{\dim W_{L,L}}.

Now, the number of off-diagonal classes is

|\{(L,M)\in\mathcal{T}:L\neq M\}|=(p+1)^{2}-(p+1)=p(p+1),

and each such class contributes dimension
\displaystyle p-2
. Similarly, the number of diagonal classes is
\displaystyle p+1
, and each contributes dimension
\displaystyle p-2
. Hence, we have

\chi_{A_{p}}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\,p(p+1)(p-2)}(\lambda+1)^{(p+1)(p-2)}\chi_{B_{p}}(\lambda).

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 4.2

For every odd prime
\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\operatorname{nullity}(A_{p})\geq p(p+1)(p-2),

\displaystyle m_{A_{p}}(-1)\geq(p+1)(p-2),

\displaystyle-1

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}

\displaystyle(2p+1)(p-1)-2(p-1)=(2p-1)(p-1).

\displaystyle GL_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

\displaystyle M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{gL,gM}

he following theorem computes the spectrum of

Theorem 5.1
.

In
\displaystyle\mathbb{L}_{2}
, let
\displaystyle u=1+i,
and
\displaystyle v=1+j.
Then

Energy of the adjacency matrices

The perspective of the adjacency matrix presented in the preceding sections inherently culminates in the
energy
of the graph. Energy serves as a fundamental spectral invariant in algebraic graph theory and mathematical chemistry, as it quantifies the overall magnitude of the adjacency spectrum, represents the trace norm of the adjacency matrix, and frequently exhibits sensitivity to repeated rows, equitable quotients, and low-rank reductions
[
14
,
17
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
.
The zero-divisor graphs
\displaystyle G_{n}=\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{n})
are particularly appealing as Sections
4
and
5
provide the essential structural components necessary for energy estimation, including bounding eigenvalues, quotient matrices, precise spectral radii, and large complete principal blocks.

From a ring-theoretic standpoint, energy enhances the invariants already calculated in this study. The quantity of vertices and edges specifies the size of graph, the spectral radius indicates its dominant mode, and the nullity reflects linear dependence among rows, in contrast, the energy quantifies the cumulative weight of the entire spectrum. Consequently, it provides a comprehensive assessment of the extent to which the adjacency matrix deviates from spectral sparsity. In the current context, this is especially evident as for odd primes, the graph
\displaystyle G_{p}
deviates significantly from regularity yet still allows for a robust block reduction, whereas for powers of
\displaystyle 2
, the graph encompasses substantial large subgraphs. Both phenomena are seen in the energy.

Let
\displaystyle G
be a finite simple graph with adjacency eigenvalues
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{|V(G)|}.
The
energy
of
\displaystyle G
is
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(G):=\sum_{r=1}^{|V(G)|}|\lambda_{r}|.
Equivalently, since
\displaystyle A(G)
is real symmetric, so

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(G)=\|A(G)\|_{*},
is the trace norm of the adjacency matrix.

We record two elementary inequalities that will be used repeatedly.

Proposition 7.1
.

[
17
]

Let
\displaystyle G
be a finite simple graph with adjacency matrix
\displaystyle A
and spectral
radius
\displaystyle\rho(A)
. Then
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(G)\geq 2\rho(A),
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(G)\geq\tfrac{\operatorname{tr}(A^{2})}{\rho(A)}=\tfrac{2|E(G)|}{\rho(A)}.

The subsequent theorem presents the inaugural explicit energy decomposition for the odd-prime zero-divisor graphs
\displaystyle G_{p}

\displaystyle(p+1)^{2}

\displaystyle B_{p}

8
Conclusion

This study presents a matrix-based approach for analyzing zero-divisor graphs of Lipschitz quaternion rings modulo
\displaystyle n
. The primary contribution in the odd-prime case is a definitive block model for
\displaystyle A_{p}
derived from the kernel-image geometry of singular matrices in
\displaystyle M_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{p})
. This description compresses the graph into a somewhat smaller incidence structure, yielding precise degree and edge formulas, a factorization of the characteristic polynomial, lower bounds for nullity and the multiplicity of
\displaystyle-1
, as well as an explicit calculation for the spectral radius. It also indicates that a significant portion of the spectrum is dictated by recurrent block structure.

For powers of
\displaystyle 2
, while a corresponding matrix-ring model is missing, the filtration by ideals continues to offer valuable adjacency information. The graph at
\displaystyle n=2
can be fully specified, and for higher powers
\displaystyle 2^{t}
, large complete subgraphs provide explicit spectral and combinatorial lower bounds. The energy estimates further highlight the disparity between the odd-prime family, which has a reduced spectral model, and the two-adic family, where only partial block information is presently obtainable.

The results indicate that the adjacency matrices of these zero-divisor graphs have significantly greater structure than what is apparent from conventional graph invariants alone. A logical subsequent step is to ascertain the complete spectrum for
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{p^{t}})
for
\displaystyle t\geq 2
and to derive more precise structural characterizations for the graphs
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{2^{t}})
.

We therefore conclude with two natural directions for further work.

Open Problem 8.1
.

Determine the full spectrum of
\displaystyle A(\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{p^{t}}))
for odd primes
\displaystyle p
and integers
\displaystyle t\geq 2
. In particular, decide whether the reduced matrix
\displaystyle B_{p}
admits a further projective-geometric decomposition that makes
\displaystyle\chi_{A_{p}}
completely explicit.

Open Problem 8.2
.

For
\displaystyle t\geq 2
, determine the exact clique number and the exact characteristic polynomial of
\displaystyle A_{2^{t}}
. The clique in Theorem
5.2
provides a large lower bound, but it is not yet clear whether it is maximal.

The adjacency-matrix approach developed here suggests that a substantial portion of the fine structure of
\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathbb{L}_{n})
remains to be uncovered. In particular, rank, nullity, quotient matrices, and block reductions seem promising for other noncommutative finite-ring graph families as well.
