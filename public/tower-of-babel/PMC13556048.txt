Neurological manifestations in Nigerian children with sickle cell disease: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Evan Adetoye, Adetola Adebanwo, Funmilola Banjo, Comfort Akanni, AbdulHafiz Abdus-Salaam, Temitope Otunaiya, Timilehin Abisola, Olumide Bankole, Temitope Folorunso, Favour Darah, Jokotade Okuribido, Yunus Dele, Fatimah Odumuyiwa, Chisom Dickson, Anthonia Adebambo, Gbenga Ogunbayode, Iyinloluwa Senjobi, Segun Odejide, Damilola Odemona, Bukola Adenuga, Comfort Olofin, Adewole Bello, Samuel Ogunlade, Tobi Olajide, Luqman Ogunjimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary hemoglobinopathy that poses a significant public health problem in Nigeria, associated with significant morbidity and mortality among affected children. Neurological manifestations contribute substantially to disability and adverse outcomes, however, comprehensive data on their burden and determinants in pediatric SCD patients remain limited.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study examined children under 15 years of age with confirmed SCD managed at Olabisi Onabanjo University Teaching Hospital (OOUTH) between March 2022 and February 2025. A structured proforma was used to extract demographic data, clinical history, neurological manifestations, associated factors, and outcomes. The neurological manifestations assessed included seizure, stroke, paralysis, headache, visual disturbance, and silent cerebral infarct. Laboratory and imaging data, where available, were used to support the diagnoses. Data were analyzed using descriptive and bivariate statistical methods.ResultsAmong the 133 children with SCD, 28 (21.1%) had documented neurological manifestations. Seizures were the most frequent (12.8%), followed by headaches (9.8%), paralysis (9.0%), and stroke (6.8%). Most neurological events occurred between 5 and 10 years of age, with a mean onset of 7.2 ± 3.8 years. Neurological manifestations were significantly associated with age at presentation (p = 0.032), higher frequency of crises (p = 0.02), and prior history of neurological manifestations (p < 0.001). No statistically significant associations were observed between neurological manifestations and sex (p = 0.232), anemia (p = 0.6), or infections (p = 0.812). Neurological manifestations were associated with increased mortality, residual deficits, and longer hospital stay. Neurological manifestations were present in half (n = 4) of all recorded deaths, with an overall mortality of 6% in the patients studied. Among survivors, approximately one-third had residual neurological deficits.ConclusionNeurological manifestations in children with SCD are relatively common and are associated with adverse outcomes, including disability and prolonged hospitalization. These findings underscore the urgent need for improved early detection and context-appropriate preventive and management strategies to reduce this burden in resource-limited settings.Keywords: Sickle cell disease, Neurological manifestations, Children, Seizures, Stroke, Nigeria

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary hemoglobinopathy characterized by the production of abnormal hemoglobin S (HbS), which causes red blood cells to assume a rigid, sickle-like shape under deoxygenated conditions [1]. Under this reduced oxygen tension, HbS polymerizes, resulting in erythrocyte deformation, hemolysis, vaso-occlusion, and multiorgan ischemia, which underlie the major clinical manifestations of the disease.

Globally, SCD affects approximately 300,000 newborns annually, with projections suggesting an increase to over 400,000 births by 2050 due to population growth and limited access to genetic counseling and newborn screening programs [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the greatest burden, accounting for more than 75% of affected births worldwide [3]. Nigeria alone contributes an estimated 150,000 new cases annually, making it the country with the highest global burden of SCD [4].

SCD is a multisystem disorder, with clinical manifestations typically beginning around six months of age following the physiological replacement of fetal hemoglobin with adult hemoglobin [5]. Common complications include recurrent vaso-occlusive crises, severe infections, acute chest syndrome, stroke, and chronic pulmonary hypertension [6]. Among these complications, neurological manifestations are particularly significant because of their association with long-term morbidity, impaired neurocognitive development, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality in affected children.

Neurological manifestations in SCD arise primarily from chronic hemolysis, vaso-occlusion, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and impaired cerebral perfusion, all of which contribute to cerebral vasculopathy and hypoxic injury within the central nervous system [7–10]. Reported manifestations include headaches, seizures, ischemic stroke, silent cerebral infarcts, and central nervous system infections [9]. These complications may occur early in life and can adversely affect cognitive, behavioral, and functional outcomes. Kago-Tague et al. [11] reported that more than one-quarter of children with SCD developed neurological manifestations before the age of six years, with some cases occurring as early as two years of age.

Several factors have been associated with an increased risk of neurological manifestations in children with SCD. Severe anemia may worsen cerebral hypoxia, recurrent infections may precipitate vaso-occlusive events and central nervous system injury, while socioeconomic status may influence access to healthcare, early diagnosis, and continuity of care. Despite the high burden of SCD in Nigeria, there is limited institution-based data describing the prevalence, spectrum, associated factors, and outcomes of neurological manifestations among affected children in resource-limited settings where diagnostic and neuroimaging facilities may be constrained. This limits early recognition, risk stratification, and evidence-based management of affected children.

The aim of this study is to determine the prevalence, pattern, associated factors, and outcomes of neurological manifestations among children under 15 years with sickle cell disease at OOUTH.

Methods

This study was conducted at OOUTH, a tertiary healthcare facility located in Sagamu, Ogun State, South-Western Nigeria. It has a dedicated hematology unit within the pediatrics department, which caters to children under 15 years of age. The study utilized a retrospective hospital-based design, focusing on pediatric patients diagnosed with SCD and experiencing neurological manifestations. The study covered three years from March 2022 to February 2025 and involved pediatric patients between the ages of 0 and 15 years.

Children aged 0 to under 15 years with a confirmed diagnosis of sickle cell disease who received care at OOUTH during the study period and had complete medical records, including clinical assessments and laboratory results, were eligible for inclusion. Records with missing data on variables not relevant to the primary outcome were retained whenever neurological outcome data were available. Patients excluded from the study were those with a documented history of neurological conditions due to non-SCD causes, such as traumatic brain injury, congenital brain malformations, or infections.

A total population sampling technique was employed to minimize selection bias and enhance the reliability of the study. Patients were selected based on the predefined eligibility criteria to ensure comprehensive inclusion of all available cases within the specified period. All children diagnosed with sickle cell disease who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. Participants were subsequently stratified according to the presence or absence of neurological manifestations, with additional stratification based on specific neurological manifestations identified.

A structured proforma was used to extract data from case files of children being treated for SCD and was organized into five sections aligned with the study objectives. The data collected included demographic characteristics, age at diagnosis, frequency of hospital admissions, type and frequency of sickle cell-related crises, and co-existing medical conditions. Information on documented neurological complications, as well as potential associations such as socio-economic status, infections, anemia, prior neurological events, and nutritional status, was recorded. Potential associations included in the study were selected based on careful review of evidence from existing literature. Details of relevant laboratory investigations, neuroimaging, and treatment modalities were also reviewed where available.

Data extraction was performed by three pairs of independent investigators using the structured proforma. The extracted data were subsequently cross-checked for consistency and accuracy by another three pairs of independent investigators, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion and review of the medical records.

For this study, neurological manifestation was defined as any clinically documented disorder involving the central or peripheral nervous system occurring in a child with SCD during the study period, as recorded by a physician in the case files and supported by neurological examination findings, neuroimaging where available, and specialist review. Anemia and socio-economic status were defined according to the WHO age-specific hemoglobin cut-offs and the Oyediji socioeconomic classification based on parental occupation and educational attainment.

The data collected were analyzed with SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize both categorical and continuous variables, with the results presented using tables. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and the differences were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test when expected counts were less than five. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations, and comparisons were made using the t-test for normally distributed data after assessment of normality via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Multivariate analysis was explored but not performed due to the limited number of outcome events; therefore, the analysis was restricted to descriptive and bivariate methods. The extent of missing data was minimal for included variables; therefore, analyses were performed using available complete records without imputation.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Human Research Ethics Committee of OOUTH (OOUTH/HREC/816/2024AP). As this was a retrospective review of medical records involving children under 15 years of age, conducted without direct patient contact, and all data anonymized, the requirement for written informed consent from parents or legal guardians was waived by the Ethics Committee in accordance with institutional guidelines. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Clinical and sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 133 SCD patients, 75 (56.4%) males and 58 (43.6%) females, were included in this study, with 126 (94.7%) and 7 (5.3%) having the HbSS and HbSC genotypes, respectively. The mean age of the participants was 8.12 ± 4.22 years, with the majority, 100 (75.2%), being at least 5 years old. The mean age at sickle cell disease diagnosis was 4.17 ± 4.05 years. Children with the HbSS genotype were diagnosed earlier, with a mean of 4.05 ± 3.97 years, than were those with the HbSC genotype, whose mean at diagnosis was 6.29 ± 5.2 years (Table 1).

Neurological manifestations

Among the 133 children with SCD, 28 (21.1%) had experienced at least one neurological manifestation. Of these, 13 (46.4%) were male and 15 (53.6%) were female. 26 (92.9%) patients with neurological manifestations had the HbSS genotype, whereas 2 (7.1%) had the HbSC genotype. Neurological manifestations were recorded in all age groups, with the majority (78.6%) being between 5 and 15 years. The mean age at first neurological manifestation was 7.23 ± 3.83 years (Table 2). Most children 20 (71.4%), had at least one episode of neurological manifestation within 5 years; 7 (25%) children had yearly episodes, and only 1 (3.6%) had monthly episodes (Table 3).

Of the 13 cases of headache reported, 12 (92.3%) had acute episodes while only 1 (7.7%) had chronic episode (Table 4). Nine (69.2%) of the 13 children had frontal headache, 2 (15.4%) had generalized headache, and 2 (15.4%) had right-sided headache (Table 5).

Twelve (9%) children suffered from paralysis. Hemiplegia (58.3%) was the most common type of paralysis, with 33% and 25% having left and right hemiplegia, respectively. Other types of paralysis recorded were right facial paralysis (16.7%), right hemiparesis (16.7%), and less frequently, left facial paralysis (8.3) (Table 5).

Ischemic stroke was the predominant stroke type, representing 8 of the 9 stroke cases. The middle cerebral artery was affected in more than 62.5% of the patients, followed by combinations of ACA-MCA (12.5%) and MCA-ICA (12.5%). Cortical involvement alone was most common (62.5%), with subcortical (25%) and mixed cases (12.5%) also recorded. The only case of hemorrhagic stroke affected the MCA and right basal ganglia (Table 6, 7, 8 and 9).

Factors associated with neurological manifestations

There was a statistically significant association between age at presentation (p = 0.032) and the presence of neurological manifestations. SCD children with neurological manifestations had a significantly greater (p = 0.02) number of episodes of sickle cell disease crisis per year, with a mean of 1.93 ± 1.39. 7 (25.0%) of the 28 SCD children with neurological manifestations had a previous history of neurological manifestation prior to the study period, and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Sex, anemia, infections, and socioeconomic status, however, were not significantly associated (p > 0.05) (Table 11).

Outcome of neurological manifestations

One hundred and twenty-five children studied lived whereas 8 died. Of the 8 recorded deaths, 4 (50%) occurred in children with neurological manifestations. The overall mortality was 6.0%, while mortality among patients with neurological manifestations was 14.3%. Children with neurological manifestations had longer hospital stays (13.46 ± 10.74) compared to children without these manifestations (8.38 ± 6.64). Residual neurological deficits were present in 32.1% (9) of the children with neurological manifestations (Table 12). The most common residual neurological deficit at discharge was left hemiparesis (44.4%), followed by right hemiparesis (22.2%), right facial paralysis (11.1%), right esotropia (11.1%), and impaired speech (11.1%) (Table 13).

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence, patterns, associated factors, and outcomes of neurological manifestations in children under 15 years of age with SCD at a tertiary hospital in Nigeria. Neurological manifestations were identified in 28 of the 133 children, giving a prevalence of 21.1%. Seizures, headaches, paralysis, and strokes were the most frequently observed complications. These findings show that neurological manifestations remain a significant burden among the pediatric SCD population and underscore the importance of early identification and intervention to prevent progression and long-term disability.

The prevalence observed in this study is comparable to that reported in Saudi Arabia by Basuni et al. [12], who reported a prevalence of 26.7%, but it is lower than that reported in several sub-Saharan African studies. For example, Lagunju et al. [13] reported neurological manifestations in more than 36% of children with SCD. The relatively low prevalence in this study may reflect methodological limitations, including reliance on retrospective clinical records and limited access to neuroimaging, which may explain the underdiagnosis of subclinical conditions such as silent cerebral infarcts. This is further supported by the low prevalence of silent infarcts in this cohort (3.0%), compared with the wide range of 5.6–80.6% reported by Houwing et al. [14].

Seizures were the most common neurological manifestation (12.8%), with the majority of episodes occurring between 1 and 10 years of age. Generalized seizures were predominant, accounting for 76.5% of the cases. This pattern may reflect broader cortical involvement rather than localized lesions and may also indicate underlying cerebral vasculopathy in some patients. The prevalence of seizures in this study exceeded both the pooled African estimate of 4.4% [15] and the 9.3% reported in Ibadan [16]. Referral bias may partly contribute to this, as tertiary centers manage more severe cases. Additionally, the inability to detect silent infarcts, which have been associated with seizures, may have shifted the observed burden toward seizure diagnoses.

Headaches were recorded in approximately 9.8% of the children, with most cases presenting acutely and predominantly involving the frontal region. Diagnosing headaches in young children is challenging because of difficulties in symptom description, leading to underreporting of symptoms. Research has highlighted recurring headaches in SCD patients more significantly with low hemoglobin levels and frequent pain crises rather than with silent infarcts [17].

Stroke was predominantly ischemic, consistent with the recognized pattern of large vessel vasculopathy in pediatric SCD [18]. Cortical infarcts were more common than subcortical lesions, corresponding with the predominant MCA involvement [19]. Stroke commonly presented with focal motor deficits, particularly left and right hemiplegia. Hemorrhagic stroke was rare but has been associated with increased severity and mortality in older pediatric patients [20, 21].

Visual difficulties were recorded in 6 children (4.5%) and included cortical blindness, strabismus, ocular pain, and other nonspecific visual complaints. Cortical blindness accounted for one-third of the cases, which may suggest posterior circulation involvement. There is additional evidence that ocular complications are regularly overlooked. For example, Oladimeji et al. [22] reported that 60% of Nigerian children in the steady state had ocular defects that were primarily subclinical changes, highlighting the need for routine ophthalmic examinations.

No patients with migraine or neuropathic pain were identified in this study. This may reflect diagnostic challenges rather than a true absence of these manifestations. Children may struggle to properly describe migraine features, while the absence of standardized diagnostic tools, such as the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD) or neuropathic pain scales such as DN4 in this center further reduces detection. In contrast, Sigalla et al. [23] reported neuropathic pain in 41% of children when DN4 was applied, suggesting likely underrecognition in the present study.

The majority of neurological manifestations were observed among children aged 5–10 years (42.9%), with statistical analysis demonstrating significant association between age at presentation and neurological manifestations. Older children accounted for a larger proportion of the study population and had longer cumulative exposure to SCD-related complications, which may explain the greater number of neurological events observed. However, younger children may be particularly vulnerable to neurological injury because of early cerebrovascular changes, recurrent anemia, infections, and declining fetal hemoglobin levels during early childhood. In addition, frequent crises and prior neurological manifestations were significantly associated with neurological complications, possibly reflecting more severe disease phenotypes and recurrent cerebrovascular injury in affected children. Together, these findings suggest that neurological complications may begin early in life, while the cumulative burden becomes more apparent with advancing age [11]. There was no statistically significant association between sex and neurological manifestations (p = 0.232), indicating close vulnerability among males and females, which is consistent with reports by Kago-Tague et al. [11].

The absence of associations with other recognized risk factors contrasts with findings from international cohorts [24] and regional studies in Tanzania and Nigeria [25, 26]. This discrepancy may be because of our limited sample size, and retrospective design. It is also possible that regional differences in SCD phenotype, preventive treatment (e.g., hydroxyurea, transfusion programs), and health-seeking behaviors modify these associations in our setting.

Neurological manifestations were associated with increased mortality, residual deficits, and longer hospital stay. Half (4) of all SCD-associated deaths had neurological manifestations, with an overall mortality rate of 6.0%. Approximately one-third of the survivors had lingering neurological impairments, the most common being left hemiparesis (44.4%). The mean hospital stay was greater among affected children (13.5 vs. 8.4 days), which may contribute to increased healthcare burden and greater strain on family members. These findings are in line with other African data that show both higher morbidity and mortality rates than those reported in high-income countries, where the impact of the disease is reduced by interventions involving early diagnosis and preventive measures such as transcranial doppler (TCD) screening, chronic transfusions, and increased use of hydroxyurea.

Limitations

The retrospective, single-center design and small sample size limit the statistical power, statistical analysis exploration (inclusion of multivariate analysis) and generalizability of the findings. The exclusion of patients with incomplete medical records may have introduced selection bias. Limited access to diagnostic investigations such as CT scan, MRI and TCD, may have led to misclassification and underestimation of the burden of neurological manifestations. These factors indicate that our estimates probably represent a conservative picture of the true burden.

Recommendations

Improved diagnosis and prevention are critical. Expanding access to affordable rapid tests suitable for low resource settings could facilitate earlier diagnosis in endemic regions. Routine neurological and developmental surveillance should be integrated into standard care of children with sickle cell disease particularly younger children and those with recurrent vaso-occlusive crises or prior neurological complications, who are at the highest risk. Early and regular neurological assessments, including structured developmental and cognitive screening, may facilitate timely identification of both overt and subclinical complications.

Preventive strategies such as early initiation and wider accessibility of disease modifying therapies such as hydroxyurea, as well as appropriate chronic transfusion programs for high-risk children should be strengthened to reduce neurological morbidity and mortality.

Further prospective multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, and standardized neurocognitive and ophthalmologic assessments are recommended to better characterize the burden, determinants, and long-term outcomes of neurological complications in pediatric SCD patients.

Conclusion

Neurological manifestations in children with SCD are relatively common, and are associated with adverse outcomes, including disability and prolonged hospitalization. These findings underscore the urgent need for improved early detection and context-appropriate preventive and management strategies to reduce this burden in resource-limited settings.
