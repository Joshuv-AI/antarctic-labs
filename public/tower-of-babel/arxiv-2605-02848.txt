Search for a new heavy scalar resonance decaying into the Higgs boson and a new scalar particle in the \PQb ​ \PAQb ​ \PQb ​ \PAQb \PQb\PAQb\PQb\PAQb final state using proton-proton collisions at s = 13 ​ \TeV \sqrt{s}=13\TeV


Abstract
A search for a new heavy scalar resonance ( \PX ) decaying into the 125 \GeV standard model Higgs boson (H) and a new scalar particle ( \HepParticle Y) in proton-proton collisions at a center-of-mass energy of 13 \TeV is presented. The analysis is performed using a data sample corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138 \fbinv collected with the CMS detector during LHC Run 2. The \PQb ​ \PAQb ​ \PQb ​ \PAQb \PQb\PAQb\PQb\PAQb final state is used as a probe to search for phenomena beyond the standard model where, in the \PX → \HepParticle ​ Y ​ \PH \PX\to\HepParticle{Y}{}{}\PH process, the Y and H each decay into a bottom quark-antiquark pair. A range of masses from 400 \GeV to 1.6 \TeV for the resonance \PX and from 60 \GeV to 1.4 \TeV for the scalar \HepParticle Y is investigated. The observations are in agreement with the background-only hypothesis. The largest excess, with a local (global) significance of 3.47 (2.44) standard deviations, is observed for hypothetical \PX and \HepParticle Y masses of 600 and 400 \GeV , respectively. Upper limits at 95% confidence level on the production cross section times branching fraction are presented for signal mass hypotheses in the range of the search. Results are interpreted within the next-to-minimal supersymmetric standard model scenario.

Introduction

The discovery of the Higgs boson (
\PH
) by the ATLAS and CMS Collaborations at the CERN LHC
[
5
,
22
,
23
]
was the confirmation of a cornerstone of the standard model (SM) of particle physics. At the same time, the Higgs boson could be a portal for the discovery of new physics.
Many proposed models of physics beyond the SM (BSM) extend the SM scalar sector with additional fields, resulting in a rich phenomenology with more scalar particles. Examples of such models are the next-to-minimal supersymmetric SM (NMSSM)
[
61
,
71
]
, the two-real-scalar-singlet extension of the SM
[
75
]
, and models with warped extra dimensions
[
20
]
.

The analysis described here explores one possible phenomenological scenario of these theoretical models: the production of a BSM resonance that decays into the SM Higgs boson and another scalar,
\PX\to\HepParticle{Y}{}{}\PH
, where Y may be either a BSM scalar or the SM Higgs boson. This is motivated in, for example, NMSSM
[
64
]
, where a scalar
\PH_{\mathrm{MSSM}}
or pseudoscalar
\HepParticle{A}{}{}_{\mathrm{MSSM}}
can decay into the SM Higgs boson and a scalar
\PH_{\text{singlet}}
or pseudoscalar
\HepParticle{A}{}{}_{\text{singlet}}
, respectively. The branching fraction for this process
B(\PH_{\mathrm{MSSM}}\to\PH+\PH_{\text{singlet}})
or
B(\HepParticle{A}{}{}_{\mathrm{MSSM}}\to\PH+\HepParticle{A}{}{}_{\text{singlet}})
can be as high as 50%
[
69
,
70
,
19
,
63
,
56
]
. Previous searches for this process have been published by the ATLAS Collaboration, using the
\bbbar\PGg\PGg
final state
[
11
]
, and by the CMS Collaboration, using the
\bbbar\PGg\PGg

[
53
]
,
\bbbar\PGt\PGt

[
47
]
,
\PQb\PAQb\PQb\PAQb
with Lorentz-boosted topology
[
49
]
final states, and a combination of these three channels
[
55
]
.

The decay
\PX\to\PH\PH
is of particular interest, since it can be the manifestation of Randall–Sundrum radion or a massive Kaluza–Klein graviton production in models with warped extra dimensions
[
58
,
65
]
, as well as the charge-parity-even scalar predicted by NMSSM
[
14
]
. Multiple searches for Higgs boson pair production have been published by the ATLAS and CMS Collaborations in the
\PQb\PAQb\PQb\PAQb

[
36
,
7
,
8
]
,
\bbbar\PGt\PGt

[
34
,

,
\bbbar\PGg\PGg

[

,
\bbbar\ell\nu\ell\nu

[

,

, and
\bbbar ZZ

[

\PGt\PGt
, and
\PGt\PGt\PGt\PGt

[

, and in combinations of multiple channels
[

,

.

In order to be sensitive to a broad range of scenarios, a model-independent approach is followed in this analysis.
Figure
1
shows an example of X production, in which a heavy scalar resonance X is produced via gluon fusion and then decays into an H and another scalar, Y, with both of them decaying into a
\bbbar
pair. This analysis makes use of the Higgs boson decay mode with the largest branching fraction,
\PH\to\PQb\PAQb
, providing strong motivation for this channel.

This paper presents a search for X resonances with masses from 400
\GeV
up to 1.6
\TeV
and a scalar Y with masses between 60
\GeV
and 1.4
\TeV
.
The boundaries of the search region are defined to encompass the region of phase space where this analysis provides greater sensitivity than other channels probing similar resonances; lower masses are covered by the
\bbbar\PGg\PGg
channel, while higher masses are targeted by analyses using Lorentz-boosted, merged
\PQb
jet topologies.
The signal is extracted from a two-dimensional fit using the masses of the reconstructed X and Y scalars,
m_{\text{Xreco}}
and
m_{\text{Yreco}}
, respectively.
The data samples used for the search correspond to an integrated luminosity of 138
\fbinv
collected by the CMS experiment in the years 2016–2018.
An approach based on control samples in data, utilizing a boosted decision tree (BDT) for reweighting kinematic variables, is implemented for the estimation of the background.
A simultaneous fit to the data samples collected from each year is used to infer the results.
The tabulated results of the analysis are provided in the HEPData record
[
1
]
.

0.2
The CMS detector

The CMS apparatus
[
21
,
51
]
is a multipurpose, nearly hermetic detector, designed to trigger on
[
42
,
28
]
and identify electrons, muons, photons, and (charged and neutral) hadrons
[
45
,
33
,
24
]
. A global “particle-flow” (PF) algorithm
[
27
]
aims to reconstruct all individual particles in an event (PF candidate), combining information provided by the all-silicon inner tracker and by the crystal electromagnetic calorimeter (ECAL), and a brass and scintillator hadron calorimeter (HCAL), operating inside a 3.8
\unit
T superconducting solenoid, with data from the gas-ionization muon detectors embedded in the flux-return yoke outside the solenoid. The PF candidates are used to identify and reconstruct
\PGt
leptons, jets, and missing transverse momentum
[
32
,
26
,
38
]
.

The HCAL cells have widths of 0.087 in pseudorapidity (
\eta
) and 0.087 radians in azimuthal angle (
\phi
), in the region
\abs{\eta}<1.74
. In the
\eta
-
\phi
plane, and for
\abs{\eta}<1.48
, the HCAL cells map on to
5{\times}5
arrays of ECAL crystals to form calorimeter towers that project radially outwards from a location close to the nominal interaction point.
For
\abs{\eta}>1.74
, the transverse dimensions of these towers increase progressively, up to a maximum of 0.174 in both
\Delta\eta
and
\Delta\phi
.

Events of interest are selected using a two-tiered trigger system. The first level (L1), composed of custom hardware processors, uses information from the calorimeters and muon detectors to select events at a rate of around 100
\unit
kHz, within a fixed latency of 4
\mus

[
42
]
. The second level, known as the high-level trigger (HLT), consists of a farm of processors running a version of the full event reconstruction software optimized for fast processing, and reduces the event rate to around 1
\unit
kHz before data storage
[
28
,
52
]
.

0.3
Event reconstruction

In the PF event reconstruction, the identification of the particle type (photon, electron, muon, charged or neutral hadron) plays an important role in the determination of the particle direction and energy.
For each event, hadronic jets are clustered from PF candidates using the infrared and collinear-safe anti-
\kt
algorithm
[
16
,
17
]
with a distance parameter of 0.4. Jet momentum is determined as the vectorial sum of all particle momenta in the jet, and is found from simulation to be, on average, within 5 to 10% of the true momentum over the entire transverse momentum (
\pt
) spectrum and detector acceptance.
The jets in the events are required to have
\abs{\eta}<2.4
for data-taking year 2016 and
\abs{\eta}<2.5
for 2017–2018. The
\abs{\eta}
range for jet reconstruction was increased as a result of the installation of the new pixel detector at the beginning of 2017
[
40
]
.

Additional proton-proton (pp) interactions within the same or nearby bunch crossings (pileup) can contribute additional tracks and calorimetric energy depositions to the jet momentum.
To mitigate this effect, charged particles identified to be originating from pileup vertices are discarded and an offset correction is applied to account for remaining contributions
[
43
]
.
Jet energy corrections are derived from simulation to bring the measured response of jets to that of particle level jets on average. In situ measurements of the momentum balance in dijet, photon+jet, Z+jet, and multijet events are used to determine any residual differences between the jet energy scale in data and in simulation, and appropriate corrections are made
[
26
]
.
Additional selection criteria are applied to each jet to remove jets potentially dominated by anomalous contributions from various subdetector components or reconstruction failures
[
43
]
.

In order to suppress the background originating from leptonic decays of the top quark, events are vetoed if they contain an isolated electron (muon) candidate with
\pt>15\;(10)\GeV
and
\abs{\eta}<2.4
. Criteria for identifying these objects include passing a multivariate identification discriminant and passing selections on isolation and impact parameter with respect to the primary interaction vertex observables
[
45
,
33
]
.

Jets originating from a bottom quark (
\PQb
jets) are identified using the
DeepJet
algorithm
[
15
]
and the loose or medium
\PQb
tagging working point
[
30
]
.
The working points are defined by the rate of jets that are initiated from light flavor (up, down, or strange) quarks or gluons, but are incorrectly identified as
\PQb
jets.
The loose and medium working points are defined such that the misidentification rates of light flavor and gluon (charm) jets are about 10 and 1 (40 and 10)%, respectively.
The loose (medium) working point has a
\PQb
tagging efficiency in the range 85-90 (68-78)%, depending on the data-taking year.

0.4
Simulated samples

Signal samples have been generated at leading-order (LO) in perturbative quantum chromodynamics (QCD) using
\MGvATNLO

[
4
]
2.4.2 (2.6.0) for 2016 (2017–2018).
Signal events are generated with the width of the X and Y scalar resonances set to 1
\MeV
, which is negligible compared to the experimental resolution.
The parton distribution function (PDF) set NNPDF3.0 with next-to-LO (NLO) accuracy
[
12
]
and NNPDF3.1 with next-to-NLO accuracy
[
13
]
are used in the signal generation in 2016 and 2017–2018 samples, respectively.

Background samples are generated to qualitatively assess the processes that contribute to the background composition and to optimize the event selection. However, the analysis uses control samples in data to model the background, described in Section
0.6
.
Simulated events from QCD multijet processes are generated with
\MADGRAPH
at LO precision, with up to four additional partons.
The production of pairs of top quarks is simulated at NLO precision with
\POWHEG
2.0
[
3
,
73
]
, and events are normalized to the next-to-NLO precision theoretical cross section
[
59
]
.
Events with a
\PZ
boson pair decaying into hadrons, with up to one additional jet emitted at the matrix element level, are simulated with
\MADGRAPH
at NLO precision and normalized to the cross section at NLO precision
[
18
]
.

For all simulated samples, the parton shower and hadronization is performed with
\PYTHIA
version 8.226 (2016) and 8.230 (2017–2018)
[
77
]
, and
\GEANTfour

[
2
]
is used to model the CMS detector response.

Contributions due to pileup are simulated and added to the signal and background samples.
The simulated events, including both the signal and background samples, are weighted to match the number of pileup observed in the data.

0.5
Event selection and categorization

Events passing a combination of L1 triggers that require the presence of one or more jets are of interest to this analysis; between 5 and 18 L1 trigger algorithms are used in each individual CMS data-taking period. A range of jet
\pt
and
\HT
(the scalar sum of the
\pt
of all jets in the event) thresholds are used to select events.
The L1 triggers requiring at least one jet in the event have a
\pt
threshold on a jet ranging from 170 to 200
\GeV
.
Triggers based on
\HT
selection use values for the threshold from 280 to 500
\GeV
.
The L1 triggers requiring four jets have
\HT
thresholds ranging from 250 to 340
\GeV
with individual
\pt
selection for each jet; the minimum
\pt
requirement can be as low as 35
\GeV
for the lowest
\pt
jet.
Other L1 trigger algorithms are also utilized, including those with requirements for at least two or at least three jets.

Dedicated HLT algorithms targeting the signal process have been used to refine the trigger-level selection. The HLT criteria include selections on the
\pt
and the
\HT
of the
\PQb
tagged jets in the event.
Each HLT algorithm requires the presence of at least three
\PQb
tagged jets.
The Combined Secondary Vertex (
CSV
) algorithm
[
30
]
(2016–2017) and the
DeepCSV
algorithm
[
15
]
(2018) are used for
\PQb
tagging at the HLT level.
The thresholds of the
\pt
and
\HT
selection criteria were increased in 2017–2018, with respect to those in 2016, to sustain data-taking in higher pileup conditions.
An offline selection at 90% of the HLT turn-on curves, close to the plateau where the online selection reaches 100% efficiency, for the
\pt
and
\HT
of
\PQb
tagged jets is made. These offline selection criteria are listed in Table

To reduce the number of physics objects that are poorly reconstructed, jets with

[

To take into account undetected energy carried by a neutrino originating from

The four jets with the highest

\Delta R=\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\Delta\eta)^{2}+(\Delta\phi)^{2}}}

Two datasets are created with different

The main dataset (4

\PQb

The H and Y candidates are reconstructed by pairing the four jets in the 3
\PQb
and 4
\PQb
samples. Out of the possible jet pairs, the one with an invariant mass closest to 125
\GeV
is selected as the
\PH
candidate and the remaining pair is the Y candidate, with mass
m_{\text{Yreco}}
. As a consequence of this choice all events used in this analysis will meet the condition
\abs{m_{\text{Yreco}}-125\GeV}>\abs{m_{\text{Hreco}}-125\GeV}
. The efficiency of correctly paired candidates is determined using the simulated samples.
The angular separation
\Delta R
between the generated Higgs boson (generated Y) and the reconstructed Higgs boson (reconstructed Y) candidate is calculated. The candidates are considered correctly paired if
\Delta R<0.25
.
The efficiency ranges from 77.9 to 99.5%, depending on the signal mass hypothesis, where the efficiency increases as the difference
m_{\text{Xreco}}-m_{\text{Yreco}}
becomes larger.

For both the 3
\PQb
and 4
\PQb
samples, events are subdivided into analysis regions that depend on the reconstructed mass of the Higgs boson candidate (
m_{\text{Hreco}}
). The signal region (SR) is defined by
|m_{\text{Hreco}}-125|<20\GeV
, the validation region (VR) by
20<|m_{\text{Hreco}}-125|<30\GeV
, and control region (CR) by
30<\abs{m_{\text{Hreco}}-125}<60\GeV
.
The search for signal is performed in the SR of the 4
\PQb
sample, SR(4
\PQb
).

After the analysis regions are defined, a kinematic constraint is imposed on the
\PQb
jets forming the
\PH
candidate. The
\eta
,
\phi
, and
\pt
of the
\PQb
jets are adjusted within their uncertainties to enforce the mass of the
\PH
candidate to be 125
\GeV
. The
\PX
candidate is then reconstructed with the updated
\PH
candidate. This technique is described in Ref.
[
60
]
.

0.6
Background modeling

The search for a signal in SR(4
\PQb
) requires an estimation of the expected background in that region. The background composition is dominated by QCD multijet (
\sim
90%) and
\ttbar
(
\sim
10%) events, with the relative contributions estimated using the simulated samples discussed in Section
0.4
. The modeling of the background is done using control samples in data, based on the two-dimensional distribution of 3
\PQb
events in the (
m_{\text{Xreco}}
,
m_{\text{Yreco}}
) space. This approach inclusively accounts for all processes that contribute to the background. The background modeling is performed separately for all three data-taking years to account for the different event selection criteria and trigger algorithms.

A BDT-based reweighting method, described in Ref.
[
76
]
, is used to weight events in the background-dominated 3
\PQb
sample to effectively model the differences between the 3
\PQb
and 4
\PQb
samples as a function of multiple observables.

The BDT is trained with collision data in the CR(3
\PQb
) and CR(4
\PQb
).
The obtained weights take into account both residual differences in the event yield normalization and the differences in the shapes of the kinematic distributions between the 4
\PQb
and 3
\PQb
samples.
The ratio of the event yields of the 3
\PQb
to 4
\PQb
samples ranges between 2.19 to 2.55, depending on data-taking year. The differences in the yields for each year depend on the trigger algorithms and the
\PQb
tagging efficiencies.

Variables used as input to the BDT are:
m_{\text{Xreco}}
,
m_{\text{Yreco}}
, the
\pt
of all four selected
\PQb
jet candidates, the
\pt
and
\eta

of both the reconstructed H (
\PH_{\text{reco}}
) and reconstructed Y (
\HepParticle{Y}{}{}_{\text{reco}}
) candidates,
the angular separation
\Delta R
between the two jets that form
\PH_{\text{reco}}
,
and
\Delta R
between the two jets that form
\HepParticle{Y}{}{}_{\text{reco}}
.
Additionally, the orthogonal distance of the event in the (
m_{\text{Xreco}}
,
m_{\text{Yreco}}
) plane from the kinematic boundary defined by
m_{\text{Xreco}}-m_{\text{Yreco}}=125\GeV
is included as an input variable. This variable is used to provide correlated
m_{\text{Xreco}}
and
m_{\text{Yreco}}
information to the BDT during training.

To optimize the training parameters of the BDT model, data in the CR(4
\PQb
) are compared with the data in CR(3
\PQb
) after the reweighting is applied. This step makes use of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) distance test
[
67
]
, as well as a dedicated BDT discriminator trained to separate the two samples. After optimization, both techniques indicate that the reweighting model performs well. The KS metrics for each reweighted variable decrease, approaching zero, and the areas under the receiver operating characteristic curves of the discriminator are equal to 0.5, indicating the reweighted sample can no longer be distinguished from the target sample.

As a consequence of the
\PQb

95<m_{\text{Yreco}}<155\GeV

\PQb
and 4
\PQb

m_{\PH}
of 205
\GeV

205

0.7
Systematic uncertainties

Systematic uncertainties are introduced by two components of the analysis: the simulated signal and the background estimate, which relies on control samples in data. Systematic uncertainties in the signal expectations arise from discrepancies between simulation and data, due in part to imperfect modeling of the detector response and the limited number of events in the simulated samples.
These correspond to uncertainties that arise from the trigger efficiency,
\PQb
tagging efficiency,
\PQb
tagging energy regression smearing, jet energy scale, jet energy resolution, pileup reweighting, PDF, parton shower, and integrated luminosity.
The dominant sources of uncertainty for the signal hypotheses arise from the trigger and
\PQb
tagging efficiencies.
The trigger efficiency is the largest contributor to the total uncertainty for signals close to the kinematic boundary in the (
m_{\text{Xreco}}
,
m_{\text{Yreco}}
) plane, defined by
m_{\text{Xreco}}-m_{\text{Yreco}}=125\GeV
.

The difference between the trigger efficiency in data and simulated events, referred to as a scale factor, is determined by measuring the efficiency in a
\ttbar
-dominated data sample and the corresponding simulated sample.
The uncertainties in the trigger scale factor are implemented as the trigger efficiency systematic uncertainty for the signal events.
This component of the uncertainty is higher in the region shortly before the efficiency plateaus to its maximum, leading to the larger impact along the
m_{\text{Xreco}}-m_{\text{Yreco}}=125\GeV
kinematic boundary in the (
m_{\text{Xreco}}
,
m_{\text{Yreco}}
) plane.
Inaccuracy in the trigger efficiency measurements contributes up to 30% of the total uncertainty, depending on the signal and data-taking year.
The uncertainties in the
\PQb
tagging
DeepJet
discriminants are accounted for following Ref.
[
15
]
. This uncertainty contributes up to 30% of the total uncertainty, with variations based on the signal and the corresponding data-taking year.
Jet energy scale and resolution uncertainties are taken into account by varying the jet properties within their uncertainties.
A shape uncertainty associated with the
\PQb
jet energy regression smearing is determined by shifting the jet resolution up and down by 10% and rerunning the analysis procedures.
The uncertainty in the pileup model is determined by varying the inelastic pp cross section by 4.6%
[
31
]
.
The PDF uncertainties are based on the estimation performed in Ref.
[
36
]
and range from 1 to 3%, increasing linearly as a function of the
m_{\text{Xreco}}
value of the signal. The parton shower uncertainty of about 7% is derived as in Ref.
[
48
]
.
The integrated luminosities for the 2016, 2017, and 2018 data-taking years have 1.2–2.5% individual uncertainties
[
46
,
29
,
37
]
, with an overall uncertainty of 1.6%. The systematic uncertainties are treated as uncorrelated across the data-taking years, except for the PDF and parton shower uncertainties.

Given that the search covers a large range of masses, the uncertainty on the background estimation depends on the mass hypothesis being considered.
The dominant source of background systematic uncertainty arises from the limited number of data events in the SR(3
\PQb
) region. These uncertainties are uncorrelated across the individual bins of the background model, and propagated as bin-by-bin Poisson uncertainties from the SR(3
\PQb
) to the SR(4
\PQb
). This source of uncertainty contributes up to 95% of the total background uncertainty, depending on the signal and data-taking year. The uncertainty on the 4
\PQb
-to-3
\PQb
normalization factor is determined by comparing the yields in the 4
\PQb
-to-3b VRs and contributes at most 25% of the total uncertainty for any given signal. The potential dependence of the background estimate on the selected boundaries of the CRs is determined by measuring the background in alternative CRs (
35<\abs{m_{\text{Hreco}}-125}<55\GeV
and
25<\abs{m_{\text{Hreco}}-125}<65\GeV
). The derived systematic uncertainty contributes less than 15% of the total uncertainty for all signal hypotheses.
Finally, a 10% yield discrepancy for the bins in the range
62<m_{\text{Yreco}}<105\GeV
and
145<m_{\text{Yreco}}<188\GeV
was observed between VR(4
\PQb
) and VR(3
\PQb
).
This is implemented as a systematic uncertainty for the bins in the range
62<m_{\text{Yreco}}<188\GeV
in the SR.
The value of this systematic is consistent with the disagreements observed in SR
205
.
This uncertainty is included in the VR plots of Fig.
2
.

Summary

This analysis presents a search for a new heavy scalar resonance (
\PX
) decaying into the 125
\GeV
standard model Higgs boson and a new scalar particle (
\HepParticle
Y), in the
\PQb\PAQb\PQb\PAQb
decay channel.
The search explores a range of masses from 400
\GeV
to 1.6
\TeV
for X and from 60
\GeV
up to 1.4
\TeV
for Y. A data sample corresponding to an integrated luminosity of 138
\fbinv
collected in proton-proton collisions at
\sqrt{s}=13\TeV
is used for the search. No evidence for a new signal is observed. Upper limits on the signal cross section times branching fraction are set at the 95% confidence level. Results are interpreted in the context of the next-to-minimal supersymmetric standard model scenario and constrain the phase space of this model beyond previous experimental exclusion limits.
