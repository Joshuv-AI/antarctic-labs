Color-Changing Hydrogels for Visual pH Monitoring of Confined Living Systems
Authors: Wei-Ting Chang, Matthew M. Hausladen, Olivia J. Armendarez, Jenevieve Kuang, Neel S. Joshi, Daniel J. Wilson, Leila F. Deravi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maintaining pH homeostasis is essential for cell viability, yet confined culture systems such as bioreactors are susceptible to pH drift, even under controlled nutrient supply. We developed a noninvasive method for continuous pH monitoring of microbial cultures using dual-dye colorimetric sensors covalently bound to pH-responsive hydrogels. A 3D-printed dual-chamber device spatially separates the sensors from active cultures, allowing only proton and metabolite diffusion across the semipermeable membrane. This eliminates the risk of sensor obscuration from cell turbidity. Quantitative pH measurements were obtained through image-based color analysis and numerical processing of the hydrogel response, resolving pH within ±0.1 units of those performed using conventional sensors. Our material strategy offers an alternative approach for in situ monitoring of confined culture environments, enabling live tracking of pH dynamics in biological systems.

Introduction

Intracellular and extracellular pH values are critical indicators of cell health, playing essential roles in physiological and pathological processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, and ion transport. In microbial cultures, pH shifts are tightly linked to cellular metabolism, − growth, , and colony formation, − making pH a key readout for monitoring cellular behavior. , This is especially true in closed systems such as bioreactors, where pH reflects nutrient consumption and waste accumulation. − In these environments, precise monitoring of pH is essential for identifying abnormal cell behavior and ensuring optimal microbial activity and consistent protein production. −

Current approaches to pH monitoring in biological systems rely primarily on electrochemical sensors, fluorescence-based probes, and soluble colorimetric indicators integrated into culture media. − Electrochemical sensors, including ion-sensitive field-effect transistors and glass membrane electrodes, provide high sensitivity and reusability; however, their size, signal drift, and frequent calibration requirements limit their compatibility with some miniaturized and parallelized culture systems. The miniaturized systems that do incorporate these electrodes are often single-use consumables, as the embedded electrode performance can be impacted by sterilization strategies. Fluorescent probes offer high spatial resolution but suffer from intrinsic limitations that restrict long-term monitoring. Continuous excitation leads to photobleaching, resulting in progressive signal loss and reduced measurement reliability. For example, BCECF (2′,7′-bis­(2-carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein), a widely used intracellular pH indicator, exhibits significant fluorescence decay within hours under continuous illumination, necessitating dye reloading that limits the experiment lifetime. Similarly, the ratiometric dye SNARF-1 (Seminaphthorhodafluor-1) operates within a narrow pH window, typically spanning only ±1 pH unit and experiences substantial signal degradation during prolonged imaging. , Applications of a boron–dipyrromethene (BODIPY)-based array embedded within an ether-based hydrophilic urethane hydrogel has overcome some of these limitations in pH sensing. Chemical modifications of the dyes extend its sensing range to 0–14 pH units, where changes in fluorescence signature can reproducibly cycle to detect different environments. However, continuous pH readings of active cell cultures were not demonstrated. Alternatively, visible color indicators like phenol red have long been used in cell culture monitoring, offering quantitative pH sensing in living systems. − For instance, the spectrophotometric analysis of phenol red containing media enables accurate pH measurements in microfluidic systems, while bright-field microscopy approaches using bromothymol blue and bromocresol green have achieved single-cell level pH discrimination. − Despite these advances, practical implementation of these dyes in materials remains limited. Weak color saturation, low visual contrast, and blurry color boundaries that interfere with cell culture media prevent reliable visual detection. − Additionally, soluble dyes suffer from diffusion or leaching as well a limited reusability when embedded in materials.

While colorimetric pH indicators and hydrogel-based pH sensors have each been widely reported, ,,− this work integrates them into a single functional platform that combines dual-dye colorimetric sensing, physical separation of the sensor from the culture via a 3D-printed dual-chamber device, and an automated image-based quantitative readout. By covalently incorporating combinations of methyl red and bromothymol blue derivatives into a poly­(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) matrix, we introduce diversified, high-contrast colors for tracking pH from 4–9 while eliminating the dye leaching from the material. ,− The color-changing hydrogels are integrated into a dual-chamber, 3D-printed device separated by a semipermeable membrane, allowing proton diffusion for pH monitoring while eliminating optical interference from cell turbidity. A MATLAB-based image analysis pipeline extracts CIE (Commission Internationale de l’Éclairage) color parameters and converts them into quantitative pH values via calibration models, enabling automated, real-time monitoring. This integrated platform supports continuous visual pH monitoring of confined living systems with continuous function over 1 week in culture, providing a versatile framework for integrating responsive materials into next generation biohybrid devices.

Results and Discussion

Dual-Chamber Hardware System

Our approach for pH monitoring necessitated an engineered environment where the sensing elements were spatially separated from the biological environment by a 0.2 μm permeable membrane that permitted proton diffusion while preventing bacterial crossover (Figure and S1). When the pH sensors were placed in the same chamber as growing Escherichia coli, the culture turbidity hindered accurate color analysis. Thus, each chamber in our design featured an optically clear window to facilitate image capture and downstream color analysis to enable a direct link between color change and pH. Additionally, Luer-lock flow ports were incorporated to enable media exchange and sample handling without exposing the entire chamber to the environment, supporting closed-system operation.

Dual-Dye Hydrogel Sensor with Expanded pH Detection Range

We selected a methyl red analogue (MRene) and a newly synthesized bromothymol blue analogue (BTBene) because their overlapping but distinct pH-response windows together span the full culture range of pH 4–9, a coverage neither dye achieves individually. MRene was prepared in a similar manner to our past report, while BTBene was synthesized via Suzuki coupling of bromothymol blue and 4-vinylboronic acid (Figure and S2). Importantly, introducing a single 4-vinyl phenyl group adjacent to the phenol ring preserved the original pH-response range of BTB while enabling their covalent incorporation into the hydrogel during free-radical polymerization, which also prevented them from leaching during sensor operation.

To identify the optimal polymer matrix for supporting dye response and proton transport, we screened three candidate matrices including poly­(ethylene glycol) diacrylate 700 (PEGDA700), polyacrylamide (PAAm), and poly­(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (PAAcAAm) by incorporating a fixed 1:1 MRene:BTBene composition into each and evaluating colorimetric performance (Figures S2 and S3). Quantitative analysis using the LCH (Light, Chroma, Hue) color space was used to capture subtle differences in the presented colors. For PEGDA, we evaluated 20% and 40% w/v formulations (20PEGDA700-M1B1 and 40PEGDA700-M1B1) spanning the composition range required to maintain mechanical strength of the material (Figure S3A,B). Both PEGDA formulations produced poorly differentiated hue transitions across the pH interval, which we attribute to their semiopaque appearance and limited permeability that interferes with colorimetric response (Figure S3E,F). Polyacrylamide (PAAm-M1B1) produced notably improved hue transitions (Figure S3C,G), but the addition of acrylic acid to form poly­(acrylamide-co-acrylic acid) (PAAcAAm) matrix formulation yielded the most distinct and reproducible color changes across the full pH range (Figure S3D,H). We attribute this enhancement to the pH-responsive swelling of the acrylic acid component, which enhances proton flux throughout the gel. This is particularly evident in the pH 5 range, where proton transport is otherwise rate-limiting. PAAcAAm was therefore selected as the hydrogel matrix for all subsequent experiments.

We next optimized the hydrogel geometry to maximize colorimetric sensitivity, the hydrogel sensors were fabricated as cylindrical pucks for reproducible molding and straightforward image analysis. We systematically varied hydrogel height (0.9, 1.1, and 1.8 mm) and evaluated its effect on color response in PAAcAAm-M1B1 hydrogels using LCH color space analysis (Figure S4). Thicker hydrogels produced more saturated colors with clearer visual discrimination across pH 4–9, owing to the longer optical path length through the dye-loaded matrix, whereas thinner hydrogels appeared more muted in their colors. A height of 1.8 mm yielded the highest visual contrast (i.e., largest ΔH) and was therefore selected for all subsequent experiments.

With both the polymer matrix and hydrogel geometry fixed, we screened MRene:BTBene dye ratios to identify the compositions that yielded the most visually distinguishable color response across pH 4–9 (Figure A). We found that the 1:1 MRene:BTBene ratio (PAAcAAm-M1B1) and 2:1 MRene:BTBene ratio (PAAcAAm-M2B1) yielded the most vibrant and distinguishable colors across pH 4–9 (Figures B,C and S5). LCH color space analysis confirmed that PAAcAAm-M1B1 and PAAcAAm-M2B1 exhibited differentiated hue transitions across their respective pH intervals, with high reproducibility across triplicate measurements and multiple independently synthesized hydrogel batches (Figure S6). For these reasons, these two compositions were used in all sensing experiments moving forward.

Characterization of pH Sensors in the Closed Hardware System

In addition to the color-rich PAAcAAm-M1B1 and M2B1 sensors used for pH monitoring, all experiments included an internal white standard (poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA)) to enable background normalization and lighting correction. This was important to compensate for minor variations in lighting during our transition to an automated workflow that integrates image acquisition, color analysis, and pH conversion in one step (Figure S7A), which compared favorably to pH probe recordings (Figure S7B). To establish this workflow, we first modeled the relationship between hue and pH across the different dye formulations. For PAAcAAm-M2B1, a polynomial fit accurately described the hue intensity changes in the pH 4–7 range (y = −1.6 x 2 + 30.9x – 57.9, R 2 = 0.998), while PAAcAAm-M1B1 followed a polynomial model over pH 5–9 (y = 0.8 x 2 – 7.3x + 92.2, R 2 = 0.997) (Figure D,E). Together, these two formulations enabled quantitative colorimetric sensing across the pH 4–9 range.

Mapping the Dynamic Range of the Soft Sensors

To evaluate the response rate of the color-changing hydrogels, pH values extrapolated from image capture were compared to manual pH readings over multiple reaction conditions (Figure A,B, S8, and S9). From this data, we assessed the latency of the sensors, which we defined as the time required for the sensor to display a color representative of the true pH value within 5% relative standard deviation (RSD) of the electrode reading. As shown in Figure C, the sensor exhibited a latency of approximately 20 min in the pH range of 5–9, whereas between pH 4 and 5 the response time extended to roughly 60 min. This delay is likely due to reduced diffusion when the hydrogel is compacted at lower pH values due to the exposed acrylic acid functional groups in the sensors. At pH >4.5, the sensors swell, which is a feature that accelerates proton flux and subsequent color change.

This reponse time makes the sensors well suited to monitor pH dynamics in batch microbial cultures, fermentation monitoring, parallelized strain screening, and engineered living material applications, where metabolically driven pH changes develop progressively over the course of hours rather than minutes. , To assess sensor reusability, we monitored the hue response in the LCH color space over four complete pH cycles between pH 4.5 and 8.5 spanning 1 week (Figure D,E). Although the absolute H values were shifted relative to those in the calibration data set due to different background conditions, the sensors exhibited excellent reusability and color stability, consistently recovering the same relative H response throughout all four cycles. Dimensional analysis of the hydrogels across pH 4–9 (Figure S10) showed that the hydrogel volume increased from pH 4 to pH 6 as the acrylic acid groups deprotonate and the network swells, then remained approximately constant between pH 6 and pH 8, indicating a saturated swelling response across the operating range. Critically, the amplitude of the colorimetric response and the difference in H intensity between the acidified (pH 4.5) and basified (pH 8.5) states was maintained across all four cycles with no significant decay. This sustained signal provides direct evidence that the covalently incorporated dyes are retained within the network, despite their volumetric changes, and are not altered, bleached, or leached during repeated operation. Together, these results support the suitability of the sensors for repeated, long-term use in closed culture systems.

Testing the Biohybrid System in Inducible Microbial Cultures

We next evaluated whether our soft sensors could report quantitative pH readings of an active microbial culture within the closed bioreactor. To test this, we engineered E. coli BL21 cells to express a urease enzyme under the control of an arabinose-inducible promoter. We expected that when cells were grown in the absence of the inducer, the urease enzyme should not be produced, so the natural metabolic pathways of the bacterium converting glucose to acidic byproducts would lower the pH of the culture (Figure A). On the other hand, in the presence of the arabinose inducer, we expected cells to produce urease, allowing them to convert urea into ammonia to raise the pH of the culture. In our experiments, cells and culture media were administered through the built-in Luer lock ports of our bioreactor. This allowed us to perform cell culture and continuously monitor pH without opening the chamber (Figure B). We initially flowed cells harboring the urease-encoding plasmid along with defined culture medium at pH 9 into the device. As expected, the sensors adjusted their color to indicate a corresponding pH of 8.94 ± 0.03 after 60 min of equilibration (Figure C). We then flowed 4 mL (one device-volume) of fresh media without arabinose inducer into the device. After 22 h of culture (acidification phase), the sensors indicated a pH of 4.72 ± 0.05, corresponding with urease enzyme activity of 0.03 ± 0.02 ng/mL (Figure D). Finally, 8 h after flowing 4 mL of fresh media containing arabinose into the chamber (basification phase), pH once again increased to 9.00 ± 0.04, corresponding to urease activity of 10.9 ± 1.05 ng/mL (Figure E).

At the end point of each phase, we monitored the effluent from the chamber with an electrochemical probe, confirming that our optical sensors were reporting pH accurately (Figure E). Across all conditions, our sensor exhibited high accuracy with RSDs below 1.80%. The largest deviation occurred at hour 2 (between pH 4 and 5), where the pH value from our sensor was 4.75 ± 0.07, and the pH value from the meter was 5.04 ± 0.03 (RSD = 5.74%). We attribute this discrepancy to the latency effect shown in Figure A,B.

Conclusion

Although miniaturized and wireless pH probes now exist for real-time pH monitoring of active microbial cultures, they remain limited as single-use sensors due to their susceptibility to biofouling or contamination; our integrated platform addresses these limitations, providing a practical solution for visually monitoring pH in closed, microbial cultures. Our automated image-based detection system converts colors into pH values using a calibrated color space guide. Testing within a custom-built mini (4 mL) bioreactor enabled quantitative pH reporting with high reproducibility under both static and dynamic conditions, maintaining relative standard deviations below 6%. Based on our data in Figure B, the sensors provide an effective time resolution of approximately 20 min across the pH 5–9 range, extending to approximately 60 min in the pH 4–5 range. This change in response time reflects the diffusion-limited proton transport as the hydrogel contracts at low pH. Despite this change in response time, the sensors responded consistently over multiple cycles. Beyond pH accuracy, the stability of the dual dye-based hydrogel sensors contributes to its practical use in long-term cultures as a noninvasive pH monitoring tool.

Experimental Section

Synthesizing BTBene

Bromothymol Blue (BTB, 0.501 g, 0.813 mmol, 1 equiv), 4-vinylphenyl boronic acid (4-VBA, 0.077 g, 0.52 mmol, 0.65 equiv), and K3PO4 (0.612 g, 2.88 mmol, 3.5 equiv) were dissolved in 8 mL of a 7:3 water/ethanol mixture. The resulting dark blue solution was degassed via three pump-thaw cycles to remove oxygen. Pd­(PPh3)4 (23.2 mg, 0.002 mmol, 0.025 equiv) was then added under argon gas. The reaction mixture was stirred at reflux (80 °C) for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, an additional equivalent of 4-VBA (0.077 g, 0.52 mmol, 0.65 equiv) and Pd­(PPh3)4 (23.2 mg, 0.002 mmol, 0.025 equiv) was introduced under an argon atmosphere, followed by refluxing at 80 °C overnight. The reaction was acidified to pH 7 using 1 M HCl and extracted with ethyl acetate. The organic phase was concentrated using rotary evaporator, which yielded the crude product. Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) Analysis

The MALDI-TOF MS was conducted using an ABI 5800 MALDI (AB Sciex) equipped with a 355 nm pulsed laser. Samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes (1:1, v/v) of the analyte solution (dissolved in acetonitrile) and the matrix solution (α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA), 10 mg/mL in acetonitrile/water (50:50, 0.1% TFA)). A 1 μL aliquot of the mixture was spotted onto a stainless steel MALDI plate and dried at room temperature. Spectra were acquired in positive ion mode with an acceleration voltage of 20 kV, a laser intensity of 60%, and an m/z range of 100–1000. Each spectrum was obtained by averaging 500 laser shots. External mass calibration was performed using a small molecule standard mixture (Sigma-Aldrich MALDI-MS Calibration Kit), and data were processed using Mass-up software.

Colorimetric PAAcAAm Fabrication

Poly­(acrylic acid-co-acrylamide) (PAAcAAm) hydrogels were synthesized using the precursor component ratios outlined in Table . For the PAAcAAm-M1B1 hydrogel, BTBene (40 mg) and MRene (40 mg) were directly incorporated with acrylamide (100 mg, Aam, RPI Cat. No. A11260), bis-acrylamide (12 mg, Bis, RPI, Cat. No. A11270), and acrylic acid (94.8 μL, AA, Fisher Scientific Cat. No. AC164252500). The mixture was combined with methanol (200 μL, 10% of the total volume) and vortexed until homogenized. The pH of the solution was monitored using a pH probe (Thermo Scientific, STARA2218) and adjusted to pH 8.5 with 10 N sodium hydroxide. The final volume was brought to 1.7 mL with deionized water. A 10% (w/v) ammonium persulfate (APS) solution (320 μL, Fisher Scientific Cat. No. BP179–25) and N,N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED, 4 mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat. Hydrogel pucks were obtained via biopsy punching, yielding cylindrical hydrogels (diameter: 5 mm, height: ∼1.8 mm). For colorimetric analysis, the dye-loaded hydrogel pucks were soaked in 100 mM Tris buffer (pH 7) for 12 h to remove unbound dyes, ensuring they were ready for further testing.

pH Equilibrium for Cell-Free Hydrogels

Dye-loaded PAAcAAm hydrogel pucks (n = 3) were immersed in aqueous buffer solutions ranging from pH 4 to 9. Buffers at pH 4–6 consisted of 100 mM sodium citrate, while pH 7–9 buffers contained 100 mM Tris. After 24 h of equilibration, hydrogel pucks were imaged using an EPSON scanner for color space analysis via ImageJ or an automated MATLAB algorithm, as detailed in the color image analysis method. PAAcAAm-M1B1 and PAAcAAm-M2B1 were used for all pH-sensing quantitative analyses.

Image Capture and Scanner Setting

Images were acquired using a commercial EPSON scanner in V19 J371A mode. For pH determination, images were captured using professional mode to ensure consistency of image setting across samples. In addition to the color-rich M1B1 and M2B1 sensors used for pH monitoring, every image included an internal white standard (poly­(methyl methacrylate), PMMA) to enable background normalization and lighting correction. All images were saved in BMP format at a resolution of 1200 dpi prior to analysis.

Color Images Analysis Using ImageJ

Images were imported into ImageJ, where hydrogel pucks were selected as the regions of interest (ROI). The images were then separated into LCH color channels, and the mean color intensity of each channel was recorded. The average color intensity across three hydrogel pucks was plotted with standard deviation as error bars.

Mini-Bioreactor Device Fabrication and Assembly

The design of dual chamber devices for the soft sensors and cellular culture was fabricated via 3D printed components, custom rubber gasket, and a commercially available membrane to prevent cell migration across chambers. Additively manufactured components (base, top, and Luer lock connectors) were produced on a mSLA 3D printer (Anycubic Photon Mono M7 Pro) using a High Clear resin (Anycubic). Luer-lock connectors were joined to the top 3D printed component using the High Clear resin as an optical adhesive. The rubber gasket was made of 50A Shore 1/64″ thick neoprene rubber sheet (McMaster-Carr, product number: 9455K285), cut with a digital knife plotter (Cricut Maker Pro 3). The polycarbonate membrane with a pore size of 0.22 μm (Millipore-Sigma, product number: GTTP03700) was fixed on the bottom component with a double-sided adhesive (3M, product number: 12SQ-12–467MP); which was cut to size using the digital knife plotter. The device was assembled with the base, gasket, and top component, and mechanically sealed with 12 stainless steel captive nuts and flat head screws (McMaster-Carr, product numbers: 92210A021 and 91841A004).

Color Image Analysis and pH Estimation by MATLAB

A MATLAB-based program was developed for image recognition, image processing, and pH estimation. The program localized ROIs using image segmentation and thresholding to extract the PAAcAAm-M1B1 and PAAcAAm-M2B1 hydrogel pucks. Masking techniques were applied to isolate the target regions, as illustrated in Figure S7A. The program then performed color space analysis. For pH estimation, the system converted color space data into quantitative pH readings, following the calibration curve described in the colorimetric PAAcAAm fabrication and precise digital pH analysis sections of the Results and Discussion, and the reliability of these readings is shown in Figures S7 and S8.

Construction of Plasmids and Bacterial Strains

A urease gene from a previously reported plasmid, pBR322-Ure, was inserted into pBbB8k (Addgene #35363) using PCR amplification followed by NEB Gibson Assembly cloning kit using the primers shown in Table S1. Once assembled, pBbB8k-Urease was then transformed into Millipore Sigma BL21­(DH10) electrocompetent cells following provider protocol. Constructs were isolated and sequenced using nanopore sequencing (Twist Biosciences).

Cell Culture

E. coli bacteria transformed with pBbB8k-Urease were grown in Luria–Bertani (LB) media, which was prepared as follows: 10 g of tryptone, 5 g of yeast extract, and 10 g of NaCl, dissolved in 1 L of dH2O. The E. coli-pBbB8k starter cultures were grown overnight with LB media supplemented with kanamycin (50 μg/mL) in a 225 rpm shaking incubator at 37̊C overnight.

Preparation of Cells in Device Experiments

Each device was sterilized using 70% ethanol for 30 min. Defined media (DM) was prepared as follows: 2.0 mL of 0.1 M CaCl2 2H2O, 5% (w/v) of amino acids, 0.5 mL of 2.0 M MgCl2 H2O, 1.0 mL of thiamine (1.0 mg/mL), 0.41 mL of 0.1 M K2HPO4, 100 mL of 10× salts containing 4.65 g NaCl, 1.5 g of KCl, 1.08 g of NH4Cl, 0.54 mg of FeCl2, 0.43 g of Na2SO4, and up to 1 L of distilled water. The overnight pBbB8k cultures were centrifuged at 4000g for 10 min at 4 °C and the cellular pellet was resuspended in defined media (DM) containing 2% (w/v) glucose, 100 mM urea, 100 μM NiCl2 and 50 μg/mL kanamycin with an initial OD600 of ∼0.3. For induction, cells were resuspended in defined media (DM) containing 1% (w/v) l-arabinose, 2% (w/v) glucose, 100 mM urea, 100 μM NiCl2, and 50 μg/mL kanamycin.

Urease Activity Assay

The collected samples were normalized to an OD600 value of 0.10 and the spent media was filtered using a 0.2 μm filtered 96-well plate and vacuum manifold. The samples were washed and vacuum-filtered using 1× PBS buffer three times. For each well, the samples were resuspended in 180 μL of phenol red solution that contained 10 mg/L phenol red in 1× PBS, pH 6.8. The samples were transferred to a clear bottom 96-well plate and a 20 μL aliquot of urea solution containing 200 mg/mL urea in phenol red solution was added and mixed by pipet. The OD560 value was measured every minute for 60 min using a Spectra Max M5 spectrophotometer as a modified protocol to previous reports. The slope changes from OD560 measurements, denoted by y, were used in a linear standard curve to extrapolate urease activity (ng/mL).

Monitoring pH of Microbes in Mini-Bioreactor

Hydrogel pucks were pre-equilibrated in pH 9 Tris buffer (0.1 M) overnight and imaged prior to the experiment. For the acidification phase, E. coli pBbB8k cells were resuspended and combined with 2 mL of defined medium (DM, pH 9) containing 2% (w/v) glucose, 100 mM urea, 100 μM NiCl2, and 50 μg/mL kanamycin. The mini-bioreactor device was incubated statically at 37 °C for 22 h, after which sensor images were captured. For the basification phase, the 4 mL of media was removed and replaced with DM at pH 4.5 supplemented with 1% (w/v) l-arabinose, 2% (w/v) glucose, 100 mM urea, 100 μM NiCl2, and 50 μg/mL kanamycin. The device was then incubated at 37 °C for 8 h before imaging the soft sensors again.

For induction, defined media (DM) contained 1% (w/v) l-arabinose, 2% (w/v) glucose, 100 mM urea, 100 μM NiCl2, and 50 μg/mL kanamycin. A 2 mL aliquot of pBbB8k-Urease cells resuspended in induced media at an OD600 of ∼0.3 was added to the “cell chamber” and a 2 mL of induced media was added to the “gel chamber”. The device was static at 37 °C for 8 h. Every hour for 8 h, pH measurements were collected and images of the hydrogels in the device were taken using an Epson Scanner. For pH measurements, a 200 μL aliquot was removed from the “cell chamber” and replaced with a 200 μL aliquot of induced media, pH 7 to maintain volume.
