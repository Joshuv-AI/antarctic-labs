The role of DPP4 in viral infections: from receptor biology to antiviral target
Authors: Catalina Aguilera-Flores, Carlos F Arias
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDipeptidyl Peptidase 4 (DPP4) is a type II transmembrane serine protease with diverse physiological roles in metabolic regulation and immune modulation. Beyond its native functions, DPP4 has emerged as a critical host factor exploited by a range of viruses to drive infection and pathogenesis. This review synthesizes the multifaceted interactions between DPP4 and viral pathogens. We examine the structural basis for direct receptor binding by coronaviruses such as MERS and PHEV, as well as the dual-receptor entry mechanism used by human astroviruses. Additionally, the review explores how viruses such as HIV-1 and HCV exploit DPP4’s enzymatic and non-enzymatic functions to modulate T cell activation and promote viral persistence. Finally, we evaluate pharmacological perspectives, including DPP4 inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, and soluble decoy receptors as host-directed therapies. By mapping these diverse viral strategies, this article provides a comprehensive framework for understanding DPP4 as a moonlighting protein and a promising target for broad-spectrum antiviral interventions.

Introduction

Dipeptidyl Peptidase 4 (DPP4), also known as Cluster of Differentiation 26 (CD26), is a highly glycosylated type II transmembrane serine protease [1–3]. It is expressed in multiple cell types [3–8]. The membrane form of DPP4 (mDPP4) is a 220 kDa homodimeric glycoprotein anchored to the cell membrane via the N-terminal hydrophobic region [9]. This 766-aa serine exopeptidase belongs to the structural clan (SC), family S9B [9, 10]. Peptidases of the SC clan have an atypical sequential order of the catalytic residues Ser-Arg-His (in human DPP4, at positions 630, 708, and 740), cleaving dipeptides from the N-terminus of oligopeptides with proline or alanine at the penultimate position (i.e., X-Pro or X-Ala) [11]. The soluble form of DPP4 (sDPP4) is also dimeric, and each monomer is formed by the 727-aa extracellular domain of mDPP4 (Fig. 1).

The crystal structure of DPP4 reveals two distinct extracellular domains: a β-propeller domain and a catalytic α/β hydrolase domain. The β-propeller comprises eight blades and two subdomains: the glycosylation-rich region (blades II-V) and the cysteine-rich region (blades I, VI-VIII). The α/β hydrolase domain contains the catalytic triad (Ser630/Asp708/His740), which is essential for DPP4 enzymatic activity. The DPP4 monomer features two openings that provide access to the active site: a side opening formed by both domains and a propeller tunnel formed solely by the β-propeller domain, between its two subdomains (Fig. 2). Structural evidence indicates that substrate entry through the side opening is necessary for proper orientation and efficient cleavage [12–14].

Viruses have evolved to target distinct regions of this complex. The spike proteins of MERS-CoV & PHEV bind directly to the β-propeller domain, using it as a primary entry receptor. HIV-1 employs a two-pronged attack; the HIV-1 gp120 envelope protein binds to the β-propeller domain and interferes with its non-enzymatic immunoregulatory functions. In contrast, the HIV-1 Tat protein binds deep within the catalytic region, directly inhibiting the enzyme’s active site and thereby suppressing T cell activation. HAstV uses DPP4 as an attachment factor, but its binding site has not been defined, and it is unclear whether the virus binds directly to DPP4 (see the main text). HCV does not bind directly to the receptor; instead, it is linked to DPP4’s indirect role in pathophysiology and immune modulation

The cell and tissue expression profile of mDPP4 is broad. As an integral transmembrane protein, it is anchored to the apical surfaces of various cell types, including the brush borders of the small intestine, proximal renal tubules, biliary canaliculi, vascular endothelial cells, activated T lymphocytes, and natural killer cells. sDPP4 usually circulates as a dimer, although it can also assemble into larger complexes [15, 16]. Biologically active sDPP4 is present in blood plasma, seminal fluid, cerebrospinal fluid, bile, and other biological fluids [3–8]. sDPP4 is shed from the membrane of vascular smooth muscle cells by metalloproteinases (MMP), MMP1, MMP2, and MMP14, and from adipocytes by MMP9 [17]. sDPP4 is also shed from ovarian cancer cells by MMP10 and MMP13 in hypoxia conditions [18]. Likewise, it has been shown that in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (TD2M), sDPP4 is shed by kallikrein-related peptidase 5 in CD4 + T cells [19]. It is unknown which other proteases may be responsible for generating sDPP4 in other cell types or under different health conditions.

Proteolytic processing of mDPP4 may be an important control mechanism with fundamental significance for the pathogenesis of metabolic diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cardiovascular disease, and obesity. For example, plasma sDPP4 levels are increased in Type 2 Diabetes (T2DM), obesity, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, cancer, renal graft rejection, and osteoporosis, whereas they are decreased in rheumatoid arthritis and pregnancy [20] In humans, the main source of circulating sDPP4 remains under debate.

DPP4 cleaves a broad spectrum of peptides [9, 21–23]. Its neuropeptide substrates include neuropeptide Y [24, 25], substance P [26, 27], vasoactive intestinal peptide, and galanin-like peptide [28]. It also processes several peptide hormones, including glucagon-like peptides 1 and 2 (GLP-1/GLP-2) [29], glucose-dependent insulinotropic peptide (GIP) [30, 31], pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating peptide [32], and secretin family members such as growth hormone-releasing hormone [33, 34], insulin-like growth factor-1 [35], and gastrin-releasing peptide [36]. In addition, DPP4 targets multiple chemokines and various CCL-family ligands (see Table 1), highlighting its central role in modulating neuroendocrine, metabolic, and immune functions.

Moreover, DPP4 can undergo several modifications, including glycosylation, oxidation, sialylation, and phosphorylation. The glycosylation-rich domain is known to bind adenosine deaminase (ADA) [51–53] and caveolin-1 (Cav-1) [54–56], whereas the cysteine-rich region binds collagen [57], fibronectin [58], plasminogen receptor (PgR) [59, 60] and streptokinase [61, 62], among others (extensively reviewed in [9, 21, 23]) (see Fig. 1).

DPP4 is classified as a multifunctional or “moonlighting” protein [63, 64], with activities that depend on both its catalytic and non-catalytic mechanisms, whether it is membrane-bound or soluble. DPP4 influences essential cellular processes, including glycemic regulation, cell migration, proliferation, and immune modulation [9, 65–69]. These diverse functions underlie its central role in the pathophysiology of metabolic, cardiovascular, endocrine, fibrotic, immunological, and oncological diseases.

DPP4 and the immune system

DPP4 modulates the immune system through both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms. Its proteolytic activity directly influences the immune response by degrading target molecules. On the other hand, it also serves a non-catalytic role as a potent co-stimulatory molecule, which is fundamental for proper signal transduction during T cell activation [70]. Besides T lymphocytes, DPP4 is also expressed in B cells, natural killer cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages [67, 69].

To perform these non-enzymatic functions, DPP4 interacts with various proteins, including CD45 [71, 72], Cav-1 [54], fibronectin [58], CXCR4 [73], caspase recruitment domain containing protein 1 (CARMA1) [54], M6P/IgFr2 [74, 75] and ADA [52]. Interaction with ADA is particularly important and has been widely studied. ADA is a 41-kDa protein that catalyzes the deamination of adenosine and 2’-deoxyadenosine to inosine and 2’-deoxyinosine. In fact, DPP4 was identified as an ADA-anchoring protein that accumulates ADA at the cell surface. There, ADA metabolizes immunosuppressive adenosine, thereby positively modulating T cell activation [76].

mDPP4 can also influence T cell activation by interacting with the cytoplasmic domain of PTP2 in CD45; this interaction brings both proteins into lipid rafts. The rafts bring CD45 into proximity with T cell receptor signaling molecules, thereby preventing CD45 dimerization and reducing its activity. The result is an increase in protein tyrosine phosphorylation, a critical step for T cell activation [71, 72].

sDPP4 interacts directly with the mannose 6-phosphate/insulin-like growth factor II receptor (M6P/IGFIIR) to regulate key T cell functions [74]. This interaction is mediated by M6P residues on the carbohydrate domain of DPP4. In T cells, this binding triggers the internalization of DPP4, a process essential for costimulatory signaling and subsequent cellular proliferation. Furthermore, sDPP4 binds to M6P/IGFIIR on the surface of endothelial cells to enhance transendothelial migration [74, 75].

Additionally, on antigen-presenting cells, mDPP4 binds to Cav-1. This interaction enhances the expression, promotes T cell proliferation, and activates the NF-κB pathway [54, 77]. Because of these varied roles, DPP4 is widely regarded as a key marker of T cell function (Table 2).

Viruses and DPP4

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)

HIV is a positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus formally classified into two distinct types, HIV-1 and HIV-2, both belonging to the genus Lentivirus, within the family Retroviridae. HIV-2 is less pathogenic and predominantly found in West Africa, whereas HIV-1 accounts for 95% of HIV infections worldwide and is more virulent and has a higher resistance to antiretroviral therapy [93].

HIV infects host cells via the CD4 receptor and co-receptors such as CCR5 or CXCR4, which are expressed on the cell surface. Most often, the host cells are CD4+ T lymphocytes, which are then destroyed by the replicating virus. Over months to years, if untreated, the loss of CD4+ T cells leads to acquired immune deficiency syndrome (AIDS), in which the infected individual becomes susceptible to opportunistic infections and cancers [94].

The HIV envelope (Env) protein is also a key mediator of cell-to-cell fusion, a process that forms syncytia. These are large, dysfunctional cell clusters formed when an infected CD4 + T cell fuses with neighboring uninfected cells. This phenomenon, observed in vitro, is thought to contribute to both viral replication and the widespread loss of CD4 + T cells observed in infected individuals [95]. The ability to form syncytia is a key feature that distinguishes HIV strains, which are classified by the specific co-receptor they use for entry. Strains that use the CCR5 co-receptor, known as R5 viruses, typically do not form syncytia and can infect both T cells and macrophages [87]. In contrast, strains that use the CXCR4 co-receptor, called X4 viruses, are known to induce syncytia [96].

In the context of HIV, circulating sDPP4 is an early indicator of clinical progression and immunological damage. During the early or primary phase of HIV-1 infection, systemic sDPP4 levels and activity drop drastically. Patients with lower levels of sDPP4 activity have a significantly higher risk of becoming “rapid progressors” [97]. The early drop in blood sDPP4 levels has been strongly correlated with the loss of CD4 + Th17 T cells (RORC-expressing cells) in the gut. This makes sDPP4 a strong candidate as a surrogate biomarker for severe HIV-induced intestinal damage [97]. Evidence suggests that HIV-1 progressors have lower DPP4 enzyme activity and a lower frequency of T cells expressing DPP4 compared to HIV-1 controllers [98].

HIV-1 gp120 and Tat

HIV-1 gp120 and Tat

The interaction between HIV-1 and DPP4 represents a sophisticated example of viral “moonlighting”, where both the enzymatic and non-enzymatic functions of the host protein are targeted to promote immune evasion. HIV-1 exploits DPP4 as a molecular switch to disrupt T cell signaling and modulate the local chemokine environment (Table 3).

Molecular interactions and immunomodulatory effects of HIV-1 proteins and DPP4

HIV-1 entry is driven by its Env protein, which mediates fusion of the viral and host cell membranes. The process begins with a precursor protein, gp160, which assembles into a trimer. After assembly, the trimer is cleaved by furin to form the mature Env spike, composed of two non-covalently associated subunits. The first subunit, gp120, mediates receptor binding, while the second, gp41, mediates the final membrane fusion [99, 100].

The gp120 subunit blocks ADA from binding to DPP4 on human lymphocytes. This inhibition, driven by the C3 region of gp120, is modulated by the HIV co-receptor CXCR4. The interaction of gp120 with either CD4 or CXCR4 is required to induce this effect [78, 101].

This functional link was elucidated by the discovery that DPP4 and CXCR4 are physically associated in a molecular complex on the lymphocyte surface, functioning as a single unit that is co-internalized upon activation. Ultimately, gp120 interacts directly with this complex at a site on DPP4 distinct from the ADA binding site, inducing a conformational change that displaces ADA [73] (Table 3; Fig. 3A1).

DPP4 can also regulate HIV-1 infection by truncating CXCL12 (also known as SDF-1α) [40–42]. This chemokine normally functions as a potent inhibitor, protecting T cells from infection by X4 strains of HIV-1. It does so by binding to the CXCR4 co-receptor, which both physically blocks HIV’s entry point and induces endocytosis of the co-receptor, effectively hiding it from the virus. However, DPP4’s enzymatic activity disrupts this protective mechanism. DPP4, whether on the cell surface or in soluble form, cleaves the first two N-terminal amino acid residues from the CXCL12 chemokine. This truncated CXCL12 exhibits markedly reduced binding affinity for CXCR4. Consequently, its ability to block HIV and protect the cell is severely diminished, leaving the CXCR4 co-receptor exposed and available for the virus to use for entry [41, 102] (Table 3; Fig. 3A1).

On the other hand, DPP4 activity can enhance the body’s defense against certain strains of HIV. R5 HIV-1 strains use the CCR5 co-receptor to enter T cells. This entry can be blocked by natural chemokines, most notably CCL5 (RANTES). Unlike CXCL12, DPP4 cleavage of CCL5 [103] converts it into a potent antiviral agent. This shortened molecule binds more strongly and specifically to the CCR5 co-receptor, making it a much more effective blocker of HIV entry than the original, intact chemokine [37, 47] (Table 3; Fig. 3A1). This enhancing effect is not limited to CCL5. DPP4 also processes other CCR5-binding chemokines, including CCL3L1 and CCL4, often increasing their anti-HIV activity [44, 45].

The HIV-1 transactivator protein Tat is a regulatory protein whose primary role in the HIV life cycle is to promote viral transcription, primarily by facilitating transcript elongation. Furthermore, it directly binds to DPP4 and inhibits its enzymatic activity [104]. This interaction, which is enhanced by the hypersialylation of DPP4, occurs via Tat’s cationic domain [79]. Kinetic studies confirm that Tat binds to a secondary site on DPP4, distinct from the enzyme’s active site. The molecular basis has been mapped to the N-terminus of Tat; the first nine residues (Tat 1–9) mediate binding, whereas the N-terminal tripeptide alone (Tat 1–3) is sufficient to inhibit enzymatic activity [105] (Table 3; Fig. 3A2).

Coronavirus

Coronaviruses are enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses in the family Coronaviridae, classified into four main genera: Alphacoronavirus, Betacoronavirus, Gammacoronavirus, and Deltacoronavirus. Several members of this family have been found to interact with DPP4, using it in different ways during their replication cycle.

Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV)

Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV)

MERS-CoV, a Betacoronavirus in the subgenus Merbecovirus, was first identified in the Middle East in 2012 as a cause of severe respiratory illness in humans with high mortality [108]. In 2013, DPP4 was conclusively identified as the cellular entry receptor for the virus [80]. Dromedary camels are considered intermediate hosts for MERS-CoV [109, 110], though the virus is presumed to have originated in bats, as a wide variety of MERS-like coronaviruses have been identified in numerous bat species [86, 111–115].

High-resolution crystallographic studies have revealed that the viral spike (S) protein binds to the DPP4 ectodomain through a highly specific, complementary interface. This interaction is mediated by the Receptor-Binding Domain (RBD) of the MERS-CoV S protein (residues 367–588) [81], which engages directly with the eight-bladed β-propeller domain of DPP4 [82, 83] (Fig. 3B).

The primary contact surface is localized to blades IV and V of the β-propeller, a region distinct from the protein’s catalytic site. The binding is characterized by extensive shape and charge complementarity, with a concave hydrophobic surface on the viral RBD accommodating a short α-helix from the DPP4 β-propeller, stabilized by a network of electrostatic interactions. This precise molecular fit is governed by several critical amino acid contacts (see Table 4). Key residues on DPP4, such as lysine 267 (K267), leucine 294 (L294), isoleucine 295 (I295), arginine 317 (R317), and arginine 336 (R336), form hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, and hydrophobic contacts with corresponding residues on the viral RBD, including tyrosine 499 (Y499), leucine 506 (L506), and aspartic acid 539 (D539) [82, 83]. The specificity of this interface is underscored by naturally occurring single-nucleotide polymorphisms in humans that alter these key DPP4 residues (e.g., K267E or A291P), which can dramatically reduce S protein binding and significantly attenuate viral replication [83, 116]. These findings illustrate how minor variations at the virus-host interface can profoundly affect host susceptibility to infection.

The clinical presentation of MERS-CoV is characterized by severe lower respiratory tract infection, yet the virus exhibits limited and inconsistent human-to-human transmission. This epidemiological pattern seems to be dictated by the heterogeneous and focal expression of DPP4 across the human respiratory tract. While DPP4 is highly expressed in the lower lungs and bronchial epithelial cells, making them highly susceptible to infection, it is often absent or sparsely distributed in the multiciliate cells of the human nasal epithelium [117, 118].

Bat and pangolin coronaviruses

Bat and pangolin coronaviruses

Phylogenetic analyses place MERS-CoV in close relation to bat coronaviruses, particularly Tylonycteris-bat-CoV-HKU4 (Ty-BatCoV-HKU4). This evolutionary link prompted investigations into whether bat-derived orthologs could also utilize human DPP4 (hDPP4), thereby strongly supporting a bat origin for MERS-CoV. Indeed, the spike RBD of Ty-BatCoV-HKU4 was shown to bind hDPP4 [85] (Fig. 3B). This was later substantiated by the first successful isolation of live Ty-BatCoV-HKU4, which replicated in human cell lines and caused a self-limiting disease with inflammation in the lungs and brain of transgenic mice expressing hDPP4 [86].

Despite this shared receptor usage, the interaction was suboptimal in human cells. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assays revealed that the binding affinity of the Ty-BatCoV-HKU4 RBD for hDPP4 was approximately 1000-fold lower than that of MERS-CoV’s RBD. In addition to weak affinity, the virus faced another significant barrier to proteolytic activation. The Ty-BatCoV-HKU4 spike protein was not efficiently processed by human cellular proteases, requiring in vitro trypsin activation to facilitate virus entry [85]. Together, these limitations represent an obstacle to direct bat-to-human transmission.

The evolutionary gap between these suboptimal bat viruses and the highly pathogenic MERS-CoV was clarified by the discovery of a MERS-like coronavirus, Manis javanica HKU4-related coronavirus (MjHKU4r-CoV-1), circulating in Malayan pangolins. Although genetically similar to its bat relatives, the pangolin-derived virus exhibits key adaptations that increase its potential for human infection. Its RBD binds to hDPP4 with an affinity approximately 20-fold higher than that of the bat virus and is comparable to that of MERS-CoV, allowing recognition of human cells [84, 115] (see Table 4). Most significantly, the MjHKU4r-CoV-1 spike protein possesses a furin cleavage site (RQQR). This motif, absent in known bat HKU4 viruses but present in MERS-CoV, allows proteolytic pre-activation by the host enzyme furin during viral maturation, enabling the virus to be competent for entry into human cells without the need for external proteases like trypsin. The infectivity and pathogenicity of MjHKU4r-CoV-1 in human airway organoids and hDPP4-transgenic mice further support the hypothesis that pangolins may serve as critical intermediate or “adaptation” hosts, where bat coronaviruses can acquire mutations necessary for efficient human transmission [84].

Structural biology has provided key insights into the molecular basis of these functional differences. Crystallographic analysis of the viral RBDs complexed with hDPP4 revealed that Ty-BatCoV-HKU4, MjHKU4r-CoV-1, and MERS-CoV all engage the same region of the receptor, blades IV and V of the β-propeller domain, in a structurally analogous manner [115].

Differences in binding affinity do not arise from different binding modes, but from variations in amino acid residues at the binding interface. For example, experiments swapping amino acids between the pangolin and bat virus RBDs identified several specific amino acids in MjHKU4r-CoV-1, such as D471 and R550, that are critical determinants of its high-affinity binding to hDPP4 (see Table 4). Modifying these residues in the pangolin virus to their bat virus counterparts reduced binding and infectivity, whereas reciprocal mutations in the bat virus enhanced affinity. These findings illustrate how minor evolutionary changes within the spike’s RBM can profoundly affect receptor affinity, thereby modulating the virus’s host range and zoonotic potential [83, 115].

Porcine hemagglutinating encephalomyelitis virus (PHEV)

Porcine hemagglutinating encephalomyelitis virus (PHEV)

PHEV is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus in the subgenus Embecovirus. It is a neurotropic betacoronavirus that primarily affects pigs but can also infect other species, including mice [119]. Clinically, PHEV can infect naive pigs of any age and cause respiratory, enteric, and/or neurologic infections [120]. Recent studies have identified DPP4 as a key binding target and a functional cofactor for PHEV entry [87] (Fig. 3B). Experimental evidence shows that both porcine and murine DPP4 orthologs bind the PHEV spike RBD with medium affinity (see Table 4). Furthermore, genetic knockout of DPP4 in cell culture significantly reduces PHEV infection, confirming its physiological relevance [87]. Structurally, the interaction site mirrors that of MERS-CoV, with the PHEV spike protein binding to the outer surface of blades IV and V of the DPP4 β-propeller domain [87].

There is a significant distinction between the PHEV and MERS-CoV interactions that lies in the role of N-linked glycans on the DPP4 surface. For example, N-glycans on mouse DPP4 act as a potent barrier to MERS-CoV entry, preventing cross-species transmission [121]. In contrast, the PHEV-DPP4 interaction has adapted to this glycosylated interface. Specific N-glycosylation sites on DPP4, involving asparagine residues at positions 229 and 321, directly bind the PHEV RBD, with the carbohydrate moieties forming part of the contact surface [87] (Fig. 3).

This interaction is paradoxical. The virus has adapted to recognize a glycosylated surface, yet the glycans also act as a partial steric shield, reducing binding strength. This was demonstrated in experiments in which enzymatic removal of these N-glycans from DPP4 increased both PHEV RBD binding and viral entry. This suggests that while PHEV has adapted to interact with glycosylated receptors, the density and specific structure of these glycans still present a partial barrier. This glycan shield appears to be an important species barrier for PHEV, with minor differences in the protein sequence of DPP4 orthologs [87].

SARS-CoV-2

SARS-CoV-2

Following the emergence of COVID-19, the scientific community launched intensive investigations to identify host factors that influence disease progression. Given the established role of DPP4 as the MERS-CoV receptor, it was quickly identified as a candidate for a potential SARS-CoV-2 receptor.

Early in the pandemic, in silico molecular docking and structural modeling studies suggested a role for DPP4 in SARS-CoV-2 infection, indicating that the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein could bind to the human DPP4 ectodomain [123]. This hypothesis gained further support from clinical observations noting an overlap between comorbidities associated with severe COVID-19, namely T2DM, obesity, and metabolic syndrome, and conditions characterized by dysregulated DPP4 expression and activity [22, 124, 125].

However, despite this evidence, the hypothesis that DPP4 could serve as a SARS-CoV-2 receptor/coreceptor has not been demonstrated. Several groups utilizing highly sensitive techniques demonstrated the lack of specific, high-affinity binding between purified human DPP4 and either the full-length SARS-CoV-2 spike protein or its isolated RBD [126, 127]. In addition, in cell culture, it was reported that directed expression of human ACE2 but not human DPP4 allowed SARS-CoV-2 spike and SARS spike-driven entry of VSV into otherwise non-susceptible BHK-21 cells [128].

Independent of the potential role of DPP4 as SARS-CoV-2 receptor/coreceptor, DPP4 might have a role in COVID-19 incidence and physiopathology by regulating glucose homeostasis [22, 123–125, 127]. However, it is not yet clear whether diabetes increases the risk of infection or its severity [110], thereby magnifying the pathogenicity of SARS-CoV-2, since both COVID-19 and diabetes share some pathological mechanisms [22].

Human hepatitis C virus (HCV)

HCV is a hepatotropic, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the family Flaviviridae, genus Hepacivirus, and it causes acute and chronic hepatitis [129]. HCV is divided into seven genotypes, and an estimated 1 to 5 million new HCV infections occur each year [130].

The association between DPP4 and HCV pathogenesis is immunopathological and is linked to the protein’s catalytic activity (Fig. 3C). Clinical studies have consistently shown that elevated plasma sDPP4 activity is a hallmark of chronic HCV infection [89]. The process depends on DPP4 cleaving essential immunomodulatory chemokines. A central player in this process is the chemokine CXCL10, which is crucial for recruiting antiviral lymphocytes to the site of infection. DPP4 removes the first two N-terminal amino acids from full-length CXCL10, producing a truncated form. This truncated CXCL10 is less effective at recruiting immune cells and may also act as a receptor antagonist. This truncated form has been shown to contribute to viral persistence, thereby promoting the transition from acute to chronic HCV infection [89, 131] (Fig. 3).

By upregulating DPP4 activity, the host response to HCV creates an environment that favors viral persistence. This has significant therapeutic implications, as administration of DPP4 inhibitors such as sitagliptin has been shown to reduce HCV replication in clinical cases. The proposed mechanism is that by inhibiting DPP4’s enzymatic activity, these drugs preserve the full-length, biologically active form of CXCL10 and other critical immune peptides [132].

In chronic HCV infection, elevated levels of sDPP4 serve as a strong negative prognostic biomarker associated with immune evasion, reduced treatment efficacy, and adverse metabolic outcomes [133]. Clinically, high baseline sDPP4 concentrations correlate with a diminished likelihood of achieving a sustained virological response to antiviral therapies and a reduction in circulating, functional HCV-specific CD8 + T cells [134]. Furthermore, HCV infection may directly upregulate DPP4 activity, exacerbating extrahepatic metabolic complications, such as insulin resistance and glucose intolerance, highlighting the potential therapeutic utility of DPP4 inhibitors in reducing these clinical manifestations [135, 136].

Human astrovirus

Human astroviruses (HAstVs) belong to the family Astroviridae, genus Mamastrovirus. This genus includes viruses that infect a wide range of mammals, including humans. HAstVs were initially classified into eight serotypes, now termed classical or canonical viruses. More recently, genetically divergent HAstVs were reported and are known as nonclassical [137]. Classical HAstVs are an important cause of global viral gastroenteritis, particularly affecting children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals. Despite their clinical relevance, the host factors required for viral entry were poorly understood until recently. A study using a CRISPR-Cas9-based genetic screening identified DPP4 as an essential entry factor for classical HAstVs [91, 92].

Experimental evidence confirmed DPP4’s central role in viral infection. Knocking out the DPP4 gene in intestinal epithelial cells in vitro significantly reduces susceptibility to infection, whereas overexpressing DPP4 in non-permissive cells is sufficient to confer susceptibility. Furthermore, pre-treating cells with antibodies against DPP4 effectively prevents HAstV infection [92].

The entry mechanism of classical HAstVs appears to involve a two-step process that, in addition to DPP4, requires the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) (Fig. 3D) [91, 92]. The current model proposes that DPP4 functions as the primary receptor, mediating the initial attachment of the virus to the cell surface [90]. Following this binding event, FcRn acts as a co-receptor, facilitating the internalization of the virus via clathrin-mediated endocytosis and promoting the subsequent release of the viral RNA genome into the cytoplasm. This entry mechanism is supported by findings that the absence of FcRn significantly delays internalization and viral uncoating [90].

The HAstV spike protein appears to mediate interactions with both host proteins, although direct binding of the spike to DPP4 has not been demonstrated. The discovery of this dual-receptor mechanism has therapeutic implications, as clinically approved inhibitors of both DPP4 and FcRn have been shown to prevent HAstV infection in established human cell lines and primary human enteroid cultures, suggesting they are promising druggable targets [92].

Pharmacological inhibition of DPP4

To ensure conceptual clarity throughout this review, it is essential to define the primary therapeutic modalities targeting DPP4. The term Host-Directed Therapy refers to the broad strategy of targeting host factors rather than the virus itself to inhibit infection or immunopathology. Within this framework, decoy receptors are large, bioengineered soluble proteins (such as modified sDPP4) designed to mimic the natural host cell interface, thereby intercepting and neutralizing circulating virions by providing a competitive binding surface. In contrast, peptide inhibitors are short, synthetic amino acid sequences designed to mimic specific structural motifs at the virus-host interface. Unlike the broad surface of a decoy receptor, peptide inhibitors bind to the binding pocket or specific interaction residues to prevent viral attachment. Finally, small-molecule inhibitors, or gliptins, target the protein’s enzymatic activity to modulate the host’s inflammatory and metabolic environment.

Targeting a host protein such as DPP4 offers significant advantages over traditional direct-acting antivirals. The primary benefit of host-directed therapy is the high genetic barrier to drug resistance [138]. RNA viruses have high mutation rates, allowing them to rapidly evade therapies targeting their own proteins [139]. In contrast, host proteins are encoded by the host genome and do not mutate under therapeutic pressure, making them stable, long-term targets [138, 140]. Furthermore, targeting a host factor used by multiple related viruses could enable the development of broad-spectrum antivirals, a critical tool for pandemic preparedness.

Gliptins and their mechanism of action

Gliptins and their mechanism of action

DPP4 inhibitors, commonly known as gliptins, are a class of oral antidiabetic drugs that includes sitagliptin, linagliptin, saxagliptin, and vildagliptin. Their approved therapeutic mechanism of action is the inhibition of DPP4 enzymatic activity. By blocking this activity, gliptins prevent the rapid degradation of endogenous incretin hormones, primarily GLP-1 and GIP. The resulting increase in active incretin levels enhances glucose-dependent insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells and suppresses glucagon release, thereby improving glycemic control in patients with T2DM [141, 142].

In the case of coronaviruses, the therapeutic rationale for DPP4 inhibition centers on immunomodulation. For example, in the THP-1 monocyte and macrophage cell line infected with a lentivirus expressing the MERS-CoV spike-RBD, the virus suppressed normal macrophage immune responses; this immunosuppressive effect was reversed by sitagliptin, demonstrating an immunomodulatory benefit [143]. The clinical use of gliptins for treating MERS-CoV is still a subject of research and development. For SARS-CoV-2, gliptins have been widely used to treat COVID-19; however, there are discrepancies in their efficacy and safety [144].

In HAstV, sitagliptin treatment prevents infection in both human intestinal cell lines and primary human enteroid cultures [92]. This evidence suggests that DPP4 inhibitors could potentially be used either prophylactically or therapeutically to combat astrovirus gastroenteritis.

The evidence for using DPP4 inhibitors in HCV primarily comes from clinical and observational studies, which suggest that these inhibitors have long-term effects on the disease. For example, a published case report described a patient with both T2DM and chronic HCV. When this patient started taking the DPP4 inhibitor sitagliptin for their diabetes, their HCV RNA levels decreased [132]. This observation was later supported by a large nationwide study in Taiwan [131]. The study analyzed the health outcomes of thousands of patients with both T2DM and chronic HCV. It found that patients treated with DPP4 inhibitors had a significantly lower risk of developing liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma) over several years, compared to those on other antidiabetic drugs.

A possible mechanism underlying these findings is that HCV infection increases DPP4 activity, thereby driving chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction. By inhibiting DPP4, these inhibitors may reduce this inflammation and interrupt the processes that drive the progression from chronic hepatitis to liver cancer.

For HIV-1, scientists aim to determine whether DPP4 inhibitors can help reduce the chronic inflammation that persists even in patients whose viral load is suppressed by antiretroviral therapy [145, 146]. Research on these inhibitors has focused primarily on safety; a pilot, randomized controlled trial in HIV-positive adults who were virologically suppressed showed that sitagliptin was safe and well tolerated, with no adverse effects on CD4 + T cell counts or HIV RNA levels [145].

The study showed a reduction in SDF-1α levels, confirming that the drug was acting on its intended target [145]. This crucial safety information provides a foundation for future, larger trials, designed to determine whether the anti-inflammatory properties of DPP4 inhibitors can be used to treat persistent immune activation and related health problems, such as cardiovascular disease, that affect people living with HIV (Table 5).

Use of DPP4 inhibitors across viruses

While DPP4 inhibitors exhibit a favorable safety profile in cardiovascular outcomes, considerations for their use as host-directed antivirals are important (Table 6). Although these inhibitors do not significantly increase the overall risk of serious infections, their role in dampening T cell proliferation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production may influence the severity of certain opportunistic infections. Comparative evaluation of DPP4-targeted therapeutic strategies and clinical limitations

Monoclonal antibodies targeting DPP4

Monoclonal antibodies targeting DPP4

A direct approach to blocking the virus-receptor interaction is to develop monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that bind specifically to the extracellular domain of the host DPP4 receptor [147]. The therapeutic principle is based on classic steric hindrance: the mAb physically occupies the viral binding site on DPP4, thereby preventing the spike protein from interacting and initiating entry [148].

This strategy has a strong preclinical foundation, with studies showing that several anti-DPP4 antibodies (e.g., 2F9, 1F7, and YS110) can effectively prevent MERS-CoV infection of susceptible cells in vitro [149]. The validation of mAbs stems from the observation that adenosine deaminase (ADA), a natural binding partner of DPP4, can act as a competitive antagonist of MERS-CoV infection [149].

Although no anti-DPP4 mAbs are currently approved for viral infections, their use is established in other fields, including animal models. Preclinical studies in rats have demonstrated the potential of DPP4-inhibitory mAbs as a treatment for T2DM [147]. Furthermore, anti-DPP4 antibody-drug conjugates are being explored in oncology to target cancer cells that overexpress the protein [150]. Current research provides a practical foundation for developing and improving anti-DPP4 mAbs to treat infectious diseases. However, this approach presents a fundamental strategic trade-off compared with the more conventional strategy of developing mAbs against the viral spike protein. Anti-spike mAbs are highly specific to the pathogen but are vulnerable to viral evolution and the emergence of escape mutants, as seen with SARS-CoV-2 [151]. In contrast, anti-DPP4 mAbs bind a stable host protein, making them resistant to viral escape, but they pose a higher risk of on-target, off-pathogen side effects because DPP4 has multiple physiological functions (see Table 6). The choice between these methods requires an assessment of the potential for viral resistance to develop versus the potential harm to host cells.

Soluble DPP4 as receptors

Soluble DPP4 as receptors

The “decoy receptor” is a distinct biologic treatment approach. This approach involves administering an engineered, sDPP4 that can circulate and act as a molecular decoy, binding to and neutralizing virions before they reach their target cells [152, 153]. The biological basis for this approach is supported by clinical research that shows MERS-CoV patients with lower sDPP4 levels experienced more severe disease, suggesting that the soluble receptor acts as a protective factor [154].

To enhance the therapeutic potential of this decoy receptor, sDPP4 was bioengineered by fusing its extracellular domain to the Fc region of human immunoglobulins (e.g., IgG1 or IgA1) [152, 153]. Concerns regarding the clinical feasibility and safety of these decoy receptors (see Table 6) have led bioengineering approaches to optimize the sDPP4-Fc platform. Mutating the enzymatically active site of the decoy receptor eliminates its native peptidase activity, thereby preventing potential interference with host metabolic signaling, such as the regulation of incretin hormones. Furthermore, the fusion of the DPP4 ectodomain to an Fc region enhances the molecule’s circulating half-life and potency, providing a stable, targeted therapeutic intervention that minimizes the off-target risks associated with long-term DPP4 inhibition [152, 153]. The pharmacokinetic property of this sDPP4 was assessed following intravenous administration in mice. The plasma half-life of the engineered sDPP4 in the slow elimination phase was 48.9 h [152].

A critical advantage of engineered DPP4 decoy receptors over mAbs is their inherent resilience to viral mutational escape. While mAbs often target specific epitopes that the virus can shield or mutate, a decoy receptor presents the exact binding interface the virus requires for entry. Recent evidence highlights the emergence of MERS-CoV variants (e.g., harboring the Q1020R mutation) that exhibit partial resistance to natural, circulating sDPP4 [155]. This underscores the urgent need for bioengineered fusions, such as sDPP4-IgA1 or affinity-optimized sDPP4-Fc variants [152]. These second-generation decoy receptors, which can be further refined by single-amino acid substitutions at the RBD contact interface [116], provide a high genetic barrier to resistance: any viral mutation that evades the decoy would likely incur a fitness cost by impairing binding to the native cellular receptor.

The decoy receptor strategy is a robust platform technology that enables rapid therapeutic interventions against emerging viral threats. Unlike the de novo discovery of neutralizing antibodies, which requires a lengthy process of B cell isolation, screening, and optimization [156], the design of a decoy receptor can begin immediately once a novel virus’s host receptor is identified. The receptor’s genetic sequence can be quickly inserted into existing Fc-fusion expression vectors, thereby reducing the time needed to develop a therapeutic candidate from pathogen identification during a pandemic.

Peptide-based competitive inhibition

Peptide-based competitive inhibition

Another therapeutic modality involves short, synthetic peptides designed to inhibit the protein-protein interaction between the viral RBD and DPP4 [157]. For example, based on high-resolution crystal structures of the MERS-CoV RBD-DPP4 complex, peptides can be designed to mimic key binding motifs of either the virus or the receptor [83]. This detailed structural knowledge enables the in silico design and optimization of peptides that can specifically bind to these sites and disrupt the native interactions.

More sophisticated designs are also emerging. While early concepts focused on mimicking the binding interface, newer strategies target allosteric sites. For example, some neutralizing antibodies against MERS-CoV bind outside the primary DPP4 contact surface yet still inhibit receptor binding by inducing a conformational change in a critical loop (the β5-β6 loop) within the RBD [158]. This shows that allosteric inhibitory peptides outperform direct competitive inhibitors because they resist viral mutations at the binding site.

Also, it’s important to notice complications such as risk of proteolytic degradation in vivo and the need for advanced delivery systems, such as nanoparticles or aerosols [159, 160].

Perspectives

The multifaceted role of DPP4 as both a viral entry receptor and a potent immunomodulator underscores its central role in host-pathogen interactions. Despite the progress detailed in this review, several fundamental questions remain at the forefront of the field. Central to these is the paradox of transmissibility: why does MERS-CoV exhibit such limited human-to-human transmission compared to other coronaviruses, despite its high affinity for the human DPP4 receptor? The recent identification of the mutation in dromedary-circulating lineages suggests an ongoing evolutionary arms race in which the virus is actively adapting to overcome natural host barriers. Closely related to this is the puzzle of enteric infections: what is the exact contribution of DPP4 in determining the tissue and species-specific tropism of HAstV, and how does this protein coordinate the transition from initial attachment to FcRn-mediated internalization? Understanding these mechanisms is vital to determining if DPP4 can serve as a universal predictor of zoonotic risk for future spillover events.

Another critical challenge is whether targeting DPP4 is a realistic strategy for human medicine: can it be selectively inhibited without compromising the protein’s essential functions? Current pharmacological perspectives must move beyond global enzymatic inhibition toward precise molecular strategies. The development of allosteric inhibitors or bivalent antibodies designed to block the viral binding site on the β-propeller domain, while preserving the catalytic triad and the ADA-binding pocket, represents a necessary evolution in drug design. This approach is vital to mitigate the systemic side effects on glucose regulation and T cell signaling that have historically limited the use of DPP4 inhibitors in non-diabetic infectious contexts. To support this effort, the adoption of human-derived nasal and pulmonary airway organoids will be essential to reconcile discrepancies between animal models and human clinical outcomes regarding tissue-specific tropism.

Finally, we must address the broader “moonlighting” paradox: to what extent is DPP4 activity a passive biomarker versus a functional driver of disease severity in emerging infections? DPP4 appears to function as a master regulator of host susceptibility. Investigating how diverse pathogens differentially exploit the enzymatic versus the signaling modules of DPP4 will provide a more unified framework for global health interventions. As these strategies are refined, DPP4 stands as a clear example of the complexity of host-pathogen co-evolution and remains a primary target for the next generation of antiviral therapies.

Conclusion

This review shows that DPP4 serves as an entry point for viruses and as a regulator of the host’s immune and metabolic environment. We have categorized these interactions into two main strategies: structural exploitation, in which viruses such as MERS-CoV use the protein as a physical handle to enter cells, and functional strategies, in which viruses such as HIV-1 and HCV “hijack” the protein’s signaling and enzymatic activity to disable the immune system.

This complex situation shows that viruses have evolved to target different functional and structural parts of this single protein. Understanding these mechanisms provides a direct guide for creating specific therapeutic treatments. The clear difference between targeting DPP4 as a receptor (which requires direct blockers) and targeting it as a modulator (which requires enzyme inhibitors). Antiviral medicine must prioritize safety and precision. Because DPP4 is essential for glucose homeostasis and T cell activation, non-specific blockade could lead to severe metabolic or immunological side effects.
